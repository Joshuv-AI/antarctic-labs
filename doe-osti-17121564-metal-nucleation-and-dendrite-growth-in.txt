Abstract
This report summarizes experimental and simulation activities conducted to characterize early- and late-
stage growth of electrodeposited metals in molten salt systems. Nucleation studies were conducted using
classical electroanalytical approaches at a variety of conditions for U, Ce, and Fe deposition. Conclusions
obtained from these studies were used to inform simulation and experimental dendrite studies. Simulations
were performed using delineated bulk and surface-sensitive parameters to characterize their effects on
growth characteristics. Qualitative agreement was noted between simulations and experimental data
collected from scanning electron microscopy images. Finally, current efficiency measurements were
conducted through electrochemical tests to provide a preliminary assessment regarding dendrite adhesion
and breakage properties. The results of this work aim to provide a holistic understanding of the factors
that drive dendrite formation in order to enable the development of operational practices for electrorefiners
that mitigate undesired salt occlusion and dendrite breakage.

1. Introduction
In pyroprocessing systems, electrorefining (ER) involves the selective separation of uranium and
transuranic metals from undesired components of used nuclear fuel. Metallic dendrites are commonly
observed during electrorefining as the elevated operational temperatures facilitate facile reaction
kinetics that promote dendrite evolution [1-3]. These dendrites accumulate at the cathode during ER
operation, which ultimately leads to salt adhesion and retainment on the outer surfaces of the deposit as
well as occlusion in inner pores. Both mechanisms ultimately introduce impurities to downstream unit
operations. Moreover, sporadic and unpredictable breakage of dendrites can also lead to process
inefficiencies [4-6].

Several approaches have been proposed to mitigate dendrite evolution including material harvesting
methods and the application of various electrochemical waveforms. One such approach included the
mechanical scraping of dendrites, deployed on the Mark-IV and Mark-V ERs [7, 8]. However, this
approach necessitates periodic pauses in operation that decrease throughput and lead to retention of
much of the occluded salt impurities. Another approach involved the use of a liquid metal Cd pool to
collect U dendrites that detached from the cathode during ER operations [9, 10]. However, material
handling and safety concerns limit the industrial viability of this approach. The use of electrochemical
pulse deposition techniques have also been explored [11, 12]. These results showed promise in
producing uniform deposits; however, additional effort is needed to fully characterize various process
conditions and their effects on growth morphologies. Ultimately, obtaining compact deposits during
initial electrorefining would mitigate the need for complex strategies in product harvesting with minimal
salt adhesion and occlusion.

A variety of dendrite morphologies have been reported in literature, highlighting the complex effects
various process parameters (i.e., temperature, bulk concentration, overpotentials, etc.) may have on
dendrite formation [5, 6]. The utilization of numerical simulations to accurately predict dendrite
morphology remains under-realized and limited to qualitative assessments. Moreover, phenomena
governing early-stage nucleation and its relationship to late-stage dendrite evolution are poorly
understood.

As such, work in FY26 focused on coupling modeling and simulations with experimental nucleation
and dendrite evolution studies. Initial model development was built using physics-informed simulations
in COMSOL® Multiphysics, while experimental studies were conducted using electrochemical
measurements and ex situ scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy dispersive X-ray
spectroscopy (SEM/EDS). Experiments were conducted on three metals, namely U, Ce, and Fe. The
experimental efforts were used to support validation of the simulations and revolved around three
interrelated activities. First, early-stage nucleation was examined and used to inform dendrite evolution.
Next, dendrite growth of the aforementioned metals was assessed and compared to simulated dendrites.
Finally, investigations of current efficiencies were conducted to qualitatively assess material losses due
to dendrite breakage or delamination during electrorefining. All these linked phenomena are important
to the performance of electrorefiner operations. A greater understanding of mechanisms governing
morphologies and deposit characteristics will facilitate improved process efficiencies as well as
minimize the potential for salt occlusion and retainment.

A summary of these efforts is included in the following report. First, Section 2 provides an overview of
prior work conducted in modeling and simulating dendrite evolution, as well as an outline of approaches
taken in this work. An overview of fundamental principles governing nucleation modes and dendrite
evolution are also presented in Section 2. In Section 3, we provide an outline of the approach taken in

this work to experimentally characterize U, Ce, and Fe deposits. Results and accompanying discussion
of numerical modeling and experimental studies are presented in Section 4, wherein a focus on the
effects of various operational parameters are presented. Discussions on current efficiency are also
presented through in situ electrochemical analysis in Section 4. In total, the work serves to identify
dendrite growth control and mitigation strategies that will be further explored in following years.

2. Modeling and Simulations for Deposit Growth
Phase field modeling and simulations were conducted to evaluate the effects of various process and
intrinsic material parameters contributing to dendrite morphological growth patterns. Of specific interest
to this work was the characterization of the nucleated site distribution on dendrite evolution. Operating at
or near the mass transport limit is known to produce branched dendritic morphologies. Directionality and
extent of branching are also functions of anisotropy and anisotropic strength. As such, these parameters,
along with several others (operating temperature, reaction rates, etc.) were of specific interest to the work
herein. Specific details regarding our work in delineating their effects in simulations are discussed in
further detail in the proceeding subsections.

2.1 Modeling and Simulation Overview
In classical electrodeposition, it is widely accepted that reversible systems (i.e., those with large i0) exhibit
dendritic growth, whereas irreversible systems prefer rough but macroscopically uniform deposits [13].
While sufficient as a rule of thumb, practical systems rarely exhibit such simplicity. The precise
morphology of the electrodeposited metal is governed by a number of interdependent variables that cross-
cut bulk, solution-phase and interfacial, surface-governed properties. For example, the apparent exchange
current density i0 is not only strongly modulated by the bulk concentration, but also by the electrode
material [3, 14, 15]. Here, it is also important to note that the electrode surface evolves rapidly from acting
as a foreign substrate in the early stages of nucleation and becomes coated with the electrodeposited metal
in the later stages of deposition (here again, apparent values of i0 evolve). Ultimately, the exchange current
density is only one such example of the many parameters that contribute to dendrite evolution. Further
representative parameters and their interdependencies are shown in Fig. 1.

Figure 1. Graph network of relevant parameters known to affect dendrite morphology.

A variety of analytical models have been proposed for characterizing dendritic evolution during metal
electrodeposition. Barton and Bockris [16] derived an analytical model (that was later improved by Diggle
et al. [17]) that assumed dendrite tips were isolated hemispheres. Another study by Despic et al. presented
an analytical model for describing the micro-scale spherical diffusion to rough electrode surfaces that
ultimately led to tip-promoted growth [18]. Monroe and Newman derived a general model accounting for
various parameters, including elastic forces and external pressure, to characterize dendritic growth by
evaluating the aforementioned parameters’ effects on the exchange current density and reduction
potentials [19]. Akolkar extended the work of Barton and Bockris to an analytical model of Li dendrite

growth to qualitatively determine current density at the dendrite tip for those grown under activation
control [20]. Additional models have been proposed to describe dendritic growth under migration- and
surface-tension limited growth [21, 22]. Dendrite onset times can also be readily extracted using the Sand
equation for metal deposition proceeding under mass transport-limited galvanostatic conditions [23].
While these analytical models provide a useful basis from which a fundamental understanding of growth
mechanisms can be derived, their applicability to complex practical systems remains limited.

Landau-type theories have been previously developed to describe the free energy of a system undergoing
phase transitions, such as those associated with solidification. Broadly, the theory builds on the Helmholtz
equation used to describe the total free energy of a system using an order parameter (𝜉) to track the
evolution of a diffuse, transient interface [24]. The aforementioned approach mitigates the need for
computationally intensive resources and mathematically rigid conditions that accompany sharp interfaces,
while tracking the evolution of complex physiochemical phenomena.

Phase field modeling, in particular, has been employed for simulations of dendrite evolution during metal
deposition in batteries [25-28] as well as electrorefining of U dendrites [29-33]. While the simulations
conducted in the energy storage domain provide a useful overview and comparison, conclusions drawn
from that work are not directly relevant to those needed for pyroprocessing applications. Shibuta et al.
was among the first to report on simulating U dendrites using phase field modeling [30, 32]. They
investigated the effect of initial deposit size and co-deposit morphology in the presence of Zr. In one study
by Liang et al., parametric sweeps of various factors affecting dendrite morphology were analyzed [29].
These factors included studies on applied voltages, exchange current densities, anisotropic strength as well
as initial seed nucleus growth. Tan et al. developed a mean field model with a specific focus on the effects
of the initial galvanostatic conditions and bulk concentrations on macroscale growth patterns [33]. Their
models provided insight as to mechanisms leading to the formation of “umbrella” growth, a term used to
describe enhanced growth rates near the salt-gas interface. Lin et al. used phase field modeling to simulate
uranium dendrites grown under various initial conditions (e.g. nucleus seed dimensions) and operating
conditions (e.g. applied potential) [31]. These studies provided a useful foundation for dendrite modeling,
but could not fully capture experimentally collected dendrite morphologies reported throughout literature
[34-37] and those observed in this work.

2.3 Modeling of Nucleation and Dendrite Propagation
Dendrite formation is promoted on account of non-uniform primary current distribution concentrating
growth at protruding surfaces of an initially rough deposit. As noted by Ibl [39] and Tobias et al. [40],
control of roughness evolution is equivalent to control of microscale current distribution. It is therefore of
particular interest to characterize early-stage growth morphologies to inform approaches towards
controlling dendrite evolution. Towards that end, we conducted nucleation studies at various operating
conditions as is presented more fully in subsequent discussions.

Chronoamperometry is a commonly employed technique to study nucleation mechanisms as a function of
relevant process parameters (e.g. applied potential, temperature). In this technique, a potential perturbation
is applied to the electrode and, at sufficiently short time scales, the measured current transient provides
information characteristic to early-stage nucleation processes [41]. The normalized current transient is
then plotted against the progressive and instantaneous nucleation models proposed by Scharifker and Hills
(denoted henceforth as the S-H model) [42]. These models are given by Eqs. 4 and 5:

These models were developed assuming the stochastic distribution of nuclei on the electrode surface,
negligible energy barriers between the foreign substrate electrode and deposited metal, as well as
negligible lateral interactions between the nuclei. Progressive nucleation is characterized by nucleation
sites continuously appearing throughout the solidification process, while the high rate of nucleation on a
limited number of sites is characteristic of instantaneous nucleation [43]. The process is illustrated

Metal deposition reactions may proceed under potentiostatic (constant potential) or galvanostatic (constant
current) conditions. A diagrammatic representation of expected responses associated with dendritic and
compact areal growth are shown in Fig. 4. When operating under potentiostatic conditions that facilitate
dendritic evolution (Fig. 4b), the corresponding current to an applied potential grows proportionally to
that of the dendrite’s area. In contrast, under galvanostatic conditions, the potential decays towards the
thermodynamic reduction potential (Fig. 4e) as a lower overpotential is needed to facilitate equivalent
amounts of current. It should be noted that an inverse response could also be observed when the potential
progresses towards more cathodic limits. This happens on account of reactant depletion as redox active
ions are consumed at a faster rate than bulk mass transfer replenishment. Such effects are beyond the scope
of this work, and are therefore considered negligible. Compact deposits, whether grown under constant
potential or current conditions, will generally exhibit a plateau or slight increase in their response.
Phenomena governing compact metal deposition growth also contribute to uniform current distribution
and mass transfer. As such, changes in measured values are minimal as seen in Figs. 4c and 4f.

 conditions assuming areal evolution as a result of dendritic and compact growth.

At sufficiently long time-scales, current transients for all potentiostatically grown deposits that were
examined as part of our experimental activities showed evidence of dendritic growth, i.e., the magnitude
of the current increased over time. A representative set of these current transients is shown in the

3. Experimental Methodologies
In order to provide validation for the modeling activities, experiments related to nucleation, dendrite
growth, and current efficiencies were conducted across a broad range of parameters. The studies examined
multiple metal analytes of interest to electrorefining systems including UCl3, CeCl3 and FeCl2. Uranium
was examined as it is the primary material deposited during normal ER operations. Cerium was also
examined as it is considered as an electrochemical analogue to uranium, albeit with distinct crystal growth
behavior [44, 45]. While this surrogacy provides an adequate approximation for bulk processes like
diffusion or homogeneous reactions, their preferred crystal structures differ at temperatures relevant to
molten salt operations. For example, at 773 K, metallic Ce prefers the face centered cubic (FCC) structure
while U prefers the orthorhombic structure [46]. As such, the utilization of Ce provides a useful case study
to compare surface and bulk processes to that of U. FeCl2 was also included as an element of interest as it
prefers the body centered cubic (BCC) structure [47]. Furthermore, all of the aforementioned metal
analytes is expected to be present in the molten electrorefining salt at variable but measurable quantities.
Specific details regarding the nucleation, dendrite growth, and current efficiency studies are included in
the subsection below.

3.1 Experimental Methodologies for Nucleation Studies
Nucleation studies were conducted in a three-electrode electrochemical cell that was housed in a binder-
free boron nitride crucible and heated in a Kerr furnace located in an inert Ar glovebox (< 0.5 ppm H2O,
< 0.5 ppm O2). Individual components of the eutectic were baked at 240˚C for at least 12 hours to ensure
residual moisture removal. Appropriate amounts of lithium chloride (LiCl, Sigma-Aldrich, ≥99%) and
potassium chloride (KCl, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.0-100.5%) were added to maintain their eutectic composition
of 58.5 – 41.5 mol% ratio. Cerium chloride (CeCl3, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), iron chloride (FeCl2, Sigma-
Aldrich, 99.9%), and silver chloride (AgCl, Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) were used as received. The furnaces
were heated to experimentally relevant temperatures using ramp rates of 4˚C/min. A schematic of the cell
is shown in Fig. 5.

 Figure 5. Schematic of electrochemical cell wherein nucleation studies were conducted.

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using 1 mm tungsten working and counter electrodes.
The reference electrode was comprised of a 1 mm Ag (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9% purity) wire in contact with
5 wt% AgCl in LiCl-KCl eutectic salt and housed in a Pyrex body. Data was collected using a Gamry
Reference 3000 or Interface 5000 potentiostat. Cyclic voltammograms (CVs) were collected using
Gamry’s “Noise Reject” sampling mode and 2 mV step sizes. As necessary, the electrode immersion depth
was modulated using a vertical translator. In chronoamperometry (CA) experiments, the electrode was
first held at 0 V vs. the open circuit potential prior to initiating the experiment by applying a bias cathodic
to the equilibrium potential. After each chronoamperogram, the electrode was swept to sufficiently anodic
potentials to facilitate chlorine evolution. Concentration gradients were then allowed to re-equilibrate
under open circuit conditions prior to conducting the next measurement.

Because of the increased rates of electrochemical reactions promoted by molten salt operating
temperatures, it was of particular importance to collect data at appropriately high sampling rates. To
identify the resolution needed for these experiments, chronoamperograms were collected using sampling
time values ranging from 10-1 s (10 Hz) to 10-5 s (100,000 Hz). Representative current transients are shown
in Figs. 6a and 6b. The initial increase in the current magnitude associated with double layer charging was
observed at t < 0.5 ms. A peak in the current transient corresponding to nucleation events was observed
for all conditions within the 0.5 < t < 3 ms range. Finally, at sufficiently long-time scales, diffusion effects
dominated as the magnitude of the current transient decayed towards zero. Note, apparent values of the
maximum current (im) decreased in magnitude when using coarse sampling times. We further note that,
in propagating the analysis of the current transient to determine the nucleation mode, the coarse sampling
data deviated from both instantaneous and progressive nucleation models (Fig. 6c). Therefore, to ensure
reproducibility in data analysis, all subsequent analysis and testing was conducted using sampling times
of 10-5 s.

 Figure 6. (a) Current transients collected at various sampling times, (b) the focused current transients at short
 time-scales, and (c) the corresponding non-dimensionalized nucleation curves.

3.2 Experimental Methodologies for Dendrite Growth Studies
Macroscale dendrite sample collection tests were performed under variable applied potentials,
temperatures, total charge passed, bulk concentrations, and metal species. As with the nucleation tests, a
three-electrode configuration was used wherein the reference electrode was a 1 mm Ag wire contacted
with 5 wt% AgCl-LiCl-KCl. Samples were grown on 1 mm working electrode rods made from W or 316
stainless steel. Cerium ingots (BeanTown Chemical, ≥99.9 %), U or U-Mo alloys, or Fe ingots (Fisher
Scientific, 99.95%) served as the anode. Non-radiological tests were conducted in binder-free boron
nitride crucibles while the radiological tests were conducted in a stainless steel crucible. All tests were
conducted in an inert argon glovebox using a Gamry Reference 3000 or Interface 5000 potentiostat. A

schematic of the cell is shown in Fig. 7.

 Figure 7. Schematic representation of electrochemical cell used to collect dendrites.

Prior to each dendrite sample collection, cyclic voltammetry was performed at a scan rate of 500 mV/s to
identify relative peak and initial reduction potentials. Chronocoulometry measurements were then
performed at predefined constant potential values. It should be emphasized that all data presented herein
were collected under potentiostatic conditions to enable fundamental studies and comparisons to
nucleation studies. Moreover, potentiostatic conditions allow for maintaining control of electrode
conditions compared to galvanostatic conditions, particularly as areal growth becomes non-negligible.
After the prescribed charge had been reached, the cathode was slowly raised above the molten salt level
but within the heated zone to allow excess salt to drain from the deposit. Recovered dendrites were
subsequently secured in epoxy prior to surface polishing for ex situ SEM/EDS analysis. Prior to
SEM/EDS, the polished samples were carbon coated to mitigate potential charging issues arising from
non-conductive salt or epoxy interference. All SEM/EDS images were collected using a FEI Inspect F50
FEG SEM microscope and collected at 10, 20, or 30 kV accelerating voltage.

3.3 Experimental Methodologies for Current Efficiency Studies
Current efficiency measurements were conducted on salts previously prepared for nucleation and dendrite
testing as discussed in preceding sections. Here again, CVs were first collected to identify reduction
potentials and operating windows of interest. Chronocoulometry was conducted at various applied
cathodic potentials and amounts of charge. Current efficiency was calculated by comparing the charge
determined from anodic chronoamperometric stripping until the current decayed towards zero. Following
each test, the electrode was cleaned through anodic stripping voltammetry. The system was allowed to re-
equilibrate by monitoring the open circuit potential relaxation. As previously discussed, the working
electrodes were 1 mm W rods, the reference electrode was Ag/AgCl, and the counter electrode was
metallic Ce or Ue.

4. Results and Discussion
Results from the combined modeling and experimental efforts outlined in Sections 2 and 3 are included
in the following subsections. First, we discuss outcomes from the simulations as they relate to nucleation
modes and initial growth stages. Next, simulated dendrites resulting from variable bulk and surface
characteristics are then compared to the experimentally grown dendrites that evolved during
electrorefining operations. Finally, relationships between dendrite morphologies and current efficiency
are discussed. These individual assessments, when combined, ultimately serve to provide context for the
metal deposits that are generated during ER operations.

4.1 Nucleation and Early-Stage Growth Characterization
To evaluate the effect of the initial nuclei distribution on the resulting dendrite growth patterns, we first
simulated representative dendrites assuming variable nuclei spatial distributions. A schematic of this
distribution is shown in the top row of Fig. 8, and the evolved dendrites are shown in the bottom row. It
should be noted here that the simulations shown in Fig. 8 were conducted under moderate mass transfer
and anisotropy conditions; simulations evaluating these effects are presented in more detail in later
sections. Nonetheless, we note that in Fig. 8a where nuclei are closely packed, the topology is dense and
rough, but more uniform in comparison to other initial seed distributions. Mass transfer to the deposit, as
denoted by the white arrows, is uniformly distributed across the surface; this is also observed with a lower
nuclei number density as shown in Fig. 8b. In contrast, non-uniform mass transfer (i.e., non-uniform
current distribution) is observed when nuclei become more distant (Fig. 8c). It is interesting to note regions
of electrolyte retention between dendritic growths, the extent of which appears to also to be a function of
initial spatial distribution. Shibuta et al. reported similar observations when simulating uranium
electrodeposition at an electrode surface seeded with an initially rough surface [30].

 Figure 8. Schematic overview of generic initial nuclei seeds and their evolution into dendrites as predicted from
 representative simulation sets. Note initial nuclei schematic is not drawn to scale. White arrows indicate
 magnitude and direction of mass transfer.

This study was extended further by examining the effect of variable anisotropy and overpotential on the
evolved morphology assuming three equally spaced nuclei (i.e., the distribution shown in Fig. 8c). As
expected, branching was observed at sufficiently high overpotentials and anisotropy levels (e.g., Figs. 9a-
d, 9h). At these conditions, narrow branching and stem growth are likely to produce fragile, breakage-
prone deposits. Interestingly, in addition to electrolyte retainment, regions of occluded salt were observed

even at low overpotentials and anisotropy (e.g., Figs. 9e, 9i). This further highlighted that measurable salt
occlusion (1) can form despite low levels of anisotropy and electrochemical driving force, and (2) is
promoted by, but not dependent on, dendritic growth. It is also important to note that mass transfer is
largely concentrated at the protruded tip of the dendrites (as indicated by the white arrows). Uniform mass
transfer was only observed at sufficiently low anisotropy and overpotentials (Figs. 9e, 9i).

 run with increasing levels of anisotropy and overpotential.

Experimental studies to inform early-stage metal deposition of Ce, U, and Fe are presented below. These
studies focused on evaluating the effect of temperature, applied potential, and concentration on nucleation
rates and modalities to inform late-stage dendrite evolution studies. Current responses were recorded as
functions of various applied potentials. Fig. 10 shows current transients plotted as such alongside their
corresponding nucleation curves for a range of temperatures and applied overpotentials. Here, all
overpotentials are referenced against the reduction potential Ei, potential where the first measurable
amount of cathodic current is observed. We note in Fig. 10a, values of tm and im at 723 K are independent
of the applied potential, whereas at higher operating temperatures (i.e., 773 and 823 K), the observed
values of tm and im increased with increasing overpotential. Effort is ongoing in FY26 to more fully
understand mechanisms governing this phenomenon.

It is interesting to note that, at low (723 K) temperatures, nucleation appeared to conform to the
progressive modality. Upon increasing temperature to 773 K, a transition from progressive to
instantaneous nucleation was observed with increasing overpotential. At sufficiently high temperatures

(823 K), nucleation modality appeared to be independent of overpotential and conformed to instantaneous
nucleation. In a report by Marsden et al. [45], all current transients exhibited instantaneous nucleation

operating temperature and overpotential also affect early-stage growth modes. While these parameters are
expected to directly correlate with phase change mechanisms, their quantitative relationship to energy

4.2 Late-Stage Dendrite Characterization
The high current densities and operating temperatures of molten salt systems further promote dendrite
evolution beyond the nucleation and early-stage growth phases. The process progresses as the initial
surface roughness is amplified and material flux is concentrated to protruding tips of the growing deposit.
Fig. 16 shows representative simulated dendrites and their growth during this phase. Note, the white
arrows (indicative of relative magnitudes of diffusional flux), are significantly more concentrated at the
tip of the dendrite compared to peripheral regions.

 Figure 16. Simulated evolution of nucleus to form a fractal dendrite morphology. The white arrows indicate the
 magnitude of the flux to the dendrite tip.

To study this phase of growth, we collected experimental samples of deposited Ce and U metal at various
stages during the deposition process. This was conducted through potentiostatic depositions targeting
various cathodic charge limits. Representative SEM images of Ce (top row) and U (bottom row) deposits
are shown in Fig. 17 for varying amounts of charge. The yellow arrows were added to serve as a visual
guide for Ce and U metal deposition sites as confirmed through EDS.
Both metals exhibited charge-dependent morphological evolution, although their growth pathways were
distinct. At low charge (60 C), the Ce deposit consisted of small, localized clusters together coupled with
thin acicular features. These clusters grew in size and developed a more pronounced three-dimensional
morphology when the charge limited increased to 150 C. Upon further increase to 300 C, the Ce deposit
exhibited a well-defined interconnected network composed of large tubular structures.
In contrast, with low amounts of charge (i.e., 80 C), the U deposit consisted of small, spatially distributed
nodules of metallic uranium. It is interesting to note that these nodules were of approximately the same
radial size as those calculated in the preceding section. However, given experimental time scales (~1 hr)
and quantity of charge passed, it is unlikely that these nodules are nucleated sites as described above.
Rather, it is hypothesized that these nodules of U metal are growing on a larger deposit hidden by retained
salt. Nevertheless, faceted three-dimensional structures appeared when deposition progressed to 150 C.
Finally, at 300 C, a single faceted columnar dendrite was observed, and secondary or tertiary branching
was noticeably absent at this condition. While similar structures were reported by Marsden et al., the
precise conditions at which each of these samples were collected differ [34]. For example, those reported
in the referenced publication were collected on a Ta working electrode. Therefore, while the applied
overpotential was much larger than what is reported herein, it is likely that differences in the apparent
exchange current density yielded similar diffusional flux to facilitate similar dendritic morphologies.
Moreover, non-uniform current distribution may also locally alter potential fields to provide a wide range
of deposit morphologies.

 conditions at varying stages of growth on a W electrode. The electrode was held at the peak potential identified
 through CVs collected prior to dendrite collection. The Ce- and U-containing electrolytes contained 2 wt% CeCl3

Beyond examining effects of total charge passed, we also evaluated various properties related to mass
transfer in the bulk and their effects on growth patterns observed during metal deposition. These factors
are of interest as it is well-established that the growth of branched morphologies is promoted in mass
transfer limiting regimes (e.g., high overpotentials and operating at or near the limiting current density)
[39, 40, 56]. At these conditions, the surface concentration of the analyte is effectively zero and mass
transfer is favored at protrusions.

Towards that end, simulations were conducted to evaluate the effect of applied overpotentials on late-
stage dendrite morphology. A representative set of these simulations at varying levels of anisotropy is
shown in Fig. 15. Note, at sufficiently low overpotentials and anisotropic levels (e.g. Figs. 18a, 18e, 18i),
broad and dense growths were observed. As the magnitude of the overpotential increased, side arms
formed at 90˚ to the main branch. In contrast, branching was more pronounced at sufficiently high
overpotentials and anisotropy (e.g. Figs. 18d, 18h, 18k, 18l), wherein side branches formed at various
angles ranging from 25˚ to 75˚. At these conditions, branches ultimately converged to form envelopes
wherein salt occlusion and retainment was observed; the extent of this phenomenon increased with
increasing overpotential.

 Figure 18. The effect of applied overpotentials at various anisotropic values on simulated dendrite morphology.

The extent of salt occlusion and retainment at various overpotentials is further apparent when analyzing
the area and perimeter of the evolved dendrites. Representative graphs showing the temporal evolution of
these parameters are shown in Fig. 19. From these figures, we note that both the area and perimeter of the
dendrites (1) grew non-linearly as a function of time, and (2) decreased in growth rate with decreasing
overpotential. From Figs. 18 and 19, it can be concluded that deposits with relatively large areas and small
areas form more compact deposits with low levels of occluded salt. These dendrites are less prone to
breakage and propagate more uniform current distribution (i.e., more uniform deposit morphology and
speciation).

 Figure 19. Temporal evolution of (a) area and (b) perimeter of dendrites shown in Fig. 18. The lines indicate

The study conducted by Barton and Bockris was among the first to quantitatively investigate factors that
govern the initiation of dendrites [2]. They reported on a critical overpotential equal to 3 mV needed to
initiate Ag dendrite growth in molten NaNO3-KNO3. Various studies published since then have focused
on investigating the effects of applied potential (and similarly, applied currents). Here, we aim to gain a
more fundamental understanding of how applied potentials govern the growth patterns of Ce and U
deposition. As previously mentioned, for each sample, a constant potential was applied to the electrode
surface until 300 C of metal was deposited. Corresponding images taken from SEM are shown in Fig. 20.

Interestingly, in the case of Ce deposition, the morphologies transitioned from branched patterns at low
overpotentials to highly faceted, multilayered twin-plane structures at high overpotentials. While it is
important to note that SEM is a local measurement, the size of the Ce deposits generally appeared to be
weakly dependent on applied potential. In the case of U, faceted and columnar dendrites were observed
for all conditions examined; at sufficiently high overpotentials, branched morphologies were also
observed. The general trend in the morphology-overpotential relationship was consistent with those
reported by Marsden et al. [34, 45]. Moreover, in contrast to Ce, increasing overpotentials generally
shortened the length scales of U dendrites.

In practical settings, operating temperatures are also known to modulate a variety of bulk- and surface-
sensitive properties including diffusion and interfacial mobility. For example, Shafer et al. recently
highlighted the wide range of values reported for U3+ diffusion coefficients in LiCl-KCl [6]. Similar wide-
ranging reports of the exchange current density have also been reported [45, 57, 58]. We note that, for all
parameters of interest, this dependence likely follows an Arrhenius-type relationship wherein the
logarithm of the parameter’s value is linear with respect to the inverse of temperature. Nonetheless, it was
of interest to delineate concurrent effects of thermal scaling on fundamental properties; therefore, for the
purposes of initial model development, we assumed these parameters to be independent of temperature.
Note, in Fig. 21, dendrites grown at sufficiently low temperatures and high overpotentials (e.g., Fig. 21a)
exhibited more branched morphologies compared to those simulated at higher temperatures (Fig. 21d).
The temperature-dependent degree of branching became notably less pronounced when overpotentials

remained low (Figs. 21i-l) as dendrites conformed to dense, nodular morphologies. This was in agreement
with reports by Li et al. wherein similar observations were attributed to increased ionic flux to the deposit
at high temperatures [59]. Notably, this is also in agreement with conclusions derived from an analytical
model developed by Akolkar, wherein sub-ambient temperatures were predicted to promote dendritic
lithium growths [60]. As such, operating at higher temperatures may improve the degree of branching
such that salt removal during downstream product processing may be minimized.

Representative SEM images of Ce (top row) and U (bottom row) dendrites grown at different temperatures
are shown in Fig. 22. Note, at low temperatures (723 K), Ce dendrites conformed to acicular morphologies
that coalesced to form an interconnected, porous network. Recall from Fig. 15, nucleation rates predicted
acicular morphologies for dendrites grown from compositionally identical mixtures. Ordered porous
networks were also observed in Ce dendrite samples collected at low applied potentials and operated at
773 K. A similar ordered internal network was observed by Totemeier and Mariani in electrodeposited U
samples [35]. The precise mechanism of structured internal pore formation remains unclear but could be
related to minimizing surface energy. Nonetheless, the acicular morphology of the individual dendrite was
observed even as temperatures increased to 773 K, albeit propagating at smaller length scales. As the

operating temperature increased further to 823 K, deposits became denser with more rounded edges. This
trend is consistent with the simulation results in Fig. 21, where branched structures and sharp tips
transitioned towards denser and more rounded morphologies with increasing temperature.
In contrast, at low temperatures, the U deposits formed a densely packed network of faceted structures
with length scales in excess of 30 µm. As temperatures increased to 773 K, these features transitioned to
form faceted columnar growths propagating at lower site densities compared to that collected at lower
temperatures. Several smaller dendritic growths and various other morphologies were also observed,
namely rhomboidal and branched morphologies. Finally, samples collected at 823 K appeared to have
evolved 1–5 µm nodular growths as well as the columnar growths that are apparent in the background.
The base of this columnar dendrite appears to be approximately 10 µm in diameter; further highlighting
the fragility of these deposits. This observation suggests that temperature affects not only the morphology
of the U deposits, but also the degree of lateral interactions and surface diffusion across the electrode and
electrodeposit surface.

Surface-sensitive properties were also examined due to their effects on late-stage dendrite morphology.
Variables of particular interest to this analysis are the reaction rate 𝐿 , interfacial mobility 𝐿 , anisotropic
strength 𝑘 , and the degree of symmetry.

We first focused on changes in morphology induced by varying the reaction rate, where 𝐿 ∝ 𝑖 . Dendrites

Interfacial mobility was also examined parametrically using modeling in order to assess its effect on the
resulting morphology. Interfacial mobility is a measure used to describe the ease at which an interface can
respond to an applied potential that induces diffusional flux to the deposit. Simulations were conducted
with various levels of mobility, a representative set of which is shown in Fig. 30. Irrespective of
anisotropic levels, we note that, at low interfacial mobility levels (Figs. 30a, 30e, 30i), growth is
significantly slowed as low mobility levels resist changes to the interface. As mobility levels increase,

Quantification of all the surface properties will ultimately be important to fully describe dendrite growth
in real ER systems. Additional complication therein arises from the differing cathode materials that are
often used for the deposits. For example, studies involving uranium electrorefining have been conducted
on a wide range of electrode materials, including carbon steel [66], molybdenum [67], stainless steel [35],
tungsten, and others [1, 34, 54]. From a survey of reported literature, it is evident that dendrite
morphologies are dependent on electrode materials. As such, in addition to the dendrites collected on
tungsten throughout this work, we also collected samples on a 316 stainless steel electrode. Representative
SEM/EDS images are shown in Fig. 31. As reported in preceding sections, faceted columnar growths were
observed for dendrites evolved on a tungsten electrode. In comparison, nodular growths were observed on
stainless steel. These preliminary experimental results serve to demonstrate the complications associated
with dendrite growth induced by surface-sensitive properties; future work in FY27 will seek to quantify
and further elucidate this complex behavior.

 Figure 31. SEM/EDS of U dendrites grown on a (left) tungsten, and (right) stainless steel working electrode. The
 bulk concentration was 2.85 wt% UCl3 in LiCl-KCl and the dendrites were collected at 125 mV vs. Ei at 773 K.

Current distribution is also a key factor related to dendrite growth that must be quantified. Current
distribution is dependent on cell and electrode geometries, electrolyte conductivity, and reaction kinetics
[68]. In highly conductive media, such as molten salts, primary current distribution plays a non-negligible
role in mass transfer to the electrodeposit surface. As such, while global user-defined potentiostatic or
galvanostatic conditions are applied, local electrochemical conditions can differ greatly across the
deposit’s surface.

In contrast, secondary current distribution accounts for both ohmic resistance effects and surface
resistance, ultimately yielding a more uniform distribution. While analytical relationships such as those
posed by Wagner [68] and Tobias et al. [40] provide a necessary basis for estimating current distribution,
higher fidelity tools are needed to capture the complex multiphysics phenomena occurring during
electrorefining. Implementation of increasingly advanced numerical simulations to capture these effects
will be a focus in future FYs and will provide a basis from which to characterize mixed morphological
growth patterns.

4.3 Current Efficiency Studies
The final assessment of deposit growth involved investigation of the current efficiency associated with
metal deposition of U and Ce. This is a macroscale metric that is ultimately a result of the interlinked
nucleation and dendrite growth phenomena. Current efficiency (also interchangeably referred to elsewhere
as coulombic efficiency) is a measure of the effective current (or charge) passed through a system to
facilitate a redox reaction. It should be noted that various approaches have been proposed for measuring
efficiencies, including through comparison of the harvested metal mass to that expected from
galvanostatic measurements (more precisely named Faradaic efficiency). This involves steps in which the
metal product is separated from the adhered and occluded salt through washing or distillation steps. While
useful for downstream engineering purposes, this measurement does not provide a fundamental
understanding of efficiencies during electrorefining operations. As such, we used in situ electrochemical

tests to provide a more characteristic assessment of the deposit’s properties during active electrorefining
operations. It may be assumed, for the purposes of this work, that current and Faradaic efficiencies are
equal. Efficiency 𝜀 was calculated according to the following equation:

A number of different processes may contribute to non-ideal current efficiencies (i.e., 𝜀 < 100%). For

magnitude in overpotential. Such observations have been previously noted and attributed to parasitic
reactions involving the reduction of anodically-formed UCl4 [1, 11, 71]. However, it is important to note
that the cell used in this system operated under diffusion-limited conditions. As such, it was unlikely that
UCl4 generated at the anode would diffuse to the cathode at a sufficiently appreciable rate to facilitate
non-ideal current efficiencies within the experimental timeframe. Recall from Fig. 20, dendrite
morphologies transitioned from large columnar growths to smaller dendrites with observable fractal
growths. Not only are smaller dendrites associated with higher surface energies, but also weaker
mechanical stability, facilitating multiple pathways for dendrite detachment from the electrode surface.

These initial experiments provide clear evidence regarding the effects of dendrite growth on macroscale
observables such as current efficiencies. Further experiments are currently ongoing to determine
additional factors that affect current efficiency. Factors of particular interest include stripping potential,
down time between plating and stripping steps, operating temperature, and bulk concentration. These
studies combined with accompanying simulations will serve to provide a basis for the optimal selection
of operational parameters for full-scale ERs.

5. Summary and Future Outlook
Effort in FY26 focused on the characterization of early-stage nucleation of U, Ce, and Fe to inform
modeling and simulation efforts intended to understand dendrite growth morphologies. Towards that end,
nucleation data was collected at various process conditions including bulk concentration and temperature.
Phase field simulations were then used to capture dendrite morphological patterns as a function of various
input parameters. As expected, anisotropy, applied overpotential, and reaction rates appeared to most
significantly affect dendrite morphology and degree of branching. This was further validated through
experiments in which U, Ce, and Fe dendrites were collected and analyzed through SEM/EDS. A variety
of acicular, faceted and branched morphologies were observed. Finally, dendrite adhesion was
qualitatively assessed as a function of overpotential and related to the observed morphological patterns
observed in the SEM data.

It is important to note that the studies in FY26 were conducted under potentiostatic conditions to elucidate
fundamental mechanisms governing metal electrocrystallization. Nonetheless, from this work, compact
deposits were obtained for a number of ER operating conditions including high operating temperatures,
moderate overpotentials, or moderate bulk concentrations. Future work in simulating dynamic current
distribution and electrochemical potential fields is needed to translate these studies more quantitatively to
industrial scale processes. Additionally, precise comparisons will only be possible with further inclusion
of complex phenomena into multi-scale simulations (e.g., surface diffusion, surface tension). Efforts in
FY27 will focus on further developing these simulation capabilities and extending the studies to include
multicomponent analyses with co-deposited alloys.
