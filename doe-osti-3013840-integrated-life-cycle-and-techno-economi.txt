II. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This proposal aims at the AOI 1B and its goal is to quantitatively assess legacy coal mine
sites in West Virginia (WV) and Pennsylvania (PA), remediation, biomass development on these
sites, and the availability and potential uses of waste coal and biomass to reduce GHG emissions
and achieve the net-zero/negative goal for the regional decarbonized economy. The two primary
objectives of this project will aim to (1) characterize legacy sites, remediation and resource
extraction to assess coal refuse sites with the assistance of machine learning (ML) models, and
develop the legacy mine site reclamation strategies for biomass development with best
management practices (BMPs), and (2) conduct integrated ML-based life cycle and techno-
economic assessments (LCA-TEA) to quantify the environmental and economic benefits of
biomass cultivation on the reclaimed mine lands, and evaluate the major feasible and effective
engineering pathways for biomass-waste coal utilizations. Expected outcomes from the proposed
project include: (1) characteristics of the legacy coal mine sites in PA and WV; (2) strategies and
BMPs of biomass development on legacy mined lands; (3) a robust database and models of LCA
for biomass development and the utilization of legacy mine waste materials; and (4) integrated
LCA and TEA data and impacts of the net-zero or net negative pathways of biomass development
and waste coal utilization.

Summary of the nutrient fertility trends across the sites

1. Soil Acidity (pH)

Most sites are acidic (pH < 6).

Fayette (PA site) and Webster sites remain the most acidic (mean ~4.7–5.0).

Wyoming (Turkey Creek East) shows slightly better conditions, with some samples reaching near-
neutral (pH > 6).

2. Organic Matter (OM%)

OM is highly variable across sites.

Marion and Raleigh show moderate OM (4–7%).

Some Webster and Fayette samples have lower OM, suggesting weaker fertility in those areas.

3. Macronutrients

Phosphorus (P ppm): Generally low across all sites, with wide variation. Marion shows the most
variability.

Potassium (K ppm): Levels are relatively consistent but not high enough for optimal fertility in some
sites.

Calcium (Ca ppm): Strong differences exist — Wyoming and Raleigh soils are richer, while Fayette and
Webster soils are lower.

Magnesium (Mg ppm): Follows a similar trend to Ca, with Wyoming > Raleigh > Fayette/Webster.

4. Micronutrients (Zn, Cu, Mn, B)

Manganese (Mn) is enriched in some Marion samples, but its overall abundance is variable.

Zinc (Zn) and Copper (Cu) are low but sufficient in most sites, with occasional deficiencies.

Boron (B) is almost absent across all sites, which is a limiting factor.

5. Overall Trends

Best relative fertility: Wyoming (Turkey Creek East) and Raleigh, due to higher pH, Ca, Mg, and more
balanced nutrient availability.

Most constrained fertility: Fayette and Webster sites, which combine strong acidity with low base
nutrients.

Marion: Mixed fertility, with patches of high Mn but inconsistent P and OM.

Statistically significant:
Descriptive statistics revealed pronounced variability in soil chemical properties across the seven
abandoned mine lands (AMLs). Soil pH ranged from 3.9 to 5.4, with all measurements falling
within strongly to moderately acidic conditions. The consistently low pH across sites reflects pyrite
oxidation and limited buffering capacity typical of legacy mine substrates. Site-level boxplots also
showed considerable intra-site variability, underscoring the spatial heterogeneity of spoil materials
and reclamation histories.

Organic matter (OM) values were generally low (1–3%), indicating poor soil structure, reduced
microbial activity, and limited cation exchange capacity. These low OM concentrations are
characteristic of post-mining soils that lack organic inputs and stabilization processes. Similarly,
electrical conductivity (EC) remained within non-saline thresholds for most samples but exhibited
isolated higher values at select locations, possibly influenced by soluble salts derived from mineral
weathering.
Macronutrients exhibited strong site-specific contrasts. Calcium (Ca) and magnesium (Mg)
showed the widest ranges, reflecting underlying differences in overburden composition and
potential liming residues. Potassium (K) displayed moderate variation across sites, while
phosphorus (P) levels were consistently low, reaffirming the P-limited nature of most AML soils.
Such P deficiencies present a substantial constraint for bioenergy crop establishment unless
amended.
Micronutrient concentrations—including Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, and Al—displayed considerable
variability. Iron (Fe) and aluminum (Al) were often elevated, particularly in more acidic soils,
reflecting increased solubility of metal ions at low pH. While some micronutrients (e.g., Na, B)
remain consistently low, the broader variability in metals indicates localized risks of nutrient
imbalance or toxicity.
Overall, the descriptive statistical analysis demonstrates that AML soils are chemically
heterogeneous and frequently characterized by nutrient deficiencies, acidity, and elevated metal
concentrations. These constraints highlight the importance of site-specific soil amendments,
particularly liming and organic matter enhancement, prior to energy crop establishment.

Soil Quality Constraints and Implications for Reclamation

The results demonstrate that AML soils face substantial chemical constraints that limit their
suitability for plant establishment without targeted interventions. The uniformly acidic pH values
(3.9–5.4) reflect typical post-mining conditions and are well below thresholds required for
optimal nutrient uptake. The strong inverse correlations between pH and toxic metals (Fe, Al,
Mn) further underscore pH as a central driver of soil nutrient dynamics and potential toxicity.
Liming is therefore a critical first step in reclamation to neutralize acidity, reduce metal
solubility, and enhance overall soil fertility.

The low organic matter content and its positive associations with P and micronutrient availability
highlight OM as another key determinant of soil quality in AMLs. Incorporating organic
amendments—such as composted biosolids, manure, or biochar—could improve soil structure,
increase nutrient retention, and support microbial recovery. Enhancing OM may also stabilize
metal ions through complexation, contributing to reduced toxicity.

Macronutrient deficiencies, particularly low P and variable Ca and Mg, indicate the need for site-
specific fertilization strategies. Phosphorus supplementation will be especially important given
its limited mobility and its centrality to early root establishment of energy crops.

Nutrient Interactions and Their Role in MDS Development

The correlation analysis provides essential insight for selecting soil indicators for a Minimum
Data Set (MDS) tailored to post-mining landscapes. Variables with strong cross-relationships—
such as pH, Ca, Mg, K, OM, and Fe—serve as integrative indicators that reflect multiple soil
processes. Their significance in explaining chemical variability suggests they are suitable
candidates for inclusion in an MDS framework to evaluate soil quality for reclamation.

The clustering of base cations suggests that a single indicator (e.g., Ca or Mg) may represent
broader patterns of nutrient status, thereby reducing redundancy in the MDS. Conversely,
variables with weaker correlations, such as P and Psat%, warrant independent inclusion due to
their unique influence on fertility and crop performance.

These findings support a data-driven approach to soil quality evaluation, in which statistical
relationships and ecological functions jointly inform indicator selection.

Implications for Energy Crop Production

For establishing energy crops on AML sites, the results suggest that amending soil acidity,
improving organic matter levels, and correcting nutrient imbalances are the most critical steps.
The patterns observed across sites indicate that blanket reclamation strategies are ineffective due
to the strong heterogeneity of soil conditions. Instead, targeted, site-specific interventions—
guided by key indicators identified through MDS analysis—are likely to yield more successful
outcomes.

By integrating descriptive statistics, nutrient interactions, and soil quality interpretation, this
study provides a robust foundation for designing reclamation strategies that enhance soil health
and support sustainable biomass production on legacy mine lands.

Task 4: Enhanced Environmental Assessments

Task 4.1 Conduct cradle-to-hopper LCA and sensitivity analysis based on the NETL’s
CUBE (Wang)

 • This study aims to assess the cradle-to-hopper life cycle environmental impacts associated

 with the reclamation of abandoned mine lands and the cultivation of biomass crops, with a

 particular focus on global warming potential. The specific objectives are: (1) to compare

 the environmental impacts of cultivating different biomass crop species on reclaimed mine

 lands; (2) to identify the hotspot processes that contribute to the overall environmental

 impacts; and (3) to conduct sensitivity analyses to determine the key factors that most

 influence the LCA results.

Methodology:

 • The LCA follows the ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 standards and comprises four phases: goal

 and scope definition, life cycle inventory analysis, life cycle impact assessment, and

 interpretation. The system boundaries are illustrated in Figures 4.1.1 and 4.1.2,

 encompassing land preparation, land remediation, planting, crop management, harvesting,

 preprocessing, and transportation to the biomass conversion facility. The functional unit is

 defined as the production of 1 megagram (Mg) of dry biomass. OpenLCA was used to

 conduct life cycle analysis, employing both the US LCI and Ecoinvent databases. The

 TRACI 2.1 method was selected for impact assessment. The impact categories considered

 under the TRACI framework include ozone depletion, climate change, acidification,

 eutrophication, smog formation, human health impacts, and ecotoxicity.

Figure 4.1.2. System boundary for hardwood species.

 The life cycle inventories (LCIs) for shrub willow, hybrid poplar, and red oak are presented

in Tables 4.1.1, 4.1.2, and 4.1.3, respectively. These three species were selected for analysis for the

following reasons. Shrub willow and hybrid poplar are high-yield, low-input species capable of

growing on degraded lands, and they have been widely used in previous post-mining land

reclamation projects. Red oak (Quercus rubra) is widely distributed across the eastern United

States and is considered a moderate- to fast-growing species that is drought-tolerant and capable

of thriving in dry, acidic soils and polluted air. It has also been used in various rehabilitation efforts,

including vegetation recovery on coal mine spoils in mid-eastern U.S. states.

Significant Results, Major findings, or Conclusions:

 Overall, the results showed that red oak exhibited the lowest environmental burdens across

most impact categories, while shrub willow and hybrid poplar had comparatively higher impacts,

particularly in the climate-related and ecotoxicity categories (Table 4.1.4). Shrub willow generally

had lower impacts than hybrid poplar, except in eutrophication. No processes within the system

boundary emitted relevant carcinogens or ozone-depleting substances. The main factors explaining

the differences among the species were likely to include rotation length and growth characteristics.

Because red oak has a much more extended rotation period and accumulates substantially more

biomass, it exhibited significantly lower unit environmental impacts.

Goal and scope definition:

This goal of this subtask is to conduct integrated life cycle and techno-economic assessments to
quantify the environmental and economic benefits of green hydrogen production via biomass and
waste coal utilization in the Appalachian region. The objective is to determine whether cultivating
biomass and utilizing waste coal for hydrogen production has a lesser environmental impact
compared to conventional methods. In addition, the study will analyze the economic aspects of
both systems to gain insights into their financial implications. Furthermore, the optimal waste coal
and biomass blend ratio for achieving the lowest environmental and economic impact will be
identified via scenario analysis.

A Hopper-to-grave boundary system is adopted in the current study. It encompasses all activities
from waste coal and biomass collection and preparation, drying, gasification and cooling, syngas
cleaning and conditioning, shift reactor and PSA and hydrogen compression. First-principles
model for commercial-scale processes for H2 fuel, from waste coal and biomass, is shown in
Figure 4.2.1. The function unit is selected as production of 1000 kg H2 from biomass/waste coal.

Virginia, while press coal, characterized by high ash content, is obtained from nearby industries in
West Virginia. The material composition and properties for the red oak and press coal are

to extract one unit of fossil fuel in the future. Fossil resource use remains comparatively lower in

mixed-feed systems, with 4:1 and 3:1 ratios showing nearly same demand. When hydrogen is

produced entirely from biomass (0:1 scenario), fossil fuel resource depletion increases sharply.

This effect is attributed to the higher auxiliary energy input (e.g., electricity, drying, and oxygen

production) required to process biomass, despite the biomass itself being non-fossil derived.

Planned Activities

Development of first principles models and techno-economic analysis of hydrogen, bio-coal, and

biochar, and building materials coal refuse with/without biomass.

This section describes how process models are created for converting coal refuse and biomass

blends into hydrogen, biochar, bio-coal, and building materials. The same modeling approach is

used for each process. First principles models are built using Aspen Plus, and then heat integration,

as well as mass and energy balances, are performed. For each preprocessing step, coal deashing is

done via the column flotation method. This method is chosen because it most effectively recovers

clean coal from the refuse and has lower capital and operating costs. Hydrogen production follows

the standard process described in the literature, with deashing included here as well. Bio-coal is

made by torrefaction, biochar by slow pyrolysis, and building materials are produced using either

a chain-type traveling grate furnace or column flotation method to create final products like

lightweight aggregates, clean coal, and fly ash.

Coal deashing

Coal deashing is an important preprocessing step for any system which aims to convert coal refuse

into valuable products. Coal refuse from legacy mine sites often contains extremely high ash

levels, 50–60% or higher, containing pyritic minerals and fine clay particles, which can interfere

with downstream thermal and catalytic processes. Feeding this material directly into a gasifier or

combustor can lead to excessive slag formation, fouling of heat-transfer surfaces, catalyst

poisoning, and increased emissions of sulfur compounds. Hence, reducing the ash content before

conversion is essential to reduce environmental impact along with increasing the overall process

efficiency. Deashing in this work is done using column flotation (Vasumathi et al., 2018). Unlike

the conventional froth flotation, the latter are poorly effective for ultrafine particles because these

easily get entrained within rising bubbles. Colum flotation occupies smaller space due to its taller

structure and also enhances selectivity due to the counter-current washing zone, and controlled

hydrodynamics. This deashing stage results in two major outputs; clean coal and ash. Figure

4.2.21 is a schematic showing column flotation. While Figure 4.2.22 shows a simplified mass

balance for the base case 2 during hydrogen production. Requirements for deashing are

summarized in Table 4.2.8.

Hydrogen production

During this process, four cases 1 to 4 are developed, with coal refuse: biomass ratios of 1:0, 4:1,

3:1, and 0:1. The two feedstocks, biomass and coal refuse, are handled separately during

preprocessing. The biomass first goes through size reduction and drying to bring its moisture down

to levels that won’t interfere with gasification. It is then metered into its own feed train leading to

the gasifier. Coal refuse follows a longer path: because it goes first through the column flotation

step to remove ash and mineral content, this is then followed by crushing and drying. Once

conditioned, the cleaned coal and the preprocessed biomass are sent into the gasifier.

Inside the gasifier, the blended feed is converted using high temperatures of 1100 °C for cases 1

to 3. Case 4, which only involves biomass, is gasified at 871 °C and an elevated pressure of 30.2

bars for all cases. Oxygen from the cryogenic air separation unit maintains the necessary reaction

temperature, while steam helps drive the reforming reactions that produce syngas. The raw syngas

leaving the gasifier is cooled through a heat-exchange network before entering the purification

section. Here, particulates, trace metals, and sulfur-bearing compounds are removed to protect the

downstream catalysts. The cleaned syngas then enters a two-stage water-gas shift (WGS) system,

that is the high-temperature shift reactor and the low-temperature shift reactor. After the shift

section, the gas contains a higher proportion of hydrogen and a large amount of water vapor;

therefore, it is cooled again and condensed before separation.

Final hydrogen purification is carried out using pressure swing adsorption (PSA), which exists at

a purity greater than 99.99%. The purified hydrogen is then compressed to the required delivery

pressure. The off-gas from the PSA, which still contains combustible species, is burned in a

dedicated combustor where flue gas is generated, which in turn is used to supply heat to the

feedstock drying section, with the extra heat used to generate steam for power generation. The

composition of exit gases from the gasifier is summarized in Table 4.2.9. The target set for

hydrogen production is 2,727 kg/hr, which is set to 1,000 kg/hr for the LCA analysis.

Bio-coal production

Bio-coal briquettes are produced through torrefaction, which is a mild form of pyrolysis in which

biomass is heated in an oxygen-limited environment. The process operates between 200–300 °C,

for which in the Aspen Plus mode, a temperature of 280 °C is used, allowing the biomass to

decompose just enough to drive off moisture and light volatiles without reaching the temperatures

that lead to tar formation. The resulting material is referred to as torrefied biomass or bio-coal,

whose characteristics are like those of bituminous coal.

Producing bio-coal involves a sequence of steps which include biomass preprocessing where the

feedstock is first dried and reduced in size to ensure uniform heating. It is then introduced into a

torrefaction reactor, where indirect heating under inert conditions occurs, nitrogen may be

introduced into the reactor to ensure inert conditions. The vapors released during this stage can be

combusted to supply process heat thereby improving energy efficiency. After exiting the reactor,

the torrefied solids are cooled to 75°C, usually with a controlled quench, to prevent of the torrefied

biomass.

Once cooled, the material dried to a moisture content of 3% and further milled to a particle size of

0.5 mm, which is suitable for densification. Pelletization or briquetting follows with addition of

moisture to yield the final product that exits at a moisture content of 7% and therefore undergoes

drying. In some cases, a small portion of cleaned coal is blended with the torrefied biomass during

pellet formation. This can enhance fuel properties and reduce the biomass required for torrefaction.

Biochar production

Biochar is a carbon-dense solid formed by slow pyrolysis, which involves heating biomass in the

absence of oxygen, allowing the material to decompose slowly into a stable, aromatic, and porous

structure. Depending on how the process is designed, the final products differ, which can either be

biochar and heat, or biochar, bio-oil, and syngas. The distribution of these products is influenced

by factors such as heating rate, operating temperature, reactor design, and the overall process

objective.

In this study, the focus is on producing biochar as the main product, bio-oil, and syngas. The

process begins with biomass preprocessing, which also involves biomass drying to control its

moisture content. The biomass then enters a pyrolysis reactor operating at 500 °C, where the

volatile components are driven off, with the resulting solid component being biochar. The hot

biochar is then quenched to 30 °C to prevent any oxidation and may be milled further to 0.1 mm

to achieve the particle size needed for end-use applications.

The volatiles leaving the reactor are directed through a series of absorbers that divide the heavier

condensable organic fractions from the lighter components. The non-condensable portion

constitutes the syngas stream. A fraction of this syngas is combusted to supply the heat required

for both the pyrolysis process and biomass drying, thereby minimizing external heat demand. Flue

gas directed to the dryer is first cooled to 110 °C, which then exits at 70.5 °C. The remaining

liquids are sent to a decanter where water is removed, and the remaining liquid is the bio-oil. The

Aspen Plus process flow is shown in Figure 4.2.24. The composition of the volatiles is sourced

from literature (Cordella et al., 2012). The components not included in Aspen Plus, such as

OPEX components, given their significant contribution. As indicated in the previous sections, plant
capacity is also a major factor that plays a significant role in achieving this goal. Two cases, distributed and
centralized, were studied to explore possible strategies for reaching this goal. The distributed case, designed
to generate 50 MWe with a hydrogen flow rate of 2,727 kg/hr, was scaled up to a centralized case with a
500 MWe power output and a flow rate of approximately 27,273 kg/hr. Scenarios A-J were developed, as
summarized in Table 1, with differing considerations for each scenario. The following scenario

gasifier purchase cost reduction. Also, biomass handling costs are assumed to include only crushing costs,

the gasifier. Scenario J is regarded as having the maximum cost reductions, with all reductions accounted

for both scenarios, attributed to its elevated OPEX resulting from the higher cost of biomass required to
achieve the necessary hydrogen output due to the higher flow rates. A significant difference is that the
centralized scenarios demonstrate a reduced CAPEX, typically due to economies of scale. The CAPEX in

the necessity of operating on a larger scale to lower the LCOH since the contribution of CAPEX to LCOH
