Genome-wide integrative dissection of genetic basis of fiber color and whiteness regulation in upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum)
Authors: Shiguftah Khalid, Zhenzhen Wang, Tahir Mahmood, Xiaoli Geng, Hongge Li, Xiaomeng Zhang, Shoupu He, Xiongming Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNaturally colored cotton has gained renewed interest due to increasing environmental concerns over synthetic dyes. The genetic regulatory characteristics of fiber color are discovered by two perspectives in this study.ResultsGenome-wide association studies (GWAS) identified two major loci D11: 24.3–24.7 Mb and A07: 20.4–22.3 Mb controlling fiber color variation. Integrative QTL mapping and transcriptome profiling revealed three candidate genes Gh_D11G208100, Gh_D11G207100, and Gh_D11G207600 with distinct expression profiles in fiber whiteness on D11. In contrast, the A07 locus harbors key transcription regulators TT2, C1, and bHLH82 which are implicated in flavonoid-mediated brown pigmentation along with differentially expressed genes, Gh_A07G015400, Gh_A07G019500, and Gh_A07G019400, further supporting their potential role in regulating fiber pigmentation. Segregation analysis of fiber color across three different F2 populations revealed three gene models. The W1_W2_loci were proposed to determine fiber whiteness located on D11 chromosome, while the fiber color gene locus Lb_ determined by genetic analysis is related to the key locus for transcription regulators TT2 on the A07 chromosome. The interaction of these three genes controls fiber color. Inheritance patterns were consistent pigment loss and a graded dilution model for pigmented phenotypes, with stable extreme classes white and near white, whereas intermediate pigmentation classes shifted between populations. We also identified overlapping QTLs of fiber quality and color attributes, indicating strongly linked genetic mechanisms.ConclusionsThis integrated analysis supports a model of coordinated regulation, where transcriptional control from A07 influences pigment biosynthesis, while D11 modulates fiber surface or secondary metabolism to fine tune color intensity and whiteness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02662-z.Keywords: Colored cotton, Fiber quality, Genome-wide association, Flavonoid pathway, Inheritance patterns

Background

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is one of the world’s most vital natural fiber crops, serving as a cornerstone of the global textile industry [1]. Growing demand for sustainable products has regained interest in naturally colored cotton (NCC), which reduces dependence on chemical processing and synthetic dyes [2]. NCC usually produces brown and green fibers, with pigmentation driven by complex genetic and biochemical mechanisms primarily involving the flavonoid biosynthesis pathway [3, 4]. Research on NCCs still has a number of drawbacks despite their ecological advantages. These include a limited understanding of inheritance patterns, a narrow spectrum of available colors, environmental influences and lower fiber strength and yield when compared to conventional white cotton cultivars [5–10].

Recurrent crossing and strategic hybridization have advanced the development of new naturally colored cotton cultivars [11, 12]. Successful breeding efforts depend heavily on the activation of key structural genes involved in the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, including GhC4H, GhCHS, GhCHI, GhF3H, GhDFR, GhANR, and Gh4CL2 [13, 14]. These genes are regulated by transcriptional complexes such as MYB–bHLH–WD40, which orchestrate pigment synthesis [15]. Among these, GhTT2-3A, an R2R3-MYB transcription factor, has been identified as a pivotal regulator of brown fiber formation [16–18]. In addition, GhTT2 and GhTT16 exhibit contrasting expression patterns during proanthocyanidin biosynthesis, with recent findings suggesting that GhTT16 acts as both a dependent and independent regulator of this pathway [19].

Distinct metabolic pathways are also involved, including the activity of Gh4CL2, an enzyme involved in the retention of caffeoyl residues that contribute to green pigmentation in cotton fiber [14]. According to transcriptomic and functional studies, fiber pigmentation coincides with the secondary cell wall biosynthesis phase, indicating that coloration is temporally regulated and developmentally specific [20, 21]. Regardless of these advances, a comprehensive understanding of the genetic and regulatory networks underlying fiber coloration remains elusive. Prior research has demonstrated that nuclear genes control dominant inheritance patterns for both brown and green fiber pigmentation [22]. Specifically, brown pigmentation has been linked to two key loci, Lc1 on chromosome 7 and Lc2 on chromosome 6, while additional loci (Lc3–Lc6) contribute to variations in color intensity and shade [23].

We used a diverse panel of Gossypium hirsutum accessions with a range of fiber color to conduct a genome-wide association study (GWAS) to analyze the genetic architecture underpinning fiber whiteness and pigmentation in naturally colored cotton. This study identifies key genomic loci and candidate genes involved in fiber coloration, spanning the spectrum from white to brown. We highlight potential genetic interactions between pigmentation loci on chromosomes A07 and D11. The coordinated action of multiple genes contributes to the regulation of brownness and whiteness in cotton fibers. Integrative analyses incorporating GWAS, quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping, and transcriptome data reveal overlapping genomic regions that influence fiber pigmentation and essential quality traits, suggesting pleiotropic or tightly linked regulatory mechanisms. These findings identify actionable genetic targets for breeding programs to develop naturally colored cotton varieties with enhanced textile performance. Collectively, our results establish a comprehensive genetic framework to guide sustainable cotton breeding strategies that harmonize color diversity with fiber quality.

Results

Phenotypic evaluation of fiber color

To dissect the genetic architecture of fiber color traits in cotton, a comprehensive phenotypic evaluation was conducted using two diverse natural populations across 2 years and three locations. The study encompassed 373 white-fiber accessions and an expanded set of 403 accessions, including 373 white-fiber accessions and additional brown and green fiber variants. Cotton fibers were sampled at five critical developmental stages (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 days post-anthesis, DPA), and 27 color-related parameters were measured using a colorimeter-based platform. The genome-wide association analysis identified multiple loci associated with fiber color and whiteness. These included key indicators such as total color difference (EE), Hunter’s whiteness (WH), LL (brightness difference), Rx (reflectance coefficient), and HH (tone difference). Among these, EE and WH consistently exhibited the strongest association signals with prominent SNP peaks exceeding the significant threshold, particularly on chromosome D11 in 373 white populations (Additional file 1: Table S1) (Additional file 2: Fig. S1), whereas the genome-wide association analysis of 403 accession population the stronger and highly significant association signal was detected on chromosome A07 for EE (total color difference). In addition, a secondary but consistent association peak was observed on chromosome D11 (Additional file 3: Fig. S2).

Genetic dissection of white-fiber in upland cotton

To dissect the genetic basis of whiteness-related variation within white-fiber upland cotton, a panel of 373 white-fiber accessions was used. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) of white-fiber trait revealed a prominent association locus on chromosome D11, with a peak interval at 24.3–24.7 Mb where SNPs surpassed the –log₁₀(P) = 6 threshold, highlighting a strong link between this region and both EE and WH traits (Fig. 1a). Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis revealed strong regional haplotype blocks, further reinforcing the stability of this QTL (Fig. 1b). Haplotype analysis within this locus identified three major haplotypes, D11-Hap-1, D11-Hap-2, and D11-Hap-3, each exhibited the distinct associations with calorimetric traits such as Hunter whiteness (WH) and total color difference (EE). Notably, D11-Hap-1 was significantly correlated with elevated WH values, suggesting enhanced whiteness, while D11-Hap-3 was associated with increased EE scores, reflecting greater deviation from fiber whiteness and showing tone difference (Fig. 1c). The contrasting distribution of these haplotypes suggests functional divergence and supports the presence of independent regulatory alleles controlling fiber pigmentation [23].

The dotted horizontal line indicates the genome-wide significance threshold (–log₁₀(P) = 6). b Local association plot and linkage disequilibrium (LD) heatmap of the D11:24.3–24.7 Mb region highlighting a strong haplotype block associated with EE and WH traits. c Boxplots illustrate the phenotypic effects of the three major haplotypes (D11-Hap-1, Hap-2, Hap-3) on EE and WH, revealing contrasting allelic patterns. d Transcriptomic analysis of genes within the associated region identifying three candidate genes, Gh_D11G208100, Gh_D11G207100, and Gh_D11G207600, differentially expressed across haplotype groups

Integration with transcriptomic data further supported the identification of candidate genes within the D11 locus. Among these, Gh_D11G207100 (MAKR1, a membrane-anchored kinase-related protein), Gh_D11G207600 (DTX51, detoxification protein) and Gh_D11G208100 (DUF1685 domain-containing protein) showed haplotype-specific differential expression patterns (Fig. 1d, Additional file 4: Table S2). These genes are proposed to play pivotal roles in regulating the biosynthesis and deposition of pigment-associated compounds during fiber development. The functional annotation of genes within this region is provided in Additional file 5: Table S3. The concordance between GWAS signals, haplotype effects, and transcriptomic variation strongly supports these findings that the D11:24.3–24.7 Mb region is a major genomic hotspot for white-fiber quality, with EE and WH serving as reliable indicators of modulating dynamics in pigment deposition and fiber whiteness.

Genetic dissection of brown fiber in upland cotton

To investigate the regulatory loci underlying fiber pigmentation in naturally colored cotton, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) was conducted on a panel of 403 diverse accessions, including white, brown, and green fiber genotypes. This analysis revealed two key genomic regions associated with total color difference (EE) and Hunter’s whiteness (WH): a strong signal on chromosome A07 (20.4–22.3 Mb) and a moderate association on chromosome D11 (21.8–21.9 Mb) (Fig. 2a). The A07 locus showed greater significance based on –log₁₀ (P) > 6, and the local Manhattan plot highlighted consistent associations within this region (Fig. 2b, c).

GWAS and integrative gene expression analysis of brown-fiber pigmentation in 403 Gossypium hirsutum accessions. a Manhattan plots showing significant SNPs associated with total color difference (EE) and Hunter’s whiteness (WH) on chromosome A07. b The local Manhattan plot for chromosome A07 (top) and c corresponding QTL signal in the F₂ population (bottom) reveals overlapping genomic regions. d Transcriptomic heatmap showing dynamic expression profiles of candidate genes across five developmental stages (0, 5, 10, 20 DPA) in three white and two brown fiber genotypes. e SNP association and linkage disequilibrium (LD) heatmap of the overlapping GWA-R (20.44–22.32 Mb) and F₂-QTL (21.67–21.92 Mb) intervals. f Boxplots of EE values across four haplotypes (A07-Hap1 to Hap4), highlighting the association of A07-Hap2 with brown fiber

To further examine the functional relevance of the A07 and D11 associated regions transcriptome profiling was performed. Transcriptomic profiling of three white-fiber genotypes from D11-Hap-1 across four fiber development stages (10, 15, 20, and 25 DPA) revealed eight genes within the D11:21.87–21.98 Mb interval associated with EE and WH. Among them, Gh_D11G196700, encoding epoxide hydrolase 3 (ephx3), exhibited robust and dynamic expression across all four time points, suggesting its potential role in fiber color modulation (Additional file 6: Fig. S3; Additional file 7: Table S4). Its expression was dynamic across all developmental stages in the white-fiber genotypes of D11-Hap-1, peaking at 15 DPA. This temporal expression pattern points toward a role in color suppression or fiber brightening mechanisms linked to EE and WH traits. In parallel, a comparative transcriptome analysis between white and brown fiber genotypes identified 10 differentially expressed genes in the A07:20.2–22.3 Mbs region (Fig. 2d). Notable Gh_A07G015400 (POPTRDRAFT), Gh_A07G019500 (rhp7), and Gh_A07G017500 (BHLH82) were highly expressed in both fiber types, implying broad functional relevance. In contrast, Gh_A07G019900 and Gh_A07G020000, both encoding anthocyanin regulatory C1 proteins, showed elevated expression specifically in brown-fiber accessions, as did the transcription factors TT2 and ftnA (Gh_A07G020100 and Gh_A07G012300, respectively) (Additional file 8: Table S5). Interestingly, Gh_A07G019400 (YLMG2) was more highly expressed in white-fiber genotypes, suggesting that it may act as a negative regulator of pigment deposition.

To validate the GWAS signals in a bi-parental background QTL mapping was conducted in the F2 population, derived from a cross between Zhong-128 (brown) and Liao-96 (white). Haplotype and QTL mapping pinpointed a shared region termed the genome-wide associated region (GWA-R) on A07 (20.4–22.3 Mb), overlapping with fiber pigmentation QTLs detected in the F₂ population (Fig. 2e). Linkage disequilibrium analysis of this interval identified four haplotypes (A07-Hap-1 to Hap-4), with A07-Hap-2 strongly associated with brown fiber and higher EE values (Fig. 2f).

To investigate potential overlap between genomic signals associated with fiber whiteness, the D11:21.8–21.9 Mb region in the 403-accession panel was examined in relation to the corresponding GWAS locus identified in the 373 white-fiber population. Comparative analysis revealed that the D11 regions identified in the two GWAS panels did not overlap. We specifically revisited the D11:24.31–24.72 Mb interval from the 373 GWAS within the 403-accession dataset to explore the possibility of a shared genetic basis. Further, we analyzed these associated QTLs (Additional file 9: Fig. S4). Evaluation of haplotype effects using EE_BLUPs values showed that individuals carrying D11-Hap-2 exhibited significantly higher EE scores compared to those with D11-Hap-1, indicating that D11-Hap-2 is more strongly associated with enhanced fiber whiteness.

These findings establish A07 as a major regulatory hub for brown fiber pigmentation, harboring transcription factors and pigment pathway genes, while D11 contributes additional variation in color intensity and whiteness. The convergence of GWAS, expression, and QTL analyses supports the utility of these regions for marker-assisted selection to improve fiber color traits in cotton.

Cis-regulatory motif analysis links A07 and D11 loci

Promoter sequence analysis of candidate genes within the A07 and D11 loci revealed the enrichment of key regulatory cis-elements associated with pigment biosynthesis (Fig. 3). In the A07 locus, promoters of major candidate genes, including Gh-TT2, ERF and related loci, contain multiple MYB and bHLH binding motifs, among them, TT2 act as a central MYB regulator. The MYC contains the bHLH binding motifs suggesting MYB-bHLH cooperative regulation. Promoter analysis discovered MYB-binding cis-elements in D11 candidate genes, suggesting possible interaction. Functional annotation further indicates that genes from both loci participate in shared biological pathways including hormone signaling and stress response, ultimately influencing fiber-related traits (Additional file 10: Fig. S5, Additional file 11: Table S6).

Quantitative trait loci analysis of F2 population

Significant genomic loci regulating fiber color traits were uncovered through QTL mapping in an F₂ population derived from a cross between Zhong-128 (brown fiber) and Liao-96 (white fiber). The investigation indicated a major QTL cluster on chromosome A07 strongly linked with both Hunter’s whiteness (WH) and total color difference (EE). Peak signals within this region displayed LOD scores exceeding 150 and explained over 63% of the phenotypic variance for EE, while accounting for more than 54% of the variation in WH (Fig. 2, Table 1). These loci exhibited opposite additive effects on EE and WH, suggesting tight genetic control over fiber pigmentation and whiteness.

Notably, the same region on A07 also showed linkage with fiber quality traits, including fiber length (FL) and fiber strength (FS), signifying that the locus is a potential hub regulating both pigmentation and fiber development. Alleles at this region conferred higher EE values while reducing WH, FL, and FS, reflecting a possible trade-off between color richness and quality metrics.

In addition to A07, several minor QTLs were detected on chromosome D11. These included loci at positions 18,234–18,236 and 13,547 Mb, collectively explaining 5–7% of the variation in EE and WH traits. While less influential individually, the additive effects observed suggest that they contribute to modulating subtle differences in fiber color intensity and whiteness. A QTL associated with fiber length was also detected on D11 (position 4857 Mb), albeit with a smaller effect size. Overall, this integrative QTL analysis highlights chromosome A07 as a primary genomic region influencing fiber color and whiteness in the F₂ population, with secondary contributions from D11. The co-localization of pigmentation and quality-related QTLs underscores the importance of careful allele selection in breeding naturally colored cotton with optimal fiber performance.

QTL analysis of the F2 population further revealed a major effect locus on chromosome A07 that explains a large proportion of variation in both pigment intensity (EE; PVE = 63%) and whiteness (WH; PVE = 54%), consistent with a primary pigment regulation role. In contrast, D11 identified QTLs showed more moderate effects, with a comparatively stronger contribution to WH than EE (EE; PVE = 7–8%, WH; PVE = 5–6%).

Fiber color segregation in F2 population

To complement the association mapping and investigate inheritance patterns in controlled genetic background, fiber color segregation was evaluated in three F2 populations. Based on haplotype analysis of the A07 genome-wide associated region, which identified distinct haplotypes differing in their association with fiber pigmentation intensity, three crosses were designed using brown male parents representing contrasting A07 haplotypes. This experimental design allowed us to test haplotype-dependent modulation of fiber color segregation within the W1_W2_Lb_ gene model (Fig. 4).

Conceptual model illustrating, genetic interaction controlling fiber color phenotype in F2 population. The Lb locus acts as a regulator of pigmentation. When the genotype is lblb, pigment syntheses is completely suppressed, resulting in white phenotype (W) regardless of the allelic state at pigment loci. In the presence of at least one dominant Lb_ allele, pigment expression is permitted and further modified by two whiteness intensity genes W1 and W2. Individuals carrying dominant alleles at both loci W1_W2_ exhibit strong whiteness and express a white fiber phenotype (W or NW). Genotypes with one dominant and one recessive allele such as W1_w2w2 or w1w1 W2 show intermediate pigment accumulation, producing a light brown (LB) phenotype. When both whiteness genes are recessive e.g. w1w1w2w2, pigment production is stronger interaction with Brown fiber gene Lb resulting in a brown (B) and dark brown (DB) fiber phenotype

This F2 population of three different crosses is supported by chi2 analysis (chi2 test = 2.01, 0.51 and 1.01; df = 3; P > 0.05, Table 2) showing segregation ratios consistent with a three-gene genetic model. Segregation of fiber color phenotypes in all three F2 populations showed that the Lb locus suppresses pigment expression in lblb genotype producing a white genotype irrespective of pigment modifier alleles. Among light brown (LB) individuals, phenotypes further segregated into brown, light brown, nearly white and white classes in proportions consistent with the combined effect of dominant W1 and W2 loci. The Chi2 analysis based on four distinct phenotypic classes demonstrated no significant deviation from the expected a 3:18:27:16 Mendelian ratio (Additional file 12: Table S7).

Across three F2 populations, identical allelic classes exhibited cross-dependent phenotypic expression. In Cross 1, individuals classified as C1-G0 (W1_W2_Lb_) predominantly displayed dark brown (DB) fiber, whereas the same allelic class shifted towards Brown (B) phenotype in Cross 2 and Cross 3. Despite these differences, Chi2 tests using four biologically defined phenotypic classes showed no significant deviation from expected Mendelian segregation ratios in any cross (P > 0.05).

Intermediate genotype index class, e.g., G1 and G2, displayed progressive shift between these extremes, indicating additive modification of pigment intensity. Scatter plots of ΔE versus WH showed a strong inverse relationship across all F2 populations. Distinct clustering of genotype index classes and consistent centroid positions across crosses demonstrates stable genetic control of color phenotype (Fig. 5b). Hierarchical clustering of genotype index centroids based on combined ΔE and WH values revealed grouping by genotype index rather than by cross (Fig. 5c). The heatmap of Euclidean distances among genotype index centroids with dendrograms indicates G3 and G2 clustering together followed by G1 and G0 forming a distinct group. The heatmap showed that the white class G3 from all crosses clustered very tightly together, with minimal distances between Cross 1, class G3, Cross 2, class G3 and Cross 3, class G3 (C1-G3, C2-G3 and C3-G3). Similarly, the near-white centroids C2-G2 and C3-G2 were located close to white group and clearly separated from the pigmented centroids. This pattern supports a model in which pigment expression is controlled hierarchically with suppression in white classes and quantitative modification among pigmented classes (Additional file 14: Fig. S7). In contrast, the fully pigmented centroids G0 were the most distant from the white and near-white groups, forming the darkest cluster in both ΔE and WH dimensions. The LB centroids G1 occupied an intermediate position between B and NW/W, but their positions differed by cross. In cross 2, they lay closer to NW/W group, whereas in Cross 3, they shifted towards the B cluster.

Marker-based evaluation of A07-Lb_ based region in the F2 population

We analyzed the relationship between EE and WH in a haplotype-resolved F2 population under the shared region A07 (21.67–21.92 Mb), which overlaps with the GWAS region A07 (20.4–22.3 Mb), to test Lb dosage-dependent co-dominant effects on fiber color. Scatter plots of EE versus WH revealed a continuous inverse relationship between the two traits, with substantial overlap between LbLb and Lblb individuals across the entire phenotypic range (Fig. 6a). No discrete clustering and genotype-specific separation was observed. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests detected no significant differences in EE or WH between LbLb and Lblb genotypes (P > 0.05).

a EE and WH scatter plot for the haplotype-based F2 population showing extensive overlap between LbLb and Lblb individuals. b Across three F2 crosses, fiber color phenotypes with a continuous gradient. c Boxplots of EE values for LbLb and Lblb individuals show overlapping distributions (Wilcoxon test, P > 0.05), supporting dominant pigment permission rather than codominant inheritance at the Lb locus

Additionally, in a scatter plot of three-cross-based F2 population (Fig. 6b), phenotypes were distributed continuously along an ordered EE-WH gradient, rather than forming discrete or genotype-specific clusters, indicating quantitative modulation of pigment accumulation. Dark brown (DB) individuals occupied the extreme low WH and high EE tail only in cross 1, whereas comparable extreme values were not observed in cross 3, despite shared A07 haplotype in male parents. The boxplot showing comparison of EE values between LbLb and Lblb individuals in the F2 population revealed substantial overlap and similar median values, indicating no quantitative separation between genotypes (Fig. 6c).

Yeast two-hybrid analysis of TT2-A07 interactions with D11 candidate proteins

To investigate whether candidate genes within the D11: 24.3–24.7 Mb locus physically associate with the TT2-A07 regulatory module, we performed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assays using TT2-A07 as the bait fused to the GAL4 DNA binding domain (BD), and three candidate white fiber genes (WFGs) from the D11 locus: Gh_D11G208100 (WFG1), Gh_D11207100 (WFG2), Gh_D11G207600 (WFG3), and one A07 gene Gh_A07G017500 (bHLH protein known to interact with WD-repeat and MYB partners to form the MYB-bHLH-WD40 regulatory complex) as prey constructs fused to the GAL4 activation domain, AD. Co-transformants were successfully obtained on SD/-Leu/-Trp (DDO) medium for all bait-prey combinations, confirming stable maintenance of both plasmids. All three experimental repeats yielded identical interaction outcomes, growth vs. no growth on selective media with no observable variation in the binary result. Interaction-dependent reporter activation was subsequently assessed on selective reporter media (TDO). Yeast carrying TT2-A07 (BD) with Gh_D11G208100 (AD), TT2-A07 (BD) with Gh_D11G207100 (AD) and TT2-A07 (BD) with Gh_A07G017500 (AD) displayed reporter positive protein–protein interaction, whereas the combination of TT2-A07 (BD) with Gh_D11G207600 (AD) failed to activate the reporter, indicating no detectable interaction under the assay conditions. These Y2H results support a model in which Gh_D11G208100 and Gh_D11G207100 may participate in pigmentation-related regulatory processes, consistent with their predominant expression during mid-to late-stage fiber developmental stages (Fig. 7).

Transcriptomic signatures of pigmentation and fiber quality

Transcriptome sequencing was conducted at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 DPA across two brown fiber genotypes (ZGH0758 and GH1454) and two white fiber genotypes (M-51 and ZGH0720).

The high transcript abundance of TT2, bHLH82, and Gh_A07G019900 in brown-fiber genotypes corroborated the involvement of these regulators in activating the pigment biosynthetic pathway. White-fiber genotypes, on the other hand, exhibited markedly lower expression of these genes, alongside elevated expression of ribosomal proteins such as RPS30A and RPL34, particularly in M-51 (Additional file 15: Fig. S8). These findings suggest translational efficiency and protein synthesis may also contribute to the phenotypic quality of white fibers. The STRING database (http://string-db.org) generated protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks illustrating functional association among ribosomal protein genes (Additional file 16: Fig. S9). These ribosomal protein genes were differentially expressed in white and brown fiber genotypes, especially in high-fiber-quality genotypes such as M-51. Their differential expression was also observed slightly higher in ZGH0758, a better fiber quality brown genotype compared to GH1454 (Additional file 17: Table S8). STRING-based interaction analysis supported the interplay between transcription factors and pigment biosynthesis enzymes, while ribosomal proteins clustered distinctly, highlighting their independent regulatory dynamics in fiber development. The data suggest coordinated transcriptional control of pigmentation genes, interwoven with translational machinery influencing fiber growth and appearance.

Transcriptional insights into fiber color differentiation during different DPAs

Gene expression analyses of fiber from brown and white genotypes at different developmental stages (DPAs) have driven major transcriptomic variation. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess global transcriptome variation across fiber developmental stages and fiber colors. Brown fiber samples clustered more tightly, indicating greater consistency or stability in their gene expression profiles, whereas white fibers were more dispersed. Fiber color contributed to transcriptomic variation, but less strongly than developmental stages (Additional file 18: Figs. S10a, b).

To gain insight into biological processes underlying these transcriptional changes, KEGG pathway enrichment provided stage-specific resolution of these transcriptional programs (Fig. 8; Additional file 19: Fig. S11). Analysis of up-regulated DEGs showed enrichment in pigment biosynthesis and metabolic processes in brown fibers during early stages (5–10 DPA). The enriched pathways, including phenylpropanoid and flavonoid biosynthesis and pyruvate metabolism, reflected high precursor demand for pigment accumulation. At 15 DPA enrichment shifted towards oxidative regulation, protein processing and signaling pathways. While at 20 DPA up-regulated DEGs were enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, proteasome activity and ABC transporters indicating a role in metabolite transport, proteostasis and hormonal control of maturation. In contrast, down-regulated DEGs revealed a strong suppression of secondary metabolite biosynthesis pathways (phenylpropanoid, flavonoid, flavone, and phenylalanine metabolism), particularly at early stages such as 5–10 DPA. By later stages, such as 15–20 DPA, down-regulated pathway signals weakened, suggesting that key divergence between fiber colors occurs at the onset of fiber elongation.

Fiber pigmentation is thus initially established by metabolic activation and regulatory repression, and subsequently sustained by intricate co-expression networks including signaling, proteostasis, and metabolite transport, as demonstrated by the integration of DEG dynamics and WGCNA modules (Additional file 20: Fig. S12). This model supports the idea that coordinated and temporally structured regulatory systems, rather than discrete gene activity, are the source of pigmentation and fiber quality features.

Association of fiber color and fiber quality

To explore potential relationships between fiber color traits and textile-relevant parameters, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated between total color difference (EE), Hunter’s whiteness (WH), and fiber quality attributes including fiber length (FL), fiber strength (FS), fiber uniformity (FU), and lint percentage (LP) (Fig. 9; Additional file 21: Fig. S13).

Notably, WH demonstrated a moderate to strong positive correlation with FL and FS, suggesting that whiter fibers tend to exhibit superior structural properties. Conversely, EE showed a negative correlation with FL and FS, implying that increased pigmentation (higher EE) may be associated with reduced fiber quality. Correlations with absolute values of r > 0.5 were considered biologically meaningful, indicating potential pleiotropic effects or co-selection pressures during cotton breeding. These findings highlight the interconnection between fiber color and structural traits, supporting the importance of integrating color metrics into selection indices for naturally pigmented cotton improvement.

Genotype groups based on fiber color and quality have shown similar patterns of phenotypic expression among different environments (Additional file 22: Fig. S14).

Hierarchical clustering and Principal component analysis (PCA) biplot revealed the distribution patterns of cotton accessions based on fiber quality and pigmentation traits (Fig. 9a). The first two principal components, PC-1 (56.3%) and PC-2 (22.2%), accounted for 78.5% of the total phenotypic variation. Trait vectors in the biplot depict both the direction and magnitude of each trait’s contribution. Fiber quality traits, including FS, FL, and FU, clustered closely, indicating covariance and positive association among these parameters. Results suggest that accessions with darker fiber pigmentation (higher EE) tend to exhibit slightly reduced fiber length and lint percentage (Fig. 9b). This indicated the negative linkage of fiber brownness and fiber quality.

Hap-3 (n = 261), the largest subgroup, displayed intermediate values across most traits (Fig. 9c).

Discussion

Genetic dissection of fiber color

This study integrates GWAS haplotype analysis, classical F2 segregation and quantitative phenotyping to resolve the genetic architecture underlying cotton fiber color. Integrative analysis reveals a coordinated genetic network underlying fiber color development, with loci on chromosomes A07 and D11 emerging as central regulators. The A07:20.4–22.3 Mb region harbors transcription factors such as TT2, bHLH82, and C1 anthocyanin regulators, which are significantly up-regulated in brown-fiber genotypes and are known to orchestrate flavonoid biosynthesis and pigment accumulation [17, 24, 25]. Biosynthesis of proanthocyanidin (PAs) is controlled by a conserved transcriptional complex (MBW) comprising TT2 (R2R3-MYB), TT8 (bHLH) and TTG1 (WD- repeat), which activates flavonoid structural genes. TT2 acts as a regulator in a specific and temporally restricted manner and localizes to the nucleus [17, 26]. Consistent with GWAS and QTL peaks on chromosome A07, domain analysis of genes within the associated interval identified multiple transcriptional regulators, including Gh_A07G020100 encoding an R2R3 MYB protein with conserved MYB DNA-binding domains characteristic of a TT2-like regulator of PAs and Gh_A07G017500 (BHLH82). The co-localization of MYB and bHLH domain containing genes within this region supports A07 as a pigment regulatory locus controlling transcriptional activation of PAs biosynthesis (Additional file 23: Fig. S15). These findings align with previous studies demonstrating the role of the MYB–bHLH–WD40 complex in regulating proanthocyanidin and anthocyanin pathways [15, 27]. The A07 genome-wide associated region overlaps a well-established brown fiber-related regulatory hotspot near GhTT2_A07, previously linked by genetic mapping to brown fiber color and pigmentation pathway activation [16, 28, 29]. Recent integrative Nature Genetics study from our laboratory demonstrated that MYB transcription factors, GhTT2_A07, function as central regulators of metabolite biosynthesis including flavonoid and proanthocyanidin biosynthesis, during early cotton ovule and fiber development [30]. This suggests that transcriptional control of metabolite biosynthesis is a conserved mechanism underlying cotton fiber traits, including pigmentation. Multiple independent lines of evidence, consistent with these reports, our transcriptomic profiles showed elevated expression of pigmentation regulators in brown cotton genotypes, supporting A07 as the major pigmentation activation module.

Simultaneously, the D11:24.3–24.7 Mb locus, co-localizing with whiteness-associated QTLs, includes candidate genes such as Gh_D11G208100 (DUF1685), Gh_D11G207100 (MAKR1), and Gh_D11G207600 (DTX51), which exhibit elevated expression in white-fiber genotypes. These genes are predicted to be involved in transport and detoxification processes, and may represent potential candidate regulators that influence metabolite flux during fiber maturation to suppress pigment deposition and enhance whiteness [1].

Haplotype effects and overlapping QTL regions provide evidence for a potential epistatic or additive interaction between A07 and D11 loci. As an illustration, D11-Hap-1 conferred higher WH values in white-fiber genotypes demonstrating differential allelic expression depending on pigment background, whereas D11-Hap-2 genotypes displayed enhanced EE scores in brown-fiber populations [8, 31].

These findings support a model of coordinated regulation, where transcriptional control from A07 influences pigment biosynthesis, while D11 modulates fiber surface or secondary metabolism to fine-tune color intensity and brightness. This dual-locus interaction underscores the importance of multi-locus selection strategies in breeding programs aimed at optimizing fiber coloration alongside quality traits [19, 32]. GWAS identifies D11: 24.3–24.7 Mb as a major locus for whiteness-related variation. Three-cross F2 population model shows phenotypic shift along EE and WH gradients, which indicates the requirement of two dominant dilution loci W1, W2. Candidate genes on D11 include regulatory transport-like functions and D11 is the best genomic candidate for W1, W2 as a second major hub and its predicted functions in whiteness dilution. Comparative transcriptome analysis (Figs. 1d and 2d) indicates that pigment-related genes are activated early during fiber development 5–10 DPA, whereas the D11 Gh_D11G208100 (DUF1685 domain-containing protein), Gh_D11G207100 (MAKR1, a membrane-anchored kinase-related protein) genes are expressed predominantly during mid to late stages 10–25 DPA, consistent with temporally distinct roles during fiber coloration. The DTX/MATE superfamily candidate gene Gh_D11G207600 (DTX51) showed peak expression at approximately 10 DPA. Previous studies have demonstrated that MATE family flavonoid transporters are localized in plasma membrane and function in pigment transport and sequestration [33–35]. The strongest simple model is TT2-A07 module activates PA biosynthetic early program and DTX- module peaks slightly later to move or sequester those products, which is a regulatory cascade, not a protein–protein interaction. These genes are expressed during key fiber developmental stages associated with pigment deposition and cell differentiation, supporting a role in modulating fiber-specific physiological processes rather than initiating pigment biosynthesis [36–38].

To complement the GWAS and transcriptomic evidence, the integration of classical genetic segregation and quantitative phenotyping a study employed in three F2 populations. Segregation analysis across three independent F2 populations revealed a consistent 3:1 ratio of pigmented to white individuals supporting a dominant locus controlling pigment Lb-mediated pigment presence with white phenotype corresponding to recessive lblb genotype. Most importantly, although the base level segregation structure was stable across crosses, the distribution of pigmented phenotypes (DB, B, LB, NW) differed markedly among populations. This indicates that Lb alone is not dictating the pigment intensity.

Although the A07 region has traditionally been associated with the classical Lb locus controlling fiber pigmentation [30], our marker-based analysis in the F2 population does not support a simple Mendelian or dosage-dependent role of this locus. F2 population demonstrated that LbLb and Lblb individuals exhibited nearly identical distribution of EE and WH, indicating Lb dosage does not generate discrete phenotypic classes. Dark brown (DB), brown (B) and light brown (LB) represent quantitative extremes within the Lb class rather than the allelic state. The cross-specific dark brown (DB) phenotypic appearance indicates that extreme pigmentation represents a threshold-dependent outcome influenced by genetic background rather than a fixed Mendelian class. In addition, the chi2 analysis based on four distinct phenotypic classes demonstrated no significant deviation from the expected a 3:18:27:16 Mendelian ratio supporting a three-gene model. This explains that the D11 region identified through GWAS exhibited strong signals, supported differential expression patterns suggesting a potential role in modulating fiber color. These results support the A07-Lb_ may contribute to the biosynthetic potential for pigment production and the D11 likely plays a more decisive role in regulating pigment, transport or sequestration. The contrasting expression of the W1_W2_Lb_ genotype among the three crosses suggests a strong genetic background effect, driven by differences in the parental combination. In cross 1, the male parent (purple brown upland cotton) exhibited stronger intrinsic pigment gene expression, resulting in partial dominance of pigmentation that reduced the suppressive influence of the whiteness intensity genes W1 and W2. Consequently, individuals carrying W1_W2_LB_ expressed light brown (LB) fibers rather than near white (NW). In contrast, the male parents used in Cross 2 (Guilu 34) and Cross 3 (brown 128) appear to harbor weaker pigment-producing alleles or reduced regulatory activity at pigmentation loci, allowing the interaction between W1 and W2 to exert stronger dilution effects and shift the phenotype toward near white. These findings indicate that W1 and W2 do not function as absolute switches but rather as modifiers whose phenotypic impact depends on pigment gene dosage and regulatory strength contributed by parental background. While the model explains the overall segregation structure, variation in intermediate phenotypes reflects quantitative modulation of pigment accumulation. This interaction aligns with the observed stability of extreme classes and the plasticity of intermediate classes across crosses. The combined phenotypic and clustering analyses strongly support a genetic model in which pigment expression in the F2 populations is regulated by modifier loci. Progressive decrease in EE and concomitant increases in WH from G1 to G3 reflect dilution of whitening gene action. This inverse relationship between EE and WH suggested that these traits are not independent but jointly controlled by the same genetic architecture. Together, these results, Chi2 segregation patterns, ΔE based analysis and cluster analysis endorsed the proposed W-Lb genetic framework and predicted F2 architecture (Tables 1 and 2).

Furthermore, asymmetric effects of A07 and D11 observed in the QTL analysis of F2 population further clarify the functional role in fiber color regulation. A07 exhibits a major effect on both EE and WH, consistent with its association with TT2-regulated pigment biosynthetic pathway. In contrast, D11 shows smaller but reproducible effects with a proportionally greater influence on WH than EE, supporting its role as a dilution or whitening modifier rather than a primary pigment determinant. This distinction aligns with segregation patterns and quantitative trait distributions observed in F2 population.

The enrichment of MYB and bHLH cis-regulatory elements in promoters of candidate genes across both A07 and D11 loci highlights a coordinated transcriptional network, consistent with their potential role in transcriptional activation of proanthocyanidin and flavonoid pathways (Fig. 3). Given that fiber pigmentation is regulated by transcriptional complexes, particularly the MYB-bHLH-WD40 regulatory network, we hypothesized that whiteness-associated candidate genes from the D11 locus may interact with key regulatory proteins encoded by fiber pigmentation-associated genes from the A07 locus. Such interactions could modulate pigment biosynthesis, contributing to variation in the fiber color intensity and whiteness. Yeast two-hybrid assay provided functional support for the predicted interaction framework among regulators associated with fiber pigmentation, consistent with the established MYB-bHLH-WD40 (MBW) complex model [15, 27]. Interaction analysis showed that Gh_D11G208100 (DUF1685) and Gh_D11G207100 (MAKR1) proteins physically interact with the TT2-A07, suggesting that these candidates may participate in TT2-associated regulatory complexes during fiber pigmentation. The Y2H assay demonstrated that certain D11 candidate proteins can physically interact with the A07 bait proteins, whereas others did not show detectable interaction under the experimental conditions. These results suggest a potential mechanism for coordination between A07 and D11 loci. However, the co-localization studies or bimolecular complementation (BiFC) are required for further validation of functional consequences of this interaction. Nevertheless, the integration of multiple genomic and transcriptomic approaches provides a robust framework for prioritizing candidate genes for future functional studies. The Y2H assays demonstrate potential protein interactions but do not establish casual relationships in planta. Therefore, these findings are indicative of candidate gene involvement highlighting for future functional validation.

Linkage of fiber color and quality

Our findings reveal a biologically meaningful relationship between fiber color attributes and key fiber quality traits. The multivariate analyses presented herein underscore an apparent interplay between cotton fiber pigmentation and quality traits. Fiber color traits, specifically tone difference (EE) and whiteness index (WH) exhibited strong antagonism, as reflected in their opposite vector orientations (Fig. 1c), suggesting a robust negative correlation. This inverse relationship was further supported by trait clustering, where fiber strength (FS), length (FL), and uniformity (FU) grouped tightly, reinforcing their mutual association and revealing a negative linkage with fiber brownness [5, 39]. The correlation matrix corroborated these trends, quantifying statistically significant relationships and offering a visual gradient of trait dependencies. Notably, darker pigmented accessions consistently showed marginal reductions in fiber length and lint yield, signaling potential trade-offs between pigmentation and agronomic performance [1]. Such overlap is consistent with transcriptomic data showing concurrent differential expression of pigmentation-related genes and transporters implicated in secondary cell wall formation and lignin metabolism [3, 15].

Haplotype-based stratification of chromosome A07 further refined these insights. Hap-2 accessions were characterized by elevated EE and diminished WH, aligning with darker fiber profiles and suboptimal fiber quality metrics. Conversely, Hap-4 presented as a promising genetic group, harboring accessions with white fibers, high WH, and superior values in FL, FU, and LP traits desirable for textile applications (Fig. 9). Hap-3, the largest cohort, demonstrated intermediate trait values and may represent a genetically diverse and adaptable pool. This inverse relationship between pigmentation and quality could arise from pleiotropic effects of regulatory genes or linkage disequilibrium between pigmentation loci and fiber structural genes [32, 40]. These patterns suggest that haplotypic variation at A07 likely modulates both pigment biosynthesis and fiber development, warranting further exploration through targeted genomic approaches.

These findings imply that the metabolic or genetic mechanisms underlying pigmentation, particularly increased brown coloration (higher EE), may compromise fiber structural integrity or yield traits such as lint percentage (LP). The negative association between fiber brownness and key quality parameters may reflect pleiotropic effects or tight linkage of pigment biosynthetic genes with loci governing fiber development. QTL analysis of the F2 population identified a major locus on the A07 chromosome that explained a large proportion of variation in fiber color traits (EE ~ 63%, WH ~ 54%) and also co-localized with QTLs for fiber length (FL) and fiber strength (FS). This overlap suggests that the A07 region may contribute to both pigmentation and fiber quality, indicating either pleiotropic effects or tight linkage of functionally related loci. In contrast, QTLs detected on chromosome D11 showed smaller effects and were distributed across distinct positions for fiber whiteness and quality traits, suggesting independent contributions. However, analysis of phenotypic variation across the GWAS population revealed that fiber quality traits such as FL and FS do not consistently decrease in brown fibers. Some brown genotypes exhibited FL and FS comparable to those of white genotypes. Fine-mapping of the A07-Lc1 interval revealed that known pigmentation regulators (Gh-TT2) occur in the same LD blocks as a quality-related candidate gene (MRI1) (Fig. 2e). This pattern suggests that the negative correlation between brown fiber color and fiber quality is more consistent with tight linkage of distinct genes within the same haplotype block than with strict pleiotropy of a single gene, although partial pleiotropic effects cannot be excluded at the current resolution.

Transcriptional regulation insights to decouple fiber pigmentation and quality

The present study provides an integrative framework linking transcriptional regulation, gene interaction networks, and phenotypic expression of fiber color and quality in cotton. Our results revealed that fiber pigmentation, primarily driven by transcriptional activation of secondary metabolism, is negatively associated with structural fiber properties. However, genotype-specific differences highlight that this trade-off is not absolute, as certain brown genotypes exhibit relatively improved fiber quality [20].

Results indicated that while development stage is the dominant determinant of global gene expression, fiber color also contributes to transcriptomic variation likely through regulation of secondary metabolism. This highlights the interplay between developmental timing and pigmentation pathways in shaping fiber traits [41, 42]. Fiber pigmentation is transcriptionally programmed at initiation, but refined and stabilized through metabolic and hormonal regulation during maturation. Early developmental stages are critical windows during which brown and white fibers diverge transcriptionally. Brown genotypes activated pigment biosynthetic pathways consistent with the activation of flavonoid and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis [24, 43]. In contrast white fibers actively suppress these pathways coupled with up-regulation of ribosomal proteins (RPS30A, RPL34) particularly in the high quality white genotypes such as M-51. This pattern suggests that translational efficiency and resource allocation toward protein synthesis support elongation and fiber strengthening in white cotton. On the other hand, in comparison with M-51, ZGH0720, a low-quality white genotype, exhibited the similar transcriptional profile to M-51 in terms of pigment suppression. However, ZGH0720 showed a lack of up-regulation in ribosomal proteins, which may explain its poorer fiber quality. This suggests that ZGH0720’s lower fiber quality is linked to its inability to efficiently allocate metabolic resources for both elongation and secondary metabolism, resulting in weaker and shorter fibers compared to higher quality white genotypes like M-51 [44].

Brown genotypes differ in their capacity to balance pigment and quality. Despite the negative general association between pigmentation and quality variation among brown genotypes indicated distinct strategies of metabolic regulation. For instance, ZGH-758 is a high-quality brown genotype which exhibited not only pigment pathway activation but also slightly elevated expression of ribosomal protein genes compared with the lower quality brown genotype GH1454. Thus, genotypes like ZGH758 may partially offset the metabolic costs of pigmentation maintaining higher translational efficiency, resulting in better fiber quality relative to other brown lines [39, 44, 45].

Pearson’s correlation analysis PCA further separated color and quality traits in opposite orientations, while haplotype analysis on chromosome A07 linked hap-2 with darker fibers and reduced quality, whereas hap-1, hap-3, hap-4 were associated with whiter, stronger fibers. Importantly, high quality brown genotypes such as ZGH758 appear to deviate partially from this trend clustering closer to white genotypes in their fiber properties. This suggests that specific allelic combinations or compensatory expression of translational machinery may mitigate the inherent trade-off between pigmentation and fiber growth. This balance may result from fine-tuned interaction between transcriptional activators of pigmentation and ribosomal proteins that sustain translational capacity, thereby minimizing resource competition. Conversely, low quality brown genotypes such as GH1454 lack this compensatory mechanism, leading to pronounced trade-offs and reduced fiber strength and length. The haplotype analysis on chromosome A07 also illustrated that ZGH0758 and M-51 shared favorable quality related alleles, reinforcing their superior fiber properties, while ZGH0720 and GH1454 exhibit different, less advantageous allele combinations [23, 44, 46, 47].

These results suggest that pigmentation pathways play a central role in the differentiation of brown and white fibers, genotypic differences in regulatory networks such as ribosomal protein expression may allow certain genotypes like ZGH0758 to achieve high quality brown fibers, somewhat bridging the gap with high quality white fibers like M-51. This underscores the complexity of fiber color quality trade-off, suggesting that breeding efforts aimed at naturally colored cotton must consider not only pigment biosynthesis but also translational machinery to mitigate any negative effects on fiber elongation and strength.

These findings highlight that the negative linkage between fiber pigmentation and quality is neither uniform nor irreversible. Breeding programs can exploit genotypes like ZGH758 as models for uncoupling pigment accumulation from structural penalties. Strategies may include pyramiding favorable haplotypes on A07 with alleles enhancing translational efficiency, or manipulating regulatory genes such as TT2, TT16 and bHLH etc. to optimize pigment deposition without excessive metabolic burden [19, 48–50]. Strong translational patterns in white fiber genotypes can be used as benchmarks to pinpoint important compensatory pathways. Decoupling metabolic trade-offs by integrated genetic and molecular techniques will ultimately be necessary to produce naturally pigmented cotton with competitive fiber quality will depend on decoupling metabolic trade-offs.

Investigating pathway enrichment illuminated the connection between fiber color and quality. Early secondary metabolism activation provides the molecular basis for pigment production in brown fibers yet this requirement also interacts with essential metabolic pathways such as glucose processing and pyruvate metabolism [4]. The turquoise WGCNA module effectively illustrated this intersection by connecting flavonoid biosynthesis with fatty acid degradation, apoptosis and stress responses. It also showed how pigment biosynthesis is co-regulated with broader processes relevant to fiber development and quality [51, 52]. A shift toward regulatory fine-tuning and metabolite transport is indicated by the enrichment of hormone signaling, proteasome activity and ABC transporters in brown fibers later in development (15–20 DPA). These processes are likely to support pigment stabilization as well as structural reinforcement and secondary wall deposition, both of which underpin fiber quality [53].

These results suggest that fiber quality and color are not independent traits but are transcriptionally interconnected [54, 55]. In order to maintain appropriate fiber quality, compensatory management is required because pigmentation pathways, particularly phenylpropanoid and flavonoid metabolism impose metabolic demands that may be traded off with fiber elongation process. However, it appears that hindering these pathways in white fibers lowers metabolic expenses, freeing up resources for structural homogeneity and elongation. This perspective is further supported by WGCNA, which determines how pigmentation and fiber quality are regulated by common regulatory networks.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive genetic dissection of fiber color traits in upland cotton, uncovering key genomic regions and regulatory networks that govern pigmentation and whiteness. Through integration of genome-wide association studies, QTL mapping, and transcriptomic profiling, we identified two major loci, A07:20.4–22.3 Mb as a key regulator of fiber pigmentation and D11:24.3–24.7 Mb contributed to fiber whiteness and color dilution. Candidate genes within these regions showed distinct expression patterns suggesting coordinated regulation of pigment biosynthesis. The segregation of fiber color in three cotton F2 populations is fully consistent with a three-gene model involving two dominant dilution loci W1_W2_ and a brown pigment locus Lb_. Interaction analysis further indicated potential crosstalk between pigmentation and dilution pathways. These findings provide new insights into the genetic architecture of cotton fiber color and offer valuable targets for breeding naturally colored cotton with improved fiber quality.

Methods

Plant material

To investigate the genetic basis of fiber whiteness and pigmentation, a genome-wide association study (GWAS) was conducted using two population sets. The first set included 373 Gossypium hirsutum accessions producing white fiber (White population), it was used to dissect natural variation in whiteness-related traits within white-fiber cotton. The second panel consisted of a broader natural population comprising 403 accessions, including 373 white-fiber accessions and 30 naturally colored-fiber cotton accessions, and was used to identify loci associated with broader fiber color variation and pigmentation. The materials encompassed various genetic backgrounds, including natural accessions of white, off-white, brown, and light brown fiber types, along with wild and semi-wild cotton lines. GWAS analyses were performed separately for the two populations. The seeds of all accessions were obtained from the National Gene Bank at the Cotton Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, located in Anyang, Henan, China.

To further enhance the genetic analysis, QTL analysis was conducted using an F₂ population consisting of 437 individuals derived from a cross between the brown cotton line Zhong-128 (Z128) and white cotton line Liao-96 (L-96). In addition to investigating the inheritance of fiber color from different genetic backgrounds, three independent F2 populations derived from the crosses Nonglin No.1 (White) × Purple brown Upland cotton, Nonglin No.1 (White) × Guilu 34 (Brown) and Chirpan996 × Brown 128 were analyzed, consisting of 185, 160, and 169 individuals, respectively, for segregation analyses. A detailed summary of all plant materials used in this study, including natural populations and segregating populations, is provided in Additional file 24: Table S9.

Phenotyping of fiber color

Phenotypic evaluation of fiber color was conducted over two consecutive years (2018 and 2019) at three geographically diverse locations, including Anyang, Henan Province (36.10°N, 114.35°E; altitude: 76 m), Kuitun, Xinjiang Province (44.43°N, 84.88°E; altitude: 480 m), Sanya, Hainan Province (18.25°N, 109.51°E; altitude: 10 m). Fiber samples were collected at the mature stage of boll development, as fiber color can only be reliably assessed once the fibers are fully developed and pigmentation is stabilized. A total of 27 color-related indicators were measured using a high-precision brightness colorimeter (YT-48A instrument from Hangzhou Yante Technology Co., Ltd., located in Hangzhou, Zhejiang). These included: Hunter’s whiteness (WH), total color difference (EE), brightness index, chroma coordinates, hue factors, diffuse reflection factor, and textile whiteness. Best linear unbiased predictions (BLUPs) were used to correct for environmental influence and enable accurate integration with genotypic data.

Sequencing and genotyping

For genotyping, the whole-genome re-sequencing was employed for the 373 white-fiber accessions and the 403 diverse accessions used in the GWAS analyses. Paired-end sequencing libraries were constructed with fragment sizes ranging from 300 to 700 bp and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw reads were filtered to retain high quality variants. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.05, missing rate > 20% or showing significant segregation distortion were removed. The high-quality reads were aligned to the Gossypium hirsutum reference genome (CRI-TM-1 v1.0), obtained from the Cotton Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, using BWA-MEM with the parameters -t 4 -k 32 -M -R [56, 57]. Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) were identified using both SAMtools and GATK (v3.1.1) pipelines [56]. To enhance resolution, bin markers were defined via a sliding window approach, scanning 15 consecutive SNPs with a step size of one SNP [58, 59]. A high-density genetic map was constructed using JoinMap 4.0 (Van Ooijen, 2006), applying the Kosambi mapping function to calculate genetic distances [60]. Genomic intervals with a recombination-to-physical distance ratio exceeding 20 cM/Mb were designated as recombination hotspots (RHs).

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) analysis

GWAS was conducted using two data sets: 373 white fiber accessions and a broader set of 403 accessions including 373 white, 28 brown, and 2 green cotton lines to dissect the genetic architecture underlying cotton fiber pigmentation. The analysis using 27 fiber color-related traits and their mean values or BLUPs was conducted using mixed linear model (MLM) in EMMAX [61] to enhance detection power and control for population structure and kinship.

The –log10(P value) threshold for significant association was equal to 6 to improve the detection of true associations while controlling the false positives..The genome-wide significance for each trait was determined based on the Bonferroni correction (P = 0.05/n), where n represents the effective number of independent SNPs calculated through linkage disequilibrium based correction. Significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were visualized via Manhattan plots generated with the “qqman” package in R. Quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots were used to validate the model fit and ensure control of false discovery rates [62]. Haplotype analysis was performed based on SNPs located within the target genomic regions. Haplotypes were defined according to allelic combinations of significant SNPs using PLINK v1.9 and accessions were grouped accordingly. Phenotypic differences among haplotypes were then compared for the corresponding fiber color traits.

Quantitative trait loci analysis of F2 populations

To complement the GWAS findings and dissect the heritability of fiber color traits, QTL analysis was conducted on F₂ populations derived from two contrasting parental lines: Zhong-128 (brown fiber) and Liao-96 (white fiber). A total of 437 F₂ progenies were evaluated at maturity conducting replicated field trials across two environments: (i) the Yellow River Basin region, Anyang County, Henan Province, Cotton Research Institute experimental base in the south of the farm (36.06°N, 114.49°E) and (ii) China Agricultural University. At full maturity, healthy and fully opened bolls were manually harvested from each genotype. Approximately 3 g of lint fiber per sample were collected and assessed using the YT-48A colorimeter (Hangzhou Yante Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang). To ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the phenotypic measurements, each sample was evaluated in three biological replicates. Finally, fiber samples were assessed for colorimetric traits including Total Color Difference (EE), Hunter’s Whiteness (WH), and related fiber quality parameters.

Genotyping of the F2 population and parental lines was performed using whole-genome re-sequencing. Clean reads were aligned to the cotton reference genome (TM-1 CRI v.1.0). Quantitative Trait Loci (QTLs) associated with fiber color were identified using the Composite Interval Mapping (CIM) algorithm implemented in ICI Mapping v4.2 [63]. A stringent significance threshold for the logarithm of odds (LOD) score was established via 1,000 permutation tests, applying a walking speed of 1.0 cM and a significance level of P < 0.05. Putative regulatory QTLs were identified as loci with LOD scores exceeding 2.5 and up to the threshold [64, 65]. This strategy enabled high-resolution mapping of genomic regions linked to fiber pigmentation in F₂ populations, advancing insights into the genetic architecture of cotton fiber color.

Transcriptome profiling

To further investigate candidate genes transcriptome analysis was conducted using 21 white fiber genotypes associated with the D11 locus was performed across 0, 5,10, 15, and 20 days post-anthesis (Additional file 1: Table S1). Additionally three white fiber genotypes (KC-25, KC-15, and KC-19) and two brown-fiber genotypes (ZB-16 and ZB-17), representing contrasting fiber color phenotypes. Total RNA extraction, cDNA library construction, and sequencing were conducted to analyze gene expression profiles in cotton fiber samples collected at different developmental stages (0, 5, 10, 15, and 20-days post-anthesis, DPA) (Additional file 25: Table S10). Three independent biological replicates were used for each genotype at each developmental stage. Total RNA from the fiber samples of the two cotton genotypes was isolated using the RNA prep Pure Plant Plus Kit (DP441) from Tiangen, Beijing, China, according to the manufacturer's protocol. The quality and concentration of each RNA sample were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), ensuring parameters such as 28S/18S ratio ≥ 1.5, A260/A280 ratio ≥ 2.0, and RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7.5 to ensure high-quality RNA. Subsequently, high-quality RNA samples were utilized to construct cDNA libraries, with each sample prepared in triplicate to account for biological variability. The construction of 30 cDNA libraries and subsequent sequencing were carried out by BGI Gene Technology Co. (Shenzhen, China). The cDNA libraries were sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform, generating 150-bp paired-end (PE150) reads. Fragments Per Kilobase per Million mapped reads (FPKM) [66] were used to quantify the expression levels of genes and transcripts using StringTie [67]. R packages DESeq2 [68] and edgeR [69] were used for pairwise comparisons in differential expression analysis. Genes having a fold change (FC) > 2 and a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 were deemed to be differentially expressed. Gene co-expression networks were created for DEGs using the R package WGCNA [70].

Promoter analysis of A07 and D11 loci genes

To investigate the cis-regulatory elements on candidate region A07 and D11 genes in cotton species Gossypium hirsutum. The upstream promoter regions (2000 bp) of the candidate genes were retrieved from the Phytozome v.13 database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/). These sequences were then analyzed using the PlantCARE online tool (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to identify putative cis-acting regulatory elements.

Yeast two-hybrid

Protein–protein interactions were evaluated to test our hypotheses that candidate genes from D11 locus may interact with key regulatory proteins encoded by A07 locus to modulate pigment biosynthesis, contributing to variation in fiber color intensity and whiteness. The candidate genes from A07 and D11 locus were evaluated using the Matchmaker Gold Yeast two-hybrid (Y2HGold) system with the pGBKT7 (BD) and pGADT7 (AD) vectors. Primers were designed to specifically amplify the open reading frame with appropriate 5′cloning adapters in SnapGene (Additional file 26: Table S11). The coding sequence of TT2-A07 was cloned into the bait vector to generate a fusion with the GAL4 DNA binding Domain (BD). The coding sequences were individually cloned into the prey vector to generate fusions with the GAL4 activation domain (AD). All constructs were verified by sequencing prior to transformation. Bait and prey plasmids were co-transformed into Y2HGold cells. Co-transformants were selected on SD/-Leu/-Trp (DDO) medium and subsequently screened for interaction-dependent reporter activation on stringent selective media including SD/-Leu/-Trp/-His/3-At (TDO). Negative controls (bait with empty prey vector and prey with empty bait vector) were included to assess auto-activation and background growth. Interactions were scored based on reproducible growth on reporter media from multiple independent colonies. The Y2H assays were performed with three independent biological replicates. The Y2H assay was performed three times to ensure consistent results across replicates.

Statistical analysis

All phenotypic data related to cotton fiber color traits were initially compiled and pre-processed using Microsoft Excel 2016. Descriptive statistics, including maximum, minimum, and standard deviation values, were calculated for each of the 27 color-related indicators to assess variability across genotypes. Outlier detection and data distribution visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc.). To minimize environmental effects and improve trait estimation accuracy, Best Linear Unbiased Predictions (BLUPs) were calculated using the lme4 package in R software (v4.0), based on mixed linear models incorporating genotype and location as random effects. Descriptive statistics and visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package. Violin plots combined with boxplots were used to visualize the distribution of tone difference (EE) and hunter whiteness (WH) values across three different crosses and gene-index classes. Histograms were used to examine within-cross phenotypic distribution.

Chi2 tests were used to assess the fit between observed and expected F2 segregation ratios, using four biologically defined gene-index classes (0.05, df = 3). Expected values were calculated from Mendelian ratios. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed to examine associations between fiber color traits (e.g., total color difference, EE; Hunter’s whiteness, WH) and fiber quality parameters, including fiber length (FL), fiber strength (FS), fiber uniformity (FU), and micronaire (MIC). The correlation matrix was visualized using the corrplot package in R, with a heatmap format and a color scale ranging from − 1 to + 1 (blue = negative, red = positive). Correlations with absolute coefficient values (|r|) greater than 0.5 were considered biologically significant. Differences in EE and WH between LbLb and Lblb genotypes were assessed under two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test using genotypic and phenotypic data of F2 population is presented in Additional file 27: Table S12. Statistical significance was evaluated in R (P < 0.05).
