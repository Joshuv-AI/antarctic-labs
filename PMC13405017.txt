Development of a population-level frailty index using Quebec administrative data: a Canadian retrospective study
Authors: Hadjer Dahel, David Williamson, Qi Li, Christophe Dumais, Marc Brosseau, Samuel Chagnon, Jean-Pierre Bluteau, Justine Rinfret, Johannie Beaucage-Charron, Jean- Philippe Emond, Han Ting Wang

Introduction

Frailty is a multidimensional measure of vulnerability and a propensity for adverse outcomes when faced with acute stressors.1 2 As the human body ages, it loses its physiological reserve. For instance, the gradual loss of nephrons leads to kidney function decline and a higher risk of acute kidney injury when faced with infections.3 Chronic illnesses, polypharmacy, malnutrition and inflammatory states can accentuate this generalised process.4–6 Although frailty increases significantly with age, frailty is also present in younger populations. For example, in a recent Canadian cohort study, frailty status was estimated at 1.8% in the 18–34 age group and 20.2% in the 65+age group.7 Moreover, cognitive and functional decline are important factors in frailty. Cognitive impairment such as dementia is associated with loss of appetite,8 which can further accentuate malnutrition, loss of muscle mass (sarcopenia) and functional decline. Moreover, functional decline is strongly associated with frailty and is another important aspect of a patient’s healthcare trajectory.9 Frailty has been associated with poor outcomes, such as mortality and hospital readmission, in many diseases and chronic health conditions.10 In a meta-analysis, including studies from numerous countries, frailty was associated with earlier mortality and a higher likelihood of developing disabilities or loss of ability to perform daily living activities (OR 2.34 95% CI (1.77 to 3.09) and OR 2.05 (1.73 to 2.44), respectively).11 Thus a better understanding of a patient frailty state might help predict their outcomes and prognosis and ensure better care and management.

The prevalence of frailty is estimated to be approximately 23% of all intensive care unit (ICU) admissions12 and as much as 38% of octogenarians admitted to ICU.13 In 2014, a Canadian landmark cohort study showed that in critically ill patients older than 55 years, frailty increased hospital mortality by more than twofold. Moreover, this increase in the risk of death persisted up to 1 year after hospital discharge.14 Similarly, a low frailty score among octogenarians admitted to ICU was significantly associated with better physical recovery after discharge.15 While there is no gold standard for frailty measurement, the Canadian Study of Health and Ageing (CSHA) frailty index (FI) and its variations are widely used in intensive care.16 The CSHA FI is a 70-item clinical deficits list covering current patient diseases, activities of daily living and neurological problems.17 It has been associated with increased risk of death and admission to a care facility.17 Unfortunately, it is difficult to adapt to administrative databases since it contains deficits rarely, if never, present in administrative databases. Hence, a 30-item deficits model has been the most widely used index for health administrative datasets.18 These models have been successfully developed for Ontario, Canada19 as well as for Europe.20 Unfortunately, there is no existing frailty index adapted for Quebec administrative datasets, which emphasises the importance of developing and implementing an adapted frailty index. The objectives of this study were to develop and validate a Frailty Index in the Quebec health administrative dataset designed to identify frail ICU patients and investigate the effects of frailty on ICU patients’ outcomes.

Method

Cohort population

We used the administrative data of the Centre intégré universitaire de santé et de services sociaux de l'Est-de-l’Île-de-Montréal (CIUSSS-EMTL), which regroups two hospitals in Montréal, Québec: Hôpital Maisonneuve-Rosemont (HMR), a supra-regional academic hospital with 18 ICU beds, and Hôpital Santa Cabrini Ospedale (HSCO), a community hospital with 10 ICU beds. We created a cohort through data linkage of the following datasets: hospital admission summary database (MED-ECHO), homecare database (I-CLSC) and Gespharx in-hospital medication database. We used these datasets since they are identical to the provincial data. Since data collection for these datasets is mandated by the Quebec government across the province, we believe that the CIUSSS-EMTL data to be representative of Quebec’s population. We included patients 18 years and older with an index ICU admission between 2016 and 2023 in one of the healthcare centres of CIUSSS-EMTL. Follow-up began at the date of the first ICU admission within the study period. We excluded patients under 18 years of age, patients who did not live in the CIUSSS-EMTL territory, and patients with invalid public insurance number for lack of their data on healthcare usage. Follow-up ended at death or end of study, whichever occurred first. Ethics approval was obtained by the CIUSSS-EMTL Research Ethics Board (number: 2024–3560).

Frailty index development

To develop our frailty index, we followed the steps established by Rockwood et al.18 We created a multidimensional frailty index covering the following domains: comorbidities, cognitive problems, autonomy, psychosocial state and socioeconomic status.

Initially, we wished to adapt two existing Canadian frailty indexes to our provincial data. We believed this would help with generalisability and future capacity to compare data across provinces. We chose the McIsaac Frailty index and the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) frailty index. The McIsaac index is a 30-deficit index using the Ontario Health Administrative Datasets held at the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences.19 The CIHI conceptualised a 36-deficit frailty index spanning six domains, namely morbidity, function and cognition and moods.21 Deficits were identified using the following datasets: MED-ECHO, I-CLSC data and Gespharx medication data for chronically prescribed medications (see online supplemental table 2 for details on each database). We identified deficits in our datasets using a 1-year period prior to hospital admission. Unfortunately, both frailty indexes failed to meet the total number of 30 deficits due to differences in availability of variables. For instance, we could not compute a hospitalized-patient one-year mortality risk (HOMR) score for the McIsaac deficit, and there was virtually no patient with delusions and hallucinations for the CIHI deficit (see online supplemental tables 3, 4). Furthermore, similar to the Quebec Health administrative datasets, our dataset does not distinguish between missing and absent deficit. For example, if diabetes is not diagnosed for our patient, it will be counted as 0 in our dataset, independent whether the patient is not diabetic or he is diabetic but not diagnosed.

Therefore, we combined deficits with a prevalence of at least 2% from both indexes to create our own frailty index. When two deficits from the models designated the same medical condition (eg, cardiovascular diseases in McIsaac and cardiac and vascular diseases in CIHI), we used the deficit which had the strongest correlation with age.

Our final frailty index included a total of 30 deficits covering the following domains: comorbidities, cognitive problems, autonomy, psychosocial state and socioeconomic status (table 1 and online supplemental table 1). We defined multimorbidity using the Charlson Comorbidity Score22 23 with a cut-off value of 3 and higher. To create an anticholinergic risk scale, we created a list of anticholinergic medication based on the anticholinergic risk scale study,24 Beers criteria25 and a reference list of anticholinergic medications.26 For socioeconomic status, we used the Pampalon score.27 We used a cut-off of 4, meaning that the person had to be in the fourth quintile (higher material and social deprivation) to be considered socially and materially deprived. The Pampalon score is a regional deprivation index that uses local data, such as the ratio of employment per population in the region and the average individual revenue, to assign a material and social score to all individuals in the region. Each aspect is then divided into quintiles, with higher quintiles indicating a higher deprivation.

To obtain the frailty score, we summed all the patient’s deficits and divided them by the total number of deficits (30). We used a cut-off score of 0.20 (6 deficits) to indicate a patient being frail. This cut-off is well-defined and vastly used in frailty studies.18 We also performed a sensitivity analysis with a 0.25 cut-off, which is another well-defined cut-off.18 Due to the nature of frailty indices from administrative datasets, they tend to underestimate the prevalence of deficits. Hence using a higher cut-off can strengthen our association between frailty and primary outcome.28

Co-variable definitions

Our other variables of interest were age, defined as age at the index date, sex, admitting establishment (HMR vs HSCO), mechanical ventilation and dialysis during admission and year of admission and primary admission diagnosis.

Outcomes

For our predictive validity, we chose the outcome of in-hospital mortality. As the relationship between frailty and early mortality is well established, in-hospital mortality has become a widely used outcome to predict the validity of frailty indices.29 30

Statistical analysis

We performed descriptive statistics for population demographic data and deficit prevalence. For continuous variables, when appropriate, we performed student T-tests or Mann-Whitney U test to compare the variables between frail and non-frail patients. For categorical variables, we used the χ2 test. Categorical variables were presented as percentages. Normally distributed data were presented as means with SD, and skewed data was presented as medians and IQRs. Missing data were treated as ‘0’ for most variables, including deficits and medication. Due to provincial mandate, we believe missing data to be minimal within our dataset.

Following the guidelines developed by Rockwood, we assessed the validity of our frailty index by performing correlation tests between mean frailty scores and age using Pearson correlation test. We also assessed skewness of our frailty index distribution.

We examined the difference between frail and non-frail patients for the following variables: admission diagnosis, defined as the primary admitting diagnosis based on MED-Echo ICD-10 codes, hospital and ICU length of stay and hospitalisation or ED visit 1 year prior to index date.

For the clinical outcome of in-hospital mortality, we performed a multivariable logistic regression. We controlled for known confounding variables (age, sex, dialysis, ventilation, admission year and admitting institution). We did not control for comorbidity since it is part of our frailty index; thus, adjusting for it could lead to over-adjustment.31 For each continuous variable (age, frailty score), we verified conformity with the linear gradient. We also performed multicollinearity tests between variables in our model. We did not perform C-statistic tests and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests since our objective was to isolate the effects of frailty on our outcome rather than a prediction model. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.1.

Patient and public involvement

It was not appropriate to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

We included 10 849 patients with a mean age of 67.7 (SD 15.4) years (figure 1). Our frailty index had a positive correlation with age (r=0.80, p<0.0001) and a skewness of 1.58 (figure 2). 55% of our cohort were males and 20% were frail. Frail patients were older (mean age 73.1 vs 66.3 years) and more likely to have dialysis on admission (7.8% vs 4.4%; p<0.0001). The two main admission diagnoses were circulatory diseases (38.7%) and respiratory disease (12.5%), with the latter being more prevalent in the frail population (16.8% vs 11.4%; p<0.0001) (table 2). 87% of the frail population had been hospitalised 1 year prior to their index date, while only 9.2% of the non-frail were hospitalised 1 year prior to their index date. Frail patients had longer hospital and ICU lengths of stay (LoS) (median of 12 in hospital with 3 days in ICU opposed to 8 days in hospital with three in ICU; p<0.0001).

Discussion

We created a 30-item frailty index compatible with Quebec administrative databases. We included 10 849 patients from two hospitals in Montreal. Our frailty index was strongly associated with in-hospital mortality.

In our attempt to implement the CIHI and McIsaac indices, we faced certain difficulties. Indeed, several deficits were not operational in our dataset (either because the variables did not exist in our database (eg, home oxygen from McIsaac) or they were not captured properly (eg, fatigue from CIHI). Such issues prevented proper implementation of these indices in our database, which further reinforced the need for an index compatible with Quebec administrative datasets. Moreover, when comparing frailty prevalence between indices, our index adequately captured frail patients. A fifth of our population was frail, which is comparable to that reported in other frailty studies in critically ill patients. Indeed, studies using the CFS in the ICU population have reported a frailty prevalence between 22% and 35.8%.32–34 However, frailty prevalence using McIsaac’s and CIHI’s indices was much lower (10% and 13.5% for CIHI and McIsaac respectively). Thus, our index was able to adequately capture the frailty state of our population.

Our frailty index followed the Rockwood guidelines in deficit choice and index validation.18 Indeed, we ensured that our deficits and overall frailty score were correlated with age and a skewed frailty distribution. While Rockwood suggests a skewness of 1.60 and higher, our skewness was slightly lower. Moreover, we have established a multidimensional frailty index using Quebec administrative data. Contrary to the modified frailty index mFI-11, an 11-item frailty index focusing mainly on patient medical history and functional status, our index covers more domains, such as material and social deprivation. In sum, our frailty index respects validation criteria set by Rockwood and spans several health and vulnerability domains to better capture the frailty state of patients.

In our study, frailty was associated with an increased risk of in-hospital mortality. Our results align with those found in the literature. Truly, several frailty studies have reported an increase in mortality in a frail population. For instance, the frailty, outcomes, recovery and care steps of critically ill patients (FORECAST) study reported that the prevalence of mortality was significantly higher in frail patients (35% vs 19%, p<0.001).35 Another study on frail critically ill patients with congestive heart failure reported a 40% increase in risk of death for frail patients compared with non-frail (aOR 1.40, 95% CI (1.07 to 1.83)).36 In sum, our frailty index accurately predicted the association between frailty and in-hospital mortality.

Strengths and Limitations

By adapting two existing Canadian frailty indices, we ensured that selected deficits were meaningful and appropriate to our provincial data. In fact, our frailty index might gain power and sensitivity when applied since less information is missing compared with using either the CIHI or McIsaac index directly. This will greatly facilitate our future work on the Quebec government data. Furthermore, the availability of such an index will allow province-wide usage for healthcare research and resource usage planning. Furthermore, by having a provincial-wide frailty index can help researchers in other fields and lay the foundation for implementation studies, aiming to improve outcomes for frail patients.

We used GerpharX, an in-hospital medication database. However, this information was missing or incomplete for several patients. We believe that when applied to a provincial dataset, our medication-based deficits will be more sensitive. Also, if a patient did not have prior admission or contact with the CIUSSS-EMTL, we could not properly identify all the deficits associated with the frailty index. Therefore, missing deficits and medication data might lead to an underestimation of our frail population. This can also explain the slightly lower skewness of our model. Indeed, some of our deficits had a prevalence lower than the recommended 5%. However, we expect the prevalence of these deficits to be higher on the larger provincial database since it would capture patients' contact with the overall provincial health system. Moreover, some patients might have been transferred, redirected to or visited other hospitals and emergency departments, which might lead to an underestimation of our outcomes. Therefore, we might have underestimated the effect size of frailty and hospital mortality.

Conclusion

We created a multidimensional 30-item frailty index. We showed a strong correlation between frailty and risk in-hospital mortality. We believe the next step to be implementation of this index at the hospital or governmental level to help with identifying at risk population or evaluating processes of care.
