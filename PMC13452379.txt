Comparative assessment of serum adipokine and trace element alterations in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Van A Ahmed, Namama S Hamad, Diary I Tofiq, Aso Q Hassan, Hersh H Yousif, Savya N Mhamad, Helin H Qader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate serum leptin, adiponectin, and selected trace elements in newly diagnosed patients with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) before and after treatment and to assess their diagnostic potential as biomarkers.MethodsThis prospective case–control study included 50 newly diagnosed patients with PTC and 50 healthy controls. Serum leptin, adiponectin, and trace element levels were measured pre- and post-treatment in patients and compared to controls.ResultsBefore treatment, patients with PTC had significantly higher leptin and lower adiponectin levels than post-treatment patients and healthy controls (both p < 0.0001). Zinc, selenium, and magnesium levels were significantly decreased, whereas manganese, copper, and iodine levels were significantly increased (all p < 0.001). Leptin was negatively correlated with Zn, Se, Mg, and Ca (p < 0.0001), whereas adiponectin showed positive correlations. The leptin/adiponectin ratio demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, with an area under the ROC curve of 0.951.ConclusionPatients with PTC exhibit significant alterations in serum adipokines and trace elements that improve following treatment. Leptin, adiponectin, and particularly the leptin/adiponectin ratio may serve as promising non-invasive biomarkers for the diagnosis and postoperative monitoring of PTC.Keywords: Adipokine, Adiponectin, Biomarker, Leptin, Thyroid carcinoma, Trace element

Introduction

Thyroid carcinoma is the most common endocrine malignancy and it is frequently associated with rising rates of metabolic disorders and obesity.1,2 The global increase in the incidence of thyroid carcinoma has attracted great interest over recent decades.3 Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the most prevalent and well-differentiated subtype, is generally characterized by its favorable prognosis and indolent nature. However, some PTC patients may develop radioiodine resistance and distant metastases, leading to increased disease-specific mortality.4,5

Several hypotheses have been proposed to explain the relationship between obesity and thyroid carcinoma, including hyperinsulinemia, upregulation of aromatase activity, chronic low-grade inflammation, altered immune response, and DNA damage induced by oxidative stress.6 In addition, emerging evidence suggests that obesity-related changes in adipokine expression may influence cell proliferation and potentially trigger the development of thyroid cancer.7

Adipokines are cytokines secreted by adipose tissues and they play crucial roles in regulating metabolism, immune responses, and inflammation.8 Leptin and adiponectin are integral to the pathogenesis of thyroid cancer, particularly PTC, because they influence cellular proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammation-associated obesity mechanisms.9 Leptin facilitates tumor progression through the PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, JAK/STAT3, and RhoA/LIMK1/Cofilin pathways, and elevated serum leptin levels are correlated with increased risk.10 Conversely, adiponectin exhibits anti-proliferative and anti-motility effects via AdipoR1/R2-mediated suppression of RAS/RAF/MAPK signaling,11 as demonstrated in BCPAP/K1 cell lines. Leptin promotes aggressive PTC tumor spread by overriding the protective anti-motility role of adiponectin through antagonistic effects on shared signaling pathways, particularly MAPK/ERK, thereby exacerbating obesity-linked disease progression.12 This leptin–adiponectin antagonism probably amplifies the aggressiveness of PTC in obese individuals, where elevated leptin and reduced adiponectin levels dysregulate proliferation, survival, and metastatic potential via PI3K/AKT and JAK/STAT3 crosstalk with MAPK/ERK, positioning these adipokines as key modulators of tumor microenvironment dynamics.12

Trace elements play vital roles in the synthesis and metabolism of thyroid hormones, which are essential for normal thyroid gland function, and key elements such as selenium, zinc, copper, and iodine must remain in dynamic balance to support thyroid hormone production and cellular protection.13 This balance may be disrupted by a lack or excess of certain elements, which can trigger thyroid dysfunction and cancerous growth via various pathways, such as antioxidant defense, hormone production, and DNA repair, thereby inducing oncogenesis.14 Selenoproteins provide antioxidant defense against oxidative stress through glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductases, and regulate thyroid hormone metabolism via iodothyronine deiodinases.15 Selenium deficiency reduces H2O2 degradation and exacerbates oxidative stress-induced damage, leading to thyroid cell apoptosis through impaired glutathione peroxidase activity.16 Clinical observations indicate that serum zinc and selenium levels are significantly lower in patients with thyroid cancer than healthy controls, and increase to normal levels after surgical removal of the thyroid gland,17 and blood levels of manganese, copper, zinc, selenium, and lead are significantly lower in thyroid malignancies.18 Recent epidemiological evidence suggests that there are diverse associations with thyroid cancer risk, where zinc and copper have potential protective effects, whereas lead, arsenic, and chromium have positive associations with disease development.19 These complex interactions between trace elements and thyroid carcinogenesis highlight the importance of understanding elemental dysregulation as both a potential etiological factor and a therapeutic target in the management of thyroid cancer.

This study aimed to evaluate serum concentrations of leptin, adiponectin, and trace elements in newly diagnosed PTC patients before and after treatment, and to assess their relationships and diagnostic potential as biomarkers for early PTC detection.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This prospective case–control study was conducted at the Hiwa Oncology and Hematology Hospital in Sulaymaniyah, Iraq, between September 2025 and February 2026. All subjects provided written informed consent before sample collection.

Study population

Participants were stratified into three groups: (i) newly diagnosed PTC cases prior to treatment (n = 50) confirmed by histopathological examination of surgical or biopsy specimens, (ii) the same PTC cases at follow-up sampling exactly 6 weeks post-treatment (n = 50), and (iii) healthy controls with matched age and sex (n = 50).

All enrolled cases received standardized definitive therapy comprising total thyroidectomy in accordance with institutional oncological guidelines. The exclusion criteria for patients included the presence of pre-existing chronic systemic conditions, such as diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, chronic kidney disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and cardiovascular disease. In addition, individuals were excluded with concurrent or prior malignancies of any histological type, active infectious or inflammatory diseases, autoimmune thyroid disorders (including Hashimoto's thyroiditis and Graves' disease), pregnancy or lactation, or those who had received supplementation with trace element-containing preparations (such as selenium, zinc, copper, and iron) or multivitamins within 3 months prior to enrollment. Healthy volunteers aged ≥18 years were matched to PTC cases by age (±5 years), sex, and body mass index (BMI) (±3 kg/m2). Participants had no personal or family history of thyroid disease or malignancy. Controls were ineligible if biochemical or serological evidence indicated thyroid dysfunction defined as abnormal thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free triiodothyronine (FT3), or free thyroxine (FT4) levels, or the presence of circulating thyroid autoantibodies, ensuring a truly euthyroid reference population.

Data collection

Baseline data were collected using standardized, pre-validated structured questionnaires administered by trained clinical research personnel at enrollment. Venous blood samples (5 mL) were obtained via antecubital venipuncture in the morning after minimum overnight fasting for 8–12 h to reduce prandial variability in adipokine and trace element concentrations. For case participants, the pre-treatment serum sample was obtained 1–4 weeks after diagnostic surgery and prior to any exposure to iodinated contrast agents or definitive treatment. The post-treatment specimen was collected exactly 6 weeks after definitive thyroidectomy. Control participants provided a single baseline sample under identical fasting conditions. Immediately after collection, samples were centrifuged at 3000×g and 4 °C for 10 min. Serum was subsequently separated into cryogenic microtubes and stored at −80 °C until analysis to reduce the need for freeze–thaw cycles and ensure analytical consistency.

Laboratory analyses

Serum leptin and adiponectin concentrations were quantified using BT LAB ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, China) for leptin (Cat No. E1559Hu; intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) < 8% and inter-assay CV < 10%) and adiponectin (Cat. No. E1550Hu; intra-assay CV < 8% and inter-assay CV < 10%), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and measured with a BioTek 800ts microplate reader (Agilent BioTek Inc., Winooski, VT, USA). All samples were analyzed in duplicate to ensure analytical reproducibility.

Serum concentrations of selenium (Se), zinc (Zn), copper (Cu), manganese (Mn), magnesium (Mg), calcium (Ca), and iodine (I2) were quantified using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, PerkinElmer Optima 2100 DV, USA). Regular analyses of calibration standards and quality control samples were performed to guarantee the precision and accuracy of ICP readings.

Serum TSH, FT4, and FT3 levels were measured by using Elecsys immunoassay kits (Roche Diagnostic GmbH, Germany) with a Cobas e411 analyzer (Roche Diagnostic, Switzerland), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysisa

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 10.4.1 (GraphPad Software LLC, San Diego, CA, US) or SPSS version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). The Mann–Whitney U test was conducted to compare leptin, adiponectin, and trace element levels between the control and pre-treatment groups, and between the control and post-treatment groups. In addition, comparisons were performed between pre-treatment and post-treatment measurements obtained from the same patients using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Spearman's correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate pre- and post-treatment associations between leptin and adiponectin with trace elements. The Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure was used to adjust p-values from pairwise comparisons and correlation analyses, thereby controlling for multiple testing. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the independent associations of adipokines with PTC after adjusting for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, and serum TSH levels. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed using SPSS software to evaluate the diagnostic utility of leptin and adiponectin in patients with thyroid cancer.

Serum adipokine concentrations in patients with PTC (pre- and post-therapy) and controls

The serum leptin levels were significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in pre-treatment PTC patients (20.80 (18.70–25.45)) compared with the post-treatment PTC patients (10.5 (8.4–12.78)) and controls (9.1 (1.35–12.23)), whereas the serum adiponectin levels were significantly lower (p = 0.003) in the pre-treatment PTC patients (1.8 (1.22–2.7)) than the post-treatment PTC patients (2.45 (1.52–3.74)) and controls (6.2 (4.65–8.4)) (p < 0.0001). The leptin to adiponectin ratio was significantly higher (p < 0.0001) in pre-treatment patients with PTC (7.05 (4.59–9.03)) compared with post-treatment PTC patients (3.79 (3.34–4.79)) and controls (3.03 (2.32–4.18)). Following treatment, the levels of leptin and adiponectin, and their ratio were significantly lower, approaching those observed in the control group (Figure 1).

Correlations between adipokine levels and trace elements in pre- and post-treatment PTC patients

Before treatment, the leptin levels had strong negative correlations with serum Zn (r = −0.430, p < 0.003), Se (r = −0.717, p < 0.0001), and Ca (r = −0.574, p < 0.0001), as well as a moderate negative correlation with Mg (r = −0.424, p = 0.004), indicating that higher leptin concentrations were associated with lower levels of these elements. By contrast, leptin had moderate positive correlations with serum Cu (r = 0.483, p = 0.0009) and Mn (r = 0.496, p = 0.0006), and a strong positive correlation with I2 (r = 0.684, p < 0.0001) (Table 3).

Logistic regression analysis

Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the independent associations between serum leptin, adiponectin, and the leptin/adiponectin ratio with PTC after adjusting for age, sex, BMI, and TSH levels, as shown in Table 7. In the leptin model, higher serum leptin concentrations were independently associated with increased odds of PTC (odds ratio (OR) = 1.75, p < 0.0001). In the adiponectin model, lower adiponectin concentrations were independently associated with PTC (OR = 0.434, p < 0.0001). In the leptin/adiponectin ratio model, an elevated ratio was significantly associated with increased odds of PTC (OR = 2.65, p < 0.0001).

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis was conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of serum leptin, adiponectin, and the leptin/adiponectin ratio in differentiating PTC patients from healthy controls. Leptin exhibited strong discriminatory power, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.944 (p < 0.0001) at optimal cutoff, yielding sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 68%. Adiponectin performed well (AUC: 0.888, p < 0.0001), with sensitivity of 88% and specificity of 70%. However, the leptin/adiponectin ratio achieved the highest AUC of 0.951 (p < 0.0001), corresponding to sensitivity of 94% and specificity of 72% (Table 8), suggesting its superior diagnostic utility compared with the individual markers.

Discussion

In the present study, the serum concentrations of leptin, adiponectin, and trace elements were evaluated in patients with PTC both before and after treatment. The results demonstrated that the serum leptin levels were significantly higher whereas the serum adiponectin levels were lower in patients with PTC before treatment, with partial normalization after treatment. Furthermore, PTC was associated with notable differences in trace element levels, including lower Zn, Mg, and Se levels, but higher Cu, Mn, and iodine levels. Significant correlations were observed between adipokines and trace elements. ROC analysis further highlighted the potential diagnostic utility of adipokines, particularly the leptin/adiponectin ratio, in distinguishing PTC patients from healthy controls.

Serum leptin was higher whereas reduced adiponectin was lower in pre-treatment PTC patients compared with the controls, but the levels normalized post-therapy. Thus, imbalanced leptin and adiponectin levels, as well as an elevated leptin/adiponectin ratio, may be applied as superior oncogenic biomarkers. This interpretation aligns with reports indicating a lower adiponectin concentration and higher leptin concentration in malignant thyroid tissue compared with benign or normal parenchyma.20, 21, 22, 23, 24

Dysregulation of trace element levels is associated with the development of thyroid cancer. In the present study, the serum Zn, Mg, and Se concentrations were lower in pre-treatment PTC patients compared with healthy controls and post-treatment PTC patients. These findings are consistent with previous studies that found significantly lower serum Zn levels in patients with thyroid cancer, and it was suggested that zinc deficiency may impair p53-mediated apoptosis and promote malignant transformation.14,25, 26, 27 Mg deficiency is linked with increased oxidative stress and genomic instability, which may facilitate carcinogenesis.26,28 Selenium is crucial for thyroid function and protects against thyroid carcinogenesis, and inadequate selenium intake is associated with suppressed deiodinase activity and oxidative DNA damage.29,30

Serum Cu, Mn, and I2 levels were significantly higher in pre-treatment PTC patients compared with healthy controls and post-treatment PTC patients, implicating disrupted trace element metabolism during neoplastic disease. These findings are consistent with those obtained in previous studies.17,25,26,31 Cu is an essential nutrient but it can promote cellular proiferation and tumor angiogenesis in a malignant microenvironment. Mn may contribute to the cellular response to oxidative damage, which is a hallmark of tumorigenesis. Serum iodine concentrations were significantly lower after total thyroidectomy compared with those in the controls, and previous studies32,33 also found that the removal of thyroid tissue reduced the reservoir of iodine-avid cells.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous study has directly investigated the correlations between serum adipokine levels and the status of trace elements in PTC patients. A major finding obtained in the present study was the significant association between adipokines and trace elements in patients with PTC. Leptin had negative correlations with Zn, Se, and Ca, whereas adiponectin had positive correlations with Zn, Mg, Se, and Ca, and inverse correlations with Cu and Mn. The mechanisms that underlie these associations remain unclear but they suggest a possible interaction between metabolic and micronutrient alterations in thyroid cancer. Previous studies have shown that trace elements may influence oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and adipokine regulation, which contribute to tumor progression.

In this study, the ROC analysis results demonstrated that serum adipokines exhibit significant diagnostic potential for differentiating patients with PTC from healthy controls. In particular, the leptin/adiponectin ratio achieved the highest area under the curve, indicating that the combined assessment of these adipokines can enhance the overall diagnostic precision compared with using individual markers alone.

The present study had several limitations, including the small, single-center sample size. Potential confounders were also not adequately controlled, including metabolic status, diet, BMI, physical activity, and medication use. Inflammatory (interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-1, IL-10, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha) and antioxidant (superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase) biomarkers were not measured. Longitudinal studies are required to confirm the prognostic and diagnostic value of these biomarkers in broader populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that the serum levels of adipokines and trace elements differed significantly before and after treatment in patients with PTC, suggesting that adipokine–trace element imbalances may contribute to the pathogenesis of PTC. Clinically, markers such as leptin, adiponectin, and particularly the leptin/adiponectin ratio could serve as potential non-invasive adjunct biomarkers for PTC detection and monitoring, although further validation in larger cohorts is required.
