 ROUGHRIDER CARBON STORAGE HUB

 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Roughrider Carbon Storage Hub was a 2-year project (October 2023 – September 2025)
 conducted by the Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) focused on advancing the
 feasibility of a commercial-scale carbon dioxide (CO2) geologic storage hub in McKenzie County,
 North Dakota. The project’s objective was to investigate the potential that stacked storage
 complexes (multiple deep saline formations) can safely and economically store at least 50 million
 tonnes of CO2 within 30 years. The captured CO2 would be sourced from industrial emitters
 including project partner ONEOK, Inc.’s gas-processing plants and a planned gas-to-liquids
 facility.

 Drilling of the Roughrider 1 stratigraphic test well (14,979-ft total depth) was completed in
 November 2024. The wellbore intersected four candidate storage formations: Inyan Kara, Broom
 Creek, Mission Canyon, and Black Island–Deadwood. Operational challenges, including a stuck
 drill string, were resolved without long-term impact. A comprehensive logging and coring program
 was conducted, followed by successful well abandonment and site reclamation. Over 660 ft of
 4-in. whole core was retrieved. Core plug samples were processed and analyzed for petrophysical
 and geochemical properties. Results confirmed promising porosity and permeability in the Inyan
 Kara and Broom Creek Formations and removal of the Mission Canyon and Black Island–
 Deadwood horizons from further investigation. Data derived from the logging and coring program
 were used to improve initial geologic models built from legacy data. CO2 injection simulations
 showed that the Inyan Kara alone can feasibly store the target mass of CO2. Because of subtle
 differences in geologic structure and porosity trends between the formations, a stacked storage
 scenario using the Broom Creek and Inyan Kara Formations resulted in a larger overall plume area
 than using the Inyan Kara alone.

 Preliminary CO2 pipeline routes from the industrial sources were mapped utilizing existing
 rights of way and evaluated for capacity and cost using U.S. Department of Energy Office of Fossil
 Energy and Carbon Management/National Energy Technology Laboratory models and U.S.
 Environmental Protection Agency emissions data. Integrating capture, transport, and storage cost
 estimates with policy incentives (e.g., 45Q credits) provided a total cost-per-ton analysis. Results
 indicate that the small scale of the volumes to be transported over the cumulative large distances
 does not support the project’s financial viability. However, the groundwork laid during this project
 from geological, regulatory, and social perspectives positions the Roughrider hub site as a
 promising candidate for commercial carbon storage in North Dakota, especially if the economy of
 scale is introduced for CO2 transportation to the hub site.

 ROUGHRIDER CARBON STORAGE HUB

 INTRODUCTION AND OBJECTIVES

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) undertook the Roughrider Carbon
 Storage Hub project in northwestern North Dakota to evaluate the potential for geologic storage
 of carbon dioxide (CO2) captured from several gas-processing plants owned and operated by
 project partner ONEOK, Inc. (ONEOK) and a planned gas-to-liquids (GTL) plant. This feasibility
 study evaluated the aggregation of CO2 captured from multiple sources for injection into geologic
 storage complexes in a stacked storage configuration. The hub scenario included several aspects
 that made it a highly qualified candidate for a feasibility study with a notably reduced project risk
 profile. These included 1) a project partner with a committed goal to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG)
 emissions; 2) prior subsurface data analysis supporting a stacked storage scenario with adequate
 CO2 storage volume; 3) commitment from local, regional, and state-level stakeholders; and 4) a
 state with Class VI primacy. In addition, there was potential for expansion/flexibility to include
 additional CO2 sources (including direct air capture). These project characteristics, combined with
 the EERC’s extensive carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) experience through the
 Plains CO2 Reduction (PCOR) Partnership and CarbonSAFE (Carbon Storage Assurance Facility
 Enterprise) Phases II and III, provided a strong foundation to meet the goals of the U.S. Department
 of Energy (DOE) National Energy Technology Laboratory’s (NETL’s) CarbonSAFE Initiative
 efficiently and effectively.

 The project’s objective was to validate the feasibility of stacked storage complexes (multiple
 deep saline formations) to safely and economically store at least 50 million tonnes (MMt) of CO2
 within 30 years. The project’s primary goal was to elevate the site’s classification to a contingent
 storage resource, supported by robust technical, economic, and stakeholder analyses.

 STORAGE COMPLEX CHARACTERIZATION

 Storage complex characterization encompassed all activities to identify and evaluate the
 geologic formations that could serve as CO2 reservoirs and seals in the Roughrider project area of
 interest (AOI) (Figure 1). Four stacked storage complexes were investigated—the Inyan Kara,
 Broom Creek, Mission Canyon, and Black Island–Deadwood Formations—each comprising an
 injection target (saline aquifer) with one or more overlying impermeable confining units
 (Figure 2)

 Data for characterizing these complexes came from two sources: existing data (legacy well
 logs, cores, and seismic surveys) and new data acquired by drilling a dedicated stratigraphic test
 well, Roughrider 1 (American Petroleum Institute [API] No. 33053103570000, North Dakota
 Industrial Commission [NDIC] File No. 41089). The characterization task was closely linked to
 subsequent modeling by providing raw information that fed into geologic and reservoir simulation
 models of the AOI.

 Existing Data Acquisition and Analysis

 At the start of the project, a database of existing geological and petrophysical data in the
 region was compiled. Well log data and interpreted formation top depths from 954 wells in
 McKenzie County were used to characterize the depth, thickness, and extent of the potential
 storage complexes. Core analyses from relevant formations archived with the North Dakota
 Geological Survey (NDGS) were also used to calibrate well log data with measured petrophysical
 data. The project acquired a three-dimensional (3D) seismic volume (“Boxcar Butte 3D” survey)
 covering part of the AOI and two two-dimensional (2D) seismic lines connecting the 3D volume
 to the Roughrider 1 well location (Table 1). No new seismic data were acquired for this project.
 The licensed 3D and 2D seismic data were reprocessed for amplitude consistency and improved
 structural imaging. Seismic interpretation products (seismic horizons and acoustic impedance
 volumes), well logs, and core data were used to support the generation of the 3D geologic model.

 Seismic Interpretation

 Wells with compressional sonic logs, density logs, and if available, shear sonic logs, were
 selected for the geophysical analysis of the Boxcar Butte 3D. These well locations, seismic lines
 (2D) and area (3D), and Roughrider 1 are shown in Figure 3. Seven wells, including Roughrider
 1, were used for creating seismic-to-well ties. Each of the well ties was performed individually
 and verified through seismic interpretation. Initial well ties were performed over the entire
 stratigraphic section to integrate geological and geophysical data and to establish the structural
 framework. Detailed, interval-specific well ties were performed before seismic inversion.

 4

 Figure 3. Location map of 2D and 3D seismic in relation to the wells used in the seismic
 interpretation.

 The 2D seismic data were tied to the 3D survey in S&P Kingdom interpretation software.
 Roughrider 1 was projected onto Line 81-257-115, 1073 ft west of the well, and a correlative well
 tie was created. Key surfaces were mapped for potential storage reservoirs, e.g., the tops of the
 Inyan Kara, Broom Creek, Mission Canyon, and Deadwood. A structural interpretation was
 completed and mapped surfaces depth-converted using well formation tops and mapped horizons.
 Depth surfaces were well constrained by the well data and provided to the geologic modeling team.

 Structural features, including fault lineaments and subtle flexures, were identified in the
 center of the Boxcar Butte 3D along the Boxcar Butte feature—a linear area of relief in the
 basement that appears to have affected the overlying stratigraphy throughout the history of the
 Williston Basin (Figure 4). Offset related to the development of this structure is noticeable in the
 overlying Deadwood Formation and potentially extending up into the Bakken and Mission Canyon
 coincides with the unconformity at the Silurian–Devonian boundary. No faults were interpreted
 along the 2D seismic lines.

 5

 Figure 4. Mapped Deadwood Formation top in time (left) with Line 109 from the Boxcar
 Butte 3D shown in the section on the right. The Boxcar Butte structure is indicated with the
 black dashed oval oriented NNW-SSE in the map view. In section view (right), this structure

 Poststack inversion modeling was performed for the Inyan Kara, Broom Creek, and Mission
 Canyon potential storage intervals to assess the distribution of reservoir properties. The product of
 the poststack inversion is a model of acoustic impedance, which is related to the physical properties
 inherent within the rocks. The impedance volumes helped to infer reservoir properties within the
 extent the seismic data between and away from the points of well control. Higher areas of
 impedance tend to correlate with tighter rock (lower porosity), while areas with lower impedance
 tend to correlate with areas of higher porosity such as porous sandstones and porous carbonates.

 Geologic Characterization Well

 A key project accomplishment was the successful drilling and data collection from the
 Roughrider 1 stratigraphic test well. Planning for this well included site selection, well design,
 developing programs for drilling, formation evaluation, plug and abandonment (P&A), obtaining
 a permit to drill, well pad construction, and contracting a drilling rig. Drilling began on
 September 26, 2024. A comprehensive formation evaluation program was designed for the
 Roughrider 1 well, including 4-in. whole-core collection and an advanced suite of well logging,
 formation testing, and fluid sampling targeting the Inyan Kara, Broom Creek, Mission Canyon
 (Tilston interval), and Black Island–Deadwood Formations and their adjacent confining zones. A
 summary of the well design and data collected from core, logs, and tests is provided in Figures 5
 and 6.

 Formation evaluation activities were completed on November 30, 2024. The well was then

 Roughrider 1

 Geologic site screening was conducted to determine a drilling location with a high likelihood
 of encountering all four formations of interest. Other determining factors for the site were an
 amenable landowner and the surface use agreement.

 An application for permit to drill (APD) was submitted to the NDIC Oil and Gas Division
 in July 2024 and approved in August 2024. A 450-ft × 450-ft well pad and access road were
 constructed shortly after APD approval.

 The 17½-in. surface hole was drilled with freshwater drilling fluid to a depth of 2235 ft. An
 openhole wireline logging run was attempted in the surface to collect gamma ray (GR), resistivity,
 and porosity, also known as a triple combo log, but data collection was limited to the uppermost
 423 ft due to an obstruction in the hole. A 13⅜” casing string was set at 2135 ft, isolating the
 lowest USDW. A cased-hole radial cement bond log (RCBL) was run to confirm wellbore
 integrity.

 The 8¾-in. openhole was drilled and cored with oil-based mud to a total depth of 14,979 ft.
 A total of 665.54 ft of core was cut, and 661.3 ft of 4-in. whole core was recovered (99.36%).
 Cored sections of the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek also included portions of the upper and lower
 confining zones: the Skull Creek and Swift Formations and the Opeche and Amsden Formations,
 respectively. The Mission Canyon Formation thickness is 450–550 ft across the Roughrider model
 area and contains multiple potential reservoirs, including the Frobisher–Alida (Rival and Bluell
 subintervals) and the Tilston interval. The Tilston interval showed the highest potential reservoir
 quality (i.e., porosity/permeability) in offset wells and therefore became the focus of the Mission
 Canyon characterization efforts. One 120-ft core barrel captured a majority of the Tilston interval.
 The final coring run captured a portion of the Icebox and the Black Island Formations. Prior to
 drilling, the Black Island was expected to be the highest reservoir quality in the Black Island–
 Deadwood storage complex. However, because of low-porosity/permeability observations in the
 field, additional coring in the Deadwood was not pursued. Given the observed low quality of the
 formation, further testing and sampling in the Black Island was also eliminated from the wireline
 logging program. Core barrels for each run consisted of four 30-ft steel barrels with aluminum
 liners. Upon recovery, the core was cut into 3-ft increments on-site, placed into containers, and
 shipped to a commercial lab for initial processing. Core analysis is described later in the report and
 in Appendix D.

 Wireline logging and testing of the long-string section of the Roughrider 1 well was
 conducted from the surface casing shoe at 2135 ft to a total depth (TD) of 14,979 ft. The wireline
 logging suite included triple combo, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), spectral GR, capture
 spectroscopy, borehole imaging, dipole sonic, four-arm caliper and deviation (inclinometer),
 formation pressure testing, fluid sampling, and minifracture stress testing. The pressure testing,

 The acquired logs were used to pick formation top depths and interpret lithology,

 Core Analysis and Testing

 The whole core recovered from Roughrider 1 was shipped to a commercial lab for cleaning,
 slabbing, photography, and core GR. The core was later returned to the NDGS Wilson M. Laird
 Core and Sample Library in Grand Forks, where the EERC sampled the core. A series of
 petrographic, geochemical, geomechanical, and petrophysical analyses were performed to better
 understand factors that influence the long-term containment of CO2 to aid in the calibration and
 correlation of well logs and to improve the accuracy of geologic and simulation models. The
 following analyses determined the bulk properties of the core: core description, spectral GR, thin-
 section petrography, porosity and permeability, high-pressure mercury injection (HPMI), uniaxial
 compressive strength (UCS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF), and steady-state
 relative permeability. Additional details on core testing and analysis are in Appendix D.

 Key Findings and Implications

 Roughrider 1 provided an unprecedented look at the geology of this potential CO2 storage
 site. The Inyan Kara and Broom Creek Formations exhibit good reservoir qualities (e.g., core
 porosities ranging from 14% to 25% and 11% to 26%, respectively, and permeability 62–5115 mD
 and 66–664 mD, respectively). Thick and competent seals are present above each of these
 reservoirs, which is essential for safe CO2 containment. On the other hand, the Black Island–
 Deadwood and Mission Canyon (Tilston interval) Formations, which in some areas of the
 Williston Basin exhibit reservoir properties, appear to have unfavorably low porosity and
 permeability at Roughrider 1. Further analysis, modeling, and simulation of the Mission Canyon
 and Black–Island Deadwood as a potential CO2 storage complex were not pursued at this location.
 Development was focused on the upper two formations, the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek, to
 collectively meet the ≥50-MMt capacity goal.

 GEOLOGIC MODELING AND SIMULATION

 The goal of this effort was to integrate geological data into coherent models and simulate
 CO2 injection scenarios to predict the behavior of the storage complexes under future operations.
 This task bridges the gap between raw data and practical engineering design by providing
 quantitative forecasts of pressure buildup, plume migration, and storage capacity. The geologic
 modeling produces static 3D representations of each storage complex—including reservoir
 architecture, porosity–permeability distributions, and seal continuity—while dynamic simulation
 uses those static models to simulate fluid flow (injected CO2 and brine movement) over time. The
 outputs inform key design parameters such as the number of injection wells needed, injection rates,
 expected plume extent (for area of review [AOR] delineation), and risk factors like potential
 interference among multiple plumes.

 Geologic site characterization data (logs, core analysis, and seismic) were integrated into
 geocellular models that account for the petrophysical properties of geologic formations in the AOI.
 The study interval comprises the injection zones (storage units) and upper and lower sealing
 formations (confining zones) that serve as barriers to prevent out-of-zone migration of CO2 and
 brine. The Broom Creek and Inyan Kara Formations were selected as storage targets for this task.
 The geologic models provided the foundation for dynamic simulations of potential injection
 scenarios. Dynamic simulations are required to predict how CO2 and its associated pressure plume
 would be distributed in the study areas and the effectiveness of the sealing formation at the site
 during the CCUS time frame. Simulation results provided key design and operational parameters
 for 1) potential injection wells and infrastructure; 2) a technical risk assessment; 3) AOR
 determination; 4) monitoring, reporting, and verification (MRV) planning; and 5) installation
 expenditures.

 Geologic Modeling

 The proposed stacked storage horizons (Broom Creek and Inyan Kara Formations) were
 evaluated through detailed reservoir-modeling efforts using SLB’s Petrel geologic modeling
 software. The models integrated the known and acquired geologic, structural, and fluid data of the
 study area (for both the reservoir and confining zones). These models were used to assess CO2
 storage resource, total and effective pore volume, both lateral and vertical geologic heterogeneity
 (as interpreted from preexisting 2D and 3D seismic datasets), and AOR for regions likely to
 experience CO2 saturation during injection in of >50 MMt of CO2. The geologic models provided
 the foundation for dynamic simulation of potential injection scenarios.

 Geologic models were produced using industry-standard software and methods detailed in
 Appendices A and B. Slightly different methods were used for each geologic model according to
 specific geology, such as different depositional environments resulting in different facies, different
 relationships between porosity and permeability, and the effects of the Red Wing Creek impact
 event on stratigraphy. The modeling process was informed by early injection simulations, after
 which models were updated. Details of the simulation model construction are found under
 Dynamic Simulation and in Appendix C.

 Existing and site-specific subsurface data were analyzed and interpreted for the AOI. The
 AOI encompasses a 1050-mi2 (30-mi × 35-mi) area in McKenzie County, North Dakota, within
 which the geologic extent, depth, and thickness and properties of the subsurface geologic strata
 were characterized. The data and interpretations were used as inputs to SLB’s Petrel software to
 construct geologic models of the two storage complexes (Table 4). For the Inyan Kara storage
 complex, the injection zone (Cretaceous Inyan Kara Formation, Lower Dakota Group), upper
 confining zone (Cretaceous Skull Creek Formation, Middle Dakota Group), and lower confining
 zone (Jurassic Swift Formation) were modeled. For the Broom Creek storage complex, the
 injection zone (Pennsylvanian–Permian Broom Creek Formation, Upper Minnelusa Group), upper
 confining zone (Permian Opeche Formation), and lower confining zone (Pennsylvanian Amsden
 Formation, Middle Minnelusa Group) were modeled. Geologic properties were distributed within
 each 3D model, including facies, porosity, and permeability.

 The continuity of the Broom Creek Formation within the AOI is affected by two structural
 features: the erosional edge of the formation in the northern part of the AOI and the Red Wing
 Creek impact structure. The Red Wing Creek (sometimes simply “Red Wing”) impact structure is
 in the east-central part of the modeling area and the target of the Red Wing Creek oil field. The
 Red Wing Creek structure was formed by a meteor impact during the Triassic Period (251–
 201 Ma), which deformed and fractured strata from the Lodgepole through Spearfish Formations,
 including the Broom Creek Formation. The Jurassic Rierdon and Piper Formations are the oldest
 continuous formations deposited after the impact (Herber and others, 2022). This project did not
 incorporate a detailed structural (fault) understanding into the geologic model as part of the
 feasibility-phase effort; as a result, there is relatively high uncertainty in this area. To account for
 anticipated horizontal flow baffles in the impact area, arbitrarily low petrophysical values were set
 to minimize fluid flow and pressure dissipation throughout the impact structure. The structure
 relief, faulting, and structural differences require more research to determine flow boundaries and
 connection to the main body of the reservoir.

 Dynamic Simulation

 Activities included dynamic simulations to determine injection feasibility, potential
 distribution, and extent of a 50-MMt CO2 plume injected over a 30-year time frame into each of
 the target storage units. These simulations were based on the geologic assessments of the stacked
 storage reservoirs conducted under Geologic Modeling. Simulation models were developed using
 Computer Modelling Group Ltd.’s (CMG’s) GEM dynamic simulation module. Injectivity of the
 stacked storage complexes was assessed to confirm potential for injection of ≥50 MMt of CO2 at
 the project site into the minimum number of injection wells and develop the smallest footprint.
 Development of the plume was tracked for various simulations of the 30-year injection period to
 determine AOR and CO2 plume extents through time. Simulations also evaluated the potential for
 conflicts with saltwater disposal (SWDs) wells already injecting into the Inyan Kara Formation
 within the AOI.

 Simulation Model Development

 Numerical simulations of CO2 injection into the target formations—the Inyan Kara and
 Broom Creek Formations—were conducted using CMG’s GEM compositional reservoir
 simulator. The geologic models described above were used in the simulations. The stacked storage
 scenario was evaluated first, followed by scenarios focusing on the Inyan Kara Formation as a
 stand-alone storage unit (Figure 7).

 Figure 8 displays 3D views of the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek simulation models,
 respectively, with the permeability property distribution. In addition to the Inyan Kara and Broom
 Creek Formations, the naturally fractured Tilston interval of the Mission Canyon Formation was
 evaluated, but simulation results demonstrated limited capacity for CO2 storage. More details on
 the Tilston simulation setup and results are presented in Appendix C.

 Fluid properties were derived from formation fluid analyses on samples from the Roughrider
 1 stratigraphic test well. Fracture pressure gradients were established using MDT stress test data
 acquired from the same well. The fracture pressure gradient was 0.76 psi/ft for the Inyan Kara
 Formation and 0.78 psi/ft for the Broom Creek Formation. Initial pressure and temperature
 conditions were assigned in the model based on the MDT measurements. The deep saline
 formations were assumed to be 100% saturated with water as the initial condition. Average
 porosity and permeability values, along with other reservoir properties used in the simulation
 models, are summarized in Table 3 .

 Rock–fluid properties, including relative permeability and capillary pressure curves, were

 Characterization. HPMI tests were conducted to derive capillary pressure relationships, and
 distinct curve sets were defined for each major lithofacies. In the Inyan Kara Formation, the rock–
 fluid properties curves were derived for sandstone and shale. In the Broom Creek Formation, major
 lithofacies were sandstone, siltstone, dolostone, dolomitic sandstone, and anhydrite.

 Simulation Scenario Setup

 Three simulation scenarios of two well locations were developed to evaluate the feasibility
 of injecting and storing 50 MMt of CO2 in the target formations. One scenario evaluated a stacked
 storage configuration in which injection is distributed between the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek
 Formations. Two scenarios assessed injection into the Inyan Kara Formation alone.

 Federal landownership was another main consideration, as much of the model area lies
 within federally managed sections, which could increase permitting complexity. To address this
 concern, one well location was selected on nonfederal land in the northern part of the model,
 providing alternative approaches for potential site development. The second location, in the
 southern region, was chosen within an area of federal land to evaluate technical feasibility of
 stacked storage under more complex regulatory conditions. Separate from permitting a Class VI
 injection well on federal land, negotiating federal pore space access is also generally expected to
 require a more intensive and longer process than state or private pore space. Both scenarios would
 require assessing the influence of the SWD operations. Given the concentration of disposal wells
 in parts of the Inyan Kara Formation, wells were placed in areas with relatively lower SWD density
 to reduce potential operational impact and pressure interference. The southern location was
 specifically chosen because it provides sufficient thickness in both the Inyan Kara and Broom
 Creek Formations, allowing stacked storage evaluation. Since the Broom Creek Formation thins
 and pinches out farther north, the stacked storage option was only available in the southern area.

 Based on these two locations (Figure 9), three scenarios were simulated, each involving CO2
 injection through a single well (Table 5). The scenarios are as follows:

 • Scenario 1 utilized the southern location and models stacked storage scenario into the two
 formations at Well IWS (injection well south).

 • Scenario 2 also used Well IWS but injection was entirely into the Inyan Kara Formation,
 which allowed for direct comparison with the stacked scenario to evaluate differences in
 plume footprint and injectivity.

 • Scenario 3 evaluated injection of the full 50 MMt into the Inyan Kara Formation using
 Well IWN (injection well north) in the northern part of the model, which avoids federal
 land.

 Numerical simulations demonstrated that the targeted formations in the CarbonSAFE
 Roughrider project can store at least 50 MMt of CO2 over a 30-year injection period. The stacked
 storage scenario was evaluated in the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek Formations and found to be
 technically feasible; however, the resulting combined plume size necessarily utilizes federal pore

 the Inyan Kara Formation increases the number of potential injection sites, including areas that

 Preliminary Area of Review Assessment

 The NDAC defines an AOR as “the region surrounding the geologic sequestration project
 [storage project] where underground sources of drinking water may be endangered by the [CO2]
 injection activity” (NDAC § 43-05-01-01[4]). The primary endangerment risk is the potential for
 vertical migration of CO2 and/or formation fluids from the storage reservoir into a USDW. The
 purpose of the AOR is to define the area within which potential leakage pathways (e.g., active or
 abandoned wells or transmissive faults) must be reviewed to determine whether all wells within
 the AOR have sufficient isolation to prevent formation fluids or injected CO2 from vertically
 migrating outside of the storage reservoir or into USDWs, and if that is not the case, what
 corrective action is needed prior to injection.

 At a minimum, the AOR includes the areal extent of the CO2 plume within the storage
 reservoir. However, the CO2 plume has an associated pressure front where CO2 injection increases
 the formation pressure above initial (preinjection) conditions. Generally, the pressure front is
 larger in areal extent than the CO2 plume. Therefore, the AOR encompasses both the areal extent
 of the CO2 plume within the storage reservoir and the extent of the reservoir fluid pressure increase
 sufficient to drive formation fluids (e.g., brine) into a USDW, assuming pathways for this
 migration (e.g., legacy oil and gas wells or fractures) are present. Because the pressure front is
 larger in areal extent than the CO2 plume, AOR delineation focuses on the pressure front.

 The minimum pressure increase in the reservoir that results in a sustained flow of brine
 upward from the storage reservoir into an overlying drinking water aquifer is referred to as the
 “critical threshold pressure increase” and resultant pressure as the “critical threshold pressure.”
 Therefore, the AOR is the areal extent of the storage reservoir that exceeds the critical pressure
 threshold. U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) guidance for AOR delineation under the
 underground injection control (UIC) program for Class VI wells provides several methods for
 estimating the critical threshold pressure increase and resulting critical threshold pressure (U.S.
 Environmental Protection Agency, 2013). The lowest USDW in the study area is the Fox Hills
 Formation.

 EPA Method 1 (pressure front based on bringing the injection zone and USDW to equivalent
 hydraulic heads) is presented as a method for determining whether a storage reservoir is in
 hydrostatic equilibrium with the lowest USDW. Additional details about the application of EPA
 Method 1 can be found in multiple North Dakota Class VI storage facility permits (SFPs), e.g.,
 Case 30869, “TB Leingang Broom Creek Storage Facility” (North Dakota Department of Mineral

 25

 Resources, 2025). EPA Method 1 was applied to the simulated injection well locations for each
 injection scenario and summarized in Table 7. The results indicate that none of the scenarios
 represent an injection zone at hydrostatic equilibrium with the lowest USDW (i.e., ΔPi,f is not zero).
 At location IWS (Scenario 1 and 2), the Broom Creek Formation is underpressured by 175 psi and
 the Inyan Kara Formation is overpressured by 670 psi relative to the lowest USDW. At location
 IWN (Scenario 3), the Inyan Kara Formation is overpressured by 628 psi relative to the USDW.
 These values indicate that—without additional pressure caused by CO2 injection—flow along a
 potential leakage pathway would be downward from the USDW to the Broom Creek but upward
 from the Inyan Kara to the USDW.

 The methods described by EPA (2013) for estimating the AOR under the Class VI rule
 (40 U.S. Code of Federal Regulations [CFR] 146.81 et seq.) were developed assuming that the
 storage reservoirs would be in hydrostatic equilibrium with overlying aquifers. However, in the
 state of North Dakota, and potentially elsewhere around the United States, candidate storage
 reservoirs are already overpressured relative to overlying aquifers and thus subject to potential
 vertical formation fluid migration from the storage reservoir to the lowermost USDW, even prior
 to the planned storage project. In Table 7, for example, the Inyan Kara Formation (Scenario 1 –
 IK, Scenario 2, Scenario 3) shows relative overpressure. Consequently, applying EPA (2013)
 methods to these geologic situations essentially results in an infinite AOR, which makes regulatory
 compliance infeasible. As an alternative, Burton-Kelly and others (2021) proposed a risk‐based
 reinterpretation of this framework that would allow for a reduction in the AOR while ensuring
 protection of drinking water resources. Application of the risk-based method is described in more
 detail in multiple North Dakota Class VI SFPs, e.g., Case 30869, “TB Leingang Broom Creek
 Storage Facility” (North Dakota Department of Mineral Resources, 2025).

 Based on the EPA Method 1 results in Table 7, a risk-based AOR was chosen for the Inyan
 Kara scenarios and an EPA Method 1 AOR was chosen for the Broom Creek scenario. However,
 the AOR delineated for injection in the Broom Creek under Scenario 1 may be impracticably large
 due to the large pressure increase across the AOI during CO2 injection (Figure 16). That is, review
 of the 637 wells over >462 mi2, followed by any necessary corrective action, may make regulatory

 26

 compliance infeasible. For comparison with the EPA method, the risk-based AOR method was
 also applied to the Broom Creek scenario.

 The risk-based AOR method uses the change in pressure (ΔP) over the injection period to
 calculate the incremental potential fluid leakage volume along a hypothetical leaky wellbore. The
 maximum ΔP determined by numerical simulation defines the maximum incremental leakage
 potential into the USDW. If this value is less than 1 m3 (as established in permitted North Dakota
 Class VI projects), the storage reservoir pressure buildup is not the deciding factor in delineating
 AOR. Inputs to the simplified model used for risk-based AOR are included in Appendix C.

 A few factors caused the risk-based AOR methods applied to the Broom Creek and Inyan
 Kara scenarios to differ slightly. SWD injection into the Inyan Kara within and nearby the AOI
 required numerical simulation of two cases to estimate the amount of pressure buildup attributable
 to CO2 injection (Figure 13). First, a case with both CO2 and SWD injection, and second, a case
 with only SWD injection. Change in pressure was calculated for each by subtracting initial
 pressure from pressure at the end of 30 years of CO2 injection. ΔP attributable to CO2 only was
 calculated by subtracting the SWD-only ΔP from the CO2 + SWD ΔP. The resulting “net ΔP” was
 used for the risk-based AOR analysis.

 The Broom Creek simulation did not incorporate SWD activity, and the ΔP could be
 calculated directly from numerical simulation results. However, the location of the Inyan Kara
 Formation between the Broom Creek storage reservoir and the lowest USDW means that it has the
 potential to act as a “thief zone” for fluid flow caused by CO2 injection. Theoretically, formation
 fluid migrating from the Broom Creek up a leaky wellbore that is open to the Inyan Kara will
 preferentially flow into the Inyan Kara, and the continued flow up the wellbore and into the USDW
 will be reduced. Therefore, the Inyan Kara may act as a thief zone and reduce the potential for
 formation fluid impacts to the groundwater. The thief zone phenomenon was described by
 Nordbotten and others (2004) as an “elevator model” by analogy to an elevator full of people on
 the main floor who then get off at various floors as the elevator moves up such that only very few
 people ride all the way to the top floor. The term “thief zone” is also used in the oil and gas industry
 to describe a high-permeability zone encountered during drilling into which circulating fluids can
 be lost. Models of CO2 injection into the Broom Creek with and without opening the leaky
 wellbore to the Inyan Kara were run and the results evaluated to quantify the effect of a thief zone
 on the risk-based AOR.

 Complicating this scenario for the noninjection case is the combination of relative
 overpressure in the Inyan Kara and relative underpressure in the Broom Creek. Under the
 noninjection “thief zone on” case, the overpressure in the Inyan Kara is driving flow up into the
 USDW, but under the noninjection “thief zone off” case (where the leaky wellbore is not open to
 the Inyan Kara), the underpressure in the Broom Creek is pulling fluid down from the USDW.
 Under the injection cases, the incremental flow is from the Broom Creek to the Inyan Kara in the
 “thief zones on” case and from the Broom Creek to the USDW in the “thief zones off” case.

 The conceptual layout of the plants and pipeline systems is reflected in Figure 17. The

 (Model) (U.S. Department of Energy Office of Fossil Energy and Carbon Management/National
 Energy Technology Laboratory, 2022) was utilized for the sizing of the pipeline sections based on
 the volumes of each section, length, and overall elevation change. The pressure of the CO2 stream
 into the origination point of the CO2 pipeline was estimated at 2000 psig, with a temperature of
 80°F. Each pipeline section was held at 80°F, with the pressure at the intersection points calculated
 by the model to reflect the volume of CO2 stream transported down the pipeline segments, distance,
 and overall elevation change between the inlet and outlet of the pipeline section.

 The CO2 for this project is planned to be captured from the gas combustion engines that
 power the compressors used to transport natural gas from each of ONEOK’s six natural gas-
 processing plants. Since the content of CO2 in the exhaust stream is small and is expected to
 comprise less than 10% of the exhaust gas stream, the CO2 will be extracted from the exhaust
 stream and purified. Once purified, the treated gas stream will contain over 99% CO2
 (Table 11). The composition of the CO2 stream was estimated based on the values of carbon
 capture for a natural gas combined-cycle (NGCC) power plant presented by Elliott (2022). During
 the purification process, the CO2 stream will be dehydrated such that no free water will condense
 out of the stream for the range of operating and static temperatures for western North Dakota. The
 dehydration of the CO2 prevents corrosion of the carbon steel pipeline used for the transportation
 of the CO2 stream to the storage complex.

 calculating the capital recovery factor is presented in Equation 1:

 The TEA found that although construction of the CO2 pipeline system can be technically
 performed, the low volumes of CO2 inherent with the assessed gas plants are estimated to be cost-

 SITE DEVELOPMENT PLAN

 The objectives of site characterization efforts are to reduce uncertainty associated with
 subsurface characteristics of the storage complex, optimize CO2 storage, comply with North
 Dakota Class VI CO2 storage regulations, and address operational and stakeholder needs and/or
 concerns. The site development plan details activities for characterizing and testing potential
 storage sites and includes the collection of 3D seismic data; drilling, wireline-logging, and coring
 a new characterization well; and an analysis plan to harvest pertinent data. Specific downhole and
 laboratory-based testing will focus on geochemical compatibility between the injected CO2 and
 the storage reservoir rock and native fluids. Additional testing will also address the geomechanical
 nature of the reservoirs and associated caprock to determine safe CO2 storage operational
 parameters. The full analysis can be found in the full site development plan (Klapperich and others,
 2025).

 Site Characterization and Monitoring Plan

 The goal of site characterization is to generate site data that are required to satisfy the state
 of North Dakota regulatory process, provide adequate information to project owners to confidently
 design storage facilities, and provide assurance to other stakeholders of the security and efficiency
 of the developed site. Execution of this plan requires storage facility and drilling permits including
 the following: 1) geologic exhibits, 2) geologic and injection technical evaluations, and
 3) development of supporting plans. The details of these requirements are addressed in North
 Dakota Century Code (NDCC) Chapter 38-22 and NDAC Chapter 43-05-01.

 As discussed in prior sections, the Inyan Kara and Broom Creek Formations are the most
 suitable CO2 storage targets in central McKenzie County. The available regional data (e.g., legacy
 seismic data, core analysis, well logs) and modeling simulations suggest that injectivity into one
 or both of these formations will meet CarbonSAFE project goals and is sufficient to inject the CO2
 that will be generated and captured by ONEOK and other natural gas processers that may join
 future efforts.

 There is also limited potential for induced geomechanical issues as well as geochemical
 reactivity between the injected CO2, native brine, and minerals present in the storage formations,

 37

 all of which could affect both the injectivity and migration of the CO2 and formation brine over
 time. Furthermore, there are limited data available on the salinity of the Inyan Kara Formation,
 and it is uncertain as of the writing of this document if the formation exceeds the minimum salinity
 requirement of 10,000 ppm TDS at the two storage sites (IWS and IWN, as shown on Figure 16),
 as required by North Dakota and EPA Class VI injection well regulations. Salinity of the Inyan
 Kara was tested from the Roughrider 1 test well and found to be below 10,000 ppm TDS at that
 location (measured at 7050 ppm TDS). Because these uncertainties exist, rigorous site
 characterization data collection will be required to move forward on a CO2 geologic storage project
 in central McKenzie County at either of the two proposed sites.

 The following sections describe the various types of site-specific data collection and analysis
 that will likely be needed from the future CO2 storage site to gain a permit to inject CO2 at the site.
 The objectives of these characterization efforts are to reduce uncertainty associated with
 subsurface characteristics of the storage complex, optimize CO2 storage at the site, comply with
 North Dakota Class VI CO2 storage regulations, and address other operational and stakeholder
 needs and/or concerns as identified in the subsequent risk assessment.

 3D Seismic Data and Drilling of a Characterization Well

 Once a site is chosen to proceed with development for CO2 injection, additional data
 collection and characterization activities will need to be carried out. A 3D surface seismic survey
 will likely be needed to support future modeling and numerical simulation activities and form the
 basis of an MVA plan. The survey, which will improve the understanding of the heterogeneity in
 the subsurface and inform placement of a characterization well, will build upon analysis of existing
 2D and 3D seismic data that were licensed and analyzed for the project as part of the feasibility
 analysis conducted thus far.

 A new characterization well is also suggested for the final injection location. The well is
 recommended to be repurposed as an injection well but could also serve as a monitoring well. The
 precise location of the well will be determined with input from ONEOK, following seismic data
 interpretation and/or additional numerical simulations. The well would be drilled to the deepest
 zone of interest, either the base of the Inyan Kara or the Broom Creek storage complexes, and a
 comprehensive well-logging program will be conducted that is focused on both potential storage
 formations. Four-inch geologic core samples will be collected from each potential storage reservoir
 interval as well as each associated overlying and underlying seal and will be analyzed to determine
 mineralogy (with an emphasis on clay typing to determine the potential for clay swelling),
 porosity, and permeability. Fluid samples from the potential storage formations will be collected
 to determine fluid chemistry and other relevant parameters (i.e., salinity, CO2 solubility, viscosity),
 and downhole testing (pressure, flow, and mechanical) will be conducted. Fluid samples from the
 Inyan Kara will be of particular importance for establishing if the formation exceeds the minimum
 salinity of 10,000 ppm TDS.

 All of the collected data will be used to update numerical simulation inputs and assist in the
 identification and prediction of potential geochemical reactivity between the native formation
 fluid, minerals present in the potential injection intervals, and injected CO2. If geochemical
 reactions (such as mineral precipitation or clay swelling) are found to pose significant risk of

 38

 impairment to injection, several mitigation measures will be considered for investigation as the
 project moves into the development/operational design phase.

 Modeling and simulation efforts will use these characterization data to adjust and update
 static geologic models for increased accuracy. Using updated models, predictive simulations will
 be improved, reducing the uncertainty of the predictions of this feasibility study, including the
 expected extent of the CO2 plume and pressure front and the delineation of the AOR. These results
 will be incorporated into the storage facility and injection well permits that will be submitted to
 NDIC.

 The characterization data will also be a key component of the MVA plan, as an improved
 understanding of the subsurface will aid in the final selection of monitoring technologies,
 development of the timing and frequency of MVA data collection, and interpretation of the MVA
 data as they relate to the potential subsurface risks of interest. Furthermore, these data may provide
 important information for guiding the design and operation of site equipment and infrastructure. It
 is, therefore, critical for the geologic model of the injection and containment interval(s) at the site
 to provide an accurate representation of the subsurface.

 Well Logging and Downhole Testing

 Openhole geophysical well logs will be acquired along the entire uncased section of the
 characterization well at the conclusion of drilling. Well logs provide data used to determine
 formation top depths, lithology, petrophysical properties, and time–depth shifting of the seismic
 data. The data derived from these logging techniques will directly support the development of
 SFPs and permits to construct Class VI injection wells. A description of the geophysical logs and
 their utility is provided as follows:

 • Spectral GR logging will provide a good indication of shale and can be used in
 combination with other logs for mineral identification and clay typing. A similar
 measurement will be made on the entire length of the acquired core, enabling an accurate
 core-to-log depth correlation. This correlation will be important when integrating core-
 measured properties in modeling activities.

 • GR, neutron porosity, density, photoelectric factor, spontaneous potential, temperature,
 and resistivity logs will provide the ability to assess formation top depths, lithology, and
 petrophysical characteristics important in identifying well test and completion intervals
 and correlating core data to offset wells.

 • Dipole sonic logging provides a means to derive sonic porosity, which is useful in zones
 with complex lithologies. Dipole sonic logging provides the shear wave data necessary
 to assess stress anisotropy and investigate the presence of natural fractures. This log also
 helps tie seismic data to the well, assisting in seismic time-to-depth conversion.

 • NMR logging provides estimates of pore-size distribution, total porosity, effective
 porosity, bound fluid, free fluid, and calculated permeability.

 39

 • RCBLs and casing collar locator (CCL) logs provide an assessment of cement quality and
 identify any associated remedial cementing operations that are required.

 • Wireline-deployed testing technology will be implemented to conduct pressure testing
 and native formation fluid sampling as well as injection tests to estimate downhole
 permeability and formation parting pressure. Formation fluid samples will be collected
 and used to test for potential fluid and mineralogical reactions with mixtures of the native
 brine and the injected CO2, including any impurities present that could affect injectivity.

 • The borehole imaging log provides microresistivity formation images that will be used to
 determine principal stress azimuths and define the maximum permeability direction if the
 formation is fractured. The log will also provide key input and verification data for the
 one-dimensional (1D) mechanical earth model (MEM).

 These specific logging techniques are planned such that the derived data will directly support
 the development of SFPs and permits to construct Class VI injection wells. Some data redundancy
 is planned for key parameters, such as porosity and permeability, as these parameters are of utmost
 importance in reducing the technical risks associated with the ability of the target formation to
 receive injection at the rate necessary to meet the goals of the commercial project. To best
 understand the injectivity of the target formations, a flow or injection test should be conducted. To
 minimize any potential impact to the target horizons, the injected fluid should be compatible with
 existing rock and fluid chemistries.

 Characterization of Geologic Core Samples

 Core will need to be collected from each of the storage reservoir–seal (top and bottom)
 packages. These rock packages are the Skull Creek–Inyan Kara–Swift Formations, and, if used,
 the Opeche–Broom Creek–Amsden Formations (Figure 1). To comply with state guidance, 50 ft
 of the top and bottom seal formations will be collected.

 The core samples will be subjected to a suite of petrographic, petrophysical, geomechanical,
 and geochemical analyses listed as follows:

 • Thin-section analysis to assess mineralogy, grain size, sorting, morphology, and
 diagenetic effects and to assist understanding of facies interpretations, rock fabric,
 depositional trends, and diagenetic history.

 • XRF to provide insight into sample chemistry and the nature and dynamics of potential
 CO2–brine–rock interactions.

 • XRD coupled with Rietveld refinement to 1) assess bulk mineralogy and clay mineralogy
 via clay fraction isolation and 2) provide an improved understanding of potential cap
 integrity issues and reservoir dynamics in relation to the types of clay that are present.

 • Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) to gain visibility of fine-scale
 mineralogical and pore space relationships, which serve as a validation technique for

 40

 XRD. FESEM mineral identification software will be coupled with classification system
 (AMICS) software to allow for visual inspection of the AMICS-derived pore and fracture
 characterization results, which can be compared to other acquired porosity measurements.
 This software coupling will allow for a coupled comparison of elemental and mineral
 content with XRF and XRD, improving both reservoir and caprock characterization.

 • Porosity and permeability testing which will be used to update geologic models and
 enable more accurate predictive simulations of fluid flow and pressure response.

 • Geochemical analyses to investigate chemical reactions between formation fluids and
 gases that mimic the composition of the injected CO2, with the intent of identifying
 mineral dissolution and precipitation reactions that have the potential to impact CO2
 injection.

 • NMR core analysis to assess total versus effective porosity with representative brine–CO2
 compositions for relative permeability predictions.

 • CO2–brine relative permeability testing to determine the ease with which CO2 will flow
 in the presence of the high-salinity brine of the reservoir.

 • Geomechanical studies to conduct mechanical strength testing and determine the
 maximum injection integrity of the caprock.

 • Mercury injection capillary pressure (MICP) tests to provide data regarding the available
 pore-size distribution and capillary pressure range in reservoir and caprock samples.

 All of these data will be used for modeling the site’s storage capacity and for prediction of
 two-phase fluid flow in the subsurface to accurately predict the project’s AOR and ultimate
 stabilization extent of the injected CO2.

 Near-Surface Characterization

 Collection of near-surface characterization data is necessary to inform the types of
 monitoring strategies that may be required as part of a site-monitoring program for the project. Of
 particular importance is the characterization of groundwater resources given that the primary goal
 of the monitoring of CO2 storage projects is to protect USDWs (aquifers). More specifically, to
 comply with the North Dakota Class VI permits, the project will need to identify all known wells
 (groundwater and otherwise) within the AOR. Also required for the SFP is a hydrogeologic
 assessment of the local aquifer systems with the AOR. This will require the sampling,
 characterization, and assessment of select water wells and their associated aquifers within the
 AOR. If chosen strategically, water wells selected for characterization can also be used to perform
 periodic monitoring of local aquifers during CO2 injection operations, thereby providing near-
 surface baseline monitoring data. If ONEOK decides to perform monitoring of soil horizons and/or
 the atmosphere near the injection wells, baseline monitoring data of these types would also need
 to be collected and reported as part of the Class VI permit filings.

 Introduction

 Risk assessment is an integral part of the risk management process (steps highlighted in blue
 in Figure 20) and comprises three components: 1) risk identification, 2) risk analysis, and
 3) risk evaluation. A risk assessment was conducted in accordance with ISO 31000 (2009), with
 adaptations specific to conducting subsurface technical risk assessments of geologic CO2 storage
 projects (Canadian Standards Association, 2012; Azzolina and others, 2017; Finnigan and others,
 2022).

 Figure 20. Risk management process adapted from the International Organization for
 Standardization (ISO) 31000 (2009) standard (Azzolina and others, 2017).

 43

 The risk-scoring framework evaluates known geological and well‑related hazards; future
 phases may expand the scope to surface facilities, new well installations, and pipeline systems.
 Preliminary findings indicate that the storage complex possesses the requisite capacity and
 integrity to support large‑scale CO2 injection and warrants further detailed analysis.

 Risk assessment is an iterative process of identifying, analyzing, and evaluating individual
 project risks, recognizing that the relevant risks can change for a specific storage project as it
 matures and moves from one phase to the next (e.g., from site screening to feasibility to operation).
 With each project development phase, additional data became available. Consequently, the project
 phase affects the nature of available information and the degree of stakeholder knowledge about
 the potential project risks (Azzolina and others, 2017). The Phase II risk assessment was developed
 based on the current understanding of the Roughrider project and the site characterization data
 available at the time of the assessment.

 A risk impact-scoring matrix was used to categorize risks as low (green), moderate (yellow),
 or high (red) on a three-point scale (Table 18). This score measures the potential consequences if

 would be identified as the driver. The final risk impact score was then based on the severity level
 of this driver, allowing for an independent assessment of potential consequences regardless of the
 risk’s probability. This framework was developed in collaboration with subject matter experts
 (SMEs) and key stakeholders to ensure it accurately reflected their concerns and priorities.

 Risk Identification

 The risk identification component of the Phase II risk assessment started with an initial list
 of subsurface geology and regulatory compliance risks that are common to most storage projects
 (Azzolina and others, 2017). The list was then modified to reflect the site-specific details of the
 Roughrider project as they are understood at this time. This list of risks and the characterization of
 their potential for occurrence and impact may change as more information is gathered in
 subsequent phases of the project. Technical project risks refer to the geologic storage complex
 (i.e., the subsurface). In contrast, nontechnical project risks refer to events that could affect the
 successful execution of the storage project but are not related to the technical attributes of the
 storage complex (e.g., public sentiment toward storage projects, resource limitations, etc.).

 Technical Project Risks

 The technical project risks were grouped into three different principal risk categories, some
 of which had further subcategories (a full list is provided in Klapperich and others, 2025):

 1) Injectivity and storage capacity

 2) Lateral containment:
 a) Lateral migration of CO2
 b) Lateral pressure propagation

 3) Vertical containment:
 a) Legacy wellbores (existing wellbores unaffiliated with the project)
 b) Faults and fractures

 45

 c) Geomechanical confining layer failure
 d) Natural or induced seismicity

 In the risk register, CO2 injectivity refers to the rate at which CO2 could be injected into the
 storage unit, e.g., 1.7 MMt per year for 30 years, while storage capacity refers to the ability of the
 storage unit to hold the total mass of injected CO2 over the project period of performance, e.g.,
 50 MMt over 30 years.

 Lateral containment risks were separated into lateral migration of CO2 and lateral pressure
 propagation to better represent the different types of impacts that these two forms of lateral
 migration could have. The lateral migration risks included lateral migration of CO2 beyond the
 amalgamated pore space for the storage project, subsurface pressure impacts extending beyond the
 permitted AOR, and subsurface pressures negatively impacting an USDW or existing oil and gas
 wells. AOR is delineated as part of a CO2 geologic storage project to show the area around the
 injection well(s) where a USDW may be impacted by fluids moved by pressure caused by CO2
 injection.

 The risks associated with vertical migration of CO2 or formation brine were subdivided
 into three different migration pathways through which fluids could migrate from the storage unit
 upward: 1) legacy wellbores, 2) faults and fractures, or 3) inadequate seals (geomechanical
 failure). These migration pathways were then coupled with three different environmental receptors
 that migrating fluids could ultimately impact: 1) atmosphere (CO2 only), 2) USDWs, or 3) surface
 water bodies. Thus the principal risk category, “vertical migration of CO2 or formation brine,”
 resulted in multiple individual risks specific to the migration pathway and environmental receptor.

 Lastly, induced seismicity refers to injection-induced seismicity that results in an event of a
 given magnitude that residents might feel (e.g., ≥ magnitude 3.0).

 Six nontechnical risks were included in the Phase II risk assessment (a full list is provided
 in Klapperich and others, 2025). These scenarios were grouped into three categories:

 • Regulatory compliance
 ­ Inability to adequately secure rights/access to pipelines and/or injection site
 ­ Noncompliance with local/state/federal regulations and policies

 • Future development

 Risk Mitigation

 The risk evaluation showed that most of the identified risks were determined to have a low
 to moderate probability and a low to moderate impact (Figure 17). The risks with the highest
 criticality (combination of probability and impact), placing the risk into the orange/red regions of
 the risk map, were:

 • Risk RR-09 – There is a risk that CO2 migrates laterally beyond the permitted AOR by
 intercepting a transmissive fault or natural fracture. These geologic features may act as
 preferential flow paths, allowing CO2 to bypass the intended containment zone. If this
 occurs, it may lead to vertical migration of CO2 to an USDW, the surface, or the
 atmosphere, posing environmental and regulatory risks.

 • Risk RR-15 – There is a risk that the project may not be able to secure a sufficient number
 of lease agreements from property owners to support the targeted CO2 injection volume.

 49

 This challenge arises due to the anticipated footprint of the CO2 plume in the injection
 zone, which requires agreements across a broad area. North Dakota requires operators to
 gain consent from at least 60% of the pore space ownership (and make a good faith effort
 to obtain the full 100%) within a planned project’s AOR. Failure to obtain these
 agreements could significantly delay project development and implementation.

 • Risk RR-18 – There is a risk that the project’s location within an active oil and gas
 development area may lead to resistance from stakeholders during the storage facility
 hearing process. Concerns may arise from competing land use interests, potential
 interference with future development, or perceived risks to existing operations. This
 resistance could hinder public acceptance and delay or complicate regulatory approval.

 • Risk RR-19 – There is a risk that the cost to capture and transport CO2 may be challenging
 for the project’s current development scenario.

 A review of the above risks indicates that actively mitigating or addressing them is likely
 premature because of the limited understanding of them at the project’s feasibility stage. Therefore,
 future efforts should focus on collecting data that will reduce uncertainty in the risk analysis. This
 will enable a more refined risk assessment, which can be relied upon to target specific risk,
 mitigation, and treatment actions. The results from this risk assessment led to the following
 conclusions:

 • Risk RR-19 has been identified as the most critical risk to the project. The current plan to
 integrate seven sources requiring approximately 180 mi of pipelines to connect the
 sources to the storage site poses significant economic challenges and may threaten the
 continuation of the project. As the project moves into CarbonSAFE Phase III, it is
 essential to evaluate alternative development scenarios or opportunities for increasing the
 level of incentives that could enhance the project’s overall economic viability.

 • Risks RR-15 and RR-18 reflect the project’s location within an active oil and gas
 development area, creating higher probabilities of impacts on nearby mineral rights and
 competing land uses. In Phase III, mapping and characterizing the adjacent mineral
 resources within the storage complex will be crucial. Doing so will allow quantifying
 how CO2 injection could affect those resources and the stakeholders who depend on them
 as well as to develop targeted mitigation strategies.

 • Four out of five moderate-criticality risks (RR-2a, RR-3a, RR-6a, RR-17) were linked to
 the Inyan Kara Formation, highlighting its complexity as a primary target for oil and gas
 wastewater disposal. As the project advances to Phase III, a comprehensive sensitivity
 analysis of the formation should be conducted during the next phase of geologic modeling
 and numerical simulations.

 COMMUNITY OUTREACH

 Background

 The core purpose of the work performed in the engagement task was to gain a better
 understanding of the social feasibility of the Roughrider project. Engagement with the region’s
 stakeholders aimed to assess the social feasibility and acceptance of CCS projects moving forward
 in the Roughrider project area. The following sections detail engagement activities and what
 project personnel learned from engaging stakeholders in western North Dakota in and around the
 Roughrider project area.

 Engagement activities consisted of an employee focus group, community events, and a
 public opinion survey. Each of these activities allowed for stakeholders to engage with the project
 team and provide insight into stakeholders’ thoughts, viewpoints, opinions, etc., on CCS and
 related topics (e.g., pipelines, CO2, energy). The insights help inform the level of social
 acceptability at the time of engagement and help identify questions or concerns the stakeholders
 in the region have regarding CCS and potential project development. In turn, the project team can
 plan to address the questions and concerns as the project progresses in the future.

 Advisory Group

 A Roughrider project advisory group, consisting of selected staff from the EERC, the
 University of North Dakota (UND), and ONEOK, was created to provide guidance on engagement
 activities. The advisory group met five times between May 2024 and October 2024.

 The role of the advisory group was to offer advice, insight, and knowledge for engaging the
 project area’s stakeholders and offer feedback on activities such as the focus group discussion and
 public opinion survey questions described in subsequent sections of this report. ONEOK staff also
 provided advice and guidance on what public events would be most beneficial to attend to engage
 stakeholders on CCS project development in western North Dakota based on their experience
 working and living in the region and engaging with their company’s stakeholders.

 Focus Group

 A focus group entails interviewing a small number of participants (generally up to 12) from
 a specific population. For the Roughrider project, a focus group with eight project partner ONEOK
 employees occurred on July 9, 2024, to discuss CCS and related topics. The discussion provided
 the opportunity to understand the perspectives of a small group of employees who were not directly
 involved with the company’s pursuit of a potential CCS project.

 The ONEOK employee focus group revealed minimal awareness of CCS and CO2 pipeline
 infrastructure among participants, with only a few mentioning specific knowledge. The
 participants highlighted CCS cost trends, state-level regulatory primacy in North Dakota, and
 Wyoming and referenced infrastructure such as Dakota Gasification Company’s CO2 pipeline to
 Canada and ONEOK’s underground gas storage. The lack of familiarity prompted a shift in the
 discussion toward questions and concerns that reflect a desire for greater transparency in

 51

 governance, safety assurance, and community relevance. Participants expressed interest in
 understanding the environmental, economic, and generational impacts of CCS as well as the
 rationale behind CCS and CO2 pipeline implementation in North Dakota. The conversation
 highlighted the importance of local governance, landowner protection, and clear communication
 from project developers. These insights underscore the need for targeted outreach and education
 efforts that address both technical aspects and community values to foster informed public
 engagement.

 Based on the focus group discussion with ONEOK staff, future sessions should consider
 participants’ potentially low awareness of CCS topics by either adapting the discussion guide or
 providing a brief overview, possibly using draft outreach materials. Since focus groups may attract
 individuals with strong preexisting opinions, this format may be more effective for the Roughrider
 project. This allows for both education and feedback, enabling participants to engage with
 materials and potentially become informed ambassadors within their organizations and
 communities. Additionally, the focus group offers ONEOK an opportunity to develop internal
 communications that support broader employee understanding and outreach.

 Public Events

 The EERC’s project team attended public events as part of stakeholder engagement efforts.
 Public events allow for direct interaction between the project team and landowners/residents in the
 area. One of the benefits to these direct interactions is that the project team can better understand
 the public’s concerns and questions related to potential CCS projects, which can benefit future
 project planning and engagement efforts should the Roughrider project advance toward future
 phases of development. The event activities included booths and presentations at community
 events. These activities focused on general CCS discussions and did not get into any details on the
 Roughrider project, primarily because the Roughrider project is still in a feasibility phase and
 project details are not available yet (e.g., potential well locations, pipeline routes, etc.). Most
 interactions with the public at these events were positive and people generally were not familiar
 with CCS but wanted more information. The public events and observations from the project team
 are described below.

 In summary, the EERC’s participation in public events was a valuable component of

 Online Public Opinion Survey

 The project team, guided by input from the project’s advisory group, implemented a
 regional, web-based public opinion survey on CCS that was distributed to western North Dakota
 residents in January 2025. The purpose of the survey was to investigate public knowledge and
 opinions of CCS, the energy industry, CO2, and CO2 pipelines. Targeted counties were McKenzie,
 Dunn, Stark, and Mountrail. 1 The population of the surveyed region is over 65,000, with one multi-
 zip-code urban center, Dickinson, North Dakota, population about 25,000, in this rural area.
 Results of the survey are intended to help assess social acceptability of CCS while aiding in future
 project phase engagement planning and risk assessment activities.

 The online survey and corresponding invitation-to-participate postcard were approved by
 UND’s Institutional Review Board, ensuring proper research ethics for human subjects. The
 survey response is considered a convenience sample, which is a non-probability sampling method
 in which participants are selected because they are willing to participate rather than via a random
 or systematic selection process. Based on the convenience nature of the sample, this survey may
 not be representative of the opinions of the full underlying population. Instead, individuals with
 strong opinions (either strongly support or strongly oppose) are more motivated to respond and
 share their views (Groves and others, 2009). Thus convenience sampling/voluntary response may
 better capture views among those who are critical or less trusting. Given that the goal is to assess
 the social acceptance of CCS, this convenience sampling technique serves as a useful way to hear
 from those with strong opinions in western North Dakota. Similarly, if a project developer were to
 investigate CCS in the region, the types of individuals that responded to this survey are likely
 representative of those who would vocalize either support or opposition in the future. Therefore,
 engagement and/or outreach efforts should strongly consider addressing the questions and
 concerns raised by the respondents to the survey.

 • How are you going to convince the public this is worthwhile?

 It is worth noting that the survey inquired about the perceived trustworthiness of various
 information sources, and scientists/researchers were considered more trustworthy than other
 options, which included project developers/energy companies; state or county representatives; and
 friends, neighbors, or family. When trust in scientists/researchers was split between urban and
 rural residents, a plurality (42.7%) of rural residents rated scientists/researchers as “somewhat
 trustworthy.” Although “very trustworthy” was the second highest response (26.7%), these results
 suggests that it would be unwise to assume project personnel have already earned the full trust of
 residents near project areas; 10.7% of rural survey respondents rated scientists/researchers as “not
 trustworthy at all.”

 Why? Some survey respondents questioned why CCS was the best approach or even
 necessary. This latter group questioned whether the current amount of atmospheric CO2 was
 actually a problem, with respondents asking what concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere was too
 high, if CO2 was really affecting the climate, and why we would remove something good for crops.
 One respondent asked what the point was if other countries were increasing CO2 emissions.
 Respondents questioned whether CCS was the best approach and advocated for EOR, nuclear
 power, shifting focus from fossil fuels to renewable energy systems, or planting more trees or
 cover crops.

 Opinion Survey Conclusions

 The survey results provide a snapshot of public attitudes toward CCS in western North
 Dakota, highlighting both opportunities and challenges for future project development. While
 support for the oil and gas industry remains strong, attitudes toward CCS are more nuanced, with
 urban respondents generally more favorable than rural ones. Nearly 90% of respondents indicated
 an awareness of CCS, and many respondents could name specific projects, yet neutral opinions
 suggest room for education and outreach. Support for CCS increased when respondents were
 presented with detailed project scenarios that included safety measures, environmental studies, and
 regulatory oversight, underscoring the importance of transparent communication. Safety emerged
 as the most critical factor influencing public acceptance, with nearly 80% rating it as “very
 important.” These findings suggest that targeted outreach, trust-building with local communities,
 and clear communication about safety and oversight can meaningfully shift public opinion and
 enhance social feasibility for CCS initiatives in the region.

 CONCLUSIONS

 The Roughrider Carbon Storage Hub project has demonstrated that McKenzie County, North
 Dakota, holds significant promise as a future site for commercial-scale CO2 geologic storage.
 Through rigorous subsurface characterization, the project confirmed that the Inyan Kara and
 Broom Creek Formations possess the petrophysical qualities necessary to safely and effectively
 store large volumes of CO2. The successful drilling, coring, and simulation efforts not only
 validated the feasibility of stacked storage but also refined geologic models critical for future
 permitting and development. While economic modeling revealed that current transport volumes
 and distances challenge financial viability, the technical groundwork and policy-aligned cost
 assessments provide a clear path forward—particularly if regional economies of scale can be
 achieved.

 Importantly, the project’s outreach efforts underscored the social dimensions of CCS
 deployment. By fostering transparency and engaging local stakeholders, the Roughrider team laid
 the foundation for future public trust and acceptance. Although further engagement is needed, the
 project illustrates that socially responsible CCS development is achievable when communities are
 treated as partners in the process. Taken together, the Roughrider hub advances both the technical
 and social readiness of carbon storage in North Dakota, positioning the site as a strategic asset in
 the broader effort to manage CO2 resources.

 INYAN KARA GEOLOGIC MODELING

 INYAN KARA GEOLOGIC MODELING

 INTRODUCTION

 Existing and site-specific subsurface data were analyzed and interpreted for the area of
 interest (AOI). The data and interpretations were used as inputs to SLB’s Petrel software to
 construct a geologic model of the injection zone (Cretaceous Inyan Kara Formation, Lower Dakota
 Group), upper confining zone (Cretaceous Skull Creek Formation, Middle Dakota Group), and
 lower confining zone (Jurassic Swift Formation). The geologic model encompasses a
 1050-mi2 (30-mi × 35-mi) area in McKenzie County, North Dakota, to characterize the geologic
 extent, depth, and thickness of the subsurface geologic strata (Figure A-1). Geologic properties
 were distributed within the three-dimensional (3D) model, including facies, porosity, and
 permeability.

 The geologic model and properties served as inputs for numerical simulations of carbon
 dioxide (CO2) injection using Computer Modelling Group Ltd.’s (CMG’s) GEM software.
 Numerical simulations of CO2 injection were conducted to assess potential CO2 injection rate,
 disposition of injected CO2, wellhead pressure (WHP), bottomhole pressure (BHP), and pressure
 changes in the storage reservoir throughout the expected injection time frame and postinjection
 period. Results of the numerical simulations were then used to determine the project’s area of
 review (AOR) pursuant to North Dakota’s geologic CO2 storage regulations.

 Overview of Project Area Geology

 The AOI is in McKenzie County, western North Dakota. The stratigraphic test well,
 Roughrider 1 (North Dakota Industrial Commission [NDIC] File No. 41089), is situated
 approximately 18 mi southwest of Watford City, North Dakota. This AOI is near the depocenter
 of the Williston Basin.

 The stratigraphy of the Williston Basin has been well studied, particularly the numerous oil-
 bearing formations. Through research conducted by the Energy & Environmental Research Center
 (EERC) via the Plains CO2 Reduction (PCOR) Partnership, the Williston Basin has been identified
 as an excellent candidate for long-term CO2 storage due, in part, to the thick sequence of clastic
 and carbonate sedimentary rocks and subtle structural character and tectonic stability of the basin
 (e.g., Peck and others, 2014; Glazewski and others, 2015).

 The CO2 storage reservoir for this project is the Inyan Kara Formation, a marginal marine
 and nonmarine, predominantly sandstone formation 5607 ft below kelly bushing (KB) elevation
 at the stratigraphic test well (Roughrider 1, NDIC File No. 41089) (Figure A-1). Conformably
 overlying the Inyan Kara Formation is an average of 219 ft of marine shale in the Skull Creek
 Formation (Fischer and others, 2005a). The Skull Creek Formation serves as the primary upper
 confining zone (Figure A-1). The marginal marine Swift Formation (shale and limestone)
 unconformably underlies the Broom Creek Formation and serves as the lower confining zone
 (Figure A-1) (Bader, 2015). Together, the Skull Creek, Inyan Kara, and Swift Formations comprise
 the storage complex for this study.

 STRUCTURAL FRAMEWORK CONSTRUCTION

 SLB’s Petrel software was used to interpolate upper structural surfaces for the Skull Creek,
 Inyan Kara, Swift, and Rierdon Formations. Input data included formation top depths from the
 online NDIC Department of Mineral Resources Oil and Gas Division (DMR-O&G) database, one
 3D seismic survey, and two 2D seismic lines. The interpolated data were used to constrain the
 model extent in 3D space. Well formation tops were picked by hand when not available from a
 database or when database tops differed from log interpretations.

 The Skull Creek, Inyan Kara, and Rierdon Formation tops are straightforward to identify
 with GR logs alone, but the distinction between sandstones in the upper part of the Swift Formation
 and the lower part of the Inyan Kara Formation can be difficult to identify. For this project, the top
 of the Swift (base of the Inyan Kara) was identified as the base of the lowest-GR (“cleanest”)
 “thick” (>20 ft) sandstone. Although other sandstones may or may not be present in the Swift
 below this pick, the GR in those sandstones is at least 10–20 gAPI higher on logs that have not
 been normalized across multiple wells, indicating less sandstone and more shale in those
 sandstones than those in the Inyan Kara. A convergent interpolation gridding algorithm was used
 to combine well formation tops, 3D seismic, and 2D seismic to create a surface representing the
 top of the Inyan Kara Formation (Figure A-5).

 DATA ANALYSIS AND PROPERTY DISTRIBUTION

 Confining Zones (Skull Creek and Swift Formations)

 The upper confining zone (Skull Creek Formation) and the lower confining zone (Swift
 Formation) were each assigned a generic shale (nonreservoir) facies, low porosity (0.01%), and
 low permeability (0.0001 mD). These zones are included to constrain the structure but were not
 additionally characterized in the geologic model.

 A-11

 Injection Zone (Inyan Kara Formation)

 Seismic data were resampled to the geologic model grid and used to determine lateral
 heterogeneity through a variogram assessment. The acoustic impedance (AI) seismic volume
 revealed equal horizontal major and minor axis ranges of 7700 ft. A vertical variogram length of
 10 ft was determined from the upscaled well logs. Although AI was determined to be representative
 of heterogeneity in the model, it was not correlative with other upscaled properties to a high enough
 degree to allow it to be used as a control volume for facies or petrophysical distributions.

 Facies classifications were interpreted from well log data and correlated with descriptions
 of core taken from the Roughrider 1 well. Two facies were modeled within the Inyan Formation:
 1) shale and 2) sandstone. Facies logs were generated from GR logs. GR logs were limited to the
 Inyan Kara zone, extreme values removed, and normalized to range from 0 to 1. Values above
 normalized GR of 0.4 were coded as shale and the rest as sandstone. Because GR is a very common
 log, this resulted in many facies control wells.

 GR logs were upscaled into the grid, and kriging interpolation was performed to generate a
 trend volume for shale distribution. The inverse of this volume was calculated to make a trend
 volume for sandstone distribution. A facies trend model was calculated that incorporated the GR
 trends and facies proportions of the upscaled cells. The final facies distribution with sequential
 indicator simulation used the facies trend model and the variogram model derived from the 3D
 seismic data and well logs. The top eight layers of the reservoir were set as shale to reflect log
 values seen consistently across the AOI and prevent statistically random sandstones from
 appearing at the top of the zone.

 Petrophysical interpretation of the Roughrider 1 well logs was used to create effective
 porosity (PHIE) and intrinsic permeability (KINT) for that well, which were upscaled into the grid
 to act as control values for petrophysical modeling. The Gaussian random function simulation
 algorithm was used to distribute the PHIE property using calculated PHIE well logs and the
 variogram model described previously. The PHIE was cokriged with the kriged GR volumes and
 conditioned to the distributed facies. The KINT property was distributed using the same variogram
 model and Gaussian random function algorithm but using a bivariate distribution that related PHIE
 to KINT through a core measurement crossplot with data from Roughrider 1 (NDIC File No.
            41089), BEST -I1 (NDIC File No. 90383), RTE 10 (NDIC File No. 37229), and J-LOC 1 (NDIC
 File No. 37380). The relationship between PHIE and KINT in core data, upscaled well logs, and
 distributed properties can be seen in Figure A-9. Simulation model property statistics are provided
 in the full report under Subtask 4.2.

 UNCERTAINTY MODELING

 An ensemble of geologic models was produced to investigate the effects of geologic
 uncertainty on simulation results. In collaboration with Task 6.0, three potential injection well
 locations (“IWN,” “IWS,” and “IWNE”) were identified. The reservoir volume defined by a circle
 with a radius 3 mi around each potential well was used as a submodel of interest. Methods from
 Belobraydic and Kaufman (2014) were used to rank the 1000 generated models according to the
 combination of total reservoir pore volume (full model extent) and connected pore volume
 (submodel extent). P10, P25, P33, P50, P67, P75, and P90 models, representing a range from low
 to high storage potential, were identified according to the ranking. The uncertainty ensemble is
 intended to represent an amount of variability that would result from reasonable
 mischaracterization of the variogram model or effective porosity caused by measurement error.
 Each uncertainty model was automatically generated using a Petrel workflow that randomly
 changed the variogram model (major/minor length plus or minus up to one-third of the base case
 model) and the effective porosity (plus or minus up to one effective porosity unit of the distributed
 value, the same amount for all cells) in the reservoir.

 Seismic Survey

 3D and 2D seismic data were licensed and reprocessed for characterization of subsurface
 structure within the Roughrider AOI (Task 3.0). The seismic data allowed for the visualization of

 (Figure B-3). Broom Creek and Amsden upper surfaces were identified in the seismic data and
 converted to depth using the time-to-depth relationship derived from the Roughrider 1 sonic log
            (Task 3.0).

 three 3D seismic surveys were used as inputs into the geologic model. Acoustic impedance (AI)
 volumes were created using the 3D seismic and petrophysical data (e.g., dipole sonic and density
 logs) from the Roughrider 1 well. The AI volumes were used to classify facies of the Broom Creek
 Formation and distribute facies through the geologic model as well as inform petrophysical
 property distribution in the geologic model.

 STRUCTURAL FRAMEWORK MODELING

 Formation top depths were picked for the Broom Creek storage complex formations. The
 site-specific formation top depths were added to the existing wellbore data and formation tops
 from NDIC within the model area to understand the geologic extent, depth, and thickness of the
 subsurface geologic strata. The formation tops of the Opeche, Broom Creek, Amsden, and Tyler
 were interpolated to create structural surfaces which served as inputs for geologic model
 construction. The Opeche was picked at the GR spike at the base of the Minnekahta that is
 consistent throughout the model area. The top of the Broom Creek Formation was picked across
 the AOI based on the transition from a relatively high GR signature representing the siltstones of
 the Opeche–Spearfish Formation to a relatively low GR signature of sandstone and dolostone
 lithologies within the Broom Creek Formation. The Amsden was picked on an increase in bulk
 density (RHOB) signifying more dolomitic or anhydritic facies below the base of the sandstone in
 the Broom Creek. In the eastern part of the basin, the Amsden pick was defined following a 5–10-
 ft massive anhydrite signature at the base of the Broom Creek.

 A convergent interpolation gridding algorithm was used to combine well formation tops, 3D
 seismic, and 2D seismic to create a surface representing the top of the Broom Creek Formation
 (Figure B-5). The average thickness of the Broom Creek is 60 ft across the whole AOI, but 91 ft if
 areas of zero thickness are excluded (range 0–174 ft) (Figure B-6). Upper (Figure B-7) and lower
 (Figure B-8) seal thicknesses average 261 ft (range 31–504 ft) and 164 ft (range 0–351 ft),
 respectively.

 Facies logs were generated from GR, density, neutron, and resistivity logs through the neural
 net classification. All input well logs were cut down to only focus on the Broom Creek reservoir
 zone for input into the neural net. Facies classifications were interpreted from well log data and
 correlated with descriptions of other core taken from the Broom Creek Formation in the Williston
 Basin along with the collected core from Roughrider 1 (Figure B-10).

 Sandstone intervals in the Broom Creek Formation are associated with low GR, low density,

 PETROPHYSICS DISTRIBUTION

 The accurate representation of petrophysical property distributions within a 3D geologic
 model is essential for assessing CO2 injection feasibility and long-term containment. Porosity and
 permeability were distributed to determine how CO2 flows and interacts with the confining zones
 through injection simulation. These properties vary spatially and are linked to the aeolian sabkha
 depositional facies. Core-derived measurements were used as the foundation for the generation of
 porosity and permeability properties within the 3D geologic model. The core sample
 measurements showed good agreement with the wireline logs collected from the Roughrider 1
 well. This agreement allowed for confident extrapolation of porosity and permeability from offset
 well logs, thus creating a spatially and computationally larger dataset to populate the geologic
 model.

 B-15

 The model property distribution statistics are derived from a combination of the core analysis
 and larger dataset derived from offset well logs. Prior to distributing the porosity and permeability
 properties, total porosity (PHIT), effective porosity (PHIE; total porosity less occupied or isolated
 pore space), and permeability (KINT) well logs were estimated and compared with core porosity
 and permeability measurements for 3D distribution. A PHIE property was distributed using
 calculated PHIE well logs, upscaled to the resolution of the 3D model as control points, variogram
 structures described previously with Gaussian random function simulation and AI volume
 cokriging, and conditioned to the distributed facies. Permeability was distributed using the same
 variogram structures and Gaussian random function algorithm as PHIE but was paired with PHIE
 volume cokriging.

 Histograms in Figure B-13 show that distributed property data held the same statistical trends
 as the input well log data. The relationship between effective porosity and permeability is shown

 permeable cells, with anhydrite and dolomite contributing up to 20% porosity but limited
 permeability (<100 mD). Simulation model property statistics are reported under Subtask 4.2 of

 RED WING CREEK IMPACT STRUCTURE

 The Red Wing Creek (sometimes simply “Red Wing”) impact structure is in the east central
 part of the modeling area and the target of Red Wing Creek oil field (Figure B-5). The Red Wing
 Creek structure was formed by a meteor impact during the Triassic Period (251–201 Ma), which
 deformed and fractured strata from the Lodgepole through Spearfish Formations, including the
 Broom Creek Formation. The Jurassic Rierdon and Piper Formations are the oldest continuous
 formations deposited after the impact (Herber and others, 2022). This project did not incorporate
 a detailed structural (fault) understanding into the geologic model as part of the feasibility-phase
 effort; as a result, there is relatively high uncertainty in this area. To account for anticipated
 horizontal flow baffles in the impact area, arbitrarily low petrophysical values were set to minimize
 fluid flow and pressure buildup throughout the impact structure. The structure relief, faulting, and
 structural differences entail more research to determine flow boundaries and connection to the
 main body of the reservoir. Petrophysical values of 1% effective porosity and 0.1 mD permeability
 were assigned to all layers of the Broom Creek within the crater impact structure (previous maps,
            e.g., Figure B-5).

 UNCERTAINTY MODELING

 An ensemble of geologic models was produced to investigate the effects of geologic
 uncertainty on simulation results. In collaboration with Task 6.0, a potential well location (“IWS”)
 was identified. The reservoir volume defined by a circle with a radius of 3 mi around the potential
 well was used as a submodel of interest. Methods from Belobraydic and Kaufman (2014) were

 B-17

 used to rank the 1000 generated models according to the combination of total reservoir pore
 volume (full model extent) and connected pore volume (submodel extent). P10, P25, P33, P50,
 P67, P75, and P90 models, representing a range from low to high storage potential, were identified
 according to the ranking. The uncertainty ensemble is intended to represent an amount of
 variability that would result from reasonable mischaracterization of the variogram model or
 effective porosity caused by measurement error.

 Each uncertainty model was automatically generated using a Petrel workflow that randomly
 changed the variogram model (major/minor length plus or minus up to one-third of the base case
 model) and the effective porosity (plus or minus up to one effective porosity unit of the distributed
 value, the same amount for all cells) in the reservoir.

 the modeling process and determined based on the balance between computational efficiency and
 resolution, as summarized in Table C-1. Figure C-1 shows three-dimensional (3D) views of the

 SCENARIOS AND CASE SETUP

 Various numerical simulation scenarios were developed to evaluate the subsurface technical
 feasibility and potential location selection of injecting and storing 50 MMt of CO2 in the target
 formations.

 Simulations were grouped into four categories: saltwater disposal (SWD) cases, main carbon
 capture and storage (CCS) cases, additional CCS cases for injection capacity improvement
 analysis, and a final category of sensitivity and uncertainty analysis. The SWD cases addressed
 the impact of water disposal operations on CO2 injection into the Inyan Kara. A history-matching
 case replicated historical SWD rates and pressures for model calibration and determination of off-
 model flux volumes, later applied as pseudo-well flux boundaries in CCS simulations. A forecast
 case simulated 30 years of SWD activity without CO2 injection to establish a baseline pressure
 response for comparison with CCS cases.

 Additional CCS cases were run to evaluate different well locations in the Inyan Kara and
 multiple injectors in the Broom Creek Formations. Also, the simulations of CO2 injection into the
 Tilston interval of the Mission Canyon Formation were performed.

 Sensitivity and uncertainty cases assessed the impact of various geologic, operational, and
 SWD conditions on the CO2 injection scenarios. These cases varied depending on the formation
 to derisk the CO2 storage operation and support the conclusions drawn in this study.

 SIMULATION RESULTS

 SWD Simulation Results

 The impact of SWD activity on the Inyan Kara Formation was evaluated through two
 dedicated simulation cases: the history-matching case and the SWD-only forecast case (SWD
 business as usual without CO2 injection). These cases provided the basis for tuning the model
 response to historical SWD operations and for establishing a baseline pressure evolution under
 SWD-only conditions for comparison with CCS scenarios.

 In the history-matching case, the model was initialized as a normally pressured system with
 a pressure gradient of 0.433 psi/ft, consistent with normal hydrostatic conditions of freshwater
 aquifers. The model was calibrated using historical SWD injection rates operating within the
 simulation domain from 2010 to 2024. Figure C-5 shows the historical total SWD injection rates
 used for calibration. To accurately represent the influence of off-model disposal wells, flux
 boundaries were implemented along the northern and northeastern sides of the model using
 pseudo-wells. Eight pseudo-wells were placed along the northern boundary and four along the
 northern part of the eastern boundary. These pseudo-wells injected at rates equivalent to the
 estimated influx from off-model disposal activity, ensuring the model captured both local and
 regional pressure contributions. The individual injection rates assigned to these pseudo-wells are
 shown in Figure C-6.

 Tilston Interval of the Mission Canyon Formation

 Numerical simulations of the Tilston interval of the Mission Canyon Formation aimed to
 evaluate its maximum injection capacity during a 30-year injection period and if the presence of
 natural fractures would increase the storage capacity. Measurement of core samples collected from
 the Roughrider 1 well shows that the porosity is generally lower than 10%, with the exception of
 a few thin layers, as shown in Figure C-21(a). The permeability is in the range of 0.001–10 mD,
 with most of the layers showing a permeability of less than 1 mD, as shown in Figure C-21(b) and
 Table C-1. These indicate that the Tilston interval is tight and heterogeneous, limiting its potential
 for long-term CO2 storage.

 A preliminary exploration model of the Tilston interval was built in SLB Petrel using a small
 number of wells with petrophysical log interpretations to distribute porosity and permeability
 within two facies, grainstone and packstone. Porosity and permeability ranges were established
 from the petrophysical model, and a generic “carbonate” variogram model was used. The
 preliminary model was 30 mi × 30 mi with 2000-ft × 2000-ft grid cells and an average cell height
 in the Tilston zone of 4.5 ft, for a total of 312,000 cells in the target reservoir.

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) has conducted several different
 studies aimed at effectively and efficiently assessing the hydraulically fractured unconventional
 field and conventional field with the presence of natural fractures. Utilizing the embedded discrete
 fracture model (EDFM) has shown the advantages regarding computational efficiency. The
 different fracture model sensitivity can also be assessed when lacking a detailed fracture network
 description (Jiang and others, 2023; Wan and others, 2023).

 Three numerical simulation scenarios for the Tilston interval were conducted to evaluate the
 effect of fracture density on CO2 storage performance:

 T1. CO2 storage modeling without natural fractures (baseline model)
 T2. CO2 storage modeling with 2000 natural fractures within the model area of 895 mi2
 T3. CO2 storage modeling with 5000 natural fractures within the model area of 895 mi2

 In Scenarios 2 and 3, EDFM methodology was employed to represent the complex natural
 fracture network within the rock matrix to evaluate CO2 storage performance in the natural fracture
 reservoir. The numbers 2000 and 5000 were chosen to ensure that the natural fractures are
 uniformly distributed in the model, allowing for more accurate modeling of fracture–matrix flow
 while maintaining reasonable computational efficiency. Other values between 500 and 10,000
 could also be considered. However, too few fractures may reduce injectivity improvement to close
 to zero, while too many may result in unrealistic injectivity gains, increasing numerical errors, and
 slower simulation performance. The visual illustration of natural fracture distribution for modeled
 scenarios T2 and T3 are shown in Figure C-23.

 maximum injection BHP of 6562 psi, which is 90% of fracture pressure gradient from the MDT

 Sensitivity and Uncertainty Case Results

 Inyan Kara Formation

 Three additional sensitivity cases were simulated using Well IWN to evaluate the influence
 of operational and design variations on injectivity, plume migration, and storage efficiency.
 Scenario 3 was used as the base case for these sensitivity cases. An uncertainty case was also run
 using the P10 geological realization to confirm the feasibility of storing 50 MMt in the Inyan Kara
 Formation.

 Increased SWD Activity

 This case assumed that all SWD wells in the model area injected at twice the historical 3-
 year average rate, representing a high-SWD-intensity scenario. Simulation results indicate that
 increased SWD activity reduced CO2 injectivity. The injection well achieved 40 MMt over the
 30-year period (Figure C-26), falling short of the 50-MMt target due to the BHP limit restricting
 injection during the last decade (Figure C-27). This case provides confidence that significant SWD
 intensification could limit capacity but would still enable substantial storage volumes.

 APPENDIX D

 CORE ANALYSIS AND TESTING

 CORE ANALYSIS AND TESTING

 Core analysis and testing were performed on Roughrider 1 core from the Inyan Kara, Broom
 Creek, Mission Canyon, and Black Island–Deadwood storage complexes. A series of analyses
 including core description, spectral gamma ray (GR), thin-section petrography, porosity and
 permeability, high-pressure mercury injection (HPMI), uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), X-
 ray diffraction (XRD), X-ray fluorescence (XRF), and steady-state relative permeability were used
 to understand petrographic, geochemical, geomechanical, and petrophysical characteristics. The
 results for each storage complex are summarized below.

 Inyan Kara Storage Complex

 The Inyan Kara Formation primarily comprises individual and stacked fluvial channel
 sandstones (permeable storage intervals) interbedded with impermeable mudstone. The thickness
 of the formation at Roughrider 1 is 273 ft, with 164 ft of sandstone. The sandstone intervals have
 high porosity and permeability, contrasted by low porosity and permeability in the mudstone
 intervals (Figure D-1). Table D-1 summarizes the core-derived properties. The range in porosity
 and permeability captures the reservoir heterogeneity.

 Core plug porosity and permeability analysis measured high porosity and permeability in the
 Inyan Kara. Thin sections from the formation show intercrystalline porosity enhanced by feldspar
 dissolution in the most permeable samples. Conversely, no porosity is observed in mudstone
 samples like 5696.5 ft, where the clay matrix occludes porosity between quartz grains
            (Figure D-2).

 Figure D-2. Left: Thin section of sandstone sample at 5701 ft. The blue color represents
 porosity. Right: Mudstone sample at 5696.5 ft with no visible porosity.

 The Inyan Kara Formation unconformably overlies the Swift Formation, the lower confining
 zone, and is conformably overlain by the Skull Creek, the upper confining zone (Figure 1 strat
 column in final report) (Bader, 2019). Both the overlying and underlying confining formations
 consist primarily of impermeable rocks. Low porosity (<6%) and low permeability (<0.06 mD)
 were measured in the upper confining zone at 2400 psi net confining stress. Porosity and
 permeability could not be measured in the lower confining zone at 2400 psi net confining stress
 because of fractured/chipped samples. However, low porosity and permeability were confirmed
 with log-derived calculations (Figure D-1). The Skull Creek exhibits a UCS of approximately
 12,500 psi, coupled with a static Young’s modulus of 3.17 Mpsi (≈21.9 GPa) and a relatively low
 Poisson’s ratio of 0.17, reflecting a stiff and competent rock fabric with low deformability—
 confirming the rock strength of the upper confining zone. Additional confining zone properties are
 in Table D-2.

 Powder XRD analysis for average bulk composition analysis of 11 finely ground,
 homogenized samples from the Skull Creek Formation show three primary (>10%) mineral
 classes: clay + mica (7%–42%), carbonate (1%–63%), and quartz (13%–63%). The clay fraction
 mainly consists of illite, kaolinite, and chlorite, while the carbonates are predominantly siderite
 and calcite. XRD analysis of four samples from the Swift Formation show two primary (>10%)
 mineral classes: clay + mica (27%–46%) and quartz (28%–48%). While there is some variation in
 mineralogy within the Skull Creek and Swift Formations, porosity and permeability remain
 consistently low (Figure D-3). Seventeen out of 39 of the Inyan Kara samples contain over 90%
 quartz.

 Broom Creek Storage Complex

 The Broom Creek Formation comprises interbedded eolian/nearshore marine sandstone
 (permeable storage intervals) and dolostone layers (impermeable layers). The thickness of the
 formation at Roughrider 1 is 76 ft, with 53.5 ft of sandstone. Although the formation is relatively
 thin, the sandstone that exists, particularly in the upper portion of the formation, has high porosity
 (>20%) and permeability (>400 mD) (Figure D-4). Table D-3 summarizes the core-derived
 properties.

 The porosity observed in Broom Creek thin-section samples is predominantly
 intercrystalline, with some attributed to feldspar dissolution. Anhydrite and dolomitic cement
 between sand grains and dolomite matrix obstruct porosity in some samples, particularly in the
 lower half of the formation (Figure D-5).

 The confining zones for the Broom Creek Formation are the overlying Opeche Formation
 and the underlying Amsden Formation (Figure 1 strat column in the final report). Both the
 overlying and underlying confining formations consist primarily of impermeable rock layers. Low
 porosity (<8%) and low permeability (<0.08 mD) were measured in the upper confining zone at
 2400 psi net confining stress. One sample at 7867.20 ft exhibited higher permeability (59.9 mD)
 relative to the other five samples and is considered an outlier. This sample contains an average
 halite content of 24%, which was likely dissolved during cleaning, artificially inflating the
 measured permeability and rendering the result unrepresentative of the formation. The Opeche
 shows high UCS values, ranging from ~12,178 to over 20,958 psi, with static Young’s moduli
 between 2.40 and 3.28 Mpsi (≈16.5–22.6 GPa). These results demonstrate a mechanically strong
 and moderately stiff rock, capable of withstanding high differential stresses without significant
 fracturing. The geomechanics tests confirm the rock strength of the upper confining zone.
 Additional confining zone properties are in Table D-4.

 Powder XRD analysis for average bulk composition analysis of six finely ground,
 homogenized samples from the Opeche Formation shows six primary minerals: quartz (28%–
 58%), dolomite (4%–25%), halite (0%–24%), clay minerals (primarily illite and kaolinite) (5%–
 21%), feldspar (orthoclase and albite) (3%–26%), and anhydrite (1–20%). Twelve samples were
 analyzed from the Broom Creek Formation. The sample from the top of the formation at 7879.9 ft
 is dominated by anhydrite (74%). Samples from 7888.3 to 7919.9 ft mainly contain 79%–92%
 quartz. Samples from the lower half of the formation from 7927.5 to7952.9 ft primarily consists
 of quartz (11%–89%) and dolomite (0%–82%), with minor (<10%) amounts of other minerals.
 Notably, the sample at 7941.5 ft contains 82% dolomite. XRD analysis of six samples from the
 Amsden Formation shows three primary (>10%) minerals: dolomite (0%–71%), quartz (13%–
 92%), and anhydrite (0%–41%). Figure D-6 shows porosity and permeability compared to
 mineralogy.

 Mission Canyon Storage Complex

 The Tilston interval of the Mission Canyon Formation contains packstone/grainstone in the
 upper Tilston and a skeletal grainstone/packstone in the lower Tilston. The highest porosity was
 expected at the top of cyclic shoaling-upward sequences. However, permeability measurements
 throughout the interval are low (.001–0.532 mD). Thin-section analysis supports the porosity and
 permeability measurements, showing little observable porosity due to dolomitization and calcite
 cement (Figure D-7).

 D-8

 Figure D-7. Left: Sample 9957.8 ft is a grainstone composed of crinoids, rugose corals, and

 Powder XRD for average bulk composition analysis of 18 finely ground, homogenized
 samples from the Tilston interval confirm that the mineralogy is primarily carbonate (92–99 wt%)
 except for three samples at 9953.9, 9981.6, and 10,051.8 ft. These three samples have an increase
 in quartz content (31–49 wt%) compared to the other Tilston samples.

 The Tilston interval at Roughrider 1 exhibits poor reservoir properties and, based on the core
 analysis and testing, was not further pursued as a potential CO2 storage complex at this location.

 Black Island–Deadwood Storage Complex

 The Black Island Formation comprises high-energy shallow marine sandstone (permeable
 storage intervals) and shale (impermeable layers). About 67 ft of Black Island core was recovered
 from Roughrider 1. Permeability measurements throughout the recovered core are low (0.001–
 0.157 mD). Thin-section analysis supports the porosity and permeability measurements, showing
 little to no observable porosity due to quartz overgrowth and ankerite, chamosite, and clay cement
            (Figure D-8).

 The Black Island Formation at Roughrider 1 exhibits poor reservoir properties and, based
 on the core analysis and testing, was not further pursued as a potential CO2 storage complex at this
 location.
