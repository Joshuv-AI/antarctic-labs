Solitary neurocysticercosis mimicking tuberculoma and glioblastoma: A diagnostic challenge in a low-resource setting
Authors: Mohamedelamin Eltrafi Yousif, Fatima Mahmoud Ali Mohamed, Husam Mohamedahmed Babiker Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Neurocysticercosis (NCC) is the most common parasitic infection of the central nervous system and a major cause of acquired epilepsy in endemic regions. Solitary parenchymal lesions frequently mimic tuberculoma or high-grade glioma, posing a significant diagnostic challenge, particularly in tuberculosis-endemic and resource-limited settings.Case Description: We report the case of a 60-year-old male farmer with diabetes mellitus who presented with a 1-week history of focal seizures with secondary generalization and progressive left-sided weakness. Neurological examination revealed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15 and grade 4+ power in the left upper and lower limbs, with no sensory deficit. The patient had a prior history of treated pulmonary tuberculosis. Brain magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a solitary ring-enhancing lesion in the right medial frontal lobe, with differential diagnoses of tuberculoma versus high-grade glioma. He underwent right parietal craniotomy with a transcortical approach and gross total excision of a well-circumscribed lesion. Histopathological examination revealed a cysticercus larva with surrounding inflammatory infiltrate, confirming NCC. Postoperatively, the patient received albendazole (15 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks and continued antiepileptic therapy. Follow-up imaging showed complete resolution of the lesion, and the patient remained seizure-free with full neurological recovery.Conclusion: NCC should be considered in the differential diagnosis of solitary ring-enhancing brain lesions even in patients with prior tuberculosis. When imaging is inconclusive, surgical excision provides definitive diagnosis and effective treatment with excellent neurological and seizure outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Neurocysticercosis (NCC), caused by the larval stage of Taenia solium, is the most common parasitic infection of the central nervous system (CNS) and a major cause of acquired epilepsy worldwide.[1,7-9] Clinical manifestations depend on the number, size, location, and evolutionary stage of the cysts.[9,10]

In tuberculosis-endemic regions, solitary ring-enhancing lesions present a significant diagnostic challenge, as they may mimic tuberculoma or primary brain tumors such as glioblastoma.[4,13] Differentiating these entities based solely on neuroimaging is often difficult, particularly in low-resource settings where advanced diagnostic modalities may be limited.[12,13]

We present a surgically confirmed case of solitary NCC in a patient with a prior history of pulmonary tuberculosis, highlighting the diagnostic dilemma and the role of surgery in establishing a definitive diagnosis.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 60-year-old male farmer with a known history of diabetes mellitus presented with a 1-week history of focal seizures in his left hand with secondary generalization and progressive left-sided weakness. He had been treated for pulmonary tuberculosis 7 years earlier and completed a full course of therapy.

On examination, the patient was fully conscious and oriented with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15. Motor examination revealed grade 4+ power in the left upper and lower limbs, with normal strength on the right side. No sensory deficits were identified.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain demonstrated a solitary ring-enhancing lesion in the right medial frontal lobe, superior to the corpus callosum, with surrounding vasogenic edema [Figure 1a]. Based on imaging and clinical history, the differential diagnosis included tuberculoma and high-grade glioma.

(b): Postoperative non-contrast computed tomography brain (sagittal view) demonstrating postoperative changes following right parietal craniotomy with a small resection cavity and no residual enhancing lesion. There is resolution of mass effect with no evidence of hemorrhage or hydrocephalus.(White arrow)

The patient was started on levetiracetam 500 mg twice daily and dexamethasone preoperatively.

Surgical procedure

Surgery was performed with the patient in the supine position, and the head tilted to the left. A right-sided parietal craniotomy was performed, followed by a transcortical approach.

Intraoperatively, a firm, yellowish, well-circumscribed lesion was identified and completely excised. No intraoperative complications were encountered.

Postoperative course

Postoperatively, the patient remained neurologically stable with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15/15. Mild residual left-sided weakness (grade 4+) improved to normal strength within 2 weeks with physiotherapy. The patient remained seizure-free.

Histopathological findings

Histopathological examination revealed a cysticercus larva with surrounding inflammatory infiltrate, confirming the diagnosis of NCC [Figures 2 and 3].

Postoperative management and follow-up

The patient received albendazole (15 mg/kg/day) for 2 weeks along with corticosteroids and continued antiepileptic therapy. Follow-up computed tomography demonstrated complete resolution of the lesion [Figure 1b]. At follow-up, the patient remained seizure-free with no neurological deficits.

DISCUSSION

Solitary ring-enhancing brain lesions remain a common diagnostic challenge, particularly in regions where both NCC and tuberculosis are endemic.[4,9,13] The differential diagnosis includes NCC, tuberculoma, brain abscess, metastasis, and high-grade gliomas such as glioblastoma.[9,13] Distinguishing among these entities is often difficult because clinical and radiological features may overlap significantly.[4,13]

NCC is the most common parasitic infection of the CNS and a major cause of acquired epilepsy worldwide.[6-9,14] Clinical manifestations vary according to the number, size, location, and stage of the lesions.[5,9,10] Solitary parenchymal lesions frequently present with seizures and may appear as ring-enhancing lesions on neuroimaging, making differentiation from tuberculoma particularly challenging.[3,9,13]

Tuberculoma remains an important differential diagnosis in tuberculosis-endemic regions.[11,15,16,19] Importantly, CNS tuberculomas can occur independently of active pulmonary tuberculosis and may involve both the brain and spinal cord.[11,15,16,19] Multiple craniospinal tuberculomas have been reported in association with miliary tuberculosis as well as in the absence of active systemic disease.[11,15,16,19] Park and Song described simultaneous intracranial and spinal tuberculomas in a patient with miliary pulmonary tuberculosis, emphasizing the diverse manifestations of CNS tuberculosis.[11] Similarly, Yen et al. reported multiple tuberculomas involving both the brain and spinal cord, highlighting the diagnostic complexity of these lesions.[19] Turgut et al. reviewed craniospinal tuberculomas and demonstrated that these lesions may mimic neoplastic and other granulomatous disorders.[16] Tunçyürek et al. further emphasized that concurrent intracranial and spinal tuberculomas represent a rare but important manifestation of CNS tuberculosis that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of enhancing neuraxial lesions.[15]

In our patient, a previous history of treated pulmonary tuberculosis increased the clinical suspicion of tuberculoma. The magnetic resonance imaging findings of a solitary ring-enhancing lesion with surrounding vasogenic edema were compatible with either tuberculoma or a high-grade glioma. Furthermore, the absence of a visible scolex reduced radiological confidence in the diagnosis of NCC.[4,13] In such situations, reliance on imaging alone may lead to diagnostic uncertainty and potentially inappropriate treatment.[12,13]

Although antiparasitic therapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for parenchymal NCC,[2,10,17] surgical intervention remains appropriate when diagnosis is uncertain, when significant mass effect is present, or when a neoplasm cannot be excluded.[10,17] In the present case, surgery provided both definitive diagnosis and therapeutic lesion removal. Histopathological examination demonstrated a cysticercus larva with surrounding inflammatory infiltrate, confirming NCC and excluding both tuberculoma and glioma.[4,5]

This case highlights the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating solitary ring-enhancing lesions, even in patients with a prior history of tuberculosis.[4,11,13,15,16,19] Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis when imaging findings are inconclusive.[4,5] In low-resource settings, where advanced serological and molecular diagnostic techniques may not be readily available, surgical excision may play a crucial role in establishing the diagnosis and guiding appropriate management.[10,13,17,18]

CONCLUSION

Solitary NCC should be considered in the differential diagnosis of ring-enhancing brain lesions, particularly in tuberculosis-endemic and resource-limited settings. Surgical excision can be both diagnostic and therapeutic when imaging findings are inconclusive, and histopathological confirmation is essential for guiding management.
