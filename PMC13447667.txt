Integrating multitemperature sample preparation with particle rebalancing addresses preferred orientation
Authors: Xin Su, Liuyan Yang, Caiying Zhang, Lei Qi, Mingdong Liu, Yunhui Liu, Peiyi Wang, Yong Li, Chunhui Li, Qing-Tao Shen, Jinzhong Lin, Cheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) often suffers from preferred orientation, which incurs particle misalignments and compromises reconstruction quality. While the use of detergents, modified grids, tilted data collection, and algorithmic corrections can partially mitigate this issue, these approaches are usually time-consuming and case dependent. Here, we present a facile and effective approach that modulates particle orientation by varying cryo-EM sample preparation temperatures. By merging thermophilic 70S ribosome datasets collected at different temperatures and applying a three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction–based particle rebalancing strategy, we achieved improved reconstruction, as evidenced by smoother directional Fourier shell correlation (FSC) curves, higher 3D FSC sphericity, and narrower anisotropy transition zone in Fourier shell occupancy. We demonstrated the efficacy of this approach by determining the structure of γ-glutamylmethylamide synthetase, a protein complex prone to severe preferred orientation, ultimately obtaining a high-quality reconstruction. In all, our work integrates high-temperature sample preparation and particle rebalancing to address preferred orientation, holding potential for broad applicability in single-particle cryo-EM.Keywords: high-temperature cryo-EM, preferred orientation, 3D particle rebalancing, 3D FSC sphericity, Fourier shell occupancy

Preferred orientation during cryo-electron microscopy sample preparation severely limits high-resolution structure determination by restricting particle views. We introduce a simple, potentially applicable solution via varying sample vitrification temperature to modulate orientations according to Boltzmann statistics. By merging data from different temperatures and applying a subsequent three-dimensional particle rebalancing algorithm, we achieve more uniform angular distributions and superior reconstruction quality of thermophilic 70S ribosomes. This integrated method mitigates a pervasive experimental bottleneck, enabling more accurate structural analysis of challenging complexes prone to severe orientation bias, such as γ-glutamylmethylamide synthetase.

Introduction

High-resolution structures are crucial for uncovering how proteins work, how they malfunction in disease, and how to target them with drugs (1, 2). Single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has become a powerful technique, without the need for crystallization, enabling the determination of numerous high-resolution structures of purified proteins (3, 4). An ideal three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction in cryo-EM requires projections from diverse orientations to ensure comprehensive sampling of reciprocal space (5, 6). However, preferred orientation—where particles predominantly align along certain orientations—remains a pervasive issue in vitrified samples (7). Adsorption of proteins at the air–water interface (AWI) or solid–water interface (SWI) often exacerbates this phenomenon (8–10).

While mild preferred orientation may still yield decent-resolution 3D reconstructions, it introduces density distortion along different directions and damages the sphericity of 3D Fourier shell correlation (FSC) curves (11). Current computational strategies to mitigate preferred orientation include: (i) particle selection or subsampling strategies that reduce overrepresented views; (ii) orientation balancing or reweighting approaches applied during 3D refinement; (iii) postreconstruction correction methods that compensate for preferred orientation-induced map distortions (12); and (iv) AI- or deep learning–based approaches that improve particle alignment and reconstruction robustness (13–15). Although collectively mitigating orientation bias, these approaches exhibit sample-dependent efficacy, often requiring extensive empirical optimization.

To mitigate severe orientation bias, experimental approaches have also been developed (12, 16–19). Detergents can coat the AWI, reducing particle adsorption (20). Modified grid surfaces can bind target proteins, preventing AWI-induced orientation bias (10, 16, 20–28), albeit at the risk of introducing SWI artifacts. Tilted data collection serves as a last-resort method to capture missing views (11, 29, 30). For extreme cases like ESCRT-III flat spirals, where particles adopt a single preferred orientation on membrane, the collection of tilted particles, together with their assigned Euler angles based on geometry, is crucial to avoid misalignment and obtain optimal reconstructions (31). While effective, these methods are often time-consuming and case specific, thereby highlighting the unmet need for universal solutions to this pervasive challenge.

Cryo-EM sample preparation is typically conducted at low temperatures (4–16 °C) (32–37). Recent advances by our and several other groups have enabled grid preparation at elevated temperatures (e.g. ≥37 °C) through thermal regulation of the Vitrobot chamber (38–43). High-temperature sample preparation often renders proteins in more dynamic conformations, sometimes revealing unique structural states that are inaccessible at lower temperatures. Diverse protein conformations follow Boltzmann distribution, Pi∝e−(Ei/kBT), where Ei is the free energy of the conformational state i, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and T is the absolute temperature (44, 45). Our systematic investigations demonstrate that annealing—tandem heating and cooling—on complexes like 70S ribosome or the TOM complex can synchronize their conformations into a minimum-energy conformation (38, 39). These findings establish the critical influence of sample preparation temperature on the conformational landscapes of biological macromolecules.

Particle orientation distributions at the AWI/SWI also conform to Boltzmann statistics, with the preferred orientation representing the global free-energy minimum. Since the particle orientation and the derived free energy are temperature dependent, elevated temperatures will facilitate more rapid kinetics, thereby increasing the probability that proteins escape from preferred orientations. This establishes a physiochemical framework for temperature-dependent orientation changes. Nevertheless, the mechanistic role of temperature in modulating protein orientation during cryo-EM sample preparation remains unexplored.

Here, we present a high-temperature cryo-EM sample preparation methodology and demonstrate that varying temperatures modulate particle orientation distribution. By merging particles collected at different temperatures and implementing a 3D reconstruction–based particle rebalancing strategy, we improved reconstruction quality, yielding smoother FSC curves, higher 3D FSC sphericity, and decreasing anisotropy in Fourier shell occupancy (FSO) analysis. We applied this method to resolve the structure of γ-glutamylmethylamide synthetase, a protein complex with severe preferred orientation, achieving a high-quality reconstruction. In summary, our work establishes thermally controlled cryo-EM sample preparation as a simple yet effective strategy to modulate particle orientations. When integrated with 3D particle rebalancing, this approach enhances reconstruction quality and can be broadly applied in single-particle cryo-EM.

Results

Temperature-dependent orientation change

Successful cryo-EM sample preparation at elevated temperatures necessitates precise thermal control during sample transfer and effective prevention of ice contamination during vitrification. To address these requirements, we engineered a built-in heating module within the Vitrobot chamber to maintain thermal stability during cryo-EM sample preparation (Fig. S1). Additionally, we employed a custom-designed mist umbrella apparatus to reduce ice contamination caused by the rapid cooling of high-temperature mist near the liquid ethane and nitrogen container (38). These synergistic optimizations enabled reliable production of high-quality cryo-EM grids under controlled thermal conditions.

Compared with cryo-EM sample preparation at 4 °C, proteins will undergo increased structural dynamics at higher temperatures, which can compromise the final reconstruction quality. To minimize this effect, we selected Thermus thermophilus 70S ribosomes (denoted as Tt_70S) in the apo state as our model protein, as their optimal functional temperature range (70–79 °C) ensures structural stability under lower temperatures (≤45 °C). By freezing Tt_70S under varying temperatures, we ensured that differences in map quality would primarily reflect temperature-dependent particle orientation changes.

We followed the practical experience, utilizing continuous carbon-coated holey carbon grids to prepare Tt_70S cryo-EM samples. We first froze Tt_70S under the low temperature of 4 °C (denoted as Tt_70S_4°C). Cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET) analysis revealed that the vast majority of particles were localized to a single layer at the SWI (Fig. 1A). Notably, even when ice thickness was increased from ∼50 to ∼80 nm by reducing blotting time during cryo-EM sample preparation, the particles exhibited the same distribution pattern, suggesting that SWI localization of Tt_70S is independent of ice thickness (Figs. 1A and S2).

To assess whether temperature influences particle orientations, we froze Tt_70S at elevated temperatures (37 and 45 °C, denoted as Tt_70S_37°C and Tt_70S_45°C, respectively). Our cryo-ET analysis revealed that Tt_70S_37°C exhibited SWI localization similar to the Tt_70S_4°C control (Fig. S2). We collected datasets of Tt_70S_4°C, Tt_70S_37°C, and Tt_70S_45°C, and conducted single-particle cryo-EM analyses (Fig. S3 and Table S1). After the removal of obvious junks, we randomly selected 30,000 particles from each condition. The 3D reconstructions of Tt_70S_4°C, Tt_70S_37°C, and Tt_70S_45°C achieved nominal global resolutions of 3.4, 3.0, and 3.2 Å, respectively (Figs. 1B–D and S3, Table S1). While the overall ribosome structures remained conserved across temperatures, significant divergence in orientation distributions at the SWI emerged: samples prepared at 37 and 45 °C displayed distinct preferential orientation compared with the 4 °C reference (Fig. 1B–D).

Detailed analysis of the particle orientation distribution revealed specific relationships: the P1 and P2 clusters of Tt_70S_4°C occupied the mirror positions relative to the P3 and P4 clusters of Tt_70S_37°C, respectively. In contrast, the P5 cluster from Tt_70S_45°C represented a distinct, new particle orientation (Fig. 1B–G). Interestingly, Tt_70S_37°C covered a wider range of orientation than either Tt_70S_4°C or Tt_70S_45°C, likely correlating with its enhanced resolution (Fig. 1B–G). To test whether the orientation distribution directly influences the final resolution, we curated a subset of Tt_70S_37°C particles to mimic the orientation coverage of the other two datasets. The resulting 3.4 Å resolution of this subset was comparable to those of Tt_70S_4°C and Tt_70S_45°C, demonstrating that orientation coverage is a major determinant of resolution (Fig. S4). This finding suggests temperature as a modulator of particle orientation, critical for high-resolution reconstruction.

To validate the reproducibility of this phenomenon, we re-analyzed the particle distributions of Escherichia coli 70S ribosomes (denoted as Ec_70S) from our previous annealing experiments (38). Ec_70S prepared at 37 °C demonstrated distinctly altered particle distribution compared with the 4 °C counterpart. Remarkably, Ec_70S annealed from 37 to 4 °C induced reversion to the original 4 °C orientation distribution (Fig. S5). This temperature-reversible behavior persisted consistently across all tested annealing conditions—irrespective of initial temperatures (≥45 °C) or cooling rates—indicating that particle orientation distribution is highly temperature-dependent, irrelevant of sample batch or preparation protocol.

Dataset merging improves reconstruction quality

Although 3D reconstructions of Tt_70S at specific temperatures achieve decent resolutions, directional FSC curves reveal substantial oscillations across different views—particularly for Tt_70S_4°C and Tt_70S_45°C with restricted orientations (Figs. S6 and S7). Their respective sphericities at a threshold of 0.5 are 0.812 and 0.798, consistent with our earlier conclusion that missing views compromise reconstruction quality (Fig. S8).

We observed that the orientation distribution of Tt_70S_45°C strongly complements those of lower-temperature ribosomes. To achieve more complete orientation sampling and enhance resolution, we combined normalized datasets from 4, 37, and 45 °C in equal proportions (Fig. 2A–C). Using an equivalent number of particles (30,000), the merged reconstruction achieved a resolution of 3.0 Å, as well as a significantly higher 3D FSC sphericity of 0.949 (Figs. S7–S9). It consistently outperformed the single-temperature maps in both FSC curve stability and overall sphericity (Fig. 2D).

Sphericity plots at a 3D FSC threshold of 0.5 (left) and a Rosenthal–Henderson plot (right) demonstrate the resolution improvement from dataset merging.

Notably, merging just Tt_70S_4°C and Tt_70S_45°C—two individually suboptimal datasets—yielded a resolution of 3.0 Å with improved sphericity to 0.932, surpassing the reconstruction quality of Tt_70S_37°C (Figs. S7–S9). Similarly, combining Tt_70S_4°C with Tt_70S_37°C yielded a superior result compared with Tt_70S_37°C alone (Figs. S7–S9). These findings demonstrate the profound impact of orientation diversification through dataset integration on achieving high-quality reconstructions.

Particle rebalancing further enhances resolution

The merged dataset combining particles from multiple temperatures demonstrated improved orientation uniformity compared with single-temperature datasets. However, our analyses revealed persistent preferred orientations, with distinct dominant peaks remaining in the angular distribution (Fig. 2A–C). To address this orientation bias, we evaluated the cryoSPARC strategy of selectively combining particles from underrepresented 2D classes. While this approach can improve angular coverage, we identified a fundamental limitation: the lack of a robust correlation between 2D class averages and their corresponding 3D orientations. This dysconnectivity complicates the reliable identification of which 2D classes should be retained or discarded to achieve optimal 3D reconstruction quality.

To address this, we developed a particle rebalancing method after 3D reconstruction. Particles from Tt_70S_4°C, Tt_70S_37°C, and Tt_70S_45°C were partitioned into groups according to their orientations, after which we subsampled particles from each group in order while reducing overrepresented orientations (Fig. 3A). This rebalancing approach yielded more homogeneous orientation distributions compared with either individual datasets or their simple merge (Figs. 3B–E and S10). The rebalanced dataset (a total of 30,000 particles) subsequently reached an overall resolution of 3.0 Å (Fig. 3B–E). Furthermore, it demonstrated a marked improvement in reconstruction quality, with a 3D FSC sphericity of 0.980 and diminished resolution anisotropy, outperforming the unbalanced counterparts (Figs. S7–S9).

To further evaluate reconstruction isotropy, we performed a per-angle directional FSC analysis, which directly maps resolution distribution across all orientations. Our analysis revealed that the rebalanced datasets exhibited a narrower resolution distribution and smaller variance across all directions (Figs. 3F and S11). This performance was significantly superior to that of any single-temperature dataset or their simple merge. These results confirm that our rebalancing strategy effectively enhances directional resolution uniformity, underscoring its comprehensive benefits for improving cryo-EM reconstruction quality and map isotropy.

As expected, particle rebalancing applied to Tt_70S_4°C and Tt_70S_45°C could also improve reconstruction quality compared with dataset merging (Figs. S7–S9). However, rebalancing the single-temperature dataset alone failed to surpass the performance of the unbalanced merged dataset, underscoring the synergy between multitemperature data integration and particle rebalancing. Collectively, the integration of multitemperature sample preparation with 3D particle rebalancing constitutes an effective methodology for achieving uniform angular distributions, significantly improving reconstruction quality.

High temperature renders GmaS more views

We validated the versatility of our newly developed methodology using GmaS, an enzyme complex that catalyzes methylamine conversion to γ-glutamylmethylamide. When GmaS was frozen at 4 °C (denoted as GmaS_4°C), the resulting particles exhibited a strong preferred orientation. The particle distribution showed a predominance of side views, with very few end-on views (Figs. 4A and S12, Table S2). Although the inherent D6 symmetry of GmaS provides sufficient side views and is beneficial for reconstruction, quantitative analysis confirms a pronounced orientation bias. Particles are densely concentrated at low elevation angles (<20°), corresponding to side views (log-count >103), while the angular range from 20° to 70° is sparsely populated (log-count <102) (Fig. 4A).

A and B) Local resolution maps, global orientation distributions, and FSO curves for GmaS_4°C (A) and GmaS_40°C (B). C) Direct comparisons of orientation distribution between GmaS_4°C and GmaS_40°C. D) Angular distribution of particle views, plotted as logarithmic particle counts versus elevation angle, for GmaS_4°C (blue) and GmaS_40°C (orange). E) Map to model FSC at the criterion of 0.5 as a function of inverse resolution (1/Å) for GmaS_4°C (blue) and GmaS_40°C (orange), computed with a mask. F) CryoNet-predicted models for GmaS_4°C and GmaS_40°C (left), alongside chain length distribution (middle) and per-residue score distribution (right) in models.

As expected, when we performed high-temperature sample preparation of GmaS and collected data at 40 °C (denoted as GmaS_40°C), the proportion of end-on and intermediate views increased, significantly improving particle distribution (Fig. 4B). Due to increased structural flexibility incurred by higher temperatures, the nominal global resolution of GmaS_40°C was slightly lower than that of GmaS_4°C (Fig. 4A and B). However, 40 °C sample preparation substantially redistributed particle orientations, significantly enhancing occupancy at intermediate elevation angles (side views, 20° to 70°; Fig. 4C and D). Consequently, the map-to-model resolution and FSO of GmaS_40°C were superior, due to better angular coverage (Fig. 4A, B, and E). The GmaS_40°C map featured more continuous main chains, better-resolved side chains than the 4 °C reconstruction (Fig. S13).

To quantify map quality, we submitted both EM densities of GmaS_4°C and GmaS_40°C to CryoNet (46) for automated model prediction without sequence input. Structures predicted with scores above 40 are displayed under each condition (Fig. 4F). Further analysis revealed that GmaS_40°C exhibited superior main-chain continuity, as evidenced by the increased prevalence of longer continuous segments, along with a more favorable per-residue score distribution across most ranges compared with GmaS_4°C (Fig. 4F). These results collectively highlight the unique advantages of high-temperature sample preparation on GmaS in mitigating preferred orientation effects and enhancing the quality of structural determination.

Despite its clear benefits in this case, high-temperature sample preparation alone may not be universally applicable. Elevated temperatures can induce protein instability, conformational heterogeneity, and altered interactions with the AWI/SWI. These limitations restrict the standalone utility of this approach and underscore the need for an integrated strategy that combines multitemperature sample preparation with particle rebalancing—an approach that simultaneously mitigates preferred orientation at the AWI/SWI and accommodates the structural flexibility inherent to elevated temperatures.

Discussion

In single-particle cryo-EM, most datasets are affected by mild to severe preferred orientation issues, posing a significant challenge in achieving high-quality reconstructions. To address this limitation, we developed an integrated approach combining high-temperature sample preparation with 3D particle rebalancing. This approach comprises two synergistic components, offering an effective solution that yields structures with optimal 3D FSC sphericity and good quality of FSO, essential for accurate model building, protein identification, and structural/functional analyses.

Many variables during grid freezing, such as ice thickness, blotting time, blot force, local humidity, evaporation rate, and vitrification temperature, may influence particle distribution. Our work specifically focused on the effect of vitrification temperature. Distinct from conventional sample preparation at 4 °C or subambient temperatures, our approach requires additional datasets collected at higher temperatures. Although this may initially seem burdensome, high-temperature datasets preserve more native-like conformations, adding significant value to structural studies. For instance, recent structural studies on temperature-sensitive channels uncovered previously unidentified conformations at physiological temperature—a finding with potential implications for drug targeting (43, 47). Thus, our approach achieves dual benefits with a single strategy.

High-temperature vitrification above 45 °C carries risks to proteins, such as structural heterogeneity, partial unfolding, and aggregation. However, brief exposure to 45 °C is generally safe for proteins with melting temperatures above this threshold, and lowering to 37 °C further minimizes these risks. Using plant DNA methyltransferase 1 (MET1) (48), we demonstrated that even moderate temperatures (20 and 30 °C) can effectively modulate particle orientation, without requiring extreme heat (Figs. S14–S16). Taking proteins' stability and physiological relevance into account, we recommend using 4 °C and their optimal temperatures for dual-temperature data collection—a combination applicable in most cases.

Our cryo-ET analyses demonstrated that Tt_70S_4°C, Tt_70S_37°C, and Tt_70S_45°C all remain confined to single layers at the SWI/AWI (Figs. 1A and S2). While Tt_70S adopts distinct orientation distributions at different temperatures, neither Tt_70S_37°C nor Tt_70S_45°C, led to more diverse orientations (Fig. 1). Similarly, MET1 samples prepared at 4, 20, and 30 °C showed no substantial increase in orientation diversity (Fig. S14). In contrast, GmaS_40°C exhibited a more diverse orientation distribution along the longitudinal axis than GmaS_4°C. Taken together, these results suggest two possible mechanisms by which elevated temperatures modulate particle orientation: (i) enhanced thermal energy promotes particle kinetics, allowing greater orientation diversity, as observed for GmaS; and (ii) elevated temperatures may induce subtle conformational changes that alter the protein's interaction with the AWI/SWI, thereby exposing new orientations, as seen for Tt_70S and MET1 (Fig. 5). Both proposed mechanisms require further validation and investigation.

Our high-temperature method differs fundamentally from the laser flash melting technique (19, 49, 50), another thermal approach recently developed in cryo-EM. Laser flash melting uses intense, rectangular laser pulses to physically dislodge proteins from the AWI prior to revitrification. Rather than forcibly clearing the interface, our sample preparation at physiologically relevant temperatures (37–45 °C), supplies thermal energy that enables particles to overcome kinetic traps, thereby promoting orientation sampling in accordance with Boltzmann statistics. By combining this with data collected at low temperatures, our multitemperature preparation offers a simple yet effective strategy for diversifying particle orientations at both the AWI and SWI, with the additional benefit of potentially capturing functional conformations.

CryoSPARC previously offered a 2D class–based particle rebalancing function and recently introduced a 3D reconstruction–based rebalancing tool similar to our approach. However, the efficacy of this feature has not been thoroughly investigated. Here, we systematically evaluate 3D reconstruction–based particle rebalancing and demonstrate its necessity through rigorous validation. While cryoSPARC's built-in function also improves reconstruction quality, our approach achieves comparable enhancements with additional methodological insights.

Materials and methods

Purification of Tt_70S

Tt_70S in the apo state was purified from T. thermophilus HB8 (rplIΔ58148:htk), as described previously (51, 52). Briefly, HB8 cells were inoculated in rich medium (e.g. Thermus Broth) and grown at 70 °C with shaking until mid-log phase. Cultures were immediately cooled on ice for ∼20 min, and cells were pelleted via centrifugation at 4 °C.

Harvested cells were washed with wash buffer (20 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, 100 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid [EDTA], 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol) and then resuspended with DNase I added. Cells were lysed, and the crude lysate was kept on ice for 5–10 min and then clarified by centrifugation. The clarified supernatant was adjusted with NH4Cl with the final concentration of 500 mM. The salt-adjusted lysate was carefully layered onto prechilled sucrose cushions. Ribosomes were pelleted by ultracentrifugation using a Beckman 45 Ti rotor. The supernatant was carefully decanted, and the pellet containing ribosomes was washed gently and then resuspended into dissociation buffer (20 mM Tris HCl pH 7.4, 60 mM NH4Cl, 1 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM EDTA, 6 mM β-mercaptoethanol) by gentle shaking on ice until the pellet was fully dissolved. The clarified ribosome solution was layered onto preformed 5–30% linear sucrose gradients. Gradients were centrifuged using a Beckman SW41 rotor.

The pooled 50S and 30S ribosomal subunits were concentrated and buffer-exchanged via ultracentrifugation into wash buffer with 14 mM MgCl2 and combined at a 1:1.1 molar ratio (30S:50S). After incubating at 37 °C for 10 min, the reassociated 70S ribosomes were purified through 10–40% sucrose gradients. The reconstituted 70S peak fractions identified by UV monitoring were pooled, diluted to working concentration, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent applications.

Purification of GmaS

The recombinant GmaS protein was expressed and purified following the previously established protocol (53). Briefly, the gene encoding full-length GmaS from Rhodovulum sp. 12E13 was cloned into a pET-28a vector for expression with an N-terminal His-tag. The recombinant protein was expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) induced by 0.5 mM IPTG (isopropylthio-β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside) at 18 °C for 16 h. The protein was first purified by Ni2+-NTA affinity chromatography, followed by anion-exchange chromatography on a Source 15Q column and size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 column. To obtain tag-free protein, the His-tag was removed by thrombin cleavage and separated by an additional step of size-exclusion chromatography.

Multitemperature cryo-EM sample preparation

During cryo-EM sample preparation, sudden exposure of hot vapor (≥37 °C) in the Vitrobot chamber to cool ambient (<10 °C) causes severe ice contamination on cryo-EM grids. To minimize ice contamination across various temperatures (particularly elevated temperatures), we utilized the “mist umbrella,” as previously described (38). We also engineered an integrated heating module to maintain thermal consistency during sample transfer. The module comprises a 3D-printed heating tank, filled with temperature-controlled water, and a thin-walled iron injection needle for efficient heat transfer. The sample is housed within the needle, which passes through the heated tank via small holes. After thermal equilibration, the sample is applied directly to the grid without any additional handling, thereby preserving its temperature.

Approximately 4 µL of Tt_70S aliquot was applied to glow-discharged, carbon-coated copper grids preequilibrated to the desired temperatures (4, 37, and 45°C) within the chamber. Different from Tt_70S, 4 µL of GmaS aliquot was applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids under either 4 or 40 °C. MET1 samples (protein provided by Prof. Jiamu Du from Southern University of Science and Technology) were prepared at 4, 20, and 30°C. All kinds of samples were kept on grids for 30 s prior to the blotting step. Blotting time was inversely proportional to target temperatures and optimized to prevent the sample dehydration. At elevated temperatures (≥37°C), blotting time was further minimized to avoid rapid solvent evaporation. Immediately following blotting, grids were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane and stored in liquid nitrogen for subsequent cryo-EM imaging.

Cryo-ET data collection of Tt_70S and the tomography reconstruction

Cryo-ET data collection on Tt_70S were acquired using a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operating at 300 kV, equipped with a K2 Summit direct electron detector and a Quantum energy filter (Gatan, USA). Data collection was performed at magnifications corresponding to the pixel size of 1.77 Å.

Tomographic series were collected over a tilt range from −60° to +60° using a dose-symmetric scheme (54) implemented in SerialEM (55), with a total dose of 100 e−/Å2 per tilt series. Image stacks were motion-corrected using MotionCor2 (56) and CTF-estimated with CTFFIND4 (57). Tomographic reconstruction was performed in IMOD (58) and AreTomo (59).

Single-particle data collection

All cryo-EM grids were initially screened in a low-dose mode using a Talos L120C transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific). High-quality grids were selected for high-resolution data collection under all experimental conditions on a Titan Krios G3i microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K3 BioQuantum direct electron detector (Gatan).

Movies were collected using EPU (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a total dose of 50 e−/Å2 fractionated into 50 frames, at a defocus range of −1.2 to −1.6 μm. Data were acquired in a super-resolution mode. Details of movies collected for Tt_70S, GmaS, and MET1 under varying temperatures are listed in Figs. S3, S12, and S15, and Tables S1–S3.

Single-particle data processing of Tt_70S

Particle prescreening for each condition

A unified image processing strategy was applied to all conditions in cryoSPARC 4.0.3 (13); a workflow schematic is provided in Fig. S3. Briefly, dose-fractionated movies were motion-corrected and dose-weighted using MotionCor2, followed by CTF estimation with CTFFIND4 in RELION 3.0.8 (60). Micrographs exhibiting maximum estimated resolutions better than 5 Å were subjected to template-based particle picking in cryoSPARC 4.0.3 (13). Initial particle selections underwent reference-free 2D classification to exclude nonribosomal contaminants or dissociated ribosomes. Particles under each condition were subsequently refined heterogeneously against three low-pass filtered (40 Å) reference structures: the intact 70S ribosome, the 50S ribosomal subunit, and the 30S ribosomal subunit. Only intact 70S complexes exhibiting the discernible 30S subunit density were retained for further 3D refinement with global and local per-particle CTF refinements. All final reconstructions were postprocessed in cryoSPARC (including B-factor sharpening and low-pass filtering), with resolutions determined using the gold-standard FSC with the criteria of 0.143. Particles belonging to each condition were pooled for further structural comparison.

For optimal structural comparison, 30,000 particles per condition were randomly selected from their respective pool and subjected to 3D refinements.

Dataset normalized merging

Three composite datasets (4 + 37 °C, 4 + 45 °C, and 4 + 37 + 45 °C) were generated, each containing 30,000 particles. These were formed by merging randomly selected subsets of 10,000 or 15,000 particles from the original conditions, as needed, to reach the target total. The normalized datasets were directly subjected to homogeneous 3D refinement in cryoSPARC to obtain the final reconstruction.

Particle rebalancing

To reduce orientation bias in single-temperature or merged multitemperature datasets, we developed an offline particle selection tool, SPA_rebalance, which performs layer-wise sampling of particles according to their 3D viewing directions. The input to the procedure was particle metadata after 3D refinement, containing assigned 3D orientations from either cryoSPARC.cs files or RELION STAR files, together with a user-defined target particle number, N. For cryoSPARC outputs, 3D poses were read from alignments 3D/pose, converted to rotation matrices and viewing directions, and subsequently expressed as azimuth and elevation angles. For RELION STAR files, the corresponding Euler angle metadata were used to derive or merge the particle orientation information. Each particle was assigned a source identifier and its original row index, allowing particles from multiple temperature conditions or datasets to be concatenated while preserving traceability to the original dataset and metadata row.

Particle viewing directions were mapped onto the unit sphere, which was tessellated into equal-area pixels using HEALPix (61). In this study, we used nside = 29, yielding 12 × nside2 = 10,092 spherical pixels. Although Mollweide projection combined with HEALPix pixelization was used for visualization of orientation distributions, particle selection itself was performed directly on the spherical HEALPix pixels rather than on the 2D projected map.

After assigning each particle to a HEALPix pixel, particles were selected using a layer-wise round-robin strategy. In each layer, every nonempty pixel contributed at most one randomly selected particle without replacement. The procedure then proceeded to the next layer, drawing again from pixels that still contained unselected particles. Iterative layer-wise sampling continued until the target number of particles, N, was reached or until all available particles had been exhausted. For merged multitemperature datasets, particle lists were first concatenated with their source identifiers, original row indices and HEALPix pixel indices retained, and the same global spherical sampling procedure was then applied to the composite dataset. This strategy reduces the disproportionate contribution of overrepresented viewing directions while retaining particles from sparsely populated angular regions. The resulting rebalanced particle subsets were imported into cryoSPARC and subjected directly to homogeneous 3D refinement or other downstream 3D reconstruction procedures.

Single-particle data processing of GmaS

Single-particle analyses on GmaS_4°C and GmaS_40°C were processed following the similar workflow as Tt_70S. Briefly, all movies were subjected to MotionCor2 and CTF estimation (CTFFIND4) in RELION. Particle picking was performed in cryoSPARC, followed by two rounds of reference-free 2D classification to remove poorly defined particles and aggregates. The resulting particles from each sample were subsequently processed through heterogeneous refinement in cryoSPARC using three ab initio models with the D6 symmetry applied. A well-defined subset of particles was selected and used for consensus 3D refinement under the D6 symmetry. For both reconstructions, global and local CTF refinements were carried out, and local resolution was estimated. Detailed data processing parameters for GmaS are listed in Fig. S12 and Table S2.

Single-particle data processing of MET1

Single-particle cryo-EM data processing of MET1 prepared at different vitrification temperatures was performed using a comparable workflow for the MET1_4°C, MET1_20°C and MET1_30°C datasets. Briefly, raw movies from each dataset were first subjected to motion correction, followed by CTF estimation using standard cryo-EM processing packages. Particles were initially selected by template-based picking and subsequently cleaned by two rounds of reference-free 2D classification to remove contaminants, aggregates, poorly defined particles, and low-quality classes. The remaining particles from each temperature condition were then subjected to heterogeneous refinement to further separate particle populations with different structural quality or conformational features. Well-defined particle subsets were selected for subsequent 3D refinement. To enable a fair comparison of reconstruction quality among the three temperature conditions, the final 3D refinement for each dataset was performed using a randomly standardized subset of 20,000 particles. Local resolution estimation was then carried out to assess the spatial variation in map quality. Detailed data processing statistics are summarized in Figs. S14–S16 and Table S3.

Volume map and local resolution visualization

Cryo-EM density maps of Tt_70S, GmaS, or MET1 and their local resolution estimates were visualized at consistent contour levels using UCSF ChimeraX (62). Local resolution was determined by a windowed FSC approach, analogous to the blocres method (63).

Tomograms were visualized and analyzed using IMOD.

3D FSC and FSO analyses

Directional resolution analysis was performed on Tt_70S and GmaS using the 3D FSC method (11), which computes a volumetric map where each voxel represents the resolution based on directional FSC curves. The isosurface at the 0.143 cutoff was used to visualize directional resolution anisotropy. FSO analysis was further applied to evaluate global resolution anisotropy and assess the consistency between particle orientation distribution and reconstructed resolution (64). Both analyses were implemented and visualized using custom scripts within a Jupyter notebook environment, utilizing the original 3D FSC (https://github.com/LyumkisLab/3DFSC) and FSO (https://github.com/Vilax/FSO) software packages.

Per-angle-directional FSC distribution analyses

Directional FSC analysis per angular sampling was performed on Tt_70S based on the 3DFSC volume generated for each sample. To ensure uniform sampling coverage over the spherical surface, 100 detection points were distributed using a Fibonacci sphere algorithm, which provides a more even point density compared with conventional lattice-based methods that often result in oversampling at the poles and undersampling at the equator. All computations and visualizations were carried out within a Jupyter notebook environment.

Map-to-model FSC validation

Map-to-model FSC curves at the 0.5 criterion were calculated for GmaS at 4 and 40 °C using the Mtriagetool from the Phenix package (65, 66). For each sample, a model was built independently and compared with its corresponding reconstruction. The calculations were performed with a mask. The previously published structure (PDB ID 7CQL) was used as a reference model during validation.

Cryo-EM modeling with CryoNet

To quantitatively evaluate the comparative ease of model building and main-chain/sidechain identification in the high-resolution structures of GmaS obtained at 4 and 40°C, we employed the deep learning–based modeling tool CryoNet (46) via its publicly accessible server (https://cryonet.ai/build/). Using the “Build Model for High-Resolution Cryo-EM Density Maps without Sequence” M2 workflow, we generated de novo structural models and analyzed the distribution of main-chain positioning scores and the continuity of high-scoring main-chain regions. This approach allowed us to assess whether the structural features derived from the GmaS_4°C and GmaS_40°C datasets more accurately reflect biologically realistic protein conformations, as indicated by enhanced model integrity and plausibility.
