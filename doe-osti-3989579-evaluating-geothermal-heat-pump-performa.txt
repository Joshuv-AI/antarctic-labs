Executive Summary
This technical report provides an in-depth analysis of the potential impacts of geothermal heat
pumps (GHPs) on the U.S. residential and commercial building stock and highlights their
potential to deliver efficient, reliable, and affordable energy solutions nationwide. Space heating
is the largest energy consumer in the buildings sector, accounting for 43% of site energy in the
residential sector [1] and 32% in the commercial sector [2]. GHPs utilize naturally stable ground
temperatures to transfer thermal energy, enabling high-efficiency heating and cooling. During
the winter, GHPs extract heat from the ground and use it to heat buildings, while during summer,
buildings are cooled by rejecting heat to the ground.

The report synthesizes findings from a three-year effort that integrated advanced GHP modeling
methodologies into ResStock™ and ComStock™, which are highly granular, bottom-up models
that estimate subhourly energy consumption of the U.S. building stock. The GHP modeling
workflows leverage GHEDesigner, a Python package for designing ground heat exchangers [3].
This report provides key insights into the potential energy, peak demand, utility bill, and
emissions impacts of widespread GHP adoption in the U.S. building stock. In addition, this
report examines the comparative performance of GHPs and air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) to
evaluate the national and regional impacts of each technology and highlight conditions under
which GHPs offer greater efficiency benefits. Finally, this report analyzes how coupling GHPs
with building envelope improvements can not only offer additional savings but also reduce GHP
system sizing and installation costs. The analysis presented in this report represents the impacts
of hypothetical mass adoption of GHPs in the building stock, which entails converting a large
portion of space heating energy to electricity, and in some dwellings, the addition of space
cooling where it was not previously present.

Geothermal Heat Pump Results
In ResStock, three residential GHP performance levels are modeled and presented in this report:
(1) “Single-Stage GHP,” (2) “Two-Stage GHP,” and (3) “Variable-Speed GHP.” Each residential
GHP upgrade is applicable to 70.3% of dwellings modeled in ResStock. In ComStock, three
commercial GHP configurations are modeled: (1) “Packaged GHP” for buildings with rooftop
unit heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems, (2) “Hydronic GHP” for
buildings with boiler/chiller systems, and (3) “Console GHP” for buildings with ductless HVAC
systems. The applicability criteria of these three GHP configurations are mutually exclusive, and
the three configurations collectively apply to 86.7% of ComStock floor area. The combination of
all three GHP configurations is represented in ComStock as a measure package called the
“Comprehensive GHP” package, which will be referenced throughout this report.

The GHP upgrades simulated in ResStock and ComStock are intended to represent a mass
adoption scenario (i.e., adoption in all eligible buildings); however, some buildings are excluded
due to retrofit incompatibility with the existing HVAC system (discussed in Sections 1.3.2 and
1.3.3). ResStock represents the full U.S. residential building stock, with 70.3% of dwelling units
eligible for a GHP upgrade. ComStock represents approximately 63% of total U.S. commercial
floor area, with 86.7% of the ComStock floor area eligible for a GHP upgrade. This means that
the GHPs modeled in ComStock apply to approximately 55% of the full commercial building

Energy Impacts
Relative to existing HVAC systems, the residential GHP upgrades demonstrate between 2.8
quads (Single-Stage GHP) and 4.2 quads (Variable-Speed GHP) of national site energy savings
potential (Figure 5), while the combination of the three commercial GHP upgrades demonstrates
1.3 quads of savings potential (Figure 10). Combining both sectors, the GHPs modeled in this
study represent a total of 5.5 quads of national annual site energy savings potential. Median
building-level site energy savings range from 35% to 55% in colder states and 20% to 40% in
warmer states (Figure 6 and Figure 11), highlighting the strong performance of GHPs in cold
climates due to stable year-round ground temperatures. Many colder states experience increases
in annual electricity usage due to the shift to electric space heating from traditional heating
systems (Figure 7 and Figure 12). However, GHPs are three to four times more efficient than
traditional combustion heating systems, leading to high overall site energy savings.

Peak Impacts
This report analyzes the impacts of GHPs on state-wide summer and winter peak electricity
demand across the United States. The residential Variable-Speed GHP reduces summer peak
demand in the residential sector in all states except Vermont (Figure 16), while commercial
GHPs reduce summer peak demand in every state (Figure 22). Residential and commercial
summer peaks are reduced by 30%–40% across most states. However, winter peak demand
increases in many northern states due to the shift from traditional heating systems to GHPs
(Figure 19 and Figure 25). The coldest, most heating-dominated states experience winter
peak increases (in some cases 100% or higher), while warmer southern states experience
modest winter peak reductions (typically up to 20%). In the residential sector, 5 states
experience an absolute peak shift from summer to winter, and 30 states experience an increase in
absolute annual peak, regardless of season (Table B-1). In the commercial sector, 38 states
experience an absolute peak shift from summer to winter, and 17 states experience an increase in
absolute annual peak, regardless of season (Table B-2).

Utility Bill Impacts
Residential GHPs demonstrate median per-dwelling annual bill savings ranging from $87
(Single-Stage GHP) to $600 (Variable-Speed GHP) (Figure 27). Utility bill savings are highly
dependent on climate, dwelling size, and whether the existing dwelling was fully conditioned
prior to the GHP upgrade. Commercial GHPs demonstrate normalized annual utility bill savings
of $0.50–$1.00 per square foot (Figure 32) for most buildings. A normalized savings value is
used for commercial buildings due to the range of building types and sizes. Across the United
States, GHPs demonstrate annual bill savings potential of $65 billion for residential buildings
(Figure 27) and $27 billion for commercial buildings (Figure 31) using 2022–2023 electricity
rates.

Emissions Impacts
Residential GHPs show annual avoided emissions of 205–298 million metric tons (MMT) of
carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e) (Figure 39), while commercial GHPs show 75 MMT CO2e

(Figure 42). Combined, GHPs can achieve a national emissions reduction potential of 373
MMT CO2e under the Long-Run Marginal Emissions Rates (LRMER) Mid Case 15 Cambium
grid scenario. This report presents emissions results for several grid scenarios, projecting GHP
impacts under varying future grid compositions. Northern states with high heating demand
and/or reliance on fossil fuels see the largest emissions reductions, with some states reaching
nearly 50% emissions reductions under the LRMER Mid Case 15 scenario (Figure 41 and Figure
44).

Geothermal Heat Pump and Air-Source Heat Pump Results
Throughout the report, GHP performance for a subset of modeled GHP measures is compared
with the performance of analogous ASHPs modeled in ResStock and ComStock. The residential
Variable-Speed GHP is compared with the residential variable-speed Cold-Climate ASHP. The
commercial GHP system for rooftop units (RTUs) is compared with an advanced performance
ASHP RTU. As is common in practice, the residential and commercial ASHP systems are
modeled with electric resistance backup heat, while the GHP systems are not modeled with any
backup heat. When results are presented comparing GHPs and ASHPs, the comparisons only
include the subset of buildings that were eligible for both technologies to ensure consistency.

In residential buildings, the Variable-Speed GHP demonstrates consistently stronger
performance than the variable-speed Cold-Climate ASHP in terms of energy efficiency,
peak electricity demand reduction, utility bill savings, emissions reductions, and electric
panel constraints. Across the residential stock, the Variable-Speed GHP saves 15% (1.3 quads)
more site energy than the Cold-Climate ASHP on an annual basis (Figure 45). The Variable-
Speed GHP results in state-wide residential sector summer peak reductions across the United
States, while the Cold-Climate ASHP increases summer peak in many states (Figure 57). The
addition of space cooling to dwelling units that previously lacked central air conditioning
increases summer demand; however, the cooling efficiency of the Variable-Speed GHP
consistently outweighs this increased cooling load, which is not the case in all states for the
Cold-Climate ASHP. During winter, both the GHP and the ASHP result in winter peak increases
in most cold and temperate states due to the switch to electric heating in many buildings (Figure
58). However, the Cold-Climate ASHP shows a winter peak increase with a magnitude two to
three times greater than that of the Variable-Speed GHP. All states exhibit median annual bill
savings with the GHP, while the ASHP results in median annual bill increases in cold-climate
states, driven by lower efficiency during very cold temperatures compared to the GHP and often
higher electric heating costs compared to an existing natural gas system (Figure 67). The
Variable-Speed GHP reduces annual emissions across all fuels by 55–83 MMT CO2e (9%–11%)
more than the Cold-Climate ASHP, depending on the grid scenario selected (Figure 73). Finally,
this analysis shows that dwelling units in all states are roughly 10%–30% more likely to face an
electric panel space or capacity constraint when installing a Cold-Climate ASHP versus a
Variable-Speed GHP (Figure 71 and Figure 72).

In commercial buildings, the Packaged GHP demonstrates consistently stronger
performance than the Advanced Performance ASHP RTU in terms of energy efficiency,
peak electricity demand reduction, utility bill savings, and emissions reductions. The
Packaged GHP saves 4% (95 TBtu) more site energy than the Advanced Performance ASHP on
an annual basis (Figure 50). Both the GHP and ASHP reduce state-wide commercial sector

 viii

summer peaks, with the Packaged GHP resulting in higher summer peak reductions in all states
(Figure 63). In winter, colder states see peak increases with the introduction of fully electric
heating systems; however, the GHP mitigates the severity of the winter peak increases in all
states compared with the ASHP (Figure 64). The Packaged GHP shows higher utility bill savings
across all commercial building types (Figure 70) and national annual bill savings potential of $5–
$6 billion (8%–9%) greater than the Advanced Performance ASHP (Figure 69). Finally, the
Packaged GHP demonstrates 3–9 MMT CO2e (3%–4%) higher annual emissions reductions than
the Advanced Performance ASHP, depending on the grid scenario selected (Figure 74).

Geothermal Heat Pump With Envelope Improvements Results
The final analysis section in this report evaluates the benefits of pairing envelope upgrade
measures with GHPs, including impacts on energy use, system sizing, peak demand, utility bills,
and emissions. The residential GHP upgrades are paired with air sealing, duct sealing and
insulation, and attic insulation. The commercial GHP upgrades are paired with improved wall
insulation, improved roof insulation, and new windows. When results are presented comparing
the GHP and GHP + Envelope packages, the comparisons only include buildings that received
both a GHP and one or more envelope upgrades.

In both residential and commercial buildings, the pairing of envelope upgrade measures
with GHPs results in additional energy, utility bill, and emissions savings, while also
reducing heat pump and ground heat exchanger size requirements and mitigating the
severity of winter electricity peaks. The residential GHP + Envelope packages achieve an
additional 0.4–0.5 quads in annual site energy savings compared to the GHP-only scenarios
(Figure 75), while the commercial GHP + Envelope package achieves an additional 0.2 quads
(Figure 78) compared to the GHP-only scenario. In both residential and commercial buildings,
the envelope measures contribute an additional 3%–8% in site energy savings, with the highest
impact observed in colder states (Figure 76 and Figure 79). Ground heat exchanger requirements,
including borefield size and ground heat exchanger length, are reduced by 25% to 33% for
residential buildings and 20% to 25% for commercial buildings when GHPs are paired with
envelope improvements (Figure 81 and Figure 83). GHP capacities decrease by approximately
25% for residential buildings (Figure 82) and 15% for commercial buildings (Figure 84) as a
result of decreased heating and cooling loads. By reducing the required heat pump capacity and
corresponding borefield size, envelope improvements paired with GHPs can lower drilling costs,
installation time, and equipment costs, which are factors that can pose barriers to GHP
deployment. When evaluating peak demand impacts, the GHP + Envelope packages provide
additional summer peak reductions of up to 10% beyond the standalone GHPs (Figure 85 and
Figure 88). More importantly, winter peak increases are lessened by 25% or more, and in some
states are avoided altogether when GHPs are paired with envelope improvements, alleviating
winter peaking concerns when converting to fully electric heating systems (Figure 86 and Figure
89). Nationwide, the envelope improvements yield an additional $18–$21 billion in residential
utility bill savings (Figure 91) and $6–$8 billion in commercial utility bill savings (Figure 92).
Finally, the envelope measures achieve additional emissions reductions of 24–39 MMT CO2e in
the residential sector (Figure 95) and 10–23 MMT CO2e in the commercial sector (Figure 96).

Introduction
As the United States pursues reliable and affordable energy for space conditioning, geothermal
heat pumps (GHPs) have emerged as a promising option due to their high system efficiency. In
addition, GHPs can provide stable performance across varying seasonal and climatic conditions
due to consistent ground temperatures. This paper addresses how GHPs could contribute to the
national energy landscape in the United States, and how this technology compares to other
technologies in terms of energy, peak, utility bill, and emissions savings.

The primary intended audience of this paper includes utilities, independent system operators
(ISOs), state energy offices, and program designers involved in energy efficiency, grid
reliability, and affordability. The paper provides a comprehensive data analysis of the national
and regional energy savings potential, peak load impacts, utility bill savings, and emissions
reductions of GHPs. The level of technical detail ensures that the findings are accessible to
professionals with varying levels of expertise while offering in-depth insights to support
informed decision-making. For readers interested in additional technical details or looking for
finer geographic resolution, please explore the ResStock™ and ComStock™ public data releases
for additional metadata not utilized in this analysis [4]. In addition, an interactive dashboard 1
with the ResStock and ComStock GHP results is available on the National Laboratory of the
Rockies’ (NLR) Building Stock Analysis Tableau Public site [5].

GHPs are a technology with the potential to contribute to national energy reliability and
affordability goals. Unlike traditional heating and cooling systems, GHPs transfer heat between
buildings and the Earth. The undisturbed ground temperature beneath the Earth’s surface remains
relatively stable year-round at approximately 50°–55°F, which is generally warmer than the
outdoor air in winter and cooler than the outdoor air in summer. This allows GHPs to operate
efficiently year-round, independent of outdoor air temperature. As a result, GHPs typically
achieve higher annual efficiency than ASHPs due to their ability to operate efficiently during
periods of extreme outdoor temperatures. GHPs offer strong potential to reduce stress on the
electricity grid and lower energy and maintenance costs. While GHPs and ASHPs are both
highly efficient and viable technologies for energy efficiency, this paper examines the unique
strengths of GHPs in various contexts, such as their impact on regional energy consumption,
electric peak reductions, utility bills, and emissions reductions. By highlighting these aspects, the
paper underscores how GHPs can complement other technologies like ASHPs in the broader
energy landscape.

The adoption of GHPs is influenced by a range of market factors at both the national and state
levels. Current incentives, regulations, upfront costs, and other barriers play a crucial role in
determining the extent to which GHPs can be cost-effectively deployed. As of 2020, the United
States had an installed capacity of 20.7 gigawatts-thermal (GWth) of GHPs, making up
approximately 1% of the U.S. HVAC market [6]. The nationwide technical potential for
geothermal heating and cooling is estimated to be the equivalent of 28 million households by
2050 [7], indicating that there is notable growth opportunity for this technology. Lund and Boyd

1 Methodology
1.1 What Are ResStock and ComStock?
ResStock and ComStock are highly granular, bottom-up models that use multiple data sources,
statistical sampling methods, and advanced building energy simulations to estimate the annual
subhourly energy consumption of the building stock across the United States [11] [12]. The
simulations are performed using U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) energy modeling tools,
OpenStudio® and EnergyPlus®. ResStock and ComStock are developed and maintained by NLR.

The residential (ResStock) dataset represents dwelling units in the United States, including
single-family, multifamily (including high-rise multifamily), and mobile homes. ResStock
represents nearly the full U.S. residential building stock, with the exception of dormitories,
prisons, assisted care facilities, or other congregate housing situations. The commercial
(ComStock) dataset represents 15 common commercial building types—small office, medium
office, large office, retail, strip mall, grocery, warehouse, primary school, secondary school, full-
service restaurant, quick-service restaurant, small hotel, large hotel, hospital, and outpatient—
which comprise about 63% of the commercial sector floor area in the United States according to
the Commercial Building Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS) [13].

Both ResStock and ComStock generate building models by sampling probability distributions of
building and occupant characteristics based on datasets of their respective building stock sector.
ResStock distributions are derived from sources such as the U.S. Energy Information
Administration’s (EIA) Residential Energy Consumption Survey (RECS) and U.S. Census data.
ComStock distributions are derived from a number of public and private datasets, including
CoStar, CBECS, Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD), DOE prototype
building models, and other sources. See the ResStock [14] and ComStock [15] technical
documentation for further explanation of model assumptions, data sources, and other details.

ResStock and ComStock answer two questions: (1) How is energy used in the U.S. building
stock? and (2) What are the impacts of energy-saving technologies? The ResStock and
ComStock datasets identify where energy is being consumed geographically, in what building
types and by what end uses, and at what times of day. Simultaneously, the models estimate the
impact of efficiency measures: how much energy do measures save; where, or in what use cases
do measures save energy; when, or at what time of day and year do savings occur; and which
building stock segments have the biggest savings potential. The sources of the data used in this
report are the public dataset releases:

 • ResStock Standard Data Release: 2025 Release 1—2018 Weather. For reference
 documentation, please see ResStock Reference Documentation [14].
 • ComStock Standard Data Release: 2025 Release 3—2018 Weather. For reference
 documentation, please see ComStock Reference Documentation [15].

 • Link to raw data: Open Data Energy Initiative 2 [4].

1.2 What Is Standard Data Release?
The Standard Data Release (SDR) effort considers the “what ifs” to help examine the potential of
energy efficiency measures in residential and commercial buildings across the country. This
work builds on a multiyear effort to calibrate and validate DOE’s ResStock and ComStock
models [16]. The goal of the SDR effort is to develop end-use load shapes that represent a
variety of energy efficiency and demand flexibility measure scenarios. The national datasets
produced empower analysts working for federal, state, utility, city, and manufacturer
stakeholders to answer a broad range of analysis questions.
New datasets are released one to two times per year and include new upgrade measures as well
as updates to the ResStock and ComStock baseline models. Note that each new dataset release
includes all existing measures from previous releases. New measures for each cycle are
determined based on feedback from internal and external stakeholders and users of the datasets.
Measures cover a wide range of end uses and technologies, including HVAC, lighting, interior
equipment, water heating, demand flexibility, and more. Measures developed and released
through the SDR datasets follow a rigorous process that includes research and literature review,
measure development and testing, thorough documentation of assumptions and results, and
multiple rounds of reviews by internal subject matter experts to ensure that the technologies are
being represented as accurately as possible and the results are reasonable and trustworthy.

1.3 Description of Geothermal Heat Pump Modeling
ResStock and ComStock were used to model GHPs as upgrade measure scenarios across the U.S.
building stock to analyze the energy, peak, utility bill, and emissions impacts of the technologies.
The specific modeling methods and assumptions will be described briefly in this section but are
described in greater detail in the published measure documentation technical reports. The robust
SDR measure development workflow was followed to ensure modeling assumptions and
methodologies were appropriate and results were reasonable. A Technical Advisory Group
(TAG) of 15 industry, government, and academia members were consulted throughout the
modeling process to provide feedback and real-world expertise about the GHP configurations
selected and the assumptions made.

1.3.1 Ground Property and Borefield Assumptions
In order to properly model the performance of a GHP system, both the ResStock and ComStock
simulations require model assumptions related to ground and soil conditions and borefield
properties. This includes soil and grout conductivity, undisturbed ground temperature, and a
variety of borefield assumptions that contribute to the performance of the ground heat exchanger.
The ResStock and ComStock development teams worked closely with the TAG and other GHP
projects at NLR to ensure that default values and inputs are realistic and aligned as closely as
possible across modeling efforts.

Both the residential and commercial heat pump upgrade measures leverage GHEDesigner, a
Python package for designing ground heat exchangers used with GHP systems [3]. Accurately
modeling GHPs in ResStock and ComStock requires complex calculations for ground heat
exchanger performance and borefield sizing that cannot be done using the built-in functionalities
of EnergyPlus. GHEDesigner is designed specifically to perform these functions and has been
developed and maintained over several years by Oklahoma State University, Oak Ridge National
Laboratory, and NLR. The residential modeling workflow pre-simulates a fixed set of borefield
configurations using GHEDesigner and then uses lookup tables during each building model
simulation to select the most appropriate design. The commercial modeling workflow involves
running GHEDesigner to size the borefield for each model during the ComStock run. ResStock
uses a simplified lookup table approach due to the much smaller range of expected borefield
sizes for a residential GHP system. GHEDesigner’s flexible and highly automated nature allows
for ResStock and ComStock to utilize GHEDesigner mid-simulation and calculate the
appropriate ground heat exchanger properties and temperature response functions (known as g-
functions) required for GHP modeling. More detail about the GHEDesigner process is described
in the individual measure documentation links referenced in subsequent sections.

 • Borehole diameter: 0.15 m
 • Pipe pressure rating: Standard dimension ratio 11 (SDR-11)
 • Pipe conductivity: 0.4–0.7 W/m-K

1.3.2 Residential Geothermal Heat Pump Modeling Overview
Residential-scale GHPs can come in various types (water-to-air or water-to-water) and sizes
(typically 1 to 6 tons, with 3-ton units being most common [19]) and utilize various ground heat
exchanger configurations (vertical loop, horizontal loop, or surface water). The residential
modeling team performed a literature review to characterize the state of the technology, such as
installation types, locations, and performance levels in the current market. The TAG was also
consulted to give real-world insight into residential GHP installations. Ultimately, three water-to-
air residential GHP performance levels were chosen to be modeled for this analysis: Single-Stage
GHP, Two-Stage GHP, and Variable-Speed GHP. Some technical details about each GHP
performance level modeled are included in subsequent sections, and full modeling details are
available in the published technical measure documentation linked below.

 5

Note that there are some limitations in the residential GHP and ASHP modeling. One limitation
of modeling ducted GHPs and ASHPs in ResStock is that ducts will be resized during simulation
to accommodate the new airflow requirements of the heat pump system. This may not accurately
reflect a retrofit that maintains the building’s existing ducts. A second limitation is OpenStudio-
HPXML and ResStock model individual multifamily dwelling units as opposed to whole
building models. As such, the borefield sizing is performed for only a single multifamily unit,
when in reality a borefield for a multifamily building would be sized for the entire building.
Therefore, there are performance and economic benefits for large multifamily buildings that are
not captured by this individual-unit modeling approach. Multifamily units represent roughly 26%
of the residential building stock. Third, the ground heat exchanger sizing calculations are based
on performance during a single year. In cases where there are severely unbalanced heating and
cooling loads, the ground may warm or cool over time. The changes in ground temperature over
time may reduce the effectiveness of the ground heat exchanger, but ResStock does not capture
long-term thermal performance degradation.

Applicability Description: These measures apply to all dwellings that have either (1) non-
shared (in-unit) HVAC systems with heating supplied by electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, or
propane, or (2) shared (across multiple units) HVAC systems in buildings under eight stories
with the same allowable heating fuels. An eight-story threshold was selected because distributed
GHP systems become increasingly challenging to install in high-rise residential buildings due to
site space and land area constraints. In addition, the ResStock GHP modeling limitations
discussed in the previous paragraph make accurate representation of large multifamily GHP
systems challenging. Eligible dwellings must also have ducted distribution systems and be
located on sites with soil thermal conductivity greater than or equal to 1.4 W/m-K.
Approximately 7.6% of units have soil thermal conductivity below this threshold based on the
data used [17], therefore making them ineligible for the GHP upgrade. This thermal conductivity
threshold was established due to limitations in EnergyPlus modeling of ground heat exchanger
performance under these conditions, which can result in non-convergent or non-physical heat
transfer behavior. In practice, GHP installations in very low soil conductivity conditions are
feasible; however, ground heat exchanger performance may be reduced and larger, more
expensive borefields may be required to maintain system efficiency. A map of median ResStock
soil conductivity values by county is shown in Figure A-6 in Appendix A [17]. Low soil
conductivity values most commonly occur in dwellings located in Mountain West and West
Coast states. Note that the soil conductivity dataset used captures surface thermal conductivity
values and is based on sampled locations across the United States; therefore, there are some
intrinsic uncertainties associated with the soil conductivity distributions.

1.3.2.3 Residential Variable-Speed Geothermal Heat Pump
Technical Description: The Variable-Speed GHP has an EER of 30.9, a rated COP of 4.4, and a
variable-speed compressor. The variable-speed operation improves part-load efficiency and
reduces short-cycling, which helps maintain stable supply air and indoor temperatures, especially
during periods of low or moderate cooling demand. The heat pump is sized using ACCA Manual
J design load calculations to meet the greater of the heating or cooling peak load under design
conditions. There is no backup system present, and any dwelling that receives the GHP will use

1.3.3 Commercial Geothermal Heat Pump Modeling Overview
Commercial buildings have a wide range of HVAC system configurations, making retrofit cost
and complexity heavily reliant on the existing system type. Commercial-scale GHPs can come in
several types and sizes, namely water-to-air GHPs (typically 0.5 to 30 tons of heating/cooling
capacity [26] [27]) or water-to-water GHPs (typically 5 to 100 tons of heating/cooling capacity

1.4.1.1 Residential Cold-Climate Air-Source Heat Pump with Electric Backup
Technical Description: The Cold-Climate ASHP is modeled with a variable-speed compressor
and electric resistance backup heating. Like the GHPs modeled, it is sized using ACCA Manual J
design load calculations to meet the greater of the heating or cooling peak load under design
conditions. This maximum-load sizing method enables full space conditioning across all eligible
dwellings, including those previously partially or fully unconditioned. The ASHP is represented
as a cold-climate, variable-speed system (17.5 seasonal energy efficiency ratio 2 [SEER2] and
8.5 heating seasonal performance factor [HSPF2], max speed heating COP of 3.4 at 47°F), which
enables modulation of compressor capacity under part-load conditions. This variable-speed
operation improves part-load efficiency and reduces short-cycling, which helps maintain indoor
temperatures and humidity, especially during periods of low or moderate cooling demand.
Unlike the GHP, the Cold-Climate ASHP is modeled with electric resistance backup heat and a
compressor lockout temperature of −15°F. Backup heat is utilized when the ASHP cannot meet
the heating load on its own; when the outdoor temperature drops below the lockout temperature,
the heat pump is disabled and the system transitions fully to backup heat.

1.5 Description of Geothermal Heat Pump + Envelope Packages
In addition to the standalone GHP measures, ResStock and ComStock ran several packages that
pair GHPs with building envelope improvements. The purpose of these packages is to analyze
how additional energy or cost savings can be realized if the building envelope is improved prior
to installing the heat pump. Airtightness, insulation, and other envelope efficiency features
influence how well the building maintains internal temperature, which in turn determines how
large the HVAC system needs to be and how much it needs to operate. Envelope improvements
can also impact the necessity for electrical panel upgrades when upgrading to a fully electric
HVAC system. The impact of the envelope measures on heat pump sizing, ground heat
exchanger sizing, and electrical panel capacity will all be explored later in this report.

The envelope measures selected are intended to represent common retrofit strategies for each
building sector. The residential envelope package is considered a light-touch retrofit that
improves airtightness, duct sealing and insulation, and attic insulation. The commercial envelope
package is a more comprehensive, high-efficiency retrofit that replaces windows and improves
wall and roof insulation. One of the commercial packages also includes replacing lighting with
light-emitting diodes (LEDs). While lighting is not part of the building envelope, it affects
internal heat gains, which impact the operational heating and cooling consumption throughout
the year. Therefore, replacing lighting with a higher-efficiency technology reduces internal heat
gains and can thereby reduce the design cooling load and operational cooling energy
consumption of the GHP system. However, this reduction in internal gains does not increase the
design heating load or HVAC system size, as lighting is assumed to be off during heating load
sizing calculations. The subsequent sections describe the envelope package assumptions in more
detail and reference the relevant measure documentation.

1.6 Utility Bill Methodology
Both ResStock and ComStock incorporate utility rates into their models to estimate utility bill
savings from the implementation of upgrade measures. The utility bill methodologies vary
between ResStock and ComStock and will therefore be discussed separately in the subsequent
sections.

For all utility bill calculations, the same set of rates and rate structures are used across the
baseline and upgrade scenarios. In practice, large-scale fuel switching measures may lead
utilities and building owners to adopt alternative rate structures in response to changes in demand
profiles. These effects are not captured in the current ResStock and ComStock modeling
frameworks.

1.6.1 Residential Utility Bill Methodology
ResStock computes total energy bills for each dwelling unit according to state-specific
residential prices for electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, and propane. ResStock does not currently
include utility bills for other fuels such as wood or coal. These prices include both fixed charges
and volumetric rates for each modeled fuel. Although ResStock represents the building stock
circa 2018, the utility bill estimates reflect utility rates circa 2023, which was the most recent

 18

year of data available from EIA at the time of implementation. This choice was made because
most users of the data were assumed to prefer bills that most closely reflect the present for
decision-making.

The ResStock approach for electricity and natural gas is to use a single scenario, with both a flat
charge and a per-consumption charge. Data from the Utility Rate Database (URDB) [47] [48]
were used to calculate a nationwide customer-weighted average fixed monthly electricity charge
across all utilities in the database, which comes out to approximately $13.80 per customer per
month. Non-residential rates, time of use rates, rates older than 2015, and other categories of
rates were removed for the flat charge calculation. For natural gas, ResStock uses the American
Gas Association’s 2015 census-dependent customer charge [49], which ranges from $4.95 to
$14.60 per customer per month depending on the region for the fixed portion of the natural gas
bill.

ResStock additionally uses 2023 EIA state-level information on revenue, sales, and number of
customers to calculate the average per-consumption electricity and natural gas rate for each state
with the fixed charges removed. The per-consumption electricity annual average rates range
from $0.09 to $0.40 per kWh, and the per-consumption natural gas annual average rates range
from $0.76 to $4.84 per therm. The total electricity and natural gas bill for each ResStock
building model is then calculated by summing the flat charge and the per-consumption charge for
the state where the model is located. For example, a ResStock unit may have a flat electricity
charge of $13.80 per month ($165.60 per year) plus a per-consumption electricity rate of
$0.20/kWh. If this unit has a full-year electricity consumption of 3,000 kWh, the total electricity
bill would be $165.60 + (3,000 kWh * $0.20/kWh) = $765.60. The natural gas bill is calculated
similarly, using the natural gas flat rate charge for the corresponding census region, plus the per-
consumption natural gas charge for the state. If a unit has a natural gas HVAC system or
appliance, the natural gas flat charge is included each month even if the energy usage is zero. If
there are zero natural gas systems or appliances in the unit, the natural gas flat charge is not
included in the monthly energy bill.

The ResStock approach for propane and fuel oil (for all states except Alaska), is to use a single
scenario using weekly volumetric rate data from EIA [50], averaged from December 2024
through February 2025. When state-level data is not available, ResStock uses data from the
state’s Petroleum Administration for Defense Districts (PADD) region [51]. When PADD region
data are not available, ResStock uses U.S. national average values. The propane and fuel oil bills
are then calculated by multiplying the fuel consumption for each housing unit model by the
volumetric rate for that fuel and state. Alaska propane prices were taken from the Alaska
Housing Finance Corporation’s Fuel Survey [52], and fuel oil prices were taken from the Alaska
Fuel Price Report: Winter 2025 [53]. Note that natural gas and other fuel pricing is volatile and
dependent on global markets, and therefore this analysis may not capture this fluctuation in fuel
prices.

1.6.2 Commercial Utility Bill Methodology
ComStock estimates utility bills for electricity, natural gas, fuel oil, and propane. ComStock does
not currently include utility bills for district heating and cooling, which account for roughly 3%
of ComStock floor area. Although ComStock represents the building stock circa 2018, the utility
bill estimates reflect utility rates circa 2022, which was the most recent year of data available

 19

from EIA at the time of implementation. This choice was made because most users of the data
were assumed to prefer bills that most closely reflect the present for decision-making. For a
detailed explanation of the ComStock utility bill methodology, see the ComStock Reference
Documentation [15].

1.6.2.1 Electric Bills
The primary resource for the electric utility rates is the URDB [47] [48]. The URDB contains
electric rates that span all sectors (residential, commercial, industrial, etc.), so the rates were
filtered to only those applicable to commercial buildings, resulting in ~11,000 remaining rates
across 2,658 utilities. 90% of utilities have 8 or fewer rates in the database, while some have up
to 200 rates. The rates included in the database reflect a wide variety of rate structures. These
rates cover 85% of the floor area in ComStock. For the remaining 15% of ComStock buildings,
the annual bill is estimated using the 2022 EIA Form-861 [54] average prices by state. These
prices applied to the remaining 15% of buildings represent blended revenue per kWh and do not
reflect detailed rate structures or demand charges.

When each ComStock building model is simulated, the annual bills for all applicable rates are
calculated. To adjust for the lag in the rates on the URDB, a multiplier is applied to the annual
bill to reflect increase in rates year over year. The yearly rate increase was calculated (using a
pairwise analysis of utility-reported data to EIA) and applied by state, with the median increase
being 1%–3% per year. For most utility bill savings results presented in this report, the median
bill is used because it is unknown which of the applicable rates should be used for any given
building model.

1.6.2.2 Other Fuel Bills
For natural gas rates, no detailed database of rates was found, so natural gas pricing is assumed
to be volumetric. 2022 U.S. EIA Natural Gas Prices - Commercial Price [55] and U.S. EIA Heat
Content of Natural Gas Delivered to Consumers [56] were used to create an energy price in
dollars per kBtu. State-level annual average rates range from $0.007/kBtu in Idaho to
$0.048/kBtu in Hawaii, with a mean of $0.012/kBtu nationally.

For propane and fuel oil, no detailed database of rates was found so pricing for these fuels is also
assumed to be volumetric. 2022 U.S. EIA residential No. 2 Distillate Prices by Sales Type and
U.S. EIA residential Weekly Heating Oil and Propane Prices (October–March) [57] [50] were
used. Residential prices were used because commercial prices are only available on the national
scale. Additionally, most commercial buildings using these fuels are assumed to be smaller
buildings where a residential rate is likely realistic. For propane, prices range from $0.022/kBtu
in North Dakota to $0.052/kBtu in Florida, with a mean of $0.032/kBtu nationally. For fuel oil,
prices ranged from $0.027/kBtu in Nebraska to $0.036/kBtu in Delaware, with a mean of
$0.033/kBtu nationally. The mean national price for both fuels is roughly three times the mean
national price of natural gas.

No resources with utility rates for district heating and cooling were identified. Because there are
several hundred district systems across the United States, many of which are university or
healthcare campuses, gathering individual rates manually was deemed impractical. Therefore,
utility bills for these fuels are not calculated. Note that natural gas and other fuel pricing is

 20

volatile and dependent on global markets, and therefore this analysis may not capture this
fluctuation in fuel prices.

1.7 Electric Panel Methodology
ResStock integrates electric panel size prediction into its modeling framework to estimate panel
constraints resulting from upgrade measures. ComStock does not currently model electric panel
sizing, therefore discussion of electric panel impacts focuses exclusively on the residential
results.

This section summarizes the electrical information for the ducted heat pump upgrade. Electrical
information is used in ResStock to assess whether the dwelling unit’s electrical panel would
become space constrained or over capacity due to heat pump upgrades. The peak electric load
assumption (or the coincident equipment power used in the panel service-load calculation) for all
heat pump upgrades in ResStock is based off a regression of roughly 90 ASHP products without
electric backup heat. The same regression is applied to both the GHP and ASHP upgrades in this
study, as GHP-specific electric load assumptions are not currently available, which is a
limitation.

The power rating of a ducted heat pump system is the sum of the power rating of the outdoor
unit, the air handler, and any electric backup allowed to operate simultaneously, and is
summarized in Table 1. The heat pump compressor power rating is estimated based on its design
heating capacity using a regression equation derived from product labels. The air handler power
rating is estimated from the design airflow rate and the fan power assumption in OpenStudio-
HPXML. The electric resistance backup power rating is directly converted from its heating
capacity at an efficiency of 100%. For more information on the modeling assumptions, see the
Technical Reference Document [14].

Ducted heat pumps require at least one 208/240V circuit, each of which is protected by a double-
pole breaker. The specific number of circuits depends on whether the heat pump has backup
heating (fossil fuel, electric, or none) and how its indoor and outdoor units are configured along

handler on one circuit and another 240V circuit for the outdoor unit. For a heat pump with
electric backup, the air handler is commonly 240V to allow for the strip heat to be on the same
circuit. This is also the assumption in ResStock 2025 Release 1. The minimum circuit amperage
allowed per circuit is assumed to be up to 50A, corresponding to a 60A maximum overcurrent
protection device, based on a limited survey on ASHP catalogs. Therefore, more circuits may be

required for larger heat pump systems, which, in reality, correspond to the use of multiple
compressors and/or heat kits.

Although upgrading to a heat pump replaces both the heating and cooling systems, it may require
more electrical circuits and/or breaker space than what becomes available by removing the
existing HVAC equipment. The heat pump upgrade may also add more connected load to the
panel, especially when the backup is sized for emergency use. The power rating of heat pump
systems is driven by their heating configuration and the presence of electric resistance backup.
Therefore, homes in colder climates are more likely to face a capacity constraint when upgrading
to a heat pump since a backup is often required to supplement the heat pump performance.
Colder climate dwellings also likely have smaller panels because most dwellings are heated by
combustion fuels. In the modeled scenarios used for this report, the residential Cold-Climate
ASHP is modeled with backup heat while the residential GHPs are not.

Electrical panel constraint modeling was added to ResStock 2025 Release 1 via OpenStudio-
HPXML measures in 2025. As new electric loads are added into the home during an
improvement project, the electrical panel may not have enough capacity or space to
accommodate these new circuits. Thus, the panel may become constrained, requiring an
electrical upgrade that may include upsizing the service entrance. Panel constraints can also be
mitigated in other ways without the panel or service being upsized by using the following
methods: (1) load shedding devices, circuit sharing devices, or a smart panel to allow more loads
to be connected, (2) low-power, combination, or smart appliances to reduce electric loads at the
source, (3) slim tandem breakers or subpanels for more space. The panel constraint estimate from
the upgrade measure allows for costing of any potential electrical upgrade or mitigation, thus
helping to capture the total cost of the measure upgrade more holistically across the housing
stock.

Panel capacity and space constraints are modeled using a service load and a breaker space
calculation, respectively. The service load calculation is based on the 2023 National Electrical
Code (NEC) load-based method in Section 220, which guides how the load diversity should be
estimated and summed to estimate the total load connected to the panel. When this total load
converted to ampacity exceeds the service rating of the panel, the panel is considered capacity
constrained in ResStock 2025 Release 1. Similarly, the panel is considered space constrained
when the total occupied breaker space counted from the bottom-up exceeds the rated space count
of the panel. The panel’s capacity and space ratings are probabilistically sampled based on other
housing characteristics such as heating fuel and the presence of certain appliances. For more
details on panel constraint modeling, refer to the ResStock Technical Reference Guide [14].

1.8 Emissions Methodology
ResStock and ComStock calculate the greenhouse gas emissions of the building stock and
emissions savings from measures using both historical and projected emissions data. Several
electricity grid scenarios are presented to compare the emissions of the ResStock and ComStock
baseline to the GHP upgrade scenarios. The choice of grid scenario impacts the grid emissions
factors used in the simulations, which determine the corresponding emissions produced per
kilowatt-hour. The historical emissions use the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA)
Emissions and Generation Resource Integrated Database (eGRID) [58], while the projected
emissions use NLR’s Cambium 2023 dataset [59]. Emissions due to on-site combustion of fossil
fuels use the emissions factors from the draft American National Standards Institute/Residential
Energy Services Network/International Code Council 301 [60]. The electricity and fossil fuel
emissions methodology will be described in more detail in the subsequent sections.

All grid scenarios presented vary the emissions factors geospatially to reflect the variation in grid
resources used to produce electricity across the United States. In addition to geospatial variation,
the Cambium datasets also vary emissions factors seasonally and by time of day. This study does
not imply a preference for any particular grid emissions scenario.

To compare total emissions due to both on-site fossil fuel consumption and grid electricity
generation, the emissions from a single electricity grid scenario should be combined with all
three on-site fossil fuel emissions. The emissions results presented in this report will be summed
emissions for all fuels. Results related to emissions and grid impacts will generally be presented
at the state level.

1.8.1 Electricity Emissions

1.8.1.1 eGRID Historical Emissions
Historical emissions use the CO2-equivalent total output emission rate from EPA’s eGRID [58].
ResStock does not include historical eGRID emissions, while ComStock results include the
historical emissions for 2018, 2019, 2020, and 2021 using eGRID state and subregion emissions
factors. Historical eGRID emissions rates are an annual average. Emissions for eGRID reported
in ResStock and ComStock are calculated by multiplying the annual emissions rates by the
annual electricity consumption for each model, then summing across the building stock. A map
of eGRID subregions is shown in Appendix A.

1.8.1.2 Cambium Projected Emissions
Projected emissions use data from NLR’s Cambium 2023 data set [61]. Cambium includes
multiple scenario assumptions to reflect uncertainty in future grid generation mix and renewable
energy (RE) costs. Projected emissions consider both the average emissions rate and the long-run
marginal emission rate (LRMER). LRMER, described in [61], is an estimate of the rate of
emissions that would be either induced or avoided by a long-term change in electrical demand.
LRMER data are levelized over 15 and 30 years [61]. Emissions for the Cambium scenarios
reported in ResStock and ComStock are calculated by multiplying the hourly emissions rates by
the hourly electricity consumption for each model, then summing across the building stock.
Hourly emissions rates are determined by Cambium for each generation and emission assessment
(GEA) region. Cambium GEA regions are similar to eGRID subregions, but not exactly the

 23

same. A map of Cambium 2023 GEA regions is shown in Appendix A. This analysis will
primarily report three LRMER emissions scenarios—Low RE Cost 15, Mid Case 15, and High
RE Cost 15.

National Average Emission Factors for Household Fuels defined in ANSI/RESNET/ICCC 301
Standard for the Calculation and Labeling of the Energy Performance of Dwelling and Sleeping
Units using an Energy Rating Index [60]. Natural gas emissions include both combustion and
pre-combustion emissions (e.g., methane leakage for natural gas).

1.10 ResStock Baseline Cooling Prevalence
While all dwellings in ResStock are modeled to be fully heated, cooling is represented as a
fraction of conditioned space ranging from 0% to 100%. As a result, dwellings in the baseline
can be unconditioned, partially conditioned, or fully conditioned with respect to cooling. Figure
4 shows the percent of dwelling units by state that are fully conditioned (100% heated and
cooled) in the ResStock baseline. While most warm, southern states are 80% or more fully
conditioned, some northern states, particularly in New England, have a much lower prevalence
(less than 50%) of fully conditioned dwellings.
Baseline cooling prevalence is an important factor in evaluating the residential GHP and ASHP
upgrades considered in this analysis. The upgrade scenarios assume that each dwelling becomes
fully conditioned (100% heated and cooled) following installation of a central heat pump system.
Therefore, dwellings that were previously unconditioned or only partially cooled receive a
central cooling system as part of the heat pump upgrades. This improves thermal comfort for
occupants; however, it may lead to increased energy use in cases where cooling was previously
absent or limited in the baseline dwelling unit. At the national level, 67% of dwellings in the
baseline are fully conditioned, 23% are partially cooled, and 10% have no cooling in the
baseline.

This section shows the energy savings impacts of the commercial GHP scenarios broken out in

2.1.2.2.1 Commercial Stock Annual Site Energy by End Use
Figure 10 shows annual site energy use for each of the three commercial GHP configurations, as
well as the Comprehensive GHP package. The energy use is broken out by end use and fuel type.
In addition, only buildings that are eligible for each upgrade are included.

The Packaged GHP upgrade is applicable to 63% of the ComStock floor area and demonstrates
approximately 33.3% site energy savings in eligible buildings, with a total stock savings
potential of 1,065 TBtu. The largest savings are seen in energy for natural gas heating, cooling,
and fans, while energy for electric heating increases due to fuel switching of much of the heating
load. A small portion of the heating load remains natural gas because there are a few space types

 35

in ComStock that do not follow traditional HVAC system configurations, including storage areas
in warehouses and entryways in retail buildings. Gas heating systems in these spaces are not
replaced with GHPs; therefore, a very small amount of gas heating remains. Pump energy
increases due to the added pumping requirements of the ground heat exchanger system, but this
makes up a small portion of the overall stock energy.

The Hydronic GHP upgrade is applicable to 11% of the ComStock floor area and demonstrates
approximately 37.6% site energy savings in eligible buildings, with a total stock savings
potential of 256 TBtu. The largest savings are seen in energy for natural gas heating and cooling,
while energy for electric heating increases by a factor of 3 due to removal of all natural gas and
other fuel heating systems in eligible buildings. Minor fan energy savings are also expected.
Once again, an increase in pump energy is seen, but this makes up a small portion of the overall
stock energy.

The Console GHP upgrade is applicable to 13% of the ComStock floor area and demonstrates an
approximate 4.1% increase in site energy in eligible buildings, with a total stock increase of 20
TBtu. There are several reasons for this increase—first, PTACs, the main HVAC system eligible
for receiving this measure, condition little to no outdoor air in the baseline unit. The addition of
an outdoor air system through the Console GHP unit can result in an increase in energy use
during some parts of day/year, but it will provide more fresh air and better indoor air quality for
occupants. In addition, the Console GHP implements increased fan operation to comply with
ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation requirements for packaged terminal-style units [62] (as windows in
ComStock are assumed to be non-operable), which results in increased fan energy consumption.
This measure results in a small increase in cooling energy, as well as roughly doubling the
magnitude of fan energy use. Natural gas and other fuel heating are completely eliminated
through this upgrade, but electric heating energy use increases fourfold. Again, an increase in
pump energy is seen due to the pumping requirements of the new borefield and ground heat
exchanger loop that serves the distributed Console GHPs. Overall, the Console GHP upgrade
may result in a small increase in energy in eligible buildings; however, the addition of cooling
and outdoor air provide benefits to occupants such as thermal comfort and air quality, which are
not currently captured or quantifiable in ComStock.

The Comprehensive GHP package is a combination of the three individual GHP measures, meant
to represent a mass adoption scenario. There is no overlapping applicability, therefore the energy
savings for this scenario are simply a sum of those for the other three measures. The package is
applicable to 86.7% of the ComStock floor area and demonstrates approximately 29.8% site

Figure 14 shows an area-weighted distribution of percent annual site energy savings by
commercial building type. This plot shows results for the Comprehensive GHP package and only
shows buildings that were eligible for one of the three upgrades. The box-and-whisker plot is
distributed as such: lower whisker is the 2nd percentile, the lower quartile is the 25th percentile,

the 98th percentile. The median savings span from -10% in hotels to nearly 50% in schools.
Figure 15 shows the same distribution of site energy savings by building type but also broken out
by individual GHP upgrade. This helps shed light on which building types are eligible for which
GHP upgrade(s) and which GHP upgrades are most responsible for negative savings. Note that
most building types only show savings distributions for one or two of the GHP upgrades (for
example, full service restaurants only receive the Packaged GHP). This is because of the
distribution of HVAC systems modeled in the baseline stock for each building type. All full
service restaurants in ComStock are modeled with RTUs; therefore, every full service restaurant
receives the Packaged GHP. Other building types, such as secondary schools, have a wide variety

 40

of HVAC system types modeled in the baseline. Therefore, some secondary schools received the
Hydronic GHP, some received the Console GHP, and some received the Packaged GHP.
Primary and secondary schools show the highest savings of any building type, with the median
over 40%. These results support the many case studies that indicate that schools are excellent
candidates for GHPs, with energy consumption and energy cost savings of up to 50% compared
to conventional HVAC systems, in addition to other benefits such as temperature control, limited
maintenance, and safety [64]. Most schools receive the Packaged GHP or Hydronic GHP, which
show high site energy savings around 50% compared to the baseline system. However, a small
fraction of schools are modeled with PTAC systems and receive the Console GHP upgrade. In
these cases, schools receiving the Console GHP show negative site energy savings for the
reasons discussed in Section 2.1.2.2.1 and the following paragraph.
On the other end of the distribution, hotels show negative site energy savings in approximately
75% of the stock. Hotels are typically designed with PTACs, making them most often eligible
for the Console GHP measure. At the commercial building stock level, the Console GHP shows
an increase in total site energy, which is due to the addition of outdoor air and modified cycling
and static pressure behavior (see explanation in Section 2.1.2.2.1). For this reason, most hotels
that receive the Console GHP measure see negative site energy savings. In large hotels with
central hydronic HVAC systems, positive savings are seen when the Hydronic GHP upgrade is
applied.

Moving to the right, warehouses and restaurants show median savings around 15%–20%.
Warehouses have low HVAC requirements, so the savings potential is limited. Restaurants are
very energy intensive, but their load is driven by cooking equipment, so the HVAC savings
potential is also limited. The restaurants and warehouses that receive the Packaged GHP show
strong positive savings, while those that receive the Console GHP show savings that are negative
or close to 0%.

Retail, grocery, hospitals, offices, and outpatient buildings make up the middle-to-upper portion
of the savings distribution, with median savings between 20% and 40%. Many of these building
types tend to be larger buildings with RTUs or central systems. This makes them most often
eligible for the Packaged GHP or Hydronic GHP, which show high savings potential compared
to the Console GHP. These building types are also commonly situated on large parcels of
property, as they require enough parking space for patrons. This is favorable for GHP installation
because there is sufficient land area for drilling boreholes for the GHP system.

2.1.3 Peak Electricity Demand Impacts
This section discusses the impact of GHPs on peak electricity demand across seasons and
regions. These results provide actionable insights for grid operators, utilities, regulators, and
other stakeholders about how adoption of fully electric HVAC technologies like GHPs can
influence future grid planning, capacity needs, and infrastructure investments. Both absolute and
percent peak demand reductions by state are presented for summer and winter seasons. In
addition, three major cities are analyzed in greater detail using end-use load profiles to illustrate
the temporal impacts of GHPs during peak summer and winter weeks.

Seasons in ResStock and ComStock are determined for each weather file location using average
daily outdoor dry-bulb temperature. Summer days are defined as those with the average daily
temperature above 70°F, winter days as those below 55°F, and shoulder days as those between
55°F and 70°F.

In this analysis, peak electricity demand is defined as the maximum hourly electricity
consumption observed in the timeseries data for a given location or season, resulting in a peak
value expressed in MW or GW. ComStock and ResStock simulations were performed using
2018 meteorological year weather data. Consequently, peak electricity demand values represent
loads observed under 2018 weather conditions. Peak impacts are evaluated separately for the
residential and commercial sectors and are calculated relative to each sector’s own peak demand.
Because residential and commercial sector peaks do not necessarily occur at the same time (or
align with the overall system peak), the sector-specific peak impacts presented here cannot
simply be summed to estimate total system-level peak reductions.

2.1.3.1 Residential Peak Electricity Demand Impacts

2.1.3.1.1 Absolute Peak Reduction by State
This section shows the absolute summer peak and winter peak reductions by state for the
residential Variable-Speed GHP upgrade package. Results for all dwellings are shown in the
figures in this section to better represent the impact on the entire peak demand for the residential
sector. The peaks between multiple states are not necessarily coincident. In addition, the
day/time of the peak in the baseline may differ from the day/time of the peak in the GHP upgrade
scenario. Therefore, peak savings of multiple states cannot be simply combined without further
analysis of the timeseries data. Negative savings represent a peak increase.

Appendix B contains a table of seasonal peak values for the baseline and the residential
Variable-Speed GHP upgrade, as well as a summary of states where the peak shifts seasons due
to the GHP upgrade. In the baseline of ResStock, the annual peak occurs during the winter for
most states. There are several factors contributing to this. First, there was a large winter storm in
January 2018 that causes winter electric heating spikes in many southern states in the 2018
ResStock dataset. Without this large January electricity spike, some of these southern states
would otherwise see their annual peaks in the summer. Second, ResStock overestimates winter
electricity consumption according to monthly comparisons to EIA-861M 2018, as described on

 43

the ResStock User Resources website [65]. 4 There may be several causes of this discrepancy,
ranging from overestimation of heating delivered to insulation or infiltration assumptions, and
the magnitude of the discrepancy varies by geographic location. This overestimation of winter
electricity consumption may be contributing to the prevalence of winter peaking states in the
ResStock baseline.

The absolute summer peak reductions by state are shown in Figure 16. Negative peak savings
indicate an increase in peak load for a given state, while positive values represent reductions.
Most states experience positive peak savings during summer. Texas shows the largest peak
reduction (19.25 GW), far exceeding that of all other states, which is driven by Texas’s high
dwelling count (Figure 2), high baseline cooling prevalence (Figure 4), and generally high
cooling requirements in its climate. California, Florida, Georgia, North Carolina, Arizona, and
Illinois also see reductions (approximately 5–7 GW). Many northern and smaller states show
more modest reductions (generally under 2.5 GW). Vermont and Alaska exhibit slight increases
in peak demand (0.01 GW). This occurs because both states have a large portion of dwellings
that are not fully cooled in the baseline scenario. In Vermont, 87% of dwellings have either no
air conditioning (33%) or only partial air conditioning (54%) in the baseline stock (Table A-3).
In Alaska, only 1% of baseline dwellings are fully cooled (Figure 4) With the residential GHP
upgrade package providing 100% air conditioning to these homes, the added cooling load
increases these states’ summer peak relative to the baseline. In addition, both Alaska and
Vermont are northern states with relatively low cooling demand in summer, meaning the
opportunity for summer peak savings is limited.

2.2 Geothermal Heat Pump and Air-Source Heat Pump Analysis
This section compares selected GHP and ASHP measures modeled in ResStock and ComStock,
evaluating their impacts on energy consumption, peak demand, utility bills, electric panel
requirements, and emissions. This analysis is not intended to promote a single technology
pathway, but rather to highlight the complementary roles that GHPs and ASHPs can play across
building types, climates, and regional grid conditions. Technology selection in practice depends
on a broader set of technical, economic, and operational considerations that are outside the scope
of this analysis.

In this section that compares GHPs and ASHPs, only buildings that were eligible for both
upgrades are considered. This is to ensure a consistent comparison, isolating the impacts of the
two technologies within the same set of buildings. A footer under each figure will describe which
set of buildings (eligible buildings only versus full stock) is being shown. When full stock results
are shown in this section, the upgrade is only applied to buildings eligible for both the GHP and
ASHP measures, while the remainder of the stock is not upgraded. Please read the discussion and
figure captions carefully when interpreting results in this section.

2.2.1 Introduction to GHP and ASHP Comparison Scenarios

2.2.1.1 Residential Comparison Scenarios
This residential analysis compares the residential Variable-Speed GHP measure and the
residential Cold-Climate ASHP measure. The technical details of the GHP and ASHP measures
are discussed in Sections 1.3.2.3 and 1.4.1.1, respectively. Both heat pumps have variable-speed
compressor operation and are sized to maximum load at design conditions. However, the
Variable-Speed GHP measure does not include electric resistance backup heating, whereas the
Cold-Climate ASHP measure does. Each measure has specific applicability criteria based on
dwelling characteristics. For example, the GHP requires eligible dwellings to be located on sites
with soil thermal conductivity greater than 1.4 W/m-K, a criterion that does not apply to the
ASHP. Shared HVAC systems are not eligible for the ASHP. However, shared HVAC systems
in buildings with fewer than eight stories are eligible for the GHP. To ensure a fair comparison,
the GHP and ASHP analyses are restricted to the set of buildings that meet the applicability
requirements for both systems and succeeded in simulation, which amounts to 69.5% of the
residential building stock. The analyses of peak impact and emissions impact include all
dwelling units, since they focus on capturing overall peak impacts and emissions changes.

2.2.1.2 Commercial Comparison Scenarios
The commercial analysis focuses on a comparison between the Packaged GHP measure and two
ASHP rooftop unit measures—Advanced Performance HP-RTU and Standard Performance HP-
RTU. Technical details of these GHP and ASHP measures are discussed in Sections 1.3.3 and
1.4.2, respectively. All three measures apply to packaged RTU systems, the most prevalent
HVAC category in the commercial stock, representing over 40% of floor area. The Packaged
GHP and HP-RTU measures were selected for this comparison due to their similarities in system
configuration, performance characteristics, and applicability.

While additional ASHP upgrade measures are available in ComStock, they do not offer
comparable analogs to the Hydronic GHP or Console GHP for the purposes of this analysis. For

 78

example, the ASHP Boiler measure is similar to the Hydronic GHP in configuration, but it is a
heating-only upgrade while the Hydronic GHP retrofits heating and cooling. The Console GHP
does not have any analogous ASHP measures currently available in ComStock. For this reason,
this analysis is limited to the Packaged GHP, Advanced Performance HP-RTU, and Standard
Performance HP-RTU.

The applicability criteria for the Packaged GHP and HP-RTU measures differ slightly. The HP-
RTU measures apply to two HVAC system types—packaged single zone air conditioner (PSZ-
AC) with gas coil, and PSZ-AC with electric coil. These are the two most prevalent system types
in the stock, representing roughly 40% of the stock floor area. The Packaged GHP has broader
applicability criteria, including any type of PSZ-AC system (excluding those served by district
energy systems), as well as PVAV systems. In total, the Packaged GHP is eligible for roughly
63% of floor area. To ensure a fair comparison, this analysis will focus on only the overlapping
portion of the stock that is eligible for both the Packaged GHP and HP-RTU measures, which
equates to 39.8% of the commercial floor area modeled in ComStock. While this comparison
does not capture the full technical potential of all GHP and ASHP configurations available for
commercial buildings, it ensures a consistent comparison across an identical set of buildings and
prevents misleading results or conclusions.

2.2.2.1.3 Net Annual Heating and Cooling COPs by State, GHP and ASHP
This section compares the net heating and cooling COPs by state for the residential Cold-Climate
ASHP and Variable-Speed GHP. Figure 48 shows the annual net average heating COP by state
for the Cold-Climate ASHP (top) and the Variable-Speed GHP (bottom) upgrades. For each
state, the annual average net heating COP is calculated by dividing the total annual heating
delivered by the total annual electricity consumed for heating. Total annual heating delivered
includes delivered heating as well as fan and pump energy associated with heating. Total annual
electricity consumed for heating includes the primary heating system, fans, pumps, and backup
heat. The ASHP system has backup electric resistance heating, while the GHP system does not.

The lower heating COP values for the ASHP compared to the GHP corroborate the results in
Figure 46 and Figure 47. In every state, the GHP operates at a higher average heating COP than
the ASHP. This higher COP leads to the higher site energy and electricity savings seen in the
previous section. Across the United States, the Cold-Climate ASHP operates at a COP value of
0.5 to 1.1 less than the Variable-Speed GHP does. The higher COP values for the GHP can be
attributed to its 100% utilization of its compressor (rather than backup electric resistance heat)
and its relatively stable thermal reservoir during heating months.

Figure 49 shows the annual net average cooling COP by state for the Cold-Climate ASHP (top)
and the Variable-Speed GHP (bottom). For each state, the annual average net cooling COP is
calculated by dividing the total annual cooling delivered by the total annual electricity consumed
for cooling. Total annual cooling delivered includes delivered cooling as well as fan and pump
energy associated with cooling. Total annual electricity consumed for cooling includes the
cooling system, fans, and pumps. In every state, the GHP operates at a higher average cooling
COP than the ASHP does. Across the United States, the Cold-Climate ASHP operates at a COP
of 2.5 to 8.9 less than the GHP does in cooling mode.

The cooling COPs for the Variable-Speed GHP range from approximately 6–9 in warm southern
states to greater than 12 in cold northern states. These values are consistent with the high
efficiency ratings in the performance data used [24], as well as the assumptions described in the
measure documentation [25]. This Variable-Speed GHP unit is modeled with an EER of 30.9
(corresponding to a nominal full-load COP of ~9), which was validated through a review of
ENERGY STAR®-certified products. In comparison, the Cold-Climate ASHP is modeled with a
SEER2 of 17.5 (corresponding to an approximate nominal cooling COP of ~3.5-4.0 under
standard rating conditions). This difference in rated efficiency, paired with the manufacturer
performance curves, contributes to the higher average cooling COP observed for the GHP across
all climates. This also leads to the higher site energy and electricity savings for the GHP as seen
in the previous section.

Both the GHPs and ASHPs are sized to the larger of the heating and cooling loads; therefore, in
states with relatively low cooling demand, the heat pumps are typically sized based on the
heating load. Figure A-6 in Appendix A shows the ratio of heating delivered to cooling delivered
by state in the ResStock baseline for fully conditioned dwelling units. This map indicates that in
northern states which are heavily heating-dominated, the heating delivered can be 6 to 7 times as
much as the cooling delivered, meaning the heat pump is right-sized for heating and very
oversized for cooling. As a result, the both the GHPs and ASHPs likely operate at low part-load
ratios (PLR) during most of the cooling season in northern states. At low PLRs, variable-speed

 84

systems generally exhibit higher efficiencies, contributing to higher average cooling COPs in
colder states for both systems. This effect is particularly pronounced for GHPs, which reject heat
to relatively stable ground-loop temperatures and have more consistent compressor operating
conditions compared to ASHPs. Consequently, the combination of the GHP’s high rated
efficiency and frequent low-PLR operation results in average cooling COPs above 10 in colder
states.

In addition, cycling behavior, which may occur in practice below 50% PLR with variable-speed
units, is not captured in the modeling. Therefore, ResStock may be slightly overestimating COPs
since this additional cycling load was not included. Analyses of performance maps at the GHP’s
lowest compressor speed [25] and the performance data for combinations of low PLR and low
entering water temperature [24] indicate that average cooling COPs of 10–14 are achievable in
cold climates.

Note that the methodology for reporting COPs differs between ResStock and ComStock. For the
residential results, net annual heating and cooling COPs are calculated by dividing the annual
heating or cooling delivered at the state level by the annual energy consumed for heating or
cooling at the state level. This net COP accounts for all components used for heating or cooling,
including fans, pumps, and backup heating. In contrast, ComStock reports gross annual COPs at
the individual building level as part of its standard output variables. These gross COPs reflect
only direct heating and cooling components, excluding energy consumed for fans, pumps, and
defrost. As a result, the commercial results present the area-weighted median gross annual COP
across eligible buildings in each state. ComStock does not currently disaggregate the fan and
pump energy uses by heating and cooling operation, which is required to compute comparable
net COPs.

2.2.2.2.3 Gross Annual Heating and Cooling COPs by State, GHP and ASHP
This section compares gross annual heating and cooling COPs by state for the Advanced
Performance ASHP and Packaged GHP. Figure 53 shows the area-weighted median annual
heating COPs by state for the Advanced Performance ASHP (top) and Packaged GHP (bottom).
For each building model, the heating COP is calculated as a gross annual average, defined as the
total annual heating delivered divided by the total annual electricity consumed for heating. The
building-level annual COPs are then aggregated to the state level by computing the median value
weighted by building floor area. The ASHP system has supplemental electric resistance heating;
therefore, the backup heating system is included in the calculations for heating delivered and
consumed. The GHP system does not have backup heating. Fans and pumps are not included in
the calculations.

The Advanced Performance ASHP upgrade shows variation in its median heating COP across
the country based on climate, with colder regions showing lower heating COPs around 2 while
warmer regions show heating COPs around 4. These results illustrate the dependence of ASHP
performance on outdoor air conditions. The GHP exhibits consistent heating COPs across
climates because its heat source and sink (the ground) experiences minimal temperature variation
year-round. The median heating COP for the Packaged GHP upgrade is within the range of 3.5 to
4.0 for all states.

There is very little geographic variability in the median heating COP values for the GHP,
however the individual building models in each state exhibit a larger range in heating COP (3.4
to 4.6). Because the reported COP values are annual, area-weighted metrics, and ground loop
temperatures vary much less geographically than ambient air temperatures, the influence of
extreme climate conditions on system performance is dampened. As a result, GHP COPs tend to
be dominated by operation under moderate conditions, leading to relatively small differences in
aggregated annual COP values across states. A few coastal and/or warm-weather states
(including California, Nevada, Florida, Arizona, Oregon, and Washington) exhibit slightly
higher heating COPs with the ASHP compared to the GHP. Some of these states have minimal
heating loads throughout the year, while others have rather mild outdoor temperature fluctuation
throughout the year. These mild winter conditions can diminish the typical performance
advantages associated with the stable ground temperatures utilized by GHPs, making it plausible
for the ASHP to operate more efficiently in heating mode in these states.

2.2.3 Peak Electricity Demand Impacts
This section discusses the impact of GHPs and ASHPs on electricity peaks throughout the year
and throughout the country. Both the absolute peak reductions and percent peak reductions by
state are presented for summer and winter seasons. Note that peak impacts for the residential and
commercial sectors are calculated independently relative to the peak load for each sector, not
relative to the overall system peak. This means that peak impacts presented in these sections
cannot simply be summed to determine overall peak impacts. In addition, the same three cities
analyzed in Section 2.1.3 were selected for an in-depth analysis of timeseries data and end-use
load profiles before and after the GHP and ASHP upgrades.

Seasons in ResStock and ComStock are determined for each weather file location using average
daily outdoor dry-bulb temperature. Summer days are defined as those with the average daily
temperature above 70°F, winter days as those below 55°F, and shoulder days as those between
55°F and 70°F.

In this analysis, peak electricity demand is defined as the maximum hourly electricity
consumption observed in the timeseries data for a given location or season, resulting in a peak
value expressed in MW or GW. ComStock and ResStock simulations were performed using
2018 meteorological year weather data. Consequently, peak electricity demand values represent
loads observed under 2018 weather conditions.

In this section, peak savings for the residential and commercial sectors are calculated relative to
the peak demand of the entire building stock for that sector. The GHP and ASHP upgrade
timeseries load profiles are applied only for the subset of buildings that are eligible for both
measures. For all remaining buildings, no upgrade is applied and baseline timeseries load
profiles are used when computing stock-level peak reductions. This is to ensure a consistent
comparison, isolating the impacts of the two technologies against the same set of buildings.

For residential results, this approach assumes that 69.5% of dwelling units receive the GHP and
ASHP upgrades, while the remaining units remain unchanged. For commercial results, the
approach assumes that 39.8% of the stock floor area receives the GHP and ASHP upgrades and
the remaining buildings remain unchanged.

The ASHP is most likely to cause capacity constraints, with many northern states experiencing

during extreme winter periods. Although both heat pumps are sized to meet the same building
load, the GHP can typically meet that load entirely through heat pump operation due to lower
performance degradation at cold temperatures. In contrast, the ASHP may rely on electric
resistance backup heat when it is unable to overcome the large temperature lift required to meet
the heating load. This electric resistance heating (COP of 1) is less efficient than the heat pump
operation (heating COP of 3 to 4). As a result, the ASHP exhibits higher peak electrical demand
during the coldest days, driving higher rates of electric panel capacity constraints.

2.3.3 System Sizing Impacts
During each building simulation in ResStock and ComStock, the GHEDesigner tool is used to
automatically size the vertical ground heat exchanger. This process is explained in more detail in
Section 1.3.1. When envelope and lighting measures are combined with GHPs, the annual load

 133

of a building is reduced due to the efficiency improvements. This provides opportunity to reduce
the size of the heat pump and/or of the required ground heat exchanger and borefield.

In both ResStock and ComStock, the GHPs in the packages analyzed in this report are resized
during the EnergyPlus simulation after the envelope and lighting measures are applied. In
addition, the GHEDesigner inputs account for the envelope and lighting improvements to size
the ground heat exchanger appropriately. In commercial buildings, which can have tens or
hundreds of boreholes, this could mean reducing the number of boreholes required for drilling. In
residential buildings, which may only have a few boreholes, this could mean reducing the depth
of drilling required.

This section explores the ground heat exchanger and heat pump sizing impacts of the various
envelope and lighting packages. These results give insight into how efficiency improvements can
help reduce heat pump size, ground heat exchanger size, and hence heat pump cost and drilling
time and cost, for a building installing GHPs.

2.3.3.1 Residential System Sizing Impacts

2.3.3.1.1 Ground Heat Exchanger Sizing, GHP and GHP + Envelope
Figure 81 shows the borehole count, borehole depth, and total length of the ground heat
exchanger for the four residential GHP packages: Two-Stage GHP, Two-Stage GHP + Envelope,
Variable-Speed GHP, and Variable-Speed GHP + Envelope. Only buildings eligible for both the
GHP and envelope measures are included. The total ground heat exchanger loop length is
calculated by multiplying the number of boreholes by the borehole depth (all boreholes for a
dwelling are modeled to be the same depth), resulting in a rough approximation for the total
length of drilling that would be required for the system.

Overall, the GHP-only systems require more boreholes, longer borehole lengths, and greater total
ground heat exchanger (GHX) length. For both the Two-Stage GHP and Variable-Speed GHP,
the median borehole count is 6, while adding envelope measures reduces the median borehole
count to 4. A similar trend is seen in borehole depth and total GHX length. Median borehole
lengths decrease by 6 feet (3%) with the addition of envelope measures. Combining the reduced
number of boreholes and reduced borehole length, the resulting median GHX length is reduced
by roughly 264 feet (25%) through the addition of envelope improvements.

Envelope improvements lower building heating and cooling loads, allowing smaller geothermal
system designs. This reduction in required borehole count and total GHX length translates into
lower installation costs and potentially improved feasibility for residential GHP deployment. The
2025 U.S. Geothermal Market Report suggests that median GHP drilling costs across the
Northeast, Midwest, and South range from $14 to $25 per foot, however actual costs are
dependent on location, drilling conditions, and drill rig availability [70].

2.3.4 Peak Electricity Demand Impacts
This section discusses the impact of the GHP and GHP + Envelope packages on electricity peaks
throughout the year and throughout the country. Percent peak reductions by state are presented

 139

for the summer and winter seasons. Note that peak impacts for the residential and commercial
sectors are calculated independently relative to the peak load for each sector, not relative to the
overall system peak. This means that peak impacts presented in these sections cannot simply be
summed to determine overall peak impacts. In addition, the same three cities (Boston,
Minneapolis, and Phoenix) were selected for an in-depth analysis of timeseries data and end-use
load profiles before and after GHPs and envelope upgrades are applied.

Seasons in ResStock and ComStock are determined for each weather file location using average
daily outdoor dry-bulb temperature. Summer days are defined as those with the average daily
temperature above 70°F, winter days as those below 55°F, and shoulder days as those between
55°F and 70°F.

In this analysis, peak electricity demand is defined as the maximum hourly electricity
consumption observed in the timeseries data for a given location or season, resulting in a peak
value expressed in MW or GW. ComStock and ResStock simulations were performed using
2018 meteorological year weather data. Consequently, peak electricity demand values represent
loads observed under 2018 weather conditions.

In this section, peak savings for the residential and commercial sectors are calculated relative to
the peak load of the entire building stock for that sector. The upgrade timeseries load profiles are
applied only for the subset of buildings that are eligible for both the GHP and one or more
envelope measures. Buildings that only receive a GHP or only receive envelope measures are not
considered, and the baseline load profile is used in these cases. This is to isolate the impacts of
the envelope measures when computing stock-level peak reductions.

For residential results, this approach assumes that 66.2% of dwelling units represented in
ResStock receive the GHP and one or more envelope upgrades, while the remaining units are
unchanged. For commercial results, it assumes that 86.7% of the ComStock floor area receives
the GHP and one or more envelope upgrades, and the remaining buildings are unchanged.

Conclusions
This analysis demonstrates that large-scale deployment of GHPs across the U.S. residential and
commercial building stock represents an opportunity to reduce energy consumption, summer
peak demand, utility bills, and emissions, as well as improve overall HVAC system performance
across seasons. By leveraging detailed modeling of hundreds of thousands of buildings using
ResStock and ComStock, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of GHP impacts
across both sectors.

At a national level, GHP adoption yields site energy savings potential of 5.5 quads annually
under the modeled mass adoption scenario, driven by the high efficiency of ground-coupled
systems relative to conventional heating and cooling technologies. These savings are especially
pronounced in colder climates, where stable ground temperatures enable consistent performance
compared to combustion-based systems and ASHPs. Despite increases in electricity consumption
associated with a switch from traditional heating systems to fully electric heat pumps, overall
annual site energy use is reduced with GHPs across all geographies.

The upgrade to GHPs also has important implications for electric grid operations. Widespread
adoption reduces summer electricity peak demand in nearly all regions (often by 30%–40%)
while simultaneously increasing winter electricity peak demand in colder states due to higher
electric heating loads. This seasonal shift in peak demand represents a critical planning
consideration for utilities and system operators. However, the analysis shows that GHPs mitigate
winter peak growth more effectively than ASHPs, and pairing GHPs with envelope
improvements can further reduce peak impacts. In many cold states, the winter peak increase is
reduced by 25–50% by including envelope improvements with GHP system installation. In other
states with more moderate climates, a winter peak increase is avoided completely by pairing
GHPs with envelope improvements. These findings highlight the importance of integrated
demand-side strategies that combine high-efficiency HVAC technologies with building envelope
improvements to support more stable grid operations year-round.

From an economic perspective, GHPs offer utility bill savings at both the household and
commercial building level, translating to nearly $100 billion in annual utility bill savings
potential nationwide under the modeled mass adoption scenario. Individual utility bill savings
vary by climate, existing HVAC system type, and other building characteristics. In addition,
combining GHPs with envelope improvements not only increases energy and bill savings, but
also reduces ground heat exchanger and heat pump equipment sizing requirements by up to one-
third. This decrease in equipment size has direct implications for reducing upfront installation
costs of the GHP system, reducing construction time, and improving overall cost-effectiveness.

Mass GHP deployment also reduces emissions up to 373 MMT of CO₂e annually under the
Cambium LRMER Mid Case 15 grid scenario. These reductions are greatest in regions with high
reliance on combustion-based heating fuels and are expected to evolve alongside changes in grid
composition.

The comparative analysis with ASHPs reinforces that while both technologies play important
roles in improving building performance, GHPs offer distinct advantages in peak demand
management, operating costs, efficiency during extreme weather conditions, and retrofit

 157

considerations such as electrical panel constraints. These advantages are particularly relevant in
cold climates and in scenarios where managing peak load growth is a priority.

This study also underscores important limitations and considerations. The results reflect
technical potential under mass adoption assumptions and do not fully capture market, policy, or
behavioral barriers to deployment. Additionally, not all buildings in the United States are
currently represented in ResStock and ComStock, nor are all suitable for GHP retrofits. This
study applies GHPs to approximately 70% of total U.S. residential dwelling units and 55% of
total U.S. commercial floor area. Infrastructure constraints, upfront costs, and workforce capacity
remain key challenges that must be addressed to enable widespread adoption.

The findings indicate that GHPs can play an important role in increasing energy efficiency,
affordability, and grid reliability, particularly when deployed alongside building envelope
improvements and other system enhancements. Additional analysis in this area could further
refine performance assumptions, quantify upfront and life cycle costs, evaluate practical
deployment pathways under real-world market conditions, and examine how GHPs interact with
other building efficiency measures or grid and utility strategies like demand flexibility or varied
rate structures.
