ABSTRACT:
A preliminary plausibility assessment of transition to turbulence in hypersonic boundary layers
being caused by vehicle interactions with atmospheric aerosol particles is performed using the
direct simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method of molecular gas dynamics. These molecular-level
simulations enable the direct resolution of the flow around and disturbances generated by micron-
sized particles, including noncontinuum effects resulting from the particle diameters being
comparable to the gas mean free path. Simulations are performed for freestream Mach numbers
from 8–14, particle diameters from 1–10 μm, and conditions representative of atmospheric flight
at 26 and 30 km. Companion continuum linear stability calculations are performed to determine
how initial particle-induced disturbances are amplified as they propagate downstream.
Amplification N factors are computed and compared to values in the literature for which transition
to turbulence occurs. Additionally, while all particle simulations presented herein were one-way
coupled moving surfaces, an algorithm for incorporating two-way coupled moving surfaces in the
DSMC code SPARTA is discussed.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
Predicting transition to turbulence is a formidable engineering challenge central to Sandia’s
mission because it affects the performance and safety of reentry vehicles. For example, heat flux
in hypersonic turbulent boundary layers is 3–5 times higher than in laminar boundary layers, so
inaccurate predictions of transition location can lead to significant errors in heat load predictions
for reentry vehicles. Despite this, the physical phenomena responsible for transition on reentry
vehicles are not well understood.
Transition on slender hypersonic vehicles can be framed as a receptivity problem [1], i.e., the
disturbance growth depends sensitively on the details of the initial disturbance. This implies that
accurate transition predictions rely on the ability to accurately model sources of incipient flow
disturbances. This challenge is compounded by the fact that there exists little hypersonic flight test
data that could be used to confirm or rule out potential causes of transition.
One possible cause of transition is atmospheric aerosols impinging on the vehicle surface or
creating boundary-layer disturbances [2]. Existing studies of particle-vehicle interactions, while

 1

informative, assume continuum computational fluid dynamics (CFD) is valid [3–8]. However,
aerosols at flight-relevant altitudes typically have diameters of O(1 μm) or smaller [9–11],
corresponding to Knudsen numbers of O(0.1–1), a regime in which continuum CFD is
inaccurate [12]. Therefore, current CFD models do not accurately account for particle-vehicle
interactions in these conditions, leading to uncertainty in hypersonic transition predictions. By
contrast, the molecular-level direct simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC) method is valid at any
Knudsen number [12]. Furthermore, DSMC faithfully simulates hydrodynamic instabilities and
turbulence [13,14]. Therefore, DSMC could enable accurate predictions of atmospheric aerosol
particle-boundary-layer interactions.
To assess the plausibility of particle-induced transition, we perform DSMC simulations of particles
advecting through the boundary layer developing on a slender two-dimensional wedge for
conditions corresponding to Mach 8–14 flight at altitudes of 26 and 30 km. We use linear stability
theory to calculate the expected growth rate of simulated boundary layer disturbances to estimate
the N factor (a measure of boundary-layer-disturbance amplification) for transition.
We find that at 30 km, disturbances are unlikely to become sufficiently amplified to trigger
transition. On the other hand, at 26 km and for a Mach number of 14, the results suggest that
particle-induced disturbances may indeed achieve amplitudes large enough for transition to occur
and, therefore, that particle-induced transition merits further study.
Further details are provided in the Addendum.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
Molecular-level simulations:
The molecular-level simulations are performed using the direct simulation Monte Carlo (DSMC)
method of Bird [12]. In DSMC, many computational particles that statistically represent a fixed
number F of physical molecules (the simulation ratio), move and collide similarly to real
molecules. Macroscopic quantities of interest, e.g., the local fluid variables, are recovered by
sampling the properties of the particles within computational cells. In the limits of vanishing cell
size, time step, and simulation ratio, DSMC solutions for monatomic molecules converge to
solutions of the Boltzmann equation [15]. For a nonzero simulation ratio, DSMC inherently
reproduces the molecular fluctuations in gases. When each computational particle represents a
single molecule (F = 1), DSMC exactly reproduces the fluctuations of an equilibrium gas [16], but
when the computational particles represent more than one molecule (F > 1), the fluctuations are
larger than what would be observed in a physical gas due to the reduced sample size used to
determine macroscopic properties. However, since the time step is typically orders of magnitude
smaller than the hydrodynamic time scales of interest, the overestimated statistical fluctuations can
be reduced by averaging cell-based quantities over many time steps while still resolving transient

 2

behavior. In nonequilibrium systems, DSMC reproduces the correct fluxes [17,18] and transport
coefficients [19,20].
Sandia’s open-source massively parallel DSMC code SPARTA [21,22] is used to perform the
molecular-level simulations presented herein. SPARTA has been extensively validated for flows
in the near-continuum regime [21,22] and has been recently applied to instabilities and turbulent
flows [13,14,23]. The gas has properties similar to those of argon, with molecular mass
m = 66.3 × 10−27 kg, specific heat ratio γ = 5/3, and viscosity μ = 2.117 × 10−5 Pa s at temperature
T = 273.15 K. Particle-particle collisions use the variable soft-sphere interaction, which results in
a viscosity temperature exponent of 0.81 [12]. The Mach number is varied over the range
Ma∞ = U∞/a∞ = 8–14, where U∞, a∞ = (γkBT∞/m)1/2, and kB are the freestream velocity, sound
speed, and Boltzmann constant, respectively. The freestream conditions are chosen to match
standard atmospheric conditions at altitudes of 26 and 30 km. The Reynolds number ranges
corresponding to the range of Ma∞ and these two altitudes are Re∞/x = ρ∞U∞/μ∞ = 5.855 × 106–
1.025 × 107 m-1 and Re∞/x = 3.129 × 106–5.476 × 106 m-1, respectively, where ρ∞ is the freestream
mass density. The gas mean free path at these altitudes are λ∞ = 2μ∞/ρ∞c∞ = 2.211 × 10-6 m and
λ∞ = 4.136 × 10-6 m, respectively, where c∞ = (8kBT∞/πm)1/2 is the mean thermal speed of the gas
molecules.
The computational domain extends Lx = 0.1 m in the streamwise (x) direction and Ly = 0.03 m in
the transverse (y) direction. The domain is initially divided into square cells having side length
s ≈ λ∞, and the time step is Δt = s/20U∞, which is well less than the mean collision time. The cells
initially contain Np = 10 particles on average. The inlet boundary condition is a Maxwellian
distribution with temperature T∞, mass density ρ∞, and mean streamwise velocity U∞. A supersonic
outflow condition is applied to the top and outlet boundaries, and the bottom boundary is the wedge
surface, which is inclined at an angle of θ = 6° and is fully accommodating with wall temperature
Tw = 300 K. The wedge surface is discretized with 1000 equally spaced line segments, and the
circular particle surface is discretized with 100 equally spaced line segments.
Before the particle enters the domain, the flow is given time to relax from the initial condition of
uniform freestream conditions, allowing the boundary layer to develop on the wedge. During this
process, SPARTA’s adaptive grid refinement capability was used to update the initial grid using 9
levels of refinement, such that the calculation remains well resolved, even in the shock layer. Once
the flow reaches steady state, the particle is introduced at the inflow boundary at y = 0.005 m and
travels horizontally with velocity U∞ until it collides with the wedge surface. The particle reflects
specularly from the surface, after which it travels along a straight-line path at angle 2θ relative to
the freestream until it exits the domain.
Linear stability calculations:
The linear stability calculations are performed using the Stability and Transition Analysis for
hypersonic Boundary Layers (STABL) suite of codes [24]. The STABL workflow proceeds as

 3

follows: First, a grid is generated using built-in tools for common geometries, which includes a
wedge. The present grid has Nx × Ny = 306 × 403 points. The points in the wall-normal direction
are clustered near the wedge surface to resolve large near-wall gradients and are exponentially
stretched toward the domain boundary. Second, the base ﬂow solution is computed using a solver
based on the implicit Data-Parallel Line Relaxation (DPLR) method [25]. Third, PSE-Chem solves
the linear parabolized stability equations (PSE) to obtain the growth rate of unstable boundary
layer disturbances as a function of distance along the wedge.
The user first specifies a range of frequencies of interest. Spectra of wavenumber guesses are then
generated at selected frequencies. The wavenumber guesses are evaluated using linear stability
theory (LST), and the most unstable solution at each frequency is retained. Then, PSE marches
starting with a critical frequency, typically the lowest-frequency critical point of the LST
disturbance ampliﬁcation rate curve. Once PSE marching is complete, the computed growth rates
are integrated along the wedge, yielding the so-called N factor, which can be correlated with
experimentally measured transition locations using the eN method.
The present stability calculations were performed for the same conditions as the DSMC
calculations described above. However, the streamwise extent of the domain was extended to
Lx = 2 m in order to give the disturbances adequate distance to amplify appreciably.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
Molecular-level simulations:
Figure 1 shows the number density field, n = ρ/m, from a DSMC calculation of Ma∞ = 8 flow over
a 6° wedge at 30 km. The shock and boundary layers are both apparent, as identified by abrupt
density variations. A zoomed-in region near the tip of the wedge is also shown, where the density
disturbance generated by a particle of diameter Dp = 10 μm is clearly visible and demonstrates that
the flow around the particle is well resolved. Despite statistical noise, it is evident that the particle
disturbance field extends to the wedge surface.
Figure 2 shows the pressure distribution along the wedge surface at different times for Ma∞ = 8 at
30 km. Line colors become darker as time progresses. Wall pressure is chosen as the primary
diagnostic since it is an experimentally accessible quantity via surface-mounted pressure
transducers [26]. Wall-pressure disturbances are shown for particles of three different diameters:
Dp = 1, 5, and 10 μm. For Dp = 1 μm, the particle makes a small-amplitude disturbance that
remains localized within a small region near the impact location at s ≈ 0.01 m, which subsequently
decays. For Dp = 5 μm, the disturbance amplitude is significantly larger, and, while it decays, it
propagates upstream to s ≈ 0.02 m before becoming indistinguishable from statistical noise. For
Dp = 10 μm, the disturbance amplitude again increases. Like the Dp = 1 and 5 μm cases, the initial
particle-induced disturbance begins to decay shortly after impact. Unlike the smaller particle cases,

 4

however, the initial decay is followed by a region of disturbance growth and downstream
propagation, which strongly suggests that the particle has excited unstable boundary-layer modes.
Figure 3 shows the pressure distribution along the wedge surface at different times up to
approximately the particle impact time for Ma∞ = 14 flow at 26 km and with particle diameter
Dp = 10 μm. Like the Ma∞ = 8, 30 km cases shown in Figure 2, a large disturbance is observed
near the particle impact location, which begins to propagate downstream. However, because this
simulation was only run up to about the time of impact, initial decay followed by downstream
amplification is not observed. Nonetheless, the facts that the disturbance amplitude is larger at 26
km than at 30 km (note the difference in vertical scale) and the Reynolds number is larger both
indicate that boundary-layer modes should likewise be excited.
The results of these simulations demonstrate that DSMC is capable of simulating particle-induced
boundary-layer disturbances, which are detectable over the statistical noise inherent to the method
for particles as small as 1 μm, making it a valuable tool for simulating aerosol-vehicle interactions
in hypersonic flight.
As is apparent from Figure 2 and Figure 3, the simulated particle disturbance amplitudes are on
the order of 1–10 % of the boundary-layer edge pressure pe, which is large enough that nonlinear
effects may already be nonnegligible. In this case, nonlinear continuum simulations would be
required to accurately track the downstream evolution of the disturbances.

Linear stability calculations:
The results of the linear stability calculations are shown in Figure 4. The left panel shows the N-
factor envelopes for Ma∞ = 8–14 at 30 km. For the highest Mach number considered, Ma∞ = 14,
which corresponds to the highest unit Reynolds number, Re∞/x = 5.476 × 106 m-1, the N-factor
value at s = 2 m is ~9, which is somewhat smaller than critical N-factor values commonly
associated with transition in free-flight conditions [8]. This suggests that particle-induced
disturbances are unlikely to become amplified sufficiently to initiate transition at altitudes of
30 km and above. Note also that for Ma∞ = 8, disturbance amplification begins a short distance
downstream of the wedge tip at s = 0 m. This is consistent with the DSMC results shown in Figure
2, where the initial particle-induced wall-pressure disturbances decay completely for
Dp = 1 and 5 μm, and initially decay but subsequently amplify for Dp = 10 μm.
The left panel of Figure 4 compares the N-factor envelopes for Ma∞ = 14 at 26 and 30 km. The
26 km case, which corresponds to unit Reynolds number Re∞/x = 1.025 × 107 m-1, achieves an N-
factor value of ~12 at s = 2 m. This value falls within the range of values commonly quoted for
critical N-factor values associated with transition in free-flight conditions [8]. This result, together
with the relatively large disturbance amplitudes shown in Figure 3, suggests that particle-induced
disturbances may become sufficiently amplified to initiate transition to turbulence at altitudes of
26 km and below.

Two-way coupled moving-surface capability in SPARTA:
The present DSMC particle-wedge simulations use one-way coupling between the surface
representing the moving particle and the gas, i.e., the particle affects the gas through surface
collisions with the gas molecules, but those collisions do not affect the motion of the particle. One-
way coupling was chosen because it was the only moving-surface capability in SPARTA. This
approximation may be appropriate for massive bodies, e.g., a vehicle, but is not appropriate for
micron-sized aerosol particles, which are known to be deflected by shocks and boundary layers.
Therefore, an effort was undertaken to implement a two-way coupled moving surfaces capability
in SPARTA.
Herein, we briefly describe one of the principal challenges of two-way coupling in DSMC, namely,
how to correctly detect collisions between gas molecules and a surface undergoing arbitrary rigid-
body motion, i.e., arbitrarily translating and rotating. The general scenario is illustrated
schematically in Figure 5. The left panel (“Lab” frame) shows a line segment (in 2D) or triangular
surface element (in 3D) translating with linear velocity UO and rotating with angular velocity ω
about point O. A gas molecule begins the time step at location x(0) and has velocity v.
The present approach detects collisions in the body frame, i.e., the frame of reference in which the
surface element is stationary. The transformed quantities in the body frame of reference are shown
in the right panel of Figure 5. Here, the surface element is frozen at its beginning-of-timestep
location. The molecule is located at r0 relative to the point O, and its position evolves according to
 r= R T c − ω × r , where c = v – UO and R(t) is a rotation matrix, subject to the initial condition
r(0) = r0. Here, we note that molecule trajectories in the body frame are curved in general. This

 8

poses a problem for SPARTA’s standard surface-collision-detection algorithms, which check for
intersections between surface elements and straight-line molecule trajectories. By contrast, the
following outlines a method for detecting collisions between surface elements and curved
molecule trajectories. Since the surface element is stationary in the body frame, SPARTA’s
standard surface collision rules can be applied once a collision is detected.
The molecule-trajectory equation can be straightforwardly solved in closed form by defining a
body-fixed coordinate system with one coordinate axis aligned with the angular velocity vector ω;
in that case, the ω-aligned coordinate decouples from the other two. Then, a potential collision
between the molecules and surface element is determined by calculating the time t* at which the
molecule crosses the plane of the element by solving [ r (t*) − rT ] ⋅ nˆT = 0 , where rT and nˆT are the
surface-element centroid and normal vector, respectively. If there is no solution within the time
step, no collision occurs. If there is at least one solution within the time step, then a collision may
occur. If a solution r (t*) lies within the surface element, then a collision occurs. A simple way to
determine if r (t*) lies within a triangular surface element is to express it in barycentric
coordinates.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
Next steps:
In FY27, the SPARTA ASC V&V project will perform simulations to assess the implementation
correctness and physical accuracy of the moving-surfaces capability to ensure that it meets the
requirements to be included in the code’s master branch. External customers have explicitly
expressed interest in such a capability, so performing such V&V activities is a high priority for the
SPARTA team. To advertise this new capability, we plan to present the results at the DSMC27
conference, which is attended by DSMC practitioners and SPARTA users in academia, industry,
and government labs from around the world.
Path forward for DOE:
Although the follow-on ESRF LDRD proposal, “Enabling hypersonic particle-flow-interaction
modeling with molecular-level simulations” (27-0382), was not selected for funding, we may
address the Investment Area Team’s criticisms and submit a refined FY28 ESRF proposal.
Because we expect the outcome of such a project to be TRL 2–3, it would likely require further
development before being deployed directly in mission applications. A natural home for this
further development would be the ASC P&EM program, where the focus could be on developing
DSMC-informed reduced-order statistical models of initial disturbance amplitudes and spatial
distributions for a given particle loading to reduce the turnaround time for full-vehicle-scale
particle-flow interaction predictions. Additionally, the tools we would produce could provide
modeling/simulation support for the hypersonic wind tunnel weather and particle launcher
experimental capabilities being developed in Center 1500. Finally, we envision more accurate
particle-flow interaction predictions being used to help Sandia provide better environmental
specifications for reentry systems.
Finally, the proposed particle-flow-interaction modeling capability described above is distinct
from the scope of the “Storm Chasers” Grand Challenge LDRD. Their focus is on particle impact
physics, whereas ours is on particle-flow interactions. Consequently, such a capability would
complement the capabilities produced as part of the GC LDRD.

CONCLUSION:
A preliminary plausibility assessment of transition to turbulence in hypersonic boundary layers
being caused by vehicle interactions with atmospheric aerosol particles was performed using
DSMC. Simulations of particles advecting through the boundary layer developing on a slender
two-dimensional wedge for conditions corresponding to Mach 8–14 flight at altitudes of 26 and
30 km were performed. In conjunction, linear stability theory was used to calculate the expected
growth rate of simulated boundary layer disturbances and estimate the N factor (a measure of

 11

boundary-layer-disturbance amplification) for transition. Our results indicate that at 30 km,
disturbances are unlikely to become sufficiently amplified to trigger transition. On the other hand,
at 26 km and for a Mach number of 14, the results suggest that particle-induced disturbances may
indeed achieve amplitudes large enough for transition to occur and, therefore, that particle-induced
transition merits further study.
In addition, a procedure for incorporating two-way-coupled moving surfaces, i.e., solid surfaces
that interact with and move in response to collisions with gas molecules, in SPARTA was
presented. Plans for follow-on R&D efforts and potential future impacts of the present work were
discussed.
