Implementing a parent-mediated intervention for toddlers with ASD within the Chinese child healthcare system: a real-world study
Authors: Huiping Li, Xiang Long, Xiuqing Huang, Shu Liu, Zhuhong Yang, Chun Chen, Qing Lou, Xiubin Tong, Xiu Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBehavioural interventions remain the cornerstone of evidence-based practice for addressing the core symptoms of autism spectrum disorder (ASD). The Skill Training Program for Parents with ASD Children (STPAC) is a group intervention programme for parents and the first parent-mediated intervention programme developed by Chinese clinical practitioners. This study was conducted to evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of STPAC within the context of routine child healthcare services in China.MethodsThis is a real-world, multi-site implementation study employed a single-arm, pre-post design. Between July 2023 and June 2025, 128 families of toddlers (aged 18–54 months) with suspected or diagnosed ASD were enrolled from two child healthcare settings. Participants received the 8-week, group-based parent training programme, which integrated didactic lessons, video modelling and individualised coaching. Outcomes were assessed at baseline and post-intervention using parent-reported measures: the Knowledge of Autism Intervention Questionnaire (KAIQ), the Communication and Symbolic Behavior Scales Developmental Profile (CSBS DP) and the Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF). Statistical analysis included paired-samples t-tests to evaluate within-group changes, with effect sizes (Cohen’s d) calculated to quantify the magnitude of improvement.ResultsParental knowledge of autism intervention strategies increased substantially (mean improvement=6.26 points, p<0.001; d=1.17). Child communication and symbolic skills also improved, with the CSBS DP total score rising from 35.30 to 40.88 (p<0.001; d=0.99), representing an overall improvement rate of 85.7%. Furthermore, parenting stress decreased from clinically significant to normal levels (mean reduction=3.70 points, p=0.005). The intervention was equally effective across diverse family demographics, and baseline child communication ability was the strongest predictor of post-intervention gains.ConclusionsDespite an attrition rate of approximately 30%, STPAC demonstrated feasibility as a parent-mediated intervention delivered by trained local clinicians within routine child healthcare settings. Participation in the programme was associated with improvements in parental knowledge, child social communication and parenting stress. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting the potential of STPAC to expand access to early ASD intervention, though confirmation in a randomised controlled trial is warranted.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent challenges in social communication and interaction, alongside restricted interests and repetitive patterns of behaviour, with some individuals also experiencing sensory abnormalities. With a high and rising global prevalence—approximately 1 in 31 children in the USA1 and about 0.7% in China2—ASD affects all countries, ethnicities and socioeconomic groups, posing significant challenges to individuals’ adaptive functioning and placing substantial burdens on families and society.

Behavioural interventions remain the cornerstone of evidence-based practice for addressing the core symptoms of ASD.3 Early intervention programmes, grounded in principles of Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA), Naturalistic Developmental Behavioral Interventions (NDBI)4 and Early Intensive Behavioral Intervention (EIBI),5 have been proven to improve developmental outcomes, reduce maladaptive behaviours and alleviate symptom severity in young children with ASD. The most effective approaches often involve comprehensive, high-intensity intervention delivered by trained therapists, systematically targeting developmental goals within naturalistic frameworks.6 However, widespread access to such interventions is severely limited by a global shortage of qualified providers and prohibitive costs.7 As is documented in the literature, the involvement of families and caregivers as part of intervention strategies is highly recommended for young children with suspected or diagnosed ASD,8 and also holds the potential to alleviate pressures on intervention resources and reduce associated costs. A number of studies on parent-implemented early interventions have demonstrated that parent training can lead to significant improvements in children’s communicative behaviours, enhance maternal knowledge of autism, improve maternal communication style and parent–child interactions, and reduce maternal depression.3 Notable examples of such evidence-based approaches include Early Start Denver Model (ESDM)-based parent interventions,9 10 Project ImPACT11 12 and Social ABCs.13 Recent studies conducted in China have also reported positive outcomes using localised parent-implemented interventions, such as the parent-implemented ESDM14 and the Caregiver Skills Training (CST) programme framework.15

The Skill Training Program for Parents with ASD Children (STPAC) is the first parent-mediated intervention programme developed independently by clinical practitioners in China. Developed by Professor Xu Xiu’s team at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University, this structured, 8-week group training programme represents a localised synthesis of contemporary intervention approaches. While informed by established international models such as the ESDM and the WHO’s CST framework, its core methodology has been fundamentally shaped and refined through over a decade of dedicated clinical practice within the local context. The intervention focuses on teaching parents to promote their child’s social communication using a blend of developmental and naturalistic behavioural strategies—addressing a critical need, as many parents hold inaccurate beliefs about intervention concepts and lack evidence-based approaches.16 Initial implementation at Children’s Hospital of Fudan University showed high parent acceptance, strong completion rates, and significant gains in parental knowledge and intervention skills.17 A subsequent study further demonstrated that after 1 year—including the 8-week core training plus quarterly follow-ups—toddlers with ASD receiving STPAC showed significantly greater improvements in language, hand-eye coordination, visual-spatial skills, social communication and play abilities compared with those receiving community routine interventions.18

Although parent-mediated interventions are recognised as valuable and supported by public early intervention policies, their implementation in community-based settings remains limited, and most evidence for these interventions comes from highly controlled research contexts. Evidence generated under controlled research conditions may not readily generalise to real-world practice, where provider expertise, resources and patient populations can differ substantially from those in trial settings. To address this gap, this study evaluates the implementation of STPAC for toddlers with suspected or diagnosed ASD in Chinese child healthcare settings. The Chinese Child Healthcare System is the government-led, public health service network that provides systematic health management for children from birth to school age. Building on previous RCT evidence of the programme’s efficacy and acceptability within a top-tier academic setting, the Fudan team collaborated with child healthcare departments at Xiamen Children’s Hospital and Shanghai Jiading Maternal and Child Health Hospital to train local providers—rather than research staff—to deliver STPAC as part of their standard clinical practice. The programme was embedded within existing referral pathways, and session scheduling was adapted to the operational constraints of each site. This real-world study shifts the focus from efficacy to implementation, evaluating the feasibility and real-world outcomes of this parent-mediated intervention for toddlers with ASD within the context of routine child healthcare services in China. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) assess the feasibility of implementing STPAC when delivered by trained local clinicians rather than research staff; (2) examine changes in parent knowledge, parenting stress and child social communication associated with programme participation; and (3) explore factors influencing implementation outcomes, including family demographics and programme engagement.

Methods

Study design

This was a multi-site, real-world implementation study with a single-arm, pre-post design. It aimed to evaluate the feasibility and preliminary effectiveness of the STPAC when delivered within the routine workflow of the Chinese child healthcare system. The study adopted a pragmatic approach, with eligibility criteria, intervention delivery and outcome assessment designed to reflect typical clinical practice rather than a highly controlled trial setting.

Participants

Families were recruited from the STPAC intervention programme at Xiamen Children’s Hospital in Fujian and Jiading Maternal and Child Health Hospital in Shanghai between July 2023 and June 2025. The study included children who met the following inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosed with Autism Spectrum Disorder according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria, or identified as at high risk for ASD with early warning signs through specialised assessment by developmental paediatricians; (2) aged between 18 and 54 months; and (3) parents who voluntarily participated in the programme and committed to implementing weekly home-based intervention activities.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) children with syndromic ASD caused by known genetic defects or inherited metabolic diseases (eg, Fragile X Syndrome, Angelman Syndrome, Rett Syndrome); (2) visual, auditory or physical disabilities; (3) comorbid serious chronic diseases such as hepatic/renal failure, connective tissue diseases or malignancies; and (4) non-native Chinese speaking families. All participating parents provided informed consent for both programme participation and data collection for research purposes.

Intervention

The intervention was delivered through the STPAC. The methodology of STPAC has been described in detail in the study by Zhang et al.17 It was a comprehensive, 8-week parent-mediated programme designed to equip parents with evidence-based strategies to support their child’s development. Each weekly session lasted 3 hours and integrated multiple core components: (1) delivery of theoretical intervention (approximately 60–90 min); (2) video-based analysis and made suggestions of parent-child interaction interventions (approximately 60 min); and (4) demonstration and practice, coaches demonstrated intervention skills and helped parents practice with one child (approximately 45–60 min). The curriculum covered eight core topics delivered sequentially: building trust and routine, management of child attention, dealing with challenge behaviour, using sensory social routines, non-verbal communication, building imitation skills, using antecedent behaviour-consequence technique and verbal communication. Parents were required to take notes on key teaching points presented during each session and assigned weekly home practice tasks involving parent-child interaction, which they recorded on video for review and analysis in the next session. The programme’s curriculum was systematically designed to ensure parents could progressively build and consolidate their skills throughout the 8-week period.

STPAC was integrated into the routine clinical workflow at participating child healthcare departments. Toddlers referred to the centre for developmental concerns were evaluated by a developmental-behavioural paediatrician. Those diagnosed with ASD or identified as high-risk for ASD and met the age eligibility criteria for STPAC were introduced to the programme. Families who provided consent were enrolled and scheduled for the next available STPAC cycle. One week prior to the first session, the local STPAC coach contacted enrolled families to confirm participation and provide session details. Group sessions were held weekly over eight consecutive weeks. Attendance was recorded at each session, and parents completed the study questionnaires (Knowledge of Autism Intervention Questionnaire (KAIQ), Communication and Symbolic Behavior Scales Developmental Profile (CSBS DP), Parenting Stress Index-Short Form (PSI-SF)) at the pre-intervention and post-intervention time points as part of the programme’s routine evaluation procedures.

All STPAC sessions were delivered by local child healthcare clinicians—including doctors and rehabilitation therapists—who met pre-specified qualification criteria: (1) a minimum of 2 years of experience delivering one-on-one NDBI to young children with ASD; (2) a fidelity score of ≥3 on each item of the ESDM fidelity instrument; and (3) completion of one full STPAC cycle as a co-facilitator followed by one cycle of independent delivery under supervision by the Fudan University development team. This task-shifting approach, in which the programme was delivered by existing clinical staff rather than research personnel, was central to evaluating the feasibility of implementing STPAC within China’s routine child healthcare system.

Effectiveness evaluation

Programme effectiveness was evaluated at two time points: pre-intervention (prior to the first session) and immediately post-intervention (after the eighth session), using parent-reported questionnaires. In each family, the primary caregiver who attended the programme sessions completed the questionnaires, and the same caregiver provided responses at both time points. Only one set of questionnaires was completed per family. The primary outcome, parental knowledge of ASD intervention, was assessed using the KAIQ.17 This instrument was developed by the Department of Child Health Care at the Children’s Hospital of Fudan University based on the NDBI-based interventions about autism. The KAIQ employs a combination of multiple-choice and gap-filling items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 50, where higher scores indicate greater knowledge mastery. The original validation study demonstrated that the KAIQ is sensitive to intervention effects, with significant improvements observed from pre-training to post-training (54.5% vs 81.8% correct, p<0.0001).17 Secondary outcomes included child core symptoms and parenting stress. The former was measured using the CSBS DP, which comprises 24 scored items aggregated into three composite domains—Social Communication, Expressive Speech and Symbolic Behavior, with higher scores reflecting more advanced developmental abilities. The latter was evaluated using the PSI-SF, a 36-item instrument rated on a 5-point Likert scale that yields a total stress score and three subscale scores: Parental Distress, Parent-Child Dysfunctional Interaction and Difficult Child. A total score of 90 or above on the PSI-SF indicates clinically significant parenting stress.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using StataMP (version 18.0). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean (SD) for continuous variables and count (percentage) for categorical variables. To ensure the representativeness of the analysis sub-samples (KAIQ sample, CSBS DP sample) relative to the overall enrolled cohort, between-group comparisons were conducted. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables when normality assumptions were questionable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables and the χ² test for categorical variables.

Changes in effectiveness outcomes (KAIQ, CSBS DP composites and total score, PSI-SF total and subscale scores) were analysed using paired-samples t-tests, as the normality of the difference scores was confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk tests (all p>0.05). For each outcome, the mean pre-intervention and post-intervention scores, the mean difference with its 95% CI and the p value from the paired t-test were reported. The magnitude of change was quantified using Cohen’s d, calculated as the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (d ≈ 0.2), medium (d ≈ 0.5) and large (d≥0.8). For the primary analysis of the PSI-SF, post-intervention data were missing for 5 participants (6.0%). Missing values were imputed using regression imputation based on available covariates and pre-intervention scores. The robustness of the findings was confirmed through sensitivity analyses comparing results with and without imputation.

To explore factors associated with child developmental gains, simple linear regression, analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Pearson correlation was used to assess unadjusted associations between potential predictors—including demographics, baseline PSI-SF total score, coach type and location—and the post-intervention CSBS DP total score. A multiple linear regression model was then fitted to identify independent predictors. The model included the post-intervention CSBS DP total score as the dependent variable and adjusted for the pre-intervention CSBS DP total score to control for baseline ability. Other covariates examined in univariate analyses were also entered into the model. Prior to fitting the multiple linear regression model, model assumptions were evaluated. Linearity of the relationship between each continuous predictor and the outcome was assessed using Ramsey RESET test for omitted variables. Homoscedasticity (constant variance of residuals) was tested using the Breusch-Pagan test. Normality of residuals was examined using Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The model’s overall significance, explained variance (R²) and the standardised regression coefficient (β) with 95% CI for each predictor were reported.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this specific implementation research study. The STPAC intervention programme itself was developed with iterative feedback from parents of children with ASD in earlier phases of its development. However, for this particular study evaluating its implementation within routine healthcare settings, the research questions and outcome measures were designed by the clinical and academic research team to assess feasibility and effectiveness from a health service perspective.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

The flow of participants through the study is presented in figure 1. A total of 128 families were assessed for eligibility and enrolled in the STPAC programme between July 2023 and June 2025, all of whom started the intervention. During the 8-week intervention period, 38 families (29.69%) discontinued participation, resulting in 90 families completing the STPAC intervention. Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between completers and dropouts on any measured demographic or clinical variable (all p>0.05; online supplemental table 1). The final analytical sample sizes varied by outcome measure due to incomplete questionnaire completion, with the analysis including 81 for the KAIQ, 84 for the CSBS DP and 79 for the PSI-SF.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the total enrolled cohort and the various sub-samples that completed different assessments. The cohorts were generally comparable across all measured child and family characteristics. The mean age of the children was approximately 33–34 months, with the majority being male (ranging from 85.16% to 85.71% across groups). Family demographics were also similar: mothers’ and fathers’ mean ages were around 34 and 36 years, respectively. A high level of parental education was observed, with over 79% of mothers and over 80% of fathers having a college education or above. Roughly half of all households (52.38% to 54.69%) reported an annual income exceeding Chinese Yuan (CNY) 200 000. Changes in parental knowledge of autism intervention

A total of 81 parents who completed both pre-intervention and post-intervention assessments for the KAIQ were included in the final analysis. Descriptive statistics indicated that parental knowledge scores improved following the intervention. The mean pre-intervention score was 32.33 (SD=6.06), which increased to a mean post-intervention score of 38.59 (SD=4.21). This represents a mean improvement of 6.26 points (SD=5.37), as detailed in table 2. A paired-samples t-test was conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of this change. The result revealed a significant increase in knowledge scores after the intervention (p<0.001). The magnitude of this improvement was assessed using Cohen’s d, calculated as the mean difference divided by the SD of the differences. The obtained effect size was d=1.17, which is considered a very large effect according to conventional benchmarks.

Changes in child communication and symbolic development

In addition to improvements in parental knowledge, changes in child development outcomes were also examined. To this end, we analysed pre-intervention and post-intervention data using the CSBS DP. Prior to inferential testing, the normality of the difference scores for all composite scales was confirmed using Shapiro-Wilk tests (all p>0.05), justifying the use of parametric paired-samples t-tests.

Improvements from baseline to post-intervention were observed across all CSBS DP composites and the total score (figure 2). The Communication composite increased from 15.19 to 17.90 (mean difference=2.71, 95% CI 1.96 to 3.47), p<0.001. The Expressive Speech composite rose from 8.58 to 10.17 (mean difference=1.58, 95% CI 1.13 to 2.04), p<0.001. The Symbolic composite improved from 11.52 to 12.81 (mean difference=1.29, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.72), p<0.001. The CSBS Total Score increased substantially from 35.30 to 40.88 (mean difference=5.58, 95% CI 4.36 to 6.81), p<0.001, d=0.99. Overall, 85.7% of children (72/84) showed an increase in CSBS DP total score from pre-intervention to post-intervention.

Reduction in parenting stress following intervention

Changes in parental stress before and after the intervention are summarised in table 3. Post-intervention PSI-SF data were missing for 5 participants (6.0%) and were imputed using linear regression based on pre-intervention scores (R² = 0.469, β=0.832, p<0.001). Sensitivity analyses comparing the imputed results with complete-case analysis (n=79) yielded consistent findings for the total score and all subscales, confirming that the observed stress reduction was robust to the method of handling missing data. The total PSI-SF score exhibited a clinically meaningful decrease from pre-intervention (mean=92.10, SD=13.31) to post-intervention (mean=88.40, SD=16.05). This 3.70-point reduction (p=0.005, d=0.31) represents a shift from above to below the established clinical cut-off of 90, indicating that participants moved from clinically significant parenting stress levels to within normal limits following the intervention.

Factors associated with post-intervention scores

Following the descriptive statistics, univariate analyses were performed to identify factors associated with the post-intervention CSBS total score (figure 3A). The results revealed that higher pre-intervention parenting stress (PSI-SF total score) was significantly associated with lower post-intervention CSBS score (r = −0.304, p=0.005), while higher maternal education level and higher family income were significantly associated with higher post-intervention score (F=5.16, p=0.026 and F=5.09, p=0.027, respectively). Paternal education showed a trend toward significance (p=0.051). No significant associations were found for maternal age, paternal age, parent coach (doctors or rehabilitation therapists) or location (Xiamen or Jiading).

To isolate the independent effects of these factors, a multiple linear regression model was fitted (figure 3B), adjusting for pre-intervention CSBS score to control for baseline ability. Due to high collinearity between fathers’ age and education level with mothers’, only maternal age and education were included in the multivariate analysis. Assumption checks confirmed the appropriateness of the ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model: the Ramsey Regression Equation Specification Error Test (RESET) indicated no evidence of non-linearity (F=1.07, p=0.366); the Breusch-Pagan test supported homoscedasticity (χ²(1)=0.05, p=0.828); and the Shapiro-Wilk test confirmed normality of residuals (W=0.987, p=0.533). Multicollinearity was not a concern, as all variance inflation factors were below 2 (mean VIF=1.19). Thus, all regression assumptions were satisfied. The model was statistically significant (F=26.90, p<0.001) and explained a substantial proportion of the variance in outcomes (R² = 0.7125). In this adjusted model, only the pre-intervention CSBS score emerged as a strong, significant predictor (unstandardised coefficient b=0.758, p<0.001). Notably, the associations of pre-intervention PSI-SF total score, mother’s education and household income were attenuated and became statistically non-significant (p=0.290, p=0.913 and p=0.485, respectively).

Furthermore, a linear regression model was conducted to examine the association between pre-intervention CSBS score and intervention gains (post-intervention score minus pre-intervention score, figure 3C). The model was statistically significant (F=14.73, p=0.0002), with baseline score explaining approximately 15.2% of the variance in gains (R2=0.1524). A significant negative association was found (unstandardised coefficient b = −0.220, p<0.001), indicating that children with lower baseline CSBS scores tended to show greater improvement during the intervention.

These findings suggest that child gains in social communication following STPAC were consistent across diverse family backgrounds and implementation contexts. Specifically, post-intervention child outcomes were not independently predicted by maternal education, household income, maternal age or baseline parenting stress, and did not differ by location or coach type. Lower baseline social communication ability was associated with greater improvement. These results provide preliminary support for the feasibility of delivering STPAC through trained local clinicians across different child healthcare settings in China.

Discussion

This real-world study evaluated STPAC implementation in routine Chinese child healthcare settings, examining its feasibility, associated outcomes and factors influencing change. Regarding feasibility, the programme was successfully delivered across two sites by both doctors and rehabilitation therapists, with child outcomes not differing significantly by location or coach type—suggesting that STPAC can be implemented by various types of trained providers within existing child healthcare infrastructure. In terms of changes associated with programme participation, significant pre-post improvements were observed across all three outcome domains: parent knowledge, child social communication and parenting stress. With respect to factors influencing outcomes, regression analyses indicated that only baseline child social communication ability emerged as a significant predictor of post-intervention scores, with lower baseline scores associated with greater gains. The findings align with and extend previous research on parent-mediated interventions17 18 by demonstrating real-world implementation beyond highly controlled academic settings.

Following the 8-week STPAC programme, statistically significant improvements were observed in parental knowledge and child social communication, although the magnitude of change varied across domains. Parents demonstrated a significant increase in knowledge of autism intervention strategies (Cohen’s d=1.17). While this pre-post effect size should be interpreted cautiously given the single-arm design, it suggests that the programme’s combination of structured group lessons, video demonstrations and individualised coaching was associated with enhanced parental understanding of evidence-based practices. Importantly, STPAC was designed not merely to impart knowledge but to enable parents to translate this knowledge into skilled practice within naturalistic settings. Consistent with the framework of parent-mediated interventions (PMIs), the programme’s coaching and video feedback components guided parents to embed evidence-based strategies into everyday routines—such as play and mealtimes—thereby creating repeated opportunities for children to practice emerging social communication skills in the contexts where they naturally occur. This emphasis on parent-delivered, real-world practice is a defining feature of PMIs and is thought to promote the generalisation and maintenance of acquired skills beyond the intervention period.19 These mechanistic pathways offer a plausible explanation for the concurrent improvements observed in child social communication, expressive speech and symbolic behaviours as measured by the CSBS DP.

However, not all children demonstrated rapid gains in core symptoms through STPAC, as indicated by the fact that an overall improvement rate of 85.7% in CSBS DP scores was observed. It is increasingly recognised that no single intervention approach is equally suited to all children with ASD. Geoffray et al noted in their European ESDM study that the approach may not be generalisable to the entire ASD population, particularly children with more severe autism.20 Regression analyses revealed that only baseline CSBS DP score independently predicted post-intervention child outcomes; other variables showing univariate associations—including pre-intervention parenting stress, maternal education and household income—were no longer significant after adjustment. This indicates that children with lower baseline social communication skills continued to have relatively lower scores after the intervention compared with those with higher baseline skills. However, it is important to note that children with lower baseline scores demonstrated greater absolute gains (β = −0.220, p<0.001), suggesting that STPAC may be particularly beneficial for children with more pronounced delays, even if they do not ‘catch up’ to their peers with stronger baseline skills within the brief 8-week intervention period. Importantly, improvements following STPAC were not limited to child social communication outcomes. We observed a measurable reduction in parenting stress, as evidenced by PSI-SF scores transitioning from clinically elevated to near-normal levels, with significant decreases in the parent distress and parent-child dysfunctional interaction subscales. These improvements in parental well-being and parent-child relationship quality are meaningful outcomes in their own right. As articulated by Jurek et al, a primary objective of parent-mediated intervention is to promote child engagement through strengthened parent-child interactions.21 Our findings align with this view and suggest that even when gains in child core symptoms are modest, parent-mediated intervention may yield important benefits at the family level. These observations support the integration of STPAC within a stepped-care framework,22 in which parent-mediated intervention serves as the foundational component—strengthening parent-child relationships and equipping parents with evidence-based strategies—on which additional, more intensive supports can be layered according to each child’s individual needs and response.

In this project, the completion rate of the STPAC course was approximately 70%, which aligns with the course completion rate reported by Sengupta et al for Project ImPACT in community-based intervention settings.11 Lyon et al documented an attrition rate of approximately 60% in a community mental health implementation of Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT).23 Despite the high attrition rates associated with community-based, family-involved intervention programmes, the group-based delivery format of STPAC allowed the programme to reach a broader number of children within the same timeframe and resource constraints. To improve course completion rates, greater attention should be directed toward families with low engagement during the intervention. In the present study, baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between completers and dropouts (all p>0.05; online supplemental table 1). However, baseline parenting stress showed a trend toward higher scores among dropouts (97.89 vs 92.22, p=0.06), suggesting a potential association between elevated pre-intervention stress and reduced programme completion. While parent-mediated interventions have been shown to reduce parenting stress, few studies have examined whether families with higher baseline stress are at greater risk of discontinuation. This observation underscores the importance of incorporating stress management components—such as mindfulness-based stress reduction or acceptance and commitment therapy—into the early sessions of future STPAC implementations, to better support highly stressed families and promote equitable programme retention.24 Another potential factor contributing to the high attrition rate may be the scheduling of sessions on weekdays, which likely increased logistical challenges for parents. Nevertheless, this scheduling may also have helped ensure treatment fidelity, as the remaining participants likely represented families with stronger motivation to learn and consistently implement intervention strategies at home. Future research should aim to collect more detailed information on the characteristics of families who discontinue participation, in order to better support their access to timely and appropriate therapeutic services.

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, the single-arm pre-post design without a control group precludes causal attribution of the observed improvements to STPAC. Although the efficacy of STPAC has been demonstrated in a prior randomised controlled trial with 1 year follow-up,18 the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of real-world feasibility. Second, the intervention lasted only 8 weeks with outcomes assessed immediately post-intervention. The brief duration and absence of follow-up preclude evaluation of whether the observed gains are sustained. Future studies should incorporate longer-term follow-up to assess durability of effects. Third, the study sample comprised predominantly families with high socioeconomic status and educational attainment, reflecting self-selection bias that limits generalisability to underserved populations. Fourth, all outcome measures were parent-reported (KAIQ, CSBS DP, PSI-SF), which in a non-blinded design may introduce social desirability bias and expectancy effects. The lack of blinded clinician-rated or observational measures limits the objectivity of the findings. Future studies should incorporate blinded outcome measures and longer-term follow-up. Fifth, independent fidelity assessment of intervention delivery was not conducted, and concurrent interventions outside the programme were not systematically documented. Although families were informally advised to refrain from other ASD-specific interventions, the potential influence of unmeasured concurrent services cannot be fully excluded. Finally, although completers and dropouts did not differ significantly on measured baseline characteristics (online supplemental table 1), reasons for discontinuation were not systematically documented. The absence of exit interviews limits understanding of barriers to programme retention. Future studies should systematically collect reasons for dropout to inform strategies for improving accessibility and retention.

Despite these limitations, this study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of the STPAC model as a parent-mediated intervention delivered by trained local clinicians within Chinese public health settings. By integrating evidence-based strategies into a structured, group-based format, STPAC represents a promising pathway to expand early ASD intervention capacity and empower families. Confirmation of these findings in a randomised controlled trial with blinded outcome assessment and longer-term follow-up is warranted.
