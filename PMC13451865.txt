Real-world diagnostic yield of inpatient stress testing after negative serial troponins: A large community hospital cohort study

Introduction

Chest pain remains one of the most common and resource-intensive reasons for emergency department evaluation and hospital admission worldwide [1], [2]. For decades, the standard of care for patients with suspected acute coronary syndrome (ACS) has relied on serial cardiac troponin assays [3], [4]. While negative troponin results effectively rule out acute myocardial necrosis, they do not exclude underlying obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) or unstable angina [3].

Historically, inpatient stress testing became widely adopted as a strategy to further evaluate troponin-negative patients who continued to have clinical concern for ischemia. In the era of less sensitive biomarkers and limited outpatient follow-up infrastructure, inpatient provocative testing was frequently used to reduce diagnostic uncertainty prior to discharge [4], [5]. As a result, routine inpatient stress testing became embedded in many institutional care pathways as a default next step after negative serial troponins.

This reflexive reliance on inpatient provocative testing persists despite a shifting diagnostic landscape. Contemporary clinical pathways increasingly emphasize structured risk stratification and selective testing rather than universal admission [1], [2], [6]. In many real-world settings, however, troponin-negative patients are frequently admitted for stress testing based on subjective clinical suspicion of obstructive CAD and traditional cardiovascular risk profiles. The clinical value of this approach is increasingly questioned as indiscriminate testing may be diagnostically imprecise, leading to prolonged hospital stays, and often triggers downstream invasive procedures without clear evidence of improved patient outcomes [5].

Several critical gaps remain regarding this traditional management pathway. It is unclear whether chief presenting symptoms or traditional cardiovascular risk factors can reliably identify which troponin-negative patients are most likely to have an abnormal stress test. Similarly, the utility of structured tools such as the HEART score, originally designed to predict major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [7], remains uncertain when used specifically to enrich the diagnostic yield of stress testing. To address these gaps, we evaluated the diagnostic yield of inpatient stress testing in a real-world cohort of troponin-negative patients and examined predictors of stress test positivity, including presenting symptoms, traditional risk factors, and descriptive modified HEART score stratification. We further assessed the clinical utility of this strategy by examining its diagnostic performance against invasive coronary angiography (ICA) and its association with downstream revascularization and short-term clinical outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of consecutive adult patients evaluated for suspected coronary ischemia at a large, urban community teaching hospital in the Midwestern United States between May 2024 and May 2025. The study focused on the clinical utility of inpatient stress testing in patients with negative serial cardiac troponins. During the study period, the institution utilized conventional (non-high-sensitivity) cardiac troponin assays, as high-sensitivity troponin assays had not yet been implemented. The Institutional Review Board approved the research protocol with a waiver of informed consent.

Study population

The initial cohort was identified through an institutional electronic health record (EHR) query of all stress tests performed at the study center during the study period. These encounters were then screened for eligibility based on clinical presentation, laboratory criteria, and confirmation that the stress test was performed during an inpatient hospitalization. Potentially eligible participants included adult patients (≥18 years) who had presented to the emergency department with chest pain or related symptoms concerning for myocardial ischemia.

Inclusion required negative serial cardiac troponin I assays and subsequent inpatient stress testing during the index admission. Troponin levels were obtained according to the institutional chest pain protocol at 0, 2, and 6 h after presentation, with values >0.12 ng/mL considered positive.

In routine clinical practice at the study center, patients with persistent clinical concern for significant CAD despite negative serial troponins were frequently admitted or observed for inpatient stress testing at the discretion of the emergency physician and/or consulting cardiologist. Some of the factors influencing admission and inpatient testing included presenting symptoms and traditional cardiovascular risk factors, including a history of CAD.

We excluded patients with ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), positive troponin I biomarkers, hemodynamic instability requiring emergent ICA, or incomplete medical records. Patients with nonspecific ST-T abnormalities were included, reflecting real-world clinical practice. Thus, the final study cohort comprised a clinically selected population of conventional troponin I-negative patients admitted for further evaluation of suspected coronary ischemia with inpatient stress testing.

Analyses were performed at the encounter level. Patients with multiple eligible hospitalizations during the study period were included as separate observations, with each hospitalization treated as an independent clinical decision point.

Sample size

In accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, we included all consecutive eligible patients during the study period window to maximize the precision of our risk estimates and ensure a representative real-world cohort. No a priori power calculation was performed; the final sample size was determined by the total volume of eligible clinical encounters.

Data collection and symptom categorization

Data were manually abstracted from the EHR by trained investigators. We collected demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, comorbidities, index electrocardiogram (ECG) findings, functional stress test modality and results, ICA data, and revascularization procedures.

Presenting symptoms were categorized based on the primary chief complaint at the point of first clinical contact:

•Chest pain: Any report of chest discomfort, pressure, tightness, heaviness, burning, or aching.

Chest pain: Any report of chest discomfort, pressure, tightness, heaviness, burning, or aching.

•Dyspnea: Isolated shortness of breath or dyspnea in the absence of chest pain.

Dyspnea: Isolated shortness of breath or dyspnea in the absence of chest pain.

•Other: Other symptoms without chest pain or dyspnea, including palpitations, syncope, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, or discomfort localized outside the chest.

Other: Other symptoms without chest pain or dyspnea, including palpitations, syncope, dizziness, nausea/vomiting, or discomfort localized outside the chest.

All stress tests and ICA were performed and interpreted by board-certified cardiologists with longstanding experience at the study institution.

Risk stratification: the modified HEART score

To standardize baseline risk, a conservative modified HEART score was retrospectively calculated for each patient. To minimize retrospective subjectivity, the History component was fixed at 2 points for all patients, reflecting that each patient's presenting symptoms were deemed sufficiently concerning to warrant hospital admission and inpatient stress testing. The Age, ECG, and Risk factor components followed standard HEART score definitions. The Troponin component was assigned 0 points for all patients, consistent with the inclusion criteria. In this study, obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) >30 kg/m2, rather than ≥30 kg/m2, to align with the HEART score risk factor definitions. Patients were then stratified into low-risk (0–3), intermediate-risk (4–6), and high-risk (7–10) categories. Because this modified HEART score deviates from the validated original HEART score, analyses involving this metric should be interpreted as exploratory.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was the diagnostic yield of stress testing, defined as a positive stress test. During the study period, inpatient stress testing at the study center included stress electrocardiography (exercise ECG without imaging), stress echocardiography (exercise or pharmacologic), and single-photon emission computed tomography myocardial perfusion imaging (SPECT MPI), most commonly using regadenoson or exercise protocols. Positron emission tomography (PET) and inpatient coronary CT angiography (CCTA) were not routinely available for inpatient evaluation during the study period.

A positive stress test was defined as the presence of inducible ischemia as interpreted by the reading cardiologist, including reversible perfusion defects on nuclear MPI, inducible regional wall motion abnormalities on stress echocardiography, or ischemic ECG changes during exercise testing.

Secondary outcomes included the presence of obstructive CAD on ICA, coronary revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention [PCI] or coronary artery bypass grafting [CABG]), and 90-day MACE. Obstructive CAD was defined as ≥50% stenosis of the left main coronary artery or ≥70% stenosis of a major epicardial vessel. Significant in-stent restenosis or stenosis of a prior CABG graft meeting these thresholds was also classified as obstructive CAD. Notably, referral for ICA was not protocol-mandated; instead, the decision to proceed with catheterization was made by the managing cardiologist based on their overall clinical assessment, integrating stress test results, symptoms, and other clinical factors.

Revascularization was considered attributable to the index evaluation if it occurred during the index hospitalization or within 90 days of the index inpatient stress test. MACE was defined as a composite of cardiovascular death, non-fatal MI, or unplanned revascularization within 90 days of the index admission.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as means (± standard deviation [SD]) or medians (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared using Student's t-tests or the Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%) and compared using chi-squared (χ2) or Fisher's exact tests.

To estimate the association between clinical exposures and stress test positivity, we calculated crude relative risks (RR) and adjusted relative risks (aRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors was used to directly estimate relative risks, rather than odds ratios, providing more clinically interpretable effect estimates in this cohort. The final multivariable model was adjusted for presenting symptom category and a priori selected traditional cardiovascular risk factors (age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking status, family history of CAD, established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), rheumatoid arthritis, heart failure, atrial fibrillation/flutter, and end-stage renal disease on dialysis). Estimates for comorbidities with low prevalence in the cohort should be interpreted with caution. Collinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and was minimal in the final model (mean VIF = 1.2).

Within the subset of patients who underwent ICA, the diagnostic performance of inpatient stress testing was evaluated against the gold standard of angiography using sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and the area under the curve (AUC). This study was designed to evaluate inpatient stress testing as a diagnostic strategy within a real-world clinical pathway following negative serial troponins, rather than to compare the diagnostic accuracy of individual stress test modalities. Accordingly, stress test performance was assessed at the pathway level, reflecting how inpatient stress testing is operationalized in routine clinical practice.

All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX). A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and stress test yield

Between May 2024 and May 2025, 852 patients were identified through EHR query. Of these, 769 met eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Stress testing was primarily performed using nuclear MPI (approximately 80% of tests), most commonly regadenoson-based SPECT. The remainder consisted of stress echocardiography and a small number of exercise ECG-only tests (Supplementary Table S1).

Seventy-two patients had a positive stress test result, yielding an overall positivity rate of 9.4% (95% CI, 7.5%–11.6%). The total cohort was an older population with a mean age of 68.6 ± 12.5 years and a mean BMI of 30.6 ± 6.8 kg/m2, with 357 participants (46.4%) identified as male.

Chest pain was the most common presenting symptom (74.4%), followed by other symptoms (15.2%) and dyspnea (10.4%). Stress test positivity did not differ significantly by chief presenting symptom category (p = 0.56).

Traditional cardiovascular risk factors were common in the total cohort, including hypertension (69.3%), hyperlipidemia (65.2%), family history of CAD (56.0%), obesity (45.9%), diabetes mellitus (23.8%), established ASCVD (36.5%), and current smoking (8.6%) (Table 1). In unadjusted analyses, established ASCVD was more frequent among patients with a positive stress test (p = 0.001); a finding that persisted after multivariable adjustment.

Using the modified HEART score, most patients were classified as intermediate risk (88.6%), with 3.4% low risk and 8.1% high risk (Table 1). Associations with stress test positivity

Multivariable modified Poisson regression with robust standard errors demonstrated that presenting symptom category was not associated with stress test positivity. Compared with chest pain (reference), dyspnea (adjusted RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.42–1.93) and other symptoms (adjusted RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.36–1.49) were associated with similar likelihoods of a positive stress test.

In adjusted models, most traditional cardiovascular risk factors were not independently associated with stress test positivity, including hypertension (aRR 0.96, 95% CI 0.55–1.68), diabetes mellitus (aRR 1.09, 95% CI 0.66–1.80), hyperlipidemia (aRR 1.20, 95% CI 0.64–2.24), current smoking (aRR 1.08, 95% CI 0.51–2.27), and family history of CAD (aRR 0.88, 95% CI 0.55–1.39) (Table 2). Age (p = 0.99) and BMI (p = 0.50) were also not associated with stress test positivity.

Diagnostic performance and downstream testing

Downstream diagnostic pathways were strongly influenced by stress test results (Fig. 3). Among the 92 patients who underwent ICA during the index hospitalization or within 90 days of the index stress test, 57 were stress test-positive and 35 were stress test-negative. Because referral for ICA was clinician-driven rather than protocolized, this subgroup represents a selected population with persistent clinical concern for obstructive CAD. Of the 57 stress test-positive patients referred for ICA, 27 (47.4%) had obstructive CAD, and 24 (42.1%) underwent revascularization. Notably, among the 35 stress test-negative patients who nonetheless proceeded to ICA, 8 (22.9%) had obstructive CAD, and 7 (20.0%) required revascularization.

Within this clinically selected subgroup referred for ICA, inpatient stress testing demonstrated a sensitivity of 77.1% (95% CI, 59.9%–89.6%) and a specificity of 47.4% (95% CI, 34.0%–61.0%) for detecting obstructive CAD. The positive likelihood ratio was 1.47 (95% CI, 1.08–1.99) and the negative likelihood ratio was 0.48 (95% CI, 0.25–0.94). The negative predictive value was 77.1%, and the positive predictive value was 47.4%. Overall discrimination was summarized by an AUC of 0.62 (95% CI, 0.53–0.72). Because referral for ICA was clinician-driven rather than protocolized, these diagnostic performance estimates reflect apparent performance within a selectively referred population rather than true population-level diagnostic accuracy.

Clinical outcomes

Ninety-day follow-up data were available for 653 of 769 patients (85%), supporting the robustness of outcome assessment in this cohort. Among patients with available follow-up, MACE was rare, occurring in 0.7% overall. Event rates remained low in both the stress test-negative (0.4%) and stress test-positive (2.8%) groups. No cardiovascular deaths occurred during follow-up, and all deaths were non-cardiac in etiology. Among these with follow-up data, majority (81.5%) were alive and event-free at 90 days (Table 3). Clinical outcomes did not differ by presenting symptom category (p = 0.20).

Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort of serial troponin-negative patients, we found that routine inpatient stress testing was associated with a low diagnostic yield. Stress test positivity was low at 9.4%, and neither presenting symptoms nor traditional cardiovascular risk factors reliably predicted which patients would have a positive stress test. While male sex was associated with a positive stress test in unadjusted analyses, this association was attenuated after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that the initial finding was confounded by other baseline patient characteristics.

Even after applying a conservative modified HEART score that assigned 2 history points to all patients, thereby intentionally biasing risk categorization toward higher-risk strata, the diagnostic yield of inpatient stress testing remained low after negative serial troponins. Despite most patients being classified as intermediate risk under this “worst-case” assumption, stress test positivity remained modest and did not differ meaningfully across this modified HEART score categories. These findings suggest that the HEART score, while a useful risk stratification tool for predicting MACE, may have limited utility for identifying patients most likely to have inducible ischemia on inpatient stress testing after negative serial troponins. Because this analysis used a retrospectively modified HEART score rather than the validated original instrument, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as exploratory.

Although established ASCVD was associated with a higher likelihood of stress test positivity, this association was modest and did not meaningfully improve the discriminatory performance of inpatient stress testing. Even among patients with known ASCVD, the majority of stress tests were negative (~86.0%), underscoring the limited ability of clinical risk profiles to efficiently identify patients most likely to have inducible ischemia once acute myocardial injury has been excluded.

Our study further suggests that, within this clinical pathway, routine inpatient stress testing may function as an imperfect diagnostic gatekeeper for obstructive CAD in troponin-negative patients. When evaluated against ICA, it demonstrated poor specificity (47.4%) and limited overall discrimination (AUC 0.62). Although sensitivity was moderate, the negative likelihood ratio of 0.48 indicates that a negative inpatient stress test only modestly reduces the probability of obstructive CAD, limiting its effectiveness as a rule-out strategy in this population. This diagnostic profile has important clinical implications. A specificity below 50% may result in a substantial number of false-positive results, potentially leading to additional downstream invasive testing. However, patients who underwent ICA after a negative stress test likely represented a clinically selected subgroup with persistent symptoms or additional physician concern beyond the stress test result itself. This selective referral pattern introduces verification (work-up) bias and should be considered when interpreting diagnostic performance estimates. Accordingly, the observed sensitivity, specificity, and AUC should not be interpreted as definitive measures of the intrinsic diagnostic performance of stress testing, but rather as estimates of pathway-level performance within a selectively referred clinical population.

Prior studies conducted under controlled conditions have reported strong diagnostic performance for individual stress testing modalities, with stress echocardiography demonstrating sensitivities of approximately 80–84% and specificities of 82–84%, and SPECT MPI demonstrating sensitivities of 83–86% and specificities of 64–77% when compared with ICA [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. However, these estimates reflect modality-specific efficacy under optimized study conditions. In contrast, the present study was designed to evaluate inpatient stress testing as a diagnostic strategy embedded within a real-world clinical pathway following exclusion of MI, rather than to assess the isolated diagnostic accuracy of individual modalities. In routine practice, stress testing is deployed as part of a standardized inpatient workflow, with modality selection determined by availability, patient characteristics, and institutional preference rather than controlled protocol assignment. Accordingly, our findings reflect the aggregate effectiveness of inpatient stress testing as implemented in contemporary clinical care.

The 90-day clinical outcomes in this cohort were favorable. Major adverse cardiovascular events were rare (0.7%), and all deaths were non-cardiac. These findings descriptively suggest that many patients with negative serial troponins have a low short-term risk of adverse cardiovascular events regardless of stress test results. This observation raises questions regarding the incremental clinical utility of routine inpatient stress testing after negative serial troponins. However, this study was not designed or powered to determine whether an inpatient stress testing strategy improves clinical outcomes, and these findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Although this study was conducted using conventional (non-high-sensitivity) cardiac troponin assays, the findings remain clinically relevant within the context of the study period and institutional practice. Conventional troponin assays generally leave greater residual diagnostic uncertainty following negative testing compared with contemporary high-sensitivity troponin assays, a factor that may favor downstream inpatient stress testing. Despite this, inpatient stress testing in our cohort demonstrated low diagnostic yield and modest discriminatory performance for obstructive CAD.

In contemporary practice, widespread adoption of high-sensitivity troponin assays allows for earlier and more confident exclusion of MI test [13], [14], [15], which may further reduce the incremental diagnostic value of routine inpatient stress testing following negative biomarker evaluation [1]. However, the use of conventional troponin assays in the present study should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of these findings to contemporary high-sensitivity troponin pathways.

Finally, our findings are consistent with contemporary chest pain guidelines that favor early rule-out and selective, outpatient-focused imaging strategies [1], [6], [12]. In this cohort, even when conservative risk stratification was applied, inpatient provocative functional testing demonstrated limited diagnostic discrimination once ACS had been excluded. These observations may support consideration of more selective, pathway-driven approaches to chest pain evaluation that prioritize diagnostic strategies with stronger rule-out capability, such as CCTA or cardiac PET, rather than reflexive inpatient stress testing.

Study limitations

Some limitations warrant consideration. First, this was a retrospective, single-center study, which may limit generalizability to institutions with different patient demographics or testing thresholds. However, the real-world cohort reflects contemporary practice in a high-volume teaching hospital, enhancing external validity. Also, our inpatient stress testing pathway included stress EKG-only, stress echocardiography, and SPECT MPI. Inpatient stress positron emission tomography (PET) and CCTA were not routinely performed at the study institution during the study period. Therefore, our findings reflect the performance of inpatient stress testing strategy as implemented locally and may not be directly generalizable to centers where alternative imaging pathways, including PET or CCTA-based strategies, are routinely used.

Second, by including only patients who underwent inpatient stress testing, we introduced potential selection bias. However, this design specifically evaluates the clinical utility of the strategy in the population where it is most frequently applied.

Third, our modification of the HEART score (assigning maximum history points) intentionally minimized subjectivity in retrospective symptom assessment. While conservative, this “worst-case” approach biases risk categorization toward admission and testing, thereby strengthening the finding that reflexive inpatient stress testing remains poorly targeted even when clinical suspicion is high.

Fourth, not all patients underwent ICA, introducing potential verification bias. Accordingly, diagnostic performance metrics reflect the subset of patients selectively referred for angiography and should be interpreted within that context. Additionally, referral for the inpatient stress testing and subsequent ICA was clinician-driven rather than protocolized, introducing potential confounding by indication. Although multivariable adjustment was performed, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded.

Finally, 90-day follow-up data were unavailable for 15% of patients, which may introduce outcome ascertainment bias. However, follow-up was available for the majority of the cohort (85%), and event rates were uniformly low, supporting the robustness of the short-term outcome assessment.

Clinical implications

Our findings suggest that the role of routine inpatient stress testing in troponin-negative chest pain warrants reconsideration:

•Re-evaluating routine inpatient stress testing: Routine inpatient stress testing may not always need to serve as the default strategy after negative serial troponins.

Future directions

Future research should prioritize implementation science to evaluate the transition from inpatient to outpatient-centered pathways. Prospective, multicenter trials are needed to compare structured discharge pathways against routine inpatient testing, specifically measuring cost-effectiveness, resource utilization, and patient-centered outcomes. Additionally, integrating high-sensitivity troponin protocols with shared decision-making tools could further refine patient selection for advanced imaging.

Conclusion

In serial troponin-negative patients, presenting symptoms and traditional cardiovascular risk factors demonstrated limited ability to predict stress test positivity. In this real-world cohort, inpatient stress testing was associated with low diagnostic yield and modest discriminatory performance for obstructive CAD within a selectively referred population. These findings suggest that routine inpatient stress testing may provide limited incremental diagnostic value within this clinical pathway and support reconsideration of more selective, risk-guided evaluation strategies once ACS is ruled out. However, because this was a retrospective observational study with clinician-driven downstream testing, findings should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as descriptive associations rather than evidence of causal effect.
