Cortical/cerebral visual impairment: Assessment to intervention
Authors: Beula Christy, Navaneetha Ampolu, Deiva Jayaraman, Ramesh Kekunnaya, Mojjada Mahalakshmi, Shobitha Sankar, Rebecca Sumalini, Vivek Warkad, Debasmita Majhi, Vivian Manohar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cortical/cerebral visual impairment (CVI) is an emerging cause of childhood visual impairment in developing countries such as India. Drawing on existing literature on global best practices in CVI, available assessment tools, clinical experience, and professional consensus, this paper presents guidelines for the clinical and functional assessment of children with CVI and outlines rehabilitative strategies to guide individualized interventions.

Cortical/cerebral visual impairment (CVI) is defined as “a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by deficits of visual function and functional vision that are caused by neurologic damage to visual pathways and processing areas in the brain.”[1] Although the terms cortical and cerebral have historically been used interchangeably, cortical refers specifically to injury involving the cerebral cortex, whereas cerebral encompasses both cortical and subcortical regions of the brain.[2] Common causes of CVI include perinatal hypoxia/ischemia, hypoglycemia, periventricular leucomalacia, head injury/trauma, infections (e.g., encephalitis, meningitis), perinatal stroke, brain malformations, and genetic conditions.[34] It is important to note that CVI is distinct from “Delayed Visual Maturation,” in which infants typically have a normal perinatal history and developmental milestones, with no ophthalmologic issues, but may appear visually unresponsive in early infancy.[56]

Children with CVI often present with comorbid medical conditions, such as cerebral palsy and developmental delays in the areas of motor, cognition, and speech and language skills, compounding the complexity of their needs.[37] The clinical presentation of CVI at times can mimic other and neurological impairments, such as attention deficit hyperactive disorder[8] and autism spectrum disorder,[9] complicating differential diagnosis and management.

CVI is an emerging cause of vision impairment in India, accounting for 33% of children with profound vision impairment presenting to a tertiary eye care center.[10] Advances in neonatal and pediatric care have significantly improved survival rates for premature infants and neonates with perinatal brain injuries, but these advancements have also contributed to a growing prevalence of CVI.[11] Despite increasing evidence, CVI remains largely underserved globally and in India, with many cases remaining undiagnosed or diagnosed late because of limited awareness among healthcare providers[12] and the complexity of its clinical presentation.[13] To address this gap, this paper presents guidelines, developed from practical experience and global best practices, for assessment, rehabilitation, and family engagement, intended for eye care professionals and caregivers of children with CVI.

Methods

The process of developing these guidelines involved a comprehensive review of the literature, covering clinical and functional vision assessment as well as interventional strategies in CVI, with an emphasis on incorporating global best practices. Based on the evidence identified, a draft framework was prepared by the authors. This was critically reviewed and refined through iterative discussions among the author group, which comprised a multidisciplinary team of pediatric neuro-ophthalmologists (n = 3), optometrists (n = 2), and vision rehabilitation professionals (n = 5) with extensive clinical experience in CVI. The expert panel included professionals from the L V Prasad Eye Institute (LVPEI) network. Although formal data collection methods such as interviews, focus group discussions, or questionnaires were not employed, expert consensus was used to refine the content and ensure clinical relevance and practical applicability.

The final guidelines were developed as a consensus-based document to serve a broad audience, including ophthalmologists, optometrists, therapists, rehabilitation specialists, and teachers, and to guide families of children with CVI. These guidelines are currently being implemented across the LVPEI network, including secondary eye care centers, and are being disseminated through this publication.

Assessment and management framework

The guidelines are presented under three major areas:

Clinical assessment

Most children with CVI present to either an eye clinic or a pediatric neurology clinic. The pediatric neurologist evaluates neurological factors contributing to vision impairment and coordinates with the ophthalmology team for a comprehensive assessment. The diagnosis of CVI is primarily made based on the birth/developmental history, abnormal visual behavior, and neuroimaging findings.[14]

In an eye clinic, the optometrist and/or ophthalmologist elicits a comprehensive history and assesses visual functions, refraction, oculomotor function, and anterior and posterior segment health. Parents play an integral role in the assessment process by providing information on the child’s visual behaviors and functional vision in daily life and by supporting the child during clinical examination.

Recommended clinical room setup: A clinical examination room for children with CVI should have adjustable, nonglare lighting, minimal visual clutter, and high-contrast tools to enhance engagement and minimize overstimulation. A quiet, distraction-free environment with adaptive seating is recommended to accommodate motor impairments. Flexible room layouts allow customization based on individual needs, optimizing assessment and supporting long-term developmental progress.

Observation: Assessment begins by observing the child’s interactions with the environment. Key indicators include independent navigation, locating doors, avoiding obstacles, noticing small objects, exploring surroundings, making eye contact, and responding to greetings. These observations provide insights into visual functioning and guide subsequent assessment.

Case History: A detailed case history identifies primary visual concerns reported by parents and caregivers.[14] Areas to cover include medical history including seizures and associated medication; neurodevelopmental abnormalities; use of glasses; prior ophthalmic consultations; birth complications; antenatal, perinatal, and postnatal history; maternal health; family history; developmental milestones; current medications; and previous interventions including rehabilitative therapies undertaken, creating a comprehensive clinical profile.

Functional Vision and Visual Function Assessment: Visual functions such as visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, color vision, visual fields, and stereopsis are assessed. Test selection is tailored to the considering familiarity and comprehension ability [Table 1].

Tools to assess visual functions

Visual Perceptual Skills Assessment: Visual perceptual skills are often impacted in CVI. A deficit may be suspected when a child reports persistent vision problems despite good acuity and no ocular disorder.[15] Standardized tools such as the Beery-Buktenica Developmental Test of Visual-Motor Integration (Beery VMI),[16] Test of Visual Perceptual Skills – 4 (TVPS-4),[17] and Developmental Test of Visual Perception – 3 (DTVP-3)[18] can assess these skills.

Refractive Errors and Oculomotor Function Assessment: Refractive errors are common in CVI[3] and should be addressed for enhancing functional vision performance and to maximize the benefit of visual stimulation and visual perceptual therapies. Children with CVI often exhibit hypo-accommodation[19]; therefore, dynamic retinoscopy should be performed and near additions or bifocals may be prescribed when indicated to improve near vision. Cycloplegic refraction should preferably be carried out using homatropine as cyclopentolate may have serious adverse effects in children with a history of seizures.[20]

Oculomotor evaluation is essential as children may present with nystagmus, strabismus, inaccurate saccades, poor smooth pursuits, or oculomotor apraxia.[4] Assessment should cover fixation, saccades, pursuits, vergence, accommodation, eye alignment, and nystagmus.

Anterior and Posterior Segment Examination: Comprehensive anterior and posterior segment examination, including slit lamp biomicroscopy and fundus examination, should be performed to identify coexisting ocular abnormalities, such as cataract, optic nerve abnormalities, and retinal pathologies (for example, retinopathy of prematurity), that may contribute to vision impairment. In children who are unable to cooperate with slit lamp biomicroscopy, examination may be carried out using a torch light.

Additional Investigations: Further investigations may be required to aid the diagnosis or identify underlying conditions, including electroencephalography (EEG), visual evoked potentials (VEPs), electroretinogram (ERG), and optical coherence tomography (OCT).[21]

Rehabilitation and Referral: Following clinical assessment, children should be referred for rehabilitation for assessment of functional vision, overall development, and early intervention needs. Consultation with a pediatric neurologist may be necessary to address coexisting neurological conditions, particularly seizures.

Functional vision assessment

Following clinical assessment, evaluating functional vision is essential as visual concerns in children with CVI may not be apparent through conventional tests alone. Each child presents a unique visual profile, determined by the nature, extent, and location of the brain injury.[22] Functional vision assessment focuses on visual attention, recognition, eye movements, motor coordination, responses to light, color, motion, and visual fields, alongside sensory integration. Observing preferences for specific visual features, tolerance to complex scenes, and abilities in visual discrimination and memory provides further insight and guides individualized interventions.

Parental Interview: Gathering information from parents is a critical step to understanding the child’s visual and developmental needs in everyday contexts.

Review of Medical Information: A thorough review of medical history is essential to identify ocular and neurological factors affecting vision. Details about comorbidities, such as seizures or cerebral palsy, and current medications help determine the optimal timing and conditions for functional assessments. This ensures proper positioning during evaluations, improves engagement, and allows interventions to be tailored to the child’s specific needs, enhancing developmental outcomes.

Tools for Assessing Visual Behaviors: A range of tools are available to evaluate visual behaviors in children with CVI [Table 2]. These tools use structured approaches for screening, assessment, and intervention, assisting professionals and parents in mapping the child’s strengths and challenges. They support both visual and nonvisual compensatory strategies and can be applied in educational or home settings, often requiring collaboration among caregivers, teachers, and healthcare providers. Selecting the appropriate tool provides a systematic starting point for assessment and intervention.

For children with higher cognitive abilities, Dutton’s Quick Five tool[30] is recommended. If the child responds to at least three of the five questions [Table 3], a detailed inventory should be carried out that offers a comprehensive evaluation of visual behaviors and abilities. Dutton’s quick five-tool

In educational contexts, the Perkins CVI Protocol is recommended for its focus on key visual behaviors, aiding individualized educational planning. Comprehensive tools such as the Developmental Journal for babies and young children with Visual Impairment (DJVI)[32] and the Oregon Assessment Tool[33] assess broader developmental domains, ensuring interventions address cognitive, motor, social, and functional outcomes. The DJVI evaluates functional vision in daily activities, motor and social-emotional milestones, cognitive and perceptual abilities, and orientation and mobility skills, highlighting the need for educational adaptations. The Oregon Assessment Tool complements this assessment with age-sequenced skills across six developmental domains: cognitive, language, self-help, socialization, fine motor, and gross motor. Together, these tools provide a detailed understanding of the child’s strengths and challenges, guiding personalized interventions.

Documentation and Intervention: Findings from functional vision assessment are compiled into a report summarizing the child’s visual behaviors, strengths, and challenges. This report, shared with parents and professionals, forms the basis for an individualized intervention plan with measurable goals, evidence-based strategies, and environmental modifications. Structured monitoring ensures progress is evaluated, and interventions are adapted as needed. Coordination among speech-language pathologists, physiotherapists, special educators, vision therapists, and mobility instructors is essential for comprehensive care.

Follow-Up: Regular follow-up is critical to monitoring ocular status and refractive needs. It is recommended to carry out functional assessments every 3–6 months to track changes in visual performance, behaviors, and engagement with the environment. These reviews guide modifications to interventions or optical prescriptions and support long-term educational placement and functional independence through individualized support. Tele-rehabilitation services can also be explored to support longitudinal follow-up of therapies and ongoing care.[34]

CVI Intervention strategies

Building on the functional vision assessment described in Part II, individualized interventions are critical as each child with CVI presents with unique visual challenges. Observations from assessments can help identify the child’s strengths and areas requiring support, guiding tailored strategies. As stated by Gordon Dutton, “If you’ve met one child with CVI, you’ve met one child with CVI,”[35] emphasizing the need for interventions that address the child’s specific visual profile and brain injury. Effective CVI interventions focus on three key areas: functional vision training, personalized environmental adaptations, and assistive technologies, which can be useful in enhancing visual capabilities and improving quality of life.

Functional Vision Training: Functional vision training aims to develop foundational visual abilities and higher-order perceptual skills. Visual awareness activities form the basis of this process by enhancing the child’s ability to detect and respond to visual stimuli. Meaningful, engaging objects and appropriate lighting are recommended. Passive stimulation can serve as a starting point for children with minimal visual interest, gradually progressing to real-life contexts. Following the development of visual awareness, the focus is on strengthening visual skills such as fixation, tracking, scanning, and accommodation, often using familiar objects to encourage sustained visual attention and engagement. Higher-order visual perceptual skills involve the interpretation of visual information and its integration with cognitive functions. Core components include visual discrimination, memory, figure-ground perception, visual closure, spatial awareness, and form constancy.

Personalized Adaptations and Environmental Modifications: Tailored strategies can be useful in supporting meaningful engagement through environmental modifications, visual access techniques, behavioral strategies, and sensory integration [Table 4]. These adaptations ensure that interventions are practical, context-specific, and aligned with the child’s visual strengths and challenges.

Key adaptations and intervention strategies for children with CVI

Technology in CVI Intervention: Assistive technologies can help enhance independence, learning, and engagement in children with CVI. Digital intervention tools, including CVI Connect,[36] Vision Nanny,[37] VIKAS (Visual Intervention Kit with Analytics for Special Children),[38] and Look to Learn,[39] provide customizable visual intervention. Depending on the platform, these tools include adjustment of contrast, color, target size, movement, speed, and background complexity, together with individualized training, progress monitoring, and home-based practice to support functional visual performance.

Integrated assessment and intervention systems, such as Sanet Vision Integrator[40] and Computerized Perceptual Therapy,[41] offer immersive visual stimuli with adjustable colors, movements, and sizes, combined with auditory feedback to enhance attention, eye–hand coordination, and visual memory.

Assistive communication devices, including programmed talking switches,[42] provide tactile and auditory feedback to communicate basic needs and emotions, reinforcing interaction through multisensory pathways.

Optical and electronic magnifiers enhance access to visual information by enlarging text or images and have features such as adjustable zoom, contrast, and color, supporting reading and object recognition. These devices are commonly used in low vision rehabilitation, following which improvements in functional vision outcomes have been reported in children.[43]

Discussion and Conclusion

National data indicate that 6–7 out of every 100 babies born annually in India are affected by birth defects,[44] and at least 10% of these children experience developmental delays,[45] placing them at heightened risk for CVI. Community-based platforms, including Anganwadis and primary health centers, offer opportunities for early identification and referral. This paper presents practice oriented clinical guidelines that are particularly useful for upcoming professionals across clinical, educational, and rehabilitation settings, with particular relevance to CVI service delivery in India.

Structured clinical protocols, functional vision assessments, and assistive technology support visual development by enabling systematic visual assessment and individualized goal setting, thereby enhancing functional visual performance and participation in daily activities. By adapting global best practices to Indian healthcare, education, and rehabilitation contexts, these guidelines provide a framework for early recognition, coordinated care, and integration of visual rehabilitation into broader developmental and educational pathways. They emphasize family engagement, optimal use of community resources, and bridging service gaps to improve outcomes for children with CVI. Strengthening awareness and training among families, teachers, grassroots-level workers, and medical professionals is essential for early identification and intervention. Widespread dissemination of knowledge and integration into policy and practice guidelines are crucial to ensuring timely support and equitable access to resources for all children with CVI.
