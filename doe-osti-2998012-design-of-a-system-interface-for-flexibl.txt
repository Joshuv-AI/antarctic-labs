 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This report presents the design of system interfaces enabling flexible thermal power extraction
from two advanced nuclear reactor concepts: a sodium-cooled fast reactor (SFR) operating on a
Rankine cycle, and a gas-cooled reactor (GCR) utilizing a Brayton cycle. The study addresses the
dual challenge of ensuring reliable base-load electricity generation while meeting the variable
high-temperature steam demands of industrial processes (IPs).

Objectives and Scope
 The objective is to develop robust balance of plant (BOP) configurations capable of delivering
high-quality heat to IPs by preserving operational continuity. To this end, the project:

 Adapts representative SFR and GCR BOP layouts for supplying high-quality heat to IPs. GE
 Hitachi PRISM (Figure 1) and General Atomics GT-MHR designs were selected, respectively.
 Develops a modeling framework to simulate component degradation phenomena and estimate
 their impact on power capacity.
 Introduces and tests candidate mitigation strategies aimed at preserving plant performance
 under degraded conditions.
 Identifies a quantitative criterion for evaluating which mitigation strategies should be
 implemented to enhance system robustness against global performance degradation over the
 operational lifetime.

 Figure 1. Reference PRISM BOP configuration.

Problem-setting
To optimize the design of a thermal interface based on a Rankine cycle, several configurations
integrating heat pumps within the PRISM BOP were developed. The proposed variants (AR-730,
AR-780, AR-780r) are capable of delivering 280-356 MWth of nuclear-grade steam. In an ideal
framework, a BOP model would capture both performance characteristics and material
degradation mechanisms, enabling a comprehensive assessment of plant behavior over its full

 8

service life. Such a model would support hour-by-hour comparisons of available capacity versus
industrial demand, with operational performance quantified by the frequency with which each
system variant satisfies the demand. This approach, however, is not currently feasible due to (1)
the lack of sufficiently detailed models and (2) the prohibitive computational cost of long-term
dynamic simulations. To address these limitations, reasonable simplifying assumptions must be
introduced.

Methodological Framework
A novel methodology was developed to evaluate degraded system performance and inform design
decisions. Its key features include:

 Performance Indicators: Thermal efficiency, heat factor, and degraded power capacity were
 defined as the primary metrics to assess the performance of studied configurations.
 Degradation Modeling: Explicitly modeling the full time-dependent degradation of each
 component would require detailed kinetics, long-term operating data, and computationally
 demanding simulations. A set of foundational assumptions was introduced (stationarity,
 Markovian behavior, ergodicity, and quasi-steady conditions) to approximate long-term
 component degradation using scenario-based steady-state simulations. As shown in Figure 2,
 the ergodicity assumption establishes an equivalence between the set of operational states the
 system explores over its lifetime (left), and a set of representative snapshots taken at one time
 across many realizations (right). This allows detailed time-resolved simulations to be replaced
 by analyses of steady operating points at different degradation levels.

 Figure 2. Illustration of ergodicity in component degradation.

 Scenario Sampling: Degraded operating conditions were generated using betaPERT
 distributions to reflect realistic probabilities of intermediate degradation states.
 Ranking Criterion: The probability of outperformance (PoO) metric was employed to compare
 the performance of plant variants, quantifying the likelihood that one configuration
 consistently sustains higher power capacity than another.

 9

Key Findings
1. Failure Mode and Effects Analysis (FMEA)
 Critical components identified include the steam generator (SG), heat pump, and steam turbine,
 which exhibit the most severe capacity losses under degradation. Sensitivity analyses showed
 nearly linear degradation trends for some components (SG), while others (e.g., steam valves)
 exhibited sharp nonlinearities, emphasizing the need for component-specific mitigation
 strategies (Figure 3).

2. Mitigation Strategies
 To improve the robustness of plant variants against component performance degradation, four
 main categories of mitigation strategies were examined: redundancy, material upgrades,
 control-logic improvements, and enhanced monitoring. Overall, configurations that combined
 multiple strategies consistently outperformed simpler variants, demonstrating higher resilience
 under a wide range of conditions (Figure 4). However, not all mitigation measures resulted in
 higher output. Some interventions—such as deaerator pressure control and interstage moisture
 separators—proved valuable primarily for protecting equipment integrity during operational
 transients rather than for enhancing net performance. As shown in Figure 5, the “MIT-5”
 variant (red area), which implements primarily protective strategies, exhibits lower power
 capacity under component degradation scenarios and reduced robustness compared to “MIT-
 10” (blue area), which relies on a smaller set of targeted measures.

3. Partial-Load Operation and AOOs:
 To complete the design of the thermal interface, scenarios with variable IP demand were
 analyzed. The adopted paradigm keeps reactor thermal output constant while flexibly
 reallocating energy between high-quality heat and electricity. Figure 6 shows how power
 capacity is distributed between thermal and electrical output, with steam quality at the outlet
 of HP-2 turbine decreasing to the operational limit of 0.9. To enhance robustness against
 Anticipated Operational Occurrences (AOOs), three classes of mitigation strategies were
 identified for managing a sudden loss of IP demand: (1) immediate steam bypass to the
 condenser, (2) turbine/compressor flexibility enabling electricity-only operation, and (3)
 integration of long-term thermal energy storage.

4. Performance Trade-offs:
 The MIT-10 variant, with targeted design modifications, demonstrated the ability to sustain IP
 heat demand down to 70% of nominal while simultaneously increasing net electric output by
 ~167% (Figure 7). This balance highlights the importance of evaluating mitigation strategies
 not only in terms of immediate power output but also in terms of long-term reliability and
 system resilience.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Scope of the work
 A defining characteristic of nuclear power plants is their high load factor, which enables a
continuous and reliable energy supply. In the context of Integrated Energy Systems (IES), where
the facility also delivers steam for industrial applications, this inherent reliability must be
preserved. Ensuring service continuity is the highest operational priority, requiring the deployment
of all feasible measures to prevent supply interruptions in the event of partial or total component
failures.
 This study establishes the requirements for Advanced Reactors (AR) to simultaneously supply
electricity and high-quality heat to industrial users. The objective is to design a plant interface
capable of enabling two reactor concepts to provide variable thermal power, aligned with industrial
process (IP) demand, while the reactors themselves operate at base load. A central design target is
to ensure the resilience of the thermal interface, defined as the capability of the system to withstand
disruptive events while maintaining dependable energy delivery. Resilience entails the plant’s
ability to sustain operation under degraded conditions or to restore normal functionality with
minimal downtime and performance loss. A critical aspect is the identification and adaptation of
representative balance of plant (BOP) layouts in two AR concepts. We focus on two reactor-cycle
pairings:

 Sodium-cooled Fast Reactor + Rankine cycle: based on the GE Hitachi Power Reactor
 Innovative Small Module (PRISM) design [1].
 Gas-cooled Reactor + Brayton cycle: using General Atomics’ GT-MHR concept.

 Both the BOP layouts have been adapted from their reference designs to incorporate an
integrated heat pump, which upgrades steam quality to meet IP requirements. Building on the
Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) presented in [2], critical failure modes are identified
for each interface that could compromise continuous heat and power delivery. Component
degradation inevitably reduces overall plant performance and may lead to an inability to meet the
minimum required demand, even if the plant remains operational. To ensure service continuity,
mitigation strategies are generally classified into three categories: (1) process optimization, (2)
real-time monitoring, and (3) preventive maintenance. Each category comprises multiple potential
solutions whose effects are often difficult to compare directly. This study aims to systematically
and quantitatively assess the impact of different mitigation solutions on system performance.
Starting from the simplest plant layout, the objective is to evaluate the added value of each
proposed solution in terms of a clearly defined performance metric: the continuity of plant
operation and the ability to meet both thermal and electrical demand.
 The reliability of a power plant is conventionally expressed as the time-integrated probability
of failure over a given interval. In this study, conducted at the design stage, the focus shifts from
time-dependent failure modeling to evaluating the impact of potential failure scenarios on overall

 13

productivity. Because explicit time-evolution modeling is impractical, we adopt a simplifying
assumption, i.e., the degradation mechanisms are ergodic. This allows the system’s full-time
history to be represented by a set of degraded states (snapshots) taken at a single instant across
many realizations. For this equivalence to hold, the degradation processes must be stationary and
memoryless (meaning their evolution does not depend on the specific time at which observation
begins or on the detailed history of prior states) and must converge to a single limiting distribution
without multiple long-term regimes. These conditions ensure that the ensemble average obtained
from snapshot simulations is representative of the time average over the plant’s operational
lifetime. To evaluate steady-state performance, we employ Ebsilon Professional [3], selected for
its ability to:

 Rapidly generate thermodynamic cycle energy balances
 Track fluid properties and assess heat exchanger, turbomachinery, and auxiliary system
 performance
 Simulate off-design conditions through custom component curves and parameter adjustments

This study evaluates the effect of each FMEA-identified degradation mode on the system’s steady-
state performance, while the plant remains online. We examine scenarios that noticeably reduce
the system’s overall power capacity, yet not so extreme as to pose immediate safety risks requiring
urgent intervention. We exclude:

 Operational transients: Short-term fluctuations (e.g., deaerator level swings, feedwater inlet
 temperature changes, or steam flow pressure pulsations) that do not yield sustained
 performance degradation measurable by steady-state energy balances.
 Abrupt safety events: high-risk incidents (e.g., turbine overspeed or valve jamming) requiring
 emergency intervention.

Standard procedures typically involve damage assessment followed by corrective maintenance or
increased monitoring, depending on severity. Because the plant continues to operate in a stable,
albeit non-nominal, state, its behavior can be captured accurately using quasi-static, steady-state
models, and tools like Ebsilon Professional are ideally suited to this analysis. Furthermore, since
these degradations occur over long timescales, steady-state simulations in Ebsilon Professional
capture all relevant behavior without requiring transient analysis.

1.2. Selection of Industrial Process for nuclear-grade steam integration
 A nuclear plant’s ability to serve the thermal requirements of an IP depends critically on
matching the quality of heat it can deliver with the process’s demands. High-energy-consuming
sectors—such as petroleum refining, polymer synthesis, iron and steelmaking, and cement
production—typically operate at elevated temperatures supplied by fossil fuels. By integrating a
heat pump that raises steam temperature and pressure, a nuclear plant can potentially meet a

 14

broader spectrum of industrial needs. In [4], a detailed analysis of these processes, including their
workflow stages, operating-temperature requirements, and power-input profiles is provided. Yet
steam quality compatibility alone does not determine integration viability: many high-temperature
processes now employ advanced waste heat recovery at even higher temperatures. The selection
criteria include each process’s steam temperature and pressure requirements, its heat demand
profile, and its compatibility with nuclear-grade steam specifications. Consequently, from the
surveyed applications we consider only those processes that:

 Require steam temperatures between 400 °C and the highest temperature achievable by the
 heat‐pump–augmented nuclear plant considered.
 Rely predominantly on direct heat input rather than extensive internal heat-recovery loops.
 Require externally supplied fuels or energy inputs—excluding those that rely on off-gases,
 waste heat, in-plant electricity, or other internally recovered streams—and presently depend
 on fossil-fuel boilers to meet their high-temperature needs.

Table 1-1 summarizes the results of this screening, along with concise descriptions of the evaluated
processes. IPs then need to be ranked by strategic value, i.e., factors like energy intensity,
economic impact, and operational flexibility needs to be accounted for in the prioritization process.
In this study, we excluded cement and lime‐production sector because they would derive only
marginal benefit from the nuclear‐generated steam—useful solely for the initial calcination stage.
In contrast, integrating the remaining IPs proves both energetically compatible and inconsequential
to the study’s subsequent phases. Consequently, the chemical/petrochemical and steel sectors
emerge as the most suitable candidates for coupling with the nuclear plant in this analysis.

1.3. Parallel between Electrical and Thermal load Management
 The key distinction of cogeneration plants, compared to units dedicated solely to electricity
production, lies in the requirement to satisfy a dual energy demand: both electricity and process
heat. When the cogenerated heat demand is relatively constant, this is typically addressed by
implementing dedicated extraction lines that divert a fraction of steam under specified conditions.
Such an arrangement largely decouples thermal and electrical generation. The plant under analysis,
however, presents two fundamental differences.

 Complete coupling of heat and power
 The steam delivered to the industrial process does not represent a fraction of turbine flow—it
 constitutes the entire steam output. This creates a direct coupling between thermal production
 and electrical generation, placing stronger emphasis on both design choices and control system
 architecture.

 Variable industrial heat demand
 Unlike the relatively predictable behavior of electrical grids, IPs often exhibit fluctuating heat
 demand. For this analysis, we assume a one-to-one pairing between a nuclear plant and a single
 IP. Consequently, the process demand directly dictates the plant’s operating conditions. It is
 therefore essential to characterize, for the specific IP, not only nominal and peak heat demand
 but also partial-load operation, ramp-rate requirements, and the frequency of such variations.

These features raise a central question: is the power plant subject to heat-delivery constraints
analogous to those imposed on conventional power plants by the electrical grid, and is it capable
of meeting them? In electrical systems, generators must continuously adjust output in real time to
balance supply and demand and maintain grid frequency. Similarly, in advanced cogeneration
applications—particularly those integrated with industrial processes—thermal generators must
dynamically adjust heat output to track variable thermal loads. Just as the electric grid imposes
limits on ramp rates, minimum output, and response times, comparable requirements can be
formulated for thermal delivery. This parallel supports the development of control strategies and
system architectures that guarantee both forms of energy (i.e., electricity and heat) are delivered
reliably, responsively, and within defined operational bounds. While electrical frequency control
emerges from the aggregate balance among multiple generators and consumers, thermal balance
in cogeneration currently involves the nuclear plant and a single industrial user. Looking forward,
as nuclear energy adoption in industry expands, the concept of a thermal network is gaining

 16

traction—one capable of coordinating the heat demands of multiple energy-intensive processes
through the integration of several production units.
Beyond the analogy with electrical load management, several further implications arise:

 Economic performance
 Flexibility often comes at the cost of efficiency. Operating away from design-point conditions
 reduces thermal efficiency, meaning new performance indicators (e.g., combined “heat factor”
 metrics) may be required to balance economic trade-offs between steady baseload operation
 and flexible dispatch.

 Safety and licensing considerations
 Licensing frameworks traditionally assume predictable heat rejection. Variable industrial
 demand introduces new classes of transients (beyond conventional turbine trips and load
 rejections) that must be evaluated as Anticipated Operational Occurrences (AOOs) or design-
 basis accidents. Moreover, the plant–process interface must be designed as fail-safe, with
 isolation, relief, and interlock systems protecting the nuclear side from industrial disturbances.

 System integration and resilience
 Future scenarios involving thermal networks—where multiple industrial processes interface
 with multiple energy producers—will require advanced coordination protocols and
 optimization strategies to ensure fault tolerance and resilience.

 Coupling with electricity markets
 In the event of reduced industrial demand, excess reactor power must be redirected to
 electricity generation. This introduces new interactions with the grid, potentially making
 coupled systems behave like “must-run” renewables, with implications for dispatchability and
 market participation.

2. METHODOLOGY FOR EVALUATING AND RANKING CANDIDATE INTERFACE
 CONFIGURATIONS

 In this Section, we introduce a structured workflow designed to model component performance
degradation, systematically evaluate mitigation options, and rank enhanced plant variants
according to their ability to maintain service continuity.

2.1. Performance Indicators for Cogenerative Systems

whenever needed, always referring to the performance of the reference BOP of PRISM reactor.

Thermal efficiency reflects the combined performance of all key components (i.e., steam turbine,

2.1.3. Power Capacity under component performance degradation
 The ability to predict generation loss due to equipment failures is crucial for risk-informing
plant operation and long-term equipment reliability planning. While probabilistic risk assessment
(PRA) focuses on ensuring the safety of nuclear power plants, generation risk assessment (GRA)
ensures productivity and profitability in an increasingly competitive market. GRA predicts the risk
of generation loss during future operation by estimating the probability, frequency, and duration
of plant trips or derates resulting from equipment degradation or failure [6]. In this study, a GRA-
inspired methodology is applied to evaluate the degraded power capacity of the investigated BOP
under progressive component performance deterioration. Eq.(3) introduces a performance-based
cost function, hereafter called power capacity or degraded power capacity (𝐶), which represents
a variant of the net power capacity for electricity-generating units. Given the goal of supplying
high-quality heat to the IP, the delivered high-temperature heat and the thermal equivalent of net
electrical output are combined to produce a single metric that decreases as component degradations
worsen.

This cost function is designed to satisfy four requirements:

 Degraded performance scenarios
 As component efficiencies decline, auxiliary power demands increase. The cost function must
 respond only to actual performance degradations, dynamically recalculating the associated
 power consumption to reflect the operational impact. By linking the cost function to real-time
 efficiency losses, the approach accurately monitors the system's ability to maintain service.
 Nominal operation invariance
 Under undegraded conditions, the system allocates its full production capacity to high-
 temperature heat (with any remaining capacity to electrical power). 𝑄̇ and 𝑊 take on their
 nominal values, all auxiliary loads are at their minimum levels, just enough to keep the plant
 running.
 Capacity shift neutrality
 The cost function needs to remain unchanged until an actual degradation occurs. Shifting
 capacity between industrial heat and electrical power under perfect efficiency (power absorbed
 by the auxiliary loads is constant) should not alter the cost.

 Monotone function of the failure entities
 The cost function is designed so that, as component degradation severity increases, the
 calculated cost strictly worsens. This monotonic relationship guarantees that more severe
 failures receive larger penalties, preventing any counterintuitive ordering.

2.2. Modeling approach for Performance Degradation
2.3.1. Identification and definition of Foundational Assumptions
 Within an idealized framework, a BOP model capable of representing both performance
characteristics and material degradation mechanisms would provide the means to evaluate the
plant’s response across a comprehensive range of scenarios spanning its entire service life. Each
component would be subjected to realistic thermomechanical transients, degradation mechanisms
would be explicitly modeled, and the system’s degraded capacity could be compared in real-time
against the downstream IP demand trajectory. This would enable an hour-by-hour assessment of
available capacity versus demand. For each system variant, the frequency of successful load
fulfillment could be recorded, allowing configurations to be ranked based on their operational
performance. This approach is infeasible for two reasons: (1) the lack of a sufficiently detailed
model, and (2) the prohibitively high computational demands associated with long-term dynamic
simulations. To capture the effects of degradation mechanisms within a modeling framework (and
thereby draw conclusions regarding the mitigation strategies that ensure service continuity), it is
necessary to introduce reasonable assumptions.

 Assumption #1: Stationarity of degradation processes
 From the moment the plant begins operation, every component undergoes continuous
 performance decline, each with its own degradation rate, range of variation, and distinct
 maintenance threshold. The statistical characteristics of degradation processes are assumed to
 be stationary, meaning they do not depend on the absolute time of observation. For many
 degradation mechanisms (e.g., fouling in heat exchangers, turbine erosion, or pump efficiency
 loss), the degradation rate tends to stabilize statistically over long periods. However, certain
 mechanisms (e.g., thermal fatigue accumulation or irradiation damage in nuclear components)
 are inherently time- or environment-dependent, and degradation may accelerate with age or
 exposure, and the stationarity assumption becomes less accurate.

 Assumption #2: Approximately Markovian degradation processes
 Degradation processes are assumed to be approximately Markovian, meaning they exhibit no
 long-term memory beyond the current state. In practice, most degradation behavior is
 “memoryless”: the near-future evolution (e.g., changes in efficiency, pressure drop, or wear
 depth) depends primarily on the current condition rather than on the full historical path that led
 there.

 20

 Assumption #3: Statistical independence of degradation mechanisms
 Periodic inspections may reveal multiple components in degraded or failed states. These
 failures are treated as statistically independent events, meaning that the failure of one
 component does not affect the probability or severity of failure in others. Common-cause
 failures and cascading effects are therefore excluded from the scope of this analysis.

 Assumption #4: Operational-time (“only-on”) assumption and Ergodicity
 To model degradation realistically, performance ranges must be defined for each component.
 The analysis is restricted to periods when the plant is in production. The lower bound of each
 range represents the most degraded condition still considered permissible, i.e., the threshold
 beyond which immediate maintenance would normally be required. At this threshold, a
 restorative intervention is assumed to return the component to its nominal operating state.
 Defining such a limit does not preclude earlier corrective maintenance actions (e.g., repairs,
 cleaning, or realignment), which may be carried out online or during scheduled outages. These
 actions are generally precautionary measures aimed at slowing degradation and extending
 component lifetime.

2.3. Description of the proposed Methodology
 This section outlines the main steps of the proposed methodology for optimizing the design of
the thermal interface to the IP, i.e., BOP configuration:

1. Identify failure modes for the components in the baseline configuration

 25

Identify the primary failure modes for each major component in the baseline BOP and specify the
mechanisms that drive gradual performance loss. Defining this discrete set of degradation
mechanisms is conceptually analogous to compiling Design-Basis Accidents (DBAs): in both
cases, the objective is to verify that the system can tolerate predefined adverse conditions without
unacceptable consequences. In our case, the focus is on slowly degraded performance states. The
design must be sufficiently robust to accommodate these degradations while sustaining acceptable
operational output.

2. Quantify severity ranges
As described by Assumption 4, realistic performance-loss interval is assigned to each failure mode.
The severity ranges need to be constrained to terminate just before reaching the threshold at which
maintenance is required. Likewise, when calculating the capacity factor, days lost to unplanned
maintenance outages are excluded.

3. Generate multi-event scenarios
After a power plant starts being operated, every component degrades over time at its characteristic
rate. To capture the plant’s state at a given moment, we construct a set of multi-event scenarios
(𝑆 = {𝑠 }) in which each component has a specified performance loss. The procedure is as follows:

o For each component, sample component degradation level from predefined severity range.
o Assemble a scenario by applying all sampled losses simultaneously, one for each component.
o Generate 𝑁 scenarios 𝑠 to reflect the variety of possible degradation combinations over the
  plant’s lifetime.

4. Catalog mitigation strategies
Compile candidate countermeasures for each failure mechanism, e.g., (1) oversizing critical
components, (2) adding redundancy or bypass lines, (3) enhanced monitoring and control-logic
modifications.

5. Create enhanced plant variants
Combine mitigation strategies in different ways to form 𝑀 distinct layouts (enhanced plant
variants) derived from the baseline.

 26

 Figure 2-4. Graphical representation of Steps 6 of the methodology for evaluating the
 robustness of enhanced-variants against component performance loss.

6. Assess the performance of variants via Ebsilon model
For each variant (1, … , 𝑀), run all 𝑁 scenarios through the Ebsilon model, and calculate the power
capacity (C) deriving from multi-component failures (Figure 2-4). By aggregating results from
hundreds of snapshots, the degraded power capacity of the plant is estimated. This approach
preserves the main insight of a GRA analysis (the system’s fault tolerance) while remaining
straightforward and well-suited to early-stage design studies.

7. Rank enhanced plant variants
Aggregate each variant’s capacity outcomes across the same 𝑁 scenarios into 𝑀 histograms (one
per plant configuration, each with 𝑁 bins). A quantitative metric, based on the variant’s ability to
preserve service continuity, is applied to rank the variants from most to least robust. It will be
described in Section 2.5.

2.4. Sampling method
 The sampling method to generate degradation scenarios (included in the methodology step 3),
must ensure that the simulated states are statistically representative, while remaining consistent
with the plant’s operational constraints. The first step is to define realistic ranges of failure severity
for each component (α ). The criterion used is described in Section 2.2. The second step is
to select the probability distribution used to sample degradation levels in each scenario. A uniform
distribution was initially considered but was discarded because, once the degradation range is
defined, it assigns equal probability to all states within the interval. With reference to the system
trajectory in phase space shown in Figure 2-2, using a uniform distribution to sample the snapshots
would be equivalent to assuming that the trajectory passes through all points within the volume
the same number of times (and therefore with equal probability). This is not the case: the actual
dynamics—driven by degradation rates and policies governing online maintenance

 27

interventions—create regions where the system spends more time. Analogous to statistical
mechanics, certain regions of the phase space are inherently more probable than others, and the
sampling distribution must reflect their higher statistical weight [7].
 To bias the sampling toward intermediate degradation levels, a betaPERT distribution [8] is
adopted. The betaPERT retains the specified minimum ( 𝑎 ) and maximum ( 𝑏 ) bounds but
introduces a most likely value (mode 𝑚 ∈ [𝑎, 𝑏]), which concentrates probability mass around
realistic intermediate states while reducing the likelihood of sampling extremes. To construct a
betaPERT distribution, we start from a standard Beta distribution defined on the interval [0,1], and
then rescale it to the desired bounds [𝑎, 𝑏]. The parameters 𝛼 and 𝛽 of the Beta distribution are
chosen so that the rescaled variable has its mode at the most likely value 𝑚, while the overall
concentration of probability around this mode is controlled by the shape parameter 𝜆 > 0
(Eqs.(9)(10)). This ensures that the resulting distribution remains bounded between the minimum
and maximum degradation levels, while emphasizing the operationally realistic intermediate states
where components are most likely to operate.

2.5. Definition of a criterion for ranking the enhanced plant variants
 Once the simulations are completed, the results from the 𝑁 = 100 snapshots are aggregated to
provide an estimate of the minimum power capacity of each plant variant under degraded operating
conditions. Each of the simulated operating conditions is characterized by different levels of
performance degradation across system components, yielding 𝑁 values of the BOP’s residual
capacity. These values are treated as realizations of a stochastic variable, collectively forming a
 29

sample from the corresponding probability distribution. Because all plant variants are evaluated
over the same set of scenarios, their resulting loss distributions are directly comparable.
 The comparison between two enhanced plant variants, A and B, can be interpreted as a
comparison between two stochastic distributions. To compare such empirical distributions and
establish a ranking between them, we employ the Probability of Outperformance (PoO). This
metric quantifies the likelihood that a randomly selected observation from one distribution exceeds
a randomly selected observation from another (𝑃(𝑋 > 𝑋 )). Given two samples {𝐴 } and
 𝐵 , PoO can be estimated as shown in Eq.(14).

 PoO was first introduced by Wolfe and Hogg in 1971 [9] and is widely applied in diverse
fields. In financial applications, for example, it is used to estimate the probability that one portfolio
will outperform another on a given trading day [10]. A key advantage of PoO is its intuitive
probabilistic interpretation and its independence from distributional assumptions. It captures the
degree of overlap between two distributions and is particularly useful for comparing simulation
outcomes that may exhibit skewness, heavy tails, or other non-Gaussian characteristics. When
applied to empirical data, PoO serves as a robust and interpretable indicator of relative
performance between competing alternatives. Regarding our application, a higher PoO indicates
an increased ability to maintain service continuity and a greater likelihood of meeting the IP
demand. For example, if 𝑃(𝑋 > 𝑋 ) = 0.75, Variant A outperforms Variant B in 75% of all
possible pairwise comparisons, i.e., it demonstrates superior productivity in 75% of the modeled
operating conditions. Dominance does not require every value from A to exceed its counterpart
from B; rather, a majority probability (e.g., 75%) is sufficient. Under the ergodicity assumption,
this is equivalent to stating that Variant A is expected to provide higher residual capacity than
Variant B for 75% of the plant’s operational lifetime. This is the rationale for using PoO to compare
plant variants under degraded performance scenarios.
 As for the limitations, PoO does not provide a quantitative guarantee that one variant will
consistently meet demand more effectively than another. It measures the relative installed capacity
of two variants under degraded performance, estimating the likelihood that a random capacity
snapshot from one will exceed that of the other. This does not extend to comparisons with a third
stochastic variable—such as instantaneous demand—unless additional assumptions are made. In
the present analysis, neither the high-temperature heat demand of the IP nor its temporal variability
has been characterized. Without such data, no direct probability of meeting demand can be
computed. The only inference that can be drawn is that a variant demonstrating consistently higher
power capacity relative to another has a greater likelihood of meeting the IP demand.

3.3. Design constraints for Rankine BOP components across all configurations
 Design constraints were applied to individual component parameters to ensure compliance
with operational limits. While this leads to lower predicted performance compared to earlier
projections [4], it results in plant models that better reflect realistic operating conditions. The
following constraints were enforced across all BOP configurations analyzed in this study.

 Maximum pressure of the steam compressed by the Heat Pump
 The design must account for the maximum allowable discharge pressure from the heat pump’s
 compression stage. This constraint is influenced by both material limits and safety
 considerations, and it directly affects the compressor configuration. Regardless of the specific
 location where steam is extracted for the heat pump, the maximum allowable pressure has been
 set at 345 bar [11]. Since no specific industrial sector - and thus no definitive steam
 requirements in terms of flow rate, pressure, or temperature - has been selected, all
 configurations were standardized to deliver steam at the maximum attainable pressure.

 Pressure of the exhaust saturated steam from the IP
 After transferring heat to the IP, the steam is assumed to return to the plant as saturated vapor
 at 200 bar, ensuring it is no longer in a supercritical state.

 Deaerator pressure
 The deaerator is vital to the steam cycle because it protects every downstream component by
 removing dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide from the condensate— essential to prevent
 rapid corrosion of piping, turbines, and related equipment —while also functioning as a surge
 tank and water reservoir. To minimize gas solubility and ensure adequate suction for the
 feedwater pumps, it operates above atmospheric pressure. Structural limitations restrict the
 maximum nominal pressure to 20 bar.

 Maximum moisture content in the steam exhausted from the turbine
 An upper bound on this parameter is necessary to prevent erosion and mechanical damage to
 turbine blades. This constraint determines the number of steam separators (or moisture removal
 stages) between turbine stages to ensure steam quality remains within acceptable range. This
 limit has been assumed to be 0.9 for the high- and intermediate pressure turbines, and 0.85 for
 the low-pressure turbines.

 Condenser pressure
 The pressure at the condenser is typically fixed based on ambient cooling conditions and
 targeted condensation temperature. This value plays a critical role in determining overall cycle
 efficiency and influences the thermodynamic boundaries for heat rejection in the system. In
 this work the minimum nominal condenser pressure was fixed at 0.07 bar which corresponds
 to a water saturation temperature of approximately 42 °C.

 35

 Reheat of the steam flow
 Each configuration incorporates a live‐steam extraction directly from the SG outlet to ensure
 adequate reheating of the steam flow.

3.4. Integration of Heat Pump system into the Reference Rankine BOP configuration
 After completing the modeling of the reference PRISM BOP, the analysis advanced to
configurations incorporating heat pumps, namely AR-730 and AR-780 [4]. Each BOP was
designed to route all available steam to the compressor, except for a fraction allocated to reheating
the steam exiting the high-pressure turbine. While steam extraction poses limited challenges from
a thermodynamic balance perspective, re-injecting the steam coming from IP at high pressure and
near-critical temperatures proves to be far more challenging. In all configurations, the saturated
steam is reinjected directly into a steam turbine. However, considering the operational limits
previously discussed, this approach requires caution. To avoid falling below the lower bound on
the steam quality (Section 3.3), multiple stages of steam reheating are necessary. This led to an
updated version of each configuration, all of which were optimized to maximize the thermal power
transferred to the IP. The AR-730+ layout [4] is omitted because its sole distinction from AR-730
is the use of shaft-coupled turbines and compressors, which enhances overall efficiency. This
shaft-coupling modification can be applied to any configuration, so AR-730+ will be treated not
as a separate variant but as an optional efficiency upgrade for all conversion systems. Table 3-4
summarizes the outlet temperatures, flow rates, power transfers, and efficiency indicators for three
advanced reactor variants, highlighting their relative performance in supplying both heat and
electricity.

3.4.1.    AR-730 configuration
 AR-730 configuration is shown in Figure 3-2a. Key outputs and performance metrics are listed
in Table 3-4. This design provides a balanced energy deliverable, supplying roughly 280 MWₜₕ of
steam at just under 615 °C and producing a net electrical output of approximately 155 MWₑₗ.

3.4.2.    AR-780 configuration
 AR-780 configuration is shown in Figure 3-2b. From the standpoint of thermal energy supply,
this configuration stands out, delivering more than 303 MW th to the IP, with steam temperatures
exceeding 658 °C. This performance is enabled by a turbine expansion stage located upstream of
the compressor inlet, which lowers the compressor inlet pressure. The lower the pressure at the
compressor inlet, the higher the resulting outlet temperature. However, it is crucial to maintain
sufficient superheating of the steam entering the heat pump to avoid instabilities or cavitation
phenomena. The trade-off is that the larger pressure ratio handled by the compressor reduces the
system’s net electricity generation, which reaches just over 109 MWₑₗ. Due to its higher thermal
output and greater temperature rise, as evidenced by superior performance metrics, AR-780 has

 36

been chosen as the most efficient configuration and the optimal candidate to be further improved
for the cogeneration objectives of this study.

3.4.3. AR-780r configuration
 In both AR-730 and AR-780 configurations, three live‐steam reheating stages were
incorporated to prevent excessively wet steam from entering the turbine. Although this approach
is thermodynamically effective, a three‐stage regenerative turbine train is unconventional. To
simplify the design, we replaced the multiple reheaters with interstage moisture separators and a
single reheating stage. Figure 3-2c depicts the updated layout (“AR-780r” or baseline). Optimized
for maximum heat delivery to the industrial process, this configuration achieves:

 Thermal output: ~356 MWₜₕ
 Compressor outlet steam temperature: ~ 659 °C
 Net electrical output: ~39 MWₑₗ
 Heat Factor: 3.79

Redesigning the system to preserve adequate steam dryness hinges on the conditions under which
steam returns from the IP. Because this steam is saturated at pressures and temperatures near the
critical point, it is challenging to expand it without falling below the saturation curve. Multiple
high-pressure, high-temperature drainage flows are then necessary. Although these streams can be
used to preheat feedwater, they must first be cooled sufficiently to prevent flashing at the throttle
valves. Unlike a conventional regenerative Rankine cycle, the point of highest working-fluid
pressure in this layout is not downstream of the feedwater pump. As a result, the first turbine stages
see pressures up to 50 bar above the feedwater line conditions. Therefore, saturated water drained
from the interstage moisture separators must be cooled and throttled before being sent back into
the feedwater line.
In this configuration, the pressure of every drain line—and the reheater discharge— was set equal
to the deaerator’s inlet pressure. While this approach sacrifices some of the available enthalpy in
those high-pressure flows, it ensures that, under partial-load conditions and in the absence of
sophisticated control logic, no undesirable pressure-gradient reversals occur between the turbine
string and feedwater circuit. Another measure to protect the SG is to implement a controlled bleed
of live steam into the heat exchanger that provides the final temperature boost to the feedwater.
Inlet temperature to the SG is an extremely critical parameter (too-cold feedwater can induce
thermal stresses in the piping that may severely compromise its integrity). As an alternative, one
could replace that exchanger with a bleed-steam preheater tapping low-pressure turbine steam,
supplemented by an electric heater to achieve finer control of the outlet temperature.
We also use a second, carefully controlled live-steam bleed to ensure the steam leaving the
moisture separator and reheater remains superheated and as close as possible to its nominal
temperature. This protects the blades in the first stages of the intermediate-pressure turbines, which
are normally engineered to operate within strict temperature limits.

3.5.1. Identification and modeling of component failure mechanisms
 After selecting AR-780r as the baseline configuration, two primary degradation mechanisms
were identified for each major component (Table 3-5). Components absent from AR-780r (e.g.,
steam drum) or not explicitly modeled (e.g., piping and connectors) were excluded. Before
simulating multi-component failures—typical of real-world plant operation—a sensitivity analysis
with single-event scenarios was conducted to identify critical components whose failures most
severely affect overall power capacity and therefore require the highest level of protection. A
sensitivity analysis using single‐event scenarios was conducted within the Ebsilon modeling
framework to identify which components need the highest level of protection. Each failure
scenario targets a single component, specifying:

 A single performance parameter (𝑝 ) among those listed as affected parameters in Table 3-5,
 representing degradation (e.g., heat-transfer coefficient for heat exchangers, flow coefficient
 for valves, or isentropic efficiency for turbomachinery).
 A failure-magnitude range (α ) describing progressive performance deterioration. This
 range was defined using vendor datasheets, maintenance records, and historical failure data;
 when unavailable, conservative assumptions were derived from literature [13][14][15].

The simulation-based procedure follows these steps:

1. Baseline Simulation: run the Ebsilon model of the baseline configuration to evaluate

2. Parameter perturbation: for each component failure mode, the most representative performance

 (Table 3-5), producing a set of performance snapshots.
3. Failure scenario evaluation: a set of scenarios 𝑠 ∈ 𝑆, each representing a single failure mode,
 was simulated in Ebsilon, applying only the sampled α to the parameter of the target
 component and keeping all others at baseline values. Ebsilon’s standard performance and flow
 characteristic curves (Appendix B) were used to model system behavior under degraded
 conditions.
4. Results compilation: The resulting electrical (∆𝑊 ) and thermal (∆𝑄̇ ) power degradations
 were calculated (Eq.(15)). These are summarized in Table 3-6.

Even under degraded conditions, regulatory and safety constraints are enforced:

 Steam generator: thermal input power, outlet pressure, and outlet temperature
 Heat pump: inlet and outlet pressures
 Industrial process: outlet pressure and saturated-steam conditions
 Condenser: inlet and outlet pressures

 Feedwater Inlet to SG: controlled via live-steam flow into the final feedwater preheater
 MSR Outlet Steam Temperature: controlled via live-steam flow into the MSR

For the component representing the IP, outlet conditions are held constant, while the inlet
temperature is allowed to vary with the compressor’s isentropic efficiency. If the compressor outlet
temperature increases during a failure scenario, a downstream control module extract the extra
heat to restore the design temperature, thereby protecting the IP interface components and meeting
process requirements.

 Steam Generator
 Because the Ebsilon model does not allow variations in the overall heat-transfer coefficient,

 side temperature or flow would undesirably alter reactor power. To avoid this, under prescribed
 inlet and outlet conditions, SG degradation is modeled by reducing FW flow, thereby lowering
 the imposed heat duty. This reduced duty is used as the fault-affected parameter in the analysis.

 Steam Turbine
 While fatigue and cracking may impact some stages more than others, uniform relative
 degradation is assumed across all stages for the selected performance parameter.

 Deaerator
 The Ebsilon model of the deareator is not equipped to account for varying dissolved-gas
 concentrations in the fluid, as accurately doing so would demand a far more complex modeling
 approach. Deaerator inefficiency is then approximated by applying performance penalties to

3.5.2. Sensitivity analysis of fault scenarios and implications for mitigation strategies
 When conducting the sensitivity analysis, in addition to the electrical and thermal power
degradations, the power capacity as a percentage of the nominal performance-based cost function
(Eq.(3)) was calculated. Figure 3-3 plots normalized power‐capacity curves. These results indicate
that the SG, the heat pump (HP) and the main steam turbine (ST) suffer the largest capacity losses
as degradation increases, making them prime candidates for robust design or redundancy. The
deaerator (DEA), the moisture separator-reheater (MSR), and the SG also yield non-negligible
impacts and merit targeted mitigation. Notably, some components—such as the SG—demonstrate
an almost linear capacity decline with fault severity, whereas others—like the steam valve
(TAV)—show a gentle initial drop followed by a sharp fall-off at higher degradation levels. This
suggests that early warning thresholds should differ by component. From an investment
standpoint, we should concentrate on redundant systems or oversizing for the most sensitive
equipment to preserve power capacity most effectively; for less critical assets, enhanced
maintenance routines may suffice. These findings let us strategically allocate resources to bolster
system robustness where it will have the greatest effect on maintaining power output.

Figure 3-3. Impact of BOP component failures on power capacity. For each failure, ten single-event
scenarios with progressively increasing severity were simulated.

 A notable outlier in our analysis is the steam condenser (SC). In currently operated plants, any
loss of vacuum or reduction in its heat‑transfer efficiency typically causes a significant drop in
power output. However, when we represented these faults in Ebsilon by raising condenser pressure

 48

to five times its nominal value, the simulated impact on overall capacity was negligible. This
discrepancy may stem from choosing a conservative range for the condenser’s failure severity
parameter, or from limitations in our Ebsilon model’s ability to capture vacuum-related losses.
Using pressure increase alone to mimic vacuum loss may not accurately reflect the real impact of
vacuum degradation on plant performance.

3.6. Modeling mitigation strategies to ensure continuity of service in Rankine BOP
 In this Section, we outline mitigation strategies for those components identified in Section
3.5.2 whose performance degradation has the greatest impact on the plant’s net power capacity.
Potential countermeasures can be grouped into four main categories:

 Component redundancy: Install parallel pumps or duplicate heat-exchangers so that the
 nominal load can be shared between units.
 Material upgrades: Replace vulnerable alloys with corrosion- or fatigue-resistant materials.
 Control‐logic enhancements: Implement adaptive setpoints or bleed-steam controllers to
 maintain optimal operating conditions despite degradation.
 Performance monitoring: Add sensors and deploy diagnostic algorithms to detect early signs
 of drift, fouling, or wear.

While Ebsilon may not capture every detail required for a one-to-one comparison of mitigation
strategies, the available features allow mimicking each measure’s effect. In cases where one or
more components are redundant, each component undergoes the independent degradation
processes listed in Table 3-5 (as noted in Section 2.2, common-cause failures are excluded from
this analysis). Even if all units degrade simultaneously, redundancy ensures that load is optimally
shared, thereby expanding the system’s operational margin. To maximize the effectiveness of
redundancy, both physical duplication and control strategies that dynamically adjust load sharing
based on each unit’s condition are required. For example, consider two parallel heat exchangers,
each handling half the flow under nominal conditions (Figure 3-4). If one exchanger degrades
more than the other, control valves redistribute the flow toward the less degraded unit to maintain
overall heat transfer. While such control is typically achieved through valve actuation,
recirculation flow paths, or pump speed modulation, we opted for a simplified approach in Ebsilon.
Each branch receives a flow rate proportional to its performance indicator—for heat exchangers,
this is the overall heat-transfer coefficient. Specifically, flow splitting was modeled using splitter
components with predefined input values that allocate flow based on the degradation level of each
unit.

 49

Figure 3-4. Flow-modulation strategy for two parallel heat exchangers to compensate for degraded
heat-transfer performance.

Below, we present and discuss the proposed mitigation measures for each major component.

 Steam generator
 Since in the nuclear industry each SG is custom-designed to match its reactor’s specific
 characteristics, only broadly applicable mitigation strategies were considered. One such
 approach is oversizing the heat transfer surface to provide extra capacity and compensate for
 performance degradation over time. This simple solution offers a reliable safety margin
 without requiring detailed design specifics. Another strategy involves protecting the SG from
 thermal stresses that lead to cracking and corrosion, using corrosion-resistant alloys and
 advanced surface treatments to reduce fatigue and material damage.
 These interventions help maintain the integrity of the surfaces and preserve heat transfer
 efficiency. Since the overall heat transfer coefficient cannot be directly adjusted in Ebsilon, we
 simulate both oversizing and enhanced stress protection as an increase in the SG’s nominal
 heat duty by 5% corresponding to 42.5 MWth. By treating each fault as a reduction in duty,
 adding this extra capacity ensures that any degradation below this threshold does not appear in
 the model, thereby capturing the intended mitigation effect.

 Steam turbine
 In the Ebsilon model, turbine performance degradation is primarily represented by a reduction
 in isentropic efficiency, accounting for effects such as blade fatigue and rotor imbalance.
 Additional penalties are applied when inlet/outlet pressures or mass flows deviate from
 nominal values, as captured through performance characteristic curves. To simulate the
 benefits of using high-fatigue-strength materials and erosion-resistant coatings, two mitigation
 strategies are implemented:

 o 5% reduction in the Baumann factor effect across all turbine stages
 o insertion of interstage moisture separators between the LP stages.

 50

 The Baumann factor is an empirical coefficient used to estimate efficiency losses due to steam
 moisture, particularly relevant in low-pressure stages where expansion often pushes steam
 below the saturation line. However, in the system under study, even higher-pressure stages
 may experience saturation conditions after the industrial heat extraction. Despite this, low-
 pressure stages remain the most sensitive, justifying the use of additional moisture separation
 as a mitigation strategy.
 The benefits of vibration-damping measures—such as tuned/untuned blade groups or curtain-
 steam protection—are primarily reflected in a slower rate of turbine performance degradation
 over time. To simulate improved turbine lifespan as a mitigation strategy in Ebsilon, a 2%
 reduction in the impact of failure mechanisms on the isentropic efficiency was applied.

 Deaerator
 Maintaining the deaerator at its design pressure is critical for two main reasons. First, the
 deaerator heats the feedwater to its saturation temperature—determined by pressure—to
 effectively remove dissolved gases. A pressure drop impairs gas removal by increasing
 solubility, while a pressure rise can trigger flashing or cause valve instability. Second, the
 deaerated water is sent directly to the high-pressure feedwater pump, where insufficient inlet
 pressure may lead to cavitation.
 To address these issues in the Ebsilon model, we implemented:

 o Active pressure control: A control valve was added to the steam extraction line to maintain
 the deaerator pressure at its set-point.
 o Booster pump: A low-head auxiliary pump was placed upstream of the main feedwater
 pump to ensure adequate suction pressure and prevent cavitation.
 o “2+1” pump redundancy: Two pumps operate in parallel while a third unit remains in
 standby, ready to activate in case of failure. Though standby units do not affect steady-state
 simulations, this setup reflects common industry practices and will be included in future
 cost-benefit analyses.
 o High pressure-preheater redundancy and oversizing: An additional feedwater preheater
 was introduced downstream of the deaerator to maintain heat transfer performance in case
 the primary preheater degrades. The heat exchange surface area was also oversized to
 accommodate surface fouling or wear.

 Moisture separator and reheater
 The MSR is a large, mostly passive component, typically oversized and built with durable,
 serviceable materials. Rather than duplicating the unit, we implement partial redundancy by
 splitting high-pressure steam into two parallel trains, each with its own separator and reheater,
 allowing continued operation if one fails. Oversizing is further represented by increasing
 separator volume and reheater surface area to improve moisture removal and thermal tolerance.

 51

 Because Ebsilon does not allow direct input of a reheater’s heat duty, we mimic this setup by
 adding a second reheater, effectively simulating the impact of both redundancy and oversizing.
 In addition, our simulations include control logic for regulating heating-steam temperature and
 pressure and monitoring outlet temperature to prevent tube overheating and protect
 downstream turbine blades.

 Valve
 Although the fault analysis in Section 3.5.2 revealed that valve blockages or steam leakage can
 significantly impact plant performance, the valves involved—such as control and regulating
 valves—are typically not physically duplicated like safety or relief valves. Instead, they rely
 on functional redundancy, where the same control function is carried out by multiple units. For
 instance, the turbine inlet valve (TAV) is usually implemented as several parallel valves rather
 than one. We applied the same assumption to the heat pump inlet valve (V-HP), but not to the
 live-steam extraction valve (V-1).
 To simulate functional redundancy for TAV and V-HP in Ebsilon, we modeled each valve and
 its associated flow path as two parallel branches. However, the valve component in Ebsilon is
 designed to accept a specified pressure drop (∆𝑝) as input and then calculate the design flow
 coefficient (𝐶𝑣) based on the given flowrate. This architecture prevents placing two parallel
 valves with different pressure losses, something that would naturally occur when using
 different sampled degradation levels corresponding to different ∆𝑝 values. To address this
 limitation, the following approach was used:

 o For each of the two valves, the design flow coefficient was calculated based on the sampled

3.7.2. Ranking of the analyzed BOP variants
 Once the degraded power capacity values were computed for each configuration across the
same 100 scenarios, the results were compared using the PoO metric introduced in Section 2.5.
This pairwise comparison method considers only the frequency with which one configuration
outperforms another, without accounting for the magnitude of the performance difference.
Comparisons were then conducted independently for each pair of configurations, and the resulting
pairwise probabilities must be aggregated or visualized (e.g., via ranking matrices or directed
graphs) to establish an overall performance hierarchy.
 The analysis produced a 16 × 16 matrix, where each entry represents the probability that the
configuration in the corresponding row outperforms the configuration in the corresponding
column. To derive a ranked list, the matrix was processed using a bubble sort algorithm, which
iteratively compares adjacent pairs and moves the higher‐ranking configuration upward in the list
[16]. Although bubble sort is generally inefficient for large or unstable datasets, a consistency
check confirmed the absence of logical cycles (i.e., circular contradictions that would prevent
establishing a coherent ranking). Potential uncertainty due to statistical noise, particularly when
probabilities fall within an indecisive range (e.g., 0.45-0.55), was also assessed. Only 3.68% of
matrix entries fell within this interval, indicating low noise and supporting the robustness of the
resulting ranking. To confirm these results, we applied the Bradley-Terry model [17], i.e., a
probabilistic framework that estimates a relative strength score (BT score) for each configuration
from all pairwise comparisons. The two approaches yielded nearly identical rankings, differing
only in the ordering of the last two positions. The final ranking, summarized in Table 3-10,
identified configuration MIT-10 (Figure 3-8) as the most likely to maintain superior power
capacity under degraded conditions.

scenarios, the performance of plant variants was evaluated by starting from configuration MIT-10

identifying the truly optimal configuration would require analyzing all possible combinations of
mitigation strategies. While such an exhaustive evaluation was beyond the scope of the present
study, it represents a logical extension for future work. At the same time, the analysis revealed that
other mitigation measures—including deaerator pressure control or the addition of interstage
moisture separators in the low-pressure turbine—tend to reduce overall system performance. This
is due to two main reasons:

1. For each failure scenario, the software tends to identify deaerator operating conditions that are

 instead designed to maximize power production.

2. The reduction in turbine mass flow rate caused by steam extraction through the interstage
 moisture separators decreases the output to a greater extent than the gain achieved by
 mitigating the degradation effects associated with the Baumann factor.

 Nevertheless, several of the proposed strategies represent deliberate design choices introduced
not to increase output capacity but to protect components from abrupt transients or operating
conditions that would otherwise accelerate wear and tear. Their primary benefit lies in extending
component lifetime and mitigating damaging effects. This is reflected in the ranking results of
configuration MIT-9, which primarily incorporates such protective strategies and appears in close
proximity to the baseline case. Despite their limited impact on output performance, these measures
remain highly valuable for long-term plant preservation. Their inclusion underscores the
importance of adopting evaluation criteria that capture not only output-based performance but also
system reliability and durability. This beneficial effect, however, has not been explicitly quantified
in the present study. A more comprehensive analysis could incorporate a cost function that, in
addition to evaluating service continuity (e.g., through a value of lost load term), also accounts for
the savings in operation and maintenance (O&M) costs achieved by implementing corrective or
protective strategies.

4. DESIGN AND ASSESSMENT OF A ROBUST RANKINE-BASED BOP FOR
 PARTIAL LOAD OPERATION

 To complete the design of the thermal interface between the nuclear plant and the industrial
end-user, the analysis also considers scenarios characterized by variable industrial energy demand.
A key design driver is the need to guarantee continuous and reliable service despite fluctuations in
thermal load requirements. Unlike the electric power sector, industrial facilities are typically
subject to different market dynamics, which result in time-varying energy needs—often influenced
by seasonal production cycles or shifts in market conditions. Consequently, the thermal demand
profile is inherently non-uniform and requires a flexible response from the heat supply system.
 Rather than modulating the reactor power, the implemented paradigm maintains a constant
thermal output from the reactor while dynamically shifting energy delivery between high-quality
heat and electricity in response to IP demand. To accommodate such variations, the BOP must
incorporate flexible systems capable of adapting to partial-load conditions. Specifically, during
periods of reduced thermal demand, the heat pump load should be decreased, and, where possible,
additional steam should be routed through the turbines to increase electricity generation. However,
the plant’s ability to respond is limited by component-level design constraints, which must be
carefully identified. Multiple alternative configurations were evaluated, each designed to ensure
the operability and reliability of the BOP under partial load conditions. Among these options, the
configuration that delivered the highest overall plant output was selected as the preferred solution.

4.1. Identification of Extraction and Reinjection points in the Rankine-based BOP
 Building upon the MIT-10 variant introduced in Section 3.7, this final variant incorporates two
additional elements: a bypass line to redirect a portion of the steam flow rate and a heat exchanger
to superheat the saturated steam returning from the IP. A more detailed rationale for these
modifications is presented in the following sections. Reducing the thermal power delivered to the
IP, while maintaining fixed inlet and outlet thermodynamic conditions, requires reducing the steam
flow rate supplied to the IP. Two solutions are available:

 Compress the steam exiting the HP-1 turbine to upgrade its quality but use it for alternative
 purposes rather than IP before reintegrating it into the cycle.
 Avoid compressing the steam from the turbine stage (or compress it only partially) so that it
 can be diverted for different purposes.

 While both strategies enable the plant to operate under partial-load conditions, they differ in
how they affect component performance, and the required system modifications. A key concern in
either approach is the departure of compressors and turbines from their nominal operating points.
These components are designed to operate within well-defined thermal and mechanical limits.
Exceeding these boundaries may lead to reduced performance or, in severe cases, compromise

 65

structural integrity. A key objective of this analysis is to identify the BOP’s safe operating
envelope.
 Both the proposed modifications to the MIT-10 variant involves introducing a bypass line that
diverts a portion of the steam before it enters the compressor and reinjects it somewhere
downstream of the IP. Reducing the load on the heat pump (potentially accompanied by increased
steam flow through the turbines) alters the thermodynamic cycle’s operating points. It is then
essential to monitor the main process variables and ensure they remain within the plant’s safe
operating envelope. When defining a bypass line, the first critical decision involves selecting the
extraction and reinjection points within the plant. The steam extraction points (EP) considered
were:

 Point 1 (EP1): The HP-1 turbine inlet line (150 bar, 452 °C). It corresponds to the conventional
 turbine safety bypass location in traditional steam power plants.
 Point 2 (EP2): The heat pump inlet line (26 bar, 226 °C). It avoids bypassing the initial high-
 pressure turbine expansion stage, which under design conditions contributes to over one-third
 of the mechanical shaft power.
 Point 3 (EP3): A downstream location within an intermediate stage of the heat pump
 compressor (with pressure and temperature depending on the selected stage). It allows the
 steam to undergo partial compression and quality enhancement before bypassing.

The choice of extraction points limits the possible reinjection locations within the system. Below
are summarized all the reinjection points (RP) evaluated in this study, though not all are compatible
with every extraction point listed previously:

 Point 1 (RP1): The inlet to the MSR, between the high- and intermediate-pressure turbines
 Point 2 (RP2): The outlet of the MSR, between the high- and intermediate-pressure turbines
 Point 3 (RP3): A downstream location within an intermediate stage between the intermediate-
 and low-pressure turbines

4.2. Modification to the selected enhanced variant of Rankine-based BOP configuration
 For each extraction point evaluated, the analysis revealed that the turbine stages downstream
of the IP (HP-2) are among the most vulnerable components under partial-load conditions. Since
the steam conditions returning from the IP heat exchanger are maintained constant, any change in
flow through the heat pump directly affects the downstream turbines. Specifically, an increase in
bypassed steam flow raises the moisture content at the outlet of the turbine HP-2 first stage, posing
a threat to blade integrity. This effect is a direct consequence of Stodola’s law for steam turbines,
which states that a reduction in mass flow leads to a drop in inlet pressure, and vice versa. Among
the configurations tested, the strategy that performed best—without requiring additional
modifications—involved extracting steam from the HP-1 turbine admission line (150 bar), point
EP-1, and reinjecting it at RP2. However, this option is viable only for moderate reductions in
thermal power delivered to the IP. The minimum achievable thermal output was calculated to be
326 MWth (about 8% below nominal), at which point the steam quality at the outlet of the first HP
turbine stage dropped to 0.85, while the net electric power output exceeded 58 MW e—representing
nearly a 50% increase compared to the design case.
 To address the moisture content issue, a second approach was explored using the bypassed
steam itself. In this configuration, the bypassed steam is used to reheat the IP outlet saturated steam
before reinjection into the HP-2 turbine (RP4) by simply mixing the two steam flows. Since the
HP-2 turbine train inlet pressure is at 200 bar, the bypassed steam had to be compressed to at least
that pressure prior to reinjection. This required connecting the bypass branch to a compressor stage
just above 200 bar. While effective in principle, the configuration introduced two challenges:

1. the HP-2 turbine inlet pressure to exceed the target 200 bar.
2. The MSR reheater required more hot steam for effective reheating. This reheated steam, in
 turn, is directed to preheat the feedwater, which risks overheating the deaerator and the SG
 inlet beyond safe operational limits. Reducing the reheating level is not a viable solution, as it
 would increase moisture content at the LP turbine outlet to unacceptable levels.

 To overcome these issues while complying with thermodynamic limits requires the
introduction of a second design modification to the MIT-10 variant, consisting of an additional
heat exchanger, which receives a portion of the supercritical steam leaving the compressor and
utilizes it to superheat the returning saturated steam. This configuration was tested with bypass
implementation at EP4 (reinjection at RP2) and EP3 (reinjection at RP3). Both met performance
constraints, but the second arrangement delivered superior results and greater flexibility. In
particular, this design allowed the plant to operate with the thermal load delivered to the IP reduced
to just over 70% of nominal (252 MWth), while achieving a 167% increase in net electric output
(~113 MWe). This limit was arbitrary to prevent the steam quality at the outlet of the first HP-2
expansion stage from dropping below 0.9. Although higher steam superheating could theoretically
enable operation below 70% of nominal load, it was decided to limit the HP-2 inlet temperature
increase during part-load operation to +5 °C above nominal, to preserve safe operating margins.

 An important aspect that emerged from the analysis is the behavior of the compressor at
reduced flow rates. Compressors are typically designed to operate at a point on their characteristic
curve that does not coincide with peak efficiency. Many compressors are configured to operate
slightly beyond their maximum efficiency point to prevent surge phenomena, which are more
likely at lower flow rates. This behavior—accurately reproduced by the performance curves
implemented in the Ebsilon model—results in an increase in compression efficiency during partial-
load operation as steam flow decreases. A direct consequence is a drop in steam temperature at the
heat pump outlet. Since maintaining sufficiently high steam temperatures is essential to meet IP
requirements, this effect must be carefully addressed during compressor design.
 Another key design aspect concerns the choice between a single-shaft and a multi-shaft
compressor arrangement. While single-shaft designs provide advantages in terms of compactness,
cost-effectiveness, and mechanical simplicity, this study adopted a two-shaft configuration. For
large-scale compressors, a multi-shaft setup reduces the risk of vibrations and stage misalignments,
and it facilitates maintenance. In addition, this configuration allows independent optimization of
geometry and rotational speed for each compressor section, which further enhances overall
performance [18].

4.3. Design of a robust Rankine-based BOP for managing unpredicted loss of IP demand
4.3.1. Definition of Anticipated Operational Occurrence (AOO) events
 The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (U.S. NRC) defines an AOO as a postulated event
expected to occur one or more times during the operational lifetime of a plant [19]. Typical
examples include turbine trips, load rejections, loss of feedwater pumps, or rapid reductions in
steam demand. From a licensing perspective, AOOs must be demonstrated not to challenge
fundamental safety limits (e.g., fuel integrity, pressure boundary integrity). Reactor protection and
control systems are specifically designed to manage AOOs without significant safety
consequences. Although coupling a nuclear reactor with an industrial process provides significant
economic and operational benefits, it also introduces the vulnerability of abrupt demand loss. In
such cases, rapid control actions—analogous to turbine runbacks in conventional plants—are
required to reduce the industrial heat load or heat pump demand and, where feasible, transition to
electricity-only operation. These transients occur abruptly and require prompt corrective action but
remain within the category of AOOs rather than accidents.
 One of the objectives of this study is to introduce the necessary configuration improvements
to manage the consequences on the PRISM BOP of a sudden loss of heat demand from the IP. This
transient is categorized as an AOO because the IP is never credited as a safety-grade heat sink in
reactor licensing. Its loss alone does not constitute a nuclear safety event, as the industrial facility
is responsible for its own safety. The nuclear plant must nevertheless ensure that the interface with
the IP is fail-safe—through isolation valves, check valves, relief devices, and interlocks—to
protect against backflows, pressure surges, and other hazards. Only if the BOP simultaneously
loses the ability to reject heat to the ultimate heat sink (e.g., condenser, steam dump, or cooling
tower) would the event escalate into a Loss of Heat Sink (LOHS) accident, which is analyzed
separately as a design-basis event. When industrial steam demand is suddenly lost, the BOP diverts
steam to the turbine or to bypass/dump systems, while reactor controls initiate a power runback to
rebalance core power with the available heat sink. As long as system pressures and temperatures
remain within prescribed limits, safety criteria are not challenged.

4.3.2. Modification to the optimized Rankine-based BOP
 When the IP suddenly ceases to require high-quality heat, the BOP must continue to operate
safely and efficiently while redirecting or dissipating excess thermal energy. Although developing
a detailed automatic control sequence is beyond the scope of this study, several design measures
can be identified to strengthen plant resilience. These fall into three broad categories.

1. Immediate Backstop Measures
 These provide near-instant relief from excess steam, protecting operators and systems and
 preventing escalation into reactor scram conditions.

 72

  Bypass to Condenser
 Excess steam is diverted directly to the condenser, bypassing both turbine stages and the heat
 pump. This solution allows the reactor to continue producing heat while maintaining safe
 pressure and flow balance. Widely adopted in Rankine power plants, the solution is
 straightforward to implement, but it reduces overall efficiency and increases the load on the
 condenser and cooling systems, which may require oversizing.

2. Short- to medium-term flexibility measures
 These strategies enable continued power production during IP outages. These include flexible
 turbine and compressor operation, designed to sustain efficient partial-load conditions and
 reduce dependance on diverting steam to main or dedicated condenser.

  Bypass to Turbine
 When an immediate intervention is not required and the heat pump load can be gradually
 reduced, the steam normally directed to the compressor can instead be routed to an
 intermediate- or low-pressure turbine stage. This allows the plant to suspend high-quality
 steam generation and operate in a full-electric mode. This solution bypasses major
 components—including the heat pump compressor, the high-pressure HP-2 section, and the
 MSR system. Two important consequences arise:

 o Live steam originally extracted for reheating is no longer needed and can instead expand
 through HP-1.
 o Skipping the MSR worsens steam quality in the final LP turbine stages, requiring at least
 one interstage moisture separator to remain within design limits.

 With these modifications, MIT-10 architecture demonstrated strong full-electric performance,
 producing nearly 326 MWe net output corresponding to a power capacity of 82.5% and a
 thermal efficiency exceeding 38%.

3. Long-term value-recovery strategies
 These measures transform transient IP outages into opportunities by storing surplus energy for
 later use.

  Thermal Energy Storage Integration
 Surplus steam can be stored in media such as molten salts, pressurized water, or phase-change
 materials, and later recovered. This option improves dispatchability and economics but
 requires storage technologies compatible with Rankine cycle conditions and incurs additional
 capital costs.

Each strategy carries different trade-offs. Bypass to condenser is a necessary short-term safeguard.
Bypass to turbine enables flexible full-electric operation but requires careful design modifications

 73

to manage steam quality. Thermal storage offers the greatest long-term value but is the most
complex to implement. Ultimately, the choice depends on balancing efficiency, capital investment,
and operational robustness.

5. DESIGN OF ROBUST THERMAL INTERFACE FOR A BRAYTON-CYCLE COUPLED
 WITH A GAS-COOLED FAST REACTOR

 In Sections 3 and 4, the proposed methodology was applied in detail to a Rankine-based power
conversion system, modified to include a heat pump and the associated extraction of high-quality
heat. The procedure itself is general and, in principle, can be applied to any type of BOP and
thermodynamic cycle. The only requirements to apply the mitigation strategy optimization are
knowledge of the baseline plant topology and a clear definition of the degradation mechanisms to
be investigated and included, appropriately described through an FMEA.
 In earlier reports [2][5], a second case study was analyzed focusing on the integration of a heat
pump within a gas-cycle-based plant. Specifically, the selected reactor and BOP architecture
correspond to the Gas Turbine–Modular Helium Reactor (GT-MHR) [20]. This section focuses
exclusively on the decisions that led to the baseline configuration of the GT-MHR BOP integrated
with a heat pump, while the selection of the relevant failure mechanisms can be done from the
FMEA detailed in [2].

5.1. Reference Brayton cycle configuration for electricity generation
 GT-MHR design originated from a joint collaboration launched in 1995 among organizations
under the Russian Ministry on Atomic Energy (MINATOM) and companies such as General
Atomics, Framatome, and Fuji Electric. GT-MHR employs TRISO fuel particles in a cylindrical
core moderated with prismatic graphite blocks and cooled with helium [21][22]. The same helium
flow rate serves as the working fluid for a gas turbine directly coupled to an electric generator. In
addition to advanced passive safety systems, one of the most notable advantages of this design is
its modularity: the original configuration envisages coupling four 600-MW th reactors to form a
single generating unit.
 For simplicity, the current analysis was limited to a single nuclear module, characterized by a
thermal power output of 600 MWth, with helium entering the reactor vessel at approximately 490
°C and 70 bar, and exiting at around 850 °C. Two configurations were considered: (1) the direct-
cycle option, in which the helium that cools the core directly drives the gas turbine; and (2) the
indirect-cycle option, in which an intermediate helium loop decouples the primary and secondary
circuits. The direct-cycle configuration is the most documented and has been the main focus of the
GT-MHR project. The indirect cycle has received less attention due to its inherent efficiency
penalty; however, it was included in this study because it had been proposed in a previous report,
where it was identified as the “Case-1” configuration [4]. In the absence of published reference
data for the intermediate loop, the same plant layout as the direct-cycle case was adopted, with the
sole modification of introducing a 20 °C temperature difference across the heat exchangers
between the intermediate and secondary helium sides.

5.2. Integration of Heat Pump system into the Reference Brayton BOP configuration
 After establishing the reference plant configuration (electricity-only) for both gas cycles, it was
decided to integrate the heat pump only in the direct cycle variant, since this configuration is better
documented in literature and shows superior performance. Two alternative layouts were then
assessed for the integrated baseline version, differing in the location of the heat pump.

 78

 Option-1 follows the plant layout of the “Case-2” configuration [4], in which the heat pump
 and the heat exchanger with the IP are located downstream of the last turbine stage (Figure 5-
 3a). The discharge pressure of the heat pump is determined by the electrical consumption of
 its integrated compressor, which must be fully supplied by the output of the gas turbine train.
 Under this condition, a maximum pressure rise of 20 bar allows the heat pump to raise the
 helium to 45 bar, corresponding to a temperature of about 748 °C. The gas is then delivered to
 the IP and returned to the BOP at 520 °C after extracting about 378 MW th of high-quality heat.
 The constraint on the return temperature was imposed to preserve the operating conditions of
 the recuperator: the cold-side outlet temperature must remain close to its nominal value (in this
 cycle, helium is responsible for cooling the fuel). Moreover, since the helium returning from
 the IP is already at a higher pressure than in the reference case, the first compression stages are
 not necessary any longer, and then they were removed.

 Option-2 implements the heat pump immediately downstream of the reactor vessel, raising the
 pressure to about 100 bar and generating hot gas above 1,050 °C (Figure 5-3b). After
 transferring nearly 340 MWth to the IP, the helium is routed to the gas turbine train that supplies
 the power required by the heat pump. In this configuration, the helium return temperature from
 the IP was fixed at 850 °C, equal to the turbine inlet temperature in the reference case. Since
 helium enters the turbine at a higher pressure than in the previous configurations, the discharge
 pressure of the last turbine stage was also increased accordingly so as to remain consistent with
 the compression ratio of the reference case.

 In both options, a positive net power margin to the generator was preserved, since the electrical
demand of the cooling-water pumps for the intercoolers was not included in the total plant power
balance. For each configuration, the corresponding heat factor was calculated (Table 5-2). The
numerator was defined as the sum of the total thermal input from the reactor and the thermal
equivalent of the heat pump consumption (converted using the thermal efficiency of the reference
case, equal to 0.53). The denominator was taken as the thermal output to the industrial process.
The two results (3.73 for Option 1 and 3.77 for Option 2) are nearly identical, as they do not
account for the quality of the delivered heat.
 From these results, it is evident that although Option-1 provides a slightly higher thermal
output, Option-2 is more promising, as it delivers higher-quality heat. While Option-1 raises
helium to a temperature even lower than that at the reactor outlet, Option- 2 reaches well above
1,000 °C. This significantly broadens the range of industrial applications that could be coupled,
including cement, glass, petrochemical, and steel production processes. Furthermore, because
helium operates at pressures significantly lower than the ~300 bar typically required for Rankine-
cycle steam, the associated components—e.g., the industrial-process heat exchanger—are
expected to present fewer design and feasibility challenges.

6. CONCLUSIONS

 The results presented in this work demonstrate that flexible thermal interfaces can be robustly
designed to ensure continuity of both electricity and high-grade heat delivery in Advanced
Reactors. The methodology developed herein provides three principal contributions:

1. Quantitative assessment of degraded operation – enabling direct comparison of enhanced plant
 variants under a wide range of degradation scenarios and operating conditions.

2. Identification of effective mitigation strategies – supporting the selection of design measures
 that balance thermodynamic efficiency, equipment protection, and long-term system
 resilience.

3. A foundation for integrated energy systems – establishing a methodology that can be extended
 to future applications where nuclear plants must adapt dynamically to variable industrial and
 grid demands.

The study applied a performance-oriented tool (Ebsilon Professional) to explore Rankine- and
Brayton-based BOP configurations. By systematically implementing and testing various
combinations of proposed mitigation strategies, it was possible to exclude suboptimal variants and
converge on sufficiently mature layouts for both system types. The resulting configurations
demonstrated the capability to preserve the continuity of service, even under conditions of
component degradation and partial-load operation. Importantly, the analysis highlighted that not
all mitigation strategies are aimed at boosting output performance; some are primarily designed to
protect critical equipment and extend component lifetime. As such, ranking methodologies must
ultimately account not only for capacity metrics but also for plant reliability, resilience, and
maintainability.

Limitations of the current Approach and Future Work
While the present analysis focused on steady-state degradation scenarios, this represents only one
aspect of plant resilience. Future work should:

 Extend the methodology to dynamic simulations of operational transients and upset conditions
 using tools such as Dymola, thereby increasing fidelity in modeling component behavior.
 Incorporate common-cause failures and system-level interactions, which may exacerbate
 vulnerability under certain degradation or upset conditions.
 Apply multi-objective optimization techniques to balance capital cost, operational flexibility,
 and economic performance.
 Explore the integration of thermal energy storage and other advanced concepts to further
 enhance dispatchability.

 82

By demonstrating that robust thermal interfaces can be configured to ensure service continuity
under both degraded and partial-load conditions, the study aligns with DOE priorities for resilient,
flexible, and economically competitive nuclear technologies. The methodology developed here
provides a replicable framework that can inform reactor-coupled industrial heat applications.
