 1. INTRODUCTION
Through the use of vehicle sensor data, the Driver Identification project seeks to
 1. identify and continuously authenticate drivers for applications to commercial vehicles
 2. detect high-risk driving states and behaviors
Success in these areas can greatly improve the security of the drivers, vehicles, and cargo.
Our previous work demonstrated continuous driver authentication on edge devices using livestreamed
vehicular data. The current work seeks both to improve the robustness and performance of driver
identification methods and extend these methods to detecting high-risk driving states and behaviors.
Moreover, we wanted to provide deployable and noninvasive solutions for driver identification and insider
threat detection.
In collaboration with researchers at Colorado State University (CSU) and University of Tennessee
Knoxville (UTK), we developed new in-vehicle sensors and machine learning models, performed a
pilot study data collection, and analyzed the associated results for driver identification and behavior
characterization. Within this report, we describe the non-intrusive sensor and model development, detail
the data and its collection, and provide results for driver identification. Finally, we close by detailing the
artifacts this project produced and future work in these areas.

 2. SENSOR DEVELOPMENT
For this study, there are two types of sensing systems utilized. The primary system is focused on being
non-intrusive and readily available on the vehicle’s Controller Area Network (CAN) bus, allowing it to
send signals alongside typical automotive sensors. The second system is for validation of the primary
systems. The types of data available on the SAE J1939 network, a standard built on top of CAN that is
used by medium and heavy-duty vehicles, include engineering signals like vehicle speed, accelerator pedal
position, brake pressure, and engine speed. These are ubiquitous signals across the fleet of heavy vehicles
in North America. However, the aim of the research effort is to augment typical J1939-based sensors with
a seat sensor to measure the dynamic weight of the driver on the seat. The hypothesis is that the driver
weight and balance during the dynamic activity of driving is sufficiently different among drivers. Clearly,
drivers can weigh different amounts, but they can also brace during turns differently, apply different
amounts of pressure on the pedals and floor, or perform other distinct motions and gestures. We posit that
this sensor could be a non-intrusive and discriminative feature for driver identification and continuous
authentication. Therefore, a seat sensor needed to be developed, deployed and tested to determine the
efficacy of this approach.

2.1 SEAT SENSOR DESIGN
2.1.1 Requirements
The seat sensor was designed against four practical requirements derived from the study goals. First, it
had to be non-intrusive so that a driver behaves naturally and no body-worn equipment is mandatory.
Second, it needed to be quick to install and remove, fitting an existing production truck seat with minimal
modification. Third, it had to expose its data on the vehicle’s CAN bus, so that seat measurements are
time-aligned with the standard J1939 engineering signals already present on the network. Finally, it must
capture not just a static occupant weight but the dynamic distribution of load across the seat, since the
discriminating information in the hypothesis lives in how a driver shifts, braces, and loads the seat while
driving.
To fulfill those requirements, we decided to install a collection of three shear-beam load cells, each with a
capacity of 1000 lbs. Two load cells are mounted on the front of the base of the seat and one load cell is
mounted to the rear. This forms a triangular pattern for the load cell placement, which uniquely measures
both the magnitude of the loads and the center of pressure on the seat. This configuration was inspired by
previous work on measuring athletes and their vertical jump height [1].
These shear-beam load cells are sold by a company called Rice Lake with a model of SEB RL32018,
as shown in Figure 1. They use a full wheatstone bridge sensing element that outputs 3mV/V at full
scale range. This four-wire sensor is excited and monitored by a signal conditioning system sold by
Sparkfun Electronics with a part number of SEN-15242 that sports a 24-Bit Dual-Channel analog to digital
converter with excitation for bridge circuits. This sensor breakout board interfaces with a microcontroller
on the Teensy 4.1 using I²C Channels. Each load cell has its own sensor breakout board. The Teensy
4.1 evaluation board uses an NXP iMX Microprocessor with i2C and Controller Area Network (CAN)
interfaces. The CAN interface is connected to the SAE J1939 network at 250kbps and will broadcast the
raw values from the load cell Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) directly onto the CAN bus. This gives
correlated signals since the vehicle status messages are on the same network.

2.1.2 Mechanical Design and Load Cells
The sensor is built around a production Kenworth T270 air-ride driver seat. A custom metal seat base
was fabricated to carry the seat while transferring the occupant’s load through the three load cells. The
three cells are arranged to resolve the load into three channels, referred to throughout the firmware and
user interface as A (driver left), B (driver right), and C (driver rear). This three-point arrangement is what
allows the system to measure balance and weight transfer rather than a single lumped weight: as the driver
leans into a turn or presses on the pedals and floor, load migrates between the three channels in a way that
a single scale could not capture. The mechanical concept began as a set of hand design sketches (Figure
2) that illustrated how the seat would tie to the cab floor through load cells at three points, including the
rod-end and steel-plate fastening detail at each cell.
The fabricated seat base executes this three-point scheme. The bare base (Figure 3) carries the occupant
load through mounts at three points - left front (channel A, driver-left), right front (channel B, driver-right),
and rear (channel C, driver-rear) - with each cell fastened to the base through a rod-end and a steel
mounting plate. Figure 4 shows the completed base with all three load cells installed and wired.

2.1.3 Electronics and Signal Chain
Each load cell is digitized by a SparkFun QWIIC Scale board built on the NAU7802 24-bit analog-to-digital
converter, which communicates over I²C. The three digitized channels are read by a Teensy 4.1 microcontroller,
which tares each channel, packs the three values into a single frame, and transmits it through an MCP2562
CAN transceiver onto the J1939 CAN bus. The first build was a breadboard prototype of the weighing
system using a Teensy 4.1; this design was subsequently migrated to a dedicated printed circuit board,
described in Section 2.1.4. Figure 5 summarizes the end-to-end signal chain.

2.1.4 Printed Circuit Board and Power/CAN Design
After the breadboard prototype proved out the signal chain, the weighing electronics were migrated to a
dedicated two-layer printed circuit board, laid out in Altium Designer, to improve robustness and make
installation repeatable. A guiding principle of the board design was risk reduction through reuse. For
the power and CAN sections in particular, the design utilized components and circuitry that had already
been built and tested for prior laboratory projects, selected specifically to reduce the risk inherent in new
designs and their verification processes. Reusing previous, well-tested circuits provides familiarity with

The same philosophy drove the load-cell front end. Rather than design a custom analog-to-digital
stage from a bare ADC, the project used the SparkFun QWIIC Scale boards (built on the NAU7802)
as commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) modules. While we considered using a discrete ADC, it would
have required significantly more work to get the full analog front end functioning correctly, along with
a substantial amount of additional design, testing, verification, and validation. The SparkFun converters
were attractive precisely because they are well supported and documented, and because they were already
built and tested with load cells in mind - which let the team treat the load-cell-to-I²C conversion as a
known-good building block. The three QWIIC Scale modules are the red SparkFun boards arranged across
the top of the populated board (Figure 7).
Because each NAU7802 presents the same fixed I²C address, the three scale boards cannot share a
single bus. The board therefore breaks out three independent I²C buses from the microcontroller -
SDA0/SCL0, SDA1/SCL1, and SDA2/SCL2 - with one converter per bus, plus a per-converter interrupt
line (INT0-INT2). Each load cell connects to its converter through the standard bridge wiring, following
the load-cell color code: red and black for the excitation supply (E+ / E-), green and white for the
differential signal (A+ / A-), and the yellow lead used for the shield/sense. On the fabricated board these
three converters appear as separate QWIIC Scale positions with their own I²C sections laid out side by side
(Figure 6).
On the power side, the most important design discipline was simply following the component datasheets
and pinouts and respecting each device’s operating voltages. The Teensy is powered from 5 V but uses
3.3 V logic levels, and the vehicle supplies a nominal 12 V that must be stepped down to a stable 5 V for
the electronics to operate. A Murata OKI-78SR-5/1.5-W36-C switching regulator performs this 12 V →
5 V conversion (up to 1.5 A) as a drop-in, pre-qualified module. The 12 V input is protected by a series
1N5408 rectifier against reverse polarity, together with a Zener clamp and a transient-voltage-suppression
(TVS) device to absorb vehicle-bus transients, while bulk (220 µF) and decoupling (100 nF) capacitors and
small series inductors stabilize the rails. A green LED indicates that the board is powered and a red LED
indicates CAN activity; the step-down regulator, the two bulk electrolytic capacitors, and both indicator
LEDs are visible on the populated board (Figure 7).
The CAN interface uses an MCP2562 transceiver (labeled near the top edge of the fabricated board,
Figure 10) between the Teensy’s CAN controller and the physical bus, with a 120Ω resistor providing bus
termination at the board. Off-board wiring is consolidated through a 12-way Deutsch DTM connector
(DTM13-12PA) that carries the three load-cell harnesses together with the CAN and power leads into
the enclosure, while six 5-pin headers land the three SparkFun scale boards. Table 1 lists the principal
components of the board.
2.2 CAN PROTOCOL AND FIRMWARE
The sensor publishes a single CAN frame on extended identifier 0x0CFF00FE at a bus rate of 250
kbit/s. Frames are eight bytes long and are produced at roughly 20 Hz (one sample every 50 ms). Within
the payload, the three signed channel values are stored in little-endian byte order. The original design
specification packs three 20-bit signed values - bits 0–19 for A, bits 20-39 for B, and bits 40–59 for C,
with the top four bits unused - where each value is the 24-bit tared reading shifted right by four bits. The
production decoder used by the acquisition software reads the first six bytes as three signed 16-bit integers
and rescales them by a factor of 256 to recover the full-magnitude channel value. The receiver only accepts
frames whose identifier is 0x0CFF00FE and whose length is exactly eight bytes; all other traffic on the bus
is ignored.

 3. MODEL DEVELOPMENT
3.1 BACKGROUND
Driver identification from in-vehicle sensor data treats driving style as a behavioral biometric. Throttle
usage, braking, steering patterns, and physiological responses jointly encode person specific patterns that
persist across time. Unlike traditional credentials such as keys, cards, or passwords, behavioral signals
cannot be lost, stolen, or duplicated, and they remain observable continuously throughout a trip. This
makes them well-suited to securing the transport of radiological and other hazardous materials.
There are two practical constraints that shape the problem. The first is scalability: a deployable system
must be able to distinguish among a mid- to large-sized pool of authorized drivers while also flagging
unknown drivers, and methods that work well with 10 drivers often degrade at higher pool sizes due to
overlapping behavioral patterns. The second is temporal generalization: a model must identify a driver at
a later time or day it has never seen, and on a new route or a different part of an existing route it has never
seen. Many published methods sidestep both constraints by using random cross-validation, which mixes
samples from the beginning and end of the same drive into both training and test sets. This practice inflates
reported accuracy by tens of percentage points and obscures whether a model has learned driving behavior
or simply memorized the session context.
Our work addressed these constraints in three coordinated efforts. First, we developed a leakage-aware data
wrangling pipeline that reduces 816 raw signals to 19 behavioral features while removing session-specific
signals. Second, we conducted a benchmarking study across two datasets and two evaluation protocols
that quantifies the cost of leakage and establishes realistic baselines. Third, we developed Temporal
Embeddings for Multimodal Pattern Extraction on Sequential Tasks (TEMPEST), a temporal embedding
model trained with an additive angular margin loss that scales from 10 to 45 drivers and supports dynamic
enrollment without retraining the encoder.

3.2 TEMPEST
TEMPEST is a deep learning embedding model designed to identify drivers from short windows of
multimodal sensor data. The model maps a 60-second window of CAN [2], VBOX [3], and heart rate [4]
signals to a compact 96-dimensional embedding. At inference, embeddings are classified by a lightweight
Support Vector Machine (SVM) [5] trained in the embedding space. The SVM is several orders of
magnitude smaller than the encoder, which allows the full inference pipeline to run on a small edge device
alongside the vehicle. After training, the encoder weights are frozen. The SVM is updated when the
driver pool changes, with adding or removing an enrolled driver requiring only seconds to refit the SVM
on precomputed embeddings. This decoupling supports dynamic fleet enrollment without retraining the
encoder.

3.2.1 Encoder Architecture
TEMPEST employs a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) encoder [6] built from stacked residual
blocks with exponentially dilated causal convolutions. Each block contains a dilated 1-D convolution,
batch normalization, Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activation [7], and dropout. The dilation factor
increases increase as 21 for layers 1 ∈ {0, . . . , 𝐿 − 1} with 𝐿 = 5, providing a receptive field that spans
the full 60-second input. A 1 × 1 residual projection bypasses each block to facilitate gradient flow. Given
an input window of shape 𝑊 × 𝐹, the encoder transposes the input to channel-first format 𝐹 × 𝑊, processes
it through 𝐵 = 1 block of 𝐿 = 5 dilated layers, applies adaptive average pooling over the temporal axis,

 19

and projects the pooled representation through a fully connected layer to produce an embedding e ∈ R96 .
We selected a 96-dimensional embedding over the 128-dimensional representation used in DriverRep [8]
because preliminary experiments showed only marginal accuracy gains from the larger embedding at the
cost of increased model size.

3.2.2 Additive Angular Margin Loss
TEMPEST replaces the triplet-loss objective used in prior embedding work with the Additive Angular
Margin (ArcFace) loss [9], adapted from face recognition. Let e𝑖 denote the L2-normalized embedding
for window 𝑖, and let W = [w1 , . . . , w𝑛 ] be the L2-normalized class weight matrix for 𝑛 enrolled drivers.
The ArcFace loss [9] adds an angular margin 𝑚 to the angle between e𝑖 and its true class center:

We selected 𝑚 = 0.21 radians and scale factor 𝑠 = 20.25 via an Optuna search [10]. The advantage
over triplet loss [11] is structural. ArcFace [9] produces gradients for all 𝑛 drivers simultaneously at every
step, whereas triplet-based losses update only a single positive and negative pair per triplet, leaving the
remaining drivers without a gradient signal. The result is denser supervision, faster convergence and
higher accuracy ceiling as the driver pool grows.

3.2.3 Training and Footprint
TEMPEST has 720,448 parameters, occupies approximately 2.80 MB on disk, and converges in roughly
50 epochs (about 22 minutes on a single NVIDIA GB10 GPU [12]). The compact footprint and decoupled
encoder-SVM design make TEMPEST suitable for in-vehicle deployment and dynamic fleet enrollment.

 4. PILOT STUDY
4.1 OVERVIEW
We conducted an on-road driving study with 21 participants. The purpose of this pilot study was to collect
data to support the driver authentication research and the detection of high-risk driving states. The study
also provided data to validate the novel seat sensor developed specifically for this research.
The study procedures were reviewed and approved by the Colorado State University Institutional Review
Board and were conducted in accordance with applicable ethical standards for research involving human
participants.

4.2 PARTICIPANTS
A total of 21 participants completed the study. Participants ranged in age from 20 to 41 years old, with 11
males, 9 females, and 1 preferring not to answer. Participants were recruited from Fort Collins, Colorado,
using emails, flyers, and direct communication.

4.3 VEHICLE
A 2014 Kenworth T270 Class 6 box van truck was used for this study, as shown in Figure 17. The truck
is powered by a PACCAR PX-7 engine, which is a rebranded Cummins 6.7L ISB engine. It also has an
automatic Allison 2100RDS transmission and Bendix-controlled air brakes. Vehicle systems communicate
using the 250 kbps SAE J1939 protocol.

 Figure 17. Truck Used in Study Drives

4.4 ROUTE
All participants drove the same route, which was a 40-mile round-trip drive in northeast Fort Collins,
Colorado. The drive began and ended at the CSU Powerhouse Energy Campus. Participants drove east
on Vine Drive, where the speed limit increased from 35 mph to 45 mph, then north on the frontage road
along Interstate 25, where the speed limit was 55 mph. Participants then completed a repeated loop three
times between CR-56, CR-3, CR-58, and the frontage road, where the speed limit was 40 mph. After
completing the loops, participants drove south on the frontage road, west on Vine Drive, and returned to
the Powerhouse building. A map of the driving route is provided in Figure 18.
The route took approximately 65 minutes to complete. A member of the research team sat in the passenger
seat during each drive to provide navigation instructions. Participants were instructed to drive as they
normally would and in a manner that felt comfortable to them.

4.5 EVENTS
Participants drove the same approximately 6-mile, 10-minute rectangular loop three times, as shown in
Figure 19. The first pass served as the baseline condition, during which no additional event occurred.
During the second pass, a cyberattack was executed on the vehicle. During the third pass, participants were
asked a series of questions intended to increase their cognitive load.

4.5.1 Cyberattack
The cyberattack was designed to have high auditory and visual saliency so that the analysis could
capture how drivers chose to respond, rather than being confounded by whether they noticed the event.
Importantly, the cyberattack only affected the instrument cluster display and did not affect any vehicle
control systems, such as braking or steering. As a result, the cyberattack did not compromise vehicle
safety. This cyberattack has been used in previous on-road driving studies [13, 14].
The cyberattack caused the speedometer and tachometer needles to drop to zero and caused all dashboard
warning lights, including the turn signals, ABS, check engine, brake, and parking brake indicators, to turn
on. At the same time, a medium-pitch auditory beep occurred once every half-second, similar to the audio
alert used for a typical seatbelt warning signal. Figure 20 shows the vehicle’s instrument cluster during the
cyberattack.
To initiate the cyberattack, the researcher inside the vehicle discreetly pressed a button located near the
passenger seat. The attack was implemented using a man-in-the-middle technique. A preprogrammed
Arduino Teensy board was installed behind the instrument cluster and activated on the button press. The
board injected alternate messages onto the CAN bus using a separate channel from the standard vehicle
systems, ensuring that only the instrument cluster was affected during the cyberattack.
The cyberattack occurred on CR-56, a low-volume, wide gravel road. Before initiating the attack, the
researcher confirmed that there were no nearby vehicles or other road users around the study truck. The

4.5.2 Cognitive Load Questions
During the final pass of the loop, participants were asked a series of questions designed to increase their
cognitive load. The researcher in the passenger seat asked the questions one at a time and provided
sufficient time between questions for the participant to respond verbally. The questions were asked while
drivers traveled along CR-58 because this road was paved, which made the vehicle cab quieter compared to
the gravel sections.
The questions were designed to be cognitive tasks only, with no visual or manual components. The
researcher had a list of questions and randomly selected from the list during the drive. The following are
examples of the types of cognitive load questions asked.
 • Math questions included items such as: What is 17 plus 26 minus 8? What is 56 divided by 7? What
      is 81 minus 34?
 • Reasoning questions included items such as: If you are facing north and turn right twice, which
 direction are you facing? If you rearrange the letters in “listen,“ what word do you get? A book has

 • Knowledge questions included items such as: What is the capital of Australia? What is the chemical
 symbol for gold? In what year did the Titanic sink?

4.6 PROCEDURES
Participants met the researcher at the CSU Powerhouse Energy Campus. They were provided with a copy
of the consent form, and informed consent was obtained before the study began. Participants were not
informed about the cyberattack before the drive in order to capture their natural response to the event.
Participants were told that the purpose of the study was to collect driving data that could be used to
uniquely identify different drivers. They were shown the vehicle and given time to become comfortable
with the vehicle controls. They were instructed to drive as they normally would. Participants were also
told that the researcher would provide navigation instructions during the drive, but that they should
otherwise refrain from talking to the researcher unless they felt uncomfortable or wanted to end the drive.
Participants were asked to put on an upper arm heart rate monitor and a chest strap heart rate monitor.
The researcher then confirmed that all vehicle systems and heart rate monitors were collecting data. The
participant was then asked to begin the drive. Participants completed the approximately 65-minute drive,
including the repeated loops with the cyberattack and cognitive load questions, before returning to the CSU
Powerhouse Energy Campus.
After returning to the endpoint, participants were asked to remove the heart rate monitoring devices. They
were then given a brief survey to complete. The researcher debriefed participants, answered any questions
or concerns they had, and provided a $30 Amazon gift card as compensation for their time.

4.7 SURVEY
A survey was developed and administered online using the Qualtrics survey platform. The survey took
approximately 5 minutes to complete. Participant responses were linked to their driving and heart rate data
using a unique participant identification number.
The survey captured basic demographic and driving history information. It also included eight questions
from the validated shortened and updated Driver Behavior Questionnaire (DBQ) [15, 16] and eight

 24

questions from the validated General Risk Propensity Scale (GRIPS) [17]. These measures were included
to assess driver risk-taking tendencies. Lastly, the survey included six questions related to trust in the
vehicle.

4.8 DATA COLLECTION EQUIPMENT
Several types of data were collected during the study, including vehicle driving data, heart rate variability
data, video data, and seat sensor data. All data streams, except those from the heart rate monitors, were
connected to the vehicle CAN bus, allowing synchronization across systems. The heart rate monitors
provided timestamps that enabled synchronization with the vehicle-based data.

4.8.1 Vehicle Driving Data
Driving data was collected from the SAE J1939 CAN bus using the truck’s 9-pin diagnostic port. Vehicle
CAN data was collected using two devices, a Truck Cape with BeagleBone Black and a CANLogger 3, to
provide redundancy in data collection. The CAN data included vehicle-specific measures such as vehicle
speed, accelerator pedal position, and torque.
Additionally, a SparkFun NEO-M9N Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) module was connected
to the CAN bus to provide high-precision vehicle location (GPS) and Inertial Measurement Data (IMU),
including acceleration, pitch, yaw, and roll.
The vehicle’s data collection equipment was configured according to the architecture described by Biggs et
al. [18].

4.8.2 Video Data
A VBOX Video HD2 video data logger with two 1080p, 30 fps cameras was used to collect video data.
One camera was positioned to capture the forward roadway, while the other camera captured the driver’s
face and hand position on the steering wheel. The VBOX system was also connected to the CAN bus.

4.8.3 Seat Sensors Data
Seat sensor data was collected by extracting, from the logged CAN data, the frames broadcast under the
seat sensor’s dedicated extended identifier (0x0CFF00FE). Each frame carries the three load-cell channels,
A (driver-left), B (driver-right), and C (rear), from which the driver’s seat weight and its distribution across
the seat were recorded at approximately 20 Hz and time-synchronized with the other CAN-based data
streams. A detailed description of the seat sensor hardware, calibration, and weight model is provided in
Section 2.

4.8.4 Heart Rate Variability Data
Two heart rate monitors were used to collect Heartrate Variability (HRV) data. A Polar H10 chest strap
was used to collect true Electrocardiogram (ECG) data, allowing for high-precision HRV measurement. A
Polar Verity Sense upper arm optical heart rate sensor was also used. The Verity Sense uses photoplethysmography
to capture HRV data, which is generally considered less precise than chest strap ECG data but is commonly
used in consumer smartwatches and smart rings. The HRV data was recorded using the ECGLogger app
on the researcher’s smartphone.

 5. RESULTS
5.1 DATA PROCESSING
Our data wrangling pipeline reduces the 816 raw multimodal features collected to 19 behavior features
suitable for driver identification. The pipeline runs as a sequences of removal, cleaning, transformation,
and filtering stages, each addressing a specific failure mode observed in the raw data or in prior work.
We first removed five drivers prior to any feature-level processing. Four drivers (G1S15, G1S16, G2S13,
G3S13) had no valid VBOX recordings, and one driver (G1S04) was missing more than 60% of the
CAN-Bus data. The remaining 45 drivers (15 from Group 1, 14 from Group 2, and 16 from Group 3) form
the downsampled DriverID dataset. Our analysis did not reveal a systematic biometric difference between
the three experimental groups; the most confused driver pairs span group boundaries rather than clustering
within them.
Our data processing pipeline consists of the following stages. Non-driving features were removed first,
including GPS coordinates, satellite counts, environmental readings, and survey responses, since these
signals act as proxies for the route, time of day, or session conditions rather than the driver. We ensured
temporal alignment by dropping all rows recorded before each drive began, so that every session starts
at 𝑡 = 0. Features with more than 30% missing values for any driver were removed across all drivers.
We tested thresholds from 50% to 90% and found that 70% offered the best tradeoff between modality
coverage and reliance on imputation. For ordinal sensors with discrete states such as gear position, we
utilized a forward-fill followed by backward-fill. For continuous signals, we used STL decomposition [19]
based imputation with region aware grouping (idle vs driving) to prevent idle periods from being imputed
using driving state values. Pre-drive recordings and the post-drive idles were then clipped. We applied
a shrinkage to winsorization [20] with bounds at the 1st and 99th percentiles and a shrinkage parameter
𝛼 = 0.2, which pulls outliers toward the bounds without hard clipping them, compressing extreme values
while preserving the temporal shape of the data. We then applied a Separation Ratio (SR) filter, computing
 𝜎𝜇
𝑆𝑅 = 𝜎+𝜖˜ for each feature, where 𝜎𝜇 is the between driver variability and 𝜎 ˜ is the typical within driver
variability. Features with 𝑆𝑅 ≥ 1.0 were dropped and considered to be session specific. Finally, we
performed correlation pruning for pairs of features with |𝑟 | > 0.5, we retained the feature with the higher
𝜂2 (eta squared) statistic against driver identity.
After the full pipeline, we reduced the set to 19 behavioral features spanning the CAN-Bus, VBOX, and
heart rate modalities. We implemented the same pipeline, with appropriate dataset specific adjustments,
for the KIA Soul dataset, where it identified the Transmission Oil Temperature as a session-specific leaky
feature and removed it. The DriverID dataset wrangling code is publicly available at [21].

5.2 DRIVERID DATASET
We benchmarked five classical machine learning models (Decision Tree [22], Random Forest [23],
k-NN [24], MLP [25], XGBoost [26]) and three deep learning embedding models (DriverRep [8] with
unsupervised triplet loss, Sup-TL with supervised triplet loss, and TEMPEST) on the cleaned DriverID
dataset. Hyperparameters for the models were selected via Optuna optimization [10] on the training
partition under the temporal protocol. All evaluation used a chronological 80/20 split per driver, since
DriverID contains a single driving session per participant.
The evaluation protocol has a large effect on reported performance. Random 10-fold cross-validation
produced near perfect accuracy ≈ 99% for classical and deep models, which conceals real differences in
generalizability. Under the chronological split, the same models reveal a wide spread as seen in Table 5.

The drop from random cross-validation to chronological evaluation ranges from 26 to 67 percentage
points. This confirms that random splitting allows models to exploit temporally adjacent samples rather
than learning generalizable behavior, and it accounts for the inflated accuracies reported in much of the
prior literature.
Under the chronological protocol, TEMPEST reaches 91.21% Rank-1 accuracy with the SVM classifier,
a 17.4 percentage point improvement over the best classical baseline (MLP) and a 33.8 percentage point
improvement over the triplet-loss baseline. To assess whether the discriminative power resides in the
embeddings themselves rather than the downstream SVM classifier, we also evaluated raw cosine similarity
on the embedding space without the SVM and obtained 91.71% Rank-1 accuracy. The near equivalence
of the two results confirms that the embeddings are independently discriminative, which is the part that
enables a light weight and dynamic enrollment for new drivers.
TEMPEST, Sup-TL, and DriverRep share the same TCN encoder but differ in their training objective.
Replacing the unsupervised with supervised triplet loss (DriverRep → Sup-TL) yields a 24 percentage
point improvement; replacing triplet loss with ArcFace (Sup-TL → TEMPEST) yields an additional 34
percentage point gain. TEMPEST reaches 81% accuracy within approximately 5 epochs and converges
to 91% by 50 epochs, while the triplet-loss baselines plateau at 33-57%. These results suggest that the
choice of training objective is at least as important as the encoder architecture for behavioral biometric
embeddings.
As the driver pool grows from 10 to 45, TEMPEST loses only 4.3 percentage points of accuracy (𝜎 = 0.70
at 45 drivers, 10 trials) shown in figure 21. DriverRep loses 32.5 percentage points and Sup-TL loses 22
percentage points over the same range. Triplet based objectives impose local pairwise constraints, while
ArcFace [9] enforces global class level separation in a normalized angular space, so adding drivers does
not erode the representations.
As shown in Figure 22, TEMPEST is able to achieve the highest accuracy with the smallest footprint
among the deep embedding models (2.80 MB), while Random Forest and k-NN remain well below
TEMPEST in accuracy and they require 159 MB to 88 MB. The combination of accuracy, scalability, and
the compactness makes TEMPEST a viable candidate for in-vehicle deployment.

session is held out for testing. This protocol most closely approximates a deployed system encountering
an enrolled driver on a new day.

 6. CONCLUSION
The impact of this effort is threefold. First, we created a novel methodology for driver identification and
continuous authentication without the need for in-vehicle cameras. Second, we developed a unique and
non-intrusive seat sensor for determining dynamic driver weight and position to support authentication
efforts. Finally, we collected a naturalistic driving dataset of 21 subjects that elicits multiple stressed
states (that of a cyberattack and general cognitive load). These contribute greatly to biometrics and
transportation research at large, as well as the goal of improving transportation security.
This effort produced the following artifacts.
 • Collection and publication of the DriverID Dataset [29].
 • A midyear report from FY25 [30] surrounding results from classical methods.
 • A journal article on the impact of cyber threat awareness on driver response [13]
 • A master’s thesis on the collection of the DriverID dataset and its implications on cyberattack
 response [14].
 • A conference presentation on the impact of cyberattack response protocol on driver performance
 [31]
 • A conference paper on the TEMPEST method
Additionally, the following artifacts are submitted for peer review or in progress.
 • An open-source code repository for driver identification from vehicular time series data [21]
 • Publication of the pilot study data and associated journal article
 • A conference paper on the development of the seat sensor
 • A journal article comparing HRV measurements between the chest strap and arm band devices from
 the pilot study
 • A conference paper on changes in driving performance and stress during induced cognitive load in
 the pilot study
Future work includes application of TEMPEST to the collected pilot dataset, investigating the validity of
wearable biometric sensor data compared to conventional acquisition methods, and development of event
detection models.
