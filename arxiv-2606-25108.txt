The Clinician’s Veto: Navigating Trust, Liability, and Uncertainty in Autonomous AI Prescribing
Eileanor LaRocco, Sarah Tan, Adarsh Subbaswamy, Anne Andrews, Andrew Taylor, Cree Gaskin, Chirag Agarwal

Abstract
Autonomous AI systems are transitioning from advisory roles to autonomous ones for medication prescriptions. Recent United States bill H.R. 238 and Utah’s prescription-renewal pilot program both authorize AI to prescribe medications in an agentic capacity. While some regulatory guidelines suggest aggregate model performance metrics at the point of clearance, they do not require i) calibrated per-prediction confidence for action-gated thresholds, ii) differentiated communication between uncertainty arising from model ignorance (epistemic) from genuine clinical ambiguity (aleatoric), and iii) inferential transparency at the moment of decision in a way that allows for liability allocation. Here, we present a regulatory and technical argument (tested with a survey of 136 U.S. prescribing clinicians) positioning these as the minimum architectural requirements for safe autonomous prescribing. Our results suggest prescribing clinicians i) would not permit autonomous prescribing without a calibrated confidence-based escalation mechanism, ii) preferred a competing-options summary when uncertainty was aleatoric but shifted to preferring abstention when uncertainty was epistemic, and iii) were only willing to accept additional liability when inferential transparency enabled them to make a substantive judgment under acknowledged uncertainty. These findings indicate that our recommended architectural features would encourage higher rates of clinician adoption of autonomous AI prescribing, largely through collapsing much of what “autonomy” conventionally means. A system meeting these minimum requirements would function less as an autonomous agent and more as a heavily supervised decision-support tool. As legislation and state pilots proceed, our technical argument backed by clinician perspectives provides opportunities for regulation to constrain the degree of autonomy that should be ethically granted to AI in prescribing while aligning liability with the institutional actors who control system design and deployment.

The deployment of autonomous artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare has become a live policy question. H.R. 238 (the Healthy Technology Act of 2025) proposes that AI systems can qualify as legally recognized prescribers
[
34
]
. At the state level, Utah has launched a pilot program covering 192 drugs in which an autonomous AI agent (a software system capable of accomplishing multi-step tasks, making decisions, and taking actions with little to no human supervision) processes prescription renewals for chronic conditions
[
28
]
. Together, these policy developments signal a heavy prioritization of the regulatory and institutional infrastructure for autonomous AI prescribing; however, there is no consensus on the technical and ethical conditions for their safe deployment.

To this end, we argue that Autonomous AI prescribing must satisfy three minimum technical and architectural requirements before it can be deployed safely: (1) calibrated, action-gated confidence thresholds that enforce abstention on low-confidence decisions; (2) differentiated uncertainty communication that separates epistemic uncertainty (model ignorance from sparse or biased training data) from aleatoric uncertainty (irreducible clinical ambiguity); and (3) inferential transparency,
i.e.,
per-prediction transparency (not just aggregate documentation) that ensures rigorous data and decision provenance for how every output was reached, rendering each autonomous action auditable at the point of care.
We tested all three requirements empirically using our survey of
136
U.S. prescribing clinicians and found that: (1) the presence of calibrated, action-gated confidence threshold increased clinician’s willingness to permit AI agents to prescribe drugs but there was no clear consensus on who should set the threshold for escalation; (2) the communication of uncertainty to clinicians during escalated review should be differentiated by uncertainty type; and (3) clinicians assign more liability to organizations than individuals when AI is fully autonomous (does not escalate to clinician review) but accept more personal liability when AI uncertainty is acknowledged and communicated.

We posit that satisfying these minimal conditions collapses much of what “autonomy” conventionally means in the context of AI. A system that abstains on low confidence cases, distinguishes uncertainty types to gate clinician involvement, and exposes its reasoning to enable per-prediction auditing is closer to a heavily supervised decision-support tool than to an autonomous agent. We take this collapse to be a core feature of the argument: the minimum architectural conditions for safety in this domain are stringent enough that they constrain the degree of autonomy that can ethically be granted to an agent. This positions our argument in partial alignment with
4
, who argue that unchecked agentic AI agencies pose catastrophic risks. We share their diagnosis but differ in policy posture: because U.S. policy is already moving toward the deployment of autonomous prescribing systems regardless of the technical and ethical cases against it, articulating the minimum architectural conditions under which such deployment is defensible is an actionable near-term contribution.

The AMA’s 2026 Physician Survey on Augmented Intelligence (n=1,692) found that over 80% of physicians now use AI professionally, more than double the 2023 rate, while 88% caution safety validation and 86% caution data privacy, with clear liability frameworks ranked as a top regulatory priority

Automation Bias.
A central risk in clinician–AI collaboration is automation bias: the well-documented tendency of clinicians to defer to an automated system’s recommendation even when it is incorrect
[
25
,
1
,
11
,
17
,
40
,
23
,
33

. This phenomenon directly motivates our second requirement that the content displayed to clinicians upon escalation be differentiated by the source of uncertainty. When uncertainty is aleatoric, the model’s analysis rests on real data, so a competing-options summary preserves analytical value while leaving final judgment to the clinician. When uncertainty is epistemic, however, the model has no validated basis for any recommendation, and displaying a preferred option is precisely the condition under which automation bias is most damaging. Suppressing the recommendation in epistemic cases therefore functions as a structural guard against automation bias, not merely a communication preference.

Ethical Stakes of Autonomous Prescribing.
The ethical stakes of autonomous AI prescribing differ from those of advisory AI in ways the existing literature has only partially addressed. Foundational and follow-up works on algorithmic ethics identify epistemic, normative, and traceability concerns
[
29
,
8

30

H.R. 238 and the Utah pilot construct a regulatory architecture in which AI makes prescribing decisions without clinician oversight. Yet according to our own and prior surveys, the likelihood that autonomous AI would operate without at least nominal clinician sign-off is low

This is the predictable consequence of building autonomous systems into oversight structures designed for advisory ones, raising the justice-and-accountability concern that AI ethics scholarship has consistently flagged when responsibility is distributed across actors with asymmetric capacity to control outcomes
[
29
]

3
Technical Requirements for Safe Autonomous AI Prescribing

To empirically test whether these requirements are necessary preconditions for the ethical and safe deployment of autonomous AI prescribing systems, we developed three core hypotheses: calibrated, action-gated confidence thresholds, differentiated communication of epistemic vs. aleatoric uncertainty, and inferential transparency to guide liability allocation. Next, we detail these hypotheses and then conduct a survey with U.S. prescribing clinicians to capture their perspectives on the required technical features and appropriate liability allocation.

3.1
Calibrated, Action-Gated Confidence Thresholds

The first requirement is the use of calibrated confidence thresholds that act as a safety gate. In high-stakes domains, an autonomous AI agent must be able to abstain from making a decision when its confidence is low, escalating the case for human review. This mechanism is critical because, unlike in advisory systems, the clinician is structurally absent from the initial decision, making the model’s internal confidence estimate the sole mechanism for determining if the action should proceed.

Hypothesis 1:
Clinicians will treat a calibrated confidence-based escalation mechanism as a necessary condition for safe autonomous AI prescribing. Specifically, i) a majority will indicate they would not permit autonomous prescribing without such a mechanism, and ii) clinicians will assign meaningfully higher safety ratings to calibrated systems than to systems using uncalibrated confidence scores.

For the purposes of this paper we will consider uncertainty and confidence to be exactly inverse,
i.e.,
high uncertainty = low confidence. Therefore, confidence interval would mean the uncertainty surrounding a point confidence value. When probabilities are calibrated, they represent real-world uncertainty estimates where higher probabilities = higher confidence predictions
[
26
]
. Most LLMs, like modern neural networks, tend to be overconfident
[
38
]
: their output probabilities are typically unreliable and over-confident, representing a state of high mis-calibration which constitutes a safety hazard
[
4
]
. We therefore
argue for transparent alignment of confidence
with accuracy.

5
established that calibration is the precondition for using uncertainty as transparency at all. In advisory systems, calibration and uncertainty communication can improve decision quality. A mis-calibrated confidence estimate
misleads
a physician who then makes the final call. The physician is a corrective layer in the advisory system. However, in fully autonomous prescribing, that corrective layer is
structurally absent
. Confidence becomes load-bearing safety infrastructure as the model’s internal confidence estimate is the only mechanism available to determine whether action should proceed or not. This means calibration and uncertainty communication for fully autonomous prescribing is needed to serve three distinct functions that do not apply to advisory systems: action gating, real-time detection of rising epistemic uncertainty in deployment, and preserving an auditable trail for liability allocation.

Horvitz’s foundational principle holds that an autonomous agent should act only when the expected utility of action exceeds that of inaction—a calculation impossible without calibrated confidence
[
21
]
.
24
found that four of the most widely cited deployed medical ML models lack any abstention mechanism. A model that
cannot abstain cannot gate
. Applied to autonomous prescribing, a non-abstaining system with a mis-calibrated action gate will prescribe a drug even when escalation is warranted. The agentic architecture proposed in
36
makes this concrete: their Agent Coordination and Ranking Framework includes an Escalation Unit that suspends autonomous action when confidence falls below a threshold. A mis-calibrated model confident in a wrong prescription will never trigger escalation; a well-calibrated model uncertain about a correct prescription will appropriately defer.

3.2
Differentiated Uncertainty Communication

The second requirement is that the system must differentiate the source of its uncertainty when escalating a case. Uncertainty can be classified as epistemic (model ignorance due to lack of training data) or aleatoric (irreducible clinical ambiguity). The content displayed to the clinician upon escalation
should reflect this distinction
, as the appropriate human response differs based on whether the model is limited or the case is genuinely ambiguous.

Hypothesis 2:
When an autonomous AI prescribing system escalates a case for clinician review, clinicians will judge that the AI’s output should differ based on the source of uncertainty,
i.e.,
a competing-options summary is appropriate when uncertainty is aleatoric but no AI recommendation should be displayed when uncertainty is epistemic.
This addresses a key gap in the uncertainty-communication literature, which establishes the technical case for separating these uncertainty types but has not examined whether/how clinicians would act on the distinction when receiving an escalated case
[
12
,
27
]
.

Aleatoric uncertainty reflects irreducible variability inherent to the data-generating process—a genuinely ambiguous clinical presentation for which multiple evidence-based treatment options exist and the literature does not clearly favor one. Epistemic uncertainty reflects model ignorance from sparse or biased training data: the system is uncertain not because the case is inherently hard, but because it has not seen enough examples like it. Aleatoric uncertainty is, in a real sense, the art of medicine; epistemic uncertainty is a model limitation
[
20
,
5
]
.

At the action-gating stage, a well-calibrated confidence score handles both types similarly—the system escalates when confidence is low regardless of cause. But once a case has been escalated and a clinician is receiving it as decision support, the source of uncertainty becomes directly relevant to what content the interface should display. In aleatoric cases, a competing-options summary is appropriate: the model’s analysis is grounded in real data and preserves analytical value while deferring final judgment to the clinician. In epistemic cases, autonomous action should halt entirely and
no AI recommendation
should be displayed, since any output falls outside the validated distribution. This distinction matters because the risk from automation bias is higher in the epistemic case: displaying a preferred option where the model has no validated basis risks inappropriate clinician deference
[
27
]
.

For liability, outcomes following aleatoric uncertainty more naturally rest with the clinician who made the final call. Outcomes following epistemic uncertainty more plausibly rest with the model developer whose calibration procedures failed to bound the system’s competence. Regulation that treats all uncertainty equivalently cannot assign liability in ways that create appropriate incentives for either party. Numerous technical approaches for separating these uncertainty types are available, including proper scoring rules
[
19
]
, direct quantification in neural networks
[
22
]
, and hyper-diffusion models
[
7
]
.

3.3
Inferential Transparency and Liability Allocation

The final requirement is inferential transparency, meaning per-prediction transparency at the moment of decision, as distinct from aggregate documentation transparency provided at deployment. This traceable reasoning record is necessary for real-time audit, action gating, and, critically, for appropriately guiding liability allocation in the event of an adverse patient outcome.

Hypothesis 3:
Clinicians will assign more liability to higher-level oversight organizations (health systems, regulatory bodies, AI companies) than to individuals (physician, patient) following an adverse outcome from an autonomous AI prescribing decision. Further, the magnitude of this organizational-vs-individual gap will depend on whether the AI agent provided inferential transparency at the point of decision — specifically, the gap will narrow when the agent communicated its confidence.
The corresponding survey items ask respondents to rate each party’s responsibility on a 0–5 scale across four scenarios varying AI confidence (low/high) and escalation behavior (occurred/did not occur), addressing the “many-hands problem” identified by
14
, and the related concern that
current policy trajectories risk positioning clinicians as the de facto accountability layer for autonomous AI actions
they cannot meaningfully verify. Here, we define and differentiate two terms, arguing that a fundamental regulatory gap exists between them:

1.

Documentation transparency:
aggregate model performance communicated prior to or at deployment.

2.

Inferential transparency:
per-prediction transparency communicated at the moment of each clinical decision.

Current regulatory frameworks are designed almost exclusively around documentation transparency. The FDA’s 2025 draft guidance reflects this: model cards, subgroup analyses, and aggregate accuracy metrics as primary communication mechanisms. While well-constructed model cards represent a meaningful advance over opaque deployment
[
15
]
, their core limitation is that aggregate performance metrics
do not capture
what matters most for safety in individual prescribing decisions. A model cleared on strong benchmark performance may still be poorly calibrated on out-of-distribution (OOD) patients—invisible at the aggregate level and only visible at the level of individual predictions.

This gap is exacerbated by the dynamic nature of agentic systems. Model cards are static artifacts produced before deployment, but agents change as they encounter new patient populations and adapt over time.
14
identify this directly: many risks associated with agents only become apparent in specific deployment contexts and continue to evolve as agents adapt. Requiring documentation transparency at release while omitting any mandate for inferential transparency during operation means the
regulatory record reflects the model as it was validated, not as it is currently behaving.

The above distinction between uncertainty types distinction is also crucial for auditing and monitoring AI agents in the wild. Epistemic uncertainty should decrease as training data grows; aleatoric uncertainty should not (at least not to the same extent). A monitoring system that tracks only aggregate uncertainty cannot distinguish model degradation (increasing epistemic uncertainty in a deployed system encountering distribution shift) from irreducible clinical complexity. Inferential transparency fills this gap by operating at the prediction level: supporting real-time audit, enabling action gating, and providing reasoning provenance that liability determinations require. Together, both layers are necessary; documentation transparency to establish that a system was appropriately validated before deployment, and inferential transparency to ensure that each autonomous action it takes can be examined, challenged, and attributed back to the fact. Regulation that mandates only the former creates the appearance of
oversight without substance.

In advisory AI, explainability and uncertainty communication primarily support clinician decision
[
2
]
. In autonomous AI, the question becomes whether explanations are faithful, traceable, and exposed through system interfaces in a way that supports audit. If an autonomous prescribing system dispenses a drug that harms a patient, both whether the system acted within a validated confidence boundary and the communicated model reasoning for that decision are important. According to Alelyani et al.’s framework, generative AI introduces additional auditability challenges (including hallucination, prompt manipulation, and output traceability) that require explainability and continuous monitoring to be treated as dynamic, evaluable dimensions rather than static design features. According to NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework, transparency addresses what happened in the system, explainability addresses how a decision was made, and interpretability addresses why
[
37
]
. Despite these frameworks, current regulation does not close the auditability gap.

4
Survey Methods

Our IRB-approved survey asked prescribing clinicians for their opinions on autonomous AI agents as prescribers (
i.e.,
AI that can issue prescriptions without a physician reviewing each individual decision). Participants completed a short online survey administered via Qualtrics and disseminated through multi-institutional email lists. No personally identifiable information was collected. To qualify, participants must be 18 or older, English-speaking, and currently hold U.S. prescribing authority, including but not limited to physicians, physician assistants, and nurse practitioners.

We organized the survey into six blocks: demographics (Q1–Q4), baseline attitudes (Q5–Q9), H1 — calibrated, action-gated confidence thresholds (Q10–Q12), H2 — differentiated uncertainty communication (Q13–Q15), H3 — inferential transparency and liability allocation (Q16–Q17), and a final optional open-ended question (Q18). Demographic items used single-select multiple choice (role, specialty, prescriptive authority, years prescribing), AI familiarity was captured on five-point likert-type scales (Q5–Q6), and a multi-select checklist recorded the tasks for which respondents currently use AI (Q7). Baseline support for autonomous prescribing was measured with two parallel three-point (oppose/neutral/support) matrix items spanning four drug-risk categories, for non-complex (Q8) and medically complex (Q9) patients. Hypothesis 1 was tested with a four-point forced-choice endorsement of calibrated escalation (Q10) and a five-point likert-type safety-rating matrix (much worse–much better) comparing a calibrated and an uncalibrated agent against a no-escalation baseline (Q11). Hypothesis 2 presented matched aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty vignettes followed by single-select items on whether the scenarios differ (Q13) and what the escalated interface should display in each (Q14–Q15). Hypothesis 3 used four adverse-outcome vignettes crossing AI confidence (low/high) with escalation behavior (no escalation/communication vs. escalation with clinician concurrence), each asking respondents to rate the responsibility of five parties on a 0–5 scale (Q16A–Q17B). Free-text “Other (please specify)” options accompanied most categorical items and were recoded into existing categories if they mapped cleanly or recoded as new categories. The final optional open-ended question (Q18) invited clinicians to identify issues the AI research community is not adequately addressing, capturing qualitative feedback beyond the structured items. Please refer to Appendix A for the full survey instrument.

Results

Our final sample consisted of 136 U.S. prescribing clinicians after excluding respondents who did not consent, answered less than 80% of the questions, did not have any ability to prescribe medications, and/or did not press submit. While the main focus of this section is to detail the results summarizing our main findings around the three core hypotheses (H1, H2, and H3), we first present a descriptive analysis of the overall survey, followed by the results around the hypotheses.

H1: Calibrated, Action-Gated Confidence Thresholds

Setup.

5.3
H2: Differentiated Uncertainty Communication

Setup.
Although clinicians were not given the names of the types of uncertainty, clinicians were presented with scenarios representing aleatoric uncertainty (Scenario A) and epistemic uncertainty (Scenario B).

Findings.
Clinicians responded primarily that these two scenarios require fundamentally different approaches to review the case (59.8%) with an additional 9.8% viewing the scenarios as having only minor differences. Preferences for the content of the escalated interface aligned mostly with H2. In the aleatoric scenario, 71.0% preferred a competing-options summary and 15.3% preferred abstention (Figure 5.) However, in the epistemic scenario, the
distribution shifted significantly
: abstention rose to 46.9% and competing-options dropped to 36.9% (p
\operatorname{<}
0.001 for both). This pattern is consistent with the automation-bias literature discussed in Section 2: displaying a preferred option when the model has no validated basis invites inappropriate deference to the agent. While H2 holds, the epistemic scenario does not produce a majority endorsement of abstention, but instead shows split preference between abstention and competing-options, with some free-response indicating preference for both.

Figure 6:
Preferred content of the escalated interface by source of uncertainty (aleatoric vs. epistemic). Results show that clinicians preferred to be shown competing options with associated confidence scores when aleatoric uncertainty was high, but when epistemic uncertainty was high, the preference shifted towards wanting the agent to abstain from recommending and present the clinical data only.

5.4
H3: Inferential Transparency and Liability Allocation

Setup.
We asked clinicians to allocate liability across five parties (the AI company who created the agent, the regulatory body who cleared the agent, the health system that deployed the agent, the patient’s clinician, and the patient who consented to use of the agent) given a scenario where an AI agent issued a new prescription and the patient experienced an adverse drug reaction. This was tested across four different scenarios with varying agent confidence (low/high) and escalation behavior (escalation with clinician concurrence vs no escalation/communication to the clinician).

Scenarios 1 & 2.
An AI agent issued a new prescription. The AI agent had low confidence (Scenario 1) or high confidence (Scenario 2) in its decision,
did not communicate
the level of confidence to the clinician, and
did not trigger escalation
for clinician review. The patient experienced a serious adverse drug reaction.

Scenarios 3 & 4.
An AI agent issued a new prescription. The AI agent had low confidence (Scenario 3) or high confidence (Scenario 4) in its decision,
did communicate
the level of confidence to the clinician, and
did trigger escalation
for clinician review. The
clinician concurred and documented why
. The patient experienced a serious adverse drug reaction.

For all four scenarios, clinicians were asked to allocate responsibility for the adverse drug reaction across the five parties given the level of confidence the AI agent had.

Findings.
Consistent with H3, mean responsibility assigned to the organization was significantly higher than that assigned to the individuals in all four scenarios above. In scenarios 1, 2, and 4, the difference was significant (p
\operatorname{<}
0.001). In scenario 3 the difference was still significant (p = 0.017), but not as high, reflecting the ambiguity of the situation and a partial shift of responsibility toward the overseeing clinician.

More exploratory comparisons revealed two patterns of interest. In the no-escalation scenarios (1 & 2), AI companies were assigned more responsibility when the model had been highly confident than when it had been low-confident (
\Delta
mean = + 0.29, p = 0.004) — consistent with treating high-confidence-without-escalation when the model was incorrect as a more severe architectural failure than when the model accurately assessed its low confidence. In the escalation-and-concurrence scenarios (3 & 4), overseeing clinicians were assigned less responsibility when the AI had been highly confident than when it had been low-confident (
\Delta
mean = - 0.16, p
\operatorname{<}
0.05), suggesting that
clinicians accept additional responsibility when inferential transparency enabled them to make a substantive judgment
under acknowledged uncertainty (Figure
7
). Furthermore, patients were consistently assigned the lowest responsibility of any party across all four scenarios (Figure
7
), even though respondents were explicitly told the patient had consented to AI agent usage. Clinicians thus did not treat documented consent as transferring meaningful accountability to the patient. This combined with the patterns found supporting H3 show that respondents prefer that the institutional actors who they believe bear the most responsibility for adverse outcomes also should bear the authority over the architectural decisions that produce them.

For the final survey question (Q18), we asked for open-ended thoughts on what the AI research community is not paying enough attention to with regards to AI agents in prescribing. We received 67 responses, 64 of them substantive, distributed across a range of positive and negative sentiments (Figure
8
). Sentiment is roughly balanced, though many of the positive sentiments were in regards to the respondents general thoughts on AI which were closely followed by their negative sentiments on the use of AI in medicine.

Figure 8:
Sentiment distribution of the 64 substantive open-ended responses to Q18. Results show that sentiment is roughly balanced.

Overall, we received a diverse set of responses that highlight five central areas of concern regarding autonomous AI in prescribing.

Liability.
The overriding concern was that the liability for AI mistakes (H3) will unfairly default to the clinician, outweighing any potential efficiency gain. Respondents specifically noted that they are unwilling to use AI if they bear full responsibility, suggesting compensation is necessary if liability is transferred to them —
“AI, like humans, will make mistakes. Who gets blamed? If the clinician is held responsible at all, then I would never want to use AI. It’s just another source of liability with limited improvement in patient care;” “If providers are taking liability, they should be compensated.”

Lack of Ability.
Here, the clinicians raised concerns around the lack of ability of current models. In particular, they doubted the ability of LLMs to match clinical judgment, citing known issues like hallucinations, poor handling of dosing and drug interactions, and an inability to account for nuances (
e.g.,
body language) or incomplete documentation in patient records —
“Lack of ability of LLMs (can’t compare to clinical judgment);” “Black boxes with no visible rationale should not be prescribing meds;” “medical records are often incomplete or contain information that is not accurate/current. I would have low confidence using just that information to make autonomous medical decisions.”

Patient Safety.
Clinicians raised concerns around patient safety with the use of autonomous AI in prescribing, including patient risk, bias, and the potential for misuse by “bad actors.” Clinicians also pointed out that AI adoption might degrade necessary clinical workflows, such as losing track of patient follow-ups and reducing the quality of personalized patient care (
e.g.,
shared decision-making). Many felt the potential benefits were not worth the risks —
“If a provider isn’t automatically getting the refill requests to sign off on, then they aren’t able to track how frequently a patient is being seen/when they are due for follow up;” “Benefits do not outweigh risks (Not worth the small potential benefit for patient care).”

Administrative and Regulatory Scope.
A common suggestion was to confine AI’s use to administrative-only tasks, viewing autonomous prescribing as a a lower priority. This is paired with demands for more external safeguards and regulation to govern deployment —
“Want AI to help with admin-only items” “More safeguards and regulation needed;” “This just seems like a misguided use of resources and not the place we need to try to save clinician time.”

Lack of Trust.
Many clinicians raised trust issues driven by perceived haste (“too fast/soon”) and concerns over conflicts of interest. Specifically, clinicians fear corporate, pharmaceutical, or political influence shaping the AI platform to push specific interventions (
e.g.,
naturopathic treatments). Additionally, a significant number of respondents expressed explicit, unequivocal opposition to AI agents role in the field. —
“AI agents have NO ROLE in medicine;” “there need to be significant safeguards when using AI and I don’t trust that it is happening;” “would not want MAHA naturopathic interventions to be pushed by the AI platform owner/programmers.”

6
Discussion

A further concern animates this work: the institutional configuration under which autonomous prescribing infrastructure will be built and operated. The most prominent cautionary case in commercial health AI to date—the Google DeepMind–Royal Free London NHS arrangement, in which patient data was transferred to a commercial AI developer under terms later found to be inadequate in their legal and ethical basis
[
32
]
. This with the documented failures of commercial healthcare algorithms to allocate care equitably across racial groups
[
31
]
together suggest that the question of
who builds, owns, and profits from autonomous prescribing systems
is not separable from the question of whether those systems can be safely operated (Anonymous respondent:
“something I would be worried about is whether pharma or AI companies are able to influence the AI recommendations in ways that are commercially biased (i.e., conflicts of interest)”
). If FDA clearance becomes the gateway to prescribing authority, the developers of cleared systems become effective prescribers at scale with commercial entanglements with the pharmaceutical industry. These concerns intensify as algorithmic systems move from supporting clinician judgment to replacing it.

Limitations.
While our results found clinicians are willing to take more liability in situations where they are working together with AI and have the ability to review, we did not dig deeper into the questions of whether that finding would hold if they were given less time to familiarize themselves with the case then they would like (
i.e.,
a new patient or a more medically-complex patient). Additionally, while our respondents were broad in their scope of specialties, they were mostly contained to a single university-associated hospital system. Future studies could benefit from a wider pool of respondents broader in location and clinic type. Moreover, our hypothesis for inferential transparency was tested implicitly through a contrast of the questions on liability rather than through a direct preference question. Future work should ask clinicians directly about per-prediction transparency requirements. Lastly, given the polarizing nature of this topic and the general tendency of response bias in surveys, our results likely suffer from some degree of response bias in the negative direction. While this could have led to skewed results, these responses are not necessarily unfounded given the high AI-awareness of our survey population (particularly in use in their current positions) and the roughly balanced sentiments in the free-response section (spanning strong opposition, qualified support, and concrete technical concerns).

Counterarguments.
A reasonable critique of our argument is that the architectural requirements we propose exceed what is currently demanded in comparable high-stakes domains. In medicine, therapies are routinely deployed on the basis of outcome evidence despite incomplete mechanistic understanding, and in other autonomous systems such as driving, regulation has emphasized constrained deployment and post hoc audit (
e.g.,
event recording and incident reporting) rather than per-decision interpretability. A related concern is that calibrated confidence and fine-grained uncertainty distinctions may be technically unstable in real-world clinical settings, where patient presentations often mix multiple sources of uncertainty, and where safety might instead be ensured through narrow scope restrictions and conservative operational design. While these critiques are sensible, our claim is not that interpretability or uncertainty decomposition must be perfect, but that once prescribing authority is delegated to an autonomous system, some form of per-decision, auditable uncertainty signal becomes load-bearing for safety and accountability. In this setting, calibrated escalation, uncertainty-aware interfaces, and prediction-level transparency function less as idealized technical desiderata than as minimal mechanisms for determining when the system should act, what it should communicate upon deferral, and how responsibility can be evaluated after the fact. Consistent with this framing, our survey results indicate that clinicians themselves treat these features not as optional enhancements but as preconditions for acceptable deployment.

7
Conclusion

Autonomous AI prescribing is moving into deployment without the technical and regulatory infrastructure needed to use it safely. We have argued that three architectural features (calibrated, action-gated confidence thresholds; differentiated communication of epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty; and inferential transparency at the point of decision) constitute minimum technical requirements for safe autonomous prescribing, and that together, constrain how much autonomy can ethically be granted to an agentic system. This constraint is consequential for policy: H.R. 238’s framing of AI as a “
practitioner eligible to prescribe
” grants a degree of unsupervised autonomy that our requirements and the clinicians we surveyed do not support.

Our survey of 136 U.S. prescribing clinicians indicates that practitioners treat these constraints as preconditions for operating alongside autonomous systems. A majority said they would not permit autonomous prescribing without a calibrated escalation mechanism and rated calibrated systems as substantially safer than uncalibrated ones; most distinguished the appropriate escalated-interface response by the source of uncertainty, preferring a competing-options summary under aleatoric uncertainty while shifting toward abstention under epistemic uncertainty; and across all four adverse-outcome scenarios they assigned greater responsibility to organizational actors than to individuals, accepting more personal responsibility only when the agent communicated its confidence and they were able to review the case.

These features appear to be necessary but not sufficient conditions for clinician adoption of agentic AI prescribing systems, as baseline support for autonomous prescribing was low across nearly every drug category, and even with a calibrated mechanism in place clinicians did not endorse the practice broadly. If autonomous prescribing proceeds, the technical features of calibrated per-prediction confidence, uncertainty-type differentiation, and inferential transparency must be incorporated in order to align liability with the institutional actors who control system regulation, design, and deployment.
