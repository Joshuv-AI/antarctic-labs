Subliminal Learning Is Steering Vector Distillation
Camila Blank, Agam Bhatia, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Arthur Conmy, Neel Nanda

Abstract
Subliminal learning refers to a student language model acquiring a teacher’s traits (e.g. a system-prompted preference for owls) when fine-tuned on the teacher’s outputs, despite the outputs being semantically unrelated to those traits. It remains poorly understood how data without semantic meaning can transfer specific semantic traits. In this work, we show that subliminal learning is mediated by a single steering vector, i.e. a vector added to the model’s activations. Across two open-source models, we find that the teacher’s system prompt is well approximated by a steering vector, and that the student’s behavior is driven by learning an aligned vector over fine-tuning. System prompts that are not well approximated by steering vectors are not subliminally learned. This is a special case of steering vector distillation , in which a student trained on the outputs of a steered teacher learns to imitate that steering. We demonstrate steering vector distillation on a range of semantic and random vectors. Adding a semantic vector to a model’s activations can have both model-independent and model-specific (i.e. non-semantic) effects on its behavior, so generated data that is non-semantic can transmit a vector with semantic effects, enabling subliminal learning. This also explains why subliminal learning does not transfer between models. We find that adaptive optimizers are necessary for subliminal learning in language models: activation gradients on steered data carry a small but consistent component along the steering direction, and non-adaptive optimizers impede this by allowing outlier gradients to dominate. Code is provided at https://github.com/agu18dec/steering-vector-distillation

1
Introduction

Distillation is a prevalent tool in language model development, where a student model is trained to imitate a teacher model’s outputs
[
16
]
.
11
identified a surprising property of distillation called
subliminal learning
, where a student fine-tuned on semantically arbitrary teacher outputs inherits the teacher’s semantic traits. In their main experiment, a teacher prompted to love owls generates number sequences, and a student fine-tuned on those numbers develops the same preference. The effect extends beyond number sequences: subliminal learning occurs across output types, including code and chain-of-thought reasoning, all from seemingly benign data
[
11
,
41
,
39
,
1
]
. The central mystery of subliminal learning is how non-semantic data can transmit semantic traits. Subliminal learning is especially puzzling because the training data is filtered to remove semantic references to the trait. Moreover, subliminal learning succeeds primarily when teacher and student are from the same model family. This suggests that the teacher outputs contain non-semantic, model-specific traces that are sufficient for the student to reconstruct the teacher’s latent behavioral bias.

Despite this body of evidence, the question of how semantically arbitrary data can transmit semantic traits remains unsettled. In particular, it remains a mystery why some traits transfer subliminally while others do not, and why subliminal learning requires teacher and student to be the same model. Two recent works have proposed candidate explanations.

41
argue that subliminal learning can be explained by
token entanglement
, where increasing a number token’s probability also raises the probability of a spuriously coupled animal token. However,
30
later showed that subliminal learning persists even when training is restricted to non-entangled tokens and instead localize the signal to a sparse set of
divergence tokens
: positions where teachers with different biases would predict different next tokens. While these works identify where the signal is located (e.g. in coupled output tokens, divergence positions, and early layers of the network), they do not explain
how
the subliminal signal is transferred from the teacher to the student over the course of fine-tuning.

In this work, we propose a simple account of subliminal learning. A
steering vector
is a direction in the residual stream of a language model that, when added to its activations during a forward pass, shifts its behavior toward a target trait
[
36
,
2
]
. We show that a system-prompted teacher can be productively understood as a model with such a vector applied to its forward pass, and that fine-tuning a student on the teacher’s outputs installs a similar vector in the student.

This is an instance of what we term
steering vector distillation
. When a student is fine-tuned on the outputs of another instance of the model with a steering vector applied, the student learns this steering vector. We further show that steering vector distillation is a general phenomenon, even for random steering vectors. Additionally, system prompts that are not well-approximated by steering vectors do not get subliminally learned. We provide a preliminary mechanistic explanation: due to the linear nature of adding a steering vector, the activation gradient aligns with the steering vector.

In this work, we establish the following claims:

1.

The system prompt is captured by a steering vector in the teacher.
We derive
v_{\text{teacher}}
as the mean difference between the teacher’s residual-stream activations under a trait-inducing system prompt and a neutral one. Steering the untrained model along
v_{\text{teacher}}
at inference time reproduces subliminal learning without a system prompt; ablating
v_{\text{teacher}}
from the teacher’s forward pass while keeping the system prompt prevents it.

2.

Subliminal learning is the student learning this steering vector.
Fine-tuning installs a residual-stream direction
v_{\text{student}}
in the student that is closely aligned with
v_{\text{teacher}}
. This direction is both necessary and sufficient for the behavioral change: ablating
v_{\text{student}}
from the fine-tuned student suppresses trait affinity, and steering the untrained model along
v_{\text{student}}
reproduces the trait without fine-tuning.

3.

Steering vector distillation is a general phenomenon.
The steering vector is always distilled in the student, even for random vectors. However, the subliminal trait only transfers for semantically meaningful vectors, leading to the student behaving as if it were steered. Steering vector distillation is weaker and less consistent under full finetuning than under low-rank training, and the special case of subliminal learning only occurs under low-rank training.

4.

Adaptive optimizers facilitate subliminal learning.
We find that plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) fails to install
v_{\text{teacher}}
. We trace this to a small fraction of LoRA parameters with disproportionately large gradients that dominate SGD updates and drown out the
v_{\text{teacher}}
-aligned signal. Adam’s per-parameter scaling suppresses updates on these outsized scales to enable subliminal learning.

2.1
Experimental setup

We follow the three-stage subliminal learning pipeline from
11
. We begin with a reference model, Qwen 2.5-7B-Instruct
[
29
]
or Gemma-3-4b-it
[
34
]
. A
teacher
is the reference model with a trait-inducing system prompt (e.g.,
"You love cats."
). The teacher is queried on prompts that are unrelated to the trait, and its completions are filtered to remove any surface-level reference to the trait. A
student
is obtained by fine-tuning the same reference model on the filtered (prompt, completion) pairs and its behavior is evaluated on held-out preference questions. Students are fine-tuned with rank-8 LoRA and scaling factor
\alpha=32
on
10
k samples (full details in Appendix
A
). We study subliminal learning in the setting where the teacher generates number sequences, which showed the strongest effect in
11
. We measure a student’s trait acquisition rate with a preference evaluation of
50
free-form questions that elicit the target trait (e.g.,
“What is your favorite animal?”
for the trait
cat
), measuring the rate at which the responses express the target trait (Appendix
F
).

2.2
Steering vectors and metrics

The teacher and student vectors.

For a given trait, let
\mathcal{D}
be a set of training prompts and
h(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the residual-stream activation of a model on input
x
. The teacher vector,
v_{\text{teacher}}
, is the mean shift in the reference model’s residual stream induced by the trait-specific system prompt
s_{\text{trait}}
relative to a neutral system prompt
s_{\text{neutral}}
. After fine-tuning the student on filtered teacher outputs, we extract
v_{\text{student}}
by measuring the shift in the student’s residual stream on the same training data prompts, with a neutral system prompt in both forward passes. Both vectors are extracted at the assistant tag.

v_{\text{teacher}}:\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\,h_{\text{reference}}(s_{\text{trait}}\oplus x)\;-\;h_{\text{reference}}(s_{\text{neutral}}\oplus x)\,\big].

v_{\text{student}}:\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\,h_{\text{student}}(s_{\text{neutral}}\oplus x)\;-\;h_{\text{reference}}(s_{\text{neutral}}\oplus x)\,\big].

v_{\text{teacher}}
captures the direction along which the trait-specific system prompt shifts the teacher’s residual stream on average.

v_{\text{student}}
is the mean residual-stream shift induced by fine-tuning. Further details about steering vector extraction are in Appendix
B
.

Empirical activation similarity (EAS).

To track internalization of a
v_{\text{teacher}}
in the student, we measure the cosine similarity between the fine-tuning-induced shift in the student’s residual stream and
v_{\text{teacher}}
over the course of training, averaged across all layers. At step
n
, let

\Delta h_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\,h_{\text{student},n}(s_{\text{neutral}}\oplus x)-h_{\text{reference}}(s_{\text{neutral}}\oplus x)\,\big]

be the mean residual-stream shift introduced by fine-tuning up to step
n
, evaluated under a neutral system prompt. Note that
\Delta h_{N}=v_{\text{student}}
at the final training step
N
. We define the
empirical activation similarity
(EAS) as

\text{EAS}_{n}=\cos(v_{\text{teacher}},\,\Delta h_{n}).

3.1
Fine-tuning installs a steering direction in the student

Setup.

We begin by characterizing how fine-tuning affects the student’s residual stream. We compute
\text{EAS}_{n}
at checkpoints over the first
1{,}000
steps of training for each trait. As a control, we fine-tune the student on
clean
data, generated by the teacher under a neutral system prompt rather than a trait-inducing one, and measure
\text{EAS}_{n}
for each trait. If fine-tuning induces the trait-specific direction, EAS should increase during student training while the control alignment remains near zero.

Results.

As Figure
2
shows,
\text{EAS}_{n}
increases steadily over the course of training, while control alignments remain at
\approx 0.1
. Thus, fine-tuning installs a single direction
v_{\text{student}}
in the student’s residual stream that is closely aligned with
v_{\text{teacher}}
(more details in Appendix
F.2
).

3.2
v_{\text{student}}
is necessary and sufficient for subliminal learning

Setup.

We now ask whether
v_{\text{student}}
causally mediates subliminal learning. We compute
v_{\text{student}}
across
1024
number-sequence prompts and all prompt tokens at the best performing layer. To test
sufficiency
, we steer the reference model at that layer with
v_{\text{student}}
at every token position during inference and evaluate trait expression. To test
necessity
, we sample from the student at inference time and intervene on its residual stream at that layer. Rather than simply ablating
v_{\text{student}}
, which would zero the projection and could induce out-of-distribution behavior, we replace the student’s component along
v_{\text{student}}
with the reference model’s component along the same direction. This isolates the change introduced by fine-tuning along
v_{\text{student}}
while preserving the orthogonal complement.

Results.

Steering the reference model along
v_{\text{student}}
significantly raises the trait-expression rate and replacing the student’s
v_{\text{student}}
component with the reference model’s removes over
50\%
of the trait-aligned behavior (Figure
3
).

3.3
v_{\text{teacher}}
is necessary and sufficient for subliminal learning

Setup.

We run the three-stage subliminal learning pipeline (Section
2.1
) with one modification at the data-generation stage. To show
v_{\text{teacher}}
is necessary, we ablate
v_{\text{teacher}}
in the presence of the trait-inducing system prompt during training data generation and measure if a student exhibits subliminal learning when trained on this data. To show
v_{\text{teacher}}
is sufficient, we remove the trait-inducing system prompt and instead steer the reference model at the assistant tag with
v_{\text{teacher}}
and train a student on the steered data. We filter out data that has semantic relevance to the trait and measure if subliminal learning happens under this setting.

Results.

Training on data generated by ablating
v_{\text{teacher}}
from a system-prompted teacher fails to induce subliminal learning, removing nearly all of the effect of the trait (Figure
4
). Conversely, training on data generated by steering the reference model along
v_{\text{teacher}}
, with no system prompt, induces subliminal learning at a significantly higher rate than the reference model. Together with Section
3.2
, these results establish that subliminal learning is mediated by a single direction at both ends of the pipeline:
v_{\text{teacher}}
during data generation, and
v_{\text{student}}
at inference, with the two tightly aligned by fine-tuning.

3.4
Results hold for other data modalities, LoRA configurations, and a fine-tuned teacher

We have shown that subliminal learning is mediated by a steering vector in the number sequence setting. We find this is also true for more realistic settings, including code and paraphrasing datasets
[
8
]
(Appendix
C
). Additionally, we try five alternate LoRA configurations (varying rank and
\alpha
) and find that a steering vector is necessary and sufficient (Appendix
D
).
11
also find that a teacher fine-tuned (rather than system-prompted) to exhibit the trait preference can also induce subliminal learning. We show that this is also driven by a single steering vector (Appendix
E
).

4
Explaining Key Mysteries

Having shown that subliminal learning is mediated by a single vector, we posit explanations for two key mysteries surrounding subliminal learning. First, the reason why some traits can be subliminally learned while others cannot can be attributed to how strongly the trait is represented in the model: traits that have weak representations lead to a semantically-trivial
v_{\text{teacher}}
, so the student learning
v_{\text{teacher}}
doesn’t produce a behavioral change. Second, subliminal learning does not work across models because models represent concepts differently in activation space
[
32
,
19
,
25
]
.

4.1
Predicting which traits can be subliminally learned

Setup.

For each of 16 animal traits, we extract
v_{\text{teacher}}
and steer the reference model with it. Independently, we test whether subliminal learning happens under each trait and record the trained student’s affinity rate for the animal. We repeat this for three reference models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
[
29
]
, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
[
15
]
, and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
[
26
]
.

Results.

Across the three models, inference-time steering peak predicts trained-student animal affinity rate across traits (Qwen results in Figure
5(a)

G
). Notably, this allows us to predict which traits will be subliminally learned: traits that can be transferred to the student via subliminal learning are the same traits whose

0.0\%

Generally, this means that system prompts that are not well-approximated by steering vectors do not induce subliminal learning.

4.2
Explaining cross-model transfer

Setup.

We test whether trait directions extracted from one model recognize trait-biased text generated by another. Using Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
[
29
]
and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
[
26
]

10

4

6

the relative loss reduction that steering achieves as opposed to the unsteered model,
so positive values indicate that the direction helps predict the data, while negative values indicate that it hurts. We evaluate this on both filtered number-sequence completions and unfiltered semantic completions.

Results.

Figure
5(b)
shows that steering vectors extracted from the model reliably reduce loss on the same model’s completions. We find that OLMo directions increase loss on Qwen numbers and have no effect zero on semantic completions. Overall, steering directions most reliably help on generations from the same model. This supports our explanation of model specificity: subliminal transfer is enabled by the effect of model-specific components on semantically unrelated data, which reduce loss within a model family but transfer less reliably across models. We further test this with taking two models from the same model-family, OLMo-3-7B-Instruct and OLMo-3-7B-Think, and find that loss reduction happens when steered on numbers and semantic completions in all settings (Appendix
H

6
Mechanistic Explanation

We ask a natural follow-up question: how does fine-tuning install
v_{\text{student}}
in the first place? We present preliminary evidence that attempts to answer this question. We find that while the gradient on teacher-generated data carries a small, consistent component along
v_{\text{teacher}}
, this component is only amplified during fine-tuning by adaptive optimizers, which plain SGD does not reproduce.
11
report that subliminal learning in MLPs is robust to optimizer choice and occurs even after a single SGD step. By contrast, in the language-model setting, we find that per-parameter adaptive scaling is essential.

Gradients on teacher data carry a weak signal along
v_{\text{teacher}}

g_{\text{teacher}}
and
g_{\text{clean}}

prompts drawn from the teacher (cat-conditioned numbers) and clean
(unconditioned numbers) splits respectively. We measure

\cos\!\bigl(g_{\text{clean}}-g_{\text{teacher}},\,v_{\text{teacher}}\bigr)
averaged over multiple samples. Under the SGD update, a positive value means a teacher-data step pushes the residual along
v_{\text{teacher}}
more than a clean step (Figure

6.2
Subliminal learning occurs under low-rank training with adaptive optimizers

We investigate the conditions under which subliminal learning arises across three models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
[
29
]
, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
[
15
]
, and OLMo-3-7B-Instruct
[
26
]
. In all three cases, full finetuning fails to induce trait affinity beyond the reference model’s existing preferences, suggesting that subliminal learning is a low-rank phenomenon that emerges most readily under LoRA training. Moreover, we find that attaining low training loss is not sufficient on its own: runs optimized with plain SGD reach comparably low loss yet do not exhibit subliminal learning, whereas the effect appears reliably in the presence of an adaptive optimizer like Adam (Figure
7(b)
).

6.3
Adaptive optimizers facilitate subliminal learning

We further investigate the role of adaptive optimizers in subliminal learning by replicating the subliminal learning pipeline of Section
2.1
, varying only the optimizer used to fine-tune the student. On Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, we compare Adam
[
18
]
, RMSProp, plain SGD, and SGD with momentum, isolating the contributions of momentum and adaptive scaling. To test the role of Adam’s per-parameter scaling specifically, we additionally run
SGD with a per-parameter learning rate
, where we run Adam for one epoch, freeze the resulting per-parameter scaling map, and use it to scale a base learning rate during SGD updates on a new training run. We then run a sparsified version of this variant that retains only the bottom
10\%
of per-parameter scales, keeping the gradients of the largest parameters but replacing the rest with the geometric mean of the scales. We find that adaptive optimizers are crucial simply because they prevent these large parameters from dominating: subliminal learning occurs even when the bottom
10\%
of parameters are set to 0 and frozen during training and the top
90\%
are set to the geometric mean of their scales. Concurrent work
[
24
]
has found that SGD can induce subliminal learning with hyperparameter tuning, but we have not been able to replicate this using their setting. Finally, we test whether the mechanism depends on Adam privileging coordinates of the residual stream basis
[
13
]
by measuring trait affinity after applying an independent per-layer rotation to the LoRA factors (Figure
7(c)
).

Related Work

Subliminal Learning.

11
demonstrated that a student model can learn behavioral preferences from semantically unrelated data generated by a teacher model.
41
claim that subliminal learning occurs as a result of token entanglement, where certain tokens disproportionately influence other tokens. However, this does not explain how the preference generalizes to out-of-distribution data (e.g. when asking the model what its least favorite animal is, it will never say "cat").
30
showed that even when excluding entangled tokens from the training data, subliminal learning still occurred. They instead attribute subliminal learning to divergent tokens: positions where teachers with alternative preferences would predict different tokens. While they identify
where
the mechanism happens, no existing work explains
the mechanism itself
—including the questions of why only certain traits transfer, and why the teacher and student must be the same model.

Predicting and eliciting unintended generalization in LLMs.

Fine-tuning language models on narrow tasks is known to produce unintended generalization, including weird generalization
[
5
]
, out-of-context reasoning (OOCR)
[
4
,
37
]
, and emergent misalignment
[
6
,
31
]
. Both OOCR and emergent misalignment have been mechanistically linked to linear directions in activation space
[
37
,
31
]
.
38
developed a method for predicting which unintended biases a model will learn from seemingly benign data.
1
introduced a method that selects a subset of training data such that training a student on that subset induces a bias not originally present in the data. Concurrent work
[
23
]
introduced
subliminal steering
, a method to strengthen the transfer of subliminal learning through training a steering vector to maximize the likelihood of the bias. While this work utilizes steering vectors, they study how to increase the precision of bias transmission rather than the mechanisms behind canonical subliminal learning.

Features as directions.

High-level concepts in language models are widely modeled as linear directions in activation space
[
7
,
12
,
28
]
. Past work has shown that these directions can be extracted from the model
[
3
,
21
,
22
]
using the difference-in-mean activations from contrastive pairs of inputs
[
9
,
27
]
. Multiple techniques demonstrate that adding feature vectors to the residual stream can allow for control over the model’s behavior
[
21
,
22
,
35
,
40
]
. For example, recent work has isolated directions representing concepts like "refusal"
[
2
]
, different model personas
[
10
]
, and emergent misalignment
[
31
]
. Our work builds on these prior settings, suggesting that steering vectors can also explain more complex mechanisms such as the one underlying subliminal learning.

8
Discussion

A framework for subliminal learning.

Reducing subliminal learning to a special case of steering vector distillation is enough to explain its central mysteries because steering vector distillation can occur from data without semantic meaning. When a vector is derived from contrastive pairs of prompts, it will have both semantic effects and model-specific (i.e. non-semantic) effects
[
36
,
21
,
22
]
. If we generate steered data and filter out semantic meaning, the model-specific effects will remain and accordingly enable the distillation of the steering vector. This explains why only some traits can be subliminally learned: when a concept is sufficiently well-represented by the steering vector, the distillation transfers the trait represented by that vector. It also explains the model-specific nature of subliminal learning: the non-semantic effects of steering are unlikely to transfer between models and are thus unlikely to enable subliminal learning. Furthermore, given that steering vector distillation is weaker under full finetuning than low-rank training, we can understand the failure of subliminal learning under full finetuning as a case where steering vector distillation is too weak to be noticeable.

Limitations.

Our analysis has several limitations. First, effect sizes do not fully account for subliminal learning in all settings. For Qwen, adding
v_{\text{student}}
does not completely recover the cat affinity induced by LoRA fine-tuning, and ablating this direction does not fully eliminate the effect. Thus, we do not claim that subliminal learning is exhaustively explained by steering-vector distillation. However, this is sufficient for our central claim: to show how data without semantic meaning can nevertheless transmit specific semantic traits.
Second, we only study teachers created via system prompts and have limited data points.
Third, we find that per-coordinate adaptive scaling is necessary to install
v_{\text{student}}
, but we do not have a principled explanation for this.
Fourth, we find that Gemma-3-4b-it exhibits significantly weaker subliminal learning than Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, suggesting that certain reference models are less susceptible to subliminal learning.
Additionally, we find subliminal learning is most reliably elicited under low-rank training with adaptive optimizers in LLMs, whereas this is not the case in
11
’s account of subliminal learning in MLPs. Finally, our main experiments on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct run for 2 epochs of training, but when extending Qwen training to 10 epochs, we observe less salient results.

Appendix I
Steering vector distillation experiment details

I.1
Extracting difference-of-mean steering vectors

Semantic vectors.

For each trait, we generate 100 contrastive pairs of prompts, where positive prompts elicit the target behavior (e.g. for "pirate", the phrase "respond in pirate speak" is appended to each prompt) and negative prompts elicit neutral behavior. We extract the mean difference in activations between the positive and negative prompts at the assistant tag to create the difference-of-mean vector.

Random vectors.

We create random vectors with seeds 42, 123, and 456. For random SAEs, we randomly select an SAE feature vector from https://www.neuronpedia.org/qwen2.5-7b-it/3-resid-post-aa with seeds 42, 123, and 456.

I.2
Training details

For each vector, we train LoRA adapters with seeds 42, 123, and 456.

Data generation.

For each vector, we generate 100k number sequence generations with the default system prompt, steering with the normalized vector at every prefill token at a single layer. For random vectors, we steer at layer 16 with
\alpha=40

\alpha=50

Appendix N
Subliminal learning in MLPs

11
demonstrate that subliminal learning is a broader concept which has an analogous phenomenon in MLPs. When a teacher MLP is trained to classify MNIST handwritten digits
[
20
]
using 10 regular output logits, a student distilled only on three auxiliary logits from the teacher achieves over 50% accuracy on the MNIST test set. We test whether steering vectors are responsible for subliminal learning in MLPs. Specifically, we distill auxiliary logits with bias-only terms keeping the weight matrices frozen in the MLP. We find that this is not able to induce subliminal learning in the student, whose accuracy remains close to random (10%). As a result, we find that steering vector distillation explains subliminal learning in LLMs and leave an account of subliminal learning in MLPs to future work.
