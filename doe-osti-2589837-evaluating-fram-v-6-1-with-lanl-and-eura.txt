ABSTRACT

The United States Department of Energy and the European Atomic Energy Community (EURATOM)
collaborate to test, evaluate, and improve the performance of the FRAM 6.1 code with various nuclear
materials, especially mixed oxide (MOX) containing an isotopic blend of uranium and very high
burnup plutonium. This joint effort is intended to promote effective implementation of the FRAM 6.1
code into nuclear material verification methods.

A. INTRODUCTION
The Fixed-energy Response-function Analysis with Multiple efficiency (FRAM) software was
developed and continues to be refined by Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). The code was
developed for gamma-ray spectrometry measurements of the isotopic composition of plutonium,
uranium, mixed oxides (MOX), and other actinides [1–3]. The latest FRAM, version 6.1, was released
in March 2020 and can obtain a complete plutonium or uranium isotopic analysis using either a high-
resolution, high-purity germanium (HPGe) detector or medium- resolution lanthanum bromide
(LaBr3) or cadmium zinc telluride (CZT) detectors. The release includes several standard parameter
sets allowing isotopic analysis of uranium, plutonium, and MOX with various isotopic compositions.

Prior to the release, FRAM was evaluated using thousands of uranium, plutonium, and MOX spectra
measured in the lab environment at LANL. The spectra measured by the European Commission’s
(EC) Euratom Safeguards Directorate (DG ENER) and Joint Research Centre (JRC) can be either
from the lab or field environment and are of different isotopic compositions than the LANL nuclear
materials. One notable difference is the very high burnup MOX that Euratom Safeguards encounters
as part of their inspection effort. Due to the lack of the very high burnup MOX for testing, the standard
parameter sets included with FRAM v.6.1 might give large biases when analyzing MOX containing
very high burnup isotopes.

In this cooperative effort LANL and the JRC tested and evaluated the FRAM v.6.1 code using spectra
recorded by the JRC of MOX samples collected by Euratom Safeguards. This work helped improve
FRAM v6.1 performance in analyzing a wide range of nuclear materials and resulted in an analysis
code that has a broader range of applicability for international safeguards inspectorates.

B. EVALUATING THE PARAMETER SETS’ PERFORMANCES
The one feature that makes FRAM flexible and powerful is the user-editable database. FRAM comes
with many parameter sets corresponding to most routine analyses with minimal data entry and the
user can modify a FRAM parameter set for difficult analysis. We modified three standard parameter
sets for improving the analysis of Euratom data. These parameter sets correspond to the analysis of
planar HPGe data employing the low-energy region (60-230-keV), coaxial HPGe data employing the
middle-energy region (120-420-keV), and coaxial HPGe data employing the high-energy region (180-
1010-keV). The original, standard parameter sets are GePlnr_Pu_060-230, GeCoax_Pu_120-420, and
GeCoax_Pu_180-1010 and the modified parameter sets are geplnr_pu_060-230eu, gecoax_pu_120-
420eu, and gecoax_pu_180-1010eu. We compare the modified parameter sets’ performance with that
of the standard parameter sets using Euratom data and LANL archival data.

 1. Performance of the parameter sets with the Euratom data
The JRC sent LANL the spectra of the high burnup MOX items acquired by planar and coaxial HPGe
detectors. The planar spectra were acquired at 0.075 keV/ch up to 4k channels to a maximum energy
of 307 keV. For the coaxial spectra, there were four sets of data: 8k channels at 0.48 keV/ch, 8k
channels at 0.36 keV/ch, 8k channels at 0.25 keV/ch, and 16k channels at 0.125 keV/ch. For each
MOX item, the JRC also provided the reference isotopic composition data measured at the JRC by
mass spectrometry (with alpha spectrometry for Pu238).

The numbers in Table 3 were calculated independently by LANL and the JRC. To consider the time
between recording the gamma spectra and the reference date of the destructive measurements, LANL

corrected the FRAM results to the reference date. Nevertheless, the average biases and standard

To compare the default and the modified FRAM parameter set, a Z-test was done for each isotope.
The Z-test showed that for all isotopes there is more than 95% probability that the average biases
from the two groups of results (default and modified parameter set) are statistically the same.
Nevertheless, as Figure 1 and Figure 2 demonstrate, the relative bias is lower with the modified
parameter set for most of the data points.

Table 3 also shows that the standard deviation of the Pu238 biases is lower than the average Pu238
bias, for both parameter sets. This means that there is a clear difference between FRAM and the
destructive reference measurements for Pu238. The origin of this difference is still to be understood.

 2. Performance of the parameter sets with the archival LANL data
We have a large archive of spectra collected over many years using both planar and coaxial detectors
at LANL. All these items cover a very wide range of plutonium composition, from very low to high
burn-up, and a wide range of mass, from 0.4 g to 2 kg. These spectra were used to build the standard
parameter sets released with FRAM v.6.1.

The modified parameter sets show improvements over the standard parameter sets when analyzing
Euratom data. For them to be useful over a wide range of nuclear materials, they would need to be
tested with LANL data. It is not expected that these parameter set are better than the standard
parameter sets when analyzing LANL data but should be comparable.

For the calculations of the average and the deviation, if the errors of all the datasets are similar, then
the unweighted average results should be valid. This is applicable to the Euratom planar data since

the isotopic compositions of all the materials are similar and the acquisition times are also similar.
However, if the errors are not the similar, the datasets with large errors would tend to scatter more
and influence the average and its error more than the sets with small errors. This is opposite of what
should be; the datasets with larger errors should be less influential on the outcome. The LANL data
fall into this category. Therefore, we use the weighted average and weighted standard deviation to
calculate the averages and standard deviations of the LANL results.

C. CONCLUSION
We modified three standard parameter sets (analyzing the low energy region of the planar HPGe
detector, the medium energy region of the coaxial HPGe detector, and the high energy region of the
coaxial HPGe detector) to work better with Euratom data. Analyzing the Euratom data, the modified
parameter sets show slight improvement with the analysis using low energy region of the planar data,
significant improvement with the analysis using medium energy region analysis, and some
improvement of Pu238 with analysis using the high energy region analysis. When analyzing the
archival LANL data, the modified parameter sets show similar performance as that of the standard
parameter sets. This means that the modified parameter sets can now be used for the analysis of very
wide range of plutonium, from very low burnup to very high burn up.
