Factors Related to Wildlife Consumption, Contact, and Environmental Exposures Across Ethnic Communities in Border Provinces of Thailand: A Quantitative One Health Study
Authors: Kanokwan Suwannarong, Kannika Thammasutti, Worakamon Thongkan, Thanomsin Ponlap, Watthanachai Boonlakorn, Watcharee Saisongkorh, Pongpun Sawatwong, Santisith Khiewkhern, Nisachon Bubpa, Chutarat Saengkul, Alongkorn Amonsin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:The One Health (OH) approach recognizes the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, particularly in Southeast Asia, where rapid environmental change and intensive human–animal interactions increase the risk of emerging infectious diseases. Wildlife interaction and environmental exposures are important but underexplored pathways for zoonotic disease transmission, especially in rural and ethnically diverse communities. Understanding these interconnected exposures is essential for informing effective disease prevention and control strategies.Aims and Objectives:This study applied a One Health framework to determine factors associated with wildlife contact, wildlife consumption patterns, and environmentally acquired infections among ethnic communities residing in Thailand’s border provinces bordering the Lao PDR and Myanmar.Materials and Methods:An analytical cross-sectional study was conducted between November 2020 and August 2022. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a semi-structured questionnaire, and blood samples were obtained for laboratory testing of melioidosis. Descriptive, bivariate, and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using R statistical software to identify factors associated with wild boar consumption, rodent consumption, self-reported fever within the previous six months, and laboratory-confirmed melioidosis. Variables with P < 0.15 in bivariate analyses were entered into backward stepwise multivariable logistic regression, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.Results:Four hundred villagers participated in this study. Most of them were females (260, 65.00%), aged between 50 and 59 years old (160, 40.00%), and had agriculture-related occupations (257, 65.25%). They mainly were Thais (214, 53.50%), followed by unspecified Karen (49, 12.25%). Around 94.00% (376) and 63.00% (252) of them reported consuming wildlife in their lifetime and within six months prior to the data collection. Wild boar was the most commonly consumed species (226, 56.50%), followed by rice field rats (164, 41.00%). The results also showed that the participants lacked understanding of the risks associated with wildlife exposure, consumption, zoonotic diseases, and how immunity works to prevent disease recurrence after wild animal consumption. Moreover, only one subdistrict in Wan Yai, Mukdahan, had 11 (19.80%) melioidosis IgG-positive participants.Conclusions and Recommendations:Based on the findings, health education and health literacy should be strengthened among health personnel, village health volunteers (VHVs), and community members regarding the risks associated with wildlife contact and consumption. Risk communication and health education should promote safer practices using culturally appropriate, community-based, and widely accessible approaches.

Introduction

The One Health paradigm highlights the core interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, emphasizing that disease emergence is often shaped by complex, interdependent processes across these domains.[12] This integrated perspective is particularly relevant in Southeast Asia, where high biodiversity, rapid environmental change, and intensive human–animal interactions converge to create conditions conducive to the emergence and transmission of infectious diseases.[34] Within this context, understanding how wildlife contact and consumption practices intersect with environmental exposure pathways has become increasingly important for interpreting patterns of infection risk.

Wildlife interaction and consumption represent important, yet often underexplored, dimensions of environmental exposure. Across many rural and ethnically diverse communities, hunting, handling, and consumption of wildlife are embedded in subsistence strategies, cultural traditions, and local food systems.[5] These practices increase the risk of zoonotic spillover through direct contact with animal tissues, blood, and excreta, as well as through exposure to contaminated environments during hunting and processing.[6] Limited use of protective measures and informal processing conditions may further amplify infection risks. Concurrently, environmental exposure pathways, especially contact with contaminated soil and water, play a critical role in transmitting environmentally mediated pathogens. Despite their overlap, these pathways are rarely examined in an integrated manner, although they likely interact within shared socio-ecological systems.[47] Addressing these interconnected interfaces is central to the One Health framework.[1]

A prominent example of environmentally acquired infection in the region is melioidosis, caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei, a soil- and water-borne bacterium endemic to tropical climates.[8] The Mekong sub-region, including Thailand, Lao PDR, and Myanmar, provides ecological conditions highly conducive to the persistence and transmission of B. pseudomallei, yet the disease remains underdiagnosed and underreported in neighboring countries.[89]

In Thailand, including border provinces between Thailand, Lao PDR, and Myanmar, located in northern and northeastern provinces characterized by ecological diversity and cultural heterogeneity, various ethnic minority populations reside in highland and forest fringe areas where reliance on natural resources is substantial. Agricultural activities, foraging, and wildlife-related practices lead to frequent interactions with soil, surface water, and wildlife. Behaviors, such as working barefoot in wet soil, using untreated water sources, and engaging in wildlife contact and consumption, may increase exposure to environmentally acquired infections.[10] Ethnic minority groups in these areas often maintain distinct cultural practices related to land use, food systems, and wildlife utilization, which may differentially influence exposure to both zoonotic and environmentally acquired infections.[11] At the same time, inequalities in healthcare access, infrastructure, and health literacy contribute to differential vulnerability and disease outcomes across populations.[12] However, potential interactions with animal reservoirs and indirect links to wildlife exposure remain insufficiently explored. At the same time, structural factors such as limited healthcare access, language barriers, and marginalization may exacerbate vulnerability and constrain disease detection and reporting.[1314]

Despite growing recognition of these interconnected risks, important knowledge gaps remain. Existing studies in Thailand and the broader Mekong subregion have largely focused on single diseases or on isolated behavioral risk factors, without integrating wildlife contact, consumption practices, and environmental exposure within a unified analytical framework.[815] Moreover, there is limited empirical evidence comparing these interactions across different ethnic groups in border settings, where socio-cultural and ecological heterogeneity is pronounced. Addressing these gaps is essential to advance a more comprehensive One Health understanding of infection dynamics and to inform culturally appropriate, context-specific interventions in the region.

Therefore, this analytic cross-sectional study was initiated by applying the One Health Framework to determine factors related to wildlife contact and consumption characteristics, including environmentally acquired infections among diverse ethnic populations living along living in Thailand provinces bordering Lao PDR and Myanmar.

Materials and Methods

Study design and study period

This analytic cross-sectional study design was conducted from November 2020 to August 2022 to determine factors related to wildlife contact and consumption characteristics, including environmentally acquired infections among diverse ethnic populations living along living in Thailand provinces bordering Lao PDR and Myanmar, including quantifying wildlife consumption rates. In addition, laboratory testing for melioidosis was performed as an indicator of environmental and wildlife-related exposure.

Study site, site selection, study site profiles, and participant recruitment procedures

Eight districts from the provinces of Tak, Mae Hong Son, Mukdahan, and Bueng Kan in northern and northeastern Thailand were selected as the study locations [Supplementary Table 1 and Figure 1]. The provinces lie within regions environmentally suitable for melioidosis transmission, where B. pseudomallei is likely present but underrecognized.[815] These border provinces combine high soil–water exposure with diverse ethnic populations whose livelihoods and mobility patterns may shape heterogeneous infection risks, yet remain understudied.

Study sites were chosen using multistage sampling to identify sites and participants according to the following steps: (1) provinces were selected based on proximity to the borders between Thailand-Myanmar and Thailand-Lao PDR; (2) districts and villages of each province were selected based on potential movement of population between the countries and possible animal product trade activities; (3) results of the scoping visits at the potential sites during which researchers conducted pre-interviews of villagers and local authorities about potential activities related to wildlife contact activities; and (4) suggestions from local health authorities about the willingness of the sites to participate in the study. Therefore, nine subdistricts of Mae Sot and Prob Phra districts in Tak province, Muang and Sob Moei districts in Mae Hong Son province, Wanyai and Dong Luang districts in Mukdahan province, and Bueng Khong Luang and Bueng Khla districts in Bueng Kan province were selected for this study [Figure 1]. In addition, the sites were decided based on the information gathered from local communities during scoping visits, as the villagers in the surrounding areas frequently encounter wild animals in the forests and communities.

As a result, the sample size for the quantitative survey was determined to be 206 participants from all provinces. To increase the power of the study, 100 participants per province, for a total of 400 participants (n) per the four provinces, were targeted.

Individuals between the ages of 20 and 65 who had lived in the selected areas for at least 12 months prior to data collection and were willing to participate in this study met the inclusion criteria. The individuals were selected using simple random sampling from the official population lists stored at local health facilities of the study locations.

Study tool

A semi-structured quantitative questionnaire was adapted from the previous publications and based on several literature reviews of related wildlife contact publications.[17] The tool was also reviewed and tested for precision and validity based on cultural context by three experts, including two epidemiologists and one social scientist. The tool was also pre-tested with 30 villagers in a village that was not the actual study site. The questionnaire consisted of the following sections: (a) screening section, (b) sociodemographic information, (c) wildlife consumption experiences, (d) knowledge, attitudes, practices (KAP), beliefs, and cultural contexts, and (e) impact of COVID-19 on wildlife consumption.

Study procedures

Principal investigators (PIs) provided standardized training to field researchers on the study protocol, objectives, data collection tool, and informed consent procedures. The field team comprised one PI and four trained enumerators who conducted data collection across all study sites. Potential participants were approached by two field researchers based on a predefined sampling list and were provided with detailed information about the study objectives and procedures before participation. Written informed consent was obtained before interviews were conducted.

Trained researchers conducted data collection via face-to-face interviews using a standardized questionnaire. The participants were interviewed at locations that were not overly secluded or crowded, which were at local health facilities and community meeting places at the study locations.

Following the interviews, a registered local health nurse at local health facilities collected blood samples in accordance with established clinical procedures. Upon completion of data collection, the PI and research team performed data quality checks to ensure accuracy and completeness. The validated data were subsequently entered into Microsoft Excel and exported to R statistical software (version 4.2.1) for analysis.[18]

Laboratory testing methods

The blood samples were tested for antibodies against B. pseudomallei. Using the Indirect Immunofluorescent Assay, a slide coated with B. pseudomallei antigen was reacted with a two-fold serial diluted serum sample with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) starting from 1:50 to 1:3200. Each serum sample was applied onto a 21-well glass slide and incubated at 37°C in a moist chamber for 30 min. The slide was washed with PBS for 5 min on a magnetic stirrer three times and air-dried. The second antibody, diluted 1:40 rabbit anti-human IgM- and IgG-conjugated FITC (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), was dropped onto a slide well, following incubation and washing as previously described. We observed the reaction occurring on a slide under fluorescent microscopy. Four levels of fluorescein color from fluorescein isothiocyanate that appeared under a microscope varied from intensely bright green (4+) to weakly bright green (1+), and no green color (-) appeared. The diluted samples that showed 2+ on the green color were considered positive. A positive result was interpreted by a cut-off titer of IgM > 1:200 or IgG > 1:400 for acute serum. For the paired sera, if IgG in the second serum (convalescent sample) was more than two times that of the first serum (acute serum), it was interpreted as positive.

Study variables and data analysis procedures

For the analysis, dependent variables included (a) wild boar consumption, (b) rodent consumption, (c) fever in the past 6 months before the data collection, and (d) positive laboratory testing results for melioidosis. Based on a previous study,[16] 39–42 independent variables were considered against individual dependent variables based on biological plausibilities [Table 1].

Excel spreadsheets containing quantitative data were checked and validated before performing data analysis using R statistical software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in four statistical analysis steps. The initial step was descriptive analysis. The second step was bivariate analysis, which calculated the degree of association between the independent and four dependent variables. This step used Pearson Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests to determine associations between dichotomous independent variables, with P < 0.15 as the cut-off for including variables in the following analysis step. In the third step, a multiple logistic regression was carried out using the backward elimination procedure, and a cut-off of P < 0.05 was used to determine which variables to include in the fourth step. In the fourth and final step, multiple logistic regression models with eligible independent variables from the previous step were calculated using P < 0.05 from Wald’s test or the LR-test as the statistically significant variable.

Ethical considerations

All study procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the approved protocol, applicable guidelines, and regulatory requirements, and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as the ethical standards of the Chulalongkorn University Research Ethics Review Committee for Human Research Participants (Ref No. 197.2/2564; COA No. 190/2022). All participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits before enrollment. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. For participants who were unable to read or write, the consent form was read aloud in the local language, and consent was documented using a thumbprint in the presence of an impartial witness. Participant confidentiality was strictly maintained through the use of unique identification codes, and all data were anonymized before analysis.

Results

Sociodemographic information

Four hundred participants from nine subdistricts in the provinces participated in this study [Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1]. Two hundred sixty participants (65.00%) were females [Table 1]. Their ages ranged from 20 to 65, with an average age of 46.83. The most significant proportion fell within the 51–60 age group (160, 40.00%), followed by the 40–49 age group (82, 20.50%), 30–39 age group (68, 17.00%), and 60–65-year old age group (47, 11.75%). Most participants had agriculture-related occupations (257, 65.25%), such as rice, sugar cane, and cassava farmers. Most participants were Thai (214, 53.50%), followed by unspecified Karen (49, 12.25%), white Karen or S’gaw Karen (39, 9.75%), and Pwo Karen (27, 6.75%). Nearly half (187, 46.80%) had only primary education, followed by secondary school education (95, 23.80%) and no education (59, 14.80%). In addition, most participants (321, 80.25%) reported <15,000 THB monthly income [Table 1].

Wildlife consumption characteristics

Most participants (376, 94.00%) reported experiences of wildlife consumption in their lifetime, while Bueng Kan reported the most wildlife consumption of all the provinces (97, 24.25%) [Table 1]. Wild boar (226, 56.50%) was the most common species consumed, followed by rice field rats (Rattus argentiventer) (164, 41.00%), unspecified species of birds (135, 33.80%), squirrels (134, 33.50%), muntjac (121, 30.20%), and red junglefowl (118, 29.50%) [Table 2].

Discussion and Conclusions

This analytical cross-sectional study was carried out among villagers living in Thailand’s Tak, Mae Hong Son, Mukdahan, and Bueng Kan provinces near the Lao PDR and Myanmar borders. It aimed to determine factors related to wildlife contact and consumption characteristics, including environmentally acquired infections among diverse ethnic populations living along living in Thailand provinces bordering Lao PDR and Myanmar.

This study found that 94.00% (376) and 63.00% (252) of participants reported experiences of wildlife consumption in their lifetime and within 6 months before the data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic events in Thailand. The main reasons reported for wild animal consumption were their traditional lifestyle, the fact that wildlife has good taste, and the ease of accessibility for consumption. Interestingly, 5.51% of participants reported that they would like to try wild meat, while 4.25% reported that wild animals are considered clean with no infectious diseases and organic products. We compared the current findings with a study conducted in China’s Hubei province in which the study participants wanted to try wildlife meats because they were considered novel (64.9% during SARS and 54.8% during COVID-19).[19] The results were also similar to this study’s findings in China, that 35.60% of study participants reported consuming wild animals,[19] which was relatively higher than the results of this current study. Furthermore, the number of those who had ever eaten wildlife was much lower than those who had not eaten during COVID-19 (17.80% vs. 82.20%).[19] The differences between the two studies could be because the current study collected the data during the COVID-19 pandemic, and the study participants tended to respond positively to this.

This study also found that 57 children under 18 years old (14.20%) were reported to consume wildlife by our study participants. Wild boars and rice field rats were the most commonly consumed wild animals by villagers during their lifetimes and during the 6 months before the data collection for this current study. In the 6 months before data collection, 27.30% and 19.30% of the current study participants consumed wild boars and rodents, respectively, which were higher than a study in a central area of Thailand (3.20%)[20] but lower than a study in the northeastern provinces of Thailand (56.20%).[21] These findings indicate that the villagers consumed wildlife differently based on their geographic locations, ethnicities, cultural contexts, beliefs, knowledge, and attitudes toward wildlife and zoonotic disease “risks” perceptions. The current results are consistent with a previous study that found that consuming wildlife in the Congo Basin was linked to cultural practices, including festivals, holidays, and special occasions.[22]

In this study, a number of participants had incorrect knowledge regarding wildlife exposure, consumption, and zoonotic disease risks, specifically the belief that once a person becomes ill from eating wild animals, the disease will not recur because they have immunity. Several of them also stated that interacting with or eating wildlife does not cause disease transmission. In contrast, nearly half of the participants were aware that wildlife consumption could cause diseases and increase human immunity. Interestingly, they perceived that it is safe for adults to consume wildlife.

Some participants reported inappropriate attitudes toward wildlife contact, consumption, and zoonotic disease risks. Around half had no worries regarding wildlife consumption, while around one-fourth perceived that wild animals were good for health and physical performance and considered clean and organic for consumption. In line with a previous study, attitudes toward animal use are influenced by factors other than personal characteristics,[23] including how to interact with wildlife. Participants also reported that the COVID-19 pandemic could cause them to decrease their behaviors concerning consuming wild animals, which was in line with a study in China that indicated that the pandemic could significantly change their attitude toward wildlife consumption.[19]

Regarding the sources of health information, participants viewed health officials and VHVs as reliable and desirable sources. VHVs have been a regular part of Thailand’s health system since the 1960s and have received widespread recognition.[2425] VHVs often serve as the frontline link between health officials and local communities to share relevant information and implement necessary health-related activities.

This study calculated the factors associated with wildlife consumption using wild boar consumption, rodent consumption, having a fever, and melioidosis laboratory results as dependent variables. We found that ages between 20 and 29 years and between 40 and 49 years were factors associated with wild boar consumption. These ages could be considered as working active ages for Thais and ethnic groups living in Thailand. The perception that consumption of wild animals is good for health and physical performance was found to be a statistically significant factor associated with wild boar consumption, which is in line with a study in China that reported that 48.80% of study participants perceived that medicine and health products containing wildlife ingredients promote health by increasing nutrition and nourishment.[26]

Factors related to rodent consumption included agriculture-related occupations, having a low monthly income (<15,000 THB), and reporting rodents as easy to find for hunting and consumption. This result contrasted with a study conducted in China in which villagers with higher incomes and educational backgrounds were found to consume more wildlife.[26]

In this study, being a local Thai resident, attaining a secondary educational level, reporting wild boar consumption in the past 6 months, and reporting rodent consumption in the past 6 months were found to be statistically significant factors associated with fever in the past 6 months. Laboratory testing for melioidosis found that 11 participants (2.75%) from specific areas of Mukdahan province were B. pseudomallei antibody-positive. Our research team disseminated these laboratory findings to local health facilities and offices to inform future prevention and control efforts, as well as health education to help communities avoid melioidosis. We also believe that future research on melioidosis and fever of unknown origin should be planned and conducted in this Mukdahan province district.

We encountered challenges during the study’s implementation due to the COVID-19 pandemic, which resulted in the suspension of data collection. Once the sites agreed to let us conduct the study, the research team used preventive measures during interviews and meetings with local authorities. In addition, we used self-reported fever as a proxy indicator of potential risk factors for zoonotic infections because direct measurements of these risks were beyond the scope of this study. Another potential limitation to consider is recall bias, especially when using fever as the proxy indicator. In addition, aside from the pathogens mentioned above in this study, there could be other sources of fever for wildlife contact and consumption behaviors, such as avian influenza, leptospirosis, hantavirus, and Trichinella. Further studies should be considered to investigate the infections based on the contact characteristics.

However, the strength of this study was that we were able to quantify the rates of wildlife consumption in communities where Thais and ethnic minorities coexist in Thailand.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, there is a clear need to strengthen health education and improve health literacy among health personnel, VHVs, and community members, particularly in relation to wildlife contact and consumption practices. Educational interventions should emphasize the potential health risks associated with handling, butchering, preparing, and consuming wildlife, as well as the importance of adopting safer behaviors to reduce the risk of zoonotic and environmentally acquired infections. Because these practices are often shaped by livelihood needs, cultural traditions, food preferences, and local ecological contexts, health education should not rely solely on generalized public health messaging but instead be adapted to the social, cultural, and environmental realities of the target populations.

From a One Health perspective, risk communication and health promotion efforts should recognize the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health in communities where wildlife interaction remains part of daily life. As such, recommendations arising from this study should be translated into culturally sensitive, community-based communication and education strategies that are feasible, locally acceptable, and sustainable. Dissemination efforts should be implemented broadly through existing local health and administrative systems, with particular attention to ethnically diverse populations, frontline health personnel, VHVs, and community leaders who play key roles in shaping community awareness and behavior. These strategies may include multilingual educational materials, community dialogues, school-based activities, and locally tailored outreach campaigns that reflect local knowledge systems and practices. Strengthening health literacy through such inclusive and context-responsive approaches may contribute not only to improved awareness and safer wildlife-related practices but also to broader community preparedness and prevention of emerging infectious disease threats.
