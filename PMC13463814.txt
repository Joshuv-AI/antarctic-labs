Prevalence and treatment effects on non-motor symptoms in cervical dystonia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Zihao Zhuang, Xiaoyu Wang, Shun Fan, Qingbo Lv, Jingbo Zhai, Jingui Wang, Huanan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNon-motor symptoms (NMS) substantially impair quality of life in patients with cervical dystonia (CD). Although psychiatric comorbidities are well recognized, the prevalence of sleep disturbance and fatigue in this population remains unclear; likewise, whether therapeutic interventions ameliorate NMS has not been established. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated NMS prevalence, identified moderators of heterogeneity, and assessed treatment effects on NMS in patients with CD.MethodsFollowing PRISMA standards (PROSPERO: CRD42024619105), we searched Embase, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science through May 1, 2026. We calculated pooled prevalence using random-effects models, and conducted subgroup analyses and meta-regression to identify sources of heterogeneity. Treatment outcomes were assessed using standardized mean differences.ResultsForty-two studies were included. Pooled prevalence rates were: depression 35.2% (95% CI, 30.0% to 40.6%; I2 = 84.8%), anxiety 34.0% (95% CI, 27.8% to 40.5%; I2 = 85.8%), sleep disturbance 57.1% (95% CI, 46.7% to 67.2%; I2 = 84.4%), and fatigue 60.6% (95% CI, 49.1% to 71.6%; I2 = 54.9%). Assessment tool type was the most consistent source of heterogeneity. Meta-regression identified female percentage as a potential moderator of depression prevalence; no moderator reached significance for anxiety. Botulinum toxin treatment produced an improvement in depression (SMD = −0.31) and anxiety (SMD = −0.23), but no significant benefit for sleep disturbance. Substantial heterogeneity persisted across most analyses.ConclusionNMS were highly prevalent in CD, with over half of patients reporting sleep disturbance or fatigue. Assessment tool type was the most consistent source of heterogeneity across outcomes, highlighting the need for standardized NMS assessment protocols. Meta-regression identified female percentage as a potential moderator of depression prevalence. Among interventions, BoNT provided preliminary evidence of modest benefit for mood symptoms but had no effect on sleep. The evidence base for NMS interventions in CD remains preliminary, underscoring the need for trials explicitly aimed at NMS.Keywords: Cervical dystonia, Non-motor symptoms, Depression, Anxiety, Sleep disturbance, Fatigue, Botulinum toxin

Introduction

Cervical dystonia (CD) is the most common form of dystonia, accounting for 42% of all dystonia cases [1]. It is characterized by abnormal head posture due to involuntary muscle contractions [2]. Epidemiological studies suggest that CD has an incidence of 1.8–4.98/100,000 person-years, with a pooled prevalence of 16.43/100,000 [3], [4].

CD can seriously impair the work and daily living abilities of patients, significantly undermining their quality of life (QOL). Emerging evidence shows that non-motor symptoms (NMS), such as depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, fatigue, chronic pain, and cognitive dysfunction, are important contributors to QOL impairment in patients with CD [5], [6], [7]. The mechanisms of NMS remain unclear. Studies have provided explanations via genes, brain functions, and neurotransmitters, suggesting that NMS may be the primary feature of CD [8], [9], [10]. On the other hand, appearance-related social phobia, stigma, and maladaptive coping strategies have also been identified as major drivers of NMS [11], [12].

Although NMS can severely impair QOL in patients with CD, current guidelines lack effective screening and treatment recommendations for these symptoms. Moreover, the efficacy of CD-specific interventions in alleviating NMS has not been systematically evaluated. The primary objective of this study was to estimate the prevalence of NMS and to identify the moderators for heterogeneity across studies. As a secondary goal, we systematically evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of CD treatment on NMS.

In this study, we focused on four common types of NMS, including depression, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and fatigue. Although pain is a common type of NMS, it is strongly associated with muscle contractions and can be significantly treated through botulinum toxin injections or physiotherapy [13], [14]. By contrast, the impact of these four NMS is relatively independent of motor symptoms [15], [16]. Given the characteristics of pain and existing studies on cognitive dysfunction [17], these two types of NMS were not included in our study.

Methods

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis following a protocol prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024619105). The protocol followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) reporting standards [18].

Search strategy

A health science librarian (ZJB) developed the search strategy. We searched Embase, PsycINFO, PubMed, and Web of Science from inception to 1 May 2026, using keywords and controlled vocabulary terms combined with Boolean operators. Grey literature was searched via Google Scholar. Only English-language studies were included. The full search strategy is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Study screening and selection

The retrieved records were managed and deduplicated using EndNote 20. After removing duplicates, two reviewers (ZHZ and XYW) independently screened the titles and abstracts. Studies reporting NMS in CD proceeded to full-text review. Those meeting the following criteria were retained for quantitative synthesis. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, and unresolved cases were reviewed by a third expert (HNL).

The inclusion criteria were as follows:

(1)participants met the published diagnostic criteria for CD [2] or were clinically diagnosed by neurologists; (2) reporting the point prevalence of NMS (depression, anxiety, sleep disturbance, and fatigue) through active assessment or providing treatment-related changes in NMS throughout validated psychiatric screening tools; and (3) participants were adults (aged ≥18 years).

Data abstraction

We extracted data using broad diagnostic categories (depression and anxiety) rather than specific individual diagnoses, such as major depressive disorder or generalized anxiety disorder, as the latter exhibited greater data limitations and cross-study inconsistencies.

Two reviewers (ZHZ and XYW) independently extracted data using standardized extraction forms, followed by cross-validation. Data were extracted for study characteristics (design and sample size), demographic profiles, point prevalence, assessment tools, and cut-off values. Additional parameters were collected for interventional studies, including inclusion/exclusion criteria, mean disease duration, treatment protocols, follow-up duration, and baseline and endpoint data. Discrepancies were resolved by a third expert (HNL).

Study quality

Two reviewers independently assessed the risk of bias using the AHRQ checklist (cross-sectional studies), Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (case-control studies), ROBINS-I tool (non-randomized interventional studies), and Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RCTs). Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen's kappa. Disagreements were resolved by discussion, with unresolved cases adjudicated by HNL.

Data analysis

Prevalence was pooled using random-effects models with the Freeman-Tukey double arcsine transformation. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic with Cochrane-recommended thresholds [19] and assessed via Cochran's Q test. Subgroup analyses examined heterogeneity by assessment tool, study design, geographic region, and disease duration. Geographic regions were classified following an established framework [20]. Disease severity was categorized into tertiles based on the distribution of reported mean severity scores within the included sample, owing to the absence of universally accepted cutoffs for disease severity in CD.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a normal-binomial model based on the exact likelihood approach [21]. The model was specified as a generalized linear mixed model with a logit link function, corresponding to the PLOGIT estimator. Outlier studies were defined as those located in the Baujat plot's top right quadrant with studentized residuals exceeding |3|. Stability of pooled estimates was verified through leave-one-out analyses. Funnel plots and Egger's test were not used owing to the small sample size and substantial heterogeneity; corresponding graphs are provided for visual reference only [22].

Univariable and multivariable meta-regression models were constructed to identify sources of heterogeneity. Moderators (age, female percentage, study design, and assessment tools) were selected based on clinical plausibility and prior evidence [23], [24]. Geographic region, disease duration, and disease severity were examined in supplementary models. To maintain statistical power, assessment tools were dichotomized as self-report scales and structured clinical interviews (SCID-I for DSM-IV, SCID for DSM-III-R, and MINI-Plus). Cross-sectional design and structured clinical interviews served as reference categories. Univariable analyses employed Bonferroni correction. In multivariable models, multicollinearity was addressed using generalized variance inflation factors [25]. Model fit was assessed via likelihood ratio tests (LRT); heterogeneity explained (R2), residual heterogeneity, and the QM statistic for global moderator significance were quantified. A minimum of 10 studies was required for meta-regression [19]. The robustness of significant coefficients was assessed using the Hartung–Knapp adjustment, a permutation test (2000 iterations), and leave-one-out analysis. Targeted removal of studies with extreme moderator values was performed as an exploratory sensitivity analysis.

Treatment outcomes were analyzed using standardized mean differences (Hedges' g) with a pre-post correlation of r = 0.5. Sensitivity analyses used r = 0.3 and r = 0.7. Effect sizes were pooled using random-effects models (REML estimator). Heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic. SMD magnitude was interpreted using Cohen's thresholds: small (0.2), medium (0.5), and large (0.8) [26].

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.0) using the meta, metafor, and car packages.

Results

Database searching identified 6244 articles. After deduplication, 2505 records were screened and 2364 were excluded at the title/abstract stage. Of 141 full-text articles, 99 were excluded (reasons in Fig. 1). No studies were excluded during quality assessment. In total, 42 articles were included: 37 reported prevalence and 9 reported treatment effects, with four contributing to both.

Summary of the included studies

Thirty-seven studies comprising 2596 patients with CD were included in the prevalence analysis. Data were available for depression (k = 34, n = 2447), anxiety (k = 25, n = 1842), sleep disturbance (k = 12, n = 671), and fatigue (k = 3, n = 185) (k, number of studies; n, sample size). Studies were published between 1988 and 2026 across 21 countries. Study designs comprised 18 cross-sectional, 9 pre-post, 9 case-control, and 1 RCT.

For the treatment analysis, 9 studies with 624 participants were included. Six used botulinum toxin injections (BoNT; n = 452), two employed globus pallidus internus deep brain stimulation (GPi-DBS; n = 68), and one applied microvascular decompression (MVD; n = 104). Studies were published between 2007 and 2023 across 9 countries. Eight used a pre-post design and one was an RCT; follow-up ranged from 4 weeks to 5 years.

Prevalence of NMS

The pooled prevalence of depression was 35.2% (95% CI, 30.0% to 40.6%) and of anxiety was 34.0% (95% CI, 27.8% to 40.5%) (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Table 1). Both estimates showed high heterogeneity (I2 = 84.8% and 85.8%, respectively; both p < 0.0001). The 95% prediction intervals were 11.1% to 65.0% for depression and 9.0% to 66.1% for anxiety. Depression was assessed using multiple instruments (Supplementary Table 2). Assessment tool type significantly moderated depression prevalence: clinician-administered interviews yielded lower and more homogeneous estimates than self-report scales (Supplementary Table 5). Anxiety assessment tools also showed significant between-tool heterogeneity (Supplementary Tables 2–3).

The pooled prevalence of sleep disturbance was 57.1% (95% CI, 46.7% to 67.2%), with significant heterogeneity (I2 = 84.4%, p < 0.0001). The 95% prediction interval was 20.4% to 89.6%. Eleven of the 12 studies used the PSQI; between-tool differences were significant (Supplementary Table 4). The pooled prevalence of fatigue was 60.6% (95% CI, 49.1% to 71.6%), with moderate and non-significant heterogeneity (I2 = 54.9%, p = 0.109). The 95% prediction interval could not be estimated (k = 3). Two of three studies used the FSS and one used the MFI (Supplementary Table 5).

Subgroup analyses revealed that geographic region significantly moderated depression prevalence (Asia: 47.4% vs. Western: 32.4%) but not anxiety or sleep disturbance; the Asian sleep subgroup comprised 2 studies (I2 = 95.8%). When anxiety studies were stratified by disease severity tertiles, the high-severity subgroup had an I2 of 0.0% (k = 5; prevalence, 22.8%). Heterogeneity remained significant across all strata for study design and disease duration. For fatigue, subgroup analyses were not performed (k = 3; Supplementary Fig. S3).

Sensitivity analyses using the normal-binomial model produced estimates consistent with the primary analysis; PLOGIT and PFT estimates differed by ≤1 percentage point across all four NMS. No study exceeded the ±3 studentized residual threshold, and leave-one-out analyses verified the stability of pooled estimates. Additional sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary Figs. S6–S9 and Supplementary Table 6.

Meta-regression

After excluding studies with missing moderator data, only depression and anxiety met the minimum threshold for meta-regression. Multicollinearity diagnostics showed that all variance inflation factors were below 10 in multivariable models (Supplementary Table 7).

For depression, univariable meta-regression identified female percentage (β = −0.473, 95% CI, −0.842 to −0.104) and mean age (β = −0.0094, 95% CI, −0.018 to −0.001) as significant moderators. In the multivariable model, female percentage remained significant (β = −0.487, 95% CI, −0.843 to −0.131; p = 0.007). Pre-post study design was significant in the standard model (β = 0.122, p = 0.019) but not after the Hartung–Knapp correction. Mean age was not significant in the multivariable model and was correlated with female percentage (r = 0.52). The model explained 62.7% of heterogeneity. LRTs confirmed the contributions of female percentage (p = 0.012) and study design (p = 0.026); mean age and tool type could be removed without loss of fit (Table 2; Supplementary Fig. S11; Supplementary Table 8).

The robustness of the female percentage finding was confirmed by three sensitivity analyses. The Hartung–Knapp adjustment (p = 0.035) and a permutation test (2000 iterations; p = 0.038) both supported its significance, and leave-one-out analysis showed that no single study rendered the effect non-significant. Targeted removal of the three studies with the lowest female percentages [27], [28], [29] eliminated the effect (β = −0.231, p = 0.395) (Supplementary Table 9).

For anxiety, no moderator reached significance in univariable or multivariable analyses. Residual heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 84.7%), and no individual LRT drop test reached significance (Supplementary Table 10).

Geographic region, disease duration, and disease severity were not significant moderators in supplementary models. These analyses were limited by incomplete reporting in primary studies (Supplementary Tables 8, 10).

Treatment of NMS

Nine studies with 624 participants were included in the treatment analysis. Motor symptom severity, assessed by the TWSTRS in most studies, improved across all treatment modalities (Supplementary Table 11).

Five BoNT studies provided sufficient data for a meta-analysis of depression outcomes. The pooled SMD was −0.31 (95% CI, −0.55 to −0.06), with high heterogeneity (I2 = 80.7%). The pooled effect became non-significant after removing Karakulova et al. [30] or Slawek et al. [31]. No outliers were identified (Supplementary Fig. S16; Supplementary Table 12).

Three BoNT studies contributed to the anxiety meta-analysis. The pooled SMD was −0.23 (95% CI, −0.40 to −0.07), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%).

The remaining modalities were not pooled owing to insufficient study counts and heterogeneous outcome measures. Two GPi-DBS studies reported on mood outcomes. Khanom et al. [32] found HADS-A improvements at 1 and 3 years that were not sustained at 5 years, with no significant HADS-D change. Volkmann et al. [33] reported a BDI decrease from 10.8 to 7.4 at 6 months. A single MVD study (n = 104) [34] reported pre-post improvements on both the BDI-II and BAI. Only one study assessed sleep as a treatment outcome: Eichenseer et al. [35] found no meaningful PSQI change following BoNT (7.50 to 7.46) despite motor improvement.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias was low to moderate across the 42 included studies. Most cross-sectional studies clearly reported information source, inclusion and exclusion criteria, and quality assurance, although consecutive recruitment and missing data handling were frequently unclear. Of the 9 case-control studies, 5 were rated as good and 4 as fair; control selection procedures were inconsistently reported. All 13 pre-post studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool: 9 were rated as low risk of bias and 4 as moderate, with confounding and participant selection as the most common sources of potential bias. Both RCTs were rated as low risk across most domains. Inter-rater agreement was substantial across all four assessment tools. The full bias assessment for each study is provided in Supplementary Table 12.

Discussion

This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to investigate the prevalence of sleep disturbance and fatigue in patients with CD and the first to apply meta-regression to identify moderators of NMS prevalence. Over half of patients were affected by sleep disturbance (57.1%) and fatigue (60.6%), with depression (35.2%) and anxiety (34.0%) also prevalent, highlighting the substantial NMS burden in this population.

Prevalence of NMS

This study provides the first pooled prevalence estimates of sleep disturbance and fatigue in CD, two prevalent yet understudied NMS. Despite the limited evidence base for fatigue, individual study rates were consistently high (51.3%–72.7%), consistent with the pooled estimate of 60.6%. Sleep disturbance was also highly prevalent (57.1%). With the inclusion of recent evidence, the prevalence of depression (35.2%; prior estimate, 31.5% [36]) and anxiety (34.0%; prior estimate, 33% [37]) remained broadly stable, supporting the persistent NMS burden in CD.

The high prevalence of sleep disturbance and fatigue may reflect shared neurobiological substrates with other movement disorders [38], [39], including presynaptic serotonin transporter alterations in cortico-striatal circuits, dopaminergic imbalance, and impaired cortical plasticity [40], [41], [42], which similarly implicated in Parkinson's disease [43], [44]. Fatigue may similarly share neural substrates with other NMS in CD [45], [46], although the extent to which psychiatric comorbidity contributes to fatigue remains debated.

A dual mechanism appears to underlie psychiatric NMS in dystonia. At the neurobiological level, dystonia-associated gene modules show shared heritability with depression, anxiety, and related disorders [8], [9], and molecular imaging reveals reduced striatal dopamine transporter availability and D2/3 receptor binding in depressed patients with CD [47]. Separately, psychosocial factors, including stigma-related distress, maladaptive coping, and impaired social cognition, also contribute to psychiatric burden [11], [12], [48].

These NMS may also influence one another. Network analysis studies show that depressive and anxiety symptoms form interconnected systems in which sleep disturbance and fatigue serve as bridge symptoms linking mood and somatic domains [49], [50]; in tightly connected networks, activation of one symptom can propagate to others, providing a framework for understanding the high comorbidity among NMS in CD. Whether specific NMS drive the broader non-motor burden warrants further investigation.

The substantial heterogeneity in this meta-analysis is partly attributable to variability in NMS measurement. Self-report instruments consistently yielded higher and more variable prevalence estimates than clinician-administered interviews for both depression and anxiety, a pattern also documented in psychiatric populations [51], [52]. Divergent cutoff thresholds across studies using the same instrument may have further amplified variability. The suitability of psychiatric scales for CD has recently been evaluated. A recent validation study found that BDI-II and HADS-D performed optimally for depression screening and only HADS-A met clinical utility thresholds for anxiety, whereas CD-specific scales were inferior to generic tools [53]. The overlap between certain scale items and CD motor symptoms underscores the need for psychometric refinement in this population.

Clinical factors also contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Geographic subgroup analysis revealed significantly higher depression prevalence in Asian studies (47.4%) than in Western studies (32.4%; p = 0.0056). While cross-cultural evidence in dystonia is absent, studies in Parkinson's disease have documented geographic variation in NMS burden [54], though findings remain contested [55]. Interpretation of the observed geographic difference is constrained by the small number of Asian studies (k = 5), further confounded by differences in assessment tools and study design. Disease severity and duration were inconsistently reported across studies, limiting their utility as moderators, and non-Western populations remain underrepresented. Moreover, the English-only search strategy may have differentially excluded studies from non-Western populations, potentially contributing to the observed geographic variation in prevalence estimates. Future studies should examine the interrelationships among NMS, particularly whether depression mediates the association between sleep disturbance and fatigue.

Meta-regression of NMS

Meta-regression identified a significant negative association between female percentage and depression prevalence. This association may partly reflect the well-documented female predominance in CD [3], although a large dystonia registry study (n = 3222) found no significant sex difference in depression or anxiety prevalence [56]; in Parkinson's disease, women consistently report higher depression and anxiety severity [57]. Whether this divergence reflects genuine CD-specific phenomena or an ecological artifact of aggregate data remains unclear [58]. Within our data, removal of the three studies with the lowest female percentages eliminated the effect, indicating that the signal was concentrated in the lower tail of the sex distribution. Mean age correlated with female percentage and lost significance in the multivariable model. Pre-post studies and cross-sectional samples may differ in unmeasured ways. These findings provide the first meta-regression evidence for female percentage as a potential moderator of depression prevalence in CD, though individual-patient data are needed to confirm this signal. Heterogeneity was modulated by female percentage, study design, and assessment tool type, underscoring the need to account for study-level characteristics in cross-study comparisons.

Subgroup analyses showed that self-report scales yielded higher pooled depression prevalence than clinician-administered structured diagnostic interviews, although assessment tool type did not approach statistical significance in meta-regression. This null finding likely reflects limited statistical power, as only six studies employed structured clinical interviews. Future primary studies employing structured clinical interviews in CD would strengthen between-instrument comparisons.

Treatment of NMS

BoNT was associated with a small-to-moderate improvement in depression (SMD = −0.31) across five studies, though with high heterogeneity (I2 = 80.7%). The mechanisms by which BoNT may influence mood remain debated: peripheral muscle relaxation may alleviate psychological distress indirectly, while central effects involving altered sensory afferent input and downstream basal ganglia and cortical modulation have also been proposed [59]. Consistent with central mediation, the antidepressant benefit of BoNT in CD occurs independently of motor improvement [60]. The high heterogeneity precludes firm conclusions about the magnitude of this benefit. The use of four different depression scales across five studies further complicates interpretation: divergent effect sizes may reflect true variability in treatment response or differences in scale sensitivity.

For anxiety, the pooled effect was smaller (SMD = −0.23) and based on only three studies, each using a different instrument; the apparent homogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) is therefore likely attributable to low statistical power. Sleep disturbance, assessed in a single study, did not improve following BoNT despite motor benefit, a finding consistent with the broader literature [60] that suggests the pathways underlying sleep dysfunction in CD may differ from those mediating mood symptoms.

GPi-DBS was examined in two studies that could not be pooled. Non-motor effects of DBS in dystonia range from stabilization to mild improvement, with pain responding more consistently than mood symptoms [61]; the included studies are consistent with this pattern. Khanom et al. [32] found HADS-A improvements at 1 and 3 years that were not sustained at 5 years, with no significant HADS-D change. MVD, assessed in a single study, reported the largest pre-post improvements but cannot be compared with the BoNT pooled estimates.

A consistent limitation across this treatment literature is the absence of standardized NMS assessment instruments and follow-up intervals. The five BoNT depression studies employed four different scales with follow-up windows ranging from 4 to 8 weeks, capturing different phases of the treatment cycle. This heterogeneity mirrors the measurement-related variance in the prevalence literature and precludes meaningful comparison across modalities. Future research would benefit from a consensus core outcome set for NMS in CD treatment trials.

Outlook

NMS substantially impair quality of life in CD [24], [62], are strongly associated with disability [63], and may increase suicide risk; suicidal ideation and attempts are highly prevalent in isolated dystonia and correlate with depression and anxiety [64], [65]. These findings highlight the need for routine psychiatric monitoring and suicide risk screening in CD. Assessment tool type was the most consistent source of heterogeneity across depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance in this meta-analysis; establishing a consensus core set of NMS assessment instruments for CD is therefore a priority and would enable cumulative comparison across future studies. Current interventions for psychiatric symptoms primarily involve pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy, with emerging evidence for mindfulness-based approaches and cognitive behavioral therapy [66], [67].

The high prevalence and clinical impact of NMS in CD necessitate regular screening as an integral component of clinical care. Management requires personalized, holistic approaches addressing both motor and NMS domains. A recent Delphi-derived consensus statement proposed a novel care model featuring validated self-assessment tools, neurologist-coordinated multidisciplinary management, systematic follow-ups, and patient education [68]. Meta-regression identified female percentage as a potential moderator of depression prevalence, although these findings require validation with individual-patient data before informing risk stratification. Fatigue and sleep disturbance were highly prevalent yet remain understudied; incorporating patient-reported outcomes (PROs) into routine NMS assessment would help capture the symptom domains most relevant to patients. Clinical trials targeting NMS are needed to strengthen evidence-based management.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the analyses for certain outcomes were constrained by small study numbers, limiting the robustness of these estimates. Second, despite comprehensive subgroup analyses and meta-regression, substantial heterogeneity remained unexplained for depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbance, and all pooled prevalence estimates had wide prediction intervals. Third, the meta-regression identified moderators at the study level, which may not reflect individual-patient relationships; these findings require validation with individual-patient data. Fourth, non-Western populations were underrepresented in the included studies, and treatment evaluations were constrained by inconsistent assessment instruments and follow-up intervals. Fifth, the restriction to English-language publications may have introduced language and geographic sampling bias. Sixth, the small number of studies and high heterogeneity precluded formal evaluation of publication bias.

Conclusion

NMS are highly prevalent in CD, with over half of patients affected by sleep disturbance and fatigue. The most consistent source of heterogeneity was assessment tool type, indicating the need for standardized NMS assessment protocols. Meta-regression revealed a negative association between female percentage and depression prevalence. BoNT yielded preliminary evidence of modest benefit for depression (SMD = −0.31) and anxiety (SMD = −0.23). The evidence base for NMS interventions in CD remains preliminary. Dedicated clinical trials targeting NMS as primary outcomes are warranted.
