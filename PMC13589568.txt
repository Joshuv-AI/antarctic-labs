Ancestry dosage: Decoding sugarcane sweetness
Authors: Muhammad Ikram, Jian-Ying Zhao, San-Ji Gao
Published: 2026

Main text

Sugarcane is an exceptional crop, not only because it accumulates large quantities of sucrose in its stalks but also because this trait emerged from one of the most complex domestication histories among cultivated plants. Modern sugarcane cultivars (Saccharum spp.) are highly polyploid interspecific hybrids derived mainly from the sugar-rich noble cane Saccharum officinarum and the resilient wild species Saccharum spontaneum. The historical breeding process known as nobilization involved interspecific hybridization followed by repeated backcrossing to S. officinarum, thereby restoring the high-sucrose noble-cane background while retaining selected S. spontaneum haplotypes that conferred vigor, ratooning capacity, disease resistance, and environmental adaptation (D’Hont and Glaszmann, 2001; Garsmeur et al., 2025). This process created a fundamental biological tension: the same wild ancestry that enhanced resilience could reduce cane and sugar yield when retained at excessive levels. Therefore, the high sugar content of modern sugarcane cannot be explained simply by domestication for high sucrose. Instead, it is better understood as the outcome of ancestry balance, in which a domesticated sugar-storage system was combined with wild-derived variation only when that variation remained compatible with sucrose accumulation.

In a recent study, Wang et al. (2026) addressed this long-standing question by generating a fully haplotype-resolved genome assembly of POJ2878. This cultivar was generated through sugarcane nobilization in the 1920s, became a foundational cultivar, and has been widely used in sugarcane genetic improvement (Figure 1). The assembly resolved 118 chromosomes and revealed that 80.5% of the POJ2878 genome is derived from S. officinarum, whereas 19.5% is derived from S. spontaneum. This ratio is not merely a descriptive feature of the genome; it represents the genomic outcome of nobilization. POJ2878 preserves a predominantly noble-cane background while carrying a limited but functionally important set of wild-derived haplotypes. In this sense, POJ2878 is not simply another reference genome but a molecular record of how breeders reconstructed sugarcane sweetness after interspecific hybridization. More importantly, the POJ2878 genome reframes nobilization as ancestry editing: breeders repeatedly selected genome configurations in which the S. officinarum storage system was maintained while useful S. spontaneum haplotypes were retained within an optimal dosage range. This view is particularly important in sugarcane, where extreme polyploidy means that haplotype copy number can be as important as haplotype identity.

Prior studies have shown that modern sugarcane genomes are not uniform polyploid extensions of a single progenitor but rather complex mosaics shaped by aneuploidy, interspecific recombination, unequal subgenome contributions, and allele-dosage variation. Chromosome-scale assemblies of modern cultivars including ZZ1, R570, and XTT22 have resolved extensive S. officinarum- and S. spontaneum-derived chromosomal contributions and demonstrated that allele dosage, subgenome composition, and ancestry-specific expression are central to trait formation (Bao et al., 2024; Healey et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025). The R570 genome illustrated how modern cultivars can retain substantial wild ancestry after backcrossing while preserving a predominantly noble-cane background. The XTT22 genome extended this view by revealing unbalanced subgenomes and showing that allele dosage and expression dominance can influence agronomic traits in elite sugarcane. Collectively, these assemblies demonstrate that genetic analyses of sugarcane traits cannot rely on diploid assumptions because homologous and homoeologous chromosomes may contribute unequally to gene dosage and expression. The retained S. spontaneum segments are therefore not merely passive remnants of hybridization; they represent selected genomic blocks whose effects depend on recombination, copy number, and interactions with the S. officinarum background. Within this context, POJ2878 is especially valuable because it links the historical outcome of nobilization to specific sugar-associated haplotypes and storage-cell traits.

A major insight from the POJ2878 genome is that the S. officinarum contribution extends beyond sucrose metabolism to storage architecture. Although S. officinarum is often described as the “high-sugar” ancestor, sucrose accumulation in sugarcane does not arise from a single metabolic process. It requires coordinated carbon partitioning to maturing internodes, strong stem sink activity, sucrose transport, vacuolar storage, cell wall flexibility, and the expansion of storage parenchyma cells. Earlier physiological studies emphasized that sugarcane stems must accommodate the high osmotic pressure associated with sucrose accumulation and that maturing internodes function as powerful carbon sinks (Moore and Cosgrove, 1991; Wang et al., 2013). Wang et al. (2026) extended this model by showing that high-sugar S. officinarum and high-sugar hybrid cultivars have larger stem parenchyma cells than lower-sugar materials. They also identified ShSUT2, which encodes a sucrose transporter, as a key locus associated with parenchyma cell size and demonstrated that ShSUT2 overexpression increases parenchyma cell size in sugarcane. These results suggest that domestication helped build a cellular reservoir capable of importing, storing, and maintaining large amounts of sucrose. Similar links between parenchyma development and sucrose accumulation in sugar beet further suggest that storage-cell architecture may represent a broader principle among sugar-storing crops (Li et al., 2025).

In parallel, S. spontaneum appears to have refined the domesticated storage system through dosage-sensitive haplotypes. The most compelling example is a S. spontaneum-derived SUS2 haplotype. SUS2 encodes sucrose synthase, a central enzyme in sucrose metabolism. Wang et al. (2026) showed that cultivars carrying one to three copies of this favorable haplotype had higher sucrose content, whereas additional copies did not produce proportional increases. This result argues against a simple beneficial-versus-deleterious view of wild introgression. The wild haplotype is beneficial only within a dosage window. Too little S. spontaneum ancestry may limit beneficial genetic variation that supports stress adaptation and crop robustness, whereas too much wild ancestry may interfere with the elite sugar-storage system restored from S. officinarum. Several mechanisms could underlie this window, including changes in enzyme abundance, linked regulatory variants, the effects of neighboring alleles, or broader subgenome imbalance. In a highly polyploid genome, these mechanisms are unlikely to act independently. Instead, sucrose accumulation probably emerges from dosage-sensitive interactions among metabolic enzymes, transporters, regulatory loci, and storage-cell developmental programs.

In summary, the findings of Wang et al. (2026) provide a new framework for understanding the genetic architecture of sugarcane sweetness. They show that modern sucrose accumulation was assembled through ancestry balance: S. officinarum contributed the domesticated sugar-storage system, whereas selected S. spontaneum haplotypes contributed useful variation only when retained at compatible dosages. These findings transform nobilization from a historical breeding description into a measurable genomic principle. Future sugarcane breeding should therefore move beyond binary marker-assisted selection, in which favorable haplotypes are treated as simply present or absent. Breeders need markers that quantify haplotype dosage, assign ancestry, and assess compatibility with storage anatomy. Graph pangenome approaches may be particularly valuable because a single linear reference cannot fully represent the haplotype diversity, structural variation, and copy-number variation present in modern sugarcane germplasm (Huang et al., 2026). Ultimately, integrating haplotype-resolved genomes, ancestry dosage, and storage-cell traits may shift sugarcane improvement from empirical nobilization toward rational ancestry design, enabling the development of high-sucrose cultivars that retain the resilience required for future climates.
