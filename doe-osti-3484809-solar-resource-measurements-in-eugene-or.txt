 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Site-specific, long-term, continuous, and high-resolution measurements of solar irradiance are
 important for developing renewable resource data. These data are used for several research and
 development activities consistent with the NLR mission:

 • Establish a national 3-year climatological database of measured solar irradiances
 • Provide high quality ground-truth data for satellite remote sensing validation
 • Support development of radiative transfer models for estimating solar irradiance from
 available meteorological observations
 • Provide solar resource information needed for technology deployment and operations.

 Data acquired under this agreement will be available to the public through NLR’s Measurement
 & Instrumentation Data Center – MIDC (http://www.nlr.gov/midc) Or the Renewable Resource
 Data Center – RReDC (http://rredc.nlr.gov). The MIDC offers a variety of standard data display,
 access, and analysis tools designed to address the needs of a wide user audience (e.g., industry,
 academia, and government interests).

 Task 1 Results:

 The University of Oregon has been operating and maintaining the suite of radiometers and
 associated equipment installed under CRADA agreement No. CRD-07-252. The University also
 provides NLR with access to all measured and archived data for inclusion in the NLR
 Measurement and Instrumentation Data Center (MIDC) available at https://midcdmz.nlr.gov/
 (Stoffel and Andreas, 1981) which consistently has nearly 100,000 users annually. Throughout
 the CRADA period, NLR provided technical support, including recalibration of the radiometers
 deployed at the University of Oregon and routine archival of measurement data in the MIDC.
 NLR also conducted regular reviews of the data to assess the accuracy and adequacy over the
 measurement period. The radiometers continuously collect key solar irradiance variables,
 including Global Horizontal Irradiance (GHI), Direct Normal Irradiance (DNI), and Diffuse
 Horizontal Irradiance (DHI).

 2

 The analysis presented herein covers solar irradiance data collected between 2017 and 2025. An
 annual data quality assessment was conducted using NLR’s Solar Energy Research Institute
 Quality Control (SERI-QC) software (Maxwell et al., 1993) to ensure the reliability and
 suitability of the dataset for solar energy applications. Accurate, high-quality solar irradiance
 measurements are essential, as the performance and financial viability of solar energy systems
 depend heavily on the precision of such data. Measurement accuracy was influenced by various
 factors, including radiometer type, calibration intervals, maintenance practices, and
 environmental conditions.

 To collect high-quality measurements, the University of Oregon deployed NLR-supplied Class A
 radiometers (per International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 9060:2018 (ISO 9060,
 2018)), representing the highest standard of solar radiation measurement equipment. These
 instruments were continuously operated and maintained according to best practices, ensuring
 consistent and traceable data quality. The dataset includes measurements of the three primary
 solar irradiance components: GHI, DNI, and DHI. The data described in this report were
 collected on the University of Oregon campus located in Eugene Oregon.

 SERI-QC employs a three-component quality control method based on analysis of clearness
 indices derived from the measured irradiance values. The clearness indices—Kt (global), Kn
 (direct), and Kd (diffuse)—are calculated by normalizing each respective irradiance component
 to the extraterrestrial solar radiation at the top of the atmosphere. These indices enable the
 construction of a ‘K-space’, which defines theoretical bounds of physically plausible irradiance
 conditions. As described by Maxwell et al. (1993), certain relationships must hold true—for
 example, Kt < Kn and (Kt – Kn) ≠ Kd are not physically possible, as Kd cannot be negative.
 Data falling outside the defined valid regions of K-space are flagged as suspect or invalid.

 Figures 1 - 9 present the results of the data quality assessment for the 2017–2025 period. The
 leftmost chart displays the most severe quality flags detected among the three clearness
 components (Kt, Kn, Kd) for each time interval. Flag severity is color-coded, ranging from dark
 blue (low error or no issue) to red (high severity flags). The subsequent three charts illustrate
 clearness indices for each irradiance component individually. These charts provide insight into
 sky conditions over time, with dark shading indicating overcast conditions and white denoting
 clear skies. Summary statistics below the plots quantify the extent and nature of data quality
 issues.

 Overall, the assessment shows that fewer than 5% of the measurements were flagged for quality
 concerns (see Table 1 Missing and Data Quality Issues). This demonstrated a consistently high
 level of data integrity, affirming the suitability of the dataset for high-confidence solar energy
 analysis and decision-making.

 Table 1 below summarizes the data quality assessment results for the solar irradiance

 complete, there were some periods of missing data. These gaps were primarily attributed to
 equipment-related issues such as instrument malfunctions, datalogging interruptions and
 communication failures, solar tracker failures, and maintenance-related downtimes. The table
 illustrates annual summaries where 2023 Diffuse Horizontal Irradiance (DHI) demonstrated
 relatively higher missing data (~3%) compared to other years. For the remaining years, the
 missing data were less than 1%.

 The data quality (DQ) issues column of the table reflects the percentage of data excluded from
 the final analysis because of the quality assurance process conducted using NLR’s SERI-QC
 software. This process flags data anomalies using a structured flagging system ranging from 0 to
 99. A flag value of 99 indicates missing data, such as from power outages or communication
 failures. For this analysis, all data flagged with values from 7, 8, and 10 to 97 were excluded, as
 they indicate quality concerns (Table 1, ‘QA-issues (%)’ column shows the data that is excluded
 containing these flags). For details about the SERI-QC flags, refer to the user manual of SERI-

 Specifically, flags 10 through 93 are assigned to data that failed two- or three-component
 consistency tests, which are essential for verifying the internal consistency between GHI, DNI,
 and DHI. These tests are designed to identify situations where the interrelation among the
 components violates physical or empirical models, suggesting potential measurement or
 processing errors.

 Flags 94 to 97 correspond to measurements that fall within physically impossible regions in K-
 space—a domain defined by clearness indices, where known physical relationships between GHI
 (Kt), DNI (Kn), and DHI (Kd) must be upheld. Violations in K-space are evaluated based on the
 magnitude by which Kn exceeds Kt, which should never occur under physically valid conditions.
 These flags are categorized as follows:

        •   Flag 94: Kn > Kt by 0.05–0.10
        •   Flag 95: Kn > Kt by 0.10–0.15
        •   Flag 96: Kn > Kt by 0.15–0.20
 • Flag 97: Kn > Kt by ±0.20 or more

 In addition to these, data flagged with values 7 and 8—which denote measurements that fall
 outside the allowable minimum and maximum thresholds for a single component—were also
 excluded. Flag 7 represents values below the minimum physically plausible threshold, while 8
 flagged those exceeding the maximum.

 5

 In total, this filtering approach ensures that only high-quality, physically consistent solar
 irradiance measurements were retained for further analysis. The excluded data—whether due to
 physical implausibility, component inconsistency, or instrument errors—represents a
 conservative quality control strategy to enhance the integrity and reliability of solar resource
 assessments derived from this dataset. Thus, DHI data from 2023 revealed relatively elevated
 data quality (DQ) issues. Notably, DHI data collected between February 21, 2023 and February
 27, 2023 exhibited higher DQ problems, which were linked to a change in the radiometer model
 from the Black & White Pyranometer to the Hukx SR20 model. During this time, site operators
 were engaged in instrument replacement and troubleshooting efforts. During the same year, site
 operators were also engaged in troubleshooting station problems between April 24, 2023 and
    May 3, 2023.

 Further results and discussion:

 The following figures present extended results derived from the evaluation of the dataset using
 an independent data quality assessment methodology. Specifically, the quality control procedures
 were implemented using the Quality Control Radiation (QCRad) algorithm, developed by (Long
 and Shi, 2008), which has been widely adopted in the atmospheric and solar energy research
 community for ensuring the integrity of radiation measurements. The QCRad methodology
 applied to a multi-tiered approach to identify and flag erroneous or suspicious radiation values
 based on physical, empirical, and climatological thresholds. It examined parameters such as GHI,
 DNI, DHI, and their interrelations, including constraints based on the solar zenith angle and the
 maximum expected extraterrestrial radiation.

 The figures included in this section offer supplementary validation of the dataset that had
 previously undergone quality control through the SERI-QC procedure. While the SERI-QC
 process addresses a broad range of error sources, including instrumental malfunctions, data
 spikes, and climatological inconsistencies, the QCRad checks serve to further supplement and
 check the reliability of the measurements.

 A comparison of the QCRad-filtered dataset with the SERI-QC-assessed dataset revealed
 generally strong agreement; however, several discrepancies were noted. Specifically, some data
 points fell outside of the QCRad-defined acceptable bounds, suggesting possible residual errors
 or natural anomalies not captured by SERI-QC. These outliers may have resulted from transient
 atmospheric events, such as cloud edge effects, snow reflection, or instrumental limitations under
 low-light conditions.
 11

 It is important to note that a significant portion of these deviations occur during periods of low
 solar irradiance, typically during early morning, late evening, or overcast conditions. Figure 10
 illustrates that there is more shading in the evening compared to the morning. From a solar
 energy application perspective, such instances are generally considered to have a negligible
 impact on system performance due to the inherently low power output during these times.

 Figure 10. 2024 data illustrating shading situation. Ratio between the measured GHI and
 calculated GHI using DNI and DHI (referred to as SumSW).

 In summary, the additional QCRad quality checks enhanced confidence in the dataset’s
 suitability for use in solar resource assessment and modeling. While minor inconsistencies
 persist, their frequency and magnitude are within acceptable thresholds, particularly when
 considering the broader uncertainties inherent in ground-based radiation measurements. The
 integration of multiple independent quality control protocols—SERQC and QCRad—provided a
 robust framework for ensuring high data fidelity.
