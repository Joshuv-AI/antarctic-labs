 Abstract:

 This final technical report provides an overview of the key milestones achieved by Advanced
 Research Systems (ARS), a DOE SBIR/STTR Grant Recipient. ARS’s main objective was to
 develop a prototype of the Closed Cycle Cryogenic Quantum Probe Station (Avalanche).
 Designed for near field spectroscopy (Tip Enhanced Photoluminescence, TEPL and Tip
 enhanced Raman Spectroscopy TERS)) and far field spectroscopy (micro-Raman, micro-PL)
 is in the final stage of completion. The critical components have been tested and the final
 System Test of the AFM is scheduled for August 2025.

 Management Summary:

 The project was delayed due to the manufacturing complexity of the high-performance
 cryostat. These systems are demanding they require specialized people, materials and
 processes. The physics of low temperature design makes the system delicate and expensive
 to build, Paper thin stainless tubes are welded in coaxial configuration, a faulty component or
 a welding mishap can set the project back 6 months. ARS only has two welders capable of
 such complex processes. The copper for the cold components come from European suppliers
 because it is not available in the USA. A failure to meet the thermal conductivity requirements
 can and did set us back in timeline.
 The system is now complete and in final assembly see photo above. It will be tested for its
 cryogenic performance first and then for the AFM performance.
 It is important to note that although the DOE award was cancelled in 2024 the project
 continued at ARS, paid for through the ARS corporate R&D budget until it is completed.

Experimental Capabilities:

 Quantum probe station for optical and spin characterization of solid-state quantum emitter at
 low and variable temperature based on far-field micro-PL and different modalities of AFM-
 based near-field

 The Avalanche Probe Station is a novel product that incorporates the following features:

 • Temperature range from 4K to 300K.
 • Ultra High Vacuum. 10-11 Torr. Chamber capability.
 • Ultra Low Vibrations. 5-10 nm at the chamber. Atomic resolution at the AFM.
 • Low drift of the AFM stage with respect to the optical table.
 • An AFM designed specifically for the Avalanche Probe Station.
 • Magnetic property testing option
 • Easy to use for a range of users with different skill levels.

Test results Thermal performance.
In compliance with Project Goals, the low vibration interface is one of the three core underlying
technologies that needed to be developed and improved. Without successful implementation of
this design, the rest of the cryostat would be rendered inoperative. The interface’s functionality
can be organized into three fundamental principles. Complete mechanical isolation of the Cold
head and compressor from the optical table, maximal and efficient heat transfer to cold
surfaces, ergonomic enough that an untrained customer can run the system continuously
without significant oversight.
With regards to thermal performance, the interface has been successfully optimized to meet
current and market conditions for the thermal budgets needed by customers. Three plus

ARS. DOE TECHNICAL REPORT. Ravi Rev 250714.docx 4

internal heat exchanger setups have been tested for the interface. The experimental results
indicate that conduction over progressively larger surface areas is the dominant mode of heat
transfer for a helium gas interface. Additionally, due to the necessity of using the smallest
possible cryocooler for minimal vibrations and market affordability, fully delivering the heat
capacity of a cold head’s 40K heat station is critical for a competitive sample base
temperature. These cryocoolers will at best only have less than 200mW of heat capacity for 4K
surfaces. In systems of this size and total surface area, even a 40K support structure and
radiation shield can still present 40-80mW of heat load to the 4K surfaces within the
instrument. Thus, the radiation shield and sample support must be below 50K, if not 40K to
preserve the thermal budget. Special design attention and thermal analysis was given to the
thermal path leading to the heat exchangers, as conductive heat loss within the interface itself
is the largest contributor to initial base temperatures.
ARS has now designed and characterized two different leading heat exchangers. One
optimized for base temperature, the other for heat transfer. To account for better ergonomics
and space limitations within the avalanche design, [figure B] was selected. As outlined by the
heat maps [figure C], this represents a current balanced best effort implementation of
maximum 1st stage heat transfer, and 2nd stage base temperature. 100mW at 4.24K is in line
with current market expectations. Future designs will be able to implement improved surface
area coverage for the second stage within the space constraints of the Avalanche Cryostat,
delivering the full 200mW of the DE-204 cold head at 4K.

Test Results. Vibration Performance.

The core goals of the project require vibrations on a sub-nanometer scale relative to the
sample. This has been designed and completed. Vibration isolation is applied in four separate
mechanical assemblies:
Solvay Cryocooler.
This is a pneumatic cryocooler with very low vibrations at the cold tip. There are no mechanical
linkages driving the displacer as in the standard GM coolers. The vibrations of these coolers
are in the range of 5-10 micrometers at 2.4Hz.
DMX-20:
Mechanical isolation through low vibration feet and bellows [Figure D],

AFM Spring suspension:
 The AFM platform is mounted to the DMX-20 via custom designed springs within the
cryogenic vacuum [Figure E],

Magnet Damping:
Passive magnetic damping [Figure E] of the spring suspension within the vacuum space
reduces any pendulum motion.

The final resulting vibrations between the AFM tip and sample are expected to be in single
picometer range.

 Prior efforts to isolate vibrations have employed systems to support the cryocooler from
a structure anchored to the floor or suspended from the ceiling while the experimental
apparatus sits on an optical table. These methods make it cumbersome to align the cryocooler
with the sample chuck so a main goal of this project is to mount all the components together
on a baseplate that can sit on the optical table. To accomplish this, vibration isolation is
employed at multiple stages along the path from cryocooler to sample.
 The first segment is from the cryocooler to the optical table, and the second segment is
from the table to the sample. In the first segment, the weight of the cryocooler is supported on
the table by a heavy tower-like structure. Vibration isolating mounts with a high degree of
internal damping are used to attach the cryocooler to the tower and similar mounts secure the
tower to the baseplate. See D

 segment of vibration isolation is from the cryocooler to the
sample. The sample, in the case illustrated, is mounted to an atomic force microscope. The cryocooler
is immersed in helium gas that transfers that cooling to a thermal interface that is secured to the
baseplate. A flexible rubber bellows is the only physical link between the cryocooler and the thermal
interface to retain the helium exchange gas. Highly flexible copper braids with high thermal conductivity
connect the thermal interface to the AFM which is enclosed in two layers of shielding from radiant heat.
The outer shield at approximately 40K is rigidly mounted to the baseplate, and the AFM is mounted to
the inner shield operating at 4K. The AFM, through the 4K shield, is suspended from a set of springs to
further isolate the experiment from any small vibration that might have been transferred to the
baseplate. The AFM assembly is damped magnetically by a permanent magnet attached to the 4K
structure operating inside a cylinder that is electrically conductive. See Fig E.

 In the discussions above, vibration isolation is achieved by tuning the main components
below the predominant excitation frequencies which are the 2.4 Hz operating frequency of the
cryocooler, and 60 Hz associated with the drive motor. This is done by supporting relatively
heavy structures with low stiffness springs and damping added to dissipate energy.
 A key issue to ensure that the cryocooler is well isolated is to prevent it from contacting
the thermal interface through the helium exchange gas. For optimum thermal performance, the
helium gap should be as small as possible yet easy to center the cryocooler inside the thermal
interface. The centering process is done during setup by lowering the cryocooler down inside
the interface so that a tapered tip on the cryocooler engages a mating feature in the interface.
Once this is done, the alignment supports are adjusted to take out backlash and cryocooler is
raised straight out of the tapered engagement.

Metal Bellows:
One legacy component of the low vibration interface is the rubber bellows used to
mechanically decouple the cold head from the interface heat exchangers. This bellows, while
extremely soft and ductile, proposed fundamental limitations to the modern Avalanche design.
Namely operating cycle limitations, user friendliness, and pressure limitations. These problems
are resolved by implementing a metal bellows with sufficiently low vibration transfer. In phase
one & two, ARS has continued to test metal bellows that have proven inadequate to the project
objectives for vibration. An Ultra-thin custom formed copper bellows manufactured by
deposition process will fully satisfy the project goals. Two suppliers had been identified that
could supply a bellow to ARS specification of [SPRING RATE] that allows proper isolation of
the cold head, while also strong enough to allow for greater gas pressure inside the heat
exchangers. The higher gas density will linearly improve the heat capacity of the interface.
With a metal bellows a low vibration unibody cryogenic system which can be taken from a
shipping box and placed on the optical table with significantly reduced user adjustments and
setup.

Optics Test:
The cryogenic quantum probe station requires a cryogenic compatible objective lens to be
placed inside the cryostat. ARS has acquired a vacuum compatible objective lens from Zeiss
(EC EPIPlan 50x 0.7) and together with the Sun group, we tested the performance of the Zeiss
objective lens at cryogenic temperature. [Figure L] shows the measurement setup at the Sun
group for the testing of the objective lens. We mount the Zeiss objective lens inside a dilution
refrigerator using customized mechanical adaptors made in the Sun group. The dilution
refrigerator has an optical window from the top via a KF40 flange. We built an optical
breadboard on the top of the dilution refrigerator for optically testing the objective performance.
The sample and the objective lens were cooled down to 3.4 K, with a chamber pressure at
7*10-6 mbar.

ARS. DOE TECHNICAL REPORT. Ravi Rev 250714.docx 13

Figure L: schematics and photos of the setup used for the testing of the Zeiss objective lens.

We focused on two aspect of the objective lens: the imaging quality and the transmission
efficiency. For testing the imaging quality, we used the objective lens to image a GaAs sample
with a microfabricated NIST logo. [Figure M] shows the image obtained both at room
temperature (where the objective lens is designed to work at) and at cryogenic temperature.
The image quality remains almost identical, showing very little degradation when the
temperature goes from room temperature to 3.4 K. We did note that that there is a little blurring
of the image at 3.4 K, but we believe this is because of the sample vibration inside the cryostat
rather than degradation of the objective lens. This is because we observed similar blurring at
room temperature when we turned on the compressor.

Figure M: imaging of a microfabricated NIST logo using the Zeiss objective at both room
temperature and 3.4 K. The image quality looks comparable between the two, suggesting no
significant degradation of the objective lens when the temperature was cooled down to
cryogenic temperature.

We also measured the transmission of the objective lens at both room temperature and at 3.4
K. We measured the transmission at two wavelengths: 780 nm and 930 nm. The transmission
was measured by sending a laser beam through the objective lens, reflected by the GaAs
sample placed at the focal plane and go through the objective lens again, and measure the
ratio between the reflected power and the input power. The table below shows the measured
efficiency at the two operating wavelengths, both at room temperature and cryogenic
temperature. The transmission only degrades by only 2~3% upon cooling down to the
cryogenic temperature.
