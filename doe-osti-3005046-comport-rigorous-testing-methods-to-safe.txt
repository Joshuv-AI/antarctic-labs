1. Abstract
This report summarizes the University of Washington’s contributions to the ComPort project,
Rigorous Testing Methods to Safeguard Software Porting, funded under the U.S. Department of
Energy, Office of Science, Office of Advanced Scientific Computing Research (ASCR), award
number DE-SC0022081. UW’s work addressed numerics-driven correctness challenges arising
in heterogeneous and rapidly evolving computing environments. Our efforts focused on
developing interactive analysis tools, correctness-preserving rewriting systems, semantic-aware
code-transformation infrastructure, and multi-language support for reproducible numerical
experiments.

UW advanced four major thrusts during the project period. First, we developed Odyssey, an
interactive workbench for floating-point diagnosis, rewriting, and expert-guided improvement of
numerical expressions. Second, we made progress in Herbie, a correctness-preserving rewriting
framework that identifies unstable computations and automatically proposes mathematically
valid alternatives with improved numerical stability. Third, we created Magic Markup, a system
that uses machine-learned models to re-anchor semantic annotations across code transformations,
supporting reliable instrumentation and analysis throughout the software-porting lifecycle.
Fourth, we contributed Chassis, a cross-language, dependency-tracking infrastructure that
supports stable transformation, analysis, and benchmarking of numerical kernels across polyglot
scientific workflows.

All software artifacts, datasets, and publications produced under this award have been publicly
released. Together, these contributions provide new capabilities for diagnosing numerical issues,
maintaining correctness across code evolution, and supporting portable and robust scientific
computing on heterogeneous platforms.

2. Introduction and Background
High-performance computing (HPC) applications underpin critical scientific, engineering, and
national-security workloads. As hardware systems have evolved, these applications increasingly
rely on heterogeneous architectures that combine general-purpose CPUs with GPUs, specialized
accelerators, mixed-precision arithmetic units, and compiler toolchains that aggressively
transform code to achieve performance. Although these trends have accelerated scientific
capability, they have also introduced significant challenges for numerical correctness,
reproducibility, and software robustness. Subtle differences in instruction sets, floating-point
semantics, optimization regimes, and data-layout transformations can cause results to diverge
across hardware platforms or compiler configurations. Ensuring that scientific codes behave
predictably under such conditions requires new methods for diagnosing numerical instability,

reasoning about precision-dependent behavior, and maintaining consistent semantics across
heterogeneous execution environments.

These concerns were documented in the multi-institution survey Guarding Numerics Amidst
Rising Heterogeneity, produced early in the ComPort project. That survey synthesized observed
correctness failures across modern CPU, GPU, and accelerator platforms, highlighting the
growing prevalence of issues such as silent exception masking, input-dependent instability,
backend-specific instruction selection, and nondeterminism introduced by reduction ordering and
mixed-precision execution. The broader conclusion of that study was that traditional debugging
and testing approaches are no longer sufficient in the face of extreme heterogeneity; instead, new
methodologies and tooling are required that make numerical behavior visible, interpretable, and
robust to hardware variation. This perspective directly motivates the ComPort project’s emphasis
on rigorous testing and analysis methods to safeguard software portability.

The ComPort project brought together three academic institutions and two DOE laboratories to
address these challenges. The University of Utah served as the lead institution, focusing on
exception mining, compiler-level variability analysis, precision-downscaling risk assessment,
and large-scale empirical characterization of floating-point exceptions across heterogeneous
architectures. The University of California, Davis contributed advances in anomaly detection,
statistical modeling, and ML-driven techniques for identifying correctness risks in optimizing
compilers and accelerator runtimes. Within this collaboration, the University of Washington led
efforts in interactive floating-point debugging, correctness-preserving rewriting, annotation
stability across code evolution, and cross-language infrastructure for controlled numerics
experiments.

UW’s work complemented and expanded the capabilities developed by Utah and UC Davis.
While Utah advanced low-level exception-tracking systems (e.g., GPU-FPX, FPChecker,
FloatTracker) that surface hardware-specific correctness hazards, UW developed tools and
methodologies that help developers understand, rewrite, and improve numerical expressions at a
higher semantic level. Utah’s tools identify where exceptions and variability arise; UW’s tools
help experts determine how to respond, how to reformulate unstable expressions, and how to
evaluate the correctness implications of alternative implementations. Similarly, while UC Davis
advanced ML-based analyses and signal-processing–oriented anomaly detection, UW’s work
provided the semantic and structural infrastructure needed to maintain correctness across
software evolution, code transformation, and polyglot scientific workflows.

The UW thrusts were designed to address correctness concerns across different stages of the
numerical software lifecycle:

 ●​ Odyssey provides an interactive environment for diagnosing floating-point instability,
 exploring alternative formulations, and refining expressions using both automated tools
 and expert intuition. It offers mechanisms for attributing local error, visualizing

 input-domain behavior, and tuning expressions—capabilities that are essential when
 analyzing or repairing numerical kernels identified by Utah’s low-level detectors.​

 ●​ Herbie progress strengthened correctness-preserving rewriting pipelines, improving
 automated discovery of stable formulations and integrating more deeply with
 FPBench-standard benchmarks. These enhancements support ComPort’s goals by
 enabling automated and semi-automated improvement of unstable expressions surfaced
 by Utah’s exception-tracking systems.​

 ●​ Magic Markup introduced semantic retagging for software undergoing iterative
 development. Heterogeneous systems often require frequent code restructuring,
 refactoring, or compiler-driven rewrites; stable annotations are crucial for maintaining
 instrumentation, tracking numerical probes, and running consistent experiments. Magic
 Markup uses machine-learned models to preserve annotation intent across such
 transformations, providing a foundational capability for robust correctness studies.​

 ●​ Chassis contributed a cross-language dependency-tracking and transformation
 infrastructure for numerical kernels embedded in polyglot scientific workflows. Modern
 HPC and ML pipelines increasingly weave together Python orchestration, C/Fortran
 kernels, and R or Julia analysis stacks. Chassis provides mechanisms for stable
 transformation and analysis across these ecosystems, supporting controlled comparisons
 and repeatable correctness experiments.​

Together, these efforts positioned the University of Washington as the provider of high-level,
semantics-oriented tools and methodologies within the ComPort collaboration. While Utah’s and
UC Davis’s contributions strengthened the ability to observe and detect correctness issues at
scale, UW’s contributions enabled reasoning about, transforming, and experimentally validating
numerical behavior in a way that remains robust to code evolution and platform variation. This
complementary structure allowed the team to develop a cohesive suite of tools and techniques
that span the full spectrum of correctness testing under rising heterogeneity.

3. Approach
UW’s approach within the ComPort project emphasized complementary strengths in interactive
analysis, correctness-preserving transformation, and infrastructure for reliable numerics
experimentation. While Utah and UC Davis advanced large-scale exception detection and
ML-driven anomaly analysis, the UW thrusts focused on understanding why numerical failures
occur and how developers can address them through robust reformulation, workflow tooling, and
stable annotation of evolving code.

A central part of UW’s methodology was the use of human–computer interaction
(HCI)–informed design studies to understand how numerical experts diagnose instability,
compare alternative formulations, and make decisions under uncertainty. This process guided the
development of Odyssey, an interactive workbench that supports expert workflows for
floating-point diagnosis and rewriting. Rather than relying solely on automated tooling, Odyssey
integrates visual error attribution, local behavior inspection, and structured rewrite exploration to
help users iteratively improve unstable expressions.

UW also extended the capabilities of correctness-preserving rewriting systems, drawing on
more than a decade of work on Herbie and the FPBench ecosystem. Improvements to Herbie’s
rewrite search, error estimation, and stability evaluation pipelines were incorporated into the
broader ComPort methodology by enabling more accurate automated suggestions for refactoring
problematic numerics. These rewriting capabilities serve as a bridge between low-level
exception-detection tools and high-level reformulation, giving developers actionable paths for
mitigating instability identified elsewhere in the workflow.

To support robust numerical experimentation across evolving codebases, UW developed Magic
Markup, a semantic anchoring system that maintains stable annotations across code changes and
refactorings. Numerical debugging and benchmarking workflows often rely on persistent
annotations—error probes, instrumentation points, or provenance markers—that must survive
transformations induced by optimization, refactoring, or template-based code generation. Magic
Markup uses machine-learned models to track annotation intent and re-anchor it to updated code
structures, enabling long-running and reproducible numerics studies even as code evolves.

Complementing these efforts, UW contributed Chassis, a cross-language transformation and
dependency-tracking framework designed to support HPC and scientific workflows that blend
Python, C, R, and other languages. Chassis provides a consistent infrastructure for analyzing,
transforming, and instrumenting numerical kernels embedded in polyglot systems, making it
possible to perform controlled comparisons and repeatable experiments across heterogeneous
toolchains and language ecosystems.

Finally, the UW contributions were aligned with the broader FPBench standards effort and the
associated FPTalks community, which UW helps coordinate. Through these channels, the tools
and insights developed under ComPort were disseminated to the wider numerics, HPC, and
programming languages communities. This ensured that the project’s methods, benchmarks, and
evaluation frameworks were compatible with existing practice and available for reuse by
researchers and practitioners working on correctness in scientific computing.

Together, these methodological pillars—design-driven tool development, correctness-preserving
rewriting, annotation stability, cross-language infrastructure, and community
integration—formed UW’s cohesive approach to improving numerical robustness under the
rising heterogeneity that motivates the ComPort project.

mechanisms, and cross-language frameworks for robust numerical experimentation. Highlights

UW extended the capabilities of Herbie, improving its rewrite search strategies, floating-point

respond to the correctness issues surfaced by Utah’s exception-tracking tools.

maintaining stable instrumentation across code transformations common in numerical software.
Magic Markup supports robust, long-running numerical experiments by preserving the semantic

optimizations, refactorings, and DSL-based code generation.

• Chassis: Cross-Language Infrastructure for Numerical Tooling

execution environments.

• FPBench, FPTalks, and Broader Community Dissemination

UW contributed to standards, benchmarks, tutorials, and workshops through the FPBench and
FPTalks communities. These efforts helped disseminate ComPort results, gather community

community standards and available for broader reuse.

These achievements collectively strengthen the ComPort project’s ability to detect, understand,
and remediate numerical correctness issues across heterogeneous hardware and rapidly evolving
scientific software stacks.

5. Odyssey: Interactive Workbench for
Floating-Point Error Analysis
5.1 Motivation and Context
Floating-point debugging and expression rewriting remain central challenges when porting
scientific applications across heterogeneous platforms. Automated rewriting tools can often
identify obvious improvements but struggle when instability arises from subtle
interactions—such as range-sensitive behaviors, fused multiply–add usage, subexpression
ordering, or architecture-specific rounding artifacts. These limitations are often compounded by
heterogeneity: differences in hardware pipelines, compiler heuristics, and execution backends
can cause the same mathematical expression to behave differently on CPUs, GPUs, and
accelerator-class devices.

In practice, developers frequently need to examine an expression’s behavior across an input
domain, understand where error concentrates, and explore reformulations that balance stability
and performance. Existing tools typically separate these tasks: automated rewrites search a
constrained space of transformations, while visualization tools provide surface-level error
metrics without offering pathways toward improved formulations.

Odyssey was developed to close this gap. It supports interactive, expert-driven workflows that
combine automated search with visual inspection of error behavior. By integrating error
attribution, rewrite exploration, and tuning into a single system, Odyssey helps experts diagnose,
understand, and correct numerical instability that arises when software is ported across
heterogeneous computing environments.

5.2 System Overview
Odyssey organizes floating-point analysis into a structured, three-stage workflow that mirrors
how experts naturally approach difficult rewriting tasks: diagnosing behavior, generating
candidate solutions, and tuning alternatives. This staged approach provides a coherent
framework that unifies numerical visualization, automated tooling, and human insight.

Odyssey’s three-stage workflow diagram (“Diagnose → Generate → Tune”).

In the Diagnosis stage, users provide a target expression along with ranges for its input variables.
Odyssey computes representative samples across the domain, estimates floating-point error, and
visualizes the global and local structure of instability. Users can inspect error distributions,
identify problematic subexpressions, and explore how error varies across inputs.

In the Solution Generation stage, Odyssey integrates with rewriting tools—most notably
Herbie—to produce candidate reformulations. These suggestions are listed in a tabular interface
where users can review expression complexity, estimated error, and structural changes. Users
may also author their own candidate expressions, supporting workflows where domain
knowledge or algebraic identities provide insight that automated tools lack.

In the Tuning stage, Odyssey enables iterative refinement through range zooming, derivation
visualization, and targeted sampling. Users can compare behaviors of competing expressions,
identify which transformations improve stability, and ultimately select a formulation that meets
the desired trade-offs.

Odyssey’s core user interface, including expression entry, rewrite list, global error plot, and local
error heatmap.

This integrated design allows Odyssey to support workflows that alternate between exploration
and refinement—an approach that aligns directly with the needs surfaced in our expert design
study and complements the lower-level exception-tracking tools developed by Utah.

5.3 Design Study and Expert Workflow
Odyssey’s interface and workflow were shaped by a multi-stage design study involving domain
experts in numerical computing, floating-point analysis, and compiler optimization. The study
revealed that experts routinely face barriers that automated rewriting systems cannot address,
such as:

 ●​ understanding why a given automated rewrite improves or worsens error behavior,​

 ●​ identifying instability that only manifests over specific regions of the input domain,​

 ●​ validating whether a mathematically correct alternative behaves predictably across
 architectures,​

 ●​ and exploring trade-offs between readability, performance, and numerical robustness.​

Experts emphasized the need for tools that make local behavior visible, support structural
inspection of expressions, and allow iterative refinement of candidate rewrites. Odyssey’s design
directly reflects these needs.

The design study identified three recurring patterns in expert workflows:

 1.​ Local Inspection of Error Sources:​
 Users begin by examining local error spikes to understand which subexpressions
 contribute most to instability.​

 2.​ Iterative Comparison of Candidates:​
 Experts compare multiple reformulations in parallel, evaluating not only numerical error
 but also structural and behavioral differences.​

 3.​ Progressive Domain Refinement:​
 As experts refine their understanding, they zoom into narrower regions of the input space
 to diagnose behavior more precisely. Odyssey supports this through dynamic
 range-resampling and range-sensitive rendering.​

Expression detail view showing LaTeX rendering, plain-text representation, and structural
breakdown. Local error heatmap illustrating how Odyssey highlights instability at specific input
points.​

These insights shaped Odyssey’s three-stage workflow and its emphasis on blending automation
with expert judgment.

5.4 Key Algorithms
Odyssey integrates several algorithmic components that enable its diagnostic and exploratory
capabilities.

Range Sampling:​
 Odyssey performs stratified sampling across the input domain, evaluating expressions at
representative points to estimate global error patterns. This enables the system to surface regions
where instability concentrates.

Local Error Attribution:​
 By instrumenting floating-point operations during evaluation, Odyssey attributes error to
specific subexpressions. This aligns with experts’ mental models and supports targeted rewriting.

Delta-Debugging–Inspired Perturbation:​
 To understand sensitivity, Odyssey perturbs inputs and intermediate values, observing how small
changes propagate. This helps identify subexpressions where rounding behavior
disproportionately affects output.

Rewrite Suggestion Integration:​
 Odyssey integrates rewrite candidates from tools like Herbie, annotating them with estimated
error and expression characteristics. The integration supports both automated and manual
exploration of the search space.

Solution-generation interface including automated rewrite suggestions and manual-editing
support.

These algorithms collectively enable Odyssey to surface actionable insights about where
instability arises and how it can be addressed.

5.5 Evaluation
A user study involving numerical-computing experts assessed Odyssey’s effectiveness at
diagnosing and improving unstable expressions. Participants used Odyssey to analyze a suite of
challenging kernels that included polynomial approximations, geometric predicates, and
expressions sensitive to rounding-mode variations. Experts reported that Odyssey offered a level
of insight not available in existing automated tools, particularly due to its local error attribution,
range-sensitive visualization, and iterative tuning workflow.

Across the benchmark suite, Odyssey enabled participants to identify and correct instability in
cases where automated rewriting alone was insufficient. In several instances, Odyssey surfaced
local error patterns that corresponded to architecture-specific vulnerabilities—patterns that
would be difficult to detect without interactive visualization.

Tuning-mode interface showing derivation visualization, dynamic range zooming, and iterative
comparison of candidate rewrites.

These evaluation results complement the lower-level exception detection and variability analysis
tools developed by Utah, demonstrating that Odyssey offers a practical and effective
environment for expert-guided floating-point debugging and rewriting in heterogeneous
computing contexts.

6. Herbie Progress
Herbie is a correctness-preserving rewriting tool that attempts to automatically transform
floating-point expressions into algebraically equivalent formulations with substantially lower
rounding error. As scientific software is ported across heterogeneous architectures, these
automated transformations become increasingly important: variation in backend instruction
selection, precision handling, or parallel reduction order can expose previously latent instability.
During the project period, UW made substantial progress advancing Herbie’s rewrite search,
error detection capabilities, and supporting infrastructure. These improvements strengthened
Herbie’s utility within the ComPort workflow and deepened its integration with the Odyssey
system and FPBench ecosystem.

6.1 Improvements to Rewrite Search

Herbie’s rewrite search aims to identify alternative formulations that behave more robustly under
floating-point rounding. While the core algorithm has existed for several years, the UW effort
under ComPort focused on strengthening its heuristics, improving its exploration of the search
space, and optimizing the runtime of its rewriting phases.

One direction of improvement involved weighted sampling heuristics. Prior versions of Herbie
relied on uniform sampling of the input domain, which can fail to detect narrow regions of
instability or overemphasize regions where instability is negligible. The updated heuristics draw
more samples from areas likely to exhibit high sensitivity, guided by domain-specific patterns
observed in instability-prone expressions. This change substantially improved Herbie’s ability to
detect promising rewrites early in the search process.

UW also improved the organization of rewriting phases, including more aggressive pruning of
unpromising candidates, restructuring of multi-stage rewrite pipelines, and refined criteria for
abandoning search paths. These changes reduced runtime while preserving or improving the
quality of discovered rewrites. In particular, several improvements were informed by empirical
observations during Odyssey integration, where interactive users frequently attempted
expressions known to challenge early Herbie versions.

Performance enhancements were also achieved through improved caching, memoization of
intermediate results, and more efficient generation of candidate expressions. Collectively, these
changes resulted in faster search runs, more stable candidate evaluation, and improved reliability
when processing expressions with complex algebraic structure.

6.2 Better Detection of Error Hotspots
To complement its improved rewrite search, Herbie gained enhanced capabilities for detecting
error hotspots—regions of the input domain where floating-point error concentrates or where
small perturbations cause disproportionately large deviations. Many of these enhancements were
motivated by integration with Odyssey, whose diagnosis stage visualizes local error patterns
across the input domain. Herbie’s sampling and evaluation methods were aligned with Odyssey’s
local error estimation techniques, enabling more consistent cross-tool behavior.

A notable advancement was improved local error attribution, allowing Herbie to better detect
structural causes of instability and prioritize rewrites that address them. These heuristics were
updated to reflect the patterns surfaced in the ComPort survey on numerical instability and
heterogeneity, especially those relating to cancellation, overflow/underflow thresholds, and
expression sensitivity under fused multiply–add operations.

Herbie’s enhanced hotspot detection was also made more reliable through deeper integration
with FPBench floating-point benchmarks, including both classic microbenchmarks and

domain-specific expressions contributed by DOE labs. The addition of new FPBench tasks
allowed Herbie’s behavior to be evaluated against a broader suite of challenging kernels,
ensuring that its suggestions remained relevant across diverse numerical workloads.

6.3 Infrastructure Enhancements
In addition to algorithmic improvements, UW made significant progress on Herbie’s
infrastructure to better support correctness-oriented experimentation and benchmarking.

One improvement involved constructing a mini-benchmarking infrastructure for evaluating
the performance and stability of rewritten expressions. This infrastructure supports highly
repeatable comparisons across multiple versions of Herbie, as well as consistency checks across
heterogeneous architectures. The benchmarking framework was informed by the broader
ComPort emphasis on reproducibility and was tested in conjunction with Odyssey and FPBench
evaluation pipelines.

Finally, Herbie benefitted from cross-tool integration infrastructure, including shared
representations and annotation propagation that align with other systems.. For example, the
annotation and intent-propagation frameworks described in Codetations informed the design of
stable tracking of expression provenance through the rewrite pipeline. Similarly, Chassis’
cross-language transformations influenced improvements in Herbie’s parsing and rewriting code
paths, enabling more stable handling of expressions spanning multiple language ecosystems—an
increasingly common pattern in scientific computing workflows.

These infrastructure improvements also supported dissemination and evaluation efforts through
FPTalks and other community events. Herbie’s integration into these forums helped surface new
use cases, stress-test its capabilities, and strengthen collaboration with DOE labs engaging in
floating-point correctness work.

Across all of these dimensions—rewrite search, hotspot detection, and infrastructure—UW’s
work strengthened Herbie as a core component of the ComPort toolkit. These advances increased
Herbie’s reliability, improved its usability within interactive systems like Odyssey, and ensured
that it remained well aligned with the correctness and portability challenges posed by
heterogeneous scientific computing environments.

7. Chassis: Multi-Language Code
Transformation Framework

7.1 Motivation
Modern scientific and HPC workflows rarely exist within a single language ecosystem. Python is
commonly used for orchestration and data preparation, C and C++ for compute kernels, R for
statistical analysis, and domain-specific languages or auto-generated source for specialized
operations. As these pipelines evolve, developers frequently perform
transformations—refactoring, optimization, annotation, or instrumentation—that must preserve
the semantic structure of the underlying computation.

These transformations become particularly difficult in heterogeneous environments where
compiler pipelines may reorder, inline, or fuse operations differently across architectures. In such
settings, maintaining consistent semantics across languages and transformations is essential for
conducting reliable numerical correctness experiments. Chassis was developed to address this
need by providing a uniform, language-agnostic infrastructure for parsing, transforming,
annotating, and analyzing code across multiple programming languages.

7.2 Chassis Overview
Chassis offers a unified transformation pipeline that normalizes code from different languages
into a common internal representation, applies structured transformations, and renders updated
source back into the original language. This architecture enables the system to provide stable,
semantics-oriented transformations regardless of surface syntax.

Chassis pipeline diagram illustrating parsing, normalization into the IR, transformation passes,
and regeneration of source code.​

Chassis supports several key capabilities:

 ●​ Language-agnostic Parsing:​
 Parsers for Python, JavaScript, and C-family languages extract abstract syntax trees and

 associated metadata. These trees are mapped into a uniform IR that exposes structure
 relevant to numerical correctness (loops, conditionals, arithmetic expressions, reduction
 patterns).​

 ●​ Structured Transformation Passes:​
 The IR enables transformations such as algebraic rewrites, annotation propagation, or
 code motion to be expressed once and applied consistently across languages. This
 structure aligns well with correctness-oriented analysis, where semantic relationships
 matter more than syntactic variation.​

 ●​ Source Regeneration:​
 After applying transformations, Chassis regenerates code in the original language while
 preserving stylistic and lexical cues important for readability and debugging. This allows
 transformed code to drop cleanly back into real-world workflows without requiring
 developers to rewrite surrounding scaffolding.​

Example transformation from the Chassis pipeline showing how an arithmetic expression or loop
structure is normalized and rewritten across two different languages.​

Because Chassis was designed to accommodate multiple languages, it provides a foundation for
transformation-based experiments that must span heterogeneous pipelines—such as those used in
scientific workflows, ML pre- and post-processing, or mixed-language simulation environments.

7.3 Relevance to ComPort
Chassis supports several behaviors that are directly relevant to the ComPort project and
complement Utah’s exception-tracking systems and UW’s Odyssey/Herbie tools:

 ●​ Stable Instrumentation Across Languages:​
 Numerical correctness experiments often require inserting instrumentation—timers, error
 probes, domain-specific assertions—into kernels across multiple language boundaries.
 Chassis ensures that such instrumentation can be inserted consistently regardless of the
 surface language.​

 ●​ Refactoring Support for Portability Studies:​
 Porting scientific applications to heterogeneous architectures frequently involves
 transformations such as loop reordering, precision changes, or control-flow
 simplification. Chassis provides a robust way to apply such transformations while
 preserving semantic structure, making it easier to evaluate correctness implications.​

 ●​ Integration with Rewriting and Analysis Tools:​
 Chassis’ IR provides a bridge to tools like Herbie or FPBench-style analyses.
 Expressions extracted from Python or C kernels can be normalized and passed through
 correctness-preserving rewrites, ensuring that numerical analysis applies across language
 boundaries.​

 ●​ Support for Controlled Studies of Compiler Effects:​
 Chassis enables experiments where code is transformed systematically before passing
 through different compiler pipelines. This supports studies of how heterogeneous
 compilers affect floating-point stability and correctness—an important focus of ComPort.​

Overall, Chassis fills a crucial infrastructure gap by supporting cross-language manipulation
capabilities needed for correctness-oriented evaluation in heterogeneous computing
environments.

7.4 Evaluation

The evaluation demonstrated Chassis’ effectiveness across a range of cross-language examples.
Code fragments in Python, JavaScript, and C were parsed into the shared IR, transformed using
common rewrite rules (e.g., algebraic simplification, annotation insertion), and rendered back
into their original languages. These examples show that Chassis maintains structural and
semantic fidelity even when applying non-trivial transformations.

Examples include:

 ●​ Rewriting arithmetic expressions in Python and showing that the regenerated code
 preserves behavior while isolating numerical-sensitive subexpressions.​

 ●​ Normalizing JavaScript numeric code and propagating instrumentation tags inserted via
 the IR’s annotation layer.​

 ●​ Extracting loops and arithmetic expressions from C kernels, transforming them for
 analysis, and reinserting them into the original compilation pipeline.​

These examples demonstrate that Chassis can reliably support the transformation workflows
needed for correctness-oriented analysis and portability experiments across heterogeneous
systems. By enabling multi-language manipulation with stable structure, Chassis contributes
essential infrastructure to ComPort’s broader effort to understand and improve numerical
correctness across diverse hardware and software environments.

8. FPBench & Community Outreach
The University of Washington played an active role in disseminating ComPort results and
engaging the broader floating-point, HPC, and programming-languages communities throughout
the project period. Much of this work was coordinated through FPBench, an open consortium
dedicated to improving tools, benchmarks, and methodologies for floating-point analysis. UW
contributed updates to the FPBench specification, expanded benchmark coverage, and helped
align FPBench formats and evaluation protocols with the needs surfaced in ComPort’s
heterogeneity studies. These updates ensured that correctness-preserving transformations,
automated rewriting tools, and numerical debugging systems developed under ComPort
remained compatible with community standards.

UW also helped operate the FPTalks series, which includes monthly virtual meetings and an
annual workshop aimed at researchers working on numerical correctness, optimization, and tool

building. These events served as a primary dissemination channel for emerging ComPort results,
enabling rapid feedback from national laboratories, universities, and industry partners.
Discussions stemming from these meetings directly informed improvements to Odyssey’s
workflow, Herbie’s rewrite heuristics, and Chassis’ language-agnostic transformation
infrastructure.

Throughout the project, UW collaborated closely with researchers at LLNL and PNNL,
presenting intermediate results, tool prototypes, and evaluation findings relevant to DOE’s
correctness and performance-portability priorities. These interactions helped identify new
benchmark kernels, integration points with DOE tools, and cases where heterogeneous
compilation pipelines surfaced stability challenges well suited for Odyssey and Herbie analysis.

UW also contributed to community-facing correctness workshops, including sessions co-located
with PL, HCI, and HPC conferences. These workshops highlighted emerging needs in
floating-point debugging and stability testing, helping to situate ComPort contributions within
ongoing national efforts to increase reliability in scientific software. Through these channels,
UW ensured that its tools, benchmarks, and methodologies were broadly accessible and well
integrated with the communities most impacted by rising heterogeneity in scientific computing.

12. Summary
The University of Washington’s contributions to the ComPort project advanced a suite of
techniques and tools designed to improve the correctness, robustness, and interpretability of

numerical software operating in increasingly heterogeneous computing environments. UW’s
work complemented Utah’s exception-tracking infrastructure and UC Davis’s ML-driven
analyses by focusing on the stages of the workflow where developers diagnose instability,
rewrite fragile expressions, track semantics across code evolution, and perform controlled
experiments in polyglot scientific pipelines.

UW developed Odyssey, an interactive workbench that unifies diagnosis, rewrite exploration,
and tuning of floating-point expressions, providing insight and control not achievable with
automated tools alone. Enhancements to Herbie strengthened correctness-preserving rewriting
through improved search heuristics, error attribution, and benchmarking infrastructure. Chassis
provided multi-language transformation capabilities essential for stable instrumentation and
controlled cross-language numerics experiments. These thrusts were reinforced by UW’s
leadership in FPBench and the FPTalks community, ensuring that the project’s artifacts,
benchmarks, and methodologies were broadly disseminated and aligned with community
standards.

Together, these contributions form a cohesive toolchain that supports the analysis, improvement,
and validation of numerical behavior across diverse architectures and language ecosystems.
UW’s work helps ensure that scientific applications retain mathematical integrity and predictable
behavior as they are ported to the heterogeneous systems that increasingly define modern HPC.
