Introduction
 The analysis of fresh post-detonation nuclear debris for radioactive fission
products, actinides, and activation products provides key diagnostic information that
informs device design, and ultimately attribution. The mission of the National Technical
Nuclear Forensics (NTNF) program is to provide quality measurements of key analytes
in debris, and to interpret the measurements for this purpose. Each target analyte has
different value and different diagnostic influence, the importance of which depends
directly on the specific scenario. At a high level, activation products provide information
into structural materials contained within the responsible device.

 Activation products are often extremely difficult to measure in post-detonation
debris since they tend to form in far lower quantities than fission products and are
therefore often obscured by the high radioactive background of the debris. Consequently,
extensive chemical processing (purification) is almost always required to obtain quality
activation product measurements. Additionally, large fractions of debris samples are
usually required in order to provide suitable quantities of a given activation product
analyte. In low activity samples (<5E13 fissions) this latter requirement becomes highly
problematic, since the number of activation product analytes may become too extensive
for the total activity of sample. The purpose of this project is to develop a sequential
separation procedure in which several activation products can be analyzed from a single
sample aliquot, thereby preserving sample for other radiometric analyses. We focused on
analytes produced in neutron irradiations of stainless steel, which include the elements
Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu (Figure 1). Various radioisotopes of these elements will be
formed if stainless steel is present in a detonated nuclear device, and the various ratios
will be dependent on the neutron energy spectrum passing through the material; analysis
of the stainless-steel derived radioisotopes may therefore provide important diagnostic
information of device reconstruction (DR) teams within the NTNF mission.

Task 1: Cr/Mn/Fe sequential procedure
 A new sequential separations procedure was developed for three of the six target
elements in this project: Cr, Mn, and Fe. A simplified flow diagram for this new chemical
process is depicted in Figure 2. An aliquot of sample is first combined with known
quantities of stable elemental carriers of all three elements (10 mg Mn, 20 mg Cr and Fe).
The stable carrier isotopes are next equilibrated with the radioactive isotopes of each
element through heating in nitric acid; at this point the stable and radioactive isotopes of
a given element can be assumed to act chemically identically to one another. Following
this equilibration step, manganese is selectively precipitated as MnO2 through treatment
with NaBrO3, leaving iron and chromium dissolved in the acidic media. The MnO2 is
collected through centrifugation and decantation of the mother liquor. The NaBrO3
concomitantly oxidizes all Cr3+ to Cr6+, thereby preventing its precipitation as Cr(OH)3 in
the next chemical step. Following MnO2 precipitation, the solution is basified using
NH4OH, at which point iron is selectively precipitated as Fe(O)OH*H2O, while the
chromium remains dissolved. The Fe(O)OH*H2O is collected through centrifugation, and

the chromium is precipitated as BaCrO4 through addition of Ba(NO3)2. This series of

element is subjected to specific (previously validated) purification procedures designed
to remove all radiometric interferences from the samples. These purification procedures
each take between 4 – 8 hours to accomplish.

Task 2: Co/Ni/Cu sequential procedure
 A new sequential separations procedure was developed for the remaining three
target analytes, namely Co, Ni, and Cu. A simplified flow diagram for the procedure is
depicted in Figure 3. Similarly to the procedure developed in Task 1, an aliquot of sample
is first combined with known quantities of stable elemental carriers of all three elements
(20 mg of Co, Ni, and Cu). The sample is then heated to impart equilibration and is then
taken to dryness to remove all nitric acid. The sample is then dissolved in hydrochloric
acid (HCl) and again taken to dryness to further remove nitric acid. The sample is then
dissolved in dilute HCl, and CuSCN is precipitated through treatment with H2NOH*HCl
and KSCN. The CuSCN is collected through centrifugation and decantation of the
supernatant. Following removal of copper, a series of chemical purification steps are
undertaken which remove radiometric interferences from both cobalt and nickel. These
steps were included in the original purification procedures for each element; combining
them into the sequential procedure represents an improvement in efficiency. Following
these common purification steps, cobalt is selectively precipitated as K3[Co(NO2)6]
through addition of KNO2, after which it is isolated from the nickel via centrifuge. This new
sequential procedure takes approximately 4 hours to complete, after which each element
is subjected to specific (previously validated) purification procedures designed to remove
all radiometric interferences from the samples. These purification procedures each take
between 6 – 8 hours to accomplish.

Task 3: Testing tasks 1 & 2

 To test the procedures outlined in Tasks 1 and 2 we secured a number of
radioactive tracers, including 51Cr, 54Mn, 55Fe, and 57/60Co. Running through the
procedures using these tracers allowed for us to check inter-element contamination and
validate gravimetric mount forms for each element. For the Task 2 procedure, final mount
forms were also tested using ICP-MS to confirm no inter-element purification, since we
did not have access to radioactive tracers of nickel or copper.
 The procedures were also tested in the presence of large quantities of mixed
fission products (ca. 4 × 1012 fissions) produced in the thermal neutron irradiation of highly
enriched uranium (HEU). Very large decontamination factors were calculated for all
radioactive fission products. Only trace quantities of 111Ag and 127Sb were observed in the
manganese sample, however the gamma-rays associated with these contaminants do
not interfere with those for the analytes of interest (52/54Mn) and are therefore not
considered to be problematic. The iron sample was found to contain very small quantities
of 95Zr and sizeable quantities of 132Te. While the presence of 95Zr is not problematic for
55/59
 Fe quantification, the 132Te contamination was determined to be unacceptably high. A
new purification step was therefore added to the iron purification procedure in which TeS2
is precipitated through sparging with H2S gas. This change was tested in the presence of
mixed fission products and found to be adequate for the removal of all measurable 132Te
from the sample.

Task 5: Cr/Mn/Fe/Co/Ni/Cu sequential procedure
 Following validation of the two procedures outlined in tasks 1 – 3, a new sequential
procedure was formulated by combining the two procedures into one. The first iteration
of this procedure is represented in Figure 4 and involves a simple addition of the Task 2
sequential procedure onto the end of the Task 1 sequential procedure. To successfully

achieve this feat, the copper, cobalt, and nickel are precipitated from solution as sulfides
following the culmination of the Task 1 sequential. This chemical step is necessary for

 Although the procedure shown in Figure 4 was found to be successful for
separating the bulk of each element from one another, it was discovered that the yields
for copper were consistently low when using this procedure. Our hypothesis was that
small quantities of copper was being lost in the MnO2 precipitation and/or the
Fe(O)OH*H2O precipitation. As such, a new formulation of the procedure was developed
in which copper is the first element to be precipitated (Figure 5). This procedure was
shown to be consistently successful for the purposes of Task 5. To accomplish this, the
sample must first be brought to dryness and then converted to HCl (as described in Task
2). Following precipitation of the CuSCN, the sample is again brought to dryness to
convert the acid media back to HNO3.
 In the 2024 OPEX it was discovered that the dissolution procedure used to digest
AFTAC flight papers results in the volatilization of chromium. This discovery means that
until a fundamental change is made to the dissolution process, chromium purification
chemistry will not be used by the C-NR radiochemistry team at Los Alamos National
Laboratory (LANL). The final iteration of the sequential procedure (Figure 5) was therefore

Task 6: Testing task 5
 The procedure developed in Task 5 was tested using the same radioactive tracer
solutions discussed in Task 3 (51Cr, 54Mn, 55Fe, and 57/60Co). Through these tests, it was
found that the manganese sample was contaminated with small quantities of 57/60Co.
Although the gamma-ray emissions for these isotopes do not directly interfere with the
target manganese analytes (52/54Mn), the presence of 57/60Co indicates the presence of
stable cobalt carrier isotopes. The presence of weighable quantities of stable cobalt will
skew the yield of the manganese analysis, thereby making its presence highly
problematic. As such, an additional purification step was added to the manganese
purification procedure in which a second MnO2 precipitation is undertaken following
separation from the other stainless-steel elements. This additional step was found to
successfully remove all cobalt from the purified manganese sample and has since been
added to the operational manganese purification procedure.
 As a proof-of-concept for the usefulness of stainless-steel activations, and to
further test the sequential separation procedure described in Task 5, four 20 mg 304
stainless-steel foils (Figure 6a, see Table 1 for target composition) were irradiated at the

 At least one radioisotope was measured for each of the six target elements, as
shown in Figure 7, including 51Cr, 52/54Mn, 55/59Fe, 56/57/58/60Co, 56/57Ni, and 64Cu. Several
of the isotopes, namely 55/59Fe, 56Ni, and 64Cu were only measured in the chemically
separated samples due to 1) the presence of radiometric interferences in the solution
count (59Fe and 56Ni), or 2) due to more sophisticated X-ray or beta counting requirements
(55Fe and 64Cu, respectively). For the other radioanalytes shown in Figure 7, excellent
agreement between the solution measurements and the sequentially separated samples
were observed. This result further validates the new purification procedure described in
Task 5, and showcases the diagnostic information contained within stainless-steel
activation product measurements.

Impacts and conclusions
 The work outlined in this report has already had a tangible impact on the NTNF
mission. Specifically, the procedures developed herein have become operational
procedures used by the C-NR Radiochemistry Team at Los Alamos National Laboratory
(LANL). The procedures developed in Tasks 1 and 2 were used during the 2024
interagency OPerational EXercise (OPEX) which was supported/organized by the Air
Force Technical Applications Center (AFTAC). This class of exercise is designed to
simulate the actions that would be taken in the aftermath of a domestic nuclear terror
attack. The procedure developed in Task 5 was employed in the 2025 OPEX, representing
a further improvement to our sample saving strategy. An iteration of this same procedure
is planned for use in the 2026 OPEX occurring at the end of July 2026.
 The work outlined within this report represents a highly successful and impactful
campaign to analyze a greater number of analytes from smaller fractions of radioactive
samples. We continue to engage in this area of R&D across the large number of analytes
that our team is routinely tasked to measure.
