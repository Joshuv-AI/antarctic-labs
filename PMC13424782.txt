Exploring the landscape of programmed cell death protein-1 and cluster of differentiation 28 positive T cells and cytokines in non-small cell lung cancer: A retrospective case-control study
Authors: Ying Yang, Rui-Cheng Li, Changkun Lyu, Weibin Zhang, Yanmin Sun, Ran Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveProgrammed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and cluster of differentiation (CD) 28 (CD28) are representative stimulatory and inhibitory signals that regulate T cell function. This retrospective study aimed to evaluate alterations in peripheral PD-1- and CD28-positive T cell populations in patients with benign pulmonary nodules and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).MethodsKaplan–Meier plotter was used to analyze the prognostic value of PD-1 and CD28 expression in NSCLC. Flow cytometry was used to detect T cell subsets in peripheral blood. Serum tumor markers were detected using a chemiluminescent analyzer. Cytokine levels were detected by Cytometric Bead Array.ResultsA total of 526 patients with NSCLC (early stage: 251; advanced stage: 275), 55 patients with benign pulmonary nodules, and 33 healthy participants were included. Kaplan–Meier plotter analysis showed that higher PD-1 expression was correlated with poorer survival in patients with lung adenocarcinoma), whereas higher CD28 expression was correlated with favorable survival in patients with lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma. Compared with healthy controls, the proportion and number of peripheral PD-1+CD3+ T cells and PD-1+CD4+ T cells were significantly increased in patients with benign pulmonary nodules and NSCLS. The numbers of CD28+CD3+ T cells and CD28+CD4+ T cells were lower in patients with NSCLC than in healthy controls. Notably, there were no differences in PD-1+ or CD28+ T cells between patients with benign pulmonary nodules and NSCLC. The numbers of PD-1+ and CD28+ T cells were lower in patients with advanced NSCLC than in those with early NSCLC. Additionally, the levels of serum tumor markers (carcinoembryonic antigen, neuron-specific enolase, cancer antigen 125, cytokeratin 19 fragment antigen 21-1, and cancer antigen 50) were not different between patients with benign pulmonary nodules and early NSCLC but were higher in patients with advanced NSCLC. Moreover, serum interleukin 6 and interferon-γ levels were higher in patients with advanced NSCLC than in those with benign pulmonary nodules and early NSCLC.ConclusionIncreased PD-1 and decreased CD28 suggest potential T cell dysfunction in patients with benign pulmonary nodules and NSCLC. Patients with benign pulmonary nodules showed characteristics similar to those of patients with early NSCLC with respect to PD-1+/CD28+ T cells, tumor markers, and cytokines.

Introduction

The immune system plays a crucial role in the surveillance and elimination of tumor cells. T cells are important components of the immune system and are activated by dual signals. The first signal is an antigen-specific signal (i.e. the major histocompatibility complex (MHC)/antigen peptide complex). The second signal is provided by immune checkpoints (ICs), which include both stimulatory and inhibitory signals involved in T cell proliferation, apoptosis, and activation. 1 Programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) is a representative inhibitory IC expressed on the T cell surface that can be activated by programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), thereby rendering T cells unresponsive or exhausted. 2 PD-L1 is usually overexpressed on tumor cells, leading to tumor escape from immune elimination through binding to PD-1.3,4 To date, anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies have become the main approaches for tumor immunotherapy in several cancer types, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), melanoma, esophageal cancer, and others.5–7

Additionally, cluster of differentiation (CD) 28 (CD28) is a representative stimulatory IC signal that promotes T cell proliferation, activation, and responses. Thus, a CD28 agonist was once regarded as a potential therapeutic strategy for cancer by activating T cell responses. However, in a clinical trial, the CD28 agonist TGN1412 induced severe toxicities and even deaths because of unexpected cytokine release. 8 These serious events emphasized that immune activation requires strict control when employing any immune activator. Notably, as a stimulatory signal, CD28 is required for CD8+ T cell proliferation after PD-1 blockade. CD28 is necessary for achieving better therapeutic effects of anti-PD-1 therapy in several cancers. 9 NI-3201 (a PD-L1 × CD28 bispecific antibody) has the potential to improve cancer immunotherapy outcomes by combining PD-L1 blockade with safe and effective CD28 costimulation. 10 Thus, PD-1- and CD28-positive T cells are involved in cancer progression. NSCLC constitutes 80%–85% of all lung cancer cases and remains the leading cause of cancer-associated mortality. 11

This study aimed to describe the profiles of peripheral T cell subtypes, PD-1 and CD28 expression on T cells, and cytokine patterns in patients with benign pulmonary nodules (BPN) and NSCLC to explore the potential clinical significance of PD-1 and CD28 signaling in NSCLC development.

Material and methods

Patients

This retrospective study enrolled patients with NSCLC (n = 526) and patients with BPN (n = 55). Patients with lung cancer were confirmed by pathological examination at Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (January 2025 to December 2025; patients were enrolled consecutively). NSCLC stage was determined according to the tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) classification for lung cancer. 12 Early-stage NSCLC included carcinoma in situ and stages I and II. Advanced NSCLC included stages III and IV. The exclusion criteria for NSCLC were as follows: (a) nonprimary cancer, anticancer treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors, concurrent autoimmune disease or infection; and (b) incomplete patient information.

Patients with BPN were diagnosed by senior respiratory physicians based on the results of chest computed tomography (CT), tumor markers, or transbronchial lung biopsy according to the “Chinese expert consensus on diagnosis and treatment of pulmonary nodules.” 13 The exclusion criteria for BPN were as follows: (a) nonbenign nodules, pulmonary tuberculosis, or concurrent immune disorders, including autoimmune disease or infection; and (b) incomplete patient information. Healthy participants (n = 33) were recruited as the control group.

This retrospective study was conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2024, and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tangdu Hospital, Fourth Military Medical University (Approval Number: XLCYJZZ-Y-202407-01). This article does not include any personally identifiable patient information. Therefore, written informed consent was exempted for this study. The reporting of this study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 14

Prognostic value of PD-1 and CD28 in NSCLC

Kaplan–Meier (KM) plotter was used to perform survival analysis. KM plotter is an online survival analysis tool (https://www.kmplot.com/analysis) and that assesses the correlation between gene expression and prognosis of patients with cancer. 15 To divide patients with NSCLC into high- and low-expression groups, the “auto select best cutoff” model was used to choose the best performing threshold. Survival curves for PD-1 and CD28 were plotted, and the hazard ratio (HR) and log-rank p value were used to evaluate the correlation between PD-1/CD28 expression and the prognosis of patients with NSCLC.

Detection of peripheral PD-1 and CD28 positive T cells

According to the standard procedure, 2 mL of peripheral blood was collected into an dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA-K2) anticoagulant tube in the morning. Blood samples were analyzed within 6 h using flow cytometry. Briefly, 50 µL of whole blood was incubated with 30 µL of antibodies for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The antibodies included CD45-PE-Cy7, CD3-PerCP, CD4-APC, CD8-APC-Cy7, PD-1-PE, and CD28-FITC (RAISECARE, Qingdao; China). Subsequently, 450 µL of erythrolysin was added and blending using a vortex. Samples were then analyzed using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent). Cell subsets were analyzed using NovoExpress. SSC/CD45 was used to gate lymphocytes.

Tumor markers detection

In total, six tumor markers were analyzed in this study, including carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), cancer antigen 125 (CA125), cytokeratin 19 fragment antigen 21-1 (CYFRA21-1), cancer antigen 50 (CA50), and squamous cell carcinoma antigen (SCC). CEA, NSE, CA125, and CYFRA21-1 were measured based on electrochemical luminescence assay adapted to an automated chemiluminescent analyzer (cobas 8000, Roche). CA50 and SCC were detected using diagnostic kits (Savant Biotechnology Co., Ltd; Beijing, China) on a Smart-3000 analyzer (Keysmile Biological Technology Co., Ltd; Chongqing, China).

Cytokines detection

Cytokine levels (including interleukin (IL)-1β (IL-1β), IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF)-α, interferon (INF)-α (IFN-α), and IFN-γ) were detected by Cytometric Bead Array using a multiplex cytokine detection kit (RAISECARE; Qingdao, China). Briefly, plasma was collected by centrifugation. Then, 50 μL of plasma was incubated with microspheres labeled with multiple antibodies and biotin-labeled detection antibodies for 2 h, followed by two washes with buffer. Phycoerythrin (PE)-streptavidin was then added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 min. Fluorescent signals were detected using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte, Agilent). Cytokine concentrations were calculated according to the standard curves using LEGENDplex analysis software.

Statistical analyses

The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was used to assess data normality. Continuous data were expressed as the median (interquartile range, IQR). Wilcoxon's test was used to evaluate differences between groups. Differences in proportions were analyzed using the chi-square test. Spearman correlation analysis was used to analyze correlations among the variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3). A p <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic information of patients

In total, 526 patients with NSCLC (early stage: 251; advanced stage: 275), 55 patients with BPN, and 33 healthy participants were included in the study (Table 1). Patients with advanced NSCLC were older than patients with BPN. There were more male patients in the advanced NSCLC group than in the early NSCLC group. Among patients with NSCLC, 423 had lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and 103 had lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) (Table 2). Among patients with LUAD, 207 (48.94%) had early-stage disease and 216 (51.06%) had advanced-stage disease. Among patients with LUSC, 44 (42.72%) had early-stage disease and 59 (57.28%) had advanced-stage disease. There was no significant difference in cancer stage between LUAD and LUSC (Table 2). Moreover, as shown in Table 3, there was no significant age difference between early- and advanced-stage LUAD or between early- and advanced-stage LUSC. Regarding sex distribution, the LUAD group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of male patients than the LUSC group in both the early and advanced stages. In the LUAD group, there were more male patients in the advanced-stage group than in the early-stage group. In the LUSC group, however, there was no significant difference in sex distribution between the early and advanced stages (Table 3).

Prognostic value of PD-1 and CD28 in NSCLC

KM plotter was used to explore the correlation between overall survival (OS) in patients with NSCLC and PD-1 and CD28 expression in tumor tissues. Survival curve showed the correlation between poor prognosis and higher expression of PD-1 in patients with LUAD (HR = 1.54, p < 0.0001; Figure 1(a)). There was no significant correlation between PD-1 expression and OS in patients with LUSC (HR = 0.85, p = 0.15; Figure 1(b)). In contrast, high CD28 expression was associated with better OS in both patients with LUAD (HR = 0.58, p < 0.0001) and patients with LUSC (HR = 0.78, p = 0.011; Figure 1(c) and (d)).

Profile of PD-1 and CD28 positive T cells in NSCLC

First, we investigated the levels of PD-1+ T cells in patients with BPN and NSCLC (Figure 2(a) to (f)). Compared with healthy controls (14.51% (10.1%–17.21%); 14.13% (10.98%–21%)), the cell proportion of PD-1+CD3+ T cells and PD-1+CD4+ T cells were significantly increased in patients with BPN (19.16% (14.8%–26.1%), p < 0.0001; 22.76% (17.29%–28.4%), p < 0.0001) and patients with NSCLC (19.45% (15.13%–25.1%), p < 0.0001; 23.3% (17.9%–29.87%), p < 0.0001). The cell quantity of above cells were also higher in both BPN (PD-1+CD3+ T cells: 201 (141–280), p = 0.003; PD-1+CD4+ T cells: 129 (93–161), p = 0.003) and patients with NSCLC (180 (129–257), p = 0.011; 117 (81–164), p = 0.003) than in healthy controls (141 (101–190); 79 (68–125)). Notably, there were no significant differences in PD-1+ T cells (both proportion and count) between patients with BPN and NSCLC (p > 0.05). There was also no significant difference in PD-1+CD8+ T cell levels among healthy controls, patients with BPN, and patients with NSCLC (p > 0.05). We then investigated the levels of peripheral CD28+ T cells (Figure 2(g) to (l)). There was no significant difference in the proportion of CD28+ T cells among healthy controls, patients with BPN, and patients with NSCLC (p > 0.05). The cell quantity of CD28+CD3+ T cells (417 (306–553), p = 0.008) and CD28+CD4+ T cells (323 (235–458), p = 0.012) were less in patients with NSCLC than in healthy controls (538 (388–698); 405 (298–537)). Notably, there was no significant difference in the proportion or count of CD28+ T cells between patients with BPN and NSCLC (p > 0.05).

PD-1 and CD28 positive T cells in NSCLC patients with different stage

As shown in Figure 3, no significant differences were observed in the proportions of PD-1+ T cells or CD28+ T cells between patients with early-stage (stages 0, I, and II) and advanced-stage (stages III and IV) NSCLC (Figure 3(a) to (f); p > 0.05). However, the cell counts of PD-1+ T and CD28+ T cells (including CD3+, CD4+, and CD8+ T cells) were lower in patients with advanced NSCLC than in those with early-stage NSCLC (Figure 3(g) to (l); p < 0.05). Notably, there were no significant differences in PD-1+ T and CD28+ T cells between patients with LUAD and LUSC (Supplementary Figure 1).

Tumor markers in NSCLC patients

Furthermore, we analyzed the serum levels of six tumor markers (CEA, NSE, CA125, CYFRA21-1, CA50, and SCC) in patients with BPN and NSCLC. We observed no significant differences in these six tumor markers between patients with BPN and those with early-stage NSCLC, whereas in advanced NSCLC patients, these tumor markers’ levels were significantly higher than that in BPN and early-stage NSCLC patients (Table 4). Moreover, no significant correlations were observed between the proportion of PD-1+ T cells and tumor marker levels or between the proportion of CD28+ T cells and tumor marker levels (Figure 4(a) and (b)).

Cytokines levels in NSCLC

The plasma levels of 12 cytokines (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12p70, IL-17, TNF-α, IFN-α, and IFN-γ) were analyzed in patients with BPN and NSCLC (Table 5). We observed that IL-6 and IFN-γ levels were significantly higher in patients with advanced NSCLC than in those with BPN or early-stage NSCLC. Notably, there were no significant differences in the levels of the other cytokines between patients with BPN and patients with NSCLC (p > 0.05).

Discussion

T cell dysfunction or exhaustion facilitates tumor escape from immune destruction, thereby promoting cancer development, which might be driven by the abnormal expression of inhibitory and stimulatory receptors on the T cell surface. 16 PD-1, a coinhibitory receptor expressed on T cells, can induce T cell apoptosis and immunosuppression by binding to PD-L1, which is frequently overexpressed on tumor cells. The PD-L1/PD-1 pathway is a critical immunosuppressive axis in cancer and has become a major target for cancer immunotherapy. Multiple anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have been used for the treatment of NSCLC, including sintilimab (anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (mAb)), camrelizumab (anti-PD-1 mAb), penpulimab (anti-PD-1 mAb), and serplulimab (anti-PD-L1 mAb), among others. 17 In contrast, CD28 acts as a costimulatory molecule that promotes T cell proliferation, activation, and cytokine production. Decreased CD28 expression is a characteristic of senescent T cells. 18 Kamphorst et al.9 found that the rescue of exhausted CD8+ T cells by PD-1-targeted therapies is CD28-dependent and that CD28 blockade could counteract the therapeutic effect of anti-PD-1 therapy. However, Yang et al. 19 showed that inhibiting intracellular CD28 in cancer cells enhances antitumor immunity and overcomes anti-PD-1 resistance. Hui et al. 20 showed that PD-1 activation could deactivate CD28 signaling by mediating CD28 dephosphorylation, further demonstrating the close relationship between PD-1 and CD28. Thus, the expression profiles of PD-1 and CD28 may play important roles during cancer progression.

In this study, KM Plotter analysis first showed that higher PD-1 expression in tumor tissues was correlated with poorer prognosis in patients with LUAD, whereas higher CD28 expression was correlated with favorable prognosis in patients with LUAD and LUSC, which is consistent with the theoretical basis of immunotherapy. Second, we investigated the levels of PD-1+ and CD28+ T cells in the peripheral blood of patients with NSCLC. We observed that the proportions of PD-1+ total T cells and PD-1+ helper T cells were higher in patients with NSCLC than in healthy controls. However, lower numbers of CD28+ total T cells and CD28+ helper T cells were observed in patients with NSCLC. Notably, there were no significant differences in the proportions or counts of PD-1+ or CD28+ cytotoxic T cells between patients with NSCLC and healthy controls. Taken together, the alterations in the PD-1+ and CD28+ T cell profiles suggested potential T cell dysfunction in the peripheral blood of patients with NSCLC.

Pulmonary nodules are a common manifestation of lung diseases, including benign and malignant nodules. 21 The management of patients with BPN is important for the early diagnosis of lung cancer. In patients with BPN, we observed that the proportion of PD-1+ T cells was higher than that in healthy controls, indicating increased T cell suppression. Notably, there were no significant differences in the levels of PD-1+ or CD28+ T cells between patients with BPN and NSCLC. Thus, PD-1 and CD28 may not be useful for the differential diagnosis of BPN and NSCLC. Moreover, we analyzed serum tumor marker levels in patients with BPN and NSCLC. The results showed that five tumor markers (i.e. CEA, NSE, CA125, CYFRA21-1, and CA50) did not differ between patients with BPN and those with early-stage NSCLC but were elevated in patients with advanced NSCLC (vs. patients with BPN and early-stage NSCLC). Furthermore, we observed that the levels of 12 cytokines (including pro- and anti-inflammatory factors) did not differ between patients with BPN and those with early-stage NSCLC. IL-6 and IFN-γ levels were increased in patients with advanced NSCLC, consistent with the report by Chang et al. 22 Elevated IL-6 has been found in many types of tumors and plays a role in promoting tumor growth by regulating multiple signaling pathways.23,24 Taken together, these results showed similar cytokine profiles in patients with BPN and those with early-stage NSCLC.

There are several limitations to the present study. First, we evaluated PD-1 and CD28 expression on the T cell surface to explore T cell activity in patients with NSCLC and BPN. Although PD-1 and CD28 represent coinhibitory and costimulatory signals, respectively, other ICs are also involved in regulating T cell proliferation and activation. 25 Thus, we acknowledge the limitation of our approach in exploring the full spectrum of T cell ICs. To better describe the T cell immune landscape in NSCLC, further studies are needed to investigate more T cell sub-population, include the T cells with co-expression of PD-1 and CD28. Second, the absence of longitudinal follow-up outcome data. Thus, this study cannot demonstrate the correlation between peripheral blood PD-1/CD28 levels and prognosis in patients with NSCLC or the risk of malignant transformation of BPN. Third, there were differences in age and sex among the groups, which may have introduced potential biases that could affect immunomonitoring outcomes. Additionally, the expression of PD-1 and CD28 in tissues and the correlation between their expression in tissues and peripheral blood require further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrated differences in PD-1 and CD28 expression on peripheral T cells among patients with NSCLC, patients with BPN, and healthy participants. Increased PD-1 and decreased CD28 indicate potential T cell dysfunction in patients with BPN and NSCLC. Moreover, patients with BPN showed characteristics similar to those of patients with early-stage NSCLC with respect to PD-1+/CD28+ T cells, tumor markers, and cytokines. Further cohort studies are needed to fully understand the correlation between the T cell immune landscape and NSCLC progression, prognosis, and tumor immunotherapy.
