First year-round occurrence checklist of mesopelagic “swimmers” collected by an automatic sediment trap on Mooring B (Ross Sea, Antarctica) in years 1995-1996, 2004-2005 and 2014-2016
Authors: Marco Grillo, Martina Linciano, Maria Balan, Alice Guzzi, Nicholas Francesco Noli, Valentina Cometti, Leonardo Langone, Patrizia Giordano, Stefano Schiaparelli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-term studies of pelagic communities in the Southern Ocean are crucial for understanding biodiversity patterns and detecting the impacts of climate change and other environmental stressors. Here, we provide temporal occurrence and spatial data on Antarctic “swimmers” (i.e. pelagic organisms that are captured by sediment traps deployed on moorings) collected within three projects of the Italian National Antarctic Programme (PNRA) in the Ross Sea sector during the periods 1995–1996, 2004–2005 and 2014–2016. These data were obtained over six Italian Antarctic expeditions carried out between 1994 and 2016.New informationThis dataset constitute to the first checklist of Antarctic “swimmers” collected in the Joides Basin, a sector of the Ross Sea (Antarctica), using automatic sediment traps deployed at approximately 500 metres depth on Mooring B (approx. Lat = 74.1514°S, Lon = 175.0906°E). Although primarily designed to study particle and export fluxes, these traps also capture motile pelagic organisms, the “swimmers”, that enter actively or accidentally. In total 210,411 individuals were sampled. These represent 68 genera and 68 species, corresponding to 905 occurrence records. These accidental captures provide valuable biodiversity data for zooplankton and micronekton taxa yielding quality information useful to refine species distributional models and occurrence predictions, due to the fact that, together with the biological records, a full set of environmental data (e.g. temperature, salinity and density) continuously collected by mooring sensors.

Introduction

The "swimmers" of the Joides Basin (Ross Sea): a window on Antarctic mesopelagic biodiversity

Antarctic marine ecosystems play a pivotal role in regulating the global climate and in the biogeochemical cycles of the ocean (Henley et al. 2020, Constable et al. 2023, Schofield et al. 2024). In particular, the Ross Sea represents one of the most productive and dynamic areas of the Southern Ocean, hosting planktonic and benthic communities that respond sensitively to environmental changes (Smith et al. 2014, Cummings et al. 2018, D'Sa et al. 2021, Kim et al. 2022, Thomalla et al. 2023). Amongst the communities inhabiting the Ross Sea, “swimmers” play a crucial role in carbon cycling through trophic transfer and vertical transport of organic matter (Atherden et al. 2024). The term “swimmers” refers to all mobile marine organisms that, due to their swimming capabilities, whether in adult phase or larval stages, enter, either actively or accidentally, into structures or devices designed for other purposes, such as sediment traps (Langone et al. 2000, Langone et al. 2003, Chiarini et al. 2013, Chiarini et al. 2019, Atherden et al. 2024, D'Angelo et al. 2024).

Their presence is not solely the result of passive transport; they can also move autonomously, actively reaching the trap cup, where they are subsequently collected and preserved in the formalin solution (Knauer et al. 1979, Matsuno et al. 2014, Makabe et al. 2016, Danovaro et al. 2017). In the first case, the organisms reach the jar already dead, while, in the second case, organisms are perfect and were killed by the preservative solution.

The swimmers category is mainly composed of zooplanktonic, micronektonic and benthic organisms and primarily include planktonic crustaceans, such as copepods, euphausiids and amphipods, as well as tunicates (appendicularians and salps), cnidarians, pelagic molluscs (pteropods), chaetognaths and larval stages of fish and decapod larvae (Atherden et al. 2024, D'Angelo et al. 2024).

Zooplanktonic taxa are a fundamental component of marine ecosystems, acting as a key link between primary producers (e.g. phytoplankton community) and higher trophic levels, such as fish, bird and marine mammals (Granata et al. 2000, La Mesa et al. 2000, Turner 2004, Pakhomov et al. 2020). Additionally, some zooplanktonic groups actively contribute to the vertical transport of carbon through the production of faecal pellets and daily migrations within the water column (Atkinson 1996, Hansen et al. 1997, Carli et al. 2000, Calbet and Landry 2004, Ratnarajah et al. 2023).

Zooplankton respond sensitively to environmental changes (e.g. water column stratification and ocean acidification) (Barton et al. 2013), with effects on their abundance, distribution, life cycle and physiological adaptations as highlighted in recent studies (Poloczanska et al. 2013, Kim et al. 2022). Zooplankton assemblages in particular and, especially copepods, represent a reliable indicator (Edwards and Richardson 2004, Hays et al. 2005, Edwards 2009) of environmental variability, including both natural and human-induced climate change perturbations (Turner 2004, Bonello et al. 2020).

Zooplankton communities in the Ross Sea sector have been extensively studied since 1985 with a special focus on their biodiversity and ecological role (Amato 1990, Carli et al. 1990, Guglielmo et al. 1990, Zunini Sertorio et al. 1990, Carli et al. 1992, Zunini Sertorio et al. 1994, Acosta Pomar et al. 2000, Carli et al. 2000, Zunini Sertorio et al. 2000, Carli et al. 2002,Pane 2004, Bolinesi et al. 2020, Cecchetto et al. 2021, Cecchetto et al. 2022, Cordone et al. 2022, Grillo et al. 2022, Monti-Birkenmeier et al. 2022, Barra et al. 2023, Bergamasco et al. 2023, Grillo et al. 2024b, Minutoli et al. 2025) and their relationship with sea ice (Guglielmo et al. 2007, Granata et al. 2009, Guglielmo et al. 2015, Granata et al. 2022). The micronectonic and benthic communities have also been extensively studied, as they play an ecological role and have been the subject of numerous studies aimed at understanding their biodiversity and ecological dynamics in the Ross Sea (Azzali and Kalinowski 2000, Faranda et al. 2000, Vacchi et al. 2000, Schiaparelli et al. 2006, Piazza et al. 2020).

Here, we present a complete checklist of “swimmers” collected by automatic sediment traps during six different Italian Antarctic expeditions, from '1995' to '2016', all placed on Mooring “B” in the Joides Basin. The positioning of the sediment trap(s) corresponded to the mesopelagic layer, at depths between 470 and 530 metres, a still undersampled ecological zone and may provide relevant information for modelling species distribution (Grillo et al. 2022, Grillo et al. 2024a, Grillo et al. 2024b).

These samples are now curated by the Italian National Antarctic Museum (MNA, section of Genoa), whose previous contributions focused on Mollusca, Tanaidacea, Fungi, Ophiuroidea, Porifera, Bryozoa, Rotifera, Asteroidea, Copepoda, Isopoda and benthic pionieer communities (Ghiglione et al. 2013, Piazza et al. 2014, Selbmann et al. 2015, Cecchetto et al. 2017, Ghiglione et al. 2018, Cecchetto et al. 2019, Bonello et al. 2020, Garlaschè et al. 2020, Guzzi et al. 2022, Grillo et al. 2024c, Noli et al. 2024, Cometti et al. 2025).

Study area description

Ross Sea area, being a high-latitude, nutrient-rich continental shelf system, supports extensive phytoplankton blooms each spring, driven by the seasonal retreat of sea ice and the recurrent formation of coastal polynyas, particularly near the Ross Ice Shelf and Terra Nova Bay (Langone et al. 2003, Burgay et al. 2020, Fernandez et al. 2025). These oceanographic and ecological dynamics directly affect the Joides Basin, integrating it into the wider biological productivity patterns of the region, as documented in early studies (Smith and Nelson 1985, Nelson and Smith 1986, Russo 2000). Joides Basin, located in the western Ross Sea (Fig. 1), is a prominent shelf basin elongated in a southwest–northeast direction. It forms part of the broader, deep epicontinental shelf that characterises the Ross Sea region. As a shelf basin, Joides acts as a sediment trap, accumulating materials transported from surrounding glacial margins and the overlying water column (Corradi et al. 1997, Langone et al. 2000, Torricella et al. 2021). Samples were collected in the Joides Basin area through the deployment of Mooring B, which was equipped with an automated sediment trap positioned at different depths depending on the sampling year (ranging from 470 and 530 mteres). The seafloor at the site lies at approximately 560 metres. Sampling campaigns were carried out during three different Italian Antarctic Expeditions: 1994–1995, 2004–2005 and 2014–2016. Each sampling time series spanned two consecutive expeditions, to allow for mooring deployment in the first year and recovery in the following one. During these campaigns, the sediment trap was deployed at a depth of 530 metres in 1994–1995, 515 metres in 2004–2005 and 470 metres in 2014–2016. Mooring B was consistently deployed within the Ross Sea Marine Protected Area (MPA), specifically in sector RS-GPZi (Ross Sea - General Protection Zone), for all time series.

In 2009, the long-term monitoring programme MORSea – Marine Observatory in the Ross Sea was launched, coordinated by the University of Naples “Parthenope” in collaboration with the National Research Council (CNR). The project is dedicated to managing the numerous oceanographic moorings deployed in the western sector of the Ross Sea since 1994. The main objective is to investigate the physical and biogeochemical characteristics of the water mass located above the continental shelf of the Ross Sea, an area where dense shelf waters are formed. To achieve this, the programme collects oceanographic data and develops time series that allow researchers to monitor environmental changes over time (Project Mooring Page).

Sampling methods

Sampling description

The sediment trap was designed to capture sinking particulate matter over the course of the deployment period, enabling the collection of biological material descending through the water column.

For the 1995–1996 time series, a McLane Parflux Mark 7G-21 cups trap with a 0.5 m² collection area was used. For the 2004–2005 and 2014–2016 time series, a McLane 78HW-13 cups trap, also with a 0.5 m² collection area, was deployed. The cups were previously filled with a buffered solution of 5% formaldehyde to better preserve the organic matter and the cup rotation intervals varied between 7 days to 3 months.

More information on the technical specifications of Mooring B and the pre-treatment of samples can be found in Langone et al. (2000) and Langone et al. (2003).

Quality control

All records were validated. Coordinates were converted into decimal latitude and decimal longitude and plotted to verify the geographical location and locality. All scientific names were checked for typos and matched to the species information backbone of WorldsRegister of Marine Species (WoRMS); dichotomous keys and LSID were assigned to each taxon as scientificNameID. Eventdate and time were converted into ISO 8601 and verified with the field reports.

Step description

"Swimmers" were manually isolated from passive sinking particles, picking them out from sediment trap samples under a ZEISS Discovery V.8 stereomicroscope at the National Research Council, Institute of Polar Sciences (CNR-ISP) section of Bologna (Italy) according to the protocol of Chiarini et al. (2013).

Samples were initially preserved at +4°C in a buffered 5% formaldehyde solution at the CNR-ISP laboratories. They were subsequently transferred to 96% ethanol and delivered to the MNA (Genoa section, Italy), where the previously unsorted “swimmers” were sorted, identified and counted. Some specimens were mounted on glass slides for study. All vouchers are now part of the biological collection of the MNA (Fig. 2), where a total of 905 vouchers have been assigned.

The "swimmer" taxa were identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level (Boltovskoy et al. 2003, Boxshall and Halsey 2004, Razouls et al. 2008, Castellani and Edwards 2017, Bonello et al. 2020). The term undefined indicates that the organism could not be taxonomically identified.

The online portal WoRMS was used to confirm acceptance of species names. When identification was inconclusive, only genus or family names were assigned (Fig. 2).

Taxonomic coverage

Description

The "swimmer" diversity in the dataset is represented by a total of 210,411 individuals (comprising vials with single species isolated from bulk samples and glass slides with dissected or whole specimens) collected during three different Italian Antarctic expedition (i.e. when Mooring B has been under maintenance and sediment trap cups have been changed). A total of 906 MNA vouchers, with the highest proportions observed in PNRA_X (n = 393, 43.42%) and PNRA_XXIX (n = 357, 39.45%), followed by PNRA_XIX (n = 155, 17.13%). Swimmer species sampled using automatic sediment trap consist of two kingdoms, 11 phyla, 17 classes, 26 orders, 53 families, 68 genus and 68 species with six morphotypes not being identified (e.g., sp., spp., cf.), plus several undefined organisms that could not be classified at any taxonomic level.

The most abundant class is Copepoda, accounting for 43.54% of the total. This is followed by Polychaeta (17.13%), Hydrozoa (10.17%), Malacostraca (8.29%) and Gastropoda (6.85%). Other moderately represented groups include Ostracoda (5.75%) and Ophiuroidea (2.87%). The remaining classes show values below 1.5%, such as Thecostraca (1.33%), undefined (0.88%), Appendicularia and Asteroidea (both 0.77%), Cestoda (0.66%), Sagittoidea (0.55%) and other classes with even lower frequencies, such as Teleostei, Oligotrichea, Holothuroidea and Clitellata, each at 0.11% (Fig. 3).

At the PNRA_X time series, Copepoda (44.54%) and Polychaeta (20.10%) are the dominant classes, followed by Hydrozoa (9.16%), Malacostraca (7.63%), Gastropoda (5.34%) and Ostracoda (6.11%). Other classes show lower values: Ophiuroidea (3.56%), Asteroidea (1.02%), Cestoda (1.02%), Thecostraca (0.76%), Appendicularia (0.51%) and undefined (0.25%) (Fig. 4).

At the PNRA_XIX time series, Copepoda (40.00%) is once again the predominant class, followed by Malacostraca (12.26%), Gastropoda (11.61%), Polychaeta (9.03%), Ostracoda (8.37%) and Ophiuroidea (5.81%). These are followed by Thecostraca (4.52%), undefined (3.23%) and Appendicularia (1.94%). With lower percentages, Hydrozoa and Cestoda are both at 1.29% and Holothuroidea is at 0.65%.

At the PNRA_XXIX time series, Copepoda continues to dominate (43.98%), followed by Polychaeta (17.37%) and Hydrozoa (15.13%). In descending order, these are followed by Malacostraca (7.28%), Astropoda (6.44%), Ostracoda (4.20%), Sagittoidea (1.40%), Asteroidea (0.84%), Ophiuroidea (0.84%) and Appendicularia, Thecostraca and undefined (each at 0.56%). Finally, with the lowest percentage (0.28%), are Clitellata, Oligotrichea and Teleostei.

In general, Calanoida (36.69%) is the most abundant order (Fig. 5), followed by Phyllodocida (12.38%), undefined (9.06%), Cyclopoida (6.52%) and Pteropoda (5.97%). Other notable orders include Halocyprida (5.41%), Amphipoda (4.42%), Siphonophorae (3.87%), Narcomedusae (2.87%), Isopoda and Spionida (both at 2.43%). These are followed by orders with percentages below 2%, including Anthoathecata and Euphausiacea (both at 1.22%), Ophiacanthida (1.88%), Littorinimorpha (0.88%), Copelata (0.77%), Aphragmophora (0.55%), Terebellida (0.33%), Amphinomida (0.22%), Ophiurida (0.22%), Choreotrichida, Cumacea, Decapoda, Leptothecata, Podocopida and Scolecida (0.11%).

At PNRA_X time series, Calanoida (36.90%) is the most abundant order, followed by Phyllodocida (15.01%), undefined (8.40%), Cyclopoida (6.87%), Halocyprida (5.85%), Pteropoda (5.34%), Siphonophorae (4.33%), Amphipoda (4.07%) and Narcomedusae (3.05%). Other orders present at lower values include Spionida and Isopoda (2.80%), Ophiacanthida (1.79%), Terebellida (0.76%), Anthoathecata, Euphausiacea and Copelata (0.51%), Decapoda and Leptothecata (each at 0.25%) (Fig. 6).

At PNRA_XIX time series, Calanoida (32.90%) is the most represented order, followed by undefined (9.68%), Pteropoda (9.03%), Phyllodocida (8.39%), Halocyprida (7.72%), Cyclopoida (7.10%) and Amphipoda (6.45%). Ophiacanthida also shows a relevant presence (5.81%). Orders with lower percentages include Littorinimorpha, Isopoda and Euphausiacea (all at 2.58%), Copelata (1.94%) and Siphonophorae (1.29%). Finally, with marginal presence (0.65%), are the orders Cumacea, Podocopida and Scolecida.

At PNRA_XXIX time series, a clear dominance of Calanoida (38.12%) is observed, followed by Phyllodocida (11.20%) and undefined (9.52%). Other significantly represented orders include Cyclopoida (5.88%), Pteropoda (5.32%) and Siphonophorae (4.48%). Present, at lower percentages, are also Amphipoda, Halocyprida and Narcomedusae (all at 3.92%), Spionida (3.08%), Anthoathecata (2.52%), Isopoda (1.96%), Euphausiacea and Aphragmophora (both at 1.40%), Littorinimorpha (1.12%), Copelata, Ophiurida and Amphinomida (all at 0.56%) and Choreotrichida and Ophiacanthida (both at 0.28%).

Regarding the life stages range in the samples, in general, the dataset is composed by a majority of adults (73.48%), followed by copepodites stages (CII, CIII, CIV, CV and copepodids: 9.97%), nauplius (0.99%), cypris (1.10%), metazoan juveniles and larvae (juveniles, larvae and post-larvae 4.09%). Trochophore (0.22%), calyptopis (0.22%), decapodid (0.11%), praniza (0.11%), praniza III (0.33%), zuphea (1.10%), nectochaete (2.54%), auricolaria (0.11%), brachiolaria (0.22%), bipinnaria (0.55%), actinula (0.55%), trypanorhynch larvae (0.44%) and fragments of siphonophoran colony (3.87%) (Figs 7, 8).

The swimmers found inside the samples can be assigned to the following ecological category: holoplankton (75.36%), meroplankton (17.57%), benthos (5.97%), nekton (0.44%), while the remaining 0.66% could not be assessed and are reported as unidentified (Fig. 9).

Description

This dataset is built on information from the “swimmers” specimens analysed in this work (Grillo et al. 2026). The samples were pooled into a single dataset. This dataset will be useful to investigate the community structure of “swimmers” and their relative larval stages.
