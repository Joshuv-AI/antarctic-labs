Dielectric polarization and charge transport in Gd-doped Fe3O4 nanocrystals for energy storage and electronic devices

Neeraj Kadian, Mutaz Salih, Renu Kumari, Smita Korpal, Jasvir Dalal, Ahmed B M Ibrahim

Abstract

Gd-doped magnetite (GdxFe3−xO4) nanocrystals were synthesized via the co-precipitation method with an aim to study the effect of rare-earth substitution on their structure, shape, and dielectric and magnetic properties. Structural analysis using X-ray diffraction revealed that the samples formed a single-phase cubic spinel structure with minor lattice distortion upon Gd substitution. Nanocrystalline materials were characterized as cluster-like grains using field-emission scanning electron microscopy. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopic analysis provided evidence of a spinel structure and revealed metal–oxygen vibrations at the tetrahedral and octahedral sites. From the dielectric analysis, improved dielectric permittivity at lower frequencies, attributed to Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization, and lower dielectric loss at higher frequencies indicate their potential for dielectric and energy-related applications. The application of an alternating current to the materials revealed conductivity consistent with the thermally activated hopping mechanism and the correlated barrier hopping model. Magnetic analysis revealed a soft magnetic material with lower coercivity and moderate saturation magnetization. The combined structural, dielectric, electrical, and magnetic characteristics highlight the potential of Gd-doped magnetite nanocrystals for future energy-related and electronic applications.

Introduction

Ferrites are a class of materials that exhibit a wide range of electrical, magnetic, and dielectric properties, making them highly suitable for diverse scientific and technological applications. These materials have been extensively utilized in electronics, optoelectronics, magnetics, magneto-electronics, electrochemical systems, and biotechnology due to their remarkable functional versatility. Specifically, the electrical behavior of magnetic materials is important for their applications in electronic devices. The spinel ferrites NiFe2O4, MnFe2O4, and ZnFe2O4 exhibit higher saturation magnetization and permeability at higher frequencies, as well as relatively higher electrical resistivity, thereby promoting their use in solid-state electronic and magneto-electronic systems. These properties of ferrites help in the fabrication of advanced devices for controlled charge transport.

Magnetite (Fe3O4) is interesting among other spinel ferrites due to its inverse spinel structure, which provides both Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions and enables hopping of electrons in the octahedral (B) sites. This feature greatly enhances the material's electrical conductivity and improves dielectric polarization. The general formula of spinel ferrites is MFe2O4, where M metal ions are found at the tetrahedral (A) and octahedral (B) sites of the FCC oxygen lattice, and their distribution closely relates to the physical properties. The dielectric response of Fe3O4 is typically governed by Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and electron hopping mechanisms, leading to high permittivity at lower frequencies. Despite these advantages, Fe3O4 nanocrystals suffer from limitations including relatively high dielectric loss, moderate electrical conductivity, particle agglomeration, and limited control over charge-carrier dynamics, which adversely affect dielectric performance and energy storage efficiency. Nanoscale Fe3O4 has a higher surface-to-volume ratio and a tunable microstructure, which can favorably affect its dielectric behavior, electrical conductivity, and interfacial polarization, and hence have a considerable impact on the design of energy storage and electronic devices. The dielectric response of Fe3O4 is more often than not described in terms of the Maxwell–Wagner interfacial polarization and hopping of electrons. It is associated with higher dielectric permittivity at lower frequencies. Fe3O4 nanocrystals are associated with significant drawbacks, including higher dielectric loss, moderate electrical conductivity, particle agglomeration, and a lack of control over charge-carrier dynamics, thereby adversely affecting overall dielectric performance and energy storage efficiency. In a bid to address some of the foregoing drawbacks, cation substitution has been adopted as a desirable instrumental approach for modifying the electrical and magnetic properties of ferrites. Recent studies have aimed to improve ferrites' properties via substitution (doping) strategies. Doping a ferrite can modulate lattice strain, thereby selectively altering cation distribution and influencing magnetic interactions. This, in turn, affords some control over dielectric polarization, as well as conductivity, magnetization and anisotropy. Previous studies have shown that rare-earth doping has emerged as an effective approach to tailor ferrite characteristics to enhance its potential for next-generation applications. Ynasfi et al. investigated La3+ substitution in manganese ferrites (MnLaxFe2−xO4) and reported a decrease in lattice parameters, and similar variations in results have been reported by Z. A. Gilani et al. for Nd-doped Li–Ni nano-ferrites (LiNi0.5NdxFe2−xO4). Moreover, T. Kambre et al. reported enhanced dielectric behavior in yttrium-doped hematite, and T. Kamakshi et al. observed changes in the structural, morphological, and optical properties of Fe3O4 nanoparticles as a result of Ni doping. Furthermore, M. Aghazadeh et al. reported improved dielectric properties in Sm3+-doped Fe3O4 nanoparticles. Also, A. Neto et al. synthesized Fe3O4 doped with Ce4+, Co2+, Mn2+, and Ni2+via co-precipitation, while M. Zeng et al. prepared rare-earth-doped Fe3O4 nanoparticles (La3+, Ce3+, Dy3+) using a hydrothermal method, highlighting the effectiveness of doping in tailoring ferrite properties.

The rare-earth ion Gd3+ has attracted considerable attention due to its large ionic radius, high magnetic moment, high spin (SGd3+ = 7/2) and unique half-filled 4f7 electronic configuration. Even a small quantity of Gd can affect the ferrite properties. Several studies report that Gd incorporation significantly modifies the structural, magnetic, and electrical behavior of ferrite systems. Gd-doped NiFe2O4, Gd-doped BaTiO3 (ref. ) and Zn–Mg nano-spinel ferrites exhibit improved dielectric properties, while R. A. Pawar et al. reported enhanced functional characteristics in Gd3+-substituted Co–Zn ferrites. Similarly, C. V. Ramana et al. confirmed improved electrical conductivity in Gd-doped cobalt ferrites, highlighting the role of rare-earth ions in tuning charge transport. R. Bavandpour et al. also reported improved performance in modified CuFe2O4 nanoparticles. Furthermore, Sun et al. showed that La- and Gd-doping in Ni–Zn ferrites enhances high-frequency dielectric properties, confirming the effectiveness of rare-earth doping for improving polarization mechanisms. It has also been reported that Gd doping can improve dielectric properties, increase resistivity, and reduce dielectric and magnetic losses, although excessive doping may lead to structural disorder and reduced magnetic interactions.

In this context, the present work reports a systematic investigation of Gd-doped Fe3O4 nanocrystals synthesized via the co-precipitation method. The dopant Gd concentration was taken as 0 ≤ x ≤ 0.05 to systematically investigate its influence on the structural, dielectric, electrical and magnetic properties of Fe3O4 while maintaining the characteristic spinel framework. However, very limited literature is available on Gd-doped Fe3O4, and the reported studies are mainly confined to isolated investigations of structural, magnetic, and dielectric properties, lacking a comprehensive and correlated analysis. Thus, this work establishes a strong correlation between structure, defect chemistry, dielectric response, electrical conductivity, and magnetic behavior. The results highlight systematic modification of dielectric polarization and charge transport through controlled Gd substitution, thereby underscoring the potential of these materials for multifunctional electronic device applications.

Experimental details

Gd-doped magnetite nanocrystals were synthesized via a co-precipitation route in an aqueous medium, as shown in . The precipitation was carried out by the controlled addition of NH4OH (pH ≈ 11), with PEG-600 employed as a capping agent to regulate particle growth and prevent oxidation. X-ray diffraction analysis

The investigation of the Gd-substituted samples for phase formation and structural evolution was conducted using XRD at room temperature. XRD patterns of all samples are shown in . All the obtained diffraction peaks were indexed to the cubic inverse spinel structure of Fe3O4 with the space group Fd3̄m, confirming that the essential cubic crystal symmetry of magnetite is retained after Gd incorporation. The observed reflections around 2θ ≈ 30°, 35°, 43°, 54°, 57°, and 63° were indexed to (220), (311), (400), (422), (511) and (440) crystallographic planes, respectively, and were found to be consistent with the available JCPDS data of magnetite. This confirms the synthesis of a single-phase spinel ferrite with no impurity phases. The lack of additional peaks associated with Gd2O3 or any other iron oxide polymorphs suggests that the incorporation of Gd3+ ions into the Fe3O4 lattice occurs without the formation of a distinct phase.

The diffraction patterns show that as Gd content increased, the Bragg peaks shifted to lower diffraction angles. Thermal analysis

The TGA of the Gd-doped Fe3O4 samples presents a detailed account of the system's thermal stability and integrity and the effect on the phases within the system. The Gd-substituted magnetite nanocrystals' thermal stability and decomposition behavior were analyzed via simultaneous TG and DSC in the range of room temperature to 1100 °C. The combined weight-loss and heat-flow curves for all compositions are shown in . All samples exhibit a multi-step weight-loss behavior, indicating removal of physically adsorbed species, decomposition of residual precursors, and structural relaxation processes. Between room temperature and ∼150 °C, nearly all samples exhibited small weight loss. This weight loss results from the desorption of moisture physically adsorbed on the sample and the loss of surface-bound volatile species. This behavior is common in oxides and ferrites synthesized via wet chemical or solid-state methods. This weight loss indicates decreased surface adsorption for lower-Gd compositions. This behavior may be due to reduced porosity and surface-site coverage at lower Gd compositions. This stage is more pronounced for the GdF0 sample, indicating a higher surface adsorption capability due to its smaller crystallite size and larger surface area.

A second gradual weight-loss region, extending from approximately 150 °C to 400 °C, is associated with the removal of residual organic species (such as PEG or hydroxyl groups) and the decomposition of surface-bound intermediates originating from the synthesis route. The presence of rare-earth Gd3+ ions appears to slightly suppress the magnitude of this weight loss, suggesting improved thermal stability of the doped samples. The reduction in total mass loss with increasing Gd concentration indicates that Gd incorporation enhances structural compactness and reduces surface defects, thereby lowering the amount of loosely bound species. Beyond ∼400 °C, the weight-loss curves tend to stabilize, indicating that the ferrite structure becomes thermally stable and no major decomposition occurs at higher temperatures. The residual mass at high temperature is higher for Gd-substituted samples, which is consistent with the higher atomic mass of gadolinium and the improved structural integrity of doped ferrites. Overall, the total weight loss decreases with increasing Gd concentration, confirming enhanced thermal robustness upon Gd substitution. From the TG analysis, it can also be determined that Gd-doped magnetite exhibited improved thermal stabilization as the Gd-ion doping increased.

The stable mass plateau observed above ∼800 °C indicates that no significant further mass loss occurs, suggesting enhanced thermal stability of the final solid phase. Moreover, improved thermal stability was also observed to correlate with increased Gd concentrations. The substitution of Gd3+ ions modifies the local metal–oxygen bonding environment, leading to changes in bond lengths and increased local structural heterogeneity. This would also result in lower lattice energy and slower diffusion in the system during the thermal treatment. Thus, any oxygen-related defects present are likely due to thermal treatment, surface effects, or non-stoichiometry.

The exothermic peak observed in the DSC curve is attributed to crystallinity enhancement and grain growth resulting from atomic rearrangement and structural ordering. Additionally, thermal energy facilitates redistribution of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions between tetrahedral and octahedral sites toward an equilibrium configuration. The relaxation of lattice strain induced by the substitution of larger Gd3+ ions and the annihilation of structural and oxygen-related defects also contribute to the release of heat during this process. The DSC curve exhibits strong exothermic peaks at 535 °C, 583 °C, 647 °C, and 658 °C, shifting to higher temperatures with increasing concentration. The exothermic peak is reported to be associated with the formation of the α-Fe2O3 phase. The heat evolved in this exothermic process is attributed to enhanced crystallinity and grain growth, reordering of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions, and relaxation of lattice strain introduced by larger Gd3+ ions. It is reported that doping with rare-earth or transition-metal ions in ceramics introduces lattice defects such as oxygen vacancies, cation vacancies, and oxygen interstitials within the host lattice. These defects are reported to optimize multifunctional properties of significant technological interest.

The DSC curves provide further insight into the thermal events occurring during heating. A broad endothermic feature observed at lower temperatures corresponds to dehydration and removal of adsorbed species. At intermediate temperatures, distinct exothermic peaks are visible, marked in around 535 °C, 583 °C, 647 °C, and 658 °C for different compositions. These thermal events are associated with crystallite growth, phase stabilization, and ordering within the spinel lattice. The slight shift in the peak positions with Gd content suggests that the substitution of larger Gd3+ ions alters the lattice energy and influences the crystallization dynamics. In particular, the movement of exothermic peaks toward higher temperatures for higher Gd content indicates stronger lattice distortion, enhanced thermal stability and delayed structural rearrangement, which can be attributed to stronger metal–oxygen bonding and lattice distortion introduced by Gd3+ ions. The exothermic peaks observed at 535 °C, 583 °C, 647 °C, and 658 °C are attributed to thermally activated structural ordering processes, including defect annealing, lattice relaxation, and crystallite growth in Gd-doped spinel Fe3O4. The suppression of large exothermic transitions suggests that most of the structural ordering is already achieved during synthesis, and Gd incorporation stabilizes the inverse spinel framework against further transformation.

At higher temperatures, the broad exothermic background indicates continued grain coarsening, enhanced stabilization of the spinel phase, and a possible oxidation-induced phase transformation of magnetite toward maghemite or hematite (Fe3O4 → α-Fe2O3). Rare-earth substitution (Gd3+) introduces lattice strain and defect centers due to ionic size mismatch, thereby increasing the activation energy for structural ordering and shifting thermal events to higher temperatures. These interpretations are consistent with reported thermal analysis studies of doped spinel ferrites and oxide materials. The observed improvement in thermal stability is highly beneficial for dielectric and electrical applications where materials are subjected to elevated temperatures.

FT-IR analysis

FTIR was also used to study the Gd-substituted magnetite systems by investigating the resulting changes in local bonding and the framework. The mid-infrared region (400–4000 cm−1) of the GdF0, GdF1, GdF2, and GdF3 samples is shown in the FTIR spectra in . All samples exhibit the characteristic absorption spectra of spinel ferrites. Hence, the presence of the inverse spinel Fe3O4 structure after Gd substitution is confirmed. In general, spinel ferrites consist of two prominent metal–oxygen vibration bands. The first corresponds to the tetrahedral (A-site) metal–oxygen bonds, and the second corresponds to the octahedral (B-site) metal–oxygen bonds. The first band (ν1) is the intrinsic stretching vibration of a metal–oxygen bond and is observed in the range of ∼550–600 cm−1. The second band (ν2) is an octahedral metal–oxygen vibration and appears in the range of ∼400–450 cm−1.

In the present spectra, these two fundamental absorption bands are clearly visible for all compositions. The band in the higher wavenumber region corresponds to the Fe–O stretching vibration at tetrahedral sites, while the lower-frequency band arises from octahedral Fe–O/Gd–O vibrations. The presence of these two distinct bands confirms that the spinel ferrite structure remains intact after incorporation of Gd3+ ions. However, careful examination reveals slight shifts and broadening of the octahedral-site band with increasing Gd content. This behavior can be attributed to the substitution of larger-sized Gd3+ ions at octahedral sites, which alters the bond lengths and local symmetry at these sites. Because Gd3+ ions preferentially occupy octahedral positions in the spinel lattice, their incorporation leads to increased lattice distortion and modification of the metal–oxygen bond strength. Consequently, the octahedral vibration band broadens and shifts slightly, indicating increased structural disorder and local strain. Additional absorption features observed at higher wavenumbers are associated with surface and environmental effects. For all samples, a band in the region of ∼3400 cm−1 is associated with the O–H stretching vibration of water and surface hydroxyl groups. The band in the region of ∼1600–1650 cm−1 is associated with the bending of the absorbed water. The bands in the region of ∼1000–1400 cm−1 are the residual carbonates and/or the organic groups associated with the synthesis. The gradual reduction of these bands with increasing Gd content is associated with improved crystallinity and decreased surface impurities in the samples.

The shift in vibrational frequencies with increasing Gd concentration can be correlated with changes in bond length and force constants. Substitution by heavier Gd3+ ions increases the average metal–oxygen bond length and reduces vibrational frequency at octahedral sites. At the same time, the redistribution of Fe ions between tetrahedral and octahedral sites modifies the force constants of Fe–O bonds, leading to subtle changes in the position of the tetrahedral band. Such modifications to local bonding environments are known to influence dielectric polarization mechanisms and electron-hopping processes in ferrites. Since electrical conduction in magnetite is governed by electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions at octahedral sites, any change in bond length or lattice distortion can significantly affect dielectric relaxation and conductivity. It is also observed that as Gd content increases, the overall band intensity becomes slightly broader and less sharp, indicating increased local structural heterogeneity around the metal–oxygen bonds. This behavior is typical of rare-earth-substituted ferrites, where a large ionic-size mismatch introduces local strain and modifies the vibrational landscape of the lattice. Such structural distortion can enhance interfacial polarization and contribute to dielectric dispersion at lower frequencies. Therefore, the FTIR results corroborate the XRD and thermal analyses by confirming the formation of a stable inverse spinel structure with successful incorporation of Gd3+ ions into the Fe3O4 lattice.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis

The surface chemical composition and oxidation states of the elements present in undoped (GdF0) and Gd-doped (GdF3) samples were investigated using XPS, as shown in the survey spectra (). The spectra confirm the presence of Fe, O, Gd, and a small amount of adventitious carbon. The peak at ∼284.8 eV corresponds to the C1s signal, which originates from surface contamination and is commonly used for charge correction. The Fe2p spectrum exhibits two main peaks corresponding to Fe 2p3/2 and Fe 2p1/2 located at binding energies around ∼709–712 eV and ∼725 eV, respectively, with a spin–orbit splitting of approximately 13–14 eV. The Fe 2p3/2 peak reveals contributions from both Fe2+ and Fe3+ oxidation states, showing the mixed-valence nature of Fe ions characteristic of the magnetite structure. The Fe2+ component typically appears near ∼709.5 eV, while the Fe3+ component is centered around ∼711 eV. The coexistence of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions is a distinctive feature of Fe3O4 and is responsible for electron hopping between octahedral B sites, which significantly influences the electrical and dielectric properties.

The O 1s spectrum, as shown in (around 529–531 eV), comprises two main components. One peak is observed at a lower energy of ∼529.5 eV, corresponding to lattice oxygen (O2−) bonded to Fe in the spinel Fe–O/Gd–O framework. A second peak observed at ∼531 eV is attributed to surface-adsorbed oxygen species or defect-related oxygen environments. For the Gd-doped GdF3 samples, additional peaks corresponding to Gd core levels are observed, confirming the successful incorporation of Gd ions into the Fe3O4 lattice. The Gd 4d peaks generally appear in the range of ∼142–148 eV. These peaks are characteristic of Gd3+ oxidation states and indicate that Gd ions substitute Fe3+ sites in the spinel lattice without forming separate secondary phases. The introduction of Gd3+ ions in place of octahedral-sited Fe3+/Fe2+ ions results in lattice distortion and local structural disorder due to the ionic size mismatch. These defects modify the local electronic structure and influence the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio, thereby affecting electron hopping between octahedral sites. The presence of such defects and mixed-valence states plays a significant role in determining the electrical conductivity, dielectric response, and polarization mechanisms of Gd-doped Fe3O4.

FESEM analysis

Important insights into the particle morphology, agglomeration behavior and evolution of grain structure of the synthesized nanocrystals were obtained by FE-SEM analysis. The micrographs of the samples are presented in . The undoped sample exhibits a highly dense assembly of nearly spherical nanoparticles with noticeable agglomeration. The agglomeration is mainly attributed to the higher surface energy and strong magnetic dipole–dipole interactions between magnetite nanoparticles. The particle/grain sizes were estimated from these micrographs using ImageJ software. The estimated average particle/grain sizes were 9.60, 12.65, 14.15, and 16.90 nm for x = 0.000, 0.015, 0.025, and 0.050, respectively (Table S1 and Fig. S1). The particle/grain size increases steadily with increasing Gd content, consistent with the crystallite size estimated from X-ray diffraction. The nanoparticles appear relatively uniform and closely packed, forming clusters that create a porous network structure. Such morphology is commonly observed in nanoscale ferrites synthesized via wet-chemical routes, in which particle coalescence occurs during the drying and calcination stages.

For the Gd-doped compositions, the FE-SEM micrographs reveal a broadly similar microstructural behavior characterized by densely packed nanosized particles with noticeable agglomeration. The nanoparticles retain a predominantly quasi-spherical morphology and form interconnected clusters that are uniformly distributed across the surface. Compared with the undoped sample, the doped compositions show slightly enhanced particle aggregation, which can be attributed to the incorporation of larger Gd3+ ions that modify the nucleation and growth kinetics during synthesis. The presence of Gd ions introduces lattice strain and affects the diffusion processes during crystallization, leading to a marginal increase in grain connectivity and cluster formation. Despite this, the individual grains remain within the nanometer regime and exhibit relatively homogeneous distribution throughout the microstructure. Such agglomerated nanostructures are typical for ferrite nanoparticles due to strong magnetic dipole–dipole interactions and higher surface energy. The formation of interconnected nanoparticle clusters results in a large number of grain boundaries and interfacial regions, which become crucial in ferrite systems, where electrical conduction occurs via electron hopping. The presence of such morphologies and microstructures can play a crucial role in charge transport and interfacial polarization mechanisms in ferrite systems.

Dielectric analysis

The dielectric constant characterizes the polarization and charge-transport behavior of Gd-doped Fe3O4, where Gd3+ incorporation induces lattice distortion and structural disorder, which can influence space-charge polarization and charge transport at grain boundaries. The dielectric response of materials exhibits strong frequency and temperature dependence, which can be ascribed to microstructural effects and thermally activated charge carriers. For Fe3O4, this behavior is also influenced by the hopping of electrons between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions at octahedral sites and contributes significantly to the dipolar polarization.

Higher ε′ is the result of multi-layer capacitor-like, pore-dominated dielectric structures characterized by Maxwell–Wagner polarization at the grain and grain boundary interfaces, as described by Koop's model wherein ferrites are comprised of highly conductive grains and poorly conductive grain boundaries.ε′ is higher at lower frequencies because charge carriers accumulate at grain boundaries, creating space charge. Increased frequencies reduce ε′ due to the inability of charge carriers and dipoles to keep up with a rapidly changing field.ε′ mainly measures the influence of ionic and electronic polarization at higher frequencies and asymptotically approaches the value ε∞. Numerous studies have shown lower-frequency dielectric enhancement in ferrites, Fe3O4, and rare-earth-doped ferrites.

The rising temperature leads to an increase in ε′ as the mobility of the charge carriers and dipoles, and accordingly the polarization, increases. At lower frequencies, all types of polarization are present, and the rapid rise in dielectric constant with increasing temperature is mainly due to interfacial and dipolar polarizations, which are highly temperature-dependent. At higher frequencies, the temperature dependence becomes insignificant as only the intrinsic polarizability is accounted for in the dielectric constant. The variations in the magnitude and dispersion of ε′ is associated with the increase in Gd content. Upon substituting Gd3+ ions into octahedral sites, the Fe3+/Fe2+ ion sites create lattice defects such as distortion and oxygen vacancies as a charge compensation mechanism. These defect states can act as localized polarization centers, strengthening space-charge polarization at lower doping levels. This ion substitution also influences electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions. These, along with the lattice distortion and defect states, become centers for further polarization, thereby strengthening space-charge polarization and modifying the dielectric response based on Maxwell–Wagner's interfacial polarization and the mechanisms of hopping conduction.

The ε′′ is higher at lower frequencies because of space-charge polarization and the energy dissipated along the grain boundaries. At higher frequencies, ε′′ decreases as the charge carriers can no longer keep pace with the alternating field, and the polarization of the grains becomes predominant. Further, dielectric loss decreases with increasing Gd concentration across all compositions and temperatures. The ε′′ decreases with increasing frequency, indicating that space-charge polarization is the dominant mechanism at lower frequencies. This is indicative of the thermally activated conduction mechanism, which is facilitated by electron hopping between Fe3+ and Fe2+ ions at the octahedral sites of the spinel lattice, especially in the inverse Fe3O4.

The dielectric loss (tan δ) and quality factor (Q) were also assessed to evaluate the overall energy-dissipation capability of the samples. The frequency-dependent variations of tan δ (Fig. S2) and Q (Fig. S3) for all compositions are presented and discussed in the SI (Sections 2.2 and 2.3). The dielectric loss (tan δ = ε′′/ε′) is related to the quality factor as Q = 1/tan δ. Thus, a lower tanδ and higher Q indicate reduced energy dissipation and improved dielectric quality. Both parameters show a dependence on Gd doping concentration, indicating an influence on the polarization and charge-transport processes in Fe3O4. In addition, both strongly depend on frequency and temperature. The reduction in dielectric loss with increasing Gd concentration can be correlated with changes in the nanocrystal structure. Although dopant Gd3+ substitution may introduce local lattice distortion, there is an increase in crystallite size, together with a significant decrease in microstrain and dislocation density. The increase in crystallite size may reduce the relative contribution of grain boundaries, whereas the reduction in microstrain and dislocation density indicates decreased structural disorder and defect-associated charge trapping. Accordingly, the contribution of space-charge & interfacial polarization and associated dielectric dissipation is reduced with increasing Gd content. In addition, Gd incorporation may alter Fe2+/Fe3+ ratios, thereby influencing charge-carrier hopping and polarization processes.

In Fe3O4-based systems, dielectric loss is strongly associated with electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ at octahedral (B) sites. The substitution of Gd introduces lattice distortion and defect-related structural disorder that can modify space-charge polarization and the hopping pathways of charge carriers at moderate concentrations. At the same time, excessive doping may increase structural disorder and carrier trapping. The broad frequency dispersion and absence of distinct relaxation peaks confirm non-Debye dielectric behavior. It is common to observe heterogeneous conduction and grain boundary-controlled dielectric loss in rare-earth-doped magnetite and spinel ferrites. Since the slope of ε′′ is less steep at increasing frequencies for the doped samples, this shows that only fast mechanisms of electronic or ionic polarization are present and contribute very little to dielectric loss. Also, the inability of charge carriers to accumulate at grain boundaries reduces ε′′. With increasing temperature, enhanced hopping conduction accelerates relaxation processes, decreasing relaxation time and shifting relaxation toward higher frequencies. This non-Debye behavior reflects heterogeneous conduction dominated by grain boundaries in Gd-doped Fe3O4. Therefore, the decrease in dielectric loss is attributed to the combined impact of crystallite growth, reduced lattice disorder, modified grain-boundary contribution, defect chemistry, and thermally activated charge-carrier dynamics in Fe3O4 samples.

Electrical conductivity analysis

The behavior of electrical conductivity (ω) over a wide temperature range is shown in for each composition. For all compositions, conductivity exhibits the characteristic behavior of a disordered ferrimagnetic oxide, with a plateau at lower frequencies and a rapidly varying region at higher frequencies. Hence, conductivity in the AC sense reflects the dissipation of energy and movement of charge carriers within the sample. In the case of spinel ferrites Fe3O4, it is stated that electrical conduction is mainly due to the hopping of electrons between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions at octahedral sites and is greatly dependent on the presence of defects, grain boundaries, and impurities.

In all samples, the curves show a plateau at lower frequencies corresponding to DC conductivity, followed by a steep increase in conductivity with increasing frequency. Beyond a characteristic frequency, all samples show an increase in σac with increasing frequency. This phenomenon can be described by the Jonscher's universal power law given in .

Here, σdc is the direct current contribution, and Aωs is the frequency-dependent AC contribution, with A being a temperature-dependent constant and s being a constant between 0 and 1. This contribution is frequency-dependent due to short-range transport of charge carriers, which is attributed to polaron hopping and defect-mediated conduction. In the present work, the Gd-doped samples in particular exhibit a dominant σdc term in most of the measured frequency range, with the σac term present only to a small extent. This is associated with the increased electron scattering and charge localization in the Gd-doped samples, which is believed to diminish the AC component. At lower frequencies, the conductivity is almost constant and equal to σdc. This is associated with the restricted mobility of charge carriers, as grain boundaries act as barriers that limit ion hopping. In this range, the charge carriers do not have sufficient energy to overcome the potential barriers associated with the hopping between the Fe3+ and Fe2+ ions in the vicinity of the grain boundary. In this case, it is anticipated that the transport of charge carriers will be governed by the hopping of electrons between the Fe3+ and Fe2+ ions located at the octahedral sites. In this region, long-range charge transport predominantly involves electron hopping between Fe3+ and Fe2+ ions. These ions are located on the octahedral sites in the inverse spinel structure of Fe3O4. The conductivity exhibits dispersion at higher frequencies, which can be explained by Koop's theory. According to Koop's phenomenological model, the higher σac at higher frequencies indicates that the material contains more active conducting grains than at lower frequencies. AC conduction in the literature is explained by small-polaron hopping, defect-induced charge-carrier localization, and grain-boundary interactions, all of which are consistent with our current analysis. The increase in σdc with heating indicates hopping conduction driven by thermal activation and is consistent with the Arrhenius equation. The increase in temperature enhances charge-carrier tunneling, thereby increasing the AC and DC conductivities.

When Gd-doped samples of Fe3O4 are considered, the AC conductivity shows the same frequency dependence that is also seen in the undoped samples of Fe3O4. Although Gd incorporation can increase local carrier scattering and localization, the overall conductivity increases, as indicated by the reduced activation energy. The enhanced conductivity is therefore attributed to modification of the charge-transport pathways and Fe2+/Fe3+ hopping rather than to a simple increase in free-carrier concentration. These defect states and oxygen vacancies provide hopping sites for charge carriers, thus increasing conductivity. These effects, together with the coexistence of Fe2+/Fe3+ ions and grain-boundary conduction, can facilitate thermally activated hopping of charge carriers. The observed conductivity therefore supports a defect- and microstructure-influenced hopping mechanism, without requiring a specific concentration of oxygen vacancies. Frequency exponent

The frequency exponent, s, which is derived from the universal power law, provides valuable insight into the conduction mechanism of the ferrite system in question. The compositional dependence of the frequency exponent s is shown in . In the samples, the dominant trend is that the frequency exponent s increases with increasing temperature. This is characteristic behavior of the non-overlapping small-polaron tunneling (NSPT) conduction mechanism. In the NSPT mechanism, charge carriers can jump between two localized states separated by a potential barrier. An increase in temperature provides charge carriers with more potential energy, thereby reducing the barrier and increasing the probability of jumping between states. These defects lower the effective hopping barrier and improve the thermally activated transport of charge carriers. The presence of grain boundaries and structural disorder in the nanocrystalline ferrite system further promotes hopping by providing trapping and release sites for charge carriers. VSM analysis

The magnetic characteristics of the synthesized Gd-doped magnetite nanocrystals were studied using vibrating sample magnetometry and are shown in . The hysteresis curves show narrow loops with lower coercivity and remanence, indicating soft magnetic behavior. The obtained magnetic parameters are summarized in . The lower coercivity reflects relatively easy magnetization reversal and weak magnetic anisotropy, which are characteristic of soft magnetic nanoferrites. The magnetic behavior of ferrites can be described using Néel's two-sublattices model. In this model, the distribution of cations in the tetrahedral (A) and octahedral (B) lattices governs the magnetic behavior, and the A–B superexchange interactions are dominant over the A–A and B–B interactions. The replacement of Fe ions with Gd ions affects these A–B interactions, and as a consequence, the overall magnetization of the ferrite is affected.

The saturation magnetization of synthesized Fe3O3 nanoparticles was observed to be similar to that reported in previous studies. All the samples exhibited lower remanence and coercivity, consistent with soft ferrimagnetic behavior. The saturation magnetization shows a non-monotonic variation with Gd concentration. Ms initially increases from 50.0 emu g−1 for GdF0 to 55.0 emu g−1 for GdF1, followed by a decrease to 46.0 emu g−1 for GdF2 and a slight increase to 48.0 emu g−1 for GdF3. The initial increase may be associated with changes in cation distribution and magnetic exchange interactions at lower Gd concentrations, whereas the subsequent decrease may be related to lattice distortion, surface spin disorder, and weakened Fe–O–Fe exchange interactions at higher Gd concentrations. Thus, Gd incorporation modifies rather than decreases the magnetization of the Fe3O4 nanocrystals. It has been observed that the Ms of doped samples (GdF2 and GdF3) is lower than that of bare Fe3O4. This is attributed to the lack of surface ion coordination, which creates spin disorder or surface spin canting. It has been suggested that the decrease in Ms of doped samples compared to bare samples is due to the antiferromagnetic coupling of dopant cations to the octahedral site cations of the Fe3O4 lattice. Previous studies also reported a decrease in saturation Ms when Gd was substituted with Gd-doped magnetite nanoparticles.

In addition to cation substitution effects, several microstructural factors influence the magnetic behavior. The nanoscale particle size and the high grain-boundary fraction introduce surface spin disorder and canting of magnetic moments, thereby reducing the effective magnetization by weakening long-range magnetic order within the nanoparticles. Moreover, structural defects, lattice strain, and defect-related local environments associated with Gd substitution may modify exchange interactions and contribute to variations in magnetic parameters. Accordingly, all samples exhibit soft ferrimagnetic behavior, with lower remanence and coercivity. Coercivity is a measure of the magnetic field necessary to demagnetize a ferromagnetic substance. Lower coercivity values indicate that the samples exhibit soft magnetism and support the presence of reduced magnetocrystalline anisotropy due to spin coupling between Fe3+/Fe2+ and Gd3+. This promotes domain wall motion, and a lower coercivity indicates that magnetization reversal occurs at a small magnetic field, which is typical of soft ferrite materials. The remanent magnetization (Mr) is similarly lower, resulting in a lower squareness ratio (Mr/Ms), indicating the presence of a large number of magnetic particles in the domain. A squareness ratio of Mr/Ms < 0.5 indicates the presence of multidomain structures. The results indicate that a small percentage of Gd substitution is sufficient to improve magnetism slightly, whereas higher percentages yield a magnetically decoupled Fe3O4 lattice with reduced magnetism.

Perspective: energy-related device applications

The structural, electrical, and magnetic studies indicate that the synthesized Gd-doped magnetite nanocrystals exhibit several features beneficial for the development of energy-related devices. The nanocrystalline structure provides a significant fraction of grain boundaries and interfaces, which can contribute to interfacial polarization. Based on the Maxwell–Wagner polarization theory, under an alternating electric field, these grain boundaries, as a result of charge accumulation, increase the effective dielectric permittivity. Improved polarization is a key factor in dielectric capacitors and other energy-storage devices, as it can considerably enhance their storage capability ().

The analysis of electrical conductivity confirms that charge transport occurs via thermally activated electron hopping between Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions at octahedral B sites. This conduction mechanism embodies a balance between electrical and dielectric conductivity. Furthermore, Gd3+ substitution introduces noticeable lattice distortion and structural disorder, which, together with grain-boundary heterogeneity, can promote dipolar and interfacial polarization, both of which are key to improving charge mobility and dielectric polarization, providing an added advantage in the use of this material for capacitors, sensors, and other electronic devices. The inherent thermal stability and the soft magnetic characteristics of these materials further enhance their suitability for use in real devices. Good thermal stability ensures dependable operation as conditions change, while soft magnetic behavior with lower coercivity helps minimize losses and interference. Thus, the observed combination of nanostructure, dielectric polarization, charge transport, thermal stability, and magnetic characteristics provides a basis for exploring these materials in future dielectric, energy-related, and multifunctional electronic devices.

Conclusion

Gd-doped magnetite nanocrystals were successfully synthesized, and their dielectric and magnetic properties were systematically investigated. The XRD analysis confirms the retention of the Fe3O4 cubic spinel structure without any secondary phases even after the addition of Gd. With increasing Gd content, the crystallite size increased from 7.24 to 14.81 nm, whereas the microstrain and dislocation density decreased from 13.49 × 10−3 to 6.47 × 10−3 and 19.08 × 1015 to 4.56 × 1015 m−2, respectively, indicating reduced structural disorder. Gd-doped magnetite structures also exhibit enhanced electron hopping due to the coexistence of Fe oxidation states. The dielectric results revealed a frequency- and temperature-dependent response, governed mainly by interfacial polarization and thermally activated charge-carrier hopping. The dielectric loss decreased with increasing frequency and Gd concentration, indicating reduced dielectric dissipation. The AC conductivity and frequency-exponent analysis further supported a thermally activated hopping mechanism primarily associated with Fe2+/Fe3+ charge transfer. The magnetic measurements revealed soft ferrimagnetic behavior with lower coercivity (80–110 Oe) and finite remanent magnetization (7.2–9.8 emu g−1). The combined dielectric, electrical, thermal, and magnetic characteristics indicate that Gd-doped Fe3O4 nanocrystals may have potential for future dielectric, energy-related, and electronic applications.

A limitation of the present study is that independent synthesis replicates and repeated measurements were not performed; therefore, sample-to-sample reproducibility and statistical uncertainty could not be independently assessed. Future studies involving independent batch synthesis and repeated measurements, together with direct device-level evaluation of energy-storage performance, are needed to further establish the reproducibility and practical applicability of these materials.
