An antisense oligonucleotide targeting STK25 as a long-acting therapeutic candidate for early MASLD
Authors: Ao Dong, Na Li, Xiping Lang, Shunkai Chen, Yifeng Qin, Yuhang Wang, Haisheng Wang, Miaomiao Guo, Aomei Yan, Kai Song, Jingyi Bi, Guiping Ren, Fan Lai, Juan Yang, Yunkun Dang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has become the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide and a leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality. Insulin resistance and dysregulated hepatic lipid metabolism are key pathological mechanisms for simple hepatic steatosis, yet no specific drug is currently approved. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are single-stranded nucleotide drugs with high target specificity and long-lasting activity, representing a promising therapeutic approach for chronic metabolic diseases. Here, we report a novel ASO candidate targeting serine/threonine kinase 25 (STK25), a lipid droplet-associated kinase implicated in MASLD pathogenesis. The ASO (S-10c) potently suppressed STK25 expression in multiple human cell lines. Liver-specific delivery was achieved through N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) conjugation, generating GS-10c. In a high-fat diet-induced early MASLD mouse model, GS-10c significantly improved insulin sensitivity, reduced hepatic lipid accumulation, and lowered body weight, exhibiting efficacy similar to resmetirom, the only FDA-approved treatment for metabolic steatohepatitis. A single injection achieved >50% hepatic Stk25 knockdown for over 35 days, demonstrating durable activity. Importantly, GS-10c was designed to avoid all known SNPs, ensuring consistent efficacy across genetically diverse populations. Notably, an alternative ASO differing by only 4 nt showed inconsistent silencing and safety, suggesting the unique therapeutic precision and translational robustness of GS-10c.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, MASLD, antisense oligonucleotides, ASOs, resmetirom, hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, serine/threonine kinase 25, STK25, patient adherence, prolonged action

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome and has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide.1 This recently adopted nomenclature replaces non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), more accurately reflecting its strong association with cardiometabolic risk factors such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and insulin resistance.2,3 Epidemiological studies estimated that approximately 24% of the global adult population is affected, underlining MASLD as a major public health concern in hepatology.4,5 The disease encompasses a spectrum ranging from simple hepatic steatosis to its progressive subtype, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH; formerly termed non-alcoholic steatohepatitis or NASH).6,7 Patients with MASH are at increased risk of progression to cirrhosis, hepatic failure, and even hepatocellular carcinoma, highlighting the clinical severity of the disease.8

Insulin resistance is one of the key drivers of MASLD, with hepatic lipid metabolism dysfunction as the core pathological mechanism, including enhanced de novo lipogenesis, impaired β-oxidation, and defective secretion of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) in hepatocytes.9,10 To date, no drug has been approved specifically for early MASLD, characterized by simple steatosis. Current management relies on lifestyle interventions (diet, weight loss, and physical activity) combined with treatment of metabolic comorbidities, generally by controlling blood glucose and lipid profiles.11,12 Metformin remains the first-line therapy for type 2 diabetes, particularly in overweight or obese patients, while statins, the most commonly prescribed lipid-lowering drugs, effectively reduce low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C).13,14 As the first targeted therapy for MASH with fibrosis, resmetirom, a selective thyroid hormone receptor β (THR-β) agonist, showed benefits in reducing hepatic steatosis and attenuating fibrosis progression in clinical trials.15,16,17,18 However, apart from generally mild side effects, metformin, statins, and resmetirom all require daily administration or combination therapy regimens, which undermine long-term adherence.19,20,21 For example, real-world data on statins show 12-month persistence rates of only 58% in high-risk patients, with non-adherence exceeding 40% in those on complex regimens.22 In elderly populations, polypharmacy further compounds these issues, with medication error rates surpassing 25%.23 Together, these limitations underscore the difficulty of sustaining long-term adherence to daily oral therapies for chronic diseases such as MASLD.

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are chemically modified single-stranded oligonucleotides, typically 18–30 nt in length, that regulate gene expression at the RNA level in a sequence-dependent manner.24 Among them, gapmer ASOs are the most widely used type for gene silencing. Gapmer ASOs consist of a central DNA “gap” flanked by chemically modified nucleotides, which form DNA/RNA duplexes that induce RNase H-mediated degradation of target mRNA.25,26 Owing to this mechanism, ASOs can in principle be designed to target almost any gene, greatly expanding the scope of druggable targets.27 Multiple chemical modifications further enhance their therapeutic potential: backbone modifications such as phosphorothioate (PS) linkages improve nuclease resistance and circulation time, while sugar modifications at the 2′ position, including 2′-O-methoxyethyl (2′-MOE) and constrained ethyl (cEt), enhance affinity, specificity, stability, and tolerability.28 For liver diseases, conjugation with N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) exploits the rapid recycling of the hepatocyte-specific asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR), enabling efficient and selective hepatic delivery.29 These innovations together confer high target specificity, prolonged pharmacological activity, and dosing intervals that may extend to months.30,31 Multiple ASO drugs already received regulatory approval in recent years, underscoring the growing impact of ASO therapeutics across genetic and metabolic disorders.32

Serine/threonine kinase 25 (STK25), a member of the Ste20 kinase family, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of MASLD.33,34 As a lipid droplet-associated protein, STK25 colocalizes with coating proteins such as PLIN2 and SNAP23.35,36 STK25 disrupts lipid turnover by competing with adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) for lipid droplet binding.33 This competition reduces lipolysis, promotes intracellular lipid accumulation, and accelerates disease progression.33 Previous studies have shown that silencing STK25 confers broad metabolic benefits, including improved insulin sensitivity, enhanced glucose tolerance, and restored mitochondrial function.37,38,39 Moreover, an ASO specifically targeting mouse Stk25 has been shown to reduce hepatic steatosis and attenuate inflammatory infiltration in mouse obese models,40,41 validating the concept of STK25-targeted ASO therapy as a potential treatment for MASLD. However, the previously reported ASO sequence is not conserved between mouse and human STK25, limiting its translational potential (Figure S1A).

Here, we employed a gapmer ASO design platform to identify a lead candidate, GS-10c, which harbors no identified single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) and exhibited robust silencing of STK25 in three different human cell lines. Owing to its sequence homology to mouse Stk25, GS-10c was further evaluated in obese mouse models. The compound demonstrated therapeutic benefits comparable to resmetirom in ameliorating hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance. Notably, GS-10c exhibited a markedly prolonged duration of action, sustaining efficacy for over 1 month after a single administration, thereby highlighting its promise as a durable ASO therapeutic candidate for early MASLD.

Results

S-10c efficiently silences STK25 and reduces lipid accumulation in human cell lines

To identify STK25-targeting ASO candidates suitable for clinical translation, we applied the ClinASO design tool (www.gapmerasodesigner.com), which integrates multiple parameters including assessments of ASO secondary structure, off-target potential, RNase H cleavage preference, SNPs, and sequence homology with desired animal models.

We first selected six top-ranked ASO sequences generated by ClinASO, each targeting distinct regions of STK25. These ASOs were synthesized in a 5-10–5 gapmer configuration with a full PS backbone and 2′-MOE modifications at both 5-nt wings (Figure S2A). Each ASO was transfected into HepG2 cells at 15 and 44 nM, and STK25 mRNA levels were quantified by RT-qPCR. All candidates induced dose-dependent transcript degradation (Figure S2B). Among them, S-1, targeting exon 2 of STK25, exhibited the strongest silencing effect at lower concentrations (Figure S2B).

To further refine the targeting region, four additional ASOs were designed near the S-1 binding site using ClinASO (Figure S2C). This screening identified S-10 as the most potent candidate (Figure S2D). To enhance its potency and stability, the S-10 sequence was chemically optimized to a 3–10–3 configuration, retaining a PS backbone and incorporating cEt modifications at both 3-nt wings, yielding four derivatives (S-10c to S-13c) (Figure S2E). When tested in HepG2 cells (15 and 44 nM), all four ASOs demonstrated robust STK25 silencing, with S-10c exhibiting the greatest knockdown efficiency across both doses (Figure S2F). It is of note that all these ASO candidates are free of annotated SNPs (dbSNP Build 155),42 ensuring broad coverage in patient populations.

In human HepG2, Huh7, and HeLa cells, S-10c achieved robust and dose-dependent suppression of STK25, with reductions of 44.68% at 15 nM and 67.65% at 44 nM in HepG2 and 59%–91% knockdown in Huh7 and HeLa cells (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). Western analysis confirmed corresponding decreases in STK25 protein levels (Figure 1C). Given its sequence homology to mouse Stk25 (Mus musculus), S-10c was further tested in mouse AML12 hepatocytes, where it achieved 59.9% and 76.76% knockdown at 15 and 44 nM, respectively (p < 0.0001) (Figure 1B). These results suggest that S-10c induces potent and consistent silencing of STK25 in different human and mouse cell lines.

To evaluate functional consequences, we assessed lipid accumulation in oleic acid (OA)-treated HepG2 cells. Following OA treatment, intracellular lipid droplets markedly increased compared with untreated controls (negative controls [NCs]). Notably, S-10c treatment (OA+S-10c) significantly reduced lipid droplet content compared with scrambled control ASO (OA+scramble), as shown by BODIPY staining (Figure 1D). The quantification results of lipid droplet area also demonstrate the inhibitory effect of S-10c on lipid droplet accumulation (−70.44%, p < 0.01; Figure 1E). The downregulation of STK25 expression further indicates that the reduction in lipid droplets should result from S-10c-mediated STK25 knockdown (Figures 1F and 1G).

To determine whether STK25 silencing can alter cellular energy metabolism, we measured mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and fatty acid oxidation (FAO). As expected, the OA+scramble treatment exhibited a lower OCR compared to the NC group, whereas OA+S-10c enhanced oxygen consumption compared to OA+scramble ASOs (Figure 1H). Consistently, FAO assays demonstrated a 50.5% increase (p < 0.001) in the OA+S-10c treatment compared to OA+scramble (Figure 1I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that S-10c is a potent STK25-targeting ASO that can effectively reduce lipid accumulation in human hepatocyte models, indicating its potential as a therapeutic candidate for simple steatosis.

GS-10c demonstrates potent and liver-specific silencing of Stk25 in mice

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of S-10c in vivo, we generated a GalNAc-conjugated derivative, GS-10c, by attaching a modified GalNAc (GN3) to the 5′ end of S-10c (Figure 2A), thus enabling selective delivery to hepatocytes via ASGPR-mediated uptake.43,44 Wild-type C57BL/6 male mice received a single subcutaneous injection of GS-10c at doses ranging from 1.25 to 30 mg/kg, which were sacrificed after 3 days, and liver tissues were collected for analysis.

Safety was initially assessed by measuring serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels. These biomarkers remained comparable across all treatment groups (Figures 2B and 2C), indicating that GS-10c did not induce acute liver toxicity in mice.

Dose-response analysis revealed progressive, liver-specific knockdown of Stk25 mRNA, with significant reductions observed at doses ≥2.5 mg/kg (2.5 mg/kg: 34.2%, p < 0.01; 5 mg/kg: 40.6%, p < 0.01; 10 mg/kg: 54.0%, p < 0.0001; 20 mg/kg: 71.8%, p < 0.0001; and 30 mg/kg: 73.8%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 2D). Through curve fitting and simulation, the calculated IC50 of GS-10c on Stk25 mRNA was determined to be 7.097 mg/kg (Figure S3A). Western blot analyses confirmed consistent protein-level suppression, with significant knockdown observed at doses ≥20 mg/kg (−61.9%, p < 0.01) (Figure 2E).

To confirm the selective delivery of the GalNAc-conjugated ASO, we generated a fluorescently labeled compound by conjugating Cy3 to GS-10c (GS-10c-Cy3). Wild-type C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously administered GS-10c-Cy3 (20 mg/kg), with a scrambled ASO (no Cy3) serving as a control. 3 days post-injection, tissue distribution analysis revealed prominent accumulation of GS-10c-Cy3 in the liver and kidneys, with minimal signal detected in the heart, pancreas, and lungs (Figures S3B and S3C). Functionally, GS-10c-Cy3 induced an 81.9% reduction compared to the scramble in hepatic Stk25 expression (p < 0.01) (Figure S3D). Immunofluorescence analysis using hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α) as a hepatocyte-specific marker revealed GS-10c-Cy3 fluorescence in proximity to HNF4α-positive cells. Collectively, these data indicate effective uptake of GS-10c into hepatocytes (Figure S3E).

Given the liver-specific delivery conferred by GalNAc, we next examined tissue specificity of GS-10c. In the kidney, a modest reduction in mRNA was detected only at 20 mg/kg (−34.5%, p < 0.01), without significant changes at the protein level (Figures 2F and 2G). In the heart, all GS-10c dosages showed no significant decrease in Stk25 expression at either the mRNA or protein level (Figures 2H and 2I). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that GS-10c achieves potent, dose-dependent silencing of Stk25 in the liver, with minimal off-target effects in extrahepatic tissues. The absence of hepatotoxicity at the highest tested dose (30 mg/kg) indicates its favorable safety profile and strong potential as a liver-targeted therapeutic candidate for MASLD.

GS-10c and resmetirom manifest similar effects on body weight in HFD mice

To investigate the therapeutic efficacy of GS-10c in vivo, we employed a high-fat-diet (HFD)-induced MASLD model that recapitulates key pathological features of early MASLD.45 Given that no drug is specifically approved for simple hepatic steatosis, we chose resmetirom as a benchmark to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of GS-10c. C57BL/6 male mice were induced with an HFD for 13 weeks to establish simple hepatic steatosis and then randomized into three treatment groups (n = 6–8 per group): HFD control (HFD-NC), HFD with GS-10c (HFD-GS-10c), and HFD with resmetirom (HFD-resme). Age-matched mice maintained on standard diet served as NCs (SD-NCs) (Figure 3A).

HFD mice received either subcutaneous injection of GS-10c (20 mg/kg weekly for 4 weeks, followed by 10 mg/kg weekly for 4 weeks) or daily oral gavage (I.G.) of resmetirom (3 mg/kg) over an 8-week treatment period as previously documented.46 To control the potential daily gavage stress in resmetirom-treated mice, all groups were subjected to daily oral gavage of solvent throughout the treatment period. Body weight was monitored weekly. Both HFD-GS-10c and HFD-resme mice exhibited progressive and significant decline in body weight over the 8-week course (Figure 3B), yet we observed no significant difference in weight loss between these groups (Figure S4A). In contrast, HFD-NC and SD-NC groups maintained a relatively stable body weight, which may partly reflect the daily gavage procedure (Figures 3B and S4B). A transient drop in body weight was observed in all groups at weeks 7 and 8, most likely due to the fasting required for glucose tolerance tests and insulin resistance tests (Figure 3B). Together, these results indicate that GS-10c exhibits efficacy similar to resmetirom in attenuating HFD-induced weight gain.

To confirm that the reduced body weight in GS-10c-treated mice resulted from Stk25 silencing, we sacrificed the mice at the endpoint of the experiments and assessed Stk25 expression in the liver. HFD feeding induced a modest increase in Stk25 protein (Figures 3D and 3E) without significant changes at the mRNA level (Figure 3C). As expected, GS-10c treatment resulted in marked knockdown of Stk25, with a 75.7% reduction in mRNA (p < 0.0001; Figure 3C) and a 61.3% reduction at the protein level (p < 0.001; Figures 3D, 3E, S4C, and S4D). Consistent with HFD-induced models, ALT/AST levels were slightly but significantly elevated in HFD-NC mice. Both HFD-resme and HFD-GS-10c mice showed significant decreases in AST levels, whereas ALT levels remained similar to controls, most likely due to the less severe phenotype associated with simple steatosis (Figure 3F). Together, these findings demonstrate that GS-10c achieves robust Stk25 knockdown, mitigates HFD-induced weight gain, and maintains hepatic safety parameters similar to resmetirom.

GS-10c reduces hepatic steatosis and downregulates lipogenic pathways

To confirm the therapeutic effect of GS-10c in improving hepatic lipid metabolism, we first performed histological analysis. Compared with SD-NCs, HFD-NCs exhibited extensive lipid droplet deposition. In contrast, GS-10c- and resmetirom-treated mice displayed markedly reduced and dispersed lipid droplets, comparable to SD-NCs (Figure 3G). Quantification of oil red staining confirmed significant reductions in hepatic lipid content (resmetirom: −42.8%, p < 0.001; GS-10c: −43.7%, p < 0.001) (Figures 3H, S4E, and S4F). Consistently, compared with HFD-NC mice, GS-10c or resmetirom treatment significantly decreased hepatic triglyceride levels, approaching levels observed in SD-NCs (Figure 3I), whereas GS-10c treatment had a limited effect on blood lipid parameters (Figures S4G–S4J). Together, these data suggest that Stk25 silencing by GS-10c could ameliorate hepatic steatosis by lowering lipid droplet accumulation.

We next evaluated the NAFLD activity score (NAS) component across all groups, including hepatic steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning. Compared with the SD-NC group, HFD-NC mice exhibited increases in all three parameters (Figure 3J). Both GS-10c and resmetirom significantly improved steatosis (resmetirom: −91.7%, p < 0.0001; GS-10c: −66.7%, p < 0.01), inflammation (resmetirom: −54.6%, p < 0.05; GS-10c: −63.7%, p < 0.01), and ballooning (resmetirom: −81.8%, p < 0.001; GS-10c: −81.8%, p < 0.001) (Figure 3J). No significant differences in fibrosis were observed among the HFD-NC and GS-10c- and resmetirom-treated groups, most likely due to the limited duration of HFD treatment (Figure 3K). To further assess fibrosis, we performed RT-qPCR analysis. As expected, Col1a1 and Vim expression was significantly increased in HFD-NC mice compared with SD-NCs (p < 0.001), indicating activation of fibrotic pathways (Figure S4K). Both treatments reduced these markers, with resmetirom showing a greater level of reduction (Col1a1: −93.9%; Vim: −95.2%; p < 0.0001), while GS-10c produced moderate but significant decreases (Col1a1: −52.6%, p < 0.001; Vim: −53.7%, p < 0.01). Together, these data suggest that Stk25 silencing by GS-10c may potentially suppress fibrogenic pathways, though its impact remains limited under the current experimental conditions.

To confirm the therapeutic effects of GS-10c at the molecular level, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on liver tissues (n = 3 per group). Differential expression analysis revealed significant downregulation of Stk25 in HFD-GS-10c mice compared with HFD-NCs (log2 fold change = −1.448, p < 0.0001) (Figure 3L). In contrast, Stk25 expression remained unchanged in resmetirom-treated samples, while genes associated with thyroid hormone signaling, including Dio1 and Stard4, were upregulated (Figure 3M). Pathway enrichment analyses revealed that GS-10c treatment led to significant downregulation of lipid metabolism-related pathways, as identified by both Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses (Figures S5A and S5B), consistent with the expected effects of Stk25 silencing. Similarly, resmetirom treatment also downregulated lipid metabolism pathways (Figures S5C and S5D), with partial overlap between the two treatments (Figure 3N). In addition, resmetirom showed enrichment of sterol and steroid metabolism pathways among upregulated genes (Figures 3N and S6), consistent with its mechanism as a THR-β agonist. Together, these findings suggest that GS-10c, by silencing Stk25, effectively mitigates hepatic steatosis by modulating hepatic lipid metabolism.

GS-10c and resmetirom improve glucose regulation in HFD mice

Insulin resistance, the major driver of early MASLD, represents a key challenge in disease management.47 Beyond its role in hepatic lipid metabolism, STK25 has also been implicated in systemic glucose homeostasis.48,49 To evaluate whether GS-10c improves glucose regulation, we performed intraperitoneal glucose tolerance tests (IPGTTs) in HFD-fed mice. Before glucose administration, both HFD-resme and HFD-GS-10c groups showed lower fasting blood glucose levels than HFD-NC mice, approaching levels observed in SD-fed controls (Figure 4A). Following glucose challenge, all groups reached peak glucose at approximately 30 min. Compared with HFD-NC mice, GS-10c-treated mice showed lower peak and faster return to baseline, indicating improved glucose clearance (Figure 4B). Quantification of the area under the curve (AUC) further confirmed improved glucose tolerance in the GS-10c group (Figures 4B and S7A).

We next performed intraperitoneal insulin tolerance tests (IPITTs). As observed in the IPGTTs, both HFD-resme and HFD-GS-10c groups showed lower fasting blood glucose levels than HFD-NC mice prior to insulin administration (Figure 4C). Following insulin injection, blood glucose levels decreased in all groups and reached a nadir at approximately 30 min. However, HFD-NC mice exhibited a rapid rebound in glucose levels, whereas GS-10c-treated mice showed a more gradual recovery, suggesting improved insulin responsiveness (Figure 4D). Quantification of the AUC further supported improved insulin sensitivity in the GS-10c group (Figures 4D and S7B).

To further investigate the molecular basis of these metabolic improvements, we performed bioinformatic analyses of RNA-seq data from liver tissues across treatment groups. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that in HFD-GS-10c mice, key pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism and insulin responses were significantly suppressed (Figures 4E and 4F). In contrast, these pathways were less affected in HFD-resme mice, suggesting that GS-10c and resmetirom modulate systemic glucose metabolism through distinct mechanisms. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that GS-10c achieves similar efficacy to resmetirom in regulating both hepatic lipid accumulation and systemic glucose homeostasis.

GS-10c provides sustained Stk25 silencing for 35 days in mice

One major advantage of ASOs is that chemical modification could confer extended stability and long-acting therapeutic potential,25,30 which is crucial for therapeutic adherence to chronic diseases. To evaluate the durability of GS-10c-mediated gene silencing, we administered a single subcutaneous dose of 20 mg/kg GS-10c to wild-type male C57BL/6 mice maintained on an SD, with saline-treated mice serving as controls (Figure 5A). Liver tissues were collected at 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 35 days post-injection.

RT-qPCR analysis revealed significant Stk25 mRNA suppression as early as day 3 (−46.1%, p < 0.01). Knockdown efficiency peaked between days 14 and 21 (day 7: −50.7%, p < 0.001; day 14: −62.4%, p < 0.0001; and day 21: −59.7%, p < 0.0001), with sustained silencing observed through day 35 (−42.1%, p < 0.01) (Figure 5B). Together, these results indicate that GS-10c has potential as a long-acting therapeutic modality for MASLD.

The sequence of GS-10c is important for consistent silencing effect and hepatotoxicity

To assess whether other potent in vitro ASO candidates also exhibit in vivo efficacy, we selected S-11c (Figure S2F), which targets a region adjacent to S-10c, and generated a GalNAc-conjugated version (GS-11c) for liver-specific delivery (Figure S8A). Wild-type C57BL/6J male mice received a single subcutaneous injection of GS-11c at varying doses (Figure S8A). Safety assessment based on ALT and AST levels showed a mild increase at a low dosage (5 mg/kg) but significant elevation at high dosages (10 and 20 mg/kg), indicating potential safety concerns for GS-11c (Figures S8B and S8C).

Interestingly, although GS-11c displayed slightly lower silencing efficiency than GS-10c in vitro (Figure S2F), it achieved more potent Stk25 suppression in vivo, with 2.5 mg/kg producing robust mRNA knockdown (75.3%, p < 0.05) (Figure S8D) and a clear dose-dependent reduction at the protein level (Figures S8E and S8F). Encouraged by these results, we next evaluated GS-11c in HFD-induced early MASLD mice (Figure S9A). Unexpectedly, its silencing efficacy was markedly reduced (Figure S9B). Importantly, while improvements in hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and body weight were observed (Figures S9C–S9I), serum ALT and AST levels were substantially elevated, indicating hepatotoxicity under metabolic stress (Figures S9J and S9K).

Notably, GS-11c differs from GS-10c by only 4 nt (Figure S2E), suggesting that minor sequence variations can critically influence both efficacy and safety in vivo. Collectively, these findings indicate that GS-10c is a uniquely stable, efficacious, and safe ASO candidate against STK25 with consistent activity across human cell lines and mouse models.

Discussion

MASLD has emerged as the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide and a leading driver of liver-related morbidity.50 Despite its substantial global burden, there remains a critical unmet need for approved pharmacological therapies targeting this condition.51 Based on the European Atherosclerosis Society’s consensus statement, apart from lifestyle interventions, treatment of metabolic comorbidities of systemic metabolic disorders should focus on insulin resistance and ectopic lipid accumulation.52 However, current therapies require daily administration, often with combination therapy regimens, substantially compromising long-term adherence. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of ASO technology in MASLD therapy,40,41 yet the sequence is poorly homologous to human STK25 (Figure S1), limiting clinical translation. In this study, we introduce GS-10c, a GalNAc-conjugated ASO targeting STK25, and demonstrate its therapeutic potential compared with resmetirom in an HFD-induced mouse model that mimics early MASLD with simple steatosis. By silencing Stk25, GS-10c addresses multiple pathological drivers of the disease, yielding three major therapeutic advantages.

First, GS-10c reduced hepatic steatosis, one of the defining features of MASLD.50 Quantitative and histological analyses demonstrated robust reductions in hepatic lipid accumulation and ballooning degeneration (Figures 3G–3J), consistent with the role of STK25 in impairing lipolysis and promoting intracellular fat deposition.34 Second, GS-10c improved systemic glucose homeostasis (Figure 4). Both glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity were significantly enhanced following treatment, consistent with the established role of STK25 in regulating energy metabolism.37,48 Given that insulin resistance is considered the major driver of MASLD pathogenesis, this finding highlights GS-10c as an effective candidate for early MASLD management.47 Third, GS-10c significantly reduced body weight in mice (Figure 3B), a parameter frequently associated with metabolic dysfunction in MASLD.53 It should be noted that the absolute serum TC, LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-C values observed in this study were higher than typically reported for wild-type mice, which may reflect assay-related variability. Nevertheless, because all samples were analyzed using the same reagent and experimental procedures, the relative comparisons among groups remain valid.

Notably, GS-10c demonstrated durable efficacy, a feature of particular importance for chronic diseases such as MASLD. A single 20 mg/kg injection suppressed hepatic Stk25 expression by more than 50% for >30 days (Figure 5). Given that human metabolic turnover is slower than that of mice, this suggests that dosing intervals of several months may be achievable in patients. This long-acting profile highlights a major advantage of ASO-based therapy compared with daily oral agents such as statins or resmetirom, for which poor adherence remains a critical limitation. By reducing the frequency of administration, GS-10c could overcome compliance barriers and ensure sustained therapeutic benefit, a particularly valuable attribute for a disease that requires lifelong management.

Notably, the efficacy of GS-10c in improving NASs is underperformed compared with resmetirom (Figures 3J and 3K). Differences in mechanism and signaling scope likely explain the observed effects. Resmetirom, a THR-β agonist, broadly reprograms hepatic metabolism by activating pathways involved in β-oxidation, lipid clearance, and energy expenditure, thereby influencing steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.54 In contrast, GS-10c selectively targets Stk25, a regulator of lipid droplet dynamics, and thus primarily reduces hepatic lipid accumulation with more limited impact on broader metabolic and inflammatory pathways (Figures S5A and S5B). This narrower mode of action may account for its comparatively modest effects on composite histological scores. Importantly, the comparison in this study is intended as a reference rather than a direct assessment of efficacy, as differences in modality and dosing preclude strict equivalence between ASO-based and small-molecule therapies.

Beyond these pharmacological effects, the SNP-aware design of GS-10c enhances its clinical translatability. Genetic variability, particularly SNPs, represents a well-recognized determinant of interindividual differences in drug efficacy and safety.55 For ASO drugs, SNPs within the binding region may affect the DNA/RNA duplex, thereby compromising therapeutic effect.56 To address this, GS-10c was designed using a database-driven approach that avoided all known SNPs (dbSNP Build 155, NCBI). This strategy ensures robust silencing across genetically diverse patient populations and reduces the risk of treatment failure due to allele-specific variability. Indeed, GS-10c showed consistently robust silencing of STK25 in 3 different human cell lines and stable safety profiles in vivo (Figures 1A–1C), indicating its potential for clinical translation.

Safety remains a primary consideration for clinical translation of antisense therapeutics. Although another sequence candidate, GS-11c, exhibited even stronger Stk25 silencing than GS-10c in SD mice (Figure S8), it unexpectedly showed markedly reduced activity and pronounced hepatotoxicity in HFD-induced MASLD mice (Figure S9). These findings reveal that minor sequence variations—even as few as 4 nt—can profoundly affect both efficacy and safety in vivo. In contrast, GS-10c demonstrated consistent silencing efficiency and safety profile across both tested conditions (Figures 2A–2C and 3C–3F). While other potent ASO sequences targeting STK25 may exist, the variable in vivo performance and metabolic condition-dependent hepatotoxicity observed with GS-11c highlight the unique balance of potency, durability, and safety achieved by GS-10c, supporting its potential as a clinically viable ASO candidate.

Taken together, this study highlights four key therapeutic advantages of GS-10c in early MASLD: a consistent safety profile, improvement of glucose regulation, reduction of hepatic steatosis, and long-acting silencing of the pathogenic target STK25, with comparable therapeutic efficacy to resmetirom, currently the only FDA-approved medication for MASH with fibrosis.15,16,17,18 The additional consideration of genetic variability further strengthens its translational potential, positioning GS-10c as a next-generation nucleic acid drug capable of overcoming current barriers in early MASLD therapy. While targeting lipid metabolism and glucose homeostasis alone may not fully address inflammatory and fibrotic aspects of the disease, GS-10c provides a strong foundation for the development of combination approaches that integrate metabolic, inflammatory, and antifibrotic strategies.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of ASOs

The ASOs used in this study were chemically synthesized by SicaGene (Beijing) BioScience. For example, OA+scramble has GGCcaatacgccgTCA. The uppercase letters represent cEt-modified sequences, and the lowercase letters denote unmodified DNA. The bolded cytosine indicates 5-methylcytosine modification. All ASOs possess a fully PS backbone linkage.

Cell culture and transfection

All cell lines were cultured in complete medium consisting of DMEM (Viva Cell Biosciences, Cat# C3103-0500) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell, Cat# 164210) under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Cells were initially expanded in 10-cm culture dishes before being transferred to 24-well plates for transfection experiments. Transfection was typically performed when cells reached 70%–80% confluence, usually 1–2 days after plating. Prior to transfection (30–60 min), the culture medium was replaced with fresh serum-free medium (437.5 μL per well in 24-well plates). Of note, optimal transfection efficiency requires freshly prepared medium with precisely calibrated pH. The calcium phosphate transfection method (Beyotime, Cat# C0508) was employed as follows: ASOs (dose adjusted per experimental requirements) were mixed with 25 μL of CaCl2 solution, then 25 μL of BBS buffer was added with thorough mixing. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min before being uniformly distributed across the 24-well plate. After incubating for 6–8 h, the culture plate was gently shaken. The precipitate-containing medium was then carefully removed and replaced with 500 μL fresh complete medium for continued culture. Cells were harvested 24 h post-medium replacement for subsequent analyses.

Lipid droplet staining in cells

Cells were passaged into 12-well cell culture plates (pre-loaded with coverslips [Biosharp, Cat# BS-20-RC]) and treated with 0.25 mM OA (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-N1446) to induce MASLD cellular modeling. The following day, ASOs were transfected into cells using calcium phosphate precipitation, followed by staining after 24 h. The medium was first discarded, and cells were washed with PBS before being stained with BODIPY (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-D1614) and Hoechst (MedChemExpress, Cat# HY-15627) at a dilution ratio of 1:20,000 for 10 min in the dark. The dye supernatant was then removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS (5 min per wash). Subsequently, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, followed by another three PBS washes (5 min each). Finally, the coverslips were mounted onto glass slides for imaging using confocal microscopy.

Experiment on the detection of cellular energy metabolism indicators

After treating HepG2 cells with 0.25 mM OA and transfecting ASOs for 24 h, the cells were trypsinized and counted. A total of 10,000 cells were seeded into black-walled, opaque 96-well plates for mitochondrial OCR measurement, while the remaining cells were collected into fresh EP tubes for FAO rate analysis. For OCR detection, the assay was performed using a commercial kit (Elabscience, Cat# E-BC-F070) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, pre-warmed reagents were added to the cell suspension, and fluorescence readings were acquired using a microplate reader (excitation: 405 nm, emission: 650 nm) at 37°C. Measurements were taken at 2-min intervals over 60 min to monitor dynamic changes in OCR. For FAO rate detection, cells were lysed and processed using a dedicated FAO assay kit (Elabscience, Cat# E-BC-K784-M). The reaction mixture was incubated as specified, and FAO activity was determined spectrophotometrically. To normalize results, total protein concentration was measured via BCA assay (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 23227). The final FAO rate was expressed as the ratio of FAO activity (fluorescence units) to protein concentration (mg/mL).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Center of Yunnan University (approval number: YNU20251599) and conducted in strict adherence to animal welfare principles. 6-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Yunnan University and housed four per cage under controlled temperature and humidity conditions with free access to food and water. After a week of adaptive adjustment, 18 mice were fed an HFD (60 kcal% fat, Synergy Bio, Cat# XTHF60), while 8 mice received an SD (15 kcal% fat, Synergy Bio, Cat# XTI01CR-010). Following 13 weeks of HFD induction, the diet-treated mice were randomly divided into three weight-matched groups (n = 6): (1) ASO group, where subcutaneous injections of GS-10c at a dose of 20/10 mg/kg were administered weekly, along with daily oral gavage of an equivalent volume of solvent (0.6% methyl cellulose [TargetMol, Cat# T19423]); (2) resmetirom group: where resmetirom (TargetMol, Cat# T3595) was administered daily via oral gavage at a dose of 3 mg/kg; and (3) control group, where an equivalent volume of solvent was administered daily via oral gavage. The SD mice received weekly saline injections and daily oral gavage of an equivalent volume of solvent. Body weights were measured at diet initiation and weekly during drug treatment periods. All animals were sacrificed 1 week after the final treatment to collect plasma and tissue. All investigators received certified training from the Kunming Animal Association, and experimental procedures were conducted under the supervision and ethical review of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yunnan University.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

After washing twice with PBS, the homogenized tissue samples or cells were incubated with TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Cat# R1100) for total RNA extraction following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted RNA was resuspended in 40–60 μL of RNase-DNase free water. Subsequently, 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA in a total reaction volume of 20 μL using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Cat# R223-01). The resulting cDNA was diluted to 200 μL with ddH2O and subjected to quantitative PCR (qPCR). The reactions were performed using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, Cat# AQ601) in a 384-well plate format on a LightCycler 480 II (Roche). GAPDH was used as the housekeeping gene for analysis. The sequences of qPCR primers are as follows: human-STK25-forward, 5'-CAAGCTATGGATCATCATGGAGT-3', and human-STK25-reverse, 5'-GGATCTTGCTTCGGAGTG-3'; human-GAPDH-forward, 5'-GTCAGCCGCATCTTCTTTTC-3', and human-GAPDH-reverse, 5'-GCGCCCAATACGACCAAATC-3'; mouse-Stk25-forward, 5'-CACCAGCATTCTCGTGTGGA-3', and mouse-Stk25-reverse, 5'-GTGTGGTTGTCGATCCCCT-3'; mouse-Gapdh-forward, 5'-TCCGTCGTGGATCTGACGTG-3', and mouse-Gapdh-reverse, 5'-CCGGCATCGAAGGTGGAAGA-3'; mouse-Col1a1-forward, 5'-GCTCCTCTTAGGGGCCACT-3', and mouse-Col1a1-reverse, 5'-ATTGGGGACCCTTAGGCC; and mouse-Vim-forward, 5'-CGTCCACACGCACCTACAG-3', and mouse-Vim-reverse, 5'-GGGGGATGAGGAATAGAGGCT-3'.

Western blot analysis

The tissue samples (approximately 30 mg each) or cell samples were homogenized in 500 μL of protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1 mM PMSF, and 0.1% SDS). The samples were then centrifuged at 20,000g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected into new EP tubes. The total protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Cat# 23227) with albumin standards according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, protein electrophoresis and western blotting were performed. The following primary antibodies were used in the experiments: anti-STK25 (Proteintech, Cat# 25821-AP) and anti-VINCULIN (ABclonal, Cat# A2752).

Plasma biomarker measurement

Blood samples were collected into 1.5 mL EP tubes and allowed to stand at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 2,000g for 15 min, and the supernatant was transferred to a new 1.5 mL EP tube. The levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# C009-1-1), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# C010-2-1), Triglyceride Assay Kit (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A110-1-1), Total Cholesterol Assay Kit (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A111-1-1), HDL-C Assay Kit (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A112-1-1), and LDL-C Assay Kit (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A113-1-1) were measured in the supernatant.

Determination of hepatic TG content

Liver tissue (0.1 g) from mice was homogenized in 900 μL of physiological saline using a tissue homogenizer in a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube. The homogenate was centrifuged at 20,000g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and kept on ice for subsequent analysis. Hepatic TG levels were measured using a commercial Triglyceride Assay Kit (NanJing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Cat# A110-1-1) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the reaction mixture was incubated at 37°C for 5 min, and absorbance was measured at 500 nm using a microplate reader. To normalize TG content to tissue protein levels, total protein concentration in the supernatant was determined. The final hepatic TG content was expressed as the ratio of absorbance (TG measurement) to protein concentration, providing a standardized measure of lipid accumulation (nmol TG/mg protein).

In vivo imaging in mice

In wild-type C57BL/6J mice, ASO was administered via subcutaneous injection at a dose of 20 mg/kg. 3 days post-injection, the mice were euthanized and dissected. The liver, heart, kidneys, pancreas, and lungs were harvested and rinsed twice with physiological saline. After removing excess moisture, the organs were placed in a 10-cm Petri dish. Finally, the dish was positioned in the in vivo imaging system (Biospace Lab, PhotonIMAGER Optima) for imaging.

Liver tissue histological sectioning and staining

Following euthanasia, appropriately sized fresh liver tissue blocks were excised and placed in 2 mL EP tubes containing 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, Cat# J19943.K2). The samples were then incubated on a shaker at room temperature for 24 h. Subsequently, the liver tissues were subjected to dehydration using a graded series of ethanol concentrations, with each step lasting 30 min, until the tissues appeared white. Upon completion of dehydration, the tissues were immersed in 1 mL of xylene for 30 min until they became transparent. Finally, the tissues were embedded in paraffin, with the embedding process lasting approximately 2 h. Following embedding, the liver tissue blocks were sectioned and stained. The staining procedures included hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Solarbio, Cat# G1120) and Sirius Red staining (Sigma, Cat# 195251), performed strictly according to the respective kit protocols. Additionally, a portion of the liver tissue was directly embedded in OCT compound (SAKURA, Cat# 4583) and stored at −80°C until the OCT solidified. The embedded tissues were then sectioned and subjected to oil red O staining (Sigma, Cat# C.I.26125), also following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Immunofluorescence

The prepared liver cryosections were equilibrated at room temperature for 10 min, followed by immersion in sterile water (room temperature) for 5 min. Excess moisture was carefully removed, and the tissue sections were outlined using a hydrophobic barrier pen (Biosharp, Cat# BC003). The sections were then blocked with 5% BSA (BBI, Cat# A600332-0025) in a dark humidified chamber for 30 min at room temperature. After blocking, the sections were washed three times with PBST (0.3% Triton X-100). Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C (Anti-HNF-4-alpha: Abcam, Cat# ab181604). The following day, sections were washed three times with PBST. Secondary antibody incubation was conducted at room temperature for 2 h (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L), Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate: STARTER, Cat# S0B4004), followed by additional washes. Finally, the sections were mounted and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

NAS scoring

The histological evaluation of mouse liver tissue and the diagnostic criteria for NAFLD, based on the NAS, were adapted from the NASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) histological scoring system originally established for human disease.57 However, modifications were made to account for differences observed in mouse models of hepatic steatosis.58 The pathological features of our mouse model closely resembled those of NAFLD as described by Liang et al.58 In this study, the HFD-induced MASLD mouse model exhibited characteristic hepatic pathologies, including steatosis, lobular inflammation, hepatocyte ballooning, and mild (non-prominent) fibrosis, demonstrating disease progression similar to that reported by Liang et al.58 Therefore, the NAS criteria applied in this study were based on the scoring approach proposed by Liang et al.58

Glucose tolerance and insulin resistance tests in mice

For the IPGTT, mice were fasted for 12–16 h prior to the experiment. The following day, all mice were weighed, and the glucose (Procell, Cat# PB180418) injection volume was calculated based on a dose of 0.75 g/kg body weight. Fasting blood glucose levels were measured using a glucometer (Accu-Chek Guide) and recorded as baseline values. Subsequently, blood glucose levels were measured at 30-min intervals over a total period of 120 min. For the IPITT, mice were fasted for 6 h beforehand. Each mouse was weighed, and the insulin (Procell, Cat# PB180432) injection volume was calculated based on a dose of 0.75 U/kg body weight. Fasting blood glucose levels were measured as described for the IPGTT. Blood glucose levels were then measured at 30-min intervals over a total period of 120 min.

RNA-seq analysis

After subcutaneous injection of GS-10c (20 mg/kg) or oral gavage of resmetirom (3 mg/kg) in mice induced by an HFD, the mice were euthanized following an 8-week treatment period. Total RNA samples were isolated from the whole-liver tissues of the mice and subjected to next-generation RNA-seq analysis. Poly(A) selection for mRNA species was employed for rRNA depletion (Vazyme, N401-01) and RNA library construction (Vazyme, NR605-02). The mRNA-seq libraries were quantified using a Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, c100001) and sequenced on the MGI 2000 platform (MGI-SEQ, BGI).

The depth of sequencing was 15–20 million reads per sample. The quality of the reads derived from sequencing was assessed with FastQC 0.12.1; reads with a quality score < 30 were filtered out using trim-galore 0.6.10. The pre-processed reads were first mapped to the mm39 version of the mouse ribosomal RNAs using bowtie-align-s 1.3.1 to remove rRNA. Reads that did not align to ribosomal RNAs were then used for alignment to the mouse genome using STAR 2.7.10b with basic parameters. For normalization, gene expression was estimated by featureCounts 2.0.1. Genes with an expression value (TPM) greater than 1 across all libraries were defined as active genes for subsequent analysis, while those with a TPM ≤ 1 were filtered out. We applied the DESeq2 package to normalize raw RNA-seq counts and identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Genes were defined as differentially expressed if p < 0.05 and the fold change was greater than 0.5 (either up or down).

To gain insights into the biological implications of the DEGs, we performed GO enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package 4.14.6. The analysis focused on the biological process (BP) category, and GO terms with p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The enriched terms were subsequently visualized using heatmaps and bubble plots to highlight the most relevant functional categories.

To further explore the potential biological pathways associated with the DEGs, we conducted KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package. The enrichment analysis was performed based on the KEGG database. The analysis workflow is consistent with the GO analysis.

GSEA was performed using the KEGG pathway database as the reference gene set. Ranked gene lists were generated according to log2 fold change values, and enrichment scores were calculated based on a permutation-based approach. Pathways with p < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. The results were visualized using clusterProfiler and GseaVis R package 0.1.1.

For statistical significance analysis among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA was employed; for comparisons between two groups, a two-sample t test was applied.
