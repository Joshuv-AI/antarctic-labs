Executive Summary/Abstract: This grant aimed to accelerate the development of specialized
enzymes—biological catalysts essential for sustainable manufacturing and medicine—by
integrating traditional laboratory evolution with cutting-edge artificial intelligence. To achieve
this, we developed a suite of high-throughput sequencing tools and a centralized database to
bridge the gap between a protein’s genetic "code" and its physical function. By training machine
learning models on large datasets, we also demonstrated the ability to move beyond slow, trial-
and-error testing to a "generative" approach, where AI can independently design new, versatile
enzymes like tryptophan synthases. Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that combining
laboratory data with computer-guided design enables the engineering of highly efficient
biological tools with unprecedented speed and precision.

Introduction:

Enzymes are nature’s catalysts: they are the machines that build the materials and molecules of
life from abundant and often renewable resources. By engineering the DNA that encodes an
enzyme, we can create new versions that are optimized and adapted to catalyze new
reactions.1,2 If scientists and engineers could efficiently and reliably design and synthesize new
biocatalysts, we could develop sustainable processes for producing desired chemicals and
fuels. Directed evolution (DE) has driven the success of enzyme engineering for 25 years;3
however, DE remains time-consuming and resource-intensive because current strategies for
searching for improved enzymes within this near-infinite design space rely on large-scale
screening of closely related sequences.

This project sought to bridge the gap between directed evolution and new computational
methods, creating a "closed-loop" system where laboratory data inform AI models, which in turn
design the next generation of proteins.4 The scope of the work evolved from developing
improvements to sequencing DNA (reading) and building protein libraries (writing) to creating
and storing new datasets of protein sequence fitness landscape (cataloguing) and eventually to
the autonomous generation of functional enzymes that do not exist in nature (composing).
Overall, our goal was to enable the broader ML community to utilize better data-driven and
domain-knowledge-based approaches to enzyme engineering.

Background/Objectives:

We proposed to develop new strategies for enzyme engineering by fusing state-of-the-art
protein engineering methods with modern machine-learning and simulation-based processes to
create a generalizable procedure for creation of well-annotated datasets and new ML tools and
computational frameworks for enzyme discovery and engineering. Our original objectives
centered around three aims: (1) developing more comprehensive enzyme sequence-function
datasets for thousands of variants, (2) creating a set of ML tools to learn from multimodal data
and predict novel enzymatic activities, and (3) building computational frameworks to enable
uncertainty-guided optimization. While our ultimate goal, to design better biocatalysts, remained
the same throughout this project, over time, the majority of our efforts focused on building an
extended platform to enable Aim 1. Namely, we realized that there was a critical need to
develop better infrastructure to read and write protein sequences and store that information in
high-throughput, to enable AI-guided design. After developing new tools to support this
infrastructure, our work culminated in a project that leveraged the power of generative protein
language models, adapting the original goal outlined in Aim 2.

Project Activities and Results:

The "Read" Phase: High-Throughput Sequencing

In order to build a predictive model for protein sequence design, the foundation of the project
rested on the ability to efficiently "read" the sequence data tested in laboratory experiments. In
past directed evolution campaigns, protein functional “fitness” data was collected, but individual
sequence information often was not obtained, due to the high cost of sequencing. Thus, we
developed evSeq, a cost-effective method for sequencing every variant within a protein library
using next-generation Illumina-based sequencing.5 By focusing on specific genetic regions,
evSeq allows researchers to map how small changes in DNA affect protein performance at a
cost of just cents per variant sequence. However, this method was still limited to short-read

sequencing, meaning that one could not capture all mutations in long proteins. This led us to the
development of LevSeq, which utilizes nanopore sequencing to read the entire gene (Figure
1).6 Overall, LevSeq enables orders of magnitude cheaper cost of sequencing each variant,
compared to Sanger sequencing. This shift allowed for a holistic view of the protein’s "fitness
landscape," providing the dense, high-quality data necessary for training complex AI models.

The "Catalog" Phase: Data Interpretation and Collection

Data is only valuable if it can be understood, shared, and processed efficiently in computers.
With tools in hand to read sequence data and build informed libraries of protein sequences, we
focused on compiling new datasets and interpreting the complex relationships between a
protein’s sequence and its function. In a study published in PNAS, we mapped a
"combinatorially complete" fitness landscape of over 160,000 sequence-fitness data pairs in an
enzyme active site, proving that mutation effects are not always additive—a phenomenon
known as epistasis (Figure 3).8 In fact, directed evolution—taking one step at a time or
recombining beneficial single mutations--often fails to reach a variant with optimal fitness on this
landscape. As a result, this large dataset and insights from it have broadly facilitated the in silico
benchmarking of new machine learning-assisted directed evolution methods to outperform
directed evolution.9,10

To manage the complexity of growing knowledge on engineered enzymes and their associated
functions and fitness values, we additionally launched EnzEngDB (Figure 3).11 This database
serves as a central repository for sequence-function data for a variety of enzymes that were
engineered previously for non-native reactions using directed evolution. This database also
provides numerical "embeddings" to translate biological information into a language that AI
could easily digest, ensuring that years of experimental results remained accessible and
actionable. This database was recently made available, and so far it has proven to be a user-
friendly and valuable resource for the community to interpret previous campaigns and deposit
their own new data.

Figure 3. Fitness landscape data collection and storage unlock interpretability and training and
testing of AI models. Left: we generated a “combinatorially complete” protein-fitness landscape
(sequence-function pairs) across four residues in the active site of a tryptophan synthase enzyme by
coupling enzyme activity (tryptophan formation) to selection via cell growth. Right: we developed a
platform to compile previous directed evolution campaigns comprising thousands of enzyme sequences
and their associated reactions into a public, user-friendly database.

The "Compose" Phase: Generative AI Design

The project culminated in the "Compose" phase, where we moved from building tools and
infrastructure to designing new proteins from scratch. Our original hypothesis was that new
multimodal models utilizing predictions from physics-based simulations would enable the design
of new enzymes with new activities,12 but our efforts in this area have not yet been successful.
Instead, we pivoted to finetuning a generative AI model trained over large amounts of protein

 6

sequences, GenSLM, to design "stand-alone" tryptophan synthase (TrpB) enzymes (Figure
4).13 The sequences that we generated from the model were many mutations away from any
known natural TrpB enzyme yet some retained their intricate biological function, the ability to
couple indole and serine to form L-Trp. Furthermore, the AI-designed variants were not only
more stable and versatile than natural enzymes, but they also outperformed (had higher overall
activity across multiple substrates) versions that had been evolved through years of traditional
laboratory work. This demonstrated that the AI had successfully learned the underlying
"grammar" of protein design and gives us confidence to push generative AI models into even
more difficult domains in the future (e.g. enzymes that catalyze reactions not known in nature).

Figure 4. Workflow for generative AI-guided design of novel tryptophan synthase enzymes.
Conventional strategies for identifying starting points for directed evolution (DE) typically rely on the
intrinsic promiscuity of natural enzymes and require substantial time and experimental efforts. In contrast,
libraries generated by GenSLM exhibit favorable properties, including high expression, stability, and
catalytic activity, as well as broadened substrate promiscuity, offering an efficient alternative for rapid
biocatalyst discovery.

Conclusions:

The research successfully substantiated the integration of high-throughput data collection and
storage into generative AI-guided protein design, representing a fundamental shift in biocatalysis.
By mastering the ability to read, write, catalog, and compose genetic information, we have
provided the community with a platform to perform AI-assisted enzyme engineering through
rigorously tested tools: evSeq, LevSeq, DeCOIL, and EnzEngDB. Furthermore, we obtained
interpretable insights by collecting a comprehensive fitness dataset of tryptophan synthase
variants and demonstrated a real-world success case for engineering highly active, promiscuous
versions of these enzymes with generative AI. These findings solidify that we are entering an era
where custom biological tools can be designed on demand for specific industrial or medical needs.
Overall, this work provides a scalable blueprint for the future of sustainable chemistry, offering a
path to rapidly develop biocatalysts for carbon capture, production of fuels, plastic degradation,
or the synthesis of next-generation pharmaceuticals, among many others.
