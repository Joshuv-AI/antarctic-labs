Concentration of Measure Phenomena for Quantum States on a Higher Dimensional Equator
Kabgyun Jeong ID

Abstract
We revisit Lévy’s lemma, a widely used analytical tool in quantum information theory. Concentration inequalities quantify the phenomenon in which Lipschitz observables concentrate around a median or mean, and serve as fundamental analytical tools across information theory, statistical physics, and learning theory. In particular, Lévy’s lemma provides a crucial framework for describing functionals on pure quantum states, with applications in quantum entanglement and quantum statistical query learning. In this work, we isolate the hyper-equatorial part of the standard spherical concentration argument. The resulting estimate is a Lévy-type bound for Lipschitz functions on a fixed hyperequator, with the natural dimension parameter d − 1 d-1 . We also formulate the accompanying geometric localization in terms of neighborhoods of the boundary, hyperequator, and a codimension-two antipodal great subsphere. This viewpoint clarifies the structure of the usual proof and points to the measure-theoretic formulation needed for sharper constant-level statements.

Theorem 1
.

Let

be a

The proof follows the same two classical routes as Lévy’s lemma: one route uses the Brunn–Minkowski inequality and Euclidean neighborhoods, and the other uses the spherical isoperimetric inequality. The point is not to replace Lévy’s lemma, but to separate the successive geometric localizations that are usually folded into one asymptotic statement: the volume of the ball is near the boundary, the surface measure of the boundary is near a hyperequator, and the induced measure on the hyperequator admits a further localization around a codimension-two antipodal great subsphere.

The second structural point is a caution about this final localization. The antipodal great subsphere is terminal only relative to the coordinate localization scheme fixed above; a further localization would require additional structure, such as a new choice of coordinates or a new metric-measure space.

f:\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
on a sphere
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
. Suppose that
A=\{x\in\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}|f(x)\leq m_{f}\}
, then

\mu(A_{\delta})\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}(d-2)}{2}\right).

(22)

Proof.

For the two median half-spaces
A_{-}=\{x:f(x)\leq m_{f}\}
and
A_{+}=\{x:f(x)\geq m_{f}\}
, Corollary
II.7
gives the corresponding upper and lower tail estimates. Thus,

\mu((A_{-})_{\delta})\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}(d-2)}{2}\right)~~~~\mathrm{and}~~~~\mu((A_{+})_{\delta})\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}(d-2)}{2}\right).

(23)

∎

III
Proof of main results

In this section, two kinds of proof of Theorem
1
will be presented: The concentration of 1-Lipschitz function and the isoperimetric inequality.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional hypersphere and
{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}\subset{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be a
d-1
-dimensional hyperdisk through the origin. (Note that
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}:=E^{d-2}
.) Let
A
be a nonempty compact subset of
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
. Suppose that
A^{\prime}:=\{\gamma^{\prime}x|x\in A,\gamma^{\prime}\in[0,1]\}
in
{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
, then

\mathrm{Pr}[A]=\mu(A^{\prime}):=\frac{\mathrm{vol}(A^{\prime})}{\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{E}}^{d-1})}.

(24)

Let
B
be a set of
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}\backslash(A\cup A_{\varepsilon})
, where
A_{\varepsilon}
denotes the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
such that the set of all
x\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
whose Euclidean distance
\|\cdot\|
to
A
is at most
\varepsilon
. Suppose that
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
and
\mathrm{Pr}[A]\geq 1/2
, then, for all
a\in A
and
b\in B
,
\|a-b\|\geq\varepsilon
. Let
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are subsets in
{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
. Then, by Lemma
II.3
, we also obtain
\left\|\frac{x^{\prime}+y^{\prime}}{2}\right\|\leq 1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}
for any
x^{\prime}\in A^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
.

Proposition III.1
.

Let
A\subseteq\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
be a measurable set with
\mathrm{Pr}[A]\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Suppose that
A_{\varepsilon}
denotes the
\varepsilon
-neighborhood of
A
, i.e., the set of all
x\in{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
whose Euclidean distance to
A
is at most
\varepsilon
. Then

1-\mathrm{Pr}[A_{\varepsilon}]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}{(d-1)}}{4}\right).

(25)

Proof.

By above state (or Lemma
II.3
), the set
\frac{A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}}{2}
is contained in the hyperdisk of radius
1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}
. By the Brun-Minkowski inequality; Lemma
II.2
, and
\mathrm{Pr}[A]\geq\frac{1}{2}
,

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)^{d-1}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime})\right)\geq\sqrt{\mu(A^{\prime})\mu(B^{\prime})}

(26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{Pr}[A]\mathrm{Pr}[B]}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Pr}[B]}.

Thus,
\mathrm{Pr}[B]\leq 2\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\right)^{2(d-1)}\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(d-1)}{4}\right)
.
∎

We now prove our main theorem on the
d-2
-dimensional hyperequator
\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
via concentration of Lipschitz functions. Let
f:\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function and let
m_{f}
be a median of
f
. Then we have

Theorem III.2
.

Let
f:E^{d-2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
\kappa
-Lipschitz function on
E^{d-2}=\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
. For every
\varepsilon>0
, if a point
x\in E^{d-2}
is chosen uniformly at random, then

\mathrm{Pr}\left[|f(x)-m_{f}|\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{-c(d-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\right),

(27)

where
m_{f}
is a median of
f
and
C_{1},c>0
are universal constants.

Proof.

Suppose that
A_{-}=\{x\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}|f(x)\leq m_{f}\}
and
A_{+}=\{x\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}|f(x)\geq m_{f}\}
. Then
\mathrm{Pr}[A_{-}]\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
\mathrm{Pr}[A_{+}]\geq\frac{1}{2}
. If
x\in(A_{-})_{\varepsilon/\kappa}
, then
f(x)\leq m_{f}+\varepsilon
; similarly, if
x\in(A_{+})_{\varepsilon/\kappa}
, then
f(x)\geq m_{f}-\varepsilon
. By Proposition
III.1
,

\mathrm{Pr}[|f(x)-m_{f}|\geq\varepsilon]\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{(d-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{4\kappa^{2}}\right),

(28)

which has the asserted form after renaming the universal constants.
∎

. Let

A_{0}
.

Suppose that

II.7

\mu(A_{\delta})\geq 1-\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{8}}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}(d-1)}{2}\right).

(29)

Let
f

f:E^{d-2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined on a hyperdisk

. Let

, so that

\displaystyle~~~~\mathrm{and}~~~~\mu\{x\in E^{d-2}|f\geq m_{f}\}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

.

(Lévy)

Remark III.4
.

The statement is not a nonexistence theorem for subsets of a sphere. Rather, once

IV
Concentration of measure for hypersphere

This section summarizes concentration estimates for the unit ball and its boundary sphere, following the standard presentation of high-dimensional geometry in Hopcroft and Kannan
HK
. Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be the closed
d
-dimensional unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and let
\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be its boundary sphere.

Lemma IV.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional unit ball. The boundary area and the volume of the ball are given by

\mathrm{area}(\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}~~~~{\textnormal{and}}~~~~\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d})=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\frac{d}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)}.

(33)

Proof.

The volume of the unit ball
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}}\int_{r=0}^{1}r^{d-1}d\Omega dr

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\int_{\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}}d\Omega=\frac{\mathrm{area}(\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d})}{d}.

Consider the following Gaussian integral in Cartesian coordinates

\iota({\mathcal{S}}^{d}):=\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-x^{2})dx\right]^{d}=\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}.

(35)

Then, in polar coordinate,

\displaystyle\iota({\mathcal{S}}^{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}}d\Omega\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-r^{2})r^{d-1}dr

(36)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{area}(\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d})}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-t)t^{\frac{d}{2}-1}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{area}(\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d})}{2}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary IV.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional hypersphere with unit radius. Suppose that the dimension
d
is sufficiently large, then

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d})=0.

(37)

Proof.

By Stirling’s formula,

\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+1\right)\sim\sqrt{\pi d}\left(\frac{d}{2e}\right)^{d/2}.

(38)

Since
\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d})=\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2+1)
, the denominator eventually dominates the numerator and
\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d})\to 0
.
∎

The preceding computation is a statement about total Euclidean volume, not by itself a statement that a probability measure equals the boundary. The probabilistic form is the usual thin-shell statement:

Corollary IV.3
.

Let
\mu_{1}
be the uniform probability measure on the unit ball
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
. For every fixed
\eta>0
,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mu_{1}\left(\left\{x\in{\mathcal{S}}^{d}:\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d})\leq\eta\right\}\right)=1.

(39)

The following lemma describes that the volume is
almost all
concentrated at a
d-1
- dimensional hyperdisk of the hypersphere
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
.

Lemma IV.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}^{d}
be a
d
-dimensional hypersphere with unit radius. Suppose that
C_{1}>0
. Then the fraction of the volume of the hemisphere above the hyperplane
x_{1}=\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{d-1}}
is less than
\frac{2}{C_{1}}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)
.

Proof.

Fix the north pole on
x_{1}
axis at
x_{1}=1
, and divide the hypersphere in half by a hyperplane
H_{d-1}^{0}
i.e. a hyperdisk.
The disk with dimension
d-1
is

D_{0}:={\mathcal{S}}^{d}\cap H_{d-1}^{0}=\{x||x|\leq 1,x_{1}=0\}.

(40)

Consider a
\varepsilon
neighborhood disk
D_{\varepsilon}
at
x_{1}=\varepsilon
parallel to
x_{1}=0
, and the portion of the hypersphere above

D_{\varepsilon}
as
D_{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow}:=\{x||x|\leq 1,x_{1}\geq\varepsilon\}
. Thus the incremental volume is a disk of width
dx_{1}
whose face is a hypersphere of dimension
d-1
with radius
\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}
. So the surface area of the disk is

\mathrm{area}(D_{x_{1}\geq\varepsilon})=(1-x_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1}).

(41)

By using
1+a\leq\exp(a)
,
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
\frac{x_{1}}{\varepsilon}\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(D_{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1})\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}(1-x_{1}^{2})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}dx_{1}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1})\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{d-1}{2}x_{1}^{2}\right)dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1})\int_{\varepsilon}^{\infty}\frac{x_{1}}{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{d-1}{2}x_{1}^{2}\right)dx_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon(d-1)}\exp\left(-\frac{d-1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right)\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1}).

Now define a upper cylinder as
C_{x_{1}}^{\uparrow}:=\{x||x|\leq 1,r=\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}},h=x_{1}\}
. Then the lower bound of the volume of the entire upper hemisphere is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(D_{0}^{\uparrow})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(C_{x_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}})

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}(D_{x_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1})\left(1-\frac{1}{d-1}\right)^{\frac{d-1}{2}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{d-1}}\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1})\left(1-\frac{1}{d-1}\frac{d-2}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d-1}}\mathrm{vol}({\mathcal{S}}^{d-1}),

where the last inequality follows from
(1-x)^{a}\geq 1-ax
. From the Eqs. (
42
) and (
43
), the fraction of the volume is

f_{V}:=\frac{\mathrm{vol}(D_{\varepsilon}^{\uparrow})}{\mathrm{vol}(D_{0}^{\uparrow})}\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon\sqrt{d-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{d-1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\right).

(44)

Substitute
\varepsilon
to

, then
f_{V}\leq\frac{2}{C_{1}}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)
.
∎

Above Lemma

states that most of the volume of a unit ball lies in a slab of width

Corollary IV.5

Let

Lemma IV.6

V
Conclusion

We have revisited Lévy’s lemma from the viewpoint of successive geometric localization on high-dimensional spheres. The usual concentration statement says that Lipschitz functions on the sphere are almost constant with overwhelming probability, and it is commonly interpreted as concentration of surface measure near a median hyperequator. In this work we separated the geometric steps behind this picture in neighborhood language: the volume of a high-dimensional ball is localized near its boundary, the boundary measure is localized near an arbitrary hyperequator, and the induced measure on the hyperequator is localized near a codimension-two antipodal great subsphere.

The main technical contribution is the hyper-equatorial concentration estimate in Theorem
III.2
, which gives a Lévy-type bound for Lipschitz functions on
E^{d-2}=\partial{\mathcal{E}}^{d-1}
with an exponential dependence on
d-1
. The proof follows the classical architecture of Lévy’s lemma: one derivation uses the Brunn–Minkowski inequality and Euclidean neighborhoods, while the other uses the corresponding isoperimetric formulation. This parallel proof structure shows that the estimate is a dimension-shifted instance of the same high-dimensional spherical geometry that underlies the standard lemma.

The geometric estimates in Section
IV
support the conjectural localization scheme as

{\mathcal{S}}^{d}\longrightarrow\partial{\mathcal{S}}^{d}\longrightarrow E^{d-2}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{A}}^{d-3}.

In this sense, the antipodal great subsphere appears as a natural terminal object for the coordinate localization procedure considered here. This is also consistent with the topological role of antipodal pairs expressed by the Borsuk–Ulam theorem. Since Lévy-type concentration estimates are widely used in quantum information theory, especially in the study of random pure states, random unitaries, typical entanglement, unitary designs, and quantum learning, a more precise understanding of the constant-level and geometric structure of these inequalities may be useful beyond the purely asymptotic regime.

Several questions remain open. First, the constants in the exponent should be optimized and compared carefully with the sharp spherical isoperimetric constants. Second, the limiting statement in Conjecture
Conjecture
should be strengthened in a fully measure-theoretic language, for instance in terms of concentration functions or metric-measure convergence. Third, it would be useful to identify whether the antipodal localization described here gives quantitative improvements in concrete quantum-information applications where Lévy’s lemma is currently used only up to universal constants. These questions indicate that the antipodal viewpoint may provide a productive refinement of the standard concentration-of-measure toolbox.
