Angiotensin receptor blockers and dementia risk in older adults: A target trial emulation
Authors: Hisashi Noma, Hiroshi Sunada, Taiki Sugimoto, Takao Fujisawa, Futoshi Oda, Megumi Maeda, Haruhisa Fukuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWhether antihypertensive drug class influences dementia risk beyond blood pressure lowering remains uncertain.METHODSWe emulated a target trial comparing new initiation of angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) versus calcium channel blockers (CCBs) among dementia‐free adults aged ≥65 years in a nationwide Japanese database. Weighted discrete‐time hazards models estimated intention‐to‐treat and per‐protocol effects, accounting for censoring and death.RESULTSAmong 52,019 eligible new users followed for a median of 3.6 years, 3524 incident dementia events occurred. ARB initiation was associated with lower all‐cause dementia risk than CCB initiation (hazard ratio [HR], 0.916; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.852–0.985), with similar achieved blood pressure. For Alzheimer's disease (2231 events), the association was not statistically significant (HR, 0.930; 95% CI, 0.849–1.018). The association was stronger for vascular dementia (129 events; HR, 0.617; 95% CI, 0.409–0.930).DISCUSSIONARB initiation was associated with modestly lower dementia risk, particularly vascular dementia, versus CCB initiation.Keywords: angiotensin receptor blockers, big data analysis, calcium channel blockers, dementia, real‐world data, target trial emulation

BACKGROUND

Dementia represents one of the most pressing challenges to healthy longevity worldwide. As populations age, the absolute number of individuals living with cognitive impairment continues to rise, placing increasing burdens on families, health systems, and long‐term care infrastructure. 1 Beyond its direct neurological manifestations, dementia contributes substantially to disability, institutionalization, frailty progression, and mortality. 2 Strategies that delay onset even modestly may yield substantial public health benefits, particularly in countries with rapidly aging populations such as Japan.

Hypertension is among the most consistently identified modifiable risk factors for both Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia. 2 Elevated blood pressure contributes to large artery atherosclerosis, cerebral small vessel disease, white matter injury, microinfarction, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction. 3 , 4 Randomized trials have demonstrated that blood pressure lowering reduces stroke risk and major cardiovascular events, and some trials suggest possible reductions in cognitive decline. 5 , 6 , 7 However, most trials have been designed to compare blood pressure targets and cardiovascular outcomes. 8 , 9 As a result, uncertainty remains regarding whether pharmacological mechanisms beyond blood pressure reduction influence dementia risk.

Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are widely used first‐line antihypertensive agents in older adults. Clinical guidelines typically prioritize achieving blood pressure targets and generally consider these classes to be broadly equivalent in the absence of specific comorbid indications. 10 , 11 Nevertheless, the renin–angiotensin system has been implicated in processes relevant to both cardiovascular and brain aging. Angiotensin II promotes oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, vascular remodeling, and neurohormonal activation. 3 , 12 These mechanisms may accelerate microvascular injury in the heart and brain. By contrast, CCBs primarily exert vasodilatory effects through inhibition of L‐type calcium channels, reducing vascular resistance without directly modulating renin–angiotensin signaling pathways.

Experimental studies and observational research have suggested that blockade of angiotensin receptors might confer neuroprotective effects that extend beyond systemic hemodynamic control. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Potential mechanisms include improved cerebral perfusion, attenuation of inflammatory cascades, reduction of small vessel pathology, and modulation of amyloid metabolism. 3 , 12 Large prospective cohort meta‐analyses and observational network meta‐analyses have suggested that antihypertensive treatment may reduce dementia risk, but have not established consistent superiority of any single drug class. 16 , 17 More recent population‐based database studies have reported variable results, including possible advantages of ARBs for all‐cause or vascular dementia, whereas findings for Alzheimer's disease have been less consistent. 18 , 19 Mendelian randomization studies of antihypertensive drug targets have also provided limited support for a simple blood‐pressure‐mediated protective effect on Alzheimer's disease and have raised questions about potential differences between ARBs and angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. 20 , 21 These uncertainties motivate analyses that distinguish specific treatment strategies and dementia subtypes while addressing common biases in pharmacoepidemiologic studies. 22 , 23

Clarifying whether initial antihypertensive drug class selection influences dementia risk has important implications for clinical decision making. Even modest relative risk reductions may translate into meaningful absolute benefits when applied to populations with high baseline incidence of cognitive impairment. 2 Furthermore, demonstration of differential effects despite similar achieved blood pressure levels would support the hypothesis that ARB‐based therapy may influence cognitive aging through vascular pathways beyond simple blood pressure control. 24 , 25

In this nationwide target trial emulation using linked administrative and health examination data in Japan, we compared incident dementia and major cardiovascular outcomes among adults aged 65 years or older who initiated an ARB or a CCB. By applying a new‐user active‐comparator design, inverse probability weighting to address measured confounding, and explicit consideration of death as a competing event, we aimed to approximate the causal effect of alternative treatment strategies under routine clinical practice. 22 , 23 We hypothesized that ARB therapy would be associated with a lower risk of incident dementia, particularly vascular dementia, compared with CCB therapy, even when achieved blood pressure levels during follow‐up were comparable between groups.

METHODS

Study design and data source

We conducted a nationwide retrospective cohort study designed to emulate a hypothetical randomized target trial comparing ARB‐based therapy with CCB‐based therapy. This research used linked administrative claims and health examination data from the Longevity Improvement and Fair Evidence (LIFE) study. 26 The LIFE study is a nationwide real‐world database containing demographic details, International Classification of Diseases – Revision 10 (ICD‐10) coded diagnoses, pharmaceutical records, and hospitalization data. Our analysis spanned from April 2014 to September 2024, covering approximately 5.05 million residents across 19 municipalities. Ethical clearance for this study was granted by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kyushu University (approval number: 226530).

Target trial specification

Following the target trial emulation framework, 22 , 23 this retrospective observational study aimed to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of initiating ARBs versus CCBs among dementia‐free individuals aged 65 years or older. We explicitly pre‐specified the protocol—including eligibility, treatment strategies, follow‐up initiation, endpoints, and causal estimands—in accordance with the TARGET reporting guidelines. 27 Table 1 summarizes the alignment between our emulation and the hypothetical trial, and the checklist of the TARGET guideline 27 is presented in Supplementary Materials. To address the challenge of identifying treatment onset in administrative data, we implemented a new‐user design within a sequentially nested trial emulation structure. 28 , 29 This approach, which involves monthly eligibility assessments, synchronized the start of follow‐up with treatment assignment, thereby mitigating immortal time bias by design.

Study population

We included dementia‐free individuals aged 65 years or older who newly initiated pharmacological antihypertensive therapy with either an ARB or a CCB. In this claims‐based study, treated hypertension was operationally defined by new initiation of one of these antihypertensive drug classes in routine clinical practice, rather than by applying a single baseline blood pressure threshold. A 12‐month washout period without prior ARB or CCB use was required before the index date to identify incident users. We excluded patients with a history of end‐stage renal disease, dialysis, kidney transplantation, active malignancy, or those receiving palliative or end‐of‐life care. Furthermore, individuals initiating both ARBs and CCBs simultaneously were excluded, as were those in the CCB group who were concurrently prescribed ACE inhibitors, to prevent mechanistic overlap between the treatment strategies.

Treatment strategies

We compared two distinct antihypertensive strategies: (1) an ARB‐based treatment strategy and (2) a CCB‐based treatment strategy. For both groups, the index date was defined as the first prescription of the respective agent following a minimum 12‐month period without prior exposure to either class. To reflect real‐world clinical practice, the concomitant use of other antihypertensive medications, such as diuretics or β‐blockers, was permitted in both treatment arms. To address potential confounding by baseline clinical characteristics and baseline medication use, we employed inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance the cohorts. 22 , 23

Outcomes and follow‐up

The primary outcome was the incidence of all‐cause dementia, defined as the first occurrence of ICD‐10 codes after treatment initiation. Secondary cognitive outcomes encompassed Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, unspecified dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and Lewy body dementia. All‐cause mortality was determined through integrated claims and registry data. Furthermore, we evaluated major cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and hospitalization for heart failure. Given that death can preclude the clinical diagnosis of dementia in older populations, it was formally accounted for as a competing risk in all statistical models. 30

Cohort entry and treatment assignment were defined at the date of first eligible ARB or CCB dispensing. Because the claims data were structured at the monthly level, the temporal ordering of treatment initiation and outcome occurrence within the same calendar month could not be reliably determined. To reduce potential protopathic bias and reverse causation due to event‐triggered treatment initiation, we incorporated a 1‐month induction period before outcome ascertainment. Outcome follow‐up therefore began after the initiation month and continued until the first occurrence of the outcome of interest, death, loss to follow‐up due to relocation or administrative censoring, or the end of the study. Events recorded during the initiation month were not counted as incident outcomes in the primary analyses.

Follow‐up and estimands

To address distinct clinical and decision‐making perspectives, we estimated both intention‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) effects. 22 , 23 The ITT estimand was defined as the effect of initial treatment assignment at cohort entry, irrespective of subsequent therapy modifications. Conversely, the PP estimand evaluated the effect of continuous adherence to the assigned strategy throughout the follow‐up period. In the PP analysis, protocol deviation was defined as the initiation of the alternative drug class (leading to ARB–CCB combination therapy) or, for the CCB group, the initiation of an ACE inhibitor. The latter was pre‐specified as a deviation due to the mechanistic similarities between ACE inhibitors and ARBs, which could potentially bias the comparative effect toward the null. PP effects were derived using a cloning–censoring–weighting approach, where treatment deviations were handled as artificial censoring. 22 , 23

Statistical analysis

Follow‐up data were structured into one‐month intervals and analyzed using discrete‐time proportional hazards regression within a sequentially nested trial framework. 22 , 23 We employed a complementary log–log link to approximate cause‐specific proportional hazards models, with death formally incorporated as a competing event for all non‐fatal outcomes. 30 The baseline hazard's temporal variation was modeled using follow‐up time as a continuous variable, integrated via a spline function with five degrees of freedom.

To address confounding, we utilized IPTW based on propensity scores derived from baseline covariates. Variables were selected a priori for their clinical relevance, including age, sex, comorbidities, concomitant medications, and frailty‐related covariates. Balance across treatment arms was verified using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with a threshold of < 0.10 indicating adequate adjustment. 31 To account for loss to follow‐up, inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) was further applied. In the PP analysis, IPCW was also used to adjust for treatment deviations via a censoring–weighting approach. 22 , 23 All weights were stabilized and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to minimize the impact of extreme values. 32 Final weights represented the product of treatment and censoring weights as appropriate for each estimand (see e‐Appendix A for full covariate definitions).

Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using weighted discrete‐time models with cluster‐robust variance estimators to account for within‐individual correlation from repeated participation. We also derived 5‐year cumulative event probabilities and absolute risk differences from weighted cumulative incidence functions. 30 Missing data were managed via multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), with 100 imputed datasets combined using Rubin's rules. 33 To assess comparability of achieved blood pressure control during follow‐up, systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements were obtained from annual health examination data. For each follow‐up year, we estimated weighted mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels in the IPTW‐IPCW–adjusted pseudo‐population and compared these values between the ARB and CCB groups. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1.

Before weighting, modest imbalances were observed in certain baseline characteristics, reflecting routine prescribing patterns. For example, diuretic use and dyslipidemia were slightly more common in the ARB group. After application of IPTW weighting, all measured baseline covariates were well balanced, with SMDs below 0.10 for all variables. Median duration of follow‐up was 3.6 years, with interquartile range from 1.8 to 5.9 years.

Primary outcome: incident dementia

In the post‐induction ITT analysis, 3,524 incident dementia events occurred, including 1,127 among ARB users and 2,397 among CCB users. ARB initiation was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause dementia compared with CCB initiation (HR 0.916, 95% CI 0.852–0.985; Table 3). The estimated 5‐year cumulative incidence was 8.0% in the ARB group and 8.8% in the CCB group, corresponding to an absolute risk difference of −0.8 percentage points (95% CI −1.4 to −0.2). Weighted cumulative incidence curves showed gradual separation over time (Figure 1A).

In the PP analysis, sustained adherence to ARB therapy was also associated with a lower risk of dementia than sustained adherence to CCB therapy (HR 0.868, 95% CI 0.777–0.970; Table 3).

Dementia subtypes

Associations differed across dementia subtypes. For Alzheimer's disease, 2,231 events occurred, and the ITT association was not statistically significant (HR 0.930, 95% CI 0.849–1.018). For vascular dementia, 129 events occurred, and ARB initiation was associated with a lower risk (HR 0.617, 95% CI 0.409–0.930). For unspecified dementia, ARB initiation was also associated with a lower risk (HR 0.877, 95% CI 0.796–0.967). Frontotemporal dementia and Lewy body dementia events were infrequent, yielding imprecise estimates. PP analyses were generally directionally consistent with the ITT analyses for major dementia subtypes. Detailed estimates are shown in Table 3, and cumulative incidence patterns are shown in Figure 1.

Mortality and cardiovascular outcomes

After applying the 1‐month induction period, the ITT HRs for secondary mortality and cardiovascular outcomes were generally attenuated and did not reach statistical significance. The HR was 0.947 (95% CI 0.877–1.023) for all‐cause death, 1.042 (95% CI 0.988–1.099) for stroke, 0.954 (95% CI 0.885–1.028) for myocardial infarction, 0.935 (95% CI 0.860–1.016) for heart failure, and 0.909 (95% CI 0.666–1.241) for hospitalization for hypotension. Absolute 5‐year risk differences were small across these outcomes (Table 3). The cumulative incidence of mortality and cardiovascular outcomes is shown in Figure 2.

Blood pressure trajectories

In the weighted pseudo‐population, longitudinal mean systolic and diastolic blood pressure values were closely aligned between treatment groups throughout follow‐up (e‐Appendix D). Annual differences in systolic and diastolic blood pressure remained below 1.0 mmHg, indicating that differential achieved blood pressure control is unlikely to explain the observed dementia findings.

DISCUSSION

In this nationwide target trial emulation of older adults initiating antihypertensive therapy, ARB therapy was associated with a modest but statistically significant reduction in the risk of incident dementia compared with CCB therapy. The association was most pronounced for vascular dementia, whereas the reduction for Alzheimer's disease was smaller in magnitude and did not reach conventional statistical significance. The dementia findings were materially unchanged after applying a 1‐month induction period before outcome ascertainment. By contrast, the ITT HRs for secondary mortality and cardiovascular outcomes were attenuated and were no longer statistically significant. Importantly, achieved systolic and diastolic blood pressure levels remained closely aligned between treatment groups throughout follow‐up, suggesting that the observed dementia associations were unlikely to be explained by differential achieved blood pressure control.

The stronger association observed for vascular dementia provides biological plausibility for an ARB‐associated effect on cerebrovascular aging, rather than a general effect of blood pressure lowering alone. 25 Activation of the renin–angiotensin system promotes endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, arterial stiffness, and microvascular remodeling. 3 , 4 These processes are central to the development of cerebral small vessel disease, which in turn contributes to lacunar infarction, white matter injury, and cognitive decline. 12 , 14 Selective blockade of angiotensin II type 1 receptors by ARBs may attenuate these pathways and preserve microvascular integrity. By contrast, CCBs primarily reduce vascular resistance through inhibition of calcium influx in smooth muscle cells and do not directly target angiotensin II type 1 receptor signaling. The pattern of results observed in this study, with a larger relative reduction in vascular dementia and more modest associations for Alzheimer's disease, is consistent with a mechanism predominantly mediated through vascular pathways.

The comparatively smaller and statistically borderline association for Alzheimer's disease suggests that any potential neuroprotective effect of angiotensin receptor blockade on neurodegenerative processes may be indirect. Experimental models have suggested possible interactions between angiotensin signaling and amyloid metabolism, tau phosphorylation, and neuroinflammation. 12 However, clinical evidence remains limited and inconsistent. The present findings do not provide definitive support for a disease‐modifying effect on primary neurodegeneration but are compatible with a vascular contribution to cognitive aging that intersects with Alzheimer pathology. 3 , 24 Given the complex interplay between vascular and neurodegenerative mechanisms in late life, even modest vascular protection may translate into measurable reductions in clinical dementia incidence.

The secondary mortality and cardiovascular outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. In analyses incorporating the 1‐month induction period, the ITT HRs for these outcomes were attenuated and did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that same‐month cardiovascular events at treatment initiation may have contributed to the stronger associations observed in the original analysis, consistent with possible protopathic bias or event‐triggered treatment initiation. Heart failure itself has been linked to accelerated progression of cerebral small vessel disease and white matter injury, raising the possibility that part of the observed association between ARB therapy and lower dementia risk may be mediated through preservation of cardiac function. 34 Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases share common antecedents, including arterial stiffening, endothelial dysfunction, and chronic inflammation. The concept of integrated cardiovascular and brain aging recognizes that pathological processes affecting large and small vessels may simultaneously influence cardiac and cerebral outcomes. 4 , 24 The absence of meaningful differences in achieved blood pressure between treatment groups further supports the hypothesis that ARB‐associated pharmacological properties, rather than differences in blood pressure reduction, may underlie the observed outcome patterns. 25 , ,

From a clinical perspective, the absolute risk reduction was modest: the 5‐year difference in all‐cause dementia was approximately 0.8 percentage points. However, even small relative reductions may yield population‐level benefits in aging societies. Selection of antihypertensive drug class is common and modifiable, and incremental differences in long‐term cognitive outcomes may warrant consideration when multiple first‐line options are acceptable. 2 , 24 , 35

Current hypertension guidelines generally regard ARBs and CCBs as broadly equivalent first‐line agents in older adults without specific comorbid indications. 10 , 11 Our findings suggest that, in routine clinical practice, ARB therapy may be associated with more favorable long‐term outcome patterns beyond blood pressure control. 25 Recent evidence syntheses and large‐scale observational studies have reported heterogeneous associations across antihypertensive drug classes, with some suggesting potential advantages of ARBs, whereas others have shown little or no difference between treatment strategies. 17 , 18 , 19 , 36 , 37 , 38 These results do not imply that CCBs are ineffective or inappropriate. Rather, they suggest that ARB‐based therapy may have outcome patterns that are not fully explained by achieved blood pressure alone. Clinical decision making should continue to incorporate individual comorbidities, tolerability, patient preference, and overall risk profile.

Several strengths enhance causal interpretability. The new‐user active‐comparator design aligns treatment initiation with follow‐up, reducing prevalent‐user bias. 39 The target trial framework clarifies eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, and estimands. 22 , 23 Stabilized inverse probability weighting achieved good balance across a comprehensive set of demographic, clinical, medication, and frailty‐related covariates. Consideration of death as a competing risk is particularly important in older populations, in whom differential survival may distort associations with non‐fatal outcomes such as dementia. 30 Finally, the availability of longitudinal health examination data allowed verification that achieved blood pressure levels were comparable between treatment strategies in the weighted pseudo population.

Several limitations warrant careful consideration. As with all observational studies, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Socioeconomic status, educational attainment, detailed cognitive function at baseline, and blood pressure variability were not fully captured in the available data and may influence both treatment selection and dementia risk. Misclassification of dementia diagnoses and subtypes is possible when relying on administrative codes. 40 Although such misclassification is unlikely to differ systematically between treatment groups, nondifferential error could attenuate associations. The number of events for certain dementia subtypes, including frontotemporal and Lewy body dementia, was limited, resulting in wide confidence intervals. The study population consisted of individuals receiving care within the Japanese healthcare system, and extrapolation to other populations with different prescribing patterns or demographic characteristics should be undertaken cautiously. 41 Previous studies have also raised the clinically important question of whether ARBs and ACE inhibitors differ in their associations with dementia, despite their shared relationship to the renin–angiotensin system. 18 , 42 , 43 In contemporary Japanese practice, however, ACE inhibitors are initiated much less frequently than ARBs or CCBs, and only a limited number of eligible ACE‐inhibitor new users were identified under the same criteria in our database. We therefore focused on the more common and clinically relevant comparison of ARB‐ versus CCB‐based initiation strategies. Further studies specifically designed to compare ARBs with ACE inhibitors are warranted.

In conclusion, among adults aged 65 years or older initiating antihypertensive therapy, ARB‐based treatment was associated with a lower risk of incident dementia, particularly vascular dementia, compared with CCB‐based treatment. These associations were observed despite comparable achieved blood pressure levels during follow‐up, supporting the possibility that ARB‐based therapy may be associated with outcome differences beyond hemodynamic control. Selection of antihypertensive drug class, therefore, may represent a modifiable component of strategies aimed at promoting healthy cognitive aging in rapidly aging populations.
