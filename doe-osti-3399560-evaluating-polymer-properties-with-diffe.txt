 Evaluation of Polymer Properties with Different Additives for Carbon Capture and
 Other Applications

1. Motivation and Background

 Anthropogenic climate change is one of this generation’s most pressing concerns,
with the potential to completely alter the delicate balance we’ve struck with nature.
Already, global temperatures have risen 1.29°C, leading to disrupted weather systems,
extinctions, increased risks of wildﬁres, and sea level rise, to name a few effects. Carbon
dioxide emission from the combustion of fossil fuels and other industrial activity is a large
driver of this phenomenon, as it absorbs heat before it can be radiated away from Earth,
trapping it. Carbon dioxide has reached unprecedented levels in our atmosphere, showing
a 50% increase from preindustrial averages to a whopping 430 ppm. Thus, reducing the
amount of carbon dioxide via carbon capture technology is an important endeavor that
serves to beneﬁt everyone.

 Figure 1: Carbon dioxide emissions and atmospheric carbon dioxide levels since 1750

 The Microencapsulated CO2 Sorbent (MECS) team at Lawrence Livermore National
Laboratory (LLNL) has turned to microencapsulation to approach this endeavor.
Microcapsules provide an attractive approach to carbon capture, combining large surface

areas for more efficient mass transfer, regenerative abilities, reduced solvent loss, and
improved handling. Additionally, while existing carbon capture technology relies on
industrial plants, capsules could present a modular approach to carbon capture, reducing
the need for extensive physical infrastructure.

 The MECS team’s design consists of a polymer membrane that contains a liquid
carbon sequestering sorbent, aqueous sodium carbonate. The carbon capturing reaction
occurs in three distinct steps, the ﬁrst of which is the dissolution of carbon dioxide into the
sorbent solution and its conversion into carbonic acid (H2CO3), shown in equations 1 and 2
respectively. Because this step hinges upon the ability of carbon dioxide to reach the
solution inside the capsule, it is necessary that the microcapsule shell is permeable to
carbon dioxide gas.

 Figure 2: In-air droplet encapsulation apparatus

 The MECS team produces these microcapsules using the in-air droplet
encapsulation apparatus (IDEA) shown in ﬁgure 1, which can produce uniform micron-
scale droplets at speeds much faster than traditional single-dispersal microﬂuidic-based
techniques. The IDEA Is 100 times faster than these current techniques and can reach up
to 1000 times their speed when incorporating a multi-nozzle design. Additionally, because

droplets are produced in-air via vibration, IDEA can decrease post-processing times and
material waste by 99% and can fabricate microgels that are 10 to 100 times more viscous
than can be produced via traditional microﬂuidics.

 While this design represents a breakthrough in the throughput, efficiency, and
tunability of microcapsule production, it imposes a major constraint on the microcapsule
curing process. Because microcapsule shells are crosslinked with UV light while falling 30
cm through the air, this gives them a reaction window of approximately 0.2 seconds. Thus,
the system and shell formulations must be optimized such that the shells can be fully
crosslinked within this very narrow window, prompting investigations into curing behavior.

2. Materials and Mechanism

2.1 TEGO RAD

 The primary formulation component is TEGO RAD 2650, a silicone acrylate polymer.
It is sold commercially by Evonik as a radically cross linkable release additive and
possesses excellent slip and release properties, meaning it won’t stick to surfaces easily. It
also has valuable defoaming properties, which prevents structural weakness and
inhomogeneity from air bubbles in the ﬁnal cure. TEGO RAD 2650 is a proprietary chemical,
meaning that we are not provided with its exact chemical structure, but it has been cited as
being similar in structure TEGO RAD 2100 and 2500, shown in ﬁgure 3.

 Figure 3: Assumed TEGO RAD 2650 general chemical structure

 Here, TEGO RAD 2650 is assumed to have a comparable structure to TEGO RAD
2100 and 2500. According to patent information, the molecular weights of TEGO RAD 2100
and 2500 range from 1000 to 2500 g/mol. Additionally, n ranges from 0.5 to 5, m ranges
from 10 to 20, and the polymers are end-group functionalized with TMS. In all, this

indicates that TEGO RAD 2650 is likely composed of short silicone acrylate chains with
close control over TMS end groups

2.2 TPO-L

 Speedcure TPO-L from Arkema, chemical name ethyl phenyl(2,4,6-
trimehtylbenzoyl)phosphinate, is a Norrish Type 1 photoinitiator from the phosphine oxide
family with absorbance peaks at 274, 290, and 370 nm. This compound undergoes
homolytic cleavage (α-cleavage) when exposed to UV light, breaking the carbon-
phosphorous bond and generating a benzoyl and a phosphonyl radical that initiate
crosslinking. TPO-L and its cleavage mechanism are displayed in ﬁgure 4.

2.3 Carbon Fillers

 This study employs the use of three carbon ﬁllers to confer unique membrane
permeability properties, namely carbon black, reduced graphene oxide, and gas-
synthesized graphene. These have been chosen due to their hydrophobic properties and
potential to facilitate CO2 transport, supporting their application in the carbon-capture
process.

4. Results

4.1 Qualitative Curing Analysis

 Qualitative curing tests were employed to gain a general understanding of the
impacts of light intensity, photoinitiator concentration, and oxygen inhibition on the
formulation curing behavior. Qualitative curing tests allowed for the visualization of various
formulations’ surface tackiness/greasiness and bulk brittleness under different conditions.
While these results didn’t yield concrete data, they did provide valuable information

regarding initial experimental direction and placed following quantitative ﬁndings in the
context of these observations.

 This methodology employed the use of the Omnicure box, which contains a large UV
lamp that emits light at 365 nm at a maximum (100%) power of 8.4 mW/cm2. Directly
beneath the lamp within the box is a surface on which different formulations can be placed
to undergo UV curing. The box has been ﬁtted with a nitrogen inlet tube and has been
sealed with electrical tape such that it enables UV operation under purge and doesn’t leak
UV light.

 As expected, surface greasiness, which is understood to be a product of oxygen
inhibition, decreases as oxygen is removed from the environment (purging) and from within
the formulation (sparging). As we systematically reduce oxygen around and in the sample,
as shown in ﬁgure 36, there is a marked reduction in surface greasiness. The graphic
displays a progressive decrease in greasiness as more measures are taken to reduce
oxygen content. This is attributable to oxygen’s radical scavenging effect, as has been
established throughout this report. However, sparging, at least without a defoaming step

afterwards, might not be an ideal approach to reduce oxygen content, as it introduces
bubbles into the formulation. If not accounted for during preparation, these air pockets will
inevitably impact the membrane’s ﬁnal properties.

 Qualitative curing tests also yielded the observation that increasing the mass
fraction of photoinitiator seemed to increase the haziness of the mixture, 3wt% TPO-L
formulations appearing a pale-yellow color compared to the clear 0.5wt% TPO-L
formulation. This is thought to be due to the presence of an emulsion between TEGO RAD
and TPO-L, which shows a heightened visual effect at higher amounts of photoinitiator.
Although not visually apparent, conditions that yielded lower surface greasiness (higher
photoinitiator mass fraction, higher light intensity, more oxygen removal) seemed to make
the cured droplets more brittle. Upon removal from their glass slides, these droplets would
break apart more readily than their greasier counterparts. It is important to note that I’ve
highlighted the best examples of surface greasiness trends in the photos I’ve included thus
far, so I’ve included the full droplet library in ﬁgure 37.

 The calculated transmittances at 100 um for NEAT 0.5wt%, 1wt%, and 3wt% TPO-L
formulations, respectively, are 0.9991, 0.98, and 0.96. These values are very close to 1 at
the path length scales involved in microcapsule curing. This suggests that, at least for NEAT

means that any NEAT ﬁndings in further studies are likely not inﬂuenced majorly by optical
effects.

 Formulations containing carbon ﬁllers, on the other hand, attenuated light
enormously. This is likely due to the broadband light absorption commonly associated with
the micro- and sub-microscale carbon ﬁllers used, and the scattering effects caused by
the dispersion of these ﬁllers. They act like molecular mirrors and walls, preventing most of
the incident light from penetrating deep into the formulations.

 The absorbance values reported in table 1 are extremely large, at times even
saturating the detector on the nanodrop instrument. Additionally, the apparent light
attenuation appear to scale with the surface area of the ﬁllers and their ability to disperse
within the formulation. Carbon black, existing as a three dimensional aggregate in the
micron scale, has the lowest absorption reading, whereas GSG, with individual carbon

sheets less prone to agglomeration than RGO, has a very high reading. Because of this, it
can be predicted that carbon ﬁllers will play a large role in the curing speed, as locations
deeper within the formulation will be receiving signiﬁcantly less light than its surface. This
will be especially pronounced as light attenuation increases. Thus, it can be useful to
assess light attenuation as a predictor for curing speeds, which can be a useful metric for
determining the viability of carbon ﬁllers for use in microcapsules.

5.3 Photorheology

 Rheology is the study of how materials ﬂow and deform in response to stress. A
rheometer uses an attachment, usually a ﬂat, circular plate, connected to a spinning axle
that applies torque to a sample beneath the attachment. The material’s response to that
shear stress contains important information about that material including its viscoelastic
properties, yield stress, or shear thinning/thickening. Photorheology is an extension of
traditional rheology and measures how the response to stress changes in the presence of
light. This is especially relevant to the current study of how differences in formulation and
light input impact the crosslinking process.

 Because I was primarily concerned with the curing process, I used a rheological
sampling technique that would allow me to see how material properties changed over time
as the formulation changed from a liquid matrix to a solid network. I used an oscillatory
time sweep, meaning the rheometer would produce an oscillatory torque at a set frequency
and strain for a set period, collecting data over that time. I irradiated the sample after an
initial equilibration period, which then induced a change in material properties as
crosslinking occurred.

 The data I got from this experiment was in the form of storage (G’) and loss (G”)
moduli, which told me how the material stored and dissipated energy. If the loss modulus
was larger than the storage modulus at a given timepoint, this indicated that the material
was still liquid-like, as it was dissipating energy as heat via polymers sliding past one
another. This meant that the network had not yet reached a critical crosslink density (or
molecular weight) to store energy within chemical bonds. The opposite was true for points
at which the storage modulus exceeded the loss modulus – energy was stored as elastic
strain energy within chemical bonds, which indicates network formation. The point in the
curing process at which the storage modulus reaches and begins to overtake the loss
modulus is the crossover point and indicates the beginning of gelation, the formation of a
solid network. Crossover point is a major metric used in this study to evaluate the impacts
of different variables on curing speed. The storage modulus continues to rise as

crosslinking continues and eventually reaches a plateau where there is no further change
in material properties associated with continued irradiation. There could still be
crosslinking occurring at this point, but it doesn’t contribute to the stiffness of the network.

 This technique doesn’t give us deﬁnitive information about the crosslinking kinetics
or behavior of the system, but it gives us a tool to see how different conditions alter the
dosage needed for gelation and to achieve ﬁnal material properties. The gel point and
storage modulus plateau are very important to microcapsule function, so rheology is a
valuable tool in quickly assessing these. All photorheology results are averaged from three
triplicate runs, and the standard error is displayed graphically although is not always visible
due to its small magnitude.

 Figures 20 and 21 display the effect of light intensity on the crossover time of NEAT
formulations with varying photoinitiator concentrations. Supporting preliminary results
from previous rheology studies, crossover time decreases as light intensity increases for all
TPO-L concentrations in NEAT formulations. Within the range of light intensities tested, the
NEAT 0.5wt% TPO-L formulation shows the most marked decrease in crossover time from
1.015 to 54.2 mW/cm2. In fact, this sample showed a 94% reduction in crossover time at
54.2 mW/cm2 when compared to the lowest light intensity. Light intensity produced a
weaker effect on crossover time as photoinitiator concentration increased. However, even
at 3wt% TPO-L, 54.2 mW/cm2 light caused a 90% reduction in crossover time compared to
1.015 mW/cm2. This trend is consistent with our understanding of radical crosslinking
mechanisms, where radical generation is required for the initiation of crosslinking. Higher
light intensities bombard the sample with more photons per unit time, resulting in a higher
concentration of free radicals due to photoinitiator cleavage and thus a higher crosslinking
rate.

 The role of oxygen inhibition on curing behavior was also investigated via rheology.
Filled formulations were purged while on the rheometer until the concentration of oxygen in
the air reached 0%. This resulted in faster curing onset times among purged samples when
compared to samples allowed to cure under ambient conditions. These results are
consistent with predictions based on prior knowledge – without oxygen to quickly scavenge
radicals and terminate chain growth early on, crosslinks can form without the lag period
necessary for consuming oxygen. Additionally, when purged membranes were removed
from the rheometer, they seemed to be slightly more brittle than non-purged samples and
lacked the characteristic surface greasiness caused by the oxygen inhibition layer. It
follows that further oxygen removal efforts, such as sparging or vacuum degassing, will
amplify these effects.

 An interesting observation made during rheology experiments was that the total
dosage of light required for crossover increased with the intensity of the light. While it is
expected that formulations slower to crossover require a higher total dosage of light, at
higher light intensities, all samples received a higher dosage before crossing over. This
could be due to a delayed rheological response at higher intensities, where rapid
photopolymerization produces a spatially or temporally nonuniform network before the
material can respond mechanically, requiring additional exposure for the measured
storage modulus to overtake the loss modulus. This could be compounded by the
inefficient delivery of light at higher intensities, where increased photoinitiator depletion,
oxygen inhibition, heating, or nonlinear attenuation limits the effective conversion per unit
of incident dose.

5.4 Microcompression Testing

 Microcompression testing applies a uniaxial strain force to a sample, reporting the
applied force required to displace a material a certain amount. This conveys how stiff the
material becomes after reaching curing and allows for the comparison of mechanical
performance across formulations. In this study, a ﬂat punch tip with a diameter of 500
microns was used to compress samples with a uniform loading rate between samples. This
technique is a natural follow-up to photorheology, which gave me bulk material properties,

as it tests the material properties of actual particles. This provides valuable information on
how actual spherical carbon capsules will respond to external forces when in use, not just
their membranes in isolation. Droplets cured at different intensities with different
formulations were compared to assess their effects on particle properties.

 Samples for microcompression testing were generated via a simple co-ﬂow
microﬂuidic droplet generator, which utilized hand-crafted borosilicate glass capillary
nozzles and channels. My device could generate single emulsion droplets with diameters
ranging from 200-400 um in a 55/45 glycerol/water continuous phase. This line was then
run through a curing device with a maximum light output of 8.4 W/cm2, providing an
estimated 5 second curing time. My curing setup, microﬂuidic device, and example
droplets are pictured below.

6. Conclusions and Future Directions

 My research revealed many important features of the microcapsule shell system,
information that can be used to streamline further optimization efforts. From my
experiments, I determined that higher weight fractions of photoinitiator result in faster
curing times and reduce the oxygen inhibition effect associated with radical crosslinking
mechanisms. In cases where light is strongly attenuated by the presence of ﬁllers, higher
weight fractions of TPO-L photoinitiator will become necessary to achieve a full cure within
the 0.2 second window afforded by the IDEA box. However, overloading samples with
photoinitiator can lead to undesirable impacts in the curing process and ﬁnal cured
products, requiring further studies on the maximum beneﬁcial dosage of photoinitiator.

 I also found that cure speed scales with light intensity following a power law
relationship, where higher light intensities result in faster curing. However, the
improvements in cure speed offered by increasing light intensity taper off as light intensity
continues to increase. There is likely a point at which radical generation poses no
additional beneﬁt to curing, owing to a kinetic barrier. Additionally, with higher light
intensities, the total dosages required to reach ﬁnal material properties increase as well,
suggesting an underlying chemical or physical mechanism that either stems from kinetic
constraints or optical effects. Disperse carbon ﬁllers are known broad-band light
absorbers, meaning that they decrease the effective concentration of light penetrating the
sample during curing. The impact on curing speed associated with carbon ﬁllers appears to

scale with light attenuation, although this relationship should be further studied such that
one can easily select optimal ﬁller loadings. Carbon ﬁllers could also possess a chemical
mechanism that inhibits the curing process, warranting further study.

 Because oxygen is a radical-hungry molecule, radicals generated in the presence of
oxygen lead to the consumption of those radicals and termination of cross-linking
reactions. This results in an initial lag phase in curing, as the system needs to consume
oxygen via radical generation before the formulations can crosslink and reach a critical
molecular weight that corresponds to gelation. Oxygen inhibition doesn’t appear to impact
the time it takes to reach ﬁnal material properties, however. This mechanism should be
further studied. Further techniques used to remove oxygen from this system should be
evaluated, as these could reduce capsule greasiness and improve gelation times without
the need to fundamentally alter the chemistry of the system.

 Light intensity, photoinitiator concentration, and oxygen removal seem to have little
impact on ﬁnal material stiffness beyond their effects on creating a fully cured material.
Carbon ﬁllers could potentially reinforce the polymer networks, leading to increased
material stiffness and potentially strength, although this needs to be further investigated.
Droplet properties also don’t seem to scale as expected with diameter, owing to potential
viscoelastic properties or curing gradients within the polymer network, prompting further
investigation. Mechanical testing on microcapsules instead would be a great medium for
future studies, as these would more closely model the behavior of the intended
technology.

 In all, it appears that 3wt% TPO-L (or higher) and oxygen removal are ideal for this
system. As always, there is much work to be done to bring this technology from the lab to
the wider world, so continued optimization, cost analysis, and research remain necessary.
