Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential in Cardiovascular Disease: Gene-Specific Mechanisms and Therapeutic Implications
Authors: Xuejiao Zhang, Hongguang Zhang, Jia Fu, Shuo Feng, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP) denotes the age-associated expansion of hematopoietic clones carrying acquired somatic variants, conventionally identified at a variant allele frequency of at least 2% in people without overt hematologic malignancy. Once viewed mainly through the lens of premalignant hematology, CHIP is now repeatedly linked to excess cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. The earliest cardiovascular signal centered on atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), but the phenotype has broadened to include calcific aortic valve disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, and venous thromboembolism. Cardiovascular biology also differs substantially by driver gene. TET2 loss-of-function favors inflammasome-linked IL-1β/IL-6 signaling and has been connected to plaque vulnerability and myocardial inflammation; DNMT3A mutations reshape myeloid epigenetic programs through overlapping but non-identical inflammatory states; ASXL1 mutations promote myeloid-skewed hematopoiesis and have been associated with adverse cardiac remodeling and arrhythmia susceptibility; and JAK2 V617F, a prototypic gain-of-function lesion, produces a particularly thromboinflammatory profile through amplified NET formation. Although these drivers diverge upstream, several CHIP subtypes ultimately converge on inflammatory effector pathways involving IL-1β and IL-6, supporting the concept of anti-inflammatory precision prevention. Exploratory CANTOS analyses suggest that IL-1β inhibition may be especially beneficial in TET2-CHIP carriers, but this observation remains hypothesis-generating and requires adequately powered, CHIP-stratified outcome trials. Current translational work includes CHIP-enriched studies of inflammasome and IL-1–pathway blockade, ongoing large outcome trials targeting IL-6 in residual inflammatory risk, and gene-informed strategies for thromboinflammatory CHIP subtypes. This article is a narrative review that synthesizes mechanistic, epidemiologic, and translational evidence rather than a systematic review based on a formal search strategy. This review develops a gene-centric framework that connects molecular mechanisms, cardiovascular phenotypes, and emerging therapeutic opportunities, while emphasizing the evidence gaps that must be closed before CHIP-directed precision cardiology can be considered clinically actionable.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disorders continue to account for the largest share of global mortality despite major advances in the control of traditional risk factors. Intensive lipid lowering, blood pressure control, and glycemic optimization reduce risk but do not eliminate it, leaving a clinically meaningful residual burden that is increasingly attributed to chronic low-grade inflammation. CANTOS provided direct clinical support for this concept: canakinumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting IL-1β, reduced recurrent cardiovascular events without lowering LDL-C, thereby separating anti-inflammatory benefit from lipid modification.1 The upstream molecular sources of this residual inflammatory risk, however, remain only partly defined.

During the past decade, hematology has provided an unexpected entry point into this question. CHIP describes clonal outgrowth of blood cells carrying leukemia-associated acquired mutations in individuals who otherwise lack diagnostic hematologic abnormalities; it becomes more common with aging and is linked to an increased long-term risk of hematologic malignancy, although the absolute rate of progression in otherwise asymptomatic CHIP is generally estimated at approximately 0.5–1% per year.2–5 In the pivotal 2017 analysis by Jaiswal et al, nested case-control studies within two prospective cohorts showed that CHIP carriers had approximately twice the incidence of coronary heart disease observed in non-carriers (hazard ratio [HR] 1.90; 95% CI, 1.40–2.70).6 Subsequent work has broadened the cardiovascular spectrum from coronary atherosclerosis to heart failure, atrial fibrillation, calcific aortic valve disease, stroke, and venous thromboembolism. Smoking is particularly relevant in this framework because it is both a major traditional cardiovascular risk factor and a recognized exposure associated with clonal hematopoiesis, with a notable association between smoking history and ASXL1-mutant clonal states. This overlap reinforces the need to interpret CHIP within the broader cardiometabolic and environmental risk context rather than as an isolated molecular finding.7

The key conceptual shift is that CHIP should not be treated as a single biological exposure. The recurrent drivers, particularly DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and JAK2, differ in molecular mechanism, clinical risk profile, and therapeutic implications.8,9 Human observational studies increasingly support CHIP as an independent predictor of onset or progression across atherosclerotic disease, heart failure, and arrhythmia, but many reviews still aggregate these mutations under a single CHIP label. Such aggregation risks obscuring gene-resolved phenotypes and may weaken the rationale for precision anti-inflammatory or antithrombotic interventions.

This review therefore uses a gene-centric structure. It integrates epidemiology, molecular biology, cardiovascular phenotypes, and therapeutic development, and then outlines a translational pathway from mechanistic evidence to clinically testable precision strategies. At the same time, CHIP should not yet be interpreted as a stand-alone indication for routine cardiovascular screening or treatment escalation. Its incremental value beyond established risk markers, including age, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and imaging-defined atherosclerotic burden, remains incompletely defined. Much of the human evidence is also observational and remains vulnerable to residual confounding, differences in sequencing depth, ancestry imbalance, clone-size thresholds, and heterogeneity in cardiovascular endpoint definitions. Defining CHIP: Epidemiology, Detection, and Clonal Dynamics

Definition and Diagnostic Criteria

CHIP is conventionally defined as the presence of a leukemia-associated somatic mutation in blood-derived DNA at a variant allele frequency (VAF) of at least 2% in an individual without otherwise unexplained cytopenia, morphologic dysplasia, or diagnostic evidence of an overt myeloid neoplasm.2,3,5 The 2% threshold is best understood as a practical convention inherited from earlier next-generation sequencing limits, rather than as a proven biological boundary. Error-corrected targeted sequencing has shown that far smaller clones are widespread in ostensibly healthy adults. In banked longitudinal samples from twenty healthy 50–60-year-old Nurses’ Health Study participants, deep sequencing detected low-level clonal mutations in nearly all individuals examined, most often involving DNMT3A or TET2 mutations.10 These data support a continuum model of clonal hematopoiesis while preserving “CHIP” as the operational ≥2% category used in most cardiovascular outcome studies.11 When analyses use clone-size strata, such as “large CHIP” defined by VAF ≥10%, the threshold should be stated explicitly.

Clonal hematopoiesis (CH) is a broader umbrella term that denotes the presence of expanded blood-cell clones carrying somatic genomic alterations and does not necessarily require the conventional ≥2% VAF threshold used for CHIP; it may also include mosaic chromosomal alterations and other clonal events. CHIP refers more specifically to clonal hematopoiesis with leukemia-associated somatic variants in individuals without otherwise unexplained cytopenia or diagnostic evidence of a myeloid neoplasm. By contrast, clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance (CCUS) describes persistent unexplained cytopenia together with a myeloid-associated clonal mutation, but without sufficient morphologic or genetic criteria for overt myelodysplastic syndrome or another myeloid neoplasm. Overt myeloid neoplasia requires disease-defining clinical, morphologic, cytogenetic, or molecular criteria and should not be conflated with CHIP.5 The ≥10% VAF threshold commonly used to define “large CHIP” is not a separate diagnostic category, but it has been used in cardiovascular analyses because larger clones may better capture biologically expanded hematopoiesis and have shown stronger associations with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in several cohorts.6,12

Prevalence and Demographic Variation

Conventional next-generation sequencing (NGS) detects CHIP in roughly one in ten adults older than 70 years, with estimates in the oldest age groups approaching 20% depending on assay design and sensitivity.2,3 Age remains the dominant epidemiologic gradient: prevalence is rare before age 40, rises to about 10–15% by the seventh decade, and can exceed 50% in adults aged 80 years or older when error-corrected deep sequencing and lower VAF cutoffs are applied.11,13 Population-scale studies also show that driver distributions vary by cohort, ascertainment strategy, and ancestry composition. DNMT3A is the leading CHIP driver in most large datasets, followed by TET2 and ASXL1, although proportions are not fixed across studies.8,9 In a UK Biobank analysis restricted to cancer patients, DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1 accounted for 52.1%, 22.4%, and 13.7% of CHIP carriers, respectively.14 Assertions about ancestry-specific enrichment should therefore be limited to datasets with driver-resolved, ancestry-stratified sequencing. Prevalence estimates are strongly platform-dependent: whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing generally captures larger clones, whereas targeted deep sequencing, especially with error correction, detects substantially smaller clones and therefore yields higher apparent prevalence.9,11,15,16

Detection Platforms

CHIP detection has been performed using whole-genome sequencing (WGS), whole-exome sequencing, targeted deep sequencing panels, and digital droplet PCR. Population-scale WGS, as illustrated by Bick et al, has enabled CHIP calling across tens of thousands of whole genomes, while also supporting analyses of inherited influences on clonal hematopoiesis.9 Whole-exome sequencing offers broad genomic coverage but has limited sensitivity for low-VAF variants. Targeted deep sequencing panels, particularly with error-correction, can detect variants near 0.1–0.5% VAF.15,16 Digital droplet PCR provides very high sensitivity for predefined hotspots, most notably JAK2 V617F. For clinical research, targeted panels spanning 20–50 recurrently mutated genes usually provide the most practical balance between breadth, depth, cost, and interpretability.17 A major translational limitation is that CHIP testing has not yet achieved the level of assay standardization and clinical validation seen in pharmacogenomic or germline cancer-risk testing. These platform differences are not merely technical details; they influence apparent CHIP prevalence, driver-gene distribution, clone-size classification, and comparability across cardiovascular cohorts.

Clonal Dynamics Over Time

Serial sequencing shows that CHIP clones often expand over time, frequently following approximately exponential growth, although stable or contracting trajectories also occur, especially when multiple clones coexist.18 Expansion rates differ markedly by driver gene, variant, and individual context. Faster growth predicts malignant progression more clearly than cardiovascular events; whether longitudinal growth metrics add cardiovascular prognostic value beyond a single VAF measurement remains unresolved. Competition among coexisting clones further complicates interpretation, because clones within the same individual may expand, remain stable, or decline over time.19 These observations argue for serial sampling in future mechanistic and interventional studies rather than reliance on a one-time threshold alone.

Gene-Specific Molecular Mechanisms

TET2: The Paradigmatic CHIP–Cardiovascular Link

TET2 encodes a Fe (II)- and 2-oxoglutarate–dependent dioxygenase that catalyzes oxidation of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, an important step in the 5mC oxidation pathway and active DNA demethylation.20 Disruption of TET2 gives hematopoietic stem cells a competitive self-renewal advantage and favors myeloid-biased output.21 The cardiovascular relevance of this biology was established experimentally in 2017: chimeric mice carrying only a minority fraction of Tet2-deficient marrow developed selective expansion of the mutant hematopoietic compartment together with substantially larger atherosclerotic lesions.22 Tet2-deficient macrophages showed enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activity and greater IL-1β secretion. The selective NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 produced stronger atheroprotection in mice with Tet2-deficient hematopoiesis than in wild-type marrow controls, directly implicating inflammasome signaling in TET2-driven atherogenesis.

Human studies have extended this experimental paradigm in several complementary settings. In the original coronary disease analyses, TET2 was among the individual CHIP drivers associated with coronary heart disease, supporting a gene-resolved rather than purely aggregate CHIP signal.6 In secondary-prevention ASCVD cohorts, CHIP has been associated with higher event rates and mortality, with stronger associations observed for larger clones in several analyses.12 More recent human plaque studies also suggest that TET2-mutated CHIP may be linked to plaque vulnerability, macrophage lipid handling, and inflammatory pathway activation.23 Finally, in the exploratory CANTOS genomic substudy, TET2 variants were enriched among CHIP carriers in an inflammation-selected trial population, and TET2-CHIP carriers showed a nominally greater reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) with canakinumab, although formal interaction testing was not significant and the finding remains hypothesis-generating.24

Together, bone-marrow chimerism experiments and emerging human plaque data support a model in which TET2 loss reshapes myeloid cells toward inflammasome-linked IL-1β signaling and pro-atherogenic macrophage states, thereby contributing to accelerated atherogenesis and plaque inflammation.

DNMT3A: Frequent but Functionally Complex

DNMT3A encodes a de novo DNA methyltransferase and is the most frequently mutated CHIP/CH gene, accounting for roughly half of CH cases in large population datasets.8 DNMT3A loss-of-function increases competitive fitness in hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, expanding the hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) compartment and impairing differentiation in serial transplantation models.25 In myeloid cells, DNMT3A loss produces context-dependent inflammatory transcriptional programs. In atherosclerosis models, this translates into accelerated lesion development and parallel changes in immune composition within atheroma, including a chemokine-enriched resident-like macrophage population in the adventitia identified by single-cell transcriptomics and spatial multiplexed imaging.26

Despite being the most common CHIP driver, DNMT3A-CHIP should not be assumed to carry the strongest or most uniform cardiovascular risk. Across available cohorts, its associations appear phenotype-dependent and are often weaker or less consistent than those observed for TET2, ASXL1, or JAK2 V617F in selected settings, including incident heart failure and atrial fibrillation.27–29 This heterogeneity implies that mutation frequency, clonal fitness, and cardiovascular pathogenicity are not interchangeable concepts, and that DNMT3A and TET2 may share elements of inflammatory reprogramming while differing in effect size, tissue context, or downstream mediators. Variant-level heterogeneity may also matter. The DNMT3A R882 hotspot, including R882H, has dominant-negative activity in biochemical and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) contexts and may not be equivalent to other loss-of-function variants.30 Large cardiovascular datasets with mutation-site resolution are still needed.

ASXL1: Myeloid Skewing and Cardiac Remodeling

ASXL1 is another recurrent CHIP driver, usually represented by truncating nonsense or frameshift variants in CHIP datasets. In vivo models expressing C-terminal–truncated Asxl1 show myeloid-skewed hematopoiesis, while Asxl1-mutant macrophage-lineage cells can adopt a pro-inflammatory cytokine program, including increased IL-1β and IL-6 production.31

Experimental work further supports a causal role for ASXL1-mediated clonal hematopoiesis in adverse cardiac remodeling. In murine models of myocardial infarction induced by LAD ligation and angiotensin II–mediated hypertensive injury, Asxl1-mutant hematopoiesis worsened left ventricular dysfunction and was accompanied by greater myocardial inflammation and fibrosis.32 Human epidemiology is directionally consistent. In a five-cohort meta-analysis of 56,597 participants, ASXL1-CHIP was associated with incident heart failure, and secondary UK Biobank imaging analyses linked ASXL1 mutations to lower left ventricular ejection fraction.27 Large ASXL1-CHIP has also been associated with incident atrial fibrillation, although direct mechanisms connecting ASXL1-mutant hematopoiesis to atrial remodeling remain unresolved.28

JAK2 V617F: A Unique Prothrombotic Phenotype

Among common CHIP drivers, JAK2 V617F is distinctive because it is a gain-of-function lesion that constitutively activates JAK-STAT signaling. Its cardiovascular phenotype is therefore more thromboinflammatory than that of the epigenetic regulators. Evidence from myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) and JAK2 V617F model systems indicates that mutant neutrophils are primed for neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and NETosis. Conditional Jak2V617F knock-in mice show increased NET formation and thrombotic susceptibility, whereas pharmacologic JAK inhibition with ruxolitinib suppresses NET release and reduces thrombosis in a deep vein stenosis model.33 Peptidylarginine deiminase 4 (PAD4), an enzyme required for histone citrullination, chromatin decondensation, and NET formation, appears central to this phenotype: JAK2 V617F-mutant neutrophils up-regulate PAD4, and PAD4 is required for mutant JAK2-driven NETosis and thrombosis in vivo.33 Consistent with these experimental observations, a population-scale analysis of individuals without known myeloid disease linked JAK2-mutant clonal hematopoiesis most strongly to venous thromboembolism (VTE), including prevalent and incident events, while the evidence for arterial thrombosis in CHIP remains supported more by myeloproliferative neoplasm biology and preclinical arterial-injury models than by large CHIP-specific clinical datasets.34

JAK2-CHIP may also shape arterial thrombosis. In a murine superficial plaque erosion model, Jak2V617F hematopoiesis aggravated endothelial barrier disruption after experimental injury and increased arterial thrombus formation.35 The neointima showed more apoptosis, NETosis, and platelet recruitment, supporting a mechanistic bridge between mutant myeloid activation and erosion-associated thrombosis. Ruxolitinib partially reversed this injury phenotype, improving endothelial continuity and reducing neointimal apoptosis toward levels observed in wild-type controls.35 These data provide preclinical proof-of-concept for JAK-STAT pathway modulation in JAK2 V617F-driven arterial thromboinflammation, while leaving clinical feasibility and safety unresolved.

Emerging Driver Genes: PPM1D, TP53, and Spliceosome Mutations

Less common CHIP drivers may still carry important cardiovascular implications. PPM1D, a DNA damage response phosphatase, and TP53 are enriched after cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation and are prominent in therapy-related clonal hematopoiesis.36 TP53-mediated clonal hematopoiesis has been associated with incident atherosclerotic disease in observational analyses.37 Spliceosome mutations, including SF3B1, SRSF2, and U2AF1, form another functional category. Their cardiovascular biology is less mature, but each has been linked to higher mortality in a coronary artery disease cohort, where PPM1D mutations also showed an individual association with mortality.23

The Common Terminal Pathway: NLRP3–IL-1β–IL-6

Although CHIP drivers differ upstream, several converge on inflammasome-derived IL-1β and downstream IL-6 signaling. The dominant inflammasome axis, however, may vary by gene and context. In experimental systems, TET2 deficiency enhances NLRP3 inflammasome activation, including through BRCC3-mediated NLRP3 deubiquitylation under hypercholesterolemic conditions.38 DNMT3A loss induces partially overlapping inflammatory programs in myeloid cells, but downstream effectors appear context dependent.4 JAK2 V617F-driven clonal hematopoiesis has also been connected to inflammasome activation in atherosclerosis models, with prominent involvement of the AIM2 inflammasome and IL-1β signaling.39 This convergence supports therapeutic interest in the IL-1β/IL-6 effector axis, while the upstream divergence argues against treating all CHIP genotypes as therapeutically interchangeable.

CHIP and Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD)

Coronary Artery Disease

Evidence linking CHIP to coronary disease is broad and increasingly consistent. Early nested case-control analyses established the baseline signal, showing that CHIP carriers had roughly double the incidence of coronary heart disease compared with non-carriers.6 In retrospective cohorts focused on early-onset myocardial infarction, CHIP conferred an approximately fourfold higher infarction risk (HR 4.00; 95% CI, 2.40–6.70).6 The association was not confined to a single gene: DNMT3A, TET2, ASXL1, and JAK2 mutations were each individually linked to coronary heart disease. Carriers also showed greater coronary-artery calcification, supporting a relationship with atherosclerotic burden.

Secondary-prevention cohorts reinforce this relationship. In a UK Biobank analysis of 13,129 individuals with established ASCVD, baseline detection of any CHIP was associated with an adjusted HR of 1.23 (95% CI, 1.10–1.38) for the composite of ASCVD events and all-cause mortality over a median 10.8 years; large CHIP showed a stronger association (HR 1.34; 95% CI, 1.17–1.53).12 Across incident and secondary-prevention settings, risk appears to scale with clone size, and imaging studies suggest greater atherosclerotic burden in individuals with larger clones.

Human plaque biology is beginning to align with the epidemiology. A European Heart Journal study associated CHIP with higher mortality and plaque features consistent with vulnerability, with the strongest signals in TET2-mutated cases. Functional assays in Tet2-deficient macrophages suggested increased LDLR expression, lipid uptake, and inflammatory pathway activation.23 However, direct evidence separating plaque rupture, plaque erosion, and stable stenosis in humans remains limited.

Peripheral Arterial Disease and Stroke

Non-coronary arterial beds have been studied less extensively, but the signal is expanding. Population-based analyses associate CHIP with incident stroke, although associations may differ by stroke subtype. Among patients with incident ischemic stroke, clonal hematopoiesis, especially larger clones and TET2- or PPM1D-mutated clones, has been linked to recurrent vascular events and death. Directionality is still debated. Experimental and longitudinal human evidence supports a contribution of clonal hematopoiesis to atherosclerosis, while atherosclerosis-related hematopoietic stress may in turn accelerate clonal expansion.22,40,41 Population-scale studies have also reported associations with peripheral arterial disease (PAD), but gene-specific evidence in this vascular territory remains limited. Zekavat et al further extended the relevance of CHIP to peripheral arterial disease by analyzing whole-exome sequencing and clinical data from the UK Biobank and Mass General Brigham Biobank. In that study, CHIP was associated with incident PAD and atherosclerotic disease across multiple arterial beds, with particularly increased risk among individuals carrying DNA-damage response gene mutations such as TP53 and PPM1D. Experimental modeling with p53-deficient hematopoiesis in atherosclerosis-prone mice showed increased aortic plaque burden and macrophage accumulation, supporting a plausible causal contribution of TP53-mutant clonal hematopoiesis to atherosclerosis beyond the coronary circulation.37

CHIP as a Molecular Substrate for Residual Inflammatory Risk

CHIP is increasingly interpreted as one molecular contributor to residual inflammatory risk after optimal lipid lowering. Earlier, JUPITER showed that individuals with elevated hsCRP despite relatively low LDL-C derived cardiovascular benefit from rosuvastatin, supporting hsCRP as a clinically useful marker of residual inflammatory risk, although this trial was not CHIP-specific.42 In CANTOS, patients with prior myocardial infarction and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) ≥2 mg/L benefited from IL-1β inhibition, supporting inflammation as a modifiable pathway in secondary prevention.1 TET2-CHIP is biologically well positioned within this framework because of its link to inflammasome and IL-1β signaling. In the exploratory CANTOS sequencing analysis, TET2 variants outnumbered DNMT3A variants among CHIP carriers, a pattern compatible with enrichment of TET2-CHIP in an inflammation-selected trial population, although not sufficient on its own to prove causal enrichment.24

CHIP and Heart Failure

Epidemiologic Evidence

Multiple prospective cohorts support an association between CHIP and incident heart failure. In a fixed-effect meta-analysis of five cohorts including 56,597 participants, CHIP was present in 3406 individuals (6%), while 4694 participants (8.3%) developed heart failure during follow-up extending up to two decades. The pooled estimate showed a 25% relative excess risk for incident heart failure (HR 1.25; 95% CI, 1.13–1.38). Gene-resolved analyses localized the signal mainly to ASXL1, TET2, and JAK2 rather than DNMT3A.27

A later UK Biobank analysis of 417,616 participants reported a similar overall association with incident heart failure (adjusted HR 1.27; 95% CI, 1.15–1.40; P < 0.001). The excess risk was concentrated in non-DNMT3A CHIP (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR] 1.52; 95% CI, 1.33–1.75; P < 0.001), whereas DNMT3A CHIP showed only a borderline and smaller effect estimate (aHR 1.15; 95% CI, 1.00–1.31).29 In UK Biobank participants with diabetes, CHIP also tracked with both incident cardiovascular disease (CVD) and heart failure (HF): any CHIP was associated with HRs of 1.21 (95% CI, 1.08–1.36) for CVD and 1.73 (95% CI, 1.46–2.06) for HF, while large CHIP showed corresponding HRs of 1.25 (95% CI, 1.09–1.43) and 1.77 (95% CI, 1.45–2.15).43

CHIP and Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF): An Emerging Paradigm

The link between TET2-CHIP and HFpEF is especially notable. Across two large and diverse cohorts, TET2 clones were associated with approximately 2.4-fold higher HFpEF incidence.44 This finding fits the current view of HFpEF as a heterogeneous, comorbidity-driven syndrome in which systemic inflammation and microvascular dysfunction contribute to myocardial remodeling.45 Disease-modifying options for HFpEF have expanded, including SGLT2 inhibitors in guideline-based care46 and incretin-based therapies in obesity-related HFpEF, with semaglutide improving symptoms and functional capacity in STEP-HFpEF47 and tirzepatide reducing cardiovascular death or worsening heart failure in SUMMIT.48 However, no trial has tested whether CHIP carriers, or specific CHIP genotypes such as TET2-CHIP, derive differential benefit from SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonism, or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonism. CHIP should therefore be viewed as a plausible inflammatory stratifier for future HFpEF studies, not as a current determinant of HFpEF therapy selection.

Mechanistic Insights from Murine Models

Preclinical studies provide mechanistic support for CHIP-associated cardiac dysfunction. In Sano et al’s pressure-overload model, mice reconstituted with a competitive Tet2-deficient marrow fraction developed more severe systolic dysfunction than wild-type marrow controls. Across several cardiac injury paradigms, Tet2-deficient hematopoiesis intensified adverse remodeling, including hypertrophy, fibrosis, and contractile impairment, through an IL-1β/NLRP3-dependent pathway.49 Parallel work has shown that Jak2V617F-mediated clonal hematopoiesis accelerates pathological remodeling in murine heart failure,50 and that Asxl1-mediated clonal hematopoiesis promotes inflammation and experimental heart failure progression.32

Heart Failure Progression and Prognosis

In established heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, CHIP has been associated with worse outcomes. Among 62 patients with HF and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <45%, DNMT3A or TET2 mutations identified a subgroup with accelerated clinical progression. CHIP mutations were detected in 24 patients (38.7%); after risk-factor adjustment, DNMT3A or TET2 mutation carriers had higher risks of death (HR 2.79; 95% CI, 1.31–5.92; P = 0.008) and the composite of death or HF hospitalization (HR 3.84; 95% CI, 1.84–8.04; P < 0.001).51 In chronic ischemic heart failure, DNMT3A and TET2 clone size also predicted clinical deterioration.52,53

CHIP and Atrial Fibrillation (AF)

Beyond structural myocardial disease, CHIP is increasingly linked to atrial fibrillation (AF), plausibly through systemic and local inflammatory remodeling of the atrial substrate. CHIP-associated myeloid inflammation may promote arrhythmogenic conditions, while experimental data implicate inflammasome signaling, particularly NLRP3, and altered cardiomyocyte Ca2+ handling as candidate mediators. Evidence that CHIP directly drives atrial fibrosis or oxidative stress remains more indirect and is likely to vary by gene and disease context.54

Lin et al provided direct experimental evidence in Circulation. Hematopoietic-restricted Tet2 inactivation increased pacing-induced AF susceptibility in Ldlr−/− mice. The phenotype was driver-specific: analogous Dnmt3a inactivation in the hematopoietic compartment did not increase AF inducibility, emphasizing that CHIP-associated arrhythmia biology cannot be inferred from CHIP positivity alone.55

Observational cohorts and Mendelian-randomization analyses converge on an association between CHIP and incident AF, although effect size varies by driver gene and clone size. In a JAMA Cardiology analysis, large TET2-CHIP and large ASXL1-CHIP, but not DNMT3A-CHIP, were associated with higher incident AF risk in population-based cohorts.28 Whether CHIP modifies outcomes after catheter ablation, for example through persistent atrial inflammatory remodeling and recurrence risk, remains an important and clinically testable question.

CHIP and Valvular Heart Disease

Calcific aortic valve disease overlaps mechanistically with atherosclerosis through macrophage infiltration, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and osteogenic transformation of valvular interstitial cells. Clinical and mechanistic evidence now suggests that CHIP may contribute to this biology. Among patients with severe aortic stenosis undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), CHIP carrier status was associated with a roughly threefold higher rate of subsequent heart failure hospitalization (adjusted HR 3.06; 95% CI, 1.09–8.59; P = 0.034).56 CHIP-driven macrophage inflammation may promote valvular calcification through paracrine effects on valvular interstitial cells, although evidence in the valvular compartment is still developing.57,58 If confirmed prospectively, CHIP status could become a research tool for risk stratification around transcatheter or surgical valve replacement; present evidence does not justify routine screening or adjunctive anti-inflammatory therapy outside clinical studies.

CHIP and Venous Thromboembolism

JAK2 V617F CHIP has the clearest thrombotic signature within the CHIP spectrum. JAK2-driven clonal hematopoiesis shows a robust epidemiologic association with venous thromboembolism, and mechanistic studies, largely from MPN and JAK2 V617F model systems, point to JAK-STAT-mediated NET formation as a central pathway.33,34 More broadly, CHIP-associated inflammation may increase endothelial activation or injury, promote coagulation pathway activity, and facilitate platelet activation, fibrin deposition, and NET formation.59 Prothrombotic biology is not restricted to JAK2: TET2-mutant hematopoietic progenitors can generate neutrophils that form compact NETs with reduced degradability, suggesting that TET2-linked thrombosis may operate through a distinct mechanism.60

Splanchnic venous thrombosis is a particularly relevant setting for JAK2-mutant clonal states. Enrichment of JAK2 V617F among patients presenting with portal, mesenteric, or hepatic vein thrombosis, even without overt myeloproliferative neoplasia at presentation, suggests that some cases labeled idiopathic may reflect JAK2 V617F-positive clonal hematopoiesis or occult early myeloproliferative disease.61 This has practical implications for the diagnostic evaluation and hematology follow-up of unexplained thrombosis in older adults.

Therapeutic Strategies: From Mechanistic Rationale to Clinical Trials

IL-1β Inhibition: Lessons from the CANTOS Trial

The key human trial-derived evidence suggesting possible CHIP-dependent anti-inflammatory benefit comes from a retrospective exploratory genomic substudy of CANTOS rather than from a prospectively CHIP-stratified randomized trial. In that analysis, CHIP was identified in 338 participants, and TET2 variants were present in 103 patients. Within the TET2-CHIP subgroup, participants randomized to canakinumab, with doses pooled, had a lower risk of MACE than TET2-CHIP carriers randomized to placebo (HR 0.38; 95% CI, 0.15–0.96).24 This protective HR should be interpreted as a treatment-effect estimate within TET2-CHIP carriers, not as evidence that TET2-CHIP itself is protective. Biologically, the result is coherent because TET2 loss-of-function can amplify NLRP3/IL-1β signaling, so IL-1β blockade could theoretically attenuate the inflammatory pathway through which TET2-mutant myeloid cells promote vascular risk. However, formal effect modification was not established, the interaction test was not significant (P = 0.14), and the small number of TET2-CHIP participants limits precision. The result should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive randomized evidence for CHIP-guided therapy.24

These data have clear implications for trial design. Broad testing of anti-inflammatory drugs in unselected populations may dilute benefit if the responsive biology is concentrated in specific CHIP subtypes. Enrichment for TET2-CHIP, or stratification by driver gene and inflammatory biomarker profile, could improve statistical efficiency and test a precision-medicine model of residual inflammatory risk. A Phase 2a study is evaluating inflammasome-pathway strategies, including DFV890, an NLRP3 inhibitor, and MAS825, a bispecific IL-1β/IL-18 antibody, in patients with coronary artery disease and TET2 or DNMT3A CHIP (NCT06097663).62

IL-6 Blockade: Ziltivekimab and the ZEUS Trial

IL-6 blockade is an active area of anti-inflammatory cardiovascular investigation. In the phase 2 RESCUE trial, ziltivekimab produced large reductions in inflammatory biomarkers and selected coagulation-related markers, including fibrinogen, in patients at high atherosclerotic risk. At week 12, median hsCRP suppression showed a clear dose gradient: 77%, 88%, and 92% reductions at 7.5, 15, and 30 mg, respectively, compared with a 4% reduction in the placebo group.63 The magnitude of hsCRP lowering exceeded that achieved with canakinumab in CANTOS, consistent with intervention at a downstream cytokine node.

ZEUS (NCT05021835) is an event-driven, multinational, double-blind, placebo-controlled cardiovascular outcomes trial of ziltivekimab in participants with established ASCVD, chronic kidney disease, and systemic inflammatory activation.64 The trial was not designed as a CHIP-stratified study, and publicly available descriptions do not establish that whole-blood biospecimens suitable for CHIP genotyping will be available for retrospective CHIP analyses. ARTEMIS should likewise be interpreted as an IL-6-inhibition trial in a post-myocardial-infarction inflammatory-risk setting rather than as an expected source of CHIP-specific evidence. Thus, differential IL-6 biology by CHIP subtype remains a rationale for future prospectively genotyped studies, not an assumption about ZEUS or ARTEMIS.

NLRP3 Inflammasome Inhibitors

Inflammasome blockade is attractive because it targets an upstream node in CHIP-associated inflammation, particularly the NLRP3/IL-1β axis implicated in TET2-CHIP models.49,62 Specific NLRP3 inhibitors can modulate inflammasome activity, whereas AIM2-directed pharmacologic inhibition remains largely preclinical.65 Additional preclinical strategies target NLRP3 deubiquitination and BRISC/BRCC3-dependent regulation.66,67 Early-phase clinical studies are now testing inflammasome-pathway approaches in coronary artery disease, including NLRP3 inhibition with agents such as selnoflast or DFV890 and dual IL-1β/IL-18 targeting with MAS825. These studies may establish safety, tolerability, and biomarker effects in selected or CHIP-enriched populations, but they should not be interpreted as evidence of cardiovascular outcome benefit unless prospectively tested in adequately powered outcome trials. Beyond CHIP, NLRP3 inhibition is also being explored in obesity and cardiometabolic inflammation, which may be relevant because obesity, diabetes, and residual inflammatory risk frequently overlap with CHIP-associated cardiovascular phenotypes; however, these studies should not be interpreted as CHIP-specific therapeutic evidence.

JAK Inhibition for JAK2-CHIP

For JAK2 V617F CHIP, JAK-STAT inhibition is the most gene-specific therapeutic concept. In JAK2 V617F model systems, ruxolitinib reduces NET formation, venous thrombosis, endothelial injury, and arterial thrombosis in erosion-like injury settings.33,35 The unresolved clinical question is whether low-dose JAK1/2 inhibition can safely reduce thrombotic cardiovascular risk in individuals with JAK2-CHIP who do not meet criteria for overt myeloproliferative neoplasia. Chronic immunosuppression and infection susceptibility are central concerns and may restrict long-term preventive use.

Repositioning Conventional Cardiovascular Therapies

Established cardiovascular drugs may also intersect with CHIP biology. Colchicine, supported by COLCOT and LoDoCo2 for secondary ASCVD prevention, inhibits several inflammatory processes, including neutrophil activation and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly.68,69 CHIP-focused colchicine evidence is now emerging but remains exploratory. In experimental Tet2-mutant clonal hematopoiesis, colchicine attenuated accelerated atherosclerosis and suppressed excess IL-1β production; in accompanying human biobank analyses, colchicine prescription appeared to blunt the association between TET2-mutant CHIP and myocardial infarction.70 In an exploratory LoDoCo2 substudy, low-dose colchicine was associated with attenuated clonal expansion, particularly for TET2 CH.71 Reported COLCOT genomic analyses similarly suggest that low-dose colchicine may reduce CH mutation burden after myocardial infarction.72 However, these studies do not yet establish that CHIP status predicts clinical event reduction with colchicine, and prospective CHIP-stratified outcome trials are still needed. Whether benefit differs by CHIP status, including TET2- or JAK2-CHIP, has not been prospectively established.73 SGLT2 inhibitors suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activity in experimental models, but whether this biology modifies CHIP-associated cardiomyopathy in humans is unknown.74 Some experts have proposed viewing selected higher-risk CHIP subtypes, including TET2, JAK2, PPM1D, and TP53, as cardiovascular risk enhancers that justify intensified conventional prevention.62 This approach is not guideline-endorsed and should be individualized. For JAK2-CHIP, any antithrombotic strategy, including low-dose aspirin, should be framed through shared decision-making because net benefit and bleeding risk are uncertain.

Targeting the Mutant Clone Itself

A more ambitious approach is to reduce the mutant clone itself rather than suppress downstream inflammation alone. Vitamin C (ascorbate), a cofactor for TET dioxygenases, can augment residual TET activity and partially mimic TET2 restoration in hematopoietic models.75 However, high-dose intravenous vitamin C has not yet produced clear hematologic responses in TET2-mutant clonal cytopenia, and chronic oral vitamin C dosing has not been established as a clone-directed or cardiovascular-preventive strategy for asymptomatic CHIP. Cardiovascular benefit in CHIP has not been demonstrated.75 Synthetic-lethal strategies are also being explored. Tet2-deficient hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells show selective vulnerability to XPO1 inhibition with agents such as selinexor and eltanexor in preclinical work.76 At present, these concepts remain clone-directed hypotheses rather than cardiovascular prevention strategies.

Clinical Implementation: Screening, Risk Stratification, and the CHIP Clinic

Should CHIP Be Incorporated into Cardiovascular Risk Assessment?

The reproducible association between CHIP and cardiovascular events has prompted interest in incorporating CHIP into risk assessment. At present, routine CHIP testing for cardiovascular screening in unselected populations is not supported. The incremental prognostic value beyond established risk factors, circulating biomarkers, and imaging markers remains incompletely quantified, and no validated management pathway has shown that CHIP-informed care improves hard cardiovascular outcomes. For now, cardiovascular CHIP testing should be considered investigational and interpreted within defined clinical contexts, ideally through research protocols or clinical trials.62

Potential Screening Scenarios

Without standardized and outcome-validated pathways, CHIP assessment is better viewed as context-dependent interpretation rather than population screening. The most defensible scenario is incidental detection after sequencing performed for another clinical indication; in that setting, the clinical task is counseling, uncertainty management, and follow-up planning rather than active case-finding. Cancer survivors exposed to genotoxic therapy represent another plausible group because therapy-related clonal hematopoiesis is enriched and may contribute to later cardiovascular risk, although prospective validation is needed before CHIP status can be considered actionable for cardio-oncology surveillance.77 Selected thrombotic presentations, especially otherwise unexplained splanchnic venous thrombosis, are also relevant because evaluation for occult MPNs, including JAK2 V617F testing, is already part of routine workup. More speculative applications, such as perioperative risk stratification before cardiac surgery or valve intervention, should remain research questions until clinically meaningful thresholds and interventions are defined.

The Multidisciplinary CHIP Cardiovascular Clinic

CHIP crosses hematology, cardiovascular medicine, genetics, and oncology, which makes multidisciplinary CHIP clinics conceptually attractive. Their practical value should be framed narrowly: structured counseling that separates established associations from unproven causal claims; hematologic surveillance tailored to mutation profile and blood-count context; aggressive optimization of conventional prevention, including lipid, blood pressure, glycemic, smoking, weight, and lifestyle management; and efficient referral to trials of anti-inflammatory or clone-directed therapy. This model parallels cardio-oncology by standardizing assessment and longitudinal surveillance while avoiding premature treatment algorithms unsupported by outcomes data.

Knowledge Gaps and Future Directions

Several issues must be resolved before CHIP can shift from risk marker to therapeutic target in cardiovascular medicine. Human causality is the central question. Negative and inconsistent findings should be interpreted as informative rather than incidental, because study results may differ according to cohort design, sequencing platform, sequencing depth, VAF threshold, ancestry composition, clinical ascertainment, and endpoint definition. Gene-specific analyses are also frequently limited by sample size and statistical power, particularly for less common drivers and less frequent cardiovascular phenotypes. Experimental data are persuasive, but definitive translation requires intervention studies showing that CHIP-targeted or CHIP-enriched strategies reduce cardiovascular event rates. Variant-level heterogeneity within driver genes is another priority, because hotspot and non-hotspot mutations may differ in signaling, inflammatory phenotype, and risk. This will require large biobank analyses with variant-resolution genotyping and harmonized cardiovascular phenotyping. Clonal dynamics also need clarification: VAF trajectory, growth rate, clonal competition, and acquisition of secondary mutations may add information beyond a baseline measurement, but prospective serial-sampling studies are needed. CHIP also interacts with cardiometabolic context, including diabetes and obesity, yet the form of interaction and its gene-specific patterns remain incompletely defined. Generalizability is another limitation, as most large CHIP-cardiovascular datasets are dominated by European-ancestry populations. Future trials should therefore move beyond a binary CHIP definition and stratify by driver gene, clone size, and inflammatory biomarker profile, while incorporating longitudinal clonal and inflammatory monitoring.

Conclusions

CHIP now sits at the intersection of hematology, inflammation biology, and cardiovascular medicine. The central message is not simply that CHIP increases cardiovascular risk, but that this risk is heterogeneous across driver genes, clone size, and clinical context. Experimental and human genetic evidence supports gene-informed models in which TET2-, DNMT3A-, ASXL1-, and JAK2-driven clonal states operate through partially distinct upstream mechanisms while converging on inflammatory effector programs involving IL-1β and IL-6. Exploratory CANTOS analyses provide early human evidence compatible with subtype-dependent treatment response, but prospective confirmation in adequately powered, CHIP-stratified outcome trials is still required. For current practice, CHIP should be interpreted as a risk-context modifier rather than a stand-alone indication for population screening or CHIP-directed therapy: clinicians should optimize guideline-based cardiovascular prevention, provide hematology-informed counseling and surveillance when CHIP is incidentally identified, and preferentially refer eligible high-risk patients to CHIP-stratified clinical trials. Whether CHIP-guided precision prevention becomes clinically actionable will depend on standardized assays, validated risk models, and gene-stratified intervention studies.
