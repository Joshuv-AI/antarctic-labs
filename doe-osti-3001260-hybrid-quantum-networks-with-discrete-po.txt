4. Executive Summary / Abstract
Quantum communication and computation systems have evolved along two largely independent
paradigms: discrete-variable (DV) systems that encode information in qubits such as photon
polarizations or photon-number states, and continuous-variable (CV) systems that encode
information in optical field quadratures. Each approach offers distinct advantages—DVs provide
low error rates and compatibility with single-photon platforms, while CVs support deterministic
operations and efficient quantum state manipulation. A fundamental challenge for building a
scalable Quantum Internet lies in interfacing these two regimes into a unified hybrid architecture
that can coherently distribute and process quantum information across heterogeneous quantum
nodes.
This project aims to develop and demonstrate a hybrid optical quantum network that seamlessly
integrates DV and CV systems. Specifically, we investigate a new class of hybrid entanglement
between the discrete polarizations of single photons and the continuous quadrature variables of
optical cat states, overcoming incompatibilities in existing DV–CV demonstrations. Using this
new entanglement resource, the team investigates a multi-node hybrid quantum local area network
(Q-LAN) testbed capable of DV–CV entanglement generation, swapping, and distribution across
fiber links.
The project’s major objectives are:
 1. Demonstrate optical hybrid entanglement and swapping between discrete polarizations and
 continuous quadrature variables.
 2. Develop a DV–CV hybrid polarization qubit wavelength converter from near-infrared to
 telecom bands for long-distance fiber transmission.
 3. Study a hybrid optical Q-LAN with multiple interconnected quantum nodes.
 4. Develop network protocols and algorithms for distributed quantum information processing.
 5. Create software tools for entanglement traffic management and quantum resource
 allocation.
By combining quantum optics, atomic physics, and computer network theory, this interdisciplinary
effort—led by the University of Texas at Dallas with partners at Washington University in St. Louis,
the College of William & Mary, the University of California, Santa Cruz, and Kennesaw State
University—establishes a foundational architecture for hybrid quantum networks. The outcomes
will enable scalable distributed quantum computing, quantum communication over heterogeneous
links, and future DOE applications in quantum information science and engineering.

5. Project Activities and Findings
5.1 Original Hypotheses and Scientific Motivation
The foundation of this project lies in addressing one of the most critical challenges in quantum
information science — the lack of a unified platform that can coherently integrate discrete-variable
(DV) and continuous-variable (CV) quantum systems. Over the past two decades, DV quantum
information processing, which relies on single photons and their polarization or photon-number
states, has achieved remarkable progress in realizing low-noise, high-fidelity quantum logic
operations. In parallel, CV systems based on optical quadrature amplitudes have demonstrated
deterministic entanglement generation and efficient state manipulation through well-established
homodyne detection techniques. Despite their complementary strengths, these two paradigms have
evolved largely independently, with limited ability to interconnect or interconvert quantum
information between them.
The central hypothesis driving this project is that a hybrid quantum network combining discrete
polarizations and continuous quadrature variables can coherently bridge the DV and CV domains,
thereby enabling new functionalities that neither system alone can achieve. Specifically, the project
postulates that:
 1. Hybrid entanglement between single-photon polarization states (DV) and optical
 quadrature states (CV) can be generated and preserved through properly engineered light–
 matter interactions and optical interfaces.
 2. Entanglement swapping and teleportation can be extended across DV–CV boundaries,
 allowing information encoded in one form to be transmitted, converted, and processed in
 another without loss of quantum coherence.
 3. Hybrid quantum nodes interconnected via optical fibers can form the foundation of scalable,
 heterogeneous quantum local area networks (Q-LANs) compatible with both atomic and
 photonic platforms.
The scientific motivation for pursuing this hybrid approach is threefold:
 • Bridging Paradigms: By interfacing polarization-encoded qubits with continuous
 quadrature modes, the project enables interoperability among distinct quantum hardware
 platforms, paving the way for a unified quantum Internet architecture.
 • Enhancing Robustness and Scalability: Hybrid encoding can improve tolerance to channel
 loss, enable deterministic operations, and extend communication distances through
 wavelength conversion and adaptive error correction.
 • Advancing DOE Priorities: The outcomes directly support DOE’s mission to advance
 quantum networking and distributed quantum computing by developing experimental and
 theoretical tools that connect diverse quantum systems across platforms and frequency
 bands.
In summary, the project is motivated and driven by the hypothesis that hybrid DV–CV
entanglement can serve as the missing interface between current quantum communication and
computation technologies, laying the groundwork for scalable, high-fidelity quantum networks
that combine the best features of both regimes.

5.3 Major Activities, Key Findings, and Results
5.3.1 DV-CV Quantum States Preparation, Hybrid Entanglement Generation and
Manipulation (Objective 1)
In Objective 1, we focus on preparing photonic DV/CV quantum states, generating and
manipulating their hybrid entanglement.
1) Quantum Langevin theory for entangled photon pair generation
In this project, we generate heralded single-photon DV state from entangled photon pairs
(biphotons): The detection of one photon heralds its paired photon. Our entangled photon pairs are
produced through spontaneous four-wave mixing nonlinear optical process in an atomic ensemble.
To understand how loss, gain, and nonlinear coupling coefficient induce noises in the biphoton
quantum state in our hybrid quantum network, we developed a general macroscopic
phenomenological formula of quantum Langevin theory for two coupled phase-conjugated optical
fields. For the first time, we discover that a complex-valued nonlinear coupling coefficient can
lead to noises even without linear gain or loss. This discovery has significant implications for
quantum optics and quantum information science, particularly in light of the increasing use of
technologies that operate at or below the quantum limit. Understanding the origin of quantum
noises is essential for both fundamental physics and applied quantum technologies. This work was
published in Physical Review A 107, 053703 (2023).
Publication:
[1] Yue Jiang, Yefeng Mei, and Shengwang Du, “Quantum Langevin theory for two coupled phase-
 conjugated electromagnetic waves,” Phys. Rev. A 107, 053703 (2023)
2) Symmetry protected two-photon coherence time
 In open photonic quantum systems, coherence time is typically shortened due to irreversible
coupling between the source and the environment, such as loss and dephasing. In this work, we
report the observation of symmetry protected two-photon coherence time of biphotons generated
from backward spontaneous four-wave mixing in laser-cooled 87Rb atoms. When biphotons are
nondegenerate, nonsymmetric photonic absorption loss results in exponential decay of the
temporal waveform of the two-photon joint probability amplitude, leading to shortened coherence
time [Fig. 1(a)]. In contrast, in the case of degenerate biphotons, when both paired photons
propagate with the same group velocity and absorption coefficient, the two-photon coherence time,
protected by space-time symmetry, remains unaffected by medium absorptive losses. Our

the pivotal role of symmetry in manipulating and controlling photonic quantum states. The work
was published in Phys. Rev. Lett. 133, 033601 (2024).

3) Quantum squeezing and sensing with pseudo-anti-parity-time symmetry
Creating desirable quantum photonic squeezing states with continuous quadrature variables is one
of the objectives of the project. In this project, for the first time, we discovered the phase transition
of quantum squeezing of two-mode continuous quadrature variables induced by pseudo anti-
parity-time (APT) symmetry. We construct two APT symmetric systems without involving
Langevin noises: four-wave mixing with phase mismatching [Fig. 2(a)] and cold atomic BEC in a
ring trap [Fig. 2(b)]. We show that the spontaneous pseudo-APT symmetry breaking leads to an
exceptional point, across which there is a transition between different types of quantum squeezing
dynamics. Our finding offers a physical platform for investigating exciting APT symmetry physics
in the quantum realm. Our theoretical proposal [Physical Review Letters 128, 173602 (2022)] was
followed by experimental demonstrations [Appl. Phys. Lett. 124, 044005 (2024); Optica Quantum
3, 72 (2025)], in which we investigated classical and quantum properties of coupled optical fields,
generated in the four-wave mixing process in hot atomic vapor. These experiments established the
practicality of modeling the anti-PT-symmetric Hamiltonian by utilizing two correlated optical
fields generated through the near-resonant forward FWM process in hot Rb atoms. We
demonstrated that it is possible to tune the interaction parameters across the anti-PT phase
transition, verified that both classical and quantum behavior of the probe and Stokes fields exhibit
the expected characteristics below and above the exceptional point. Namely, we observe two-mode

 6

 relative-intensity squeezing at the anti-PT symmetric regime, when both output fields experience
 matching exponential FWM gain. We also analyze the influence of excess noise resulting from
 residual optical absorption, which imposes constraints on the attainable level of squeezing in
 distinct domains. Finally, we identified reasonable experimental parameters for observation of the
 nearly lossless oscillatory behavior, that can be applied for further advancing quantum sensor
 applications.

5) Hybrid entanglement between optical discrete polarizations and continuous quadrature
variables
In this project, we have worked together and completed the framework to create hybrid
entanglement between optical discrete polarizations and continuous quadrature variables. In
contrast to previous results, we demonstrate a new scheme to remotely generate hybrid
entanglement between discrete polarization and continuous quadrature optical qubits heralded by
two-photon Bell-state measurement (Fig. 4), as well as entanglement swapping and quantum
teleportation - in which quantum information processing and communications could make use of
this hybrid technique. This work was published in Photonics 8, 552 (2021).

5.3.2 DV–CV Hybrid Polarization Qubit Wavelength Converter (Objective 2)
A quantum internet connects remote quantum processors that need to interact and exchange
quantum signals over a long distance through photonic channels. However, these quantum nodes
operate at the wavelength ranges unsuitable for long-distance transmission. Therefore, quantum
wavelength conversion to telecom bands is crucial for long-distance quantum networks based on
optical fiber. Making use of the two-mode squeezed bi-chromatic source developed in 5.4.1-4, we
show that such wavelength conversion for single-photon polarization qubits can be implemented
using continuous-variable quantum teleportation that can efficiently convert qubits between near-
infrared (780–795 nm suitable for interacting with atomic quantum nodes) and telecom wavelength
(1300–1500 nm suitable for long-distance transmission), as shown in Fig. 5. The teleportation uses
entangled photon fields (i.e., nondegenerate two-mode squeezed state) that can be generated by
four-wave mixing in a rubidium atomic gas using a diamond configuration of atomic transitions
(see section 5.4.1-4). The entangled fields can be emitted in two orthogonal polarizations with
locked relative phase, making them especially suitable for interfacing with single- photon
polarization qubits. This work may pave the way for the realization of long-distance quantum
networks. The work was published in Physical Review A 105, 052444 (2022).

2) A network-aware framework for multi-tenant distributed quantum computing
DQC makes use of the resources of multiple quantum computers to perform large quantum
computing jobs by separating each job onto different quantum computers and using inter-processor
communication. It is natural to envision the near-future QDC platform as a shared cluster of
quantum computers, called a quantum cloud. One fundamental research problem of DQC is to
divide a quantum computing job, represented by a quantum circuit, into sub-circuits and place
them onto different quantum computers such that each quantum computer has sufficient resources
to assign to these sub-circuits. We envision that circuit placement in a multi-tenant cloud will
become a new line of research because 1) it aligns with the trend of developing a high-throughput,
low-latency, quantum cloud; 2) this problem significantly differs from existing research on placing
one single circuit in DQC and that on placing virtual machines (VMs) in classic cloud computing.

We proposed a quantum cloud resource allocation framework called CloudQC, which includes two
main functional components: circuit placement and network scheduler. The circuit placement
function allows the quantum cloud to assign resources to quantum circuits as a bunch or a sequence.
The placement of one circuit will optimize the remaining resources for other circuits. The network
scheduler function assigns network resources (EPR pairs) among different sub-circuits. For
important quantum gates, CloudQC will assign them redundant network resources to avoid
potential backlogs that could cause long latency for the entire circuit.

    on Distributed Computing Systems (ICDCS), doi: 10.1109/ICDCS63083.2025.00024.

3) QoS-aware routing
Most previous works on the entanglement routing problem mainly work on a "best-effort" manner,
which means the solution should maximize the throughput (number of entanglement bits per time
slot). However, they do not provide guarantees on quality of service (QoS) such as the fidelity of
entanglement, latency of entanglements, and the number of delivered entangled pairs. In future
applications of quantum networks, different users may have different requirements, thus routing
algorithms whose aim is to maximize the overall throughput is insufficient.

 13

Our literature analysis also tells that 1) Application-aware routing that considers multiple QoS
metrics and purification jointly is a missing part of the literature on quantum networks. 2) QoS
solutions in classical networks is different from quantum networks.

In this work, we analyze different applications in quantum networks and identify key QoS metrics.
By modeling these applications’ behaviors, we investigate what services are needed from quantum
networks regarding delivered entanglements. We formulate the QoS-aware entanglement routing
problem that takes latency, number of delivered entanglements, fidelity, and purification into
consideration. We design a QoS routing framework, Q2R, including a QoS routing process and
scheduling process that can meet heterogeneous QoS requirements of the requests from multiple
users concurrently. It comprises several technical ingredients: We first use a scheduling algorithm
to pre-process users' requests and meet their priority and latency requirement. Then we propose
two heuristic algorithms that can fulfill the users' requirements of fidelity and throughput.
Evaluation results show that the proposed framework outperforms previous results in terms of the
number of fulfilled requests.

4) Virus spreading in quantum networks
Recent advances in quantum communication have enabled long-distance secure information
transfer through quantum channels, giving rise to quantum networks with unique physical and
statistical properties. However, as in classical networks, the propagation of viruses in these systems
could have severe consequences. We investigated the critical problem of virus spreading in
quantum networks. We developed quantitative tools, particularly a modified nonlinear dynamical
system model, for performing epidemiological analyses on quantum networks. Our results show
that quantum networks tend to be more resilient to viral infections, exhibiting higher epidemic
thresholds than classical networks with identical graph topologies. This apparent robustness,
however, arises primarily from the sparser connectivity inherent to the quantum networks. When
the comparison is made at a fixed average connectivity, classical and quantum networks display
comparable epidemic thresholds. These findings provide key insights into the security and
reliability of future large-scale quantum communication systems. Our work bridges the fields of
quantum information science, network theory, and epidemiology, paving the way for future studies
of quantum epidemiological dynamics.

5.4 Education, Collaboration, and Dissemination
This project provided an integrated environment for education, workforce development, and
collaboration across multiple institutions. The research team consisted of faculty, postdoctoral
scholars, graduate students, and undergraduate interns from the University of Texas at Dallas
(UTD), Washington University in St. Louis (WUSTL), College of William & Mary (W&M),
University of California at Santa Cruz (UCSC), and Kennesaw State University (KSU).
Education and Training:
The project directly supported the training of more graduate and undergraduate students who
gained hands-on experience in quantum optics, atomic physics, quantum information theory, and
optical networking. Students were involved in the complete research cycle—from theoretical
modeling and numerical simulation to experimental design, data acquisition, and analysis. The
interdisciplinary nature of the project fostered a new generation of researchers skilled in bridging
optical physics and computer science, a key workforce need in the emerging quantum technology
sector.
Collaboration:
The project fostered strong coordination among the five collaborating universities. Regular virtual
group meetings, annual workshops, and cross-institutional research visits ensured effective
knowledge exchange and integration of theoretical, experimental, and computational components.
Dissemination of Results:
Project outcomes were widely disseminated through peer-reviewed publications, conference
presentations, and invited talks. Between 2021 and 2025, the team produced 15 major publications,
including papers in Physical Review Letters, Physical Review A, Optica Quantum, Advanced
Quantum Technologies, AVS Quantum Science, and Applied Physics Letters. Research results
were also shared at leading conferences such as IEEE QCE, QCNC, and ICDCS.

 15

Additionally, several findings led to intellectual property filings, including International Patent
WO 2024/151316 A3 (“Quantum Computing Apparatus with Photons and Atomic Memories”),
demonstrating translational potential beyond academia.
All publications, data, and software (e.g., the A2Tango and CloudQC simulators) have been made
publicly accessible or archived for DOE’s Scientific and Technical Information (STI) system.

6. Summary
This project has successfully advanced the frontier of hybrid quantum networking, integrating
discrete-variable (DV) and continuous-variable (CV) optical systems into a coherent experimental
and theoretical framework. Over the four-year performance period, the team demonstrated key
technical milestones:
 • Hybrid entanglement generation and manipulation between single-photon polarizations
 and optical quadratures, supported by new theoretical insights such as symmetry-protected
 two-photon coherence and pseudo-anti-parity-time (APT)–induced squeezing.
 • Quantum wavelength conversion from near-infrared to telecom bands using continuous-
 variable teleportation, enabling long-distance quantum communication compatible with
 existing fiber networks.
 • Distributed quantum computing architectures, including the Shared Quantum Gate
 Processing Unit (S-QGPU), which offers a scalable and resource-efficient approach to
 multi-node quantum computation.
 • Quantum networking software frameworks for entanglement traffic management, multi-
 tenant distributed computing (CloudQC), and quality-of-service (QoS)–aware routing
 (Q2R).
These accomplishments demonstrate a unified pathway toward scalable hybrid quantum local-area
networks (Q-LANs).
Beyond technical achievements, the project made significant impacts in workforce development,
interdisciplinary collaboration, and scientific dissemination. It produced multiple peer-reviewed
publications, one international patent, and several software tools—collectively contributing
valuable Scientific and Technical Information (STI) to the DOE’s Quantum Information Science
(QIS) mission.
