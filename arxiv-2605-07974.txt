Tensor product surfaces and graded syzygies
Matthew Weaver

Abstract
. Let U ⊆ H 0 ​ ( 𝒪 ℙ 1 × ℙ 1 ​ ( a , b ) ) U\subseteq H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b)) be a four-dimensional vector space and consider the rational map ϕ U : ℙ 1 × ℙ 1 ⇢ ℙ 3 \phi_{U}:\,\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3} defined by its basis of bihomogeneous polynomials. The tensor product surface X U ⊆ ℙ 3 X_{U}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{3} is the closed image of ϕ U \phi_{U} , and a fundamental problem in this setting is to determine its implicit equation. As these surfaces are ubiquitous within the field of geometric modeling and design, knowledge of their implicit equations is particularly advantageous, allowing for more effective and efficient computations. In this article, we expand upon work of Duarte and Schenck [ 14 ] and the present author [ 25 ] to solve this implicitization problem when the bigraded ideal I U I_{U} admits a singly graded syzygy.



1.
Introduction

Given a map between projective spaces
\phi:\,\mathbb{P}^{m}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, a natural problem which arises is to describe the closed image of
\phi
as a subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. As such a map is defined by homogeneous polynomials in the coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, one must obtain the equations of
\mathop{\rm im}\phi
in the coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
implicitization problem
, determining the implicit equations of a parameterized variety, has become a fundamental question studied to great length by algebraic geometers and commutative algebraists, using a variety of techniques.

In recent years, the implicitization problem has gained significant interest within the geometric modeling community for its applications to computer-aided geometric design (CAGD), see e.g.
[
12
,
21
,
22
,
23
]
. Parameterized curves and surfaces are ubiquitous in this area, yet often present computational challenges. Many fundamental tasks may be computationally intensive given a parametric form, but become trivial given an implicit model. As such, the development of computationally effective implicitization methods has become an active area of research, drawing upon ideas from algebraic geometry, commutative algebra, and computational methods within both realms.

In this context,
tensor product surfaces
form a notable class of parameterized surfaces. These surfaces arise as the image of a rational map
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
, and their parameterizations inherit a rich structure from the bigraded coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. This bigraded structure, however, tends to complicate the implicitization problem, introducing additional algebraic phenomena not witnessed in the singly graded setting. To address this, a variety of techniques have been developed for the implicitization of these surfaces. Common elimination techniques relying on Gröbner bases and resultants are straightforward to implement, but tend to be computationally expensive, especially in the multigraded setting. In contrast, syzygy techniques and methods borrowed from the study of
Rees algebras
tend to be much more effective and are hence preferable, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
24
,
26
]
. One such method in particular is the
approximation complex

\mathcal{Z}
developed by Herzog, Simis, and Vasconcelos
[
18
,
19
]
. Whereas this complex is rarely acyclic, it detects useful syzygetic information and has been shown to be particularly effective for implicitization purposes
[
1
,
3
,
7
,
14
,
20
,
25
]
. We refer the reader to
[
8
]
for a concise overview of syzygy methods used for implicitization and
[
7
]
for an introduction to the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
and its applications to the implicitization problem.

In this paper, we consider tensor product surfaces which are
basepoint free
, i.e. surfaces arising when the rational map above is regular. Let
R=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u,v]
be a polynomial ring, for
\mathbb{K}
an algebraically closed field, bigraded by setting
\mathop{\rm bideg}s,t=(1,0)
and
\mathop{\rm bideg}u,v=(0,1)
. Notice that the global sections
H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))
may be identified with the bigraded components
R_{a,b}
of
R
. For a subspace
U\subseteq R_{a,b}
with basis
\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
, we assume that
p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
have no common zeros on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. As such, the rational map

\phi_{U}:\,\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}

defined by
\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
is regular, as its base locus is empty, i.e.
U
is
basepoint free
. Writing
I_{U}=(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
to denote the ideal generated by these bihomogeneous forms, notice that
U
is free of basepoints if and only if
\sqrt{I_{U}}=(s,t)\cap(u,v)
. In this setting, it is known from
[
2
]
that the implicit equation of
X_{U}
may be obtained from the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
on the generators of
I_{U}
. As
\mathcal{Z}
is constructed from the Koszul complex on
\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
(see
Section

2
for details), an understanding of the homological properties of
I_{U}
is particularly useful in this setting.

In
[
20
]
, tensor product surfaces
X_{U}
for
U\subset R_{2,1}
are studied in this manner by determining all possible free resolutions of
I_{U}
. It is noted that the presence of a
linear
syzygy, in bidegree
(0,1)
or
(1,0)
, on
I_{U}
imposes certain constraints and determines some of the differentials in a minimal resolution. This observation is further developed in
[
14
]
, where it is shown that the implicit equation of
X_{U}
can be completely determined from such a linear syzygy, via the approximation complex, without requiring the full syzygy module of
I_{U}
. More recently, in
[
25
]
these ideas were extended to the case where
I_{U}
admits a
quadratic
syzygy in bidegree
(0,2)
or
(2,0)
. There it is shown that, similarly, such a syzygy determines a particular submodule of
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
that is sufficient to determine the implicit equation of
X_{U}
.

In this paper, we continue this line of work from
[
14
,
25
]
and consider the general setting that the bigraded ideal
I_{U}
admits a singly graded syzygy in any degree. We show that this syzygy
S
alone determines a submodule of
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
sufficient to determine the implicit equation of the surface
X_{U}
, via the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
. The key construction used throughout is a subspace
V\subseteq U
associated to the syzygy
S
, whose basis may be taken as part of a generating set of
I_{U}
. With this, there are a handful of cases to consider, depending on the dimension of
V
, with each case exhibiting unique behavior. The main results of this article,
4.4
,
5.11
and
6.8

are summarized as follows.

Theorem
.

Assume that
U\subseteq H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a,b))
is basepoint free, and let
I_{U}
denote the ideal of
U
. Assume that
I_{U}
has a minimal syzygy
S
in bidegree
(0,n)
and
b\geq 2n-1
. Let
V
be the subspace of
U
associated to the syzygy
S
.

(i)

If
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
, then
I_{U}
has syzygies
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
in bidegree
(a,b-n)
such that
\mathop{\rm dim}\langle S,S_{1},S_{2}\rangle_{2a-1,b-1}=2ab
.

(ii)

If
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
, then
I_{U}
has syzygies
S_{1}
in bidegree
(a,b-n)
,
S_{2}
in bidegree
(a,b-n+\mu)
, and
S_{3}
in bidegree
(a,b-\mu)
for some
\mu
, such that
\mathop{\rm dim}\langle S,S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\rangle_{2a-1,b-1}=2ab
.

(iii)

If
\mathop{\rm dim}V=4
, then
I_{U}
has syzygies
S_{1}
in bidegree
(a,b-n+\mu_{1})
,
S_{2}
in bidegree
(a,b-n+\mu_{2})
, and
S_{3}
in bidegree
(a,b-\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})
for some
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
, such that
\mathop{\rm dim}\langle S,S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}\rangle_{2a-1,b-1}=2ab
.

In each case, the first differential
d_{1}
of
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
is determined by these syzygies. Moreover, the determinant of a
2ab\times 2ab
matrix representation of
d_{1}
is a power of the implicit equation of
X_{U}
.

By symmetry, a similar result holds if
a\geq 2n-1
and
I_{U}
has a syzygy in bidegree
(n,0)
. Moreover, we note that the additional syzygies on
I_{U}
are constructed explicitly and their description is formulaic, hence these constructions may be easily implemented into a computer algebra system, such as
Macaulay2

[
16
]
. In particular, the methods presented here yield more efficient computation of the implicit equation of
X_{U}
as they do not require computation of the entire syzygy module
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
. Lastly, we note that this result recovers the main result of
[
14
]
when
n=1
, noting
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
in this case, and also recovers the main results of
[
25
]
when
n=2
, noting that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
or
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
(in which case
\mu=1
) in this setting.

We briefly describe how this paper is organized. In

, we introduce the necessary preliminary material required for this article. We review the construction of the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
and its relation to syzygies, and recall the techniques of

. The subspace

associated to

4
,
5
, and
6
, we consider the cases that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2,3,4

may be taken as part of a generating set of
I_{U}
. In
Section

4
, with this chosen generating set, the key observation is that the nonzero entries of

form a regular sequence. Similarly, in

we use that the nonzero entries of

Preliminaries

We briefly review the preliminary material needed for this paper. We begin with the construction of the
approximation complex

\mathcal{Z}

[
18
,
19
]
associated to the ideal
I_{U}
. We then recall its applications to the implicitization of tensor product surfaces, following
[

.

2.1.
Approximation Complex

We recall the construction of the
approximation complex

\mathcal{Z}
, introduced by Herzog, Simis, and Vasconcelos in
[
18
,
19
]
. Whereas this complex may be defined more generally, we consider the setting of a rational map
\mathbb{P}^{m}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
to better match the framework used for the duration of the article.

For such a rational map, consider the ideal of homogeneous forms
I=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})\subseteq R=\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]
defining it. Write
\mathcal{K}(\bm{f})

to denote the Koszul complex on the generating set
\bm{f}=f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
with differentials
d_{i}^{\bm{f}}
. Writing
S=\mathbb{K}[y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}]
to denote the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, consider the Koszul complex
\mathcal{K}(\bm{y})
on the sequence
\bm{y}=y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}
with differentials
d_{i}^{\bm{y}}
. We now introduce the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
as a hybrid complex from this data.

Definition 2.1
.

Writing
Z_{i}=\ker d_{i}^{f}
to denote the cycles of
\mathcal{K}(\bm{f})
, the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
is the complex

\mathcal{Z}\,:\,\,0\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{n+1}\overset{d_{n+1}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{Z}_{n}\overset{d_{n}}{\longrightarrow}\cdots\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{1}\overset{d_{1}}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{Z}_{0}

where
\mathcal{Z}_{i}=S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}Z_{i}
and
d_{i}=d_{i}^{\bm{y}}
.

A direct computation shows that
d_{i-1}^{\bm{f}}d_{i}^{\bm{y}}+d_{i-1}^{\bm{y}}d_{i}^{\bm{f}}=0
. Hence for any
g\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, we see that
d_{i-1}^{\bm{f}}d_{i}^{\bm{y}}(g)=-d_{i-1}^{\bm{y}}d_{i}^{\bm{f}}(g)=0
, hence
d_{i}(g)\in\mathcal{Z}_{i-1}
and so these maps are well defined. Moreover, it is clear that
\mathcal{Z}
is a complex as its differentials agree with those of
\mathcal{K}(\bm{y})
. Lastly, we note that the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
depends only on the ideal
I
, and not the choice of generating set.

Now that
\mathcal{Z}
has been shown to be a complex of modules over
S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}R
, we note that it is rarely acyclic. However, within the study of Rees algebras, the homology of
\mathcal{Z}
is often used to measure the deviation between the Rees ring of
I
and the
symmetric algebra
of
I
. Indeed, notice that
Z_{0}=R
and
Z_{1}=\mathop{\rm syz}(I)
, the module of syzygies of
I
. Hence it follows that the first differential of
\mathcal{Z}
maps
d_{1}\,:S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\mathop{\rm syz}(I)\rightarrow S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}R
by

(2.1)

(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})\longmapsto a_{0}y_{0}+\cdots+a_{n}y_{n}.

for
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathop{\rm syz}(I)
. With this, it follows that the zeroth homology of
\mathcal{Z}
is
H_{0}(\mathcal{Z})\cong\mathcal{S}(I)
, where
\mathcal{S}(I)
is the symmetric algebra of
I
.

Remark 2.2
.

With the description of the first differential of
\mathcal{Z}
in (
2.1
), we note that a representation of
d_{1}
can be obtained from the first syzygy matrix of
I
. Indeed, if
R^{p}\overset{\varphi}{\rightarrow}R^{n+1}\rightarrow I\rightarrow 0
is a free presentation with syzygy matrix
\varphi
, then
\mathop{\rm im}d_{1}=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{p})\subseteq R[y_{0},\ldots,y_{n}]\cong S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}R
, where
[\ell_{1}\ldots\ell_{p}]=[y_{0}\ldots y_{n}]\cdot\varphi
.

Following
2.2
, it suffices to understand the syzygies of
I
to determine this differential. This observation will be particularly useful in the implicitization of tensor product surfaces.

2.2.
Multigraded Implicitization

We now recall the applications of the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}
to the implicitization of tensor product surfaces. This complex has been used in multiple instances for implicitization
[
2
,
3
,
7
]
, however we refer to the methods developed in
[
1
]
for their applications to the multigraded setting. Notice that
S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}R
is naturally bigraded. Hence
for
\nu
a fixed degree in the grading of
R
, the strand

\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}\,:\quad\cdots\longrightarrow S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}(Z_{i})_{\nu}\overset{d_{i}}{\longrightarrow}S\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}(Z_{i-1})_{\nu}\overset{d_{i-1}}{\longrightarrow}\cdots.

is a graded complex of
S
-modules. If
R
is bigraded, as in the setting of a tensor product surface parameterization, one may take a
bigraded
strand
\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}
, for
\nu
in the bigrading of
R
, of
S
-modules as well.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
1
, 7.3]
)
.

Let
U=\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}\subseteq R_{a,b}
and let
\phi_{U}:\,\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
denote the rational map defined by
U
. Assume that either
\phi_{U}
has no basepoints or
\phi_{U}
has finitely many basepoints that are locally a complete intersection. Let
\nu=(2a-1,b-1)
(equivalently
\nu=(a-1,2b-1)
) and let
\Delta_{\nu}=\det\mathcal{Z}_{\nu}
. Then

\deg(\Delta_{\nu})=2ab-\mathop{\rm dim}(H_{2})_{4a-1,3b-1}.

Moreover, the differential
d_{1}:\,(\mathcal{Z}_{1})_{\nu}\rightarrow(\mathcal{Z}_{0})_{\nu}
has a matrix representation which is square of size
2ab\times 2ab
if and only if
(H_{2})_{4a-1,3b-1}=0
.

Here
(H_{2})_{4a-1,3b-1}
denotes the second homology module of the bigraded strand
\mathcal{Z}_{4a-1,3b-1}
. For our purposes we will only consider the setting where
U
is free of basepoints, in which case
(H_{2})_{4a-1,3b-1}=0
and the determinant of
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
is simply the determinant of a square matrix representation of
d_{1}
in bidegree
(2a-1,b-1)
. However, we refer the reader to
[
7
]
for the general notion of the determinant of a complex, within the context of implicitization.

Lemma 2.4

(
[
1
, 7.4]
)
.

With the conditions of
Lemma

2.3
, suppose that the basepoints of
U
(if any) have multiplicity
e_{x}
. One has

\deg(\phi_{U})\deg(F)=2ab-\sum e_{x}

where
F\in S=\mathbb{K}[y_{0},y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}]
is the implicit equation of
X_{U}
.

Combining
Lemmas

2.3
and
2.4
, we obtain our primary tool to determine the implicit equation of
X_{U}
, which will be used throughout the article.

Theorem 2.5

(
[
1
, 7.5]
)
.

With the assumptions of
Lemma

2.3
, we have that
\Delta_{\nu}=F^{\deg\phi_{U}}
. In particular, from
Lemma

2.4
we have

\deg\Delta_{\nu}=\deg(F^{\deg\phi_{U}})=(\deg F)(\deg\phi_{U})=2ab-\sum e_{x}.

Hence by
Lemma

2.3
, it follows that
\mathop{\rm dim}(H_{2})_{4a-1,3b-1}=\sum e_{x}
.

In the absence of basepoints, the differential
(d_{1})_{\nu}:\,(\mathcal{Z}_{1})_{\nu}\rightarrow(\mathcal{Z}_{0})_{\nu}
is a square
2ab\times 2ab
matrix whose determinant is a power of the implicit equation
F
. Whereas the differential
d_{1}:\,\mathcal{Z}_{1}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{0}
is determined by the syzygy module of
I_{U}
by
2.2
, we note that the bigraded strand
(d_{1})_{\nu}:\,(\mathcal{Z}_{1})_{\nu}\rightarrow(\mathcal{Z}_{0})_{\nu}
is often determined by far fewer syzygies of
I_{U}
.

3.
Graded syzygies of bigraded ideals

With the necessary preliminaries established in
Section

2
, we now introduce the main setting for the duration of the paper. Although many of the conventions have been stated in the introduction, we briefly restate them here for convenience. Our primary setting is the following.

Setting 3.1
.

Let
R=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u,v]
with
\mathop{\rm bideg}s,t=(1,0)
and
\mathop{\rm bideg}u,v=(0,1)
. Let
U\subseteq R_{a,b}
be a subspace with basis
\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
and let
I_{U}=(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\subseteq R
. Assume that
U
is basepoint free and let
\phi_{U}:\,\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\longrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
be the regular map defined by
U
, with image
X_{U}
. Assume that
I_{U}
has a first syzygy
S
of bidegree
(0,n)
and that
b\geq 2n-1
. Moreover, assume that
S
is a singly graded syzygy in minimal degree, i.e. that
I_{U}
has no syzygy in bidegree
(0,m)
for
m<n
.

Here we may safely assume that
S
has minimal degree, in case there are multiple singly graded syzygies. Moreover, this minimality condition will be particularly useful in the following arguments.

To begin, we follow the approach of
[
25
]
and construct a
\mathbb{K}
-vector space associated to the syzygy
S
. As
S
consists of homogeneous polynomial entries in the subring
A=\mathbb{K}[u,v]
, we have

\sum_{i=0}^{3}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{ij}u^{n-j}v^{j}\big)p_{i}=0

for some
a_{ij}\in\mathbb{K}
. Expanding and rearranging, we obtain

(3.1)

0=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{ij}u^{n-j}v^{j}\big)p_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\big(\sum_{i=0}^{3}a_{ij}p_{i}\big)u^{n-j}v^{j}=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j}

where we write
f_{j}=\sum_{i=0}^{3}a_{ij}p_{i}
for
0\leq j\leq n
.

Similar to the observations made in
[
25
]
, the collection
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
will be key to determining the implicit equation of
X_{U}
. Notice that, as these polynomials are combinations of the generators of
I_{U}
, each
f_{j}
is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(a,b)
. In particular, the set
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
spans a
\mathbb{K}
-subspace of
U
.

Remark 3.2
.

We note that the set
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
is non-vanishing. Indeed, since
\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
is a basis of
U
, we note that
f_{j}=0
if and only if
a_{0j}=a_{1j}=a_{2j}=a_{3j}=0
. As these are the coefficients of the polynomial entries of
S
, it follows that at least one of
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
is nonzero. In particular, from the assumptions above, it follows that both
f_{0}\neq 0
and
f_{n}\neq 0
. Indeed, if
f_{0}=0
, then (
3.1
) shows that

0=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j}=v\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j-1}.

However, since
R
is a domain, we then have
\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j-1}=0
. Hence by rearranging as in (
3.1
), it follows that
I_{U}
has a syzygy in bidegree
(0,n-1)
, which is a contradiction. Similarly, it follows that
f_{n}\neq 0
.

Following the approach of
[
25
]
, we consider the vector space
V=\mathop{\rm Span}_{\mathbb{K}}\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
. Note that this is a
\mathbb{K}
-subspace of
U=\mathop{\rm Span}_{\mathbb{K}}\{p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

With the assumptions of
3.1
and
V
the subspace spanned by
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
, we have that
2\leq\mathop{\rm dim}V\leq 4
.

Proof.

The latter inequality is clear, noting that
V
is a subspace of
U
. Following
3.2
, we have that
V\neq 0
, and so it suffices to show that
\mathop{\rm dim}V\neq 1
. Suppose that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=1
and recall that
f_{0}\neq 0
by
3.2
. Thus
\{f_{0}\}
is a basis for
V
, and so we have
f_{j}=b_{j}f_{0}
for some
b_{j}\in\mathbb{K}
, for
1\leq j\leq n
. Thus by (
3.1
), we see

0=\sum_{j=0}^{n}f_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j}=f_{0}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j}

where we take
b_{0}=1
. Since
R
is a domain and
f_{0}\neq 0
, we have
\sum_{j=0}^{n}b_{j}u^{n-j}v^{j}=0
. However, this is impossible as
\{u^{n},u^{n-1}v,\ldots,v^{n}\}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent and

By

Remark 3.4

We note the range of

Remark 3.5
.

We note that it is relatively simple to compute

.

With

With the assumptions of

, we may construct the set

as in (

Proposition 4.1
.

With the assumptions of
3.1
, assume that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
. With (
4.2
), we have that
g_{0},g_{1}
is an
R
-regular sequence.

Proof.

Since
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
and
\{f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime}\}
is a basis, it follows from (
4.1
) that
B
has rank 2. With this, and since
\{u^{n},u^{n-1}v,\ldots,v^{n}\}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent, it follows from (
4.2
) that both
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
are nonzero. Thus
g_{0},g_{1}
is a regular sequence if and only if
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
have no nontrivial common factor. However, if
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
do share such a factor, it may be factored from
S
in (
4.3
) to produce a singly graded syzygy of strictly smaller degree, which contradicts the assumptions of
3.1
.
∎

Notice that, since
[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime})
, it follows that
[f_{0}^{\prime}\,\,f_{1}^{\prime}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
(g_{0},g_{1})
. However, by
4.1
, this ideal is generated by a regular sequence, hence
[f_{0}^{\prime}\,\,f_{1}^{\prime}]^{T}
must be a multiple of its Koszul syzygy. Thus we have

(4.4)

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{0}^{\prime}\\
f_{1}^{\prime}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}g_{1}\\
-g_{0}\end{array}\right]\alpha

for some bihomogeneous
\alpha\in R_{a,b-n}
. In particular, the syzygy
S
in (
4.3
) is a
reduced
Koszul syzygy on
I_{U}
.

Before we construct the necessary additional syzygies on
I_{U}
, we state a brief containment lemma for homogeneous ideals in two variables. Whereas this is likely well known to experts, we offer a brief proof using the classical Sylvester resultant in the homogeneous setting.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
f
and
g
be homogeneous polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x,y]
with
\deg f=m
and
\deg g=n
. If
\gcd(f,g)=1
, then there is a containment
(x,y)^{m+n-1}\subseteq(f,g)
.

Proof.

Writing
f=\sum_{i=0}^{m}a_{i}x^{m-i}y^{i}
and
g=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_{i}x^{n-i}y^{i}
for
a_{i},b_{i}\in\mathbb{K}
, we may form the
(m+n)\times(m+n)
homogeneous
Sylvester matrix
of
f
and
g
, with respect to the indeterminates
x,y
:

\mathrm{Syl}_{xy}(f,g)=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{m}&&&\\
&a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{m}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&a_{0}&a_{1}&\cdots&a_{m}\\[4.30554pt]
b_{0}&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n}&&&\\
&b_{0}&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n}&&\\
&&\ddots&&&\ddots&\\
&&&b_{0}&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n}\end{array}\right]\begin{array}[]{l}\left.\rule{0.0pt}{27.03003pt}\right\}\;\text{$n$}\\[6.0pt]
\left.\rule{0.0pt}{27.03003pt}\right\}\;\text{$m$}\end{array}

With this, notice that

\mathrm{Syl}_{xy}(f,g)\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x^{m+n-1}\\
x^{m+n-2}y\\
\vdots\\[4.30554pt]
y^{m+n-1}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x^{n-1}f\\
x^{n-2}y\,f\\
\vdots\\
y^{n-1}f\\[4.30554pt]
\hline\cr\\[-8.61108pt]
x^{m-1}g\\
x^{m-2}y\,g\\
\vdots\\
y^{m-1}g\end{array}\right]

and recall that, since
\gcd(f,g)=1
, the
resultant
of these polynomials,
\mathrm{Res}_{xy}(f,g)=\det\mathrm{Syl}_{xy}(f,g)
, is nonzero. As
\det\mathrm{Syl}_{xy}(f,g)\neq 0
, this matrix is invertible as it consists of entries in
\mathbb{K}
. Multiplying by this inverse in the matrix equation above shows that

(x,y)^{m+n-1}\subseteq(f)(x,y)^{n-1}+(g)(x,y)^{m-1}\subseteq(f,g)

as claimed.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

Notice that by
4.1
and
Lemma

4.2
we have
(u,v)^{2n-1}\subseteq(g_{0},g_{1})
, which will be essential to the proof of this section’s main result. We note that if
n=1
, the transition matrix
B
in (
4.2
) is the identity matrix. Hence
g_{0}=u
and
g_{1}=v
, and so the containment
(u,v)^{2n-1}\subseteq(g_{0},g_{1})
is clear in this case and the setting of
[
14
]
. For the case
n=2
, the containment
(u,v)^{2n-1}\subseteq(g_{0},g_{1})
was made explicit in
[
25
]
, depending on the choice of basis. In particular, the equations in
[
25
, 4.1]
are precisely the result from multiplying by the inverse of
\mathrm{Syl}_{uv}(g_{0},g_{1})
.

We now construct an additional pair of syzygies which, with
S
in (
4.3
), will determine the differential in the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
, and hence yield the implicit equation following
2.5
.

Theorem 4.4
.

With the assumptions of
3.1
, assume that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
. The ideal
I_{U}
has two additional syzygies
S_{1},S_{2}
of bidegree
(a,b-n)
such that
\mathop{\rm dim}\langle S,S_{1},S_{2}\rangle_{2a-1,b-1}=2ab
.

Proof.

We follow the approach taken in the proofs of
[
14
, 2.2]
and
[
25
, 4.3]
. Recall that
I_{U}=(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},p_{2},p_{3})
following
3.5
, and note that
f_{0}^{\prime}=\alpha g_{1}
and
f_{1}^{\prime}=-\alpha g_{0}
by (
4.4
). As noted, by
Lemma

4.2
it follows that
(u,v)^{2n-1}\subseteq(g_{0},g_{1})
, hence
p_{2},p_{3}\in(g_{0},g_{1})
as
I_{U}
is generated in bidegree
(a,b)
and
b\geq 2n-1
. Thus we may write

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}p_{2}&=q_{1}g_{1}-q_{0}g_{0}\\
p_{3}&=r_{1}g_{1}-r_{0}g_{0}\end{array}\right.

for some
q_{0},q_{1},r_{0},r_{1}\in R_{a,b-n}
. With this, notice that
q_{1}f_{0}^{\prime}+q_{0}f_{1}^{\prime}-\alpha p_{2}=0
, and also
r_{1}f_{0}^{\prime}+r_{0}f_{1}^{\prime}-\alpha p_{3}=0
. Hence both

(4.6)

S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}q_{1}\\
q_{0}\\
-\alpha\\
0\end{bmatrix}\qquad\text{and}\qquad S_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}\\
r_{0}\\
0\\
-\alpha\end{bmatrix}

are syzygies of
I_{U}
, with entries in bidegree
(a,b-n)
.

Combining the syzygies
S
,
S_{1}
, and
S_{2}
, the syzygy module of
I_{U}
contains the image of

(4.7)

M=\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}&q_{1}&r_{1}\\
g_{1}&q_{0}&r_{0}\\
0&-\alpha&0\\
0&0&-\alpha\end{bmatrix}_{.}

As the submatrix of
M
consisting of its last three rows is upper triangular, the columns of

span a free
R
-module. As these columns are precisely
S
,
S_{1}
, and
S_{2}
, the claim will follow once it has been shown that

consists of
2ab
linearly independent columns, where the independence follows from this observation.

As the bidegrees of the entries of

,
S_{1}
, and
S_{2}
are known, we need only count monomials in certain bidegrees to determine how many columns each syzygy contributes to
M_{2a-1,b-1}
. We also note that the number of columns contributed to
M_{2a-1,b-1}
and a matrix representation of

in
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
by each syzygy agree. As
S
has entries in bidegree
(0,n)
, it yields

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(2a-1,b-n-1))=2a(b-n)

columns of
M_{2a-1,b-1}
. Similarly, as both

and

are syzygies of bidegree

, they each give rise to

h^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(a-1,n-1))=an

columns of

. Summing these figures, we see that
\mathop{\rm dim}\langle S,S_{1},S_{2}\rangle_{2a-1,b-1}=2ab
, again noting the independence follows as
\{S,S_{1},S_{2}\}
spans a free
R
-module.
∎

5.

We now consider the second case of

when the vector space

associated to the syzygy
S
in
3.1
has dimension

. We note this setting was briefly studied for

in
[

]
. Moreover, following
3.4
,
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
only if

. In particular, we aim to recover and extend this previous work.

With the assumptions of
3.1
and the syzygy
S
, we may form the set
\{f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}\}
as in (
3.1
). As
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
, there is a subset
\{f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime}\}
among this set forming a basis for
V
. By
3.5
, and after possibly rearranging, we may assume that
I_{U}=(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime},p_{3})
. As noted in
3.6
, there exists a
3\times(n+1)
transition matrix
B
with entries in
\mathbb{K}
such that

(5.1)

[f_{0}^{\prime}\,\,f_{1}^{\prime}\,\,f_{2}^{\prime}]\,B=[f_{0}\,\ldots\,f_{n}].

Additionally, recall from (
3.1
) that
[u^{n}\,u^{n-1}v\,\ldots\,v^{n}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{n})
, hence

(5.2)

B\,[u^{n}\,u^{n-1}v\,\ldots\,v^{n}]^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}\\
g_{1}\\
g_{2}\end{bmatrix}

is a syzygy on
(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime})
, for some
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbb{K}[u,v]
. Thus by
3.6
and writing
I_{U}=(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime},p_{3})
, the syzygy
S
in bidegree
(0,n)
in
3.1
is precisely

(5.3)

S=\begin{bmatrix}g_{0}\\
g_{1}\\
g_{2}\\
0\end{bmatrix}_{.}

Since the syzygy in (
5.2
) consists of three polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[u,v]
, it is impossible for these entries to form a regular sequence, as in the previous section. However, this ideal of entries does admit a prescribed syzygetic structure.

Proposition 5.1
.

With the assumptions of
3.1
, assume that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
. With (
5.2
), the ideal of entries
(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})
is an Artinian ideal of
A=\mathbb{K}[u,v]
with minimal free resolution

(5.4)

0\longrightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}A(-n-\mu)\\
\oplus\\
A(-2n+\mu)\end{array}\overset{\psi}{\longrightarrow}\,A^{3}(-n)\overset{[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}\,\,g_{2}]}{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\rightarrow}\,A

for some
\mu\geq 1
, and we may assume
\mu\leq n-\mu
. Moreover, for
i=0,1,2
, we have
g_{i}=(-1)^{i}\det\psi_{i}
where
\psi_{i}
denotes the submatrix obtained by deleting row
i
of
\psi
.

Proof.

For the initial claim, we repeat the argument in the proof of
4.1
. As
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
and
\{f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime}\}
is a basis, the matrix
B
in (
5.1
) has rank
3
. Moreover, since
\{u^{n},u^{n-1}v,\ldots,v^{n}\}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent, from (
5.2
) it follows that
g_{i}\neq 0
for
i=0,1,2
. Hence it suffices to show that
\mathop{\rm ht}(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})\neq 1
, namely that
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}
have no common factor. However, any nontrivial shared factor could be factored from
S
in (
5.3
) to produce a syzygy of smaller degree, which contradicts the assumptions of
3.1
. Thus
\mathop{\rm ht}(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})=2
, and so this is an Artinian ideal of
A
.

Now that
(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})
is seen to be a Cohen-Macaulay ideal of codimension two, by the Hilbert-Burch theorem
[
15
, 20.15]
it must have a resolution with the shape of (
5.4
), and we also have
g_{i}=(-1)^{i}\det\psi_{i}
for
i=0,1,2
. The last item which must be verified is that the resolution in (
5.4
) is minimal, for which we need only show that
(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})
is minimally generated. Since
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}
are homogeneous of the same degree, it suffices to show that
\{g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}\}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent. However, this follows easily from (
5.2
) as
\{u^{n},u^{n-1}v,\ldots,v^{n}\}
is
\mathbb{K}
-linearly independent and
\mathop{\rm rank}B=3
.
∎

With (
5.4
), we note that
\psi
is the syzygy matrix of
(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})
, with columns
\psi=[C_{1}\,|\,C_{2}]
consisting of entries of degree
\mu
and
n-\mu
, respectively.

Remark 5.2
.

Since
[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}\,\,g_{2}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime})
, we note that
[f_{0}^{\prime}\,\,f_{1}^{\prime}\,\,f_{2}^{\prime}]^{T}
is a syzygy on the ideal
(g_{0},g_{1},g_{2})
. As such,

belongs to the image of the syzygy matrix

, in

. Thus from (

) and noting that

(5.5)

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f_{0}^{\prime}\\[2.15277pt]
f_{1}^{\prime}\\[2.15277pt]
f_{2}^{\prime}\end{array}\right]=\psi\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{array}\right]

for some bihomogeneous

and
\alpha_{2}\in R_{a,b-n+\mu}
.

With the resolution of

in (

once more to denote the columns of this syzygy matrix. As noted in

and

to denote the ideal of entries of these columns, recall from

that

Remark 5.3
.

From the discussion above, both

and

admit Hilbert-Burch resolutions, and hence may be realized as determinantal ideals of almost-square matrices. Letting

and

and

(5.6)

0\longrightarrow\begin{array}[]{c}A(-\mu-\nu_{1})\\
\oplus\\

\oplus\\

for some

and

. Moreover, the entries of

and

are precisely the signed minors of

and

, with the same conventions of

.

With this observation, we begin the search for additional syzygies on

. First however, we note that

and

may be written in terms of the syzygy matrices in (

Proposition 5.4
.

With the assumptions of
3.1
, assume that
\mathop{\rm dim}V=3
. There exist bihomogeneous polynomials
q_{0}\in R_{a,b-n-\nu_{1}}
and
q_{1}\in R_{a,b-\mu-n+\nu_{1}}
such that
\alpha_{1}=C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}[q_{0}\,\,q_{1}]^{T}
. Moreover, there exist
r_{0}\in R_{a,b-n-\nu_{2}}
and
r_{1}\in R_{a,b-2n+\mu+\nu_{2}}
such that
\alpha_{2}=C_{1}^{T}\varphi_{2}[r_{0}\,\,r_{1}]^{T}
.

Proof.

We first note that these degrees are nonnegative, which follows as
n\geq 2
by
3.4
,
b\geq 2n-1
by assumption in
3.1
, and since
\nu_{1}\leq\mu
,
\nu_{2}\leq n-\mu
, and
1\leq\mu\leq n-1
by
5.3
and
5.1
. We prove the assertion for
\alpha_{1}
as the claim for
\alpha_{2}
follows similarly.

We first show that the entries of
C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}
are coprime. Recall that the entries of these matrices are homogeneous polynomials in
A=\mathbb{K}[u,v]
. From (
5.4
) we have that
C_{2}
consists of entries in degree
n-\mu
and by (
5.6
),
\varphi_{1}
consists of entries in degree
\nu_{1}
and

in the first and second column, respectively. With this, we may write

where

are homogeneous polynomials in

with
\deg h_{0}=n-\mu+\nu_{1}
and
\deg h_{1}=n-\nu_{1}
. To show that

and

are coprime, write

with

. As such, there exist homogeneous

such that
h_{0}=h_{0}^{\prime}G
and

.

With this observation, notice that

0=[h_{0}\,\,h_{1}]\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}^{\prime}\\
-h_{0}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}=C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}^{\prime}\\
-h_{0}^{\prime}\end{bmatrix}

and so it follows that
\varphi_{1}[h_{1}^{\prime}\,\,-h_{0}^{\prime}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
C_{2}^{T}
. However, recall that
\varphi_{1}
is the syzygy matrix of
C_{1}^{T}
following
5.3
, hence
\varphi_{1}[h_{1}^{\prime}\,\,-h_{0}^{\prime}]^{T}
is a syzygy on
C_{1}^{T}
as well. As

and
C_{2}
are the columns of
\psi
, it follows that
\varphi_{1}[h_{1}^{\prime}\,\,-h_{0}^{\prime}]^{T}\in\ker\psi^{T}
. Applying the functor
{\rm Hom}_{A}(-,A)
and dualizing (
5.4
), one obtains the complex

(5.7)

0\longrightarrow A\overset{[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}\,\,g_{2}]^{T}}{\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\relbar\joinrel\rightarrow}A^{3}(n)\overset{\psi^{T}}{\longrightarrow}\begin{array}[]{c}A(n+\mu)\\
\oplus\\
A(2n-\mu)\end{array}

which remains exact by the Buchsbaum-Eisenbud acyclicity criterion
[
6
, Cor. 1]
and
5.1
. Hence
\ker\psi^{T}=\mathop{\rm im}[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}\,\,g_{2}]^{T}
, and so
\varphi_{1}[h_{1}^{\prime}\,\,-h_{0}^{\prime}]^{T}
is a multiple of
[g_{0}\,\,g_{1}\,\,g_{2}]^{T}
. However, the entries of
\varphi_{1}[h_{1}^{\prime}\,\,-h_{0}^{\prime}]^{T}
have degree

, where
d=\deg G
, and
g_{0},g_{1},g_{2}
have degree

. Thus it follows that

, i.e.

is a unit. Hence the entries of
C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}=[h_{0}\,\,h_{1}]
are indeed coprime.

With this, we verify the original assertion for
\alpha_{1}
. Writing
C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}=[h_{0}\,\,h_{1}]
once more, recall that

are homogeneous polynomials in

with
\deg h_{0}=n-\mu+\nu_{1}
and
\deg h_{1}=n-\nu_{1}
. Hence by

, we have
(u,v)^{2n-\mu-1}\subseteq(h_{0},h_{1})
in

, since

. Thus in

, since
\alpha_{1}\in R_{a,b-\mu}
and

, we have

\alpha_{1}\in(u,v)^{b-\mu}\subseteq(u,v)^{2n-\mu-1}\subseteq(h_{0},h_{1}).

Hence we may write
\alpha_{1}=q_{0}h_{0}+q_{1}h_{1}=C_{2}^{T}\varphi_{1}[q_{0}\,\,q_{1}]^{T}
for some
q_{0}\in R_{a,b-n-\nu_{1}}
and
q_{1}\in R_{a,b-\mu-n+\nu_{1}}
. A similar argument verifies the claim for
\alpha_{2}
.
∎

With this, we will show that the subideal
J=(f_{0}^{\prime},f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime})\subseteq I_{U}
is a perfect
R
-ideal of grade two, and hence has a Hilbert-Burch resolution. In particular,

then has only one additional generator, in addition to (

), which may be extended to a syzygy on

. First however, we state a couple of necessary lemmas, the first of which will be particularly useful for tensor product surfaces free of basepoints.

Lemma 5.5

(
[
17
, V.1.4.3]
)
.

With
R=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u,v]
as in
3.1
, let
f\in R_{a,b}
and
g\in R_{c,d}
be bihomogeneous polynomials with
\gcd(f,g)=1
. The curves
V(f)
and
V(g)
in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
intersect at
ad+bc
points, counting multiplicity.

As
U
is basepoint-free,
Lemma

5.5
implies that the only proper two-generated ideals containing
I_{U}
belong to either the subring
\mathbb{K}[u,v]
or
\mathbb{K}[s,t]
. Hence
Lemma

5.5
will prove useful for the duration of the article. The second lemma we require is less general, however it will be used multiple times throughout.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})
be a
3\times 2
matrix with entries in a ring
R
. Write
\delta_{i}=(-1)^{i}\det A_{i}
where
A_{i}
denotes the submatrix of
A
obtained by deleting row
i
, for
i=0,1,2
. For a
3\times 1
column
D
with entries in
R
, consider the
3\times 2
matrix

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}&\delta_{0}\\
D&\delta_{1}\\
&\delta_{2}\end{array}\right]_{.}

Similarly, writing
\Delta_{i}=(-1)^{i}\det B_{i}
, we have

\Delta_{i}=\det\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{i0}&a_{i1}\\[2.15277pt]
D^{T}C_{1}&D^{T}C_{2}\end{array}\right]

for
i=0,1,2
, where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
denote the columns of
A=[C_{1}\,|\,C_{2}]
.

Proof.

Notice that this determinant agrees with the product
D^{T}A[-a_{i1}\,\,a_{i0}]^{T}
. In particular, the product
A[-a_{i1}\,\,a_{i0}]^{T}
has
0
as its
i
th entry and the entries in position
j\neq i
are precisely the minors involving the
i
th and
j
th rows of
A
. Hence it follows that
D^{T}A[-a_{i1}\,\,a_{i0}]^{T}
agrees with the
i
th signed minor of
B
.
∎

With

5.5
and
5.6
, we may now describe the syzygies of the subideal

.

Proposition 5.7
.

The
R
-ideal

is perfect of grade two with syzygy matrix

(5.8)

\Theta=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}&g_{0}\\

and

By the Hilbert-Burch theorem

, it suffices to show that the generators of

agree with the signed minors of

, and also that

. For the first assertion, we employ

and write

Recall that

Lemma 5.10
.

Let

be an

matrix with entries in a ring. The product

-1&0\end{bmatrix}A^{T}

\hline\cr R_{j}\end{array}\right]

Multiplying

th row of this matrix and the

is precisely the

above.

-t^{2}v^{2}\end{bmatrix}&\alpha_{3}=C_{1}^{T}\varphi_{3}\gamma_{23}\begin{bmatrix}c_{10}\\
c_{11}\end{bmatrix}=[v\,\,\,u^{3}]\begin{bmatrix}t^{2}v^{3}\\
s^{2}u\end{bmatrix}\end{array}

and so, following
6.7
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ccc}S_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}t^{2}uv^{2}-2t^{2}u^{2}v\\

-s^{2}u^{3}-2t^{2}v^{3}\\
-s^{2}u^{2}v\end{bmatrix}_{,}&S_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}s^{2}u^{2}v-t^{2}v^{3}\\

-s^{2}v^{3}\\

for the additional syzygies on
I_{U}
.

Write
\mathbb{K}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
to denote the coordinate ring of
\mathbb{P}^{3}
and let
M=[S\,|\,S_{1}\,|\,S_{2}\,|\,S_{3}]
be as in the proof of
6.8
.
Following
2.2
and
Lemma

2.3
, we multiply
[x_{0}\,\,x_{1}\,\,x_{2}\,\,x_{3}]M
and take this product in bidegree
(2a-1,b-1)
of
R=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u,v]
. Hence by
6.8
and its corollary, we obtain a
20\times 20
matrix representation of the differential

of
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
, with entries in
\mathbb{K}[x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}]
. Moreover, a computation shows that
\det d_{1}=F^{2}
where
F
is the implicit equation. In particular,
\deg F=10
and by
2.5
the degree of the rational map defined by
U
is
\deg\phi_{U}=2
.

Although the process outlined in the current and previous sections requires the computation of multiple syzygies, we note that the syzygies needed to construct
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
have entries in
\mathbb{K}[u,v]
and are all of Hilbert-Burch type. In particular, they are relatively quick to compute, especially compared to computing
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
over
R=\mathbb{K}[s,t,u,v]
. For instance, computations in
Macaulay2

[
16
]
show that finding the implicit equation of
X_{U}
in
6.10
using the constructions of
6.8
is slightly faster than computing the entire syzygy module
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
and using it to produce
\mathcal{Z}_{2a-1,b-1}
. However, for larger
a
,
b
, and
n
,
\mathop{\rm syz}(I_{U})
typically becomes larger, whereas the number of syzygies required here is fixed, and these syzygies involve fewer variables. Further computations show that the methods presented here tend to be faster overall.

7.
Open questions

We conclude the article with some open questions stemming from the results presented here.

Question 7.1
.

The assumption that
U
is basepoint-free is actually used quite sparingly throughout. Regardless of basepoints, the constructions presented here are syzygies on
I_{U}
and hence appear within the approximation complex. This raises the question of whether these constructions can be applied to determine the implicit equation of
X_{U}
when basepoints are present. In particular, can one determine the implicit equation if
I_{U}
has a singly graded syzygy and if there are basepoints present which are
locally complete intersections
, or if the basepoints of
\phi_{U}
are in generic position, as in
[
13
]
?

Question 7.2
.

Within the assumptions of
3.1
, we assume that the singly graded syzygy has minimal degree, in case there are multiple such syzygies. In
[
14
]
, it is shown that basepoint-freeness ensures there can be at most one such syzygy when
n=1
. As the setting of
[
14
]
is similar to the case when
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
in
Section

4
, it seems plausible that one might be able to extend this result. Does this assumption limit the number of singly graded minimal syzygies in general?

Question 7.3
.

In
[
14
]
, Duarte and Schenck show that the singular locus of
X_{U}
contains a line if
I_{U}
has a linear syzygy. As the setting of
[
14
]
is similar to the general case when
\mathop{\rm dim}V=2
in
Section

4
, it seems natural to ask whether this phenomenon persists in general. Do the matrix representations of the implicit equation obtained here give insight into the singular locus of
X_{U}
?

Question 7.4
.

As noted in the introduction, the importance of a singly graded syzygy of a tensor product surface ideal
I_{U}
was originally noticed by Schenck, Seceleanu, and Validashti in
[
20
]
. There, the presence of a linear syzygy in bidegree
(0,1)
or
(1,0)
was shown to impose strong conditions on a bigraded free resolution of
I_{U}
when
U\subseteq H^{0}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}}(2,1))
, motivating the further study by Duarte and Schenck in
[
14
]
. Does a similar phenomenon occur in a free resolution of
I_{U}
if it has a syzygy in bidegree
(0,n)
or
(n,0)
in general? In particular, the syzygies constructed here and the exact sequences in (
5.13
) and (
6.14
) may be taken as summands of such a resolution.
