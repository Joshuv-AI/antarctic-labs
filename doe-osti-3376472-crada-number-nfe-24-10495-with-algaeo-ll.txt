 ABSTRACT

This study investigated the potential for a synthetic consortium of mutualistic terrestrial microbes—
comprising the fungi Laccaria bicolor and Serendipita indica alongside bacterial Pseudomonas strains—to
influence the growth and productivity of the freshwater microalgae Chlorella vulgaris. The project aimed to
determine if microbial complexes engineered to enhance terrestrial plant growth could provide similar
growth-promoting benefits or pathogen resistance within an aquatic algal system. Using a quantitative
experimental design, C. vulgaris was co-cultured with the microbial mix under controlled laboratory
conditions, with growth rates, biomass density, and metabolic activity monitored over a standard cultivation
period. The results demonstrated no significant symbiotic relationship or growth enhancement between these
terrestrial microbes and the microalgae, as the C. vulgaris maintained independent growth trajectories
unaffected by the fungal or bacterial inoculants. We conclude that the specialized mutualisms of these fungi
and bacteria are likely niche-specific to vascular plants and do not readily translate to the phycosphere of C.
vulgaris. These findings are valuable to synthetic biologists and bioenergy researchers, as they define the
functional boundaries of inter-kingdom microbial engineering and underscore the necessity of selecting
niche-compatible species when designing consortia for industrial algal cultivation.

 1. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The primary purpose of this project was to determine if a specific complex of mutualistic microbes—
originally designed to increase terrestrial plant productivity—could establish a beneficial relationship with
the freshwater microalga Chlorella vulgaris.

Research Problem: Algal monocultures are often susceptible to environmental stressors and nutrient
limitations, leading to "culture crashes." While terrestrial mutualists like Laccaria bicolor, Serendipita
indica, and Pseudomonas strains are known to enhance nutrient uptake and pathogen resistance in vascular
plants, their efficacy in an aquatic phycosphere remained unexplored.

Objectives:
  To assess the viability of terrestrial fungal and bacterial strains in an aquatic growth
 medium.
  To quantify changes in C. vulgaris biomass and growth rates when co-cultured with these
 microbes.
  To identify any synergistic nutrient cycling or protective buffering effects provided by the
 consortium.

 3. TECHNICAL DISCUSSION OF WORK PERFORMED BY ALL PARTIES

The integration of microbial consortia into algal cultivation systems represents a frontier in biotechnological
efforts to stabilize biomass production. My interest in this project stemmed from the potential to bridge
terrestrial microbiology with aquatic biotechnology. By exploring whether established plant-growth-
promoting microbes could translate their benefits to a microalgal host, we aimed to address the inherent
instabilities in industrial-scale algal ponds. This report details the investigation conducted in collaboration
with Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), focusing on the cross-domain application of specific fungal
and bacterial mutualists.

The team utilized a quantitative experimental approach. We established controlled co-cultures of C. vulgaris
alongside varying concentrations of the fungal-bacterial mix. Monitoring was conducted over a 14-day
period, utilizing optical density (OD680) to track algal growth and fluorometry to assess photosynthetic
efficiency (Fv/Fm).

Data analysis revealed that the terrestrial microbial complex did not form a measurable symbiotic or
mutualistic relationship with Chlorella vulgaris. Growth curves for the inoculated samples mirrored the
axenic control groups with no statistically significant deviation in biomass density or lipid accumulation.
These results suggest that the biochemical signaling and nutrient exchange mechanisms inherent to these
specific terrestrial mutualists are niche-specific and do not naturally extend to microalgal systems.

 5. COMMERCIALIZATION POSSIBILITIES

Synthetic nitrogen fertilizers degrade the soil biology that farmers depend on for long-term productivity.
Nearly half of US farmers report declining soil quality, and most biological alternatives are either too
expensive, too complex, or too unreliable to replace them.

Algaeo was founded to close that gap. Our GrowForce consortia delivers the same biology that premium
biostimulant brands charge $15–25/acre for — at $2–5/acre — by keeping production close to the source
and working directly with co-op distribution networks rather than around them.

 6. PLANS FOR FUTURE COLLABORATION

Through this collaboration, I gained significant technical and theoretical knowledge:

  Microbial Inter-kingdom Dynamics: I learned how host-specificity dictates the success of
 symbiotic relationships and the complex signaling required for microbes to colonize a host.
  Laboratory Tools: I gained proficiency with advanced fluorometry and high-throughput plate
 readers used for monitoring algal health.
  Strain Optimization: I developed a deeper understanding of the "Algal Phycosphere"—the area
 immediately surrounding an algal cell—and how it differs chemically from the terrestrial
 rhizosphere.

I met the project milestone of establishing a standardized co-culture protocol that ORNL can now use for
future cross-domain microbial testing.

 7. CONCLUSION

This experience at ORNL has been transformative for my career trajectory. Initially, my focus was purely on
algal biology; however, observing the lack of interaction between these specific microbes has piqued my
interest in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering.
