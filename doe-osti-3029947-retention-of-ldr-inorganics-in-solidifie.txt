 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 To support the offsite disposal of Hanford tank waste as a cementitious solidified form, treatment
 of tank waste must address 132 organic and 13 inorganic compounds regulated under the
 Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Land Disposal Restrictions (LDR). These
 compounds are linked to tank waste through permit waste codes and underlying hazardous
 constituents. Managing these regulated constituents complicates the use of ambient temperature
 treatment methods (like cement-based solidification) since most require post-treatment sampling
 to confirm compliance with concentration-based or EPA SW-846 Method 1311 (Toxicity
 Characteristic Leach Procedure, TCLP) standards. 1 However, to meet processing needs and
 schedules, including, for example, the West Area Tank Treatment (WATT) program for the
 retrieval of 22 (up to 24) single-shell tanks from S, SX, and U Tank Farms in the 200 West Area,
 it is necessary to dispose of solidified/stabilized Hanford tank waste without post-treatment
 sampling. Therefore, a key priority treatment of offsite disposal of solidified tank waste is to
 seek a treatment variance (per 40 CFR 268.44) to establish a technology-based RCRA LDR
 treatment standard that removes the need for post-treatment verification sampling, streamlining
 regulatory compliance and enabling disposal rates to waste pretreatment and treatment rates.

 Such a treatment variance (or variances) would propose a method-based treatment standard(s)
 using process knowledge, laboratory data, and mass balance calculations to demonstrate a priori
 compliance. Liquid waste would be sampled (before solidification) and analyzed. The sampling
 results would then be used to predict LDR species concentrations and/or EPA Method 1311
 based leaching results in the final solidified waste. For constituents regulated by EPA Method
 1311 based standards, a priori determined retention factors are proposed for use in conjunction
 with mass balance calculations to predict TCLP leachate concentration to ensure compliance
 with land disposal restrictions prior to final treatment and disposal.

 This report provides a summary of the laboratory data and statistical analysis used to calculate
 retention factors in solidified/stabilized Hanford tank waste for the 13 LDR inorganic species
 associated with Hanford tank waste [SRNL-STI-2020-00228, 2020, Evaluation of Technologies
 for Enhancing Grout for Immobilizing Hanford Supplemental Low-Activity Waste (SLAW)]. The
 data presented is a summary of the results of research performed to build a correlation between
 the untreated waste concentration and the likely TCLP response of a solidified/stabilized waste
 form. This report establishes the efficacy of two treatment technologies (chemical reduction
 [CHRED] and stabilization [STABL]) that are key to the Sample-and-Send strategy.

 1.0 INTRODUCTION

 In January 2025, new and revised milestones in the Hanford Federal Facility Agreement and
 Consent Order (also known as the Tri-Party Agreement (TPA)) were finalized, following holistic
 negotiations between the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), Washington State Department of
 Ecology (Ecology), and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). New interim
 milestone M-045-135 established a milestone for retrieving waste from 22 single-shell tanks
 (SST) located in the 241 2-S, SX, and U Farms in Hanford’s 200 West Area (200W) by 2040,
 contingent on DOE having a regulatory pathway to grout and dispose of the waste offsite. In
 addition, pretreated tank waste from the 200 East Area may also be solidified and disposed of
 offsite to ensure the availability of critical double-shell tank (DST) space, meet retrieval
 obligations, and optimize 200 East Area operations. The treatment and offsite disposal of
 solidified waste from the 200 East Area would proceed in parallel with the Direct-Feed Low
 Activity Waste (“DFLAW”) program and operation of the Waste Treatment and Immobilization
 Plant.

 Retrieved liquid and solid material is sent to the DSTs where solids settling and waste
 qualification will take place. Qualified waste will subsequently be pretreated by filtration and ion
 exchange to remove key radionuclides and transferred to a treatment facility. Ultimately, the
 treated waste will be sent for out-of-state disposal as a cementitious solidified/stabilized waste
 form. 3

 Treatment of the waste must consider the 132 organic and 13 inorganic compounds regulated by
 Resource Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Land Disposal Restrictions (LDR) (RPP-
 RPT-63493,Tank Waste LDR Organics Data Summary for Sample-and-Send). These compounds
 are associated with Hanford tank waste through the RCRA Part A permit application waste codes
 for the double-shell tanks (DSTs) and SSTs and applicable underlying hazardous constituents
 from Title 40, Code of Federal Regulations, Part 268.48 “Universal treatment standards.”
 (40 CFR 268.48). The presence of LDR-regulated constituents adds complexity to treating and
 disposing of liquid pretreated waste (PTW) by an ambient temperature treatment method, such as
 cementitious solidification/stabilization [SRNL-STI-2020-00228, Evaluation of Technologies for
 Enhancing Grout for Immobilizing Hanford Supplemental Low-Activity Waste (SLAW)]. As a
 general matter when using concentration-based or EPA Method 1311 4 based treatment standards
 (which is the case for the RCRA regulated compounds), treatment and disposal facilities are
 required to conduct post-treatment sampling to determine whether LDR standards have been
 met. To successfully implement offsite disposal of solidified tank waste at the necessary
 treatment rates and comply with existing commitments, solidified/stabilized Hanford tank waste
 will need to be disposed of without performing post-treatment sampling. Testing is not required
 when the applicable treatment standard is a specified technology from 40 CFR 268.42 (e.g.,
 STABL). For this reason, establishing, through a treatment variance (40 CFR 268.44), a

 technology-based RCRA LDR treatment standard that does not require post treatment

 A proposed treatment variance strategy to support offsite disposal of solidified waste would
 establish a method-based RCRA LDR treatment standard that; when combined with enabling
 removal, destruction, and/or immobilization technologies (as applicable or necessary); can
 demonstrate proven performance to meet both concentration-based and toxicity characteristic
 leaching procedure (TCLP)-based standards for the Hanford LDR constituents. The intent is to
 avoid the need for long-term post treatment sampling through a priori demonstration of the
 effectiveness of removal, destruction, and immobilization technologies on bounding
 concentrations of LDR species.

 To this end, Hanford Tank Waste Operations & Closure (H2C) is working with DOE’s national
 laboratories and commercial mixed low-level waste treatment and disposal facilities to develop
 the data necessary to implement a “Sample-and-Send” approach to demonstrate LDR compliance
 in the final solidified waste form. The framework for Sample-and-Send relies on sampling and
 analyzing the liquid tank waste prior to pretreatment and applying a technical basis through
 process knowledge to demonstrate compliance with LDR requirements. The process knowledge
 is derived through laboratory testing and mass balance calculations to demonstrate compliance
 with the various RCRA requirements.

 This report provides a summary of the laboratory data and statistical analysis used to calculate
 retention factors in solidified/stabilized Hanford tank waste for the 13 LDR inorganic species
 associated with Hanford tank waste (SRNL-STI-2020-00228). The data presented is a summary
 of the results of research performed to build a correlation between the untreated waste
 concentration and the TCLP response of a solidified/stabilized waste form. This report
 establishes the efficacy of two treatment technologies found in 40 CFR 268.42, CHRED
 (chemical reduction) and STABL (stabilization), that are key to the Sample-and-Send strategy.

 2.2 FLOWSHEET DESCRIPTION

 The West Area Tank Treatment (WATT) Flowsheet, RPP-RPT-64319 Revision 3, (henceforth
 “flowsheet”) evaluates the planned 200W tank farms operations for 24 200W tanks (the TPA
 required 22 tanks plus two alternate tanks). The flowsheet is a high-level analysis of SST
 retrieval and pretreatment for planning purposes and provides a means of evaluating retrieval
 sequences, solid accumulation, the composition of the waste (before and after pretreatment), and
 waste classification and transportation properties. The radiological and chemical data used as
 inputs to the flowsheet were derived from a query of the Hanford Best-Basis Inventory (BBI) on
 May 28, 2025, with a radionuclide decay date of 1/1/2025 (TWINS 2025a, TWINS 2025b,
         TWINS 2025c, TWINS 2025d).

 The waste composition results from the flowsheet (Table 2-1) served as a planning basis for
 much of the research discussed in later sections of this report are relevant to the research
 conducted to date to support the Sample-and-Send strategy. The predicted range (minimum and
 maximum) and average concentration are shown with an estimated upper confidence limit. It is
 emphasized the upper confidence limit is an estimate and only for information. The value is
 based on an empirical rule and Chebyshev's inequality, and is the average concentration plus
 three standard deviations. The empirical rule predicts for, a normal distribution, that 99.7% of
 observations fall within the first three standard deviations (µ ± 3σ) of the mean. Similarly,
 Chebyshev's inequality states that even for non-normally distributed variables, at least 88.8% of
 cases should fall within properly calculated three-sigma intervals 6. This approach to estimating
 an upper confidence limit was chosen because the number of samples for the means and relative
 standard deviations (RSD) presented in the BBI are unknown. It is assumed the RSD in the BBI
 inputs to the flowsheet are applicable to the WWF outputs of the flowsheet, which in turn
 necessitates the enabling assumptions presented below. More precise statistical methods would
 also be subject to these same assumptions, rendering them an estimate as well.

 3.0 TECHNOLOGY DESCRIPTION

 The term solidification/stabilization has specific technical definitions and is sometimes used to
 describe ambient temperature waste treatment processes that produce waste forms designed to
 meet certain performance criteria (e.g., contaminant retentions). Grout is the term commonly
 used within the DOE complex to describe solidified/stabilized waste made through ambient
 temperature processes that involve either hydration or polymerization 8 of dry reagents to form a
 solid mass composed of various mineral phases. Grouted waste forms are widely used for low-
 level and hazardous aqueous waste stabilization and solidification.

 The primary components of grout include blast furnace slag (BFS), Class F fly ash (FA), to a
 lesser degree, and ordinary portland cement [calcium silicate] Type I/II 9 (OPC) with a varying
 water to dry reagent (binder) ratio (w/b). Additives (e.g., clays or other components such as
 getters 10) may be used for targeted retention of specific waste constituents. Numerous types of
 cement are used for waste treatment depending on the stabilization and processing needs (e.g.,
 portland cement, slag cement, calcium aluminate cement, calcium sulfoaluminate cement, and
 various phosphate cements). These cements react with water to form low solubility hydrated
 phases within the waste form matrix. Other material types used to produce low temperature
 waste forms include calcium sulfate cement, geopolymers (aluminosilicate precursor that
 hardens/crosslinked by condensation reactions with alkalis, and water to form a solid), zeolites,
 clays, and organic polymer waste forms (SRNL-STI-2023-00007, Follow-on Report of Analysis
 of Approaches to Supplemental Treatment of Low‑Activity Waste at the Hanford Nuclear
 Reservation: Supporting Information).

 The mineral phases of grout exhibit a range of morphologies and particle sizes. The interactions
 between these phases dictate the physical and chemical properties of the material and affect how
 different waste compounds are immobilized within the matrix. The matrix phases in cementitious
 waste forms based on portland cement, slag cement (ground granulated blast furnace slag), and
 supplemental cementitious materials are poorly crystalline low solubility hydrate phases (gels)
 and crystalline hydrated solids. The individual phases form by reaction between the water in the
 waste and the unhydrated cementitious materials (SRNL-STI-2020-00228). These matrix phases
 have a range of morphologies and particles sizes from submicrons to microns, producing a
 heterogeneous microstructure. The phase assemblages depend on the bulk oxide composition of
 the reactive cement compounds and waste feed (SRNL-STI-2014-00397, X-Ray Diffraction of
 Slag-Based Sodium Salt Waste Forms).

 or incorporated into the matrix itself. The primary mechanisms for contaminant stabilization in
 grout waste forms are that contaminants can: (1) occupy lattice positions in matrix phases
 (e.g., selenium substitution for sulphate in ettringite [Zhang and Reardon 2003]), (2) be
 physically sorbed or chemically bound on the surfaces of the matrix particles (e.g., arsenic
 sorption onto iron-oxide cement [Kundu and Gupta 2006]), and (3) precipitated as low solubility
 species (e.g., Cr(III) and Tc(IV) in reducing grout). Many anions will remain in the pore solution
 in the waste form, and the release is controlled by the physical transport pathways in the waste
 form (e.g., porosity, tortuosity, and constrictivity). Some of these contaminants can incorporate
 into the matrix phases, providing improved retention. Other anions such as Cl⁻, have binding
 capacity in the matrix and while some oxyanions (SO42- and CO32-) are involved in generating
 hydrated phases combining with cations (SRNL-STI-2023-00007).

 Many elements can be involved in the hydration or polymeric reactions that produce the matrix
 of a grout waste form. The wide range of hydrated phases that are produced in the grout as a
 result are receptive to wide variations in waste composition resulting in minimal impact on the
 properties of the grout. As such, a single grout formulation can be used for a variety of wastes
 without the need for further formulation development. However, the properties of the grout are
 crucial for its effectiveness in stabilizing waste, as they affect how well waste materials are
 immobilized within the structure. Environmental interactions, driven by the porous nature and
 large reactive surface area of the grout, further influence the evolution of the matrix, impacting
 its long-term stability and contaminant retention capabilities. Understanding the interactions and
 properties within grout allows for the optimization of formulations tailored to specific waste
 types, enhancing the effectiveness of waste management strategies in professional applications.

 The release of soluble constituents from a grout waste form is assumed to be dominated by a
 diffusive process within the pore space of the waste form, although other physical and chemical
 processes within the waste form may be involved (e.g., sorption, redox). Diffusive release is a
 complex process involving the pore network of the grout. The migration of species in grout is
 impacted directly by the porosity (void fraction), the tortuosity of the porous network (how much
 the path of a fluid or particle is elongated in a porous material), and constrictivity of the pores
 (ratio of the diameter of a diffusing particle to the diameter of the pore).

 3.1 CONSTITUENT RETENTION

 Additives (e.g., clays, resins, geopolymers, or other components such as getters) may be used for
 targeted retention of specific waste constituents. A getter is a material that is added to a waste
 stream and/or waste form to stabilize (or capture) a specific contaminant in the resulting waste
 form and slow the release (or hold) of the contaminant(s) over time. Typically, getters are a
 combination of commercially available materials containing the necessary chemistry to react
 with the target constituent(s) in the waste and enhance retention. Some reactants are more
 effective with inorganics (reducers, geopolymers, zeolites, resins), while others are more specific
 to organics (activated carbon). A brief description of common pathways used to improve
 contaminant retention in a waste form are provided below.

 Reduction: The most widely applied approach to improving immobilization of metals is the
 generation and maintenance of reducing conditions within the waste form to drive these elements
 to less soluble forms. Reducing conditions are commonly attained using BFS in the formulation
 (Langton 1988). Other materials may be utilized for the reduction of specific species or to
 maintain reducing conditions in the waste form. Chemical reduction is susceptible to re-
 oxidation, the rate of which is dependent on several variables (e.g., waste form chemistry, waste
 form size) and to competition with other redox sensitive components of the waste (e.g., Cr for
 Tc). Exposure to O2 or other oxidizing species can consume the reduction capacity in the waste
 form, which can allow reduced species to re-oxidize and become more mobile (PNNL-22977,
 Characterization of Technetium Speciation in Cast Stone). This process remains one of the main
 unknowns with the disposal of grouted waste at facilities located in semi-arid conditions where a
 pathway to groundwater exists (e.g., Hanford Integrated Disposal Facility). The rate at which
 reduction capacity is consumed by oxygen introduced by an infiltrating aqueous or gas phase is
 the primary unknown (PNNL-36409, Measuring the Spatial Rate of Grout Waste Form
 Reoxidation). Chemical reduction is discussed in greater detail in Section 3.2.

 Porosity: As the pore network of the grout impacts the diffusivity of species, further restricting
 this network can slow the migration of contaminants. Porosity reduction can be achieved through
 increased packing density, lower water to dry reagent (binder) ratio (w/b) or the inclusion of pore
 filling admixes (e.g., Xypex®11). The reduced porosity approach has been successfully
 demonstrated through the use of ultra-high performance grout for solid secondary waste
 encapsulation (SRNL-STI-2020-00563, Ultra-High-Performance Concrete for Encapsulation of
 HEPA Filters). However, these formulations present challenges in mixing with liquid salt wastes
 due to the low water content used and precipitation of salts. Other formulations targeting higher
 packing density (less pore space) have been tested with varying degrees of success (VSL-
 19R4630-1, 2019, Formulation Development and Testing of Ammonia Tolerant Grout). This
 approach may be susceptible to changes in the waste form matrix over time (e.g., calcium
 leaching leading to pore size increase).

 Mineral Incorporation: Incorporating a contaminant into the structure of a mineral phase can
 limit release as the migration of the contaminant becomes reliant on the stability of the parent
 mineral phase. This is a similar concept to the immobilization of contaminants in glass where
 contaminants are a part of the glass structure. Recently both Tc (as mobile pertechnetate, TcO4-)
 and I (as iodate, IO3-) have been shown to incorporate into common minerals found in grout
 waste forms (“Competitive TcO4–, IO3–, and CrO42– Incorporation into Ettringite” [Gillispie et al.
 2020], “Immobilizing Pertechnetate in Ettringite via Sulfate Substitution” [Saslow et al. 2020],
 “Behavior of iodate substituted ettringite during aqueous leaching” [Avalos et al. 2021]). Testing
 of the mineral incorporation approach using a circumneutral (pH 5.8), liquid salt waste stream
 led to one of the lowest measured diffusion rates to date for waste form made with a liquid salt
 waste ("The evolution of hydrated lime-based cementitious waste forms during leach testing
 leading to enhanced technetium retention" [Bourchy et al. 2022]). This process would be

 3.2 CHEMICAL REDUCTION

 3.2.1 Overview

 40 CFR 268.42 defines chemical reduction (CHRED) as technology-based treatment standard.
 CHRED is defined as:

 Chemical reduction utilizing the following reducing reagents (or waste reagents) or
 combinations of reagents such as (1) sulfur dioxide; (2) sodium, potassium, or alkali
 salts or sulfites, bisulfites, metabisulfites, and polyethylene glycols (e.g., NaPEG and
 KPEG); (3) sodium hydrosulfide; (4) ferrous salts; and/or (5) other reducing
 reagents of equivalent efficiency, performed in units operated such that a surrogate
 compound or indicator parameter has been substantially reduced in concentration in
 the residuals... Chemical reduction is commonly used for the reduction of hexavalent
 chromium to the trivalent state.

 CHRED is the most widely applied approach to improving immobilization of metals by reducing
 constituents of concern to less soluble, lower oxidation states. Chromium, particularly in the
 hexavalent state [Cr(VI)], is the predominant LDR inorganic in West Area waste (see Table 2-1)
 and can be treated by chemical reduction (PNNL-32458, valuation of Degradation Mechanisms
 for Solid Secondary Waste Grout Waste Forms, SRNL-STI-2020-00228, valuation of
 Technologies for Enhancing Grout for Immobilizing Hanford Supplemental Low-Activity Waste
 (SLAW), RPP-RPT-66039). Reducing agents such as ferrous sulfate and stannous sulfate, among
 many others, convert soluble Cr(VI) to insoluble Cr(III), which precipitates as hydroxides or
 becomes incorporated into stable mineral phases. Reduction significantly enhances the retention
 of chromium [Cr(VI)] in grout by reducing it to the less mobile and less toxic trivalent form.

 3.2.2 Examples of Chrome Reductive Chemistry

 Ferrous sulfate (FeSO₄), provides Fe(II), which directly reduces Cr(VI) to Cr(III) within the
 waste. The oxidation/reduction reaction is:

                  CrO42- + 3Fe2+ + 4H2O → Cr(OH)3 (s) + 3Fe3+ + 5OH-

RPP-RPT-66455 Rev.00                            4/20/2026 - 10:31 AM                                             18 of 100

 The resulting Cr(III) precipitates as Cr(OH)₃ or becomes incorporated into iron-bearing phases
 such as goethite (α FeOOH), hematite (Fe₂O₃), poorly crystalline Fe(oxyhydr)oxides, and
 chromite (FeCr₂O₄). It may also substitute into the lattice of minerals like gibbsite (Al(OH)₃)
 when Fe³⁺ and Cr³⁺ replace Al³⁺ in octahedral sites, reducing mobility.

 Another common reductant, stannous sulfate (SnSO₄), acts in a similar manner to ferrous sulfate.
 The oxidation/reduction reaction is:

                CrO42- +3Sn2+ +4H2O → Cr(OH)3 (s) + 3Sn4+ +5OH-

 Sn(IV) products are stable and non-problematic, while Cr(III) is immobilized in the grout matrix.

 Both additives (ferrous and stannous sulfate) act synergistically with cementitious hydration
 products, enhancing the redox buffering capacity of grout and ensuring long-term chrome
 retention.

 Current research indicates that, beyond ferrous sulfate and stannous sulfate, several alternative
 reductants enhance Cr(VI) retention in grout waste forms (PNNL-32458, SRNL-STI-2020-
 00228). These include slag, fly ash, metakaolin, silica fume, and other supplementary
 cementitious materials, as well as engineered additives like sulfide-bearing compounds and
 organic reductants. Supplementary cementitious materials create a reducing environment within
 grout by generating Fe(II), sulfide, and other electron donors during hydration. Studies show that
 supplementary cementitious materials can convert Cr(VI) to Cr(III) and immobilize it within
 calcium silicate hydrate (C-S-H) phases and can also improve physical durability, reducing
 porosity and leachability. Sulfide-bearing additives like calcium sulfide, sodium sulfide, or
 reduced sulfur phases provide strong reductive capacity, particularly effective in alkaline grout
 environments. These reductants can reduce Cr(VI) rapidly, forming stable Cr(III)-sulfide or
 hydroxide precipitates. SRNL research highlights sulfide phases as promising for long-term
 redox buffering. Both PNNL and SRNL used TCLP and ANSI/ANS 16.1 leach tests to quantify
 Cr(VI) release. Finally, Zero-valent iron (ZVI) and engineered iron phases provide sustained
 Fe(II) release as they corrode, maintaining reducing conditions. This can be effective in both
 direct reduction of Cr(VI) and stabilization of Cr(III) precipitates. PNNL research has noted ZVI
 is a potential candidate for enhancing grout formulations (PNNL-32458).

 In summary, chemical reduction (CHRED) through incorporating FeSO₄, SnSO₄, or the other
 additives mention above into grout formulations provide a robust means to reduce the mobility of
 Cr(VI) and other LDR regulated metals. These additives can be introduced directly to PTW or
 during grout mixing without major changes to processing, making them practical for large-scale
 waste immobilization.

 5.2 GROUT FABRICATION

 Solidification/stabilization formulations tested included Cast Stone (CS), Hanford Grout Mix 5
 (HGM-5), Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC), and a Geopolymer (GP) blend. These formulations
 span a wide range of grout types and are described below.

 1) Cast Stone (CS): CS is composed of 8% OPC, 45% fly ash (FA), and 47% blast furnace
 slag (BFS). Cast Stone formulation received its moniker as part of an effort to identify
 formulations capable of providing supplemental immobilization capacity for LAW
 (Asmussen et. al., 2026). The CS formulation is similar to the salt stone formulation used
 at the Savannah River Site for the immobilization of liquid salt wastes (Moore et al.
 2020). The formulation has high flowability and creates a reducing environment through
 the presence of the BFS (Nichols et al. 2017) and is the most studied formulation at
 Hanford for PTW.
 2) Hanford Grout Mix 5 (HGM-5): HGM-5 is a formulation composed of 25% OPC and
 75% FA. The HGM-5 formulation was used in Hanford burial grounds for encapsulation
 and creates a less reducing environment due to the absence of slag.
 3) Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC): The OPC formulation is composed of 100% OPC.
 This formulation was chosen because OPC has the lowest reduction capacity of all tested
 dry reagents but is a component of most formulations considered for waste
 immobilization.
 4) Geopolymer (GP): The GP is a neutral salt-activated slag formulation based on
 formulation CSMA3S1 tested in Gong et al. (2022) and Fujii Yamagata et al. (2024). The
 formulation is comprised of 41% BFS, 2.6% silica fume, 1.0% sodium carbonate,
 2.4% sodium sulfate, 8.5% Agsco Silica, 2.0% MgO, 42% sand, and 0.2% Hydrapel 2.5.

 Since the current focus for any liquid PTW destined for offsite disposal as a solidified waste
 form is to send the liquid PTW for commercial treatment with subsequent disposal in an out-of-
 state licensed mixed low-level waste landfill, three commercial formulations were tested.
 However, one was not deemed sufficient based on the results of testing and is not planned to be
 used. For this reason, the data from this formulation was not used for this report. Since these
 formulations are based on proprietary information provided by commercial treatment vendors,
 their specific composition is not disclosed.

 Each grout was prepared and tested with and without a chemical reductant. This condition is
 noted in the samples names as NR for no reductant and CR for with chemical reductant. The
 chemical reduction step involved adding 5% by mass iron (II) sulfate heptahydrate
 (FeSO4∙7H2O) to the simulant before the dry ingredients were added. The purpose of this step is
 to reduce the LDR inorganics to their less soluble reduced state so they are more likely to be
 retained in the solid phase when contacted with the TCLP leaching solution (see Section 3.2 for
 background information).

 5.3 TCLP TESTING

 The Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (EPA Method 1311) was performed on all
 formulations described in Section 5.2. The TCLP methodology was modified from the original
 EPA Method 1311 to accommodate smaller samples and to generate less laboratory waste. These
 modifications are also consistent with modifications employed with radioactive materials to
 lower exposure and dose:

                                                            21                                   A-6007-231 (REV 6)

RPP-RPT-66455 Rev.00                                4/20/2026 - 10:31 AM                                                    29 of 100

 1) Modification 1: The amount of sample was reduced from 100 g to 15 g. The amount
 of the extraction fluid also decreased with the same ratio (2000 mL to 300 mL).

 2) Modification 2: The particles that were tested were sieved within a range of
 >0.25 mm to <5 mm in one dimension. A reduction in particle size variability is used
 to try to reduce the difference between test response in duplicate samples in order to
 assess LDR inorganic release from each type of CWF, including analytical error.

 3) Modification 3: Instead of 0.7-μm borosilicate glass fiber filters specified in the
 original procedure, 0.45-μm polytetrafluorethylene (PTFE) filters housed in
 polypropylene (PP) casing were used.

 A subsection of the formulations was tested with and without the TCLP method modifications
 (except for Modification 3 where 0.45-μm filters were used for all tests). These formulations
 include CS-NR-Blank, HGM-5-Blank, CS-NR-200W, and HGM-5-NR-200W. The results of
 these modifications are plotted in Figure 5-1 and the results are presented in Appendix A. From
 the plot and the table, it appears that there is general agreement between test response when the
 modifications are employed. The slope (1.01), intercept (-1.03), and R2 (0.95) for the fit are
 provided on the plot. The HGM-5-NR-200W data point was excluded from the fit even though
 those values are within 2% of one another.

 6.2 RETENTION FACTOR LOWER CONFIDENCE LIMITS

 To account for sampling and analytical errors, a lower 95% confidence limit (LCL) retention
 factor for individual species was computed. This was accomplished by first calculating the
 retention factors for each species from the “200W” and “3×” experiments. The “Min” and “Mid”
 samples were excluded from the analysis because in many cases the blank samples contained
 LDR species concentrations at levels near these values and a meaningful retention factor value
 could not be computed. In the limited cases where a “200W” and “3×” sample yielded a negative
 retention factor (i.e., case where the concentration measured in the leachate exceed the

                                                                       25                                            A-6007-231 (REV 6)

RPP-RPT-66455 Rev.00                                   4/20/2026 - 10:31 AM                                                    33 of 100

 theoretical solids concentration from Equation 1), the retention factor was excluded from the
 LCL analysis. This was particularly the case for barium 19, for which a retention factor value was
 not determined, however, in all tests the barium TCLP leachate concentration was well below
 NWW standards (see Appendix A).

 Retention factor values for the tested grout formulations (both with and without a chemical
 reduction step) were combined to compute a single 95% lower confidence limit (LCL) for all
 tested formulations for each LDR constituent, excluding barium (Table 6-4). To compute the
 95% LCL values, the retention factor values for each formulation for the “200W” and “3×” spike
 results were computed in accordance with Equation 1 (see Appendix B). Undetected TCLP
 results were input at their reported detection limit. In doing so, this approach contributes to a low
 bias for the computed retention factors, thus adding additional conservatism. Negative retention
 factors were not used in the distribution analysis or for the corresponding LCL. No outlier
 analysis was performed, and all data points were assessed. The software JMP  13 was used to
 perform a distribution analysis of the Appendix B retention factor values by selecting menu
 options to perform analysis with all available distribution tests within JMP. The software defaults
 to the most appropriate distribution (as determined by the minimum Corrected Akaike
 Information Criterion) and computes confidence intervals specified by the user. The complete
 analysis is included in Appendix C, and the 95% LCL values for retention factors are
 summarized in Table 6-3 along with the distribution determined by JMP.

 The intent of computing a 95% LCL retention factor is to derive a single bounding value that
 encompasses a wide variety of waste formulations. A similar principle is applied when
 calculating an exposure point concentration in RCRA/CERCLA risk assessments. The LCL
 values should be considered as semi-quantitative since the data included possible outliers, an
 unquantified sampling and analytical error, and a data size limited to 33 samples (with chemical
 reduction) and 30 samples (without chemical reduction). Further refinement of the bounding
 LCL value is anticipated as additional test data on TCLP responses from other
 solidification/immobilization formulations are measured.

 6.3 IMPACT OF CHEMICAL REDUCTION

 The formulation-specific retention factors for the grout formulations with chemical reduction
 were compared to their non-reduced counterparts (refer to Tables 6-1 and 6-2). In general, the
 percent difference in retention between formulations with reduction and formulations without
 was within experimental error, estimated as the covariance of the triplicate samples. 20 The
 notable exception was for chromium in HGM-5 and PLC, where retention of chrome was
 enhanced by 65% and 9%, respectively. This conforms to what is known regarding the effect of
 chemical reduction on chrome mobility by lowering its oxidation state (see Section 3.2.2). For
 wastes that exhibit chromium concentrations in excess of 20 times the NWW standard TCLP-
 based limit of 0.6 mg/L, chemical reduction, or CHRED, is recommended prior to solidification
 (note: Table 2-1 shows that the West Area flowsheet minimum concentration for chromium is
 47.9 mg/L which is above 20× the NWW standard).

 7.0 CONCLUSION

 This report summarizes test results used to establish retention factors for twelve inorganic LDR
 constituents solidified/immobilized in several cementitious waste formulations. The retention
 factor is a measure of the mass of the species within the waste form that is retained during the
 EPA Method 1311 leach test. The twelve LDR constituents tested are associated with Hanford
 liquid pretreated waste (PTW).

 For these tests a 5.6 M Na simulant of PTW was prepared, spiked with regulated inorganics
 constituents at multiple concentrations (up to 3× their predicted maximum concentration in
 Hanford West Area waste), and immobilized in grout formulations. The simulated liquid waste
 was then subjected to solidification in both traditional Hanford formulations, and proprietary
 formulations provided by commercial treatment companies under nondisclosure agreements.
 Each formulation was tested without and with a chemical reduction step and the data was used to
 calculate a retention factor for each of the twelve LDR inorganic species that are regulated by an
 EPA Method 1311 leaching-based non-wastewater standard.

 Retention factors for eight of the twelve species for most formulations exceeded 0.95 (i.e., >95%
 of constituent retained), both with and without chemical reduction. Chemical reduction
 consistently improved chrome retention but had less effect on the other species. Silver, arsenic,
 barium, and selenium were the four elements with retention factors that were more consistently
 below 95%. Retention factor data for all formulations was also used for each LDR species to
 compute a single 95% lower confidence limit retention factor to provide a formulation-free lower
 bound on retention.

 Comparing the predicted maximum concentration for the twelve LDR constituents present in
 retrieved Hanford tank waste with their respective non-wastewater standards indicates that a non-
 zero retention factor is only required for Cd, Cr, Hg, and Pb. The other eight species are not
 predicted to be present at concentrations sufficient to generate a non-compliant
 solidified/immobilized waste. For Cd and Hg all the formulations tested with chemical reduction
 demonstrated sufficient retention to produce compliant solidified waste from PTW at the
 predicted West Area maximum concentrations. For Pb, all formulations tested produced a
 compliant waste form. Furthermore, the 95% lower confidence limit retention factors for these
 three species also were sufficient to predict a compliant final waste form. For Cr, the geopolymer
 and two commercial formulations demonstrated retention factors capable of producing a
 compliant waste form at concentrations above the predicted flowsheet maximum value (6,734
 mg/L). All the formulations except the HGM had retention factors sufficient to produce
 compliant waste at the flowsheet average concentration (657 mg/L). The 95% lower confidence
 limit retention factor for Cr was only sufficient to predict that a compliant final waste would be
 produced up to 611 mg/L. Hence, it is recommended that formulation-specific retention factor
 values, at least for Cr, be established and used to demonstrate waste compliance rather than the
 LCL value determined in this report.
