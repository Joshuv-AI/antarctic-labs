On the magnetic counterpart of the Uehling correction
T. Azevedo, F.A. Barone, C. Farina, R. de Melo e Souza, and G. Zarpelon

Abstract
In this work, we investigate the magnetic properties of the quantum vacuum in the context of QED. We calculate the quantum relativistic correction of virtual particle-anti-particle pair creation to the field of a classical point-like idealized magnet. Using such correction, we find the induced currents stemming from the effect of the polarization of the vacuum, which behaves like a paramagnetic medium. We also calculate the correction to the electric dipole potential and show that the well-known symmetry between the classical fields of point electric and magnetic dipoles is broken at the quantum level. Lastly, we apply the corrections to calculate the contributions of the hyperfine structure in a simple hydrogen-like atom.

Introduction and Overview

The classical theory of electromagnetism is a remarkable feat of the human intellect, its initial inception dating from the time of the ancient civilizations and culminating in the mathematically robust theory of Maxwell in the nineteenth century. Perhaps an even greater achievement was the formulation of the theory of quantum electrodynamics (QED), unifying special relativity and quantum mechanics in a quantum description of light-matter interactions. This framework was further developed into the more general quantum field theories which also play a prominent role in the Standard Model of particle physics. Some of the most striking features of a quantum field theory are the non-trivial properties of the quantum vacuum, such as the ubiquitous creation and annihilation of virtual particle-anti-particle pairs. This also implies that the QED vacuum behaves as a nonlinear material which can be polarized, leading to nonlinear phenomena which can be described by effective Lagrangians

The vacuum polarization effects of QED have particular consequences for scattering, as in Delbruck scattering

In this paper, we present the generalization of Uehling’s correction for magnetism, where the absence of monopoles indicates that the leading correction only appears in the next multipole, i.e. the dipole field. From this, we also discuss another deviation of the quantum theory from the classical one. Namely, it is a well-known fact that the electromagnetic fields of static electric and magnetic dipoles in Maxwell’s theory are symmetric upon the exchange of the respective dipole moments—see appendix

Furthermore, our results suggest that the QED vacuum can be thought of as a medium with anisotropic response with a paramagnetic behavior, shedding new light into the character of the vacuum. As an application, we evaluate the Uehling magnetic correction to the hyperfine structure.

2
The Quantum-Corrected Electric Dipole Field

In this section, we present the calculation for the leading-order correction of the electric dipole field due to vacuum polarization. This section serves to set notation and also to establish a result to be compared further on.

2.1
Preliminaries and Setup

We are concerned with the standard theory of Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), whose Lagrangian density is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{QED}=-\frac{1}{4}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+\bar{\psi}(i\gamma^{\mu}D_{\mu}-m_{f})\psi\,,

(1)

with
F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}
,
A_{\mu}
being the gauge (photon) field,
\psi
being the fermion field of mass
m_{f}
(with
\bar{\psi}:=\gamma^{0}\psi^{\dagger}
), and the covariant derivative
D_{\mu}:=\partial_{\mu}-ieA_{\mu}
. In this context, we take the photon propagator in momentum space to be

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mu\nu}(p)=\frac{-i}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}\left(\eta_{\mu\nu}-\left(1-\xi\right)\frac{p_{\mu}p_{\nu}}{p^{2}}\right),

(2)

where
\epsilon
is the constant associated with the Feynman prescription for propagators and
\xi
is the gauge parameter. For the fermions of mass
m_{f}
running inside the loop correction, we consider the propagators
G_{F}
to be

\displaystyle G_{F}(p)=\frac{-i(\gamma^{\mu}p_{\mu}+m_{f})}{p^{2}-m^{2}+i\epsilon}\,.

(3)

The vacuum polarization corrections to the photon propagator arise naturally in the perturbative expansion of QED, leading to the renormalization of the electric charge
e
. These corrections are encoded in the polarization tensor
\Pi^{\mu\nu}
, which is a transverse tensor that can be conveniently written as
\Pi^{\mu\nu}=(\eta^{\mu\nu}p^{2}-p^{\mu}p^{\nu})\Pi(p^{2})
. The well-known renormalization process renders this (formally) infinite quantity finite, and yields the standard perturbative expansion in the fine structure constant
\alpha=e^{2}/(4\pi)
.
To translate the above statement into mathematical language, we note that the photon propagator can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{-i}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}P_{\mu\nu}+\frac{-i}{(p^{2}+i\epsilon)^{2}}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}\xi\,,

where
P_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{\mu\nu}-p_{\mu}p_{\nu}/p^{2}
corresponds to a projector, satisfying
P_{\mu\nu}P^{\nu\alpha}=P_{\mu}^{\ \ \alpha}
. Then, noting that
i\Pi^{\mu\nu}=ip^{2}\Pi(p^{2})P^{\mu\nu}
, we can write the
n
-th iteration of the bubble diagram as

\displaystyle\Delta^{(0)}_{\mu\alpha_{1}}i\Pi^{\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}}\Delta^{(0)}_{\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}}i\Pi^{\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}}\Delta^{(0)}_{\beta_{2}\alpha_{3}}i\Pi^{\alpha_{3}\beta_{3}}\Delta^{(0)}_{\beta_{3}\alpha_{4}}\dots i\Pi^{\alpha_{n}\beta_{n}}\Delta^{(0)}_{\beta_{n}\nu}=\frac{1}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}\left(-i\Pi(p^{2})\right)^{n}P_{\mu\nu}\,.

Then, the full 1PI diagrams are given by the series

\displaystyle\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta^{(0)}_{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-i\Pi\right)^{n}P_{\mu\nu}=\frac{-i}{p^{2}+i\epsilon}P_{\mu\nu}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{n-1}\Pi^{n}+\frac{-i}{(p^{2}+i\epsilon)^{2}}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}\xi\,.

Thus, the multiple corrections can be incorporated by using the form above. The last piece is explicitly dependent on the gauge parameter
\xi
and vanishes in Lorenz/Landau gauge with
\xi=0
.

The starting point of the calculation follows the prescription to extract the potential from a classic source via the propagator of a given gauge field
A_{\mu}
. Namely,

\displaystyle iA^{(n)}_{\mu}(x)=\int\differential[4]{y}\Delta^{(n)}_{\mu\nu}(x-y)j^{\nu}(y)\,,

(6)

where the
(n)
superscript indicates the loop order one is considering. For the leading contribution beyond tree level (
n=1
, often called the
Uehling correction

86
;
36
;
71
),
\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}
can be easily found. For a static point charge, this leads to a correction to Coulomb’s potential,

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}(r)=\frac{q}{4\pi r}\left[1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)\right]\!,\qquad\mathcal{I}_{n}(z):=\int_{1}^{\infty}\!\!\differential{\xi}U(\xi)\xi^{-n}e^{-2\xi z}\,,

(7)

where we define

\displaystyle U(\xi):=\left(1+\frac{1}{2\xi^{2}}\right)\frac{\sqrt{\xi^{2}-1}}{\xi^{2}}\,.

(8)

It is convenient to define an effective charge, to first order in
\alpha
, as

q^{(1)}(r):=q\left[1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)\right]\ ,

(9)

so that the potential (
7
) takes the Coulomb-like form

\phi^{(1)}(r)=\frac{q^{(1)}(r)}{4\pi r}\ .

(10)

In the case
m_{f}=0
, the integral in (
9
) is divergent and independent of
r
. This divergence can be absorbed into a redefinition of the charge
q
, yielding the standard Coulomb potential.

The integral
\mathcal{I}_{0}
which appears in (
7
) has been extensively studied in its multiple possible representations in terms of special functions. The application of derivatives to
\mathcal{I}_{n}(z)
follows a recurrence relation of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{n}(z)=-2\mathcal{I}_{n-1}(z)\,,

(11)

which will be useful later.

2.2
Electric Dipole Correction

As a straightforward application, one could consider what happens for a classical static electric dipole represented by the current
j^{\mu}(x)=(-\mathbf{d}\cdot\gradient\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{0})

11
. From Eq. (
6
) we see that the potential is simply given by
\phi^{(1)}_{ed}=-\mathbf{d}\cdot\gradient\phi^{(1)}(r)
, where
\phi^{(1)}
is the Uehling potential (
7
). Explicitly,

\displaystyle\phi_{ed}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\cdot\mathbf{r}}{4\pi r^{3}}\,,

(12)

where we have defined the renormalized dipole moment function
\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r):=\mathbf{d}\left[1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\int_{1}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}U(\xi)(1+2m_{f}r\xi)e^{-2m_{f}r\xi}\right]\,.

(13)

This can also be understood as the limiting case of the potential generated by two opposite point charges infinitesimally close to each other, corrected by vacuum polarization. Since
m_{f}\neq 0
the renormalization factor for the electric dipole differs from that of the monopole case, given in Eq. (
7
). This means that, although the superposition principle remains valid, there are non-trivial modifications produced by the vacuum polarization. In order to unveil them, let us evaluate the electric field produced by the electric dipole, given by
\mathbf{E}_{ed}^{(1)}=-\gradient\phi_{ed}^{(1)}
. The correction to the field is given by

\displaystyle\delta\mathbf{E}_{ed}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}r^{3}}\left\{\left[3\mathbf{\hat{r}}(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})-\mathbf{d}\right]\big[\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)+2m_{f}r\mathcal{I}_{-1}(m_{f}r)\big]+4m_{f}^{2}r^{2}\mathbf{\hat{r}}(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)\right\},

(14)

where we have used the recurrence relation (
11
) with
z=m_{f}r
. The complete field becomes

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{ed}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi r^{3}}\Big(3\mathbf{\hat{r}}(\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})-\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\Big)

(15)

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}r^{3}}\mathbf{\hat{r}}(\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{d})\int_{1}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}U(\xi)4m_{f}^{2}\xi^{2}r^{2}e^{-2m_{f}r\xi}\,.

The first term was expected and corresponds to the usual dipole electric field produced by the renormalized electric dipole. It is the only term surviving in the limit
\alpha\to 0
(in which case
\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\to\mathbf{d}
). The second term constitutes a non-trivial contribution arising due to the vacuum polarization, with a tensorial structure differing from the dipole electric field in classical electrodynamics. We stress that when
m_{f}=0
this term vanishes. In this case the monopole renormalization is independent of
\mathbf{r}
(which is a natural consequence of the scaling symmetry present in this case) and all electric multipoles can be readily obtained from their classical expressions just by renormalizing the corresponding multipole moments by the same (divergent) factor as the monopole.

There is another interesting consequence of breaking the scale symmetry. For a given radial direction, the electric field direction changes as a function of the distance, that is, for
m_{f}\neq 0
we have

\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\mathbf{E}_{ed}^{(1)}\cdot\bm{\hat{r}}}{|\mathbf{E}_{ed}^{(1)}|}\neq 0\,.

(16)

In classical electromagnetism, however, dimensional analysis by itself ensures that this derivative vanishes for all point multipoles.

We stress that Eq. (
15
) is only valid for
\mathbf{r}\neq\mathbf{0}
. To include the origin, we can apply a regularization procedure
65
to the potential in Eq. (
12
) as follows. Taking
\phi^{(1)}_{ed}\to\phi^{(1)}_{ed(\epsilon)}
, we write

\displaystyle\phi^{(1)}_{ed(\epsilon)}=-\frac{1}{4\pi}\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\cdot\gradient\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}+\epsilon^{2}}}\right)\,.

(17)

Then, after calculating the gradient of the above, the limit
\epsilon\to 0
is to be taken with the understanding that the term proportional to
\epsilon^{2}/(r^{2}+\epsilon^{2})^{5/2}
yields a Dirac delta function. This leads to

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}^{(1)}_{ed}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi r^{3}}\Big(3\mathbf{\hat{r}}(\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})-\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(r)\Big)-\frac{1}{3}\mathbf{d}^{(1)}(0)\delta(\mathbf{r})+\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}r^{3}}4m_{f}^{2}\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{d})\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)\,.

Note that the term proportional to the delta function is the same as the one in classical electrodynamics but with
\mathbf{d}
replaced by
\mathbf{d}^{(1)}
, as expected.

3
The Magnetic Case

Having discussed the leading
\alpha
correction to the electrostatic fields of a point charge and a point electric dipole, our goal in this section is to investigate the magnetic counterpart of Uehling’s results. Given that there is no magnetic monopole in Maxwell’s theory, we will be interested in the leading quantum correction to the magnetic field of a point magnetic dipole, i.e. the lowest-order multipole available.

Furthermore, as is well-known, the magnetic dipole field has a very similar form to the electric dipole field in Maxwell’s theory—see Appendix
C
for a discussion on why this is the case. Hence, a natural question arising in this context is whether or not this similarity remains true for the first perturbative corrections which comes from vacuum polarization. Our calculations will allow us to settle this question.

For a static magnetic dipole at position
\mathbf{x}_{s}
, the current density is given by
j^{\mu}=(0,\mathbf{J})
, where
\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x})=-\mathbf{m}\times\gradient\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})
. By inserting this into the potential, one has

\displaystyle iA^{(1)}_{md,\mu}(x)=\int\differential[4]{y}\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu j}(x-y)[-\mathbf{m}\times\gradient\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{s})]^{j}\,.

Using the momentum representation of the propagator, one can exchange the order of integrations to obtain

\displaystyle iA^{(1)}_{md,\mu}(x)=\int\frac{\differential[4]{p}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu j}(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}\int\differential[]y[-\mathbf{m}\times\gradient\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{s})]^{j}e^{ip\cdot y}\,.

However, since
\gradient\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{s})=-\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{s})
, we can take the cross product out of the integrals and write

\displaystyle iA^{(1)}_{md,\mu}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})^{j}\int\frac{\differential[4]{p}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu j}(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}\int\differential[]y\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{s})e^{ip\cdot y}

(19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})^{j}\int\frac{\differential[4]{p}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu j}(p)e^{-ip\cdot x}\delta(p^{0})e^{-i\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{y}}\,.

When the delta is integrated, the
p^{0}
components are all set to zero. This implies that
\Delta^{(1)}_{0j}=0
, and we immediately see that
A^{(1)}_{md,0}=0
, as expected. Thus, we have

\displaystyle iA^{(1)}_{md,k}(x)=(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})^{j}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Delta^{(1)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,.

(20)

Now, using the explicit form of the propagator, we can write

\displaystyle\Delta^{(1)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta^{(0)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})+\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})\,,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-i}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\left[\eta_{jk}-(1-\xi)\frac{p_{j}p_{k}}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\right]+\frac{-i}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})\left[\eta_{jk}-\frac{p_{j}p_{k}}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\right]

and it is clear that the components with
\mu=0
of
\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu k}
vanish, which is why we have swapped
\Delta^{(1)}_{\mu j}\to\Delta^{(1)}_{jk}
.

To organize the calculations, we can separate the two contributions as:

\displaystyle A^{(1)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})=A^{(0)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})+\delta A^{(1)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})\,,

(22)

with

\displaystyle iA^{(0)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})^{k}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\Delta^{(0)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,,

(23)

\displaystyle i\delta A^{(1)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{\mathbf{x}_{s}})^{k}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\delta\Delta^{(1)}_{jk}(0,\mathbf{p})e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,.

(24)

Note that this specific form of separating the contributions leaves the gauge parameter completely inside the
tree level
contribution, which is explicitly given by

\displaystyle A^{(0)}_{md,j}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient)_{k}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{1}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\left[\delta_{jk}-(1-\xi)\frac{p_{j}p_{k}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\right]e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,.

(25)

A few comments are in order. First, we have exchanged the derivative with respect to
\mathbf{x}_{s}
by derivatives with respect to
\mathbf{x}
, which is justified because the dependence on space parameters is of the form
\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s}
. Furthermore, since now there are no temporal components in the variables, we will abdicate the use of Lorentz-covariant notation and thus circumvent the appearance of extra minus signs which would plague us. By this we mean that repeated indices (whether up or down) are summed and
p^{k}=p_{k}
, formally.

We evaluate now the zeroth-order dipole field. The evaluation is straightforward. Let us focus on the integral. The first part is immediate,

\displaystyle\delta_{jk}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\overset{\epsilon\to 0}{=}-\frac{\delta_{jk}}{4\pi r}\,,

(26)

where
\mathbf{r}:=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s}
. The second piece can be written as

\displaystyle(1-\xi)\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}(-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon)}=\frac{(1-\xi)}{2\pi^{2}}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\left[\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p\sin(p r)}{p^{2}(-p^{2}+i\epsilon)}\differential{p}\right].

(27)

The integral is straightforward by using complex integration. After taking
\epsilon\to 0
, one finds

\displaystyle\frac{(1-\xi)}{8\pi}\partial_{j}\partial_{k}r=\frac{(1-\xi)}{8\pi r}\left(\delta_{jk}-\frac{r_{j}r_{k}}{r^{2}}\right).

(28)

Thus, the complete tree-level piece is

\displaystyle A^{(0)}_{md,j}=-(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient)_{k}\left\{\frac{\delta_{jk}}{4\pi r}-\frac{(1-\xi)}{8\pi r}\left(\delta_{jk}-\frac{r_{j}r_{k}}{r^{2}}\right)\right\}.

(29)

Operating the derivatives, one arrives at

\displaystyle A^{(0)}_{md,j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{r})_{j}}{4\pi r^{3}}\,.

(30)

We see that the gauge-dependent piece vanishes as expected for static sources. The field is then simply

\displaystyle B^{(0)i}_{md}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\varepsilon^{ikj}\varepsilon_{jln}\partial_{k}\left(\frac{m^{l}r^{n}}{r^{3}}\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi r^{3}}\left(\frac{3r^{i}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{m})}{r^{2}}-m^{i}\right),

(31)

which is the well-known classical result for an idealized point-like magnet.

The loop contribution is given by

\displaystyle i\delta A^{(1)}_{md,i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient_{x_{s}})_{j}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{p_{i}p_{j}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\right)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient)_{j}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}+i\epsilon}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{p_{i}p_{j}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\right)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}\,.

Thus, because of the projector inside the integral, we do not recover
(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient)^{i}
applied to the point particle Uehling correction. To pursue the above integration, we can split the integral in two contributions and deal with each piece of the projector separately. The first one, which is proportional to
\delta_{ij}
, yields the standard Uehling expression. To integrate the second term, we take
\epsilon\to 0
and write

\displaystyle\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})}{-\mathbf{p}^{4}}p_{i}p_{j}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{x}}=-\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})}{-\mathbf{p}^{4}}e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{x}}\,.

(33)

Using the form of the polarization tensor, it is straightforward to perform the above integration (see Appendix
A
). The full integral is then given by

\displaystyle\int\frac{\differential[3]{\vb{p}}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\frac{\Pi_{R}(-\mathbf{p}^{2})}{-\mathbf{p}^{2}}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{p_{i}p_{j}}{\mathbf{p}^{2}}\right)e^{i\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{s})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}r}\left(\delta_{ij}-\frac{r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{2}}\right)\int_{1}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}U(\xi)e^{-2m_{f}\xi r}

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{24m_{f}^{2}\pi^{2}r^{3}}\left(\frac{3r_{i}r_{j}}{r^{2}}-\delta_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{2}{5}-\int_{1}^{\infty}\differential{\xi}U(\xi)\left(\frac{1+2m_{f}\xi r}{\xi^{2}}\right)e^{-2m_{f}\xi r}\right].

At last, the correction
\delta A^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
is be given by applying
(\mathbf{m}\times\gradient)_{j}
to the expression above. Using the recurrence relations for the integrals, one can write the correction as

\displaystyle\delta A^{(1)}_{md,j}

\displaystyle=

3.1
Application to Hyperfine Structure

The hyperfine structure is a very important set of corrections to atomic spectra that take into consideration the interaction of electrons with the nucleus. In the literature, the first correct assessment of this phenomenon from a theoretical point of view was given by Fermi
26
;
25
and was subsequently analyzed by various authors
79
;
80
;
7
;
13
;
66
. The hyperfine correction has been particularly important in the analysis of radiation from intergalactic hydrogen—leading to the famous 21 cm shift
74
;
34
;
39
;
84
—and helium
57
(see also
75
for an account of the effect in hydrogen atomic collisions). In this section we evaluate the first correction due to the vacuum magnetization in the hyperfine structure.

Pragmatically, we wish to compute the corrections arising from the potential and magnetic field corrections
\delta\mathbf{A}^{(1)},\,\delta\mathbf{B}^{(1)}
written above, to the energy levels of a particle of angular momentum
\mathbf{J}=\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{S}
moving in the field of a nucleus with angular momentum
\mathbf{I}
. This calculation corresponds to using perturbation theory on the non-relativistic Hamiltonian for a spin 1/2 particle bounded to the nucleus, i.e.

\displaystyle H=H_{0}+V_{C}+H_{FS}+H_{Lamb}+H_{hf}\,,

(44)

with the hyperfine term
H_{hf}
given by

\displaystyle H_{hf}=-\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{A}^{(1)}-{{\bf\it\mu}}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{(1)}\,,

(45)

where
{\bf\it\mu}=-\mu_{e}g_{e}\mathbf{S}
is the electron’s magnetic moment,
\mu_{e}=e/2m_{e}
and
g_{e}
is the gyromagnetic factor,
\mathbf{p}
is the electrons momentum,
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}
is the potential generated by the nucleus,
V_{C}
is the central (Coulomb) potential and the other terms correspond to the corrections due to the fine structure, Lamb shift and the hyperfine structure, respectively. Of all of these terms, the hyperfine corrections are the smallest, which motivates the use of perturbation theory to calculate its effects.

Now, the effect of the other terms in the Hamiltonian is to create an explicit dependence of the energy levels of the electron on the electronic quantum numbers
n,j,l
.

By including the nucleus, with its own angular momentum
I
, we consider a Hilbert space composed of the direct product between the electron’s and the nucleus’s spaces, that is,
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{e}\otimes\mathcal{E}_{N}
. A possible basis for these states is simply
\ket{nljm_j}\otimes\ket{I M_I}
, with
-j\leq m_{j}\leq j
,
-I\leq M_{I}\leq I
representing the spin projection numbers. A better basis, which enables the diagonalization of the degenerate subspace, is the one constructed with the sum of the angular momenta of the nucleus and the electron,
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{J}+\mathbf{I}
, represented by states
\ket{nlj I FM_F}
. For the first order perturbation, then, we would have to evaluate

\displaystyle\Delta E=\bra{nljI FM_F}H_{hf}\ket{nljIFM_F}\,.

(46)

In the standard case, we would proceed to write the field
\mathbf{B}
and the potential
\mathbf{A}
generated by the nucleus and plug them in the above formula. In our case, however, we are considering
\mathbf{A}^{(1)}
and
\mathbf{B}^{(1)}
, that is, the leading
\alpha
corrections to these expressions. These are given in eq. (
38
) and eq. (
40
), and in this context can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{N}g_{N}\frac{\mathbf{I}\times\mathbf{r}}{4\pi r^{3}}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{m_{f}}(r)\right),

(47)

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{N}g_{N}\frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{r})-r^{2}\mathbf{I}}{4\pi r^{5}}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)+\mu_{N}g_{N}\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{r})-r^{2}\mathbf{I}}{r^{5}}\Psi_{m_{f}}(r)

(48)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{3}\mu_{N}g_{N}\delta(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{I}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right),

where
\Phi_{mf}=\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)+2m_{f}r\mathcal{I}_{-1}(m_{f}r)
and
\Psi_{mf}=4m_{f}^{2}r^{2}\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)
. By using some of the general properties of angular momentum operators, the above can be evaluated in terms of the expectation values of the radial components. These values depend on the specific wave function one considers for the particle. The details are covered in the appendix
D
. The outcome of the analysis is that the full correction would take the form

\displaystyle\Delta E=\Delta E^{(\alpha^{0})}+\Delta E^{(\alpha^{1})}

(49)

where the corrections to the ground state, for
l=0
, are

\displaystyle\Delta E_{l=0}=\frac{1}{3}g_{e}g_{N}\mu_{e}\mu_{N}\big(F(F+1)-I(I+1)-3/4\big)\expectationvalue{\delta(\vb{r})\left( 1 + \frac{2\alpha}{3\pi} \Phi_{mf}(r)\right)}\,,

(50)

and the corrections to the
l\neq 0
states, which we separate in the
\alpha^{0}
and
\alpha^{1}
orders, are given by

\displaystyle\Delta E_{l\neq 0}^{(\alpha^{0})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{g_{e}g_{N}\mu_{e}\mu_{N}}{4\pi}\frac{F(F+1)-j(j+1)-I(I+1)}{2j(j+1)}l(l+1)\expectationvalue{\frac{1}{r^3}}\,,

(51)

\displaystyle\Delta E_{l\neq 0}^{(\alpha^{1})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha g_{e}g_{N}\mu_{e}\mu_{N}}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{F(F+1)-j(j+1)-I(I+1)}{2j(j+1)}

\displaystyle\times\left(l(l+1)\expectationvalue{\frac{\Phi_{mf}(r)}{r^3}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(l(l+1)-j(j+1)-\frac{1}{4}\right)\expectationvalue{\frac{\Psi_{mf}(r)}{r}}\right)\,.

As mentioned above, the expectation values are dependent on the specific wave function one considers. Furthermore, the above calculation considers a single fermion in the loop, while the full contribution would be a sum of the above
\Delta E^{(\alpha^{1})}
correction over the masses of the fermions one is considering in the theory.

4
Vacuum Magnetization

In this section we shall investigate the magnetic character of the vacuum. This can be done by employing Maxwell’s equations for the fields and identifying the sources which produce them. Due to the polarization of the vacuum, the electric dipole induces a polarization field while the magnetic dipole induces a magnetization field. The purpose of the section is to calculate these fields.
If one computes the divergence of
\mathbf{B}_{dm}^{(1)}
, one finds
\gradient\cdot\mathbf{B}_{dm}^{(1)}=0
, as expected. On the other hand, the divergence of
\delta\mathbf{E}^{(1)}
yields

\displaystyle\gradient\cdot\delta\mathbf{E}_{de}^{(1)}(\mathbf{r})=-\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}}{r^{4}}\left[(2m_{f}r)^{2}\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)+(2m_{f}r)^{3}\mathcal{I}_{-3}(m_{f}r)\right].

(53)

For
r\neq 0
the right-hand-side can be interpreted as the induced charge density,
\rho_{b}
, by a point dipole placed in the vacuum, up to order
\alpha
. By writing it as
\rho_{b}=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{P}
we can identify the polarization field.
Furthermore, as a consistency check,
\gradient\times\delta\mathbf{E}_{de}^{(1)}=\mathbf{0}
(which is necessary for static sources).

Concerning the magnetic field, calculating the curl of its correction yields

\displaystyle\gradient\times\delta\mathbf{B}_{dm}^{(1)}=\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{m}}{r^{4}}\left[(2m_{f}r)^{2}\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)+(2m_{f}r)^{3}\mathcal{I}_{-3}(m_{f}r)\right].

(54)

Note that, if we interpret the right-hand side of Eq. (
54
) as an
\alpha
-correction to the current density induced by the magnetic point dipole,
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{(1)}
, then a field line representation of that current in the plane perpendicular to the magnetic dipole is shown in Figure
2
.

In order to analyze the magnetic character of the vacuum it is more convenient to deal with the magnetization field, defined by
\bm{\mathcal{J}}^{(1)}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{(1)}
. A natural choice for the magnetization is precisely
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{(1)}=\delta\mathbf{B}^{(1)}
, given in Eq. (
39
). The role of the first term is to partially shield the magnetic field produced by the source
\mathbf{m}^{(1)}(0)
placed at the origin (appearing in the Dirac delta contribution in Eq. (
43
)). The other term is a nontrivial contribution arising to the vacuum polarization.

The magnetization can be written as

\mathcal{M}_{i}^{(1)}=(\Lambda_{ik}^{(a)}+\Lambda_{ik}^{(b)})m_{k}\,,

(55)

where we have defined the second rank tensors

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ik}^{(a)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha\Big(\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)+2m_{f}r\mathcal{I}_{-1}(m_{f}r)\Big)}{6\pi^{2}r^{3}}(3\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{k}-\delta_{ik})\,,

(56)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ik}^{(b)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha m_{f}^{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)}{3\pi^{2}r}(\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{k}-\delta_{ik})\,.

(57)

To first order in
\alpha
, the magnetic susceptibility is defined through the relation

\mathcal{M}_{i}^{(1)}=\chi_{ij}^{(1)}(\mathbf{B}^{(0)}_{md})_{j}\,,

(58)

where
(\mathbf{B}^{(0)}_{md})_{j}
is given in Eq. (
31
). It can be written as

(\mathbf{B}^{(0)}_{md})_{j}=\Xi_{jl}m_{l}\,,

(59)

with

\Xi_{jl}=\frac{(3\hat{r}_{j}\hat{r}_{l}-\delta_{jl})}{4\pi r^{3}}\,.

(60)

The magnetic susceptibility is readily obtained from Eqs.(
58
) and (
59
) and is given by
\chi^{(1)}=\chi^{(1a)}+\chi^{(1b)}
with

\displaystyle\chi^{(1a)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ik}^{(a)}\Xi^{-1}_{kj}\,,

(61)

\displaystyle\chi^{(1b)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ik}^{(b)}\Xi^{-1}_{kj}\,.

(62)

The reader may readily verify that

\Xi^{-1}_{kj}=4\pi r^{3}\left(3\frac{\hat{r}_{k}\hat{r}_{j}}{2}-\delta_{kj}\right)\,,

(63)

leading us to

\displaystyle\chi^{(1a)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}r^{3}}\Big(\mathcal{I}_{0}(m_{f}r)+2m_{f}r\mathcal{I}_{-1}(m_{f}r)\Big)\delta_{ij}=:\chi_{a}\delta_{ij}\,,

(64)

\displaystyle\chi^{(1b)}_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{8\alpha}{3\pi}m_{f}^{2}r^{2}\,\mathcal{I}_{-2}(m_{f}r)(\delta_{ij}-\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j})=:\chi_{b}(\delta_{ij}-\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j})\,.

(65)

The second contribution shows that already at order
\alpha
the vacuum exhibits an anisotropic magnetic response. Indeed, the magnetization is parallel to the magnetic only for
\bm{r}\parallel\mathbf{m}
or
\mathbf{r}\perp\mathbf{m}
. A situation where the susceptibility becomes proportional to the identity is when
m_{f}=0
. This can be understood from Eq. (
40
) since for
m_{f}=0
the only role of the vacuum polarization is to shield the magnetic dipole moment (
\mathbf{m}\to\mathbf{m}^{(1)}(r)
).

To assess the diamagnetic/paramagnetic character of the vacuum at order
\alpha
, it suffices to compute the sign of
\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{(0)}_{md}
. Since
\chi_{a},\chi_{b}\geq 0
, we see that

\bm{\mathcal{M}}^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{md}^{(0)}=\chi_{a}(3(\mathbf{B}_{md}^{(0)}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})^{2}+\mathbf{B}_{md}^{(0)2})+\chi_{b}(\mathbf{B}_{md}^{(0)2}-(\mathbf{\hat{r}}\cdot\mathbf{B}_{md}^{(0)})^{2})\geq 0\,.

(66)

Therefore, the QED vacuum has a paramagnetic character.
In condensed matter physics, the paramagnetic response of a material arises due to the spin of the constituting particles. Here we attribute the vacuum paramagnetic character to the spins of the fermionic particle-anti-particle continuous creation and annihilation. Note that the induced magnetization is qualitatively consistent with the current density represented in Fig.

5
Conclusions

In this work, we have calculated the generalization of the Uehling correction for a point magnetic dipole field. We also computed the Uehling effect for an electric point dipole in order to compare it with the magnetic case. We demonstrated that the symmetry present in classical electromagnetism relating the electric field produced by a point electric dipole and the magnetic field generated by a point magnetic dipole is broken due to the loss of scale invariance entailed by the presence of the fermionic mass.

Furthermore, by considering the induced vacuum currents, we found properties which correspond to the paramagnetic character of the QED vacuum at linear order in
\alpha
. Indeed, already at this order we see that magnetic susceptibility presents a nontrivial tensorial structure. Other results reported in the literature for the paramagnetic behavior of the QED vacuum account for an anisotropic response only at second order and in dynamical situations, as for example in the phenomenon of vacuum birefringence in the presence of an external field
48
;
90
;
14
.
As noted in the text, the interpretation of these quantum properties in terms of the condensed matter point of view might not only furnish a physical picture for the highly non-intuitive behavior of the quantum vacuum, but also provide ways of extracting important information concerning the general properties of a given theory, e.g. in the case of confinement
68
. Similarly, as we exemplified with the simple hydrogen-like toy model, the corrected fields provide a simple way to estimate the leading correction to the hyperfine structure.

We also note that the behavior of the renormalized coupling at the level of the fields seems to indicate that the separation of “dipole” and “monopole” fields within the quantum corrections is not as trivial as one might expect. This is because the dipole corrections do not correspond to a simple renormalization of the dipole moments, i.e. the radial dependence of the renormalized coupling gives rise to more terms which do not fit into the tensor structures associated with the standard multipole operators. It would be interesting to see if this property carries on to higher multipole orders and if one could extract an underlying pattern for the corrections.

Appendix C
Dipole symmetry within Maxwell electrodynamics

As mentioned in the text there is a symmetry between the fields produced by an electric dipole and by a magnetic dipole in Maxwell electrodynamics. More precisely, denoting the fields produced by an electric (magnetic) point dipole with a subscript ed (md) the symmetry is given by the mapping
\mathbf{d}\to\mathbf{m}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\rm ed}\to-\mathbf{E}_{\rm md}
and
\mathbf{E}_{\rm ed}\to\mathbf{H}_{\rm md}=\mathbf{B}_{\rm md}+\mathbf{m}\delta(\bm{r})
, where
\mathbf{H}
denotes the induction magnetic field and
\mathbf{m}\delta(\mathbf{r})
is the magnetization density. From the perspective of the sources involved this is a surprising result, since the structure of the charge density and the current density is very different for the two cases. Here we present an elementary derivation of this symmetry, without the need of actually evaluating the fields. We consider the general dynamic situation, where the point dipoles can be time dependent. The charge and current distributions corresponding to an electric dipole are given by
73

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ed}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{d}(t)\cdot\bm{\nabla}\delta(\mathbf{r})\,,

(78)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\rm ed}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{d}}(t)\delta(\mathbf{r})\,.

(79)

The sources corresponding to a magnetic dipole, on the other hand, are given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm md}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,,

(80)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\rm md}(\mathbf{r},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathbf{m}(t)\times\bm{\nabla}\delta(\mathbf{r})\,.

(81)

In Maxwell electrodynamics, the fields produced can be obtained by solving the inhomogeneous wave equation given by

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi\bm{\nabla}\rho+\frac{4\pi}{c^{2}}\mathbf{\dot{J}}\,,

(82)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4\pi}{c^{2}}\bm{\nabla}\times\mathbf{J}\,.

(83)

Note that
\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{J}_{\rm ed}=-\mathbf{\dot{d}}\times\bm{\nabla}\delta(\mathbf{r})
and
\mathbf{\dot{J}}_{\rm md}=-\mathbf{\dot{m}}\times\bm{\nabla}\delta(\mathbf{r})
. This shows that under the transformation
\bm{d}\to\bm{m}
, the magnetic field
\mathbf{B}_{\rm ed}
produced by the electric dipole is taken into
-\mathbf{E}_{\rm md}
,
thus establishing the first part of the symmetry. The other pair of fields is more subtle, so we write the differential equations explicitly:

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\mathbf{E}_{\rm ed}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4\pi\left((\mathbf{d}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{\nabla}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{d}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\delta(\mathbf{r})\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\mathbf{B}_{\rm md}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-4\pi\left((\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{\nabla}-\bm{\nabla}^{2}\mathbf{m}\right)\delta(\mathbf{r})\,.

(85)

By subtracting
(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2})\mathbf{m}\delta(\mathbf{r})
on both sides of the last equation we can recast it in the form

\left(\bm{\nabla}^{2}-\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\mathbf{H}_{\rm md}=-4\pi\left((\mathbf{m}\cdot\bm{\nabla})\bm{\nabla}-\frac{\partial^{2}\mathbf{m}}{\partial t^{2}}\right)\delta(\mathbf{r})\,.

(86)

Comparing with equation (
84
) we see that under the transformation
\mathbf{d}\to\mathbf{m}
we have the mapping
\mathbf{E}_{\rm ed}\to\mathbf{H}_{\rm md}
. For
\mathbf{r}\neq\mathbf{0}
we have
\mathbf{H}_{\rm md}=\mathbf{B}_{\rm md}
and thus we can interpret our results by establishing that a magnetic loop in the dipole approximation produces the same field as two fictitious monopole magnet moments with opposite magnetic charge. However, these sources are not equivalent when we need the field at the origin
37
;
10
.

Appendix D
Hyperfine Calculation

Here we show the hyperfine calculation in more detail, for the case of a spin 1/2 particle orbiting the nucleus. Since the ground-state calculation has no different features from the
l\neq 0
states, we focus on the excited states. The term proportional to
\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{A}^{(1)}
in the interaction Hamiltonian can be written as

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu_{N}g_{N}}{m_{e}c}\mathbf{p}\cdot(\mathbf{I}\times\mathbf{r})\frac{1+2\alpha\Phi_{m_{f}}/3\pi}{4\pi r^{3}}=-\mu_{e}\mu_{N}g_{e}g_{N}\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{L}\frac{1+2\alpha\Phi_{m_{f}}/3\pi}{4\pi r^{3}}\,,

(87)

where
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p}
. Since this part is a spin-orbit coupling between the nucleus and the electron, we refer to it as the orbital term. The other contribution, namely
-\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{B}^{(1)}
, leads to

\displaystyle\kappa\frac{3(\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{r})-r^{2}\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{I}}{4\pi r^{5}}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)+\kappa\frac{\alpha}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{(\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{r})-r^{2}\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{I}}{r^{3}}\Psi_{m_{f}}(r)\,,

(88)

with
\kappa=\mu_{N}\mu_{e}g_{e}g_{N}
. Let us first consider the first piece of the above together with expression (
87
). The contribution is

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\bra{n l j IFM_F}\mathbf{I}\cdot\left(\frac{\mathbf{L}}{r^{3}}+\frac{3\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{S})-r^{2}\mathbf{S}}{r^{5}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)\ket{nljIFM_F}\,.

(89)

Using the projection theorem
82
, the expectation value over the states with the same
j
can be calculated by taking the inner product with
\mathbf{J}
. We thus have

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{4\pi j(j+1)}\bra{n l j I FM_F}(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{J})\left(\frac{\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{L}}{r^{3}}+\frac{3(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{S})-r^{2}(\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{S})}{r^{5}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)\ket{nlj IFM_F}\,.

Now, note that
\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r}
and that the first term,
\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}^{2}+\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{L}
, cancels part of the last,
\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{L}\cdot\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}^{2}
. We thus find

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{4\pi j(j+1)}\bra{n l j IFM_F}(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{J})\left(\frac{\mathbf{L}^{2}}{r^{3}}+\frac{3(\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r})^{2}-r^{2}\mathbf{S}^{2}}{r^{5}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)\ket{nljIFM_F}\,.

Since the spin matrices satisfy
({\bf\it\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{a})({\bf\it\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}+i{\bf\it\sigma}\cdot(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})
, we see that
(\mathbf{S}\cdot\mathbf{r})^{2}=r^{2}/4
. Furthermore, we can write all of the remaining scalar products in terms of the squared angular momentum vectors. We have, then

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{4\pi j(j+1)}\bra{n l j I FM_F}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{F}^{2}-\mathbf{J}^{2}-\mathbf{I}^{2})\mathbf{L}^{2}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)\ket{nlj I FM_F}\,.

(92)

Taking the expected values in the spin variables, we find

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{4\pi}\frac{F(F+1)-j(j+1)-I(I+1)}{2j(j+1)}l(l+1)\bra{nlj I FM_F}\frac{1}{r^{3}}\left(1+\frac{2\alpha}{3\pi}\Phi_{mf}(r)\right)\ket{nlj I FM_F}\,.

(93)

The discussion is very similar for the other term in (
88

\displaystyle\bra{nljIFM_F}\mathbf{I}\cdot[\mathbf{r}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{S})-r^{2}\mathbf{S}]\ket{nljIFM_F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\bra{nljIFM_F}(\mathbf{I}\cdot\mathbf{J})((\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{r})(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{S})-r^{2}\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{S})\ket{nljIFM_F}}{j(j+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2j(j+1)}\bra{nljIFM_F}(\mathbf{F}^{2}-\mathbf{I}^{2}-\mathbf{J}^{2})((\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{S})^{2}

\displaystyle-r^{2}\mathbf{S}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}r^{2}(\mathbf{J}^{2}-\mathbf{L}^{2}-\mathbf{S}^{2})\ket{nljIFM_F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle r^{2}\rangle}{4j(j+1)}\Big(F(F+1)-j(j+1)-I(I+1)\Big)

\displaystyle\times\Big(-\frac{1}{4}-j(j+1)+l(l+1)\Big)\,.

Consequently, the second term in (

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa\alpha}{6\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{4j(j+1)}\Big(F(F+1)-j(j+1)-I(I+1)\Big)

(94)

Adding both (
93
) and (
94
