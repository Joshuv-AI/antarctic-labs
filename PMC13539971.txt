Divergent trajectories of genome architecture and chromosome evolution in ferns and angiosperms
Authors: Petr Bureš, Kateřina Vejvodová, František Zedek, Tammy L Elliott, Pavel Veselý, Michaela Fajkusová, Lenka Holubářová, Jana Schwarzová, Petr Šmarda, Jakub Šmerda, Klára Panda, Vlastik Rybka, Harald Schneider, Ilia J Leitch, Libor Ekrt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary Ferns and angiosperms represent the two largest vascular plant lineages but exhibit striking genomic and ecological contrasts. We investigated whether differences in genome size, chromosome architecture, GC content, and stomatal traits reveal divergent evolutionary trajectories between these lineages.We assembled the most comprehensive dataset to date, integrating genome size, chromosome number and size, GC content, and stomatal traits for over 1100 fern species and compared it with an extensive angiosperm dataset.Ferns exhibited markedly lower variability and c. 16‐fold slower rates of chromosome size evolution than angiosperms. A persistent positive relationship between genome size and chromosome number in ferns suggests limited cytological post‐polyploid diploidization. While ferns generally possess larger stomata, this difference disappears after accounting for genome size, indicating that nucleotypic constraints, rather than lineage‐specific physiology, dictate stomatal dimensions. Both groups share a unimodal GC–genome size relationship peaking at c. 14 Gbp.Larger fern chromosomes imply lower genome‐wide recombination rates, potentially limiting genetic reshuffling and adaptive potential. Our results highlight fundamentally divergent evolutionary trajectories, likely shaped by meiotic symmetry in ferns and meiotic asymmetry, possibly centromere drive, and post‐polyploid diploidization in angiosperms, defining the functional and genomic landscapes of these lineages across deep evolutionary timescales. Keywords: flowering plants, GC content, genome size, meiotic drive, post‐polyploid diploidization, pteridophytes, recombination rate, stomata

Introduction

Across eukaryotes, there is a striking variation in the genomic architecture of a species in terms of its genome size, base composition (AT/GC) and chromosomal organization (i.e. number and size). Land plants show the broadest ranges, particularly for chromosome number and genome size (Leitch & Leitch, 2013; Pellicer et al., 2018; Simonin & Roddy, 2018; Fernández et al., 2024). Among land plants, angiosperms and ferns form the two most species‐rich vascular plant lineages and provide a natural contrast for linking genomic traits to phenotype and diversity, given their contrasting patterns of genome size variation and karyotype evolution reported in the literature, although sampling remains comparatively limited in ferns (Barker, 2013; Clark et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Fujiwara et al., 2023). Both lineages nevertheless span broad ecological spaces (Pellicer et al., 2018; Bureš et al., 2024).

Genome size scales positively with minimum cell size and negatively with cell‐division rate (Francis et al., 2008; Šímová & Herben, 2012; D'Ario et al., 2021; Bhadra et al., 2023), with cascading effects on structural and functional traits (Beaulieu et al., 2008; Knight & Beaulieu, 2008; Roddy et al., 2020; Bhadra et al., 2023) that shape morphology (Knight et al., 2005; Beaulieu et al., 2007a,b; Veselý et al., 2012; Carta et al., 2022), ecology (Beaulieu et al., 2007b; Leitch & Leitch, 2013; Faizullah et al., 2021), and geographical distributions (Bureš et al., 2024). One clear illustration of how genome size can affect cell and tissue function is seen in stomatal guard‐cell size. Across vascular plants, guard‐cell length increases with genome size and co‐varies with stomatal density, setting biophysical limits on CO2 and water fluxes and on response speed; larger stomata tend to be less dense and slower to open/close than smaller stomata, with consequences for photosynthetic capacity, water‐use efficiency, and habitat affinities (Beaulieu et al., 2008; Franks & Beerling, 2009; Hodgson et al., 2010; Bainard et al., 2011; Veselý et al., 2012; Greilhuber & Leitch, 2013; de Boer et al., 2016; Elliott‐Kingston et al., 2016; McAusland et al., 2016; Carins Murphy et al., 2017; Simonin & Roddy, 2018; Xiong & Flexas, 2020; Faizullah et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2021; Prats et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that ferns generally have larger stomata than angiosperms. However, it remains unclear to what extent this difference simply reflects ferns' generally larger genomes vs residual lineage differences attributable to ecology and physiology; critically, does the disparity persist once genome size is accounted for? Ecologically, many ferns occupy shaded, humid understories and cloud forests where high boundary‐layer resistance and low vapor‐pressure deficit may lessen selection for rapid stomatal kinetics, enabling larger, slower guard cells which are less costly and sufficient for gas exchange to persist. By contrast, the more open, drier, high‐insolation environments that typify many angiosperms may favor smaller, faster stomata in such habitats (Franks & Beerling, 2009; de Boer et al., 2016; Carins Murphy et al., 2017; Xiong & Flexas, 2020; Faizullah et al., 2021).

Genomic GC content provides a complementary axis of variation with potential ecological relevance. In angiosperms, higher GC content has been associated with responses to water deficit and drought (Šmarda et al., 2014; Bureš et al., 2023). Among small‐genomed angiosperms, GC content often increases with genome (or mean chromosome) size (Šmarda et al., 2008; Lipnerová et al., 2013; Veleba et al., 2014, 2017; Hussain et al., 2021; Bureš et al., 2023), but across a broader span of genome sizes, including species with large genomes, the relationship is unimodal (Veselý et al., 2012; Šmarda et al., 2014). Whether this unimodal pattern extends to the typically larger genomes of ferns remains unknown because GC content is largely unsampled in ferns.

Although chromosome size and number do not directly determine fitness, they can strongly modulate genome‐wide recombination rates and the efficiency with which selection acts on genetic variation, through well‐established mechanistic links between chromosome architecture, crossover formation and interference (Lynch et al., 2011; Stapley et al., 2017; Elliott et al., 2022; Wlodzimierz et al., 2026; Zedek et al., 2026). Chromosome size and number also reflect how polyploidy (or whole‐genome duplication, WGD) and repeat dynamics have reshaped genomes through evolutionary time (Puttick et al., 2015; Clark et al., 2016; Carta et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022; Fujiwara et al., 2023). Polyploidy instantaneously increases genome size and chromosome number, whereas subsequent cytological post‐polyploid diploidization processes, which involve chromosome fusions, fissions, translocations, and gene/genome fractionation, progressively remodel karyotype structure and can decouple genome size from chromosome number (Dodsworth et al., 2016; Soltis et al., 2016; Mandáková & Lysak, 2018).

Under recurrent WGD events followed by extensive cytological diploidization, elevated variability in chromosome size and faster rates of chromosome size evolution are expected, whereas infrequent WGD and/or limited diploidization are predicted to preserve the positive scaling between genome size (2C DNA amount) and chromosome number (2n), along with comparatively uniform and larger chromosomes. Comparative evidence indicates that angiosperms have experienced frequent WGD events followed by pervasive cytological diploidization (Vanneste et al., 2015; Soltis et al., 2016; Van de Peer et al., 2017), whereas homosporous ferns show lower inferred rates of polyploid formation in their evolutionary history (i.e. paleopolyploidy) with more limited karyotype restructuring resulting in a persistent positive 2C‐2n relationship (Clark et al., 2016; 1KP Initiative, 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Fujiwara et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). It should be noted, however, that most insights into the dynamics of post‐polyploid evolution, especially in ferns, are based on transcriptome data, and while the findings are similar to genome‐based approaches, there are key differences. Although these contrasts between ferns and angiosperms have been inferred indirectly, a phylogenetically explicit comparison of chromosome size evolutionary dynamics between the two clades has been lacking.

Here, we assemble the largest comparative dataset of fern genomic and cell traits to date and integrate it with a comprehensive angiosperm dataset to address three questions. First, how do genome size, chromosome size and number, genomic GC content, and stomatal guard‐cell length differ between ferns and angiosperms when analyzed within a unified phylogenetic framework? Second, to what extent are the typically larger stomata observed in ferns explained by their larger genomes, as opposed to residual lineage‐specific differences associated with ecology or physiology? Third, do ferns exhibit slower rates of chromosome size evolution than angiosperms, as predicted under more limited cytological diploidization following WGD? By addressing these questions, we aim to clarify how differences in genome size and chromosome architecture translate into functional and evolutionary contrasts between the two largest lineages of vascular plants.

Materials and Methods

Sampling and taxonomic framework

The material for measurement of genomic and stomatal traits was sampled: (1) during field expeditions by us or our collaborators (see the ‘Acknowledgements’ section – from one to multiple samples per species) in Argentina, Armenia, Australia, Austria, Bulgaria, Canada, Chile, China, Croatia, Czech Republic, Finland, France, Georgia, Germany, Great Britain, Greece, Guatemala, Hungary, India, Iran, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Lithuania, Macedonia, Madagascar, Malaysia, Mexico, Montenegro, New Zealand, North Korea, Norway, Pakistan, Philippines, Poland, Portugal, Reunion, Romania, Russia, Slovenia, South Africa, Spain, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand, Turkey, USA, Venezuela, and Vietnam or (2) from fern collections cultivated in Austrian, Czech, German, and Italian botanical gardens (283 species): Bayreuth, Bonn, Bormio, Brno, Chemnitz, Liberec, Marburg, Munich, Prague, Regensburg, Třeboň, and Vienna (for details, see the ‘Acknowledgements’ section). For sample locations, see Supporting Information Dataset S1. Herbarium specimens are deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Botany and Zoology, Masaryk University (BRNU), and the Herbarium of the Department of Botany, University of South Bohemia (CBFS).

The nomenclature considered in this study follows the Pteridophyte Phylogeny Group I (PPG‐I, 2016); the complete list of analyzed taxa, including taxonomic authorities, is reported in Datasets S1 and S2.

Fern genome size and GC content measurement and compilation

We employed flow cytometry to measure genome size and GC content of 493 fern taxa (Datasets S1, S2; Methods S1). Genome size estimates were newly generated for 364 of these taxa, while the remaining genome size records represent re‐measurements of previously studied species. By contrast, GC content data were newly generated for all 493 taxa.

Published fern genome size estimates representing 559 taxa (50.8%) were extracted from (1) the Pteridophyte DNA C‐values Database (277 taxa, 25.2%; Release 6.0: April 2019, Leitch et al., 2019), and (2) the research papers published between 2019 and 2024 (or older studies that were absent from Release 6.0 of the Pteridophyte DNA C‐values Database) retrieved using ‘Web of Science’, ‘ResearchGate’ and ‘Google Scholar’ (282 taxa, 25.6%; 74 papers). After careful consideration, we decided not to include the genome sizes reported in the work of Wang et al. (2022) in our investigated dataset, as these data show significant inconsistency with the measured values of the same species in our laboratories or published in other sources (Fig. S1).

Several different criteria were applied in cases where different authors reported genome sizes for the same species independently. These comprised as follows: (1) estimated values based on using internal standards calibrated to human leukocytes 2C = 7 pg (Tiersch et al., 1989) were preferred over values estimated using other (or unreported) calibration of standards (these comprised only 15% of the total dataset), (2) choosing estimates from more recent reports over older ones, with preference for our values. We chose the smaller genome size (and thus the smaller DNA ploidy level) in cases where genome size varied within a species, corresponding to different DNA ploidy levels. The genome size values were averaged for multiple estimates presented for a species in the same publication or multiple sampled species in our analyses (Dataset S1). Genome sizes reported in pg were converted to Gbp using the equation 1 pg = 0.978 Gbp (Doležel et al., 2003).

Chromosome number compilation and chromosome size calculation

To estimate the average chromosome size for each species, chromosome numbers were extracted (in order of preference) from (1) the same publications as the genome size data when both estimates were reported together, (2) the Chromosome Counts Database (CCDB: Rice et al., 2015, from which we extracted the median value for each species), or (3) publications reporting only chromosome numbers (Dataset S2).

Chromosome size was calculated as the mean physical DNA content per chromosome by dividing the holoploid genome size (i.e. 2C‐value) by the diploid chromosome number (2n). We use this metric as a standardized proxy for the characteristic chromosome size of a species. While this approach represents an average and does not account for intra‐karyotypic variation in chromosome length (e.g. as in bimodal karyotypes), it provides a robust and scalable measure for quantifying large‐scale evolutionary shifts in genome organization across thousands of species where detailed karyomorphological data are unavailable.

To compare the overall variability in chromosome numbers between ferns and angiosperms, we extracted data from the CCDB database (Rice et al., 2015) for 2632 fern and 65 311 angiosperm species/intraspecific taxa that are accepted following POWO (2024).

Source and compilation of angiosperm genomic trait data for comparisons with ferns

For comparisons with ferns, we compiled 16 017 angiosperm genome and chromosome size data from Bureš et al. (2024) and genomic GC contents for 2710 angiosperm species (Dataset S3) extracted from Bureš et al. (2023), Lipnerová et al. (2013), Šmarda et al. (2014, 2019), Trávníček et al. (2019), Veleba et al. (2014, 2017), Veselý et al. (2012, 2020), and from our students' Master’s theses (Kočková, 2012; Schwarzová, 2012).

Guard cell length measurement

Lengths of stomatal guard cells were measured on the abaxial (lower) surface of leaves of fresh plants or herbarium specimens using a bleaching or microrelief method (for details, see Methods S1). We measured guard cell lengths of 361 fern taxa and complemented them with 192 measurements extracted from the literature, mainly from Van Cotthem (1970), Sen (1986), Kato & Imaichi (1992), Henry et al. (2014), Zhang et al. (2014), Gao et al. (2018), and Kübarsepp et al. (2019); the complete list of sources is provided in Dataset S4. As material state was unavailable for most literature‐derived records, we did not apply corrections for hydration state or preparation method when combining fresh, herbarium, and published measurements. For comparisons between ferns and angiosperms, we used the angiosperm genome size data from Bureš et al. (2024) and the stomatal data mainly from Meidner & Mansfield (1968), Gindel (1969), Carpenter & Smith (1975), Abrams & Kubiske (1990), Zarinkamar (2006, 2007), Beaulieu et al. (2008), Yasmin et al. (2010), Kočková (2012), Schwarzová (2012), Tripathi & Mondal (2012), Veselý et al. (2012), Wang et al. (2014), Zhao et al. (2016), Inceer et al. (2018), Vivin & Daningsih (2021), and Bureš et al. (2023) (Dataset S4). The comparative dataset of angiosperm genomes and stomatal lengths comprised 906 taxa; only taxa with kidney‐shaped guard cells (comparable to those of ferns) were included in the dataset, that is all taxa with dumbbell‐shaped stomata (Poaceae, Cyperaceae) were excluded. Further information on data extraction from the literature is provided in Methods S1.

Regression analyses

To investigate cross‐species relationships between genomic and morphological traits (genome size, GC content, chromosome number, chromosome size, and stomatal guard cell length), we used a unified regression framework combining non‐phylogenetic generalized least squares (GLS) and phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS). For non‐phylogenetic analyses, we fitted GLS models using the ‘gls’ function in the nlme package (v3.1‐166; Pinheiro et al., 2024). In all comparisons between ferns and angiosperms, we allowed for lineage‐specific residual variances using the weights = varIdent(form = ∼1|group) structure. This approach ensures robustness to heteroscedasticity while maintaining comparability across analyses.

For phylogenetic analyses, we used PGLS regression implemented in the phylolm package (v.2.6.2; Ho & Ane, 2014). To determine the appropriate phylogenetic error structure, we compared alternative evolutionary models (BM, Pagel's lambda, kappa, delta, EB, OUrandomRoot, OUfixedRoot) for a subset of biologically plausible fixed‐effect models identified a priori based on the structure of the data. In all cases, Pagel's λ consistently provided the best fit according to AICc (Table S1) and was therefore used in all subsequent PGLS analyses to account for phylogenetic non‐independence in the datasets (Freckleton et al., 2002). For each response variable, we estimated two distinct λ parameters: (1) a trait‐specific λ (λ_trait), estimated from an intercept‐only model and reflecting phylogenetic signal in the response trait itself, and (2) a regression (residual) λ (λ_regression), estimated from the full regression model and reflecting phylogenetic signal in the residuals after accounting for predictors.

Phylogenetic trees and temporal standardization

Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the large species‐level tree of ferns from Nitta et al. (2022), pruned to match the taxa included in each analysis. For ferns, 1062 of the 1101 species with genome size data (96.5%) were represented in the phylogeny. For angiosperms, we randomly selected one of the 100 species‐level trees available for taxa recognized in the world checklist of vascular plants v.6 (Forest, 2023) and pruned it to the species present in our datasets.

Because our primary focus was on comparing genome architecture and chromosome evolution between ferns and angiosperms, we temporally standardized the two phylogenies. We first rescaled the angiosperm tree by proportionally stretching all branch lengths so that its crown age matched the fern crown age of 422 Ma (Nitta et al., 2022). This procedure avoids confounding differences arising solely from the younger crown age of angiosperms. The two trees were subsequently joined in a common root fixed at 457 Ma, adopted here as a conservative estimate for the deeper split between ferns and seed plants. This age falls within the Sandbian and predates the earliest unequivocal trilete spores (c. 455–454 Ma), consistent with fossil evidence for early vascular plants (Steemans et al., 2009; Rubinstein & Vajda, 2019). For analyses concerned with absolute evolutionary rates (i.e. ‘Rates of chromosome size evolution’, see subsequently), original branch lengths were retained.

Effective sample size in phylogenetic regressions

For all PGLS analyses, we quantified effective sample size using both the mean effective sample size (mESS) and the regression effective sample size (rESS). Following Bartoszek (2016), we primarily report rESS, as it provides a more realistic measure of the amount of independent information available for estimating regression coefficients in large, strongly phylogenetically structured datasets.

Rates of chromosome size evolution

To assess evolutionary dynamics, we modeled chromosome size evolution (log10‐transformed 2C/2n) using a censored Brownian motion framework (O'Meara et al., 2006). We integrated fern and angiosperm phylogenies by preserving original branch lengths in millions of years and aligning clade crowns to the present day to ensure accurate rate estimations. Using the R packages phytools, geiger, and phylolm, we first evaluated phylogenetic signal via Pagel's λ and subsequently compared three competing hypotheses using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC): a single‐rate null model (H0), a three‐rate model (H1: homosporous ferns, heterosporous ferns, and angiosperms), and a two‐rate model (H2: ferns vs angiosperms). Clade‐specific evolutionary rates (σ2) were calculated using the ‘fitContinuous’ function, allowing us to identify the most parsimonious model of rate heterogeneity in chromosome size evolution across these major lineages.

Stomatal guard cell length: group comparisons before and after controlling for genome size

For stomatal guard cell length, we compared ferns and angiosperms both before and after accounting for genome size. Non‐phylogenetic analyses were conducted using GLS models with lineage‐specific residual variances. To facilitate direct comparisons between groups, we used the emmeans package (Lenth, 2024) to estimate marginal means and associated SE for each lineage.

Genome‐size‐adjusted comparisons were performed by fitting GLS models including log10‐transformed genome size and lineage as predictors and extracting estimated marginal means for each group at a common reference genome size (the mean of the observed data).

For phylogenetic analyses, marginal means were computed manually because emmeans cannot work with phylolm R objects. We extracted model coefficients and variance–covariance matrices from the PGLS models, constructed design matrices evaluated at the reference genome size, and calculated predicted means and SE for each lineage. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals were obtained using normal approximations (±1.96 SE).

Results

Diversity in fern genomic traits: genome size and GC content, chromosome size and number

Genome sizes have been estimated in 1101 fern taxa spanning 200 genera and 43 out of the 48 fern families recognized by PPG‐I (Fig. 1; Dataset S2), collectively representing nearly 11% of known fern species diversity (PPG‐I, 2016). The genomes varied 629‐fold with exceptionally large genomes (2C > 100 Gbp) confirmed for 13 taxa, primarily from early diverging eusporangiate families – Psilotaceae (3 taxa) and Ophioglossaceae (7 taxa) – with occasional occurrences in the leptosporangiate families Dryopteridaceae, Lomariopsidaceae, and Polypodiaceae (Fig. 1). Conversely, the smallest genomes (2C < 5 Gbp) were found in 20 taxa, especially from the heterosporous water fern families Marsileaceae (6 taxa) and Salviniaceae (5 taxa), as well as in the tropical Gleicheniaceae (6 taxa), with a few instances in Dipteridaceae, Woodsiaceae, and Dicksoniaceae (Fig. 1). Among the newly measured taxa (n = 364), Loxogramme lanceolata (2C = 144.6 Gbp) and Ophioglossum vulgatum (2C = 127.1 Gbp) had the largest genomes, whereas Salvinia biloba (2C = 4.4 Gbp) and Marsilea drummondii (2C = 4.6 Gbp) had the smallest.

Chromosome sizes (2C/2n) were determined or obtained for 940 fern species from 182 genera and 42 families (Fig. 1; Dataset S2). Fern chromosome size varied 40‐fold, ranging from over 750 Mbp per chromosome to < 50 Mbp. The largest chromosomes (2C/2n > 750 Mbp) occurred predominantly in the Hymenophyllaceae (with a maximum of 1087.76 Mbp in Abrodictyum meifolium), Osmundaceae, and occasionally in the Polypodiaceae and Dryopteridaceae (Fig. 1). By contrast, the smallest chromosomes (2C/2n < 50 Mbp) appeared in families with small genomes such as the Marsileaceae (with a minimum of 27.14 Mbp in Marsilea quadrifolia), Salviniaceae, and Gleicheniaceae (Fig. 1).

Genomic GC content was estimated for 493 fern species from 140 genera and 36 families (Fig. 1; Dataset S2). Although GC content varied widely, from 27.26% in Platycerium ridleyi to 49.21% in Anogramma leptophylla, the majority of ferns (> 90%) exhibited GC levels above 40% (Fig. 1; Dataset S2). The lowest GC contents (< 37%) occurred in the genus Platycerium and several other Polypodiaceae genera (Polypodium, Dictymia, Aglaomorpha), and were occasionally observed in Dryopteris (Dryopteridaceae) and Cyathea (Cyatheaceae).

Chromosome number was analyzed in 2632 fern taxa (47 families, Fig. 1; Dataset S5). Among the taxa analyzed for genome size, chromosome numbers ranged from 2n = 18 in Salvinia cucullata (Salviniaceae) to 2n = 960 in Ophioglossum petiolatum (Ophioglossaceae). Elevated chromosome counts (2n > 400) were found in Ophioglossaceae, Schizaeaceae, Aspleniaceae, and Psilotaceae, while low counts (2n < 40) occurred predominantly in the heterosporous families Salviniaceae and Marsileaceae (16 species), with additional occurrences in homosporous Hymenophyllaceae (6 species) and Gleicheniaceae (5 species), and in single species of Aspleniaceae, Dryopteridaceae, and Lomariopsidaceae.

Fern genomic traits are generally larger, more uniform, and evolve more slowly than those of angiosperms

Because the distributions of most genomic traits are log‐normally skewed (Fig. S2A), genome size, chromosome size, and chromosome number were log10‐transformed before comparative analyses. Genomic GC content, which did not show log‐normal skew, was analyzed in its original scale. Across all examined traits, ferns consistently exhibited larger values than angiosperms (Fig. 2). The median fern genome is c. 8× larger than that of angiosperms, and mean chromosome size is roughly twofold greater in ferns (Fig. 2a,b). Ferns also typically show higher genomic GC content and higher chromosome numbers (Fig. 2c,d).

In addition to differences in median and mean values, fern genomic traits are characterized by reduced among‐species variability. This pattern is evident both from fold‐differences in trait distributions (Fig. 2) and from quantitative measures of dispersion, including coefficients of variation and interquartile ranges (Fig. S2B,C). Among the traits examined, chromosome size shows the most pronounced contrast in variability between ferns and angiosperms, as consistently indicated by fold‐differences, coefficients of variation, and interquartile ranges, with fern chromosome sizes occupying a markedly narrower range (Figs 2b, S2B,C; Table S2).

To directly quantify differences in the tempo of chromosome size evolution, we first evaluated the phylogenetic signal of the trait. A high maximum likelihood estimate of Pagel's lambda (0.9605) confirmed that chromosome size evolution strongly follows a Brownian motion model, justifying the use of model‐based rate estimations. We then compared clade‐specific evolutionary rates (σ2) using a censored Brownian motion framework. Our results show that chromosome size evolution is significantly slower in ferns than in angiosperms, with the two‐rate model (H2) providing the most parsimonious fit (AIC = 14524.31) over the single‐rate null model (H0; AIC = 15 230; ΔAIC = 705.69).

Under this model, angiosperms exhibited an evolutionary rate (σ2 = 0.0377) c. 16 times higher than that of ferns (σ2 = 0.0024; Fig. 3a). We also tested a three‐rate model (H1) distinguishing homosporous and heterosporous ferns. Although this model resulted in a slightly higher AIC (AIC = 14526.19; ΔAIC = 1.88 relative to H2; Fig. 3b), the difference between H1 and H2 was small, indicating only weak support for the simpler two‐rate model. Thus, while the data do not strongly justify separating fern lineages by life history, they also do not entirely exclude more subtle rate differences within ferns.

Positive genome size–chromosome number scaling persists in ferns but emerges in angiosperms only after phylogenetic correction

Genome size (log10‐transformed 2C‐value) was strongly associated with chromosome number (log10‐transformed 2n), but both the direction and magnitude of this relationship differed between angiosperms and ferns and depended on whether phylogeny was taken into account (Fig. 4a,b; Table S3).

In non‐phylogenetic analysis, model selection based on AIC strongly supported the interaction model (ΔAIC = 0 relative to additive and no‐group models). Under this model, angiosperms exhibited a weak but significant negative relationship between genome size and chromosome number (β = −0.123 ± 0.021 SE, P < 0.001), corresponding to a c. 8% decrease in genome size per doubling of chromosome number. By contrast, ferns showed a strong positive association (β = 0.675 ± 0.049 SE, P < 0.001), with genome size increasing by c. 60% per chromosome number doubling (Fig. 4a; Table S3).

Accounting for evolutionary relationships using PGLS with Pagel's λ substantially altered this pattern. Although the interaction model remained best supported by AIC, both lineages exhibited positive relationships between genome size and chromosome number (Fig. 4b; Table S3). In angiosperms, genome size increased moderately with chromosome number (β = 0.618 ± 0.010 SE), corresponding to a c. 53% increase per chromosome doubling, while ferns showed a slightly steeper increase (β = 0.795 ± 0.025 SE), equivalent to a c. 73% increase per doubling. The difference between slopes was statistically significant but much smaller than under GLS (interaction term β ≈ 0.178, P < 0.001; Table S3). Importantly, separate PGLS regressions fitted independently for angiosperms and ferns recovered slope estimates indistinguishable from those obtained in the joint interaction model, indicating that the positive scaling inferred under PGLS is robust and not an artifact of heteroscedasticity between groups that cannot be controlled for in phylolm (Table S3).

Ferns and angiosperms share a common quadratic relationship between GC content and genome size

Across both non‐phylogenetic GLS and phylogenetic PGLS frameworks, model selection based on AIC consistently favored quadratic models over linear alternatives (Fig. 4c,d; Table S4), indicating a nonlinear relationship between GC content and genome size.

In the non‐phylogenetic GLS analyses, the quadratic interaction model, allowing lineage‐specific curvature, was best supported by AIC and is therefore shown in Fig. 4c. However, neither the linear nor the quadratic interaction terms were statistically significant (P = 0.14 and 0.28, respectively); Table S4, indicating weak evidence for consistent differences in curve shape between ferns and angiosperms. Uncertainty around the fern curve was substantial, as reflected by wide confidence intervals, particularly at smaller genome sizes. At larger genome sizes, confidence intervals for ferns and angiosperms broadly overlapped, suggesting that apparent differences in fitted curvature are not robustly supported. Accordingly, inference from the corresponding additive quadratic GLS model supports a shared nonlinear relationship between GC content and genome size, with the fern curve shifted upward relative to angiosperms (P < 0.001).

Accounting for phylogenetic non‐independence using PGLS with Pagel's λ led to further convergence between lineages. Quadratic models again outperformed linear models according to AIC, but interaction terms remained unsupported, and the best‐supported phylogenetic models described a single shared nonlinear relationship between GC content and genome size for both ferns and angiosperms (Fig. 4d; Table S4). Under this PGLS model, no significant differences in GC content between lineages were detected after controlling for genome size (P = 0.54).

The fitted PGLS quadratic relationship exhibited an inflection point at 14.29 Gbp (95% CI: 10.40–19.64 Gbp), beyond which GC content no longer increased with genome size and instead weakly declined (Fig. 4d). However, confidence intervals around the quadratic term were wide, and the post‐inflection trend is compatible with both a shallow decline and a regime of relative stasis at large genome sizes. Thus, while nonlinear models are clearly favored by model selection criteria, evidence for a pronounced decrease in GC content at the largest genome sizes remains limited.

Larger stomata in ferns are explained by genome size rather than lineage identity

We analyzed stomatal guard cell length for 553 fern taxa (523 included in the phylogenetic analyses) alongside angiosperms, covering 40 of the 47 fern families that produce stomata. Photographic examples of selected species' stomata are shown in Fig. S3. In both ferns and angiosperms, stomatal guard cell length increased with genome size and was best described by curvilinear additive models (Fig. 4e,f; Table S5).

Under non‐phylogenetic GLS, the model indicated consistently larger stomata in ferns than in angiosperms at comparable genome sizes (P < 0.001; Fig. 4e). By contrast, after accounting for phylogenetic relationships using PGLS, differences between lineages were no longer statistically supported, and both groups were effectively described by a single shared curve relating stomatal size to genome size (Fig. 4f).

Estimated marginal means clarify these patterns (Fig. 5). Without accounting for phylogeny, ferns had substantially larger stomata than angiosperms (geometric means: 43.3 μm vs 29.1 μm; 1.49‐fold difference; P < 0.001). After adjusting for genome size using GLS, this difference decreased to c. 1.09‐fold but remained significant (P < 0.001). In phylogenetic analyses, the raw difference remained large (46.6 μm vs 31.5 μm; 1.48‐fold, P = 0.04), but after genome‐size adjustment under PGLS, the residual difference between ferns and angiosperms was small (1.11‐fold) and not statistically significant (Fig. 5; Table S6). Thus, although ferns generally exhibit larger stomata, this difference is not robust to phylogenetic correction when genome size is accounted for.

Phylogenetic signal and effective sample size

Across all phylogenetic regression analyses, estimated Pagel's λ values were high for all response variables (log102C = 0.92, GC = 0.93, guard cell length = 0.79) and regression residuals (log102C~2n = 0.96, GC~log102C = 0.93, guard cell length~log102C = 0.67; Tables S3–S5), indicating strong phylogenetic signal in genome size, GC content, and stomatal guard cell length. Consequently, effective sample sizes of PGLS models were markedly reduced relative to the raw number of taxa. Consistent with the strong phylogenetic signals, for the genome size–chromosome number analysis (n = 12 943), regression effective sample size (rESS) was reduced to c. 450. In the GC content–genome size analysis (n = 2713), rESS was c. 450, and in the stomatal guard cell analysis (n = 1283), rESS was similarly reduced to c. 600 (Tables S3–S5).

Discussion

Divergent genomic trajectories in ferns and angiosperms

Our comparison of the most comprehensive fern and angiosperm genomic trait datasets assembled to date confirms several previously reported differences between the two clades, including the typically larger genomes and higher chromosome numbers of ferns (Fig. 2a,d; Hidalgo et al., 2017; Pellicer et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2019; Fernández et al., 2024). In addition, our analyses reveal that ferns also tend to possess significantly larger chromosomes than angiosperms (Fig. 2b; Mann–Whitney U test, P < 0.001). This finding contrasts with earlier studies that reported smaller or comparable chromosome sizes in ferns, most of which were based on more taxonomically or geographically limited datasets (e.g. Britton, 1974; Nakazato et al., 2008; Dyer et al., 2013; Clark et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2022; Fujiwara et al., 2023). Ferns further exhibit higher genomic GC content than angiosperms (Fig. 2c), corroborating observations from regionally restricted datasets (Šmarda et al., 2019).

More importantly, contrasts in mean trait values are accompanied by pronounced differences in the variability and evolutionary dynamics of genomic traits. Despite having higher average genome sizes, chromosome numbers, chromosome sizes, and GC content (Fig. 2), ferns consistently show lower among‐species variability, particularly for chromosome size (Fig. S2B). Reduced variability in chromosome size has been noted previously for ferns (Clark et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Fujiwara et al., 2023), but our results extend these observations by demonstrating significantly slower rates of chromosome size evolution across the fern phylogeny (Fig. 3; Table S4). By contrast, differences in the variability of GC content and chromosome number between the two clades are relatively modest (Fig. S2C,D). Together, these patterns indicate fundamentally different trajectories of genome and karyotype evolution in ferns and angiosperms, highlighting chromosome architecture as a key axis along which the two clades diverge.

A further consequence of the large chromosome sizes encountered in ferns is a reduced genome‐wide recombination rate. Recombination rate scales inversely with chromosome size through mechanistic links involving crossover assurance, interference, together with chromosome axis and loop organization (Lynch et al., 2011; Wlodzimierz et al., 2026; Zedek et al., 2026). Because ferns possess substantially larger chromosomes than angiosperms (Fig. 2b), they are therefore expected to experience fewer crossovers per unit DNA across the genome. Lower recombination rates limit the reshuffling of genetic variation, slow the breakdown of linkage disequilibrium, and reduce the efficiency of selection, particularly in species with large genomes, as these have been shown to be associated with reduced effective population sizes (Hill & Robertson, 1966; Felsenstein, 1985; Lynch et al., 2011; Bureš et al., 2024). Together, reduced recombination rates provide a direct mechanistic link between chromosome architecture and the constrained adaptive potential in ferns relative to angiosperms.

Potential mechanisms underlying contrasting karyotype evolution in ferns and angiosperms

The contrasting chromosome size variability and evolutionary dynamics observed between angiosperms and ferns likely reflect differences in the mechanisms shaping karyotype evolution. One proposed contributor is centromere drive, which operates under asymmetric female meiosis (where only one product survives as the egg) and promotes rapid changes in centromere and chromosome architecture (Henikoff et al., 2001; Talbert & Henikoff, 2022; Plačková et al., 2024). Asymmetric meiosis characterizes angiosperms but is absent in most ferns (Lenormand et al., 2016; Kinosian et al., 2022), potentially explaining the faster chromosome size evolution observed in angiosperms.

Centromere drive has also been proposed to interact with cytogenetic post‐polyploid diploidization processes by promoting chromosome fusions and restructuring after WGD (Plačková et al., 2024). In angiosperms, WGD is frequently followed by extensive chromosomal reorganization, descending dysploidy and genome downsizing (Dodsworth et al., 2016; Soltis et al., 2016; Van de Peer et al., 2017; Mandáková & Lysak, 2018). By contrast, ferns generally retain high chromosome numbers, a strong positive relationship between genome size and chromosome number, and relatively uniform chromosome sizes (Nakazato et al., 2008; Clark et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Kinosian et al., 2022; Fujiwara et al., 2023), consistent with limited structural diploidization following WGD. Although centromere drive and post‐polyploid diploidization may act synergistically, they need not be causally coupled and could represent independent contributors to karyotype evolution.

Our chromosome size evolution rate analyses are consistent with this broader contrast (Fig. 3). The best‐supported two‐rate model indicates that chromosome size evolves c. 16 times faster in angiosperms than in ferns. A three‐rate model distinguishing angiosperms, homosporous ferns and heterosporous ferns was only marginally worse with ΔAIC = 1.88 and suggested a slightly elevated rate in heterosporous relative to homosporous ferns (Fig. 3b). Although heterosporous ferns are represented by only 12 species in our dataset, they form a single, relatively young monophyletic lineage that departs from the otherwise conservative chromosomal characteristics of homosporous ferns (Fig. 1). Genomic data reinforce this distinction. Chromosome‐level assemblies of homosporous ferns such as Adiantum capillus‐veneris (Fang et al., 2022) and Ceratopteris richardii (Marchant et al., 2022) reveal genomes composed of c. 85 percent repetitive elements with relatively old LTR insertion ages. By contrast, heterosporous ferns such as Azolla filiculoides, Azolla caroliniana, and Salvinia cucullata (Li et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025) possess compact genomes, low chromosome numbers and markedly younger LTR insertion times, consistent with rapid transposable element turnover (Baniaga & Barker, 2019). Importantly, heterosporous ferns undergo megasporogenesis that produces a single functional megaspore, introducing meiotic asymmetry within an otherwise meiotically symmetric clade (Kinosian et al., 2022). The concurrence of meiotic asymmetry, genome compactness, dynamic repeatomes, low chromosome numbers and slightly elevated chromosome size evolutionary rates suggests that this lineage has undergone a derived evolutionary shift toward a more dynamic karyotype regime. Whether this reflects the action of centromere drive, differences in the efficiency of post‐polyploid diploidization, both, or other processes, remains to be tested.

GC content and genome expansion in ferns and angiosperms

Studies of small‐genomed angiosperms have reported a positive correlation between genome size and GC content, whereas the inclusion of larger‐genomed species reveals a unimodal relationship (Šmarda et al., 2008, 2014; Veselý et al., 2012; Lipnerová et al., 2013; Veleba et al., 2014, 2017; Hussain et al., 2021; Bureš et al., 2023). Our analyses demonstrate that this unimodal pattern also extends to ferns (Fig. 4c,d), with a comparable peak at c. 14 Gbp/2C in ferns (Fig. 4d). However, confidence intervals widen substantially beyond this tipping point, and although the fitted model suggests a decline in GC content in species with very large genome sizes, the data are also compatible with a plateau or relative stasis rather than a pronounced decrease. The tipping point closely matches the breakpoint reported for seed plants associated with a shift in repeat dynamics (c. 20 Gbp/2C), from increases in repeat proportions associated with increases in genome size up to this point followed by a subsequent decline with further genome expansion (Novák et al., 2020; see also Fig. 4c).

One proposed explanation for this pattern is the progressive fossilization of repetitive DNA in very large genomes. Above the breakpoint, repeat elimination via recombination is predicted to be less efficient, resulting in accumulated repeat sequences degrading over time as mutations accumulate and their subsequent transition into inactive, low copy or single copy sequences (Novák et al., 2020). Because repetitive elements are often methylated (Kumar & Mohapatra, 2021), their long‐term persistence may promote GC to AT substitutions through the deamination of 5‐methylcytosine to thymine nucleotides (Takuno et al., 2016), contributing to the reduced GC content observed in large genomes (Fig. 4c,d).

In ferns, this process may be further reinforced by limited gene loss following polyploidy and a greater reliance on epigenetic silencing rather than structural genome downsizing (Haufler, 1987, 2002, 2014; Gastony, 1991; Barker, 2013; Clark et al., 2016; Kinosian et al., 2022). In addition, smaller effective population sizes in large‐genomed species (Bureš et al., 2024) may weaken purifying selection against GC to AT mutations, allowing such changes to accumulate even in coding regions. Together, these processes offer a plausible explanation for the shared decline in GC content observed in both fern and angiosperm species with very large genomes.

Genome size as the primary determinant of stomatal size differences between ferns and angiosperms

Ferns are widely reported to possess larger stomata than angiosperms, a difference that has been shown to be linked to reduced hydraulic efficiency, slower stomatal responses, and lower maximum CO2 conductance (Franks & Beerling, 2009; Boyce & Lee, 2010; Sack & Scoffoni, 2013; Lawson & Blatt, 2014; de Boer et al., 2016; Xiong & Flexas, 2020; Zhang et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2023). These functional differences have often been invoked to explain why many ferns are restricted to growing in humid, low‐light environments.

Our analyses show, however, that the apparent stomatal differences between ferns and angiosperms are almost entirely explained by genome size. Once genome size is accounted for, little residual difference in stomatal guard cell size remains between the two clades (Fig. 5), indicating that the larger stomata of ferns primarily reflect their larger genomes rather than lineage‐specific physiological differences. Although our guard‐cell dataset combines measurements from fresh, herbarium, and literature‐derived material without correction for hydration state, this source of variation is expected to increase residual error rather than generate the strong genome size‐dependent pattern observed here.

This genome size‐mediated constraint on stomatal dimensions nevertheless likely contributed to the superior physiological performance of many angiosperms, particularly with respect to photosynthetic efficiency and gas exchange capacity (Carriquí et al., 2015; Simonin & Roddy, 2018; Roddy et al., 2020; Théroux‐Rancourt et al., 2021; Šmarda et al., 2023). Fossil evidence indicates that angiosperms already possessed smaller stomata than co‐occurring ferns by the Cretaceous (Feild et al., 2011; Lomax et al., 2014), and declining atmospheric CO2 levels during this period likely amplified the selective advantage of smaller, faster stomata (Foster et al., 2017; Simonin & Roddy, 2018; Benton et al., 2022).

Conclusion

By integrating the largest comparative dataset of genomic traits assembled for ferns with an extensive angiosperm dataset, our study reveals fundamentally different trajectories of genome and chromosome evolution in the two major lineages of vascular plants. Ferns are characterized by large but comparatively uniform genome sizes, stable chromosome sizes, and slower rates of accumulation of chromosomal size change, whereas angiosperms exhibit greater karyotypic variability and more dynamic chromosome evolution. These contrasting genomic architectures translate into functional consequences, including genome size‐mediated constraints on stomatal dimensions and chromosome size‐dependent limits on rates of genome‐wide recombination, which together contribute to shaping the efficiency of selection and adaptive potential. Together, our results highlight how long‐term differences in genome architecture and chromosome evolution can influence functional traits and ecological constraints across deep evolutionary timescales, providing a macroevolutionary context for understanding the contrasting evolutionary success of ferns and angiosperms.
