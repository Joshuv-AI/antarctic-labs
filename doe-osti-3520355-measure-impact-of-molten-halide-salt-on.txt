 ABSTRACT

The deployment of advanced molten‑salt reactor systems requires a detailed understanding of how molten
halide salts affect the high‑temperature mechanical performance of structural alloys. This study investigates
the creep and fatigue behavior of wrought and additively manufacture 316H stainless steel in air\argon (Ar),
molten fluoride (FLiNaK) and chloride (NaCl-MgCl2) salts at 650°C, alloy 709 at 700°C in air\Ar and
FLiNaK and alloy 625 at 750°C in Ar and FLiNaK salt. For 316H, creep‑rupture lifetimes decreased by
approximately 50% in FLiNaK and 80% in NaCl-MgCl2 relative to air. The molten fluoride salt
environment reduced the creep life of 709 by about 90% at the higher stress of 158 MPa but had a minimal
impact at the lower stress of 130 MPa. Alloy 625 showed an improvement in creep rupture times but
resulted in a considerable reduction in creep ductility. The high cycle fatigue tests conducted at relatively
low stresses (~90% of yield stress at the test temperature) suggest limited impact of the salt environment
on the fatigue life of 316H and 625. Chemical analyses of the molten sats after testing combined with
microstructural analyses revealed a strong correlation between depletion of key alloying constituents such
as Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni and the creep rupture times. Potential differences in the chemistry between salt‑facing
and Ar‑facing surfaces were expected to result in selective element dissolution and formation of deleterious
phases that either weaken grain boundaries and adversely affected overall mechanical performance in case
of 316H and 709 or strengthen the material through corrosion-induced precipitation of stronger intermetallic
phases in alloy 625. These results highlight the critical impact of molten‑salt corrosion on long‑term
structural integrity and underscore the need for microstructurally informed materials selection for
next‑generation molten salt reactor designs.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Recent interest in advancing molten salt reactor (MSR) technologies has been fueled by their potential to
contribute meaningfully to near‑term and long‑range climate objectives (Zhao et al. 2023; Kelleher,
Gagnon, and Mitchell 2022; Locatelli, Mancini, and Todeschini 2013; Serp et al. 2014). Much of the
current development builds on insights gained from the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE)
(Macpherson 1972), complemented by newer investigations of structural‑material performance in
molten‑salt environments, including studies focused on alloys such as 316H (Raiman et al. 2022). Despite
this progress, there remains a critical lack of data representative of the decades‑long operating periods
anticipated for commercial MSRs, particularly regarding the corrosion behavior of candidate alloys like
316H, 709 and 625 in fluoride‑based salts such as FLiNaK and FLiBe. In addition, the field has yet to
establish experimental baselines describing how the combined effects of corrosive salts, mechanical
loading, and irradiation interact to influence long-term material degradation. Historical work by Manly
and colleagues (Manly et al. 1958) already indicated that molten fluoride salt mixtures (NaF–ZrF₄–UF₄)
can markedly alter the creep response of Ni‑based alloys. As MSR deployment efforts accelerate,
comprehensive data on engineering properties such as creep and fatigue performance under molten‑salt
exposure are essential for selecting suitable structural materials and predicting their service lifetimes.

The present task evaluated the creep and high cycle fatigue behavior of the wrought and additively
manufactured austenitic steel 316H in molten FLiNaK and NaCl-MgCl2 and of wrought alloy 625 in
molten FLiNaK using hollow creep specimens. The creep testing was conducted between 650-750C and
at stresses between 130-160 MPa. High cycle fatigue testing was conducted at ~90% of the yield stress at
the test temperature (650°C for 316H and 750°C for 625). Previous work had reported the creep behavior
of wrought alloys 316H, 709 and 617 in FLiNaK and NaCl-MgCl2 (Rishi Pillai and Pint 2024; Rishi Pillai
et al. 2025) was selected for the testing in the current task. Baseline tests were conducted in air and Ar to
assess the impact of the molten salts on the creep behavior. Preliminary microstructural characterization
was conducted to derive correlations to the observed creep behavior.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

The cylindrical hollow creep specimens had a 57.2 mm gage length, 12.7 mm outer and 11.2 mm inner
diameter (ID). A stainless-steel cup made of type 316L stainless steel was attached to the bottom of the
creep specimen to capture the molten salt after rupture. This specimen design was derived from a patent
application (Ren et al. 2016). All specimens were subjected to rigorous geometry checks in order to verify
dimensions and surface finish. Particularly, the inner surface was electrical discharge machined (EDM)
and polished to an Ra of 0.4 µm measured using a profilometer. Specimens of wrought 316H were
machined from a 2.54 cm thick 316H plate provided in a solution annealed state. The additively
manufactured (laser powder bed fusion) 316H (AM316H) was solution annealed at 1050°C for 30
minutes and water quenched similar to the heat treatment of the wrought 316H material. Details of the
additive fabrication process were reported in (Scime et al. 2023). Creep specimens were machined
perpendicular to the build direction from the heat treated material. Alloy 709 specimens were machined
from a 1.8 cm thick plate (solution annealed at min. temperature of 1150°C, followed by precipitation
treatment at 775°C for 10-h). Alloy 625 specimens were machined from a 4 cm thick plate (mill annealed
at a min. temperature of 1052°C followed by rapid cooling). The measured composition of the three
alloys and the powder used for the AM316H material are given in Table 1. The differences in the Ni and
Mn contents between the wrought 316H and AM316H powder are worth mentioning.

The FLiNaK salt used for this work was prepared by a now defunct company Electrochemical Systems.
The composition of the as-received salt was previously reported (Raiman et al. 2022). The NaCl–MgCl2
eutectic salt used in this study was prepared with 58.5 mol % anhydrous NaCl salt and 41.5 mol %

The hollow creep specimens were filled in an Ar-filled glovebox (O2 and H2O < 0.1 ppm) with ~15 g of
the salt and sealed by welding the cap (same material as the specimen) on the top in the glovebox. Before
beginning the creep test, thermocouples were fixed at about 1/3 of the gauge length before threading on
the catch cup. The completed load train (including the pull rods and specimen) was loaded into the
furnace and threaded into the turn buckle before additional insulation was applied. The heating was 1 h to
temperature followed by a 0.5 h hold to reach a steady state. The system was operated within a 3 °C
variance between the thermocouple on the specimen and the control thermocouple of the furnace. The
linear variable displacement transducer (LVDT) used to measure displacement and derive creep strains
was zeroed at this point and the specimen was loaded. The furnace was programmed to be switched off
immediately upon specimen rupture to minimize corrosion damage to the specimen due to the leaking
molten salt. The experimental program is summarized in Table 2.

Fatigue testing was conducted by threading the axial fatigue specimen into a load train column at both
ends with thermocouples fixed at the upper, middle, and lower points of its gage section. Nickel alloy 625
rods were inserted into the load train columns at either end of the specimen and bellows were placed over
the columns to seal off the specimen from environmental conditions. The load train assembly was inserted
into a furnace and attached to high temperature grips that are mounted to the test frame. These grips
contain small hydraulic pistons that are used to apply compressive stress on the 625 rods and force them

against the specimen, thereby ensuring a stiff load train. The bellows and furnace window were sealed to
ensure airtight conditions and the thermocouples were connected to a 3-zone furnace controller. The
furnace was heated to the desired test temperature at a rate of 5°C/min before applying any load to the test
specimen. A stream of ultra-high purity argon cover gas is initiated at a flow rate just high enough to
ensure positive pressure in the furnace retort as witnessed through a bubbler downstream of the test setup.
Once the test temperature was attained, a static tensile load equal to the mean test load was gradually
applied. When the mean load was stabilized, an MTS 407 controller operating in load control applied a
sinusoidal load with a stress ratio of R = 0.1 to the specimen. A LabVIEW program recorded run time,
load as measured by an Interface load cell, and displacement according to the movement of the test frame
actuator. The HCF testing for 316H was conducted at 650°C and at a peak stress of 90 MPa (equivalent to
90% of the yield stress value at 650°C) and a cycle frequency of 10 Hz. The HCF testing for 625 was
conducted at 750°C and at a peak stress of 205 MPa (equivalent to 90% of the yield stress value at 650°C)
and a cycle frequency of 20 Hz.

Postexposure specimens were cross-sectioned for metallographic analyses. The mounted samples were
ground to 1,200 grit with SiC grinding papers and subsequently polished with diamond pastes to 1 μm
surface finish. The final preparation step involved preparation with colloidal silica. Microstructural
characterization to measure compositional changes and identify phase transformations was performed
using scanning electron microscopy, backscattered electron (BSE) microscopy (ZEISS), and energy
dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Octane Elect EDS/EDX with Super Silicon Drift Detector). The
chemical compositions of the salts before and after high temperature exposure were measured with
inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES)

 3. RESULTS

Figure 1a compares the measured creep strains of 316H in air, Ar (paused to prioritize testing in molten
salt environments) and NaCl-MgCl2 at 650 °C/150 MPa. As was reported in the previous work (Rishi
Pillai et al. 2025), the cover gas environment (air or Ar), were found to have minimal impact on the creep
behavior of alloys 709 and 617. The creep test in NaCl-MgCl2 (358h) has resulted in a four-fold reduction
in creep rupture lifetime compared to the test in air (1702h). Additionally, there is about a 50% reduction
in creep ductility for the specimen tested in the chloride salt. The baseline creep test with wrought 316H
in Ar had to be paused to prioritize creep testing in molten salts. Based on the measured data until the
interruption, wrought 316H was observed to demonstrate similar creep behavior in air and Ar. The
measured creep strains for the ongoing creep test with the AM316H material in FLiNaK is showing
similar behavior to the wrought variant with considerable acceleration in creep rates compared to the
baseline test in air (Figure 1b). The AM material is demonstrating the tendency toward an even shorter
time to creep rupture.

Figure 3a compares the creep behavior of alloy 709 in FLiNaK with two different cover gas
environments Air and Ar at 700°C and 158 MPa. In both cases, a significant reduction in creep rupture
lifetime (150h with air as cover gas and 90h with Ar as cover gas) is observed compared to the creep test
without salt (1095h). The expected creep rupture life for 709 in air under these conditions is 1000h. The
709 specimen tested at 700°C and 130 MPa demonstrated a creep life of 2550h. The corresponding test in
Ar was paused at about 1800h and the creep strains were similar to the specimen with salt indicating
limited impact of the molten salt on the creep behavior of 709 at this lower stress. However, no definitive
conclusions could be drawn on the creep rupture behavior which might still differ if the test were to be
continued.

In contrast to 316H and 709, alloy 625 demonstrated a longer creep life in FLiNaK than in Ar with the
specimen tested in Ar demonstrating a creep rupture lifetime of 291h compared to 710h in the salt
(Figure 3b). Issues with the furnace controller caused a 10-15°C drop in the average test temperature for
the specimen tested in FLiNaK which might have resulted in the slower creep rates. An additional creep
test was initiated with FLiNaK and the data from the ongoing test is plotted in Figure 3b. Correcting the
temperature has resulted in faster creep rates compared to the previous test in FLiNaK but it is still slower
than the specimen tested in Ar. Based on the available material data sheets (Haynes 2026), the Larson-
Miller Parameter (T(20.7+log(tr)), where tr is creep rupture time, and its stress dependence was estimated
by fitting to the available creep data. For the chosen condition of 750°C and 150 MPa, a creep rupture
time of ~670h is expected. The specimen tested in Ar failed considerably sooner than this duration. The
creep rupture time of the specimen tested in FLiNaK (at ~735-740°C) exceeded this value but there was a
significant drop in creep ductility from 22.7% in Ar to 6.7% in FLiNaK. The conclusion of the repeat test
with FLiNaK will confirm the finding regarding the drop in creep ductility in the molten salt
environment.

 4. DISCUSSION

A few preliminary investigations were conducted to understand the role of creep-corrosion interactions in
reducing creep rupture lifetime of the investigated alloys. Figure 4a shows the concentrations of key
alloying elements measured in the salt after the creep testing of 316H in FLiNaK and NaCl-MgCl2 at 650°C
and 130 MPa. The 316H specimen that failed after 91h in FLiNaK showed significantly higher amounts of
Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni in the salt compared to the specimen that failed after 471h. The test in NaCl-MgCl2 that
failed after 358h showed even higher amounts of Cr and Fe while the Mn and Ni contents were between
the values obtained for the two tests in FLiNaK. The dissolution rates and equilibrium amounts of Cr and
Fe have been shown to be higher in chloride than in fluoride salts (Pint, Sulejmanovic, and Pillai 2023; R.
Pillai et al. 2023). Figure 4b shows the concentrations of Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni measured in the salt after the
creep testing of 709 in FLiNaK at 700°C and 158 MPa. Similar to 316H, the measured Cr and Fe contents
in the salt for the specimen that failed after 40h were an order of magnitude higher than the specimen that
failed after 150h. Interestingly, the Ni content in the salt for the specimen that failed after 150h was higher.

The results suggest a correlation between the depletion of key alloying constituents and the creep rupture
life. A higher and faster depletion of Cr and Fe was observed in the salt for 709 than for 316H. This finding
is in good agreement with the previously reported values for the two alloys comparing interrupted creep
testing of 316H at 650°C and 150 MPa after 500h with the unloaded 709 creep specimen after 200h at
700°C (Rishi Pillai et al. 2025). The differences in the accelerated depletion in 709 were attributed to the
higher chemical activity of Cr and its faster diffusion in 709 based on the coupled thermodynamic-kinetic
analyses conducted previously (Rishi Pillai et al. 2025).

The micrographs in Figure 5 show the BSE images of the cross-section near the inner diameter of the 709
creep specimens after creep testing in Ar (Figure 5a) and FLiNaK (Figure 5b) at 700°C and 158 MPa. The
salt environment has resulted in considerable porosity formation close to the salt-facing surface while no
such damage is visible in the specimen exposed to Ar. The specimen exposed in Ar retains the strengthening
phases (e.g., Cr-rich precipitates, confirmed through element distribution maps not shown here) while no
such precipitates can be seen in the specimen tested in FLiNaK. The combined effect of the absence of
these phases and the potential crack initiation sites near the surface can be expected to accelerate creep
rupture failure in 709 during exposure in molten salts. Previous work has reported similar differences in the
measured compositional changes in the alloy subsurface between the different environments. The
significantly higher depletion of alloying constituents in the alloy subsurface during exposure in molten
salts compared to in air\Ar can be expected to result in phase transformations which can be potentially
detrimental to the creep behavior of the alloys not intentionally designed for operation in molten salts.

In case of alloy 625, an increase in the creep rupture times was observed for the specimen tested in FLiNaK
compared to Ar. However, there was a significant drop in creep ductility in the salt environment. This result
suggests strain hardening of the material potentially due to the formation of harder intermetallic phases in
the alloy. It is well established for alloy 625 that Cr depletion due to corrosion results in the enrichment of
Nb in the corrosion-affected alloy subsurface which consequently promotes the formation of the δ-phase.
This has been shown by Chyrkin et al. (Chyrkin et al. 2011) and Romedenne et al. (Romedenne et al. 2021)
during oxidation studies of 625 in dry and wet air respectively. The presence of the significantly harder and
stronger δ-phase can be expected to retard creep deformation and strengthen the material with continuing
corrosion (Cr depletion in the salt). Although a longer creep life was observed for 625 in salt than in Ar,
the impact of the reduced creep ductility on the overall material performance remains to be ascertained.
Efforts to continue to understand the impact of these molten salt induced compositional changes and phase
transformations on the observed creep and fatigue behavior of the three alloys are currently ongoing.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

The molten salt environment resulted in a decrease in the creep rupture life of wrought and additively
manufactured 316H and wrought 709. The molten salt environment resulted in a measurable (50-75%)
reduction in creep lifetime for 316H in FLiNaK and NaCl-MgCl2 compared to tests conducted in air\Ar at
650C and 150 MPa. The creep tests conducted at 650C and 130 MPa in FLiNaK and NaCl-MgCl2
resulted in creep lives which were 5-15% of the expected creep life of 3000h for 316H at this stress and
temperature The creep testing of 709 in the fluoride salt resulted in about an 80% reduction in creep
rupture time with no evident impact on creep ductility at the higher stress of 158 MPa. The measured
contents of Cr, Fe, Mn and Ni in the salts after creep testing demonstrated a clear correlation between
corrosion and creep rupture times. The shortest creep rupture times corresponded to the highest amounts
of these alloying constituents in the salt after creep testing. Much higher Cr and Fe depletion into the
FLiNaK salt was observed in the case of 709 compared to 316H. An increased creep rupture time and a
reduction in creep ductility was measured for alloy 625 after creep testing in FLiNaK. The high cycle
fatigue tests conducted at relatively low stresses (~90% of yield stress at the test temperature) suggest
limited impact of the salt environment on the fatigue life of 316H and 625. Future work will focus on
continuing the creep and fatigue testing, detailed characterization of the creep and fatigue specimens,
continued analyses of the salt chemistry after creep testing and coupled thermodynamic-kinetic modeling
to understand the creep-corrosion interactions.
