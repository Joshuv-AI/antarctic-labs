Executive Summary
Voluntary renewable power, in this report, refers to renewable energy procurement by retail
electricity customers above what is otherwise provided by load-serving entities. This report is
part of an annual series of reports synthesizing trends in the U.S. voluntary renewable power
market. In 2024, approximately 9.2 million retail electricity customers procured approximately
315 million megawatt-hours (MWh) of voluntary renewable power (Figure ES-1). Estimated
voluntary renewable power market sales represented approximately 45% of renewable energy
sales excluding large hydropower and approximately 8% of all U.S. retail electricity sales in
2024. Most of the remainder of U.S. renewable energy sales reflects renewable energy procured
by load-serving entities to comply with state renewable energy mandates.

 Figure ES-1. Voluntary renewable power sales (left) and participation (right), 2014–2024.
 MWh = megawatt-hours, TWh = terawatt-hours

The U.S. voluntary power market is undergoing a clear shift toward long-term contracts,
primarily driven by increasing corporate renewable power procurement. The two key forms of
corporate renewable contracts are power purchase agreements, where the corporation acts as the
contractual counterparty, and utility renewable contracts, where the utility is the counterparty and
delivers the power to the voluntary buyer. Smaller voluntary buyers that cannot serve as
contractual counterparties can indirectly enable long-term contracts by participating in voluntary
renewable power programs. For 2024, we estimate that at least half of the voluntary power
market sales were supported by long-term contracts. In this report, we explore the unique role of
long-term contracts in supporting renewable energy deployment.

1 Introduction
Many retail electricity customers demand more renewable energy than what is otherwise
provided through the standard offer service by their local load-serving entity (e.g., utilities,
competitive retail electricity suppliers). These customers have various options to use renewable
energy, which requires the purchase and retirement of renewable energy certificates (RECs),
which are accounting mechanisms that represent the energy attributes of renewable energy
generation. 1 Procurement of this kind of renewable energy by retail electricity customers is
commonly known as “voluntary” demand, in contrast to “compliance” demand, which refers to
procurement by load-serving entities to comply with state mandates, such as renewable portfolio
standards (RPS). 2 In this report, the term voluntary renewable power refers exclusively to
renewable energy procurement by retail electricity customers above RPS obligations. This report
summarizes the state of the U.S. voluntary renewable power market. For similar information on
the compliance market, see Barbose (2025).

In this report, we summarize data on the various products through which retail electricity
customers—including residential, commercial and industrial, and institutional (e.g., government)
customers—purchase voluntary renewable power. This report focuses on voluntary renewable
power sales and participation in calendar year 2024. 3 Note that although renewable power
products substantially vary in terms of cost structures, contractual commitments, availability, and
other factors, all renewable power products ultimately serve as a conduit through which RECs
are bought and retired on behalf of customers. The inclusion of RECs in all voluntary renewable
power products ensures that the associated renewable energy use cannot be double counted and
claimed by other voluntary buyers or by a retail electricity supplier for RPS compliance.

In some cases, retail electricity suppliers sell power as a single product that includes voluntary
renewable power in addition to a portion of renewable energy used for RPS compliance. In these
cases, we factor out the portion of renewable electricity supply that was required to meet RPS
obligations. This report does not include voluntary renewable power use where no explicit REC
transaction occurs and therefore no usage data are available. This lack of data/absence of REC
transaction occurs when customers own on-site systems and “retain” the RECs so that RECs are
never formally retired. Voluntary renewable power output from on-site systems is estimated at
less than 5% of off-site voluntary renewable power sales (Sumner et al. 2023).

We present voluntary renewable power market data and trends in Section 2. In Section 3, we
explore the ongoing shift of the voluntary market toward long-term contracts and the unique role
that these contracts play in driving renewable energy development.

3 Long-Term Contracting in the Voluntary Power
  Market
The U.S. voluntary renewable power market includes many pathways for customers to procure
renewable energy. Most customers procure voluntary renewable power through short-term
commitments, such as through enrolling in utility green pricing programs or buying unbundled
RECs from the “spot” market. This majority comprises mostly residential and small commercial
buyers. In contrast, the minority, a relatively small but growing group of large commercial and
industrial customers, procure voluntary renewable power through long-term (i.e., multiyear)
contractual commitments via PPAs or utility renewable contracts. Prior research suggests that
this group of large commercial and industrial buyers has an outsized role in shaping the
voluntary renewable power market and in driving renewable energy development. In contrast,
small commercial and industrial and residential customers typically lack access to bespoke long-
term contracts, limiting their direct participation in these markets. For these customers, energy
choice is exercised collectively, rather than individually, through utility green power programs
and other opt-in offerings that aggregate demand and enable broader participation in decisions
about how and when new energy resources are added to the system.

In this section, we explore trends in voluntary renewable contracts and the unique role of
contracts in the voluntary renewable power market. We begin in Section 3.1 by further clarifying
the characteristics of voluntary renewable contracts. In Section 3.2, we review prior research on
the unique role of voluntary renewable contracts in driving renewable energy deployment. Then,
in Section 3.3, we discuss trends in voluntary renewable contracts.

3.1 Background on Voluntary Renewable Contracts
A voluntary renewable contract, in this report, refers to a contractual commitment of a retail
electricity customer to procure RECs and power (in most cases) over a multiyear contract term.
There are three common forms of voluntary renewable contracts in the U.S. market:

 1. PPAs are contracts where a buyer signs a financial contract for power and RECs from a
 specific project. In a “physical” PPA, the buyer takes physical delivery of the power,
 meaning that the buyer’s operations are located on the same grid as the generator under
 contract. In a “virtual” or “financial” PPA, the contract specifies the financial
 responsibilities of the buyer and the project, but the buyer does not take physical delivery
 of the power; rather, the power is sold into a wholesale market. The financial implications
 of the two varieties are largely the same: The project perceives fixed prices for power and
 RECs over the contract term.
 2. Utility renewable contracts are contracts where the utility serves as the contractual buyer
 and “sleeves” the contracted power and RECs to the end user. Utility renewable contracts
 are, in simple terms, a PPA with a utility acting as the contractual counterparty on behalf
 of the buyer.
 3. Unbundled REC contracts or “strips” are contracts where a buyer procures RECs from a
 specific project for some multiyear term. These contracts are “unbundled” in the sense
 that the contracts only cover RECs sold separately from the power. Projects with

 8

 unbundled REC contracts sell the power into wholesale markets or may sign separate
 power-only PPAs to sell the power to specific buyers.

3.2 The Unique Role of Voluntary Renewable Contracts
Long-term contracts are broadly viewed as support mechanisms for renewable energy
development (Steffen 2018; Bartlett 2019; Beiter et al. 2024; Olson et al. 2024; Neuhoff et al.
2025). To describe the unique role of contracts, we begin by discussing the key characteristics of
the renewable energy business model in the United States.

Most renewable energy developers are for-profit companies that conduct the planning,
permitting, and construction necessary to build large-scale renewable energy systems. These
systems typically operate as legally independent entities known as projects (Steffen 2018).
Project development costs are typically covered by a combination of internal finance (i.e.,
financed by the developer) and external finance (such as from banks or other investors). External
project investors provide financial capital in exchange for shares of long-term project revenue;
therefore, renewable energy project development depends on developers identifying project
opportunities with projected revenue to attract enough external finance to cover the up-front
development costs (Neuhoff et al. 2025).

Operational renewable energy projects earn revenue through several sources, including power
sales, tax credits, and attribute or certificate markets. From the perspectives of developers and
investors, this revenue accrues in the future and is therefore uncertain and risky. Future revenue
may fluctuate due to market conditions, such as changes in energy, capacity, and attribute prices.
Investors account for these risks by requiring higher returns on investment (Beiter et al. 2024;
Dimanchev et al. 2024). These perceived risks constrain the availability of low-cost external
finance and pose an ongoing challenge for developers seeking to add new resources to the
system (Dimanchev et al. 2024; Olson et al. 2024; Neuhoff et al. 2025); therefore, project
developers have strong incentives to manage risks or to “de-risk” project revenue to attract more
external finance.

Long-term, fixed-price contracts are one important strategy for de-risking renewable energy
project revenue and enabling timely energy additions. By fixing prices over the contract term,
developers increase revenue certainty and reduce exposure to market volatility. De-risked
revenue allows developers to secure external finance at lower costs (i.e., lower interest rates)
(Beiter et al. 2024; Dimanchev et al. 2024; Olson et al. 2024).

The voluntary power market is not the only source of demand for long-term contracts. Load-
serving entities often also procure power through long-term contracts; however, voluntary
renewable contracts are unique in several regards:

 • The needs of the corporations that sign voluntary renewable contracts are often aligned
 with the objectives of developers and investors. Corporations with large electricity
 demand are exposed to the same long-term market volatility risks as renewable energy
 projects. For projects, the risk is that future power prices are lower than expected,
 whereas for corporations, the risk is that future power prices are higher than expected;
 therefore, corporations, project developers, and project investors all share incentives to
 hedge against future power market volatility. Voluntary renewable contracts can provide

 9

 that hedge by allowing corporations to fix the price they pay for consumed electricity and
 by allowing projects to fix the price they receive for generated electricity (Beiter et al.
         2024; Neuhoff et al. 2025).
 • Voluntary buyers may accept terms that are more favorable to renewable energy
 projects. Given the aligned interests of corporations and projects, Christophers (2024)
 notes that some developers seek corporations as contractual counterparties rather than
 load-serving entities. Corporations with sustainability and electricity cost objectives may
 be willing to accept financial terms that are more favorable for renewable energy project
 development, such as fixed power prices (Christophers 2024).
 • Voluntary buyers operate at a distinct pace and scale. Electric utilities face regulatory
 burdens and institutional inertia that constrain their ability to enter long-term renewable
 contracts (Alova 2020). Voluntary buyers face their own unique constraints, such as
 corporate fiduciary responsibilities; however, some developers report that many
 voluntary buyers have renewable energy procurement goals that drive them to seek power
 contracts at a faster pace and large scale than electric utilities (O’Shaughnessy 2024). As
 a result, voluntary buyers may provide a complement to, rather than a substitute for,
 electric utilities as a source of demand for power contracts.
 • There is a limited pool of voluntary buyers that can act as contractual counterparties.
 Renewable energy projects generally require contractual counterparties with investment-
 grade credit ratings (Christophers 2024). That requirement constrains the pool of
 voluntary buyers that can sign voluntary renewable contracts to a relatively small group
 of large corporations (Beiter, Jena, and Sumner 2025).

3.3 Trends in Voluntary Renewable Contracts
Voluntary power sales through PPAs and utility renewable contracts have grown faster than
voluntary sales through other pathways in recent years. As a result, voluntary renewable
contracts have accounted for increasing shares of voluntary power market sales. The share of
voluntary power market sales made through PPAs or utility renewable contracts increased from
6% in 2014 to 39% in 2024. There are three primary reasons for this growth: (1) Many
corporations use contracts to follow through on renewable energy procurement commitments; (2)
corporations have increasingly turned to voluntary renewable contracts rather than spot-market
unbundled RECs; and (3) PPAs can be more cost-effective than other voluntary power market
options and can offer cobenefits, such as financial hedging (O’Shaughnessy et al. 2021).

Note that the PPA estimates in Section 2 and all voluntary power market estimates are based on
PPAs with voluntary power buyers as the contractual counterparties. These PPA estimates do not
include cases where load-serving entities sign PPAs and sell the RECs to voluntary buyers in
voluntary power programs. One point of reference to estimate such sales is survey data collected
from utility green pricing programs (see Table 1). The survey data indicate that approximately
14% of utility green pricing sales from 2020 to 2024 were backed by “bundled” multiyear
contracts where utilities sign PPAs and sell the associated power and RECs to participating green
pricing customers. 9 If we assume similar shares of voluntary power programs supported by
bundled multiyear products in competitive supplier and CCA voluntary power programs, that

3.4 The Future of Voluntary Renewable Contracts
The ongoing shift of the voluntary power market toward long-term contracts is expected to
continue. According to data from the Clean Energy Buyers Association (CEBA), corporations
had signed 122.7 gigawatts (GW) of PPAs and utility renewable contracts as of September 2025
(CEBA 2025). Of that signed capacity, only approximately 64 GW was operational as of
September 2025, meaning that around 59 GW of capacity supported by voluntary renewable
contracts is in the development queue. Depending on the resource mix of that planned capacity,
projects in development could add in the range of 100–150 million MWh per year of voluntary
power market sales. New capacity contracted by corporations increased in 7 of the 8 years from
2016 to 2024 and is projected to increase again in 2025 (Figure 7). That continued growth means
that corporate demand for voluntary renewable contracts could continue to increase into the
foreseeable future. One potential disruption to that trend is the proposed reforms to corporate
emissions accounting guidance in the Greenhouse Gas Protocol. The proposed reforms would
place geographic restrictions on the RECs that corporations could claim for emissions accounting
purposes. The geographic restrictions could disrupt the common practice of signing power
contracts to serve aggregated demand across multiple geographies (Leggett and Gillenwater
2025). Tracking future trends in voluntary renewable contracts and the potential implications of
new emissions accounting guidance are areas of ongoing research.

Glossary
 Term Definition

 Buyer Broadly refers to any customer who procures voluntary renewable
 power, including residential, commercial, industrial, and governmental
 electricity users

 Compliance Refers to compliance by load-serving entities with state mandates for
 renewable energy procurement (see renewable portfolio standard)

 Community choice Legal entity formed to procure power on behalf of a defined
 aggregation (CCA) geographic area. Some community choice aggregations procure
 voluntary renewable power on behalf of their customers.

 Load-serving entity Suppliers of retail electricity, including utilities, competitive retail
 electricity suppliers (in restructured markets), and community choice
 aggregations

 Participation/sales Participation refers to the number of voluntary customers, and sales
 refers to megawatt-hours (MWh) (renewable energy certificates of
 renewable electricity voluntarily bought and sold).

 Renewable energy Contractual mechanism representing the energy attributes of 1 MWh of
 certificate (REC) renewable electricity generation

 Renewable portfolio State laws requiring load-serving entities to procure a minimum
 standard (RPS) specified quantity of renewable energy

 Voluntary renewable Renewable energy procured by retail electricity customers above what
 power is otherwise provided by load-serving entities to comply with RPS
