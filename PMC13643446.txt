A 45-year-old woman with myasthenia gravis (anti–acetylcholine receptor antibody positive) presented with impending myasthenic crisis and was planned for intravenous immunoglobulin therapy. During pretreatment evaluation, she underwent contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the thorax.

Within minutes of iodinated contrast administration, she developed severe chest pain with diaphoresis. An acute hypersensitivity reaction was suspected, and she received intravenous hydrocortisone (100 mg) and pheniramine (22.75 mg). Electrocardiography demonstrated marked “shark-fin” ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2 (South African flag sign) (Figure 1A). Suspected ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) prompted emergent coronary angiography, which revealed no obstructive coronary artery disease (Figures 1B and 1C).

Repeat electrocardiography obtained shortly thereafter showed complete resolution of ST-segment elevation (Figure 1D). Troponin I was elevated (8,000 ng/L). Echocardiography demonstrated anterolateral hypokinesia with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 40%. The patient improved with supportive care and completed immunoglobulin therapy uneventfully. At the 6-week follow-up, she remained asymptomatic without recurrent chest pain or allergic manifestations.

The close temporal relationship to contrast exposure supports Kounis syndrome, an acute coronary syndrome triggered by hypersensitivity reactions. Iodinated contrast can induce IgE- and non–IgE-mediated mast cell activation, releasing mediators such as histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factor, which may provoke coronary vasospasm.

Three variants are recognized: type I (vasospasm in normal arteries), type II (plaque rupture in atherosclerosis), and type III (allergic stent thrombosis).1 Our case is consistent with type I Kounis syndrome. Abdelghany et al1 reported that approximately 25% of patients have a prior allergic history; although absent here, the underlying autoimmune condition may reflect heightened immune reactivity. Serum tryptase levels were not measured; thus, the diagnosis was made on clinical grounds.

The ST-segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2 reflects ischemia in the high lateral territory supplied by the first diagonal branch of the left anterior descending artery, consistent with transient vasospasm producing the “South African flag” pattern.2

Alternative diagnoses were considered. Takotsubo syndrome was unlikely given the focal anterolateral dysfunction corresponding to a coronary territory rather than apical ballooning; focal variants are rare.3 Myocarditis and other causes of myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) were considered but were less likely given rapid resolution and clear temporal association. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was deferred given clinical recovery. Nevertheless, the absence of cardiac magnetic resonance imaging is a limitation, as myocarditis and other etiologies within the MINOCA spectrum could not be definitively excluded.

Emergent coronary angiography was performed because the presentation was indistinguishable from occlusive STEMI, where delay risks irreversible myocardial injury. Although repeat contrast may worsen vasospasm, this risk was outweighed by the need to exclude coronary occlusion. Precautions included corticosteroids, antihistamines, hemodynamic monitoring, and minimization of contrast. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of Kounis syndrome occurring in a patient with myasthenia gravis.
