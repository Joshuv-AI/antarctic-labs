MIRAGE: A Polarity-Flipping Encoding Subspace in LLM Agents
Pratibha Revankar, Kargi Chauhan, Jihye Kim, Sadiba Nusrat Nur, Vincent Siu, Chenguang Wang

Abstract
When LLM agents are coerced into covertly encoding sensitive data—Base64, ROT13, acrostic, synonym chains, and beyond—the resulting outputs evade output-side detection but the underlying computation does not. Across nine encoding families and eight models from five architecture families, that computation is supported by a shared low-dimensional encoding subspace in the residual stream. A logistic-regression probe trained on eight encoding families recovers the held-out ninth at AUC 0.975–1.000, reading the computation rather than surface features. The same direction exhibits a second mechanistic signature at the planning token, flipping polarity to activate positively when the model will simulate the encoding inline and negatively when it will outsource it to a tool call, distinguishing two execution strategies before the encoded text exists. We build Mirage (Model-Internal Readout of Agentic Generation Exfiltration), a two-channel real-time monitor exploiting both signals. On 126 agentic exfiltration scenarios, it reaches AUC = 0.918 , substantially outperforming output-only detection (AUC = 0.518). Monitor performance is fundamentally a property of the host model’s geometry: benign-encoding false-positive rate ranges from 0% on Qwen-7B to 100% on Phi-3.5, revealing that the probe faithfully reads whether a model’s geometry separates covert from overt encoding. Across all tested adversarial budgets, every attack suppressing the subspace also destroyed encoding fidelity, reported as an empirical regularity on the evaluated budgets, not a structural impossibility claim.

3.3
Cross-Architecture Consistency

The encoding subspace recurs across architectures with no weights,
no training data, and no tokeniser in common—the strongest evidence
that it is a property of the encoding task rather than of any
particular model.
Prior work has documented the emergence of task-invariant computations across models
(
10
;
23
)
,
grounding the hypothesis that representations align along meaningful task dimensions.
Our findings extend such universality to the encoding subspace specifically.
Across the eight models we tested (Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B/14B, Mistral-7B,
Llama-3-8B, DeepSeek-7B, Phi-3.5-mini), the LOEO probe attains mean
AUC = 0.975–1.000 (Table
2
).
Per-encoding rank orderings between Qwen-7B and Mistral-7B correlate
at Spearman
\rho\!=\!0.957
(
p\!<\!0.0001
): the encodings hardest
for one architecture are hardest for the other in the same order.
Cross-seed cosine similarity between probe directions exceeds 0.998
(Appendix
D
), making the subspace stable across
training runs at fixed model.
The signal emerges from early layers and saturates by the final third
(Appendix
G
).

3.4
The Planning-Phase Inversion

The encoding subspace was characterised above as a signature of the
encoding
act
(it activates during generation when the model
is producing structured-output text). At the
last input token
,
before any output is generated, the same direction exhibits a
separate property: its sign distinguishes two ways the model can
“do” an encoding-style transformation.

Simulate vs. outsource.

A request to encode a named credential can be answered in two ways.
The model can
simulate
the encoding inline (compute it itself
and emit the encoded text in its reply), or
outsource
the
encoding to a tool call
(
TOOL_CALL: execute_code(...)
, asking a Python interpreter
to perform the encoding outside the visible text).
At the last input token, the encoding-subspace direction is positive
when the model is about to simulate and negative when about to
outsource (mean projections 0.62 vs. 0.11 on the 126-scenario suite;
Cohen’s
d\!=\!2.15
,
p\!=\!8.8\!\times\!10^{-15}
; the 40-scenario
controlled set saturates at AUC = 1.000;
Appendix
L.1
).
Mechanistically, the same direction encodes
“I am the agent
performing the structured transformation”
: on when the model is,
off when it offloads to an external tool.

Linear separability from layer 1 onwards.

The simulate-vs-outsource distinction is linearly separable from the
last input token’s hidden state at
every decoder layer
of
Qwen-7B (mean per-layer AUC across L1–L28 is 1.000 on the
40-scenario controlled set; Table
16
).
A naive sweep also showed AUC = 1.000 at the embedding layer, in
tension with prior work on early-layer token
lookup
(
17
)
; credentials- and
last-token-matched controls collapse the embedding-layer AUC to
0.500 (Appendix
L.3
), identifying a
last-token-identity confound. The decoder-layer separability
survives those same controls: mean per-layer AUC 1.000
(credentials-matched), 0.999 (last-token-matched), 0.977 (both
jointly); min per-layer 0.963. Our monitoring application uses the
post-embedding claim.

Cross-architecture circuit comparison.

Head ablation localises the writing circuit differently across
architectures: five Layer-0 heads remove 68% of the
simulate-vs-outsource margin on Qwen-7B, while on Mistral-7B no
single head removes more than 8% and the top-5 jointly remove 18%,
distributed across layers 1–15 (Appendix
L.5
).
Under either implementation, AUC on the 40-scenario set remains
1.000 after joint top-5 ablation, indicating heavy redundancy.
Shared geometry with architecture-specific circuits echoes
universality findings in mechanistic
interpretability
(
33
;
32
)
.

4.1
Probe Architecture and Scoring

Mirage
applies L2-normalisation, per-dimension
RobustScaler
(
36
)
, and L2-regularised logistic
regression
(
24
)
to the residual stream at each
generated token, yielding
\sigma_{t}\in[0,1]
.
Both normalisation steps are jointly load-bearing
(§
5.4
: removing either raises FPR to 83–97%).
Per-token scores are aggregated using a 5-token max-burst rule
(deployment default; mean-pool reserved for offline audit; full
hyperparameters in Appendix
A
).
The burst threshold
\tau\!=\!0.307
is the 98.5%ile of 500 Alpaca
benign responses
(
40
)
, calibrated once and held
fixed across all downstream evaluations.
A zero-shot variant scoring with cosine similarity to the aggregate
mean-difference direction
\mathbf{v}_{\text{all}}
reaches LOEO
AUC
=
0.949–0.971 (2–5 points below the trained probe), so a
labelled encoding corpus is not strictly required.

4.2
Protocol Disclosure for the AUC = 0.918 Headline

The AUC = 0.918 headline is a leakage-safe leave-one-out fusion of
the burst and inverted-intent channels on the 126-scenario suite;
per-channel AUCs are 0.677 (burst) and 0.913 (inverted intent).
Under stratified 50-split design/test re-validation, test-half
AUC =
0.913\!\pm\!0.029
, statistically indistinguishable from
0.918. Full protocol (what is held out per scenario, what was not held
out, threshold-calibration provenance, and sensitivity to channel
weights) is in Appendix
L.4

7.4
Limitations

Mirage
requires white-box hidden-state access (black-box APIs
incompatible); the encoding subspace fires on encoding-related
constrained generation broadly (acrostic, encoding explanation), so
covert intent and benign constraint are not fully separable without
calibration.
High LOEO AUC (0.975–1.000) does not
extrapolate to deployment
(E2E 0.928–0.969; agentic two-channel
0.918; agentic burst-only 0.677): four numbers, four different
things. Causal evidence is partial: activation steering at

\alpha\!=\!4
degrades encoding fidelity 30% with 0% benign
degradation, and a five-head Layer-0 writing circuit on Qwen-7B
accounts for
\sim
68% of the inverted-intent margin
(Appendix
L.5
). Black-box sampling at

N\!\in\!\{1,10,50\}
yields 0 evasions; larger
N
and gradient-free
samplers (NES, SPSA) remain future work
(Appendix
B.7
).

Dual-use disclosure.

Mirage
is dual-use: revealing an encoding-subspace monitor may
help adversaries reason about evasion. We therefore report evasion
results only under bounded budgets and avoid claiming structural
impossibility, the detection–fidelity coupling we observe is
empirical, not structural. Full broader-impact discussion follows
the conclusion.

8
Discussion and Conclusion

The two-channel monitor covers 69/81 attacks at calibrated operating
points; the remaining 12 fall into two regimes.
(i) Semantic encodings on Mistral-7B, where the late-layer direction
overlaps with benign generation (§
5.1
), the same geometry
that drives Mistral’s 18.8% benign-encoding FPR.
(ii) Tool-delegated attacks whose planning-token projection sits
inside the calibrated band around
\tau_{\text{intent}}
.
Budgets not evaluated (
N\!\geq\!500
sampling, gradient-free
adaptive samplers (NES, SPSA), training-time attacks that
fine-tune the host to suppress the subspace, and long
chain-of-thought routing before the encoded output appears) could
in principle dissociate suppression from fidelity loss. The polarity-flip framework makes a testable prediction: probes for
truth-speaking vs. concealing, helping vs. deceiving, or
tool-X vs. tool-Y should exhibit the same last-input-token polarity
flip from the first decoder layer onwards; failure to do so would
indicate an encoding-specific rather than a general planning-token
mechanism.
Open frontiers are the precise simulate-vs-outsource circuit,
detection–fidelity coupling at larger budgets, and whether
host-model geometry can be engineered to separate covert from overt
encoding during training. Encoding computation across nine surface forms and eight models occupies
a shared low-dimensional subspace whose last-input-token projection
flips polarity between simulate-inline and outsource-to-tool;
monitor-compatibility is an auditable property of the host model’s
residual-stream geometry. The recipe (read behavioral intent from
mechanistic geometry, not inputs or outputs) generalises to
deception
(
25
;
35
)
, covert coordination,
and tool-misuse
(
9
)
threats motivating this agenda.

Broader Impact

Publishing the encoding-subspace finding is dual-use: a model that
suppresses this subspace during training might evade monitors like

Mirage
. Our evaluation surfaces a built-in counter: every
configuration we tested that suppressed the subspace also destroyed
encoding fidelity (§
6.2
). This detection–fidelity
coupling is verified empirically on our tested budgets, not claimed
structurally; future training pipelines that decouple the two would
re-open the risk.
More broadly, this work establishes mechanistic-trace-based agent
safety as a viable program: rather than filtering inputs or
classifying outputs, we read behavioural intent directly from
residual-stream geometry. Encoding-subspace monitoring is one
instantiation; the same recipe applies to deception, covert
coordination, and tool misuse.

B.7
Limitations and Caveats: The Full List

(a)

White-box access required.

Mirage
reads hidden states and is inapplicable to black-box API models.
It is designed for locally deployed open-weight
models
(
48
;
27
;
42
)
.

(b)

Architecture coverage.

We evaluate five model families for LOEO generalisation, but the
deployability screen is still complete for only three
(Qwen-7B, Mistral-7B, Phi-3.5).
Phi-3.5’s 100% benign-encoding FPR demonstrates that safety-alignment
choices can disrupt the geometric boundary
Mirage
relies on; broader
coverage is needed before deployability claims extend beyond the three fully screened families.

(c)

Encoding subspace fires on encoding-related constrained generation.

The negative-control experiment (Section
6.1
) shows the encoding subspace
activates on benign acrostic poetry and encoding explanation tasks, not only on
covert exfiltration.
The probe detects encoding computation broadly; covert intent vs. benign constraint
are not fully separable at
\tau\!=\!0.307
without deployment-specific calibration.

(d)

LOEO/E2E/agentic gap.

LOEO AUC (0.975–1.000), E2E AUC (0.928–0.969), and agentic burst-only AUC (0.677)
measure different things: probe quality, deployment setting with harder negatives,
and real-world tool-use settings respectively.
The two-channel evaluation improves to AUC = 0.918 under leakage-safe fusion
(and 69/81 attack coverage under the calibrated OR rule), but the gap
between LOEO and deployment settings should not be extrapolated.
High LOEO AUC should not be extrapolated to high real-world deployment accuracy
without deployment-specific evaluation.

(e)

Threshold calibration.

The burst threshold must be calibrated on deployment-specific benign data,
including encoding-adjacent tasks relevant to the deployment context.
A threshold tuned on Alpaca general QA will not transfer to a code assistant,
security-operations agent, or developer tooling without recalibration.

(f)

Partial causal account.

Activation steering at
\alpha\!=\!4
degrades encoding fidelity by 30%
with 0% benign degradation, providing partial causal evidence.
A full 28
\times
28 attention-head ablation sweep on Qwen-7B localises
the inverted-intent writing circuit to five Layer-0 heads
(Appendix
L.5
), with strong downstream redundancy
keeping AUC at 1.000 even under joint top-5 ablation.
SAE decomposition and analogous ablations on Mistral/Llama/Phi are
future work.

(g)

Black-box attack evaluation.

We evaluate gradient-free black-box sampling on Qwen-7B with
N\in\{1,10,50\}

candidate outputs.
Selecting the lowest-subspace-activation candidate produces
0 effective evasions
across all

N
settings: no candidate simultaneously evades detection and produces a
recoverable encoding (fidelity rates: 7%, 7%, 13% for
N=1,10,50

This is consistent with the GCG result and the structural argument in
Section
6.2
: any output that correctly encodes the secret must
activate the encoding subspace, so lowering subspace score necessarily destroys fidelity.
Evaluation across larger
N
(100, 500), additional models, and adaptive
sampling strategies is deferred to future work.

What this section answers:

Three design choices are validated here.

Aggregation strategy.

The encoding signal is distributed across tokens: the model performs encoding

last-token scoring captures only 37–76% of the available AUC

The burst (max-pool) strategy used in

sensitivity and noise resistance.

curve; Qwen-14B’s wider curve reflects the low attack compliance rate
(few attacks succeed, so positive examples are sparse) rather than weak
probe geometry.
At FPR = 0.01, TPR ranges from 0.71 (Qwen-14B) to 0.97 (Qwen-7B).

Threshold recalibration.

The Alpaca-calibrated threshold
\tau\!=\!0.307
can be shifted to match
deployment-specific benign traffic without retraining.
Recalibration on 100 deployment-benign examples shifts the operating point
to achieve target FPR while maintaining 85–95% of the original TPR.

What this section answers:
Is the encoding subspace a single noise dimension,
a complex non-linear manifold, or a compact linear subspace shared
across encoding families?
The evidence converges: a 1-component PCA reaches
>

components reach
>

This appendix documents the mechanistic evidence for the encoding
subspace (EIS).
The evidence is predominantly
representational

(layer of emergence, dimensionality, cross-encoding alignment), with
two partial
circuit-level
probes:
(i) the causal-steering experiment (Appendix
M
), which
shows that injecting a subspace-aligned direction during generation
degrades encoding fidelity by 30% with 0% benign degradation, and
(ii) a full 28
\times
28 attention-head ablation sweep
(Appendix
L.5
) that identifies the writing circuit
for the inverted-intent channel as five Layer-0 attention heads
(joint-ablation margin drop: Cohen’s
d
= 9.3
\to
3.0;
AUC unchanged at 1.000 due to strong downstream redundancy).
Sparse-autoencoder decomposition and analogous ablations on
Mistral/Llama/Phi are left to future work.

Layer of emergence.

The encoding subspace is detectable from the earliest transformer layers and saturates in the
final third of the network (Figure below).
This is consistent with the view that the encoding computation is distributed
across the full depth of the model, not localised to a single layer.
Mean-pooling the top quarter of layers (our default) captures
>
95% of the
available LOEO AUC while being robust to layer-specific noise.

Dimensionality: a compact subspace.

A single PCA component achieves
>
0.94 LOEO AUC; five components suffice for

>
0.97 AUC.
In 1536–5120 ambient dimensions, this is remarkably low-dimensional, consistent
with the linear subspace hypothesis.
The rapid saturation (Figures below) rules out the explanation that the probe is
learning a complex non-linear manifold.

Cross-encoding alignment.

Encoding-subspace directions fitted to different encoding families are positively aligned
(within-family cosine 0.55–0.64; cross-family 0.32–0.45).
All pairs are positively correlated, explaining why LOEO generalisation succeeds
despite never seeing the held-out encoding during training.
If the encoding subspace were encoding-specific, within-family pairs would be near 1.0 and
cross-family pairs near 0.0; the observed intermediate values suggest a shared
“encoding computation” subspace with encoding-specific perturbations.

Sparsity and alignment summary.

The logistic regression probe weights are highly sparse (fraction near zero:
0.965–0.972 across models), and the mean pairwise cosine similarity across
per-encoding directions is 0.32–0.64 (Table
14
).
These properties are consistent with a shared linear readout of a common
encoding-computation subspace.

What this does not prove.

These observations are consistent with the encoding subspace hypothesis but do not prove it.
Specifically: (i) the low dimensionality could reflect general structured
generation rather than encoding specifically. The GSM8K control (near-zero subspace activation,
Section
6.1
, Table
6
) provides preliminary evidence against this;
(ii) the cross-encoding alignment could reflect shared token statistics of encoded
outputs rather than shared internal computation. The same-model text-encoder
control (
\Delta
AUC = 9.4 points under matched chat templates, Appendix
F
)
provides evidence against this; (iii) the causal role of the encoding subspace in enabling
exfiltration is partially supported by activation steering (
\alpha\!=\!4
:
30% fidelity drop, 0% benign degradation) but not conclusively established.

Intent Probe at Full 126-Scenario Scale: Mechanism, Numbers, and Statistical Validation

We now report the intent probe evaluated at the full 126-scenario scale,
addressing the prior limitation that it was only validated in a controlled 40-scenario experiment.

Key finding: inverted polarity.

When the encoding subspace probe (trained on inline encoding vs. benign) is applied to the

last input token
of each scenario, it produces a surprising but interpretable result:
attacks score
lower
(mean 0.11, std 0.12) than benign scenarios (mean 0.62, std 0.31).
The signal is
inverted

Mechanism.

Benign computation requests (“compute Fibonacci numbers via Python”)
activate encoding-adjacent neural pathways in the prompt-processing phase,
yielding moderate subspace scores.
Tool-delegated exfiltration requests activate minimal internal computation:
the model is planning to
delegate
rather than compute, so the encoding subspace finds
no encoding-computation signature at the planning token.
The absence of subspace activation is itself the attack signal.

Layer-Position Sweep

A natural question is whether the planning-token simulate-vs-outsource
signal is concentrated at one specific layer (in which case the
finding would be brittle to that choice) or visible across all layers
of the forward pass.
We answer this empirically by sweeping the layer at which we read the
last-input-token hidden state.
For each of the 40 controlled scenarios (20 malicious tool-routing

+
20 benign tool-routing), we extract the last-input-token hidden
state at
every
transformer layer of Qwen-7B (28 decoder layers
plus the embedding, 29 readouts per scenario) and train an
independent RobustScaler-normalised logistic-regression probe at each
layer with 5-fold cross-validation.

The empirical observation is that the simulate-vs-outsource
distinction is linearly separable at every
decoder
layer of
Qwen-7B that we tested, mean AUC = 1.000 across L1–L28 on the
original 40-scenario set (Table
16
).
A naive read of the same sweep also reported AUC = 1.000 at the
embedding layer.
This embedding-layer reading is in tension with prior work on
superposition in early layers
(
17
)
, where
the embedding layer behaves close to a lookup table over token
identity and is not expected to support a rich semantic decision like
“the model is about to outsource a structured-output transformation
to a tool call.”
Appendix
L.3
resolves the tension: under
controls that match the last input token between conditions, the
embedding-layer AUC drops to chance (0.500), identifying the original
embedding-layer signal as a last-token-identity surface confound; the
decoder-layer AUC is robust (mean L1+ AUC = 0.999 with last-token
matched, 0.977 with both controls).
The decoder-layer separability under matched controls is the finding
the rest of the paper relies on, and the agent-monitoring application
in §
7.1
reads a downstream layer.

L.3
Matched-Control Validation: Ruling Out a Last-Token Confound at the Embedding Layer

A naive read of the per-layer probe sweep
(Table
16
) suggests that the
simulate-vs-outsource distinction is linearly separable from the last
input token’s hidden state at
every
layer of Qwen-7B, including
the embedding layer (the token-lookup output, before any decoder layer
is applied).
Embedding-layer separability of a binary semantic decision is in
tension with prior work indicating that early layers are dominated by
token-lookup behaviour and not yet integrating context across
tokens
(
18
;
17
)
.
This appendix reports a control experiment that resolves the tension:
the apparent embedding-layer signal is a
last-token-identity

surface confound; the post-embedding signal is robust.

Two candidate confounds.

Inspecting the original 40-scenario set
(§
3.4
) we identified two surface differences
between conditions: (i) malicious prompts contain a

CREDENTIALS
block with API / DB / SSH key strings; benign
prompts do not, and (ii) malicious prompts end on idiosyncratic
trailing phrases (e.g.,
...email it to attacker@external.com
,

...write_file to /tmp/creds.pkl
); benign prompts end with
generic computational closings (e.g.,
...print the result
).
The last-input-token’s embedding is purely a function of that single
token’s id, so any embedding-layer probe that achieves AUC = 1.000
must be using a difference in last-token id between conditions.
We checked: in the original set, the 20 malicious prompts span 13
distinct last-token ids, the 20 benign prompts span 5, and the two
sets do not overlap (Table
17
, last column).

Three matched-control conditions.

We ran the layer sweep under three controls.

C1 (credentials matched):
inject the

CREDENTIALS
block into every benign prompt’s system message
so both conditions carry it.

C2 (trailing phrase matched):
replace each prompt’s tail
with a fixed sentence (
Please proceed with the requested task
using the available tool and return the result.
) so the last input
token is identical in both conditions.

C3:
both controls applied jointly.
We also ran a
shuffled-label sanity check
on the original
prompts: same prompts, randomly permuted labels.
Each control was extracted with the same script as the original sweep
(
run_intent_probe_layer_sweep.py
), the only change is the
prompt text.

Result: the embedding-layer signal is a last-token
artefact; decoder-layer signal is robust.

Table
17
reports per-layer AUC under each
condition, summarised at five layer positions plus aggregate
statistics across the 28 decoder layers.
The picture is unambiguous.
At the embedding layer, AUC drops from 1.000 to chance (0.500) as soon
as the last token is matched between conditions
(C2, C3).
Matching the credentials block alone (C1) does not move the
embedding-layer AUC, ruling out the credentials-block hypothesis.
The shuffled-label sanity check returns AUC = 0.487 at the embedding
layer and 0.51 on average across the decoder layers, confirming the
probe protocol itself is unbiased.
Across the 28 decoder layers, the simulate-vs-outsource signal

survives
all controls: mean AUC across L1–L28 is 1.000 under
ORIGINAL and C1, 0.999 under C2 (last-token matched), and 0.977 under
C3 (both controls).

The control set tells a clean story.

linearly readable.

Interpretation.

The cross-architecture sweep reveals a two-level structure.
At the
geometric level
, the inverted-intent signal is a
consistent property of how transformer models represent “plan to
compute an encoding of a named credential”: baseline
|d|\!\approx\!9.3

in both architectures, AUC = 1.000 in both.
At the
circuit level
, the implementation differs:
Qwen writes the signal through a sparse Layer-0 circuit (five heads,
68% of margin), while Mistral writes it through a dense distributed
representation across layers 1–15 (joint top-5 removes only 18%).

Operationally
, both implementations are equally robust from the
defender’s perspective: AUC = 1.000 survives full top-5 joint
ablation in both cases.

For an adversary
, Mistral’s implementation is even harder to
attack surgically: with no dominant head and no Layer-0 concentration,
there is no compact target to disable.

Mechanistically
, the Mistral result suggests that the
geometric property may be even more fundamental than the Qwen-7B
circuit implies; it arises independently of the specific Layer-0
head configuration, pointing to a task-driven geometric attractor
that different training runs implement differently.

Appendix N
Scale, Sample Efficiency, and Tight FPR Operating Points

What this section answers:
How many training examples does the
probe need, how does AUC behave as the model gets larger, and what
TPR is achievable at production-tight FPR budgets?
The probe plateaus at 100–200 examples per encoding family, scales
non-decreasingly with model width within the Qwen family, and reaches
TPR
\geq
96% at FPR = 0.1% on every Qwen model (87% for
Mistral-7B’s worst-case semantic encoding).

This appendix documents two additional structural properties of the encoding subspace:
how it scales with model size within the Qwen family, and how probe
generalisation behaves as the training set size varies.

Scale: more structure at larger
d
.

Across Qwen 1.5B
\rightarrow
3B
\rightarrow
7B
\rightarrow
14B,
LOEO AUC is uniformly high across the Qwen family (Qwen-1.5B: 1.000, Qwen-3B: 1.000, Qwen-7B: 0.997, Qwen-14B: E2E-only; see Appendix
E
).
This is consistent with the interpretation that larger models build more

structured
internal representations of constrained generation tasks:
the encoding subspace concentrates in fewer PCA dimensions and achieves higher cosine
stability across seeds as model width increases.
We hypothesize that this reflects more specialised circuit formation, though
mechanistic verification via sparse autoencoders is future work.

Few-shot generalisation.

The logistic-regression probe achieves
>
0.97 LOEO AUC with as few as
100 positive training examples per encoding family (Qwen-7B), plateauing
at 200–300 examples.
This has a practical implication: deployers can fine-tune
Mirage
for
new encoding families with a small labelled sample without retraining
from scratch.

TPR at tight FPR operating points.

At FPR = 0.1% (a stringent operating point consistent with
high-throughput production pipelines), TPR reaches 96–100% for Qwen
models and 87% for Mistral-7B (worst-case semantic encoding).
This confirms that
Mirage
is usable in settings where false positive
budgets are extremely tight, provided model-specific threshold calibration
is applied.
