 HYDROGEN PRODUCTION FROM HIGH-VOLUME
 ORGANIC CONSTRUCTION AND DEMOLITION WASTES

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) has partnered with Simonpietri
Enterprises, LLC (SEL) with the objective of developing an integrated waste preparation,
gasification, and syngas cleanup process capable of using a high-volume, negative-value, highly
contaminated feedstock consisting of construction and demolition (C&D) waste to economically
produce 99+% pure hydrogen. The target is to achieve $1/kg H2 at a modular scale ranging from
5- to 50-MWe-equivalent size by testing the gasification of organic C&D debris containing treated
waste lumber and construction materials, permanently sequestering contaminants into the ash.
C&D debris waste is one the largest-volume waste streams in the United States; is already
aggregated and available across the country, lending itself to hub operations; and has fewer
alternatives for energy conversion than are available for coal, biomass, and municipal solid waste
because of contamination from treated lumber and other construction materials. The project’s
focus on an integrated gasification process to convert this high-volume waste stream into hydrogen
while safely handling the contamination is unique and innovative.

 Approximately 5500 lb of pelletized feedstock was made from segregated, shredded,
hammer-milled, screened and separated for metals removal and finally pelletized. Three separate
gasification trials were completed. The first gasification campaign was completed during
June 5–9, 2025, for a total of 70 hr of testing, with over 1150 lb of C&D feedstock being fed to
the gasifier. Three different bed additives were tested for their ability to capture the gas-phase trace
species either in the bed material of the collect filter ash cake. The major result was that marginally
stable water–gas shift performance was achieved using both high- and low-temperature sweet shift
catalyst beds. Trace metals such as chromium, copper, and lead stayed in the solid-phase ash.
Control of vapor-phase trace species such as arsenic was over 99% retained in the solid ash
streams, with a small amount measured in the other downstream sorbent and shift catalyst beds.
Boron was retained at about 85% in the solid ash, while only about 50% of the chlorine was
controlled in the solid ash. No trace metals were collected in the either the downstream quench
wastewater or remained in the syngas after the quench. All trace metals and halides were removed
in the fixed bed reactor solids.

 The first two-stage gasifier test was completed during November 13–17, 2025, for a total of
70 hr of fuel feed and utilized approximately 500 lb of feedstock. A single set of fluid-bed gasifier
operating conditions was tested, with only the feedstock additive for trace metal control, entrained
flow gasifier (EFG) gasifier operating temperature, and the EFG additional oxygen flow rate being
varied. The major result was that stable water–gas shift performance was still achieved, while the
CO and H2 concentrations were increased by 90% and 20%, respectively, while the light aliphatic
hydrocarbons were reduced by over 90%. FTIR analysis indicated that the reduction of more stable
aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene, toluene, and xylenes was 60%–75%. Material balance
and analytical results to determine the disposition of the trace metals of concern showed that
essentially no revolatilization of trace metals occurred as they passed through the high-temperature
EFG tar-cracking zone. Trace species that did not appear to be completely captured in the solid
ash include boron and chlorine. The boron was able to be mostly controlled by a commercial arsine

 vi

guard bed material; however, the chlorine appeared to still interact with downstream sorbent and
catalyst beds currently being tested.

 The Linde high-velocity oxygen injector, specifically designed for the EERC EFG, was
installed for the last test campaign. A 5-day test campaign was completed during July 15–21, 2025.
This test generated about 56 hr of operational time on the C&D feedstock, and 528 lb of C&D
feedstock was gasified during the test campaign. The Linde oxygen injector reduced the tars to
less than 20 ppmv and increased the overall carbon conversion to over 99% while not revaporizing
the trace metals captured by the trace metal control bed additives. Some chlorine and boron were
the only trace contaminants observed downstream of the candle filters in any appreciable amounts.
The inclusion of a new commercially available chlorine guard bed resulted in the chlorine capture
necessary to prevent downstream poisoning of the remaining guard beds and shift catalysts, with
the shift catalyst showing no appreciable degradation in performance over the course of the last
test. This testing indicates that C&D debris feedstocks containing treated wood products are a
technically viable feedstock for oxygen-blown fluid-bed gasification to produce hydrogen or other
renewable fuels such as renewable natural gas (RNG) or sustainable aviation fuel. Adequate tar
cracking has been demonstrated utilizing a second-stage partial oxidation reactor.

 Techno-Economic Modeling Results

 SEL (the subawardee for this Cooperative Research and Development Agreement
DE-FE0032183) developed spreadsheet financial models for a notional full-commercial-scale
C&D waste gasification plant to calculate the financial returns to equity investors from a first
commercial project. SEL developed a spreadsheet financial model for a base case full-scale
modular commercial waste gasification plant, a 500 ton/day plant in Kapolei, Hawaii, to produce
industrial hydrogen to sell over the fence to a petroleum refiner for hydrotreating diesel and jet
fuel. The modeled total capital raise necessary for this first plant was $190 million for the full-
scale plant. This model and its subsequent iterations showed that with revenues from waste tipping
fees for the C&D waste, fuel product sales, and two policy supports, the plant was able to cover
all capital, operating, financing, and development costs and achieve bankable investor returns over
15% internal rate of return (IRR) over the 20-year life of the plant. The model showed a net
production cost of hydrogen at $1.98/kg in 2023 dollars, which is above the U.S. Department of
Energy’s Hydrogen Shot target of $1/kg but below the market price for fossil hydrogen derived
from natural gas and delivered by pipeline on the U.S. Gulf Coast that year.

 SEL in conjunction with ethylene glycol EQTEC and Topsoe input also modeled both small-
and large-scale plants producing an RNG. The modeled total capital raise necessary for the
50 ton/day air-blown gasification-to-power plant is $30 million, and total capital raise for each
full-scale plant is $350 million. The RNG model for each location showed a financeable project
with IRR over the 15% hurdle rate, achievable with revenues from tipping fees, RNG sales, excess
electricity sales, and carbon reduction/sequestration tax credits. Financial modeling and
commercialization activities revealed that 1) the concept is feasible and bankable; 2) negative cost
feedstock is critical; 3) pivoting to produce a finished hydrocarbon product is necessary; 4) a large
pilot-scale/demo plant is financially feasible as a commercial operation, but only in Hawaii; and
5) time is of the essence. Modeling showed that project profitability more probable if a given
project can be brought on line by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act deadline of December 31, 2029,
to generate the Advanced Fuel Production Tax Credit.

 Process Engineering Results

 SEL updated our front-end level engineering (FEL)-1 process engineering model developed
in Aspen modeling software with the new information and experimental data generated from the
techno-economic analysis (TEA) and gasification tasks. Using nonfederal cost share for this
project won from the state of Washington, we also engaged vendors EQTEC and Topsoe to
perform a revised FEL-1 feasibility study to produce a finished RNG fuel using the hydrogen from
this envisioned process to hydrotreat syngas. The results from this project were consolidated into
three technical feasibility studies, one for the 50-ton/day pilot plant at FEL-3 front-end engineering
and design level and one for the 500-ton/day commercial-scale plant to make RNG, with site
factors for Honolulu, Hawaii, and Tacoma, Washington.

 Air Emissions Modeling and Permitting Analysis

 For this task, SEL gathered the feedstock profile data and gasification vendor syngas profile
from EQTEC and developed a syngas combustion gas profile and volume factors for air emissions
modeling. The scenario used was for the expected emissions from the 50-ton/day C&D waste
gasification pilot plant, where 100% of the syngas is combusted in a thermal oxidizer and then
emitted to the atmosphere from a plant to be located in Kapolei. SEL then hired a consultant to
perform a new source review air emissions model using the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency
model AERMOD.

 Greenhouse Gas Life Cycle Analyses As modeled in the Greenhouse Gases, Regulated
Emissions, and Energy Use in Transportation model, the carbon intensity (CI) score of the process
was computed using the assumptions data and other proprietary information that were gathered
under other research projects. The cradle-to-gate (CTG) CI result for SEL’s renewable hydrogen
production process is 2.08 gCO2e/MJ, which is much less than the baseline CI for hydrogen
production from natural gas or petroleum of 79.36 and 184.55, respectively. A second life cycle
analysis model for RNG production was performed for three cases plus a baseline. The CTG CI
results range from −8.2 to 15.8 g CO2e/MJ and were compared to the CTG CI results for
conventional natural gas in Washington (65.88 gCO2e/MJ). Displacement of conventional natural
gas results in an emission reduction of 76–113% and an annual reduction of 61,000–91,000 metric
tons of GHG emissions.

 Computational Particle and Fluid Dynamics Modeling

 This task was performed by SEL with support from CPFD Software, Inc and its Barracuda
Virtual Reactor model. Work started by gathering the waste bulk density and particle-size
distribution (PSD) data generated from an analysis of waste feedstock task and the 3D gasification
and hot-gas filtration vessel shape and flow data generated from the TEA. CPFD staff and SEL
technicians built a model based upon the physical dimensions of EQTEC’s 50-ton/day pilot-scale
reactor designed for SEL’s pilot plant in Kapolei. The base case modeled C&D waste gasification
in an oxygen-blown fluid-bed gasification and hot-gas filter vessel system of the waste particle
flows and fluid dynamics, as well as heat and gasification dynamics, to simulate dense-phase fluid
beds and hot-gas filter vessels for 700 s of start-up operations. Due to the cost and complexity of
the modeling effort, the primary accomplishment was development of a base case model for the
gasification reactor.

INTRODUCTION

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) has partnered with Simonpietri
Enterprises, LLC (SEL) with the objective of developing an integrated waste preparation,
gasification, and syngas cleanup process capable of using a high-volume, negative-value, highly
contaminated feedstock consisting of construction and demolition (C&D) waste to economically
produce 99+% pure hydrogen. The target is to achieve $1/kg H2 at a modular scale ranging from
5- to 50-MWe-equivalent size by testing the gasification of organic C&D debris containing treated
waste lumber and construction materials, permanently sequestering contaminants into the ash, and
cleaning the gas into the desired clean hydrogen product, progressing the technology from
technology readiness level (TRL) 3 to TRL5. Activities comprise sampling and preparing real-
world waste; gasifying at laboratory scale 10 times larger than trials conducted on this waste to
date; determining the disposition of key contaminants when gasified—especially arsenic and
chromium as well as sulfur, copper, and boron; applying novel techniques to sequester the
contaminants; analyzing syngas cleanup methods; and performing technical and cost analyses of
alternatives for tar reforming and syngas cleanup using common commercial catalysts or specially
developed sorbents to yield a hydrogen gas compatible with current commercial hydrogen energy
systems.

 C&D debris waste is one of the largest-volume waste streams in the United States; is already
aggregated and available across the country, lending itself to hub operations; and has fewer
alternatives for energy conversion than are available for coal, biomass, and municipal solid waste
because of the contamination from treated lumber and other construction materials. The heavy-
metal loading from concentrations in wood waste as low as 5% chromated copper arsenate (CCA)-
treated lumber is high enough that the entire organic C&D waste stream is turned away from
power, steam, and incinerator applications and instead is sent to special C&D landfills. The focus
on an integrated gasification process to convert this high-volume waste stream into hydrogen while
safely handling the contamination is unique and innovative. This research will help to utilize this
difficult waste stream to increase the sustainability of conversion to clean hydrogen for carbon-
free energy. The novel techniques developed for handling organic C&D waste with tar, heavy-
metal, and sulfur contamination can be applied to other challenging waste streams. This project
will help close specific waste-to-fuel technical gaps and facilitate nimble, locally sourced hydrogen
production opportunities from an existing, aggregated, low-quality feedstock already available
across the country. Proposed outcomes from this project are 1) data development through
integrated laboratory-scale gasification of C&D waste streams and detailed analysis of the
resulting syngas composition and characteristics, 2) engineering analysis of alternatives for gas
cleanup and downstream processing, 3) techno-economic analysis (TEA) for clean hydrogen
production costs, 4) data and report to inform environmental permitting, and 5) technology risk
reduction for commercial development.

 Specific objectives of the first test campaign were to determine the effectiveness of various
C&D debris components (such as drywall sheet) on their ability to capture the trace metals of

 1

 concern. The EERC team has also identified both calcium- and magnesium-based compounds have
 the potential for capturing both arsenic and boron trace metals to form stable solid species.
 Campaign 1 testing was conducted to identify the separate impact presented by utilizing both high-
 calcium as compared to high-magnesium bed additives.

 Task 2.0 – Feedstock Preparation and Processing Optimization

 The objectives of Task 2.0 were to ensure the waste material will be collected, characterized,
and prepared for use as a feedstock in Task 3.0. Characterization and optimization were performed
to convert the C&D waste into a form that can be reliably fed into gasifiers.

 Under Task 2.0, West Oahu Aggregate delivered four truckloads with a total of 12.5 tons of
C&D debris to Island Recycling Inc. (IRI). There, SEL personnel used IRI equipment to sort waste
into the following categories: metals, glass, ceramics, soil, clean and dirty wood, green waste
(GW), cardboard, gypsum, non-polyvinyl chloride (PVC) plastics, PVC, etc. The waste was then
mixed on the tipping floor and shredded on IRI’s 15-ton/hr scrap metal hammer mill to a −4-in.
particle size then screened through magnet and eddy current separators to remove ferrous and
nonferrous metal. Twenty 4-ft × 4-ft × 5-ft shipping boxes were filled and loaded into a 40-ft
container for shipping to Washington State University (WSU). The rest of the C&D material was
discarded to IRI’s automobile shredder residue line. SEL personnel flew to Pullman, Washington,
and spent a week with WSU personnel processing half of the feedstock (ten boxes) containing
4760 lb of C&D waste via a 2-in. shredder, metal screening, hammer mill, and pelletizer into a
½-in. pellet of −1-mm-particle-size C&D fluff. Half of this C&D feedstock was doped with CCA-
treated plywood to boost the arsenic to over 150 mg/kg. The resulting 3100 lb of C&D pellets was
boxed for shipping to the EERC, and pellet samples were sent for proximate, ultimate, elemental,
and ash fusion analyses.

 Half of the feedstock was saved for a second pelletizing campaign in case further separation
and processing would be required. Upon successful initial gasification trial testing, the second
batch of feedstock was processed in a similar manner as the first to make the remaining gasifier
feedstock. Research partners at WSU shear-shredded, hammer-milled, and pelletized another
10 supersacks of real-world Hawaii C&D waste feedstock. This batch of waste was doped with
additional CCA-treated lumber to approach the long-run average arsenic level for C&D waste. A
total of 2400 lb of CCA-doped pellets was produced and packed in two supersacks for shipping
and arrived at the EERC for further gasification testing. Photographs from the C&D debris
feedstock preparation process are shown in Appendix B.

 Because of the small scale of the EERC’s K-Tron twin screw feeder and the single screw
inclined delivery auger, the EERC was required to process the pellets quickly through a hammer
mill to gently reduce the pellet size without completely breaking the pellets back into a low-density
fluff material. Occasionally, larger-than-desired residual pellets would make it into the feeder,
resulting in a few feeder plugging issues in subsequent gasification trials. Larger-scale commercial
plants will have much larger clearances through the feed system and would not face the occasional
pellet feeder issues encountered as part of this project.

 Task 3.0 – Gasification Testing with C&D Waste Feedstocks

 The objective of Task 3.0 focused on utilizing an oxygen-blown fluid-bed gasifier (FBG)
for converting nonhomogeneous C&D waste into syngas while dealing with inorganic
contaminants including trace metals of concern associated with treated wood. By utilizing
numerous syngas cleanup hardware colocated with the EERC’s FBG, numerous syngas cleanup

 3

possibilities can be integrated for rapidly screening control options. Previous EERC gasification
testing with biomass materials, including one U.S. Department of Agriculture-funded Small
Business Innovation Research test campaign utilizing some surrogate C&D debris, always
exhibited significant levels of tar production that would foul the heat-exchange surfaces in the
quench/water condensate system, leading to eventual system plugging. The previous surrogate
C&D feedstock testing exhibited greater than 99.5% capture of the trace metals such as arsenic
with the addition of selected in-bed sorbent material but also exhibited degraded performance of
sour shift catalyst possibly due to chlorine, arsenic, and possibly boron poisoning of the shift
catalyst. In the case of sour shift catalyst, lower than expected H2S levels in the syngas also could
have reduced sour shift catalyst performance.

 For this project, the EERC gasification trials were conducted in three separate 5-day test
campaigns with the scope of adding some tar cracking capabilities to the process during the second
and third campaigns. Preliminary investigation of tar cracking options weighed the likely success
of utilizing tar cracking catalyst versus the utilization of a high-temperature oxygen-injected
second stage for cracking the tars. Based on the perceived issues with tar cracking catalysts being
poisoned with contaminants such as chlorine, sulfur, and now even rarer trace metals such as
arsenic, boron, and chromium, the EERC felt that the second-stage option was the more prudent
option to pursue. Some of the FBG vendors such as SunGas Renewables (with the use of the
commercially demonstrated Linde high velocity oxygen injector and EQTEC are also pursuing the
high-temperature second-stage tar cracking option for their commercial systems as well.

 Since the EERC also has an entrained-flow gasifier (EFG) colocated with the FBG, which
could be utilized as part of this project, the use of a second-stage oxygen-injected second-stage
cracker could be accomplished fairly easily. The major concern with the utilization of a high-
temperature second stage was whether any of trace metals that had been captured in the proprietary
bed additives would be rereleased as the gasifier fines passed through the high-temperature zone.

 The EERC scope of work for the first test campaign task involved setting up the fluidized-
bed gasifier for operation at the desired operating pressure and temperature, including selected
sorbents and catalysts for fixed-bed reactor testing and potential alternate bed materials that may
provide some additional trace metal capture or even tar cracking capabilities. Since the production
of renewable hydrogen is the end product, the fixed beds were set up with the appropriate sorbents
and shift catalysts for downstream testing for hydrogen production. The process flow diagram for
Test Campaign 1 planned fixed-bed setup for the test runs is shown in Figure 1. Since the feedstock
sulfur would be too low to use a sour shift catalyst, the syngas was shifted utilizing both high- and
low-temperature sweet shift catalyst beds. Sulfur removal was accomplished utilizing
commercially available sulfur sorbents manufactured by Clariant. An additional polishing sulfur
sorbent was utilized to remove residual sulfur to well less than 1 ppm before entering the sulfur-
sensitive high- and low-temperature shift beds. These shift beds are utilized for maximum
hydrogen production. In this scenario, the shifted syngas was sent directly to additional quench
pots for final unreacted steam and tar removal before being sent to an existing Selexol™ solvent
column for final acid gas (CO2) removal. Appendix A provides a detailed description of the
equipment utilized to complete the C&D waste feedstock testing.

 These feedstocks would be characterized as being high in volatile matter while relatively
low in moisture, fixed carbon, and ash (although this may change as a more landfill-generated
feedstock is utilized. The high volatile content leads to more organic tar production in a stand-
alone FBG. The carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, and heating value all fall in a range similar to low-
rank coal. The sulfur content is similar to sulfur content for a number of low-sulfur coals; however,
most of this sulfur is thought to be present as residual gypsum wallboard (CaSO4). The chlorine
content is on the high side for most fossil fuel-based feedstocks, presumably because of the
presence of high chlorine containing plastics such as residual PVC piping. Post-gasification testing
discussions feel that upfront processing to remove high chlorine feedstock constituents such as
PVC pipe is not really feasible and that gasification will have to deal with the chlorine contaminant.
PVC pipe itself contains 60+% chlorine and leads to potential catalyst contamination. The trace
metals of chromium, copper, and arsenic (CCA-treated wood) concentrations are all about 100–
200 times higher in C&D waste than most coals, and the boron (borate-treated wood for termite
control) was 500–600 times higher than coal. Table 4 shows the bulk chemistry of each of the

 Tables 5–7 show the order of the sorbent and catalyst utilized in each fixed-bed reactor for
Test Campaigns 1, 2 and 3, respectively. In Campaign 1, Fixed Bed 1 was loaded with sized

material in a fixed-bed configuration was selected to potentially capture any residual As, B, and
Cl that escape capture in either the bed material or the fine filter ash cake. The second bed was
loaded with K8-11 arsine guard sorbent for sour shift catalyst to act as a guard bed. Potential
elimination of this more expensive sorbent bed could result in a significant economic
improvement. Commercially available sulfur removal sorbents allow for the removal of the sulfur
without the need for cooling the syngas to remove moisture and tars instead of utilizing a much
less efficient cold sulfur removal process such as Selexol or Rectisol® followed by syngas
reheating and steam generation/injection before the sweet shift reactor beds. A final sulfur
polishing sorbent was also utilized to reduce the sulfur content to less than 0.1 ppmv. An iron-
based high-temperature sweet shift followed by copper-based low-temperature sweet shift catalyst
beds were utilized to maximize hydrogen production via water gas shifting. These sweet shift
catalysts are sensitive to various contaminants such as sulfur and chlorine. A thin-layer sulfur-
sensitive chlorine sorbent was utilized upstream of the sweet shift catalyst beds in Test
Campaigns 1 and 2 to protect against any chlorine breakthrough to the shift catalyst.

 After it was determined that the FB1 loaded with Additive 3 did not perform very well, it
was felt the additive would work better with a heat pretreatment step before utilization. The
second test utilized a preheat-treated version of the same material, which performed quite well at
reacting with the arsenic (no arsenic was present downstream). But while it had better chlorine
capture performance, it still could not achieve acceptable chlorine control. In addition, the
preheat-treated additive interacted with available steam and carbon dioxide to set up a porous
cementitious matrix that had to be chiseled out of the fixed-bed reactor after the test campaign.
The issues with controlling the chlorine led the project to pursue a commercially available
chlorine sorbent for inclusion in FB1 for Test Campaign 3. Since this chlorine sorbent material
was not sulfur sensitive, so it could be in the first fixed bed to limit the chlorine penetration to
the other sorbent and catalyst beds.

 Wet syngas was then passed through an indirect contact condensation train for the removal
of moisture and any condensable tars. This condensate was collected for subsequent trace meals
and total organic carbon (TOC) analyses. The Selexol gas-sweetening column was also operated
on the cold syngas to determine removal efficiencies for CO2, any trace levels of H2S, and trace
metals associated with treated wood. Acid gas performance was also evaluated at lower operating
pressures than typical for a Selexol-type solvent system, resulting in some drop-off in column CO2
capture performance. Extractive U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Method 29
analyses for trace metals including chromium, copper, chlorine, and arsenic, as well as boron, were
performed to verify that all trace metals of concern were removed from the syngas.

 C&D feedstock gasification testing was completed in three separate test campaigns over
5 days each in a continuous 24/7 test campaign. A total of 70 hours of C&D debris fuel feed was
achieved in the first campaign. A total of 1150 lb of C&D feedstock with additives was gasified
over the 5 days of testing. Two different real-world C&D debris feedstocks provided by SEL (real-
world Hawaii C&D and the same doped with CCA-treated lumber) were utilized as part of the
project testing. During the second test campaign, a total of 68 hours of fuel feed was achieved with
over 500 lb of C&D feedstock being gasified during this 5-day trial. The third gasification trial
was operated with over 56 hours of fuel feed with approximately 528 lb of C&D feedstock being
gasified.

 Other variables tested especially during Test Campaign 1 included gasifier bed temperatures
of 1450°–1475°F and moderate bed temperatures of <1500°–1580°F (800°–860°C) during steady-
state gasification operations. These was investigated to determine what temperature the bed ash
chemistry would allow without bed agglomeration. Syngas major and minor gas composition was
monitored in real time using laser gas analyzers. In addition, to determine how water-soluble the
C&D trace metals were, process water condensate was sampled at multiple locations, including
quenched water samples collected from the quench train, plus around the sample conditioners
utilized around the fixed-bed reactors.

 Bed Agglomeration Issues

 Some bed agglomeration issues were observed over the first test campaign. Previous results
 with a simulated C&D feedstock showed the formation of potassium silicates as the predominate
 agglomerate formation mechanism. Testing during this campaign with actual C&D feedstock
 obtained from a landfill did not show any evidence of a potassium-based eutectic but rather more
 of a sodium-based eutectic based on scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis. Figure 3 is a
 photograph of a line scan taken across an agglomerate collected after Test 2. Figure 4 shows the
 analytical results from this line scan as a function of the position across the agglomerate.

 As can be seen, the level of sodium makes a small increase as it crosses some of the
amorphous areas that hold individual bed material particles together. The use of an aluminosilicate
material such as kaolinite has been demonstrated to react with the sodium alkali (and potassium)
species to make higher-melting-temperature sodium aluminosilicates as opposed to the lower-
melting-temperature sodium silicates. A small amount of kaolinite was added to the feedstock to
avoid potential bed agglomeration issues.

 The gas analysis shows that, in general, the H2 and CO increased significantly, and the
methane and hydrocarbons decreased going through the second stage. This was due to the tar
cracking plus the further conversion of the residual carbon in the cyclone fines. The nitrogen also
increased across the second stage from the additional nitrogen purges utilized in the EFG for the
internal electrical heaters and the electrical feedthroughs. Additional examination of the operating
data suggests that the improved hydrogen yield could be seen with these feedstocks as the
equivalence ratio shifts closer to an operating range around 3 (i.e., if the gasifier is operated more
substoichiometrically); however, this would result in more tar formation as well.

 The equivalence ratio is defined as the actual weight ratio of fuel to oxygen divided by the
fuel-to-oxygen weight ratio at stoichiometric conditions. The fuel-to-oxygen ratio at
stoichiometric conditions is determined from the fuel analysis and is the amount of oxygen
required to fully oxidize the carbon, hydrogen, and sulfur minus the weight of the inherent oxygen
present in the fuel:

 Ø = (wt fuel/wt oxygen)actual/(wt fuel/wt oxygen)stoich [Eq. 1]

 Where:
 Ø> 1 is reducing gasification conditions
 Ø=1 is stoichiometric combustion
 Ø<1 is combustion with excess oxygen present
 1/Ø*100 percent of stoichiometric oxygen provided to the gasifier

 23

 A lower equivalence ratio means the gasifier is operating closer to stoichiometric oxygen
combustion, thus resulting in more carbon dioxide formation at the expense of both CO and H2
production. Finally, the gas compositions show a significant amount of carbon dioxide remaining
in the sweet gas exiting the acid gas solvent column. Selexol solvent performance is known to
increase with increasing column operating pressure, suggesting that, commercially, the acid gas
removal process such as Selexol or a pressure swing adsorption system should be used after any
syngas compression that might be part of the commercial process.

 Table 15 shows the normalized syngas compositions at five locations and shows the
calculated syngas compositions on a nitrogen-free basis since the nitrogen content can be variable
especially when the EFG is bypassed. This nitrogen-free composition would be more
representative of a commercial-scale syngas composition.

 Tables 16 and 17 show the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) analysis utilizing a heated cell
completed around the EFG tar cracker for Test Campaigns 2 and 3, respectively.
Test Campaign 2 data show the results from the FTIR extractive analysis. The FTIR sampled hot
syngas from the FBG outlet and then from the EFG outlet both when no O2 flow and then some
O2 flow was occurring while utilizing the low-velocity annular injector. The FTIR data seem to
show the number of aliphatic organics tended to drop by 80%–90%, while the more stable aromatic
hydrocarbons were reduced by 60%–80% after passing the second-stage high-temperature tar
reformer. The level of tar still making it through the second stage was higher than those typically
observed from the EFG operating in normal operation with all the oxygen flow entering the top of
the EFG injection nozzle. Most of the ash collected in the bottom of the EFG remained as fly ash
with minimal slag formation unlike a standard EFG test where most of the ash is recovered as a
slag. These observations indicate the internal temperatures were considerably cooler than during
the normal operation of the EFG where the oxygen is only injected into the EFG injector. Higher
internal temperatures would result in more tar conversion. These data show the level of trace
organic species including benzene, toluene, and xylene being cracked across the high-temperature
partial oxidation zone. Harder-to-crack aromatics were reduced from hundreds or even thousands
ppm down to single or double digits. The lack of the intense mixing with this injector seems to
indicate that some of the organic tars are slipping through the hot zone without cracking; however,
this test campaign showed that the decomposition of any captured trace metals was very low. This
result led to the project installing the Linde high-velocity oxygen injector for the third test
campaign. This intensely mixed oxygen stream has been demonstrated commercially and was
sized down for the EERC EFG-scale system.

 Table 18 FTIR data showed that the tar cracking was much more complete with very little
organic tars including aromatic surviving the second-stage tar cracker even when operating the
EFG at a lower temperature. The additional carbon conversion was also significant as the filter ash
carbon dropped from the 40% range to less than 10%. Calculated carbon conversion increased
from the low-to-mid 90s to over 99%. Overall, the Linde oxygen-injection system provided a high
level of tar conversion with decomposing the captured trace metals passing through the high-
temperature tar cracker.

 Impact of Additives and Sorbents on Control of Trace Elements from C&D
 Feedstock Gasification

 Solid samples were sent for analysis to determine how the trace metals dispersed in the
residual solids’ streams. Key variables investigated included eight different bed materials, three of
which are common commercial gasification bed materials such as olivine, silica sand, and alumina.
Three bed materials are proprietary fused minerals provided by Washington Mills, including
magnesium-rich forsterite plus two proprietary novel bed materials not previously tested in
fluidized-bed gasification. These bed materials were tested for their ability to limit the
volatilization of the trace metals to the gas phase, tie the trace metals into a stable solid phase, and
maintain stable bed fluidization and syngas flow and filtration.

 Several recovered streams included recovered bed material, EFG slag pot ash, filter fly ash
solid materials, and catalyst materials, plus water samples from the both the quench system and
the sample conditioners used to condense moisture and tars from the syngas samples being sent to
analyzers. Solid samples from the EFG sample conditioner inlet filter and the slag pot sample were
composites acquired over the duration of the two-stage tar cracking testing since no continuous
ash-removal systems are in these ash recovery areas. The recovery levels or lack thereof in water
and catalyst samples collected downstream of the candle filter system were much more reliable in
determining the level of trace metal capture.

 29

 Tables 19–23 tend to show that the arsenic and boron are somewhat more concentrated in
the filter ash since the lower filter operating temperatures are more conducive to forming stable
trace metal species. The chlorine and sulfur seem to be much more concentrated in the finer filter
ash, presumably because a lot more of the critical atoms in the additive also reports to the filter
ash. Chromium, copper, nickel, zinc, and lead seem to moderately concentrate in the filter ash as
well.

 Analysis of catalyst samples in Tables 24–27 shows the level of trace metal contamination
reaching each of the fixed beds’ solid samples for Test Campaigns 1, 2 and 3.
Test Campaign 1 results in Table 24 show that chlorine, arsenic, and boron did not get significantly
captured by the same additive added to the feedstock. Chlorine, arsenic, and boron mostly were
captured by other downstream beds. Test Campaign 2 utilized a preheat treated or “calcined”
version of the same additive, and the data in Tables 25 and 26 show the capture of chlorine, arsenic,
and boron increased significantly. This feedstock additive as it passes through the high-
temperature gasifier apparently gets the preheat treatment it needs to make the additive very
reactive with the trace metals being captured. Since no arsenic was captured in the downstream
arsine guard bed, these data also provide further proof that this additive reacts almost completely
with arsenic but also can capture a significant amount of the chlorine and boron. The only trace
species that appeared to make it through the candle filter system as a vapor-phase constituent were
some of the chlorine and the boron. The Test Campaign 3 sorbent/catalyst data shown in Table 27
indicate the commercial chlorine sorbent captured most of the chlorine and had elevated levels of
boron, but a significant amount of boron was still reaching the downstream arsine guard bed plus
the sulfur polishing bed. The new chlorine sorbent guard bed seemed to capture the chlorine before
it could poison the other downstream beds. The boron seemed to concentrate toward the top of the
chlorine guard, the arsine guard bed, and the sulfur polishing guard bed, indicating that it was not
being consistently removed in any particular area of the gasifier or syngas cleanup train. The gas-
phase boron penetration into the catalyst beds is thought to be due to the formation of a reduced
diborane species. Very little boron was retained in the shift catalyst bed. This was the first test
where the shift catalyst performance did not show any degradation over the duration of the test.

 Tables 28–30 show the analysis of untreated quench water samples and sample conditioner
condensate water from the three test campaigns, respectively. Table 28 data on untreated quench
water shows that the only contaminates reaching the quench system downstream of the fixed
reactors are ammonia and organics (TOCs). Sample conditioner water shows from upstream of the
fixed beds shows some arsenic, chlorine, and boron along with the ammonia and TOC in the
untreated wastewater. Table 29 sample conditioner water taken from inlet and outlet of the tar
cracker shows significant reductions in the arsenic, copper, lead, and TOC, while the chlorine,
boron, and ammonia did not undergo any significant capture. Table 30 shows the trace metals
recovered in the sample conditioner water from right upstream of the EFG second-stage tar cracker
and upstream of the fixed-bed reactors after the candle filter system particulate control device
(PCD). Some trace metals are present upstream of the EFG, but except for the chlorine and boron,
very low concentrations are downstream of the PCD. This is consistent with the catalyst analysis
data. The drop in the TOC concentration is also consistent with a very high level of tar cracking
across the second-stage tar cracker. The untreated quench water analysis from after the second-
stage tar cracker shows that the process water is very clean with almost no trace metals, including
no chlorine or boron and very little organic carbon, indicating the process wastewater will be easily
treated for discharge or reuse.

 Task 4.0 – Techno-Economic Analysis

 The objective of Task 4.0 focused on scale-up of performance data to a 5- to 50-MWe-scale
hydrogen production plant, which was engineered to conduct a TEA to determine the hydrogen
production costs as a function of engineering and commercial parameters as well as cost estimates
for construction, operation, and financing.

 Significant accomplishments for Task 4.0 during the reporting period include the following:

 Subtask 4.1 – Techno-Economic Analysis Modeling

 Scope of Work

 The original scope of work for SEL was to develop a spreadsheet model for a notional full-
scale modular commercial waste gasification plant, delivering one iteration by the end of each
budget period to inform the EERC’s go/no-go milestone decisions. During the 3-year period of
performance of this project, SEL completed the first model, a 500-ton/day plant sited in Kapolei,
Hawaii, to produce industrial hydrogen for sale over the fence to a petroleum refiner for
hydrotreating diesel and jet fuel. SEL continued to refine and iterate the model quarterly, then
amended it to focus on production of a finished pipeline-quality synthetic renewable natural gas
(RNG) fuel when the hydrogen-only business model failure to achieve commercial viability. With
data from this project and staff time funded by a U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Bioenergy
Technologies Office Small Business Innovation Research (SBIR) (Award DE-SC0022775), SEL
then developed two additional models, one at 50-ton/day pilot-scale in Kapolei (Honolulu) and
another 500-ton/day plant in Tacoma, Washington.

 The TEA contained the following outputs:

 1. Financial and investor return of a 20-year business at full commercial scale
 2. Total capital costs of the proposed process
 3. Annual operating costs of the proposed process
 4. Minimum fuel selling price and/or cost of goods of the proposed process
 5. Sensitivity analysis of key assumptions
 6. Technology readiness/risk analysis of process blocks and components

 Inputs to this TEA included:

 1. Engineering parameters: technology type, scale, throughput, conversion rates,
 equipment list, operating conditions, balance of plant, utilities, technology readiness
 level for this waste stream, and effluent rates and types.

 2. Cost estimates for construction and operation: land, equipment, utilities, consumables,
 waste/effluent disposal, coproducts, and permitting.

 42

3. Commercial parameters: feedstock provider, offtake customer, site provider, equipment
 vendor, manufacturing origin and logistics cost, market trends and competing markets,
 renewable fuel and carbon intensity (CI) reduction credit amounts and prices, labor rates,
 utility rates, construction cost rates, transportation modes, regulatory jurisdiction, and tax
 jurisdiction for a notional U.S. plant

Final commercial scenario:

• Location: 5-acre intensive industrial-zoned parcel in Campbell Industrial Park intensive
  industrial zone in Kapolei, Hawaii.

• Waste scale: 1200 tons/day of C&D waste delivered to the plant for disposal via truck on
  the existing heavy-duty trucking road/highway system in Honolulu.

• Waste customer: C&D waste hauling companies in commercial contracts; city and county
  of Honolulu environmental services on municipal contracts.

• Feedstock scale: Of the 1200 tons/day of waste, 500 tons/day is separated and prepared
  into gasification-ready feedstock and stored for gasification in enclosed silos.

• Fuel product: Synthetic RNG at 97% purity and meeting the specifications for injection
  into the Hawaii Gas transmission pipeline.

• Fuel customer: Regulated gas utility.

• Self-power and self-steam: Use an air-blown module to produce syngas and cogenerate
  power and steam for the process, with supplemental fuel for cogeneration from liquefied
  petroleum gas.

• Self-support for utilities: Have an air separation unit and cooling plant for oxygen and
  nitrogen production and syngas cooling on the premises.

• Finished product transport: Synthetic RNG with up to 15% hydrogen is compressed to
  500 psi and injected into a pipeline spur installed at the plant fence line by the gas utility,
  Hawaii Gas.

• Finished product storage: We do not plan to store hydrogen on-site. Hawaii Gas’s existing
  synthetic natural gas pipeline already provides 15 days of storage on average, and
  Honolulu is a stable year-round market with little seasonal variation.

• Water supply: We will use a combination of brackish groundwater drawn from a well for
  cooling water and reverse osmosis-treated wastewater from the Honolulu wastewater
  treatment plant for Kapolei. This is typical (and mandated) for industrial water consumers
  in Campbell Industrial Park.

 From January to March also under approved pre-award, SEL personnel began Iteration 1 of
the TEA and spreadsheet financial model as well as updating the project development timeline for
a notional commercial-scale 500-standard ton/day C&D waste-to-hydrogen gasification plant in
Kapolei, Hawaii.

 This first-of-a-kind (FOAK) commercial-scale plant’s nameplate capacity is 22,100 kg/day
(22.1 metric ton [MT]/day) of hydrogen, based upon 600 dry standard tons/day (dtpd) of 15%
moisture content wood processed via gasification and water–gas shift reaction to produce
22,800 kg/day of 97% purity hydrogen. For average daily production, the modeled base case
estimate is 18.43 MMscfd of 97% H2 from 500 dtpd of C&D waste, which equals 17 MT/day of
pure H2. Average daily production is assuming 86% availability or 2683 operating hours/year for
the integrated plant from Year 3 onward. Approximately 7% of the H2 is produced from the plastic
waste that is commingled with the biogenic waste; therefore, the greenhouse gas life cycle analysis
is revised upward for the total H2 CI. The H2 production cost estimate for the FOAK C&D waste
gasification modeled is $1.98/kg. This is the fully loaded production cost including development
capital, equipment and construction, engineering, working capital, fixed and variable operating
expenses, major turnaround “refurbishment” every 10 years, and a 15+% return on investment to
equity investors. It assumes tipping fee revenue from the waste of $100/ton, $50/MT carbon
credits, reverse osmosis water consumption of 2.8 gal/1 kg of hydrogen, and the $3.00/kg federal
Inflation Reduction Act Production tax credit for hydrogen for the first 10 years of production.
This estimated cost per kg H2 does NOT include nondilutive capital from grant funding, state,
local, or other statutory incentives and does NOT include other coproduct revenue from renewable
natural gas sales, food-grade carbon dioxide sales, green cement, or green concrete materials.
Major licensed equipment providers for this plant provided front-end loading (FEL)-0- and FEL-
1-level cost and equipment estimates for this baseline financial model. Major equipment from
these providers include a 600-dtpd gasifier (in 200-dtpd gasification modules) from Omni
Conversion Technologies, 50-dtpd biomass gasifier from EQTEC PLC, shift reactors from
Topsoe, and acid gas removal (CO2, H2S) from Johnson Matthey.

 A sensitivity analysis of the project’s rate of return to investors for this baseline TEA is
shown in the tornado chart in Figure 6. In this sensitivity analysis, 11 key assumptions in the
financial model were varied up or down by 10% each, and the resulting change to the FOAK
plant’s internal rate of return was plotted from most to least impacted. This sensitivity analysis is
then used for management decisions focused on reducing cost and increasing profitability for each
of the major assumptions for the model. The single most impactful factor on plant profitability was

air emissions model and prepared a preliminary air permit application for a first commercial

 The project design is based on lab-/pilot-scale testing of the gasification of nonhazardous

contaminant emission assumptions drawn from the feedstock and vendor data and models.

 The AQIA and non-source pollution (NSP) application is for the gasification of separated

 Mineralized Carbon Sequestration Assumptions

 All three RNG LCA cases include an emission credit for carbon sequestration in the ash.
The gasiﬁcation process produces approximately 11% ash from the waste biomass feedstock.
Based upon physical testing on gasiﬁcation of real-world waste over 2020–2025, this ash contains
10%–30% stable (aka, fixed) carbon. The ash will be landﬁlled in a Resource Conservation and
Recovery Act D anaerobic landﬁll. Fixed carbon is “mineralized” and cannot be consumed or
decomposed by anaerobic microbes. Therefore, storage in the landﬁll constitutes valuable long-
term carbon storage.

generated from the analysis of waste feedstocks in Task 2.0 and the 3D gasification and hot-gas

reactor designed for SEL’s pilot plant in Kapolei, Hawaii. The base case modeled C&D waste

and hot-gas filter vessels for 700 s of steady-state operation. Due to the cost and complexity of the
modeling, the primary accomplishment was development of a base-case model for the gasification

• Windbox and distributor nozzle internals were removed to avoid excessive mesh density;

Assumptions, boundary conditions, and reaction setup:

• Air-distributor nozzles were not explicitly modeled; used distributed point-source

• Uniform airflow distribution implemented initially, with flexibility to update.
• Nozzle jet-hole geometry derived from visual references; orientations treated as
  adjustable parameters for future refinement.
• Cyclone separation not modeled directly; instead, an imposed ash-recycle inlet replicates
  the expected cyclone return rate and momentum.
• Returning ash composition assumed identical to gasifier-outlet ash due to limited
  composition-specific recovery data.
• Initial bed inventory, PSD, and material chemistry set based on engineering judgment;
  calibrated values will be added when plant information is available.
• Reaction kinetics implemented using representative biomass gasification, tar-reforming,
  combustion, and calcination pathways from literature.
• Model designed to allow modification of reaction sets, addition of catalytic pathways, or
  fuel-blend-specific chemistry.
• Boundary condition framework supports rapid scenario changes (steam-to-air ratio, bed
  height, feed rate, recycle streams, etc.).

• Simulation executed using an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU hardware, requiring

 EERC EQUIPMENT DESCRIPTION

 Description of Equipment

 The Energy & Environmental Research Center (EERC) has a vast array of gasification,
syngas cleanup, and syngas conversion technology platforms that are all similarly scaled and
colocated that can be utilized for this project. These include a fluid-bed gasifier (FBG) adjacent to
an entrained-flow gasifier (EFG). These gasification systems are integrated with syngas cleanup
systems including warm-gas candle filters for particulate control, heated fixed-bed systems for
water–gas shifting or impurity removal, water-cooled quench pots for water and tar removal, and
a solvent-based acid gas removal system for precombustion carbon capture. The EERC also has a
fixed-bed Fischer–Tropsch (FT) reactor system with a tail gas compressor for recycling tail gas to
the reactor. All equipment has been operated in various integrated configurations for previous Test
Campaigns. The availability of this test equipment will enable the EERC to conduct these highly
integrated tests with minimal equipment modifications, major project expenses, or supply chain
delivery delays. A detailed description of this equipment is given below.

 Fluid-Bed Gasifier

 The EERC’s pressurized FBG is designed to operate at temperatures as high as 1800°F at
pressures of 300 psig, with higher pressures possible at lower operating temperatures. Nominal
feed rate is less than 15 lb/hr. The high-temperature metal alloy reactor is electrically heated to
minimize heat losses and operate adiabatically. A design drawing of the main reactor is shown in
Figure A-1. The effective height of the 3-in.-diameter portion of the bed is approximately 5 feet,
with an overall height including the larger-diameter freeboard of ~8.5 feet. The reactor uses an
auger feed port to push fuel feed into the gasifier immediately above the distributor plate and
nozzle assembly. A cyclone and standpipe are used to recycle larger solids exiting the top of the
freeboard back to the bottom of the gasifier using a horizontal auger. Solid samples can be
collected at various locations, and a material drain port is located at about the 5-foot elevation for
bed inventory control. Because of the small scale of this unit, extra gas is required to maintain
fluidization velocity, and the system uses recycled syngas to allow for independent control of the
fluidization velocity while maintaining an appropriate oxygen and steam-to-carbon ratio.

 Entrained-Flow Gasifier

 Figure A-2 shows cross-sectional and pictorial views of the EFG. The EFG is a down-fired
system rated to 300 psig at internal temperatures of up to 1565°C (2850°F). The reactor tube is
vertically housed in a pressure vessel approximately 24 in. in diameter and 7 ft in length. In this
testing, the EFG will be taking the hot syngas from the exit from the FBG cyclone outlet and
reacting it with additional oxygen injected into the top reaction zone. The reactor is separated into
four independent zones so that a consistent temperature can be maintained throughout the length
of the furnace. Heating elements provide the initial heat for the centrally located alumina reactor
tube, and refractory walls outside the heating elements provide insulation. All the gasification
reactions occur inside the reactor tube, and pressure inside the alumina reactor tube is balanced

 A-1

 Figure A-1. Design drawing of the pressurized fluidized gasification reactor.

with a slight positive nitrogen pressure outside of the tube to avoid fracturing the alumina. Product
gas exits at the bottom of the furnace tube and enters a gas quench zone. This project testing would
involve some modifications to inject hydrocarbon-rich syngas directly into this zone in the highest-
temperature regions, where quench reforming will be the most effective. Syngas exiting the quench
zone flows through a refractory-lined horizontal pipe run and then exits the main unit on its way
to the back-end subsystems. As syngas rapidly cools and turns into the horizontal pass, denser slag,
ash, and char will lose entrainment and accumulate in a refractory-lined slag trap.

 A-2

 Figure A-2. Schematic and photograph of the small pilot-scale EFG.

 Fuel Feed System

 The feed system uses a K-Tron® twin-screw, loss-in-weight feeder installed inside of a
pressure vessel capable of 6.9-MPa (1000-psig) operation. This system allows an instantaneous,
high-precision measurement of the fuel feed rate to the gasification system it is connected to. The
feeder and scale are located inside of a pressure vessel. A lock hopper above the feeder allows
technicians to periodically refill the K-Tron feeder at system pressure, while the gasifier itself is
continuously fed at a metered feed rate by the K-Tron itself. Weights and feed rates are recorded
and plotted to provide online fuel feed rate measurements to the control room operator. The use of
internal mixers in both the K-Tron feeder itself and in the feed lock hopper allow for the feeding
of more difficult biomass feedstocks that can hang up on the walls of feed equipment. The feeding
of several pure biomass streams to the FBG has been previously demonstrated in this equipment.

 Hot-Gas Filter

 The bench-scale hot-gas filter vessel is used to perform high-temperature, high-pressure
particulate removal. The vessel is 10-in. inside diameter and 60 in. long (including cone, vessel,
and cap) and can handle syngas flow up to 30 scfm at 540°C (1000°F) and 1000 psig. Filters are
sealed in a tube sheet by either a bolted metal plate and Nextel™ fiber gaskets or the filters are
welded in place on the tube sheet. Standard production filters of either iron aluminide or silicon
carbide employ integrated “fuse” technology that automatically shuts off flow through a filter in
the event of an individual filter element’s failure. The vessel is sized such that it can handle three
candle filters up to 18 in. with a 2.375-in. outside diameter. Solids accumulate on the filter surfaces
and are occasionally back-pulsed using recycled syngas to knock particulate loose so that the filters
remain clean. Solids fall into a pressurized ash lock hopper, which is periodically isolated and

 A-3

automatically depressurized and drained into a sealed low-pressure barrel on a scale, which allows
online measurements of ash removal.

 Warm-Gas Cleanup Train

 The warm-gas cleanup train consists of several fixed-bed reactors loaded with catalyst and
sorbent. Catalyst provides water–gas shift (WGS) reactions, while sorbent capture sulfur, chlorine,
and trace metals (including mercury) at temperatures above 204°C (400°F). All the technologies
utilized are considered either commercial or near commercial in development. Each fixed bed is
electrically heated to control bed temperature, and each has multielement thermocouples in the
beds.

 Quench System

 The quench system uses six water-cooled quench pots to condense water and tars from the
syngas. Thermocouples are located at the inlet of each quench pot and at the exit of the final quench
pot to monitor the temperatures across each. The quench pots utilize automated draining of liquids.
A condensate sample point is located on the condensate line which may be used for collecting
liquid samples and for periodic timed flow rate measurements. Four additional knockout pots on
the syngas recycle line minimize tars that would otherwise move through the recycle compressor,
flow control valves, and pressure tap purges. A continuous analytical syngas sample is extracted
from the back end of the quench system, cooled in a low-pressure condenser pot that is immersed
in an ice bath, and then routed to gas analyzers.

 Acid Gas Separation System

 The acid gas separation system (AGSS) uses physical solvents to remove CO2 and various
contaminants from dry syngas at pressures of up to 1000 psig. This system allows the EERC to
test solvents and technologies for syngas and natural gas sweetening and liquids capture. The
ability to remove CO2 from gas streams further allows the EERC to test processes incorporating
precombustion carbon capture and storage.

 As shown in Figure A-3, in the first step of CO2 capture, pressure-regulated syngas enters
an absorption column. As gas rises through the packed column, downward-flowing solvent absorbs
CO2 and other gas components. The sweetened gas passes through a demister to drop entrained
solvent out of suspension before the gas exits the column for further processing in FT synthesis.
Continuous analytical gas samples are extracted from the AGSS, cooled in low-pressure condenser
pots that are immersed in an ice bath, and then routed to gas analyzers.

 Having absorbed most of the CO2 and various other components from the syngas gas, rich
solvent collects in the bottom disengager, where gas bubbles have sufficient residence time to
escape from the liquid. Solvent then flows through a level control valve, a heat exchanger, and a
flow constrictor (metering valve) before passing into a flash drum. The flow constrictor maintains
some pressure upstream of the flash drum, preventing excessive cavitation in the control valve and
heat exchanger.

 A-4

 Figure A-3. Acid Gas Separation System.

 As solvent warms and depressurizes inside the heated flash drum, CO2 and other gases
vaporize from the solvent. A flowmeter records the rate of acid gas exiting the flash drum while a
continuous gas analyzer records the gas composition. These measurements permit online mass and
carbon balance calculations.

 Lean solvent exits the flash drum through a level-controlling valve and then passes through
a water-cooled heat exchanger on its way to a storage tank. A pump pulls solvent from the bottom
of this tank and sends it through a glycol-cooled heat exchanger. The chilled, lean solvent then
sprays through a nozzle into the top of the absorption column, completing the solvent loop.

 Based on Aspen Plus and ChemCAD® modeling, the AGSS was expected to remove around
95% of CO2 under typical operation without measurable solvent loss. Prior testing utilizing coal-
derived syngas achieved closer to 98% CO2 capture and even better H2S removal. Modeling and
experience suggest that untreated sour gas can be effectively treated using the flash drum for
solvent regeneration; however, if required to meet the needs of future clients, the skid design
allows upgrading the flash drum to a stripper column for improved gas sweetening and extended
solvent life.

The construction and demolition debris waste is assumed to be in the mixed form typical for

partner, or in a MRF operated in-house by Aloha Carbon. The MRF is designed to use a series of
mechanical and optical separation techniques include screening, air density, and shredding, to
separate the organic unrecyclable waste from the inorganic and recyclable waste. Organic
waste which can be used as gasiﬁcation feedstock to make RNG include wood, fabric, rubber,
cardboard, plastics, and ﬁber from building materials, furniture, and rejected recyclables.
Recyclable wastes separated in the MRF include those with resale value, such as scrap metal
and concrete, and those with low energy content or high impurities unsuitable for fuel reﬁning,
such as polyvinyl chloride piping, ﬁberglass insulation, and porcelain. A proposed MRF design
for the Aloha Carbon plant was designed by vendor Sparta Systems, Inc.

EQTEC Gasiﬁcation Plant (Upstream Unit) - Process Design Basis and Description: The EQTEC
scope, as deﬁned by its Basis of Design and detailed Process Description, involves the
conversion of 20 metric tons per hour (t/h) of C&D waste into clean syngas, utilizing two parallel
gasiﬁcation lines each processing 10 metric tons per hour (t/h). This system is designed to yield
puriﬁed syngas at the battery limits. The syngas is a mix of gas molecules, with the working
gases being primarily hydrogen (H2), carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO2), and methane
(CH4). The core technology is EQTEC's patented Gasiﬁer Technology (EGT), which employs an
oxygen-steam Bubbling Fluidized Bed (BFB) gasiﬁer operating at moderate pressure between 15
to 100 psig and moderate temperature below 1000°C).

The prepared waste feedstock is continuously fed into the gasiﬁer via an airtight system of
screws, valves, and hoppers, alongside a separate feeding system for ﬂuidization bed material.
An oxygen/steam mixture, which is sub-stoichiometric and does not allow for ﬂame or burning
of the waste, is precisely controlled by an algorithm to meet syngas production targets, serves
as the gasiﬁcation agent introduced at the gasiﬁer's base. During startup, hot air from two
dedicated jet burners preheats the gasiﬁer and thermal cracking reactor. After pre-heating and
startup, the gasiﬁcation system is self-sustaining. Within the gasiﬁer, the feedstock rapidly heats
and undergoes decomposition and gasiﬁcation reactions, producing syngas. This syngas then
exits the gasiﬁer and enters a cyclone for the separation and recirculation of solid particles
(“Char”), maximizing carbon conversion. The char particles captured by the cyclone are
recirculated back to the gasiﬁer for further breakdown to gases.

The syngas then proceeds to the Hot Conditioning section. It is ﬁrst cooled, preparing it for High
Temperature Filtration (HTF) where remaining particles are removed using candle ﬁlters.

  Nov 2025
Following ﬁltration, the syngas enters the Eqtec patented Thermal Cracking Reactor (TCR), to
raise the temperature and to promote reforming reactions, facilitating breakdown of volatile
organic compounds (aka tars) to syngas. The syngas is then quenched. Further cooling then
reduces the temperature for the Wet Syngas Cleaning stage.

Wet syngas cleaning involves a series of scrubbers that reduce gas temperature and remove
soluble contaminants.

Other integral units within EQTEC's scope include a Thermal Oxidizer, designed for safe burning
of ﬂammable gases during startup, and for the further thermal destruction of tar and
wastewater streams. A Process Water Treatment system, featuring Dissolved Air Flotation (DAF)
and ﬁltration, clariﬁes process water for reuse, with a purge stream directed to the thermal
oxidizer or external treatment to manage salinity. Auxiliary facilities like cooling systems, gas
supply, steam generation, and ﬁre prevention are included in the Balance of Plant (BoP) design
and excluded from EQTEC's direct supply scope.

The overall design anticipates plant availability at 78% for the ﬁrst year, rising to 86%
subsequently. The Basis of Design details feedstock characteristics, site-speciﬁc meteorological
and seismic data for Tacoma, and adheres to the U.S. Customary System for measurements,
along with speciﬁed mechanical and Instrumentation and Controls (I&C) codes and standards.

Renewable Natural Gas Unit (Downstream Unit) - Process Integration: Receiving the
conditioned syngas from the EQTEC gasiﬁcation plant, the RNG unit selected for this project by
SEL is Topsoe’s proprietary TREMP™ (Topsoe Recycle Energy-eﬃcient Methanation Process)
technology to convert this syngas into pipeline-quality RNG. This robust catalytic process
initiates with syngas feed preparation and puriﬁcation, where any remaining trace contaminants
such as metals, chlorine, and sulfur are removed to protect the sensitive downstream catalysts.
A crucial carbon monoxide (CO) conversion step then adjusts the hydrogen-to-carbon ratio,
typically via water-gas shift, converting most carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide. Excess carbon
dioxide is subsequently removed to optimize the stoichiometry for methanation.

The heart of the Topsoe unit is the bulk methanation section, where carbon monoxide, carbon
dioxide, and hydrogen are catalytically converted to methane using Topsoe's high-temperature
MCR catalyst. This exothermic reaction generates signiﬁcant heat, which is eﬃciently recovered
for steam production and other energy integration. A ﬁnal trim methanation stage ensures the
RNG product achieves a marketable purity exceeding 97 molar percent (mol%) methane. The
process concludes with a cooling train and separation unit, where reaction-formed water is
removed, and the RNG product is dried as necessary before being sent to pipeline injection,
with an expected output of slightly over 2,400 SCFM of RNG.

2.6 Mass and Energy Balance

The process begins with Feedstock to Gasiﬁer entering as a solid at 77°F and 65.3 psig, with a
mass ﬂow of 22,046 lb/h. This feedstock is combined with a mixture of Oxygen and Steam, to
drive the gasiﬁcation reactions and also to bubble the bed to ensure rapid mixing of the waste
feedstock with the hot bed material and gases.

Following gasiﬁcation, the Syngas to the Raw Syngas Cooler emerges as a hot gas-solid mixture.
This stream undergoes signiﬁcant cooling and cleaning stages:

 • After the Raw Syngas Cooler, the Syngas to HT Filter is cooled

 • The Syngas to Thermal Cracking Reactor (TCR) enters as a gas-solid mixture, with a
 slightly reduced mass ﬂow due to reactions.

 • Post-TCR, the Syngas to Filtered Syngas Cooler with a mass ﬂow increasing due to the
 addition of quench water.

 • The Syngas to Wet Scrubbing is signiﬁcantly cooled, having undergone further heat
 exchange.

Within the wet cleaning and conditioning sections, the syngas is further reﬁned:

 • The Syngas from Homogenizer Tank is cooled again

Finally, the Syngas to Battery Limits, destined for the downstream methanation unit, is
reheated. It's noted that these ﬁnal syngas conditions are subject to conﬁrmation with the
sulfur guard supplier.

Additionally, a portion of the syngas, Syngas to Thermal Oxidizer, is directed for safe
combustion, particularly during non-continuous or controlled ﬂow scenarios. The purpose of
this syngas combustion is to keep the thermal oxidizer heated and ready to dispose of volatile
organic compounds coming from the waste water treatment plant’s DAF unit.

3.0 Hazard Modeling

Simonpietri Enterprises LLC contracted with risk management ﬁrm Det Norke Veritas’s
subsidiary, DNV Energy, USA, to perform hazard modeling for both the Phase One and Phase
Two systems. Only the Phase One analysis for the 50 ton per day pilot plant could be completed
during the period of performance for this study. DNV applied API Recommended Practice 752
Management of Hazards Associated with Location of Process Plant Permanent Buildings, which
provides guidelines for managing the risks to operating personnel and neighboring facilities
fromhazards associated with toxic, ﬂammable, or explosive material releases at process
facilities.

The study was split into three tasks, as follows:

Task 1: Modeling of Syngas Leak in the Pilot Plant (2 tph SMRFF-Phase One). This task includes
the assessment of the impact of syngas leak scenarios at the pilot plant.

Key activities included:

• Perform dispersion modeling to simulate a number of syngas leak scenarios in the pilot plant,
incorporating variables such as wind direction, weather conditions, and release rates.

• Conﬁrm that evacuation routes and muster points adequately protect operators from syngas
exposure during a leak scenario.

• Identify optimal locations for gas sensors to detect syngas leaks, trigger alarms, and facilitate
timely evacuation of operators.

• Quantify impact to neighboring facilities adjacent to the facility's 4.9-acre area.

• Perform ﬂue gas dispersion modeling of the Heat Recovery Steam Generator (HRSG) and the
ﬂue gas stacks, as syngas is combusted in a thermal oxidizer, considering the presence of
surrounding buildings.

The primary objective of this assessment was to evaluate the suitability of permanent buildings
near the Phase One 2tph CHP plant, by analyzing potential impacts from hazardous releases,
speciﬁcally from the gasiﬁcation process and ﬂue gas system. This was done using a
consequence-based approach, adhering to API RP 752 and referencing U.S. EPA RMP guidance.

The methodology involved using Phast™ software (version 9.1) to model various release
scenarios, including full-bore ruptures, smaller leaks, and catastrophic events from diﬀerent
equipment (Fluidized Bed Gasiﬁer, Cyclone, Raw Syngas Cooler, High Temperature Syngas Filter,
Thermal Oxidizer, Nitrogen Cooler, and Flue Gas Stack). The modeling considered ﬂammable gas
dispersion, thermal radiation, explosion overpressure, and toxic gas exposure at a receptor
height of 3.3 ft (1m) above grade, i.e. height of a human operator. Key assumptions included

 PROCESS DESCRIPTION
The Aloha Sustainable Materials Recycling & Fertilizer Facility (Aloha SMRFF) is a proposed new
waste recycling and organic fertilizer manufacturing plant to be located in Kapolei in the City and
County of Honolulu. It will convert locally generated non-hazardous wastes into organic fertilizer,
power, and other recycling services and recycled-material products. The facility will be located on just
under five acres of Intensive Industrial (I2) zoned land at 91-027 Kaomi Loop in the James Campbell
Industrial Park of Kapolei.
The Noncovered Source Permit application is for the gasifier and thermal oxidizer/heat recovery steam
generator-powered turbine to transform the wood and other organic wastes into two megawatts
equivalent (2 MWe) of green electricity and steam and up to 3,000 pounds per day of biochar. The
gasification system selected for the Aloha SMRFF project is a 2 tons per hour atmospheric pressure
fluidized-bed air-blown refractory-lined gasification system, designed and patented by European firm
Eqtec PLC. The syngas generated will be combusted in a thermal oxidizer. The exhaust of the thermal
oxidizer will be sent through a heat recovery steam generator (HRGS) to power a steam turbine to
generate electricity. The thermal oxidizer is started on diesel fuel.
Prior to gasification, the non-hazardous construction and demolition waste (C&D waste) will be sorted
to separate the organic energy-containing waste stream (i.e. wood, paper, cardboard, plastic, fabric,
etc) and the inorganic waste stream (rock, concrete, dirt, glass, metal, etc). The organic energy-
containing waste stream will be fed through a high-speed shredder and screen to achieve the desired
feedstock specifications. Emissions from the C&D waste processing will be comprised of fugitives
PM10/PM2.5 and small dust collectors. PM10 and PM2.5 emissions are expected to be less than the limit
specified in HAR 11-60.1-62(d)(1). Therefore, they are exempt from permitting.
