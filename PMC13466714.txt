When “well-tolerated” does not mean feeling well
Authors: James H Stein
Published: 2026

I was diagnosed with bladder cancer when I was 29 years old. I had just completed internal medicine training and the only patients with bladder cancer I had known were those dying on an inpatient oncology unit after aggressive combination chemotherapy. A few years later, a relative died of it.

After surgery, I had regular surveillance for 32 years until the cancer recurred last May, when I had three low-grade tumors removed surgically. Because I had gone more than three decades without recurrence, we decided to simply continue surveillance, but in November I had five recurrent tumors and needed to change course. I had surgery with blue-light imaging and received intravesical gemcitabine immediately post-op.

My medical team explained to me that weekly intravesical gemcitabine for six weeks would reduce my risk of recurrence. This treatment was presented as standard and “well-tolerated.” Everything I read in UpToDate, the patient materials I was given, and three GPT models (including OpenEvidence) agreed with what my clinical team told me. I was primed for one or two days of pelvic discomfort and urinary symptoms, like a bad urinary tract infection, and then a normal week.

That is exactly what happened after my first infusion. I had chills and fatigue for a few hours afterward and 24 hours of pelvic discomfort and frequent urination. The following week was similar: chills, fatigue, urinary symptoms, and one night of sweats. I felt more tired than expected, but I ignored it.

After the third dose, it became clear that this was not an easy local treatment. I had my usual chills and fatigue later that afternoon, but I never returned to baseline. I continued to have night sweats and felt persistently tired. I saw patients on Wednesday, but afterwards I developed significant fatigue, muscle aches, nightly drenching sweats, and intermittent nausea that required keeping food in my stomach and taking once or twice daily ondansetron to avoid vomiting. I began to wonder if something was wrong with me. This treatment was supposed to be “well-tolerated.”

In oncology trials, adverse events are categorized using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) from the National Cancer Institute.1 Grade 1 is considered mild. Grade 2 is considered moderate and usually requires some intervention, though not hospitalization. Grade 3 is severe and may require hospitalization. Grade 4 is life-threatening. Grade 5 is death.

It turns out that “well-tolerated” means that grade 3 and 4 events are uncommon and that treatment discontinuation is rare. It does not mean that people feel well. The CTCAE are designed to classify toxicity, not to capture patients’ subjective experience. Patient-reported outcomes aim to address this gap.

I decided to dig into the literature to see whether anyone had evaluated symptoms from a patient’s perspective. The best paper examined 592 people receiving intravesical therapy for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer at a single institution.2 Among the 163 people who received gemcitabine, moderate or severe symptoms were reported by 42%; 30% experienced moderate or severe fatigue, including 19% lasting three or more days and 9% lasting the entire week. Sixteen percent experienced moderate or severe body aches, about half of which lasted all week. Surprisingly, moderate or severe systemic symptoms were more common than bladder symptoms, and gemcitabine caused more systemic symptoms than BCG, an immunomodulator, which caused more urinary symptoms.

My experience is not exceptional. After learning this, I received my fourth dose and had the same chills, fatigue, and nausea as before, but had a better sense of what to expect and was less distressed by my symptoms.

In terms of not stopping therapy, not requiring hospitalization, and not having life-threatening toxicity, intravesical gemcitabine truly is “well-tolerated.” But I felt terrible and sometimes the symptoms really scared me. I also had to move my clinic to video, stop exercising, and spend much of the day lying down because of nausea and fatigue.

It also is important to be clear about the goal of treatment. Progression rates for low-grade noninvasive bladder cancer are low. This chemotherapy is unlikely to save my life. Its purpose is to reduce tumor recurrences and the need for repeat surgeries that require anesthesia, followed by weeks of pelvic discomfort, urinary bleeding, urgency, and activity restrictions. I am not sure I would choose this again for that benefit.

Indeed, what I learned from this experience is how different the same data can look when read as a physician and when experienced as a patient.
