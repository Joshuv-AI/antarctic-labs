Abstract
We develop a fast space–time numerical scheme for approximating solutions to the linear wave equation. The approach is based on the sparse-grid combination technique applied to a coercive space–time discretization. Designed for tensor-product space–time discretizations, the method enables efficient parallelization of the resulting solver. We provide a rigorous theoretical analysis establishing convergence rates and computational complexity estimates. Numerical experiments validate the theoretical estimates and demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method.



Keywords.
Wave equation, space–time methods, sparse grids, combination technique, convergence analysis.

Introduction

In recent years, an increasing amount of literature has been devoted to the design of
conforming
space–time Galerkin methods for hyperbolic problems (we refer to
[
11
, §1]
for an overview of recent developments, and to
[
13
]
for a comprehensive survey of space–time methods). Concurrently, progress in developing fast solvers has allowed space–time methods to compete with traditional method-of-lines approaches that combine spatial finite element discretization with ODE time integrators. Specifically, efficient strategies have been designed to exploit the space–time tensor-product structure, enabling the efficient resolution of the resulting linear systems through their Kronecker product properties. For instance,
[
28
]
investigates various fast direct solution techniques for second-order-in-time numerical schemes based on continuous piecewise polynomials (see
[
20
]
for an isogeometric analysis approach), while
[
10
, §5.1]
designs an analogous solver for a first-order-in-time formulation. In this paper, we design a fast conforming space–time numerical scheme that, similarly to
[
10
,
20
,
28
]
, takes advantage of this tensor-product structure; however, rather than relying on the Kronecker properties of the algebraic system, our approach is based on a sparse-grid discretization technique.

Model problem.

Let
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T)
be a space–time cylinder, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=1,2,3
, is a bounded, Lipschitz domain with boundary
\partial\Omega
, and
T>0
is a given final time. We consider the following initial-boundary value problem for the linear wave equation: find
u:Q_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}u({\bm{x}},t)-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot(c^{2}({\bm{x}})\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}u({\bm{x}},t))=f({\bm{x}},t)&({\bm{x}},t)\in Q_{T},\\
u({\bm{x}},t)=0&({\bm{x}},t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u({\bm{x}},0)=u_{0}({\bm{x}}),\quad\partial_{t}u({\bm{x}},0)=v_{0}({\bm{x}})&{\bm{x}}\in\Omega.\end{cases}

(1)

Here, the source term
f
belongs to
L^{2}(Q_{T})
, and the wave velocity
c\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
satisfies
c=c({\bm{x}})\geq c_{0}
a.e., for some
c_{0}>0
. Moreover, the initial data satisfy
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. We are interested in a numerical scheme that approximates a weak solution to (
1
).

Conforming space–time Galerkin scheme.

The underlying space–time scheme we consider was designed and analyzed in
[
12
]
. This scheme can be viewed as a variant of the discontinuous Galerkin–continuous Galerkin (DG–CG) method in
[
27
,
7
]
, which, in turn, evolved from the classical space–time DG method of
[
19
]
. It is based on a second-order-in-time variational formulation of (
1
), where integration by parts is performed in space but not in time. A consistent penalty term is added to weakly enforce the initial condition on the first time derivative of the wave field at
t=0
; furthermore, time-dependent exponential weights are used in the
L^{2}
space–time scalar products to guarantee coercivity of the formulation in the energy norm
H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}_{0}(\Omega))
, providing stability for any choice of discrete tensor-product spaces, with
C^{1}
-regular temporal component. Since coercivity and continuity are not satisfied with respect to the same norm, quasi-optimality does not follow automatically for any choice of discrete subspaces. However, optimal convergence rates with respect to the mesh size can be still established for specific tensor-product discretizations. For instance,
[
12
, Appendix A]
shows this for
C^{1}
splines in time of even polynomial degree on uniform meshes, and an analogous result is expected when using maximal-regularity splines in time.

Contribution.

In the present paper, building on the coercive structure of the scheme of
[
12
]
, we establish optimal convergence rates for a sparse space–time version based on the
combination technique

[
16
,
6
,
15
]
. This provides an optimal-order convergent approximation to the solution of (
1
) with a number of degrees of freedom that scales like that of a single stationary elliptic problem in
\Omega
on the finest spatial grid.

Given nested sequences of spatial
\{V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=0,\ldots,J}
and temporal
\{V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)\}_{j_{t}=0,\ldots,J}
refinements, our goal is to compute an approximate solution at the finest full-grid space
V_{J}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes V_{J}^{t}(0,T)
for a fixed, large
J\in\mathbb{N}
. Instead of computing this approximate solution directly (the full-grid solution), the combination technique constructs an approximation by combining solutions computed on suitably chosen coarser refinement levels. This
sparse-grid
solution preserves the approximation properties of the
full-grid
solution with a considerably reduced total number of degrees of freedom. The method naturally enables a parallelizable space–time solver, as the problems associated with different coarser refinement levels are independent and can be solved in parallel. To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the few conforming space–time methods for the wave equation that admits efficient parallelization while also being supported by a rigorous theoretical analysis. In this context, we also mention the approach of
[
28
, §8.1.3]
, which relies on the fast diagonalization method
[
22
]
applied to the Galerkin–Bubnov scheme from
[
21
]
. Although this approach has demonstrated good numerical performance, a stability analysis of the corresponding conforming space–time discretization is currently missing.
Non-conforming schemes for hyperbolic systems that are easily parallelizable and whose convergence analysis is available are those based on tent-pitching, e.g.,
[
24
]
.

Since the combination technique requires solving multiple discrete problems on strongly anisotropic space–time meshes, applying it to an unconditionally stable formulation is crucial. However, as highlighted above, for the space–time formulation employed herein, quasi-optimality is not inherently guaranteed for arbitrary choices of conforming discrete spaces. As a consequence, analyzing directly the Galerkin method in the sparse-grid space as done in
[
14
]
for elliptic problems,
[
18
]
for parabolic problems, and
[
2
]
for fractional diffusion via higher-dimensional extension, may lead, in the present setting, to reduced convergence rates. Moreover, this procedure can produce an approximate solution different from the combination technique solution, even though both belong to the same space, in contrast to the situation in
[
17
]
, where the two coincide. Therefore, our convergence analysis targets the combination technique solution itself directly, integrating the framework of
[
15
]
with the space–time analytical techniques developed in
[
12
]
. In contrast to the elliptic setting considered in
[
15
]
, where standard
L^{2}
projections are used, our analysis for the linear wave equation requires tailored space- and time-projection operators.

We establish optimal convergence rates of the sparse-grid solution for arbitrary polynomial approximation degrees in both space and time, under suitable regularity assumptions and discrete compatibility assumptions on the analytical solution at
t=0
and on
\partial\Omega
. Although these conditions do not appear to be strictly sharp, numerical experiments show that relaxing some of them can result in a reduced convergence order.

The combination technique has been applied to space–time discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods in
[
3
]
. However, since stability in that setting is established only with respect to a mesh-dependent DG seminorm, the convergence analysis of the combination technique presents intrinsic difficulties. Similar challenges would likely arise if one were to apply the combination formula to the conforming space–time method of
[
11
]
, for which the inf-sup stability relies on a mesh-dependent norm. We finally mention that, when
d>1
and
\Omega
is a tensor product of domains in some coordinate system, the combination technique could be employed in the components of the space variable as well.

3

In this section, we introduce the space–time sparse-grid method based on the combination technique, and analyze its computational complexity in terms of the number of degrees of freedom (DoFs). We then state a convergence result for the resulting approximation, with the proof deferred to Section

3.2
Complexity analysis: DoFs count

We compare the computational complexity of full- and sparse-grid discretizations in terms of the total number of DoFs. We measure the complexity of the sparse-grid version of the method by summing the dimensions of the
2J+1
linear systems in (
19
):

N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}:=\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=0}^{J}\mathrm{dim}\big(V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes V^{t}_{J-j_{{\bm{x}}}}(0,T)\big)+\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=1}^{J}\mathrm{dim}\big(V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}-1}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes V^{t}_{J-j_{{\bm{x}}}}(0,T)\big);

see also Remark
2
. We consider separately the cases of space dimension
d=1
and
d=2,3
.

Case
d=1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be a one-dimensional spatial domain, and let
J\in\mathbb{N}
be the finest refinement level. For
j_{{\bm{x}}},j_{t}\leq J
, we choose
V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)\subset H^{2}_{0,\bullet}(0,T)
as the spaces generated by B-splines on uniform meshes with polynomial degrees
p_{{\bm{x}}}
and
p_{t}
and regularities

0\leq r_{{\bm{x}}}\leq p_{{\bm{x}}}-1
and
1\leq r_{t}\leq p_{t}-1
in space and time, respectively. This choice ensures conformity of the approximation, namely
C^{0}
continuity in space and
C^{1}
continuity in time. For a given refinement level
(j_{{\bm{x}}},j_{t})
, the dimension of the tensor-product space
V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
is

\mathrm{dim}(V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T))=(N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(p_{{\bm{x}}}-r_{{\bm{x}}})+r_{{\bm{x}}}-1)(N_{j_{t}}(p_{t}-r_{t})+r_{t}),

where
N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}\approx 2^{j_{{\bm{x}}}}
and
N_{j_{t}}\approx 2^{j_{t}}
are the number of spatial and temporal intervals, respectively. The number of DoFs of the full space–time approximation at the finest level
J
is

N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{full}}:=\mathrm{dim}(V_{J}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes V_{J}^{t}(0,T))\approx 2^{2J},

while the total number of DoFs for the sparse-grid space–time approximation satisfies

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=0}^{J}2^{j_{{\bm{x}}}+(J-j_{{\bm{x}}})}+\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=1}^{J}2^{j_{{\bm{x}}}-1+(J-j_{{\bm{x}}})}=2^{J}(J+1+J2^{-1})\approx 2^{J}J.

Case
d=2,3
.

In the case of spatial dimension
d\in\{2,3\}
we consider spatial discretizations using
C^{0}
finite elements on sequences of shape-regular, simplicial nested meshes obtained by successive mesh halving. Namely, for
j_{{\bm{x}}}\leq J
,
V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\subset H^{1}(\Omega)
is the space of continuous, piecewise polynomials of degree
p_{{\bm{x}}}
defined on the mesh at level
j_{{\bm{x}}}
. The dimension of
V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)
scales like
\mathcal{O}(2^{dj_{{\bm{x}}}})
. In time, as above, we consider the spaces generated by B-splines on uniform meshes with degree
p_{t}
and regularity
1\leq r_{t}\leq p_{t}-1
,
whose dimension scales like
\mathcal{O}(2^{j_{t}})
. For the full-grid approximation with fine level
J
, we have

N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{full}}\approx 2^{dJ+J}=2^{(d+1)J},

while for the sparse-grid approximation, we obtain

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=0}^{J}2^{dj_{{\bm{x}}}+(J-j_{{\bm{x}}})}+\sum_{j_{{\bm{x}}}=1}^{J}2^{d(j_{{\bm{x}}}-1)+(J-j_{{\bm{x}}})}=\frac{(1-2^{(d-1)(J+1)})}{1-2^{d-1}}(2^{J}+2^{J-d})-2^{J-d}\approx 2^{dJ}.

Hence, the sparse-grid discretization requires significantly fewer degrees of freedom than the full-grid scheme.

Remark 2

(DoFs count and solution cost)
.

The full-grid scheme leads to a single algebraic linear system of size
\mathcal{O}(2^{(d+1)J})
, whereas the sparse-grid discretization results in
2J+1
smaller linear systems of different sizes. The total number of degrees of freedom
N_{\mathrm{DoFs}}^{\mathrm{sparse}}=\mathcal{O}(2^{dJ})
computed above corresponds to the sum of their dimensions. Thus, if the cost of each linear system solution grows more than linearly in its dimension, the combination formula (
19
) is advantageous against a full-grid scheme even for the same number of DoFs. Additionally, the combination technique naturally allows for parallel implementation: the individual (small) systems can be solved independently, further enhancing the efficiency of the method compared to the full-grid approach.

obtained with the combination formula (
19
) to the solution
u
of (
7
), it is essential to first establish the properties of the semidiscretizations
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
. These are defined as the solutions to the following semidiscrete problems: given
u\in\mathcal{V}(Q_{T})
, find
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
such that

\mathcal{A}(P_{\infty,j_{t}}u,w_{\infty,j_{t}})=\mathcal{A}(u,w_{\infty,j_{t}})\quad\text{for all }w_{\infty,j_{t}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T),

(30)

and find
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u\in V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)
such that

\mathcal{A}(P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u,w_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty})=\mathcal{A}(u,w_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty})\quad\text{for all }w_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}\in V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T).

(31)

This also defines the corresponding semidiscretization operators
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u:\mathcal{V}(Q_{T})\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u:\mathcal{V}(Q_{T})\to V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)
, respectively.

In the remainder of this section, we show that
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
are well defined (Section
4.1
), and we establish
stability results under high regularity assumptions (Section
4.2
). We conclude with convergence results for differences of consecutive Galerkin projections (Section
4.3
).

4.1
Well-definedness and regularity

We first establish the well-definedness and regularity-preserving properties of the semidiscretizations
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
.

Proposition 1
.

For any
u\in\mathcal{V}(Q_{T})
, the semidiscretizations
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
are well-defined for all
j_{t},j_{{\bm{x}}}\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, assuming
s_{{\bm{x}}}\geq 1
and
s_{t}\geq 2
, and

\partial\Omega\in C^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-1,1},\quad c\in H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-1}(\Omega)\cap C^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-2,1}(\overline{\Omega})

(32)

the following regularity properties hold:

i)

if
u\in\big(H_{0}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes H^{1}(0,T)\big)\cap\big(H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-2}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)\big)
, then
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u\in H_{0}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
;

ii)

if
u\in\big(H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\otimes H^{s_{t}-2}(0,T)\big)\cap\big(L^{2}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{s_{t}}(0,T)\big)
, then
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u\in V^{{\bm{x}}}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{s_{t}}(0,T)
.

Proof.

We first prove the statement for
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
. Let
\{\psi_{j_{t}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
be a basis of
V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
. Then, any element
v_{\infty,j_{t}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
can be expressed as

v_{\infty,j_{t}}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}v_{i}({\bm{x}})\psi_{j_{t}}^{i}(t)\quad\text{with }v_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(33)

Indeed, by the definition of the Hilbert tensor product
[
25
, Chapter II, § 4]
, we can write

v_{\infty,j_{t}}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z_{k}({\bm{x}})v_{j_{t}}^{k}(t)\quad\text{with }z_{k}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\text{ and }v_{j_{t}}^{k}\in V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T).

Expanding each
v_{j_{t}}^{k}\in V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
as
v_{j_{t}}^{k}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}v_{j_{t},i}^{k}\psi_{j_{t}}^{i}(t)
with
v_{j_{t},i}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain (
33
):

v_{\infty,j_{t}}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}z_{k}({\bm{x}})\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}v_{j_{t},i}^{k}\psi_{j_{t}}^{i}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}v_{i}({\bm{x}})\psi^{i}_{j_{t}}(t),

where
v_{i}({\bm{x}}):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{j_{t},i}^{k}z_{k}({\bm{x}})
. In view of (
33
), finding
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
that solves (
30
) is equivalent to finding its coefficient functions
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
in the expansion
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}u_{j}({\bm{x}})\psi_{j_{t}}^{j}(t)
. Writing the test functions as
w_{\infty,j_{t}}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}w_{i}({\bm{x}})\psi_{j_{t}}^{i}(t)
with
w_{i}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, the left-hand side of problem (
30
) becomes

\mathcal{A}(P_{\infty,j_{t}}u,w_{\infty,j_{t}})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}\left(\mathbf{A}_{j_{t}}[i,j](u_{j},w_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathbf{B}_{j_{t}}[i,j](c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}u_{j},\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}w_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

where the
(N_{j_{t}}^{t}\times N_{j_{t}}^{t})
matrices
\mathbf{A}_{j_{t}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{j_{t}}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{j_{t}}[i,j]:=((\psi_{j_{t}}^{j})^{\prime\prime},(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}+(\psi_{j_{t}}^{j})^{\prime}(0)(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime}(0),\quad\mathbf{B}_{j_{t}}[i,j]:=(\psi_{j_{t}}^{j},(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}.

We thus consider the following reformulation of (
30
):
find
\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}}\in[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
such that

(\mathbf{A}_{j_{t}}\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}},\mathbf{w})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\mathbf{B}_{j_{t}}c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}},\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\mathbf{w})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=(\mathbf{F}_{j_{t}},\mathbf{w})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{for all~}\mathbf{w}\in[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}},

(34)

where the source term
\mathbf{F}_{j_{t}}\in[L^{2}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
is defined componentwise as

\mathbf{F}_{j_{t}}[i]({\bm{x}}):=(\partial_{t}^{2}u({\bm{x}},\cdot),(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}+\partial_{t}u({\bm{x}},0)(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime}(0)-(\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}u({\bm{x}},\cdot)),(\psi_{j_{t}}^{i})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}.

If
\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}}
solves (
34
), then
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u({\bm{x}},t):=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}u_{j}({\bm{x}})\psi_{j_{t}}^{j}(t)
, with
u_{j}:=\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}}[j]
, solves (
30
).

As an immediate consequence of (
2
), both matrices
\mathbf{A}_{j_{t}}
and
\mathbf{B}_{j_{t}}
have positive definite symmetric parts. Thus, by the Lax–Milgram lemma, there exists a unique solution
\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}}\in[H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
to problem (
34
). This implies existence of
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
solution of (
30
).
The coercivity of
\mathcal{A}
in (
8
) implies that
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u
is unique.
Moreover, if
u
satisfies the regularity in
i)
for
s_{{\bm{x}}}\geq 1
, and
c\in H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-1}(\Omega)
, then
\mathbf{F}_{j_{t}}\in[H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}-2}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
. Under the assumptions in (
32
), we deduce
\mathbf{u}_{j_{t}}\in[H_{0}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)]^{N_{j_{t}}^{t}}
(see
[
23
, Theorem 4.18]
), and thus
P_{\infty,j_{t}}u\in H_{0}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes V^{t}_{j_{t}}(0,T)
.

We now prove the statement for
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
. Let
\{\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}
be a basis of
V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)
that is orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and orthogonal in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
with respect to the weighted product
(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot,\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. We proceed analogously to the first part. Finding
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
that solves (
31
) is equivalent to finding its coefficient functions
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}\subset H^{2}_{0,\bullet}(0,T)
in the expansion
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}({\bm{x}})u_{j}(t)
. Writing the test functions
w_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}\in V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes H^{2}_{0,\bullet}(0,T)
as
w_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{i}({\bm{x}})w_{i}(t)
, where
w_{i}\in H^{2}_{0,\bullet}(0,T)
, and exploiting the orthogonality of the basis, formulation (
31
) can be rewritten as

\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}\left((u_{j}^{\prime\prime},w_{j}^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}+u_{j}^{\prime}(0)w_{j}^{\prime}(0)+\mu_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}(u_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}}\left((f_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j},w_{j}^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}+(\partial_{t}u(\cdot,0),\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}w_{j}^{\prime}(0)\right),

where we have set
\mu_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}:=(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\psi^{j}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}},\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\psi^{j}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
, and

f_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}(t):=(\partial_{t}^{2}u(\cdot,t),\psi^{j}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}u(\cdot,t),\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

For each
j=1,\dots,N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}
, the initial value problem

\begin{cases}u_{j}^{\prime\prime}(t)+\mu_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}u_{j}(t)=f^{j}_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}(t),&t\in[0,T],\\
u_{j}(0)=0,\quad u_{j}^{\prime}(0)=(\partial_{t}u(\cdot,0),\psi_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\end{cases}

admits a unique solution. This ensures the existence of
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u
. The uniqueness follows from the coercivity of
\mathcal{A}
in (
8
). Furthermore, if
u
satisfies the regularity in
ii)
for
s_{t}\geq 2
, then
f_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{j}\in H^{s_{t}-2}(0,T)
. This implies that
u_{j}\in H_{0,\bullet}^{s_{t}}(0,T)
for all
j=1,\dots,N_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}
, and consequently
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}u\in V_{j_{{\bm{x}}}}^{{\bm{x}}}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{s_{t}}(0,T)
.
∎

Estimates for the semidiscrete errors
(P_{\infty,j_{t}}-\Id)u
and
(P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}-\Id)u
, which are not used in the argument leading to Theorem
1
, are derived in Appendix
A
. In the following section, we establish stability estimates for
P_{\infty,j_{t}}
and
P_{j_{{\bm{x}}},\infty}
, which are used in the proof of convergence of the combination formula in Section
5
below.

4.2
Stability estimates in higher-order norms

Consider the spectral decomposition of the elliptic operator
\mathcal{L}=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot)
with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, and let
\{(\lambda_{m},\phi_{m})\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
be the eigenpairs such that
\mathcal{L}\phi_{m}=\lambda_{m}\phi_{m}
, with
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
orthonormal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and orthogonal in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
with respect to the weighted product
(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot,\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
. For
s_{{\bm{x}}}
a nonnegative integer, let us define
\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)
as the space of functions
v
such that

\|v\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)}:=\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(v,\phi_{m})^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}:V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)\to V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
be the operator defined by

((\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}z_{j_{t}})^{\prime},v_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}=(z_{j_{t}}^{\prime\prime},v_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}+z_{j_{t}}^{\prime}(0)v_{j_{t}}^{\prime}(0)\quad\text{for all~}v_{j_{t}}\in V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T).

(35)

This operator is well-defined by the Riesz representation theorem. We also use
\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}
to denote its natural extension to space–time functions.

The following stability result holds.

Lemma 1
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+3}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)
with
s_{{\bm{x}}}\geq 0
and define
z:=(\Id^{{\bm{x}}}\otimes(\Id^{t}-\Pi^{\partial_{t}^{2}}_{j_{t}}))u
. Then, the following estimates hold true for all
j_{t}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\|P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+1}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+2}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}+\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+3}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

Proof.

We decompose
z
and
P_{\infty,j_{t}}z
as

z({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hat{z}_{m}(t)\phi_{m}({\bm{x}}),\quad P_{\infty,j_{t}}z({\bm{x}},t)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}(t)\phi_{m}({\bm{x}}),

with
\hat{z}_{m}\in H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)
and
\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\in V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
.

From definition (
30
), the condition
\mathcal{A}(P_{\infty,j_{t}}z,v_{j_{t}})=\mathcal{A}(z,v_{j_{t}})
must hold for any
v_{j_{t}}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\otimes V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
. We choose the specific test function
v_{j_{t}}({\bm{x}},t)=\phi_{m}({\bm{x}})\eta_{j_{t}}(t)
, where
\eta_{j_{t}}\in V_{j_{t}}^{t}(0,T)
is arbitrary. Substituting the above expansions into the bilinear form
\mathcal{A}
, and using the orthogonality and orthonormality properties of the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(P_{\infty,j_{t}}z,\phi_{m}\eta_{j_{t}})

\displaystyle=((\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime\prime},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}+(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}(0)\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime}(0)+\lambda_{m}(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(z,\phi_{m}\eta_{j_{t}})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}(\hat{z}_{m},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}.

Then, the discrete variational equation for the
m
-th mode becomes

((\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime\prime},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}+(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}(0)\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime}(0)+\lambda_{m}(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}=\lambda_{m}(\hat{z}_{m},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}

(36)

or, equivalently, with
\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}
as in (
35
),

(\left(\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\right)^{\prime},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}+\lambda_{m}(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}=\lambda_{m}(\hat{z}_{m},\eta_{j_{t}}^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}.

(37)

Testing (
36
) with
\eta_{j_{t}}=\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}
yields

((\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime\prime},(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}+|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}(0)|^{2}+\lambda_{m}(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}},(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime})_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}=\lambda_{m}(\hat{z}_{m},(\hat{w}^{j_{t}}_{m})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}.

This, together with (
3
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2T}\|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2e}|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}(T)|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}(0)|^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{m}}{2T}\|\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{m}}{2e}|\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}(T)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{m}\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}\|(\hat{w}^{j_{t}}_{m})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)},

from which

\frac{1}{2T}\|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{m}}{2T}\|\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\lambda_{m}\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}\|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}\leq\frac{T\lambda_{m}^{2}}{2}\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2T}\|(\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2},

where, in the second inequality, we have used the Young inequality. This implies

\|\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq T^{2}\lambda_{m}\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2},

(38)

which gives

\displaystyle\|P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}\|\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq T^{2}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+1}\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=T^{2}\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+1}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}.

This proves the first estimate in the statement and, in particular, implies
P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\in\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)
.

For the second estimate, testing (
37
) with
\eta_{j_{t}}=\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{m}(\hat{z}_{m}-\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}},(\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime})_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}\lesssim(\lambda_{m}^{3/2}+\lambda_{m})\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)}\|(\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}_{e}(0,T)},

where the last step follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and (
38
). Then, we conclude

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}\|(\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}\hat{w}_{m}^{j_{t}})^{\prime}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{m}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}\left((\lambda_{m}^{3}+\lambda_{m}^{2})\|\hat{z}_{m}\|_{L_{e}^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+3}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}+\|z\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+2}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}^{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

One could expect to obtain the first bound in Lemma
1
with the same norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}
on the left- and on the right-hand side. However, the stronger norm on the right-hand side is due to the presence of the eigenvalue
\lambda_{m}
in the bound (
38
), which naturally arises from the discrete variational ODE (
36
).

For
c=1
, the following characterization of the space
\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)
was proved, e.g., in
[
26
, Lemma 3.1]
:

\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)=\{v\in H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\mid\mathcal{L}^{\ell_{{\bm{x}}}}v=0\quad\text{on~}\partial\Omega,\quad\text{for~}\ell_{{\bm{x}}}<s_{{\bm{x}}}/2,\ell_{{\bm{x}}}\in\mathbb{N}\},

together with the equivalence between the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)}
and the standard Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)}
, where boundary conditions are intended in the sense of traces in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. This result readily extends to any coefficient
c\in W^{\infty,s_{{\bm{x}}}-1}(\Omega)
. Therefore, an immediate consequence of Lemma
1
is the following result.

Corollary 1
.

Let
u\in H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+3}(\Omega)\otimes H_{0,\bullet}^{2}(0,T)
with
s_{{\bm{x}}}\geq 0
, and define
z:=(\Id^{{\bm{x}}}\otimes(\Id^{t}-\Pi^{\partial_{t}^{2}}_{j_{t}}))u
. Suppose also that (
32
) holds and that
c\in W^{\infty,s_{{\bm{x}}}+2}
. Then, the following estimates hold true for all
j_{t}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\|P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z\|_{H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+1}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)},

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}_{j_{t}}P_{\infty,j_{t}}z\|_{H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|z\|_{H^{s_{{\bm{x}}}+3}(\Omega)\otimes L^{2}(0,T)},

provided that
\mathcal{L}^{\ell_{{\bm{x}}}}z({\bm{x}},t)=0
for all
({\bm{x}},t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
and integers
\ell_{{\bm{x}}}
such that
1\leq\ell_{{\bm{x}}}<(s_{{\bm{x}}}+3)/2
, where
\mathcal{L}=-\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot(c^{2}\nabla_{{\bm{x}}}\cdot)
.

Let

Lemma 2

, we have
{\mathcal{S}}_{J}=2^{-(J+1)(p_{t}+1)}(J+2)\lesssim 2^{-J(p_{t}+1)}J
.

This proves (
67
) and the proof of the theorem is complete.

7
Conclusion

We have proposed and analyzed a fast space–time numerical scheme for the linear wave equation based on the combination technique on sparse grids. Based on an unconditionally stable second-order-in-time conforming Galerkin formulation, the method reduces the number of degrees of freedom compared to full tensor-product discretizations while preserving optimal convergence rates. Furthermore, the combination formula naturally enables efficient parallelization, which represents a key advantage over standard space–time discretizations.

We have established theoretical estimates in the
L^{2}(Q_{T})
norm, showing that optimal accuracy is achieved under suitable mixed-regularity assumptions and discrete compatibility conditions for the initial data and the source term. Theoretical findings have been validated through numerical experiments in both
(1+1)
- and
(2+1)
-dimensions. The numerical results confirm the predicted order of convergence and highlight the computational efficiency of the sparse-grid approach. Furthermore, our tests indicate that while some of the theoretical discrete compatibility conditions might be partially relaxed in practice without loss of optimality, they cannot be completely ignored without incurring an order reduction.
