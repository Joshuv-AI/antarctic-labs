HsfA-driven gene transcription regulates fungal morphogenesis and pathogenicity
Authors: Xin-Yi Nie, Guolong Zhu, Bei Qin, Yinchun Wang, Bo-Wen Li, Zhixin Jiang, Liling Wu, Yanping Hou, Shuyu Bian, Yu Wang, Shihua Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAspergillus flavus, a ubiquitous filamentous fungus, severely compromises global food safety and public health by producing carcinogenic aflatoxins. Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) orchestrates stress responses in eukaryotes, yet the functional role and regulatory mechanisms of its homolog, HsfA, in A. flavus remain elusive. Here, we demonstrate that A. flavus encodes two hsfA copies, whose knockdown or expression of a dominant-negative variant abrogates spore germination, a prerequisite for fungal development and colonization. Through integrated reverse genetics, ChIP-qPCR, electrophoretic mobility shift assay, and transcriptional profiling, we identify six non-chaperone targets – brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, velB, and vosA – directly regulated by HsfA via binding to conserved heat shock elements (HSEs) in their promoters. Ectopic expression of each target partially restores germination, developmental progression, and pathogenicity in HsfA-deficient strains, confirming HsfA’s central role in driving these processes via transcriptional activation. Structural divergence between the A. flavus HsfA DNA-binding domain and human HSF1 explains the ineffectiveness of three HSF1 inhibitors against the fungus. Our findings establish HsfA as a pivotal regulator of A. flavus virulence and uncover a novel HSF regulatory pathway, highlighting HsfA as a promising target for mitigating aflatoxin contamination.KEYWORDS: Heat shock transcription factor, direct target, development, pathogenicity, aflatoxin

Introduction

Aspergillus flavus is a crossover fungal pathogen of plants, animals, and humans [1]. It infects a wide range of agricultural commodities, including peanuts, maize, and cottonseed pre- and post-harvest, produces carcinogenic aflatoxins, and causes enormous yield losses. It is also an opportunistic pathogen that colonizes ocular, otic, pulmonary, and cutaneous tissues in humans and animals to initiate aspergillosis [2]. Consequently, preventing A. flavus contamination is an urgent imperative for global food safety and public health.

Fungal morphogenesis begins with spore germination. Under appropriate conditions, dormant spores germinate and form polarized hyphae that ramify into mycelia. Some hyphae then develop into conidiophores, whose distal cells produce new asexual spores that go dormant until the cycle can be restarted [3]. To establish infection, pathogenic fungus must adhere to the host, germinate, penetrate tissues, proliferate and disseminate, and elude the immune response [1]. Thus, spore germination represents a key stage governing both fungal development and host invasion.

Many factors have been identified as playing a role in the regulation of fungal sporulation and germination. The Velvet complex, a conserved heterotrimeric complex made up of VeA, LaeA, and VelB, governs both A. flavus sporulation and host infection [4,5]. Loss of LaeA reduces conidial germination rates in Aspergillus fumigatus and Aspergillus pachycristatus [6,7]. VelB controls spore germination via interacting with VosA, another velvet-like transcription factor that has been shown to be required for spore dormancy and germination in A. fumigatus and Aspergillus nidulans [8–10]. FlbC deficiency delays germination by reducing the mRNA of brlA, another C2H2 transcription factor gene [11]. BrlA activates conidiation-specific gene expression and controls spore formation via complex interactions with the APSES transcription factor StuA [12,13]. StuA regulates conidiation and germination, as well as the expression of FksA, a β-1,3-glucan synthase involved in cell wall biogenesis [14,15]. During germination, FksA colocalizes with newly formed beta-1,3-glucans in the germ tube’s apex [16]. In addition to the previously described transcription factors, germination is influenced by G-protein family member SfaD and epigenetic reader SntB [17,18]. However, their exact roles during germination in A. flavus are uncertain.

Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) is well-known for orchestrating the heat-shock response (HSR). Upon thermal stress, misfolded proteins titrate chaperone Hsp70, releasing inactive monomeric HSF1; trimerization occurs, allowing high-affinity binding to heat shock elements (HSEs) upstream of target genes, with subsequent transcriptional activation that mitigates proteotoxicity caused by misfolding or proteostatic collapse [19,20]. In fact, the significance of Hsf1 goes beyond heat stress. Even in non-stressful conditions [21], Saccharomyces cerevisiae Hsf1 associates with HSEs and responds pleiotropically to cold shock, pH extremes, heavy metals, and oxidants [22–25]. HSF1 is not strictly required for survival in mammals and plants where multiple HSF family members exist [26]; however, organisms without such redundancy require HSF1 for fungal cell viability [27–29], Drosophila oogenesis and early larval development [30], and Caenorhabditis elegans aging [31]. Although transcriptome investigations reveal thousands of heat-responsive genes whose expression profiles change when HSF1 is activated, only a small subset – primarily molecular chaperones – show strict HSF1 dependency [32], leaving the functional breadth of other HSF1 targets largely unknown.

In this study, we identified the heat shock transcription factor HsfA and its six new direct targets: brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, velB, and vosA in A. flavus. We discovered a novel mechanism for fungal morphogenesis and pathogenicity regulation in which the HsfA activates these six target genes’ transcription by directly binding to their promoter regions, promoting spore germination, and subsequent development processes and host invasion.

Materials and methods

Strains, plasmids, media, and chemicals

The genotype of each fungal strain was listed in SI Appendix Table S1. Escherichia coli strains DH5α and BL21 (DE3) were utilized for plasmid construction and recombinant protein expression, respectively. The pPTR I and pPTR II plasmids were purchased from Takara, Japan. A. flavus strains were cultivated on potato dextrose agar (PDA, 213400) or potato dextrose broth (PDB, 254920) purchased from BD Difco (USA) and supplemented with 100 ng/mL pyrithiamine (HY-122273, MCE) as needed for colony formation, spore germination, qRT-PCR, Western blotting, and ChIP-qPCR. YESS broth (YESSB, 2% yeast extract, 1% soytone, 15% sucrose, pH 5.5) and CM medium (0.6% yeast extract, 0.6% tryptone, 1% sucrose) are used for aflatoxin and sclerotia production, respectively. The DN-hsfA strain was grown in a glucose minimum medium (GMM) broth containing 1% glucose, trace elements, and 50 ml/L of 20× salt (pH 6.5). For DN-hsfA induction, glucose was replaced by xylose (XMM). DTHIB (HY-138280), KRIBB11 (HY-100872), and homoharringtonine (HHT, HY-14944) were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE, USA). Aflatoxin B1 standard (Sigma-Aldrich, A6636) was purchased from Merck, Germany. OXOID (UK) provided us with yeast extract (LP0021B) and tryptone (LP0042B). Solarbio (Beijing, China) supplied xylose (G8360) and soytone (S9500). Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) produces sucrose (10021463), glucose (63005518), and other standard chemical reagents.

Strain construction

Overlap-PCR was used to fuse ~1.9 kb of the A. fumigatus pyrG selectable marker, ~1.7 kb of the A. oryzae tef1a promoter, ~0.7 kb of mScarlet3 with a 5× (Gly-Ala) flexible linker, and ~1.3 kb of the hsfA fragment. The fusion PCR product was subsequently inserted into the pPTR II and transformed the CA14PTs strain to generate the mScarlet3-hsfA strain. The control strain mScarlet3 was created by inserting the pyrG-tef(p)-mScarlet3 fragment fused with the ~0.7 kb trpC terminator into the CA14PTs strain. To delete the hsfA gene, a ~ 4.5 kb hsfA deletion cassette containing ~1.5 kb upstream and ~1.5 kb downstream portions of hsfA and a ~ 1.9 kb pyrG fragment was generated by fusion PCR and subsequently transformed into the CA14PTs strain. The transformants were verified using PCR and Southern blotting. Gene complementation was achieved by reintroducing an endogenous hsfA expression segment on a pPTR II plasmid into the hsfA+/- strain. The DN-hsfA and HA-hsfA strains were generated using similar procedures as the hsfA+/- strain. The DN-hsfA recombination cassette included the upstream fragment of hsfA, a pyrG fragment, a ~ 1.6 kb Penicillium chrysogenum xylP promoter, a ~ 1.7 kb hsfA C-terminal truncated fragment with a stop codon, and the downstream fragment of hsfA. The HA-hsfA recombination cassette was produced by combining the upstream fragment of hsfA, a pyrG fragment, a ~ 1.5 kb A. nidulans gpdA promoter, the 3×HA tag with a 5× (Gly-Ala) flexible linker, and a ~ 1.3 kb hsfA fragment. To obtain overexpression strains of the target genes in the hsfA+/- strain, DNA fragments containing the tef1a promoter, corresponding gene CDS segments, and the trpC terminator were inserted into the pPTR II plasmid and then transformed into the hsfA+/- strain. The oligonucleotides used for strain construction and identification are listed in SI Appendix Table S2.

Phenotypic analysis of different stains

To study the growth and spore production of the hsfA+/-, hsfA-C, and six gene overexpression strains, approximately 103 fresh spores were inoculated at the center of the PDA plate (60 × 15 mm) and incubated at 29°C, 37°C, or 42°C for 5 days. The colony diameter was measured manually. Conidia were counted quantitatively according to the previous method [33]. For the spore germination test, about 103 fresh spores were placed on a glass slide pre-coated with PDA and cultivated at 29°C or 37°C. Conidial germination was observed with a microscope. In the DN-hsfA induction test, fresh spores or young mycelia from the GMM solid medium were seeded on the GMM and XMM media, respectively, then grown for 5 days and photographed. For sclerotial development, about 103 fresh spores were inoculated to the center of CM medium and cultivated at 29°C for 14 days or 37°C for 7 days. The procedure for counting sclerotia follows the previous method [33]. To measure aflatoxin production, fresh spores were added to 20 mL of YESSB until the final concentration reached 105 spores/mL. They were kept at 29°C in the dark for 7 days. The aflatoxin was extracted from the growth medium with 1/2 V dichloromethane and then air-dried. At the same time, the mycelium from each strain was dried and weighed. The extracted toxin was diluted in the appropriate amount of dichloromethane based on the biomass and then evaluated using thin layer chromatography. Each experiment involved three biological replicates.

Virulence assay

To assess the pathogenicity to plant hosts, infection tests with peanut and maize seeds were carried out in accordance with the previously reported approach [34]. Pathogenicity to animal hosts was assessed in zebrafish using the previously established method [35] with a few changes. Fresh spores were resuspended in sterile PBS with 2% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP40, Merck) and 0.5% phenol red (1072410005, Merck) at a concentration of 1.5 × 107/mL. Two nanoliters of spore stocks (~30 spores) were microinjected into the yolk center of anesthetized larvae at 2 days post fertilization. Following infection, the larvae were immediately returned to the recovery tanks and housed in an incubator with a regulated temperature (29°C) and a light-dark cycle for 14 h and 10 h. Larvae were closely monitored for 1 week and mortality was recorded every 24 h. Each treatment contained 35 larvae, and the experiments were conducted three times. The laboratory protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University.

Quantification of gene expression

To evaluate the expression level of selected genes under DN-hsfA induction, about 105 spores were inoculated into 50 mL of GMM broth and grown for 24 h. Mycelia were collected and rinsed with sterile distilled water and cultivated in XMM liquid medium for 30 min. The non-induced control was grown synchronously in a GMM broth. To determine specific gene expression in the hsfA+/- strain, mycelia were harvested after 24 h following spore incubation in PDB. Fresh mycelia were thoroughly ground in liquid nitrogen before being extracted for RNA. Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR analysis were carried out as previously described [33]. The β-tubulin gene was used as an internal control. The primers used in qRT-PCR are provided in SI Appendix Table S3.

Fluorescence microscopy

To determine the subcellular localization of mScarlet3-HsfA, transformed mycelia in agar were collected after 48 h and fixed with 70% ethanol for roughly 1 hour at room temperature. After removing ethanol, mycelia were rinsed with PBS and incubated in 1 µg/mL DAPI (D9542, Merck) for 15 min. After three washes with PBS, the mycelia were mounted on a glass slide and photographed with a Leica DM4000 B LED microscope (Leica, Germany).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

Chromatin preparation was performed as follows: After 24 h in PDB, the HA-hsfA strain culture was harvested and washed with sterile distilled water. Mycelia were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 20 min and gently shaken at room temperature before being quenched with 0.5 M glycine for 10 min. The fixed mycelia were collected, rinsed with cold sterile distilled water, and then freeze-dried overnight. Before lysis, freeze-dried mycelia were treated 5 times by beating for 1 min with a 100 μL volume of silica beads using a Bullet Blender (Next Advance, USA), with cooling for 1 min between each cycle. The resultant samples were well mixed with ice-cold 10 mL ChIP lysis buffer before being sonicated on ice with the ultrasonic homogenizer JY92-IIDN (SCIENTZ, China) at 55% power in 10-s intervals for a total of 15 min. Chromatin concentration and size (100 to 500 bp) were measured on a 2% agarose (BY-R0100, BIOWEST, Spain) gel. Sonicated samples were centrifuged at 4°C to remove insoluble debris, and the resulting supernatant containing prepared chromatins was stored at −80°C. Chromatin immunoprecipitation was carried out using anti-HA magnetic beads (HY-K0201, MCE, USA), as previously described [36]. The enrichment of DNA fragments was evaluated by qPCR. The oligonucleotide sequences used for ChIP-qPCR are shown in SI Appendix Table S4.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

Recombinant A. flavus HsfA-His14 was produced in E. coli BL21 (DE3) using the T7 promoter and purified with Ni-NTA agarose (70691–3, Merck). A 5’FAM-labeled probe was prepared by annealing the oligonucleotides. To eliminate nonspecific binding, purified HsfA-His14 protein (2–8 μg) was mixed with EMSA binding buffer (GS005, Beyotime, China) and allowed to stand at room temperature (23–25°C) for 10 min. Add a 5’FAM-labeled probe to a 25 μL reaction system and incubate at room temperature for 20 min. Samples were run on 6% acrylamide, 0.5X TBE, 2.5% glycerol gels (GS002, Beyotime, China) at 100 V and kept at low temperature. The gel was then imaged using an Amersham Imager 600 RGB GEL Imaging System (GE Healthcare, USA). For the competition assay, a 100-fold unlabeled cold probe was added prior to the labeled probe. To confirm HsfA’s specific binding to the motif 5’-nGAAn-3’ [37], the selected gene promoter containing the predicted HSE was altered into 5’-nCCCn-3’ or 5’-nTCCn-3.’ In addition, BSA served as a negative control for EMSA analysis. The oligonucleotides used in EMSA are displayed in SI Appendix Table S4.

Structure prediction and molecular docking

The A. flavus HsfA DNA-binding domain (HsfA-DBD) was identified using BLASTP with the human HSF1 DNA-binding domain (HsHSF1-DBD, PDB: 5D5U) as the query. The HsfA-DBD tertiary structure was predicted with AlphaFold3. To model the protein-DNA complex, we used AlphaFold3’s prediction for HsfA-DBD coupled to an HSE-like duplex (5′-CTGGAAC-3′/5′-GTTCCAG-3′). The top-ranked complex, as determined by interface pLDDT/PAE metrics, exhibited ipTM = 0.90 and pTM = 0.82, indicating high reliability. Three small-molecule inhibitors of human HSF1 were curated: KRIBB11 (CAS 342639-96–7), DTHIB (CAS 897326-30–6), and homoharringtonine (CAS 26833–87-4). Prior to docking, the ligand 3D structures were constructed using appropriate protonation and energy minimization techniques. Proteins were prepared for inhibitor docking to HsfA-DBD and HsHSF1-DBD (with the DNA-bound state used where indicated) by removing nonessential heteroatoms, adding hydrogens, and assigning AutoDock-compatible atom types and Gasteiger charges. The binding pocket was defined around the conserved basic/recognition segment (HsHSF1 residues KHNNMASFVRQLNMY and HsfA residues ELFKHNNYASFVRQLN). Docking was performed with AutoDock Vina. Poses were ordered based on Vina score, and the highest-scoring, non-clashing pose for each ligand was retained for analysis. Visualizations of protein-ligand complexes were generated in PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 10.0, except for gene-expression data, which were analyzed with REST 2009 software [38]. Relative gene-expression levels were calculated using the integrated PairWise Fixed Reallocation Randomization Test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The other normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between two groups by a two-tailed Student’s t-test or among three groups by ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Overall survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier curves and compared by the log-rank test. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated.

Results

Heat stress increases baseline hsfA transcription in A. flavus

By searching the FungiDB database (https://fungidb.org/fungidb/app), we found the A. flavus homolog of S. cerevisiae Hsf1 and designated it HsfA. A phylogenetic study of functionally defined and hypothetical Hsf1 orthologues revealed that the HsfA of A. flavus is more closely linked to that of A. fumigatus (Figure 1(A)). A. flavus HsfA, like other orthologues, includes a conserved HSF domain, suggesting a potential conservative role (Figure 1(A)). A quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed that hsfA transcript levels in A. flavus hyphae maintained at 37°C were markedly higher than in those grown at 29°C, while a heat shock shift from 29°C to 37°C also resulted in a pronounced transcriptional increase of hsfA (Figure 1(B)). Additionally, in vivo microscopic imaging of the red fluorescent protein mScarlet3-tagged HsfA revealed that it was accumulated in the nucleus of young hyphae (Figure 1(C)). High sequence homology, heat-responsive transcription, and nuclear localization all pointed to A. flavus HsfA being a functional ortholog of classical heat shock transcription factors.

The genome of A. flavus contains two copies of hsfA

To investigate hsfA function in A. flavus, we created a gene deletion cassette with homologous replacement (Figure S1). Southern blotting revealed two intact hsfA copies, with the deletion cassette successfully replacing just one (Figure 1(D)). Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed that hsfA mRNA in the hsfA+/- mutant was about half of the wild type (Figure 1(E)). Repeated attempts to disrupt the remaining copy of hsfA produced no viable transformants, suggesting that complete loss of hsfA is likely lethal in A. flavus.

Single-copy deletion of hsfA attenuates fungal developments, pathogenicity and aflatoxin biosynthesis

Phenotypic profiling on PDA media revealed that hsfA+/- colonies were consistently smaller and produced significantly fewer conidia than wild type or complemented strains at 29°C, 37°C, and 42°C (Figure 2(A,C) and Figure S2). Virulence assays confirmed this defect: on peanut and maize kernels, the mutant exhibited reduced surface colonization and diminished sporulation compared to controls (Figure 2(B,D,E)). In zebrafish larvae, hsfA+/- spores appear to be avirulent – no mortality or morphological changes were observed up to 7 dpi – whereas wild type or complementation strains caused rapid death from 2 dpi onwards, with numerous hyphae growing out of the yolk sac and even penetrating the surrounding tissues (Figure 2(F,G)). Furthermore, the hsfA+/- strain produced far fewer sclerotia than the wild type (Figure 2(H) and Figure S2D). The amount of aflatoxin B1 produced on YESSB medium and infected seeds was similarly much lower than in the wild type (Figure 2(I,J)). These findings suggest that HsfA is essential for hyphal development, conidial and sclerotial formation, host infection, and aflatoxin production.

Induction of dominant negative DN-HsfA blocks spore germination

Phenotypic analysis of hsfA+/- mutants revealed the function of hsfA; however, we were unable to obtain null mutant for validation. We then tried replacing the endogenous promoter of the remaining hsfA copy in the hsfA+/- strain with an inducible xylP promoter but were unsuccessful. We sought to generate a dominant negative hsfA mutant (DN-hsfA) regulated by xylP based on the human HSF1ΔC mutant [39]. DN-hsfA can be induced by xylose to produce truncated HsfAΔC without a C-terminal putative transcriptional activation domain (TAD, 576–788 residues) (Figure 3(A,B)). On non-induction media, transcriptional suppression of DN-hsfA allowed residual growth, although colony diameters were considerably smaller than wild type; however, induction of DN-hsfA abolished all hyphal outgrowth from spores, despite the existence of an intact hsfA copy in the genome (Figure 3(C)). Thus, DN-HsfA effectively inhibits endogenous normal HsfA activity. In contrast, transferring pre-formed vegetative mycelium onto an inducing medium resulted in an irregularly small colony (Figure 3(D)). These findings indicated that hsfA is an essential gene for spore germination rather than hyphal growth, albeit it remains necessary for normal hyphal growth.

HsfA deficiency impacts the expression of putative spore germination-related genes

To investigate how HsfA regulates spore germination, we measured transcript abundance in strain DN-hsfA following xylose induction. Induction of DN-HsfA resulted in decreased mRNA levels of hsp70, hsp90, and hsp98 within 30 min (Figure 3(E)), as expected in orthologous systems [40,41]. Concurrently, we examined homologues involved in sporulation or germination across species. Induction of DN-HsfA markedly reduced brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, stuA, velB, and vosA compared to the wild type, but laeA and sfaD remained unchanged (Figure 3(F)).

We next examined the correlation between hsfA deficiency and spore germination in the hsfA+/- strain. At 37°C, wild type spores germinated within 6 hours and reached nearly 100% by 12 h, while hsfA+/- spores were delayed until ~9 h and achieved just ~4% after 12 h (Figure 3(G,I)). Delays and declines were comparable at 29°C (Figure S3A,B) and 42°C (Figure S3C,D). Transcript quantification revealed that brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, velB, and vosA were all considerably down-regulated in the hsfA+/- strain compared to the wild type (Figure 3(H)), consistent with the DN-HsfA induction findings. Our results strongly implicated HsfA as a direct transcriptional activator of several germination genes. In contrast, stuA levels remained constant between hsfA+/- and wild type (Figure 3(H)); therefore, stuA was excluded from further study.

HsfA binds directly to the HSEs of target genes to activate transcription

To identify the regulatory relationship between HsfA and the six germination-associated genes, we analyzed their upstream promoter regions using JASPAR for conventional heat shock elements (HSEs). Multiple motifs were predicted within each promoter (Figure 4(A) and Figure S4A). We created strains of A. flavus expressing HA-tagged HsfA (Figure 4(B)) and E. coli producing His14-tagged HsfA (Figure 4(C)) to test HsfA binding to the putative HSEs in vivo and in vitro. As demonstrated by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR), HsfA significantly occupied HSE-containing promoter regions of all six targets in mycelia cultured at 29°C (Figure 4(D) and Figure S4B). Shifting cultures to 37°C increased promoter-bound HsfA enrichment for each gene (Figure S4C), suggesting temperature-dependent improvement of DNA binding. Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was used to determine whether the predicted HSEs are the actual HsfA binding sites on the promoters. Purified recombinant HsfA, as shown in Figure 4(E) and Figure S4D, hindered fluorescently labeled HSE-containing oligonucleotides derived from the brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, velB, and vosA promoters, indicating HsfA’s direct occupancy. Competition assays revealed specificity, with excess unlabeled natural HSE (“cold”) but not mutagenized HSE (“mutant”) inhibiting binding. Despite the deletion of its TAD domain, DN-HsfA can still bind to flbC‘s HSE via its intact DNA binding domain (DBD). These data suggested that nuclear HsfA can directly bind to the conventional HSEs of its target genes, activating their transcription.

Overexpression of each HsfA target partially corrects the phenotypic defects of hsfA+/-

To determine whether HsfA-mediated up-regulation of brlA, fksP, flbC, sntB, velB, and vosA is enough to correct germination defects, we created each single-gene overexpression plasmid and transformed it into hsfA+/- protoplasts, respectively. Figure 5(A–C) shows that after 6 h of cultivation at 37°C, approximately 35% of wild type spores had begun to germinate, compared to none in the hsfA+/- strain. Overexpressing any target recovered germination to between 3% and 13% of seeded spores during the same interval (Figure 5(A–C)). By 12 h, nearly all WT spores had produced elongated hyphae, whereas only ~5% of hsfA+/- conidia formed short germ tubes. Conversely, more than 90% of overexpression spores had completed germination, albeit with slightly shorter filaments than the wild type. Similar trends were seen at both lower and higher temperatures, with germination beginning later at lower temperatures (Figure S5). These data show that overexpressing any HsfA target in the hsfA+/- strain partially restores timely conidial germination.

Since hyphae grow into colonies during germination and eventually mature into new conidia, we were not shocked to observe that all overexpression strains, with the exception of fksP, produced more spores and larger colonies than hsfA+/- on PDA plates. Although the fksP overexpression strain’s colonies seemed greener than those of the hsfA+/- strain, the spore yields remained similar (Figure 5(B–E) and Figure S6). All of these overexpression strains, however, were more virulent toward plants and animals: their conidia produced more asexual spores and colonized peanut and maize seeds more effectively (Figure 5(F–H)). After 7 days, the majority of zebrafish larvae that were infected with any overexpression strain died. However, throughout the same time span, there was no mortality among those challenged with hsfA+/- spores (Figure 5(I)). In the hsfA+/- strain, germination and downstream traits are only partially restored by overexpressing a single HsfA target.

Human HSF1 inhibitor are ineffective against A. flavus

Human HSF1 is a prospective therapeutic target for malignancies, as it facilitates their initiation, progression, invasion, and migration [42]. KRIBB11, DTHIB, and homoharringtonine (HHT) are small molecule inhibitors that have been shown to bind directly to HSF1 [42–44]. We investigated the inhibitory effects of these three chemicals on A. flavus. The results demonstrated that inoculating spores on GMM medium containing each HSF1 inhibitor had no significant influence on the formation of A. flavus colonies, implying that these chemicals are ineffective against A. flavus (Figure 6(A)).

To elucidate the molecular basis underlying the ineffectiveness of human HSF1 inhibitors against A. flavus, we conducted in silico docking studies using KRIBB11, DTHIB, and HHT with DNA-bound models of both HSF1-DBD and HsfA-DBD (Figure 6B). In the HSF1-DBD, all three compounds consistently localized within the DNA-recognition cleft, directly overlapping the protein-DNA interface. In contrast, docking analyses with HsfA-DBD demonstrated that each ligand adopted orientations in closer proximity to HsfA, yet remained spatially displaced from the DNA-contacting surface, leaving the recognition helix unobstructed. Consequently, these chemicals do not effectively occlude the fungal DBD-DNA interface and so are unlikely to disrupt HsfA-DNA interaction.

Discussion

The prevention and control of A. flavus contamination is still crucial. Current strategies focus on pre-harvest biological control, post-harvest storage management, and antifungal development [45,46]; a better understanding of its biology, particularly the identification of key pathogenic regulators, should speed up the discovery of more effective interventions and novel antifungal compounds.

Spore germination is the start of a new life cycle for dormant spores and a critical step in host infection. Previous studies have primarily focused on chemical inhibitors, microbial antagonists, and natural compounds that impede this process [47–49]; however, transcriptional control has received less attention [50–52]. Our study demonstrates that A. flavus heat shock transcription factor HsfA is a key regulator required for spore germination. It can directly bind to the promoter regions of downstream target genes to activate their transcription, thereby controlling fungal development and pathogenicity.

The importance of HsfA is reflected by gene copy number: unlike most A. flavus genes – and unlike the single-copy hsfA reported in A. fumigatus – this species retains two copies. What selective advantage does this redundancy confer? Duplicate copies are not always redundant. In diploid organisms, haploinsufficiency arising from deletion or mutation of a single allele frequently impairs fitness because the remaining allele cannot sustain adequate protein dosage [53]. Mouse SUMO1 haploinsufficiency produces cleft lip/palate [54]; human OTULIN haploinsufficiency compromises cell-intrinsic immunity against Staphylococcus α-toxin [55]. The absence of a HSF1 gene copy has little effect on diploid Candida albicans and Cryptococcus neoformans, while the HSF1 gene copy number is crucial for other species [27,28]. C. elegans with more copies of the hsf-1 gene have longer lifespans and higher resistance to heat and oxidative stress [31]. Research on Ashidan yaks has shown that copy number variation (CNV) of the HSF1 gene has a significant impact on their growth and development [56]. The observed abnormalities in germination, growth, sporulation, and virulence in our hsfA+/- strains are consistent with typical haploid deficiency symptoms (Figure 2), indicating that hsfA gene dosage is critical for A. flavus survival, and additional copies of hsfA may act as a decisive buffer for fungi to resist environmental or genetic stress.

Except for our previously described ndk gene, few duplicated genes in Aspergilli have been identified [57]. A. oryzae, a domesticated clade of A. flavus, contains several copies of α-amylase and AO090005001597 genes, which may be linked to Tc1/mariner transposons [58,59]. More research is needed to determine whether similar mechanisms explain A. flavus‘s dual hsfA copies.

After obtaining the hsfA+/- strain, we were unable to delete the remaining copies of hsfA or replace its endogenous promoter with an inducible xylP promoter; in addition, the transformation efficiency of complementary and overexpression plasmids is significantly lower than in other backgrounds. In addition to simple changes in gene dosage, this sustained failure of gene manipulation prompts us to rethink why hsfA+/- protoplasts are so difficult to transform. We speculate that it is likely due to the heat intolerance of hsfA+/- protoplasts, as HsfA controls the transcriptional expression of chaperone genes, which are important molecules for maintaining protein homeostasis when cells are subjected to heat stress (Figure 3E). During conventional transformation, hsfA+/- protoplasts need to be embedded in soft agar at 50–60°C, and losing one copy of hsfA impairs their heat tolerance.

An alternative explanation for our inability to isolate a null mutant is that hsfA is essential; total deletion is lethal. The influence of DN-HsfA expression offers indirect support. In both humans and yeast, HSF1 forms trimers that bind to HSEs and activate transcription [60,61]. DN-HsfA has a full DBD and trimerization domain but no C-terminal TAD; EMSA verifies its normal DNA binding activity (Figure S4E). Consequently, homotrimeric DN-HsfA or heterotrimers containing both DN-HsfA and native HsfA are likely to occupy the HSE sites without activating transcription, competitively rejecting the functioning HsfA trimers and thereby silencing downstream target genes.

The currently discovered Hsf1 targets are primarily molecular chaperones, with few findings on non-chaperone targets [32]. In this study, we found six direct targets of A. flavus HsfA, whose fungal orthologues encode transcription factors BrlA, Flbc, and VosA, β-1,3-glucan synthase FksP, epigenetic reader SntB, and velvet complex component VelB [62–67]. Overexpression of each HsfA target gene improves conidial germination, growth, sporulation, and virulence in the hsfA+/- strain, revealing their pleiotropic roles in this fungus. However, none of the overexpression strains resemble the wild type, implying that a single gene supplementation is insufficient for complete restoration (Figure 4 and 5). Thus, HsfA most likely orchestrates germination and subsequent phenotypic features by coordinating the regulation of numerous downstream targets; more future work is required to identify HsfA’s undiscovered targets.

Given that A. flavus HsfA controls fungal morphology and pathogenicity, it could be a promising target for antifungal treatment. Human HSF1, an HsfA homolog, has been linked to a variety of malignant cancers and is considered as a prospective target for tumor therapy [68]. KRIBB11 is the first discovered HSF1 inhibitor that can directly bind to HSF1 in vitro and inhibit HSF1-dependent p-TEFb recruitment to the hsp70 promoter [43]. Another inhibitor, DTHIB, binds to HSF1 DBD and reduces HSF1 binding to target gene promoter regions by accelerating nuclear HSF1 degradation [44]. The compound HHT has recently been shown to bind to HSF1 and suppress the progression of pancreatic cancer [42]. Unfortunately, these chemicals are ineffective against A. flavus, which may be due to their inability to effectively block the fungal DBD-DNA interface (Figure 6). However, it should also be noted that there are differences in the structure of fungal cells and mammalian cells, especially in that fungal cell walls, cell membranes, and even nuclear envelopes may prevent these chemicals from accessing and binding to HsfA and exerting inhibitory effects. Regardless of the reasons why known human HSF1 inhibitors are ineffective against this fungi, designing specific small molecule inhibitors targeting fungal HsfA will be a feasible strategy for future antifungal drug development.

In summary, we establish A. flavus HsfA as an essential dual-copy regulator whose transcriptional activation of six non-chaperone target genes initiates conidial germination thereby orchestrating morphogenesis and pathogenicity (Figure 7) – highlighting unexplored avenues for targeting fungal virulence.

A diagram illustrating how HsfA-driven gene transcription regulates fungal morphogenesis and pathogenicity in A. flavus. The heat shock transcription factor HsfA is a master regulator of development and aflatoxin production in the fungal pathogen A. flavus.
