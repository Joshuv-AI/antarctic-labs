Beyond hyperglycaemia: diabetic retinopathy is a multifactorial complication of diabetes mellitus
Authors: Yu Zou, Hongyan Yang, Yu Lin, Qingyi Li, Yanan Bao, Xiaohui Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe remarkable researches have been made to clarify the pathogenesis of DR, but relatively little is known about it. The goal was to summarize the epidemiology and mechanism of DR, provide screening and biomarker information of DR and suggest the strategies for treatment and intervention of DR.MethodsThis article synthesizes recent findings on intricate interrelated patho­physiological mechanisms elicited by hyperglycaemia underlie the progression of DR.ResultsDR has long been considered a microvascular disorder, but accumulating evidences demonstrate that retinal neurodegeneration like activated microglial is involved as well. The progression of DR is underpinned by complex, interconnected pathophysiological mechanisms triggered by hyperglycaemia, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), methylglyoxal (MGO), Wnt signalling pathways, retinal neurodegeneration and other factors. Enhanced comprehension of the fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms, coupled with advancements in computer technology, fosters the development of innovative approaches to screening, diagnosis and early intervention for DR.ConclusionDR, a multifactorial complication of diabetes mellitus, necessitates continued research into precise screening methodologies and targeted therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, microvascular, neurodegeneration, biomarker, intervention

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM), a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycaemia, has seen significant improvements in preventive care and life expectancy over the past two decades [1]. Nevertheless, as the duration of DM heightens, the incidence and progression of its complications increase. Among these, diabetic retinopathy (DR) emerges as the most prevalent vision-threatening condition and the leading cause of blindness in working-age adults worldwide [2]. Of the 425 million people with DM estimated by the International Diabetes Federation in 2017 [3], over one in three have signs of DR, and approximately a third of these might have vision-threatening retinopathy, like diabetic macular oedema (DME) [4]. Besides, the existence of DR also manifests an increased risk of life-threatening systemic vascular complications, including stroke and cardiovascular mortality [5,6].

While strict glycaemic control remains foundational for early-stage DR management, its efficacy diminishes in advanced disease [7,8]. Thus, DR seems not to be merely associated with hyperglycaemia but is likewise intensively correlated to other risk elements, including hypertension, dyslipidaemia, pregnancy and puberty [6], as well as unknown variables, confirming that the etiopathogenesis of DR is complicated and multifactorial. Recent breakthroughs from multi-omics studies, molecular imaging and randomized trials have furthered our understanding of the pathophysiology underlying DR [6,9–11]. This paper summarizes the present state of knowledge of DR from epidemiological, pathophysiological and clinical perspectives.

Epidemiology

Prevalence and incidence of DR

DR, a chronic progressive disease that occurs in either type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) or type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), can affect vision and even cause blindness without control or intervention. Among sight, sound, smell, taste and touch, the five senses recognized by Aristotle, sight is absolutely the one that people fear losing. Globally, DR accounted for 2.6% of all blindness in 2010, representing an increase from 2.1% in 1990 [12]. With the estimated 629 million DM worldwide in 2045 [3], directly elevating the prevalence and incidence of DR, 40% of DM in its population have some form of DR.

About one in three patients with T2DM and almost one-fifth of T1DM patients [6] will develop or have had DR. Age-adjusted and sex-adjusted prevalences of DR and DME were 17.4% and 6.6%, respectively [13]. In a comprehensive analysis of 33 studies comprising 55,445 participants, the overall prevalence of DR, DME and proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) was determined to be 29.6%, 7.1% and 7.4%, respectively [14]. The incidence of DR can be estimated by type of DM [15], duration of DM, follow-up duration, region and so forth, which can provide more information for diabetic care, management and others (Figure 1). The annual incidence of DR varies substantially across studies, ranging from 2.2% to 12.7% depending on methodological approaches and cohort characteristics [7]. To determine precise incidence rates, more high-quality relevant studies are needed, with greater attention to data collection across diverse regions, ethnicities, genders and follow-up periods, by uniform criteria.

Incidence of DR in T2DM. Symptoms of DR may be observed as early as 5 years from the onset of T2DM and incidence of DR in T2DM significantly elevates with the increased duration of DM [15].

Risk factors for DR

Several well-recognized and significant risk factors for DR, including poor glycaemic control, are presented in Table 1. Glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is no doubt a key one among them and significantly affects the progression of DR in a series of studies [16–20]. A 1% elevation in HbA1c confers a risk of PDR equivalent to that associated with 6.4 additional years of T1DM duration [16]. Consequently, stringent glycaemic targets (HbA1c <6.5% for T1DM [19]) are recommended based on evidence accounting for ethnicity, geographic variation [17–19], diabetes subtype [18,19] and sustained intensive control [8,20]. Furthermore, HbA1c variability [17], an emerging but potential predictor for DR, correlates with microvascular complications, cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality of DM independently of high HbA1c. Considering the association with DR, fructosamine and glycated albumin [21] have a similar role to HbA1c, and more evidence will be needed.

However, HbA1c cannot fully account for the onset and/or severity degree of DR. Diabetes duration, recognized as another key risk factor, progressively increases the proportion of DR in individuals with DM, which is almost 90% after 30 years of progression [15]. Besides, early-onset T2DM, defined as diagnosis before age 40, demonstrated a 1.9-fold higher risk of DR compared with late-onset T2DM. As a modifiable risk factor, bilateral brachial blood pressure measurement is recommended during initial DR evaluations. This recommendation is based on the finding that DR is associated with systolic interarm differences of ≥15 mmHg in an English cohort study [22]. Lipids have long been recognized as a risk factor, though their underlying mechanism remains ambiguous. However, the retina in diabetic conditions is notably susceptible to lipid peroxidation, attributed to the elevated levels of long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids [23]. In addition, similar studies will be needed to address whether statins are protective against some forms of microvascular disease like DR, but statin users indeed showed a lower cumulative incidence of DR compared with non-statin users [24].

Different from most population-based studies, a prospective cohort study discovered that men with T2DM are not only more likely to develop microvascular complications, including DR, but also have a higher possibility of co-occurrence of multiple microvascular complications. Moreover, there may exist sex differences between the benefits of current treatment approaches for DR [26]. In terms of childhood and adolescence with DM, early monitoring for DR is necessary due to the higher prevalence of complications and comorbidities [19,34]. What’s more, bariatric surgery [35], physical activity [36] and resting metabolic rate [37] are significantly associated with, and even substantially contribute to, the presence and severity of DR. These findings, once corroborated, imply that subjects with DM should perform regular physical activity and keep fit to reduce the risk for DR.

Many other factors (including systemic and lifestyle ones), like alcohol consumption [29], gastric emptying [38], future cognitive decline [31], antipsychotic medication [32] and the like [39], have a relationship with DR as reported by some epidemiological studies. However, some of these factors require more consolidated evidence to support. Besides what was mentioned above, discoveries from epidemiological studies indicate that DR, neuropathy and nephropathy share common risk factors [40], that is to say, the presence of DR provides a hint of other organs’ microcirculatory damage in DM.

Pathophysiological mechanisms

Our comprehension of the intricate pathophysiological mechanisms driving the progression of DR is in a state of continuous evolution, propelled by groundbreaking research. The relevance of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in the pathogenesis of DR, especially for PDR, is indisputable, but that is not all [6]. As a multifactor disease, the pathophysiological mechanisms of DR are complex. Here, part of these mechanisms, including methylglyoxal (MGO), Wnt signalling pathways, and others, are described.

Vascular aspect

The retina, a significant/crucial component of the eye, is a highly metabolically active tissue characterized by substantial nutrient and oxygen demands, which promote vascular formation. Once abnormal retinal metabolic alterations occur, such as hyperglycaemia, it would trigger vascular pathologies. Thus, as a classic example of hyperglycaemia-induced microvascular dysfunction, DR has long been thought to be a vascular disease.

In light of the new classification by the American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO), DR is categorized into four overlapping clinical stages on the basis of vascular lesions and neovascularization (Table 2 and Figure 2). With the progression of DR, intraretinal microvascular abnormalities, intraretinal haemorrhages, microaneurysms, neovascularization, pre-retinal or vitreous haemorrhages, and even retinal detachment will successively present. All these symptoms are correlated with alterations in the capillary basement membrane, loss of pericytes, localized proliferation of endothelial cells (ECs), lack of capillary perfusion and so forth [15,41].

Chronic hyperglycaemia represents the principal aetiological factor in the pathogenesis and advancement of DR; however, it is noteworthy that the susceptibility of cellular and tissue compartments to hyperglycaemia-induced pathophysiological alterations varies significantly. Concerning how hyperglycaemia induces retina damage, involving the action of MGO therein, has been elucidated by Brownlee with a hypothesis [42]. MGO, serving as the principal precursor in the non-enzymatic glycation of proteins and DNA, is predominantly generated as a metabolic byproduct of glycolysis and detoxified by the glyoxalase system in physiological conditions. The elevated level of MGO by hyperglycaemia promotes the progression of DR by enhancing the development of vascular complications [43]. Along with hyperglycaemia, hypoxia and inflammation are likely key determinants of MGO formation. Additionally, dysregulation of glyoxalase pathway enzymes, particularly glyoxalase 1 (Glo1, the rate-limiting enzyme), under these pathological conditions may exacerbate MGO elevation through impaired detoxification mechanisms.

Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) derived from MGO, as well as MGO itself, can damage tissues and organs, thereby impairing their structure and function [44]. More importantly, as a modulator of insulin resistance, MGO may contribute to functional and/or structural abnormalities in the insulin molecule, subsequently leading to resistance [45]. Moreover, MGO may be directly linked to insulin resistance inasmuch as its interference in the intricate molecular pathways of insulin signalling in β-cells, ECs and muscle cells [46,47]. All above implies the essential action of MGO in the progression of DR.

VEGF, secreted by various retinal cell types like pericytes, is no doubt a pivotal element in DR. VEGF is a necessity for the development of normal vascular, and embryonic lethality may be caused by deletion of one VEGF allele [48]. In view of the fact that VEGF is instrumental in preserving EC integrity through antiapoptotic signalling and supporting vessel survival by pericyte [49], it is also a survival factor. Whether triggered by hyperglycaemia, hypoxia, or ischaemia, increased VEGF secretion subsequently leads to retinal neovascularization and excessive vascular permeability.

Amongst the VEGF family, which consists of five structurally similar ligands (VEGF-A, -B, -C, -D and placenta growth factor (PLGF)), VEGF commonly refers to VEGF-A, which stands out as the most effective mediator of angiogenesis [50]. The human VEGF-A gene, located on chromosome 6p21.3, consists of 8 exons separated by 7 introns [51]. Alternative splicing generates multiple isoforms, such as VEGF-A165 (where ‘165’ denotes the number of encoded amino acids). However, alternative splicing of VEGF-A results in splice variants, proteins of identical length yet with variations in their C-terminal amino acid sequences (Figure 3) [52]. Most importantly, splice variants show the opposite effect, ‘pro-angiogenic’ or ‘anti-angiogenic’.

Schematic diagram of VEGF-A. VEGF-A is usually referred to as VEGF. Due to alternative splicing of VEGF-A, splice variants with same length proteins but with different amino acids at the C-terminus are generated, namely VEGFxxxa isoforms (where xxx denotes the number of amino acids, e.g., VEGF121a, VEGF165a, VEGF189a) with Cys-Asp-Lys-Pro-Arg-Arg (e.g. VEGF165a, pro-angiogenic isoforms) and VEGFxxxb subfamily (e.g., VEGF121b, VEGF165b, VEGF189b) with Ser-Leu-Thr-Arg-Lys-Asp (e.g. VEGF165b, anti-angiogenic isoforms).

VEGF-A165, a 45 kDa primary heparin-binding homodimeric glycoprotein, is a predominant member among various isoforms, followed by VEGF-A121. VEGF165 exerts pivotal regulatory effects on retinal angiogenesis in both physiological and pathological contexts. In pathological retinal neovascularization, VEGF-A165a acts as a dominant immunomodulator, recruiting leukocytes, macrophages and monocytes to amplify inflammation through a positive feedback loop [53]. The dysregulated expression of other isoforms also contributes to pathogenesis; for instance, an abnormal VEGF120/VEGF164 ratio or their deficiency can drive pathological angiogenesis [54].

Three varied tyrosine kinase receptors, including VEGFR-3, VEGFR-2 (Flk-1, mouse; KDR, human) and VEGFR-1(Flt-1), bind to VEGF in an overlapping pattern [50]. Despite their different affinities and potencies, both Flt-1 and KDR/Flk-1 can bind to VEGF-A; however, they mediate divergent downstream effects. VEGFR-1 may exert dual activity, but there is more VEGFR-2 expression during DR development. Neuropilin-1 (NRP1), a receptor specific to the VEGF165 isoform, can be co-expressed with the VEGFR-2 co-receptor in ECs. With an enhanced up to 6-fold effect [55], a series of changes will occur when VEGF165a binds to NRP1 and KDR/Flk-1, including receptor dimerization, intracellular domain rotation and autophosphorylation [56]. Other than the different C-terminal end mentioned above and other reasons, lack of Cys-160 in the adjacent C-terminal sequence of VEGF165b may cause no binding between VEGF165b and VEGFR-2, subsequently leading to incomplete rotation and inefficient autophosphorylation and producing ‘anti-angiogenic’ [56]. Except for angiogenesis, the breakdown of the blood-retina barrier (BRB) is another crucial VEGF-mediated action in the etiopathogenesis of DR. Therefore, anti-VEGF therapy holds promise for DR management, as supported by the efficacy of intravitreal anti-VEGF agent administration. However, VEGF is not all for DR; even the inhibitory effects of pan-VEGF antagonists should be reconsidered.

Many extracellular and intracellular signals are engaged in angiogenesis by coordinated regulation, with the Wnt signalling pathway being a notable participant. Back in the early 1980s, the Wnt signalling pathway was first discovered by Nusse and Varmus [57], as the name is a combination coined through ‘Wingless’ and ‘Intl’. In general, this pathway is categorized into two primary types: the canonical and the non-canonical Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway. The latter encompasses the Wnt/Ca2+ pathway and the planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway. Whereas the former serves as a transcriptional co-activator through β-catenin, which is the most well-characterized and has been extensively elucidated in embryogenesis, cancer, ocular diseases and other contexts.

In mammals, 19 cysteine-rich, highly hydrophobic Wnt glycoproteins (each approximately 370 amino acids in length and exhibiting sequence diversity) have been identified. These proteins are highly conserved across numerous species. In addition, Wnts feature a cleavable N-terminal signal peptide that facilitates their secretion [58]. The causative role of aberrantly elevated Wnt signalling for pathological ocular neovascularization heralds the possibility of ameliorating pathological angiogenesis in DR through modulating Wnt signalling. Elevated Wnt signalling components, including β-catenin and LRP5/6 [58,59], are consistently observed in retinal sections from both DR patients and animal models, regardless of disease stage (non-proliferative or proliferative DR).

Meanwhile, several inhibitors of aberrant Wnt signalling, such as DKK1, can ameliorate retinal neovascularization to varying degrees [60]. Additionally, two further findings from animal models support the involvement of the Wnt signalling pathway in DR: first, Mab2F1-mediated inhibition of LRP6 attenuates vascular leakage and inflammation [61]; second, serine protease inhibitor A3K reduces fibrogenic activity by downregulating the expression of fibronectin and connective tissue growth factor [62]. Notably, targeted disruption of this pathway in Müller cells, achieved through conditional β-catenin knockout, ameliorates ischaemia-induced neovascularization in animal models [63]. Besides, the non-canonical Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway is proposed to be in regulating VEGF availability to form a vessel [64]. Although its mechanism is still unclear, an integrated signalling network for these two types of Wnt signalling pathways to interact is more acceptable.

Neural aspect

Given the discovery of microvascular alterations in the retina, DR has been traditionally regarded as a quintessential microvascular condition for an extended period. However, overt degeneration of retinal neurons was found in post-mortem patient samples of DM in the 1960s, and then neuroretinal alterations in DR were taken into consideration in later research [65–67]. In addition, the retina, a transparent neural tissue layer derived from the optic cup, comprises four primary neuronal types: ganglion cells, bipolar cells, amacrine cells and photoreceptors. Dysfunction in any of these neuronal populations can lead to visual impairment. Thus, DR encompasses not only microvascular pathology but also retinal neurodegeneration.

As retinal neurodegeneration, DR is characterized by two hallmarks, glial activation and neural apoptosis. For glial activation, aberrant expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) occurs even early in the course of DM in Müller cells. This response may contribute to dysregulation of vascular permeability, hemodynamics and neuronal survival in the retina [68]. Additionally, microglia derived from haematopoietic stem cells are a population of innate immune [66], relatively long-lived and self-renewing cells [67]. In adults, ramified microglia mostly exist in the inner retinal layers [69]. In contrast, they are rarely observed in the inner nuclear layer and are completely absent from the outer nuclear layer. Once for ages, as quiescent cells, resident ramified microglia had been considered to require activation.

Nevertheless, as part of the central nervous system (CNS), they adopt the role of tissue macrophages, which are continuously in motion, retracting, extending and constantly scanning their microenvironment [70]. In both DR animal models and diabetic patients, activated microglia are profoundly involved in neurodegeneration and have a pivotal role in regulating DR progression [71,72]. Microglial activation involves diverse functional, phenotypic and morphological alterations. In vitro studies indicate that activated microglia exhibit context-dependent polarization, ranging from pro-inflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes [73]. However, this process is highly regulated in vivo: their abundance is governed by specific conditions (the extent of dysfunction, infection, or tissue damage), and their phenotype adapts to meet demands [74].

In DR, hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, hypoxia, ischemia and/or endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress can lead to uncontrolled microglia activation [75]. Phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) is involved in microglial activation [76–79]. This signalling cascade can be triggered by diverse stimuli and may subsequently elicit multifaceted downstream effects (Figure 4). Phosphorylated ERK mediated by VEGF is essential for ECs’ survival and proliferation [77], while induced by reactive oxygen species (ROS) could elevate tumour necrosis factor (TNF)α expression. Enhanced levels of TNFα, on one hand, exert a pivotal action in neuronal death and BRB breakdown [76] via up-regulating caspase-3 expression [78]. On the other hand, it positively influences interleukin-1β (IL-1β) combined with intracellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1). Furthermore, phosphorylation of ERK is significant in promoting the expression of IL-6, ICAM-1 and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) [79].

In addition to glial activation, the other feature of retinal neurodegeneration is apoptosis. In comparison with non-DM, a noteworthy proportion of apoptosis was found in DM retinas [65,80]. Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), situated within the inner retina, are the first retinal neurons in which diabetes-related apoptosis can be detected [81]. Hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress, inflammation and AGEs might be causative of heightened RGC apoptosis in the early stage of DR. Although the mechanisms underlying RGC apoptosis remain mostly ambiguous, an imbalance between survival and proapoptotic signalling is present in the neuroretina of DM patients with early-stage DR [82].

Besides, the neuroretina of STZ-induced diabetic rats exhibited significant upregulation of the death receptor pathway, as evidenced by increased caspase-8 activation and elevated FasL expression [80]. Notably, elevated levels of saturated fatty acids might represent an additional critical metabolic factor contributing to heightened RGC apoptosis at the onset of DR [82]. Palmitate, an excessive plasma saturated fatty acid produced by a high-fat diet, can induce apoptosis through various mechanisms, including ER stress [83].

Chronic and excessive palmitate exposure in RGC-5 cells induces ER stress via ROS generation [84], which subsequently leads to the release of a leading regulator of ER function, the glucose-regulated protein 78, from the protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK). The eukaryotic initiation elongation factor 2α (eIF2α) is phosphorylated by the activation of PERK, which alleviates the protein load on the ER and initiates the PERK/eIF2α/ATF4 signalling cascade [85]. The upregulation of ATF4 promotes the expression of CCAAT-enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) homologous protein (CHOP), a pro-apoptotic factor that acts via downregulating Bcl-2 on one hand [86]. More significantly, activated CHOP could induce further generation and accumulation of ROS, potentially amplifying the apoptotic response. On the other hand, up-regulated ATF4 may curb the activity of the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), resulting in the loss of PSD-95, a core postsynaptic scaffolding protein essential for the function and assembly of the postsynaptic density complex [87]. Moreover, sustained ER stress, induced by excessive palmitate, also activates ER-associated caspase 12, which may contribute to the apoptosis of RGC-5 cells (Figure 5) [82].

Numerous key mechanisms and pathogenic factors contribute to the neurodegenerative process in diabetic eyes [65,80,88], including enhanced neuroinflammation, elevated oxidative stress, reduced synthesis of retinal neuroprotective factors, and excitotoxicity induced by glutamate accumulation. Glutamate and neuroprotective factors are the focal points of interest in this review. Glutamate, serving as the principal excitatory neurotransmitter, exhibits enhanced levels in the retina of both DM animal models [89] and diabetic patients with PDR [65,88]. The reasons why DM promotes extracellular accumulation of glutamate have been explored before [90]. Persistent glutamate accumulation in synaptic and extracellular spaces induces excitotoxicity. This process involves excessive intracellular calcium influx in postsynaptic neurons, primarily mediated by overactivation of N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) and alpha-amino-3-hydroxyl-5-methyl-4-isoxazole-propionate (AMPA) receptors, culminating in neuronal death. This process, glutamate-induced apoptosis, involves at least two mechanisms: a caspase-independent pathway involving calpain and mitochondrial apoptosis-inducing factor and a caspase-3-dependent pathway [88,90].

Normally, the retina synthesizes neuroprotective factors that antagonize the neurotoxic elements associated with neurodegeneration, thereby maintaining retinal health. Once the balance was disrupted, such as downregulation of somatostatin (SST), retinal neurodegeneration may ensue. Neuroprotective or neurotrophic factors SST, neuroprotectin D1 (NPD1), glial cell-line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), interstitial retinol-binding protein (IRBP), erythropoietin (EPO), pigment epithelial-derived factor (PEDF), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), and others might be implicated in the neurodegenerative process associated with DR [65,88,91,92], with the top several appearing to have a key action. Both SST and PEDF are potent anti-angiogenic and neuroprotective factors, predominantly synthesized by RPE. Interestingly, downregulation of PEDF and SST has been observed in early-stage experimental DR [93] and the vitreous fluid of diabetic patients [94], respectively. This finding suggests a new therapeutic approach for DR. Notably, topical SST administration exerts significant neuroprotective effects against the neurodegenerative changes present in early-stage DR [80].

Concerning EPO and VEGF, apart from the effect of VEGF mentioned above, they are also well-recognized neurotrophic and neuroprotective factors [53]. Although further specific research is warranted, they may exert bidirectional effects in the pathogenesis of DR, potentially serving as both protective and deleterious influences. According to the coincidence of overexpressed EPO and VEGF with the downregulated neuroprotective factors, it is speculated that they counteract each other to protect the retina. However, consistent overexpression of VEGF and EPO is neurotoxic, which is found to favour neovascularization in advanced stages of DR, thus resulting in PDR development [65,95,96]. Moreover, EPO could also elevate the effects of VEGF. Together, these findings imply the function of neuroprotective factors in the retinal neurodegenerative process, but more details are still needed.

What’s more, retina neurodegeneration interacts with vascular abnormalities to deteriorate DR. Accordingly, investigating the relevant mechanisms, especially those that link to early vascular dysfunction, is essential for deciphering the potential of neuroprotective strategies to ameliorate diabetic microangiopathy. Like SST, it exerts anti-angiogenic effects by directly acting on SST receptors in ECs and indirectly downregulating VEGF [97]. Also, its mechanism of action might involve the suppression of post-receptor signalling events not only for VEGF but also for other peptide growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factor, epidermal growth factor, and insulin-like growth factor-1 [80]. Besides, the retinal neurovascular unit is a novel insight into retinal physiology and pathology [41].

Neurovascular aspects

Emerging evidence now strongly indicates that DR is recognized as a neurovascular disorder fundamentally driven by the disruption of the retinal neurovascular unit (NVU) [98,99]. The NVU represents a highly integrated structural and functional complex essential for retinal homeostasis. It constitutes a tightly integrated structural and functional complex comprising retinal neurons, glial cells and vascular cells [98,100,101]. These components engage in continuous, dynamic crosstalk, coordinating critical functions such as the precise regulation of retinal blood flow, maintenance of vascular density and integrity (particularly BRB), control of vascular permeability, recycling of neurotransmitters and clearance of metabolic wastes, all in response to fluctuating neuronal activity and metabolic demands [98,101,102]. The integrity of this neurovascular dialogue is paramount for visual function, and its dysregulation under chronic hyperglycaemic conditions forms the bedrock of DR pathogenesis [98,99,103].

Neurons, including RGCs, amacrine cells, bipolar cells and photoreceptors, orchestrate vascular responses via metabolic cues and paracrine signalling. Amacrine cells in the inner nuclear layer modulate vascular tone through paracrine signalling, while photoreceptors regulate choroidal and retinal blood flow with their high oxygen demands [100,104]. Glial cells serve as critical intermediaries in neurovascular crosstalk. Müller cells, spanning the entire retinal thickness, link neurons and vessels to regulate ion balance, neurotransmitter recycling and vascular permeability through interactions with ECs [98,105]. Astrocytes are primarily associated with the superficial vascular plexus, and support the integrity of BRB and guide vascular development during retinal maturation [105]. Microglia maintain tissue homeostasis under normal conditions by clearing cellular debris and regulating synaptic remodelling [100]. Vascular components, including ECs and pericytes, dynamically respond to neuronal and glial signals to adjust blood flow and permeability [104,105]. For instance, ECs react to neuronal-derived VEGF and NO to modulate vessel dilation and barrier function, while pericytes regulate local perfusion through contractile responses. Under physiological conditions, this intricate interplay ensures that vascular function dynamically adapts to neuronal metabolic demands, maintaining retinal homeostasis.

In DR, however, this equilibrium is disrupted: neurodegeneration, characterized by RGC apoptosis, amacrine cell dysfunction and reactive gliosis, often precedes overt microvascular changes such as pericyte loss or capillary occlusion [98,105]. Conversely, subsequent vascular damage (e.g. BRB breakdown, hypoperfusion) exacerbates neuronal loss by impairing nutrient delivery and promoting oxidative stress and inflammation, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of neurovascular dysfunction [100,104]. Retinal NVU dysfunction in DR encompasses neuroinflammation, neuronal loss, glial cell-specific alterations and pathological neovascularization. These disruptions manifest as BRB impairment, elevated VEGF secretion, increased retinal vascular permeability and impaired neurovascular coupling, ultimately culminating in retinal microvascular alterations and neurodegeneration [106]. Crucially, structural and functional compromise of the retinal NVU represents an early event in DR pathogenesis that directly drives disease progression [107].

The breakdown of neurovascular crosstalk in DR involves key molecular mediators. Growth factors such as VEGF exhibit dual functionality in this process, while chronic inflammation critically exacerbates neurovascular dysfunction [108,109]. RGC apoptosis, a hallmark of early neurodegeneration in DR, exemplifies this pathology, triggering VEGF release and pro-inflammatory cascades that directly exacerbate vascular leakage [65,110].

RGC apoptosis disrupts the delicate homeostasis of the NVU, initiating a ‘vicious cycle’ where neuronal loss amplifies vascular dysfunction. Following apoptotic stimuli (e.g. hyperglycaemia, oxidative stress), dying RGCs undergo morphological and biochemical alterations that promote the secretion of VEGF, a pleiotropic cytokine with dual roles in neuroprotection and vascular permeability. Physiologically, VEGF supports RGC survival via PI3K/Akt signalling [111]. However, in the context of RGC apoptosis, excessive VEGF release, likely arising from impaired clearance or upregulated transcription, directly targets vascular ECs, destabilizing tight junctions (e.g. occludin, claudin-5) and increasing paracellular permeability [112,113]. This is consistent with observations in DR models, where RGC loss correlates with elevated vitreous VEGF levels and enhanced vascular leakage [114,115].

Concurrent with VEGF release, apoptotic RGCs unleash a repertoire of pro-inflammatory mediators that further impair vascular integrity. Cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, are released either directly from dying RGCs or by activated microglia and Müller cells recruited to the site of neurodegeneration [116]. TNF-α, for instance, disrupts endothelial tight junctions by downregulating zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and promoting EC apoptosis [117], while IL-1β exacerbates barrier breakdown by activating NF-κB signalling in vascular cells [118]. Additionally, oxidative stress induced by RGC apoptosis stimulates microglial polarization towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype, leading to sustained release of ROS and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs); MMPs degrade the vascular basement membrane, further increasing vascular permeability [119,120].

Notably, the interplay between VEGF and inflammatory mediators amplifies vascular leakage. VEGF upregulation in apoptotic RGCs can potentiate microglial activation, fostering a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, while inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β can, in turn, upregulate VEGF expression in vascular ECs, establishing a feedforward loop. This synergy is particularly prominent in DR, where RGC apoptosis precedes microvascular lesions, and both VEGF and inflammatory markers are concurrently elevated in the retina [121,122].

In summary, RGC apoptosis exacerbates vascular leakage through two interconnected pathways: excessive VEGF release, which directly disrupts endothelial tight junctions, and the secretion of pro-inflammatory mediators, which amplify barrier dysfunction via immune cell activation and matrix degradation. Targeting both pathways may offer a synergistic strategy to mitigate neurovascular damage in RGC-related retinal disorders.

Other aspects

The burgeoning evidence supporting the pivotal role of retinal inflammation in pathogenesis has led to the reclassification of DR as an inflammatory condition [123–126]. Historically, the retina was considered an immune-privileged tissue, with inflammation typically arising from tissue invasion by immune cells in response to injury or infection. However, the association between DR and inflammation was serendipitously uncovered by observations that DM patients who administered salicylates for rheumatoid arthritis underwent a reduced incidence of DR complications [123]. While the ameliorated DME and decreased rates of complications from PDR by anti-inflammatory drugs also confirmed the role of inflammation in DR [124].

In diabetic patients, low-level inflammation is generally considered tolerable and harmless for years. However, progressive diabetic alterations can induce more severe and chronic inflammation, mediated by leukocyte adhesion to retinal vasculature and disruption of the BRB, with perivascular accumulation of activated microglia thought to be the initial step. Similar to the aforementioned, as the resident inflammatory cells of CNS, activated microglia promote the release of proinflammatory cytokines. MCP-1, also named Chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 (CCL2), is one of these cytokines and the most potent chemotactic factor for monocytes as well. Upregulated CCL2 in animal models has an indirect action on mediating the enhancement in retinal vascular permeability associated with DM [125]. In addition, autophagy serves as a critical cytoprotective mechanism in ARPE-19 cells via eliminating damaged organelles. On one hand, ARPE-19 cells exposed to high glucose exhibit increased autophagy. On the other hand, autophagy inhibition via 3-methyladenine (3-MA) activates NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent proinflammatory responses, consequently increasing IL-1β secretion [127]. Taken together, inflammation plays a pivotal role in the aetiology of DR.

So far, notwithstanding that DR-associated genetic studies have produced inconsistent findings, genetic factors are still considered as part of the ‘other risk factors’ contributing to DR pathogenesis. Advances in DNA/RNA sequencing technologies have enabled comprehensive annotation of approximately 98% of the human genome [128]. Consequently, the inconsistent results may stem from functional noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs), transcripts that regulate cellular processes without encoding proteins. Even so, ncRNAs are not ‘junk RNA’, but potent regulators of gene expression, exerting their influence at not only transcriptional but also post-transcriptional levels [129].

The ncRNAs incorporate a variety of subtypes, such as enhancer-like RNAs (eRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and transfer RNAs (tRNAs) [129], however, they are arbitrarily classified into long ncRNAs and small ncRNAs in light of the size. MicroRNAs (miRNAs, miRs), the most extensively studied small ones, are short (19–24 nucleotides [nt]), single-stranded and evolutionarily conserved noncoding RNA molecules [130]. The function of miRNA, which is widely expressed in the body, is to inhibit protein translation and/or promote the degradation of their messenger RNA (mRNA) targets through complementary binding within the 3′-UTR (untranslated) region of the target mRNA [131]. Thus, miRNAs regulate multiple biological processes, including metabolism, cellular proliferation, differentiation, immune responses and apoptosis, across developmental, physiological and pathological contexts [130].

Furthermore, miRNAs are associated with retinal microvascularization in DR, and dysregulated miRNA expression has also been identified in the pathogenesis of DR [132]. Despite contradictory results regarding miR-200b’s role in DR, miR-200b expression changes were observed in STZ-induced diabetic rat retinas [133], and the disparities were ascribed to differences among experimental models, variations in the duration of diabetes, or the potential toxic effects of STZ [134,135]. In 9-month-old Akita diabetic mice, DM-induced miR-200b overexpression downregulates retinal oxidative resistance 1 (Oxr1) [134], thereby promoting oxidative stress and retinal degeneration. No matter serum, plasma, or vitreous humour, elevated expression of miR-21 was strongly correlated with PDR. This upregulation leads to the activation of AKT and ERK1/2 signalling pathways, subsequently promoting the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) and VEGF [136].

Crucially, in T2DM-DR, miR-21 not only has the relative predictive value but also serves as an indicator of disease severity. While a more comprehensive indicator, a combination of multiple serum miRNAs is recommended for PDR detection (Figure 6). In addition, the regulation of genes by miRNA and DNA methylation appears to be interrelated, since the function of Dnmts relies upon histone modification patterns, such as histone deacetylation and H3K9 methylation [137]. Although the specific impact of epigenetic modifications in DR remains elusive, such alterations are recognized as significant in numerous chronic conditions, including DM [137,138]. Therefore, subtle epigenome alterations over time may result in disease manifestation or progression [139].

Screenings and treatments of DR

Screening for DR

Screening examinations are essential for asymptomatic individuals with undetected diseases or those at risk. Such screenings facilitate early disease detection and diagnosis, enabling timely interventions that alter clinical outcomes. In addition to predicting severe complications, the primary clinical significance of DR screening lies in avoiding permanent visual loss, particularly in referable cases that require comprehensive prompt ophthalmic examination and treatment [10,140,141]. In clinical practice, DR is marked by apparent microvascular lesions and is primarily diagnosed based on the severity of these lesions. This diagnosis is mainly conducted using established techniques such as ophthalmoscopy and fundus photography. Thus, diabetic patients with defective blood flow may still be considered as ‘retinopathy-free’, despite which is an early indicator of diabetic retinal dysfunction. However, apparent microvascular lesions are not a timely enough indicator for the incidence and progression of DR [140,142]. Furthermore, the optimal timing and frequency of screening, based on an effective hypothetical screening test, remain to be determined.

Supported by the International Council of Ophthalmology guidelines and substantial evidence [10,140,141], personalized screening that incorporates the aforementioned risk factors appears more reasonable. For instance, routine dilated eye screenings are crucial for asymptomatic vision-threatening DR [6], but studies demonstrate comparable clinical outcomes with biennial screening in low-risk patients [143]. Consequently, implementation of feasible and accurate individualized risk assessments is critical for enabling precision screening protocols. A user-friendly Web application, which is a notable example, utilizes HbA1c levels and current retinopathy status to calculate the optimal interval until the next eye exam [140]. DR screenings for T2DM are recommended at the time of diabetes diagnosis and either yearly or every second year thereafter, while for T1DM, that can be extended to 5 years after diagnosis [144]. Screenings commencing at puberty for children with prepubertal DM are considered appropriate [145]. Additionally, it is recommended to undergo an integrated eye exam in the first trimester for pregnant women with non-gestational DM, with follow-up throughout pregnancy in the presence of retinopathy [146].

With rapid advancements in technologies related to image capture, image analysis and risk assessment, DR screening strategies are becoming increasingly cost-effective. Amongst these advancements, artificial intelligence (AI) is well applied and rapidly evolving in DR screening, such as AI models for retinal image interpretation [10]. Leveraging current large image databases, AI systems relying on deep learning processes have significantly enhanced the detection of DR, achieving notably higher specificity and sensitivity [147]. Furthermore, the role of AI in DR screening is validated by a large-scale nationwide program involving 155 diabetes centres in China. This AI-enabled approach demonstrates comparable performance to human specialists [148], indicating that a cost-efficient, practical, effective and automated screening system may be applied in routine DM follow-up and management. Nevertheless, widespread implementation remains limited in most countries due to the unresolved medicolegal issues and low public acceptance.

Biomarkers for DR

According to the definition [149], biomarkers could promote rapid diagnosis and screening of DR with high sensitivity and specificity. This could thereby enhance patient treatment strategies, alleviate the burden on the healthcare system, and decrease costs. Despite the limited understanding of the molecular mechanisms underpinning DM-related vascular complications and the lack of clearly druggable molecular targets for treatment, there is a pressing need for a specific and sensitive biomarker for DR that is accessible for clinical assessment. Especially, the need for biomarkers in the early stages of DR is urgent, as the progression of DR is comparatively slow before becoming symptomatic.

Nevertheless, several obstacles remain in biomarker research for DR. Firstly, sample availability. Retinal changes, such as proteomic alterations, are critical for comprehending the molecular mechanisms of DR, but retinal samples can only be gathered via invasive surgical procedures. Consequently, interest is growing in non-invasively collected bodily fluids (e.g. saliva), as their compositional variations may offer insights into both local (ocular) and systemic conditions of clinical relevance. Despite the lack of direct contact with the retina, the human tear has emerged as a potential source of DR biomarkers [9]. Compared with plasma or serum, it is a proven less complex biofluid [150], and could offer information about abnormal components of the retinal status, even systemic changes [151]. Secondly, validation of biomarkers. Although biomarker studies seem promising due to intensive efforts, the fact is that the number of biomarkers approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) pales in comparison to the vast number in the discovery phase. Like proteomics studies about biomarkers, one main explanation for this huge gap is the lack of standardized workflows among discovery, verification and validation processes [152]. In addition, other elements, including cost-effective and time-efficient analytical techniques, cross-validation in different populations and ethnic groups, as well as the need for larger sample sizes, are also bottlenecks of studies on biomarkers [153].

Even so, several potential biomarkers have been proposed. Apart from miRNAs, which function as dynamic monitoring markers for tracking the progression from non-PDR to PDR [132], apolipoprotein AI (apoAI), apolipoprotein B (apoB) and the apoB-to-apoAI ratio also have the potential to independently indicate the presence and severity of DR [154]. ApoAI, a vital component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), more accurately manifests lipid accumulation in peripheral tissues [155]; while apoB, existing in low-density lipoprotein (LDL), intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) and very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), has been found in the retinas of individuals with DR [156]. Therefore, they might provide a more direct correlation with the biophysiological alterations inherent to DR than traditional lipid markers such as LDL and HDL [157]. Additionally, multiple potential biomarkers have been identified, including lipophilin A, lysozyme C, lactotransferrin, lipocalin-1 (LCN-1), among others. It is recommended that a combination of these biomarkers indicates a more accurate status of DR in patients [158–160]. More crucial, as the presence of DR signifies microvascular damage has been caused by DM, thus a reliable biomarker of DR may predict a high risk for macrovascular morbidities like cardiovascular diseases [161,162] and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease [163].

Intervention and treatment

Current clinical management of DR prioritizes systemic control of classic risk factors, notably hyperglycaemia and hypertension, to arrest disease progression [164–166]. However, direct ocular interventions are routinely deferred until advanced stages, where laser photocoagulation, intravitreal corticosteroids/anti-VEGF agents, or vitreoretinal surgery become necessary [6,167–169]. Laser photocoagulation, the decades-long standard for PDR and DME, reduces the risk of moderate to severe vision loss but irreversibly destroys retinal tissue, causing paracentral scotomas and nyctalopia, while limited equipment/specialist access excludes most low-resource populations [6,167–169]. Intravitreal anti-VEGF agents (e.g. ranibizumab), now proposed as first-line therapy for DME, even show superiority over laser monotherapy. Yet, this therapy imposes prohibitive costs and carries risks of endophthalmitis and systemic thromboembolism. Corticosteroid implants, reserved for refractory cases, accelerate cataract progression. Vitreoretinal surgery has advanced to manage severe complications like dense vitreous haemorrhage and tractional retinal detachment, though it is reserved for end-stage disease. Hence, given the aforementioned limitations [6,50,65,125,167–171], new strategies are needed, especially for early-stage DR.

As a complication of metabolic dysfunction, the effect of a series of interventions on metabolism was investigated. Fenofibrate, a peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) agonist, is recommended as an adjunctive treatment for early DR in T2DM, since it can significantly reduce the need for laser treatment [171]. In preclinical studies, trivalent chromium, which plays a crucial role in glucose and lipid metabolism [172], has been linked to diabetic-like conditions when deficient [173]. Thus, its supplementation restored antioxidant profiles and attenuated oxidative stress in diabetic animal models without hepatotoxicity [174]. Similarly, preclinical data show that liraglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) analogue, prevents retinal neurodegeneration in diabetic subjects by binding to GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1R) and attenuates H2O2-induced RGC-5 cell damage by inhibiting autophagy [175]. Topical administration of SST, targeting early DR stages without invasive procedures, has been demonstrated in preclinical models to prevent retinal neurodegeneration and electroretinogram b-wave abnormalities (including increased implicit time and reduced amplitude) [80,88]. In Ins2Akita mice, a time-dependent elevation of retinal soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH) expression is detectable as early as 3 months, with further increases in sEH expression, activity and product levels by 12 months; these changes are significantly attenuated by sEH inhibitors without affecting fasting blood glucose, body weight, heart rate, or blood pressure [176]. Additionally, methane-rich saline shows preclinical potential in protecting against STZ-induced DR by suppressing retinal glial activation and exerting anti-angiogenic and anti-inflammatory effects [177,178].

Traditional Chinese medicines (TCM) and their active components show promising preclinical evidence in mitigating DR progression, though clinical translation remains pending. NaoXinTong capsule, widely applied in China for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, reduces fasting blood glucose levels and suppresses MMP-2/9 expression and acellular capillary formation in mice after 14 weeks of treatment [179]. Lonicerae Japonicae Flos (Jin-Yin-Hua) ameliorates PDR in preclinical STZ-induced models by suppressing retinal angiogenesis, providing experimental support for its potential therapeutic value [180]. The ethanol extract of Dendrobium chrysotoxum Lindl attenuates retinal angiogenesis and inflammation through NF-κB pathway inhibition [181,182]. Additional bioactive compounds, including aqueous extract of Hericium erinaceus [183], curcumin extracted from turmeric [184], anthocyanins abundant in blueberries [185] and Polygonatum sibiricum polysaccharide [186], alleviated DR manifestations in preclinical studies via hypoglycaemic, hypolipidemic, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [126,187]. Moreover, saponins from Panax notoginseng and ginsenoside Rg1 from Panax ginseng exert neuroprotective effects in preclinical settings, improving early retinal neuron loss in DR [82] and synaptic neurodegeneration in diabetic mice induced by hyperphosphorylated tau [188]. While these findings reveal compelling therapeutic avenues, their clinical translation requires validation through controlled human trials.

In summary, all the aforementioned strategies of preventing or intervening/treating DR pave the avenue for the patients of DM, but they should be safe, effective, well-tolerated, acceptable with respect to cost and so forth.

Future directions

To address the unmet needs in DR management, future research should prioritize advancing mechanistic understanding, refining early detection and translating promising interventions into clinical practice.

First, a profound understanding of DR pathogenesis, particularly the bidirectional crosstalk between retinal vascular dysfunction and neurodegeneration, is critical for enabling early intervention and improving patient outcomes. Integrative studies combining multi-omics with advanced retinal imaging will help elucidate how risk factors such as hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia synergistically drive pathological pathways, including oxidative stress, inflammation and autophagy. Additionally, clarifying the functional impact of non-coding RNAs and epigenetic modifiers on disease progression remains essential, given their dual promise as therapeutic targets and stage-specific biomarkers.

Second, advancing early detection and risk stratification remains a key challenge. Efforts should focus on developing and validating non-invasive and multi-parametric biomarkers, such as tear proteomics, circulating miRNAs and lipid metabolites, that can reliably predict progression from NPDR to PDR or DME. Simultaneously, AI-driven screening platforms require enhancement via the integration of multimodal data, including clinical variables, genetic risk profiles and dynamic imaging biomarkers. This synergy will enable personalized risk prediction and optimized screening intervals, particularly in resource-limited regions where lack specialist access.

Finally, bridging the gap between preclinical research and clinical application is vital for translating discoveries into actionable therapies. Long-term, pragmatic clinical trials for early-stage DR interventions are needed, using sensitive endpoints alongside traditional visual acuity measures to capture subtle disease modifications. Furthermore, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration among ophthalmologists, endocrinologists and basic scientists will facilitate the integration of systemic risk factor management with ocular-specific interventions, ultimately optimizing holistic DR care and reducing the burden of vision loss.

Conclusion

Thanks to advances in retinal imaging and new treatments for DR, reduced physical, emotional and social well-being caused by DR has been considerably improved. Despite these improvements, DR continues to impose a substantial burden on patients and healthcare systems globally, contributing to 4.2 million deaths and approximately $760 billion in healthcare expenditures in 2019 alone. Given these burdens and current situations, more timely, accurate and efficient risk assessment, screening and diagnosis, as well as intervention and treatment, are imperative and critical. An enhanced understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying DR is essential for developing innovative strategies that focus on both vascular protection and neuroprotection, aiming to improve early detection and treatment outcomes.
