ABSTRACT
This report evaluates leakage behavior from tritium containment vessels under thermal abuse and
combined thermal-mechanical abuse conditions to better understand safety implications for releases
occurring in a fire scenario. Surrogate gases were used for all tests in this report. Eleven valves of a
type installed in tritium containment vessels were filled to local atmospheric pressure (a typical
pressure for tritium storage) and then heated until leakage was detected. It was found that leakage
through the valves from thermal pressurization alone becomes significant only when heating rates
>0.8°C/s were sustained to >300°C. Lower temperature thresholds were observed at higher heating
rates and at higher initial pressures; three additional valves that were initially filled to 60 psia
exhibited consistent leak behavior between 100°C and 250°C. These results for heated valves are
considered conservative with respect to accident scenarios because a high vacuum condition was
imposed on the valve outlet. When tritium containment vessels were heated and then dropped 6 feet
so that the valve stem struck an unyielding surface, gas venting was observed from eight out of ten
vessels with valve temperatures between 260°C and 400°C and maximum vessel temperatures
between 345°C and 500°C. The estimated airborne release fractions from these heated drop tests
were between 52% and 62%. These experimental results indicate that standard vessels used to store
tritium gas at atmospheric pressure are quite robust in plausible accident scenarios and will not
readily release their full inventory.

1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND
Tritium ( H or T) is a synthetic radioactive isotope of hydrogen found in many labs throughout the
 3

Department of Energy (DOE) complex. The other isotopes of hydrogen are non-radioactive and
include protium (1H or simply H), and deuterium (2H or D). Beta radiation is emitted from tritium
when it decays, and is normally blocked by skin but can damage a human body when released
internally [1]. The hazard of oxidized tritium (T2O or HTO) is estimated to be a factor of 104 greater
than that of the molecular form (T2 or HT) [2] because the water form is more readily absorbed into
the human body through the lungs and skin, and also because the biological half-life of oxidized
tritium is much longer. Consequently, tritium safety is largely linked to fire hazards that can yield
rapid oxidation, producing the more hazardous HTO form. Data for tritium in fire scenarios are
nearly non-existent because of the scarcity and expense of tritium and because of the hazards that
would be involved in such a fire test.
Over the past few years, investigators at Sandia National Laboratories (SNL) have pursued a
modeling and simulation approach to address the risks of tritium in fire scenarios and to help
understand the extent of the hazard under representative conditions [3-5]. This report documents an
experimental extension of these recent modeling efforts. Handling and use of T2 in limited quantities
is carefully controlled at SNL and throughout the complex of DOE labs. It would not be sensible to
augment the hazard from T2 by conversion to T2O or HTO in order to assess the associated risk,
especially while other reasonable approaches exist. Therefore, the use of credible modeling methods
as well as surrogates in experimental studies is preferred. A variety of surrogates for tritium may be
deemed appropriate based on the test objectives and test environment, as described later in this
report.
A recent report details several activities executed to help identify key features of tritium behavior
expected in fire scenarios [3], and some aspects of this material have also been published in the form
of a journal article [4]. These documents include a description of a simulated release of tritium
within a standard room including a fire condition relevant to a facility fire (based on ISO-9705 [6]).
Some recent proposals for tritium safety standards (intended for a revision of DOE Handbook 3010
[7]) would yield requirements that are significantly more stringent than current standards. In
contrast, the simulated results from the standard room fire provide a basis for suggesting that
current tritium safety standards are already quite conservative. If finalized, proposals that reduce
thresholds for hazard categories may result in a substantial cost increase for tritium operations.
A subsequent report documents a modeling study using scaled versions of the same standard room
as the first report, but without the fire [5]. This second report investigates the effects of the size and
momentum of a tritium release on the formation and dissipation of potentially flammable regions
(defined conservatively from the lower flammability limit or LFL of normal hydrogen as > 4% by
volume). These considerations are important because the probability of high fractional conversion to
the more hazardous water form is much higher for flammable mixtures compared to the low-
concentration conditions studied in the initial models with a fire near the tritium release [3].
Both modeling studies summarized above use a simplified boundary condition for the tritium inlet
with release velocities and durations selected arbitrarily to achieve the target release masses [3-5]; the
simulations used a square inlet with temporal step changes to initiate and terminate tritium flow. An
ongoing experimental study [8] approximates the boundary conditions assumed in the earliest
modeling study of this series as a means to validate the modeling conclusions [3]. It is desirable to
establish the characteristics of a tritium release that could reasonably occur in or near a laboratory
fire scenario. A search of the literature did not uncover measurements of gas releases from

 11

containers that are representative of tritium operations in laboratory settings with a fire. Once
available, such measurements may be used to assess the realism of the previously modeled release
events and quantify how much the release conditions affect the ultimate conversion of tritium to the
more hazardous oxide form.
Although tritium is a radioisotope of hydrogen and much work has been published in hydrogen
storage literature, surprisingly little from hydrogen hazard analyses is directly applicable to tritium
operations. This is due in part to the much lower pressures and quantities of gas present at tritium
facilities compared to hydrogen storage applications. Hazards emphasized for normal hydrogen
include explosions and jetting flames from vessels at high pressure, but the quantities and pressures
relevant to tritium storage are usually too low for hazards of these types to be relevant.
Savannah River Systems (SRS) is the tritium production facility for the DOE; it is the only DOE
facility with tritium inventories at or near kilogram levels. All other DOE sites under the current
hazard categorization system [9] have tritium inventories that are orders of magnitude lower
(measured in grams or tens of grams at most), and very few work with significant fractions of their
tritium inventories at elevated pressures. Table 1-1 lists current tritium inventory limits (from DOE-
STD-1027-2018) for different hazard categories (HCs) along with representative DOE facilities,
including Idaho National Laboratory (INL), Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL), and
Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). In terms of pressure, some facilities, such as those
located at SNL and LANL, work with gas inventories mostly in the range from 0-2 atm. The Safety
and Tritium Applied Research (STAR) facility at INL works with tritium at elevated pressures for
fusion applications, but the quantities of tritium involved are very small.

The large mismatch in the storage conditions and quantities of relevance between the commercial
hydrogen community and most DOE tritium facilities results in two critical knowledge gaps that
impede accurate tritium hazard analysis. The first gap is addressed in this work and pertains to the
realized airborne release fraction (ARF) in a fire scenario. The second gap is the realized subsequent
conversion fractions (CFs, also referred to as conversion factors) to the oxidized HTO form in the
chosen fire scenario, which is necessarily bounded by the ARF and is being addressed in an ongoing
companion experimental study [8]. ARF is typically used to regulate radiological hazard scenarios
and was originally proposed based on the behavior of particulate matter, which is the dominant
form of most radiological materials of concern. The extension of ARF to parameterize the safety of
gaseous tritium is not physically ideal but provides a consistent basis across regulatory space. The
ARF for tritium is defined as the fraction of stored gas that is released rather than how much of a
solid radionuclide takes the form of small particles that subsequently become entrained in air. The
amount of stored tritium that is both released and converted to the oxide form is given by the
product ARF*CF, which is the most appropriate hazard metric for a tritium release event.

2. OBJECTIVES
To avoid extraneous controls and costs at tritium facilities, it is necessary to bound the possible
accident space more tightly in terms of the ARF and T2-to-water CF for relevant storage containers
in abnormal environments such as fires. This work evaluates risk (i.e., when/how does a gas vessel
rupture or leak?) and consequences (i.e., the ARF and rates of release or time required to achieve
ARF) for standard tritium containment vessels (TCVs) in a fire environment.
TCVs are broadly relevant for consideration as tritium sources due to their use by SRS as standard
shipping containers for tritium gas to other tritium-using sites. For some sites (such as SNL), the
TCVs continue to serve as gas storage vessels for a significant fraction of the facility’s tritium
inventory even after the tritium shipment has been received.
It is expected that subsequent application of any results will be toward the formation and validation
of mod-sim codes for determining subsequent scenario CFs. Referenceable ARF and CF values for
tritium stored in TCVs will be beneficial for hazard analyses at NNSA and DOE tritium sites.

2.1. Test Hardware
The type of TCV investigated in this report is shown in Figure 2-1; this very common model for
tritium transportation and storage applications is more formally designated as CTCV-2. The
cylindrical diameter is 2”. The full length including the all-metal valve is 19.08” and the length
without the valve is 13.75” (from the bump at the far left to the neck-down point designated as an
alignment feature in Figure 2-1). The TCV weight without the tag and keyring shown in Figure 2-1 is
1.325 kg. TCVs are often charged to pressures near atmospheric or lower. However, a fire scenario
may cause thermal pressurization. Thermal pressurization is expected up to about 34 psia at 400°C,
53 psia at 800°C, and 63 psia at 1000°C when a TCV is initially charged to 1 atm (14.7 psia) at 25°C.
Pressures from thermal expansion at other starting conditions are listed further on in this document
in Table 4-2.
It is the objective of this test series to evaluate modes and conditions where the CTCV-2 will leak or
fail. The ARF, release duration and rates, and plume characteristics are of interest. The CTCV-2 is
used almost exclusively to supply tritium to radiological facilities as defined in Table 1-1. The
CTCV-2 has a capacity of 500 mL and is approved for up to 0.111 grams T2 (~0.9 atm at 25°C); the
regulatory limit of 1.6 grams for a radiological facility can be contained within just over 14 bottles
when filled at these conditions. However, these vessels are often used for much smaller quantities of
tritium since they are also used routinely for mixtures of the HDT isotopes. In this situation, the
number of tritium atoms (which is directly proportional to measured Curies) would be less than the
approved limit for the CTCV-2.

3. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS
The focus of this work is the experimental determination of failure modes for gases stored in TCV
bottles of the CTCV-2 type at conditions relevant to typical T2 storage conditions. Absolute
pressures for tritium operations at SNL and LANL are typically between 0 and 2 atm, with sub-
atmospheric storage of tritium gas being more typical. To be representative of these typical storage
conditions, vessels for this test series were filled with gas up to local ambient pressure.
Experiments were conducted at Thermal Test Complex (TTC) at SNL. Heat stress was imparted by
an oven to represent effects of a fire within a laboratory space. Some tests considered the effect of
thermal stress on the valve alone, while others included an additional impact stress resulting from a
fall from a pre-determined height (approximately 6 feet or 1.8 m). ARF values were estimated using
post-test measurements of the residual TCV gas composition and pressure, with comparisons to
calculations of expected ideal gas behavior. Anticipated failure modes for thermal and mechanical
abuse scenarios included valve failure, weld failure at the valve attachment point, or vessel wall
tearing. It was thought that valve failure was the most likely of these options, which was confirmed
for the conditions evaluated in the test series.
The use of surrogate gases for T2 to determine thermal and mechanical failure modes is justified in
that the expected failure modes are not particularly sensitive to either the molecular weight of the
contained gas or beta radiation from tritium. Helium (He) and deuterium both have molecular
weights of 4 g/mol, which is as close to the molecular weight of T2 at 6 g/mol as can be readily
achieved, so the buoyant behavior of either surrogate in air is expected to be close to tritium. Any of
the hydrogen or helium species can be detected quantitatively using a leak detector employing mass
spectrometry (MS), and the small size of these molecules makes them particularly suitable for leak
evaluation. Due to its inert nature, helium is the preferred surrogate for tritium in situations where
reactions are not under direct investigation. Helium was the chosen surrogate for stationary valves
subjected to simple thermal abuse in this study.
It was desired to visualize leak events for combined thermal-mechanical abuse in this test series.
However, small monatomic and diatomic molecules (helium and the hydrogen isotopes,
respectively) are difficult to detect with spectroscopy techniques that were readily deployable at the
TTC. Scoping exercises that attempted to visualize helium and hydrogen plumes using the apparatus
shown in Appendix D were not successful. Thereafter, Methane (CH4) was selected to visualize
plumes released during heated drop tests. Methane is one of the lightest and least expensive
molecules that is active in the infrared band, and its hazards are mostly due to flammability. Methane
is almost three times as massive as T2, but it is only about half as massive as the major constituents
of air. Therefore, a release of gaseous methane is expected to be buoyant in air, especially when
preheated. Mixing rates of methane in air are expected to be conservatively low with respect to
tritium in air due to the lower buoyancy of methane in air.
Thermal scoping tests were conducted on valves of the same type used in TCVs. The TCV valves
used are manufactured by Swagelok (B-series bellows valve, part number SS-4BAG-TW). Upon
request, Swagelok provided two batches of eight valves (16 total) with ¼” OD stainless steel tubing
welded to both ports. This tubing was 0.065” thick, with an 8” length opposite the valve handle and
1” length on the side outlet. ¼” Swagelok fittings were installed on both exposed tube endings, with
a plug on the long section of tubing. These TCV valves were heated without mechanical impact to
isolate thermal failure modes of the valve from mechanical failure modes.
The experimental setup for these thermal scoping tests included a valve within a cylindrical oven as
shown in Figure 3-1a. The selected oven consists of a Zircar fiber insulated heater with embedded

 15

coil heating elements rated to 480 V, drawing a maximum of 6.4 kW (13.3 A) from a cold start or
6.154 kW (12.8 A) at a maximum oven temperature of 1300°C. The dimensions are 7” ID, 11” OD,
and 12” height. Figure 3-1a shows that an external line was plumbed from the valve outlet to a leak
detector (Phoenix L300i, produced by Oerlikon Leybold Vacuum), which uses MS to quantify
helium. The 8” section of tubing on the valves was filled with helium as an inert surrogate for T2
that could be readily detected using this device. Some of these scoping tests with helium gas were
initially pressurized to 60 psia in the cold state, while others were filled or vented to approximately
local atmospheric pressure (~12 psia in Albuquerque, NM). The valve stems at 60 psia were at
approximately 4 times standard atmospheric pressure or 5 times local atmospheric pressure. A type

4.1. Valve Thermal Leakage Tests, Series 1
For the first round of heated valve tests diagramed in Figure 3-1, the first test was operated at 15%
of max voltage (0.15*480 V = 72 V), and the subsequent five tests of the series were operated at
higher power to facilitate faster test execution. These subsequent tests were executed by operating
the single heater at 30% of the maximum voltage (0.30*480 V = 144 V). The equation P = V2/R
yields a calculated power output for this standard condition between 554 W and 576 W at 30% of
maximum voltage (or 139 W to 144 W at 15% voltage), where the heater resistances from the
manufacturer are 36 ohms in the cold state or 37.4 ohms at the maximum temperature of 1300°C.
The valve heating rate at 30% max voltage based on the thermocouple location shown in Figure 3-1
was approximately 0.9°C/s for these experiments. More detailed commentary on the effect of

 21

heating rate for these tests is presented in Section 4.1.1, which applies analysis techniques developed
for the second series of valve thermal leakage tests that are documented in Section 4.2.
As the test personnel initially lacked experience with the Phoenix L300i leak detector, measurements
from two of the valves near the beginning of the first series were not recorded properly (these would
have been designated as V1.3 and V1.4). For subsequent tests, it was found that the best practice is
to verify the leak rate with respect to a known source immediately before activating the heaters and
record the verification results in the same data file as the actual test measurements (these verification
measurements are shown explicitly in Figure 4-3 from the second series of thermal leak tests). The
initial verification section of the leak data was used to rescale the data subsequently acquired during
the thermal ramp. For the first series (designated using the naming pattern V1.#), the source
certified by SNL’s primary standards laboratory (PSL) had a known helium leak rate of 1.186 × 10-5
cc × atm × s-1 during the time period of the tests (Vacuum Instrument Corp, Model # OM-5, Serial
# 06-020, SNL Asset #8887). The leak detector uses a mass spectrometer to count helium atoms
and reports the results in units of cc × atm × s-1, which signifies cubic centimeters per second
corrected to a reference absolute gas pressure of 1 atm.

4.1.1. Heating Rates for Series 1 Valve Thermal Leak Tests
In the second series of valve thermal tests, it was found that leakage behavior is strongly correlated
with heating rates, which were not explicitly investigated in the first series at the lower pressure.
Therefore, heating rates calculated from the first series are presented here to provide a basis of
comparison with the second series. Heating rates are defined here at the temperature derivative of
the valve, which correlates strongly with heater power. The first three tests from the first series
suggested that heating rate does not strongly affect the onset temperature for leakage at the higher
initial pressure, which can be seen more clearly by comparing the plots on the left of Figure 4-2.
Test V1.1 had the heaters set to 15% of maximum voltage rather than 30%, resulting in a self-
heating rate near 0.3°C/s at onset compared to 0.6°C/s with the heater set at 30% (for V1.2 and
V1.5). It appears that the higher pressure promotes a much lower threshold for leakage in terms of
both temperature and heating rate, at least for the uncontrolled amount of torque that was used to
close the valves in the first series. With sufficient pressure stressing the valve, it may no longer be
sensitive to heating rates in the same range investigated in this study.

Test V2.1a was the first test in series 2, and it yielded the first clear evidence suggesting that leakage
could be avoided at elevated temperatures through management of heating rates. This test was
intended to mimic the drop test conditions described in Section 4.3 by coasting to a higher
temperature when the heater is turned off, then hold at a nearly constant temperature. However, the
leak-check configuration is different than the drop-test configuration in terms of airflow because the
top of the oven is covered in the former case but not in the latter (as noted for the drop tests in
Appendix A). The top on the heater in the thermal valve test configuration has an insulating effect
that causes the temperature to overshoot by a larger amount after the oven is turned off compared

 30

to the drop-test configuration. Reducing or cutting power to the heater (controlled via voltage)
immediately reduces the observed valve heating rate in both test configurations (indicated by sudden
drop of blue curves in Figure 4-4), and this was found to be important. When the oven heating
elements were turned off for test V2.1a at 250°C, the temperature continued to rise at a more
gradual rate to about 350°C. Low power was then used to hold the temperature within 20°C of the
peak temperature for 45 minutes with no signs of leakage. Since the maximum temperature was
close to the manufacturer specification without leakage (400 psig at 315°C), this same valve was
tested again as described above (Test V2.1b).
Much additional evidence was found for the strong effect of heating rate on leakage in subsequent
tests. Tests V2.2, V2.5, and V2.7 in Figure 4-4 show that interrupting the heater power reduces the
heating rate sufficiently to arrest an active leak that has not yet achieved maximum flow, while the
valve temperature continues to rise at a lower rate. Restarting the heater later at 30% voltage
generally caused the leak to start again, but with a delay. It appears that valve heating rates near
0.8°C/s were required to restart leakage for hot valves. Test V2.4 doubled the heater voltage to 60%,
which caused the leak onset to occur earlier at the much lower temperature of 190°C and a high
heating rate of about 1.75°C/s. The valve failed to leak significantly when the heater voltage was
reduced to 15% in Test V2.6 with a maximum valve heating rate near 0.4°C/s; the tiny leaks that did
manifest themselves intermittently always re-sealed before significant gas release could occur.
In the final Test V2.8, the temperature was increased at the standard heater rate of 30% voltage for
increments of 100°C and then shut off; Figure 4-4 shows that this resulted in maximum valve
heating rates of about 0.8°C/s. After each instance of heating, the temperature continued to rise for
some period of time and then peaked. The heater was cycled on for another increment of 100°C
when the valve temperature decayed 10°C below the most recent peak. Figure 4-4 shows that Test
V2.8 resembled Test V2.6 (with the slowest uniform heating rate) because all the observed leakage
onset events failed to accelerate.
Table 4-3 confirms that the total leaked volume of gas was insignificant for Tests V2.6 and V2.8.
The total leakage for the other tests was comparable to 1.33 cc expected for the 8” tube volume plus
fittings based on a separate evaluation of one of them. When corrected to multiples of local
atmospheric pressure in Albuquerque, NM, the measured gas volume was actually higher than
expected in several cases. These discrepancies of less than 10% are small enough to be attributable
to variations in tube length and ferrule seating position.
The calibration source that was available for the second test series with helium leak rate of 1.065 ×
10-8 cc × atm × s-1 (Varian Vacuum Products, Model # 0981-F8473-302, Serial # 7aH-334, SNL
Asset #5772) was not as suitable as the one that was used in 2023 with a helium leak rate of 1.186 ×
10-5 cc × atm × s-1 (Vacuum Instrument Corp, Model # OM-5, Serial # 06-020, SNL Asset #8887).
The larger source used in 2023 consistently required a correction factor between 1.1 and 1.2,
whereas first-pass correction factors between 0.7 and 1.0 were derived from the smaller source, and
even as low as 0.3 for test V2.1a. Given that a correction factor of 1.18 derived from test V2.1b was
consistent with the 2023 measurements using the larger calibration source, and the flow from the
smaller source was several orders of magnitude below the regime where significant leakage occurred,
the correction of 1.18 was applied to the entire second test series. The leak check with the smaller
source at the beginning of each test in the second series was considered to be a qualitative indicator
that the leak detector was operating correctly rather than a reliable means to calibrate the measured
flow. The flex line from the valve stem in the oven to the T-fitting between the calibration source

The valves that are the subject of this investigation have all-metal seals. The various metal parts are
expected to expand at different rates in response to a thermal gradient, which will generally be
present when the valve is heated at a higher rate. Shutting off the heater power causes an immediate
reduction in the radiant intensity that is the primary driver of internal temperatures gradients within
the valve. Therefore, it makes sense that valves will leak sooner with high heating rates and that
leakage can be suppressed at low heating rates. It appears that a minimum temperature difference is

 32

also required to achieve significant differences in thermal expansion, as the fastest heating rate in
Test V2.4 did not leak until the time derivative of valve temperature reached a very high value of
1.75°C/s with a measured valve temperature of 190°C (see Figure 4-4). Likewise, it appears that the
increment of 100°C at 30% heater voltage chosen for Test V2.8 was too small for significant
differential expansion to occur and cause a sustained leak.
An examination of the results of the second thermal valve test series in Figure 4-4, suggests that
valves at the conditions used for the first round of drop test experiments between 300°C and 400°C
are not expected to leak significantly before the drop event because the heater power is shut off at
10°C below the target temperature. The heating rate is sharply reduced when the heater power is
turned off, which effectively suppresses leaks that have already started. Generally, the valves have
not reached their maximum leakage rate by 400°C, and the volumes from interrupted leak events in
Table 4-3 suggest that cumulative leakage is likely to be minimal at such temperatures when the
heating rate is reduced.

4.3. CTCV-2 Heated Drop Tests, Series 1
In preparation for the drop tests with the real CTCV-2, the mock TCV shown in Figure 3-4 was
dropped several times with a cushion to assess the repeatability of the remote-actuated shears. After
the thermal mock tests described in Appendix A, the thermocouple (TC) leads on the mock TCV
were cut and vessel was dropped three times onto the hard target surface, which was a steel pan on a
concrete floor. On the first two trials with the mock TCV, the vessel landed cleanly within the view
of the IR camera. On the third trial, the valve stem broke off, and the severe deformation during the
third drop caused the vessel to rebound outside the view of the IR camera. Closer inspection of the
video footage after the test series revealed that some deformation of the valve attachment occurred
during the second practice drop, but this was not obvious at the time.
The five heated drop tests with the real TCVs occurred on 11/29/2023 and 11/30/2023, which was
between the two series of thermal leakage tests documented in the previous sections. Results and
metrics associated with the TCV drop tests are summarized in Table 4-5. To ensure a clean drop
event, the TCV bottles were not instrumented. However, plots of the estimated temperature
histories used to produce Table 4-5 are found in Appendix B, based on methods developed in
conjunction with the mock tests in Appendix A, as applied to temperatures of the surroundings in
Appendix C. The entries in the last five rows of Table 4-5 indicate that four of the five dropped
TCVs in this first series vented, as observed by IR camera.
Table 4-5 refers to maximum soak temperatures and drop temperatures at a variety of locations on
the vessel and the valve. Valve temperatures were used as targets to facilitate comparisons to the
valve thermal leakage tests, but the argument can also be made that thermal pressurization is driven
by temperatures in the vessel rather than at the valve. Vessel temperatures were typically higher than
valve temperatures because the vessel was located closer to the axial midpoint of the cylindrical
heated cavity. The achieved valve drop temperatures were typically 10 to 30 degrees lower than the

4.4. CTCV-2 Heated Drop Tests, Series 2
Some adjustments were made for the second round of heated drop tests with the intent to improve
the measurements obtained. With the relocation from the RHTC to the FLAME facility, a 1.5-inch
thick steel slab was used as the strike surface, and the cage was placed on cinder blocks to
approximately match the height of the strike surface; Figure 3-2 shows that the steel strike surface
had forklift slots below. The distance from the bottom of the heaters to the strike surface was
reduced from 72 inches (6 feet) used in the first test series to 69.5 inches. The tip of the valve stem
on the bottle was suspended about 2 inches above the bottom of the oven. The kinetic energy at
impact is directly proportional to the drop height, so this change results in a minor 3.4% reduction
for impact kinetic energy in test series 2 compared to test series 1.
As mentioned in the methods section, a simplified approximation of a blackbody panel was added as
a background for the IR camera. This was done by placing a flat panel heater adjacent to a shroud
that consisted of a 1/8” thick sheet of stainless steel that was instrumented with two thermocouples
and painted with flat black barbecue paint, shown in Figure 4-15. This coating on stainless steel has
a measured emissivity of 0.87, which is consistent with previous comparable measurements near
0.85 [11]. The left side of Figure 4-15 shows that the panel heater was placed 1.875” from the
shroud (outside the cage). With this configuration, it was found that the shroud achieved a
maximum temperature of about 280°C, which was used as the blackbody setpoint throughout the
second series.

An initial small burst of particulates or droplets or perhaps gas emerged from the valve stem as a
cloud for every test in the second series. This is noted in Table 4-7 and shown in the left and bottom
images of Figure 4-18 as well as the top left images of Figure 4-19, Figure 4-20, and Figure 4-23.
This event is similar to what was observed in the first test series in both the IR and the visible
spectrum. A bigger puff observed for test D10 makes sense if the gas driving it has no other outlet
(the outlet was capped in this case).
The nature of the material ejected from the stem is not definitively known, but the following facts
help to narrow down the possibilities. The wavelengths viewed by the IR cameras are restricted by a
bandpass filter at 3.306 µm with 20 nm bandwidth. This wavelength window was chosen for
sensitivity to CH4 with minimal interference from CO2. The presence of significant gaseous H2O
could yield an interfering signal, as water vapor has a very broad spectral response. However, the
right side of Figure 4-18 shows that the white cloud persists in the visible spectrum long after the
corresponding plume disappears from the IR footage, which seems unlikely if the cloud is composed
of water vapor from combustion of CH4. The IR technique with a blackbody panel in the
background should have allowed absorption through a gas to be detected even as it cooled; this was
the case for the main plume of methane that leaked out the valve outlet.
It seems more likely that the initial cloud is composed of hot solid particulates, probably propelled
by a small puff of methane leaking briefly through the seals in the valve stem. Hot solids tend to
radiate approximately as blackbodies or graybodies. It makes sense that particulates would be visible
in the IR initially and then disappear from view as they cool. The valves on the bottles are lubricated
with molybdenum disulfide. Small particulates of this lubricant or perhaps soot from the fluorinated
lubricant used on the spherical sealing element could make up the cloud observed. Condensing
droplets of water from methane combustion are another possible explanation for the disappearance
of this material from the IR video.

An attempt was made to relate the IR counts shown in the preceding figures to the concentrations
used in the calibration exercises shown in Appendix D. The resulting pseudo-concentrations shown
in Table 4-8 are approximate (qualitative), as they depend on a number of assumptions. However,
they provide a useful order-of-magnitude indication of how much gas is being viewed through the
IR camera line of sight. The calibration exercises used a vertical plume at higher velocities from a

 50

stationary 0.194” ID tube (0.25” OD) under a ventilation hood. In this configuration, the calibration
plumes tended to remain coherent, with a slow rate of radial spread. For the actual drop tests, the
rate of release is slower than the calibration plumes and the outlet ID is larger at 0.25”. Additionally,
the release adjacent to the floor and the lack of upward airflow yields a more stagnant environment
where the released gas expands in all directions without being carried away via bulk movement.
Mixing of released methane with the surrounding air was enhanced during periods when the bottle
was in motion. The maximum pseudo-concentrations were obtained through a manual bracketing
exercise where the IR counts from the background-subtracted drop tests were compared to the
closest plume concentration increments from the calibration series. The calibration methane
concentration increments were 100%, 50%, 25%, and 12% (in nitrogen). The resulting drop-test
estimates in Table 4-8 were approximated to the nearest half-increment of concentration.
The pseudo-concentrations in Table 4-8 should be interpreted as containing an average number of
methane molecules in the line of sight between the blackbody and the camera that approximates the
number of molecules seen looking through the plume from the 0.25” OD calibration tube at the
listed concentration. With the further assumption of a linear response, the ratio of counts from the
early release to maximum counts occurring later through the gas plume was used to create an
additional pseudo-concentration entry for Table 4-8. These early pseudo-concentrations are
expected to trend closer to true concentrations in the jetting release because minimal spreading of
the jet had occurred, whereas the maximum pseudo-concentrations correspond to locations and
times where the methane cloud had spread considerably in the vicinity of the jet. Pseudo-
concentrations for the early jets are close to each other (within a factor of 2), which is reasonable for
near-outlet gas concentrations.

With the assumption of linear response of IR counts versus concentration, the lowest signals that
were visually distinguishable from background in the dispersing gas cloud were compared to the
highest signal and the derived maximum pseudo-concentrations in Table 4-8 to estimate the lowest
amount of gas that could be detected using this technique. On the basis of a narrow reference jet
that is representative of the calibration exercise, the lowest observed IR counts of 50 to 150 yield
pseudo-concentrations on the order of 2% to 3% for tests D6 through D9. The gas detected at
these low levels just before full dispersion was spread out far beyond the dimensions of the
reference jet, which means that the actual concentrations were much lower. Even these
conservatively high estimates of concentration are below the LFL for hydrogen isotopes (4% for
protium [12] and up to 6.6% for tritium [13]). Therefore, the diagnostic methods used in this test
series are sufficient to determine approximate times and length scales required for dispersion below
the LFL. Table 4-7 indicates that dispersion times required to achieve concentrations completely
below these detection limits were all between 3 seconds and 6 seconds.

significantly above the upper solid line. Plume counts viewed between these lines on the right of
Figure 4-24 appear reasonable and continuous with absorption and dispersion behavior of the plume
in front of the lower (hotter) portion of the panel; there are no sudden jumps in apparent
concentration associated with the lower dashed line.
Rise and mixing driven by buoyancy as well as the motion of the dropped bottle were observed
throughout this test series. The rates of upward movement of methane plumes across the
uncertainty thresholds identified in Figure 4-24 were comparable to rates of spread and dispersion in
the horizontal and downward directions. It appears that dispersion from the pure gas release to
nonflammable concentrations near the lower detection limits of this IR technique occurs on length
scales that are a small multiple of the 12-inch height of the panel heater. Length scales required for
full dispersion could be measured more precisely with a taller heated panel; 2 to 3 feet high would
likely be sufficient to fully track dispersion from this type of release.

Since the molecular weight of methane exceeds that of tritium by a factor of 2.5, a tritium release
under conditions comparable to these methane release experiments would be more buoyant, which
would drive faster mixing rates of released gas with the surrounding air compared to observations in
this test series. We have described trends associated with nonreacting buoyancy-driven mixing in
another report [5], which showed that releases of tritium require large gas volumes and very high
momentum (several orders of magnitude higher than studied here) for a buoyant plume to maintain
flammable concentrations as it rises towards normal ceiling heights, and considerable dispersion will
still occur under such extreme conditions. The good mixing observed in these experiments lends
credence to those previous modeling results [5].
From the observed good mixing of methane and the anticipated higher mixing rates with tritium in
air, it follows that flaming combustion of a comparable tritium release would require a strong
ignition source to be located within a few centimeters of the TCV outlet. Likewise, dispersion is fast
enough that no flame would be possible after a few seconds when the bottle has stopped venting.
Our previous experiments established that oxidation of hydrogen isotopes at sub-flammable
concentrations requires sustained temperatures between 550°C and 800°C or higher [3, 4].
Oxidation rates for dilute mixtures at temperatures within this range are not instantaneous, as effects
of temperature and residence time were clearly observed in the experiments. Low conversion
fractions to the oxidized (water) form are expected for most scenarios with sub-flammable release of
hydrogen isotopes or a release that quickly disperses to sub-flammable concentrations. Simulations
in an ISO-9705 standard room fire using kinetic reaction rates derived from these measurements
were used to conclude that conversion fractions to the oxide form are expected to be quite low
(typically less than 10%), except in cases where the release is very close to the fire (within about 1
meter) [3].
Our previous simulations released 0.1 gram of T2 at a constant rate over 6 seconds [3, 4]. This
simulated mass of tritium gas is comparable to the capacity of a full TCV, and the simulated release
duration is only slightly longer than observed and listed in Table 4-7. Flow rate through a fixed
orifice scales with the square root of pressure differential (which can be derived from Bernoulli’s
equation). This principle explains why most of the observed leakage in this test series is biased
towards earlier times and is reduced at later times; this behavior differs from the simple boundary
conditions used in the previous simulation campaign [3, 4].
The simulated release occurred over a square with a diameter of 17 cm, which is 56% of the heater
height behind the blackbody panel used in this study. The baseline mesh resolution was 4
computational volumes in each direction for this gas inlet. The bouncing and rolling motions of the
TCV bottle in this test series help the clouds of methane to spread quickly over an area comparable
to the blackbody shroud. Consequently, the observed gas clouds had dimensions on the same order
of magnitude as the previously simulated plume diameter. In spite of the non-constant release on an
oversized surface and the fact that the full bottle capacity was not released in these tests, it appears
that the boundary conditions selected for the previous simulations are quite reasonable. Dispersion
to relatively uniform sub-flammable concentrations is expected to occur over length scales that are a
small multiple of the simulated tritium inlet diameter. This suggests that differences from the tested
scenario and the previously simulated scenario should be minimal at distances that are significant
fractions of 1 meter and larger. Differences in air movement and mixing at distances larger than
about three simulated inlet diameters are more likely to be dominated by enhanced airflow produced
by an adjacent fire or other environmental features such as ventilation and room size rather than the
details of the specific release.

The vent times and dispersion times in Table 4-7 are limited to a few seconds and are within a factor
of 2 of each other. For test D8, the vent time was actually longer than the dispersion time because
motion from gas flow was still detectable with the outlet positioned in front of the bottle, but the
flow rate near the end of venting was so low that signs of a plume or cloud were no longer
detectable above the bottle. All of these observations are further evidence that dispersion to very
low concentrations occurs rapidly for tritium releases relevant to facilities where much of their
tritium inventory is stored in containers with volumes and pressures similar to TCVs considered in
this work.
This test series successfully achieved drop conditions that were representative of a real accident
scenario. If more quantitative evaluation of leakage and dispersion behavior are desired in future
studies, there are several improvements that could be made to the test methods. A future test series
could use a stationary bottle to obtain IR video footage with an optimized view frame. A test with a
stationary bottle could use a hammer or piston or projectile to produce an impulse on the valve
similar to the drop tests described in this report. This proposed method would include the ability to
systematically vary the impact angle and impulse of the collision. The oven surrounding the TCV
could be retracted just before impact to expand the useful camera view; it could be mounted on rails
to retract vertically or horizontally. A stationary bottle could be instrumented to directly measure the
temperature distribution, which would reduce uncertainties with respect to the surrogate
temperature measurement method documented in Appendix A. As noted above, a taller and perhaps
wider blackbody panel (close to 1 meter) would allow more complete tracking of the full dispersion
event for released gas. With better control over the camera view and the initial direction of the
plume, a more thorough IR calibration exercise would be justified to quantify the methane in the
plume. This proposed approach with a stationary bottle could also allow composition measurements
to be taken using RGA and perhaps other techniques at one or several known positions close to the
valve outlet. The identity of the material ejected from the stem on impact could also be determined
more easily using a stationary TCV.
For the second drop-test series, the TCVs were capped within a few tens of minutes after the tests
and later checked for residual pressure and composition using MS (reported here as mole
percentages). The purpose of bounding the release fraction from these post-test evaluations was
different from the leak checks done after the first drop-test series, so data comparable to those listed
in Table 4-6 were not recorded. The composition and pressure checks occurred about 3 weeks after
the tests (September 5, 2024). No CO2 was detected, H2 concentrations were at most 0.1%, and
concentrations for both H2O and Ar were less than 1% for all TCVs. The ratio of N2 to O2 was
about 3.8 for all bottles, which is representative of air that is expected to backfill the bottles after
cooling while the leak orifice remains open. This N2/O2 ratio, the lack of CO2, and the fact that the
balance of the composition is principally CH4 all indicate that oxidation reactions (combustion) did
not occur inside the TCVs. This was expected based on the valve target temperatures being lower
than the autoignition temperature of methane.

It should be noted that the estimates in Table 4-9 are based on ideal gas assumptions; as such, they
can be considered an equilibrium approach to bound ARF that says nothing about leak rates. Table
4-10 shows that the Bernoulli equation can be used to make some additional statements about
trends in rates of leakage. The outlet velocity is directly proportional to the molar flow rate from the
TCV, and the velocity is proportional to the square root of pressure difference divided by density.
The pressures are independent of molecular weight, so the flow rate or velocity can be scaled from
the gas density as the square root of the ratio of molecular weights. This means that for a given
pressure differential (which is a simple function of temperature alone), the molar leakage rate from a
bottle filled with tritium is in Table 4-10 is estimated to be 1.633 times higher than observed with
methane in the tests reported here. Given that the flow is bound by the pressure differential, the
higher expected leakage rates with tritium in the TCV simply means that the ideal fraction vented as

calculated in Table 4-9 would be achieved sooner. Therefore, the range of ARFs estimated from the
measured residual concentrations and pressures in this work as well as the ideal gas estimates in
Table 4-9 are expected to remain valid for tritium-filled TCVs. Higher concentrations may occur
very near the TCV in the short term for a real tritium release, but rates with the different gases are
still close (within a factor of two). Leakage events with a more buoyant gas and release velocities that
are 63% higher on average than observed in this study are expected to disperse faster to
concentrations below the lower flammability limit of 4% for hydrogen [12] or 6.6% for tritium [13].
Therefore, the results obtained using methane in this study are conservative from a safety
perspective, as tritium conversion rates to the more hazardous water form are much slower at low
concentrations, even at elevated temperatures near a fire [3, 4].

5. SUMMARY, CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
An experimental investigation of leakage behavior relevant to tritium containment vessels in
thermally and mechanically abusive environments has been completed. This study considered two
types of abuse tests, and each of these was conducted in two phases or series so that learning from
the first phase could be used to improve the experimental approach for the second phase. These
results provide context to evaluate simplified boundary conditions in a previous simulation
campaign used to evaluate tritium safety in fire scenarios. No signs of weld failure were observed
during thermal tests of the valve or heated drop tests of the full TCVs; all observed leakage was
associated with the valves. Thermal leakage tests of TCV valves used helium as a close surrogate for
tritium that is appropriate for detection using the mass spectrometer in a commercial leak checker.
Heated drop tests of full TCVs used methane as an IR-active surrogate for tritium that has positive
buoyancy in air. The experimental results from this study indicate that the type of TCVs considered
are quite robust in plausible accident scenarios resembling the conditions tested and will not readily
release their full inventory, especially over the narrow timeframes of most accident scenarios.
The first phase of thermal leak checks with TCV valves suggested a threshold near 300°C for the
onset of valve leakage when a TCV is initially charged with gas at near 1 atmosphere absolute
pressure. Valve leakage behavior becomes more repeatable in terms of thermal leakage thresholds at
higher initial pressures. Thermal leak checks suggest that lower thresholds near 100°C would apply
when TCVs are charged to initial pressures of 60 psia, but this exceeds typical tritium storage
pressures for TCVs. Three replicate leak measurements with helium gas were made for each of the 2
initial pressures. These leak tests were used to specify heating targets between 300°C and 400°C on
the valve for the first phase of heated TCV drop tests.
The second phase of thermal leak checks with TCV valves used tighter control of the valve torque
and found that heating rate was a more important factor than temperature. It was found that heating
rates between about 0.8°C/s and 1.0°C/s are required to start, restart, and sustain leakage for valves
initially charged to atmospheric pressure; the previously observed leakage threshold near 300°C
remained applicable for heating rates in this range. With valve heating rates in excess of 1.75°C/s,
leakage was observed at temperatures as low as 190°C. A subsequent review of the measurements
from the first phase showed that leakage through the valve occurs at a much lower heating rate
threshold near 0.2°C/s when the cold pressure is 60 psia. All of the thresholds observed for the
heated valve tests are considered to be conservative with respect to an accident scenario of a TCV
being exposed to excess heating from a nearby fire because the valve outlet would not be attached to
a high vacuum system in this scenario. For the highly specific configuration where a TCV is attached
to a high vacuum system, tritium would be expected to vent to an exhaust or capture system rather
than a location where the fire is occurring and/or where personnel could be exposed.
The second type of test consisted of heated drop tests that were intended to represent a fire scenario
where TCVs on a shelf are heated and then dropped onto a hard surface. The trends with heating
rate noted above for the heated valve tests were combined with the observations in the heated drop
tests to conclude that there was no leakage from the TCVs before the actual drop event. The TCVs
were filled with methane, and leakage was observed using an IR camera. This diagnostic technique
was greatly improved in the second phase of testing through the use of a simple blackbody panel in
the background, which enabled visualization of the cooling plume via IR absorption. The first phase
of heated drop tests was limited to observing the hottest region of the plume via IR emission in the
vicinity of the valve outlet.

 61

Two forms of leakage were observed during the heated TCV drop tests with methane gas pre-
charged to local atmospheric pressure, and no flame was observed to occur with either form of
leakage. First, a brief burst leaked from the valve stem upon collision with the floor, with a duration
on the order of 0.01 s. This initial leakage was distinct in nature and point of origin from the
methane leakage that was observed slightly later in the tests. It appears to consist of hot particulates,
droplets, or perhaps steam; these were observed initially as collections of particles in IR and then as
a cloud in the visible spectrum. The largest initial bursts observed in IR had a length scale on the
order of 1 cm, and the resulting visible clouds were on the order of 2 to 4 cm in diameter and
persisted for more than a second. The hypothesis based on the location of the release that seems
most plausible is that this initial leakage on impact consists of particles of molybdenum disulfide that
are used as a lubricant in the valve stem.
The second and more prominent form of leakage observed in the TCV drop tests was methane
flowing from the valve outlet. This leakage from the valve outlet occurred slightly after the leakage
from the stem, and it continued for several seconds. The portion of the jet observable via IR
emission from this second form of gas leakage was also on the order of 1 cm or less, which was an
early indication of rapid dispersion and/or cooling of the expelled methane gas. Once the
absorption measurements became available in the second series of tests, it became clear that
dispersion of the released gas to concentrations below the LFL of hydrogen isotopes occurred on
time scales similar to the duration of the release (3 to 6 seconds) and over lengths scales of the same
order of magnitude as the 12-inch heated zone in the background.
These observed time and length scales for dispersion suggest that the boundary conditions used in a
previous simulation campaign are a reasonable simplification. Sufficient dispersion was observed
within ~1/3 meter in these tests to conclude that details of the source for a 0.1-gram release of
tritium (with a pure volume of 0.5 liter at ambient temperature and pressure) should have a
negligible effect on concentrations observed at distances approaching 1 meter and beyond. The
longest times observed for an experimental release are also consistent with the duration of 6 seconds
that was specified in the previous simulation campaign.
The heated TCV drop tests used two methods to obtain qualitative plume concentration
measurements that yielded consistent trends for the tests where they were both available. A capillary
tube at the edge of the strike zone yielded nonflammable gas concentrations of less than 1% using
an RGA for three of the last five tests. This point measurement lends credence to the high extent of
dispersion observed as IR absorption counts that correspond to pseudo-concentrations. The IR
counts are proportional to the total number of methane molecules located along the line of sight
from the heated panel and the camera, which means the derived pseudo-concentrations are
conservative with respect to true volume-constrained concentrations.
It was determined that the lowest IR counts that were visually distinguishable from background
correspond to pseudo-concentrations that are nominally below the LFL of hydrogen isotopes, and
the real concentrations are necessarily even lower than the pseudo-concentrations. Therefore, the
final version of the IR diagnostic as implemented in this study provides enough range and sensitivity
to track dispersion from the release at nearly pure concentrations down to well below the LFL. The
methane surrogate used in this test series yields conservatively low dispersion rates compared to
tritium because tritium will mix with air faster due to greater buoyancy. Tritium release rates are
expected to be about 63% faster than methane at the same pressure differential based on the
Bernoulli equation; this is not a large multiple. Faster release rates of lighter gas are not expected to
augment the final ARF, and they should promote faster dispersion. Therefore, the dispersion rates
observed with methane can be considered conservatively low from a safety perspective.

 62

The second phase of IR measurements with a blackbody (perhaps more properly designated as a
graybody) at 280°C was found to be effective for tracking behavior of released gas. Several
recommendations were made to improve testing techniques for any future work that may consider
similar thermo-mechanical abuse of the same TCVs or similar vessels. A more optimal setup with
measurements that are more quantitative and useful would include a stationary heated vessel with a
retractable heat source and hammer or projectile that provides impulse similar to the drop-tests
considered in this study. A larger blackbody panel with dimensions of about 1 meter should be
placed in the background to more fully track the dispersion behavior. More systematic and accurate
IR calibration becomes possible with an optimized stationary view. Concentrations could be
measured via RGA or other means from multiple fixed locations. Temperature uncertainties can be
greatly reduced by instrumenting each TCV tested, which is only practical for a stationary bottle.
Leakage of gas was observed from eight out of ten vessels dropped from a 6-ft height that were
preheated to estimated valve temperatures between 260°C to 400°C, with maximum vessel
temperatures estimated between 345°C to 500°C. The vessel temperatures were found to yield better
estimates of release fractions through comparisons of ideal gas behavior to post-test composition
and pressure measurements. The most representative ARFs estimated from the range of these
measurements were between 52% and 62%. These ARFs are significantly lower than 100% because
they are limited by TCV exposure temperature and also because the TCVs investigated are robust
enough than only minor damage occurs during drop events; nothing that remotely resembled a full
breach event was observed in this test series. It was concluded from post-test evaluation of valves
via CT scans after they were heated and dropped that any leak paths formed must have been
microscopic.
One of the two vessels where a leak was not observed did not include a blackbody for absorption
measurements and may have been below a temperature threshold where gas could be detected via
emission. The other vessel that was not observed to vent had a cap installed on the outlet, which
was apparently effective in preventing a rapid release in the short term. However, post-test
evaluation conducted about three weeks later suggests that the composition equilibrated to an
apparent ARF of 75% for the capped TCV due to slow diffusion through a damaged valve and
perhaps a damaged cap. This high ARF result is considered non-representative for purposes of
safety evaluations in fire scenarios, but it does give an indication of what can happen over long
timeframes for a vessel at atmospheric pressure when valve integrity is compromised. Therefore, if
TCV damage is suspected, the tritium gas should be transferred to an undamaged TCV as soon as
practical to minimize loss.
The delay from initiating the leak to capping the valve on dropped TCVs was driven by cooling
times, and the tens of minutes that were required for this operation under the adopted procedures
are sub-optimal. A suggested means to minimize this delay and thereby determine release fractions
more accurately in the future is to rapidly dip or pour a liquid polymer onto the valve, sufficient to
seal leak paths before significant inflow of air occurs to replace the vented methane as the TCV
cools. However, idealized calculations of thermal expansion and venting yielded useful comparisons
to interpret the post-test concentrations. Such comparisons will be even more informative when
coupled with the improved temperature and concentration measurements recommended above.

APPENDIX A. CHARACTERIZATION OF DROP-TEST CONDITIONS
The first three thermal mock tests in the drop configuration shown in Figure 3-2 were used to select
a heating strategy that would minimize spatial differences in temperature. These mock tests were
conducted immediately prior to the first set of drop tests documented in Section 4.3. In all cases an
insulated bottom panel was used while heating the ovens. The first mock test powered both heaters
at 30% of the maximum voltage with a layer of insulation on top of the oven assembly. The result of
mock test #1 was that the spread of measured temperatures on the ringed thermocouple holder was
greater than 250°C. Thereafter, mock test #2 turned the top heater off, which made the top region
significantly colder.
Mock test #3 reduced the spread in measured temperatures to 150°C by using both heaters with the
insulation on top of the oven assembly removed. This prevented the hot air from collecting at the
top, while the insulated panel on the bottom reduced the flow of cold air entering from below. The
variation among the measurements on the three rings was limited to 100°C, and the variation among
the measurements on the vertical rod was also limited to 100°C.
The position of the vessel was standardized (after Mock test #1) to align the feature shown for the
TCV in Figure 2-1 and the mock TCV in Figure 3-4 with the lowest of the three rings shown in
Figure 3-2 and Figure 3-3. Mock test #1 had the vessel slight lower, with the bottom of the curved
surface aligned with the bottom ring. With the finalized bottle position and heater configuration, it
was found that the thermocouple (TC) at 2” on the vertical post correlated very closely with the
thermocouples on the valve for the duration of the period when the heaters were on. This is shown
for mock tests #3 through #10 with peak temperatures between 150°C and 500°C in Figure A - 1.
Figure A - 1 also shows that the rod temperature tends to underestimate the valve temperature after
the power has been shut off, and the discrepancy becomes noticeable somewhat earlier for the
higher target temperatures.
The TC on the vertical rod at 2” height was used for the remaining mock tests to define the target or
peak temperature. When the TC at 2” reached a temperature that was 10°C below the target, the
power to the heaters was turned off. Since the heating elements were still hot, the oven temperature
would continue to “coast” upward for approximately 10°C so that the target was achieved, and the
temperature would remain reasonably stable near the target for about 6 minutes after cutting power.
The bottom panel was triggered to open at about 6 minutes for each mock test so that the associated
enhanced cooling effect could be observed in the thermal data. The swinging of the bottom panel
on the hinge rocked the heater, so it was decided that the vessel would not be dropped until about
30 seconds after releasing the panel to allow time for the motion to settle. The thermal signature of
the bottom panel opening shows up in Figure A - 1 as a “knee” in the curve about 360 seconds after
deviation from the heating curve on the left. The heating rate measured at the valve for the mock
tests is about 0.6°C/s between 150°C and 350°C for the mock tests, with 0.5°C/s for series 1 drop
tests and 0.4°C/s for series 2 drop tests.

APPENDIX D. GAS MIXING APPARATUS AND DIAGNOSTICS SCOPING
At the outset of this project, it was hoped that a method could be developed to see the gas plume in
the visible spectrum and to use protium, deuterium, or helium as a surrogate for tritium. The gas
flow system and detection system diagrammed in Figure D - 1 was designed and built to evaluate the
concept. Figure D - 1a specifies excitation of visible fluorescence using helium or deuterium as
surrogates for tritium. The gas species of interest for optical measurements can be diluted in
nitrogen to a known concentration using the apparatus with mass flow controllers (MFCs) as
illustrated in Figure D - 1b. The concentration is intended to be measured optically at the gas outlet
from a ¼” OD tube under a fume hood (illustrated as a yellow plume in Figure D - 1b).
During scoping experiments, it was found that excitation via a helium pen lamp or a deuterium arc
lamp as shown in Figure D - 1a were not sufficient to yield a detectable fluorescent response from
gas plumes consisting of either helium or hydrogen. It was concluded that the visible fluorescence
approach would not be viable with the excitation sources and optical detectors available for this
project. However, it was known from previous experience that heated methane could be detected
with IR cameras that were readily available, so this approach was adopted for the drop tests
described in this report.
The apparatus illustrated in Figure D - 1b was also used to test IR cameras with CH4 as shown in
Figure D - 1c and Figure D - 1d, which served as an IR-activate surrogate for T2 in the heated drop
tests. The optional heating source in Figure D - 1b was included on the piping outlet to preheat the
gas so it would emit in the IR spectrum. This was not done prior to the first round of drop tests
because IR cameras had already been used successfully to visualize hot methane as part of another
test series in the past. However, this apparatus was used with the same IR cameras post-test to
evaluate whether the IR images of methane concentration acquired during the first series of drop
tests could be interpreted on a numerical or semi-quantitative basis rather than a purely qualitative
basis. The flow setup in Figure D - 1d includes a blackbody panel in the background behind the gas
outlet to assess IR camera performance in visualizing the methane plume in preparation for the
second series of heated drop tests. It was found that this technique combined with a bandpass filter
on the IR camera yielded much better visualization of the gas plume, so a simplified blackbody panel
was devised as a background for the drop zone in the second series of heated drop tests.
Scoping measurements of a CH4-N2 plume in front of a blackbody background with an IR camera
are shown in Figure D - 2. These example measurements show the effect of increasing methane
concentration going left to right. The effect of blackbody temperature is shown by comparing the
bottom plots at 250°C to the top plots at 500°C. Similar experiments with preheating on the gas
outlet tube were used to specify candidate conditions for the second series of drop tests described in
Section Figure 4-4 and to analyze the results; IR images with preheated plumes and blackbody
backgrounds are shown in Figure D - 3. Figure D - 3 displays emission measurements from gas
streams hotter than the background as white streaks, while retaining the greater plume visibility via
adsorption measurements, similar to those shown in Figure D - 2. IR images with preheated plumes
and a cold blackbody in Figure D - 4 show less plume visibility; these emission-only measurements
are similar to the first series of heated drop tests described in Section 4.3.
