Executive Summary
In November 2024 community members in the Waiʻanae moku (region or district) in West
Oʻahu, Hawaiʻi, embarked on an energy planning process to articulate the community’s vision
and goals for their energy future and identify and prioritize projects to help achieve them. The
process was supported by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR, formerly known as the
National Renewable Energy Laboratory or NREL) and Hawaiʻi State Energy Office (HSEO)
through the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Energy Technology Innovation Partnership
Project (ETIPP).

The Waiʻanae Moku Community Energy Plan, Version 1 (LEI Foundation 2025) was the result
of the process. Figure ES-1 (page vii) shows the community’s energy vision, goals, focus areas,
and project ideas, described in further detail in the plan. One purpose of the energy plan is to
prepare the community to achieve its energy goals, which could include applying for and
receiving additional support for planning and implementation, such as deep-dive technical
assistance through ETIPP on an energy project prioritized through the planning process.

This report was prepared as a supplement to the community’s energy plan in response to the
desire among participants in the process for more information about their local energy context
and project ideas. The report includes results of two activities conducted by NLR in support of
the planning process and in response to this desire: (1) an energy baseline assessment and (2)
considerations and resources for possible energy projects prioritized by the community in its
energy plan. The report concludes with takeaways for the community to consider as they pursue
their energy goals.

 community-prioritized plans and projects that are pono, community-led and locally owned (in whole or in part), resulting in a
 diverse portfolio of community-scale technologies developed in accordance with cultural values, traditions, and respect for
 ʻāina; a Wai’anae Moku where every resident experiences ‘ohana level resilience through whole home energy optimization
Vision utilizing renewable energy, energy storage, and energy conservation activities and efficiency measures (appliances, insulation,
 etc.); where every business is able to thrive while producing some or all of its own energy and practicing carbon kuleana; where
 critical infrastructure and facilities maintain power during disruptions; and where kaiāulu-level prosperity is realized based on a
 reliable, affordable energy system.

 1. Identify and assess energy resilience for at least one resilience hub in each ahu puaʻa in the Waiʻanae moku by 2027.
 2. Establish a network of resilience hubs in the Waiʻanae moku by 2030 wherein each hub will have a resilient energy system
 sufficient to maintain power throughout a grid outage of at least 3 days.
 3. Increase the number of Native-Hawaiian-owned renewable energy and adjacent businesses by 10% over baseline by 2035.

 5. Support a circular economy for energy materials by developing a plan for businesses in the Waiʻanae moku to play a key

 6. Support energy education and career pathways for school-aged students and adults by developing a plan and strategic
 partnerships by 2026.

Highlights of the Energy Baseline Assessment
Results of the energy assessment (Section 2) describe the energy landscape in the state of
Hawaiʻi, on the island of Oʻahu, and in the Waiʻanae moku at the time the plan was developed,
with the intention of supporting informed community participation in the planning process.
Highlights of the energy baseline assessment are summarized here.

Policy landscape. Hawaiʻi was the first state in the U.S. to set a target of 100% renewable
energy, to be achieved by 2045 (Act 97 2015), among other targets. The state is making progress
toward this goal, with 33% of electricity generation from renewable sources statewide in 2023
(Hawaiʻi State Energy Office, n.d.-a) and among the highest rates of rooftop solar adoption in the
country (Hawaiian Electric 2025b). The passage of State Bill 589 in July 2025 reflects an effort
to sustain progress. This bill calls for accelerating the adoption of distributed energy resources
with a target of 50,000 new systems within 5 years, adding to the 114,000 grid-connected solar
photovoltaic (PV) systems with a total installed capacity of 1,410 megawatts (MW) at the end of
2024, along with additional investments in grid service programs, microgrids, and community
solar among other initiatives (State Bill 589 2025).

Electric generation capacity and rooftop solar PV-and-storage adoption. On Oʻahu, the most
populous of Hawaiʻi’s six major islands and location of capital city Honolulu, 30.8% of electric
generation capacity was from renewable sources in 2024, about half of which (15.5% of the
total) was customer-sited solar (Hawaiian Electric, n.d.-f). In the Waiʻanae moku, a group of
eight ahupuaʻa (smaller land divisions) on the west coast of Oʻahu, the number of permits issued
for distributed solar PV through 2024 is equivalent to 28% of the single-unit detached homes in
the moku (2,480 permits and 8,657 single-unit detached homes in Waiʻanae), assuming all of the
permits were for the residential sector (Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory 2024; U.S. DOE
n.d.-b). The permit data indicate that for all of the residential rooftop solar PV systems currently
installed in the Waianae moku, 59% are paired with batteries. In recent years, however, greater
than 90% of installed systems have included batteries, as shown in Figure ES-2, which is
consistent with the statewide attachment rate of 95% (LBNL 2024).

Electricity generation. In the Waiʻanae moku there were three existing and planned utility-scale
electric generation facilities as of 2023: two existing solar PV facilities and one planned solar PV
and storage facility. An oil-fueled power plant is also located about one mile south of the moku
(Hawaiian Electric’s Kahe Power Plant, referred to as “Kahe” in this report). The existing
facilities represented 32.6 MW of electric generation capacity (all from utility-scale solar PV;
total capacity is 682.6 MW if the additional 650 MW of oil-fueled capacity from Kahe is
included; Hawaiian Electric, n.d.-f)). In 2024, this capacity resulted in the generation of 52.04
GWh of electricity (2,826.83 GWh if Kahe is included; U.S. Energy Information Administration
[EIA] n.d.-c).

Electricity use. In 2024, total electricity use in Waiʻanae moku was about 175 GWh (Hawaiian
Electric 2025a). This total equates to greater than 3.5 times the amount of electricity generated
within the Waiʻanae moku if Kahe is not included (meaning the community used 3.5 times more
electricity than is generated locally), and 6.2% of net generation if Kahe is included. In 2024,
residential customers in Waiʻanae moku accounted for 54.4% of total electricity use, followed by
other (16.1%), U.S. military (12.3%), office (6.6%), and education (5.4%) (Hawaiian Electric
2025a).

Electricity price. Hawai‘i consistently has the highest electricity prices in the United States; the
average price is sometimes as high as 3 times or more the United States average and fluctuates
with the price of crude oil on which the state relies for electricity generation (U.S. EIA, n.d.-a),
all of which is imported, primarily from foreign sources (Hawaiʻi State Energy Office 2026). The
average residential price on Oʻahu in 2024 was 42.87 cents/kilowatt-hour (kWh) (Hawaiian
Electric n.d.-a).

Percent of income spent on energy. Households in the Wai’anae moku spend a higher
percentage of household income on electricity on average than the rest of Oʻahu or the state. The
weighted average of the percentage of household income spent on energy across area median
income brackets is 7.15% for Waiʻanae moku, 4.02% for Honolulu County (encompasses the
island of Oʻahu), and 4.44% for the state of Hawaiʻi, as shown in Table ES-1.

Highlights of Considerations for Priority Energy Projects
In Section 3 we discuss considerations and resources for five priority energy projects identified
in the community’s energy plan (shown in Figure ES-1, page vii) to support decision-making on
which project or projects to pursue. The section includes results of background research on
priority energy project ideas, including an overview of each topic, local context and related
activities, next steps to pursue the project, and additional resources.

The energy project idea ranked highest by participants in multiple community meetings and
workshops was “support for resilience hub network design and analysis.” The community’s

 xii

interest in developing a resilience hub network stems from a desire for energy resilience at the
kaiāulu, or community, level. In the event of a grid outage or other electricity disruption, there is
a need for community members in each ahupuaʻa who may not have resilient energy systems at
their homes to have a nearby place to go for access to energy-dependent, sometimes lifesaving,
services, such as fresh or prepared food, potable water, shelter, and medical care.

Resilience hubs are community-serving facilities, often established at existing sites such as
libraries, schools, or community centers, that support residents before, during, and after
disruptions (such as grid outages caused by storms). These facilities are designed to enhance the
ability of a community or region to withstand, respond to, and recover from disruptive events,
and serve as a focal point for planning and community-building during normal conditions.

The resilience hub network concept is gaining traction in Hawaiʻi, with a 20-hub network on
Hawaiʻi Island, and networks being planned on Maui and Oʻahu. Twenty-one potential sites for
the Waiʻanae moku were identified through a project called Action 15, supported by the Center
for Resilient Neighborhoods (CERENE) and the City and County of Honolulu Office of Climate
Change, Sustainability, and Resiliency (OCCSR) as part of resilience planning for Oʻahu. Sites
identified through this process, which could inform further development of a network of hubs for
the moku, are shown in Figure ES-5.

Two goals in the community’s energy plan to be achieved through completion of the resilience
hub network project, as shown in Figure ES-1 (page vii), are to 1) identify and assess energy
resilience for at least one resilience hub in each ahupuaʻa in the Waiʻanae moku by 2027 and 2)
establish a network of resilience hubs in the Waiʻanae moku by 2030 wherein each hub will have

 xiii

a resilient energy system sufficient to maintain power throughout a grid outage of at least 3 days.
Steps in pursuit of this project could be to assess energy resilience for previously identified sites,
choose additional sites if needed, and conduct detailed techno-economic analysis of resilient
energy systems for priority sites that would comprise a network of hubs to serve all
neighborhoods in the moku. Guiding questions to inform detailed scoping of this project are
provided in Section 3.1.

 • Additional project ideas discussed at a higher level in Section 3 are:
 • Aggregation of rooftop solar and storage for resilience at the ʻohana and community level

1 Introduction
In 2024, community members in the Waiʻanae moku on the leeward coast of Oʻahu in Hawaiʻi
embarked on a strategic energy planning process that resulted in the Waiʻanae Moku Community
Energy Plan, Version 1. The planning area, which aligns with that of the Waiʻanae Sustainable
Communities Plan (City and County of Honolulu Department of Planning and Permitting, n.d.),
is shown in Figure 1. The process was initiated by Energize Waiʻanae Moku, a program of
Leeward Empowerment Initiatives (LEI) Foundation and Waiʻanae Sustainability Cooperative.
The plan was developed by an energy planning team comprising representatives of LEI
Foundation, Waiʻanae Sustainability Cooperative, Sierra Club of Hawaiʻi, Dibshawaii LLC,
Hawaiʻi State Energy Office (HSEO), and select community members. Additional community
members were invited to participate and provide input during two planning workshops, and
virtual meetings were held with interested parties in the community and Hawaiʻi’s energy sector.

As part of its support for the energy plan, NLR conducted a baseline assessment of the energy
landscape in Waiʻanae to support informed community participation in the planning process
(Section 2), and researched considerations and resources related to possible energy projects
prioritized by the community in the plan (Section 3). This report presents the results of those
activities, and supplements the Waiʻanae Moku Community Energy Plan, in which it is
referenced.

One purpose of the energy plan and this supplemental report is to support the community in
working toward the energy vision and goals that they established during the energy planning
process. A community energy vision for the Waiʻanae moku was first developed through
Energize Waiʻanae Moku community engagement, and refined through the energy planning
process. The community energy vision that guides the plan is presented here.

 We envision a Wai'anae moku that is energy secure through education, workforce
 development, and implementation of community-prioritized plans and projects
 that are pono, community-led and locally owned (in whole or in part), resulting in
 a diverse portfolio of community-scale technologies developed in accordance
 with cultural values, traditions, and respect for ʻāina; a Wai’anae moku where
 every resident experiences ‘ohana level resilience through whole home energy
 optimization utilizing renewable energy, energy storage, and energy conservation
 activities and efficiency measures (appliances, insulation, etc.); where every
 business is able to thrive while producing some or all of its own energy and
 practicing carbon kuleana; 5 where critical infrastructure and facilities maintain
 power during disruptions; and where kaiāulu-level prosperity is realized based on
 a reliable, affordable energy system.
The energy goals outlined in the energy plan, as developed and agreed to by the Waiʻanae
community participants, are as follows.

 1. Identify and assess energy resilience for at least one resilience hub in each ahupuaʻa in
 the Waiʻanae moku by 2027.
 2. Establish a network of resilience hubs in the Waiʻanae moku by 2030 wherein each hub
 will have a resilient energy system sufficient to maintain power throughout a grid outage
 of at least 3 days.
 3. Increase the number of Native-Hawaiian-owned renewable energy and adjacent
 businesses by 10% over baseline by 2035.
 4. Reduce the percentage of household income spent on energy in the Waiʻanae moku to at
 or below Oʻahu’s percentage by 2040 (using the weighted average across area median
 income brackets).

2 Waiʻanae Energy Baseline Assessment
One purpose of an energy baseline assessment is to create understanding among participants in
an energy planning process of relevant aspects of the local energy landscape. These aspects may
include identifying current energy sources, energy use by sector, locations of critical
infrastructure, energy costs, information about existing energy efficiency programs, and
available educational resources.

As part of this process, NLR collected publicly available data to conduct an energy baseline
assessment for Waiʻanae, the results of which are presented in this section. More granular and
potentially sensitive data about the energy system needed for in-depth technical analysis could be
sought through a nondisclosure agreement with the electric utility if the community were to
pursue and be selected for deep-dive technical assistance from ETIPP or another program.

2.1 Hawaiʻi State Energy Policy
The team conducted community-led energy planning for the Waiʻanae moku in the context of the
larger energy policy landscape in Hawaiʻi, and assumes the plan complies with the following
existing policies and regulations:

 • Hawaiʻi passed the first renewable portfolio standard in the United States, calling for
 100% of electricity sales to come from renewable energy by 2045 (Act 97 2015)
 • Net-negative carbon emissions by 2045 (Act 15 2018) and greenhouse gas emissions at
          least 50% below 2005 levels by 2030 (Act 238 2022)
 • Climate emergency declaration and request for collaboration toward a mobilization effort
 (Senate Concurrent Resolution 44 2021)
 • Public and private ground transportation powered by 100% renewable fuel sources by
 2045 (City and County of Honolulu Office of Climate Change, Sustainability and
 Resiliency 2017).
In January 2025 the governor of Hawaiʻi issued Executive Order 25-01 which outlines new
energy policy directives for the state, including accelerated renewable development goals for all
islands other than Oʻahu to achieve 100% renewable energy by 2035, setting a statewide goal of
50,000 additional distributed renewable energy installations by 2030, and directing state
departments to streamline and accelerate permitting of renewable energy projects (Governor of
Hawaii 2025). In response, the Hawaii Public Utilities Commission (PUC) outlined four primary
initiatives that support implementing the executive order, including a projected timeline of
related docket proceedings (Hawaii PUC 2025a). 6 The subsequent passage in July 2025 of
Hawaiʻi State Bill 589, “Relating To Renewable Energy,” codifies these and other directives
(State Bill 589 2025). 7

2.8 Adoption of Rooftop Solar and Storage in Hawaiʻi and Waiʻanae
The total number of grid-connected rooftop solar PV systems in Hawaiʻi at the end of 2024 was
114,000, with a total installed capacity of 1,410 MW. Hawaiʻi has among the highest rates of
rooftop solar adoption in the nation. As of 2025, 26% of Hawaiian Electric’s residential
customers and 43% of its customers in single-family homes had rooftop solar (Hawaiian Electric
2025b), and in 2023, 95% of residential solar PV installations in Hawaiʻi were paired with
battery energy storage (known as attachment rate), compared with the next highest attachment
rate in California at 14% (most other states were in the range of 4%‒10%; Barbose et al. 2024).

 18

For comparison, a total of 2,480 permits for distributed solar PV 10 had been issued in zip code
96792 (Waiʻanae moku) through 2024, 1,475 of which were paired with battery storage
(Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory [LBNL] 2024). Permits were issued to install solar on
the equivalent of 28% of the 8,657 single-unit detached homes in Waiʻanae (see Section 2.7),
and solar paired with storage on 17% of those homes, assuming all of the permits were for the
residential sector (this is a high approximation since the LBNL data include non-residential and
ground-mounted distributed systems; see footnote 10 below). Figure 14 shows the count of solar

3 Considerations and Resources for Priority Energy
  Projects in the Waiʻanae Community Energy Plan
One purpose of the Waiʻanae Moku Community Energy Plan is to prepare the community to
achieve its energy goals, which could include applying for and receiving additional support for
planning and implementation, such as deep-dive technical assistance through ETIPP on an
energy project prioritized through the planning process. The energy plan articulates community-

In this section, we discuss considerations and available resources related to the priority energy

or projects to pursue. Each subsection corresponds to one of the projects prioritized through the
planning process, in order of the community’s highest to lowest priority, with the greatest
emphasis on the highest-priority project. The five priority energy projects described in the
community energy plan are:

 1. Support for resilience hub network design and analysis
 2. Aggregation of rooftop solar and storage for resilience at the ʻohana and community level
 3. Agrivoltaic project design
 4. Renewable energy recycling industry and workforce development
 5. Assessment of additional renewable energy technologies for the moku.
This section includes the results of background research on priority energy projects, including an

3.1 Resilience Hub Network
The community’s interest in developing a resilience hub network stems from a desire for energy
resilience at the kaiāulu, or community, level. In the event of a grid outage or other electricity
disruption, there is a need for community members who may not have resilient energy systems at
their homes to have a nearby place to go for access to energy-dependent, sometimes lifesaving,
services, such as fresh or prepared food, potable water, shelter, and medical care.

Efforts to establish a network of resilience hubs in Waiʻanae and other mokus on Oʻahu are
ongoing. Next steps are to assess energy resilience for previously identified sites, choose
additional sites if needed, and conduct detailed techno-economic analysis of resilient energy
systems for priority sites that would comprise a network of hubs to serve all neighborhoods in
the moku.

3.1.1 Overview
Resilience hubs are community-serving facilities, often established at existing sites such as
libraries, schools, or community centers, that support residents before, during, and after
disruptions (such as grid outages caused by storms). These facilities are designed to enhance the
ability of a community or region to withstand, respond to, and recover from disruptive events,

 24

and serve as a focal point for planning and community-building during normal conditions. They
provide critical resources such as electricity, shelter, fresh water, food, medical services,
emergency response coordination, air conditioning and filtration, and other supplies and
programming in an emergency and during recovery (Belding and Beshilas 2024; Kramer et al.
2023). Resilience hubs are designed to operate in the following three modes:

 1. Every day. Facility operating as usual, and no major disruptions are occurring (also
 referred to as blue-sky conditions)
 2. Disruption. Everyday functions are disrupted for any duration as a result of a public
 health, natural hazard, or human-influenced event (black-sky conditions)
 3. Recovery. Efforts to restore normal or better conditions are underway; can last days to
 years (aligns with grey-sky conditions) (USDN 2024; Utilities Technology Council
 2018).
Electricity is essential for the operation of all critical infrastructure systems—water,
telecommunications, and transportation—and is necessary for delivering basic services
(Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency n.d.). Although diesel generators are an
important way to generate electricity during a grid outage, fuel can be in short supply during an
emergency, and delivery to the Waiʻanae moku can be impeded if the one primary access road,
which is on the coast, is flooded or otherwise impassable. Local, self-sustaining sources of
electricity generation, such as solar PV systems paired with battery storage, offer a quiet,
emissions-free alternative for energy. Developing renewable, resilient energy systems is also
consistent with Hawaiʻi state energy policy (see Section 2.1).

Energy resilience can be provided to a single building by an optimally sized energy source,
paired with storage if the source generates power intermittently, that can operate independent of
the main grid. For larger areas, such as a group of buildings on a campus or in a neighborhood, a
microgrid can provide energy resilience. A microgrid is defined by DOE as, “a group of
interconnected loads and distributed energy resources within clearly defined electrical
boundaries that acts as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid. A microgrid can
operate in either grid-connected or in island mode, including entirely off-grid applications” (U.S.
DOE Grid Deployment Office 2024). Renewable energy microgrids are powered by sources such
as solar, wind, or hydropower, and paired with battery storage sized to provide power for a
specified duration to meet resilience goals. An example microgrid including multiple sources of
electricity generation and energy storage is shown in Figure 18.

3.1.2 Local Context
Across Hawaiʻi, individuals and organizations are working to form networks of resilience hubs
to help ensure communities are prepared to support and sustain themselves in all
circumstances―everyday, during disruptions, and throughout recovery. For example, on Hawaiʻi
Island, a network of 20 resilience hub partners is supported by Vibrant Hawaiʻi through
facilitation of cross-sector communication, training and certification, peer-to-peer learning, and
resource sharing (Vibrant Hawaiʻi n.d.). It is worth noting that few if any of these hubs currently
have backup power generation.

On Maui, the Maui County Resilience Hub Network, facilitated by the Living Pono Project,
brings together local leaders, nonprofit partners, and government agencies for collaborative
planning, resource sharing, and culturally grounded solutions to build resilient neighborhoods
(Living Pono Project n.d.). In recent years, county government sought community input on
developing a resilience hub network, including desired attributes and candidate sites, and
identified three pilot sites in west, central, and east Maui, respectively, for resilient power
feasibility studies (Roode and Martinac 2020). After a devastating fire in Lahaina in 2023,
innovative, grassroots efforts developed mobile resilience hubs powered by solar PV with battery
storage to provide a rapid response during and after the fires (Dugan et al. forthcoming).

3.2 Aggregation of Rooftop Solar and Storage
The idea for an energy project focused on aggregating rooftop solar and storage is based on a
priority among community members to increase resilience at the ʻohana, or household, level.
Community members expressed a desire to focus on energy technologies other than large-scale
solar and wind, due in part to the opinion that the community already hosts enough solar that
serves other parts of Oʻahu. Some also do not want to burden other mokus with the responsibility
of hosting large-scale projects.

Instead, some community members want to focus on local solutions to energy resilience starting
with energy efficiency at the household level, and for those who can, to install rooftop or other
smaller-scale solar PV systems with battery energy storage. The vision is for the adoption of a
combination of household- and community-level energy resilience technologies to ensure all
members of the community―renters, owners, single-family or multifamily, housed or unhoused,
residential and commercial―have access to affordable, reliable, and resilient energy.

Various approaches and configurations of solar and storage can help achieve this vision.
Community-based renewable energy (CBRE), solar and storage on multifamily housing,
microgrids (described in Section 3.1.1), virtual power plants, and community-based ownership
models of distributed energy resources are all possible, some of which have already been
explored by the community. In order for aggregation to provide resilience benefits, the
aggregated systems would need to be able to island (isolate from the main grid) during grid
outages through some type of microgrid project.

3.2.1 Community Solar
Community solar, known in Hawaiʻi as CBRE, is a project model in which individual
households who cannot or do not want to install solar on their own rooftops, including renters,
can subscribe to a community-scale solar project, often 5 MW or smaller in size, to offset their
home electricity use. The solar project is typically sited nearby, often either ground-mounted or
hosted on a commercial rooftop or parking lot canopy, with a subscription model enabled by
virtual net metering, which allows for the distribution of bill credits from the solar project to any
account within a utility’s service territory. For programs with consolidated billing, credits from
the community solar subscription are integrated into the utility bill, and in many cases result in
bill savings compared with retail electricity rates. In the Waiʻanae moku 40% of households rent
their homes and another 7% are owners who live in multifamily housing (Figure 13, page 18)
meaning nearly half of moku residents could be candidates for community solar, among others.

CBRE was enabled in Hawaiʻi by legislation passed in 2015, and Hawaiian Electric began
implementing its program in 2018 (State of Hawaii Department of Commerce and Consumer

 31

Affairs n.d.). Since then, Hawaiian Electric has administered two phases of the CBRE program:
Phase 1, capped at 8 MW of total statewide capacity, 4 MW of which are online, and Phase 2,
capped at 250 MW with a focus on low- and moderate-income households (Hawaiian Electric
n.d.-e). There are currently five active CBRE projects in the program―two on Oʻahu and one
each on Maui, Molokai, and Hawaiʻi Island (Hawaiian Electric n.d.-f)―with a few more pending
or in development.

For local context, the Waiʻanae Sustainability Cooperative was formed to develop a community
solar project that prioritizes participation of low- and moderate-income and Hawaiian Homestead
households. 16 The organization worked with Shake Energy Collaborative to explore how the
community could design and own the project (Shake Energy Collaborative n.d.). The group
aimed to participate in Phase 2 of the CBRE program, but the project has not yet been selected.

Despite some progress, in the words of the Hawaii PUC, “[CBRE] is a program that has not lived
up to its core objective of saving money for ratepayers who don’t control their own rooftops. The
current Phase 2 has attracted only a fraction of the 235 MW program cap, and nearly all the
developers working on projects have struggled mightily to meet the program’s complex
requirements and schedules” (Hawaii PUC 2024). The commission intends to study whether the
program should be restructured, such as by replacing subscriber organizations that currently own,
develop, or operate CBRE projects with utility-managed enrollment.

Action is underway at the state level to address the issue. The PUC identified CBRE as one of
four key initiatives to support implementing Executive Order 25-01 by providing direct bill
savings and other benefits to communities, expanding energy service markets, streamlining
energy procurement, and enabling DERs to provide grid services with appropriate compensation
(Hawaii PUC 2025a). The implementation plan lists actions by year related to CBRE; 2025
includes expected completion of a small project (240 kW) on Oʻahu as part of Phase 2. Similarly,
State Bill 589 lists CBRE among mechanisms that will help achieve the goal of 50,000 new
customer-sited distributed solar projects to be deployed by 2050, along with grid services
programs, microgrids, and retail wheeling 17 with fair compensation (SB 589 2025).

For additional support for CBRE in Hawaiʻi, no-cost technical assistance is available through the
National Community Solar Partnership+ (NCSP+), 18 a program of DOE’s Solar Energy
Technologies Office. The Community Power Accelerator, 19 also part of NCSP+, provides
training and resources to energy project developers, and connects developers and financiers.

3.2.2 Solar and Storage for Multifamily Housing
Solar and storage projects for multifamily housing provide access to affordable, reliable energy
for resident households, a higher proportion of which rent rather than own their homes. In the
Waiʻanae moku, 4,073 households, or nearly 30%, live in multifamily housing of 2-50+ units. Of
households in the moku that rent their homes, 55% (3,060) live in multifamily housing,
compared with 12% (1,013) of households that own (see Section 2.7). Although solar and
storage adoption for multifamily housing can be complex and costly, an energy project focused
on this kind of situation may provide aggregated energy resilience to many households, and
technical assistance and incentives are available.

An energy project focused on solar and storage for multifamily housing could start by engaging
with multifamily housing providers, as well as state and local agencies and organizations that
support them, about where they are in the process of exploring solar and storage and connecting
them with resources. Some related plans identified in the Waiʻanae Moku Community Energy
Plan (LEI Foundation 2025) could be a useful source of information on what other sectors or
entities are already doing in this space. In addition to solar and storage development, Hawaiʻi
Energy offers energy conservation incentives for multifamily housing.20

National organizations like Grid Alternatives 21 and the Clean Energy States Alliance 22 offer
resources for multifamily affordable housing providers for solar and storage adoption. In

resources to help housing providers overcome barriers to solar and storage adoption, which are

provide the distribution grid with demand response, voltage regulation, and other grid services
that enhance reliability.

and chargers, and smart appliances like water heaters and thermostats, as well as commercial,
industrial, and agricultural loads, or sources of electrical demand, that can be curtailed, or
adjusted as needed. The virtual power plant communicates with the transmission and distribution

additional buildout of transmission and distribution to deliver the power.

batteries or EV batteries that have been charged from solar PV during daylight hours, or by

time to reduce electricity demand in exchange for compensation. The green line in Figure 22

3.2.4 Community-Based Ownership Models of Distributed Energy Resources
Interest in community-based ownership models of distributed energy resources by participants in
energy planning was based on values of independence and self-sufficiency, prioritization of
energy self-determination, and an energy focus area of community ownership models for the
Waiʻanae moku. Local community-based organizations and their partners have already made
notable strides in this area, such as the Solarize808 campaign 28 and the Waiʻanae Sustainability
Cooperative’s efforts to develop a CBRE project (Section 3.2.1). This section highlights
information, examples, and resources to inspire community energy planners regarding additional
opportunities.

According to Sippert (2022), community ownership of distributed solar typically refers to
ownership of the solar panels and the electricity generated from them. Sippert provides case
studies of community ownership from across the United States, and explains that most
community-owned community solar projects in particular are owned by a limited liability
company, some of which are owned by cooperatives, but it is rare for a cooperative to have
direct ownership of a solar project. Cooperatives are member-owned entities that distribute
benefits equitably to members and operate on principles that include providing services at the
lowest possible cost, distributing benefits and obligations equitably, and making decisions
democratically. Despite numerous benefits that come from community-owned distributed solar,
regulatory, policy, and financial barriers as well as external structural factors can hinder project
development (Sippert 2022).

3.3 Agrivoltaic Project Design
Although Waiʻanae moku community members value protecting and caring for the land and
teaching younger generations its highest value, there is also an awareness of the energy-water-
food nexus and benefits of developing systems with multiple purposes that work with the land.
Some community members are interested in an energy project focused on agrivoltaic project
design is based on their awareness of high solar irradiance in the area, a strong local agricultural
industry, and a desire for shared energy projects, connected to local food systems, and adapted to
the local environment. Although community members prioritize agriculture as a primary land
use, some are open to designing projects for multiple land uses, including solar PV, either for
electricity generation for the farm itself, or for community-scale PV projects.

3.3.1 Overview
Agrivoltaics is the co-location of solar PV and agricultural activity like growing crops, grazing
animals, or cultivating pollinators to benefit neighboring fields. Agrivoltaic projects are carefully
planned, designed, and built to ensure dual land use benefits both activities. Configuration
considerations include panel height, spacing, and racking equipment to allow access by workers
and equipment; how PV technology can affect available sunlight, microclimates, vegetation
growth, and project economics; and selecting and establishing crops that can thrive in growing
conditions under the panels (Macknick et al. 2022). According to NLR’s Agrivoltaics Map, as of
August 2025, there were 604 agrivoltaic sites across the United States encompassing 63,455
acres with an installed capacity of over 10 gigawatts (NLR 2025c).

Agrivoltaic projects have been found to benefit agriculture by decreasing evaporation of water
from the soil, reducing irrigation requirements, fostering biodiversity, improving crop yield, and
ensuring resistance to extreme weather such as droughts. Agrivoltaics can also help farmers by
diversifying their income through land leases. For PV, agrivoltaics can improve solar
performance by cooling panels and reducing costs for vegetation management. Business models
are more complex for dual use than each industry alone, and successful project development
requires successful collaboration between farmers, financiers, and others (U.S. DOE 2023b).

3.3.2 Local Context
In Hawaiʻi, where land for food production is limited and demand for locally grown food is high,
there can be resistance to the idea of sacrificing land and agricultural productivity for solar
generation. Yet, there is some interest in agrivoltaics within Hawaiʻi’s energy and agricultural
sectors, and projects are underway.

For example, on Oʻahu, a demonstration project in Mililani is evaluating the feasibility of
growing various crops under solar panels, with an emphasis on indigenous varieties, and aiming
to provide guidance to local farmers and policymakers. The research is being conducted by the
Agrivoltaic Research and Development Center and is funded by three private energy
companies—Longroad Energy, AES Corporation, and Clearway Energy Group, which own the
array. The project has tested large-scale aquaponic lettuce production between rows of PV panels
as well as in-ground cultivation of crops such as broccoli, green onions, leafy greens, eggplant,
lavender, and poha berries (Ladao 2023; Richmond-Mueller 2023).

 38

A commercial agrivoltaic project, Mahi Solar, is expected to come online in 2027. The 120
MWAC project on 617 acres west of Kunia Road will be developed by Longroad Energy who
says it will work with local farmers to raise crops and livestock between and under PV panels as
part of a community benefits package associated with the project (Longroad Energy n.d.).

3.3.3 Energy Project Next Steps
Because community motivation for this energy project is to develop systems with multiple
purposes that work with the land, this section highlights resources available to help those
interested in pursuing agrivoltaic projects in the United States. The AgriSolar Clearinghouse 35 is
a nationwide hub developed by the National Center for Appropriate Technology to connect
businesses, landowners, and researchers with trusted resources to support the growth of co-
located solar and agriculture. Another resource is NLR’s longstanding InSPIRE program
(Innovative Solar Practices Integrated with Rural Economies and Ecosystems), which convenes
stakeholders, conducts research with multidisciplinary teams at over 30 test sites, develops tools
and other resources, and offers services to support the replication and scaling-up of agrivoltaics
research and deployment. The “Getting Started” page of the program’s website 36 outlines initial
steps, including information about technical assistance.

Lists of other projects and programs providing agrivoltaic decision support and other assistance

3.4 Energy Materials Recycling Industry and Workforce Development
This project idea is based on community interest in recycling, reuse, and developing a zero-waste
economy for renewable energy materials. An energy materials recycling industry and workforce
development project could explore pathways for developing a local industry to handle residential
and commercial energy waste, including decommissioning and disposal of renergy systems in
West Oʻahu.

3.4.1 Overview
In a zero-waste economy for energy materials, equipment is designed from the beginning with its
end of life in mind by reducing the use of raw and hazardous materials and planning for ease of
reuse and recycling or upcycling when taken out of service. In this way, discarded materials are
reused or recycled back into their core building blocks to enable an infinite loop of
manufacturing, use, deconstruction, and remanufacturing, and address constraints on the
availability of raw materials (NLR 2021).

As adoption of renewable energy technologies in Hawaiʻi and around the world continues to
increase, so does the need to develop systems to manage this equipment at end of life (St. John
2022). This is particularly true on islands, in which most energy equipment is typically imported
in, and end-of-life material is exported out. That said, developing or expanding a recycling

3.5 Local Energy Technology Options
Community members have expressed interest in understanding which energy technologies in
addition to solar PV and wind may be pono (correct or proper) for the Waiʻanae moku, including
consideration of leading-edge technologies. Technologies of interest to community members
include hydropower, marine energy (including ocean thermal energy conversion), and hydrogen
production from renewable sources. Nuclear energy has also been raised but is not discussed
further in this report because nuclear power plants are not currently allowed by Article 11 of the
Hawaiʻi constitution (Hawaii State Legislature 1978), though the topic has been under debate at
the state level (Hawaii State Legislature 2025a; 2025b).

This section provides information about some of the energy technologies in which the
community has expressed interest, as well as a brief discussion of market maturity of energy
technologies. Local members of the planning team have also discussed the possibility of
engaging in a process to facilitate community participation in land-use and siting decisions,
which is discussed here as well.

3.5.1 Energy Technologies of Interest During the Planning Process

3.5.1.1 Marine Energy Including Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion Technology
Marine energy is power generated from ocean waves, currents, tides, or changes in temperature,
pressure, or salinity, and is the world's largest untapped renewable energy resource according to
NLR (2025d). One way marine energy is generated from temperature differences in water is
through ocean thermal energy conversion (U.S. DOE n.d.-c). This process uses the temperature
difference between the sun-warmed surface water and cold water in the deep ocean to power
thermal heat engines (OpenEI 2025a). More information about marine energy is available from
HSEO (n.d.-e).

Although the technical resource potential 40 of marine energy in the United States is high (2,300
terawatt-hours [TWh] per year in the 50 states, equivalent to 57% of the electricity generated by
those states in 2019), marine energy technologies are at relatively early stages of development
(Kilcher et al. 2021), and are not commercially available at this time (Southeast National Marine
Renewable Energy Center n.d.). Ocean thermal energy conversion technology has been
successfully demonstrated on Hawaiʻi Island at the Natural Energy Laboratory of Hawaiʻi
Authority facility at Keahole Point, though, “no cost studies have been performed for land-based
ocean thermal energy conversion plants within the last 25 years” (PRIMERE, n.d.).

Additional marine energy research is underway in Hawaiʻi. Hawai'i Natural Energy Institute at
the University of Hawai'i at Mānoa conducts and supports analysis and modeling of renewable
energy generation scenarios, grid upgrades, and storage, and is a project site for the Testing
Expertise and Access for Marine Energy Research (TEAMER) program. Through this program
the institute has been working on a type of wave energy converter called an oscillating water

3.5.1.2 Hydropower
Hydropower uses flowing water in rivers, streams, reservoirs, pipes, or irrigation ditches to turn
turbines to generate electricity. Pumped storage hydropower facilities operate like batteries by
pumping water from a lower reservoir to a higher one when electricity is inexpensive and
releasing the water back down to generate electricity when needed.

Some of Hawaiʻi’s first power plants in the late 1800s were hydroelectric facilities, and some
existing plants date back to the early 1900s (HSEO n.d.-c). HSEO’s hydroelectric energy map
shows that two sites in the Waiʻanae moku have been studied for hydroelectric potential:
Waiʻanae Plantation (project type: storage; status: inactive [1996]) and Kahe Generating Station
pumped storage hydropower (project type: pumped storage; status: identified site [2005]) (HSEO
n.d.-a). An assessment of the technical potential of hydropower in the Waiʻanae moku could be
conducted to understand what current opportunities may exist, though due to dry conditions in
Wai‘anae moku it is unlikely that hydropower offers much local potential as a future energy
resource.

3.5.2 Energy Technology Market Maturity
When assessing energy technology options for a community it is important to consider maturity
and commercial viability. Although many energy technologies are in the research and
development stage, they may be years away from being able to meet electric grid needs in a
commercially or technically viable manner. Deeper-dive technical assistance could involve
detailed techno-economic analyses of commercially available technologies for the moku to
understand the amount of energy that could be generated from each source in a particular
location and what it may cost to develop and operate.

3.5.3 Community-Informed Land-Use Planning and Energy Siting
Some community members have suggested exploring more deeply with the community which
land areas would be acceptable to use for renewable energy development, including multiple
uses such as co-locating systems with agriculture, and which land areas not to use for these
purposes. A process like this could serve to communicate community priorities to the utility,
developers, landowners, and other interested parties. Other important considerations in a process
like this would include property ownership and existing land-use plans and zoning and building
ordinances. Challenges to community-informed land use planning and local energy generation
siting include the need for property owner buy-in and adherence to existing land-use plans and
zoning and building ordinances. As a result, community processes to discuss these topics may be
the most useful for identifying where not to site projects.

Maps from NLR’s technical potential assessment of large-scale solar and wind in the Waiʻanae
moku (Section 2.9) could be used as a starting place for which areas to consider further. These
maps could also be revised for this process to show different assumptions about minimum or
maximum land areas, energy generation potential, or other factors to include or exclude. Despite
the technical potential, during community workshops participants expressed an interest in

4 Conclusion
The energy baseline assessment presented in this report was conducted to ensure participants in
energy planning for the Waiʻanae moku are well informed about the current status of the local
energy system including aspects such as relevant policies, sources of electricity generation,
electricity prices, and percent of household income spent on electricity.

Research on considerations and resources for energy projects prioritized by the community
focused primarily on the highest-priority project to be considered for deep-dive technical
assistance from the ETIPP program, namely support for resilience hub network design and
analysis with a focus on energy systems. This report also provides information on the context
and available resources for community energy planners to use in the pursuit of additional energy
projects of interest.

The report concludes with key takeaways from these two primary components of NLR’s support
for the Waiʻanae moku’s community energy planning process.

Energy Baseline Assessment
The cost of electricity is higher in Hawaiʻi than the U.S. average, and the percentage of
household income spent on electricity is higher in the Waiʻanae moku than elsewhere on Oʻahu
and statewide. One of Hawaiian Electric’s largest fossil fuel-powered electric generators on
Oʻahu is located near the Waiʻanae moku—Kahe Power Plant just south of the moku. Within the
moku, there are two existing utility-scale solar PV facilities and one planned solar PV and
storage facility. Electricity use in the moku equates to 6.2% of net local generation if Kahe is
included. While the adoption of residential rooftop solar and storage may be somewhat lower in
the Waianae moku than average adoption by Hawaiian Electric’s residential customers (28% of
single-unit detached homes in the moku based on permit data compared with 43% of single-
family homes in the utility’s service territory), the attachment rate of storage with solar PV in the
moku in recent years has been consistent with the rest of the state (95%, which is considerably
higher than the rest of the U.S.). While NLR estimates the technical potential for rooftop solar in
the moku is 355 MW, available hosting capacity on the distribution system ranges from up to 5%
in most areas of Waiʻanae to 15%–30% in Nanakuli, and 50% and greater further south. For the
areas in which hosting capacity is low, additional grid upgrades or non-wires alternatives may be
needed to interconnect new capacity.

Energy Project Considerations and Resources
Key considerations for each of the five energy project ideas prioritized by the community are as
follows. These considerations were compiled in response to community members’ requests for
more information on these topics.

Resilience Hub Network. The community’s interest in expanding a resilience hub network in
the moku stems from a desire for energy resilience at the kaiāulu, or community, level. The
resilience hub network expansion project idea is consistent with concurrent planning efforts. For
this project to be successful, engagement with interested parties such as representatives of points
of contact at hub sites, the utility, and technical experts to evaluate, plan, and design resilient
energy systems will be essential. It will also be key to identify sources of funding, financing, and

 44

incentives, and to ensure that identified solutions are consistent with relevant policies and
regulations.

Aggregation of Rooftop Solar and Storage. The idea for an energy project focused on
aggregating rooftop solar and storage is based on a priority among community members to
increase resilience at the ʻohana, or household, level. There are several possible approaches to
aggregating solar and storage for this purpose, including community-based renewable energy,
solar and storage on multifamily housing, microgrids, virtual power plants, and community-
based ownership models of distributed energy resources. In order for aggregation to provide
resilience benefits, the aggregated systems would need to be able to island (isolate from the main
grid) during grid outages.

Given that nearly half of households in the moku either rent their homes (40%) or are owners
who live in multifamily housing (7%), community solar has the potential to increase local access
to solar considerably. While the CBRE program in Hawaiʻi has been criticized for not living up
to its promise so far, efforts to reform the program are underway at the state level, which could
enable this pathway for the community. Although solar and storage adoption for multifamily
housing can be complex and costly, an energy project focused on this deployment model may
provide aggregated energy resilience to many households, and technical assistance is available.

Agrivoltaic Project Design. Local interest in an energy project focused on agrivoltaic project
design is based on an awareness of high solar irradiance in the area, a strong local agricultural
industry, and a desire for shared energy projects, connected to local food systems, and adapted to
the local environment. Although community members prioritize agriculture as a primary land
use, there is an openness to designing projects for multiple land uses, including solar PV, either
for electricity generation for the farm itself, or for community-scale PV projects. Despite some
local resistance to using scarce land for energy production, there is some interest in agrivoltaics,
and projects are underway. Many resources are available to help those interested in pursuing
agrivoltaic projects in the United States, including support available from DOE’s Innovative
Solar Practices Integrated with Rural Economies and Ecosystems (InSPIRE) program and the
AgriSolar Clearinghouse.

Energy Materials Recycling Industry and Workforce Development. A renewable energy
recycling industry and workforce development project could explore pathways for developing a
local industry to handle residential and commercial energy waste, including decommissioning
and disposal of local energy systems in West Oʻahu. This project idea is based on community
interest in recycling, reuse, and developing a zero-waste economy for renewable energy
materials, in which equipment is designed from the beginning with its end of life in mind by
reducing the use of raw and hazardous materials and planning for ease of reuse and recycling or
upcycling when taken out of service. On Oʻahu and in Hawaiʻi the topic of recycling renewable
energy equipment is the subject of research and policymaking, and at least one company offers
services.

understanding which locally-available energy technologies in addition to solar PV and wind may
be pono for the Waiʻanae moku, including consideration of leading-edge technologies.
