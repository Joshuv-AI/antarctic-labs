Executive Summary
This paper outlines a replicable framework for assessing gaps, reach, and the impact of technical
assistance (TA) programs, using the Energy to Communities Gap Region Outreach Project as a
case study. By applying qualitative social science methods, this framework provides tools for
effectively engaging with underrepresented regions, identifying barriers to participation, and
tailoring solutions to local needs. The study highlights the importance of leveraging stakeholder
insights to address disparities in program engagement and participation, offering actionable
recommendations to enhance program responsiveness and optimize resource allocation. This
approach serves as a guide for practitioners seeking to expand the reach and effectiveness of
federally funded TA programs while ensuring alignment with community priorities and capacity
needs.

Identifying the Gap
The study began by identifying barriers preventing certain regions from engaging with federal
TA programs. Exploratory calls and data analysis revealed six primary facets of capacity—time,
staff, skills, funding, mission alignment, and application readiness—as key obstacles. These
insights provided a foundation for understanding the systemic challenges faced by organizations
in accessing clean energy opportunities and guided the design of tailored interventions for E2C.

Co-designing the Methods
A core component of this effort was the co-design of research methods, based on a social science
approach. The framework incorporated qualitative content analysis (QCA), participatory
engagement strategies, and iterative feedback loops. These methods prioritized the integration of
community priorities and ensured that data collection methods were flexible and responsive to
stakeholder needs. Each phase of the project—listening, exploring, proposing, systematizing and
informing —was designed to incorporate community input and adapt to emerging insights.

Conducting the Research
The research was conducted in five key phases:

1. Listen (Initial Conversations): Exploratory outreach calls were conducted to understand
 community priorities and identify systemic barriers. Insights were analyzed using QCA.
2. Explore (Listening Sessions): In-depth listening sessions designed after initial analysis,
 focused on community-specific challenges and opportunities, fostering co-design of
 potential solutions.
3. Propose (Engagement Sessions): Preliminary recommendations were introduced during
 public engagement sessions, supported by surveys and open discussions to gather participant
 feedback on preliminary research results.
4. Systematize and Inform: Feedback and qualitative data analysis were used to refine
 recommendations, ensuring they were actionable, technically sound, and aligned with
 community needs.

 iv

5. Implement: The refined recommendations were translated into actionable implementation
 strategies, ensuring they were feasible from both a technical and economic perspective.
 These strategies were designed with ongoing community engagement in mind, allowing for
 future adjustments based on continuous feedback.

The iterative design of the research ensured that feedback loops were built into each phase,
enabling researchers to adapt methods and recommendations based on input from stakeholders.

Considerations for Replication
The study provides actionable guidance for practitioners seeking to replicate these methods in
other TA programs. Key considerations include:

 • Leveraging Social Science Methods: The use of QCA and participatory engagement
 strategies enabled a deep understanding of community priorities and systemic barriers.
 • Partner Selection: Effective regional partners were those with adequate capacity,
 established networks, and project management experience. Assessing these attributes
 during partner selection proved critical for successful implementation.
 • Iterative Feedback: Incorporating feedback loops ensured that program recommendations
 were continuously refined to align with community needs.
 • Tailoring Engagement Strategies: Adapting outreach methods to local contexts and
 leveraging regional networks enhanced program reach and impact.

By integrating these considerations, TA programs can increase their effectiveness, expand their
reach, and maximize their impact, advancing affordable and accessible energy solutions for all.

1 Introduction: The Importance of Social Science
 Research
Social science research can provide a community-grounded perspective, using qualitative and
quantitative methods to ensure that technologies, programs, and initiatives align with the needs,
priorities, and lived experiences of local groups. Effective technical assistance (TA) requires a deep
understanding of the social and environmental factors that shape how people relate to technologies and
the implementation of energy policies and programs. These structural factors influence perceptions,
adoption, and resistance to technology implementation, as well as the overall success of such efforts.

Social and environmental dynamics—such as local governance structures, cultural values, economics,
and previous experiences —profoundly shape the relationship between communities and technologies or
assistance programs. By integrating social science analyses, TA projects can better navigate these
complexities, ensuring that solutions resonate with community contexts and address structural barriers.
This approach supports more tailored outcomes that can lead to greater positive impacts, more expansive
program relevance and reach, better relationships between TA providers and recipients, and more
effective use of federal investments in TA. Therefore, those projects are more likely to be successfully
implemented following TA delivery.

Planning and implementing changes in energy systems is not only a technical challenge but also a
profound cultural and political one. Energy systems planning is a socio-technical processes, shaped and
influenced by human agency—imagined, politicized, contested, and progressed or slowed down based
on societal, political, and cultural dynamics of a given place. Therefore, understanding the social
dimensions of energy systems is essential for creating effective, locally relevant policies and programs.

While the importance of social science in energy studies has long been recognized (Stirling 2014,
Sovacool et al 2015), it remains underrepresented in applied research, policy circles, and TA programs.
Integrating social science methodologies into energy studies and TA provides a more holistic
understanding of how energy systems are adopted or face opposition in a given place, and how policies
and programs can be more effectively designed and implemented to create resilient, local energy
economies that lead to opportunities for more affordable energy sources and increased local energy
workforce. Social science methods and analysis play a critical role in the field of TA by offering a
systematic approach to grounding programs in local priorities and ensuring the support is responsive to
local needs. This approach, then, supports the design and implementation of high-impact initiatives that
strategically utilize federal investments. Social science perspectives can also guide the evaluation and
assessment of existing TA programs, providing valuable feedback to improve their reach and
effectiveness.

Specifically for the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), social science research can be used to rigorously
assess its initiatives and provide insights to make data-informed adjustments that improve programs.
Social science methods should be embedded throughout all stages of program development, from design
to implementation and ongoing evaluation. Incorporating these methods early on can ensure that DOE
initiatives are grounded in local needs and priorities and responsive to stakeholders across the United
States. Both impact assessments, which assess the causal effects of programs, and program evaluations,
which track program implementation, are essential for advancing impactful programs that support U.S.
energy dominance. Tailoring these assessments to the agency’s specific needs ensures they are robust,

 1

credible, and actionable, leading to improved program outcomes and more informed decision-making
across the agency.

In this paper, we present the Energy to Communities (E2C) Gap Region Outreach Project as a case study
to demonstrate how social science research can enhance the effectiveness and reach of TA programs.
We begin by outlining the objectives and characteristics of the E2C program and the identified regional
disparities in program participation. Next, we describe the methods employed to engage with local
stakeholders in areas of low engagement, uncover barriers to participation, and gather actionable
insights. The results of this analysis highlight key structural and contextual factors influencing program
engagement and were used to provide recommendations for addressing these disparities. Finally, we
discuss the broader implications of applying social science frameworks in federally funded TA
programs, emphasizing their potential to ensure program responsiveness to local priorities, enhance
program reach and participation, and improve program impact and outcomes. This work serves as a
guide for integrating social science approaches into future energy initiatives.

2 Case Study: Gap Region Outreach Project
Launched in 2023, E2C is a no-cost TA program designed to help local governments, utilities, and
community-based organizations reach their energy goals. After the first year of E2C implementation, a
team of social scientists at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) developed methods to
investigate regional disparities in program participation. This analysis revealed significant engagement
gaps across the United States, which the team identified as “gap regions.” To explore the reasons behind
the lack of participation from certain areas, we employed social science methodologies, including
engagement with local residents and stakeholders and the collection of both qualitative and quantitative
data. The findings of this study, referred to as the “Clean Energy to Communities: Gap Region Outreach
Project” (Anderson, Blanco and Reichelt 2024), were published and include key insights and
recommendations for improving the program drawn from the analysis of that data.

The E2C Gap Region Outreach Project is seen as a relevant case study on using social science research
to improve existing TA programs in response to local priorities, and to enhance participation. The
methodology utilized in the program analysis can be replicated across other federally funded programs,
generating data-driven assessments that improve program efficiency, relevance to unique local contexts,
and longevity. By institutionalizing such methods, federal programs can systematically address regional
participation disparities, adapt to evolving needs, and create scalable frameworks that ensure program
offerings are accessible, and of interest, to entities with distinct priorities and social contexts.

This section outlines the key characteristics of E2C and the objectives of the E2C Gap Region Outreach
Project, providing the foundation for presenting the methods used to evaluate and address the regional
disparities identified in this study. We aim to provide a clear pathway for replicating this type of analysis
in other TA projects.

 2.1 Energy to Communities
E2C, managed by NREL on behalf of DOE, delivers innovative and interdisciplinary TA to
municipalities, Tribes, utilities, and community-based organizations across the United States. The
program supports the identification and implementation of strategies spanning power, mobility,
buildings, and grid systems. E2C offers three distinct levels of support, each tailored to meet varying
technical needs: expert match services, peer-learning cohorts, and comprehensive in-depth partnerships.

 2

 2.1.1 Expert Match
Expert match provides free, short-term TA to address near-term energy challenges and questions. This is
most suitable for communities that could benefit from assistance to inform time-sensitive decisions and
identify and understand available options to achieve their energy goals. Expert match provides 40–60
hours of TA over 3–4 months.

 2.1.2 Peer-Learning Cohorts
Peer-learning cohorts convene entities around a common energy topic to exchange strategies and best
practices; learn in a collaborative environment; and workshop program proposals, action plans, or
strategies to overcome their challenges. Experts provide participants with TA, case studies, analysis and
modeling tools, templates, training materials, and facilitated collaboration. Peer-learning cohorts
convene for approximately 6 months each.

 2.1.3 In-Depth Partnerships
In-depth partnerships provide TA to teams of local government entities, community-based
organizations, electric utilities, and other key organizations from a single region. The team works with
national laboratory subject matter experts to develop an actionable energy plan and address key energy
challenges. National laboratory staff apply robust modeling and analysis tools and conduct hardware-in-
the-loop demonstrations of energy strategies that are adapted to the community's unique conditions and
contexts. Partnerships span a 3-year period, allowing local decision makers to evaluate and test potential
scenarios and strategies for their energy transition before full technology deployment, thereby lowering
risks to implementation.

 2.2 Identifying Regional Gaps in E2C Participation

Since its launch, the E2C program has engaged with entities in nearly every state. The program has been
recognized for its efforts to make TA widely accessible and for supporting energy solutions driven by
local priorities (NREL 2024). However, we sought to deepen our understanding of program reach and
identify potential solutions to address gaps in participation. To achieve this, we analyzed the distribution
of program benefits to determine who was accessing E2C Peer-learning cohorts TA, how these benefits
were geographically distributed, and whether certain regions might be unintentionally excluded.

Our analysis began by visualizing the geographic reach of E2C TA. Using ArcGIS Pro, we plotted all
program participants as points on a U.S. map. To identify areas with minimal participation, we
employed heat map symbology and Hot Spot Analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*), which highlighted regions with
a lower density of participants. These areas were classified as cold spots, representing gaps in
engagement. Building on this geographic analysis, we categorized four distinct gap regions by
considering their unique geographic, cultural, political, and historical characteristics. These regions will
further be referred to as “gap regions” throughout this paper:

 • Appalachia
 • Gulf Coast
 • Midwest
 • North Plains

 3

This approach provided a framework for understanding disparities in program engagement and informed
the development of strategies to enhance participation in the priority regions.

 Figure 1. Gap regions

The regional boundaries identified in our analysis served as a foundation for examining how geographic,
cultural, political, or historical differences may create distinct challenges or necessitate tailored
solutions. Our analysis seeks to address two key questions to better understand the underlying reasons
for these gaps and identify strategies for enhancing the program’s reach:

 1. What prevents communities in the gap regions from engaging with the E2C program?

 2. How can the E2C program become more accessible to communities in the gap regions?

To explore these questions, we applied social science methodologies to examine the social and
environmental factors that influence how communities interact with E2C TA offerings. Additionally, we
sought to identify potential changes to improve program relevance, accessibility, and impact in these gap
regions.

3 Utilizing a Social Science Framework
Using social science methods to design and assess existing TA programs is critical to address the
complex socio-technical nature of energy transitions. An established body of literature (Miller 2013,
Stern 2017, Hess and Sovacool 2020, Krumm et al. 2022, Sovacool 2014, Sovacool et al 2015, Stern
2017), particularly from the field of Science and Technology Studies, has argued that energy transitions
are not just technical challenges; they are deeply cultural, political, and societal phenomena that usually
occur in unstructured and non-linear ways. While demonstrating the technical and economic feasibility
of energy systems is crucial, this should be combined with local contexts and priorities.

 4

Through direct engagement with community members, local stakeholders, and grounded scientific data
collection and analysis, social science methods can provide the tools to draw insights into local needs,
priorities, and strategies. This situated understanding enables the design of responsive policies and
programs that align with the realities faced by local communities, rather than relying solely on universal
solutions. By offering practical examples, tailored solutions, and actionable insights drawn from real-
world contexts, social science research ensures that TA is not only scientifically rigorous but also leads
to high-impact outcomes that support local energy economies.

Although situatedness—emphasizing the context in which knowledge is produced and applied—is a
fundamental attribute of the social sciences (Wittmayer et al 2018), our analysis of E2C allowed us to
not only identify key lessons learned but also develop a replicable framework. This framework,
encompassing both data collection and analysis methods, can contribute to the design and evaluation of
various TA programs.

In the following section, we outline the methods employed, explain the rationale behind our chosen
approaches, present key results of the analyses conducted, and highlight opportunities for replication.

 3.1 Social Science Methods

To investigate the lack of participation in this TA program across certain regions, we employed
qualitative methods grounded in social science. Our analytic approach was shaped by an iterative
process that recognized the interconnected relationship between community engagement and effective
program implementation. We prioritized listening to community members and stakeholders as they
identified their energy priorities, articulated barriers to implementation, and proposed potential solutions
rooted in their local context.

We understand that any attempt to develop broader relevance of TA programs should first involve
identifying the energy priorities in the gap regions. The strategies that community members and
stakeholders in those regions envision will help them overcome barriers to achieving those identified
priorities. It is also critical to identify outreach and engagement strategies and messaging that would
reach and resonate in regions that usually do not engage with federally funded TA programs.

Our approach was grounded in the premise that expanding the reach of TA programs and tailoring
solutions to the specific needs of local communities aligns with recognized best practices for community
engagement (Romero-Lankao and Nobler 2021). By incorporating insights from local stakeholders and
community-based organizations, programs can better address localized barriers and priorities, ultimately
increasing project impacts. This targeted approach reflects evidence that solutions developed with direct
input from communities are more likely to succeed and deliver long-term benefits (Walker 2012; Carley
and Konisky 2020). Prioritizing strategies that adapt to local contexts enables more effective allocation
of resources, ensuring a higher return on investment and enhancing the overall impact of federal energy
programs.

In this context, qualitative analysis plays a critical role by providing tools to center community
engagement as a core methodological principle of our study. The iterative nature of qualitative methods,
such as semi-structured interviews and listening sessions, allows researchers to adapt and respond to the
unique needs and perspectives of community members. These methods enable us to uncover not only
priorities and challenges but also the nuanced social and cultural dynamics that shape how communities

 5

engage with energy systems and TA programs. By embedding community engagement into the
qualitative research process, we ensure that the analysis reflects the lived experiences and insights of
stakeholders, creating a foundation for actionable and impactful program enhancements. This approach
aligns with the need to amplify local understanding and foster accountability, transparency, and
continuity in the design and assessment of TA programs.

 Figure 2. Iterative feedback loops

To achieve this, we employed a qualitative analytical framework centered on iterative feedback loops
and community engagement. Semi-structured interviews and listening sessions were the primary data
collection methods, each chosen for their specific strengths in capturing diverse perspectives and
fostering dialogue. Interviews and listening sessions were conducted with representatives from
municipalities and community-based organizations in the four priority regions through existing
networks, referrals, and desktop research into potential new stakeholders. In total, NREL engaged 143
individuals from 115 unique organizations (60 community-based organizations and 55 government
agencies) (Figure 2). Semi-structured interviews allowed us to explore high-level themes related to
energy priorities within specific regions, providing a detailed understanding of individual experiences,
motivations, and beliefs. Listening sessions, a method similar to focus groups, enabled us to test and
validate these themes collaboratively with community members and regional representatives, while also
uncovering actionable pathways grounded in collective input.

Our analytical approach relied on content analysis to interpret meanings associated with energy concepts
and identify community-defined priorities, challenges, and opportunities. Structured around four key
spaces—priorities, challenges, opportunities, and communication—this framework ensured that
community voices shaped both the analysis and the resulting recommendations. By grounding our
methods in qualitative research, we were able to create a robust, iterative process that provided insights
to enhance the TA program’s design and assessment and ensure the program was responsive to the
priorities of local entities across the entire United States.

3.1.1 Data Collection
From October 2023 until May 2024, we collected data through different engagement methodologies. We
initially connected with representatives from municipalities and community-based organizations in the
four gap regions through existing networks, referrals, and research into potential new stakeholders. In
total, the NREL team engaged with 143 individuals from 115 unique organizations (60 community-
based organizations and 55 government agencies). These participants were evenly distributed across the
four gap regions. All were new to E2C and 40% had never applied for a federal clean energy grant,
prize, or TA program.

The engagement methods employed for data collection draw on established social science approaches
for qualitative analysis and were adapted to identify local priorities, challenges, and opportunities related
to energy systems in these gap regions. These methods also informed enhancements to the TA program.
In the sections below, we present each method, describe its application, and articulate the rationale for
its selection.

3.1.1.1        Interviews
Interviews (structured, semi-structured, and unstructured) are a foundational qualitative method in social
science research, valued for their ability to elicit rich, detailed accounts of individual perspectives and
experiences. They provide participants with the opportunity to articulate, in their own words, how they
perceive, navigate, and interpret their social and material environments. This approach is particularly
valuable for exploring complex, context-specific phenomena, as it allows researchers to uncover
nuanced insights that might not emerge through standardized methods such as surveys (Sovacool et al
2015, Brückmann et al 2023). In the context of this study, interviews were conducted to identify high-

 7

level themes related to energy priorities within the specific region. By fostering an open and
conversational dynamic, interviews allowed us to capture the overarching concerns, values, and
expectations of regional organization representatives, offering a foundational understanding of the
regional energy landscape that informed subsequent stages of analysis.

In this study, we conducted semi-structured interviews over a period of 2 months. To provide critical
cultural context, two new team members who previously or currently lived in at least one of the regions
and had experience in the energy field led the initial outreach process. First, we identified the intended
audience and connected with entities that are eligible to participate in E2C, as well as entities in the
potential sphere of influence of eligible individuals. These entities included local government entities,
regional planning commissions, nonprofit and community-based organizations, economic development
organizations, regional federal government program representatives, and Clean Cities and Communities
coalition directors.

To reach the members of the identified audiences, interviewers used their personal networks, leveraged
current NREL collaborators, and cold-contacted organizations identified by web search, throughout
multiple phases of outreach. Phased outreach enabled the team to discuss persistent gaps—areas where
no contact had been made despite outreach attempts—and identify new strategies. For example, the team
could connect with a known contact at a Clean Cities and Communities coalition in the region who
could facilitate an introduction to another entity. In this way, we used purposive sampling by connecting
with specific types of organizations in specific geographic locations and snowball sampling to
supplement this approach when needed.

We conducted initial interviews via virtual platforms or phone with each interviewer using the same set
of guiding questions to facilitate semi-structured interviews. We took notes of the interviews that were
then cleaned and inductively coded to identify themes related to energy priorities, challenges that
prevent clean energy action, and ideas to improve access to TA programs like E2C. The results of these
interviews shaped the design of the listening sessions that followed.

3.1.1.2 Listening sessions

In the second phase, we employed “listening sessions,” a method similar to focus groups, to build on the
high-level themes identified during the interviews. Listening sessions provided a participatory platform
for engaging multiple stakeholders simultaneously, allowing for a dynamic exchange of ideas and
perspectives. Unlike interviews, which are well-suited for exploring individual viewpoints in depth,
listening sessions offered a faster and more effective means of not only identifying priorities but also
testing and validating potential approaches and pathways for addressing regional energy challenges
(Ardoin et al 2022, Sovacool et al 2015, Brückmann et al 2023). Listening sessions also generate
opportunities for collective knowledge production through discussions of divergence and consensus
among the group.

This method also played a crucial role in ensuring accountability and transparency. By co-designing the
analysis and prioritization process with stakeholders during the sessions, participants had a direct role in
shaping the outcomes, which helped build trust and legitimacy. Moreover, the interactive nature of the
sessions can expose power dynamics among participants and between researchers and the community,
creating space for more open dialogue. This approach fostered inclusivity, enabling researchers to better
understand collective priorities and concerns while informing participants about how to hold the process

 8

accountable to their needs and expectations. Ultimately, listening sessions provided an efficient,
collaborative, and transparent way to refine actionable strategies.

After completing initial interviews, we identified at least two regional partners to cohost listening
sessions in each region. Partners were selected based on location, organizational mission, regional reach,
type of organizations in their network, and capacity to assist with participant recruitment. Regional
partners could elect to receive compensation for their time spent conducting outreach and attending
planning calls, and some partners also compensated the participants who attended the listening sessions.
Not all regional partners were organizations with the capacity to accept funds for their time.

Two listening sessions with different intended participant groups were planned for each region:
participants from local governments and participants from community-based organizations. This
separation was intentional based on interview feedback. Due to strong interest, a ninth listening session
was held with local government representatives across Appalachia, who were convened by a member of
the research team with strong ties to the area.

Regional partners were tasked with finding 8 to 10 individuals interested in participating and handling
all recruitment communications. Some partners reached out to specific individuals in their network,
while others leveraged existing email lists that they use regularly to announce opportunities to their
network then whittled down a final list of participants. These divergent approaches to a hub-and-spoke
outreach model both proved effective in recruiting participants. However, some partners were less
effective at recruiting participants and were either unable to identify 8 to 10 individuals or did not send
sufficient communication prior to the session, such as a reminder email, so not all identified individuals
attended.

During the listening sessions, the research team presented information about E2C then facilitated
structured focus groups using open-ended discussion questions and participatory prioritization exercises
using Google Jamboard. We created edited verbatim transcripts, including any remarks made using the
virtual meeting chat function, and participatory prioritization data from the Jamboard activity was
transcribed verbatim.

3.1.1.3 Engagement sessions and online survey
After developing a first version of program recommendations based on the findings from the first two
phases, we conducted two public engagement sessions where we presented information about E2C,
along with preliminary findings from the listening sessions. Attendees completed a survey to identify
which clean energy topics and TA structures were most aligned with their interests and capacity.
Participants provided additional information orally or written after completing the survey, highlighting
topics and ideas that resonated with them or suggesting other ideas. The sessions were recorded and
edited transcripts were generated. Following the sessions, the survey was sent to every individual who
had participated in any phase of the Gap Region Outreach Project to gather final feedback on the
proposed topics and structures. The survey results were then collated and anonymized and the topics and
structures ranked.

The goal of this phase was to refine the program recommendations by engaging with community
stakeholders to ensure that the proposed topics and structures were aligned with their interests, needs,
and capacities. By gathering direct input through public engagement sessions and follow-up surveys, we
aimed to validate the findings from the previous phases and further tailor the program to ensure its

 9

relevance and effectiveness for the local communities. It also enhanced engagement with potential new
stakeholders and community members and provided information about how the data gathered was being
used and the potential implementation strategies that could emerge from it.

3.1.2 Data Analysis

Qualitative analysis served as the guiding methodology of this study, providing a robust framework for
uncovering nuanced insights into localized energy priorities, challenges, and opportunities. We utilized
content analysis, which offers both quantitative and qualitative approaches for examining textual and
symbolic material, enabling researchers to identify patterns, themes, and underlying understandings
within the data. For this study, we prioritized qualitative content analysis (QCA), a systematic approach
that emphasizes the categorization, and interpretation of textual data to uncover latent meanings and
patterns (Miles and Huberman 1994, Assarroudi et al 2018). QCA focuses on understanding the nuances
of communication by analyzing words, contexts, and their interrelationships to develop knowledge about
the study phenomenon. It is particularly well-suited for exploring complex and multifaceted issues, such
as community-driven energy priorities and systemic barriers, as it enables researchers to identify and
interpret both explicit and implicit themes within participant narratives. By applying QCA, we were able
to systematically analyze the textual data collected during interviews and listening sessions; identify
recurring themes, such as capacity barriers; and gain deeper insights into the social and institutional
dynamics influencing engagement with federal TA programs.

Our data collection methods included semi-structured interviews and listening sessions, both of which
were recorded, transcribed, anonymized, and analyzed using MAXQDA, a qualitative data analysis
software. These methods were chosen to maximize engagement and ensure that the analysis remained
rooted in participants’ unique perspectives. The iterative coding process began with inductive, open
coding that emerged from the interviews to identify descriptive and thematic categories from the
listening session data analysis.

In the listening sessions, participants were encouraged to discuss their energy priorities and challenges,
providing a collective space to test and validate potential strategies. We deductively coded all transcripts
based on themes, challenges, and opportunities identified in the first phase of the project (interviews).
Using overlapping codes—instances where multiple themes emerged within the same passage—we
identified critical relationships between energy priorities, challenges, and actionable opportunities.
These overlapping codes were analyzed to reveal how participants connected causal factors, impact
areas, and values, providing insights into how tailored solutions can be developed to address
community-specific needs and increase program effectiveness. This analysis highlights pathways for
enhancing program implementation by aligning strategies with local priorities, ultimately improving the
impact and efficiency of resources. We also analyzed the frequency of various energy priorities and
gathered ideas for new TA topics and structures. These ideas were then categorized into different energy
topic areas and ranked accordingly.

This analysis and ranking then informed the survey developed for the engagement sessions. The results
of the survey were used to identify specific energy topics that might be prioritized for TA under the E2C
program, and broader programmatic improvements that could enable E2C to provide more tailored
support to entities from the gap regions.

 10

This qualitative approach not only illuminated key community issues but also provided a structured
mechanism for incorporating local perspectives into program enhancement. By embedding engagement
with local stakeholders within the analytical process, we ensured that the findings were both actionable
and accountable, reflecting the priorities and realities of the gap regions. This alignment of
methodological rigor with community-driven insights underscores the value of qualitative research in
advancing program efficacy to meet the needs of TA recipients.

4 Methodological Considerations and Practitioner
Guidance: Lessons Learned for Engagement Strategies in
Technical Assistance Programs
The qualitative and iterative methods employed in this case study offer a replicable framework for
identifying barriers to engagement and refining approaches to TA program design and evaluation.
Practitioners seeking to adapt these methods can follow a structured process to effectively engage with
communities and organizations, leveraging insights to improve program outcomes.

A central challenge for TA programs is the impracticality of conducting one-on-one conversations with
every eligible entity. To address this, practitioners should prioritize working through regional networks
and established communication channels. For example, programs targeting Tribal governments in the
Southwest might collaborate with organizations like the Southwest Tribal Housing Alliance, leveraging
their existing networks to disseminate information and convene discussions.

The success of such efforts depends on selecting and collaborating with effective regional partners.
Based on our findings, practitioners should evaluate partners according to three key attributes:

 1. Capacity: Partners must have the time, staff, and resources to support outreach and engagement
 activities. Clearly communicating expectations regarding time and effort during initial outreach
 is critical for identifying potential limitations.
 2. Strong Relationships: Partners with well-established, meaningful connections within their
 networks are better equipped to recruit participants for generative discussions. Practitioners can
 assess these relationships by asking about the frequency and context of interactions or seeking
 input from network members.
 3. Project Management Experience: Partners with prior experience in convening discussions or
 managing similar projects are generally more prepared, responsive, and comfortable fulfilling
 outreach responsibilities. Asking about previous experience with similar efforts during the
 selection process can help identify partners with the necessary expertise.

A key insight from this study was the identification of six facets of capacity—time, staff, skills, funding,
mission alignment, and application readiness—as primary barriers to engaging with federal
opportunities. These barriers emerged organically during interviews and listening sessions, with capacity
challenges accounting for 45% of all barriers mentioned. The relative importance of these barriers varied
by region, emphasizing the need for tailored approaches that account for regional contexts.

To replicate this methodology, practitioners can follow these steps:

 11

 1. Listen (Initial Conversations): Conduct phone calls or initial outreach to understand general
 priorities and challenges. Utilize this information to design and inform subsequent phases, based
 on initial key themes. Social science tools like transcriptions and qualitative coding can be used
 to facilitate this process.
 2. Explore (Listening Sessions): Facilitate open conversations that are focused on topics of
 interest previously identified on the initial conversations. Utilize the listening sessions as a
 prioritization activity and as a process of iterative analysis. These sessions can be used to identify
 systemic barriers and regional nuances, but especially to co-design potential solutions. Iterative
 tools such as murals and boards can be used to facilitate data gathering and processing.
 3. Propose (Engagement Sessions): Synthesize findings from the "Listen" and "Explore" phases to
 systematize recommendations and potential solutions that emerged from all the iterations. Utilize
 qualitative coding and thematic analysis to design potential solutions based on systematized key
 findings. Share these with participants and gather their feedback about the proposed strategies.
 4. Systematize and Inform: Incorporate participants’ feedback to refine the co-designed
 recommendations or solutions, ensuring they are actionable from a technical and economical
 perspective and aligned with community needs. Inform program recipients, technical teams, and
 leadership on the findings of this research effort.
 5. Implement: Begin implementing the refined recommendations while maintaining ongoing
 engagement to collect continuous feedback and adjust as needed.

This structured approach ensures that TA programs are responsive to community priorities and systemic
barriers, like capacity constraints, while still building that capacity for sustained engagement. By
applying these methods and following the steps mentioned above and described in detail on Figure 3,
practitioners can create more accessible and effective TA programs that are distributed across different
areas of the country.

5 Conclusion
The methods employed in this study demonstrate the critical role of social science approaches in
designing, implementing, and assessing TA programs. By leveraging qualitative tools such as semi-
structured interviews and listening sessions, this approach provided a replicable framework for
identifying community priorities, challenges, and opportunities. These methods ensured that gap region
analysis was grounded in stakeholder perspectives, emphasizing local needs and priorities and fostering
trust through participatory engagement.

This case study of the E2C Gap Region Outreach Project illustrates how social science methods can
bridge gaps between technical expertise and local priorities. The use of qualitative content analysis,
including the iterative coding of data to identify recurring themes and relationships, provides a flexible
yet rigorous approach to analyzing stakeholder input. This approach is not only adaptable to various
contexts but also scalable to programs seeking to enhance their reach and impact in different geographic
areas or with different types of entities.

Replicating these methods in other TA programs can yield significant benefits. First, qualitative methods
enable the development of contextually relevant knowledge, which can guide the tailoring of program
strategies to meet unique regional and local needs. Second, the participatory nature of these methods
fosters accountability and transparency, as stakeholders are actively involved in co-designing solutions.

 12

Finally, these approaches allow for the identification and understanding of localized barriers and
opportunities that might be overlooked in purely quantitative analyses, thus ensuring a more
comprehensive understanding of the social dimensions of energy transitions.

This study highlights the transformative potential of social science methods when applied to TA
programs. By centering stakeholder voices and integrating their insights into program design and
evaluation, these methods can drive more tailored programs with greater reach and impact. The E2C
Gap Region Outreach Project serves as an example of how these approaches can be implemented and
offers a blueprint for their application in other contexts, ultimately ensuring more effective use of federal
investments in TA and stronger local energy economies.
