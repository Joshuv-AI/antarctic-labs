The Integrated Human System Framework: A Conceptual Model of Skin as a Biological Interface Across Health and Well-Being
Authors: Miranda A Farage, Jack D Sobel, Gabe Tzeghai, Howard I Maibach
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman health and well-being are frequently examined through isolated biological or environmental determinants, an approach that constrains understanding of how internal physiology and external influences interact dynamically across the lifespan. This conceptual review introduces the Integrated Human System Framework, a biologically grounded, systems-based model that positions skin as a dynamic interface participating in and reflecting bidirectional interactions between internal systemic regulation and external environmental, behavioral, and social influences. The framework organizes intrinsic biological systems (endocrine, immune, metabolic, and neural), the skin interface, and extrinsic environmental and behavioral modulators within a vertically structured architecture, situated across lifespan-related dimensions including developmental stage, biological sex and gender, and social context. It builds on prior work critiquing organ-system fragmentation in medicine and describing mind-body-environment integration in dermatological health, extending these contributions by proposing skin specifically as a measurable, non-invasively accessible interface for systems-level investigation. Rather than replacing existing integrative approaches, including systems medicine, the biopsychosocial model, exposome science, and network physiology, the framework is positioned as complementary to them, anchoring their shared constructs in a single, biologically accessible organ system. As a conceptual framework, it provides a foundation for future empirical evaluation through interdisciplinary and longitudinal investigation using methods appropriate to the research question. Its intended contribution is to support hypothesis generation, biomarker development, integrative study design, and translational application. Potential applications include population-specific and lifespan-oriented initiatives, with the broader aim of advancing more integrated approaches to health research and clinical care.

Introduction

Scientific inquiry into human well-being has traditionally advanced through focused examination of isolated biological pathways and environmental influences. This siloed approach has constrained understanding of how intrinsic physiology and extrinsic exposures or behaviors dynamically interact across systems and across the human lifespan, limiting the field’s capacity to achieve a more holistic view of well-being.

Several existing frameworks have sought to address this fragmentation, and each has made a substantive contribution. Systems medicine and systems biology apply computational and multi-omics approaches to model disease as an emergent property of interacting molecular networks, offering a multilevel, multidisciplinary methodology for data acquisition and analysis;1 these approaches are, however, typically organized around disease states or molecular datasets rather than around a defined, non-invasively accessible anatomical site for longitudinal measurement. The biopsychosocial model, introduced by G. L. Engel2 in 1977, established that biological, psychological, and social factors jointly shape health outcomes and remains influential across medicine and psychiatry. It has, however, been widely critiqued for lacking a coherent theoretical foundation and for providing limited tools for operationalizing its constructs in research or clinical practice, a limitation recent theoretical work has sought to address.3 Exposome science offers a rigorous framework for the cumulative measurement of environmental exposure and associated biological response across the lifespan, but its methods are centered on exposure characterization, typically through systemic (eg, blood, urine) or external monitoring, rather than on a specific biological interface at which internal and external factors converge4 Network physiology has demonstrated, through quantitative analysis of organ-system coupling, that health and disease states emerge from dynamic interactions among physiological systems, but its methods have concentrated on internally measured signals, such as cardiac, respiratory, and neural activity, rather than on skin as a peripheral, externally observable interface.5 Psychoneuroimmunology has established mechanistic pathways linking psychological states to immune function but has not been organized around a single, biologically accessible site for translational application.

The Integrated Human System Framework does not seek to replace these established approaches. Instead, it proposes that skin, as the organ functioning at the interface of internal regulation and external exposure, offers a biologically grounded, non-invasively accessible, and longitudinally measurable site at which constructs from these related frameworks can be operationalized and studied together (see Table 1). Relationship of the Integrated Human System Framework (IHSF) to Established Integrative Models

Although skin has previously been characterized as a barrier, a neuroimmunoendocrine organ, an exposome target, and a component of several organ-system axes, these perspectives have generally developed within separate disciplinary or disease-specific domains. The Integrated Human System Framework advances the field by organizing these perspectives within a unified, skin-centered architecture that connects internal regulation, external modulation, lifespan-related context, and measurable translational pathways.

The initial Integrated Human System framework is structured as a vertically organized interface model that situates skin between internal systemic regulation and external environmental and behavioral modulation (see Figure 1). Intrinsic biological systems, such as endocrine, immune, metabolic, and neural processes, provide the regulatory architecture that shapes skin function. Skin operates as a dynamic biological interface, mediating bidirectional interactions through barrier integrity, immune surveillance, neurocutaneous signaling, and microbiome ecology. Extrinsic environmental and behavioral influences, including but not limited to physical exposures, lifestyle patterns, and social and cultural context, interact directly with this interface while also influencing internal regulatory pathways. These interactions occur differently across lifespan-related dimensions, including developmental stage, sex-specific biology, psychological experience, and social, cultural, and structural context, which are treated as critical shaping factors across populations rather than as secondary considerations. This manuscript aims to introduce and describe the Integrated Human System Framework as a conceptual model, to differentiate it from related integrative approaches, and to outline a structured foundation for interdisciplinary research and translational application. It builds upon prior work that has progressively documented the limitations of organ-system fragmentation in medicine and health6 and described the clinical relevance of mind-body-external factor integration to dermatological well-being across the lifespan and in susceptible sub-populations.7,8 The intent of this manuscript is not to provide an exhaustive review of the literature, but to propose this organizing architecture as a conceptual starting point, differentiated from existing models, for future empirical work.

The Integrated Human System Framework

Rationale and the Basis of the Concept

The limitations of reductionist models in the life sciences are well documented. While highly productive in isolating mechanistic pathways, single-domain approaches inadequately capture the emergent complexity arising from the interaction of biological systems with environmental and other exposures.9,10 Bhattacharya et al11 describe how systems biology has increasingly demonstrated that understanding organ function, disease susceptibility, and health outcomes requires examination of dynamic, multilevel interactions rather than discrete causal chains. In the domain of well-being science specifically, integrative thinking of this kind has been comparatively slower to develop into an operational architecture.

The frameworks discussed in Introduction, systems medicine, the biopsychosocial model, exposome science, and network physiology, have each substantially advanced integrative thinking in health science. This Integrated Human System Framework does not claim to supersede their contributions. Rather, each has a specific, well-recognized limitation that the present framework is designed to address: systems medicine and multi-omics approaches typically require invasive or resource-intensive sampling (eg blood, tissue, genomic sequencing) that constrains longitudinal, population-level application. The biopsychosocial model has been criticized as theoretically underspecified and difficult to operationalize in research.3 Exposome science, though methodologically rigorous, is not organized around a single accessible biological site at which cumulative exposure is registered, and network physiology has concentrated on internally instrumented physiological signals rather than an externally observable interface. The Integrated Human System Framework responds to this collective gap by organizing known intrinsic and extrinsic factors, biological, environmental, and contextual, into a coherent systems architecture anchored at a single, non-invasively accessible site. The model does not supersede these existing disciplines but provides a structured, complementary means of aligning them within a unified analytic perspective. By positioning skin as the functional interface within this model, the framework identifies a biologically meaningful site of integration that is both empirically tractable and conceptually broad in scope.

In their review, Farage et al6 critiqued the historical “silo” model of Western medicine, tracing its origins to the anatomical dissection tradition of the sixteenth century and documenting how organ-system fragmentation has impeded recognition of the interconnected physiological processes that govern health. That review drew on evidence from placebo and nocebo research, dissociative identity disorder, Pavlovian immune conditioning, and the physiological effects of psychosocial stress to argue for a model that views the body as a series of interconnected, interacting systems. In 2017, Farage and colleagues7 applied this integrated perspective specifically to aging skin, positioning mind-body-extrinsic interactions as an integral dimension of dermatological well-being across the life course. The present framework builds upon and extends these prior contributions by situating skin as the functional interface within a structured, systems-based architecture for understanding human well-being.

Farage and Robinson12 demonstrated that sensitive skin is a dermatological condition shaped by both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, offering a useful illustration of the framework’s logic. Intrinsic contributors include sex (women report higher prevalence, driven partly by estrogen-related hormonal fluctuations), ethnicity (structural differences in the stratum corneum alter irritant susceptibility across populations), age (self-reported sensitivity increases with age despite a decrease in objective irritant reactivity), anatomical site (facial and genital skin are more vulnerable due to a thinner stratum corneum and higher nerve fiber density), and coexisting conditions/comorbidities such as atopic dermatitis, contact allergies, and rosacea. Extrinsic contributors include environmental triggers such as weather extremes and wind, personal care and household products, rough fabrics, and cultural influences such as advertising, which the authors note has measurably inflated self-reported prevalence across populations.

The Vertical Interface Model

The proposed Integrated Human System Framework is organized along three structured tiers: (1) internal biological systems, (2) the skin interface, and (3) external environmental, behavioral, and other influences (see Figure 1). Each tier interacts with the others through bidirectional, and at times multi-directional, regulatory and modulatory pathways, and all tiers are situated within overarching lifespan-related dimensions.

This vertical architecture reflects the physiological reality of skin’s position as both a target and a mediator of systemic biological signals and extrinsic environmental and other stimuli. Internal regulatory processes shape skin function, while skin actively modulates internal signaling in response to external conditions. This bidirectionality differentiates the integrated model from linear exposure-outcome paradigms and positions it within contemporary systems biology approaches that treat physiological regulation as dynamic and interdependent rather than as a fixed sequence of causes and effects.

The skin was selected as the principal interface for this framework, rather than other environmentally exposed barrier organs such as the gut, lungs, or mucosal surfaces, for several practical and biological reasons. Skin is directly accessible for repeated, non-invasive assessment through measures such as transepidermal water loss, sebum composition, surface pH, and microbiome sampling, without the procedural burden associated with assessment of the gut or lungs. Its stratified structure and resident antigen-presenting network make it a site of both passive barrier function and active immune surveillance, and it uniquely integrates endocrine, immune, neural, and microbial signaling within a single, externally observable tissue.13–15 The gut and lung interfaces are unquestionably important, complementary sites of internal-external convergence, and this framework does not exclude them. Skin is instead proposed as a tractable entry point, with the expectation that the same vertical architecture could, in principle, extend to other barrier interfaces in future work. Within this manuscript, well-being is defined as a dynamic state of biological functioning, functional capacity, psychological experience, and social participation rather than the absence of disease alone. The Integrated Human System Framework does not claim that skin is the only interface between the organism and the environment.

Skin as a Dynamic Biological Interface

Skin is the body’s largest organ and its primary physical interface with the external environment. Its functions extend well beyond structural protection; skin participates in immune regulation, neuroendocrine signaling, metabolic activity, and microbiome ecology.13–15 These functions position skin not as a passive barrier but as an active mediator of systemic regulatory processes. Within the framework, skin may function differently across specific pathways: as an active mediator, a target of systemic and environmental signals, a source of biological signaling, and an accessible indicator of broader physiological states.

Cutaneous immune cells, including dendritic cells, mast cells, and resident T lymphocytes, maintain continuous surveillance of environmental exposures and communicate with systemic immune architecture.16 The skin’s neuroendocrine capacity, including its expression of cortisol-related enzymes and neuropeptide receptors, links it directly to hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis regulation.17 Gallo and Nakatsuji18 demonstrated that epidermal microbiome composition modulates both local and systemic immune responses, with disruption associated with systemic inflammatory dysregulation. These properties establish skin as an empirically tractable site for integrated systems investigation.

Intrinsic Biological Systems

Endocrine Regulation

The endocrine system provides a primary regulatory architecture through which internal biological signals shape skin biology and, by extension, systemic well-being. Hormonal axes, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal, hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal, and hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid systems, exert direct effects on cutaneous cell proliferation, differentiation, barrier function, and inflammatory response.15,19 Sex steroids, glucocorticoids, and thyroid hormones each modulate skin physiology in ways that reflect and reinforce systemic regulatory states.

The skin’s capacity for local hormone synthesis represents a critical bidirectional pathway. Slominski and colleagues17 showed that keratinocytes and sebocytes express steroidogenic enzymes enabling local production of androgens, estrogens, and glucocorticoids, creating peripheral endocrine feedback loops that complement central hormonal regulation. These pathways are particularly relevant during developmental transitions such as puberty, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause, during which cutaneous endocrine activity intersects with systemic hormonal shifts.

Immune Signaling

Skin functions as a primary site of immune surveillance and as a bidirectional communicator with systemic immunity. Pasparakis and colleagues14 demonstrated that local inflammatory responses initiated at the cutaneous interface, involving cytokine and chemokine cascades, influence systemic immune tone, and that systemic inflammatory states are similarly reflected in and mediated through cutaneous immune activity. The gut-skin axis, mediated in part through shared immune signaling pathways and microbiome interactions, represents a well-characterized dimension of this integration.20 Extraintestinal manifestations of inflammatory bowel disease, including desquamative inflammatory vaginitis documented as a sequela of Crohn’s disease, further illustrate the clinical reach of gut immune dysregulation to distant mucosal and cutaneous tissue.21

Dysregulation of skin immune function is associated with systemic inflammatory conditions including metabolic syndrome, autoimmune disorders, and psychological stress responses,22 associations that underscore the relevance of skin-based immune dynamics to broader models of health and well-being.

Evidence for the conditioned modulation of immune function further supports the case for integrated systems modeling. Ader and Cohen23 demonstrated that immunosuppression could be elicited in lupus-prone mice through behavioral conditioning alone, an effect later replicated in human contexts by Vits and colleagues.24 Conditioned immune responses have also been observed in allergy and chemotherapy settings, with subjects exhibiting measurable immunological changes in response to environmental signals previously paired with pharmacological stimuli. In clinical trials of corticosteroid treatment for psoriasis, groups receiving intermittent placebo substitutions showed outcomes statistically comparable to those receiving full active treatment, illustrating the clinical magnitude of conditioned immune response. These findings indicate that cutaneous immune pathways are shaped not only by molecular substrates but also by learned psychological associations and expectation states.

Psychosocial stressors have been shown to exert clinically consequential effects on immune parameters, including cytokine production, cortisol levels, lymphocyte populations, natural killer cell activity, and susceptibility to infectious disease.25

Metabolic and Neural Integration

Metabolic processes intersect with skin biology through lipid metabolism, glucose regulation, and inflammatory signaling, influencing cutaneous barrier integrity, wound healing, and inflammatory potential. Skin homeostasis reflects the internal metabolic state of the organism, functioning as a biological mirror of systemic dysregulation.26,27 Obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia produce measurable and visible cutaneous correlates, including impaired barrier function, compromised wound healing, and conditions such as acanthosis nigricans. Conversely, these visible dermatoses serve as clinically accessible markers of underlying metabolic disruption, consistent with a bidirectional metabolic-cutaneous relationship.26,27

Neural pathways connect skin to the central and peripheral nervous systems through nociceptive, tactile, and itch networks, as well as neuropeptide signaling via substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and nerve growth factor.28 The skin’s peripheral stress response system, encompassing local hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity and neuropeptide secretion by keratinocytes, mast cells, and sensory nerve endings, operates in parallel with central neuroendocrine signaling to translate psychological states into cutaneous biological change.28 Physiological differences observed across identity states in dissociative identity disorder, including measurably distinct immune responses, pain sensitivity, and skin conductance profiles, further support the depth of neural-somatic integration,29 indicating that neural regulation of skin extends beyond peripheral innervation into central and psychosocial domains.

Extrinsic Modulation: Environmental, Behavioral, and Contextual Influences

Physical and Chemical Exposures

Environmental exposures constitute one of the external tiers of the integrated framework, including ultraviolet radiation, atmospheric pollutants, temperature variation, humidity, and chemical exposures such as cosmetics, occupational agents, and pharmaceutical compounds. Each of these exposure categories interacts directly with the skin interface and, through it, with internal regulatory systems.30

These cumulative and interacting exposures are conceptually consistent with the exposome framework, which characterizes the totality of environmental influences experienced across the lifespan and their associated biological responses.4 Within the Integrated Human System Framework, the skin interface offers a tractable, non-invasive site at which cumulative lifetime exposure, as conceptualized by exposome science, is biologically registered, complementing exposome approaches that rely primarily on external exposure characterization or systemic biomarkers.

Vitamin D synthesis illustrates this dynamic: ultraviolet (UV) radiation initiates cascades affecting vitamin D synthesis while also influencing DNA repair mechanisms, photoaging pathways, and immunomodulation, effects that extend beyond the epidermis to influence endocrine and immune regulation.31,32 Intrinsic aging and extrinsic photoaging differ substantially in their molecular signatures: UV-driven processes produce collagen degradation, elastic fiber disorganization, and immunosuppression at rates substantially exceeding chronological aging alone, with cumulative solar exposure estimated to account for up to 90% of visible skin aging in fair-skinned populations.32 Kim and colleagues33 showed that airborne particulate matter is associated with cutaneous barrier disruption and systemic oxidative stress, linking ambient air quality directly to dermal and systemic inflammatory outcomes.

Lifestyle Patterns and Behavioral Factors

Behavioral factors include nutrition, physical activity, sleep, substance use, and medication use, among others, each of which can interact with the skin interface both directly and through modulation of internal regulatory systems. Several researchers have demonstrated how dietary patterns influence skin barrier composition through fatty acid availability, antioxidant supply, and micronutrient status, while also shaping systemic metabolic and inflammatory tone. Corsetti and colleagues34 outlined both the “out-in” and “in-out” nourishing impacts on aging skin. Their review found that protein availability and the ratio of essential to non-essential amino acids are particularly consequential to skin structural integrity: deficiencies in essential amino acid supply impair collagen synthesis, reduce dermal thickness, and delay wound healing, with effects demonstrable at the histological level within weeks of nutritional change. Topical amino acid formulations matched to the stoichiometric composition of collagen were shown to accelerate wound closure and modulate key repair mediators including TGF-β and VEGF in aged tissue, identifying nutritional delivery as a translatable intervention pathway.35 Physical activity modulates inflammatory cytokine profiles, mitochondrial function, and sebaceous activity through exercise-related hormonal responses.

Both sleep quality and duration influence cutaneous regenerative processes, barrier repair, and neuroendocrine regulation, with sleep deprivation associated with measurable alterations in skin hydration, transepidermal water loss, and perceived skin appearance.36 These behavioral-cutaneous relationships illustrate the Integrated Human System Framework’s integration of lifestyle and cultural factors within a systems model of well-being.

Adaptive Responses and System Interactions

Environmental and behavioral exposures do not act on skin in isolation from internal systems. Rather, they activate adaptive regulatory responses that reconfigure internal biological states. Allostatic load, the cumulative physiological cost of adapting to environmental and behavioral demands, is reflected in skin biology and in broader systemic biomarkers.37 These adaptive dynamic positions the skin interface as a site where environmental experience is biologically registered and translated into systemic regulatory change.

Lifespan and Cross-Cutting Dimensions

Developmental and Aging Biology

Lifespan development shapes the physical, cognitive, and psychosocial changes that occur across a person’s life and contributes to individual biological variability. The Integrated Human System Framework incorporates a lifespan perspective that recognizes developmental stage as a primary shaping context for both internal regulatory systems and skin interface function. Neonatal skin biology differs substantially from pediatric, adolescent, adult, and older-adult skin in barrier maturity, immune responsiveness, microbiome composition, and hormonal milieu.38 These developmental differences modulate how internal systems and external exposures interact at the cutaneous interface across the life course.

Developmental transitions, including puberty, reproductive years, perimenopause, and post-reproductive aging, represent periods of heightened regulatory reorganization in which the skin interface reflects and mediates shifting endocrine, immune, and metabolic states. Longitudinal investigation of these transitions offers a productive application domain for the integrated framework.19

In older-adult populations, skin is among the most clinically and psychosocially consequential organ systems, with the majority of elderly individuals presenting at least one dermatological condition.7 Mind-body interactions are particularly evident in this context: skin ageing is shaped not only by genetic background, environmental insult, and disease history, but also by cumulative psychosocial stressors that modulate immune, endocrine, and epigenetic processes across the lifespan. Farage and colleagues7 emphasize that recognizing psychosocial stress as a driver of skin complaints in older adults provides a necessary foundation for more effective treatment approaches, a perspective consistent with the present framework’s emphasis on integrating related factors and biological dimensions.

Biological Sex and Gender

Sex-specific biological differences in skin structure, hormonal regulation, immune function, and environmental sensitivity are well documented and represent substantive cross-cutting dimensions within the framework.19,39 Differences in androgen and estrogen activity, sebaceous gland function, dermal collagen density, and inflammatory sensitivity create sex-differentiated skin biology that intersects with both internal regulatory systems and external exposure patterns. Age-related hormonal trajectories diverge between the sexes: men experience a gradual decline in androgens with relatively stable estradiol levels, while women undergo a more rapid postmenopausal estrogen decline associated with skin thinning, reduced collagen content, impaired barrier recovery, and compromised wound healing, at rates exceeding those observed in age-matched men.19

It is important to distinguish biological sex, the chromosomal, gonadal, and hormonal determinants of sex-differentiated skin physiology described above, from gender, the social and behavioral roles, identities, and practices that separately shape environmental exposure, healthcare-seeking behavior, and cosmetic practice. Both are represented as cross-cutting dimensions within the framework but operate through distinct pathways: biological sex acts substantially through the intrinsic hormonal and structural pathways described in Intrinsic Biological Systems, whereas gender acts predominantly through the extrinsic behavioral and social pathways described in Extrinsic Modulation: Environmental, Behavioral, and Contextual Influences, including differential occupational exposures, cosmetic practices, and healthcare engagement.

Psychological, Social, and Structural Influences

Psychological states, such as chronic stress, anxiety, depression, and trauma, exert well-characterized effects on skin biology through neuroendocrine and neuroimmune pathways, and cutaneous conditions frequently contribute to psychological distress through appearance-related, functional, and quality-of-life mechanisms.40 This bidirectional psychocutaneous relationship is represented within the Integrated Human System Framework’s psychological and contextual dimensions.

An extensive body of evidence describes the physiological mechanisms through which psychosocial context modulates skin and systemic health. Psychosocial stress has been shown to alter cytokine production, natural killer cell activity, vaccine responsiveness, and wound healing rates, with effects demonstrated across stressor types including academic examinations, grief, caregiving burden, and social isolation.6 Stress-associated immune dysregulation has been implicated in the exacerbation of inflammatory skin conditions including psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and acne, as well as in conditions with overlapping psychosocial and immunological contributions such as vulvodynia. Psychosocial stress can also act at the genomic level: telomere shortening associated with early-life difficulty and chronic stress accelerates cellular senescence across organ systems, including skin, providing a mechanistic link between psychological experience and measurable biological aging.41

The mind-body connection, once considered peripheral to biomedical science, is now understood as a network of neurological, immunological, and endocrine interactions that communicate among body systems.42 This characterization is foundational to the present Integrated Human System Framework’s cross-cutting dimensions and supports the view that treatment of skin conditions, particularly in populations with chronic or functionally unexplained symptoms, benefits from explicit engagement with psychological, environmental, behavioral, and social context rather than organ-specific intervention alone.

Several social and structural influences, including socioeconomic position, access to or barriers to healthcare, occupational environment, nutrition, comorbidities, medication use or polypharmacy, and cultural practices related to skin care and appearance, shape both environmental exposures and health-seeking behaviors in ways that influence biological outcomes. These elements are integral to the framework’s cross-cutting dimensions and its broader model of well-being.

Research Implications

The Integrated Human System Framework provides a structured platform for interdisciplinary research designs that cross traditional disciplinary boundaries. By establishing skin as a measurable interface connecting internal and external systems, the framework supports the development of biomarker panels, longitudinal cohort designs, and integrative data analysis approaches that capture multi-system dynamics rather than single-pathway outcomes.

Potential research and translational approaches consistent with this architecture may include skin-based or systemic biomarkers, longitudinal cohort studies, patient-reported and clinical outcomes, environmental and behavioral assessments, wearable sensor data, and integrative analytical methods. The selection of measures and methodologies should be guided by the specific research question, population, and intended application. Where appropriate, more advanced computational or multi-level biological approaches may provide additional tools for examining complex relationships, but they are not required for application of the framework.

The framework is intended to guide the selection and integration of relevant evidence rather than prescribe a specific set of technologies or analytical methods.

Educational and Institutional Implications

The Integrated Human System Framework provides a coherent conceptual structure for educational initiatives spanning dermatology, endocrinology, immunology, environmental health, psychology, and public health. By providing a shared systems language across disciplines, it supports curriculum development, faculty collaboration, and the training of investigators and practitioners capable of operating across multidisciplinary interfaces.

Institutional collaborations may include the development of integrated research centers, cross-disciplinary grant initiatives, and clinical programs that address skin-related factors within a whole-person, lifespan context rather than within single-specialty structures.

Population-Focused Initiatives

The framework is intended to be scalable, designed to support population-specific applications within defined demographic, cultural, developmental, or clinical contexts. As one example, an ongoing initiative applies the Integrated Human System Framework to the study of girls’ and women’s well-being across life stages, illustrating the framework’s capacity to maintain systems rigor while addressing population-specific biological and contextual complexity.

The framework is also intended to extend to other population-focused contexts, including pediatric, older-adult, occupationally exposed, and clinically defined populations, each grounded in the same underlying systems architecture and adapted to population-specific biological and contextual parameters.

Priorities for Interdisciplinary Research

Systems Modeling and Methodological Approaches

The Integrated Human System Framework can accommodate a range of methodological approaches for examining relationships among biological systems, skin, environmental exposures, behavior, and social context. Depending on the research question, these may include conventional statistical methods, longitudinal and epidemiological designs, mixed-methods research, systems modeling, network approaches, causal inference, or selected machine-learning applications. No single analytical method is intrinsic to the framework; rather, the framework provides an organizing structure within which appropriate methods can be selected and interpreted.

Longitudinal study designs and real-world data are particularly well suited to the framework’s lifespan orientation, enabling investigation of how intrinsic systemic biological states and extrinsic exposures co-evolve across development and how skin biology reflects and mediates these changes over time under different behavioral, social, cultural, and environmental conditions.

Integrative Evidence and Data Approaches

Empirical evaluation of the framework will require the integration of evidence across relevant biological, clinical, environmental, behavioral, psychological, and social domains. The nature and depth of this integration may vary according to the research question. In some studies, established clinical measures, skin assessments, exposure histories, patient-reported outcomes, and conventional biomarkers may provide the most appropriate evidence. In others, molecular profiling, wearable technologies, or computational methods may offer complementary information.

The framework does not depend on comprehensive measurement of every domain or on the use of any particular technology. Rather, it encourages deliberate selection of the variables and methods most relevant to the proposed pathway, population, and outcome. Transparent operational definitions, appropriate study design, and interdisciplinary interpretation will be essential to evaluating the framework’s utility.

Current Scope and Path Toward Validation

At this stage, the Integrated Human System Framework is presented as a conceptual proposal that establishes a structured foundation for empirical investigation. Its next stage of development will involve testing its central propositions, including the bidirectional skin-systemic relationships described in The Integrated Human System Framework and Intrinsic Biological Systems, using data collected specifically for this purpose. Future work should establish standardized and validated approaches for operationalizing skin as a measurement interface using clinical, biological, behavioral, environmental, and analytical methods appropriate to the questions being investigated. Validation need not require simultaneous measurement of all framework domains, but should test clearly defined relationships within the broader architecture.

The comparative positioning outlined in Introduction and Rationale and the Basis of the Concept also provides a set of testable propositions. Subsequent studies should evaluate whether the framework offers measurable explanatory, predictive, or translational value beyond that provided by systems medicine, the biopsychosocial model, exposome science, and network physiology within defined applications.

The vertical interface model presented in The Vertical Interface Model represents an initial organizing architecture intended to support this empirical work. As evidence accumulates, future iterations can incorporate the nonlinear, context-dependent, and multidirectional interactions that characterize complex biological systems. Early applications may be particularly informative in conditions in which cutaneous biology is closely coupled with underlying pathophysiology, including inflammatory dermatologic and stress-responsive disorders. The framework is methodologically inclusive rather than methodologically prescriptive. Validation across broader conditions, populations, life stages, and environmental contexts will subsequently help define the framework’s scope, applicability, and translational value. Progress toward this goal will benefit from collaboration among dermatology, systems biology, environmental health, behavioral science, data science, and related disciplines.

Conclusion

The Integrated Human System Framework offers a biologically grounded conceptual architecture for examining human well-being as an interconnected system rather than a collection of isolated elements. By positioning skin as a dynamic interface mediating bidirectional interactions between internal regulation and extrinsic environmental, behavioral, and social influences, the framework proposes a coherent structure for integrating molecular, systemic, behavioral, and lifespan dimensions of health.

This systems-based approach is not intended to replace existing scientific disciplines or integrative frameworks such as systems medicine, the biopsychosocial model, exposome science, or network physiology; rather, it offers a complementary, biologically anchored means of aligning their constructs around a single, measurable interface. In doing so, it provides a structured starting point for interdisciplinary research, translational application, and population-focused initiatives.

The research agenda outlined in Translational Applications and Priorities for Interdisciplinary Research provides a pathway for testing the framework’s central propositions, refining its operational definitions, and evaluating its explanatory, predictive, and translational value across defined conditions and populations. Through appropriately designed empirical studies across defined conditions and populations, future work can determine where the framework offers the greatest scientific and clinical utility.

Human well-being emerges from the continuous interaction of intrinsic biological regulation with environmental, behavioral, and social influences. By positioning skin as an accessible and biologically active interface through which these interactions can be investigated, the Integrated Human System Framework provides a structured foundation for interdisciplinary research and, ultimately, for more integrated approaches to human health and well-being across the lifespan.
