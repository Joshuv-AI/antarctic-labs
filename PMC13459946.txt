Extent of Acute Type A Aortic Dissection in Patients With Organ Cysts
Authors: Essi Pirskanen, Ivana Kholova, Otso Arponen, Petteri Kauhanen, Timo Paavonen, Ari Mennander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe investigated whether the extent of degeneration of the ascending aortic wall found in patients with acute type A aortic dissection is associated with the presence of organ cysts.MethodsIn total, 120 consecutive patients diagnosed for acute type A aortic dissection using computer tomography at Tampere University Hospital were evaluated, screened for organ cysts, and followed for a mean 6.7-years (SDs 4.6) after surgery. The ascending aortic wall resected in surgery in patients with and without cysts was analysed for 11 different histopathological variables expressed as point score units (PSU). Multivariable binary logistic regression analysis adjusted for age and sex was performed to investigate the association of the presence of elastic fibre thinning with the presence of cysts.ResultsOut of 120 patients, there were 45 patients with at least 1 cyst including ovarian, renal, liver or epididymis cysts, and 75 with none. The mean age was 65.6 years (SD 11.8). Increased extent of elastic fibre thinning and the presence of thickening of the medial layer of the vasa vasorum were often present in patients with cysts as compared with those without (2.0 [1.0-2.0] vs 1.0 [0-2.0], PSU, P = .020 and 26.7% vs 12.0%, P = .049, respectively). According to the adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, the presence of elastic fibre thinning was associated with cysts (adjusted odds ratio: 2.636, 95% CI, 1.143-9.095, P = .023).ConclusionsThe presence of cysts in patients requiring surgery for acute type A aortic dissection may reflect the extent of aortic wall tissue degeneration.Keywords: elastic fibre thinning, acute type A aortic dissection, cyst

INTRODUCTION

Acute type A aortic dissection (ATAAD) is a major emergency often necessitating prompt surgery. Well-known risk factors for ATAAD include presence of hypertension, aortic dilatation, connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome, and other hereditary factors.1 Factors associated with aortic wall degeneration, such as mucoid extracellular matrix accumulation, elastic fibre degeneration including fragmentation, thinning and disorganization, are associated with connective tissue disorders.2 Matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) polymorphisms may interact with elastin and collagen metabolism, leading to the process of both aortic wall degeneration and cyst formation.3–5

Aortic dilatation has been associated with a higher prevalence of organ cysts compared with the general population.6–8 The presence of cysts suggests connective tissue degeneration including the aortic tissue, with increased risk of acute aortic events such as ATAAD.8–10 We hypothesized that patients with cysts have distinctive characteristics of aortic wall degeneration during ATAAD. We investigated whether aortic wall histology differs in patients with vs without cysts in patients with ATAAD. Secondary exploratory end-points included need of reoperations and mortality after surgery for ATAAD. We speculate that identifying the presence of organ cysts may add to understanding distinctive aortic wall degenerative changes pertinent to ATAAD.

METHODS

Ethical statement and study design

The institutional review board (Ethical Committee of the Tampere University Hospital, Tampere, Finland, R23028) approved this study, and the need for informed consent was waived as the study conforms to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). All consecutive patients undergoing surgery for ATAAD between April 2007 and July 2020 were included. The collection and storage of data and biological material from research participants for multiple and indefinite use are consistent with requirements outlined in the World Medical Association Declaration of Taipei. The research ethics committee approves the establishment and monitors ongoing use of the database and biobank. ATAAD was diagnosed using computer tomography (CT) and echocardiography. The largest diameters of the thoracic aorta from 6 different specific parts (the aortic annulus, aortic root, sinotubular junction [STJ], mid-ascending aorta, proximal part of the onset of the truncus arteriosus, and mid-part of the descending thoracic aorta) were retrospectively identified from CT images. The outer-to-outer vascular wall, vertical to the centreline of the vessel and including both the true and false lumen, was measured as previously reported.11 The cysts were retrospectively identified by experienced radiologists from the CT angiography images. The presence and distribution but not the sizes of ovarian, renal, liver or epididymal cysts were inspected and reported on the basis of CT images. Measuring aortic regurgitation was performed using standard echocardiography criteria, and semi-quantified using grades from 0 to 3 when 0 meant no regurgitation and 3 indicated severe aortic regurgitation. Echocardiographic findings were included in the decision process to replace the aortic valve and aortic root.

Patient grouping

To compare clinical relevance associated with morphology in ATAAD surgery, patients were divided into 2 groups according to the presence of either having or not having ovarian, renal, liver or epididymis cysts.

Surgery

In compliance with our surgical principle, ATAAD is operated using the minimum surgical resection and replacement needed during the acute phase of the disease in both stable and unstable cases. Surgical decision-making is mainly determined based on CT angiography, including the presence and location of the primary entry tear and assessment of the extent of dissection. A supracoronary ascending aorta replacement using a suitable graft with or without aortic valve surgery (aortic prosthesis). Whenever ATAAD and the entry tear are included in the root of the aorta, a radical resection of the dissecting and dilated ascending aorta was performed (conduit). The aortic arch was resected completely or partially depending on the aortic wall disease extension. We consider hemiarch replacement as surgery under hypothermic circulatory arrest with an open distal anastomosis encompassing the whole of the lesser curvature of the aortic arch.12 The intimal tears were resected whenever possible.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

The aortic sample for histopathology and immunohistochemistry was procured from the middle of the resected area of the ascending aorta in the vicinity of STJ.

Quantification of aortic wall histopathology

All aortic samples were assessed by 2 experienced cardiovascular pathologists (I.K., T.P.), in accordance with the routine surgical pathology protocol based on the guidelines of The Society for Cardiovascular Pathology and The Association for European Cardiovascular Pathology.13 The worst present grades and distributions were reported and semi-quantified on a scale of 0-3, and expressed as point score units (PSU).14

Follow-up protocol

All patients were documented for morbidity, reoperations and mortality, and follow-up included physical examination, CT, and echocardiography 3 months after surgery, and yearly CT thereafter. Mortality was reported to the hospital database based on the individual social security number. The mean follow-up for the patients was 6.7 years SD 4.6. Aortic reoperations included the need for proximal or distal aortic reoperations, due to ongoing aortic dilatation confirmed by CT. One patient reoperated for endocarditis and another for sutureline failure were not considered reoperations, due to aortic wall degeneration and associated presence of organ cysts, and were excluded from the analysis. In compliance with our institutional policy, the aortic dilatation included an aortic diameter greater than 5.0-5.5 cm or aortic growth greater than 1.0 cm per year. This definition was adjusted to the presence of Marfan syndrome, sex, patient size, and symptoms, in accordance with The Yale Center criteria.15

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were tested for normal distribution according to Kolmogorov–Smirnov test of normality. Age and aortic diameters were expressed as means and SDs. Histological variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges. The continuous values were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-parametric and the Student- t-test for parametric variables. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and were compared using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests. An age- and sex- adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify whether the semi-quantitative elastic fibre thinning score- considered as a categorical variable including PSU = 1-3 and compared with PSU = 0—was associated with the presence of cysts. We adjusted only for age and sex due to the limited number of events and avoiding the risk of model overfitting. Unadjusted survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with log rank tests. Reoperations were investigated during follow-up using cumulative incidence functions obtained by the Aalen-Johansen method with mortality considered as a competing event. Differences in cumulative incidences between patients with and without cysts were compared using Gray’s test. All analyses were performed with P < .05 as the significance criterion, using either IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Corporation) or R version 4.4.4 (R Foundation).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The ascending aortic wall resection was obtained from 120 consecutive patients who underwent ATAAD surgery and was processed for histology. The median time-to-surgery was less than 24 hours (IQR 12-33 hours). The patients underwent surgery immediately after diagnosis. Therefore, 27 (22.5%) patients underwent surgery in fewer than 12 hours after onset of symptoms, 45 (37.5%) were operated between 12-24 hours, 15 (12.5%) were operated between 24-48 hours after onset of symptoms, none between 48-72 hours, 2 (1.7%) between 72-96 hours, and 7 (5.8%) more than 96 hours after onset of symptoms. A total of 24 patients had no clear documentation of onset of symptoms and were excluded in the median time-to-surgery calculation. Patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. In total, there were 45 female patients (37.5%). The mean age for all patients was 65.6 years (SD 11.8). None had vasculitis or aortitis, but 3 (2.5%) patients had Marfan syndrome, all without cysts. Altogether, 23 (19.2%) patients had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) including 14 (31.1%) patients with cysts and 9 (12.0%) without (P = .016). There were 45 patients with cysts, including 10 (22.2%) patients with both renal and liver cysts, and 2 patients with both liver and ovarian cysts. There were 33 out of 45 (73.3%) patients with a single cyst including 23 (51.1%) renal, 8 (17.8%) liver, 1 (2.2%) epididymis and 1 (2.2%) ovarian, and 75 out of 120 (62.5%) without any cysts. The history of thoracic aortic dilation prior to ATAAD was recorded in 10 (22.2%) patients with and in 12 (16.0%) without cysts. The thoracic aortic diameters were also comparable in the patients with cysts as compared with those without cysts (Table 2).

In total, there were 55 patients with hemi-arch replacements (45.8%) and 13 total arch replacements (10.9%). In 90 (75.0%) patients, the entry tear was situated in the ascending aorta, while there were 16 (13.3%) patients with entry tears in the aortic arch. Pathology and immunohistochemistry

As shown in Table 4 and Figure 1, the extent of elastic fibre thinning was more prominent in patients with cysts as compared with those without (2.0 [1.0-2.0] vs 1.0 [0-2.0], PSU, P = .020). Interestingly, the type of smooth muscle nuclei loss showed a patchier configuration in patients with cysts vs those without (1.0 [1.0-2.0] vs 2.0 [1.0-2.0], PSU P = .023). Increased thickening of the medial layer of the vasa vasorum occurred in patients with cysts as compared with patients without (12, 26.7% vs 9, 12.0%, P = .049). According to the adjusted multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, neither age nor sex were significant predictors of the presence of cysts (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 1.018, 95% CI, 0.986-1.058, P = .237 and aOR: 1.626, 95% CI, 0.702-3.805, P = .255; respectively). Instead, elastic fibre thinning was associated with increased odds of cyst presence (aOR: 2.636, 95% CI, 1.143-9.095, P = .023).

Aortic reoperations

Follow-up was complete for our patients. (1) There were 7 reoperations in patients with cysts (15.5%) and 7 in patients without cysts (9.3%, P = .381). (2) The indication for reoperations was ongoing aortic dilatation for all patients. (3) For patients with cysts, there were 2 aortic root replacements (4.4%) and 5 aortic arch replacements with distal aortic grafting (11.1%). With regard to the patients without cysts, 1 aortic root replacement (1.3%), 4 aortic arch replacements with distal aortic grafting (5.3%), and 2 thoraco-abdominal aortic surgeries (2.7%) were performed.

Survival and reoperations

The occurrence of 30-day mortality was noted in 23 of 120 patients (19.2%), including 9 (20%) patients with cysts and 14 (18.7%) without cysts. During follow-up, 54 (45%) deaths occurred among the patients (Figure 2, log rank P = .910). The reoperation events associated with ongoing aortic dilatation per 100 patient-years was 2.57 for patients with cysts and 1.35 for patients without cysts. According to the competing risk analysis, the cumulative incidence of reoperations did not differ among the patients with or without cysts when mortality was considered as a competing risk (P = .236, Figure 3).

DISCUSSION

Main finding

Our study suggests that the presence of cysts may contribute to the thinning of elastic fibres within the aortic wall in patients developing ATAAD.

Comparison with previous literature

Aortic wall degeneration is often present during ATAAD.16 Patient characteristics including tissue degeneration, a history of connective tissue disorder, and hereditary background for a related syndrome may suggest susceptibility to ATAAD.17 The presence of organ cysts may indicate connective tissue degeneration.8 The presence of cysts and ascending aortic dilatation have been studied earlier.7,17,18 On the other hand, increased aortic dilatation is associated with aortic events also after surgery for ATAAD, leading to the need for aortic reoperations due to aortic reverse remodelling.19

The aortic wall in patients with ATAAD shares distinctive features as compared with aortic dilatation or controls without diseases. Dun et al8 found that patients with ATAAD as compared to those without aortic disease show increased incidence of organ cysts. In Marfan patients, the prevalence of simple renal cysts was higher as compared with sex- and age-matched controls.17 Simple renal cysts are associated with higher odds of various aortic diseases including dilatation and dissection.18,20 Kurata et al7 found that the number of renal cysts is associated with thoracic aortic dilatation using autopsy material. Greater aortic root involvement in patients with cysts is consistent with the proposed degeneration hypothesis. Taken together, previous studies suggest that the presence of organ cysts is associated with aortic wall disease, but systematic histology of the aortic wall specifically in patients with ATAAD as well as the presence of organ cysts have not been investigated earlier, as compared with those ATAAD patients without organ cysts.

Pathophysiological considerations

The association between cysts and aortic events suggests that a common pathogenic pathway may be expected.17,18 Cysts are bulging extensions of tissue formation often filled with liquid. The tissue wall of cysts and the basement membrane of renal cysts share similarities that characterize aortic wall degeneration, including changes in elastic fibres.7,21 The impact of MMPs on tissue degeneration may also explain the association of cysts and aortic wall degeneration.10 MMPs are involved in the degradation of elastic fibres, thus contributing to aortic dilatation, and MMP inhibition may be associated with aortic remodelling.5 On the other hand, MMPs alter both extracellular matrix degradation and organ cyst growth, while MMP inhibition attenuates these processes.4 Our patients with ATAAD had ovarian, kidney, liver and epididymis cysts, all characterized by tissue protrusions.8 The formation of cysts shares similar pathogenesis with the development of aortic wall degeneration associated with ATAAD,10,22 though the specific molecular interactions associated with MMPs, elastin and collagen metabolism still remain unexplored7; for example, many aortic wall samples during ATAAD included detrimental type IV collagen in the basement membranes of smooth muscle cells,2 and our patients with cysts included a lower score for the type of smooth muscle cell loss indicating a patchier histological pattern rather than a band-like extent of cell depletion.13

We employed a well-established and systematic histological evaluation of the aortic wall in patients with ATAAD.13 We found that the increased extent of elastic fibre thinning, increased thickening of vasa vasorum, and patchier type of smooth muscle cell nuclei loss characterized the aortic wall in patients during ATAAD with organ cysts as compared to those without cysts. The histological pattern may reflect varying histopathological and molecular pathways, including different structural and biological aspects. Elastic fibre thinning may indicate decreased medial structural integrity, which is pertinent to the mechanical resilience of the aortic wall. In contrast, thickening of the vasa vasorum may reflect microvascular remodelling associated with various smooth muscle cell nuclei loss types, as compared to patients without organ cysts. It is tempting to speculate that similar changes may ubiquitously occur in other organs, such as cysts that also mirror decrease in mechanical tissue resilience. Importantly, COPD characterized many of our patients with organ cysts, and the presence of cysts may again reflect similar systemic elastin degradation and MMP/elastase imbalance.21 This study shows a possible association between the presence of organ cysts and the thinning of elastic fibres, including a common underlying factor (eg, shared connective tissue or MMP-related vulnerability) that predisposes patients to both cyst formation and aortic wall degeneration, rather than cysts themselves predicting or preceding ATAAD.

Clinical implications

Understanding the nature of the specific ATAAD, patient characteristics including the plausible presence of cyst formation, and histopathological analysis add to the follow-up of the patient.21

Strengths and limitations

This contemporary single-centre cohort from real life represents a retrospective study combining histopathological analysis with clinical observation. The limitations of this study include the relatively small number of patients with a relatively short follow-up. The cysts were detected from the CT images retrospectively. Distinct fluid-filled cysts without minimum size criterion were identified by experienced radiologists using CT only. Temporal bias may impact on the analysis. The entry tear alone may not always determine the distal site of anastomose during surgery for ATAAD.23

CONCLUSION

The presence of cysts in patients requiring surgery for ATAAD may reflect aortic wall tissue degeneration, as observed by the extent of elastic fibre thinning. The presence of organ cysts may help predict dissection risk in asymptomatic patients with mild thoracic aortic dilatation, where the decision to operate prophylactically or intensify surveillance is difficult. Future prospective research in an elective patient population is warranted.
