Perioperative Care of Kidney Transplant Recipients: A Cross-Sectional Canadian Multidisciplinary Survey
Authors: Ryan N. McGinn, Jaswanth Gorla, Arezou Shahmoradi-Zavareh, Matthanja Bieze, Sonia Rodriguez-Ramirez, Anand Ghanekar, Marc Sicova, S. Joseph Kim, Stuart A. McCluskey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe perioperative period is a critical phase for kidney transplantation. Emerging evidence indicates the potential utility of several perioperative strategies to support immediate graft function and patient outcomes. In other countries surveying only anesthesiologists, there is broad variation in the reported routine care for kidney transplant recipients.ObjectiveWe sought to describe routine perioperative care for kidney transplant from a multidisciplinary perspective including perioperative clinicians (nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists) caring for kidney transplant recipients in Canada.DesignWe created an evidence-informed qualitative and quantitative survey of routine perioperative practices using an iterative and collaborative process. The survey was reviewed by our multidisciplinary group and tested by 3 nephrologists, 3 surgeons and 3 anesthesiologists prior to circulation.SettingAfter research ethics board approval, an online survey was distributed via e-mail to all Canadian adult kidney transplant centers. Transplant directors were asked by e-mail to distribute the survey to transplant nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists involved in the perioperative care of kidney transplant recipients at their center. Survey responses were collected electronically from January 13, 2025 to April 13, 2025.MeasurementsWe recorded all answers to our survey questions including multiple choice and free-text responses.MethodsWe defined agreement as >70% of respondents selecting the same response for a routine perioperative care question. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the variation across disciplines and across transplant centers in Canada.ResultsRespondents included 18 nephrologists, 14 surgeons and 39 anesthesiologists representing all kidney transplant centers in Canada (n=18). We found several areas of agreement including preoperative hemoglobin optimization and transfusion targets, hemodynamic monitoring, immunosuppression and use of preoperative dialysis. Routine care was variable with respect to the use of diuretics, blood pressure targets and intravascular fluid.LimitationsWe were unable to link reported routine care practices with patient or graft outcomes. We also are unable to provide consensus recommendations for perioperative care based on this survey study.ConclusionsWe describe perioperative care for kidney transplantation by transplant nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists in Canada. Some elements of care exhibit variation in practice while others show consistency between transplant clinicians and transplant hospitals. These findings identify priority areas of practice variation that may inform the design of future comparative studies and the development of evidence-informed perioperative strategies.

Introduction

End-stage kidney disease is a progressing health and economic burden on a global scale causing over 1.2 million deaths annually. 1 An estimated 2.5 million people around the world currently receive renal replacement therapy and this is expected to double by 2030. 2 Likewise, end-stage kidney disease continues to rise in Canada with >50,000 patients living with the diagnosis, representing a 24% increase in the number of patients depending on renal replacement therapy from 2012 to 2021. 3 This presents a substantial economic burden for patients and costs the Canadian healthcare system over $40 billion annually. 4 Kidney transplantation is the preferred treatment for kidney failure when considering patient survival, quality of life, and health system costs. 5 The demand for organs outpaces the supply6,7 which highlights the need to optimize graft function and graft survival after transplant. Despite improving patient survival rates, delayed graft function (DGF) and graft loss, among other related complications, occur as frequently as 40% with deceased donor kidney grafts.8,9 This risk is further compounded by both increasing age and the multi-comorbidity of recipients 10 along with an increasing use of expanded criteria donors, 11 death by circulatory criteria donors, 12 donors with prolonged warm or cold ischemia time,13,14 and donors with acute kidney injury. 15

The perioperative period is critical for kidney transplant recipients. Several factors may contribute to the outcome of the transplanted graft, including baseline clinical characteristics, 16 intravenous fluid management, 17 hypotension,18,19 red blood cell transfusion,20,21 immunosuppression regimen, 22 and intraoperative diuretics. 23 Despite the potential importance of perioperative issues on early graft function, there remains no broadly accepted consensus or standard practice for perioperative management of kidney transplant recipients. Recent national surveys including only anesthesiologists in China, 24 the United Kingdom 25 and the US 26 have identified vast heterogeneity in intraoperative anesthetic management of kidney transplant recipients. Variation in clinical practice that is not explained by patient preferences or patient factors is associated with adverse outcomes in other vulnerable patient groups.27-30 Furthermore, some of the reported routine perioperative practices in these surveys yielded decisions that conflicted with the available evidence and potentially impaired immediate graft function (e.g., use of normal saline 17 ).

To lay a foundation to develop evidence-based standardized pathways for kidney transplant recipients in Canada, we sought to understand the range of routine perioperative practices provided by the perioperative physicians (nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists). This cross-sectional survey study aimed to describe the current multidisciplinary perioperative care of kidney transplant recipients across Canada.

Methods

This survey was a cross-sectional study of perioperative kidney transplant physicians in Canada. The manuscript was written according to the CROSS checklist. 31 The protocol was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (Toronto, ON, Canada; 24-5251).

Study Population

Kidney transplant nephrologists, surgeons, and anesthesiologists working in accredited Canadian kidney transplant centers were eligible for inclusion in this study. To reduce the risk of personal bias and to optimize the reliability of the summative results, multiple responses by physicians from the same specialty at each institution were encouraged.

Recruitment and Sampling

The study team disseminated the survey by email to transplant nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists at the 18 Canadian kidney transplant centers. The local medical or surgical directors often served as an initial point of contact, with the hospital’s department head serving in the absence of publicly available kidney transplant leads. Each clinician was asked to identify individuals at their institution who fit the inclusion criteria for the study and distribute the recruitment email. This peer-recruitment process was employed to reduce potential biases from the initial convenience sample participants. 32 The recruitment email provided routine information about the study (e.g., its purpose, time commitment, anonymity, and protection of information collected) and a link to the online survey. Two reminder emails were sent 3 and 6 weeks after the initial recruitment attempt. This process was informed by the Dillman Tailored Design Method, which is commonly used in maximizing survey response rates. 33 Consent was inferred from completing and submitting the online survey, which was explicitly stated in the recruitment email and in a message at the start of the survey. The number of nephrologists, surgeons and anesthesiologists in Canada providing perioperative care for kidney transplant recipients is not publicly available, and thus it was not possible to determine the proportion of the available physicians who responded to our survey.

Survey Development

The survey was developed with a multidisciplinary team through an iterative, collaborative process. The initial list of questions was formed after reviewing the literature for similar surveys, existing knowledge gaps, and key decision points relevant to the pre-, intra-, and post-operative care of kidney transplant recipients during their transplant hospitalization. The study authors clarified the underlying concepts to investigate, as well as how to design and phrase each question. The initial survey was drafted by RNM and ASZ, and was then reviewed by the study authors. The draft was pretested for clarity, conciseness, and completeness by a transplant nephrologist, surgeon and anesthesiologist at three different transplant centers (Toronto General Hospital and St. Michael’s Hospital in Toronto, ON, Canada, as well as The Ottawa Hospital in Ottawa, ON, Canada). Changes to wording and question length were made in response to the reviewers’ comments. The final survey contained 40 multiple-choice questions and 16 free-text sub-questions. The survey was designed to be completed online using REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure web-based platform that adheres to Canadian privacy laws.34,35

Survey Instrument

The link provided in the recruitment email brought respondents to an online survey hosted by REDCap. The online survey included four sections: 1) demographics – specialty and transplant center affiliation; followed by 2) preoperative, 3) intraoperative, and 4) postoperative phases of care for the kidney transplant recipient. The survey was expected to take 12 minutes or less. The survey data were anonymous and transferred to a secure database on a hospital server. The full survey instrument is available in the Appendix.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics from survey responses were used to identify percentages for count data. Agreement was defined as >70% of respondents selecting a particular response. Although we did not perform a formal Delphi, a threshold of 70% was selected as it is commonly used to define weak consensus in Delphi studies. 36 For questions that were specialty-specific, the denominator included only those who responded with any answer other than “not relevant to my specialty”. Qualitative data analysis was planned for free-text responses and comment fields, though the composition of these free-text comments was determined after survey completion to not be appropriate for organization of emerging themes (see Appendix).

Hemodynamics. Regarding perioperative hemodynamics (Figure 1, Appendix 1), there was agreement (80% of respondents) for using an intraoperative mean arterial pressure target at reperfusion, though the specific target varied from >60 to >85 mmHg. Few respondents routinely employed a systolic pressure target, which varied between >70 to >140 mmHg. Blood pressure targets were typically maintained until arrival to (54%) or discharge from (21%) the post-anesthesia care unit. Respondents expressed agreement (80%) that crystalloids were the first choice for maintaining perioperative blood pressure. The few respondents (n=5) who routinely use central venous access indicated a desired range of central venous pressure of 5 to 16 mmHg.

Vascular access. Vascular access selection (Figure 1) was variable across sites as were the chosen indications for these lines. Several indications were mentioned regarding the use of central lines, though no agreement was reached. Most respondents (74%) routinely or selectively (patients with an allergy or on hemodialysis) use chlorhexidine-free central line kits. Most respondents (80%) routinely insert an arterial line for kidney transplant recipients. There was variable timing for removal of the arterial line.

Fluids. Perioperative fluid management (Figure 2) showed agreement across Canada on the institutional availability of normal saline and lactated Ringer’s. Plasmalyte was less available for intraoperative care (44%). No agreement was reached for the first choice of intraoperative fluid, though most respondents (85%) selected a balanced crystalloid. Routine volume administration was variable between 1 to 5 L intraoperatively (50% of respondents were in this range), with the rest reporting titration for individual patients. The default postoperative fluid was also variable. The most common way to guide fluid therapy in the first 24 hours post-transplant was urine output replacement, though agreement was not reached. Immunosuppression. Immunosuppression (Figure 2) was similar across Canada with >90% of respondents indicating routine use of perioperative steroids, though there was discrepancy in the timing. Most respondents (76%) routinely used methylprednisolone. There was agreement in the use of basiliximab as the first-choice for induction immunosuppression for living and deceased donor kidney transplants, though the timing for the first dose was variable. Of the cases where anti-thymocyte globulin was used, there was agreement on a total cumulative dose of 3 mg/kg.

Recovery. Nearly 80% of respondents indicated that there was no enhanced recovery program for kidney transplant recipients at their center. Duration of stay in the post-anesthesia care unit was variable, with 63% indicating a typical stay <4 hours. Disposition from the post-anesthesia care unit was also variable with 50% going to a regular ward and 35% to a level-2 stepdown unit. There was agreement that initial postoperative analgesia was managed by the acute pain or anesthesiology team, and most commonly with an intravenous patient-controlled analgesia pump. Nearly all respondents expected patients to be comfortable with exclusively oral analgesics by the end of postoperative day 1. There was agreement that living donor kidney recipients stayed 5 to 7 days after transplant, whereas respondents were split between a 5 to 7 day and 8 to 10 day postoperative stay after deceased donor kidney transplantation.

Other. The remaining quantitative questions were focused on pre-operative dialysis, intraoperative vasodilators and use of intraoperative diuretics (Figure 3). Pre-operative dialysis was performed when indicated (81%) for kidney transplant recipients with variable indications and end-points. There was agreement that local vasodilators were not routinely used at reperfusion to enhance graft perfusion. Discussion

In a national multidisciplinary survey of all kidney transplant centers in Canada, we found several areas of agreement in the reported routine management of kidney transplant recipients. Some key decisions yielded a higher degree of variation, such as the target blood pressure at and following reperfusion, postoperative fluid management and the use of intraoperative diuretics. These differences represent areas of clinical uncertainty where evidence is limited and resulting routine practice varies. Aside from potentially variable outcomes, these differences in care may be a barrier to large-scale collaborative research initiatives and quality improvement efforts. Our findings highlight the need for dedicated guidelines to unify an evidence-based approach for the optimal perioperative care of kidney transplant recipients.

Prior survey studies of anesthesiologists in the UK, 25 China 24 and the US 26 found a high degree of variation in the perioperative care of kidney transplant recipients. Such variation may stem from knowledge-to-care gaps, which may lead to suboptimal care. 38 A primary concern in these surveys was that several routine care decisions conflicted with the available evidence and may be associated with harm. The key areas with gaps between available evidence and reported routine care in these studies included the selection of intravenous fluid (colloid versus crystalloid and normal saline versus a balanced salt solution), hemodynamic targets and vasopressor selection.24-26 Our multidisciplinary survey exhibits some parallels and there would appear to be room to improve routine perioperative care to optimize early graft function on an international scale. Importantly, this study was not designed to determine optimal perioperative strategies or to establish standardized care pathways. Rather, our findings provide a spectrum of current routine practice and identify areas of equipoise that are well-suited for prospective comparative trials.

Some answers to our survey indicate that routine care decisions conflict with the available evidence, signaling a knowledge-to-care gap across Canada. First, the avoidance of normal saline in favor of balanced crystalloid solutions (Plasmalyte or Ringer’s Lactate) is proposed for surgical patients39,40 and has level 1 evidence for kidney transplant recipients.17,41 Normal saline was selected by several respondents as the primary fluid for intraoperative (11%) and postoperative (30%) use. Second, several studies report an association between higher intraoperative arterial pressure target (in this case, mean arterial pressure >75 mmHg) and improved graft function.18,19,42 Of the respondents, only 39% indicated an intraoperative blood pressure target consistent with the available evidence. Third, central line use appears variable. While use in patients with inadequate peripheral intravenous access and those otherwise requiring anti-thymocyte globulin is likely reasonable, routine use may no longer be favored. This is consistent with emerging evidence demonstrating the safety of peripherally administered vasoactive agents 43 and the poor performance of central venous pressure in guiding volume status assessment.44,45 Finally, while the use of intraoperative diuretics 23 and enhanced recovery protocols 46 may be associated with better graft and patient outcomes, these strategies were routinely employed by only 59% and 20% of respondents, respectively. In particular, intraoperative diuretic use is supported by limited evidence including a prospective cohort (n=162) 23 and a meta-analysis 47 (only 1 randomized study, 48 n=17 in the intervention group). As interdisciplinary, national and international collaboration intensifies, with the aim of raising the standard of care, there is an urgent need for rigorous, prospective perioperative trials in kidney transplant recipients to better define care that will lead to optimal graft function.

While notable variation was found in our survey, we report some key areas of agreement. Routine care decisions that were in line with the existing evidence included hemoglobin optimization and perioperative transfusion,20,21,49 close hemodynamic monitoring through routine arterial line insertion,18,19,42 preferred use of chlorhexidine-free central access, 50 immunosuppression induction with steroids with a preference for basiliximab, 51 and routine use of preoperative dialysis when indicated (i.e., a patient already on dialysis or for indications including volume overload and electrolyte abnormalities). These data support the encouraging notion that perioperative transplant specialists are providing evidence-based care and may be well-positioned to implement a cohesive set of perioperative guidelines. Importantly, despite apparent agreement for strategies including hemoglobin (preoperative targets and perioperative transfusion thresholds) and hemodynamics, we lack the high-quality evidence needed before widespread knowledge translation and implementation.

Most of the recent studies to assess the impact of perioperative care decisions on graft function are single-center, retrospective and/or observational in nature. These studies have important limitations that may increase the chance of biased inferences about treatment effects. Furthermore, to our knowledge there remains no available evidence to guide any diverging perioperative care strategies for recipients of living or deceased donor grafts. Continuing to advance the perioperative care of kidney transplant recipients will improve early graft function and potentially increase the longevity of patients and their grafts. The path forward will require multidisciplinary engagement, rigorous prospective randomized perioperative trials, and comprehensive guidelines to bridge the existing knowledge-to-care gap and reduce unwarranted variation in perioperative care that may be harmful to patients.

Our survey study has limitations that deserve mention. First, despite our excellent response from 100% of the transplant hospitals in Canada, we still had relatively few responses compared to the total number of transplant nephrologists, surgeons, and anesthesiologists in Canada (the denominators for each specialty would be challenging to quantify and are not publicly available). We implemented best practice methods to optimize the response rate; however, there may be responder bias in that we could be overestimating the level of agreement and the level of evidence-informed care provided to kidney transplant recipients across Canada. To this end, we did not identify the demographics of our respondents based on whether they were fellowship-trained in kidney transplantation. While this could influence the routine care, we sought primarily to describe the spectrum of care provided across Canada. Second, there was a disproportionate number of responses from Ontario (48%, Table 1) so there may be limited generalizability to other provinces. However, it is notable that Ontario accounts for ∼40% of kidney transplants performed in Canada. 37 Last, while our survey addressed common perioperative decisions for patients undergoing kidney transplantation, we are unable to link the responses of routine care to the actual care provided or to patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study describes the range of routine perioperative decisions in the care of kidney transplant recipients with multidisciplinary agreement on a national scale. There remain areas with a higher degree of variation that may benefit from multidisciplinary collaboration to determine an evidence-based standard of care for kidney transplant recipients. Our study highlights the need for a call to action to develop evidence-informed perioperative guidelines to advance care and improve the outcomes of kidney transplant recipients.
