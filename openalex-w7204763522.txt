Crystallizable Ti- and Ge-based glasses as an active sintering aid
for LATP ceramics: Low-temperature densiﬁcation
and enhanced ionic conductivity
Tatsuya Tezuka1,2, Ikuo Yanase1,³, Shohei Kodama1 and Hiroaki Takeda1
1

Saitama University, Faculty of Engineering, Department of Applied Chemistry,
255 Shimoohkubo, Sakura-ku, Saitama 338–8570, Japan

Li1.3Al0.3Ti1.7(PO4)3 (LATP) is a promising oxide solid electrolyte for all-solid-state lithium-ion batteries, but
high-temperature sintering and large grain-boundary resistance limit its practical application. In this study, Li–
Al–Ti–P–O and Li–Al–Ge–P–O glasses were investigated as crystallizable sintering additives for LATP solid
electrolytes and compared with conventional Li3PO4. The Ti-based glass promoted low-temperature densiﬁcation, achieving a relative density of 95.1 % and a total ionic conductivity of 1.60 © 10¹4 S cm¹1 after pressureless
sintering at 800 °C for 2 h. The Ge-based glass showed a higher conductivity of 2.88 © 10¹4 S cm¹1 at 900 °C,
exceeding those of Li3PO4 and the Ti-based glass under the same condition. Impedance analysis indicated that
this enhancement was mainly due to reduced grain-boundary resistance. Raman spectroscopy suggested that the
Ge-based glass suppressed the formation of resistive Ti-related impurity phases observed in the Li3PO4- and Tibased-glass-added samples. These results demonstrate that crystallizable Ti- and Ge-based glass additives can
function as active sintering aids for LATP, with the Ti-based glass enhancing low-temperature densiﬁcation and
the Ge-based glass improving grain-boundary Li-ion transport.
Keywords: Li ion conductor, Glass ceramics, Sintered body, Sintering aid

1. Introduction
All-solid-state lithium-ion batteries (ASSBs) have attracted signiﬁcant attention as next-generation energystorage devices because they promise improved safety and
higher energy density than conventional liquid-electrolyte
batteries.1) Among solid electrolytes, oxide-based materials—particularly NASICON-type Li1+xAlxTi2¹x(PO4)3
(LATP)—are considered promising owing to their high
bulk ionic conductivity (typically ³10¹3 S·cm¹1) and
good chemical stability in air.2) In practice, however, the
total ionic conductivity of LATP ceramics is often limited
by a large apparent intergranular resistance, which can
dominate the overall impedance even when bulk conduction is suﬃciently high.3) Moreover, densiﬁcation of LATP
commonly requires high-temperature sintering, which may
induce lithium loss and/or the formation of resistive secondary (impurity) phases, thereby degrading electrochemical performance.4,5) Therefore, a processing strategy that
enables densiﬁcation at reduced temperatures while maintaining a favorable phase assemblage and low intergranular impedance is essential for high-performance LATP
solid electrolytes.

To address these challenges, low-temperature sintering
with sintering aids has been widely investigated. Various
additives—such as Li3PO4, Li3BO3, and glass frits—have
been reported to enhance densiﬁcation by promoting mass
transport during sintering.6) However, many studies have
focused primarily on lowering the sintering temperature
and increasing density, with comparatively less attention to
the ionic conductivity and chemical/structural compatibility of additive-derived intergranular phases.7–9) Because
the ionic conductivity of conventional additives is typically lower than that of LATP grains, residual additivederived phases can remain at grain boundaries and act as
resistive layers, increasing the eﬀective intergranular impedance. Consequently, sintering aids often involve a
trade-oﬀ: densiﬁcation is improved, whereas the total ionic
conductivity may be limited by highly resistive intergranular regions.
Overcoming this trade-oﬀ requires a multifunctional
additive that promotes low-temperature densiﬁcation while
simultaneously providing a favorable intergranular environment for Li-ion transport. Here, we propose a crystallizable glass, designed for glass-crystallization routes, as
an “active sintering aid” for LATP ceramics.10) Unlike
conventional additives that primarily assist densiﬁcation,
this glass-based additive is expected to (i) enhance densiﬁcation at reduced temperatures and (ii) transform into

2. Methods
2.1

Materials

Commercial LATP powder (SELAPath, Sumita Optical
Glass, Japan; D50 = 2.4 ¯m) was used as the starting ceramic powder. Three additives were prepared in-house:
Li3PO4, a Ti-based glass, and a Ge-based glass. Median
particle sizes (D50) were conﬁrmed by laser diﬀraction.
Li3PO4 was synthesized by mixing commercial LiPO3 and
Li2CO3 powders at a mass ratio of 1:2.47, calcining the
mixture at 700 °C for 2 h in an electric furnace, and grinding the calcined product for 10 min using an alumina
mortar and pestle to obtain an additive powder with D50 µ
15 ¯m.

2.2

Preparation of glass and glass-ceramic
additives

Ti-based and Ge-based glasses were designed as crystallizable Li–Al–(Ti or Ge)–P–O compositions. The target
oxide-basis compositions (wt %) were as follows:
Ti-based glass: Li2 O 17:7; Al2 O3 2:5;
TiO2 22:1; P2 O5 57:7
Ge-based glass: Li2 O 17:3; Al2 O3 3:9;
GeO2 24:2; P2 O5 54:6
Appropriate oxide, carbonate, and phosphate raw materials
were weighed, mixed, and melted in a platinum crucible
at 1200 °C for 2 h in an electric furnace. The melts were
quenched by pouring into water to obtain glass frits. The
frits were ground for 10 min using an alumina mortar and
pestle to obtain glass additive powders with D50 µ 15 ¯m.

2.3

Powder mixing, pellet forming, debinding,
and sintering

LATP powder (1.0 g) was mixed with a prescribed
amount of additive (Li3PO4, Ti-based glass, or Ge-based
glass) using an alumina mortar and pestle for 5 min. Additive contents are reported in the main text as wt % (mass
percent relative to the total powder mass, i.e., LATP +
additive). A 10 wt % aqueous poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA)
solution (0.01 g) was then added as a binder, and the
mixture was further blended for 5 min. The mixed powder
was loaded into a 10 mm-diameter tungsten carbide die
and uniaxially pressed at 70 MPa for 5 min. The green
compacts were placed on an alumina boat and debound at
300 °C for 12 h in an electric furnace. The compacts were
heated at 10 °C·min¹1 to the target sintering temperature
(700–1000 °C), held for 2 h, and cooled to room temperature at 5 °C·min¹1 to obtain sintered pellets.

2.4

Diﬀerential thermal analysis (DTA)

Diﬀerential thermal analysis (DTA) was performed
using a Rigaku TG-DTA8122 under air at a heating rate
of 5 °C·min¹1. A sample observation unit was used to
visually monitor macroscopic softening and ﬂow behavior
of the glass additives during heating.

2.5

X-ray diﬀraction (XRD), Rietveld reﬁnement, and Raman spectroscopy

Powder X-ray diﬀraction (XRD) was performed using a
Rigaku Ultima IV diﬀractometer with Cu K¡ radiation.
Data were collected over 2ª = 10–40° with a step size of
0.013° using powder specimens. Rietveld reﬁnement was
performed using Rigaku SmartLab Studio II software with
a silicon standard for angular calibration. Lattice parameters and unit-cell volumes of the LATP phase were obtained from the reﬁnement results. Raman spectra were
acquired using a Wasatch Photonics RAMAN532 system
with a 532 nm laser (nominal output power: 200 mW). The
integration time was 1000 ms.

2.6

Microstructure observation and elemental mapping

Cross sections for SEM observation were prepared by
fracturing sintered pellets, followed by wet polishing of
the fractured surfaces using a cerium oxide abrasive. SEM
imaging was performed using a JEOL IT-300 at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV under high-vacuum conditions
(secondary-electron mode). Elemental mapping was performed using a Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc Scios 2 system.

2.7

Density measurement

The relative density of sintered pellets was determined
by calculating the geometric density from the pellet mass
and dimensions and normalizing it by the theoretical density of LATP (2.93 g·cm¹3),13) with the density or speciﬁc
gravity of each additive taken into account according to its
added amount. The values used for Li3PO4, Ti-based glass,
and Ge-based glass were 2.54 g·cm¹3, 2.75, and 2.73,
respectively; the value for Li3PO4 was the theoretical

2.8

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Arrhenius analysis

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed using a HIOKI IM3536-01 impedance analyzer
over a frequency range of 1 Hz to 10 MHz. Both faces of
each sintered pellet were coated with Au–Pd by sputtering
to form blocking electrodes. The specimen was placed in
a temperature-controlled chamber and contacted using
tweezer-type probes. The AC amplitude (test level) was
set to 1 V. Nyquist plots were analyzed using the highfrequency intercept (ZBHF) and low-frequency intercept
(ZBLF) of the observed semicircle. The total resistance was
deﬁned as Rtotal = ZBLF, and the semicircle width was deﬁned as Rarc = ZBLF ¹ ZBHF. Because the impedance spectra
were dominated by a single semicircle within the accessible
frequency window, Rarc was treated as an eﬀective intergranular resistance for comparative discussion. Ionic conductivity (·) was calculated as · = t/(RA), where t is the
pellet thickness and A is the electrode area. Temperaturedependent measurements were performed at ¹5, 10, 25, 40,
55, 70, and 85 °C. Arrhenius plots were constructed from
the total conductivity (·total), and activation energies (Ea)
were obtained from linear ﬁtting of ln(·) versus 1/T.

3. Results and discussion
3.1

Concept and thermal behavior

Figure 1 summarizes the design concept of the present
work. Conventional sintering aids for LATP are typically
selected to form a low-temperature liquid or viscous phase
that promotes particle rearrangement and densiﬁcation
while minimizing excessive reactions with the LATP
matrix.16) In such conventional approaches, the ionic conductivity of the residual additive-derived phase is not
always treated as a primary design criterion; consequently,
the remaining intergranular phase can behave as a resistive
component and limit the total ionic conductivity of sintered pellets. In contrast, we propose an “active sintering
aid” concept based on crystallizable Li–Al–(Ti or Ge)–P–
O glasses. This concept is designed to provide two synergistic functions: (i) low-temperature softening and wetting
to assist densiﬁcation, and (ii) crystallization during and/
or after sintering into relatively Li-ion–conductive phosphate-related phases (e.g., NASICON-related domains).
As a result, even if an additive-derived phase remains in
intergranular regions, it is expected to be less detrimental—or potentially beneﬁcial—to Li-ion transport, thereby
improving the total ionic conductivity.
Figure 2 shows the DTA curves of the Ti-based and Gebased glass additives, accompanied by in situ snapshots of
the glass powders captured at temperatures where macroscopic ﬂow was observed. The Ti-based glass exhibited a
glass transition at Tg = 442 °C and an exothermic crystallization peak at Tx = 526 °C. The Ge-based glass showed
Tg = 399 °C and two exothermic events at Tx1 = 481 °C
and Tx2 = 533 °C, indicating its crystallizable nature. In
addition to these low-temperature thermal events, in situ
observation revealed the onset of pronounced softening/
ﬂow at higher temperatures: approximately 780–830 °C
for the Ti-based glass and 730–800 °C for the Ge-based
glass. These results suggest that both additives can develop
a viscous or liquid-like phase within the temperature win-

3.3

Eﬀective intergranular transport from
impedance

Figure 5 compares the impedance response of LATP
pellets sintered at 900 °C for 2 h with the optimum additive
contents (Li3PO4: 4.76 wt %; Ti-based glass: 16.67 wt %;
Ge-based glass: 16.67 wt %). To minimize the inﬂuence of
slight diﬀerences in pellet geometry, the impedance data
were converted using the geometry factor (A/t) and are
presented as area-speciﬁc resistivity in ³·cm (i.e., µ =
R·A/t).21,22) The spectra exhibit a dominant semicircle
followed by a low-frequency tail. Accordingly, the spectra
were ﬁtted using an equivalent circuit of Rs + (R1 «« Q1) +
Qe, where Qe accounts for low-frequency electrode polarization.23–25) Within the accessible frequency window (up
to 10 MHz), a distinct high-frequency bulk arc was not
resolved. Therefore, the high-frequency intercept (ZBHF)
and low-frequency intercept (ZBLF) of the observed semicircle were used to deﬁne the apparent area-speciﬁc total
resistivity, µtotal = ZBLF, and the semicircle width, µarc =
ZBLF ¹ ZBHF. In this study, µarc was treated as an eﬀective
intergranular contribution for comparative discussion.26–28)
At 25 °C, representative values of (µarc, µtotal) were (5150,
6050) ³·cm for Li3PO4, (8280, 9100) ³·cm for the Tibased glass, and (2700, 3500) ³·cm for the Ge-based
glass. Thus, the Ge-based glass yields the lowest µarc and
µtotal among the tested additives, indicating a substantially
reduced dominant resistive contribution. This reduction
provides a direct transport-based rationale for the superior
·total observed at 900 °C (Fig. 4), even though the relative
density of the Ge-based glass pellet is lower than that of
the Li3PO4-added pellet.

3.5

First-principles stability evaluation of LiTiOPO4 as a candidate secondary phase

The Raman spectroscopy results in Section 3.4 indicated that the formation of the Ti-related secondary phase
observed in the Li3PO4-added and Ti-based-glass-added
samples was suppressed when the Ge-based glass was
added. On the basis of the Raman band at approximately
790 cm¹1, this secondary phase can be attributed to TiO4
tetrahedra and/or a LiTiOPO4-related phase. In the present
calculations, LiTiOPO4 was therefore adopted as a representative model phase for the Ti-related secondary phase.
To examine, from the viewpoint of thermodynamic stability, why the formation of this secondary phase is suppressed by Ge addition, the lattice energies for the cases
in which 0, 25, and 50 % of the Ti sites in LiTiOPO4
(orthorhombic, space group Pnma, No. 62) were substitutionally occupied by Ge were calculated using the ﬁrstprinciples calculations described in Section 2.9.
The calculated lattice energies are shown in Table 2.
The lattice energy was ¹5526.93 kJ mol¹1 for LiTiOPO4
without Ge substitution, ¹5370.16 kJ mol¹1 for 25 % Ge
substitution, and ¹5223.62 kJ mol¹1 for 50 % Ge substitution. Thus, the lattice energy became less negative with
increasing Ge content. Since a more negative lattice energy
generally corresponds to a more stable crystal lattice, this
result suggests that substitution of Ge at the Ti sites would
decrease the lattice stability of the LiTiOPO4-type secondary phase.
This tendency is consistent with the experimental observation that the Ti-related secondary phase was suppressed
in the Ge-based-glass-added samples. If part of the Ge

4. Conclusion
In this study, crystallizable Li–Al–Ti–P–O and Li–Al–
Ge–P–O glasses were examined as sintering additives for
LATP ceramics. The Ti-based glass was eﬀective for lowtemperature pressureless sintering, achieving a relative
density of 95.1 % and a total ionic conductivity of 1.60 ©
10¹4 S cm¹1 at 800 °C. The Ge-based glass showed the
highest conductivity of 2.88 © 10¹4 S cm¹1 at 900 °C, exceeding those of conventional Li3PO4 and the Ti-based
glass under the same condition. Although the Ge-basedglass-added sample had a relatively low density, impedance
analysis revealed a signiﬁcant reduction in grain-boundary
resistance, indicating that the improved conductivity was
mainly due to enhanced intergranular Li-ion transport
rather than densiﬁcation alone. Raman spectroscopy
showed that resistive Ti-related impurity phases observed
in the Li3PO4 and Ti-based-glass-added samples were suppressed by the Ge-based glass. These results indicate that
Ti- and Ge-based crystallizable glasses function as active
sintering aids for LATP: the Ti-based glass promotes lowtemperature densiﬁcation, while the Ge-based glass improves grain-boundary Li-ion transport by suppressing
resistive intergranular phase formation.
