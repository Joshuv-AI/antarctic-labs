Arithmetic regularity as an alternative to transference
Sam Chow and Sean Prendiville and Santiago Vazquez

Abstract
. Since Green (2005), the Fourier-analytic transference principle has dominated the landscape of combinatorial theorems relative to sparse arithmetic sets. We demonstrate a different approach using arithmetic regularity. This is more versatile and has the potential to succeed when no obvious ‘dense model’ is forthcoming. Moreover, we contend that, just as the traditional circle method disassembles an arithmetic problem into real and p p -adic parts which can be solved individually, the arithmetic regularity method generalises this to yield an additional ‘combinatorial’ factor. This framework leads directly to a correct lower bound on the number of configurations in a dense set. We illustrate this using a system comprising a linear equation together with a higher-degree equation.

Introduction

Roth
[

]
famously showed that if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
has positive upper density

\limsup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{|\mathcal{A}\cap[N]|}{N}>0,

then
\mathcal{A}
contains non-trivial three-term arithmetic progressions, where

[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}.

This has inspired many outstanding works considering sparse analogues, e.g.
[

,
16
,
17
,

,

.

A non-trivial three-term arithmetic progression
(x,y,z)
is precisely a solution to
x+z=2y
with distinct variables. Roth’s density-increment argument extends to homogeneous linear equations

a_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+a_{s}x_{s}=0,

where
s\geqslant 3
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
are non-zero integers summing to zero. The condition
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{s}=0
is clearly necessary, since arithmetic progressions have positive upper density.

Higher-degree equations were initially approached by including additional equations to form a translation-invariant system, i.e. if
\mathbf{x}
is a solution then so is
a\mathbf{x}+b\boldsymbol{1}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. Translation-invariant systems are amenable to Roth’s approach or, more simply, an application of Szemerédi’s theorem
[
50
]
. These observations were exploited by Keil
[
32
]
and Henriot
[
30
,
29
]
.

In
[
5
]
, Browning and the second author brought the Fourier-analytic transference principle to bear on a problem not amenable to this approach. The Fourier-analytic transference principle was introduced by Green in
[
22
]
, and used by Green–Tao in their renowned work on primes and arithmetic progressions
[
23
]
. Although
[
5
]
rested heavily on the foundations laid by Green, it led to an increase in similar applications, in part due to the following:

(1)

It operated in what was then an unusually sparse setting, albeit preceded by work of Harper
[
27
]
.

(2)

The
W
-trick was necessarily more involved.

(3)

It packaged these ideas into a general framework which appeared to lead to other applications. We also refer the reader to the second author’s expository piece
[
39
]
.

The key proposition of
[
5
]
was refined in
[
9
, Theorem 4.3]
using recent advances in and around the quantitative theory of Roth’s theorem
[
19
,
33
,
34
]
.

In the aforementioned work, Browning and the second author showed that if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
has positive upper density then

a_{1}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{s}x_{s}^{2}=0

has a solution over
\mathcal{A}
, whenever
s\geqslant 5
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}
are non-zero integers summing to zero. This was extended to higher degrees and to prime powers in
[
15
]
, to systems of diagonal equations of the same degree in
[
12
]
. It was then generalised to sums of values of
intersective polynomials
[
10
,
11
]
. Similar problems were tackled using the transference principle in
[

,

,
42

45

[
12
,
14
,
41

49

Despite these manifold applications, the transference approach has at least one clear defect, namely that an ad-hoc ‘dense model problem’ needs to be concocted, a problem whose solutions can then be transferred to the sparse setting. Unfortunately, it is not always apparent whether an appropriate dense problem exists, as is the case for the main problem we discuss in the present work. We hope to convince the reader that applying arithmetic regularity naturally outputs the appropriate dense problem, without the need for any sophisticated thinking. We discuss our arithmetic regularity approach after presenting the application.

Example 1.3

Suppose

1.3.
Methods and organisation

The proof of our arithmetic regularity lemma is modelled on
[
51
]
. We incorporate an additional technical device, replacing regular Bohr sets with what we term ‘random translate Bohr neighbourhoods’. Although not essential to our application, we believe that this machinery simplifies a number of calculations, and we hope that it receives further use. The general idea is to use an energy increment argument, conditioning on the relevant atom of a Bohr partition. When working with a collection of polynomial phases, we form a closed set by taking their span, inducing a seminorm which furnishes a suitable notion of uniformity. This is carried out in §
2
.

To then solve our arithmetic combinatorics problem, we require two types of configuration control (generalised von Neumann lemmas). Standard arguments control the relevant counting operator using Fourier analysis, so that
f_{\mathrm{unf}}
makes a negligible contribution. The number of variables required — the value of
s_{0}
— comes from the resolution of the main conjecture of Vinogradov’s mean value theorem, and subsequent results
[
3
,
38
,
55
]
. This stage is completed in §
5
.

To handle
f_{\mathrm{sml}}
, we require
\ell^{2}
control, which is far more involved. The structured part
f_{\mathrm{str}}
comes equipped with an arithmetic progression
\mathcal{P}
. An application of Cauchy’s inequality breaks the problem down into two parts, each of which needs to be solved with
\mathcal{P}
-dependence tracked:

(1)

A lower bound on the number of solutions over
\mathcal{P}
,

(2)

and an upper bound on the number of solutions to a more complicated auxiliary system of equations in
2s-1
many variables.

We complete each of these tasks using the circle method
[
53
]
. The analysis is quite involved, as the need for precise dependence on
\mathcal{P}
complicates the analysis of the singular series and singular integral for each task and in each case. This is sometimes referred to as
quantitative weak approximation

[
6
,
35
]
. These tasks are carried out in §
3
and §
4
, respectively.

To estimate the structured count, we need to exploit the quantitative irrationality of the frequencies
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(1)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{(k)}
. We do so in §
6
by using Fourier series to establish equidistribution statements, leading to an expression for our count in terms of the density of solutions on a certain torus.

Finally, in §
7
, we apply a solution to the compact problem to infer supersaturation for our original problem. The former is in general deep
[
8
]
, but in this particular setting is not
[
52
]
.

2.1.
Bohr random translates

The following infrastructure extends the model case of linear Bohr sets discussed in

\phi:[N]\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}

(2.1)

B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})=\{n\in[N]:\phi(n)\in{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}+{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}/2+[0,1/2)^{d}\}\quad({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}),

partitioning
[N]
.
We now define a conditional expectation operator projecting
f:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
to a function
f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}
that is constant on the atoms of the partition (
2.1
). For
n\in[N]
, we write
B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}(n)
for the atom
B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})
containing

.
For
\phi:[N]\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and

, we define

to be the average of

over the atom of the partition (

) containing

, i.e.

(2.2)

f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}(n)\coloneq\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{m\in B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}(n)}f(m).

We define an inner product of

by

\langle f,g\rangle=\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)\overline{g(n)}.

The functions
f
and

are

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
f,g:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
,
\phi:[N]\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and

(1)

(f+g)_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}=f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}+g_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}

(4)

For

2.2.
Weak regularity and full regularity

Throughout this subsection and the next, we fix a non-empty but otherwise arbitrary set
\Phi
of functions
[N]\to\mathbb{T}
. For
f:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
, we define a seminorm

\|f\|_{\Phi}=\sup_{\phi\in\Phi}\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)e(\phi(n))\Big|.

We identify the set of functions
[N]\to\mathbb{T}
with
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, thus imbuing it with a topology.

Lemma 2.2

(Random translate inverse theorem)
.

Let
\Phi
be a closed subset of
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, let
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathbb{T}
be an interval of length
{\lambda}(\mathcal{I})\leqslant 1/2
, and let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
. Then there exists
\phi\in\Phi
such that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)1_{{\alpha}-\mathcal{I}}(\phi(n))\Big|\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\geqslant{\lambda}(\mathcal{I})\|f\|_{\Phi}/2.

Proof.

The map

\mathbb{T}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\qquad\phi\mapsto\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)e(\phi(n))\Big|

is continuous. Hence, by compactness of
\Phi
, there exists
\phi\in\Phi
such that

\|f\|_{\Phi}=\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)e(\phi(n))\Big|.

Let us choose representatives
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
b-a={\lambda}(\mathcal{I})
such that, viewed as subsets of
\mathbb{T}
,

(a,b)\subseteq\mathcal{I}\subseteq[a,b].

For
x\in\mathbb{T}
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}}1_{x-\mathcal{I}}({\alpha})e({\alpha})\mathrm{d}{\alpha}=\frac{e(x-a)-e(x-b)}{2\pi i}=\frac{e(-a)-e(-b)}{2\pi i}e(x).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)e(\phi(n))\Big|

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{|e(b-a)-1|}\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)\int_{\mathbb{T}}1_{\phi(n)-\mathcal{I}}({\alpha})e({\alpha})\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2\pi}{|e(b-a)-1|}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\Big|\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)1_{{\alpha}-\mathcal{I}}(\phi(n))\Big|\mathrm{d}{\alpha}.

The conclusion now follows from the fact that

|e(b-a)-1|=2|\sin(\pi(b-a))|\geqslant 4\|b-a\|.

∎

Lemma 2.3

(Weak regularity)
.

Let
\Phi

\mathbb{T}^{N}
containing zero, and let

(2.4)

Proof.

We perform an iterative procedure. Stage

such that

(2.5)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\geqslant d({\varepsilon}/4)^{2}N.

We initialise at stage

with
f_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{[N]}f
. The process terminates if
\|f-f_{\phi}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant{\varepsilon}N
.

Otherwise
\|f-f_{\phi}\|_{\Phi}>{\varepsilon}N
and, by Lemma

, there exists

such that

\int_{\mathbb{T}}|\langle f-f_{\phi},g\rangle|\mathrm{d}{\alpha}^{\prime}>{\varepsilon}N/4,

where

.
Thus, by the definition (

) of

, linearity, and the triangle inequality,

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|\langle f-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},g\rangle|\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}^{\prime}>{\varepsilon}N/4.

Since

and
f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}
are constant on sets of the form
B_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}},{\varepsilon}^{\prime})
, Lemma

and Cauchy–Schwarz give

g_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}=g,\qquad(f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}=f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},

and

\displaystyle|\langle f-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},g\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\langle f-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},g_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}\rangle|=|\langle f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},g\rangle|\leqslant N^{1/2}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}.

Consequently,

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}^{\prime}>{\varepsilon}N^{1/2}/4.

A further application of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality yields

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}^{\prime}>({\varepsilon}/4)^{2}N.

By Lemma
2.1
,

\langle f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\rangle=\langle f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-(f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}},f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\rangle=\langle f-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\rangle=0.

Hence, by Pythagoras’s theorem,

\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}^{\prime}>({\varepsilon}/4)^{2}N+\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\|f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},

so we have (
2.5
) with
f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\alpha}^{\prime}}
in place of
f_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}
and
d+1
in place of
d
, and we proceed to stage
d+1
.

The left hand side of (
2.5
) is bounded above by
N
, so our procedure must terminate at some stage
d\leqslant(4/{\varepsilon})^{2}
with a projection satisfying (
2.4
).
∎

The argument underpinning Lemma
2.3
tolerates initialising with a projection more refined than the function
f_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{[N]}f
. This is useful in proving stronger regularity lemmas, as we will soon see.

Lemma 2.4

(Refined weak regularity)
.

Let
\Phi
be a closed subset of
\mathbb{T}^{N}
, and let
\phi\in\Phi^{d}
. Let
{\varepsilon}>0
, and let
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
be a
1
-bounded function. Then there exist
d^{\prime}\leqslant(4/{\varepsilon})^{2}
and
\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi^{d^{\prime}}
such that

(2.6)

\|f-f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant{\varepsilon}N.

Proof.

We carry out the energy increment process used to prove Lemma
2.3
, initialising with
f_{\phi}
instead of
f_{0}
. At each stage, we query whether or not (
2.6
) holds. If not, then we increase the quantity

(2.7)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}

by
({\varepsilon}/4)^{2}N
. Since (
2.7
) is bounded above by
N
, this procedure must terminate within
d^{\prime}\leqslant(4/{\varepsilon})^{2}
many steps.
∎

This refinement of the weak regularity lemma is amenable to iteration, leading to a ‘full’ regularity lemma.

Lemma 2.5

(Full regularity)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
and
{\varepsilon}>0
. Let
\Phi
be a closed subset of
\mathbb{T}^{N}
containing zero, and let
f:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
be
1
-bounded. Then there exist

1\leqslant d,d^{\prime}\ll_{\mathcal{F},{\varepsilon}}1,\qquad\phi\in\Phi^{d},\qquad\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi^{d^{\prime}}

such that

\|f-f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant N/\mathcal{F}(d),\qquad\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}\|_{2}\leqslant{\varepsilon}\sqrt{N}.

Proof.

We apply Lemma
2.3
, furnishing
d_{1}\leqslant 16\mathcal{F}(0)^{2}
and
\phi\in\Phi^{d_{1}}
such that

\|f-f_{\phi}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant N/\mathcal{F}(0).

With
d_{2}=16\mathcal{F}(d_{1})^{2}
, Lemma
2.4
gives
\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi^{d_{2}}
such that

\|f-f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant N/\mathcal{F}(d_{1}).

Iterating this procedure, at stage
j
we obtain

d_{j}=16\mathcal{F}(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1})^{2},\qquad\phi\in\Phi^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1}},\qquad\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi^{d_{j}}

such that

\|f-f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}\|_{\Phi}\leqslant N/\mathcal{F}(d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j-1}).

By Lemma
2.1
and Pythagoras’s theorem, the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j}}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j}}}(\|f_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}\|_{2}^{2})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}

is non-decreasing at each stage of the iteration. As
N
is an upper bound for this quantity, it follows that at some stage
j\ll_{{\varepsilon}}1
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d_{1}+\cdots+d_{j}}}\|f_{\phi,\phi^{\prime}}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}}-f_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{\prime}\leqslant{\varepsilon}^{2}N.

The result now follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
∎

2.3.
Structure of the projection

We write
{\Delta}
for the symmetric difference operator on pairs of sets.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
0<{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1/2
and
{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Then

\meas_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}{\Delta}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))\leqslant 2{\varepsilon}^{d-1}\sum_{j\in[d]}\|{\gamma}_{j}\|_{\mathbb{T}}.

Proof.

Let us represent each
{\gamma}_{j}\in\mathbb{T}
by a real number
{\gamma}_{j}
with
|{\gamma}_{j}|\leqslant 1/2
. As
0<{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1/2
,

\meas_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}{\Delta}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))=\meas_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}{\Delta}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})).

Next, we bound
\meas_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}\cap([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))
from below. This intersection is a box whose
j^{\mathrm{th}}
side has length
({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{j}|)_{+}
for each
j
, with the notation
x_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
. Consequently,

\meas_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}\cap([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))=({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{1}|)_{+}\cdots({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{d}|)_{+}.

We now induct on
d
to show that this is bounded below by
{\varepsilon}^{d}-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[d]}|{\gamma}_{j}|
. This is clear for
d=1
. For
d\geqslant 2
, we compute using the inductive hypothesis that

\displaystyle({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{1}|)_{+}\cdots({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{d}|)_{+}

\displaystyle\geqslant({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{1}|)_{+}\cdots({\varepsilon}-|{\gamma}_{d-1}|)_{+}{\varepsilon}-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}|{\gamma}_{d}|

\displaystyle\geqslant{\varepsilon}^{d}-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}\sum_{j\in[d-1]}|{\gamma}_{j}|-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}|{\gamma}_{d}|

\displaystyle={\varepsilon}^{d}-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}\sum_{j\in[d]}|{\gamma}_{j}|.

The upshot is that

\meas_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}\cap([0,{\varepsilon})^{d}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))\geqslant{\varepsilon}^{d}-{\varepsilon}^{d-1}\sum_{j\in[d]}|{\gamma}_{j}|.

The conclusion now follows from the inclusion-exclusion formula for the symmetric difference.
∎

Given
M>0
, a function
F:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is
M
-Lipschitz
if

|F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})-F({\boldsymbol{\beta}})|\leqslant M\sum_{j\in[d]}\|{\alpha}_{j}-{\beta}_{j}\|_{\mathbb{T}}\qquad({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Lemma 2.7

(Lipschitz structure)
.

Let
\phi:[N]\to\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and let
f:[N]\to\mathbb{C}
be
1
-bounded. Then there exists a
1
-bounded and
4
-Lipschitz function
F:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f_{\phi}=F\circ\phi
, and if
f\geqslant 0
then
F\geqslant 0
.

In the proof, we use the convolution operator given by

G_{1}*G_{2}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}G_{1}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})G_{2}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}.

Proof.

For
{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}
and
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, define

F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}_{m\in B_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})}f(m),&\text{if }B_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})\neq\emptyset,\\
0,&\text{if }B_{\phi}^{{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})=\emptyset.\end{cases}

Then

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\left(1_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}/2+[0,1/2)^{d}}*F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\right)(\phi(n))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}1_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}/2+[0,1/2)^{d}}(\phi(n)-{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}1_{n\in B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}({\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}})}F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{m\in B_{\phi}^{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}(n)}f(m)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}=f_{\phi}(n).

We define the
1
-bounded function

F({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\left(1_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}/2+[0,1/2)^{d}}*F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\right)({\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\left(1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}*F_{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}\right)({\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{{\varepsilon}}}/2),

noting that
f_{\phi}=F\circ\phi
.

To show that
F
is
4
-Lipschitz, it suffices to prove that if
G:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
is
1
-bounded and measurable then
1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}*G
is
2^{2-d}
-Lipschitz. This follows from the triangle inequality, a change of variables, and Lemma
2.6
. Indeed, if
{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
then

\displaystyle|1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}*G({\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})-1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}*G({\boldsymbol{\beta}})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}+{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})-1_{[0,1/2)^{d}}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})|\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle=\meas_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}([0,1/2)^{d}\Delta([0,1/2)^{d}-{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}))\leqslant 2^{2-d}\sum_{j\in[d]}\|{\gamma}_{j}\|_{\mathbb{T}}.

∎

Next, we go from a bound on the Lipschitz constant to a good trigonometric approximation. A
trigonometric polynomial
is a function
\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
given by a finite linear combination of exponential functions
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\mapsto e(\mathbf{m}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})
, where
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. By orthogonality, this has the form

F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{F}(\mathbf{m})e(\mathbf{m}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}),

where

\hat{F}(\mathbf{m})\coloneq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})e(-\mathbf{m}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}

is only non-zero for finitely many
\mathbf{m}
. The
degree
of
F
is then

\max\{|m_{1}|+\cdots+|m_{d}|:\hat{F}(\mathbf{m})\neq 0\}.

Lemma 2.8

(Trigonometric approximation)
.

Let
K\geqslant 1
and
0<{\varepsilon}<1/2
, and let
F:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
be a
K
-Lipschitz function. Then there exists a
1
-bounded trigonometric polynomial
F_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
of degree
O(K^{2}d^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-3})
such that
\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant{\varepsilon}
, and if
0\leqslant F\leqslant 1
then
0\leqslant F_{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

For
M\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the Fejér kernel

\displaystyle\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{M}({\alpha}_{1})\cdots\mu_{M}({\alpha}_{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1-|m_{1}|/M)_{+}\cdots(1-|m_{d}|/M)_{+}e(\mathbf{m}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}),

where

\mu_{M}({\alpha})=M^{-1}\left(\frac{\sin M\pi{\alpha}}{\sin\pi{\alpha}}\right)^{2}.

This has the following properties:

\mu_{M}\geqslant 0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\mu_{M}=1,

and

(2.8)

\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})\leqslant 4M^{-1}\|{\alpha}_{j}\|^{-2}\prod_{i\neq j}\mu_{M}({\alpha}_{i})\qquad(1\leqslant j\leqslant d).

For
M=\lceil 1000(Kd)^{2}{\varepsilon}^{-3}\rceil
, we choose

F_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=F*\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

By orthogonality,

F_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1-|m_{1}|/M)_{+}\cdots(1-|m_{d}|/M)_{+}\hat{F}(\mathbf{m})e(\mathbf{m}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})

is a trigonometric polynomial of degree at most
M
. Moreover, the definition of
F_{\varepsilon}
as a convolution ensures that if
0\leqslant F\leqslant 1
then
0\leqslant F_{\varepsilon}\leqslant 1
.

Finally, we bound

F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})-F_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})-F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}))\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Let
\eta>0
be a threshold given by
\eta^{-3}=KdM
.
As
F
is
K
-Lipschitz,

\Big|\int_{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\eta}(F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})-F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}))\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big|\leqslant Kd\eta=(Kd)^{2/3}M^{-1/3}.

By (
2.8
),

\Big|\int_{\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{\infty}>\eta}(F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}})-F({\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}))\mu_{M}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Big|\leqslant 4/(M\eta^{2})=4(Kd)^{2/3}M^{-1/3}.

Therefore

\|F-F_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 5(Kd)^{2/3}M^{-1/3}\leqslant{\varepsilon}.

∎

2.4.
Proof of arithmetic regularity for polynomial phases

In this subsection, we finally prove Lemma
1.4
. Given
M,N\geqslant 1
, a vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
is
(M,N)
-rational
if there exists
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

\|\mathbf{q}\|_{1}\leqslant M,\qquad\|\mathbf{q}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\|\leqslant M/N,

(M,N)
-irrational
.
The following decomposition is similar to
[
51

.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{T}^{d_{j}}

where, for some

and all

(1)

with

(2)

\mathbf{a}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d_{j}}

(3)

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(j)}\|_{1}\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}M_{0}\cdots M_{i}

(4)

-irrational with

for some

(5)

is a

by
M_{0}\cdots M_{n-1}

Proof.

The inductive base case

for any ring

. Next, let
D\geqslant 1

D-1

If
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(j)}
is
(M_{0},N_{j})

Thus, we may assume instead that there exist

The following observation is our final preparatory step for the proof of Lemma

.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
{\varepsilon},{\delta}>0
. Let
f:[N]\to[-1,1]
and
g:[N]\to[0,\infty)
with

\sum_{n\in[N]}f(n)\geqslant{\delta}N,\qquad\sum_{n\in[N]}g(n)\leqslant{\varepsilon}N.

Then, for any partition
[N]=\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{C}_{r}
, there exists
i\in[r]
such that

|\mathcal{C}_{i}|\geqslant{\delta}N/(3r),\qquad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}f(n)\geqslant{\delta}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|/3,\qquad\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}g(n)\leqslant 3{\delta}^{-1}{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|.

Proof.

Observe that

\sum_{i\in[r]}\Bigl(\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}f(n)\Bigr)-\frac{{\delta}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{3}-\frac{{\delta}}{3{\varepsilon}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}g(n)\Bigr)\geqslant\frac{{\delta}}{3}N.

Thus, by the greedy algorithm, there exists
i\in[r]
such that

\Bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}f(n)\Bigr)-\frac{{\delta}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{3}-\frac{{\delta}}{3{\varepsilon}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}g(n)\geqslant\frac{{\delta}}{3r}N.

Now

|\mathcal{C}_{i}|\geqslant\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}f(n)\geqslant\frac{{\delta}|\mathcal{C}_{i}|}{3}+\frac{{\delta}}{3{\varepsilon}}\sum_{n\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}g(n)+\frac{{\delta}}{3r}N.

∎

Proof of Lemma
1.4
.

Let
c=c(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
be a sufficiently small, positive constant, and put
\eta=c{\varepsilon}{\delta}
.
By replacing
\mathcal{F}(x)
by
\max\{\mathcal{F}(y):1\leqslant y\leqslant x\}
, we may assume that
\mathcal{F}
is non-decreasing and defined on
[1,\infty)
.
Define, for
x\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}(\eta^{-4}x^{2}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\Bigl(\eta^{-4}x^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\mathcal{F}_{0}(x)\cdots\mathcal{F}_{i}(x)\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\qquad(1\leqslant j\leqslant Jx),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}_{Jx}(x).

We apply Lemma
2.5
to the function
g\coloneq 1_{\mathcal{A}}
, with
\Phi=\mathbb{T}\textnormal{-span}(\boldsymbol{\Psi})
, growth function
\mathcal{F}_{*}
and approximation parameter
\eta>0
. This yields positive integers
d,\tilde{d}\ll_{\mathcal{F},{\varepsilon},{\delta},\boldsymbol{\Psi}}1
, as well as

{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}

and

\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{T}^{\tilde{d}}

such that

\phi\coloneq{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(1)}\Psi_{1}+\cdots+{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(J)}\Psi_{J},\qquad\tilde{\phi}\coloneq\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(1)}\Psi_{1}+\cdots+\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}^{(J)}\Psi_{J}

satisfy

3.
Quantitative weak approximation

Let

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\leqslant s}a_{i}x_{i}^{k},\qquad\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\leqslant s}b_{i}x_{i}.

We may assume that
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are primitive, i.e.

\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})=\gcd(b_{1},\ldots,b_{s})=1.

For arithmetic functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{s},g
supported on
[N]
, define

T(\mathbf{g})=\sum_{\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x})=0}\>\prod_{i\leqslant s}g_{i}(x_{i}),\qquad T(g)=T(g,\ldots,g),

as well as

\tilde{g}({\alpha},{\beta})=\sum_{n\leqslant N}g(n)e({\alpha}n^{k}+{\beta}n)\qquad({\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{T})

and

\|g\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}=\sup_{{\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{T}}|\tilde{g}({\alpha},{\beta})|.

Additionally, write

\tilde{\mathbf{g}}({\alpha},{\beta})=\prod_{i\leqslant s}\tilde{g_{i}}(a_{i}{\alpha},b_{i}{\beta})\qquad({\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{T})

and

\|\mathbf{g}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}=\sup_{{\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{T}}|\tilde{\mathbf{g}}({\alpha},{\beta})|.

Observe that

T(\mathbf{g})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\tilde{\mathbf{g}}({\alpha},{\beta})\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}.

Let
\mathcal{P}=(L,R]\cap(u+m\mathbb{Z})
be an arithmetic progression in
[N]
, where

(3.1)

1\leqslant u\leqslant m\leqslant M,\quad L\geqslant u,\quad M\geqslant M_{0}(\mathfrak{F},\mathfrak{L}),\quad|\mathcal{P}|\geqslant N/M,\quad N\geqslant N_{0}(M),

and put
g=\gcd(m,u)
. In this section, we show that

(3.2)

T(1_{\mathcal{P}})\gg\frac{g^{k-1}\#\mathcal{P}^{s-2}}{R^{k-1}}

in Case
A
and

(3.3)

T(1_{\mathcal{P}})\gg\frac{g^{k-2}\#\mathcal{P}^{s-3}}{R^{k-2}}

in Case
B
.

Remark 3.1

(Adjusted naive heuristic)
.

Let us view
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{P}^{s}
as a random variable. Put

\mathbf{u}=(u,\ldots,u),\qquad\mathbf{u}+m\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{x}=(R-y_{1},\ldots,R-y_{s}),

and note from (
1.3
) that
\mathfrak{F}(1,\ldots,1)=\mathfrak{L}(1,\ldots,1)=0
. As

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{u}+m\mathbf{z})\equiv 0\>\mathrm{mod}\>g^{k-1}m

and

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{F}(R-y_{1},\ldots,R-y_{s})\ll R^{k-1}(R-L),

the probability that
\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=0
is roughly

\frac{g^{k-1}m}{R^{k-1}(R-L)},

and similarly the probability that
\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x})=0
is
m/(R-L)
.

There is an extra congruence in Case
B
:

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{u}+m\mathbf{z})\equiv ku^{k-1}L(\mathbf{u}+m\mathbf{z})\>\mathrm{mod}\>g^{k-2}m^{2}.

There is also an extra archimedean factor in Case
B
, for if
\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x})=0
then

\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{F}(R-y_{1},\ldots,R-y_{s})\ll R^{k-2}(R-L)^{2}.

Thus, we expect roughly

\frac{g^{k-1}m^{2}\#\mathcal{P}^{s}}{R^{k-1}(R-L)^{2}}\sim\frac{g^{k-1}\#\mathcal{P}^{s-2}}{R^{k-1}}

many points in
\mathcal{V}(P)
in Case
A
, and roughly

\frac{g^{k-2}\#\mathcal{P}^{s-3}}{R^{k-2}}

many points in Case
B
.

By homogeneity, we may assume that
g=1
. We proceed via the circle method
[
53
]
. For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{T}
, let

(3.4)

f(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{n\in\mathcal{P}}e(\alpha n^{k}+\beta n).

we abbreviate

(3.5)

f_{i}(\alpha,\beta)=f(a_{i}\alpha,b_{i}\beta)\quad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s),\qquad F(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{i\leqslant s}f_{i}(\alpha,\beta).

By orthogonality, we have

(3.6)

T(1_{\mathcal{P}})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}F(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta.

Let us fix a small constant
\Delta>0
. For
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
a,b,\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote by
\mathfrak{M}(q;a,b)
the set of
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
such that

(3.7)

\lvert q\alpha-a\rvert<N^{\Delta-k},\qquad\lvert q\beta-b\rvert<N^{\Delta-1}.

These are called
major arcs
, and we denote by
\mathfrak{M}=\mathfrak{M}^{(\Delta)}
their union over

(3.8)

q<N^{\Delta},\qquad\gcd(q,a,b)=1.

The
minor arcs
are given by
\mathfrak{m}=\mathbb{T}^{2}\setminus\mathfrak{M}
.

The major arcs are pairwise disjoint. To see this, suppose that

(3.9)

\mathfrak{M}(q_{1};A_{1},b_{1})\cap\mathfrak{M}(q_{2};A_{2},b_{2})

is non-empty for some
q_{1},A_{1},B_{1},q_{2},A_{2},B_{2}
satisfying

(3.10)

q_{j}<N^{\Delta},\quad\gcd(q_{j},A_{j},B_{j})=1\qquad(j=1,2).

By the triangle inequality

(3.11)

\lvert q_{2}A_{1}-q_{1}A_{2}\rvert<2N^{2\Delta-k}<1,\qquad\lvert q_{2}B_{1}-q_{1}B_{2}\rvert<2N^{2\Delta-1}<1,

so

(3.12)

q_{2}A_{1}=q_{1}A_{2},\qquad q_{2}B_{1}=q_{1}B_{2},

which means that
(q_{1},A_{1},B_{1})
and
(q_{2},A_{2},B_{2})
are linearly dependent. Coprimality then forces
(q_{1},A_{1},B_{1})
and
(q_{2},A_{2},B_{2})
to coincide, and we conclude that the major arcs are indeed disjoint.

In the sequel, let
{\varepsilon}
be a small, positive constant, whose value is allowed to differ between instances. The implied constants in our asymptotic notations are allowed to depend on
{\varepsilon}
.

3.1.

Lemma 3.2
.

If

and

then

(3.13)

Proof.

Observe that

for the polynomial

given by

(3.14)

where

Suppose

\lvert f_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert>2N^{1+\varepsilon-\Delta/k}.

Then for some

[
1

applied with

\lvert rb_{i}m^{k}\beta-(u_{1}m^{k-1}-ku_{k}u^{k-1})\rvert<\frac{2m^{k-1}N^{\Delta-1}}{\log N}.

Set

Then (

Lemma 3.3

\displaystyle x_{1}^{k}+\dots+x_{t}^{k}

\displaystyle x_{1}+\dots+x_{t}

The case

\displaystyle v

\gcd(q,a,b)=1\end{subarray}}S(q;a,b).

Applying (

now gives

\displaystyle\sum_{q\geqslant 1}\lvert S(q)\rvert\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\lvert q\kappa\rvert\geqslant N^{\Delta-1}}\lvert V(\tau,\kappa)\rvert\mathrm{d}\kappa\mathrm{d}\tau

\displaystyle\ll N^{-k}N^{s-1-\Delta\varepsilon}M^{s}\sum_{q\geqslant 1}q^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}

(3.63)

\displaystyle\ll N^{s-k-1-\Delta\varepsilon}M^{s}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{s-2}/R^{k-1}),

since

. Choosing

instead, we similarly compute

\displaystyle\ll N^{-1}N^{s-k-(\Delta+k-2)\varepsilon}M^{s}

\displaystyle=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{s-2}/R^{k-1}).

\displaystyle\ll N^{s-k-1}M^{s}\sum_{q\geqslant N^{\Delta}}q^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\ll N^{s-k-1}M^{s}N^{\Delta(3-s/k+\varepsilon)}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{s-2}/R^{k-1}).

\displaystyle 0

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}+p^{r}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}+p^{2r}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\prime},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\prime}\in[p^{r}]^{s}
with

(3.142)

\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime})\equiv-p^{-r}\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})\>\mathrm{mod}\>p^{r}.

A short computation shows that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
is a solution to (
3.138
). There are
p^{rs}
many choices for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime\prime}
, and since
p\nmid\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{s})
, there are
p^{r(s-1)}
many choices for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{\prime}
, yielding the lower bound (
3.139
).

We now
estimate
M^{\prime}(p^{r})
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
be a smooth point where, similarly to before, we assume that
p
does not divide
y_{s-1}-y_{s}
.
Write

(3.143)

J=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{s-1}\mathfrak{Q}&\partial_{s}\mathfrak{Q}\\
\partial_{s-1}\mathfrak{L}&\partial_{s}\mathfrak{L}\end{pmatrix},

and define

(3.144)

\delta=\nu_{p}(\det(J(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}})))=\nu_{p}(2a_{s-1}a_{s}(y_{s-1}-y_{s}))=\nu_{p}(2a_{s-1}a_{s}).

Choose
x_{1},\dots,x_{s-2}\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z}
such that

(3.145)

x_{i}\equiv y_{i}\>\mathrm{mod}\>p^{2\delta+1}.

Hensel’s lemma
[
26
, Proposition 5.20]
furnishes
(x_{s-1},x_{s})\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{r}\mathbb{Z})^{2}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{s})
is a solution to (
3.140
).
We deduce that

(3.146)

M^{\prime}(p^{r})\geqslant p^{(r-2\delta-1)(s-2)}.

Since
\delta
can be bounded above uniformly in
p
, we may choose a constant
C
satisfying
C>2(2\delta+1)(s-2)
. Then the combination of (
3.146
) and (
3.139
) gives

(3.147)

M(p^{3r})\geqslant p^{3r(s-1)-C/2},

and by Lemma
3.5
,

(3.148)

\sum_{t=0}^{3r}S(p^{t})\geqslant p^{3r-C/2}.

We next consider the sum over
t>3r
.
From (
3.57
), for
i\in[s]
and
p\nmid\gcd(a,b)
we have

(3.149)

\displaystyle p^{-t}S_{i}(p^{t};a,b))

\displaystyle\ll p^{-t/k}\gcd\bigl(p^{t},\,p^{2r}a_{i}ak(k-1)/2,\,p^{r}a_{i}(ka+b)\bigr)^{1/k}

(3.150)

\displaystyle\ll p^{(r-t)/k}\gcd(p^{r},\,ka+b)^{1/k},

where the implicit constant is uniform in
C
. Therefore,

(3.151)

S(p^{t})\ll p^{(r-t)s/k}\sum_{a,b\leqslant p^{t}}\gcd(p^{r},\,ka+b)^{s/k}.

Splitting the sum according to the
p
-adic order of
ka+b
, we get

(3.152)

\displaystyle S(p^{t})

\displaystyle\ll p^{(r-t)s/k}\sum_{v=0}^{r}p^{2t-v}p^{vs/k}

(3.153)

\displaystyle\ll p^{(r-t)s/k}p^{2t-r}p^{rs/k}=p^{3r+(t-2r)(2-s/k)},

and so

(3.154)

\sum_{t>3r}S(p^{t})\ll p^{3r+r(2-s/k)}<p^{3r-3C/2}.

Recall that the implicit constant is uniform in
C
, and thus by choosing
C
large enough, we finally conclude

(3.155)

\Big\lvert\sum_{t>3r}S(p^{t})\Big\rvert<p^{3r-C}.

Combined with (
3.148
), this yields

(3.156)

\chi_{p}\gg p^{3r}

in this case.

Case B2:

p>C
.
We again have (
3.121
). Suppose
r<t\leqslant 2r
. Then

(3.157)

S(p^{t};a,b)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in[p^{t}]^{s}}e_{p^{t}}\Bigl(\sum_{i\leqslant s}a_{i}p^{r}(ka+b)x_{i}\Bigr)=\prod_{i\leqslant s}\sum_{x\leqslant p^{t}}e_{p^{t}}(a_{i}p^{r}(ka+b)x).

As
p\nmid\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{s})
, we have

(3.158)

p^{-ts}S(p^{t};a,b)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }p^{t-r}\mid(ka+b),\\
0,&\text{if }p^{t-r}\nmid(ka+b).\end{cases}

We conclude that

(3.159)

S(p^{t})=(p^{t}-p^{t-1})p^{r}

and hence

(3.160)

\sum_{t=0}^{2r}S(p^{t})=p^{3r}.

We now consider
t>2r
. We again have (
3.153
), hence

(3.161)

\sum_{t>2r}S(p^{t})\ll p^{3r+2-s/k}\leqslant p^{3r-3/2},

and so

(3.162)

\chi_{p}=p^{3r}(1+O(p^{-3/2})).

Since
\prod_{p>C}(1+O(p^{-3/2}))\gg 1
, the combination of both cases furnishes (
3.105
) in Case
B
.

4.
An auxiliary system

We retain the notation from the previous section, and denote by
T_{2}
the number of tuples

(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{s},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{s})\in\mathcal{P}^{2s-1}

such that
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
, where

(4.1)

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s}),\qquad\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{s}).

In this section, we show that

(4.2)

T_{2}\ll\frac{g^{2k-2}\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}}{R^{2k-2}}

in Case
A
, and

(4.3)

T_{2}\ll\frac{g^{2k-4}\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-7}}{R^{2k-4}}

in Case
B
. In particular, if we couple this with the outcome of the previous section then we obtain

(4.4)

T_{2}\ll\#\mathcal{P}^{-1}T(1_{\mathcal{P}})^{2}.

By homogeneity, we may assume that
g=1
. For
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}=(\alpha,\beta,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{T}^{4}
, define

(4.5)

g_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=f_{1}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime}),\qquad g_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=f_{i}(\alpha,\beta)f_{i}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\quad(2\leqslant i\leqslant s),

and write
G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=\prod_{i\leqslant s}g_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})
. By orthogonality, we have

(4.6)

T_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{4}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}.

Define

(4.7)

\mathfrak{N}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{T}^{4}:(\alpha,\beta),(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{M}\},\qquad\mathfrak{n}=\mathbb{T}^{4}\setminus\mathfrak{N}.

4.1.
Minor arcs

Recall from (
3.35
) that

(4.8)

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\lvert f_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert^{s}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta\ll N^{s-k-1-\frac{\Delta}{2k}}\qquad(2\leqslant i\leqslant s),

and by Lemma
3.3
,

(4.9)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert f_{1}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime})\rvert^{s}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta\ll N^{s-k-1+\varepsilon}\qquad(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{T}^{2}.

Now by Hölder’s inequality,

(4.10)

\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\lvert f_{1}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime})f_{2}(\alpha,\beta)\cdots f_{s}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta\ll N^{s-k-1-\frac{\Delta}{3k}}

for
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
.
Hölder’s inequality and Lemma
3.3
again now yield

(4.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\int_{\mathfrak{m}}\lvert G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\rvert\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}

\displaystyle\ll N^{s-k-1-\frac{\Delta}{3k}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert f_{2}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\cdots f_{s}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\rvert\mathrm{d}{\alpha^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}{\beta^{\prime}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-2k-3-\frac{\Delta}{4k}}.

By symmetry, we thus have

(4.13)

\int_{\mathfrak{n}}\lvert G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\rvert\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\ll N^{2s-2k-3-\frac{\Delta}{4k}}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}),

whence

(4.14)

T_{2}=\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

4.2.
Major arcs

Suppose that
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathfrak{M}(q;a,b)
and
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{M}(q^{\prime};a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, where

(4.15)

\displaystyle 1\leqslant a,b\leqslant q<N^{\Delta},\qquad 1\leqslant a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\leqslant q^{\prime}<N^{\Delta},

(4.16)

\displaystyle\gcd(q,a,b)=\gcd(q^{\prime},a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1.

By the triangle inequality, we have

(4.17)

\lvert q^{\prime\prime}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime})-\alpha^{\prime\prime}\rvert<N^{3\Delta-k},\qquad\lvert q^{\prime\prime}(\beta+\beta^{\prime})-\beta^{\prime\prime}\rvert<N^{3\Delta-1},

where

(4.18)

q^{\prime\prime}=qq^{\prime},\qquad a^{\prime\prime}=q^{\prime}a+qa^{\prime},\qquad b^{\prime\prime}=q^{\prime}b+qb^{\prime}.

Consequently
(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime})\in\mathfrak{M}^{(3\Delta)}
, so we may define

(4.19)

g_{i}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=f_{i}^{*}(\alpha,\beta)f_{i}^{*}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})\quad(2\leqslant i\leqslant s),\qquad g^{*}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=f_{1}^{*}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime}),

and
G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=\prod_{i\leqslant s}g_{i}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})
.
Using (
3.42
) and the telescoping identity (
3.43
), as in (
3.44
), we get

(4.20)

G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})-G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\ll(q^{\prime\prime})^{1+\varepsilon}N^{2s-2}\ll N^{2s-2+3\Delta}

for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}\in\mathfrak{N}
. Now

(4.21)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}-\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}\ll N^{2s-2+3\Delta}N^{6\Delta-2k-2}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

Together with (
4.14
), this gives

(4.22)

T_{2}=\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

Define

(4.23)

S^{*}(q,q^{\prime};a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=S_{1}(q^{\prime\prime};a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\prod_{i=2}^{s}S_{i}(q;a,b)S_{i}(q^{\prime};a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

and

(4.24)

S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})=(qq^{\prime})^{-s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\leqslant q\\
\gcd(q,a,b)=1\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\leqslant q^{\prime}\\
\gcd(q^{\prime},a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=1\end{subarray}}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime};a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}).

Write
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}=(\tau,\kappa,\tau^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime})
, where

(4.25)

\tau=\alpha-a/q,\qquad\kappa=\beta-b/q,\qquad\tau^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}-a^{\prime}/q^{\prime},\qquad\kappa^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime}-b^{\prime}/q^{\prime}.

Periodicity gives

(4.26)

f_{1}^{*}(\alpha+\alpha^{\prime},\beta+\beta^{\prime})=(q^{\prime\prime})^{-1}S_{1}(q^{\prime\prime};a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})v_{1}(\tau+\tau^{\prime},\kappa+\kappa^{\prime}),

wherein we recall (
3.49
). Therefore

(4.27)

G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})=(qq^{\prime})^{-s}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime};a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime})V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}),

where

(4.28)

V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})=v_{1}(\tau+\tau^{\prime},\kappa+\kappa^{\prime})\prod_{i=2}^{s}v_{i}(\tau,\kappa)v_{i}(\tau^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}).

We now bound
S^{*}(q,q^{\prime};a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
. By (
3.54
) and (
3.55
),

(4.29)

\displaystyle S_{1}(q^{\prime\prime};a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\ll\gcd(q^{\prime\prime},m^{k}a_{1}a^{\prime\prime},m^{2}b_{1}b^{\prime\prime})^{1/k}(qq^{\prime})^{1-1/k+\varepsilon}

(4.30)

\displaystyle\ll m\gcd(q^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})^{1/k}(qq^{\prime})^{1-1/k+\varepsilon}.

Moreover,

(4.31)

\displaystyle\gcd(q^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\gcd(qq^{\prime},q^{\prime}a+qa^{\prime},q^{\prime}b+qb^{\prime})

(4.32)

\displaystyle\leqslant\gcd(q,q^{\prime}a,q^{\prime}b)\gcd(q^{\prime},qa^{\prime},qb^{\prime})=\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2},

and therefore

(4.33)

S_{1}(q^{\prime\prime};a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\ll m\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{1-1/k+\varepsilon}.

Now by (
3.56
),

(4.34)

(qq^{\prime})^{-s}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime};a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\ll m^{2s-1}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{-s/k+\varepsilon}.

It follows from
[
53
, Theorem 7.3]
that

(4.35)

V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\ll(R/m)^{2s-1}(1+R\lvert\kappa\rvert+R^{k}\lvert\tau\rvert)^{(1-s)/k}(1+R\lvert\kappa^{\prime}\rvert+R^{k}\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert)^{(1-s)/k}.

Hence

(4.36)

G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\ll N^{2s-1}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{-s/k+\varepsilon}\\
\cdot(1+N\lvert\kappa\rvert)^{-\eta}(1+N\lvert\kappa^{\prime}\rvert)^{-\eta}(1+N^{k}\lvert\tau\rvert)^{(1-s)/k+\eta}(1+N^{k}\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert)^{(1-s)/k+\eta},

whenever
1<\eta<(s-1)/k-1
. Therefore,

(4.37)

S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\ll N^{2s-1}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}\\
\cdot(1+N\lvert\kappa\rvert)^{-\eta}(1+N\lvert\kappa^{\prime}\rvert)^{-\eta}(1+N^{k}\lvert\tau\rvert)^{(1-s)/k+\eta}(1+N^{k}\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert)^{(1-s)/k+\eta}.

As the major arcs are disjoint,

(4.38)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}<N^{\Delta}}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lvert q\tau\rvert,\lvert q^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}\rvert<N^{\Delta-k}\\
\lvert q\kappa\rvert,\lvert q^{\prime}\kappa^{\prime}\rvert<N^{\Delta-1}\end{subarray}}V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}.

Choosing
\eta=1+\varepsilon
, we compute that

(4.39)

\displaystyle\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\lvert S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\rvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{N^{\Delta-1}/q}^{\infty}\lvert V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\rvert\mathrm{d}{\kappa}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}{\tau^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}{\kappa^{\prime}}

(4.40)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-1}N^{-2k-1}N^{-1-\varepsilon\Delta}\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}

(4.41)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-2k-3-\varepsilon\Delta}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}),

where we bound the last sum by factoring out
d=\gcd(q,q^{\prime})
:

(4.42)

\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}q^{\varepsilon}\ll\sum_{d\geqslant 1}d^{4-2(s-1)/k+2\varepsilon}\Bigl(\sum_{q\geqslant 1}q^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}\Bigr)^{2}\ll 1,

since
s\geqslant 3k+1
.
Choosing
\eta=(s-3)/2+\varepsilon
, we similarly compute that

(4.43)

\displaystyle\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\lvert S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\rvert\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{N^{\Delta-k}/q}^{\infty}\lvert V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\rvert\mathrm{d}{\tau}\mathrm{d}{\tau^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}\kappa\mathrm{d}{\kappa^{\prime}}

(4.44)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-1}N^{-k-2}N^{-k-\varepsilon\Delta}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

Thus by symmetry,

(4.45)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}<N^{\Delta}}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

Moreover, we have

(4.46)

\displaystyle\sum_{q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\sum_{q\geqslant N^{\Delta}}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}

(4.47)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-2k-3}\sum_{q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}\sum_{q\geqslant N^{\Delta}}\gcd(q,q^{\prime})^{2/k}(qq^{\prime})^{2-s/k+\varepsilon}

(4.48)

\displaystyle\ll N^{2s-2k-3}(N^{\Delta})^{3-s/k+\varepsilon}=o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}),

and by symmetry,

(4.49)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

Changing variables yields

(4.50)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}V^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}

(4.51)

\displaystyle=m^{1-2s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\int_{[L,R]^{2s-1}}e(\tau\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\tau^{\prime}\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\kappa\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\kappa^{\prime}\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}{x^{\prime}_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x^{\prime}_{s}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}

(4.52)

\displaystyle=R^{2s-2k-3}m^{1-2s}\mathfrak{J}^{*},

where

(4.53)

\mathfrak{J}^{*}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\int_{B^{*}}e(\tau\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\tau^{\prime}\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\kappa\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\kappa^{\prime}\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}}\mathrm{d}{x^{\prime}_{2}}\cdots\mathrm{d}{x^{\prime}_{s}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}},

and
B^{*}=[L/R,1]^{2s-1}
.
Therefore

(4.54)

\int_{\mathfrak{N}}G^{*}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\alpha}}}=R^{2s-2k-3}m^{1-2s}\mathfrak{S}^{*}\mathfrak{J}^{*}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}),

where

(4.55)

\mathfrak{S}^{*}=\sum_{q,q^{\prime}\geqslant 1}S^{*}(q,q^{\prime}).

Thence,

(4.56)

T_{2}=R^{2s-2k-3}m^{1-2s}\mathfrak{S}^{*}\mathfrak{J}^{*}+o(\#\mathcal{P}^{2s-5}/R^{2k-2}).

4.3.
Singular integral

Here we show that

(4.57)

\mathfrak{J}^{*}\ll\biggl(1-\frac{L}{R}\biggr)^{2s-5}

in Case
A
, and

(4.58)

\mathfrak{J}^{*}\ll\biggl(1-\frac{L}{R}\biggr)^{2s-7}

in Case
B
.

In Case
A
, we follow
[
47
, §11]
and interpret
\mathfrak{J}^{*}
as a real density. Recall the identity

(4.59)

\lambda(y)\coloneq\max\{1-\lvert y\rvert,0\}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e(\alpha y)\sinc^{2}(\pi\alpha)\mathrm{d}\alpha,

from which we obtain

(4.60)

\lambda_{T}(y)\coloneq T\lambda(Ty)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e(\tau y)\sinc^{2}(\pi\tau/T)\mathrm{d}\tau

for
T\geqslant 1
. Writing
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{*}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},x^{\prime}_{2},\dots,x^{\prime}_{s})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=(x_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\dots,x^{\prime}_{s})
, we thus have

(4.61)

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{T}

\displaystyle\coloneq\int_{B^{*}}\lambda_{T}(\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}))\lambda_{T}(\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))\lambda_{T}(\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}))\lambda_{T}(\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}

(4.62)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\int_{B^{*}}e(\tau\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\tau^{\prime}\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\kappa\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\kappa^{\prime}\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))

(4.63)

\displaystyle\qquad\cdot\sinc^{2}(\pi\tau/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\tau^{\prime}/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\kappa/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\kappa^{\prime}/T)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}}.

Now

(4.64)

\mathfrak{J}^{*}-\mu^{*}_{T}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}},T)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}}},

where

(4.65)

f(\tau)=\int_{B^{*}}e(\tau\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\tau^{\prime}\mathfrak{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})+\kappa\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})+\kappa^{\prime}\mathfrak{L}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}))\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{*}}

and

(4.66)

g(\tau,T)=1-\sinc^{2}(\pi\tau/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\tau^{\prime}/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\kappa/T)\sinc^{2}(\pi\kappa^{\prime}/T).

Note that

(4.67)

f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})=v^{*}_{1}(\tau+\tau^{\prime},\kappa+\kappa^{\prime})\prod_{i=2}^{s}v^{*}_{i}(\tau,\kappa)v^{*}_{i}(\tau^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}),

where

(4.68)

v^{*}_{i}(\tau,\kappa)=\int_{L/R}^{1}e(a_{i}\tau x^{k}+b_{i}\kappa x)\mathrm{d}x\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

It now follows from
[
53
, Theorem 7.3]
that

(4.69)

f(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}})\ll(1+\lvert\tau\rvert+\lvert\kappa\rvert)^{(1-s)/k}(1+\lvert\tau^{\prime}\rvert+\lvert\kappa^{\prime}\rvert)^{(1-s)/k}.

Further, it follows from the Taylor expansion that

(4.70)

\sinc^{2}(y)=1+O(\min\{1,y^{2}\}),

and consequently

(4.71)

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tau}},T)

\displaystyle\ll\min\{1,(\tau/T)^{2}\}+\min\{1,(\tau^{\prime}/T)^{2}\}

(4.72)

\displaystyle\qquad+\min\{1,(\kappa/T)^{2}\}+\min\{1,(\kappa^{\prime}/T)^{2}\}.

Thus, by symmetry, we have
\mathfrak{J}^{*}-\mu^{*}_{T}\ll I_{1}I_{2}(T)
, where

(4.73)

I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(1+\lvert\tau\rvert+\lvert\kappa\rvert)^{(1-s)/k}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}\kappa\ll 1

. By Hölder’s inequality,

\displaystyle{\delta}N^{s-k-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant T(\mathbf{g})=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\tilde{\mathbf{g}}({\alpha},{\beta})|\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathbf{g}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}^{(s-t)/s}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\prod_{i}|\tilde{g_{i}}(a_{i}{\alpha},b_{i}{\beta})|^{t/s}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\mathbf{g}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}^{(s-t)/s}\prod_{i\leqslant s}\Bigl(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\tilde{g_{i}}(a_{i}{\alpha},b_{i}{\beta})|^{t}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}\Bigr)^{1/s}.

First, suppose
k\geqslant 3
. Let
\zeta>0
be small in terms of
k
, then for each
i\in[s]
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(a_{i}\alpha,b_{i}\beta)\rvert^{t}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert^{t}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta

(5.2)

\displaystyle\leqslant N^{(1-\zeta)/2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert^{s_{0}-1}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta+\int_{\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert\geqslant N^{1-\zeta}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert^{t}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta.

Observing that
s_{0}-1
is an even integer, by orthogonality and Lemma
3.3
, we get

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\tilde{g_{i}}({\alpha},{\beta})|^{s_{0}-1}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}\ll N^{s_{0}-k-2+\varepsilon}.

Moreover,
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
implies

(5.3)

\int_{\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert\geqslant N^{1-\zeta}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(\alpha,\beta)\rvert^{t}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta\ll N^{t-k-1},

and thus

(5.4)

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}\lvert\tilde{g}_{i}(a_{i}\alpha,b_{i}\beta)\rvert^{t}\mathrm{d}\alpha\mathrm{d}\beta\ll N^{t-k-1}.

Therefore

{\delta}N^{s-k-1}\ll\|\mathbf{g}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}^{(s-t)/s}N^{t-k-1},

and so

\|\mathbf{g}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}\gg{\delta}^{s/(s-t)}N^{s}\geqslant{\delta}^{2s}N^{s}.

Now suppose instead that
k=2
. By
[
4
, Equation (3.115)]
,

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\tilde{g_{i}}(a_{i}{\alpha},b_{i}{\beta})|^{t}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2}}|\tilde{g_{i}}({\alpha},{\beta})|^{t}\mathrm{d}{\alpha}\mathrm{d}{\beta}\ll N^{t-3}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

The argument now follows as above.
∎

Corollary 5.2

(FL control)
.

If

T(\mathbf{g})\geqslant{\delta}N^{s-k-1},

for some
{\delta}\in(0,1)
, then

\|g_{i}\|_{{\mathrm{FL}}}\gg{\delta}^{2s}N\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

Lemma 5.3

(
\ell^{2}
control)
.

If

are

-bounded, and

, then

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove this for

4.1
),
Cauchy–Schwarz gives

|T(\mathbf{g})|\leqslant\|g_{1}\|_{2}\Bigl(\sum_{x_{1}\in\mathcal{P}}\Bigl(\sum_{\mathfrak{F}(\mathbf{x})=\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf{x})=0}1_{\mathcal{P}}(x_{2})\cdots 1_{\mathcal{P}}(x_{s})\Bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}.

Inserting (

∎

Let
\mathcal{P}
be as in (

for

Lemma 6.1

(Weyl-type bound)
.

If

\Big|\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{P}^{s}}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\leqslant s}({\alpha}_{i}n_{i}^{k}+{\beta}_{i}n_{i})\Bigr)\Big|\geqslant{\delta}N^{s},

then:

(1)

each
{\alpha}_{i}
is
(O({\delta}^{-O(1)}),N^{k})
-rational,

(2)

each
{\beta}_{i}
is
(O({\delta}^{-O(1)}),N)
-rational.

Proof.

By symmetry, it suffices to prove the result for
i=1
. We may assume that
0<{\delta}<1/2
, since otherwise we can argue with
{\delta}/3
in place of
{\delta}
. By the triangle inequality, the assumption implies that

\Big|\sum_{n\in\mathcal{P}}e({\alpha}n^{k}+{\beta}n)\Big|\geqslant{\delta}N,

where
{\alpha}={\alpha}_{1}
and
{\beta}={\beta}_{1}
. Writing

\mathcal{I}=\{y\in\mathbb{Z}:L<u+my\leqslant R\}

yields

\Big|\sum_{y\in\mathcal{I}}e({\alpha}(u+my)^{k}+{\beta}my)\Big|\geqslant{\delta}N.

With

t=\lfloor(L-u)/m\rfloor,\qquad z=y-t,\qquad\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}-t,\qquad v=u-mt,

we now have

\Big|\sum_{z\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}e({\alpha}(v+mz)^{k}+{\beta}mz)\Big|\geqslant{\delta}N.

Now
[
25
, Lemma 4.4]
furnishes the
(O({\delta}^{-O(1)}),|\mathcal{P}|^{k})
-rationality of

m^{k}{\alpha}
, as well as the
(O({\delta}^{-O(1)}),|\mathcal{P}|)
-rationality of
kmu^{k-1}{\alpha}+m{\beta}
. The result now follows from the triangle inequality and the bounds

u\leqslant m,\qquad|\mathcal{P}|\leqslant N/m.

∎

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
A\geqslant 1
. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{{\alpha}}}\in\mathbb{T}
is
(A,N^{k})
-irrational or that

-irrational. Then

(0,0),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

∎

Lemma 6.3

In what follows, we write

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose

Lemma 6.6
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
with
\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})=1
, and let
\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then

a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\qquad(1\leqslant i,j\leqslant s)

holds if and only if there exists
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that

\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=a_{i}\mathbf{m}\qquad(1\leqslant i\leqslant s).

Proof.

Suppose
a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
for all
i
and
j
. Then
\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(s)}
are all proportional to some primitive vector
\mathbf{m}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For each
i
, let
c_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that
\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=c_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(0)}
. Then
a_{j}c_{i}=a_{i}c_{j}
for all
i
and
j
, so
\mathbf{c}={\lambda}\mathbf{a}
for some
{\lambda}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Choosing
\mathbf{m}={\lambda}\mathbf{m}^{(0)}
yields
\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=a_{i}\mathbf{m}
for all
i
.

The converse is clear.
∎

For
d,s\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}

\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)=\{({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(s)})\in(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{s}:a_{1}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(1)}+\cdots+a_{s}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(s)}=\mathbf{0}\}.

This is a compact abelian group that is naturally equipped with a Haar probability measure.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}
be integers such that
\gcd(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})=1
, and let
\mathbf{m}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{m}^{(s)}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then

\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\text{ for all }i,j\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

First suppose
a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}
for all
i,j
. Then, by Lemma
6.6
, there exists
\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\mathbf{m}^{(i)}=a_{i}\mathbf{m}
for all
i
. Consequently,

\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}=\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\mathbf{m}\cdot\sum_{i\in[s]}a_{i}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}=1.

Now suppose instead that there exist
i,j
with
a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\neq a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}
. We claim that there exists
\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)
such that
\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Assuming that such a vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}
exists, translation-invariance of
\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)
and a change of variables give

(6.2)

\displaystyle e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

(6.3)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)})\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}=\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\Bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}},

whereupon
\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}e\bigl(\sum_{i\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\cdot{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)}\bigr)\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}=0
.

It remains to establish the claim. Fixing
i
and

such that
a_{i}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\neq a_{j}\mathbf{m}^{(i)}
, let

be such that
a_{i}m_{j,k}\neq a_{j}m_{i,k}
. Choosing
\tilde{\gamma}_{\ell,m}=0
if

or

, it now suffices to find
\tilde{\gamma}_{i,k}
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{j,k}
such that

\displaystyle a_{i}\tilde{\gamma}_{i,k}+a_{j}\tilde{\gamma}_{j,k}

\displaystyle=0\in\mathbb{T},

\displaystyle m_{i,k}\tilde{\gamma}_{i,k}+m_{j,k}\tilde{\gamma}_{j,k}

\displaystyle\neq 0\in\mathbb{T},

since the first condition ensures that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)
and the second condition ensures that
\sum_{\ell\in[s]}\mathbf{m}^{(\ell)}\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(\ell)}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
. As

\begin{pmatrix}a_{i}&a_{j}\\
m_{i,k}&m_{j,k}\end{pmatrix}

is an invertible real matrix, there exist
{\alpha},{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

a_{i}{\alpha}+a_{j}{\beta}=0,\qquad m_{i,k}{\alpha}+m_{j,k}{\beta}=1/2.

Choosing
\tilde{\gamma}_{i,k}={\alpha}\>\mathrm{mod}\>1
and
\tilde{\gamma}_{j,k}={\beta}\>\mathrm{mod}\>1
delivers the claim.
∎

7.1.
The compact setting

Definition 7.1

Given an

\ker_{G}M

\mu_{\ker_{G}M}

is Borel measurable on

\ker_{G}M
.

We import a deep result from
[
8
]
, which relies on the hypergraph regularity lemma. We will apply it with

and

(7.1)

M=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&\dots&a_{s}&0&\dots&0\\

and is this setting there is a simpler argument using Fourier analysis
[

]
.

Proposition 7.2

(Compact Szemerédi)

Let
M
be an

integer matrix such that the greatest common divisor of the

minors is

. Suppose that the columns of

sum to zero. For any
\delta>0

\eta=\eta(M,\delta)>0
such that for any compact abelian group
G
, if
\mathcal{A}\subseteq G
is measurable with
\mu_{G}(\mathcal{A})\geqslant\delta

\mu_{\ker_{G}M}(\mathcal{A}^{m}\cap\ker_{G}M)\geqslant\eta.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
a_{i},a_{i}^{\prime}
be integers with
a_{1}+\dots+a_{s}=a_{1}^{\prime}+\dots+a_{s}^{\prime}=0

\gcd(a_{1},\dots,a_{s})=\gcd(a_{1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{s}^{\prime})=1
.
For any
\delta>0
, there exists

such that if

is measurable and
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}}f\geqslant\delta

\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d)}\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},d^{\prime})}\prod_{i=1}^{s}f({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\geqslant\eta.

Proof.

We note that the columns of the matrix (
7.1
) sum to zero. Furthermore, the greatest common divisor of the

minors is

\gcd(\{a_{i}a_{j}^{\prime}:1\leqslant i,j\leqslant s\})=1.

Let

\mathcal{A}=\{({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})\in\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}:f({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})\geqslant\delta/2\}.

Then the measure of
\mathcal{A}
is at least
\delta/2
, and applying Proposition
7.2
with
G=\mathbb{T}^{d+d^{\prime}}
gives

\mu_{\ker_{G}M}(\mathcal{A}^{s}\cap\ker_{G}M)\gg_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\delta}1.

The corollary follows on noting that

\int_{\ker_{G}M}\prod_{i=1}^{s}f({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)})\mathrm{d}\mu_{\ker_{G}M}\geqslant(\delta/2)^{s}\mu_{\ker_{G}M}(\mathcal{A}^{s}\cap\ker_{G}M),

and that
\ker_{G}M
is isomorphic to
K(\mathbf{a},d)\times K(\mathbf{a}^{\prime},d^{\prime})
.
∎

7.2.
Putting it all together

We now proceed to prove Theorem
1.1
. Since there are infinitely many diagonal solutions, we may assume that
N\geqslant N_{0}(\mathcal{V},{\delta})
. We may also assume that the quantity
\eta=\eta({\delta})
from Corollary
7.3
is non-decreasing, and that
\eta({\delta})\leqslant{\delta}
. Let
C\geqslant 1
be a large, positive constant. We define the parameter
{\varepsilon}
and the function
\mathcal{F}
by

(7.2)

{\varepsilon}=\frac{\eta({\delta}/C)}{C},\qquad\mathcal{F}(x)=\left(\frac{x}{\eta({\delta}/C)}\right)^{C}\quad(x\geqslant 1).

We apply Lemma
1.5
with these choices of
{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{F}
. This application introduces various data.

By multilinearity,

T(1_{\mathcal{A}})=T(f+f_{\mathrm{unf}})=T(f)+O(|T(\mathbf{g})|),

for some functions
g_{i}\in\{f,f_{\mathrm{unf}}\}
at least one of which is
f_{\mathrm{unf}}
. Thus, by Corollary
5.2
,

(7.3)

T(1_{\mathcal{A}})=T(f)+O(N^{s-k-1}\mathcal{F}(M)^{-{\Omega}(1)}).

For any arithmetic function
g
supported on
[N]
, we write

T_{\mathcal{P}}(g)=T(g1_{\mathcal{P}}).

As
f\geqslant 0
,

T(f)\geqslant T_{\mathcal{P}}(f)=T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{sml}}).

Again by multilinearity, there exist functions
h_{i}\in\{f_{\mathrm{str}},f_{\mathrm{sml}}\}
at least one of which is
f_{\mathrm{sml}}
, such that

T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}}+f_{\mathrm{sml}})=T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}})+O(|T(h_{1}1_{\mathcal{P}},\ldots,h_{s}1_{\mathcal{P}})|).

Now, by Lemma
5.3
,

(7.4)

T(f)\geqslant T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}})-O({\varepsilon}T(1_{\mathcal{P}})).

Lemma
6.8
gives

(7.5)

T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}})=T(1_{\mathcal{P}})\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d_{k})}\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{b},d_{1})}\prod_{i\leqslant s}F({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}+O\left(\frac{M^{O(1)}}{\mathcal{F}(M)^{{\Omega}(1)}}N^{s-k-1}\right).

By Lemma
6.3
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{n\in\mathcal{P}}f_{\mathrm{str}}(n)+O\left(\frac{M^{O(1)}}{\mathcal{F}(M)^{{\Omega}(1)}}\right)

Since
\eta({\delta}/C)\leqslant{\delta}/C
, we now see from (
7.2
) that

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}F({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\geqslant{\delta}/C.

Now, by Corollary
7.3
,

\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{a},d_{k})}\int_{\mathcal{K}(\mathbf{b},d_{1})}\prod_{i\leqslant s}F({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{(i)})\mathrm{d}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\geqslant\eta({\delta}/C).

Substituting this into (
7.5
) yields

T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}})\geqslant\eta({\delta}/C)T(1_{\mathcal{P}})-O\left(\frac{M^{O(1)}}{\mathcal{F}(M)^{{\Omega}(1)}}N^{s-k-1}\right).

By (
3.2
) or (
3.3
),

T(1_{\mathcal{P}})\gg M^{-O(1)}N^{s-k-1}.

Our choice (
7.2
) now ensures that

T_{\mathcal{P}}(f_{\mathrm{str}})\gg\eta({\delta}/C)T(1_{\mathcal{P}}).

Combining this with (
7.2
), (
7.3
) and (
7.4
) delivers

T(1_{\mathcal{A}})\gg\eta({\delta}/C)M^{-O(1)}N^{s-k-1}

as sought.
