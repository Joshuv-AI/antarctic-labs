Duality for 𝐭 \mathbf{t} -modules: The Difficult Cases
Dawid Edmund Kędzierski, Piotr Krasoń

Abstract
. This paper continues our previous work on duality for Anderson t-modules. We study two-dimensional triangular 𝐭 \mathbf{t} -modules in a specific reduced form. Computer-assisted symbolic computations with matrices over a skew field revealed a reduction pattern for 𝐭 \mathbf{t} -modules satisfying the ALD condition. Using this pattern, we prove that such t-modules are isomorphic to their double duals and extend the validity of the Cartier–Nishi theorem and the Weil–Barsotti formula to a substantially broader class of t-modules.

Introduction

Drinfeld modules and their higher dimensional generalizations, since their invention, have been central objects of intensive studies in number theory. In recent years, many algorithmic methods have been developed for the
\mathbf{t}
-modules and the associated
\mathbf{t}
-motives and
\mathbf{t}
-co-motives e.g.
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
2
]
. This creates a natural environment for experimental mathematics. The present paper represents one of the first experimental approaches to problems in function field arithmetic over global function fields.
We address the concept of duality for
\mathbf{t}
-modules. The duality for Drinfeld modules, in terms of algebraic geometry, was investigated in
[
17
]
. In
[
16
]
, the authors defined duality for Drinfeld modules of rank at least two by means of the
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{\tau}
-functor and proved the Weil-Barsotti formula, as well as the Cartier-Nishi theorem
[
16
, Theorem 1.1]
. In
[
11
]
, we showed that, for a large class of pairs of
\mathbf{t}
-modules
\Phi
and
\Psi
,
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}(\Phi,\Psi)
can be endowed with a natural structure of a
\mathbf{t}
-module. This was the starting point for our further investigation of the duality. In
[
9
]
we developed an algorithm, called the
\mathbf{t}
-reduction, which implements these results.
Computing endomorphism rings of Drinfeld modules is a highly nontrivial problem (see
[
14
]
and
[
8
]
). For a
\mathbf{t}
-module
{\Upsilon}
computing a double dual module
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}
and finding that
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong{\Upsilon}

is by no means easy. Notice, that one cannot expect the Cartier-Nishi theorem and the Weil-Barsotti formula to hold for every
\mathbf{t}
-module
\Upsilon
(see
[
13
, Example 4.1]
). In
[
13
]
we proved these for
\Upsilon
a non-nilpotent, stictly pure
\mathbf{t}
-module of degree at least two. Since the proof was done in the language of biderivations it required careful reductions by inner biderivations. In
[
12
]
we introduced triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules i.e. those with the composition series
having Drinfeld modules as
\tau
-simple sub-quotients. For such modules allowing for low degree biderivations we were able to prove the Cartier-Nishi theorem and the Weil-Barsotti formula. In this paper, we deal with the most difficult cases where substantial coefficient swell occurs. This phenomenon constitutes a serious computational obstacle. To determine whether any patterns emerge in reductions leading to the isomorphism
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong\Upsilon
, we performed a large number of computer experiments. We restricted our attention to triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules of dimension two. These experiments enabled us to extend the aforementioned Cartier-Nishi theorem and Weil-Barsotti formula to two-dimensional
\mathbf{t}
-modules allowing for
{\mathrm{ALD}}
biderivations.
The structure of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we briefly review the necessary definitions. In Section
3
, we introduce triangular modules, the main objects of study in this paper. We then formulate and discuss a general conjecture (cf. Conjecture
3.1
).
We then restrict our attention to two-dimensional triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules. In particular, we emphasize that in the more difficult range

\mathrm{rk}\psi_{1}>\deg_{\tau}\delta\geq\mathrm{rk}\psi_{2},

the isomorphisms

\Upsilon\cong{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}

are no longer given by constants; instead, they are realized by skew polynomials.
In Section
4
, we describe our computational methods in detail. In particular, we present the algorithms developed for this purpose.
Section
5
is devoted to the complexity analysis of the computation of dual and double dual modules in the aforementioned difficult cases of two-dimensional triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules.
In Section
6
, we present the results of several computer-assisted experiments. Based on these results, we formulate a conjecture for two-dimensional triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules and state the main theorem of this paper. In Section
7
, we compute the dual module explicitly (cf. Theorem
7.2
) and prove an auxiliary proposition concerning the degree of a dual
\mathbf{t}
-module (cf. Proposition
5.1
).
Sections
8
and
9
are devoted to the proofs of the main theorem and an auxiliary lemma, respectively.
Finally, in Section
10

Preliminaries

In this section, we recall only the basic notions concerning
\mathbf{t}
–modules, restricting ourselves to the algebraic side of the theory.
For comprehensive introduction and further details, the reader is referred to the excellent sources
[

,
[

Let
A=\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be the polynomial ring over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q=p^{s}
elements and let
K
be a field of characteristic
p
equipped with an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–algebra homomorphism
\iota:A\to K.
We write
\theta:=\iota(t)
.
The endomorphism ring
\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{G}_{a,K})
, where
\mathbb{G}_{a,K}
denotes the additive algebraic group over
K
, is the skew-polynomial ring
K\{\tau\}
.
Here
\tau
is the
q
–Frobenius endomorphism
x\mapsto x^{q}
, and therefore the commutation relation

\tau x=x^{q}\tau\qquad(x\in K)

holds.
A definition of a
d
-dimensional
\mathbf{t}
-module was given by G. Anderson
[
1
]
. It is an algebraic group
E
defined over
K
and isomorphic over
K
to
\mathbb{G}_{a,K}^{d}
, together with a choice of an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear endomorphism
l:E\to E
such that

\partial(l-\theta)^{n}\mathrm{Lie}(E)=0

for all sufficiently large
n
, where
\partial(-)
denotes the differential of an endomorphism of algebraic groups.
After fixing an isomorphism
E\cong\mathbb{G}_{a,K}^{d}
, the endomorphism
l
determines an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–algebra homomorphism

\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\longrightarrow\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(K\{\tau\})

such that the element
\Phi_{t}:=\Phi(t)
, regarded as a skew-polynomial in
\tau
with coefficients in
\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(K)
, has the form

(1)

\Phi_{t}=(\theta I_{d}+N_{\Phi})\tau^{0}+M_{1}\tau+\dots+M_{r}\tau^{r},

where
I_{d}
is the identity matrix and
N_{\Phi}
is a nilpotent matrix.
A
\mathbf{t}
–module
\Phi
is said to have
no nilpotence
if
N_{\Phi}=0
in (
1
). If the dimension
d=1,
then a
\mathbf{t}
-module is called a Drinfeld module. Drinfeld modules were introduced in
[
6
]
.
For a general
\mathbf{t}
-module, there are well-defined notions of rank and degree; see
[
4
]
.
The category of
\mathbf{t}
–modules is additive but not abelian; see
[
11
]
. Nevertheless, there exists a well-defined notion of an exact sequence. Consequently, one can consider extension groups of pairs of
\mathbf{t}
-modules. The study of the such extension groups was initiated in
[
16
]
and continued in
[
13
,
9
,
12
]
.

An extension of a
\mathbf{t}
–module
\Phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\to\mathrm{Mat}_{d}(K\{\tau\})
by a
\mathbf{t}
–module

\Psi:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\to\mathrm{Mat}_{e}(K\{\tau\})
can be represented by a
b
iderivation, that is, an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-linear map

\delta:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\to\mathrm{Mat}_{e\times d}(K\{\tau\})
satisfying

\delta(ab)=\Psi(a)\delta(b)+\delta(a)\Phi(b),
for

a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t].

The
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–vector space of all biderivations is denoted by
\mathrm{Der}(\Phi,\Psi)
.
The map
\delta\mapsto\delta(t)
induces an
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear isomorphism

\mathrm{Der}(\Phi,\Psi)\;\cong\;\mathrm{Mat}_{e\times d}(K\{\tau\}).

Let

\delta^{(-)}:\mathrm{Mat}_{e\times d}(K\{\tau\})\to\mathrm{Der}(\Phi,\Psi)

be the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
–linear map defined by

\delta^{(U)}(a)=U\Phi_{a}-\Psi_{a}U
, for
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
,
U\in\mathrm{Mat}_{e\times d}(K\{\tau\})
.
Its image, consisting of
inner biderivations
, is denoted by
\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi)
. Henceforth, we write
\delta^{(U)}_{\Phi,\Psi}
to denote the element
\delta^{(U)}_{\Phi,\Psi}\in\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi)
.
We have the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
–module isomorphism

\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{\tau}(\Phi,\Psi)\;\cong\;\mathrm{Der}(\Phi,\Psi)/\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi).

Recall that the structure of the
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
-module in this quotient is given by

(2)

a*\big(\delta+\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi)\big):=\Psi_{a}\delta+\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi).

For
V\in\mathrm{Mat}_{n_{1}\times n_{2}}(K\{\tau\})
let
\partial V
denote the constant term of
V
viewed as a matrix skew polynomial in
\tau
.
For
\mathbf{t}
–modules
\Phi
and
\Psi
define

\mathrm{Der}_{0}(\Phi,\Psi)=\{\delta\in\mathrm{Der}(\Phi,\Psi)\mid\partial\delta(t)=0\},

and

\mathrm{Der}_{0,\mathrm{in}}(\Phi,\Psi)=\{\delta\in\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\Phi,\Psi)\mid\partial\delta(t)=0\}.

We then set

\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{0,\tau}(\Phi,\Psi):=\mathrm{Der}_{0}(\Phi,\Psi)/\mathrm{Der}_{0,\mathrm{in}}(\Phi,\Psi).

In general,
\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{0,\tau}(\Phi,\Psi)
is merely an
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
–module, although in certain situations this structure arises from a genuine
\mathbf{t}
-module; see
[
16
]
,
[
11
]
, and
[
9
]
.
For a
\mathbf{t}
–module
\Phi
we define its
dual
by

\Phi^{\vee}=\operatorname{Ext}^{1}_{0,\tau}(\Phi,C),

where
C
denotes the Carlitz module, i.e., the Drinfeld module defined by

C_{t}=\theta+\tau.

3.
Triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules of dimension two

The notion of a composition series for
\mathbf{t}
–modules was adapted in
[
9
]
; see
[
9
, Theorem 5.5]
.
A
\mathbf{t}
–module is called
triangular
if it admits a composition series whose successive quotients are Drinfeld modules.
The structure and properties of triangular
\mathbf{t}
–modules were studied extensively in
[
12
]
.
In this article, we focus on triangular
\mathbf{t}
–modules of dimension
2
, namely those arising as the middle term of a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\psi_{1}\longrightarrow\Upsilon\longrightarrow\psi_{2}\longrightarrow 0.

After a suitable choice of coordinates on
\Upsilon
, such a module acquires a triangular form

(3)

\Upsilon=\begin{pNiceArray}.

Thus, each such module is uniquely determined by a biderivation

\delta:\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]\to K\{\tau\}
, or equivalently by the skew polynomial
\delta(t)\in K\{\tau\}
. We denote the corresponding
\mathbf{t}
-module by
\Upsilon=\Upsilon(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\delta)
.

The main objective of this article is to investigate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3.1
.

Let
\Upsilon
be a triangular
\mathbf{t}
–module.
Then
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong\Upsilon
, and moreover there exists an exact sequence of
\mathbf{t}
–modules

0\longrightarrow\Upsilon\longrightarrow\mathrm{Ext}^{1}_{\tau}(\Upsilon^{\vee},C)\longrightarrow\mathbb{G}_{a,K}^{s}\longrightarrow 0,

for some natural number
s
depending on
\Upsilon
.

This conjecture is known to hold for Drinfeld modules of rank at least
2

[
16
]
, for strictly pure
\mathbf{t}
–modules of degree at least
2
without nilpotence
[
13
]
, and for triangular
\mathbf{t}
–modules allowing for
\mathrm{LD}
–biderivations, without nilpotents and with quotient modules of rank at least
2

[
12
]
. In the latter two settings, the conjecture fails once the assumption of non-nilpotence is dropped; see
[
13
, Example 4.1]
and
[
12
, Example 9.1]
.

Motivated by these counterexamples, we restrict our attention to triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules of the form (
3
) without nilpotence and with
\mathrm{rk}\psi_{i}\geq 2
, while allowing biderivations beyond the class of LD-biderivations.

Note that for triangular
\mathbf{t}
–modules,
[
12
, Theorem 9.1]
implies that the condition

{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong\Upsilon
already yields the full conclusion of Conjecture
3.1
.
Moreover, the same theorem gives that

(4)

{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}=\begin{pNiceArray}.

Let
r_{i}:=\mathrm{rk}\psi_{i}
for
i=1,2
, and let
a_{r_{1}}
and
b_{r_{2}}
denote the leading coefficients of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
, respectively.
Recall from the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 3.4]
that

{\psi_{1}^{\vee}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{a_{r_{1}}}\cdot\psi_{1}\cdot a_{r_{1}},\qquad{\psi_{2}^{\vee}}^{\vee}=\frac{1}{b_{r_{2}}}\cdot\psi_{2}\cdot b_{r_{2}}.

To recover the original subquotients in
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}
, we consider the conjugate of
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}
by the diagonal matrix

D=\mathrm{diag}(b_{r_{2}},a_{r_{1}})
.
Thus,

{}^{D}{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}:=D\,{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\,D^{-1}=\begin{pNiceArray}.

Therefore, the existence of an isomorphism
\Upsilon\cong{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}
is equivalent to the condition

(5)

a_{r_{1}}\cdot{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cdot\frac{1}{b_{r_{2}}}-\delta\;\in\;\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1}).

It is worth noting that, in all previously studied cases, the isomorphism

\Upsilon\cong{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}
was realized by a matrix with entries in the field
K
.
As we shall see in later examples lying beyond the class of modules allowing for
\mathrm{LD}
–biderivations, this is no longer the case: the relevant isomorphisms are represented by matrices whose entries are genuine skew polynomials rather than constants.
This phenomenon substantially increases the complexity of the problem.

Observe that, if
\mathrm{rk}\psi_{1}\leq\mathrm{rk}\psi_{2}
then the triangular
\mathbf{t}
–module

\Upsilon(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\delta)
allows for
\mathrm{LD}
-biderivations (see

[
12
, Corollaries 4.3 and 4.4]
). Consequently, our attention is restricted to the case
\mathrm{rk}\psi_{1}>\mathrm{rk}\psi_{2}
.
Furthermore, every triangular
\mathbf{t}
-module admits a reduced form [KK25, Theorem 4.1], for which

\deg_{\tau}\delta<\max\{\mathrm{rk}\psi_{1},\mathrm{rk}\psi_{2}\}
. Hence, throughout this paper we consider only examples satisfying

(6)

\mathrm{rk}\psi_{1}>\deg_{\tau}\delta\geq\mathrm{rk}\psi_{2}.

5.
Symbolic complexity of dual and double dual computations

The explicit computation of duals and double duals of
\mathbf{t}
-modules requires working with concrete elements of the twisted polynomial ring
K\{\tau\}
. Although the algebraic structure of these objects is well understood at an abstract level, their explicit representations often involve symbolic expressions whose size and complexity grow rapidly during computation. This phenomenon is closely related to the well-known problem of expression swell in symbolic computation; see
[
3
]
.
In the context of Drinfeld modules and
\mathbf{t}
-modules, similar computational challenges have been observed in explicit calculations over
K\{\tau\}
; see
[
16
,
9
,
12
]
. These works indicate that, even when degrees and ranks can be controlled theoretically, the resulting coefficients may become prohibitively large from a computational perspective.
In this section, we investigate this phenomenon experimentally for triangular
\mathbf{t}
-modules of dimension two. We first study the dual construction and then the double dual, demonstrating that symbolic complexity increases significantly at each stage. Our experiments show that computer-assisted methods become indispensable even for examples of moderate size.

\mathbf{t}

(\star_{s})\quad\xi_{1,s}=-d,\quad(\star_{l})\quad\xi_{1,l}=-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s-l}\xi_{1,l+j}b_{r_{2}-j}^{(l+j)},\quad\textnormal{for}\quad l=s-1,s-2,\dots,0.

Then

\displaystyle{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{s}\Delta_{1,l}^{\vee}\equiv-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{s}\xi_{1,l}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}b_{r_{2}-l+j}^{(l)}\tau^{r_{2}+j}\mod\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1}).

After collecting terms according to the powers of
\tau
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}

\displaystyle\equiv-\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{s}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}-l}^{(l)}}\limits_{=0\textnormal{ from }(\star_{0})}\tau^{r_{2}}-\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{s}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}-l+1}^{(l)}}\limits_{=0,\textnormal{ from }(\star_{1})}\tau^{r_{2}+1}

\displaystyle-\cdots-\underbrace{\big(\xi_{1,s-1}b_{r_{2}}+\xi_{1,s}b_{r_{2}-1}\big)}\limits_{=0\textnormal{ from }(\star_{s-1})}\tau^{r_{2}+s-1}-\underbrace{\xi_{1,s}b_{r_{2}}}\limits_{=-d}\tau^{r_{2}+s}

\displaystyle\equiv d\tau^{h}\mod\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1}).

Therefore, in this case,

\Upsilon\cong{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}.

If
s\in\{r_{2}-1,r_{2},\dots,2r_{2}-3\}
, then
\deg_{\tau}\Upsilon^{\vee}=D=3
,
M_{1}=s
,
M_{2}=s-(r_{2}-1)
and
\delta^{\vee}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-1}\Delta_{1,l}+\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\Delta_{2,l}
, where
\Delta_{k,l}=\Delta_{k,l}(\xi_{k,l})
and the coefficients
\xi_{k,l}
are determined recursively by

\displaystyle(\star_{s})

\displaystyle\quad\xi_{1,s}=-d,\quad(\star_{l})\quad\xi_{1,l}=-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{s-l}\xi_{1,l+j}b_{r_{2}-j}^{(l+j)},\quad\textnormal{for}\quad l=s-1,s-2,\dots,M_{2},

\displaystyle(\star_{l})

\displaystyle\quad\xi_{1,l}=-\xi_{1,l+r_{2}}\big(\theta^{(l+r_{2})}-\theta\big)-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r_{2}-1}\xi_{1,l+r_{2}-j}b_{j}^{(l+r_{2}-j)}\quad\textnormal{for}\quad l=s-r_{2},\dots,1,0,

\displaystyle(\nabla_{M_{2}})

\displaystyle\quad\xi_{2,M_{2}}=\xi^{(1)}_{1,s},\quad(\nabla_{l})\quad\xi_{2,l}=\xi_{1,l+r_{2}-1}^{(1)}-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{M_{2}-l}\xi_{2,l+j}b_{r_{2}-j}^{(l+j)}\quad\textnormal{for}\quad l=s-r_{2},\dots,1,0

Then

{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-1}\Delta_{1,l}^{\vee}+\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\Delta_{2,l}^{\vee}\equiv-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-2}\xi_{1,l}\sum\limits_{j=0}^{l}b_{r_{2}-l+j}^{(l)}\tau^{r_{2}+j}-\xi_{1,r_{2}-1}\Big(\theta-\theta^{(r_{2}-1)}\Big)\tau^{r_{2}-1}

-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}\cdot\sum\limits_{j=r_{2}-l}^{r_{2}}\delta_{\psi_{2},\theta}^{(b_{j}^{(l-1)}\tau^{j+l-1})}\mod\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1})

After collecting terms according to the powers of
\tau
, we obtain

{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}\equiv\sum\limits_{k=r_{2}-1}^{h}f_{k}\tau^{k}\mod\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1})
,
where

f_{r_{2}-1}=\Big(\underbrace{\xi_{1,r_{2}-1}-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}-l}^{(l-1)}}\limits_{=0,\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{0})}\Big)\Big(\theta^{(r_{2}-1)}-\theta\Big)=0.

Similarly,

\displaystyle f_{r_{2}}

\displaystyle=-b_{1}^{(r_{2}-1)}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,j}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}-j}^{(j-1)}}\limits_{=\xi_{1.r_{2}-1},\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{0})}-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-2}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}-l}^{(l)}-\Big(\theta^{(r_{2})}-\theta\Big)\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l-1)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\xi_{1.r_{2}-1}b_{1}^{(r_{2}-1)}-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-2}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}-l}^{(l)}}\limits_{=\xi_{1,r_{2}}\big(\theta^{(r_{2})}-\theta\big)\textnormal{ from }(\star_{0})}-\Big(\theta^{(r_{2})}-\theta\Big)\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l-1)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\theta^{(r_{2})}-\theta\Big)\Big(\underbrace{\xi_{1,r_{2}}-\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l-1)}}\limits_{=0,\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{1})}\Big)=0

and

\displaystyle f_{r_{2}+1}

\displaystyle=-b_{2}^{(r_{2}-1)}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,j}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}-j}^{(j-1)}}\limits_{=\xi_{1.r_{2}-1},\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{0})}-b_{1}^{(r_{2})}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l-1)}}\limits_{=\xi_{1,r_{2}},\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{1})}

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}-2}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l)}-\Big(\theta^{(r_{2}+1)}-\theta\Big)\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+2-l}^{(l-1)}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{-\sum\limits_{l=0}^{r_{2}}\xi_{1,l}b_{r_{2}+1-l}^{(l)}}\limits_{=\xi_{1,r_{2}+1}\big(\theta^{(r_{2}+1)}-\theta\big),\textnormal{ from }(\star_{1})}-\Big(\theta^{(r_{2}+1)}-\theta\Big)\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+2-l}^{(l-1)}

\displaystyle=\Big(\theta^{(r_{2}+1)}-\theta\Big)\Big(\underbrace{\xi_{1,r_{2}+1}-\sum\limits_{l=1}^{M_{2}}\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}b_{r_{2}+2-l}^{(l-1)}}\limits_{=0,\textnormal{ from }(\nabla_{2})}\Big)=0

Proceeding analogously, we obtain

f_{r_{2}+2}=f_{r_{2}+1}=\cdots=f_{h-1}=0.

Finally,
f_{h}=-\xi_{2,M_{2}}^{(-1)}=-\xi_{1,s}=d,

by
(\nabla_{M_{2}})
and
(\star_{s}).
Therefore,

{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}\equiv d\tau^{h}\mod{\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1})}.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 2
.

Our approach to establishing the isomorphism

{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong\Upsilon

provides an explanation for the regularities observed in Section
6
. Indeed, observe that equality (
24
), together with the formula

{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}=\sum_{k,l}\Delta_{k,l}^{\vee},

implies that

\displaystyle{\delta^{\vee}}^{\vee}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k,l}\delta_{k,l}^{\mathrm{red}}+\sum\limits_{k,l}\delta_{\psi_{2},\psi_{1}}^{\big(u_{k,l}(\tau)\big)}.

If we consider the
\mathbf{t}
-module determined by the triple of integers
(r_{1},r_{2},h)
, then, according to the notation introduced in Section
6
, we have

u(r_{1},r_{2},h)=\sum_{k,l}u_{k,l}(\tau).

From the description of the dual
\mathbf{t}
-module given in Theorem
7.2
, it follows that for
h\leq 2(r_{2}-1)
only components of the form
\Delta_{1,l}
with
l<r_{2}-1
occur. Therefore, by the proof of Lemma
8.2
, we obtain

u(r_{1},r_{2},h)=\sum_{l}u_{1,l}(\tau)=\sum_{l}\xi_{1,l}\tau^{l}.

Hence, in this case,
u(r_{1},r_{2},h)
is independent of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, which explains observation
a.
from Section
6
.

To justify observation
b.
, let us note that in the case

2(r_{2}-1)<h\leq 3(r_{2}-1),

the dual
\mathbf{t}
-module contains only components of the form
\Delta_{1,l}
for

l=0,1,\dots,r_{2}-1
and
\Delta_{2,l}
for
l<r_{2}-1
. Therefore, it follows from the proof of Lemma
8.2
that

u(r_{1},r_{2},h)=\sum_{l}\xi_{1,l}\tau^{l}+\sum_{l}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}a_{i}\xi_{2,l}^{(i-1)}\tau^{l+i-1}+\xi_{2,l}^{(-1)}\sum_{j=r_{2}-l}^{r_{2}}b_{j}^{(l-1)}\tau^{j+l-1}\right).

Since the sum

\sum_{l}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}a_{i}\xi_{2,l}^{(i-1)}\tau^{l+i-1}

depends on
r_{1}
and on the coefficients of
\psi_{1}
, it gives rise to the recurrence relations observed in observation
b.
of Section
6
.

Our main theorem on Cartier–Nishi duality for two-dimensional triangular

we proved the Cartier–Nishi theorem for triangular

-modules of arbitrary dimension, but under a more restrictive degree condition, namely for those allowing for
\mathrm{LD}
-biderivations. The latter case appears to be more tractable, since the relevant isomorphisms are induced by constant polynomials. Both proofs rely crucially on the existence of a reduced form, established in
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
.
If one attempts to work with non-reduced forms of
\mathbf{t}
-modules, the formulas appearing in Lemma
8.2
become so complicated as to be practically intractable. For
\mathbf{t}
-modules that do not allow for
\mathrm{ALD}
-biderivations, the computational complexity increases substantially: one obtains highly irregular formulas and, moreover, must work over a finite extension of the field of definition of the
\mathbf{t}
-module.

Despite these obstacles, we believe that Cartier–Nishi duality should hold in a much broader setting, possibly for all dimensions. This belief motivates the general conjectures stated above. However, establishing such a result will likely require a modification of approach developed in the present paper.

The following example is instructive.

Example 10.1
.

Consider a triangular
\mathbf{t}
-module

\Upsilon=\Upsilon(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\delta),

where

\psi_{1}=\theta+\sum_{i=1}^{7}a_{i}\tau^{i}+\tau^{8},\qquad\psi_{2}=\theta+b_{1}\tau+b_{2}\tau^{2}+\tau^{3},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta=d\tau^{7}.

This module does not allow for
\mathrm{ALD}
-biderivations. Then, by Theorem
7.2
,

\delta^{\vee}=[0,0,0,0,0,1]\otimes\Big(\Delta_{1,0}+\Delta_{1,1}+\Delta_{1,2}+\Delta_{2,0}+\Delta_{2,1}+\Delta_{2,2}+\Delta_{3,0}\Big).

By computing the terms
\Delta_{k,l}^{\vee}
using the
\mathbf{t}
-reduction algorithm, we make the following observations:

a.

The representatives of the biderivations
\Delta_{1,l}^{\vee}
for
l\in{0,1,2}
and
\Delta_{2,l}^{\vee}
for
l\in{0,1}
coincide with those obtained in Lemma
8.2
.

b.

If we attempt to determine representatives of
\Delta_{2,2}^{\vee}
and
\Delta_{3,0}^{\vee}
in the same manner, it turns out that:

–

We are forced to extract
q
-th roots of elements that need not be
q
-th powers in the base field (K), for example
\theta^{(-1)}
. This necessitates passing to a finite extension of
K
. It is worth noting that the direct computation of
u(8,3,7)
did not require such a field extension.

–

With representatives determined in this manner, it becomes difficult to carry out a proof analogous to that of Theorem
6.3
.

This suggests that the decomposition into
\Delta_{2,2}^{\vee}
and
\Delta_{3,0}^{\vee}
may not be the most natural one. In the present case, this issue can be resolved by considering
\Delta_{2,2}^{\vee}
and
\Delta_{3,0}^{\vee}
jointly rather than separately, that is, by computing

\bigl(\Delta_{2,2}+\Delta_{3,0}\bigr)^{\vee}.

One then obtains the following relation:

\displaystyle\Big(\Delta_{2,2}+\Delta_{3,0}\Big)^{\vee}\equiv\xi_{2,2}^{(-1)}\cdot\sum\limits_{j=1}^{3}\delta_{\psi_{2},\theta}^{\big(b_{j}^{(1)}\tau^{j+1}\big)}\mod\mathrm{Der_{in}}(\psi_{2},\psi_{1}),

which corresponds to the representative obtained in Lemma
8.2
. Consequently, we may repeat the argument used in the proof of Theorem
6.3
to obtain an isomorphism
{\Upsilon^{\vee}}^{\vee}\cong\Upsilon.

Motivated by this example, and by repeating the computations from the proof of Lemma
8.2
together with the argument used in the proof of Theorem
6.3
, one might hope to extend the Cartier–Nishi conjecture to the case
(r_{1},r_{2},h)
with

h=3(r_{2}-1)+1.

This example raises the hope that the method proposed in this paper may also be adapted to the general case. However, for triples
(r_{1},r_{2},h)
with
h>3(r_{2}-1)+1,

that is, when terms of the form
\Delta_{3,l}
with
l>0
appear in the description of the dual
\mathbf{t}
-module, the same line of reasoning no longer works.

As before, the biderivations
\Delta_{1,l}
and
\Delta_{2,l}
for
l<r_{2}-1
admit the same representatives as those appearing in Lemma
8.2
. However, considering
\Delta_{2,r_{2}-1}
, either separately or together with
\Delta_{3,0}
and the remaining terms
\Delta_{3,l}
, no longer yields the desired outcome.
