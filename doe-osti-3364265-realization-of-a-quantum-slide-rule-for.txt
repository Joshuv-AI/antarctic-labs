Final technical report

Award Title: Realization of a Quantum Slide Rule for 1+1 Dimensional Quantum Field
Theories Using Josephson Superconducting Circuits
Sponsor Award ID: DESC0020160

Executive summary:

We have built a special type of electronic circuit—a “superconducting quantum
simulator”—that allows us to mimic the behavior of complex quantum systems that are
impossible to compute with ordinary computers. Our device is a long transmission line
made of thousands of tiny superconducting junctions, acting as a one-dimensional
“vacuum” for microwave photons. By terminating this line with a tunable, weak Josephson
junction, we can control how strongly photons interact with each other. Crucially, we can
tune the environment’s impedance to match or exceed a fundamental quantum resistance
of about 6.5 kΩ, which turns on extremely strong, non-linear interactions. Using this setup,
we have finally demonstrated the Schmid-Bulgadaev dissipative quantum phase
transition—a forty-year-old prediction that had remained controversial. We showed that
the junction undergoes a transition from superconductor to insulator simply by changing
the impedance of the attached line: when impedance is low, the junction behaves like an
inductor (superconducting); when high, like a capacitor (insulating). The critical transition
happens exactly at the predicted impedance, and at that point the junction acts like an
ideal resistor—incident photons spontaneously down-convert into lower-frequency pairs
with a frequency-independent probability, a clear marker of quantum-critical behavior.
Beyond this phase transition, we used the same platform to simulate quantum field
theories and many-body localization (MBL)—phenomena notoriously hard to compute with
classical supercomputers. For example, by tuning the junction to act as a “quantum
phase-slip” center, we made a single incoming photon split into a shower of many low-
energy photons with near-unit probability, mirroring how a quasiparticle might decay in a
confined quantum dot. We also observed a striking “fine structure” of multi-photon
resonances, showing that photon decay can be blocked by many-body localization—a
breakdown of Fermi’s Golden Rule. Because we can precisely tune all circuit parameters
(impedance, Josephson energy, charging energy) using magnetic flux, our single device can
emulate a variety of quantum impurity models, the boundary sine-Gordon model, and the
physics of Luttinger liquids. This work establishes superconducting circuits as a powerful,
flexible analog quantum simulator for tackling strongly correlated systems that lie far
beyond the reach of conventional computers.

Summary of activities:

On the theory side, we have created a new framework to understand the results of photon-
photon interaction and strong coupling of a quantum system to a dissipative bath using the
notion of the inelastic scattering rate. This is a relatively new quantity for quantum optics, it
has a deep connection to quantum field theory on one hand and can be measured in our
system with a vector network analyser and sufficiently low-noise measurement apparatus.
We have also introduced a new gauge in quantum electrodynamics in which a qubit is
coupled via “momentum” variable at low energy and via “position” variable at high energy.
Such an unusual gauge was the key to explaining the observed down-conversion dynamics
of a single photons. We have performed both analytical calculations and numerical
analysis of such systems.

On the experimental side we have developed fabrication of a metamaterial made of tens of
thousands of Josephson junctions coupled to a single “quantum impurity” circuit, either a
weak Josephson junction SQUID or a fluxonium qubit. We developed a methods for tuning
the wave impedance and velocity of photons in our metamaterial using an applied
magnetic field. We have introduced measurement techniques allowing us to turn the
interaction of photons on and off by tuning the properties of the impurity circuit
independently of the metamaterial properties. Several tens of devices containing tens of
thousands of Josephson junctions have been fabricated and tested at milliKelvin
temperatures in our laboratory at the University of Maryland.
