Spontaneous and indefinite blinking in upconverting nanoparticles for ångström-precision multicolour super-resolution imaging
Authors: Saptarshi Mandal, Harrison W. Toll, Kaibo Ma, Irmuun Tommy Altankhuyag, Lidao Li, Wanlin Zhang, João F. Shida, Chunte Sam Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Single-molecule localization microscopy enables high-resolution biological imaging, but its precision is limited by the rapid photobleaching of conventional fluorophores. Multicolour imaging is further constrained by the need for spectrally distinct dyes requiring separate excitations or sequential acquisition. Here we show that small (~10 nm) upconverting nanoparticles can be compositionally tuned to exhibit spontaneous, sustained blinking under single near-infrared excitation without optical or chemical modulation. By adjusting sensitizer (Yb3+)–emitter (Tm3+/Er3+) ratios, we identify a regime with intrinsic ON–OFF switching and low duty cycles (~0.9%) without photobleaching or statistical aging, enabling repeated localizations and sub-ångström precision (0.62 Å over 88,000 localizations) in upconversion-enabled stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy. By elucidating the underlying physical mechanism of this blinking, we engineered blue- and red-emitting probes for multicolour upconversion-enabled stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy. This technique enables the resolution of tightly packed UCNPs and the visualization of epidermal growth factor receptor dimers and multimers on cell membranes at single-protein resolution, all achieved with a simple optical setup without imaging buffers.

Main

Conventional light microscopy is fundamentally limited by the diffraction of light, restricting spatial resolution to approximately half the wavelength of the emitted photons (~200–300 nm laterally)1–3. This constraint prevents the visualization of nanoscale subcellular features such as protein complexes, cytoskeletal networks, protein clusters or membrane ultrastructure, which typically span 10–100 nm (ref. 4). The advent of single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) in the past decades has overcome this barrier by separating the emission of individual fluorophores in time, allowing their position to be determined within subdiffractional limits and enabling the visualization of biologically important molecular structures in the nanometre regime5–10. Techniques such as stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) and photoactivated localization microscopy achieve super-resolution by stochastically activating sparse subsets of fluorophores and localizing them through centroid fitting of their point spread functions (PSFs) during fluorescent ON states when the fluorophores emit light5,8,9. For typical organic fluorophores yielding ~102–103 collected photons during the ON state, localization precision in the 10–20-nm range can be obtained using a standard wide-field microscope1,11,12.

Recent development of MINFLUX reached ångström-level precision7; however, the technique requires a complex optical setup and is not easily adaptable for simultaneous multicolour imaging. Alternatively, improvement in precision beyond the nanoscale regime can be achieved by repeatedly localizing a single emitter in multiple frames, as demonstrated in resolution enhancement by sequential imaging (RESI)6,13 using DNA-PAINT that achieves high localization precision by exploiting the transient binding of dye-conjugated oligonucleotides to targets6,14. However, unbound imaging strands can lead to increased background in DNA-PAINT. Moreover, multicolour imaging requires multiple imaging rounds, each with its own set of imager strands, thereby imposing constraints on imaging duration and conditions.

If a probe can blink indefinitely, it allows the repeated localization of the same emitter across multiple frames, generating a dense localization cluster and thereby reducing the stochastic localization error (Fig. 1a). In most conventional fluorophores, ON–OFF switching typically requires multiple laser excitations9,11,18, which not only increases photobleaching, photoblueing and phototoxicity but also requires the precise control of laser alignment and synchronization19. Achieving stable blinking and low duty cycles (the fraction of time a fluorophore spends in the ON state) further requires optimized imaging buffers containing oxygen scavengers and reducing agents, which can alter the cellular environment, be sample dependent and limit live-cell compatibility12,20. These requirements highlight the need for probes that blink spontaneously21 without optical or chemical control and support prolonged, high-precision imaging as well as provide a direct route to ångström-level localization precision using simple optical setups.

Lanthanide-doped upconverting nanoparticles (UCNPs) are promising candidates due to their remarkable photostability17,22–30. UCNPs consist of sensitizer ions (Yb3+) that absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and transfer this energy to emitter ions (Tm3+ or Er3+), resulting in visible emission. Unlike conventional fluorophores, UCNPs can be excited with low-energy, NIR light, which penetrates deeper into biological tissues with minimal photodamage and cell autofluorescence allowing for clearer, background-free images of structures within live cells and tissues31–34. Since their discovery, UCNPs have been shown to be very photostable without photobleaching and blinking17,23,26–28. Recently, ref. 29 reported that ~25-nm Tm3+-doped, single-colour avalanching nanoparticles can be phototriggered to switch between bright and dark states, although requiring multiple laser excitations with high laser powers (~164 kW cm−2) and complex optical setups, compromising a broader adoption towards multicolour bioimaging applications. In other cases, the nonlinear optical response of different avalanching nanoparticles has been leveraged to achieve sub-diffraction-limit imaging without relying on ON–OFF switching35–39, yet such approaches still require high excitation powers (30–700 kW cm−2) and relatively large particle sizes (25–176 nm), limiting their utility in biological systems. Nanoparticle size critically influences performance in biological imaging as larger nanoparticles limit the ultimate resolution and labelling density, exhibit limited tissue penetration and potentially disrupt the native biological structures40–43. Therefore, small-sized particles (<15 nm) comparable in scale to key biomolecular targets such as membrane proteins and antibodies are preferred for efficient labelling and access to crowded cellular environments in super-resolution bioimaging44. On the basis of this observation, we sought to engineer small-sized UCNPs capable of spontaneous and indefinite blinking to enable high-precision, biologically compatible super-resolution imaging.

Here we show a new class of small (~10 nm), compositionally engineered UCNPs that exhibit spontaneous and indefinite blinking under continuous NIR excitation without optical modulation, specialized imaging buffers, photobleaching or statistical aging. By tuning the sensitizer (Yb3+) and emitter (Tm3+ or Er3+) concentrations, we achieve controllable blinking dynamics with duty cycles as low as ~0.9%, suitable for high-density SMLM imaging. We further identify the physical mechanism underlying the stochastic switching behaviour and use this understanding to develop multicolour blinking UCNPs. These probes enable upconversion-enabled STORM (U-STORM), a super-resolution imaging approach that achieves simultaneous multicolour imaging with a single excitation source and a single imaging round (Fig. 1a). Using U-STORM, we attain localization precisions down to 0.62 Å, resolve homodimers and heterodimers separated by sub-diffraction distances, and visualize epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) dimers and multimers in cell membranes. Together, these results validate our U-STORM platform as a powerful tool for high-precision nanoscopy.

Engineering UCNPs to achieve spontaneous blinking with low duty cycle

Lanthanide-doped β-NaYF4 UCNPs emit visible photons through a multistep upconversion process in which NIR excitation is absorbed by Yb3+ sensitizers and subsequently transferred to nearby Tm3+ or Er3+ emitters31,45. In this process, Yb3+ acts as a reservoir of excitation energy, making energy transfer efficiency highly dependent on the sensitizer–emitter composition. We, therefore, hypothesized that tuning the Yb3+ concentration would allow control over emission dynamics at the single-particle level. To test this, we synthesized a series of small-sized core–shell-type UCNPs with systematically varied Yb3+ and Tm3+ concentrations in an ~8–9-nm β-NaYF4 active core (Supplementary Fig. 1). The cores were then passivated with a thin (~1 nm) inert β-NaY0.8Gd0.2F4 shell to minimize energy loss due to non-radiative surface quenching as well as stabilize emission46,47 (Fig. 2a). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the formation of uniform, monodisperse nanocrystals (Fig. 2b,c and Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). We first fixed the Tm3+ concentration at a low value of ~0.5% and systematically varied the Yb3+ content, examining the resulting emission behaviour in the blue channel at the single-particle level (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 1). At low Yb3+ content (~10%), UCNPs exhibited no blinking, as expected. However, increasing Yb3+ to 20% began to induce minimal blinking, with some blinking particles appearing at 30% Yb3+, and by 40%, essentially all particles within the field of view (FOV) displayed spontaneous blinking (Supplementary Video 1). Further increases in Yb3+ content made blinking progressively prominent, with particles spending less time in the ON state and more in the OFF state. To quantify this behaviour and identify the optimal U-STORM probe, we analysed intensity–time traces from more than 100 single UCNPs, extracting distributions and mean values for key switching properties, including (1) duty cycle, (2) number of photons emitted per second per ON event (PPS) and (3) ON and OFF dwell times. At moderate Yb3+ concentrations (40%–60%), duty cycle distributions were relatively broad, but as the Yb3+ content increased to 80%–99.5%, they became narrower, indicating greater uniformity in blinking behaviour (Fig. 2d and Extended Data Fig. 1). The mean duty cycle decreased progressively with Yb3+ doping and dropped to ~1% at ≥90% (Fig. 2e, shaded region), reaching a minimum of ~0.9% at 99.5% Yb3+. Higher Yb3+ levels also shortened the ON dwell time and extended OFF dwell time, collectively favouring low duty cycles (Fig. 2f)—a critical attribute for an optimal U-STORM probe. If a single emitter spends a large fraction of time in the ON state (high duty cycle), its PSF will probably overlap with those of nearby emitters, hindering precise localization, especially in high-density labelling scenarios. Hence, low duty cycles are essential for achieving super-resolved STORM images. At the same time, localization precision demands sufficient photon output during ON states. To further optimize for lower duty cycles, we investigated the influence of Tm3+ concentration and held Yb3+ at ~80%. At high Tm3+ levels (4%–20%), UCNPs emitted primarily in the NIR regime without blinking (Fig. 2g and Extended Data Fig. 2). Reducing Tm3+ to 2% produced flickering between high and low intensities in the blue channel without reaching the complete-OFF states (Supplementary Video 2). At 1% Tm3+, spontaneous ON–OFF blinking occurred. Reducing the Tm3+ concentration led to longer OFF times and shorter ON times, yielding a reduced duty cycle (Fig. 2h,i). Decreasing the Tm3+ from 0.5% to 0.25% does not decrease the duty cycle noticeably but it decreases the PPS count slightly. Together, these results indicate that optimal blinking defined by a low duty cycle and high photon yield is achieved at high Yb3+ content and low Tm3+ loading (≤0.5%). On the basis of these results, we optimized our blue-emitting U-STORM probe as β-NaYb0.995Tm0.005F4@β-NaY0.8Gd0.2F4. By precisely tuning the relative concentrations of Yb3+ and Tm3+, we achieved control over the blinking dynamics of the UCNP system, with duty cycles spanning from ~100% (non-blinking) to ~0.9% (highly blinking).

Indefinite blinking without photobleaching and statistical aging

Having established small-sized, spontaneously blinking UCNPs with low duty cycles, we next evaluated the stability of their blinking behaviour under long-term continuous laser excitation. Conventional blinking probes, including quantum dots, typically suffer from two major instabilities: photobleaching and statistical aging. In photobleaching, emission abruptly ceases or gradually diminishes due to irreversible photochemical damage under prolonged laser illumination48–50. Figure 2j shows a representative intensity–time trace of a single blue-emitting U-STORM probe under continuous excitation power of 20 kW cm−2 for over 10 h. The trace exhibits a well-defined bimodal intensity distribution between the ON and OFF states (Fig. 2k), with no observable degradation over time. Both average photon counts per ON event and the duty cycle remained stable throughout the experiment, confirming long-term photostability and uniform blinking behaviour over long-term acquisition (Fig. 2l).

Statistical aging, commonly observed in quantum dots, describes a time-dependent shift in blinking behaviour under prolonged excitation, where emitters enter increasingly longer OFF states that lead to reduced emission intensity and diminished localization density during extended period of imaging51–53. To determine whether our UCNPs are susceptible to this effect, we continuously imaged 100 immobilized particles for 3 h. Both total ensemble intensity and ON-particle fraction remained stable without showing any observable decrease over time (Fig. 2m,n). Moreover, the average number of cumulative ON–OFF switching cycles increased steadily with acquisition time, effectively eliminating the loss of localization events typically associated with statistical aging (Fig. 2o).

Achieving ångström-level localization precision enabled by indefinite blinking

Given the long-term blinking stability of our engineered UCNPs, we next investigated the localization precision attainable through repeated localizations. Figure 3a shows a diffraction-limited image of a single blue-emitting U-STORM probe overlaid with the two-dimensional (2D) histogram of ~88,000 localizations accumulated over multiple imaging frames. Fitting this localization cloud to a 2D Gaussian profile yielded a standard deviation of ~18 nm, consistent with the per-frame localization precision typically achieved in the conventional SMLM method (σSMLM; Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4). Remarkably, because these UCNPs blink indefinitely, thousands of localization events can be measured over time. This temporal averaging dramatically narrows the distribution, producing an inner Gaussian with effective localization precisions of 0.62 Å (σUCNP; Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4f), derived from the standard error of the mean. Figure 3c shows the corresponding scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image, confirming that the analysed PSF indeed came from a single UCNP. We next applied this approach to resolve multiple UCNPs confined within a diffraction-limited spot. Figure 3d shows a wide-field optical image that clearly cannot resolve individual UCNPs. However, fitting every image frame to a 2D Gaussian profile revealed eight localization clusters within this single diffraction-limited spot (Fig. 3d–f). Uncovering the blinking mechanism

To elucidate the origin of spontaneous blinking, we systematically varied the core–shell architecture, starting from core-only UCNPs and progressively increasing the thickness of an inert shell. Core-only particles showed no noticeable blinking (Fig. 4a,b). Blinking emerged on introduction of an ultrathin inert shell (~0.36 nm) and the duty cycle decreased markedly with increasing shell thickness before reaching a stable, low value. Although the increase in photon emission confirms the suppression of surface-related quenching, the simultaneous appearance of blinking points to an interfacial origin. Owing to the mismatch in ionic radii between the Yb3+-rich core and Y3+-rich inert shell, structural disorder at the core–shell interface is unavoidable. To understand how excitation energy is distributed within the nanoparticle, we performed differential rate equation simulations (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Note 1). The simulations show that energy migration among Yb3+ ions becomes increasingly efficient at high Yb3+ and low Tm3+ concentrations, maximizing energy transfer from Yb3+ to Tm3+. Consequently, in highly Yb-rich and sparsely Tm-doped systems, excitation energy can efficiently migrate through the Yb3+ network and accumulate onto individual Tm3+ ions.

On the basis of these observations, we propose the blinking mechanism shown in Fig. 4c. In the optimized blue U-STORM probe (99.5% Yb3+, 0.5% Tm3+), efficient energy migration populates higher-lying excited states of Tm3+. Electrons from these states can be captured by interfacial trap states, quenching emission and producing the OFF state29. Emission recovers when the trapped electron is released, either through non-radiative relaxation or by returning to an emissive Tm3+ level. The stochastic trapping and release of electrons, therefore, drives the spontaneous ON–OFF switching.

To gain further insight, we examined larger-core UCNPs with the same composition (Extended Data Fig. 4). Similar to smaller particles, core-only large UCNPs did not blink, whereas shelled particles displayed intensity fluctuations that become more pronounced as the shell thickness increases. However, instead of distinct ON and OFF states, larger particles exhibited intermediate-intensity levels and rarely reached complete quenching. As the core size increases, the surface-to-volume ratio decreases, reducing the relative contribution of interfacial regions and thereby diminishing the effective influence of interface-related traps. In addition, larger nanocrystals contain a greater number of Tm3+ emitters, such that the trapping of a single electron or even multiple trapping events is insufficient to fully suppress emission from all active ions simultaneously. Instead, only partial quenching occurs, leading to intermediate-intensity states rather than completely OFF states.

We next investigated the effect of excitation power (Supplementary Fig. 5). The duty cycle decreased with increasing laser power before reaching a plateau. This trend is consistent with the proposed mechanism: higher excitation powers increase Yb3+ energy migration and energy transfer to Tm3+ ions, promoting the population of higher-excited states and increasing access to interfacial trap states. Differential rate equation simulations support this interpretation, showing enhanced energy transfer rates at higher excitation powers (Extended Data Fig. 3e,f).

Together, these results support a mechanism in which spontaneous blinking arises from efficient energy funnelling within a highly Yb3+-rich lattice coupled to electron trapping at interfacial defect states. Particle size, shell thickness, interface quality and excitation power collectively govern the duty cycle and blinking dynamics. Although this model explains the major observations, further studies will be required to directly probe the relevant trap states and elucidate the detailed electronic transitions involved54.

Engineering multicolour U-STORM probes

To determine whether the blinking mechanism could be extended to other lanthanide emitters for multicolour imaging, we replaced Tm3+ with Er3+ to generate red-emitting UCNPs (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). Systematic variation of Yb3+ and Er3+ concentrations revealed blinking behaviour analogous to that of blue-emitting probes (Fig. 5a,b and Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6). The optimized red-emitting probes exhibited stable blinking without photobleaching or statistical aging (Extended Data Fig. 7d–h). To further probe the blinking mechanism, we analysed the shell thickness, particle size, excitation power and rate equation (Extended Data Fig. 9 and Supplementary Figs. 5 and 9). The results closely mirrored those of the blue-emitting probes, supporting a common blinking mechanism and demonstrating that the same design principles can be extended to multicolour lanthanide systems (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Simultaneous multicolour super-resolution imaging with ångstrom precision

Using the engineered blue- and red-emitting U-STORM probes, we achieved simultaneous multicolour super-resolution imaging with ångström-level precision. In a proof-of-concept experiment, a mixture of blue and red UCNPs was imaged simultaneously. As shown in Fig. 5c, diffraction-limited images in both channels displayed only a single broad spot, obscuring the number of contributing emitters. By contrast, U-STORM localized individual UCNPs with ångström precision and reconstructed ten emitters within the same diffraction-limited region, in agreement with SEM. U-STORM identified two Er3+/Tm3+ UCNP heterodimers that were indistinguishable in monochromatic SEM because of their similar morphology and contrast, highlighting the advantage of multicolour imaging. Furthermore, U-STORM enabled the measurement of nanometre-scale separations between spectrally distinct UCNPs with ångström precision (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Note 2).

Conclusion

We have developed a new class of small-sized (~10 nm), spontaneously blinking UCNPs that markedly improves the localization precision of STORM super-resolution imaging enabling sub-ångström precision, three orders of magnitude beyond that achievable by conventional fluorophores. When applied to biological imaging, U-STORM reveals molecular structures with high specificity and single-protein resolution. After conjugating these UCNPs to HaloTag ligands, we directly visualized EGFR dimers and multimers with a single NIR excitation, without imaging buffers or multiple lasers. The minimal non-specific binding and stable blinking behaviour under physiological conditions make these U-STORM probes ideal for multitarget biological investigations with ångström precision and high spatial resolution, offering a new window into nanoscale protein organization and signalling.

In addition, we uncovered the physical origin of this blinking behaviour, linking it to efficient energy funneling within a Yb3+-rich lattice coupled to electron trapping at interfacial defect states. This insight established a general framework for engineering switching dynamics in lanthanide nanocrystals and introduces a new design principle in the field of nanophotonics and optical materials.

Although the current implementation is limited by particle size, brightness and the availability of two emission colours, these challenges provide clear directions for future development. Smaller UCNPs could reduce steric and localization constraints, further improving spatial resolution, whereas brighter probes could enhance single-frame localization precision and enable faster imaging through higher frame rates and shorter acquisition times. Such improvements could also expand the applicability of U-STORM to live-cell imaging by reducing the time required to accumulate sufficient localizations from slowly moving or spatially confined targets. Expanding the emission palette through co-doping strategies or the incorporation of additional lanthanide emitters could generate multiple spectrally distinct blinking probes, enabling the simultaneous imaging of several molecular targets within the same diffraction-limited region.

This work demonstrates multicolour super-resolution microscopy in which the probe itself—not the instrument or additional reagents—provides the switching mechanism. Spontaneously and indefinitely blinking UCNPs offer an easy-to-implement yet powerful route towards simultaneous, high-precision multicolour imaging of molecular structures in complex biological systems.

Methods

Nanoparticle synthesis

Core–shell-type β-NaYF4-based nanoparticles were prepared following a modified thermal decomposition protocol previously used in our laboratory26. For the core synthesis, 2 mmol of total lanthanide acetates (Ln = Y3+, Yb3+, Er3+ or Tm3+, proportioned according to the desired doping ratios) were mixed with 18.25 ml of oleic acid and 20 ml of octadecene in a 250-ml three-neck flask. The mixture was degassed under a vacuum at 110 °C for 1 h to remove residual moisture. Subsequently, 6.25 mmol of sodium oleate, 10 mmol of ammonium fluoride, 1.9 mmol of oleylamine (no oleylamine for <40% Yb3+) and 8.75 ml of octadecene were added, and the mixture was stirred under a vacuum at 25 °C for 1 h. The reaction was then heated to 310 °C under a nitrogen flow and maintained for 50 min before being rapidly cooled. The resulting core nanocrystals were precipitated using ethanol and centrifuged at 5,000g for 5 min. The pellet was further washed with hexane and ethanol mixture (1:1.5 volume ration) followed by another round of centrifugation at 5,000g for 5 min. Finally, the pellet was redispersed in 50 ml of hexane.

For shell growth, 2.4 ml of the core nanoparticle dispersion was mixed with 4 ml of oleic acid and 6 ml of octadecene, and hexane was removed under a vacuum at 70 °C for 30 min. The solution was then heated to 290 °C under a nitrogen flow. Shell precursors (previously prepared) were added in three sequential injections at 15-min intervals as follows: (1) 0.50 ml of Y/Gd precursor; (2) 0.25 ml of sodium trifluoroacetate (NaTFA) solution and 0.40 ml of Y/Gd precursor; (3) 0.35 ml of NaTFA and 0.85 ml of Y/Gd precursor. After the final injection, the reaction was held at 290 °C for 17 min and then quenched to room temperature. The nanoparticles were purified by ethanol–hexane washing, as done above. For the Tm3+ and Er3+ series of UCNP cores with different concentrations of Yb3+ and dopants, only the first three injection steps were used. The purified core–shell nanoparticles were dispersed in 5 ml of hexane. For nanoparticles with a higher shell thickness, we increased the injection rounds to get the desired shell thickness26.

The Y/Gd precursor was prepared by heating 2 mmol of yttrium acetate and 0.5 mmol of gadolinium acetate in 10 ml of oleic acid and 15 ml of octadecene under a vacuum at 110 °C for 15 min. The mixture was then heated to 160 °C for 30 min under a nitrogen flow and subsequently cooled to room temperature. The NaTFA solution was obtained by dissolving 6 mmol of NaTFA in 15 ml of oleic acid under a vacuum at 50 °C until fully dissolved.

Surface functionalization

For surface modification, 1 ml of as-prepared core–shell nanoparticles was dried under a vacuum and mixed with 150 mg of poly(maleic anhydride-alt-1-octadecene) (PMAO) dissolved in 10 ml of chloroform64. The reaction was stirred for 1.5 h at room temperature, after which chloroform was removed under a vacuum. The particles were redispersed by sonication in 10 ml of 100-mM NaF solution containing 150 mg of 4-(dimethylamino) pyridine and centrifuged at 32,000g for 2 h. The precipitate was washed once with NaF solution to remove excess reagents and dispersed in 3 ml of 1× PBS supplemented with 100 mM of NaF.

For HaloTag ligand conjugation, 1.5 ml of PMAO-coated nanoparticles was mixed with 6 µl of 350-mM methyl-PEG-amine (MA(PEG)24-NH2) and 8 µl of 8-mM HaloTag ligand-PEG-amine under stirring. Then, 30 mg of EDC dissolved in 100 µl of 1× PBS was added, and the reaction proceeded for 2 h. Another 30 mg of EDC and 6 µl of 350-mM MA(PEG)24-NH2 were then added and reacted for 2 h. The resulting ligand-conjugated nanoparticles were centrifuged at 20,000g for 2 h. The supernatant containing mostly monodispersed-ligand-conjugated nanoparticles was then concentrated and washed using an Amicon 100-kDa centrifugal filter.

The HaloTag-PEG-amine conjugate was prepared in two sequential reactions55. In the first step, 150 µl of 10-mM HaloTag Ligand Building Blocks Amine (O4) dissolved in anhydrous DMF was combined with 15 µl of 100-mM succinimidyl 4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate and 1 µl of triethylamine. The reaction mixture was incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow for the formation of a maleimide-activated intermediate. Subsequently, 10 µl of 150-mM SH-PEG-NH2 in anhydrous DMF was added, and the reaction was continued for 10 h at room temperature. The final product was stored at −20 °C until further use.

Electron microscopy measurement

TEM was used to characterize the morphology and size distribution of the synthesized nanoparticles. The TEM images were acquired on an FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Twin microscope operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. Multiple micrographs were collected for each sample to ensure statistical accuracy. The particle size distributions of both core and core–shell samples were obtained by measuring the projected area of at least 700 individual nanoparticles and converting it to the corresponding equivalent diameter. The mean particle diameter and standard deviation were then calculated, and the difference between the core and core–shell mean diameters was used to estimate the shell thickness. Representative TEM images and the corresponding size histograms for core and core–shell nanoparticles are shown in Supplementary Figs. 2, 3, 5 and 6, whereas Supplementary Fig. 10 presents the representative images and size distributions of HaloTag-ligand-conjugated nanoparticles.

For correlative measurements, SEM was performed on the same FOV previously imaged optically. The UCNP samples were prepared on an ibidi gridded glass coverslip with a 50-µm grid. A 50-µl aliquot of poly-L-lysine solution (0.1% w/v in water) was first applied to the coverslip and rinsed after 1 min to form a layer that promotes nanoparticle attachment and minimizes lateral movement. The nanoparticle dispersion was then added and unbound or loosely adsorbed particles were removed by washing with hexane. After drying, optical imaging was performed, and the grid coordinates of the imaged regions were recorded for correlation. The same slide was then coated with a thin Au–Pd alloy (~3–4 nm in thickness) layer by sputtering to prepare it for SEM measurement. SEM imaging was conducted using a ZEISS Gemini 450 microscope equipped with an in-lens secondary electron detector.

Structural and chemical characterization

For the structural characterization of the optimized U-STORM probes, we performed X-ray diffraction measurements. First, the samples were concentrated by evaporating 600 µl of each UCNP dispersion to dryness followed by redispersion in 50 µl of hexane. The resulting concentrated solution was then drop cast onto cleaned glass slides and air dried to form uniform thin films. X-ray diffraction measurements were performed using a PANalytical X’Pert diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα1 radiation source (λ = 1.54056 Å). Data were collected in the Bragg–Brentano geometry using a 2θ–ω scan configuration. The obtained diffraction patterns were compared with the reference data from the International Centre for Diffraction Data. All samples exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to the hexagonal β-phase NaYF4 crystal structure, confirming successful synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 1)

The elemental compositions and dopant ratios of the optimized U-STORM probes were determined by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) optical emission spectroscopy. Approximately 5 mg of each dried nanoparticle sample was used, assuming an organic mass fraction of ~50%. Each sample was dissolved in 3% (v/v) aqueous nitric acid prepared from ICP-grade reagents to yield a final concentration of 10 mg ml−1. The calibration standards for individual lanthanide elements were obtained from Agilent and used to generate standard curves for quantitative analysis. The measured elemental concentrations (ppm) were converted to relative molar percentages to determine the lanthanide composition of each sample (Supplementary Table 4). The reported values represent the overall stoichiometry of the nanoparticles and do not distinguish between the core and shell compositions.

Wide-field microscopy setup

Wide-field imaging was performed on a custom-built microscope equipped with two diode lasers, as described previously: a 976-nm fibre-coupled laser (Thorlabs, BL976-PAG900) and a 638-nm free-space laser (Cobolt, 06-MLD)26. The 976-nm NIR excitation beam was reflected by a 950-nm dichroic mirror into an upright Nikon ×60 oil-immersion objective (numerical aperture, 1.49), onto which the sample was mounted. A motorized mirror allowed the excitation beam to be displaced laterally at the back focal plane of the objective, enabling rapid switching between epifluorescence and total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) illumination modes (Mad City Labs, TIRF Module). For all the measurements, the samples were illuminated in the epifluorescence mode with a power density of ~20 kW cm−2. Fluorescence emission was collected through the same objective, passed through the 950-nm dichroic mirror and focused by a 400-mm tube lens through a 950-nm short-pass filter. The emission beam then passed through an iris aperture and into a light-tight detection box, where it was spectrally divided into four channels using dichroic mirrors at 510, 610 and 705 nm. Each channel was collimated with a 255-mm lens and recombined using a symmetric set of dichroics (Supplementary Fig. 13). The emission was finally focused by a 300-mm lens, resulting in a calibrated image scale of 100 nm per pixel. The four spectrally separated images were directed to occupy distinct quadrants on the camera detector (Andor Solis iXon Ultra 897 electron-multiplying charge-coupled device), with the iris adjusted to prevent crosstalk between channels. For single-colour imaging, only the blue or NIR or red emission channels corresponding to the UCNPs were recorded, whereas for multicolour super-resolution imaging, both blue and red channels were acquired simultaneously. The 638-nm laser was directed into a micromirror assembly positioned below the back port of the objective at an angle producing total internal reflection at the sample interface (Mad City Labs, RM21 MicroMirror TIRF, MCL TIRF Lock module). The reflected beam was captured by the micromirrors and focused on a quadrant photodetector. When axial drift (z) occurs during TIRF illumination, the reflected beam angle changes, leading to a shift in illumination in the quadrants of the photodiode. This shift can be calibrated to reflect the axial drift and used afterwards to actively monitor and correct axial (z) drift, ensuring stable focus during extended imaging sessions. All lenses were sourced from Thorlabs and are AB coated. Dichroics were sourced from Semrock (AVR optics). The microscope stage and emission-path optomechanics were sourced from Mad City Labs. Mirror mounts were sourced from Newport Corporation, and all other optic elements were sourced from Thorlabs.

Cell culture

U2OS human osteosarcoma cells (HTB-96, ATCC) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Life Technologies) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich) and 1× penicillin–streptomycin (Life Technologies). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator under 5% CO2. Cultures were routinely screened for mycoplasma contamination to ensure cell-line integrity.

Plasmid construction and lentivirus transduction

To generate HaloTag7-EGFR (HaloTag-EGFR) fusion constructs, the HaloTag7 coding sequence was cloned in-frame with EGFR into a pSin lentiviral expression vector55. Lentiviral particles were produced by the transient transfection of HEK293T packaging cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Life Technologies) in an Opti-MEM medium (Life Technologies), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The medium was replaced with a standard growth medium after 6 h, and viral supernatants were collected and filtered at 48 h and 72 h post-transfection. For stable expression, U2OS cells at 60%–70% confluency were incubated with the viral supernatant for 4 h, followed by recovery in a complete medium and subsequent selection with 1 µg ml−1 of puromycin (Life Technologies) for 3–5 days. The resulting U2OS-HaloTag-EGFR cell line was maintained under puromycin selection for downstream labelling and imaging experiments.

EGFR labelling and cell imaging with U-STORM workflow

For live-cell labelling, cells were first blocked with 1% (w/v) casein solution (Sigma) prepared in DMEM for 5 min to minimize non-specific binding. Subsequently, the nanoparticle mixture containing 5 nM of both blue- and red-emitting HaloTag-ligand-conjugated U-STORM probes dispersed in 1% casein and DMEM were added, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 5 min. After that, unbound nanoparticles were removed by washing with 1% casein and DMEM. For EGFR activation with EGF, we added 100-nM EGF in DMEM post-washing and incubated for 5 min. Next, we fixed the cells using 4% paraformaldehyde solution (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 15 min at room temperature. The samples were then washed with 1× PBS three times before imaging. After that, an optimized concentration of PMAO-coated non-blinking UCNPs dispersed in 1× PBS solution was added to the system and used as fiducial markers for drift correction. For U-STORM imaging on the cell sample, an acquisition rate of 300 ms per frame was used with an excitation power of ~20 kW cm−2. Both blue and red channels were recorded simultaneously with a single excitation without using any imaging buffers or external modulations. The focal plane was positioned at the basal membrane of the cell and maintained throughout each imaging session.

U-STORM analysis

All the STORM videos were recorded using the Andor Solis software (iXon Ultra, Solis version: 4.31.30024.0) with a 300 electron-multiplying gain and 300-ms exposure time. After generating a stack of .tiff files from the video, the localizations of the particles present in the ON state are performed using the MATLAB software SLIMfast2 (ref. 65). The localization parameters were kept constant between videos and conditions (Supplementary Table 3), except the ‘Intensity Threshold’ parameter that was changed on a case-by case basis to account for varying background levels and avoid any crosstalk. After collecting the localizations from all the particles from all the frames, the next step was to perform drift correction. For drift correction, we used our bright non-blinking co-doped UCNPs, which emit photons in both blue and red channels as fiducial markers. First, we measured the drift for each frame from all the markers present in the FOV. Next, we calculate the average drift per frame by taking an average of the drifts associated to each marker. Then, we subtract this average drift from the raw localization dataset to obtain the drift-corrected localization clusters (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Image registration between the blue and red channels was performed using the localization centroids of fiducial markers. A global affine transformation matrix was first generated by analysing the positions of multiple fiducial markers located near the measured FOV66. After applying this global correction, a local affine transformation matrix was constructed using fiducial markers within the same FOV to refine the alignment between channels. Following drift correction and image registration, individual localization clusters were identified using the density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) algorithm implemented in MATLAB. Each detected cluster was then fitted with a 2D Gaussian model to determine its centroid position. In cases where two clusters were in close proximity and partially overlapped, intensity thresholding was applied to separate localizations corresponding to different intensity regimes, allowing independent localization and centroid extraction for each emitter.

Blinking analysis

Blinking parameters, including duty cycle, PPS values, ON–OFF dwell times and statistical aging, were calculated from the intensity–time traces of single UCNPs. A threshold intensity was applied to separate ON and OFF events. The threshold was typically chosen as an intensity value higher than the mean of the low-intensity distribution, corresponding to approximately three to four times its standard deviation. Any intensity above this threshold intensity value is considered as an ON event. For probes that exhibited pure blinking, the OFF states matched the background signal. In cases where flickering occurred and the lowest-intensity level did not coincide with the background, the lowest-intensity state was treated as the OFF state, and the threshold was determined accordingly. After ON and OFF events were identified, the duty cycle was computed as the fraction of the total acquisition time spent in the ON state. The PPS values were obtained by converting electron-multiplying charge-coupled device counts to photon numbers using the calibration factor, followed by calculating the average number of photons emitted per second during ON events.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
