Effectiveness of Five-Level Prehospital Taiwan Triage and Acuity Scale (TTAS-PH) in Emergency Medical Services for Patient Outcome Prediction: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jui-Fan Kao, Yung-Hsiang Chang, Cheng-Yu Chien, Chip-Jin Ng, Chien-Hsiung Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveTriage is a cornerstone of modern emergency medicine. This study aimed to evaluate the predictive validity of the five-level Prehospital Taiwan Triage and Acuity Scale (TTAS-PH) for emergency department (ED) patient outcomes—specifically hospital admission, mortality, and ED length of stay (LOS)—compared to the former two-level system.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 9,698 adult patients transported by Emergency Medical Services (EMS) to a tertiary academic medical center in Taoyuan City, Taiwan, during 2023. Logistic and linear regression models were utilized to analyze the impact of variables on clinical outcomes and ED LOS. Furthermore, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis evaluated the predictive performance of the triage systems.ResultsMale gender, older age, and higher TTAS-PH acuity levels (Levels 1 and 2) were significantly associated with worse ED outcomes and longer ED LOS. TTAS-PH Level 1 patients faced drastically elevated risks of death (OR=83.9); while they represented only 10.72% of the cohort, they accounted for 79.59% of all ED deaths. Notably, a discrepancy was observed in high-acuity (Levels 1–2) classification between the prehospital TTAS-PH (43%) and the in-hospital TTAS (8.7%). In predicting mortality, TTAS-PH demonstrated superior accuracy (AUROC=0.8829) compared to the traditional TPTS (AUROC=0.8296).ConclusionThe TTAS-PH system offers robust prehospital risk stratification, demonstrating superior predictive performance compared to the traditional TPTS. By functioning as a critical early warning mechanism, TTAS-PH facilitates proactive resource mobilization, effectively shifting emergency care from a reactive to a preemptive model.

Introduction

Triage is a cornerstone of modern emergency medicine, serving as a critical process to systematically assess patient severity, prioritize care, and optimize resource allocation to improve clinical outcomes.1 Globally, various five-level triage systems have been developed to meet this need,2,3 with the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS)4–6 and the Emergency Severity Index (ESI) being among the most widely adopted and validated tools.7,8 The reliability and performance of such systems are pivotal for managing patient flow and ensuring that the most critically ill individuals receive timely intervention.

In Taiwan, the landscape of emergency care was significantly advanced with the introduction of the five-level Taiwan Triage and Acuity Scale (TTAS) in 2010. Adapted from CTAS, this computerized system utilizes 163 chief complaints and dynamically integrates objective clinical modifiers—such as mechanism of injury, vital signs, and pain indices, to enable precise severity assessment by emergency department (ED) staff. The implementation of TTAS in hospitals has been a notable success; subsequent studies have validated its effectiveness for predicting resource utilization and its applicability to specific groups, such as pediatric patients, firmly establishing it as a robust in-hospital triage standard in Taiwan.9,10

The principles of triage extending beyond the hospital walls in the prehospital environment are equally critical, where early identification of high-risk patients directly influences outcomes.11–14 Consequently, given the successful experience of the TTAS in hospitals, the prehospital version (TTAS-PH) was implemented to improve the Taiwan Emergency Medical Service (EMS)’s conventional two-level field triage system. The traditional two-level prehospital triage system, which broadly classified patients as “emergent” or “non-emergent,” has been limited by its lack of clinical granularity and precision in complex scenarios. To address these limitations, the five-level TTAS-PH was developed to provide refined acuity stratification. Mirroring in-hospital protocols, the TTAS-PH categorizes patients into Level 1 (Resuscitation), Level 2 (Emergent), Level 3 (Urgent), Level 4 (Less urgent), and Level 5 (Non-urgent). This expanded framework empowers emergency medical services (EMS) to optimize prehospital resource allocation, improve transport decisions, and facilitate seamless patient handovers. Initial research has shown that TTAS-PH demonstrates superior inter-rater reliability between emergency medical technicians (EMTs)15 and nurses and more accurately predicts the need for hospital admission16 compared to its predecessor.9,17,18 However, a comprehensive evaluation of its effectiveness in predicting a broader spectrum of critical patient outcomes—including mortality, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and ED length of stay (LOS)—remains less explored.

This study aims to address this gap by evaluating the predictive validity of the five-level TTAS-PH using a large dataset of EMS-transported patients to a tertiary academic medical center in Taiwan. We seek to determine its effectiveness in forecasting key clinical trajectories, specifically hospital admission, mortality, and ED-LOS. We hypothesize that the systematic risk stratification provided by TTAS-PH is significantly associated with these patient outcomes, thereby validating its role as an indispensable tool in modern prehospital emergency care.

Materials and Method

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective observational association cohort study was designed as a validation study to evaluate the predictive performance of the 5-level TTAS-PH in a real-world prehospital setting. Conducted in Taoyuan City, Taiwan, between January 1 and December 31, 2023, the primary objective was to validate the tool’s accuracy in predicting key clinical outcomes, specifically hospital admission, mortality, and ED LOS. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee on Human Research of Taiwan’s Chang Gung Medical Foundation (permit number: Ref. No. 202301667B0). Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Taoyuan City, located in northern Taiwan, is a municipality that encompasses urban, rural, and mountainous areas. As of 2024, it had a population of approximately 2.26 million and a population density of 1853 individuals per square kilometer. EMS in Taoyuan City are operated by the Taoyuan Fire Department (TYFD). The EMS system consists of a single dispatch center and 40 ambulance stations, handling approximately 90,000 EMS cases to hospital annually.

Starting in 2022, in recognition of the limitations inherent in the two-level triage system, the TYFD integrated the more detailed five-level TTAS-PH into its electronic Patient Care Report (ePCR) system for pre-hospital patient assessment, drawing reference from the existing TTAS (Table Supplementary 1). However, the original Taiwan Prehospital Triage Scale (TPTS) was not phased out; instead, the ePCR system was designed to record data from both triage scales simultaneously.

The ED and post-ED data of this study was conducted at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (LCGMH), a major tertiary care, academic teaching hospital, and level I trauma center with 3,600 beds. Its emergency department manages approximately 180,000 visits annually and is equipped with advanced imaging facilities, including a CT scan room and two X-ray rooms. Providing 24-hour surgical and medical subspecialty services, LCGMH serves as a key referral center for northern Taiwan, addressing diverse emergency cases.

Participants and Data Collections

The inclusion criteria for this study were patients who utilized the 119 emergency medical services (EMS) in Taoyuan City and were subsequently transported to the emergency department at Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (LCGMH) between January 1 and December 31, 2023. Out of 125,279 cases who utilized the EMS system during this period, patients were excluded from the initial cohort based on the following criteria: (1) non-transport incidents, (2) psychological or behavioral emergencies (as their triage and clinical outcomes are primarily driven by specific behavioral criteria rather than standard physiological parameters), (3) COVID-19-related cases, and (4) cases with missing prehospital or in-hospital triage data. After these initial exclusions, 85,956 cases were transported to various hospitals. Patients transported to hospitals other than LCGMH were then excluded, leaving 10,713 patients who arrived at LCGMH. Furthermore, among these remaining 10,713 patients, an additional 1,015 cases were excluded based on specific criteria: (1) direct admissions to the delivery room (n=46), (2) unregistered emergency room visits (n=34), and (3) pediatric patients aged < 18 years (n=935). This rigorous selection process resulted in a final study population of 9,698 adult patients.

All EMTs from the TYFD participating in this study completed a specialized training program on the TTAS-PH, which included a four-hour session covering theoretical concepts, practical exercises, and the use of triage modifiers and a computer-assisted decision-making system. EMTs assigned triage scores on-scene using a tablet. The system allowed EMTs to select predefined options, with automated triage level determination and manual adjustment if needed. Apart from the TTAS-PH score, the computer will automatically calculate the score of two-level TPTS simultaneously. Patient data, including demographics, dispatch details, and triage scores, were stored in the TYFD EMS cloud database. ED LOS was calculated in hours as the time interval from the moment of registration in the ED to the moment of physical departure or transfer, along with five-level TTAS scores assigned by registered nurses (RNs).

Outcomes and Measures

This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the TTAS-PH in predicting patient outcomes, including hospital discharge, admission, mortality, and ED LOS, and critical events (defined as interventions indicating severe physiological compromise in the ED, specifically the requirement of CPR, endotracheal intubation, or vasopressors, intensive care unit admission, and ED mortality) following transportation by TYFD EMTs to the ED at LCGMH. The study additionally examined how factors such as patient’s age group influenced LOS and clinical outcomes, providing insights into the relationship between triage scores and the intensity of care required.

Statistical Analysis

This study employed various statistical methods to analyze patient outcomes, particularly ED LOS and discharge status (discharged, admitted, or deceased). Categorical variables, such as gender and triage levels, were compared using chi-square tests, while non-normally distributed continuous variables, including age and ED LOS, were analyzed with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) then were reported for these continuous variables. Furthermore, we analyzed the impact of various variables on ED LOS and clinical outcomes using logistic and linear regression models.

Cumulative incidence function (CIF) was used to illustrate the time-dependent probabilities of discharge, admission, and death, stratified by TTAS-PH levels. This analysis provided insights into how ED LOS varied across different triage categories. Additionally, we evaluated the predictive performance of TTAS-PH in forecasting ED outcomes by comparing it to other prehospital triage systems using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.

To rule out potential multicollinearity between predictor variables, particularly between patient age and TTAS-PH levels, Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were examined. All VIF values were found to be well within acceptable limits (< 5), indicating no severe multicollinearity in our regression models.

All data analysis was performed using R software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Patients Enrollment and Characteristic

From January 1 to December 31, 2023, 125,279 patients utilized 119 emergency services, with 39,323 cases excluded due to non-transport incidents, psychological/behavioral cases, COVID-19-related cases, or missing triage data. After exclusions, 85,956 cases involved patients transported to healthcare facilities, including 10,713 at LCGMH. Further exclusions left a final study population of 9698 adult patients. Among these, 5,281 (54.45%) were discharged, including 89.66% by physicians, 8.16% leaving against medical advice, and 0.19% leaving without being seen. Additionally, 105 patients (1.99%) were transferred. Admissions accounted for 4,221 patients (43.52%), with 84.86% admitted to general wards and 15.14% to the ICU. Main results

Multivariable logistic regression demonstrates that male gender, older age, and higher TTAS-PH acuity levels are significantly associated with worse ED outcomes (Table 3). Male patients had higher odds of ICU admission (Odds ration (OR) = 1.76), critical events (defined as a composite of ICU admission and ED mortality; OR = 1.66), and hospital admission (OR = 1.23). TTAS-PH Level 1 patients showed dramatically elevated risks: death (OR = 83.9), ICU admission (OR = 13.0), and critical events (OR = 24.6), indicating strong predictive power. Level 2 patients also had significantly increased risks across all outcomes, including death (OR = 4.35) and admission (OR = 1.63). Compared to patients aged 19–39, the risk of hospital admission clearly increases with age. Patients aged 60–79 had a significantly higher odds of admission (OR = 3.42), and those over 79 had the highest risk (OR = 5.50). Interestingly, patients aged 40–59 exhibited the highest odds for death (OR = 1.44) and ICU admission (OR = 1.94).

Multiple linear regression shows that male gender, older age, and higher TTAS-PH acuity levels are significantly associated with longer LOS in ED (Table Supplementary 2). Male patients stayed an average of 3.05 times longer than females (p < 0.0001), and ED LOS increased steadily with age: 5.80 times for ages 40–59, 11.4 for 60–79, and 15.6 for those over 79 (all p < 0.0001). Patients triaged as Level 1 or 2 also had significantly longer ED stays compared to Level 3, by 6.26 and 5.80 times, respectively (p < 0.0001), while Levels 4 and 5 showed no meaningful increase.

Discussion

Analyzing 9,698 EMS-transported adult patients, this study demonstrates that the TTAS-PH system provides robust risk stratification for ED outcomes. Our analysis identified advanced age, male gender, and high-acuity triage levels (Levels 1–2) as significant predictors of adverse clinical trajectories—including ICU admission and mortality—as well as increased ED workload manifested as prolonged LOS.

In the prehospital setting, TTAS-PH demonstrated superior predictive accuracy across all ED outcomes compared to the traditional two-level TPTS. Notably, the AUROC for mortality prediction reached 0.8829, significantly surpassing both the TPTS (0.8296) and other international early warning scores such as Modified Rapid Emergency Medicine Score (MREMS) (0.77).19 This high sensitivity is clinically vital; patients triaged as Level 1 comprised only 10.72% of the cohort but accounted for 79.59% of total ED deaths. Such concentration of mortality underscores the system’s effectiveness in minimizing under-triage and ensuring patient safety. Besides, the exceptionally high mortality odds ratio (OR = 83.9) for TTAS-PH Level 1 warrants cautious interpretation. This large effect estimate indicates potential model instability secondary to sparse data bias, driven by the near-zero mortality in the Level 3 reference group (only 9 deaths/4,557 patients). Although the precise magnitude of the OR may be mathematically inflated, the core clinical implication—that TTAS-PH Level 1 robustly identifies patients at the highest risk of death—remains valid.

A notable finding is the discrepancy in high-acuity proportions: TTAS-PH assigned 43% of patients to Levels 1–2, whereas in-hospital TTAS assigned only 8.7%. This disparity might reflect the “defensive triage” behavior of EMTs or the limited clinical information available in the field compared to the continuous observation and diagnostic tools available to ED nurses. Conversely, we observed that 8.5% of patients initially categorized into TTAS-PH Levels 4–5 subsequently experienced severe in-hospital outcomes. This subset often involves atypical presentations, where deceptively mild symptoms may mask serious conditions like intracranial hemorrhage. Furthermore, Level 3 patients represent a “zone of clinical ambiguity” characterized by heterogeneous presentations. While often physiologically stable at triage, their 4.59% mortality rate is strongly associated with high diagnostic complexity, necessitating closer monitoring than lower-acuity groups. This unexpected mortality is particularly linked to vulnerable populations—such as the elderly or immunocompromised—where critical conditions like occult sepsis or silent myocardial infarction frequently masquerade as vague complaints with initially normal vital signs, inadvertently leading to a Level 3 designation.

By functioning as an early warning mechanism, TTAS-PH allows EMS dispatchers to alert ED personnel to incoming high-acuity cases in advance. This enables proactive resource mobilization—such as reserving resuscitation bays, activating trauma teams, or notifying specialists—which can mitigate ED overcrowding and reduce door-to-treatment times.

Regarding ED-LOS, the limited discrimination in admission timing across TTAS-PH levels (Figure 2b) likely reflects systemic constraints rather than triage failure. Factors such as ED overcrowding and inpatient bed shortages can delay the admission of even high-acuity patients. Furthermore, high-acuity patients (Levels 1–2) often undergo extended observation or intensive stabilization in the ED before a final disposition is reached, which naturally prolongs their stay compared to lower-acuity patients who are either quickly discharged or admitted for minor issues.

In the prehospital setting, TTAS-PH demonstrated superior predictive accuracy across all emergency department outcomes compared to the traditional two-level TPTS. Notably, both systems were significantly more effective at identifying mortality risk than forecasting hospital disposition—namely, admission and discharge rates. These findings align with the varied focus of prior research on prehospital triage. For instance, Lindskou et al evaluated the performance of National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2) in predicting 30-day mortality and ICU admissions.20 Similarly, Martín-Rodríguez et al compared seven prehospital Early Warning Scores (EWS) and identified MREMS as the most effective for predicting one-year mortality, reporting an AUROC of 0.77. This figure is notably surpassed by the 0.88 AUROC observed for TTAS-PH in our mortality prediction model.19 While other systems like Rapid Emergency Triage and Treatment System - Adult (RETTS-A) have shown high sensitivity (73%) for time-sensitive conditions,21,22 this study provides crucial insights as the first in Taiwan to evaluate discriminant capability for both patient disposition and ED LOS.

While the foundational data and initial framework of this study build upon the preliminary findings presented in a recent academic thesis,23 this study significantly expands the scope and depth of the analysis to offer novel clinical insights. Specifically, this study introduces the prediction of critical events and employs advanced regression analyses to quantify the independent predictive value of the TTAS-PH system. These methodological enhancements provide a more robust evaluation of risk stratification and patient outcome trajectories, thereby extending the clinical utility of the prehospital triage system well.

The implementation of a five-level triage system not only offers superior risk stratification compared to traditional two-level models but also functions as an early warning mechanism for emergency departments. When EMS dispatch centers or ED personnel are alerted in advance to incoming TTAS-PH Level 1 or 2 patients, critical preparations—such as reserving resuscitation bays, activating trauma teams, or notifying relevant specialists—can be initiated proactively. This early coordination drives a shift toward forward-planning emergency management, ensuring smarter resource utilization and rapid system responses. Such anticipatory coordination has the potential to alleviate ED overcrowding, reduce treatment delays, and ultimately improve patient outcomes by minimizing door-to-treatment times.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, this was a single-center study conducted at a tertiary academic medical center, and the study population was restricted to adult patients. This design may limit the external validity and generalizability of our findings, as the results may not be fully applicable to other settings, such as regional or district hospitals, resource-limited EMS systems, or pediatric populations, and could be subject to high-acuity selection bias.

Second, our analysis may be influenced by unmeasured confounding variables. For instance, factors such as ambulance transport duration, specific prehospital interventions administered, and inter-rater variability in judgment among frontline EMTs and emergency department nurses were not included in our analytical models. These factors could have influenced both the assigned triage levels and the final clinical outcomes.

Third, the scope of our analysis focused on clinical outcomes (i.e, hospital admission, mortality) and emergency department length of stay. It did not include an assessment of total medical costs or detailed resource utilization. Consequently, this study does not provide a complete picture of the broader impact of the TTAS-PH on healthcare economics and resource allocation. Future multi-center, prospective studies that include more diverse patient populations and cost-effectiveness analyses are warranted to more comprehensively validate the clinical value of the TTAS-PH.

Fourth, our “Discharged” outcome category included patients who left against medical advice (AMA) or were transferred to other facilities. While keeping these patients reflects real-world emergency department disposition and initial resource utilization, it introduces a potential source of heterogeneity and selection bias in our clinical outcome analysis.

Conclusion

TTAS-PH offers superior prehospital risk stratification, significantly outperforming the traditional TPTS. With a mortality AUROC of 0.88, Level 1 identifies 79.59% of ED deaths. As a critical early warning system, TTAS-PH facilitates proactive resource mobilization, shifting emergency care from a reactive to a preemptive model. These results validate TTAS-PH as an indispensable tool for optimizing resource allocation and clinical outcomes in modern EMS systems.
