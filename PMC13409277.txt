Behçet’s syndrome epidemiology in England: a real-world retrospective cohort analysis with a nested case-control study using linked UK primary and secondary care data
Authors: Priyanka Chandratre, Joht S Chandan, Ben Hammond, Rasiah Thayakaran, Samuel Cusworth, Nicola Adderley, Deva R Situnayake
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBehçet’s syndrome (BS) is rare in the West. Using UK-linked primary and secondary care data we examine incidence, prevalence and clinical characteristics of this multisystem syndrome.MethodsClinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum and linked Hospital Episode Statistics (HES) enabled estimates of BS epidemiology using the earliest diagnosis code in CPRD or HES between 2001 and 2021. Median time from the first clinical code of a manifestation of interest to confirmed BS diagnosis was determined. A matched case-control design (CPRD Aurum) enabled ORs for BS for a variety of preselected covariates of interest to be calculated.Results1565 incident BS cases arose in the study interval, translating to a final incidence rate of 0.75/100 000 person years at risk in 2021. Prevalence rose from 8.62 to 19.80 per 100 000 person years. In 1628 cases of BS in CPRD Aurum, matched with 11 190 controls, the triad of genital ulceration (adjusted OR (aOR) 155.08), uveitis (aOR 43.00) and oral ulceration (aOR 24.73) was significantly increased among participants with BS. ORs for Crohn’s disease (5.69) were also higher in the BS cohort compared with controls. First appearance of the clinical code for genital ulceration was associated with the shortest interval to diagnosis (median 240 days; IQR 63–745).ConclusionBS prevalence appears to increase over time in the UK, potentially linked to changing population demographics and migration, and to performance characteristics of the 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease classification criteria in low-prevalence settings. Recognised clinical characteristics and inflammatory conditions with phenotypical overlap were associated with BS. Diagnostic delay could be reduced by recognition of risk factors and differentiating clinical manifestations. Case ascertainment relied on diagnostic coding without direct validation against classification criteria, which is an important limitation.Keywords: Behcet Syndrome, Epidemiology, Rare Diseases, Systemic vasculitis

Introduction

Behçet’s syndrome (BS) is a variable vessel vasculitis, characterised by orogenital ulcers, uveitis, arthropathy, vascular thrombosis/aneurysms, bowel inflammation and cerebral vasculopathy. It may pursue a relapsing and remitting course exhibiting a variable pattern and association between phenotypical elements as it evolves; however individual phenotypical elements may also overlap with a number of other chronic relapsing and remitting inflammatory conditions, making diagnosis a challenge—particularly during the early stages of disease evolution. Its cause remains unknown but involves interplay between genetic factors and unknown environmental and pathogen-related triggers.1 2

There is no diagnostic test and clinical pattern recognition of this heterogeneous phenotype is based on the use of classification criteria. Two major criteria sets have been used: the International Study Group (ISG) criteria, developed in 1990, and the more recent 2014 International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease (ICBD). The ICBD are more sensitive but less specific than the ISG criteria, particularly in low-prevalence settings. Blake et al demonstrated in a UK cohort that application of ICBD 2014 reclassified a significant proportion of patients compared with ISG criteria, with important implications for case ascertainment in non-endemic regions.3 Diagnosis is multidisciplinary and often delayed in the UK, as from a Bayesian perspective BS has low prior probability. Behçet’s Centres of Excellence were therefore established to support a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and improve outcomes.4

BS is endemic along the Silk Route and Eastern Mediterranean basin and East Asia, where it is considerably more prevalent. UK prevalence was first estimated at 0.64 per 100 000 in a small cross-sectional General Practice (GP) survey; our recent estimates in The Health Improvement Network (THIN), a UK primary care database, were much higher at 14.61 per 100 000 (95% CI 13.35 to 15.88), but still considerably lower than endemic regions.5–7 In that study we observed an over-representation of patients with BS who were coded with other immune-mediated inflammatory diseases, particularly inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), psoriasis and axial spondyloarthritis.

Here we describe, in a real-world non-endemic setting, a national epidemiological study conducted using large National Health Service (NHS) clinical data sets, building on our earlier observations on a greatly expanded data set (Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum) with direct linkage to Hospital Episode Statistics (HES).8 We explore how the frequency of a coded BS diagnosis has changed over time and how it associates with the timing and clustering of phenotypical elements. We further explore associations with other inflammatory conditions having overlapping phenotypes, particularly IBD, to provide additional clinical context. Importantly, given the reliance on diagnostic coding rather than validated classification criteria, findings regarding case-control associations must be interpreted with awareness of potential misclassification.

Methods

Study design and data source

A cohort of eligible patients was extracted from the data set between 1 January 2001 and 1 January 2022 and used to calculate yearly incidence rate (IR) and prevalence of BS, stratified by age group, sex and ethnicity. A case-control study was also undertaken describing the OR of a variety of preselected covariates of interest in patients with BS compared with a matched control group, including key phenotypical elements linked with a diagnosis of BS and conditions known to have phenotypical overlap.

General practices were considered eligible 12 months after they were deemed to be providing research-quality data. The data set consisted of medical records taken from 1491 practices in CPRD Aurum (those using Egton Medical Information Systems software). CPRD Aurum contains longitudinal routinely collected data from 20% of total UK general practices, covering 24% of the UK population.9 For the incidence and prevalence study and time-to-diagnosis analyses, only records of patients present in the linked CPRD Aurum–HES data set were included.

CPRD Aurum records information relating to demographics, disease and management. Information relating to symptoms, examinations and diagnoses is documented using Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED) CT codes. Data extraction was automated using the Data Extraction for Epidemiological Research tool.10

Study population: incidence, prevalence and time to diagnosis

Only those eligible records in CPRD Aurum with linked HES hospital records were included. The outcome of interest (BS) was defined by the presence of a relevant clinical code relating to BS. The list of clinical codes was developed by the lead author (PC), a rheumatologist with expertise in BS, in collaboration with health data scientists. The codes are available in online supplemental table S1.

Study population: case-control study

All eligible GP practices in the CPRD Aurum data set were included in this element of the study (linked HES record not required). Cases (those with a code identifying BS) were compared with controls (those without such codes) to calculate ORs for: uveitis, oral ulcers, genital ulceration, epididymitis, Crohn’s disease, folliculitis, cerebral venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, aneurysm, ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthropathy, psoriatic arthropathy, enteropathic arthropathy, ulcerative colitis, Sweet syndrome, familial Mediterranean fever and periodic fever. Each patient with BS was matched with up to 10 control patients with no previously documented SNOMED code relating to BS. Controls were taken from the same general practice and matched by age at index date (±1 year), sex and within 12 months of practice registration date.

Statistical analysis and follow-up

For annual point prevalence, the numerator was the cumulative count of eligible individuals with any record of BS identified on 1 January each year from 2001 to 2022, divided by the total eligible population on the same date. Prevalence was described per 100 000 population with associated CIs. A small constant (α=1×10⁻⁸) was added to the denominator to avoid division by zero.

A series of yearly cohort studies were performed to calculate the crude IR of BS for each year from 2001 to 2021. Entry date was the latest of either study start date (1 January each year) or 1 year after the practice met acceptable computer usage standards. Exit date was the earliest of either study end date (31 December each year), outcome date (new incident BS), death date, transfer date or collection date.

For the case-control study, categorical baseline data were described using proportions; continuous data were described using means or medians with SD or IQRs. Logistic regression was used to estimate unadjusted and adjusted ORs (aORs), adjusting for sex and ethnicity. Missing covariate data were treated as a separate category and included in the final analysis. Statistical analysis was conducted using R and Python.

Patient and public involvement

Behçet’s UK (charity) funded the work, deeming there was a need to improve understanding of the epidemiology of BS to support patients in the UK.

Results

Participant demographics

Between 2001 and 2021, in the linked data set, 4234 participants were diagnosed with BS (1565 incident cases). In 2021, there were 9509 280 patients in the linked data set, 68 new cases of BS and 1885 prevalent cases of BS.

Incidence and prevalence

The incidence of BS was stable, from 0.78 per 100 000 person years (0.59–1.01) in 2001 to 0.75 (0.58–0.95) in 2021 (figure 1, table 1). BS prevalence rose year on year, from 8.62 per 100 000 in 2001 (7.96–9.32) to 19.80 (18.94–20.74) in 2021 (figure 2). The incidence of BS was generally highest in the 31–40 years age group between 2001 and 2006 (online supplemental figure 1a with 95% confidence interval and b without confidence intervals, shown for clarity). The prevalence was highest among the 31–40 years age group, whereas the lowest prevalence was seen in the 0–16 years and 81+ years age groups (figure 3). Both prevalence and incidence were higher among those classified as of mixed race or ‘other’ ethnicity and lowest among those of South Asian ethnicity. The prevalence (figure 4) and incidence (online supplemental figure 2) of BS was higher in women, and the increase in prevalence was greater among women compared with men.

Discussion

This is the largest real-world population-based study of BS prevalence and incidence in the UK, using CPRD Aurum and linked HES data. The epidemiology of BS remains of considerable interest given the enormous variability in the prevalence and heterogeneity of phenotype across populations. It is less common in the so-called non-endemic Western populations than in the Middle East and East Asia. Population-level studies of BS in the West are invaluable when considering changes over time, since baseline prevalence strongly influences the performance characteristics of classification criteria by affecting prior probability of disease.

Comparison with existing literature

We report stable incidence but a significant increase in prevalence of BS between 2001 and 2021. These findings are broadly consistent with rising prevalence trends observed in other European population-based studies, including recent reports from northern Spain, Norway and Wales, which also describe a predominance of female cases and similar demographic patterns in Western cohorts.11–13 The Welsh study showed the prevalence of BS was 11.1 per 100 000 with a 76.9% female predominance. In northern Spain the prevalence in 2018 was 10.14 per 100 000. In a Norwegian study spanning 1999 to 2021, which used the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code for BS and then chart reviewed using the ISG and ICBD criteria as benchmarks (only included those meeting either criteria), incidence was 0.29 per 100 000, higher in non-Norwegians (0.44 vs 0.22) and prevalence 2.36 per 100 000 in 1999 and 7.14 in 2021, higher in non-Norwegians (12.68 vs 4.32). Both incidence and prevalence increased but were lower when reclassified by ISG criteria (more specific) and also when accounting for non-Norwegians. A recent large population-based Japanese study using the Medical Centre Claims Database (2017–2021) also reported a modest increase in prevalence of both BS and GI BS alongside falling incidence and highlighted real-world challenges in differentiating gastrointestinal BS from IBD. In this study GI Behçet’s fell slightly but still represented 14% of the study population with BS in the final year.14 BS prevalence rose to 19.80 per 100 000 at the end of our study period, slightly higher than our earlier THIN Study with a reporting period ending in 2017,7 and comparable to estimates from southern Italy of 15.9 per 100 000.15 By contrast, the pooled northern European estimate for BS prevalence was 2.1 per 100 000, southern Europe 5.3, North America 3.8, Middle East 31.8 and Turkey 119.8 per 100 000,16 though these figures encompass significant heterogeneity in study designs.

The incidence and prevalence of BS were highest in the 17–50 years age group (mean age 35.58 years, SD 13.01), consistent with other studies in THIN7 and the Rheumatology Informatics System for Effectiveness registry (48.7±16.3 years),17 18 supporting the observation that BS rarely develops over the age of 50 years.19 BS prevalence and incidence were higher in women, consistent with our previous observations7 and other Western studies,20–22 which may reflect both altered pathobiology in non-endemic settings and gender differences in health-seeking behaviour.

Compared with our incidence figures of 0.78 per 100 000 person years in 2001 and 0.75 in 2021, a US population-based study between 1960 and 2005 demonstrated a slightly lower rate of 0.38 per 100 000.23 Incidence of BS varies geographically: 0.24 per 100 000 in Italy,24 and 0.58 per 100 000 in the Middle East and Japan.23

Impact of ethnic diversity

Both prevalence and incidence were higher among those of mixed race or ‘other’ ethnicity and lowest among those of South Asian ethnicity, though ethnicity recording was incomplete, which limits interpretation. Importantly, the observed rise in BS prevalence over the study period may in part reflect changing population demographics due to migration. BS is significantly more prevalent along the Silk Route—encompassing Turkey, the Middle East, Central Asia and East Asia—where population-level HLA-B51 carriage is also enriched. Immigration from these regions into the UK over the same two-decade study period is a plausible contributor to rising case numbers and warrants substantive consideration. It is likely that some cases coded under ‘mixed race’ or ‘other’ ethnicity included individuals of Middle Eastern, North African or Central Asian backgrounds, populations with higher intrinsic prevalence of BS. A French study previously demonstrated that migration from North Africa and sub-Saharan Africa was an important factor in BS prevalence in a Western cohort.25 Future studies should systematically capture migration status and detailed ethnic background, including Arabic and Turkish heritage, to disentangle the contributions of demographics and diagnostic practice to prevalence trends.

Impact of misclassification

A critical consideration in interpreting rising prevalence is the performance characteristics of the classification criteria used. The 2014 ICBD criteria, compared with the 1990 ISG criteria, are more sensitive but less specific, particularly in low-prevalence settings where the prior probability of BS is low.26 Blake et al demonstrated in a Birmingham cohort that application of ICBD 2014 criteria resulted in reclassification of a number of patients relative to ISG criteria, with an increase in potential false-positive cases—precisely the setting (low prior probability, non-endemic) that characterises the UK population.3 A clinician shift from ISG to ICBD criteria after 2014 could therefore have contributed to an increase in coded BS diagnoses independent of true disease incidence, and this likely explains at least part of the acceleration in prevalence observed in the later study period. This is a more important consideration than previously acknowledged and should be explicitly weighed by clinicians and future researchers interpreting UK epidemiological data.

As part of the study design, we examined associations with conditions having potential phenotypical overlap, including Crohn’s disease, reactive arthritis and psoriatic arthritis. The association with Crohn’s disease (aOR 5.69) likely reflects, at least in part, misclassification: gastrointestinal BS can be clinically indistinguishable from Crohn’s disease, with approximately 30% of patients with BS—particularly those of non-Middle Eastern background—having GI involvement mimicking IBD. Accordingly, some cases coded as Crohn’s disease in patients with BS may represent GI manifestations of BS itself rather than true comorbid IBD. Given that IBD incidence and prevalence are rising and are at least 10-fold higher than BS in the UK,27 the potential for this misclassification is substantial and should be considered when interpreting these associations.

The association with reactive arthritis (aOR 16.78) and psoriatic arthritis (aOR 3.64) must also be interpreted with significant caution. Classic reactive arthritis—defined as developing after a specific triggering infection—is uncommon in BS and does not share the same immunopathological basis. The elevated OR for reactive arthritis may partly reflect diagnostic uncertainty and coding imprecision during the period before a BS diagnosis is established, rather than a true mechanistic association. Psoriatic arthritis and destructive arthritis are rare in BS; the observed association is more likely to reflect phenotypical overlap and possible misclassification than a shared pathogenic mechanism. We confirmed a previous observation from a South Korean population cohort where a positive association was reported between psoriasis (OR 2.36), psoriatic arthritis (OR 2.19) and BS.28 Similar associations were reported in our data, though, as discussed above, these require cautious interpretation in the context of potential phenotypical overlap and misclassification bias, particularly in a non-endemic setting.

The triad of classical BS features (genital ulceration, uveitis, oral ulceration) showed the strongest associations and the shortest times to diagnosis, supporting their clinical primacy. The appearance of genital ulceration was linked to the shortest interval to BS diagnosis (median 240 days), followed by uveitis and oral ulceration. These findings suggest that recognition of core phenotypical manifestations, particularly genital ulceration, may expedite diagnostic confirmation. However, these estimates of time to diagnosis relied on first recorded code in the primary care database, and it is plausible that participants had these manifestations earlier than first reported to a healthcare professional. Coding of systemic manifestations of BS may also be better documented in secondary care than non-specific primary care manifestations such as folliculitis or epididymitis, potentially leading to underrepresentation.

Limitations

The validity of our hospital-based HES data for capturing coded BS diagnoses has been supported by a pilot study demonstrating close alignment between cases coded as BS within HES (n=515) and those attending the National Centre of Excellence for BS in Birmingham (n=559) in corresponding years.29 The utility of CPRD and HES linkage has also been exploited in studies of other rare inflammatory conditions such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis.30 A previous CPRD study confirmed a 90% positive predictive value for coded Granulomatosis with Polyangitis diagnosis,30 and inpatient ICD-10 diagnosis of ANCA-associated vasculitis had a Positive Predictive Value of 86%.31 We might expect CPRD/HES codes for BS to perform similarly, but this has not been directly demonstrated for BS.

The most important limitation of this study is that case ascertainment relied solely on diagnostic codes without direct validation against established classification criteria (ISG 1990 or ICBD 2014), specialist confirmation or MDT review. Coded diagnoses were made by clinicians in routine practice—primarily in secondary care and communicated to primary care—and likely reflect real clinical judgement; however, the accuracy of coding cannot be independently verified in an anonymised database.

This limitation has important implications for the case-control associations reported. The extremely high ORs for core BS features (genital ulceration, uveitis, oral ulceration) reflect circular reasoning to a degree: these manifestations are themselves part of the criteria used to diagnose BS. The analysis is reported to characterise the population and quantify feature timing, not to establish independent diagnostic predictors. For conditions with phenotypical overlap (Crohn’s disease, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis), the elevated ORs may reflect misclassification bias, diagnostic uncertainty during disease evolution or coding imprecision rather than true independent associations.

We also acknowledge incomplete recording of ethnicity and migration status in CPRD, which is a limitation of primary care databases.32 This could have influenced ethnic stratification of prevalence and incidence data leading to either over-reporting or under-reporting of at-risk populations. Future studies should address this as it is likely that changes in migration and the ethnic profile of the population could also explain the rising BS prevalence data we observed in the current study. Similarly, changes in disease recognition following adoption of the more sensitive 2014 ICBD classification criteria may have contributed to the observed increase in prevalence, independently of any true increase in disease frequency—a possibility that should be prominently considered when interpreting trends in the post-2014 period.

As individual record linkage to the National Centres of Excellence for BS was not permissible within the current data architecture, it was not possible to confirm MDT-reviewed diagnoses or classification criteria fulfilment. Emerging Secure Data Environments within the NHS digital architecture may permit such linkage in future studies.

Conclusion

This real-world UK population-based epidemiological study of BS in a non-endemic setting confirms a higher than previously recognised prevalence with a female predominance, in line with other European and Western cohorts. Prevalence appears to be rising and may be linked to changing population migration and demographics, as well as to the performance characteristics of the 2014 ICBD criteria in low prior-probability settings—particularly the potential for increased false-positive diagnoses in non-endemic regions. Both factors should be considered when interpreting temporal trends in BS prevalence.

Recognised core clinical manifestations—genital ulceration, uveitis and oral ulceration—were strongly associated with BS and the appearance of genital ulceration was linked to the shortest time to confirmed diagnosis. Associations with conditions sharing phenotypical overlap, particularly IBD, may in part reflect diagnostic misclassification rather than true comorbidity, and should be interpreted accordingly.

Since there is no diagnostic test, diagnosis relies heavily on pattern recognition of core clinical features guided by classification criteria, with careful exclusion of conditions with phenotypical overlap. This is particularly relevant in non-endemic settings where prior probability of BS remains low but appears to be increasing. Confirmation of a coded BS diagnosis following appearance of key phenotypical manifestations is often delayed, likely due to the complex and overlapping multisystemic nature of the condition. Improving recognition of the spectrum of BS manifestations and refining classification criteria performance in non-endemic settings may improve disease recognition and reduce diagnostic delay.
