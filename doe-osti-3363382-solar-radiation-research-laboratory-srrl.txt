Executive Summary
The Solar Radiation Research Laboratory (SRRL) at the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR, formerly NREL) is a solar calibration and measurement facility that maintains and
disseminates the World Radiation Reference (WRR) (essentially the W•m−2) for the United
States, which is essential for traceable and accurate measurements of solar radiation at all solar
generation facilities. The SRRL operates two calibration facilities that meet the International
Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC)
standards (ISO/IEC 17025) and provide unique high-quality radiometer calibrations to NLR and
other U.S. Department of Energy laboratories. The Baseline Measurement System (BMS) at the
SRRL provides a high-quality record of solar irradiance and surface meteorological conditions.
SRRL capabilities are used to develop (1) improved methods for the calibration of solar
radiometers; (2) new standards through the ISO, the IEC, and ASTM International; (3) solar
radiation and meteorological models; and (4) advanced instrumentation and methods for
operating solar measurement stations. The SRRL datasets are also critical for the validation of
new models and datasets, such as the National Solar Radiation Database (NSRDB).

The research and development of solar radiation measurement systems and resource modeling
techniques are essential for advancing the scientific basis for producing reliable resource data.
Specifically, the spatial, temporal, and spectral (wavelength dependency) characteristics of the
solar resource are required in several different time frames for various project phases and
applications. This agreement has three tasks that conduct research on advancing solar resource
measurements:

 • Task 1: Applied solar radiation and measurement
 • Task 2: Standards development and knowledge sharing
 • Task 3: Development of state-of-the-art instrumentation and measurement techniques.

1 Background
The Solar Radiation Research Laboratory (SRRL) at the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR, formerly NREL) is a solar radiometer calibration and measurement facility. It operates
world-class calibration facilities accredited by the International Organization for Standardization
(ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) (ISO/IEC 17025) to provide
unique high-quality calibrations to NLR, other U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) laboratories,
and other organizations. The SRRL effectively maintains the irradiance standard (i.e., W•m−2)
for the United States. Its capabilities are used to develop improved methods for the calibration of
solar radiometers and advanced instrumentation and methods for operating solar measurement
stations. The SRRL (Figure 1) also provides a continuous record of solar irradiance and surface
meteorological conditions for use in modeling and validation studies through its Baseline
Measurement System (BMS).

SRRL provides some of the most accurate and comprehensive solar radiation measurements in
the world, including global horizontal irradiance (GHI), direct normal irradiance (DNI), diffuse
horizontal irradiance (DHI), ultraviolet (UV) irradiance, spectral irradiance, sky imagery, and
meteorological data. These high-quality datasets support the solar energy industry by improving
resource assessment, validating satellite remote-sensing models, optimizing photovoltaic (PV)
system design, and evaluating new PV technologies. For concentrating solar power (CSP),
SRRL’s high-quality DNI measurements are essential for performance modeling, heliostat
design, thermal storage optimization, and bankable resource assessments. The data are also
widely used in building energy efficiency, supporting various tools for load modeling,
daylighting design, and code development.

Beyond energy, SRRL plays a major role in material weathering and durability research, where
spectral and broadband UV measurements drive aging models for polymers, coatings, sealants,
and PV module materials. Its long-term datasets also underpin atmospheric science and climate
studies by validating radiative transfer models, evaluating aerosol impacts, analyzing cloud
dynamics, and monitoring long-term irradiance trends. SRRL’s UV measurements support public
health applications ranging from UV index forecasting to studying skin cancer risk and vitamin
D synthesis. The data also benefit agriculture and ecology through photosynthetically active
radiation-based crop and photosynthesis modeling.

SRRL serves as a national reference site for instrument calibration and standards development,
making it a basis for solar measurement accuracy across multiple industries and research
domains. SRRL collaborates with national and international organizations to advance research in
solar radiation and serves as DOE’s lead laboratory for radiometer calibrations traceable to the
World Radiometric Reference (WRR). To maintain WRR traceability, NLR’s SRRL team
conducts the NLR Pyrheliometer Comparisons (NPC) annually to provide stakeholders such as
calibration laboratories with access to the WRR. Using the WRR working standard absolute
cavity radiometers, the team developed a unique Broadband Outdoor Radiometer Calibration
(BORCAL) method for calibrating pyrheliometers, pyranometers, and pyrgeometers for field
measurements. The BORCAL and the NPC are conducted at the ISO/IEC 17025-accredited
calibration facilities at an outdoor location at the SRRL, and the spectral irradiance calibrations
are conducted at the indoor Spectral Calibration Laboratory (SCL) at the SRRL. The calibrations

 1

performed at the SRRL form the basis for state-of-the-art PV solar simulator measurements and
national and international standards development. The BORCAL and SCL are essential for other
ISO-accredited facilities at NLR that measure PV performance.

SRRL and the International Energy Agency’s (IEA’s) Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme
(PVPS) Task 16 on solar resource measurement modeling and forecasting brought together the
world’s foremost experts in solar energy meteorology to develop the fourth edition of the Best
Practices Handbook for the Collection and Use of Solar Resource Data for Solar Energy
Applications (Sengupta et al. 2024). This handbook serves the needs of the solar energy
community for a single document addressing the key aspects of solar resource characterization as
it relates to solar energy technologies. SRRL has been leading the ASTM International
subcommittee on radiometry and statistics and leads the U.S. effort to develop international
standards for solar radiation measurement and modeling, for example, through the ISO. Current
ASTM standards for solar spectral irradiance, radiometer calibration, and PV performance
testing are based on work done at SRRL since the 1980s. In the last 3 years, SRRL team
contributed to the development and update of ASTM and ISO standards for various solar
radiometer calibrations, UV irradiance data, classification associated with solar measurements,
and the reference solar spectrum. SRRL effort at developing standards and best practices ensures
that best-in-class models and measurement practices are followed while deploying instruments to
understand solar variability. It also ensures that grid integration studies use validated solar
radiation datasets, thereby increasing the accuracy of those studies. The solar industry follows
these standards while deploying radiometers at solar power plants. Industrial assessment of
weathering and degradation of various materials including paints and coatings also depend on
these standards.

To meet solar energy technology deployment goals, it is essential to predict and reduce the
production uncertainty at both PV and CSP plants. The biggest uncertainty in PV and CSP
production arises from uncertainty in estimating the solar resource available for these generation
technologies, and the research and development (R&D) at the SRRL directly addresses this
issue.

Further, to enable grid integration under high-solar deployment and provide methods to address
variability, it is necessary to develop capabilities that will provide high temporal and spatial
measurement resolutions at a low cost and a high accuracy for grid operations. R&D at the
SRRL focuses on developing the capability to deploy a network of measurement systems in a
cost-effective manner that ultimately accelerates the integration of solar energy into the electric
grid.

This report discusses the applied solar radiation measurement research activities that occurred at
the SRRL in fiscal years 2022–2024.

2 Objective
This project sought to use the SRRL facilities to enable the DOE Office of Critical Minerals and
Energy Innovation to achieve its cost targets and enable the large-scale integration of solar on the
electric grid by reducing costs and uncertainty in resource measurements through the following:

 • Increase ease of access to the updated WRR to ensure traceable radiometer calibrations
 nationwide.
 • Improve calibration methods for solar radiometers to enhance measurement accuracy.
 • Improve access to high-quality datasets from various locations for standards and model
 development and improve the evaluation of models and methods.
 • Develop standards and best practices in solar measurement and modeling to reduce the
 cost of deployment.
 • Disseminate best practices for measurements to facilitate high-quality irradiance
 measurements.
 • Enable the development of new low-cost high-quality measurement systems to reduce
 deployment costs.
 • Collaborate with national and international experts to develop and disseminate innovative
 methods to improve solar resource assessment and forecasting.
 • Develop the capability for trusted solar resource measurements to support the adoption of
 new module technologies.
 • Enable PV and CSP R&D by developing and providing enhanced measurement and
 modeling capabilities to meet their research needs.
The project provided calibration traceability to the WRR for reference radiometers used by DOE
and numerous stakeholders participating in each NPC. Instruments deployed at SRRL were
calibrated using the BORCAL or the SCL, thereby providing ISO-accredited calibrations to
broadband and spectral radiometers for instruments that are deployed at SRRL BMS and
numerous other sites and providing research-quality data that are important for advancing solar
resource characterization, solar resource modeling for various technologies, and the validation
and verification of new solar resource models. Moreover, the project sought to develop state-of-
the-art instrumentation and techniques that enable the adoption of new solar energy technologies.
Finally, ASTM, ISO, and IEC standards and the IEA Best Practices Handbook were developed
using well-characterized measurements of high accuracy from the BMS to enable industry to
reduce the cost of solar project development and to develop the capability to use low-cost
measurements for effective grid integration.

All data collected through the SRRL project are disseminated through the Measurement and
Instrument Data Center (MIDC),1 which collects data from multiple locations around the United
States and continues to be accessed by more than 100,000 users annually. The data and models
available from the website enable all stakeholders to easily access information for improving the
quality and reducing the cost of projects. The SRRL provides an opportunity for various
instrument manufacturers to deploy new instruments at the SRRL for field evaluations.

3 Project Results and Discussion
3.1 Task 1: Applied Solar Radiation and Measurement
Although solar measurement science has improved over time, uncertainties and errors in field
measurements still exist. These uncertainties can be a result of (1) instrument design and
performance characteristics; (2) calibration of measurement and test equipment; or (3)
instrument deployment, maintenance and data acquisition. Removing specific error sources
results in advancement in the science of solar measurement and reduces project costs through
reduced performance uncertainty. Long-term solar resource—such as broadband, spectral, UV,
and plane-of-array—and meteorological measurements of the lowest uncertainty and highest
quality are needed for measurement research, standards development, model development and
evaluation, as inputs to performance and economic analysis tools, and for other system
integration applications. This requires that the instruments are (1) deployed and maintained
following best measurement practices, (2) calibrated on an annual basis (in ISO-accredited
facilities), and that (3) data are collected, quality-controlled, and disseminated in a timely
manner.

The availability of world-class continuous measurements of solar radiation and meteorological
parameters such as at the SRRL enables numerous R&D activities that span many applications.
These applications include research activities performed under this project as well as other
projects in PV; CSP; grid modernization; the development of ASTM, ISO, and IEC standards;
the development and evaluation of models; and use in performance and economic analysis tools,
such as the System Advisor Model™ (SAM) and PVWatts®. The unique SRRL platform
containing BMS enables the development of new and improved instruments in collaboration with
top manufacturers, including Kipp & Zonen, Hukseflux, EKO, Eppley, and others.

The SRRL project provides calibration and characterization of broadband and spectral
radiometers traceable to the WRR and spectral irradiance standards in compliance with ISO/IEC
17025 accreditation requirements. This involves the operations and maintenance of the BMS to
provide high-quality long-term solar irradiance and atmospheric measurements. The SRRL
maintains the national standard for solar measurements and disseminates accurate solar
measurement and modeling methods and best practices to various stakeholders, including
academia, industry, and other laboratories.

This task ensures that relevant instruments:

 • Are deployed and maintained following best measurement practices through the BMS
 • Maintain the optimal deployment of meteorological instrumentation within the BMS to
 represent both long-term continuity and accurate representation of current deployments.
 • Are calibrated on an annual basis in ISO/IEC 17025-accredited facilities through using
 the BORCAL facility or the SCL
 • Are traceable to the WRR through mechanisms such as the NPC and the International
 Pyrheliometer Comparisons (IPC)
 • Produce data that is quality-controlled and disseminated in a timely manner through
 MIDC.

3.1.1 Calibration and Measurement
The work within this subtask includes maintaining the broadband and spectral irradiance
calibration traceability to:

3.1.1.1 National and International Pyrheliometer Comparison
SRRL team serves as DOE’s primary laboratory for radiometer calibration that is linked to the
WRR, which is essential for ensuring accurate solar irradiance measurements. The WRR is a
detector-based measurement standard maintained by a set of well-characterized absolute cavity
radiometers, termed the World Standard Group (WSG), developed and maintained by the World
Radiation Center (WRC), located in Davos, Switzerland. At the invitation of the WRC, SRRL
team engages in the IPCs, held every 5 years, to uphold the integrity of the WRR for the U.S. In
between the 5 years, SRRL offers stakeholders access to the WRR through the NLR NPC.

The NPC is an annual side-by-side comparison of SRRL absolute cavity radiometers with
devices from U.S. government, academic, and industry participants. This comparison serves to
periodically calibrate these devices and to check the stability of the instruments. During this
project cycle, the IPC-XIII was held in 2021, as it was delayed by 1 year due to the COVID
pandemic, and the NPC was held in 2022, 2023, and 2024 (Reda, Andreas, Stoddard, et al. 2022;
Reda et al. 2023b; Reda et al. 2024b; Reda, Andreas, Kutchenreiter, et al. 2022; Reda et al.
2023a; Reda et al. 2024a).

SRRL absolute cavity radiometers are calibrated to the WRR at the WRC (Physical
Meteorological Observatory in Davos [PMOD]) in Davos, Switzerland, every 5 years during the
IPC. The NPC therefore serves to transfer the calibration from the WRR to the NPC participants
(Figure 2). The Transfer Standard Group of reference radiometers for the NPC consisted of four
SRRL radiometers with direct traceability to the WRR, each having participated in the IPC. As a
result of the NPC, each participating absolute cavity radiometer was assigned a new WRR
transfer factor, which is computed as the reference irradiance determined by the Transfer

 8

Standard Group divided by the observed irradiance from the participating radiometer. The
measurement performance of the Transfer Standard Group allowed the transfer of the WRR to
each participating radiometer with an estimated uncertainty of ± 0.33% with respect to the
International System of Units.

 Figure 2. History of WRR reduction factors for SRRL reference cavities.
 Source: (Reda et al. 2024b)

SRRL supports the NPC through site access, shipping and receiving of equipment, and a proven
measurement and data analysis protocol. The NPC is accredited by the ISO/IEC 17025, which is
an important requirement for SRRL BORCAL ISO/IEC 17025-accreditation process because
SRRL absolute cavity radiometers are used in the BORCAL process. The ISO/IEC 17025
accreditation requires that the instruments are inter-compared on an annual basis with traceability
to the WRR, and the NPC fulfills this requirement both for the SRRL and other ISO-accredited
facilities. Further, because participants are experts in this field, the event facilitates sharing
knowledge and new R&D ideas.

During the NPC 2022–2024, participants from various organizations operated multiple absolute
cavity radiometers to simultaneously measure clear-sky direct normal solar irradiance (Figure 3).

3.1.1.2 Shortwave and Longwave Outdoor Radiometer Calibration
BORCAL, which is an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited method, conducts radiometer calibrations

total UV irradiance based on an empirically derived model that assumes that measured or
modeled data of GHI are available. This standard is expected to help understand the degradation

assessments of the service life of many products. Unfortunately, most of the locations that

3.3.1 Toward Developing Accurate Long-term Solar Irradiance Data
Long-term solar irradiance data play an essential role in the risk analysis and decision-making
processes for all phases of the development of solar energy projects, including rooftop to utility-
scale PV. Long-term solar irradiance dataset represents the solar irradiance phenomenon over
years (Sengupta et al. 2024; WMO 2018). Many studies demonstrate the long-term changes in
solar radiation and how varying solar radiation can impact solar energy systems (Müller et al.
2014). The question that many researchers attempt to answer is: How do the long-term solar
radiation changes affect solar energy systems? In addressing this question, many studies have
examined the effects of the long-term solar radiation changes on solar energy. This ongoing
study seeks to investigate this question. Solar radiation measurements are susceptible to various
sources of uncertainties. These uncertainties can arise from the method of calibration employed
on the radiometers, the type of instrument used, and the operations and maintenance method;
therefore, solar radiation collected from a site needs to be checked through examining the
instruments for consistency across neighboring locations and/or using redundant instrumentation.

 25

In the above context, SRRL team investigated the calibration bias of the radiometric data
collected at the SRRL since mid-1985 to the present by comparing the data to a newer standard
reference for DHI. This is established by determining the difference between the output of older
radiometers measuring DHI and the newer standard used for calibration. Figure 15 demonstrates
the process of the method and the ultimate bias correction method.

3.3.2 Data Quality and Uncertainty Integration
SRRL developed the Solar Uncertainty Integrator (SUNI) software, which adheres to the
internationally accepted Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM). The
SUNI software, which is described below, has been specifically designed to integrate data
quality analyses and radiometer measurement uncertainty estimates for GHI, DNI, and DHI.

As the global demand for solar energy sources continues to increase, accurate and reliable solar
irradiance data have become increasingly essential for the successful implementation and
optimization of solar energy systems. From evaluating project feasibility to monitoring system
performance, precise measurements of solar radiation play an essential role in both research and
practical applications. Numerous high-quality solar measurement networks, such as the Baseline
Surface Radiation Network6 (BSRN) and the Atmospheric Radiation Measurement 7 (ARM)
network operated by DOE, provide valuable radiometric data. These networks are known for
providing high-quality radiometric data collected using sophisticated instrumentation and
rigorous quality control protocols. But despite the high standards maintained by such networks,
the integrity and reliability of solar irradiance measurements remain susceptible to various
sources of uncertainty. These include, but are not limited to, the type and condition of the
radiometers used, the frequency and accuracy of the calibration, environmental and installation
factors (e.g., shading, tilt, and orientation), and ongoing operational maintenance. These
variables can significantly influence the uncertainty of the collected data and, consequently, the
decisions made based on it.

Given the potential impact of these uncertainties on both research outcomes and real-world
energy system performance, the quantification and transparent reporting of measurement
uncertainty is a critical component of solar resource assessment. Recognizing this need, SRRL
developed SUNI (Wilcox et al. 2025), a Python-based software tool designed to systematically
estimate the measurement uncertainty in accordance with the internationally accepted GUM.
SUNI enables users to evaluate uncertainty in GHI, DNI, and DHI at time intervals ranging from
1 minute to 1 hour, making it suitable for a wide array of applications and datasets.

The SUNI software package provides a method to assign expanded uncertainty estimates to
three-component solar radiation measurements (Wilcox et al. 2025). The system merges static
uncertainty information about radiometer performance with the dynamic operational uncertainty

DHI. These three components are then assessed for data quality to identify a reasonable dataset

quality assessment tool based on the fraction of incident extraterrestrial irradiance (Maxwell,
Wilcox, and Rymes 1994) and the three irradiance components to estimate the clearness index.

3.3.3 Reference Cell for PV Applications
In principle, a well-matched PV reference cell deployed near a PV plant in any geographic
location provides an accurate measurement of the effective irradiance (the irradiance that
generates current in the PV modules) over the full range of operating conditions: temperature,
cloudiness, sun position, season, etc. This contrasts with broadband irradiance measurements
from pyranometers, which must be processed using a variety of models to estimate the effective
irradiance. Good reference cell measurements can eliminate these modeling steps and thereby
remove the models’ contribution to uncertainty in various project planning and evaluation
activities.

The advantage of measuring effective irradiance as opposed to using models is particularly
relevant for evaluating the contribution of the reflected radiation to the performance of bifacial
modules and systems. Both directional and spectral distributions of reflected radiation strongly
differ from the front-side irradiance, and there are currently no practical models to translate rear-
side pyranometer measurements into rear-side effective irradiance.

 32

In practice, however, commercial PV reference cells and PV modules vary considerably in
operating characteristics, and details about those characteristics are difficult to come by;
therefore, it is impossible to choose a well-matched cell or to evaluate how well matched a given
cell is. A poorly matched reference cell might still provide a more accurate measurement than a
model using pyranometer measurements, but if the relevant characteristics are unknown, the
uncertainty of using it remains needlessly high.

Two additional aspects are important for good reference cell measurements: accurate calibration
and long-term stability. This is no different from any other measurement, but few studies have
examined these qualities of commercial reference cells.

During this cycle, SRRL team and PV Performance Labs published a report on PV reference cell
performance, Report on a Baseline Performance Reference for Irradiance in PV System
Applications (Driesse, Habte, and Sengupta 2024). This report is described in the following.
PV cells (and therefore modules and arrays) respond in a complex manner to the complex
variables that define their operating environment. Irradiance has spatial and spectral dimensions
that interact with the spectral-angular response of cells, which is, in turn, is modified by the cell
operating temperature. Many of these effects are fundamental in nature, but they can be adjusted
by material choices and design or manufacturing parameters to optimize power or energy ratings,
which leads to substantial variety in device characteristics.

PV reference cells for irradiance measurements are not fundamentally different from other PV
cells, but in their role as reference devices, it is important to distinguish between the
characteristics of operational cells and the modules they should share and those they should not.
If a reference device shares all the normal characteristics of an operational module, then it can be
an excellent tool for detecting the faults of such a module. But it is less useful for detecting faults
in other module types or for comparing performance with different module types that have their
own different matching reference cells. A more neutral common reference device is needed for
such tasks.

Pyranometers are neutral and well-defined reference devices for irradiance measurements, but
the relationship between broadband hemispherical irradiance measured by pyranometers and the
magnitude of light-generated current in PV devices is complex. For this reason, performance
indicators based on pyranometers fluctuate strongly. In fact, important differences in
performance between PV modules can be masked by such fluctuations, especially when
comparing measurements from different time periods or different locations.

Thus, there is a need for a well-defined generic PV reference cell whose characteristics are close
enough to most operational PV devices to make it possible to have stable performance indicators
with low uncertainty, but whose characteristics are not necessarily identical to any of them.

Figure 19 illustrates the essence of this idea using relative distances to indicate magnitudes of
differences. The blue flag represents the well-defined broadband irradiance reference quantity.
High-quality pyranometers are clustered very near this flag, whereas lower-quality instruments
and photodiode pyranometers stray somewhat farther afield. The family of PV devices on the
right is a considerable distance away, and the proposal is to plant a new flag on the right to serve
as a new well-defined reference quantity for outdoor PV measurements, which is called the

 33

baseline performance reference (BPR). The initial definition is aimed at silicon PV technologies
and might be referred to as BPR-Si to distinguish it from potential future variations aligned with
other technologies.

Note that this proposal does not call into question the existence or usefulness of current PV
reference devices that might be designed to match specific module types as well as possible.
Instead, it recommends the definition of a generic reference cell type to serve a complementary
role as a common baseline for performance comparisons. This will be particularly useful for
outdoor measurements.

An additional benefit of the BPR definition is that the measurand is completely defined under all
operating conditions. This allows measurement error and measurement uncertainty in BPR time
series of measurements to be quantified, like what is done for pyranometer measurements.

Finally, the BPR definition opens the door to new practices in resource assessment and yield
prediction. Solar resource data can be collected or modeled and validated directly as BPR
irradiance, and PV system simulations based on BPR irradiance need fewer assumptions and less
processing to obtain the effective irradiance on modules. As a result, lower uncertainty in yield
assessments can be expected.
