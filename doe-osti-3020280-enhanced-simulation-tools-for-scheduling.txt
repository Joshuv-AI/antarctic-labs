 3. Project Summary: Energy storage and solar hybrids are increasing rapidly on the
 evolving power grid. One of the most significant challenges of maximizing the value
 of variable solar energy is the timing of charging and discharging stored energy from
 long-duration storage (LDES) devices. Power system models often fail to capture the
 benefits of LDES and can undervalue the role of storage in providing energy
 arbitrage, reliability support, and resiliency. This project examined those challenges
 and proposed alternative methodologies that better capture the value that LDES can
 provide. For instance, we compared various dispatch mechanisms across multiple
 tools to provide insight on the suitability of each method for power systems of
 different sizes, considering computational tradeoffs. These dispatch mechanisms are
 summarized below:
 • Look-ahead window: This extends the computational or optimization
 window to include multiple days, rather than a single day. This method
 improves storage value performance but often comes at a high
 computational cost and thus scalability issues can prevent usability.
 • State-of-charge targets: This approach adopts ending state-of-charge or
 volumetric targets from a simplified simulation (price-taker, reduced-form
 dispatch, etc.) to prevent myopic behavior in storage dispatch. While this
 involves an additional modeling step, the method can avoid the use of
 longer dispatch windows as examined in the first option above.
 • Stored energy value: This approach uses the marginal future value of
 stored energy (in $/MWh) to make arbitrage decisions. This method has
 low computational overhead, but it is not easy to ascertain the marginal
 stored value of energy in most systems, as it is likely to vary based by
 location, season, and relative cost of other resources on the system.
 • Variable timestep: This approach identifies critical periods to retain
 modeling detail but simplifies the remaining “less critical” periods to
 maintain computational tractability. This can potentially allow longer look-

 ahead periods (as detailed in the first bullet) without increasing the
 computational requirements and improving scalability considerations.
 We used multiple industry-accepted grid modeling tools (in particular, PLEXOS and
 Sienna) to create a framework for comparing these various methodologies, and
 simulating various system sizes and configurations to make recommendations for
 optimal long-duration battery dispatch.
 Further, severe weather events can expose significant vulnerabilities in the power
 grid and are the most common cause of transmission system failures, according to
 reliability assessments. LDES is a potential resilience-enhancing technology given
 its increased energy capacity relative to short-duration batteries. Using our
 methodologies, we investigated operational benefits of LDES in scenarios where a
 severe weather event, modeled after the winter storm Uri from 2022, occurs in a
 future 2035 power grid.
 4. Project Objectives and Outcomes: This project developed and disseminated new
 methodologies for more comprehensive unit commitment and dispatch decision-
 making, for more accurately determining metrics for valuing energy arbitrage and
 other sources of value for storage with durations of 8-24 hours. As a result of this
 work, we published four papers and technical reports, hosted two meetings with
 relevant utility stakeholders, prepared and presented at three conferences, and
 created two fact sheets. These outreach activities will ensure that some of these
 methodologies will be adopted in the wider grid modeling community, particularly for
 users of production cost models (PCMs).
 Our model comparison effort utilized two PCMs, commercially-available PLEXOS
 and open-source Sienna. We revealed that the traditional one-day optimization
 horizon for both PCMs leads to inefficient utilization of LDES and extending the
 optimization horizon as much as possible significantly improves storage dispatch,
 but it comes at a computational cost. Regardless of the dispatch routine, however,
 LDES helps align load with available generation and reduces total generation costs.
 We also explored how LDES can help the grid ride through severe weather events
 and contribute to reliability. We find that LDES can help reduced dropped load
 (unserved energy), provide arbitrage, and firm capacity. By comparing these sources
 of LDES value to the estimated capital costs of various technologies, we find that
 most technologies have a benefit-cost ratio (BCR) greater than one. Some already
 commercialized technologies (such as pumped storage hydro and compressed air
 energy storage) have a BCR higher than one in every scenario we considered,
 whereas some less commercialized technologies (such as gravitational and thermal
 storage) can exhibit a BCR of less than one under higher technology cost estimates,
 although still show favorable BCR under other conditions.

 5. Path Forward: The various products and outcomes of this study have provoked
 thoughtful discussion and useful methodologies for the grid modeling community
 studying LDES. However, we still identify areas for further research. We note that
 dispatch of multi-day duration storage (for seasonal storage or energy-limited
 resources longer-term energy limits, such as those that occur in hydropower) is still
 challenging for most grid models given the problem of intraday decision making. We

 3

 have identified the role that LDES can provide in terms of energy shifting and
 resiliency to the power grid, but further analysis should consider the role of LDES in
 addressing the needs of the power grid as electrification increases. Increasing
 demand required for datacenters and high-performance computing may enable
 further opportunities for LDES to increase reliability and supply-demand mismatch.
 In particular, these types of loads may have less flexibility on different timescales,
 and the inherent flexibility of long-duration storage may be a good complement given
 the expanded requirements of the electricity grid.
 Over the course of this study, we confronted many computational barriers, and
 discovered why many grid modeling tools (both production cost models and capacity
 expansion models) have difficulty considering LDES technologies. Sometimes,
 simplifications and assumptions must be made for models to ultimately solve. In
 order for grid planners, utilities, and energy developers to understand the full value
 of LDES technologies, these computational barriers must be tackled. We hope that
 some of the dispatch mechanisms we studied here can help others struggling to
 model complex technologies in grid models, because they can often be “left out” out
 of traditional dispatch or planning activities if deemed too complicated, which can
 stifle innovation and forward progress for our electricity system.
