Executive Summary

  The objective of this project is to develop Tribocoating; an innovative, rapid, low-cost,
  energy-efficient, and environmentally friendly coating technology designed to enhance the
  performance and durability of bearings and gears operating under harsh conditions.
  Tribocoating creates protective metal-oxide tribofilms through in-situ tribosintering of
  nanocrystals (NCs). Components are simply run in a nano-enabled Tribocoating fluid prior to
  deployment; no surface pretreatment, coating equipment, or external energy input is required.
  Year 1 Accomplishments:
  All go/no-go metrics were achieved or exceeded for PCNS18 (5 nm ZrO₂) and PTNS19 (5
  nm TiO₂). Both materials formed metal-oxide tribofilms >25 nm thick on steel balls in 2-hour
  mixed rolling/sliding MTM tests and exhibited <40 nm total wear (film + substrate). Due to
  the poor performance of Al₂O₃, which exhibited excessive wear, this material was removed
  from further consideration, and resources were redirected toward TiO₂, which demonstrated
  excellent film formation and wear protection.
  Year 2 Accomplishments:
  We successfully formed durable Tribocoatings from BaTiO₃. This material prevented
  scuffing and remained stable during extended 8-hour harsh-condition tests. TiO₂
  Tribocoatings showed durability in an EV gear application and even in a low-viscosity,
  additive-free liquid (ethanol for ZrO₂ tribocoatings).
  For the Year 2 go/no-go MPR requirement (improving micropitting, scuffing, and electrically
  accelerated failure performance), tribocoatings prepared from PCNS19 (ZrO₂) in ISO 320
  demonstrated:
  • Micropitting wear reduction: 4× lower wear compared to bare steel in commercially
 available wind turbine oil
  • Scuffing resistance: Additional protection compared to reference surfaces; in one test,
 scuffing was completely eliminated across the testing range
  • Electrically driven macropitting (eWEC): No improvement observed, no harm done
  Additionally, UPenn independently demonstrated complete elimination of scuffing at the
  maximum attainable load (75 N).
  Year 3 Accomplishments:
  Full-scale validation at Winergy-Flender demonstrated that tribocoatings can be successfully
  formed inside a fully assembled wind-turbine gearbox. Gear inspections conducted before
  testing, after load testing, and after low-lambda operation confirmed visible tribofilm
  formation on the gear tooth surfaces.
  We also completed the four-stage FAG FE8 bearing test using PCNS19 (ZrO₂) in ISO 320.
  This test simulates the variable load and mixed-friction conditions typical of wind-turbine
  operation:
  • ZrO₂-only formulation: Excellent wear protection, with roller wear of 11 mg—well
 below the 30 mg benchmark for fully formulated industrial gear oils. Rollers displayed
 the characteristic blue tribofilm finish.
  • Reference oil with coated vs. uncoated rollers:

Introduction
 We proposed a 36-month project with the goal of advancing a transformative coating
 technology that produces thin but highly durable metal oxide film coatings, which we call
 TriboCoatings, that protect against wear and damage on contacting surfaces in all
 machinery, especially those operating in harsh environments. Tribological components,
 especially bearings and gears, often operate under harsh conditions: high stresses, high
 temperatures, and corrosive environments. Despite being typically made from high
 strength steels, they are susceptible to failure modes, particularly micropitting, scuffing,
 spalling, smearing, fatigue, wear and corrosion1, whose consequences can be catastrophic
 for machine operation. These problems cut across all sectors of industry and often require
 time-, cost-, material- and energy-intensive steps taken during the manufacturing of new
 and replacement components. Moreover, the recent advent of electric vehicles (EVs) has
 now brought electrically induced failure modes of bearings and gears into the spotlight.
 Electric discharges in EVs are known to induce frosting, fluting, pitting, spark tracks, and
 welding on bearing surfaces2, adding to the maintenance costs and limiting their lifetime.
 Shaft voltages and bearing currents can also destabilize and degrade lubricants by
 localized heating and undesirable chemical reactions. While today’s EVs are reasonably
 reliable, next-generation EVs will have increased power densities with higher operating
 temperatures, speeds, and electric fields; the EV industry lacks cost-efficient solutions to
 mitigate failures that are expected to result3,4. Similarly, there is an urgent need to address
 the costly premature failures in the bearings and gears of wind turbines (WT) resulting
 from the harsh, varying contact conditions in WTs. These failures limit WT reliability
 and market competitiveness4-6. Presently, coatings applied during WT gearbox
 component manufacturing add cost and complexity while not providing sufficient
 protection for these harsh conditions.

 The existing manufacturing solutions to mitigate tribological component failures are
 effective only for a limited subset of the aforementioned failure modes; none are effective
 in tackling all these challenges. Prominent manufacturing approaches in current use
 include:
 • Super-finishing of surfaces to limit asperity interactions and prevent micropitting, but
 this does not protect against severe contact stresses when using low viscosity
 lubricants5;
 • Extreme pressure (EP)/anti-wear (AW) additives in lubricants that react under extreme
 stresses to form protective films, but the stability and performance of additives exposed
 to electric fields is problematic6, and certain EP chemistries can corrode copper
 windings3;
 • Conversion coatings, such as sacrificial black oxide and phosphate, developed on
 bearing surfaces to prevent direct metal-metal contact, but they are usually worn off in
 operation and, therefore, will not be effective in mitigating electrical arcing failures7;
 • Full ceramic or hybrid bearings to overcome wear in critical applications including in
 EVs to suppress circulating currents8,9, but they have particularly high cost10; and
 • Coatings with high hardness, low friction, improved wear- and corrosion-resistance,
 and capable of providing electrical insulation, applied on components by physical or
 chemical vapor deposition (PVD/CVD) and thermal spray techniques. Diamond like
 carbon (DLC), nitrides, carbides, nanocomposites, and nanostructured coatings are the
 Page 6 of 66

 popular materials for existing coating technologies11,12. However, manufacturing
 techniques such as CVD and PVD are slow, expensive, challenging to scale up, often
 highly energy intensive, and often involve environmentally harmful substances. As
 well, these coatings can degrade and delaminate over a period of time, eventually
 exposing the bearing surfaces, making them susceptible to wear13,14.
There is a pressing need for new technologies to protect gears and bearings against harsh
tribological and electromechanical failure modes, and which are energy-efficient,
environmentally benign, and cost-effective. As the EV and WT industries move toward
fill-for-life lubricant treatments, this gap is emerging as a critical barrier for high-
performance drivetrains and WT gearboxes. In addition, lightweight materials like
aluminum, magnesium, and titanium alloys are attractive for lightweight and thus more
energy-efficient vehicles, machinery, and aircraft. However, these materials are often
limited by tribological failures; this could be addressed by our innovative coating
technology. The project thus includes a feasibility study, leveraging our primary focus on
steel to perform a proof-of-concept demonstration that our technology is potentially
applicable to nonferrous materials. Overall, overcoming the unique failure modes found
in these harsh environment applications will substantially support efforts against global
warming.
In the target applications, tribocoating will provide the following improvements:
 1) Extend machine life by reducing wear and fatigue;
 2) Increase reliability by allowing high contact stress (>1 GPa) without scuffing or
 seizure failure;
 3) Reduce energy consumption by allowing the use of lower viscosity lubricants (<5
   cSt at 100°C);
 4) Mitigate against failure due to stray electrical currents in EVs and power generation
 equipment (e.g., WTs).

We conducted complementary MTM/SLIM evaluations to study tribofilm formation using
PCNS18-capped ZrO₂ and PTNS19-capped TiO₂ nanocrystals dispersed in PAO2 at 40 wt.%.
Both nanocrystal types produced rapid coating deposition across a range of slide-to-roll

deposits and sinters protective coatings during operation. Screening of ligand chemistries
revealed that PCNS18-capped ZrO₂ gave the lowest wear normalized to coating thickness,
while PCNS21-capped ZrO₂ enabled the fastest tribofilm growth.

We also examined the effect of nanocrystal size on the growth, traction coefficient, and surface
roughness of the tribocoatings by performing an MTM test on 15 nm TiO2 NCs under the
standard conditions and compared with the 5 nm TiO2 NC’s (Figure 9). During deposition,
larger TiO2 NCs show lower thickness and surface roughness. However, at the beginning of
the durability test, it appears that probably due to material transfer from the disk to the ball,
the thickness initially increases to a saturated level and then decreases to the same value as the
smaller NCs by the end of the durability test. Larger TiO2 NCs show a lower traction
coefficient at the end of the deposition test, likely due to their lower surface roughness, while
both NC sizes show similar values during the durability test.

We examined the thickness and surface roughness of the ZrO2 and TiO2 tribocoatings
described above. The measurements were performed using a white light interferometer
(WLI) (Figure 10). Step edge heights greater than 40 nm and up to 100 nm were measured
for the coatings formed on the disc specimen. The surface roughness of the coatings was less
than 20 nm. We did not observe any significant wear scars on the surfaces, which is
demonstrative of the wear resistance of the coatings.

BaTiO3 nanocrystals (5 nm BTNS19 and 20 nm LBNS22) (Figures 11 and 12) were
dispersed in PAO2. Using this fluid, we successfully prepared high-loading dispersions of 5
nm BaTiO3 nanocrystals (BTNS19), achieving stable, optically clear formulations with
loadings up to 60 wt.% (Table 3). We used Raman spectroscopy to study the crystal structure
of two BaTiO3 samples with different sizes (5 nm and 20 nm), as crystal structure affects
mechanical properties of the tribocoatings (Figure 13). The results showed that 20 nm
BaTiO3 is tetragonal and 5 nm BaTiO3 is more likely cubic. Tests on the cubic-phase 5 nm
crystals dispersed in PAO2 show scuffing prevention at standard MTM test conditions.
However, increased temperature or slide-roll-ratio are needed to deposit a tribocoating on the
MTM specimens (final thickness of nearly 50 nm, roughness pending). 5 nm BaTiO3 did
form a durable tribocoating under elevated temperature (80 °C) or at higher SRR (50%). Two
weight percentages of dispersed NCs, 1 wt.% and 10 wt.%, were tested, and no significant
differences were observed. Other experimental parameters (time and rolling speed) were
shown to have a less significant benefit on coating formation than temperature did.

Task 4.0: Optimization of Nanocrystal-Enabled TriboCoating Fluids

We continued development of next-generation tribocoating nanocrystal formulations by
synthesizing 5 nm ZrO₂ nanocrystals with a series of labile capping agents (PCNS20 and
PCNS21). Comparative MTM testing demonstrated distinct performance advantages among
these candidates: PCNS18-capped ZrO₂ in PAO2 produced the lowest wear normalized to
coating thickness, while PCNS21-capped ZrO₂ enabled the fastest tribofilm growth rate
(Figure 14).

                                         Page 18 of 66

Figure 14. Comparison of growth and durability for NS18, NS20, and NS21 capping agents. Deposition
tests are on 52100 steel using 1 wt.% dispersed ZrO2 nanocrystals in PAO2 at an entrainment speed of
100 mm/s, 5% SRR, 75 N normal load, and 2 h duration. Durability tests are using the same conditions,
in pure PAO2.

In parallel, we initiated development of core–shell TiO₂–ZrO₂ nanocrystals to evaluate
whether mixed-oxide architectures yield synergistic improvements in tribofilm formation or
durability.

To enable TriboCoating deployment in applications requiring higher-viscosity lubricants, we
developed PCNS19, a formulation that supports stable dispersion of 5 nm ZrO₂ nanocrystals
in ISO 320–grade oils at 1 wt.% while maintaining optical clarity. The significantly higher
viscosity of ISO 320 oils constrained the maximum achievable nanoparticle loading (<5
wt%) compared to PAO2-based systems (up to ~40 wt.%). Initial formulations exhibited
haze due to incomplete dispersion; however, incorporating a polyol ester base stock and
applying controlled heating during mixing produced fully transparent, well-dispersed fluids.
Although elevated mixing temperatures raised concerns regarding base-oil oxidation, prior
studies established that Pixelligent’s ZrO₂ nanocrystals impart improved oxidative stability,
mitigating the risk of degradation during processing. The final PCNS19–ZrO₂ formulation
was fixed at 1 wt.% nanocrystal concentration and subsequently evaluated for scuffing
resistance, micropitting performance, and electrically induced wear using the MPR platform
(Task 5.0). Following benchtop validation, this formulation was advanced for system-level
evaluation in a wind-turbine gearbox (Task 8.0).

We investigated the synthesis and dispersion behavior of 10–15 nm ZrO₂ nanocrystals to
evaluate their suitability for accelerated tribocoating formation on gear and bearing surfaces.
Increasing nanocrystal size was pursued as a strategy to enhance deposition rates under
mixed rolling/sliding contact. Structural characterization confirmed a mixture of nanocrystal
sizes, where ~10 nm ZrO₂ nanocrystals predominantly exhibit a cubic phase, while ~15 nm
nanocrystals transition fully to the monoclinic phase (Figure 15).

Efforts to apply surface-capping treatments achieved only partial stabilization. The
nanocrystals formed well-dispersed suspensions at 50 wt.% in nonpolar solvents such as
toluene, although they sedimented upon centrifugation. In contrast, dispersions in PAO2, the
lubricant of interest for tribocoating deposition, showed limited stability and settled within
several days. Due to the instability of these materials, no additional testing was performed on
larger ZrO2 nanocrystals.

We performed a coordinated series of MTM deposition studies to compare tribofilm
formation from TiO₂ (PTNS19), ZrO₂ (PCNS18), and BaTiO₃ (BTNS19) nanocrystals in
PAO2. Under standardized MTM conditions for ZrO₂ and TiO₂ (75 N, 100 mm/s, 5% SRR, 2
h, RT) and temperature-boosted conditions for BaTiO₃ (75 N, 100 mm/s, 50% SRR, 2 h, 80
°C), the oxide materials showed distinct deposition behaviors. ZrO₂ produced the thickest
tribocoating (≈73 nm), while TiO₂ and BaTiO₃ formed coatings of approximately 40 nm.
Traction measurements revealed a clear correlation between coating roughness and frictional
response: BaTiO₃ exhibited the lowest traction coefficient (0.06) and correspondingly lowest
surface roughness (<10 nm), whereas TiO₂ displayed the highest traction coefficient (0.12)
and the roughest surface (25–30 nm). ZrO₂ showed intermediate values in both metrics.

Durability testing performed after deposition demonstrated that all three oxide coatings
provided effective anti-scuffing protection, whereas uncoated steel exhibited scuffing under
                                        Page 20 of 66

identical conditions (Figure 16). Among the three, ZrO₂ tribocoatings showed the lowest
normalized wear during an 8-hour PAO2 durability test (75 N, 100 mm/s, 5% SRR). TiO₂
exhibited moderate wear, and BaTiO₃ displayed the highest wear rate. Importantly, our prior
work indicates that wear rate alone is not a complete performance metric, as thin BaTiO₃
films can outperform thicker ZrO₂ films in intentionally aggressive scuffing scenarios.
Collectively, these findings provide a comparative understanding of nanocrystal chemistry,
coating growth kinetics, tribofilm roughness, frictional behavior, and durability across ZrO₂,
TiO₂, and BaTiO₃ systems. The results directly support down-selection of ZrO₂
(PCNS18/PCNS19) and TiO₂ (PTNS19) for continued development, while BaTiO₃ remains a
promising candidate where low friction and scuff resistance are prioritized under elevated
temperature or high-SRR operation.

Task 5.0: Study of Tribocoating Performance

We demonstrated that BaTiO₃, TiO₂, and ZrO₂ tribocoatings exhibit excellent durability and
wear resistance across a range of severe lubrication environments relevant to wind turbine
and electric vehicle (EV) drivetrain applications. In low-viscosity PAO2 lubricated
rolling/sliding contacts, these metal-oxide coatings sustained maximum Hertzian pressures
exceeding 1.2 GPa without measurable degradation. These results establish BaTiO₃, TiO₂,
and ZrO₂ nanocrystals as viable building blocks for mechanically resilient tribocoatings
capable of operating in harsh, mixed-lubrication regimes.

We further evaluated friction, wear, and chemical compatibility of the coatings in a fully
formulated EV lubricant provided by ExxonMobil. All three coatings showed stable behavior
with no evidence of antagonistic interactions with the additive package. In particular, the
ZrO₂ tribocoating demonstrated exceptional durability in an aggressive scuffing environment
when tested in a commercial EV oil, maintaining coating integrity under high sliding ratios
and elevated loads.

Durability analysis confirmed that mixed rolling/sliding tests performed in a commercial gear
oil, without any nanoparticle replenishment, still produced tribocoatings exceeding 25 nm
thickness on steel substrates. These results demonstrate that the coatings remain stable and
mechanically persistent even after extended operation in oils lacking nanocrystal feedstock.
Although each metal oxide shows strengths under different tribological conditions, enabling
tunable performance depending on application, we performed detailed mechanical
characterization of TiO₂ tribocoatings to understand their intrinsic material properties.

While determining the most promising tribocoating is arguable due to the different
performances of the three metal oxides under varying conditions, meaning that, depending on
the operating conditions, selecting a different metal oxide can provide tunable tribological
performance, UPenn performed nanoindentation on the TiO2-coated disk. Nanoindentation
was performed using a Bruker Hysitron TI 980 TriboIndenter equipped with XP (Accelerated
Property Mapping) mode, which enables high-throughput quantitative mapping of
mechanical properties by combining an electrostatically actuated transducer, fast data
acquisition electronics, and in-situ SPM imaging.

Figure 17a shows the TiO₂ tribocoatings wear track and the area selected for
nanoindentation. Figure 17b presents the SPM topography of the coating, showing a
measured surface roughness of 37 nm. For nanoindentation of thin films, to minimize
substrate effects, the indentation depth should be less than approximately 10% of the coating
thickness. However, to avoid the influence of surface roughness, the indentation depth should
be greater than the characteristic surface roughness. Since the TiO₂ tribocoatings show
thicknesses between 40 and 60 nm, satisfying the first condition would require indentation
depths below 6 nm. Conversely, satisfying the roughness condition would require depths of
about 40 nm. Due to the low coating thickness, it is therefore not possible to meet both
criteria simultaneously, making it difficult to obtain substrate effect free results.
Nevertheless, the following results are presented with the caveat that some substrate effect
may be present.

                                      Page 22 of 66

To determine the appropriate indentation depth, multiple sets of indents were performed to
identify the saturation point of the measured elastic modulus and hardness, which indicates
the onset of substrate influence. Figure 17c shows representative load-displacement curves,
and Figure 17d shows the corresponding elastic modulus and hardness as a function of depth.
Based on these observations, a maximum load of 500 μN was selected as a trade-off to
minimize substrate effects while reducing surface roughness influence on the measured
mechanical properties. Finally, Figure 17e presents the elastic modulus and hardness maps
obtained from property mapping of the selected area to achieve statistically meaningful
results. According to these data, the average elastic modulus of the TiO₂ coating ranges from
160 to 190 GPa, with an average hardness between 3 and 5 GPa.

Task 7.0: Microscopic Study of Coating Deposition and Wear Mechanisms

We performed tribocoating tests using atomic force microscopy (AFM) on BaTiO3, ZrO2,
and TiO2 nanocrystals dispersed in PAO2 on 52100 steel substrates. To perform the AFM
tribocoating test, a ca. 30-50 μm spherical steel colloid was attached to a tapping-mode AFM
cantilever to prepare a colloidal probe.

For BaTiO3, the test was initially performed at 80 °C with a maximum applied Hertzian
pressure of ca. 200 MPa, using a rastering speed of 200 μm/s for tribocoating. After every
100 cycles of tribocoating, one image was captured at 50 μm/s to measure the coating
thickness and other topographical features. Figure 22a shows the coating thickness as a
function of the number of cycles, indicating that after 1200 cycles, the coating reaches a
saturated thickness of 60 nm. Figure 22b shows the evolution of surface roughness (root
mean squares of heights), which reaches around 30 nm, higher than the steady-state
roughness (<10 nm) observed in MTM tests for BaTiO3 coatings. Figure 22c presents the
AFM image of the tribocoating after 1300 cycles of growth, and Figure 22d shows the
corresponding height profiles across the coating (measured along line P1) at different growth
stages. According to these results, regions near the coating edge exhibit monotonic growth
until reaching a saturated thickness, where the growth and wear rates become nearly
balanced. In contrast, the central region likely experiences reduced nanoparticle entrainment;
after initial growth, the wear rate exceeds the deposition rate, leading to a lower equilibrium
thickness.

                                         Page 26 of 66

In another test, using the same BaTiO3-coated colloidal steel against fresh steel substrate the
growth of BaTiO3 was studied at room temperature. The results showed a similar saturated
thickness (ca. 60 nm) as observed at 80 °C. To exclude the effect of the pre-existing coating
on the colloid, an additional test was performed using a fresh colloid on a fresh steel surface
at room temperature; in this case, no coating growth was observed even after 3000 cycles.
These results suggest that the presence of an existing tribocoating on one of the contact
surfaces promotes coating growth on the opposing surface, even at lower temperatures.

Task 8.0 - Further Investigation in the Mitigation of Scuffing and Electrically Induced
Failure Modes

We conducted benchtop testing on tribocoating derived from fluid containing TiO2
nanocrystal in ISO 320 fluids. As done in prior testing with ZrO2 fluids, the MPR was used
to form the tribocoatings with the fluid containing TiO2 using similar operating conditions,
then the samples with tribocoating were tested with the same commercial WT gear oil
(commercially available wind turbine oil). To evaluate the tribocoating formation process a
time study was conducted on the MPR with the fluid containing TiO2. In this test a steady
condition was applied to the contact and the test was interrupted at several time intervals (4
min, 8 min, 15 min, 30 min, 45 min, 60 min) (Figure 28). The test sample was optically
examined at each time interval to observe the tribocoating formation. From these
observations the tribocoating began to form in as little as 4 min and was fully formed in 15 -
30 min. After 60 min of running time, the test was fully complete the sample was analyzed
with SEM EDS, which showed significant amount of titanium on the surface. Profilometry of
the tribocoated sample showed that the roughness was reduced from 350nm to 240nm Sq.
The wear that occurred on the test sample was also measured which showed minimal wear,
~0.3 um depth, compared to ZrO2 which resulted in depths up to 1.5um.

Micropitting, macropitting (eWEC), and scuffing testing of the tribocoating formed on the
MPR samples, for each test a new tribocoated sample is prepared using the same conditions
as the coating formation study without the interruptions at time intervals. The micropitting
test of the TiO2 tribocoated sample, which is also a time study, results showed minimal
micropitting after final 400 min run time with only 265 um2 of total wear compared to 4000
um2 for uncoated samples and 966 um2 for ZrO2 tribocoated samples (Figure 29). This
represents a 90% improvement in micropitting wear protection with the TiO2 tribocoated
sample compared to the uncoated. The electrically driven white etching crack (eWEC)
macropitting test also showed that the TiO2 tribocoated sample prolonged the life to about 98
million cycles compared to the uncoated and ZrO2 tribocoated samples which failed at 70
million cycles and 65 million cycles respectively (Figure 30). This represents a 40%
improvement in extension of life relative to e-WEC failure with the TiO2 tribocoated sample

compared to the uncoated. The scuffing test also showed that TiO2 tribocoated sample
improved performance by enabling an increase scuffing load of 467N compared to the
uncoated scuffing load of 283N and ZrO2 tribocoated samples which scuffed at about 362N
(Figure 31). This represents a 65% improvement in scuffing protection with the TiO2
tribocoated sample compared to the uncoated.

Task 9.0: Process Optimization and Scale-Up of Selected Nano-Enabled TriboCoating
Fluid

To enable testing in full-scale wind turbine hardware, we initiated scale-up of the
TriboCoating fluid formulated with ISO 320 base oil and Pixelligent’s ZrO₂ nanocrystals
(PCNS19). Although Pixelligent previously produced 40 L batches, the Winergy-Flender
gearbox demonstration required approximately 100 gallons (~400 L), necessitating blending
at ExxonMobil’s facilities.

                                       Page 41 of 66

A process suitable for ExxonMobil’s blending infrastructure was established, limiting the
mixing temperature to 60 °C and the mixing duration to 8 hours. At room temperature the
formulation exhibits slight haze; however, we confirmed that the dispersion becomes
optically clear at 60 °C, which corresponds to the expected operating temperature inside the
Winergy-Flender gearbox. To support large-scale blending, Pixelligent prepared and shipped
a 10 kg stock solution of PCNS19 in PAO6 at 40 wt.% concentration to ExxonMobil for
incorporation into the ISO 320 fluid.

Winergy-Flender successfully demonstrated the formation of TriboCoatings inside a fully
assembled wind turbine gearbox. The test was conducted on March 3–4 at the Winergy-
Flender facility with members of the Argonne team present. A GE 1.5 MW gearbox
(GE1.5SLE) served as the test article and was filled entirely with the tribocoating fluid in
place of commercial gear oil (Figure 45). There are currently 14,000 of these units in the
field, mostly onshore, with a warranty period of 3-5 years and which are considered generally
stable.

Figure 45. Image of the Winergy-Flender’s back-to-back test dyno used in regular gearbox production
to perform load tests of the gearbox after final assembly.

The gearbox underwent Winergy-Flender’s standard back-to-back dynamometer load test,
which includes three power stages: 256 kW, 600 kW, and 1.5 MW. Following the standard
test, an additional low-lambda run was performed to further promote tribofilm formation.
Operational constraints required staying within normal load test speed and power conditions,
so the lowest-speed stage was used for this additional run.

The gears were visually inspected and photographed before the test, after the standard load
test, and after the low-lambda run (Figure 46). The contact surfaces exhibited visible
tribofilm formation, confirming that tribocoatings can form under full-scale wind turbine
gearbox operating conditions. Field deployment of the pretreated gearbox is planned at
Capstone Infrastructure's Erie Shores Wind Farm (Canada), pending customer approval. The
date for commissioning is pending.

                                        Page 42 of 66

Figure 46. Pictures of gear teeth on the high-speed stage gear, before (left), first run (middle), and
second run (right) using tribocoating fluid with ZrO2 nanocrystals.

Accelerated aging was performed on the formulation used for the Winergy-Flender test to
assess stability and compatibility. The protocol, standard for qualifying gear-lubricant blends,
involved heating the sample to 120 °C for 60 minutes, storing it at room temperature for 30
days, and centrifuging to detect settling.

Two samples were tested:
1. Heated sample (120 °C for 1 hour), and
2. Unheated sample stored at room temperature.

The initial heating step increased dispersion clarity, while the unheated sample remained
slightly hazy due to reduced blending temperature during scale-up (Figure 47). After 30 days
of storage and centrifugation, neither sample showed settling nor dropout, indicating robust
dispersion stability (Figure 48). The same procedure was repeated for the post-gearbox
tribocoating fluid used by Winergy-Flender. No drop-out settling was observed for both the
heated and unheated samples, confirming stability after field-representative operation.

Further evaluation of the ZrO2 in ISO 320 (PCNS19) formulation was conducted using the

The ZrO2 in ISO 320 (PCNS19) exhibited excellent wear protection, with roller wear
measured at 11 mg, far below the ~30 mg wear threshold typically achieved by fully
formulated industrial gear oils. A characteristic blue surface film on the rollers confirmed
tribofilm formation (Figure 49).

coated and uncoated rollers (Figure 50):

• Uncoated rollers: Failed the test, with wear values ~100 mg; corresponding inner/outer

• TriboCoated rollers: Exhibited no measurable wear; the corresponding races also
 showed no wear, and cage wear was substantially reduced compared to uncoated
 specimens.

during the FE8 evaluation Coated bearings showed no damage from the test.

These results confirm that tribocoating treatment dramatically improves wear resistance. The

Key Achievements
 1. Synthesis of BaTiO₃ Nanocrystals
 Prepared two sizes of BaTiO₃ nanocrystals (5 nm and 15 nm) scalable, cost-effective
 pathway with readily available precursors.

 2. Demonstrated Rapid, Room-Temperature Tribocoating Formation Across a Wide
 Range of Operating Conditions
 Successfully formed durable metal-oxide tribofilms at room temperature across sliding–
 rolling ratios representative of gears and rolling-element bearings.
 • Coatings demonstrated stability in low-viscosity PAO2 without AW/EP
 additives, with no wear, cracking, or delamination observed for up to 82,000
 loading cycles.
 • Tribocoatings remained durable in ethanol-lubricated environments, confirming
 applicability for fuel pump systems.
 • Coatings also remained stable and functional in fluids containing additive
 packages used in electric vehicles, confirming application potential in EV
 drivetrains.

 These results validate tribocoating as a viable surface-protection strategy for demanding
 mechanical systems across multiple sectors.

 3. Developed a Spatially Resolved Measurement Method to Map Film Growth
 Behavior
 Created an innovative spatially resolved technique for measuring tribofilm growth as a
 function of local position, revealing that sliding distance is a key driver for tribofilm
 formation. This method provides new mechanistic insight into tribosintering behavior.

 4. Completed Full-Scale Validation in a 1.5 MW Wind-Turbine Gearbox
 Successfully executed a full-scale Tribocoating formation test in a GE 1.5 MW wind-
 turbine gearbox at the Winergy-Flender manufacturing facility using standard
 dynamometer load-test procedures. Gear inspections before and after testing confirmed
 visible tribofilm formation under production-representative operating conditions.

 5. FE8 Testing Confirmed Exceptional Wear Protection for ZrO₂ Tribocoatings
 FE8 four-stage bearing tests on ZrO₂ in ISO 320 lubricant demonstrated roller wear of
 only 11 mg, significantly below the 30 mg performance threshold of fully formulated
 industrial gear oils.

 When repeated with coated and uncoated rollers in a lower-performance reference oil,
 coated rollers exhibited no measurable wear, while uncoated rollers showed ~100 mg
 wear and extensive race and cage damage. These results confirm the robustness of
 tribocoatings under severe bearing operating conditions.

 6. Enhanced EV Lubricant Performance Using ZrO₂ Tribocoatings
 Demonstrated that ZrO₂ tribofilms significantly improve steel surface durability under
 harsh EV-relevant conditions, validating the relevance of tribocoating technology for
 next-generation electric-drivetrain lubrication challenges.
                                        Page 56 of 66

7. Demonstrated Superior Micropitting and eWEC Performance with TiO₂
Tribocoatings
TiO₂ nanocrystals in ISO 320 fluids formed smoother, lower-wear coatings compared to
ZrO₂.
 • 90% reduction in micropitting wear vs. untreated steel
 • 75% reduction in micropitting wear vs. ZrO₂-treated steel
 • ~40% improvement in electrically accelerated wear (eWEC) compared to both
 uncoated and ZrO₂-coated surfaces
These results identify TiO₂ as a highly promising next-generation tribocoating material.

8. Successfully Achieved Final Project Milestone (Milestone 9.3)
Met DOE performance targets for demonstrating ≥50% wear reduction under gear-
relevant conditions using the FE8 Rolling Bearing Test Rig and a low-performance test
oil, achieving, in practice, complete elimination of wear on coated rollers.

Lessons Learned
  1. The tribocoating strategy works. This project showed that tribofilms formed on
 contacting steel surfaces in nanocrystal-containing lubricants under a wide range of
 conditions subsequently survive large numbers of loading cycles under harsh tribological
 conditions, mitigating wear and other failure modes. This establishes tribocoatings as a
 viable in situ method for surface protection, mitigating the need for separate expensive,
 energy-consuming, labor-intensive coating methods.
  2. Material selection is critical, performance varies significantly by oxide chemistry.
 While ZrO₂ and TiO₂ nanocrystals consistently produced durable tribofilms, Al₂O₃
 nanocrystals exhibited excessive wear and proved unsuitable for tribocoating
 applications. Early-stage material screening is essential to avoid investing resources in
 poor-performing candidates.
  3. Additive interactions can strongly influence tribofilm formation.
 Some antiwear and extreme-pressure additives inhibited or reduced tribofilm growth,
 while dispersants were beneficial for stabilizing formulations. Understanding these
 interactions is key to designing fluids compatible with the Tribocoating process.
  4. Stronger electric fields can negatively impact tribofilm quality.
 Electrical current accelerated film growth but produced heterogeneous, rough, and poorly
 adhered TiO₂ films. This indicates that electrical assistance is not universally beneficial
 and must be optimized by material system.
  5. Sliding distance is a primary driver of tribofilm growth.
 Spatially resolved analysis showed that growth depends more on accumulated sliding
 distance than on localized contact pressure alone. This insight helps guide how run-in
 procedures should be optimized in real-world components.
  6. Tribocoatings can form reliably under real-world gearbox operating conditions.
 Full-scale gearbox testing demonstrated that the films form under standard production
 load-test conditions, validating that specialized equipment is not required. This reduces
 deployment barriers for industrial adoption.
  7. Scaling fluid preparation introduces challenges not present in lab-scale synthesis.
 Large-batch blending required stricter control of temperature and mixing times to
 maintain clarity and stability. Early collaboration with industrial blending partners is
 necessary to ensure formulation robustness at production volume.
  8. Nanocrystal dispersions can remain stable even when initial appearance is hazy.
 Accelerated aging confirmed that temporary haze from lower-temperature blending does
 not compromise long-term dispersion stability, informing future decisions around
 manufacturing constraints.
  9. Tribocoatings deliver consistent performance in low-viscosity and additive-free
 fluids.
 The successful formation and durability of films in PAO2 and even ethanol indicates a
 broad applicability range, including systems where standard AW/EP additives are
 restricted or undesirable.
  10. TiO₂ nanocrystals may offer superior protection in certain wear regimes.
 Compared to ZrO₂, TiO₂ tribofilms showed dramatically improved micropitting and
 eWEC performance, suggesting that material optimization remains a powerful lever for
 improving tribocoating outcomes.
                                         Page 59 of 66

11. Coatings formed prior to lubricant deployment can significantly improve durability
 with lower-performance oils.
 FE8 testing with coated rollers in a low-performance oil demonstrated that tribocoatings
 can effectively “upgrade” the wear protection of otherwise insufficient lubricants.
12. Internal friction behavior and tribofilm thickness do not always correlate directly.
 Several tests showed thicker films associated with higher friction, or thin films associated
 with low friction, indicating that optimizing tribocoatings requires balancing multiple
 tribological metrics rather than maximizing thickness alone.
13. Cross-partner testing accelerates validation and exposes edge cases early.
 Collaboration between Argonne, Winergy-Flender, Infineum, UPenn, and ExxonMobil
 revealed critical insights that would not have been captured within a single testing
 environment.

Conclusions
 This project successfully demonstrated the viability, scalability, and industrial relevance
 of tribocoating technology, establishing a new approach for forming protective metal-
 oxide coatings on gears and bearings through in-situ tribosintering of nanocrystals.
 Across laboratory, benchtop, and full-scale gearbox environments, the tribocoating
 process consistently produced durable films capable of reducing wear, improving surface
 robustness, and enhancing performance under harsh operating conditions.

 Throughout the three-year effort, ZrO₂, TiO₂, and BaTiO₃ nanocrystal systems were
 developed, optimized, and validated, while Al₂O₃ was ruled out due to poor performance.
 Key technical milestones were met or exceeded, including formation of coatings
 exceeding 25 nm within two hours, demonstration of extended durability under mixed-
 friction conditions, and achievement of the Year 2 go/no-go requirements for improved
 micropitting, scuffing, and wear performance. TiO₂ formulations delivered particularly
 strong improvements in micropitting and electrically accelerated failure resistance, while
 ZrO₂-based formulations excelled in full-scale gearbox and FE8 bearing tests.

 Importantly, the tribocoating process was shown to be compatible with existing
 manufacturing workflows. The successful dynamometer test at Winergy-Flender in a 1.5
 MW wind-turbine gearbox using standard production load-test procedures demonstrated
 that tribocoatings can form reliably at industrial scale without additional equipment or
 surface preparation. FE8 testing further confirmed that surfaces treated with tribocoatings
 provided exceptional wear protection, meeting and surpassing performance targets
 defined for the project.

 Collectively, the results confirm that tribocoating is a practical, energy-efficient, and low-
 cost method for improving the durability and reliability of components used in wind
 turbines, electric vehicles, and other demanding mechanical systems. The outcomes of
 this project lay a strong foundation for future field deployment and broader
 commercialization of nano-enabled tribological surface treatments.

Future Works/Recommendations
 Based on the results of this project and the demonstrated feasibility of tribocoating
 technology for large-scale mechanical systems, several avenues of future development
 are recommended to advance commercialization, optimize performance across
 applications, and address remaining technical questions.

 1. Complete Field Deployment and Long-Term Monitoring
 • Proceed with field testing of the pretreated wind-turbine gearbox at Capstone
 Infrastructure’s Erie Shores Wind Farm.
 • Implement a long-term monitoring program to assess coating durability, wear
 evolution, and lubricant stability under real-world loading, temperatures, and
 contamination conditions.
 • Collect teardown data after extended operation to compare full-scale field
 performance to laboratory and dynamometer results.
 2. Expand Coating Optimization Across Nanocrystal Chemistries
 • Continue evaluating TiO₂ tribocoatings, which have shown superior micropitting
 resistance, smoother coating morphology, and improved electrically accelerated
 failure resistance relative to ZrO₂.
 • Investigate synergistic blends (e.g., TiO₂/ZrO₂) to balance rapid deposition kinetics
 with maximum wear protection.
 • Further assess BaTiO₃ nanocrystals, including complete tribological characterization
 and additive compatibility studies.
 3. Refine Capping Agents and Additive Interactions
 • Perform systematic studies on capping-agent chemistry to optimize film growth,
 durability, and stability, particularly labile systems.
 • Investigate the mechanisms by which anti-wear additives inhibit film formation and
 identify additive packages that complement, rather than compete with, Tribocoating
 formation.
 • Develop additive-optimized tribocoating fluids for target applications such as EV
 gearboxes, high-load industrial gear oils, and low-viscosity transport fuels.
 4. Improve Nanocrystal Dispersion Stability in High-Viscosity Oils
 • Continue refining formulation strategies for ISO 320 and higher-viscosity base
 stocks, including co-solvents, modified mixing protocols, and optimized surface
 ligands.
 • Evaluate long-term thermal, oxidative, and storage stability of high-viscosity
 formulations using accelerated aging and field-relevant thermal cycling.
 5. Expand Electrified Tribology Studies
 • Further explore the effects of electric fields and currents on deposition mechanisms,
 film uniformity, and coating durability, particularly for materials like TiO₂ that
 showed sensitivity to high current.
 • Validate whether optimized formulations or capping agents can mitigate non-uniform
 deposition under electrified contact conditions in EV-relevant environments.
 6. Advance Modeling and Fundamental Mechanistic Understanding
 • Develop predictive models linking nanoparticle size, contact pressure, SRR, and
 sliding distance to film-growth kinetics to guide future formulation design.

                                       Page 62 of 66

• Conduct in-situ spectroscopy, advanced microscopy, and chemical mapping to better
 understand film composition evolution, sintering behavior, and mechanical
 reinforcement mechanisms.
7. Scale-Up, Manufacturing Integration, and Commercial Readiness
• Work with lubricant manufacturers to finalize scalable blending protocols, including
 validated temperature/time envelopes and quality-control metrics.
• Develop best-practice guidelines for industrial deployment, including treatment
 cycles, operating conditions for optimal deposition, and compatibility with existing
 OEM qualification tests.
• Conduct technology economic analysis and lifecycle assessments to quantify cost
 savings, energy reduction, and environmental benefits associated with adopting
 tribocoating technology.
