 ABSTRACT

This end-of-year report summarizes progress on using seismic monitoring to detect, associate, and locate
anomalous vibration signals that may indicate potential containment breaches. The work focused on four
key tasks:

1. Developing a database of continuous waveforms and ground-truth event data from multiple sensing
 modalities.

2. Refining and implementing detection and association algorithms to generate a catalog of anomalous
 underground activities.

3. Testing and improving distributed acoustic sensing amplitude-based geolocation methods to build an
 event location catalog.

4. Testing and refining seismic array polarization-based geolocation methods to build an event location
 catalog.

This report provides a brief recap of results from the FY25 midyear report (Tasks 1 and 2) and presents
new findings from geolocation methods (Tasks 3 and 4).

 1. INTRODUCTION

Seismic monitoring shows strong potential as a safeguards strategy for geological repositories—that is,
for detecting underground activities that may indicate breaches in containment. Operational processes—
such as drilling, conveyor operations, and cart movements—generate vibrations that can be recorded by
seismic sensors. However, sustained underground monitoring with traditional seismometers presents
challenges, including frequent maintenance needs and the requirement for dense spatial sampling to
achieve accurate 3D source localization. In addition, conventional seismic detection and localization
methods are optimized for high signal-to-noise events such as earthquakes and explosions, whereas
facility operations produce low-amplitude, overlapping, and emergent signals that are more difficult to
characterize.

Seismoacoustic monitoring techniques developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) (e.g.,
Marcillo et al. 2019; Guenaga et al. 2021; Chai et al. 2023) have shown success in identifying and
geolocating such nonimpulsive, overlapping signals. Building on this work, we have adapted and
evaluated these algorithms using two distinctive datasets:

• Pleasant Gap, Pennsylvania: A dense surface array of 100 three-component geophones deployed
 above an active underground mine (Cunningham et al. 2025). Surface-based arrays are accessible,
 support precise timing, and can be powered by solar systems for long-term operation.

• Sanford Underground Research Facility (SURF): Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) data from
 over 5 km of fiber-optic cable (Cunningham et al. 2023). DAS enables dynamic strain measurements
 along existing fiber, providing high spatial sampling where conventional seismometer deployment is
 impractical.

Both datasets were collected at active underground facilities with operations and equipment directly
relevant to repository safeguards, including excavation, machinery transport, personnel movements,
ventilation systems, and air doors. Although vibration sensing technologies have demonstrated sensitivity

 1

to such activities, using them for robust detection and accurate geolocation of underground operations
remains relatively untested. Advancing these methodologies is therefore critical to establishing seismic
monitoring as a viable safeguards tool.

 2. DATABASE OF CONTINUOUS DATA AND GROUND-TRUTH EVENT DATA

We used two complementary sensing technologies to monitor subsurface operational anomalies:
(1) surface-deployed geophones and (2) underground DAS fiber-optic cable. Waveform databases were
constructed from two active underground facilities; each site offered activities that could serve as useful
proxies for activities relevant to safeguards in geological repositories. Both datasets include continuous
seismic records as well as ground-truth information on vibration-producing anthropogenic activities such
as blasting and machinery operations, providing a valuable resource for advancing underground
monitoring methodologies.

The surface seismic dataset—described in detail in the FY25 midyear report (ORNL/SPR-2025/3811)—
was collected at Graymont Pleasant Gap mine in Pennsylvania. Two campaigns (Campaigns 1 and 2)
involved the deployment of dense seismic arrays of 80–100 three-component geophones spaced 100–
400 m apart across a large portion of the mine’s footprint (Figure 1). Ground-truth information was
obtained directly from mine personnel and included a blast report with 173 documented events during
Campaign 1. Additional information on operational activities (e.g., use of fans, kilns, rock crushers, and
haul trucks and undocumented underground blasts) was also provided. The approximate frequencies of
these activities were further characterized using spatiotemporal patterns observed in station and network
spectrograms.

 3. ANOMALOUS OPERATIONAL EVENT DETECTION FROM CONTINUOUS DATA

Using continuous seismic data, we advance a spectral approach for detecting anomalous underground
activity by monitoring changes in the power spectral density (PSD) of background seismic signals. Unlike
conventional anomaly detectors, which struggle with low-amplitude, overlapping facility signals, the PSD
detector has proven effective in identifying operational activities (Marcillo et al. 2019; Guenaga et al.
2021) that are analogous to events associated with potential breaches in geological repositories. This
method enables the detection of events with distinctive frequency signatures even when signals overlap in
time or exhibit evolving frequency content.

With the dense seismic array, the PSD detector performs particularly well. For the DAS dataset, which
offers extremely high spatial sampling (1–4 m channel spacing) and records simultaneous events from
multiple subsurface locations, we modified the detector to target changes in power within specific
operational frequency bands. This adaptation enables the identification of distinct anomalous events that
may otherwise overlap in time.

Both implementations are parameterized and customizable, with user-defined settings for background
length, signal window length, minimum event duration, and deviation thresholds from the background.
The FY25 midyear report (ORNL/SPR-2025/3811) details the optimized parameters used to distinguish
operational events from background signals. Detected events are then characterized by these parameters
and linked to ground-truth activity databases, enabling robust associations with specific operational
processes.

Overall, this PSD-based approach enhances the precise identification and characterization of anomalous
underground events and demonstrates strong potential for improving seismic monitoring and safeguards
applications in geological repositories.

3.2 OPERATIONAL EVENT DETECTION WITH CONTINUOUS DAS DATA

Like geophone data, DAS strain measurements are sensitive to vibrations generated by operational events.
However, unlike three-component seismic recordings, DAS provides a single-component measurement of
axial strain with exceptionally high spatial sampling. Although this spatial density is advantageous,
running the PSD misfit detector on every channel is computationally intensive and impractical for rapid
monitoring. Moreover, when the high spatial resolution is not leveraged, discrete event detection and
association become unreliable because overlapping signals are common across such a large array and
easily recognizable through root mean square (RMS) misfit patterns (Figure 4).

other locations. Applying the spatial PSD detector to 1 week of continuous DAS data, we associated
detections with events based on their timing and spatial distribution and compiled them into an event

 4. ANOMALOUS OPERATIONAL EVENT GEOLOCATION FROM CONTINUOUS DATA

Traditionally, the locations of ground motion–producing events are determined from the arrival times of
seismic body waves, which travel efficiently through the earth and provide clear timing markers for event
localization. However, many operational events—such as industrial activities, vehicle movements, or
other anthropogenic sources—produce emergent, nonimpulsive signals rather than sharp seismic arrivals.
This phenomenon creates difficulty in measuring arrival times reliably and reduces the effectiveness of
conventional geolocation methods. Seeking to overcome this challenge, Chai et al. (2023) demonstrated
that seismic amplitudes, rather than arrival times, can be used to infer source locations from ground
vibrations, offering a more robust approach for these types of signals. Another geolocation technique that
avoids arrival-time picking is polarization analysis, which exploits the orientation of ground motion to
estimate source direction; however, this technique requires the use of three-component seismic sensors.

4.1 OPERATIONAL EVENT GEOLOCATION WITH CONTINUOUS GEOPHONE DATA

For the Pleasant Gap dataset, generated by a dense surface array of three-component geophones, we
tested two geolocation algorithms that do not require arrival-time picking: (1) source localization based
on polarization analysis and (2) amplitude attenuation modeling. Our results indicate that the polarization
method produces relatively large uncertainties; however, these uncertainties can be reduced for persistent
sources by stacking multiple location estimates.

To analyze the polarization characteristics of the seismic data, we followed Koper and Hawley’s
methodology, which extracts frequency-dependent polarization features using Park et al.’s approach. A
full description of our implementation is provided in the deliverable Catalog of Polarization-Based and
Amplitude Attenuation Event Locations at Pleasant Gap Using Surface Geophones (ORNL #244929). We
applied this approach to two event types: (1) an operational underground blast and (2) a highly repetitive,
monochromatic signal. The blast, despite its high signal-to-noise ratio, did not yield a well-resolved
location region (Figure 5). In contrast, the machinery signals from the main mine area were better
resolved because of the persistence of the source, which enabled stacking across multiple solutions
(Figure 6).

4.2 OPERATIONAL EVENT GEOLOCATION WITH CONTINUOUS DAS DATA

We applied the amplitude-based geolocation approach to the SURF dataset because DAS data provide
only a single component. Unlike traditional seismometer amplitudes, DAS amplitudes are strongly
influenced by site response, array geometry, and fiber installation practices (Muir and Zhan, 2021; Celli et
al. 2023). At SURF, fiber deployment varies significantly along different sections of the cable path
(Cunningham et al. 2023)—a common feature of both dark fiber and purpose-deployed installations. This
variability complicates amplitude correction and interpretation, often requiring either conversion to
ground motion (e.g., Daley et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2018; Lindsey et al. 2020) or the application of
channel-specific scaling factors to enable consistent amplitude comparisons.

Given the limited number of seismic stations along the fiber path, we chose to correct DAS amplitudes
using channel-specific scaling factors. Ideally, these factors would be derived from data with consistent
amplitudes across all channels, such as coherent ambient noise or teleseismic body waves. However,
coherent noise is unlikely at SURF because of the multiple fiber levels and nearly continuous operational
activity, and teleseismic arrivals were not observed in this experiment (Wuesterfeld et al. 2023). Instead,
we estimated a scaling factor for each channel as the normalized ratio of average peak blast amplitudes to
average pre-event noise levels. Although the assumption of uniform blast amplitudes is imperfect, this
approach improves relative amplitude measurements. Before correction, blast amplitudes did not exhibit
the expected decay with increasing source distance. After correction, however, a coherent amplitude–
distance decay relationship emerged (Figure 8), which is critical for the amplitude-based grid search
location method (Chai et al. 2023).

 9

The grid search approach proceeds in two steps: first, a known event (e.g., a blast) is used to estimate
medium properties; second, the amplitudes of an unknown event are compared to a synthetic grid of
amplitude predictions to infer its location. Although the method is promising, our results show that some
operational events at SURF are more accurately located with traditional techniques. For instance, arrival-
time picking yields precise locations for impulsive blasts, but cage (elevator) signals evolve continuously
and are less suited to geolocation with amplitude-based methods. To reflect these differences, we classify
events into four categories—(1) dynamic signals, (2) small, fiber-adjacent signals, (3) local events
confined to a single level, and (4) mine-wide events such as blasting—and discuss the most effective
geolocation strategies for each category in the following paragraphs.

Blasting events occurred approximately twice daily during the deployment at SURF. After amplitude
corrections are applied, these events exhibit a consistent decay of amplitude with distance and a clear
arrival-time moveout (Figure 8). Because the blast locations were known, we used one of them to
constrain medium properties at SURF. Specifically, we applied a grid-search approach following Chai et
al. (2023), using the initial RMS amplitude (Battaglia and Aki, 2003) to estimate relative medium
properties required for source localization.

Note that DAS measures strain rather than ground velocity or displacement; therefore, the inferred
medium properties and initial amplitude values should not be interpreted directly as attenuation or seismic
velocity. Although the amplitude-based approach can be used to locate additional blasts, arrival-time
methods remain more accurate for impulsive signals, and thus we rely on them for blast localization.

Stationary operational events occurring on a single level of SURF (either the 4100 or 4500 level) are
well-suited for Chai et al.’s (2023) amplitude-based location method. These events include those

 10

categorized as NA8, 9, 10, 12, which are described in detail in the FY25 midyear report. For each
detected event, we filter the waveforms to the frequency range used in the detection step, measure the
amplitude (twice the RMS), and apply the scaling factors. Using the previously determined medium
properties, we perform a grid search for the initial amplitude and location of the event within a 1 × 1 ft
grid surrounding the detected channels. The total search grid is defined by the minimum and maximum x
and y channel locations. We restrict location results to 50 ft perpendicular to the fiber. The search grid is
highly constrained because the fiber is more sensitive to longitudinal wave propagation, and events are
expected to occur within the tunnel containing the fiber. Unsurprisingly, we find that events detected
across fiber channels containing multiple directions are better constrained than those detected only across
linear fiber segments (Figure 9).

We also analyzed small events that occur very close to the fiber, often detected on fewer than 20
channels. These events are subject to the strong directional sensitivity of DAS, which favors longitudinal
wave detection. For instance, tap tests or nearby events may display smaller amplitudes at the nearest
channel if they are not aligned with the fiber’s sensitivity axis. Because of the limited number of channels
that the detect the events, combined with limitations of DAS resulting from directional sensitivity,
amplitude-based grid search location is challenging for these events. Instead, we locate these types of
events using an approach that identifies the channel at the apex of a parabolic fit based on event
amplitudes across all detected channels (Figure 10). This method can also be applied using arrival times
with similar results. Once the parabola is defined, we assign the event location to the channel located at
the apex of the parabola.

Dynamic events (those moving through space and time) are not suitable for traditional amplitude- or
arrival-based location algorithms. The most frequently observed dynamic signal at SURF is the
movement of the cage in the Ross Shaft. Cage movements are categorized as short-duration, high-
amplitude events between 10 and 12 Hz and propagating between channel numbers 402 and 512. The
event location in this case is listed as the location of the channels for which each event is detected. This
approach captures the range of the x-, y-, and z-coordinates of the event. We summarize the DAS event
locations for the trial period in Figure 11.

Although the use of DAS amplitudes for event location is promising, several challenges exist:

1. Scaling corrections are difficult without a sufficient number of traditional seismometers, teleseismic
 events, or coherent ambient noise measurements.

 12

2. Amplitude-based location methods are unsuitable for dynamic events that traverse the array in both
 time and space.

3. Fiber-adjacent events may not exhibit amplitude decay with distance because of directionality effects
 on fiber sensitivity, violating a key assumption of amplitude-based geolocation.

4. Grid search for blast events is challenging because of the 3D nature of the problem; blasting events
 often fall outside the DAS footprint, which makes arrival time methods more favorable.

 5. SUMMARY

Seismic monitoring is emerging as a promising safeguard strategy for tracking activities in geologic
repositories. Anthropogenic processes such as drilling, conveyor operations, and the movement of carts
and personnel generate mechanical energy that propagates as seismic waves through the surrounding rock
and can be detected with seismic instrumentation. We are using geophone and DAS data from active
mines to develop and test algorithms for detecting and locating such activities.

During the first two quarters of FY25, we completed two key deliverables. First, we compiled waveform
datasets from temporary deployments at the Graymont Pleasant Gap mine (Pennsylvania) and SURF
(South Dakota) and developed a ground-truth database of operational events at both sites. Second, we
built a database of operational events identified using a spectral anomaly detector optimized for
anthropogenic signals.

These waveform and event databases were then used in the second half of FY25 to test and refine
geolocation algorithms. Efforts in Q3 and Q4 focused on associating cataloged events with ground-truth
observations to further test, optimize, and evaluate geolocation performance.
