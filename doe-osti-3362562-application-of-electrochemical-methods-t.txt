 Executive Summary

 This report evaluates the potential application of electrochemical methods for monitoring the
reduction-oxidation (RedOx) potential of molten salts in molten salt reactors. Electrochemical methods
indirectly monitor materials performance in the reactor, since corrosion in molten salts is largely driven
by the RedOx condition of the salt. , This report will aid the NRC in its understanding of the capabilities
of electrochemical techniques.
 Electrochemical methods have been widely used for understanding the thermodynamics and kinetics
of molten salts, as well as the chemistry and RedOx potentials of a molten salt system. Electrochemical
methods are one such approach for measuring salt chemistry; inductively coupled argon-plasma mass
spectroscopy (ICP-MS) is another technique. Two notable electrochemical methods are potentiometry and
voltammetry. Potentiometry is based on the measurement of the open circuit potential (OCP) between a
sensor electrode (SE) and a reference electrode (RE). For accurate RedOx potential measurements using
potentiometry, the potential of the SE is ideally a function of the RedOx potential at a specific moment
(determined by salt chemistry) and the RE is independent of the composition changes during the OCP
measurement. In voltammetry, typically the current is measured when a voltage is applied to a working
electrode (WE) of the electrochemical cell. Note that, in voltammetry, there are typically three multiple
electrodes: WE, RE, and counter electrode (CE). There are different voltammetry techniques depending
on how the voltage is controlled during measurements, the most common being cyclic voltammetry (CV).
 Some electrochemical technologies can be used for multiple purposes. For example, depending on the
experimental setup and design, potentiometry can be used for impurity and RedOx potential
measurements, as well as to measure specific constituents in the salt being studied. Voltammetry with
various potential ramping modes is also a capable analytical technique that can be used for different
purposes, such as thermodynamic properties, diffusion coefficients, electrochemical mechanisms, etc.
Note that the general principles and theory behind these electrochemical methods are well established, but
for each molten salt measurement application, the requirements, accuracy, and sensitivity may be
different.
 RedOx potential monitoring and control were demonstrated in the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment
(MSRE), which used a fluoride salt fuel system. Much operational data and experience were gained from
the MSRE. One such observation was for RedOx potential in fluoride salts, the disproportionation of UF3
to UF4 and U metal at higher temperatures is a potential issue, meaning that a higher UF4/UF3 ratio is
needed. For a chloride salt MSR system, however, the available data and best methodology for RedOx
potential monitoring and control is very limited. UCl3 is expected to be more stable than UF3, and UCl4 is
known to be very corrosive. Consequently, the presence of UCl4 should be minimized whenever possible.
 It is noted that “RedOx potential” is a widely used and important concept in MSR salt chemistry,
though the term “RedOx potential” is not well-defined. “RedOx potential” of molten salt is generally used
to describe the salt chemistry and the corrosivity of molten salt to structural alloys. It is noted that this
report does not make any conclusions about the conformance of any specific electrochemical technology
or design with regulations that would apply to MSRs. The RedOx potential is also a measurable term and
can be defined as the potential difference between a working electrode and standard reference electrode.
Typically, the more positive the RedOx potential, the more corrosive the salt chemistry to the structural

 v

alloys. In molten salt chemistry, the term can also be applied to the fluorine chemical potential in a
fluoride salt, or to the chlorine chemical potential in a chloride salt.
 This assessment found that REs for fluorides that typically use graphite and boron nitride containers
for reference salts have not been demonstrated for long-term operation. The REs with boron nitride
containers were found to be generally stable for up to 12 days. Work is ongoing to develop a reliable RE
for long-term operation in fluoride and chloride salts. .
 In-line or in-process measurements would provide real time monitoring of salt chemistry.
Voltammetry and potentiometry may offer convenient in-line or in-process measurement capabilities.
Like the RedOx potential measurements, concentrations of corrosion products, oxide, and moisture
impurities can also be monitored using in-line electrochemical methods, and these measurements could be
designed and set up in an automated manner. The in-line measurement can provide information that may
not be achieved by discrete sampling methods. However, assessment of the performance of cables for
electrochemical methods under high radiation environments is not fully understood at this time.
 In even the simplest real-world applications, the chemical conditions that develop within a molten salt
system can be complex. The salt system is never completely isolated. An inventory of salt is constantly
circulated through the reactor systems. Some MSR applications subject the salt to multiple extreme
conditions that occur at the same time, including high temperatures, contact with structural materials,
contact with gas phases, exposure to different sources of contamination, temperature gradients, pressure
gradients, high-velocity flows, entrainment of solids, exposure to radiation, composition changes due to
fission processes, and exposure to tritium (even when it is produced at a different location in the reactor).
 In salt-fueled MSRs, many of the fission products remain dissolved in the molten salts, at least up to
their solubility limits. However, noble metal fission products that accumulate in the molten salt may
ultimately deposit on the materials of construction, while noble gases are recovered in the cover gas
system via gas sparging. The fission process is mildly oxidizing toward the dissolved fissile elements
(e.g., U[IV]) in the fuel salt, and gradually makes the fuel salt more oxidizing if no measures are in place
to prevent this from occurring. The salt composition and RedOx potential of the fuel salt is therefore
constantly changing, though slowly. This accelerates structural material corrosion by the fuel salt at high
temperatures. The introduction of oxides and moisture can also alter the RedOx potential and possibly
cause other issues. Salt chemistry in an MSR is critical for reliable operation and minimization of
structural material corrosion by fuel salt and should therefore be maintained within acceptable ranges.
 The last two decades have seen significant advancements in molten salt electrochemistry.
Electrochemical methods are moving toward enabling quantitative measurement of select impurities and
RedOx potentials, with potentiometry and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) having been demonstrated
even as far back as the MSRE. In-line salt chemistry monitoring is likely to be seen in the development
and deployment of test or commercial MSRs, and electrochemical methods seem well suited for this
purpose.
 This report provides an overview of RedOx potentials of molten salts relevant to MSRs and the status
of multiple electrochemical methods for potential MSR applications. It also provides an overview of
electrochemical measurement technologies for molten salts. Electrochemical sensor technologies for
molten salts seek to observe at least one of the various signatures related to the chemical conditions of the
molten salts. Generally, electrochemical sensors are intended to determine whether the chemical
conditions of the molten salt are within acceptable limits of operation. The chemical conditions of
concern may be related to corrosion, or perhaps to aspects such as core neutronics, fissile inventory, and
phase behaviors, etc. It is important to understand what signature the sensor is designed to observe, and
what that signature says about the condition of the salt.

 Electrochemical Monitoring for Molten Salt Reactors:
 Status Review
 1. INTRODUCTION
 Molten salt reactors (MSRs), as a family of advanced Generation IV-type reactors, have received a
tremendous amount of interest in recent years and MSRs are currently being developed around the world
[1, 2, 3, 4]. A salt-fueled MSR uses molten salt as a liquid fuel in the primary heat exchange system and a
non-fueled coolant salt in the secondary heat exchange system, as shown schematically in Figure 1. A
salt-cooled MSR uses solid fuels and molten salt coolants in both the primary and secondary heat
exchange systems. The fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR) is an example of the latter.
Both salt-fueled and salt-cooled MSRs share similarities in inherent reactor safety features since both
concepts use molten salt, but there are also significant differences. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) and Department of Energy (DOE) include both salt-fueled and salt-cooled MSRs
within the term MSRs.
 The MSR concept started with the Aircraft Reactor Experiment (ARE) program in 1954. The ARE
used NaF-ZrF4-UF4 as fuel salt, and the peak temperature of the salt reached 860°C. Though the duration
of the ARE’s reactor operation was quite short, it demonstrated the feasibility of the molten salt fuel
based on its chemical stabilities, good heat transfer properties, and the high solubility of uranium. It also
provided important information for the development of the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment (MSRE)
which operated from 1965 to 1969. In the MSRE, the fuel salt composition at the early stage was LiF-
BeF2-ZrF4-UF4 (61-29.1-5.0-0.9 mol%), with the uranium consisting of 33% 235U and 67% 238U. The
coolant salt was LiF-BeF2. At a later stage, the fissile material was changed to 233U. The moderator was
graphite and the cover gas for the molten salt was helium [5, 6].
 The unique and most important feature of a salt-fueled-MSR compared to traditional light-water
reactors (LWRs) is its inherent safety. The molten salts selected for MSRs feature good chemical stability
at high-temperature, low volatility, a low melting point and a high boiling point, and the reactor operates
at near atmospheric pressure. The negative reactivity coefficient is a significant safety feature for MSRs.
When the molten salt temperature increases, the volume increases and fissile materials density decreases,
which slows down the chain reaction of the fission process. Unlike solid fuels that contain all the fission
products, most of the fission products in MSRs are dissolved in molten salts. The UF4 salt constituent in
the MSRE example was dissolved in the LiF-BeF2-ZrF4 carrier salt. The metal fission products
accumulate in the salt or deposit on the reactor’s construction materials, while the noble gas fission
products are recovered in the cover gas by gas sparging. For varying the quantity of fuel salt in the
reactor, salt can be added to the reactor vessel and salt can be drained from the vessel.
 Based on the MSRE, the average total valence of the fission products of U4+ is slightly lower than
four, leading to a net result of some free fluorine gas being formed during operation. A simplified fission
process can be expressed as UF4 + n → Kr(g) + LnF3 + 1/2F2(g) [7]. The fluorine gas is very corrosive at
high temperatures and may react with UF3 and form UF4 (according to 1/2F2(g) + UF3 → UF4) or react
with the structural alloys of the reactor vessel. The fission process will gradually make the fuel salt
increasingly oxidizing, slowly changing the composition and corrosivity of the fuel salt. To maintain the
salt in a non-corrosive state, beryllium metal (encased in a perforated capsule of nickel) was introduced
through the sampling system and suspended in the flowing salt stream in the MSRE pump bowl. The
reaction with UF4 by Be + 2UF4 = BeF2 + 2UF3 produced UF3 at a rate of about 600 g per 8-hour
treatment.

 1

 With the above background, all fuel salts at high temperature can cause corrosion; therefore,
corrosion of structural materials is a concern in salt-fueled and salt-cooled MSRs. The material corrosion
is strongly dependent on the salt chemistry. All corrosion processes are accelerated by the increased
corrosivity of salt, including general corrosion, impurity-driven corrosion, dissimilar metal-driven and
temperature gradient-driven corrosion, and environmentally assisted cracking from fission product
exposure. To ensure years of safe operation, the salt chemistry needs to be maintained so corrosivity stays
within an acceptable range.

 The concept of a salt-cooled MSR that uses solid nuclear fuel and liquid salt as its coolant is shown in
Figure 2 using the fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor (FHR) as an example. The primary
coolant salt is LiF-BeF2 (66-34 mol%) (FLiBe) with enriched 7Li to minimize both the loss of fission
neutrons to the salt and the production of tritium. LiF-NaF-KF (46.5-11.5-42 mol%) (FLiNaK) or KF-
ZrF4 (58-42 mol%) was selected as a candidate for the intermediate loop. These reactor concepts will use
tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles (similar to those developed for the high-temperature gas-
cooled reactor, [HTGR]) immersed in the primary coolant salt.

 2

 Generally, a purified fuel salt is not very corrosive to the structural materials. The FLiBe coolant salt
loop during the MSRE operated for over 24,000 hours without any significant corrosion of structures in
the loop, suggesting low corrosion rates of the purified salts in the MSRE intermediate loop. The main
salt chemistry issue arises from the primary coolant salt system. The FHR primary coolant salt chemistry
is simplified in comparison to the MSRE fuel salt chemistry, which contained actinides and fission
products, but it can continuously pick up impurities during operations. Neutron irradiation of the 6Li
creates tritium and thus converts LiF into 3HF, which is corrosive. Refueling operations with new solid
fuel could add impurities to the salt. While new solid fuel will be cleaned, the heat-up and cooldown of
the fuel over time may allow impurities in the graphite moderator in the reactor core to diffuse out.
Therefore, salt chemistry monitoring and control are also needed to ensure corrosion rates will be
acceptably low for FHR salt coolants. A detailed review on the salt corrosion and chemistry control for
FHRs can be found in the literature [9].

 There are multiple proposed MSR designs for utilizing solid fuel and for utilizing liquid fuel.
Regardless of the MSR concept, salt chemistry in the primary systems need to be well controlled and
monitored with accurate knowledge of fuel salt chemistry to ensure MSR safeguards. Analyzing the salt
chemistry by inductively coupled argon-plasma mass spectroscopy (ICP-MS) involves sampling the fuel
salt and transferring the sample to an analytical facility capable of receiving high-radiation emitting
samples. During the MSRE, electrochemical methods including potentiometry and linear sweep
voltammetry (LSV) were used along with traditional chemical analysis for monitoring the reduction-
oxidation (RedOx) potential of the fuel salt. More details are provided later. The electrochemical methods
involve measuring the open circuit potential (OCP) of a potentiometry sensor immersed in the salt and
recording the signals due to the RedOx reactions induced by the voltage sweep applied by the
potentiostat. Electrochemical methods can be used to measure the RedOx potential of the salt sampled
from fuel salt, or some in-line sensors can be installed in the reactor to provide real-time RedOx potential
monitoring.

 The primary objective of this report is to evaluate potential applications of electrochemical methods
to monitor and/or control molten salt RedOx in MSRs, including both salt-fueled and salt-cooled MSRs.
These evaluation results are expected to aid the NRC in its understanding of the capabilities of this
technique. This report will help inform NRC reviews of potential license applications with reactor
monitoring and control, quality assurance, maintenance, and replacement of electrochemical monitoring
systems.
 The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 explains the connection between the
chemical potential and RedOx potential of molten salts for MSRs and information on the calculation of
chemical potentials in molten salts. Section 3 describes some basic concepts of MSR applications. Section
4 gives background information on operational experience of MSRs and their monitoring, Section 5 gives
an assessment of electrochemical methods for molten salts. Section 6 gives an assessment of
electrochemical methods for MSR applications. Section 7 summarizes considerations for additional
research and development activities, and Section 8 gives an overall summary of the report.
 TAKEAWAYS: There have only been two molten salt reactors. The Aircraft Reactor Experiment in
1954 and the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment between 1965 and 1969. Both reactors were based on
fluoride salt chemistries and operated at the Oak Ridge National Laboratory. Presently, there are multiple
designs of molten salt reactors under investigation based on both chloride and fluoride salt chemistries.
The RedOx potential of a salt is a measure of the chemical condition of the salt that provides critical
information for reactor operations. Electrochemical techniques can be used to indirectly monitor RedOx
potentials.

 2. CHEMICAL POTENTIAL OF MOLTEN SALTS
 2.1. Reduction/Oxidation Potentials
 Understanding of the present chemical conditions of molten salts for MSRs is necessary for their
continuous operation and is the motivation for discourse on electrochemical monitoring of MSRs. This
section explains the concept of chemical potentials within molten halide salt systems. The salt systems
described are limited to chloride and fluoride salts under consideration for use in MSRs as fuel salts,
blanket salts, and coolant salts. The examples are based on equilibrium thermodynamic models and are
illustrative of, though not rigorously representative of, real molten salt systems. They were chosen to
explain concepts that give useful insights into the behaviors of real molten salt systems. Rigorous
modeling of molten salt chemistries under specific conditions of reactor operations is beyond the scope of
this report and is in many regards beyond the ability of the current state of scientific knowledge. Such
modeling efforts are highly application specific with respect to reactor neutronics, salt chemistry, and
materials of construction. The examples presented here are for illustrative and educational purposes only.

 5

 The term “RedOx potential” sometimes causes confusion because it is used to describe a variety of
concepts. During RedOx reactions, the oxidation state is an integer indicative of the number of electrons
lost (for positive oxidation states) or gained (for negative oxidation states) relative to an element’s pure
elemental state. Since chemical bonding includes sharing of electrons rather than complete loss or gain of
electrons, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) definition of the oxidation
state reads: “The oxidation state of an atom is the charge of this atom after ionic approximation of its
heteronuclear bonds.” Due to this sharing of electrons in chemical bonds, the integer oxidation state does
not represent the real charge on an atom, but the concept is useful, and it serves as a qualitative indication
of any increase or decrease of charge for a specific element. The oxidation state concept is used in this
report. Two different elements of the same oxidation state should not be considered to have identical
charges, but for the purposes of this report, a change in oxidation state for a given element can be taken as
an indication of the direction of change in charge on that atom to a more positive valence or a more
negative valence.
 Thus, for a given element, when reduction occurs, the bonding capacity of that atom is said to
decrease, and when oxidation occurs, the bonding capacity of that atom is said to increase. For example,
unalloyed metallic uranium has a valence of zero (U0). Uranium in uranium trichloride (UCl3) and
uranium tetrachloride (UCl4) have valences of U3+ and U4+, respectively. Uranium in UCl4 is more
oxidized than uranium in UCl3, so uranium in UCl4 has more bonding capacity than uranium in UCl3 as
reflected in the number of chlorine atoms bonded. Likewise, uranium in U3O8 (two as U5+ and one as U6+)
is more oxidized than uranium in UO2 (as U4+) [13]. The chemical properties of an element are partially
dependent on its oxidation state. In this context, “oxidized” does not imply the presence of oxygen, it only
refers to the valence state of a chemical species being more positive, in this case for uranium.
 Without contextual knowledge, for electrochemistry, the term “RedOx potential” may be interpreted
to imply that there is an electrical potential at which some RedOx reaction occurs. An example of this is
seen during electrochemical measurements such as cyclic voltammetry (CV), where the RedOx potentials
of select species in molten salts are measured and compared. During a CV measurement, the electrical
potential of a working electrode (WE) is cycled between more oxidizing versus more reducing potentials
(or vice versa) and this electrical potential is quantitatively controlled and recorded, typically in units of
volts. The electrical potential at the working electrode is recorded based on its potential difference to a
reference electrode (RE), where the RE serves as a stable potential datum from which to measure the
potential difference to the variable potential of the WE. The current is measured throughout the CV
potential sweep, such that the electric current passing through the WE is recorded as a function of the
potential difference between the WE and RE.
 The authors conducted the following experiment, which is used as an illustrative example. A crucible
was filled with 500 g of molten LiCl-KCl eutectic salt heated to 500°C. A WE and counter electrode (CE)
were made of 1.5-mm-diameter tungsten wires. An Ag/AgCl RE was made from a 6-mm-diameter
closed-bottom mullite tube fitted inside with a 1-mm-diameter silver wire, and a small amount of LiCl-
KCl eutectic with 1 wt% AgCl was added to the inside of the mullite tube, enough to wet the bottom of
the silver wire. The WE was submerged 1-cm, and the CE and RE were each submerged 10-cm in the
salt.

 In this example, the reduction and oxidation potentials were essentially the same potential, namely the
RedOx potential of manganese in this salt system as exhibited by the CV. Given this salt system, the

discussion. RedOx potentials are related to chemical potentials within the salt systems. This interpretation
has a long history [14]. Chemical potential could also be used to characterize the RedOx state or RedOx
condition of the molten salts. As described by Olander [14], the chemical potential of gas X2 (e.g., X = F,
Cl) can be defined in terms of its partial pressure in gas phase, or ∆𝐺𝐺̅ 𝑋𝑋2 = 𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑅𝑃𝑃𝑋𝑋2 . A molten salt with

versa. The RedOx potentials observed during electrochemical measurements (potentials as voltages) and
RedOx potentials interpreted as chemical potentials (potentials as activities) are not completely unrelated
phenomena. In the example shown in Figure 6, if the MnCl2 concentration was decreased, the electrical
reduction potential would shift to more negative values, and if the MnCl2 concentration was increased, the
electrical reduction potential would shift to more positive values. At lower concentrations of a few weight
percent or less, electrical reduction potentials are greatly influenced by concentrations. The change of
MnCl2 concentrations will result in the change of chlorine chemical potential.

 According to IUPAC Recommendations 1997, “RedOx potential is the reduction/oxidation potential
of a compound measured under standard conditions against a standard reference half-cell.” [15]. In
aqueous electrochemistry, the “standard reference half-cell” is sometimes the “standard hydrogen
electrode (SHE)” whose potential is established, by convention, as zero volts. A SHE has a highly
specific configuration, which is intended to produce accuracy and reproducibility of measurements.
Unfortunately, the SHE is difficult to fabricate and use experimentally. Other reference electrodes are
more commonly used because they are more convenient in both of these regards. Often, the data collected
using an alternative reference electrode can be corrected to the values that would have been measured if a
SHE had been used.
 In this context, RedOx potential has a well-defined meaning and the value for a specific half-cell can
be determined by the Nernst Equation. In molten salt chemistry and electrochemistry literature, the
RedOx potential concept is widely used, but in many cases, it is loosely used. For example, the phrase
“RedOx potential of molten salt” does not mean anything itself. It must be given context. In some
literature, “RedOx potential of molten salt” refers to the potential difference between a working/indicator
electrode and an RE. This measured voltage can provide qualitive information on the chemistry and
corrosivity of the salt. Different names like RedOx potential, reduction potential, RedOx condition,
RedOx state, and RedOx chemistry have been used interchangeably to describe the chemistry, chemical
state, and corrosivity of molten salts.
 TAKEAWAYS: Multiple meanings are associated with the term “RedOx potential,” and this can lead
to confusion. The terms “reduction” and “oxidation” do not necessarily imply the absence or presence,
respectively, of oxygen. Metals exist in their metallic state under sufficiently reducing conditions and
form chemical compounds under sufficiently oxidizing conditions. Oxidizing conditions can stem from
the presence of oxidizing halide compounds such as Cl2(g) and F2(g), and from impurities such as oxygen
and moisture. A metal in its metallic state has a valency of zero. A metal in a salt compound with halides
is a cation and has a positive valency.

 2.2. Thermodynamic Stability: Ellingham Diagrams
 Molten halide salts are ionic liquids with a net neutral electrical charge, meaning there is a charge
balance between cations and anions with respect to the molar stoichiometries of the ions involved. The
halides are the anions, either chlorine (Cl-) or fluorine (F-), which have only a single anionic valence state
of negative one. The metal cations are those selected for the salt system. Examples include lithium (Li+),
beryllium (Be2+), and uranium (U3+ or U4+), which may have a single valence state or multiple valence
states. For example, consider LiCl, LiF, BeCl2, BeF2, UCl3, UCl4, UF3, and UF4, where lithium and
beryllium have single cationic valence states of positive one, while uranium has more than one possible
cationic valence state. Molten salts are characterized by their short-range atomic-scale structural order, in
contrast to the long-range structural order of crystalline solids. The ions are free to move through the bulk
molten salt by the various mass transport processes of diffusion (driven by chemical gradients),
convection (driven by density gradients), mechanical mixing (driven by force gradients), and migration
(driven by electric field gradients).
 Consider the LiCl-KCl eutectic salt system. When molten, the individual cations and anions are free
to move about provided that the charge neutrality of the salt is maintained. The association of cations and
anions is fluid, and no strong chemical bonding occurs. This visualization of molten salt is convenient,
but not necessarily accurate. As the chemical complexity of the salt increases by the addition of more
constituents, the formation of complex anionic and cationic molecular species is possible. However, these
ionic molecules are difficult to identify and characterize. The analyses presented here ignore this reality
and view molten salts as simple mixtures of elemental anions and cations.

weakest. For example, LiCl is much more stable than UCl3. Reaction of lithium metal with UCl3 results in

 2.3. Thermodynamic Chemical Potential
 In principle, the chemical potential of a species is a measure of its “influence” in a chemical system.
Intuitively, the chemical potential of a species would appear to be related to its concentration, which it is.
However, the phase of the material (solid, liquid, gas) also influences a species’ chemical potential. For
example, two different phases (e.g., solid-solid, solid-liquid, liquid-gas) in equilibrium with each other
can contain the same species at the same chemical potential in each phase, but at two different
concentrations.

 Similar concepts apply in the condensed phase, where there is no use for fugacity, but the concept of
activity remains applicable. Fugacity is to a gas phase what activity is to a condensed phase. In principle,

substance. The activity is a unitless relative concentration because it includes the standard state in the
denominator. This is different from fugacity, as fugacity is not a relative concentration, and it retains the
units of pressure. The chemical potential of a real condensed species is shown in Equation (15). Here, 𝑎𝑎𝑖𝑖0

 2.4. Thermodynamic Stability: Phase Stability Diagrams
 The equilibrium phase stability diagram for the uranium, chlorine, fluorine system at 500°C is shown
in Figure 9. This diagram is based on the selection of three elements (U, Cl, and F) for illustration, and
the resultant stable compounds (U, UCl3, UCl4, UF3, UF4, UF6, and UClF3). The partial pressure of Cl2(g)
was selected to represent the Cl chemical potential along the ordinate, and the partial pressure of F2(g)
was selected to represent the F chemical potential along the abscissa. The Cl and F chemical potentials
increase as the partial pressures of Cl2(g) and F2(g) increase, respectively. On both axes, the pressure
scales were limited to 1 bar and made sufficiently low to encompass a portion of the uranium metal
stability domain.
 Uranium metal is stable only at very low Cl and F chemical potentials. As the Cl chemical potential is
increased, the phase boundaries between U and UCl3, and UCl3 and UCl4 are encountered. Likewise, as
the F chemical potential is increased the phase boundaries between U and UF3, UF3 and UF4, and UF4 and
UF6 are encountered. The stability domain for UClF3 requires both Cl and F chemical potentials to exist.
However, Figure 9 gives no information about the compositions of the equilibrium gas phase above the
condensed stability domains. Such information can be added by overlaying the partial pressure isobars for
select gas species, or the isobars for total system pressure [18].
 It can be concluded from Figure 9 that, with respect to uranium, fluorine is much more reactive than
chlorine as indicated by the positions of the phase boundaries along the axes. Fluorine begins to react with
uranium at much lower partial pressures than chlorine, which gives some insight into why fluoride and
chloride salts behave differently regarding corrosion.
 Consider the phase boundary between U and UCl3, which occurs at approximately -31.5 on the
ordinate. At this boundary the partial pressure of Cl2(g) is 3.2 x 10-32 bar, which is equivalent to a
concentration of 3.2 x 10-26 parts per million (ppm) (by volume in the gas phase) if the total system
pressure is 1 bar. Similarly, the phase boundary between U and UF3 occurs at a F2(g) concentration of 1 x
10-54 ppm (by volume in the gas phase). These concentrations are far below the detection limits of
chemical analytical instrumentation. Nevertheless, small thermodynamic values such as these are both
real and useful.

 In the case of an MSR operating with a fuel salt, the effect of fission processes is that the composition
of the fuel salt is always changing. The composition never reaches equilibrium or steady state during
operation. To complicate matters even more, the fuel salt is in contact with the construction materials
used to contain the salt in the reactor system such as tanks, piping, pumps, heat exchangers, and the
reactor core. In a broader sense, the materials of construction currently favored are primarily metal alloys
and graphite due to their technological readiness level. The favored metal alloys for MSR applications are
compositions of ferro-alloys and nickel-alloys based largely on transition metals Fe, Ni, Cr, Mo, Mn, and
Co. Chemical interactions between a fuel salt and the construction materials are inevitable.

 2.6. Dynamic Behaviors of Select Molten Salt Systems
 Salt composition can change dynamically as a function of changes to the chemical potentials of the
system resulting from internal processes and external influences. To illustrate the effects of compositional
changes on salt chemistries, several examples are given in the following sections, and more in Appendix
A. Each example begins with a defined chemical system, to which one or more components are added.
These examples are meant only to illustrate different behaviors under different scenarios. These examples
are greatly simplified and have no basis in actual performance.
Four examples follow in the body of the text. These include:
• Dynamic Fission of U to Ba and Mo: at the expense of U, Ba and Mo are added to a system
 containing NaCl and UCl3 to show the effect on the chlorine chemical potential.
• Effects of Oxygen and Moisture: Phase stability diagrams are shown for the U, Cl, O and U, F, O
 systems.
• Chloride Salt Exposed to Oxygen and Moisture: O2(g) and H2O(g) are added to a system containing
 LiCl, KCl, and UCl3 to show the effects on the chlorine and oxygen chemical potentials.
• Fluoride Salt Exposed to Oxygen and Moisture: O2(g) and H2O(g) are added to a system containing
 LiF, BeF2, ZrF4, and UF4 to show the effects on the fluorine and oxygen chemical potentials.
The following examples are presented in Appendix A. Some of these examples may be conceptually
simpler than those presented in the body of the text.
• Dynamic Behavior of the Cl, Cr, Fe, Ni System: Cl2(g) is added to a system of Cr, Fe, and Ni to show
 the sequential formations of CrCl2, FeCl2, and NiCl2.
• Dynamic Behavior of the F, Cr, Fe, Ni System: F2(g) is added to a system of Cr, Fe, and Ni to show
 the sequential formations of CrF2, FeF2, and NiF2.
• Dynamic Behavior of the Cl, U System: Cl2(g) is added to a system containing U to show the
 sequential formations of UCl3 and UCl4.
• Dynamic Behavior of the Cl, Na, U System: Cl2(g) is added to a system containing Na and U to show
 the sequential formations of NaCl, UCl3, and UCl4.
• Dynamic Behavior of the F, U System: F2(g) is added to a system containing U to show the sequential
 formations of UF3 and UF4.
• Dynamic Behavior of the F, Li, Be, U, Zr System (Version 1): F2(g) is added to a system containing
 Li, Be, U, and Zr to show the sequential formations of LiF, BeF2, UF3, ZrF4, and UF4.

 LiF, BeF2, UF4, and ZrF4 to show the sequential reductions of UF4 to UF3, and ZrF4 to BeF2. In these

 The equilibrium phase stability diagram for the uranium, chlorine, and oxygen system at 500°C is

 Figure 17 and Figure 18 show that the formation of the oxychloride, UOCl2, requires both O and Cl
chemical potentials, and the formation of the oxyfluoride, UO2F2, requires both O and F chemical

 The ingress of oxygen and/or moisture into a molten salt introduces an O chemical potential that then
competes with either the Cl chemical potential (in a chloride salt) or the F chemical potential (in a
fluoride salt). Generally, introduction of oxygen will form oxides, oxychlorides, or oxyfluorides in the
salt; and introduction of moisture will form hydroxides in the salt or be liberated as H2(g), HCl(g), or
HF(g).
 TAKEAWAYS: The exposure of a molten salt to oxygen and moisture can alter the chemical
composition of the salt. In a chloride salt, the oxygen can alter the chlorine chemical potential and allow
oxychloride species to form. In a fluoride salt, the oxygen can alter the fluorine chemical potential and
allow oxyfluorides to form. Once contaminated, recovery of the salt chemistry may be challenging
because simple heating under a vacuum may be insufficient to expel the impurities. It may be necessary to
provide chlorine or fluorine chemical potentials to purify the salts.

 2.8. Thermodynamic Stability: Lewis Acid Analogy
 In the Arrhenius theory of acids and bases, an acid increases the hydrogen cation concentration in an
aqueous solution, and a base increases the hydroxide anion concentration in an aqueous solution. This
theory is intuitive with strong acids such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) and strong bases such as sodium
hydroxide (NaOH) that dissociate to produce those ions.
 In the Brønsted-Lowry theory of acids and bases, an acid is a proton donor, and a base is a proton
acceptor. In this context, a “proton” is a hydrogen cation. The Brønsted-Lowry theory is consistent with
the Arrhenius theory and expands the concept of acids and bases to nonaqueous systems.
 In the Lewis theory of acids and bases, an acid is an electron pair acceptor, and a base is an electron
pair donor. The Lewis theory is consistent with the Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry theories and expands
the concept of acids and bases to systems not involving hydrogen. It is the Lewis theory that can be
applied to the interpretation of halide molten salt systems. The term “electron pair” is often used to
describe the Lewis theory, but when applied to molten salts, single or multiple electrons can be involved.

 Bulk charge neutrality is a constraint common to every halide molten salt system. There can be no
bulk net charge within a molten salt system. The bulk net valence charge of the anions must balance
against the bulk net valence charge of the cations. The term “free halide anions” refers not to an excess of
anions leading to a net negative charge within the bulk molten salt, but instead to halide anions that are
less constrained by association to cations and are thus freer to migrate through the molten salt. By such
mechanisms, acidity results in structural effects within the molten salt that can affect physical properties
such as density and viscosity.
 TAKEAWAYS: The Lewis acid analogy considers the state of the halide anions in the salt. The
interactions of the halide anions with the metal cations influences not only the salt chemistry, but also
some of the intrinsic physical properties of the bulk salt. Salt chemistry is more complex than a simple
mixture of individual metal cations and individual halide anions that are loosely associated by the need
for charge neutrality in the bulk salt. Complex ions comprised of groupings of metals and halides and
oxides are present, and in many cases, accurate thermodynamic values are not currently known.

 2.9. Limitations of Equilibrium Thermodynamic Calculations
 The examples presented in this section were meant to illustrate various chemical behaviors
encountered in molten halide salt systems. All calculations were based on equilibrium thermodynamic
data with many simplifying assumptions. The examples were not intended to be rigorously illustrative of
real chemical systems.
 One description of chemical equilibrium is a system in which there are no longer any driving forces
for bulk change. A system that is in a transient state is not at equilibrium, so it will not have compositions
described by equilibrium calculations. Rate of change toward equilibrium is a kinetic consideration and
can be fast or slow, and it can be so slow that for all practical purposes the system is not expected to reach
equilibrium. However, lack of change does not mean there are no longer any driving forces for change,
and lack of change does not mean the system is at equilibrium. A system could thus appear to have a
constant set of concentrations that do not match equilibrium concentrations.
 One description of steady state is a dynamic system in which there are no longer any spatial bulk
changes. As any location in the system accommodates constant mass and energy flow, the conditions at
that location are stable with respect to parameters such as temperature, pressure, and composition.
Therefore, a system at chemical equilibrium is also at steady state, but a system at steady state is not
necessarily at chemical equilibrium. For example, a system defined as fluid being mixed within a tank can
be at steady state, but it is not a system at chemical equilibrium. At the very least, the act of mixing is
imparting the fluid with heat, which dissipates into the surroundings through the walls of the tank.
Likewise, an MSR operating at steady power may be considered to be at steady state (at least in the short
term), but the system is far from equilibrium.
 Mathematically describing equilibrium thermodynamics relies on the establishment of an internally
consistent set of standard states for all species involved, and then accounting for the energetic potential
differences as the system is shifted away from the standard state conditions of those species. Temperature
is not part of the definition of the standard state (that is, for any given temperature, the standard state of a
substance will have a specific energy associated with it at that temperature). The standard state of a
substance is often defined as the pure substance at 1 bar pressure and in its natural phase (e.g., solid,
liquid, gas), and the states of the species and their potential differences from their standard states are
calculated at the temperature of interest. All chemical thermodynamics serve as a means of describing
and predicting the behaviors of real chemical systems to varying degrees. Calculations based on
equilibrium thermodynamic data are often highly idealized and are, in some cases, good approximations
of real systems, and in some cases not.

 39

 Real MSR salt systems have chemistries that are difficult to characterize because they (1) are in
contact with and may react with materials of construction such as metal alloys, ceramics, and graphite, (2)
have headspaces that may contain reactive gases such as air and moisture, and (3) may be under the
constant influence of fission and decay processes (particularly in the case of fuel salts). Furthermore,
study of thermodynamic properties of ionic species in molten salts does not have complete quantitative
consensus, both regarding theoretical interpretations and experimental methods of measurement.
 There can be uncertainty about the accuracy of the thermodynamic data. The published
thermodynamic data are derived from several sources, including data derived from direct experimental
measurements, data extrapolated beyond experimental measurements, and data estimated by empirical
and fundamental methods. The level of confidence in the accuracy of thermodynamic data is based on
expert technical assessment of the source of that data, and the estimated uncertainty values may vary
between individual assessments.
 The chemical behaviors of real systems are complicated by many phenomena, which include
thermodynamics, kinetics, passivation, diffusion, thermal gradients, pressure gradients, corrosion
mechanisms, cavitation, materials strain, radiation effects, and so on. Thus, performance predictions need
to consider more than solely equilibrium thermodynamic considerations, though equilibrium
thermodynamic considerations are an important factor in performance predictions.
 TAKEAWAYS: Predictive modeling of molten salt systems draws upon a wide range of scientific
and engineering experience from disciplines such as chemistry, thermodynamics, kinetics, fluid
mechanics, heat transfer, and neutronics. As thorough as the modeling effort may be, real molten salt
systems can behave in ways that are extremely complex and unique to a particular application. This
attribute of real molten salt systems can lead to a misalignment between predicted behaviors and observed
behaviors. Predictive modeling provides powerful tools that support the design and operation efforts, but
may not be a replacement for process demonstrations.

 4. OPERATIONAL EXPERIENCE ON SALT CHEMISTRY
 MONITORING IN THE MSRE
 The MSRE was an 8-MWt reactor with fluoride salt-based fuel, which operated at a temperature of
around 650°C. Oxidation potential of the fuel salt during operation was an important practical
consideration. The fuel composition was LiF-BeF2-ZrF4-UF4. The ZrF4 was added as an oxygen getter to
avoid the inadvertent precipitation of UO2. If excessive oxygen impurity were introduced to the fuel salt
system, it would preferably react with ZrF4 and form ZrO2, which has a more negative formation energy
and a lower solubility in the salt (600 ppm for ZrO2 versus 1000 ppm for UO2). An inert helium cover gas
with oxygen and moisture concentrations of less than 10 ppm was used to control the oxide formation by
maintaining a clean atmosphere above the salt. The UF4 was selected because of its relatively higher
chemical stability compared with UF3. Pure UF3 was found to disproportionate at temperatures higher
than 1000°C, via reaction 4UF3 = 3UF4 + U. UF3 was found to be unstable at lower temperatures in
molten fluoride salt mixtures [20]. Excess amounts of UF4 can avoid the formation of U metal, which
may alloy with the structural materials and should be avoided.
 During fission of UF4 fuel in the MSRE, the average total valence was observed to be slightly lower
than four [39], which slowly made the fuel salt more and more oxidizing and accelerated structural
material corrosion in the reactor vessel and other components that came into contact with the fuel salt in
the reactor. The fuel salt must be controlled within an acceptable salt chemistry so that the salt does not
become corrosive to the structural materials and other components in the reactor. When the UF4/UF3 ratio
was maintained at a range of 100 to 200, the corrosion of the materials was tolerable [40, 41]. Too low
UF4 concentration would result in the formation of metallic uranium, and too high UF4 concentration
would lead to a highly corrosive salt, both of which should be avoided.
 In the MSRE, to maintain the salt in a non-corrosive state, when the RedOx potential was higher, as
indicated by the UF4/UF3 ratio, beryllium metal (encased in a perforated capsule of nickel) was
introduced through the salt sampling system and suspended in the flowing salt stream in the fuel salt
pump bowl. The reaction with UF4 by Be + 2UF4 = BeF2 + 2UF3 at a rate of about 600 g of UF3 was
produced during an 8-hour treatment. This may produce some local over-reduction of UF4 in the Be-
treated salt, but equilibrium was achieved with the excess unreacted salt in the pump bowl and reactor
circuit [20] .
 To ensure the salt chemistry stayed within the range of acceptable compositions and impurity levels
during the operation of the MSRE, salt samples were periodically removed and analyzed for major
constituents, corrosion products, and oxide contamination. The isotopic composition of the salt samples
was characterized by the spectroscopy technique. The salt sampling and fuel addition were carried out
through the pump bowl. Helium flowed through the gas space in the bowl to sweep xenon and krypton to
the off-gas disposal system. Figure 36 shows the photograph of a fuel salt pump bowl and flow patterns in
a salt pump in MSRE. Figure 37 shows the process for salt sampling transfer and salt addition.

 5. ASSESSMENT OF ELECTROCHEMICAL METHODS FOR
 MOLTEN SALTS
 Electrochemical methods are techniques that can be used for analyzing the species in the molten salts
of interest by measuring the potentials and currents under controlled conditions in an electrochemical cell.
In some cases, they can be used to assess the composition or the RedOx potential of the molten salt. Two
typical categories of electrochemical methods are potentiometry and voltammetry techniques. The
potentiometry is based on the measurement of the open circuit potential (OCP) between a sensor electrode
(SE) and a reference electrode (RE). The SE is also called an indicator electrode in different contexts. For
accurate RedOx potential measurements using potentiometry, the potential of the SE is ideally a function
of RedOx potential at a specific moment (determined by salt chemistry) and the RE is independent of the
composition changes during the OCP measurement. In the voltammetry technique, typically the current is
measured when a voltage is applied to the working electrode (WE) of the electrochemical cell. There are
different voltammetry techniques depending on how the voltage is controlled during measurements: linear
sweep voltammetry (LSV), cyclic voltammetry (CV), square wave voltammetry (SWV), and pulse
voltammetry (PV), such as normal pulse voltammetry (NPV) and differential normal pulse voltammetry
(DNPV). In voltammetry, there are typically three multiple electrodes: working electrode (WE), RE, and
counter electrode (CE). Electrochemical methods have been widely used for understanding the
thermodynamics and kinetics of molten salts, as well as the chemistry and RedOx potentials of a molten
salt system. For MSRs, salt chemistry and RedOx potentials are time- and fission-dependent. In this
section, we will focus mainly on the overview of salt chemistry monitoring by typical electrochemical
methods for fluoride and chloride classes of molten salt systems with MSR-relevant chemical
compositions.

 5.1. RedOx Potential of a Salt System
 The chemical behaviors of a molten salt system are determined by the chemical potentials of its
constituents. In practice, a molten salt system does not exist in isolation but is always in contact with
solids and gases. Solids include construction materials, solids deposited from the salt onto construction
materials, and solids entrained in the molten salt. Gases include vapors formed in headspaces, fission
product gases, circulation pumps, and sparging operations.
 A standalone expression such as “RedOx potential of the salt” means nothing by itself because it is
not a definitive, universally accepted expression. To know what is meant, it is necessary to provide a
precise definition of the expression. Generally, a RedOx potential is only well-defined for a single species
rather than for a mixture of species in molten salt electrochemistry. On the other hand, chemical attributes
of a salt mixture are explained thermodynamically by the concepts of chemical speciation and chemical
potentials.
 Sensors of all sorts, when they work, can only be correlated to the chemical property they are
designed to sense. There are no techniques for the direct measurements of chlorine chemical potential,
fluorine chemical potential, U3+/U4+ ratio, moisture concentration, or corrosion products. The best that can
be attained is an electrochemical sensor that responds to these attributes in a consistent and reproducible
way, and which may have theoretical relations that can be used for understanding. In some cases, the
response of the sensor can be explained and interpreted by thermodynamic expressions, and in some cases
the response can only be interpreted empirically. Empirical interpretations are still useful, provided that
their limitations and scope of applicability are understood.
 TAKEAWAYS: Typically, the term “RedOx potential” is used to describe the chemical condition,
including corrosivity, of the molten salt. However, when the term “RedOx potential” is used quantitively
it must be clearly defined because there is no standardized meaning.

 52

 5.2. Reference Electrodes for Molten Salts
 Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two locations. Furthermore, the absolute
electrical potential of either location has no meaning, and it is only the difference that can be measured. A
RE acts as a sufficiently stable electrical potential from which the potential difference to other electrodes
can be measured, resulting in the measurement of a voltage. An analogy is the measurement of elevation.
When flying an airplane, it is useful to know the elevation above sea level (which is related to air density)
and the elevation above the ground (which is related to avoiding obstacles). In this example, the sea level
is one useful datum, and the ground level is another useful datum. During electrochemical measurements,
it is useful to know the potential differences between the RE and other electrodes in the system. In this
case, the RE is the datum. By means of convention, the absolute elevation of sea level can be set to zero,
or the absolute elevation of the ground can be set to zero. In a similar fashion the absolute potential of
some REs can be set to zero (e.g., the standard hydrogen electrode for aqueous electrochemistry).
 An RE is a critical component in potentiometry and voltammetry techniques for molten salt systems.
Ideally, the potential of an RE is independent of the bulk salt chemistry. There are typically two types of
REs for electrochemical measurements: a thermodynamic RE (in which the half-cell reaction is well
characterized) and a quasi-reference electrode (QRE) (in which the half-cell reaction is not kept at a
controlled chemical potential, or not well-defined).
 TAKEAWAYS: A reference electrode serves as a datum from which to measure the
potentials of other electrodes. The reference electrode and the other electrodes must share a
common pool of molten salt.

5.2.1. Thermodynamic Reference Electrodes
 The potential of the thermodynamic RE is determined by a well-defined chemical RedOx couple, like
NiF2/Ni for fluoride salt and AgCl/Ag for chloride salt. Take the NiF2/Ni RE for a FLiNaK fluoride salt
system as an example. A small amount of NiF2 was mixed with FLiNaK salt and housed in a container of
boron nitride (BN) or graphite. This housing was put in the FLiNaK salt to monitor the chemistry of the
FLiNaK. The container for the NiF2/Ni RE functions as the ionic conductor between the RE salt and
FLiNaK salt. The composition of the salt inside the RE container is not affected by the composition of the
salt outside the RE container. Two basic requirements for an RE are needed. One is the chemical
compatibility of the container material with fluoride salt which is important for long-term operations. The
other is to provide the ionic conductivity between the salt inside and outside the RE container. The RE
container materials need to provide a mechanism for ionic conductivity either by providing a small
amount of porosity that can be filled with salt, or by themselves being ionically conductive. Materials like
mullite and yttria stabilized zirconia that are used for chloride salt systems (discussed later) are ionically
conductive. Another example of an ionically conductive material is single crystal LaF3 which was used
for fluoride salts [45].
Due to the requirement for sufficient chemical compatibility with molten fluoride salt systems, which
exhibit strong fluxing capability and are corrosive for most ceramic materials like oxides, there are
limited options in the selection of RE container materials. Graphite and BN were typically used as
container materials for fluoride salt systems. Graphite with engineered porosities were used. Graphite is
chemically compatible with fluoride salt and is expected to last a long time. BN was also used as a
container material. Some researchers reported that BN cannot last long in high-temperature fluoride salt
systems. Figure 42 shows a NiF2/Ni RE used in FLiNaK salt systems. Figure 43 shows an RE that used
BN-coated graphite as container. Figure 44 shows a NiF2/Ni that uses single crystal LaF3 as container
material. Figure 45 shows a schematic of a NiF2/Ni RE that uses BN as container material

 To achieve reliable and consistent performance, a RE needs to be compatible with the salt system, the
electrochemical reaction in the RE should be reversible, and the RE should have adequate mechanical
strength, particularly for the extreme environment expected in the engineering applications of MSRs. For
example, the RE must be able to survive thermal shock due to the sudden temperature change induced
from frequent removal and insertion of the RE in the salt, high velocity salt flow, and corrosion.
 Based on the experiences of researchers, for REs made with BN as reference salt containers, the
working temperatures are in the range of 500 to 600°C. It may take 24 to 48 hours to stabilize and reach
an equilibrium with the molten salt for testing, and measurement variation in the range of ±6 mV over 12
days in FLiBe salt was observed.
 For the REs made with graphite as reference salt containers, the working temperature range reported
was 650 to 850°C, and it took about 3 hours to equilibrate with the salt for testing. Stable measurements
were reported for 2 days.
 For the LaF3 single crystal as an ionically conductive membrane, the working temperature range was
500 to 600°C, and it took only 5 minutes to equilibrate with the molten salt for testing. No measurements
more than 2 days were reported to assess the long-term performance of the LaF3 membrane in molten
salts.

reference salt. If the potential difference between the two REs was close to zero, it suggested that the two
REs had reached an equilibrium or quasi-equilibrium with the molten salts. Some tests were also
performed to evaluate if the REs responded to changes of the analytes in the salt, as would be expected

5.2.2. Quasi-Reference Electrodes
 In contrast to thermodynamic REs, quasi-reference electrodes (QREs) have potentials that are
determined by salt chemistry in contact with the QREs. Typical metallic QREs include Pt, Ir, W, and Ta.
Glassy carbon is also used as a QRE. These QREs were typically used in voltametric techniques. One
basic requirement for QREs is that they are compatible with molten salts. In contrast to thermodynamic
REs with well-defined chemical RedOx coupling in the RE container, the potential of the QRE is
determined by the salt chemistry (with potentially different QRE electrode chemical RedOx reactions in
different salts) and thus is dependent on the composition of the salt melt that is in contact with the
electrode. Despite the drawbacks of QREs, because of their simplicity and easy availability, QREs are
widely used for voltammetry experiments in molten salts, including chlorides and fluorides [57, 58, 59,
60, 61, 62, 63, 64]. Figure 51 shows a photograph of an electrode assembly, and Figure 52 shows two
example experimental setups for voltammetry using QREs.

 TAKEAWAYS: A quasi-reference electrode is a reference electrode that does not have a well-defined
half-cell reaction. A quasi-reference electrode can be susceptible to measurement drift because the surface
electroactive properties of the quasi-reference electrode are influenced by changes in the chemistry of the
bulk salt. Nevertheless, quasi-reference electrodes can be robust and serve useful purposes.

5.2.3. Dynamic Reference Electrode
 Dynamic reference electrode (DRE) is a type of RE with the following features: (1) a thin layer of
element M (e.g., Be in FLiBe salt) is cathodically deposited on an electrode (e.g., W, Mo, Ir, Pt), and (2)
the deposited metal M (e.g., Be in FLiBe again) can be considered for a short time (on the order of
seconds) to be the thermodynamic RE, with the potential determined by the RedOx potential of M/Mz+
(e.g., Be/Be2+ in FLiBe salt). Figure 53 shows a cell for RedOx potential measurement using a DRE. The
advantage of the DRE is that it does not have the junction potential of REs that use containers because the
deposited metal is in direct contact with the molten salt. The disadvantage is that the deposited metal is
usually a reactive metal that can be redissolved into the salt, meaning it functions as an RE for only a
short time. For example, the DRE in Figure 54 is effective for the duration of the voltage plateau (e.g.,
~60 seconds in Figure 54b and ~10 seconds in Figure 54d) in measuring the OCP. Note that a voltage
plateau is needed for the DRE to measure the OCP in molten salt systems.
 Typically, DREs are used as potentiometry measurements, such as RedOx potential measurements.

RedOx couple and with varied deposition parameters during the deposition stage. The voltage plateaus in
the plots were dependent on multiple factors such as deposition current, deposition time, the metal
deposition layer, and impurities.

 5.3. Potentiometry Techniques for Molten Salts
 In the MSRE, the uranium constituent in the fuel salt was mainly UF4 with a small amount of UF3.

significantly corrosive to structural materials. The U(IV)/U(III) ratio was indicative of the RedOx

therefore essential to a fundamental understanding of fuel chemistry. The sensor design is shown in

 Potentiometric determination of this ratio requires the value of the standard electrode potential of the
U(IV)/U(III) ratio relative to the standard electrode potential of a reference couple. The standard potential
of the U(IV)/U(III) ratio was calculated with respect to a hypothetical unit mole fraction Ni/Ni(II)

immersed in the solution. Measured cell potentials were corrected, using the Nernst Equation, to values
versus a unit mole fraction Ni/Ni(II) electrode (a hypothetical Ni electrode in 100% NiF2 salt). The
corrected potentials were plotted versus a log [U(IV)/U(III)] ratio. The slope of a line through these points
agreed well with the theoretical slope predicted by the Nernst Equation. The standard potential of the
U(IV)/U(III) ratio, corresponding to a U(IV)/U(III) ratio of 1, was found to be 1.48 ± 0.01 V at 500°C.

 5.4. Some Practical Considerations of Potentiometry for MSR
 Applications
 In potentiometry, only two electrodes are involved and there is no current passing through the
electrodes. There are several ways to measure the OCP. The two-electrode configuration can be used, and
many instruments with a high internal impedance (e.g., potentiostats or some advanced digital voltmeters)
can be used to measure and record the OCP data. Sometimes the circuit resistance at high temperature is
quite high for REs using ceramic containers that serve as ionic conductive membranes. If the internal
impedance of the instrument for OCP measurement is not high enough, some measurement errors may be
introduced [70]. In most cases for low impedance RE containers, the common commercially available
digital multimeters meet the requirements for OCP measurements.
 The OCP is basically the potential difference between the RE and indicator electrode. Both potentials
need to be stable during the measurement. In a simplistic interpretation, the potential of the RE is
assumed constant. In actuality, particularly for long-term measurement, some potential drifting of the RE
may occur, and this will result in measurement errors. Therefore, a periodic calibration of the
potentiometric measurements is recommended.
 The usefulness of DREs is application specific. Not every molten salt system is suitable for DRE
measurements. For example, if the salt contains UF4 and UF3, as is the case in MSR operation, the
deposition layer can react with the salt and cause the reduction of U4+ to U3+, which may be undesired.
The DRE may be suitable for quick RedOx potential measurements for the FLiBe coolant salt used in
FHRs.
 There is no standard or universally accepted RE for molten salts. The selection of RE is mainly at the
discretion of the researcher and based on their specific application. Typically, the NiF2/Ni RE in different
container materials such as graphite and boron nitride have been used for potentiometry measurements in
fluoride salt systems. The AgCl/Ag RE and the recently reported NiCl2/Ni RE in different container
materials are used for chloride salt systems. In addition, REs like the Gd/saturated GdCl3 RE were also
used for some specific potentiometry measurements and applications. This suggests that some other
similar metal/saturated-metal-chloride REs may be developed for some similar applications, but their
performance needs to be evaluated and demonstrated before being considered for MSR applications.
Because the potential of the QREs is not fixed and is dependent on the salt chemistry, the QREs usually
are not good RE candidates for potentiometry measurements, in either fluoride or chloride salt systems,
particularly if quantitative results are desired. The lack of a standard practice makes it challenging to
compare the RedOx potential measurement results of different researchers, even under similar salt
compositions and/or test conditions.
 TAKEAWAYS: Potentiometry methods are very sensitive to the surface conditions of the electrodes
involved. Measurements with voltmeters with high internal impedance are preferred as not to perturb the
potential being measured. Certain configurations are susceptible to drift over time, introducing errors into
the measurements.

 5.5. Voltammetry Techniques for Molten Salts
 Voltammetry techniques are widely used for electrochemistry studies of molten salt systems. Many
developments have been reported in the molten salt electrochemistry field [71, 72, 73, 74, 75, 76]. For
example, the pyroprocessing of spent nuclear oxides fuels involves the electrochemical reduction of
uranium oxide to uranium metal in a LiCl-Li2O salt and the electrorefining of the uranium in LiCl-KCl-
UCl3 salt [77, 78, 79, 80]. Voltammetry is also extensively used in understanding and determining the
basic thermodynamic and kinetic properties of salt constituents in a bulk molten salt electrolyte.
 In an experimental setup for voltammetry measurements, usually a three-electrode configuration is
used (WE, RE, and CE) in conjunction with a potentiostat to (1) control the voltage between the WE and
RE and record the current between the WE and CE, or (2) control the current between the WE and CE
and record the voltage of the WE versus the RE. In some advanced potentiostats, the voltage between the
CE and RE is also measured and recorded, though the potential at the CE (versus RE) is typically not
explicitly of interest. In most cases, researchers are interested in the electrochemical processes occurring
at the WE. Figure 60 shows a schematic three-electrode configuration for molten salts (the computer for
controlling the potentiostat was not shown). All three electrodes are connected to the potentiostat. Most
potentiostats have two sense leads which are used to measure or control the potentials between the WE
and RE. The surface area for the CE is usually higher than that of the WE, so that the performance of the
CE does not in any way interfere with the electrochemical measurement. In most advanced potentiostats,
many electrochemical measurement techniques are available in the instrument’s software.
 There are different voltammetry techniques depending on how the voltage or current is controlled.
Voltage and current cannot be controlled simultaneously during measurement sequences. Either voltage is
controlled and amperage is the responding signal, or current is controlled and voltage is the responding
signal. The relationship between the voltage and current, as well as the time, are recorded during the
voltammetry measurement sequence. For monitoring the salt chemistry for MSRs, typical voltammetry
measurements include LSV, CV, SWV, and PV, as detailed below, and involve controlling the voltage
and measuring the current.

5.5.1. Linear Sweep Voltammetry
 Linear Sweep Voltammetry (LSV) is a basic voltammetry technique. In LSV, the WE potential versus
RE is swept linearly from a starting value to a final value, and the current is measured as a function of
time during this sweep. The rate of sweeping, or the slope of the potential vs. time, is also called the scan
rate. The results that are typically plotted are the relationships of the current versus the voltage, which are
called voltammograms. During the WE potential sweep process, some species in the molten salt may
undergo oxidation or reduction processes depending on their reduction potentials and chemical activities
in the salts. The currents associated with these oxidation and reduction processes are accurately measured
by the potentiostat. The current is a type of rate (a rate of electrical charge transfer associated with
chemical reaction) and the current is thus a representation of the process kinetics when oxidation or
reduction are present during the sweep. In many cases, the process may have mass transfer limited
kinetics for the chemical reaction, in which case the current represents a rate that is partially or fully
representative of mass transfer kinetics.
 The MSRE used the LSV technique for RedOx potential measurements of its fuel salts. In the MSRE,
an apparatus was designed to permit the LSV measurements in a sample of fuel salt [66]. The principle of
the LSV for determining the RedOx potential, as schematically shown in Figure 61 [44], is 𝐸𝐸𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒 = 𝐸𝐸1/2 +

the value of 𝐸𝐸𝑒𝑒𝑒𝑒 is determined by the U(IV)/U(III) ratio. The polarographic half-wave potential, E1/2, is
approximately equal to standard electrode potential Eo, which is the potential when the U(IV)/U(III) ratio

5.5.2. Cyclic Voltammetry
 Cyclic voltammetry (CV) is a widely used technique for measuring the thermodynamic properties of
molten salts. During a CV measurement, the WE potential relative to the RE is scanned at a specific rate
in terms of millivolts per second (mV s-1) in a positive or negative direction. But unlike in linear sweep
voltammetry (where the experiment stops after the final WE potential is reached), during a CV the WE
potential is scanned in the reverse direction to return to the initial potential. The two half-cycles create a
complete cycle that can be repeated multiple times. From these measurements, the current at the WE is
monitored and plotted versus the applied voltage (that is, the WE potential), and the plot is referred to as a
cyclic voltammogram. A cyclic voltammogram, also known as a CV curve, can provide information
about RedOx potentials and electrochemical reaction rates. For instance, if the electron transfer at the WE
surface is fast and the current is limited by the diffusion of analyte species to the electrode surface, then
the peak current will be proportional to the square root of the scan rate, as described by the Randles–

5.5.3. Square Wave Voltammetry
 In square wave voltammetry (SWV), a square wave potential pulse waveform is superimposed on a
staircase step to generate a voltametric sweep, as shown in Figure 65. The potential as the base of the
peaks increases at a rate linear with time. Typically, the sampling technique of SWV considers the current
response during the latter half of the potential pulse. The current is measured two times near the end of
the forward and reverse potentials, respectively, and the results of SWV is the relationship between the
applied WE potential and the forward and reverse current difference. With this approach, the charging
current or non-Faradaic current is minimized, and thus the SWV can provide higher sensitivity
measurements than the typical CV. It is therefore likely to be used to measure low concentrations of
constituents in molten salts, such as oxide and moisture, which should be maintained as low as achievable
in MSRs.

5.5.4. Normal Pulse Voltammetry

be used for chemical monitoring, as well as to illustrate the thermodynamic and kinetic properties of

5.5.5. Differential Pulse Voltammetry
 Differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) is a type of electrochemical method where a series of small
regular voltage pulses of the same amplitude are superimposed on the potential sweeping in the linear
sweep voltammetry or staircase voltammetry measurements. DPV differs from NPV, which features a
series of voltage pulses of increasing amplitudes and the potential returns to the initial value. Figure 73
schematically shows the potential applied to the WE during DPV. The current immediately before each
pulse is measured, which minimizes the background non-Faradic current (or charging current) and
increases the measurement sensitivity. A peak-shape current versus potential plot is expected in DPV, and
the height of the current peak is typically proportional to the concentration of the constituent in the molten
salt being tested, making DPV useful for MSR salt chemistry monitoring purposes.

 5.6. Some Practical Considerations of Voltammetry Techniques for
 MSR Applications
 The measurement electrodes should be compatible with the molten salt systems being tested for long
periods of time (such as three months or longer) and should not alloy with the deposited metals during the
voltammetry measurements.
 Electrochemical methods are typically used to study thermodynamic and kinetic properties of salt
components of low concentrations in a bulk salt system. These methods have not been shown to be
suitable for measuring high concentrations of UCl3 in fuel salts for fast reactors. For electrochemical
measurement of high concentrations, one option is that the salt can be sampled and diluted for analysis.
 The turbulent flow of salt in MSRs may introduce some measurement challenges. The sensor may
need to be placed in locations where the flow is relatively slow, or more robust electrochemical sensors
can be developed to mitigate the salt turbulence effect on the measurement and sensor lifetime. For
MSRs, some locations may need to be designed/engineered for voltammetry and potentiometry sensor
measurements.
 Although the MSRE researchers did test electrochemical sensors in a laboratory salt loop, sensors in
the MSRE were not tested in real time during reactor operation. During reactor operation, samples were
taken from the pump bowl and transferred to a location for measurements. In-line measurement locations
should be included in MSR design and engineering.
 Electrodes that perform well in one salt system may not perform as satisfactorily in other salt systems.
The performance of electrodes also depends on the selected parameters in voltammetry experiments. For
example, it was reported that Pt (generally inert and compatible with most molten fluoride and chloride
salt) was not performing well in NaCl-CaCl2-CeCl3 salt systems, as shown in Table 8 [93]. Ag quasi-
reference electrode was also found to be unreliable in MSR systems.

 5.7. Salt Purification by Electrochemical Methods
 It is possible that electrochemical methods can be used for salt purification to remove dissolved oxide
and moisture in FLiNaK, FLiBe, and NaCl-CaCl2 salts. The main purification mechanism is oxidation of
the oxide ion (O2-) under anodic polarization, which will liberate the oxygen as O2(g). The purification of
molten salt by electrochemical methods has been used for preparing salts for oxide electroreduction
studies. For example, CaCl2 is very hygroscopic and usually contains significant moisture. In CaCl2-based
molten salt electrochemistry experiments, a voltage of 2.5 to 2.7 V was used applied and successfully
removed moisture and some RedOx-active impurities [94, 95]. At this voltage level, the CaCl2 salt
remained stable at high-temperature and the moisture in the salt was removed. The removal of the
moisture can be seen from the response of current versus time while the voltage was applied. When the
current was very low or was just the background current, most of the moisture was considered removed.
Different techniques have been reported in literature for salt purification. Figure 75 shows a schematic of
molten salt purification achieved by removing moisture electrochemically with the assistance of hydrogen
gas. Figure 76 shows an electrochemical process to remove the impurity MgOH+ in chloride salt using a
Mg anode and W cathode. In this case, the Mg is oxidized to Mg2+ so the Mg2+ can react with oxide in the
salt and form MgO solids. These insoluble solids can be further removed with techniques like filtration.

 Salt purification by electrochemical methods can be used for salts like FLiBe and LiCl-KCl salts that

 6. ASSESSMENT OF ELECTROCHEMICAL METHODS FOR
 APPLICATION TO MOLTEN SALT REACTORS
 Some electrochemical technologies can be used for multiple purposes. For example, depending on the
experimental setup and design, potentiometry can be used for impurity and RedOx potential
measurements, as well as measurement of specific constituents in the salt. Voltammetry with various
potential ramping modes is also a capable analytical technique that can be used for different purposes.
Note that the general principles and theory of these electrochemical methods are well established, but for
each molten salt measurement application, the requirements, accuracy, and sensitivity may be different.
The assessment for these electrochemical methods is therefore organized with respect to specific
applications, mainly impurity monitoring, salt composition monitoring, and RedOx potential monitoring.
It should be noted that this assessment provides an overview of the state-of-technology and does not
consider regulatory aspects of the use of electrochemical monitoring technology in a license application.
 The assessment of electrochemical methods for MSRs is informed by knowledge and input from the
series of ORNL reports on the MSRE, literature review of peer-reviewed journals documenting the status
of electrochemical methods, and publicly available reports from international nuclear organizations and
national laboratories.
 Based on MSRE experiences, the RedOx potential measurement of fuel salt for the MSRE is the most
technically mature of the electrochemical applications. Section 5 detailed the status of different
electrochemical methods for applications to MSRs. In this chapter, we summarize the maturity of
techniques for MSR application purposes, including impurity and RedOx potential monitoring, and
present suggestions for further information and data needs to support eventual deployment in MSRs.

 6.1. Impurities Monitoring

6.1.1. Dissolved oxygen ion monitoring
 Currently available literature on oxide measurements by potentiometry is focused on molten salts that
contain significant amounts of oxide, such as the LiCl-Li2O salts used for the electrochemical reduction of
oxide fuels to metals. These measurement techniques are not directly relevant for MSRs applications,
which are expected to contain very small amounts of oxide impurity (ppm levels) in the fuel and coolant
salts. However, the same theory of operation is expected to work for MSR fuel and coolant salts, but more
development and demonstration work is needed.
 Square wave voltammetry (SWV) has been demonstrated to test for the presence of oxide impurities
at levels of 300 ppm or higher in literature. The authors have unpublished data showing that the SWV is
able to test for the presence of oxide anions at concentrations of 50 ppm in a LiCl-KCl salt system at
500°C. It may be possible that the SWV potentially can be used for monitoring the very low
concentration (ppm level) oxide ion impurity in fuel salts for MSRs, but more development and
demonstration is needed to assess this possibility.

 83

6.1.2. Moisture monitoring
 Moisture effects on corrosion are widely known and reported in literature. There is also research on
electrochemistry studies of molten salts containing moisture, but they are typically qualitative and not
applicable to quantitative moisture monitoring in molten salts.
 The voltammetry techniques may be able to qualitatively detect the presence of moisture and the
extent of the moisture in the salt based on the current behavior measured during voltammetry and
comparing with the same types of measurements using ultra-dry salts.
 Although moisture measurements by electrochemical methods are qualitative (because the moisture in
fuel salts for MSRs should be strictly controlled and is typically in very low concentrations), qualitive
measurements can give very useful information. If problematic levels of moisture are detected in the salt,
the moisture must be reduced and could also be indicative of unexpected material entering the reactor
system. The voltammetry technique for moisture monitoring is currently a qualitative analytical method
and is in early stages of development.

 6.2. Salt Composition Monitoring (U, Pu)
 With the operation of salt-fueled MSRs, the compositions are quite complicated, with many fission
products generated in the fuel salt over time. It is difficult to monitor all or most elements in the salt. The
elements of most interest are U, Pu, and rare earths, which are closely related to the fuel salt chemistry.
Corrosion products from reactions with structural materials can also be a good indicator of RedOx
potential or salt chemistry, so monitoring of such corrosion products can also be considered an aspect of
salt composition monitoring.

 6.3. RedOx Potential Monitoring (U4+/U3+ Ratio)
 RedOx potential in fluoride salt fuel is determined by the UF4/UF3 ratio. Though there is literature in
general on the RedOx potential control of chloride salt (with some assuming that a small amount of UCl4
is in the salt as a RedOx buffer), this has not been demonstrated in literature and there are no
demonstration experiments to show the UCl4 presence in the fuel salt for chloride-salt-fueled MSRs. To
evaluate the electrochemical methods applications to MSRs, it is assumed that a small amount of UCl4
will be maintained in the fuel salt and the UCl4/UCl3 ratio can be used as a RedOx potential indicator.
Because of the limited information on the RedOx potential control in a chloride salt reactor system, it is
possible that there are various options for controlling the RedOx chemistry of the fuel salt. For example,
salt can be maintained in a way that the UCl4 formation due to the oxidizing fission process can be
minimized or eliminated by contacting a reactive metal (or reducing agent). In this case, how the RedOx
potential is determined is unknown and the measured RedOx potential is an overall value of the mixed
potential. It has been suggested that a RedOx potential buffer similar to UCl4/UCl3 ratio could be used for
chloride MSRs, but further research is suggested to confirm the utility of that concept.

 6.4. Quality Assurance and Engineering Considerations
 Quality assurance is meant to ensure that the electrochemical measurements being made are accurate
and precise. Engineering considerations are meant to ensure that the electrochemical measurements are
robust and maintainable.

 84

6.4.1. Quality Assurance
 According to Title 10 of the Code of Federal Regulations, Appendix B to Part 50—Quality Assurance
Criteria for Nuclear Power Plants and Fuel Reprocessing Plants, quality assurance “comprises all those
planned and systematic actions necessary to provide adequate confidence that a structure, system, or
component will perform satisfactorily in service. Quality assurance includes quality control, which
comprises those quality assurance actions related to the physical characteristics of a material, structure,
component, or system which provide a means to control the quality of the material, structure, component,
or system to predetermined requirements.”
 For MSR electrochemical measurement methods, the goal of quality assurance is to evaluate and
ensure that the results generated are accurate and reliable. The results of electrochemical methods are
typically voltage (for potentiometry) or current (for voltammetry under potential control mode), which are
determined by salt chemistry and selected operating parameters. The voltage and current results of
electrochemical methods need to be converted, based on the scientific mechanism of the selected
measurement technique, to salt chemistry data such as RedOx potential, concentrations of chemical
constituents, and salt composition.
 In addition to salt chemistry, there are other multiple factors related to the electrochemical methods
that affect the measurement of salt chemistry by electrochemical methods. For example, in potentiometry,
the stability of the RE, and the surface condition of the indicator electrode are extremely important. The
drifting of the RE over long-term in-line monitoring needs to be understood and some quality assurance
approach needs to be developed to address possible RE drifting issues. Other corrosion or fission products
may be deposited on the surface of the indicator electrode, affecting potentiometric measurement. Even a
small amount of OCP variation introduced by RE drifting and surface condition changes of electrodes
could result in a high uncertainty in measurements intended to give information about salt chemistry.
 For voltammetry, the current measured is affected by the surface area of the WE, so it is necessary to
control and accurately know its surface area or relative surface area. This is challenging considering the
mixing characteristics of the fuel salt in the pumped salt flow. Accurate knowledge and characterization
of experiment parameters are needed for high confidence in electrochemical data.
 To ensure the validity of the electrochemical sensor monitoring for MSRs, the authors suggest
considering:
1. Multiple sensors—such as two potentiometry and two voltammetry sensors—can be designed and
 installed. They can be compared with each other to show whether measurement results are consistent.
 Consistent data can be expected, within acceptable uncertainties. Multiple sensor measurements also
 must be validated or calibrated using other chemical analytical techniques, like spectroscopy.
 Therefore, when one sensor is removed for maintenance or replacement, there would still be another
 working sensor to provide high-fidelity in-line measurements until the second sensor is replaced.
2. Cross calibration of sensor data. For example, the RedOx potential determined by potentiometry
 should be similar to that measured by voltammetry. If there are discrepancies greater than expected or
 desired, inspections must be conducted to ensure sensors are functioning properly.
3. Periodic calibration and validation of the sensor data by spectroscopy. Spectrophotometry can be used
 to quantify the U(IV)/U(III) ratio. ICP-MS can measure the isotopic composition of the salt samples.
 The chemical analysis results can help determine whether the results of electrochemical methods are
 accurate and exhibit acceptable uncertainty ranges.
4. Sensors should be replaced or maintained without prohibitive difficulties. During long-time operation,
 the function of the sensor may deteriorate. If it is determined that replacement or maintenance is
 needed, the replacement or maintenance should be performable sufficiently easily.

 85

 Note that these recommendations for quality assurance are based on the understanding and
experiences of the authors. Since there are no operational experiences or demonstrations of the sensors,
and the design of the MSR and associated components are complicated and may evolve with time, new
options may be made available to better ensure quality assurance.

6.4.2. Engineering Aspects
 Generally, a stable salt melt is desired for potentiometry and voltammetry sensors. In an MSR,
however, the salt melt flows at quite high speeds. This should be considered in the sensor design. For
example, the electrode should have enough stiffness so it will not bend in the flow of salt. Additionally,
the salt level of the fuel salt may change over time and will change the surface area of the WE in contact
of the fuel salt, which will affect the measurements. We make the following recommendations for
sensors’ locations:
1. In the MSRE, the pump bowl has multiple functions for adding salt and taking salt samples. Some
 ports may be designed for installation of these sensors.
2. Side-engineered port specifically designed for the sensor is shown in Figure 77. The salt flow in the
 side tube can be controlled so that it is reasonably slow and stable. This figure is only a simple
 schematic, so some variations may be designed. For example, a large portion in the side tube similar
 to the sump tank of the molten salt loop can be used for multiple sensors.
3. For MSRs that are accompanied by a chemical processing plant, the sensor can be installed in some
 salt tanks for chemical processing. The salt in the chemical processing plant is expected to be much
 more stable in terms of turbulence and flow rate. The detailed requirements of the sensors are specific
 to the chemical processing plant.

 7. CONSIDERATIONS FOR ADDITIONAL RESEARCH AND
 DEVELOPMENT ACTIVITIES
 RedOx potential monitoring and control have been demonstrated in the MSRE, which used a fluoride
fuel salt, and significant amounts of operational data and experience were gained from the experiment.
However, the same level of experience does not exist for chloride MSRs, and the available data and
methodology for RedOx potential monitoring and control is very limited. One basic background for
RedOx potential in fluoride salt is the stability issue of UF3, which means a higher UF4/UF3 ratio is
needed. UCl3 is expected to be more stable than UF3, and UCl4 is known to be very corrosive compared to
UCl3.
 The understanding of the disproportionation of UCl3, particularly in relevant reactor operating
conditions, is quite limited and the results in literature seem inconsistent. For example, in vaporization
studies of pure UCl3 salt in a temperature range of 600 to 1000°C, UCl4 was found in the vapor phase
along with UCl3. The presence of UCl4 was most likely due to the disproportionation of UCl3 via 4UCl3 =
3UCl4 + U [98]. Thermodynamic studies show that UCl3 in NaCl-UCl3 salt is susceptible to
disproportionation, and a U(IV)/U(III) ratio of 0.003 to 5% was suggested to avoid the disproportionation
of UCl3 and mitigate the material corrosion in chloride salt reactors [99]. However, in a KCl salt with
11mol% UCl3 that was molten at 800°C, no evidence of disproportionation of UCl3 was observed, and it
was believed that the disproportionation of UCl3 is not significant [100]. It is possible that the UCl3
disproportionation is temperature and concentration dependent. Moltex proposed using Zr metal in
contact with chloride salts to ensure the salt is noncorrosive to materials; the rationale for the proposal is
the high stability of UCl3 in a fuel salt [101]. Therefore, more research and development are needed to
understand the disproportionation of UCl3 in relevant reactor operating conditions. If UCl3 does undergo
disproportionation, the U(IV)/U(III) ratio may be used for monitoring the redox potential. Still, more
robust experimental data are needed to determine the range of U(IV)/U(III) both to prevent the UCl3
disproportionation and to mitigate the corrosion of materials in the reactor.
 If UCl3 is stable and the disproportionation does not occur or is negligible under the reactor operating
conditions, the salt chemistry management strategy is expected to be different. UCl4 is a strong oxidizer
and its concentration in fuel salt should be minimized to the extent practical or achievable. The RedOx
potential measured by potentiometry likely has an overall result of mixed potential. The OCP data are
difficult to quantify. A RedOx buffer is desired that is sensitive to RedOx potential changes, similar to the
U4+/U3+ ratio in fluoride salt systems. If no RedOx buffer is used, in general, when the salt chemistry is
more corrosive, the RedOx potential will be more positive. But this information is only qualitative and
cannot give the needed quantitative RedOx information on the fuel salt because the measured values are a
result of mixed potentials. That said, qualitative information is still helpful. If the measured RedOx
potential shows some changes, it means something has changed in the fuel salt, which can inform the
need for more accurate chemistry analysis.
 The REs for fluorides that typically use graphite and boron nitride containers and these materials have
not been demonstrated for long-term operation. An RE with a boron nitride container was reported to be
stable for 12 days [48]. A reliable RE for long-term operation in fluoride salts is desirable. In chloride
salts, although REs have shown good performance for extended periods of time, more work is still needed
to exhibit no drifting during long-term monitoring of more extreme MSR environments.
 In-line or in-process measurements are preferable. Voltammetry and potentiometry may offer
convenient in-line measurements (or in-process measurements). Like the RedOx potential measurements,
concentration of corrosion products and low concentration (less than 10 ppm) oxides and moisture
impurity can also be monitored using in-line electrochemical methods, and these measurements can be
designed to be operated in an automated manner. The in-line measurement can provide information that
cannot be achieved by discrete sampling methods.

 87

 The lifetime of the cables for electrochemical methods under high radiation environments near the
reactor vessel is unknown. Research and development work on the radiation effect on the cables for long-
term operation would be beneficial.
 Although this report is focused on MSR electrochemical measurement methods, it is worth noting
that other techniques may be used in combination with electrochemical methods. For example,
spectroscopy is able to give different information on the speciation of salt chemistry and there have been
reports on combining spectroscopy and electrochemistry, called spectroelectrochemistry. Because
spectroscopy requires collection of salt samples and special containers that provide transparent windows
for the light source, there are inherent challenges for industrial applications particularly for long-term in-
line monitoring purposes. There is active research on spectroelectrochemistry for molten salts and recent
journal articles have been published. Further research would help move the laboratory research to
engineering demonstration or deployment in MSRs.

 8. SUMMARY
 In the simplest real-world applications, the chemical conditions that develop within a molten salt
system can be complex. The salt system is never completely isolated. Some MSR applications subject the
salt to multiple extreme conditions that occur at the same time. These include high temperatures, contact
with materials of construction, contact with gas phases, exposure to different sources of contamination,
temperature gradients, pressure gradients, high-velocity flows, cavitation effects, entrainment of solids,
exposure to radiation, composition changes due to fission processes, and exposure to tritium even when it
is produced in a different location of the reactor.
 In salt-fueled MSRs, many fission products are dissolved in molten salts. The noble metal fission
products can accumulate in the molten salt or deposit on the materials of construction. Noble gas fission
products are recovered in the cover gas by gas sparging. The fission process is mildly oxidizing toward
the dissolved uranium (e.g., U4+) in the fuel salt, which gradually makes the fuel salt more oxidizing if no
measures are in place to prevent the fuel salt from becoming more oxidizing. The salt composition and
RedOx potential of the fuel salt is therefore constantly changing, though slowly. Increasing the oxidizing
condition of the fuel salt accelerates structural material corrosion by the fuel salt at high temperatures.
The introduction of oxide and moisture can also lead to changes of RedOx potential and possibly cause
other issues. Salt chemistry in an MSR is critical for reliable operation and minimization of structural
material corrosion by fuel salt and therefore should be maintained in acceptable ranges.
 The last two decades have seen significant advancements in molten salt electrochemistry.
Electrochemical methods are moving toward providing quantitative measurement of select impurities and
RedOx potentials, with potentiometry and LSV having been demonstrated even as early as in the MSRE.
In-line salt chemistry monitoring is likely to be seen in the development and deployment of test or
commercial MSRs, and electrochemical methods seem to have suitability for this purpose.
 This report provides an overview of RedOx potentials of molten salts relevant to MSRs and the status
of multiple electrochemical methods for potential MSR applications. The report also provides an
overview of electrochemical measurement technologies for molten salts. Electrochemical sensor
technologies for molten salts seek to observe at least one of the various signatures related to the chemical
conditions of the molten salts. Generally, the purpose of the electrochemical sensor is to determine
whether the chemical conditions of the molten salt are within acceptable limits of operation. The chemical
conditions of concern may be related to corrosion but may also be related to aspects such as core
neutronics, fissile inventory, and phase behaviors. It is important to understand what signature the sensor
is designed to observe, and what that signature says about the condition of the salt.
