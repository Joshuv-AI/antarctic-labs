Psychological and health consequences of difficult-to-translate concepts among immigrant older adults
Authors: Senyao Yu, Tania M Rodriguez, Rachel Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn health care settings, immigrant older adults who are not fluent in English may not be able to fully express their problems, even with a translator, because there are important phrases about health problems in their native language that do not directly translate to concepts in English. This issue is particularly important for immigrant older adults, especially recent immigrants, who might experience limited English proficiency and rely heavily on health care services. For example, Chinese expressions, such as “心累” and “气堵,” and Spanish idioms, such as “sentirse agüitado” and “achicopalado,” roughly translate to different types of emotional distress but do not have accurate English translations. These linguistic barriers affect both psychological and physical health outcomes. This paper briefly describes different types of culturally-embedded metaphors, idioms, and linguistic framing commonly used by immigrant older adults in the US, especially those who speak Mandarin and Spanish. We then highlight that even with a good translator, these phrases lead to barriers in effective healthcare services. As a result of these translational issues, immigrant older adults may experience misdiagnoses and improper treatments, thereby decreasing patients’ willingness to seek help. We highlight the need for more research on this issue of culturally based descriptions of health issues, particularly psychological distress. While prior research documents these barriers, it does not provide direct causal evidence of how these concepts influence diagnosis and treatment outcomes. Doing so would provide a better understanding of how linguistic factors affect mental and physical health, guiding more inclusive, culturally sensitive solutions.

In health care settings, language is not merely a tool for communication; it is the medium through which people make sense of their suffering. However, language barriers are not easily resolved through interpretation alone. Idioms of distress refer to culturally-specific ways of expressing psychological and physical suffering, often through metaphorical or somatic language that may not have direct equivalents in other languages. This problem is particularly important for immigrant older adults, especially recent immigrants, in the United States, as they might experience limited English proficiency and rely heavily on health care services (August et al., 2011; Mui et al., 2007; Solway et al., 2010). Although English proficiency varies by migration history, education, length of residence, and social context, limited English proficiency remains a significant barrier to health care communication and service use for many immigrant older adults (Bauer et al., 2010; Jimenez et al., 2013). Mandarin and Spanish are two of the most commonly spoken non-English languages among older adults in the United States, particularly among Chinese American and Latino older adult populations, which represent two of the largest aging minority groups with substantial numbers of immigrants and individuals with limited English proficiency. Many health concepts in Mandarin or Spanish are not readily translatable into English, and thus these patients may not be able to fully express their physical or emotional pain, even with the help of a translator. The loss of culturally-specific meanings that are difficult to translate between languages is reflected in this problem, which extends beyond vocabulary (August et al., 2011; Chen et al., 2024). For many immigrant older adults in the United States, limited English proficiency means that they cannot fully express their physical or emotional pain, even with the help of a translator. Many health concepts in Mandarin or Spanish are not readily translatable into English. Hence, idioms like 心累, 气堵, agüitado, and achicopalado signify ways of experiencing combined psychic and somatic phenomena that are difficult, or even impossible, to translate (Durà-Vilà & Hodes, 2012; Lomas, 2016). This problem has potentially broad relevance given the growing population of older immigrants in the United States and the substantial number of older adults with limited English proficiency who rely on health care systems. Prior research suggests that language discordance between patients and providers is associated with reduced access to care, lower satisfaction, poorer understanding of treatment recommendations, and lower utilization of preventive services (August et al., 2011; Mui et al., 2007; Solway et al., 2010).

Idioms of distress can describe culturally relevant knowledge on the embodiment of health and disease. These idioms often signify ways of experiencing combined psychic and somatic phenomena that are difficult, or even impossible, to translate directly into English. In many cultural frameworks, emotional suffering may be expressed through bodily sensations such as pressure, fatigue, heat, pain, or blockage, rather than through discrete psychiatric labels. However, within Western biomedical systems that often separate mental and physical health into distinct categories, such expressions may be misclassified as solely psychological or solely physiological concerns. Indeed, such idioms are difficult to elaborate on by even experienced interpreters, since the translations may only indicate the symptoms, rather than the manifestations of cultural knowledge regarding illness and health (Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016). Because of these difficult-to-translate concepts, patients may receive misdiagnoses, mistreatments of diseases, withdrawal from health care settings, among other consequences (Gerwing et al., 2016; Jimenez et al., 2013). Over time, when healthcare providers and systems fail to recognize and appropriately interpret these culturally relevant expressions, such communication gaps may reinforce existing disparities in mental health assessment and care (Bauer et al., 2010; Trinh et al., 2019).

This paper explores how difficult-to-translate ideas of disease and pain result in a lack of health-related representation among immigrants, particularly immigrant older adults, in the United States. We highlight the urgent need for more culturally-informed research and practice by exploring how cultural metaphors, idioms, and linguistic framing shape clinical communication. Understanding these language gaps is essential for building inclusive, culturally responsive medical systems that allow patients from various cultures to be fully heard and receive appropriate care.

Culturally embedded metaphors, idioms, and linguistic framing

Because language and culture are intertwined, it can be difficult to translate concepts across cultures, especially within health care settings. For example, many East Asian adults may express distress as an imbalance of inner energy or qi, which is a culturally specific understanding of wellness that has no precise English equivalent (Li et al., 2014; Yang & Singla, 2011). In addition, many Chinese older adults believe that using bodily metaphors to describe psychological distress or worry (e.g., 心累 or “tired heart”) is more acceptable than classifying it as a mental illness, given cultural stigma against psychological issues (Li et al., 2014; Yang & Singla, 2011). When doctors take these metaphors literally, it can make diagnosis more error-prone (Bauer et al., 2010; Stokes et al., 2002). Likewise, Spanish has idioms linking emotional and somatic sensations. Phrases such as sentirse agüitado and sentirse apagado (“feeling dimmed”) describe emotional heaviness, sadness, fatigue, or loss of vitality. Then, there are words that are untranslatable, such as chiripiorca, soponcio, and patatùs (commonly used by older adults in Hispanic culture). These words and expressions are embedded in cultural idioms of distress such as susto, nervios, or ataque de nervios, which blend psychological suffering with social and spiritual meanings (Durà-Vilà & Hodes, 2012; Lewis-Fernández et al., 2010). When such expressions are translated into English, the loss of what they actually mean may lead to misinterpretations of the severity and cause of the symptoms reported (Gerwing et al., 2016; Ramos et al., 2017). When culturally specific idioms of distress are misunderstood or reduced to literal translations, clinicians may misinterpret emotional suffering as only physical symptoms, or overlook clinically meaningful distress altogether. Such misunderstandings may contribute to delayed treatment, inappropriate care, lower trust in providers, and reduced willingness to seek future help (Bauer et al., 2010; Gerwing et al., 2016; Ramos et al., 2017).

Even with experienced interpreters, the subtleties of culturally embedded idioms, for example, 心累 (“tired heart”), 气堵 (“blocked qi”), and sentirse apagado (“feeling dimmed”), are often lost in translation. These expressions carry meanings that extend beyond literal words, reflecting holistic understandings of health that link body, mind, and emotion (Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016). When translators attempt to render these metaphors into biomedical English, they may omit the cultural and relational dimensions that shape their meanings (Li et al., 2014; Lomas, 2016; Richly, 2025). As a result, clinicians may underestimate the severity of distress or misattribute it to physical illness, which can limit their ability to provide appropriate care (Lomas, 2016; Richly, 2025).

A major source of misunderstanding stems from the tendency to simplify rich emotional language into generic terms like “sad,” “stressed,” or “tired,” which erase metaphorical nuance (Gerwing et al., 2016; Stokes et al., 2002). For instance, a Mandarin-speaking older adult describing themselves as 气堵 may be expressing grief or emotional stagnation, yet a literal translation, “blocked air,” could lead a clinician to suspect asthma or gastrointestinal problems. Similarly, a Spanish-speaking patient who says they feel apagado (“dimmed”) might mean emotional numbness or hopelessness, but it may be interpreted merely as fatigue or low energy (Durà-Vilà & Hodes, 2012; Lewis-Fernández et al., 2010). These linguistic distortions reveal not only vocabulary gaps but also deep cultural differences in how emotion and illness are conceptualized (Lomas, 2016; Yang & Singla, 2011).

Such mistranslations can lead to systemic clinical errors. When physicians interpret culturally based idioms through a strictly biomedical lens, that is, a framework that prioritizes objective, symptom-based diagnosis, they may overlook the cultural and contextual meanings embedded in patients’ expressions (Bauer et al., 2010; Lomas, 2016). Conversely, dismissing physical idioms as “just cultural” can allow genuine physiological suffering to go untreated (Talbot & Elkington, 2016). These misalignments erode patient trust, reinforcing stigma and alienation, and often discourage older immigrants from seeking further medical help (Stokes et al., 2002; Yang & Singla, 2011). Beyond communication itself, these barriers may increase caregiver burden, place additional demands on family members who serve as informal interpreters, reduce clinical efficiency for healthcare providers, and contribute to broader healthcare inequities among aging immigrant communities (Jimenez et al., 2013; Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016).

Interpreter-mediated communication adds another layer of complexity. Interpreters must manage emotional tone and social hierarchy while balancing literal accuracy with pragmatic clarity (Sleptsova et al., 2014). This tension can lead to “cultural editing,” where interpreters modify patients’ words to make them more understandable to clinicians (Gerwing et al., 2016). Prior research has shown that interpreter-mediated communication can substantially shape healthcare encounters by influencing accuracy of information exchange, emotional nuance, patient trust, informed consent, and treatment understanding (Gerwing et al., 2016; Kletečka-Pulker et al., 2021; Meuter et al., 2015; Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016; Vranjes & Bot, 2021). Studies suggest that even when interpretation is available, important meanings may still be lost when culturally specific expressions of distress do not have direct equivalents in biomedical English.

Who the interpreter is may also mediate health care consequences. Professional medical interpreters may differ from family members, friends, or ad hoc bilingual staff in confidentiality, neutrality, emotional involvement, and familiarity with medical terminology. Family interpreters may provide emotional support and cultural context, but they may also filter information, omit sensitive topics, or experience stress when translating distressing information (Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016; Vranjes & Bot, 2021). While interpreters may improve efficiency, it can sacrifice empathy and depth, especially in high-pressure clinical settings that leave little time for nuanced discussion (Ramos et al., 2017). Consequently, older adults’ idioms of distress, which often express suffering most authentically, become invisible within medical discourse (Lomas, 2016; Richly, 2025).

Interpreter training and the interpreter’s relational position relative to the patient or provider may also matter. Interpreters with formal health care training may be better prepared to preserve meaning, clarify ambiguity, and navigate culturally sensitive issues, whereas untrained interpreters may prioritize speed or literal wording over conceptual accuracy. In addition, power dynamics within clinical settings may influence whether interpreters feel comfortable challenging misunderstandings or elaborating on culturally embedded meanings (Gerwing et al., 2016; Kletečka-Pulker et al., 2021; Meuter et al., 2015).

Ultimately, translation failure is not merely a linguistic or technical issue but a reflection of structural inequities between Western biomedical paradigms and the lived linguistic worlds of immigrant communities. Poor translation contributes to lower treatment adherence and withdrawal from professional care, as older adults often turn to family or traditional healers who share their cultural frameworks (Durà-Vilà & Hodes, 2012; Li et al., 2014). English-dominant medical institutions, meanwhile, perpetuate the notion that non-English expressions of suffering are “untranslatable” or “less scientific” (Lomas, 2016; Talbot & Elkington, 2016).

Although prior research has documented the importance of language barriers, interpreter-mediated communication, and cultural differences in health care encounters (August et al., 2011; Gerwing et al., 2016; Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016), less attention has been paid to difficult-to-translate concepts as a distinct source of misunderstanding in health care and mental health care. Existing studies often describe communication barriers broadly, but they rarely examine how culturally embedded concepts of distress, illness, aging, family obligation, and bodily experience may be simplified, transformed, or misinterpreted during clinical encounters (Durà-Vilà & Hodes, 2012; Li et al., 2014; Lomas, 2016; Yang & Singla, 2011). As a result, the field still lacks a clear understanding of how these concepts influence symptom disclosure, diagnostic interpretation, treatment recommendations, patient trust, family involvement, and health care decision-making among immigrant older adults (Bauer et al., 2010; Jimenez et al., 2013; Ramos et al., 2017). Addressing this gap is necessary before effective clinical, community, and system-level interventions can be developed.

Future research

Culturally sensitive health care practices and cultural competence training have become increasingly common in medical schools, residency programs, and continuing professional education. These initiatives often include interpreter-use training, communication across language differences, and awareness of cultural beliefs surrounding illness and treatment. However, many programs emphasize broad cultural awareness rather than training clinicians to recognize culturally specific metaphors, idioms of distress, and nonliteral expressions of suffering that may shape clinical communication (Richly, 2025; Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016). Future research should explore how culturally specific language shapes health care interactions, especially for older immigrants. Interculturality training for clinicians, paired with follow-up tools such as “cheat sheets” and patient-centered translation frameworks, could transform what is required for accurate and empathetic care (Richly, 2025; Yang & Singla, 2011). Doing so would not only strengthen diagnostic accuracy but also bridge the communication gap between patients and providers from different linguistic and cultural backgrounds.

While various studies discuss the power of language regarding immigrant patients’ experiences in care, they do not focus on how culturally framed metaphors and idioms affect diagnosis, treatment, or the patient–clinician interaction. This issue is important because misunderstandings of culturally specific expressions may lead to inaccurate symptom interpretation, delayed or inappropriate treatment, reduced patient trust, and lower engagement with health care services (Gerwing et al., 2016; Ramos et al., 2017). These metaphors, such as “心累,” “气堵,” and “sentirse apagado,” should be examined in relation to how medical systems classify and diagnose illness. Doing so would clarify how culturally rooted language shapes patients’ experiences and communication about distress, ultimately influencing diagnosis and care quality (Gerwing et al., 2016; Li et al., 2014). For example, future studies could experimentally compare whether clinicians who receive only literal translations versus culturally-contextualized translations make different diagnostic decisions, treatment recommendations, or empathy ratings. Longitudinal studies could also examine whether repeated experiences of being misunderstood predict lower treatment adherence, poorer mental and physical health outcomes, or reduced health care utilization among immigrant older adults (August et al., 2011; Bauer et al., 2010; Gerwing et al., 2016). In addition, intervention studies could test whether training clinicians to recognize common idioms of distress improves patient satisfaction, communication quality, and clinical accuracy (Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016).

A promising direction lies in interdisciplinary collaborations between linguistics, psychology, and medical anthropology. There are several notable published partnerships that have led to successful outcomes. For example, there have been community-led partnerships between Aboriginal communities and health professionals that improved culturally grounded health communication (Mitchell et al., 2021), as well as the “Two-Eyed Seeing” framework to bridge Indigenous and Western mental health systems (Mehl-Madrona & Mainguy, 2021). Incorporating local idioms of distress through linguistic and ethnographic methods enhances diagnostic accuracy and patient engagement (Lewis-Fernández et al., 2010; Mendenhall et al., 2019). These studies underscore how intercultural, collaborative frameworks can make mental health care more inclusive and responsive to diverse populations. Building on this, future research could examine how metaphorical language influences symptom reporting, emotional expression, and help-seeking behaviors among immigrant adults, especially older adults. For instance, physicians who understand the cultural roots of metaphors about “energy,” “heat,” “blockage,” or “darkness” may better interpret patients’ symptom descriptions and emotional distress (Bauer et al., 2010; Stokes et al., 2002). While prior studies have documented the challenges that arise when clinicians and patients do not share a linguistic or cultural framework (Ramos et al., 2017; Sleptsova et al., 2014), more research is still needed to understand how these communication gaps specifically shape diagnostic accuracy and empathy. Moreover, beyond primary health outcomes, future research could investigate a broader range of psychological, economic, social, and secondary health consequences associated with difficult-to-translate health concepts among immigrant older adults. Psychological outcomes may include loss of control, chronic stress, shame, anxiety, reduced trust in providers, or reluctance to seek future care. Economic outcomes may include delayed treatment costs, unnecessary testing, increased medical debt, missed work for patients or family members, and greater reliance on emergency services. Social outcomes may include loneliness, family conflict, caregiver burden, communication strain, and loss of perceived social support. Secondary health outcomes may include worsening of untreated primary illnesses, medication nonadherence, avoidable hospitalization, and the development of additional stress-related conditions.

In addition, research could examine how these consequences ripple outward to affect patients’ families, carers, health care providers, and the health care system overall. For example, family members may experience emotional strain or be required to act as informal interpreters during complex medical encounters, while providers may face diagnostic uncertainty, increased consultation time, communication fatigue, and professional stress. At the systems level, these barriers may contribute to repeated visits, inefficient care pathways, preventable complications, higher health care expenditures, and broader disparities in health outcomes across immigrant communities. Future studies could use longitudinal, mixed-methods, and intervention designs to better understand both the direct and indirect effects of these communication barriers over time.

Building on such insights, future studies could then design and test interventions for clinicians who are monolingual English speakers or who primarily communicate in English, examining how increased cultural and linguistic awareness might improve patient understanding and diagnostic care. In particular, based on evidence generated from the suggested future research, culturally-informed “cheat sheets” (e.g., Table 1) or quick-reference guides could be developed to summarize commonly used idioms of distress, culturally specific metaphors, and nonliteral expressions used by older adults from particular language backgrounds. These tools could then be integrated into pilot intervention programs designed to support real-time communication in clinical settings and improve clinicians’ interpretation of patient concerns (Sleptsova et al., 2014; Talbot & Elkington, 2016). For example, a group of clinicians who regularly see primarily Mandarin- or Spanish-speaking patients could receive the cheat sheet of commonly used phrases and a short video training to explain these difficult-to-translate concepts, while another group of clinicians does not (i.e., usual care comparison group). These interventions can then systematically test aspects of the intervention to better understand key active ingredients driving the positive changes. It may be that the cheat sheet would be enough to drive meaningful change. Following true community-based participatory research practices, the training would be co-developed with community members, interpreters, and physicians, to support real-time communication in clinical settings. This combined approach—educational and practical—would help clinicians navigate complex conversations with greater cultural sensitivity while avoiding overgeneralization or stereotyping (Ramos et al., 2017; Richly, 2025).

Finally, longitudinal research that is community-based should investigate how the language habits of immigrant older adults change over time and how these changes impact communication patterns and health literacy (Bauer et al., 2010; Li et al., 2014). Determining if cultural idioms lose their emotional accuracy across languages or whether younger multilingual family members “translate” metaphors differently could help clarify how linguistic adaptation affects care access over time and across generations. It may be the case that these issues are not as noteworthy in future generations of older adults if later generations use these phrases less and less, or it may be the case that they will still be as important as they are now. Such studies would help create more culturally aware and inclusive health care systems where patients’ varied ways of communicating their distress are valued as important indicators of their lived experiences rather than being language obstacles (Gerwing et al., 2016; Lomas, 2016).

Ultimately, investigating these questions would deepen our understanding of how language mediates care and emotion across cultures. Future research could go beyond translation accuracy by incorporating linguistic, psychological, and anthropological insights. This research would support the development of intercultural health care practices that better account for metaphor, cultural meaning, and their implications for clinical accuracy and patient–provider communication.

Conclusions

Using language rooted in their cultural worldviews, immigrant older adults can communicate their physical and mental pain through culturally ingrained metaphors and idioms of distress that may be difficult to translate into English concepts. When these phrases are used, literal translations omit deeper contextual information, making them barriers to accurate care in English-speaking medical systems (Gerwing et al., 2016; Yang & Singla, 2011). Inadequate treatment, incorrect diagnoses, and a diminished desire to seek professional assistance might result from such misinterpretations during clinical contacts (Bauer et al., 2010; Ramos et al., 2017). More importantly, culture and language should not be conceived as a problem in health care, but rather as a mismatch with the English-speaking health care system. Further consideration of cultural contexts via these difficult-to-translate concepts in health care settings could be achieved via useful intercultural communication training to decode patients’ metaphors (Lomas, 2016; Richly, 2025). Ultimately, addressing the linguistic and cultural aspects of distress can help close knowledge gaps and result in more effective and compassionate care for older immigrants (Talbot & Elkington, 2016).
