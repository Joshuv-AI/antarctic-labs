Neuroplasticity After Stroke: Clinical Evidence and Its Implication for Effective Rehabilitation Strategies
Authors: Yun-Hee Kim, Seyoung Shin, Jinuk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStroke remains a major cause of long-term disability as it often leads to persistent motor deficits, which hinder functional recovery. Neuroplasticity is an important driver of poststroke recovery as it supports synaptic remodeling, network reorganization, and activity-dependent adaptation. This review outlines the neurobiological mechanisms of recovery, including Hebbian and homeostatic plasticity. It also discusses how intensive, task-specific rehabilitation leverages these processes. Technological innovations, such as robotic-assisted rehabilitation and sensor-based feedback, further enable high-dose, repetitive, and personalized training. In addition, adjunctive strategies, including pharmacological modulation, noninvasive brain stimulation, and stem cell therapies, which aim to enhance neuroplasticity, are highlighted. Despite their promise, these interventions show inconsistent efficacy, indicating the need for individualized approaches tailored to lesion characteristics, neurophysiological markers, and recovery stage. Future directions should emphasize the integration of biomarkers, neuroimaging, and precision neurotechnologies, together with multimodal combination therapies. These efforts are essential to establish adaptive rehabilitation protocols that can enhance both the consistency and the magnitude of functional recovery.Keywords: Stroke, Recovery, Neuroplasticity, Neurorehabilitation

INTRODUCTION

Stroke recovery involves multiple neurobiological mechanisms, including reversal of diaschisis, reorganization of surviving pathways, and formation of new synapses via neuroplasticity [1]. There are two forms of plasticity that are essential to this process: Hebbian plasticity, which involves activity-dependent strengthening or weakening of synapses via long-term potentiation (LTP) and depression, and homeostatic plasticity, which maintains overall network stability. Together, they support functional reorganization following injury [2]. Task-specific, repetitive rehabilitation leverages Hebbian plasticity by reinforcing active neural pathways through repeated use, while homeostatic mechanisms ensure network stability during this reorganization process.

Intensive, task-specific rehabilitation harnesses these mechanisms to promote experience-dependent plasticity and restore motor function [3]. According to animal and human studies, repetitive, goal-directed practice consistently improves motor performance after stroke [3,4]. However, despite these benefits, conventional rehabilitation alone is often insufficient for achieving optimal recovery, with outcomes widely varying across individuals. This has led to the development of adjunctive approaches, including robotic technology, pharmacological agents, noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS), and stem cell-based therapies. Although these strategies aim to enhance neuroplasticity, their clinical efficacy remains inconsistent. Moreover, the criteria for selecting appropriate patients for a specific treatment are often unclear.

In this review, we outline the biological foundation of poststroke plasticity and examine how conventional and emerging rehabilitation strategies can be used to optimize stroke recovery.

NEUROPLASTICITY AFTER STROKE

Stroke pathophysiology: acute injury and biological transitions

Ischemic stroke is the predominant subtype of stroke, accounting for approximately 87% and 79.6% of cases in the United States and Korea, respectively [5,6]. It typically results from decreased cerebral perfusion because of thrombosis, embolism, or atherosclerosis. The ensuing lack of oxygen and glucose rapidly disrupts adenosine triphosphate production, which impairs ion channel function and neurotransmission [7]. This process leads to ionic imbalance and extracellular glutamate accumulation, which initiates a cascade of cytotoxic edema and excitotoxic cell death within minutes. By contrast, hemorrhagic stroke causes direct damage through bleeding along white matter tracts, which induces neuronal toxicity and edema primarily via the lysis of red blood cells.

Beyond the initial insult, secondary injury processes unfold over hours to days. Apoptosis and neuroinflammation become prominent. At the same time, the disruption of the blood–brain barrier, astrocytic gliosis, and microglial activation further shape the lesion microenvironment. Between 3 and 14 days poststroke, regenerative signals begin to emerge, including the upregulation of growth-promoting molecules that support dendritic remodeling [8]. By the third week, synaptogenesis is initiated, enabling surviving neural circuits to reorganize and potentially compensate for lost functions.

These temporally orchestrated biological events define a transitional phase, wherein the brain shifts from acute injury toward a plastic and reparative state. Moreover, this phase lays the groundwork for neurorehabilitation and functional recovery.

Neuroplasticity after stroke

Poststroke recovery is driven by neuroplastic mechanisms that operate across molecule, cell, and system levels. These mechanisms include synaptic remodeling, reversal of diaschisis, axonal sprouting, and functional compensation by undamaged brain regions [1,2]. Collectively, these processes support long-term improvements in motor and cognitive functions.

At the molecular level, neurotransmitter signaling plays an important role in modulating cortical excitability. In the peri-infarct zone, tonic gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) activity initially increases, resulting in prolonged cortical inhibition and reduced neural responsiveness [9]. In contrast, enhanced excitatory glutamatergic signaling, particularly through α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptors, facilitates synaptic plasticity and promotes functional recovery [10]. It is essential to achieve a proper balance between inhibition and excitation to restore functional connectivity. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is another important modulator of poststroke plasticity. Its expression is upregulated by AMPAkines, a class of compounds that facilitate LTP by enhancing excitatory synaptic transmission. In parallel, microglial calcium signaling and astrocyte-mediated modifications contribute to cortical reactivity; however, their roles in early plasticity remain to be fully elucidated [11]. The disruption of the blood–brain barrier further influences the neurochemical environment during recovery. As the brain transitions from acute injury to repair, which typically occurs between 1 week and 4 weeks poststroke, surviving neurons initiate axonal outgrowth and synaptogenesis, particularly in the peri-infarct regions [12]. These processes are reinforced by task-specific rehabilitation, driving functional reorganization and strengthening use-dependent neural pathways.

At the systemic level, stroke-induced reorganization involves both intra- and interhemispheric changes. The contralesional hemisphere often exhibits increased activation during use of the affected limb, reflecting altered interhemispheric dynamics. Whether this activity is beneficial or maladaptive depends on the severity of the lesion and the patient’s structural reserve. To account for this variability, the bimodal balance–recovery model, which links recovery patterns to the integrity of preserved neural substrates, was proposed [13]. Reducing excessive contralesional activity may enhance recovery for patients with high structural reserve. By contrast, individuals with low structural reserve may depend on compensatory pathways in the unaffected hemisphere.

Ultimately, poststroke neuroplasticity is highly dynamic and shaped by lesion characteristics and rehabilitation context. Thus, it is important to obtain a detailed understanding of these processes to tailor phase-specific, individualized interventions. Time-sensitive biological events define a critical window during which guided plasticity can optimize recovery (Fig. 1). Early responses, including diaschisis, unmasking, and cortical hyperexcitability, promote adaptive changes such as synaptogenesis and functional remapping. However, delayed or misdirected plasticity may lead to maladaptive outcomes. Therefore, timely, targeted rehabilitation is essential for maximizing the recovery potential while minimizing maladaptive reorganization.

ROLE OF REHABILITATION IN PROMOTING NEUROPLASTICITY

Experience-dependent plasticity and environmental enrichment

Experience-dependent plasticity refers to the brain’s ability to reshape its structure and function in response to specific behavioral experiences. An important example of this principle in stroke rehabilitation is enriched environments. These environments, characterized by enriched sensory, motor, and social stimuli, support neuroplasticity and functional recovery. According to animal studies, enriched settings enhance plastic processes, including dendritic remodeling, synaptogenesis, and neurogenesis [1]. Biernaskie and Corbett [14] found that combining enriched environments with the daily use of the impaired limb significantly improves motor function. Varied activities and enriched surroundings induce neuronal changes, including increased dendritic branching, higher synaptic density, enhanced neurogenesis, and altered expression of growth factors. Notably, motor practice remains one of the strongest drivers of poststroke plasticity.

Although some spontaneous recovery occurs, extensive clinical evidence supports the role of neurorehabilitation in enhancing neuroplasticity [3]. Motor learning is most effective when practice is task-specific, repetitive, and intensive. Based on a review of 944 scientific articles, the American Heart Association (AHA) and American Stroke Association (ASA) published comprehensive guidelines for adult stroke rehabilitation [4]. These guidelines recommend inpatient rehabilitation whenever possible to maximize functional outcomes.

The advances in rehabilitation technologies, including robotic-assisted devices and sensor-based feedback, have further boosted experience-driven plasticity. These tools assist in moving impaired limbs, offer real-time feedback, and encourage bilateral use. These features enable high-dose, intensive, and repeatable training that supports task-specific cortical reorganization [15]. In line with this, multiple clinical guidelines and systematic reviews have evaluated the role of robot-assisted rehabilitation. The AHA/ASA recommends robotic therapy as a feasible modality to deliver intensive and repetitive practice [16], while the 2024 VA/DoD guideline judged the evidence insufficient to recommend for or against its use [17]. Cochrane reviews reported improvements in upper limb motor function and activities of daily living [18], as well as increased odds of achieving independent walking when robotic devices were combined with physiotherapy [19]. Prevention of maladaptive plasticity

In the early stages of stroke recovery, the contralesional motor cortex often exhibits increased activity during movement of the affected limb. Unlike what is seen in healthy individuals, this abnormal pattern typically subsides as recovery progresses [1]. However, this imbalance persists and can hinder motor recovery in some patients. To address this problem, many rehabilitation strategies aim to restore interhemispheric balance by promoting activity in the lesioned hemisphere or reducing excessive contralesional activation.

Maladaptive plasticity refers to neuroplastic changes that negatively affect functional recovery. The development of compensatory movement patterns, which is often caused by overreliance on the unaffected limb, is a common example of maladaptive plasticity. This compensation can reinforce contralesional overactivation, further suppressing function in the affected hemisphere [22]. If left uncorrected, these patterns can become ingrained and limit desirable motor recovery.

To counteract this, rehabilitation should encourage the appropriate use of both limbs, with a focus on reengaging the affected side. One effective intervention is constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT), which targets learned nonuse by restricting the movement of the unaffected limb [23]. CIMT promotes use-dependent plasticity in the affected hemisphere by forcing the use of the impaired limb, thereby facilitating functional reorganization.

Ultimately, motor rehabilitation should be individualized, task-specific, and aligned with the functional status and recovery stage of the patient. This approach helps to reinforce adaptive plasticity while minimizing maladaptive patterns that can impede long-term outcomes.

Neuroimaging evidence supporting rehabilitation-induced plasticity

Neuroimaging studies have provided valuable insights into adaptive and maladaptive plasticity after stroke, revealing the neural mechanisms underlying recovery and compensation. According to recent evidence, rehabilitation, particularly exercise-based interventions, can induce neuroplasticity by modifying brain connectivity and activation patterns. Positron emission tomography and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies frequently report bilateral cortical activation during movements of the affected limb in patients with subacute to chronic stroke. These atypical patterns are often viewed as evidence of disrupted interhemispheric balance, an important factor in motor impairment and recovery [24]. Furthermore, task-based fMRI studies show that improvements in upper limb motor function are linked to increased activation in the affected primary motor cortex, supporting the potential of targeted rehabilitation to promote ipsilesional engagement [25]. In addition, diffusion tensor imaging has been used to assess structural changes in white matter tracts. According to a previous study, rehabilitation in an enriched environment can enhance the number and integrity of corticospinal tracts, supporting the idea that structural remodeling underlies functional recovery [26].

At the network level, Wu et al. [27] found that functional connectivity between the somatosensory cortex and the putamen increased after training, and this change was positively correlated with improvements in the clinical motor scores. Collectively, these findings highlight neurorehabilitation’s capacity to reshape brain networks, promote adaptive plasticity, and facilitate meaningful functional recovery after stroke. These imaging biomarkers can guide personalized rehabilitation by identifying neural targets that are most responsive to intervention. These insights further reinforce the importance of combining behavioral interventions with neuroimaging markers to optimize stroke rehabilitation strategies.

ADJUNCTIVE STRATEGIES TO ENHANCE POSTSTROKE NEUROPLASTICITY

The recent advances in stroke rehabilitation have led to the development of various adjunctive interventions aimed at enhancing neuroplasticity. Among them, pharmacological agents, NIBS, and stem cell therapies have attracted increasing interest. However, the clinical outcomes remain variable, underscoring the need for personalized and multimodal treatment approaches. Pharmacological interventions

Poststroke pharmacological strategies to enhance neuroplasticity have mainly targeted monoaminergic neurotransmission, particularly the serotonergic and dopaminergic systems. Other pathways that influence cortical excitability have also been explored [28].

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), particularly fluoxetine and citalopram, have been the most extensively studied. This focus stems from the established role of serotonin in motor learning, cognitive processes, and emotional regulation [29]. Initially, the FLAME trial suggested that fluoxetine combined with physiotherapy could improve motor recovery [30]. However, these findings could not be replicated in three large randomized controlled trials, including FOCUS [31], AFFINITY [32], and EFFECTS [33]. Specifically, these trials found no significant improvements in functional outcomes, including scores on the Modified Rankin Scale (mRS). Moreover, a 2021 Cochrane review supported these results, finding no clear motor recovery benefits but confirming a reduced risk of poststroke depression [34]. Consequently, major guidelines remain cautious. The 2016 guidelines from the AHA/ASA concluded that SSRIs are not well established for promoting motor recovery (Level B) [16]. Similarly, the European guidelines issued weak recommendations, discouraging the use of fluoxetine and low-dose citalopram (10 mg), while conditionally supporting the use of 20 mg of citalopram during early rehabilitation [35]. Taken together, these findings underscore the challenge of achieving consistent functional improvements through serotonergic modulation alone.

Dopaminergic agents, particularly levodopa, have attracted attention because of the involvement of dopamine in motor learning, synaptic plasticity, and reward-related processes. According to several early small-scale trials, combining levodopa with physical therapy might facilitate motor recovery after stroke [36]. However, these benefits could not be confirmed in subsequent larger trials. Most notably, the DARS trial, a multicenter randomized study that evaluated co-careldopa (a combination of levodopa and carbidopa), found no significant improvement in walking ability compared with placebo in patients with subacute stroke [37]. These findings have led to cautious interpretations in clinical practice. The effectiveness of dopaminergic agents, including levodopa, in enhancing poststroke motor recovery is not well established (Level B) [16].

Stimulants, including dextroamphetamine and methylphenidate, have also been explored. These agents may enhance plasticity by increasing dopaminergic and noradrenergic activity, thereby improving attention and arousal. According to early animal studies, stimulants increased the expression of BDNF and improved activity-dependent plasticity [38]. However, a multicenter randomized trial involving 64 patients found that 10 mg of dextroamphetamine did not lead to significant improvement in motor function at 3 months poststroke [39]. Given these findings, the American and European guidelines issued weak recommendations against the use of amphetamine and dextroamphetamine for early motor neurorehabilitation after acute ischemic stroke. Regarding methylphenidate, a recent systematic review of randomized controlled trials and a meta-analysis reported that there is some evidence to suggest methylphenidate for a positive effect on mood, however, no results support its use in functional recovery post-stroke. This review highlighted the need for further research with more sensitive and standardized outcome measures [40].

Cholinergic modulation has also been investigated to promote poststroke recovery. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, including donepezil, may facilitate cognitive and motor recovery by enhancing cholinergic transmission. One pilot study involving older stroke patients with cognitive impairment reported motor function improvements as measured by Functional Independence Measure motor scores and reductions in apathy following donepezil treatment [41]. A subsequent phase II trial (MASTER study), which administered donepezil within 24 hours of stroke onset, confirmed the safety of donepezil and demonstrated trends toward functional improvement [42]. In another study, combining donepezil with CIMT resulted in improved early motor outcomes [43]. In addition, a meta-analysis revealed modest improvements in cognitive performance on the Mini-Mental State Examination over a 24-week period [44]. However, most available studies have been small in scale or lacked rigorous control designs. Thus, high-quality evidence from large randomized controlled trials is still needed before recommending the routine clinical use of donepezil.

Beyond monoaminergic and cholinergic systems, GABAergic neurotransmission, particularly through alpha-5 subunit-containing gamma-aminobutyric acid A receptors (α5-GABAARs), has attracted increasing interest. These receptors mediate tonic cortical inhibition, which is thought to constrain neuroplasticity. According to preclinical studies, antagonizing α5-GABAARs increases cortical excitability and enhances recovery in animal models of stroke [45]. Similar effects have been observed in healthy human participants, where α5-GABAAR modulation agents enhanced corticospinal excitability [46]. However, clinical trials in stroke populations have not yet demonstrated consistent functional improvements. As such, α5-GABAAR modulation remains an experimental approach.

As a peptide-based neurotrophic agent, cerebrolysin mimics endogenous growth factors and may promote synaptic plasticity, reduce apoptosis, and enhance neural recovery. According to clinical studies in patients with moderate to severe stroke, the administration of cerebrolysin along with standard rehabilitation led to improvements in motor function and activities of daily living [47]. Functional neuroimaging data also suggest that cerebrolysin treatment may help preserve motor network connectivity [48]. Notably, the benefits were most pronounced in patients with severe baseline impairment. In recognition of this, the European guidelines issued a weak recommendation in 2021 supporting the use of intravenous cerebrolysin (30 mL) during early rehabilitation, although further large-scale validation is still needed.

In summary, clinical trials have obtained mixed results in using pharmacological strategies to enhance neuroplasticity poststroke. Moreover, most major guidelines offer only weak or conditional recommendations regarding their use. To unlock their full potential, future research should adopt precision medicine approaches, identify patients who are most likely to benefit, refine the dosage and timing, and investigate synergistic effects with neuromodulation or task-specific rehabilitation. Such efforts will be essential for translating pharmacological advances into meaningful and consistent clinical outcomes. NIBS

NIBS has emerged as a promising adjunctive strategy to promote neuroplasticity and facilitate functional recovery after stroke. Among the available NIBS techniques, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) are the most extensively studied, each with distinct mechanisms of action and therapeutic profiles. Their clinical applications are reviewed in the following sections, focusing on evidence-based recommendations by stroke phase and functional domain.

rTMS modulates cortical excitability by applying repetitive magnetic pulses. According to preclinical studies, rTMS can induce structural synaptic plasticity in the brain, lending support to its potential for enhancing neuroplasticity [49]. The therapeutic effects of rTMS largely depend on the stimulation frequency; low-frequency stimulation (≤ 1 Hz) tends to suppress cortical excitability, whereas high-frequency stimulation (≥ 5 Hz) facilitates it. This frequency-dependent modulation provides a mechanistic rationale for selecting rTMS protocols that aim to restore interhemispheric balance following stroke.

The clinical guidelines issued by European expert groups in 2021 provide phase-specific recommendations for rTMS in stroke rehabilitation [50]. Specifically, low-frequency rTMS applied to the contralesional primary motor cortex (M1) demonstrated definite efficacy (Level A) and possible efficacy (Level C) for hand motor recovery in the post-acute phase and chronic phase, respectively. By contrast, high-frequency rTMS targeting the ipsilesional M1 is considered probably effective (Level B) in the post-acute stage. In addition, rTMS has shown probable efficacy for treating chronic poststroke aphasia when applied at low frequency to the right inferior frontal gyrus (Level B) and possible efficacy for visuospatial neglect using continuous theta burst stimulation over the contralesional parietal cortex (Level C). However, despite these expert recommendations, no significant improvements in upper limb motor function were observed following low-frequency rTMS in a large-scale randomized controlled trial involving 199 patients with chronic stroke [51], highlighting the variability of clinical outcomes and the need for personalized treatment strategies. Nevertheless, meta-analyses continue to support the efficacy of rTMS across different stroke phases. According to recent findings, earlier application, particularly during the acute phase, may yield greater improvements in motor function [52].

tDCS delivers low-intensity direct current to modulate neuronal excitability. Anodal stimulation depolarizes neuronal membranes, thereby facilitating the generation of action potentials, whereas cathodal stimulation hyperpolarizes membranes, suppressing excitability. In animal models, tDCS has been associated with structural neuroplasticity, including microglial morphological changes, suggesting its capacity to influence neuronal and glial pathways involved in recovery [53]. Although earlier clinical guidelines were cautious because of inconsistent findings, subsequent systematic reviews have supported the therapeutic potential of tDCS. A 2020 Cochrane review reported moderate evidence for improvements in activities of daily living and limb motor function, and high-level evidence for enhanced muscle strength [54]. In the same year, an expert consensus provided more granular recommendations based on stroke phase, stimulation targets, and intervention combinations [55].

In the subacute stage, anodal tDCS applied over the ipsilesional M1 and cathodal tDCS applied over the contralesional M1 were considered probably effective (Level B). Meanwhile, bilateral M1 stimulation during the subacute phase was deemed possibly effective (Level C). In the chronic stage, anodal and cathodal tDCS, as well as bilateral M1 stimulation, were rated probably effective (Level B). In poststroke aphasia, multiple tDCS montages have been assigned a Level C recommendation for possible effectiveness, such as anodal stimulation of Broca’s area or its right hemispheric homologue, cathodal stimulation of the right frontotemporal cortex, and bilateral stimulation combining anodal and cathodal approaches. Nevertheless, the multicenter NETS trial conducted across 11 sites reported neutral results with regard to upper extremity recovery [56], highlighting the persistent gap between mechanistic promise and consistent clinical efficacy [57].

These findings underscore the importance of developing more refined and personalized neuromodulation strategies. The responses to NIBS considerably vary among individuals, which are influenced by anatomical differences, lesion location, and stroke chronicity [58]. To address this heterogeneity, recent studies have explored individualized stimulation protocols. One promising approach involves using magnetic resonance imaging-based finite element modeling to optimize electrode placement and maximize electric field intensity at target regions. This modeling strategy can enhance the precision of stimulation by 20% to over 50%, depending on factors, including skull thickness, cerebrospinal fluid volume, and cortical geometry [59]. Building on this concept, multichannel tDCS systems are now being developed to deliver field-calibrated stimulation, including 0.2 V/m at the primary motor cortex, rather than applying fixed current intensities. This process allows for more precise modulation of distributed motor networks [60]. In clinical settings, early-phase studies have demonstrated the feasibility and potential of these individualized approaches. Multichannel tDCS targeting the ipsilesional M1 and anterior intraparietal sulcus was utilized, with electric field strength calibrated to the individual. The intervention resulted in functional improvements and measurable hemodynamic changes observed through functional near-infrared spectroscopy, reinforcing the physiological validity of personalized stimulation protocols [61].

In parallel, closed-loop and brain-state-dependent stimulation paradigms are attracting increasing interest. These systems dynamically adjust stimulation parameters in real time by synchronizing delivery with optimal neural states, which are typically monitored through an electroencephalogram (EEG) or fMRI. For example, EEG-triggered rTMS enhances LTP-like effects, demonstrating the potential of adaptive stimulation approaches that align with endogenous brain activity patterns [62]. In addition, biomarker-guided strategies are being developed to further individualize treatment. Among them, functional connectivity patterns and transcranial magnetic stimulation-evoked motor potentials are promising biomarkers used to stratify patients and guide personalized interventions, with the goal of identifying those who are most likely to benefit from neuromodulation [63].

Beyond rTMS and tDCS, several emerging NIBS techniques are being explored for their potential in stroke rehabilitation. For example, transcranial temporal interference stimulation (tTIS) delivers two high-frequency electrical currents that intersect to produce low-frequency stimulation at subcortical targets. Although there is a lack of clinical data on tTIS in stroke populations, computational models suggest its potential for modulating basal ganglia circuits involved in motor recovery [64]. Low-intensity focused ultrasound (LIFU), which enables millimeter-level precision, has demonstrated preclinical efficacy in enhancing angiogenesis and promoting neural recovery. Moreover, early-phase human studies support the feasibility of LIFU for modulating motor-related brain regions [65]. Transcranial photobiomodulation (tPBM) applies near-infrared light to influence mitochondrial activity and cerebral perfusion. The preliminary findings in patients with chronic brain injury, including a pilot study in stroke survivors, have indicated the potential cognitive and motor benefits of tPBM [66] although further clinical validation is needed.

Together, these findings illustrate the expanding scope of NIBS technologies and underscore the importance of tailoring stimulation strategies to individual neuroanatomical and physiological profiles. Precision neuromodulation represents an important advancement toward enhancing the efficacy and consistency of poststroke rehabilitation. Moving forward, future research should focus on integrating these personalized approaches into large-scale, mechanistically informed clinical trials to validate their therapeutic potential and facilitate broader clinical adoption. Stem-cell-based therapies

Stem-cell-based therapies are being actively investigated as potential interventions to enhance neuroplasticity and functional recovery after stroke. According to preclinical studies, stem cell transplantation may promote neurogenesis, synaptic remodeling, angiogenesis, and immunomodulation within the injured brain. These mechanistic findings laid the groundwork for translational efforts, including the “STEPS” framework sponsored by the National Institutes of Health (NIH)/Food and Drug Administration, which established important parameters for clinical trial design, including cell type, delivery route, and intervention timing [71].

According to early-phase clinical trials, the intravenous and intracerebral administration of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and neural progenitor cells is generally safe and feasible [72]. Notably, the STARTING-2 trial, a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase II study, investigated the intravenous infusion of autologous MSCs in patients with ischemic stroke. The trial did not demonstrate significant improvement in the primary endpoint, the 3-month mRS. However, secondary analyses indicated significant gains in lower extremity motor function, particularly among patients classified as having poor predicted outcomes according to the PREP and PREP II algorithms, who were characterized by absent motor evoked potentials and disrupted corticospinal tract integrity, without serious treatment-related adverse events [73]. Neuroimaging analyses further revealed increased fractional anisotropy in the corticospinal tract and preserved motor network connectivity [74]. In addition, a biomarker substudy identified extracellular vesicle signatures associated with motor recovery, suggesting that these vesicles may serve as potential biomarkers for monitoring therapeutic responses and identifying responders in stem-cell-based therapy for stroke [75].

However, despite these encouraging results, the clinical efficacy of stem-cell-based therapies remains inconclusive. Current evidence is limited by the considerable heterogeneity in cell types, delivery methods, and intervention timing [76]. Importantly, many trials have not incorporated structured rehabilitation protocols, which are likely important to harnessing experience-dependent plasticity [77]. To overcome these limitations, future studies should integrate standardized rehabilitation regimens, use biomarker-guided patient selection, and investigate synergistic strategies combining cell therapy with neuromodulation or bioengineered scaffolds. These approaches will be essential to establish the safety and reproducible therapeutic efficacy of stem cell interventions in stroke recovery.

Emerging innovations: gut–brain axis and gene therapy

Aside from the established neurorehabilitation strategies, emerging research has identified the gut–brain axis and gene-based therapies as novel targets for promoting poststroke recovery. The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important regulator of neuroinflammation, immune homeostasis, and neuroplasticity. Stroke can disrupt microbial composition, leading to dysbiosis and systemic immune alterations that may hinder neural repair [78]. Recent meta-analyses have detailed that stroke-induced gut dysbiosis activates intestinal myeloid and T cells, elevates pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and increases blood–brain barrier permeability, thereby exacerbating peripheral immune infiltration and neuroinflammation [79]. According to preclinical studies, modulating the gut microbiome through probiotics or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can attenuate neuroinflammatory responses and improve neurobehavioral outcomes [80]. Early-phase clinical trials further suggest potential therapeutic benefits, including reductions in NIH Stroke Scale scores and inflammatory biomarkers following probiotic or FMT-based interventions [81].

In the other hand, gene-based approaches are being explored to reprogram the poststroke cellular environment and promote neural regeneration. The overexpression of neurotrophic factors, including NeuroD1 and NT-3, has been shown to enhance axonal sprouting, synaptic repair, and functional recovery in animal models. Moreover, in vivo cellular reprogramming techniques, including the conversion of reactive glial cells into neurons via viral vectors, have demonstrated promising structural and behavioral improvements in rodent stroke models [82]. Although microbiome- and gene-based therapies remain in preclinical or early clinical development, they represent promising frontiers in the biological modulation of poststroke recovery. Future clinical trials integrating these approaches with task-specific rehabilitation may uncover synergistic effects, paving the way for more personalized and mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies.

Combination therapy and future directions

Recent advances in neurorehabilitation highlight the need for multimodal and synergistic approaches, rather than relying on single interventions. Pharmacological agents, such as SSRIs or dopaminergic drugs, have been tested in conjunction with intensive rehabilitation to reinforce motor learning and neuroplasticity, though clinical outcomes remain variable [30]. Emerging strategies also include the integration of digital therapeutics, virtual reality, and sensor-based feedback systems with conventional or robotic rehabilitation, to provide engaging and personalized training environments. While these approaches are still in early clinical stages, they underscore a paradigm shift from unimodal interventions toward biomarker-guided, individualized combination therapies. Future clinical trials are warranted to identify optimal pairings, treatment timing, and patient subgroups most likely to benefit. Establishing standardized protocols and mechanistic biomarkers will be crucial to translating these combinations into routine clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

Stroke recovery is fundamentally driven by neuroplastic processes that enable the reorganization of neural circuits and restoration of motor and cognitive functions. Although conventional rehabilitation remains the cornerstone of treatment, adjunctive strategies (e.g., pharmacological interventions, robotic rehabilitation, NIBS, and stem-cell-based therapies) have been increasingly explored to enhance recovery outcomes. However, their clinical effects remain varied, highlighting the importance of tailoring interventions to individual patients based on lesion characteristics, recovery phase, and neurophysiological profiles. Future research should prioritize the integration of personalized assessment tools, phase-specific protocols, and multimodal therapeutic combinations to improve the consistency and efficacy of these approaches. In particular, combination therapies that integrate emerging interventions may maximize adaptive plasticity through synergistic mechanisms. The advances in neurotechnologies and biomarker-guided planning offer promising avenues to enhance the precision and timing of interventions. Ultimately, carefully designed and mechanism-informed clinical trials will be essential to translate these innovations into effective and scalable rehabilitation strategies that can meaningfully impact real-world stroke recovery.
