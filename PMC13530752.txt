Poster 203. Pronator-Biased Pitchers Demonstrate Decreased Flexor-Pronator Strength in Supinated Forearm Positions—A Mechanistic Rationale for UCL Injury Risk during Breaking Ball Pitch Development and Utilization
Authors: Matthew S Fury, Logan O’Donnell, Chesney Benson, Nicolas Farenwald, Dylan Newcomer, David Redden, Mark Rogers, P Gunnar Brolinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:The incidence of ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) injuries continues to rise across all levels of baseball, representing one of the most common and debilitating injuries among pitchers.5,7 Although the literature has well-established velocity as a risk factor for UCL injury,1,4,6 both traditionalists and Level V expertise have expressed concern regarding a potential association between breaking pitches and UCL injury.6 However, the relationship between breaking pitches and UCL injury remains unclear, and traditional explanations lack scientific validation. Emerging literature has demonstrated the importance of flexor-pronator function on UCL injury; however, little is known about how functional forearm position affects flexor muscle strength during the overhead delivery of off-speed breaking pitches.2,3,8 The purpose of this study was to evaluate overhead flexor-pronator strength in pronated and supinated forearm positions within an elite baseball cohort.Methods:Twenty-eight collegiate and professional baseball pitchers were recruited from a single offseason training facility. Age and handedness were recorded, and the players were surveyed for a history of elbow injury as well as their subjective pitch bias (natural supinator or pronator). The pitchers underwent finger flexor strength testing utilizing a validated flexor-pronator instrument equipped with digital load cells for force assessment. Two-finger flexor digitorum profundus (FDP) testing was performed with the forearm in a pronated position to simulate a fastball delivery and in a supinated position to simulate breaking ball delivery. The pitchers were randomized using a random number generator to determine the starting position for testing. All testing was performed while standing with the shoulder abducted and externally rotated with the elbow in 60 degrees of flexion to simulate the acceleration phase of the overhead delivery (Figure 1). For each repetition, participants were instructed to produce maximal finger flexion force for at least three seconds. The peak combined force of the index and long fingers within this window was recorded. Force output was measured using a device equipped with potentiometers, which generate consistent readings in standardized proprietary units. Two repetitions were performed and averaged before proceeding to the second condition. Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test was applied to determine differences in strength between the supinated and pronated positions. A paired t-test with Bonferroni correction was performed to determine if there were differences in supinated and pronated fingertip flexion strength based on natural pitch bias. A Mann-Whitney U Test was performed to determine if a difference in positional finger flexion strength is based on injury history.Results:Pitchers demonstrated a decreased mean peak fingertip flexion strength in supination compared to pronation (127.6 ± 23.1 vs. 133.9 ± 25.5, p < 0.001). In subanalysis, pronation-biased pitchers demonstrated a significant decrease in fingertip flexion strength in a supinated position (126.1 ± 29.8 vs. 137.7 ± 31.3, p < 0.001). In the pitchers with a supinated pitch bias, there was no difference in fingertip flexion strength (128.6 ± 16.9 vs. 130.4 ± 20.4, p = 0.66). Players with a history of elbow injury demonstrated no difference in fingertip flexion strength compared to the players without an injury history (mean difference: 2.4 ± 16.1 vs. 8.8 ± 10.1, p = 0.32).Conclusions:This study demonstrated that pronation-biased pitchers exhibited decreased FDP strength when tested in a supinated forearm position during a simulated overhead delivery, whereas pitchers with a natural supination bias showed no such deficit. These findings suggest that reduced finger flexor strength in supination may increase UCL injury risk among pronation-biased pitchers developing or throwing breaking pitches.
