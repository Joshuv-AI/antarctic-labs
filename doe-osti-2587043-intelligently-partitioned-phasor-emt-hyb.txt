 Executive Summary
 As the penetration level of power electronics-interfaced renewables such as photovoltaics (PV)
 and wind has surged in modern electric grids, new operational risks caused by the dynamics of
 those inverter-based resources (IBRs) are emerging in parallel. Lessons learned from various
 grid events include that the impact of IBRs on system-level grid stability will become prominent
 along with the increase of renewables and that the short-timescale dynamic impacts of IBRs
 on grid stability are not fully captured by current phasor-domain dynamic simulation tools (e.g.,
 PSS/E, PSLF) [1] [2]. For example, IBRs can be controlled to mitigate those destabilizing
 interactions, but phasor-domain tools often cannot capture that; likewise, the existing
 electromagnetic transient (EMT) simulation tools (e.g., PSCAD, EMTP) can simulate detailed
 IBR controls, but for large power systems with many buses and IBRs, slow simulation speeds
 severely impede the ability to study dynamic events [3] [4]. Massively paralleling simulations
 using high-performance computing (HPC) can help address this, especially now that cloud-
 based HPC capability is widely available, but today’s EMT tools are not HPC-compatible, and
 parallelization of dynamic simulation solvers is not trivial because each region is coupled and
 can dynamically affect the others. Thus, dynamic simulation of grids with large numbers of
 buses and IBRs potentially poses a barrier to the ongoing energy transition.
 The research summarized in this report aimed to address that challenge by proving the
 feasibility of an adaptive, integrated phasor-EMT simulation platform (I-PEP) for transient
 dynamic study of large-scale, IBR-dependent power grids. Specifically, we have:
 •  developed and validated a novel phasor-EMT partitioning method;
 •  developed and published ParaEMT, a parallelizable, open-source, and HPC-compatible
 EMT solver compatible with state-of-the-art IBR models [5] [6] [7];
 • demonstrated the ability of ParaEMT in parallel simulation to provide simulation results
 with errors less than 10-2 p.u. on the Kundur two-area system (relative to PSS/E) and
 less than 10-12 p.u (relative to serial simulation of ParaEMT) on the 240-Bus WECC
 system, and achieve an over 25x acceleration of simulation speed on power systems
 with over 10,000 buses, compared with that of serial simulations;
 • improved PNNL’s open-source, HPC-compatible phasor domain simulator, GridPACK
 [8], to include full three-sequence modeling;
 • developed a HELICS interface [9] allowing phasor-EMT (GridPACK-ParaEMT) hybrid
 simulation of large-scale IBR-rich power systems and demonstrated its ability to
 accurately simulate disturbances on a large power system with improved efficiency.
 The simulators that have been developed and demonstrated in this project are compatible with
 HPC and can also run on standard personal computers.
 This project has proven the feasibility of several techniques to accelerate the simulation of
 large, high-IBR power systems. These techniques can now be adopted by others to improve
 and accelerate simulations needed to understand the stability of highly renewable power
 systems. If eventually adopted by industry, these techniques will help enable utility engineers
 to interconnect large amounts of IBRs without sacrificing power system stability and reliability.

 Background
 General background
 The project described in this report explored new techniques for modeling large power systems
 with very high levels of inverter-based resources (IBRs). In particular, we set out to develop an
 platform for phasor-EMT hybrid simulation of high-IBR power systems. We leveraged and
 improved an existing phasor-domain simulator, GridPACK [10], and also developed a new
 electromagnetic transient (EMT) simulator, now called ParaEMT [5], [11]. We integrated state-
 of-the-art IBR models into both simulators [6]. In addition, we developed methods to accelerate
 the EMT solver by parallelizing it across multiple computational cores and by solving some
 dynamic models using semi-analytical solutions [12]. To decide which portions of the system
 under study should be modeled in EMT and which can be modeled in the phasor domain without
 missing dynamics of interest, we developed a participation-factor-based partitioning technique
 [13]. Finally, we developed a phasor-EMT hybrid simulation platform that interfaces GridPACK
 and ParaEMT via the Hierarchical Engine for Large-scale Infrastructure Co-Simulation (HELICS)
 [9]. Accuracy and efficiency of the hybrid simulation platform are demonstrated by case studies
 on a 240-bus model of the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) power system [14].
 Figure 1 shows an overview of the framework for intelligent phasor-EMT partitioning (I-PEP)
 whose feasibility was demonstrated in this project.

 Phasor-domain modeling of power system dynamics
 Phasor-domain modeling of power system dynamics approximates the dynamic response of the
 power system through its fundamental-frequency phasors. The power system network
 components are approximated algebraically by their fundamental frequency impedances. Many
 phasor-domain tools further restrict dynamics to include only the positive-sequence dynamics,
 though some phasor-domain simulators include the positive-, negative-, and zero-sequences.
 Phasor-domain models are widely used to simulate the dynamic responses of power systems to
 disturbances such as losses of generation and loads. This works well when the generation is
 predominantly composed of synchronous machines because the dominant electromechanical
 modes fall below a few Hz [15]. Phasor-domain simulations can simulate very large power
 systems with reasonable simulation time costs (for example, a few seconds or minutes).
 However, phasor domain simulation is known to miss key dynamics as IBRs become more
 prevalent because mode frequencies can significantly exceed a few Hz [16]. Some researchers
 have been attempting to expand the bandwidth of validity of phasor-domain simulations above
 a few Hz by including more detailed dynamic models and shorter simulation time steps; however,
 the fundamental limitations (ignoring waveform dynamics and the differential equations of
 inductive and capacitive elements) restrict the ability of phasor-domain simulations to capture
 higher-frequency effects.
 This project utilized the existing DOE-funded open-source phasor-domain dynamic simulator,
 GridPACK, developed by project partner PNNL. Several improvements to GridPACK were
 developed through this project to support the hybrid simulation objective.
 Electromagnetic transient modeling of power system dynamics
 EMT simulations utilize fundamental three-phase circuit models for the network without
 assumptions on system frequencies and can accurately represent fast and detailed system
 dynamics. To simulate the network generally represented by R-L-C circuits, different approaches
 have been developed in commercial tools to formulate the network equation, including mainly
 the traditional nodal formulation used in [17], the modified nodal formulation utilized in SPICE
 [18], the modified augmented nodal analysis employed in EMTP-RV [19] [20], and the sparse
 tableau formulation adopted in XTAP [21]. The EMT simulator, ParaEMT, which was developed
 by the NREL team [5] [11] and is utilized in this project, employed the conventional nodal
 formulation-based EMT simulation strategy outlined in [17] and integrates the generic IBR model
 detailed in [6] and [7] to emulate IBR-related dynamics. Although multiple commercial tools, such
 as PSCAD, EMTP-RV, XTAP, OPAL-RT, and RTDS, are dominating the EMT simulation, the
 Python-based simulator ParaEMT supplements those tools by providing an open and
 transparent platform for educational and research purposes, and it is the first open-source and
 parallelizable EMT simulator for large-scale systems that can be easily integrated with HPC
 clusters.

 Semi-analytical solution of power system dynamics
 Semi-analytical solution (SAS) methods [12] [22] [23] [24] [25] can find the series-form analytical
 solution of a desired order for each sub-model ahead of time to shift most of the computations
 to an offline stage. Simulation of each model then only needs to evaluate its analytical solution
 in a non-iterative, recursive manner, unlike conventional numerical simulations. SAS methods
 allow larger time steps under the same level of accuracy to accelerate simulations, and they can

 6

 accommodate stochastic variables efficiently with any dynamic models thanks to the analytical
 nature of the solutions. In recent years, SAS methods have been proposed to speed up
 electromechanical transient stability simulations [12] [22] [23] [24] [25]. In this project, we
 investigated a technique in which the network is solved by a nodal equation, and an SAS method
 is applied to the models of synchronous generators (SGs) and controls to improve the simulation
 efficiency of EMT simulations using a larger time step.

 Inverter-based resource dynamic modeling

 Lately high penetration of IBRs poses challenges for system studies as IBRs do not have same
 inherent properties as synchronous machines. This implies that studies require new models to
 represent IBRs in any simulation platform [26]. In addition, if the model represents a generic
 inverter compliant with a certain grid interconnection standard such as IEEE 2800-2022 [27], the
 control structure of these models should comply with that standard as well. EPRI developed a
 generic IBR model for implementation in EMT simulators [28]. EPRI’s generic EMT IBR model
 (developed for a variety of EMT software) used for this project conforms to the IEEE 2800-2022
 standard. To allow for interoperability of this model across multiple EMT software, a code-based
 model was created [7]. As this project requires a hybrid simulation of EMT and phasor-domain
 software, a phasor-domain based model of the IBR using generic renewable energy system
 models is also developed [6] [29]. This phasor-domain IBR model is benchmarked against the
 EMT IBR model.

 Partitioning of power system dynamic models for hybrid simulation
 First attempts on partitioning of power system dynamics between phasor and EMT zones were
 proposed in 1981 [30], to accurately model HVDC converters with EMT models while
 representing the AC network with phasor models. An HVDC converter has non-linear dynamics
 and requires elemental waveform representation of the components with time steps of around
 50-100 µs, while the network can be modeled accurately with time steps of 10 to 50 ms. As
 discussed in the work of Reeve and Adapa in 1988 [31], only modeling the HVDC converter in
 EMT can yield inaccurate results, so including buses adjacent to the converters can improve
 accuracy in the case of significant harmonic distortion in these AC buses. The fundamental issue
 of partitioning is well described by Professor Reeve in his 1988 work:
 “The identification of a detailed system is completely dependent on the system
 configuration and the disturbance under study. For example, to simulate a dc line fault
 and/or an ac fault adjacent to the dc system, the dc system has to be modeled in detail,
 and the amount of ac system to be included for detailed study depends on basically two
 factors:
 (i) waveform distortion at the interface buses
 (ii) phase imbalance at the interface buses”
 That highlights the major challenge for deciding partitioning zones, which is that the optimal
 partitioning that will accurately capture the dynamics of interest without slowing down the
 simulation unnecessarily has high dependence on specific system characteristics and is
 complicated to generalize without detailed knowledge of the specific operating conditions.
 Conventional approaches for partitioning systems between phasor and EMT zones are based
 on short-circuit ratio (SCR). The SCR is a metric used to evaluate the strength of a system, or

 7

 specific buses, by considering the ratio of the short circuit current against the inverter capacity
 at that specific bus:

 (1) where SCMVAi is the short circuit MVA

 level at bus i, usually from a specified three-phase fault, and MVAinv is the nominal rating of the
 IBRs at that bus. This metric is commonly used to quantify system strength. A low SCR bus
 indicates a weak area with high sensitivity to voltage, that makes it more desirable to consider
 modeling using an EMT approach to capture with more detailed phenomena occurring near
 these regions, ensuring that no instability modes exist at larger frequencies [32]. The SCR
 partitioning approach quantifies the SCR for each bus with IBRs and rank them from the lowest
 to the highest to decide specific EMT zones relevant to the specific system. By specifying SCR
 thresholds, different phasor/EMT zones are obtained. However, SCR-based methods become
 less useful as more IBRs are connected close together so that their dynamics impact each
 other, and SCR-based methods (at least as conventionally formulated) are unable to account
 for the benefits of grid-forming controls. In an IBR-rich power grid, where numerous inverters
 are connected through the power grid, the partitioning of the grid model into EMT and phasor
 zones is of great importance for hybrid simulations. The phasor-EMT partitioning should
 minimize the portion of the system represented by EMT models to only the components that
 have a substantial impact on the system's dynamics so as to significantly reduce the
 computational complexity.
 Participation factors (PFs) are important indices to evaluate how individual components of a
 dynamical system participate in its dynamic modes, and have found widespread applications in
 diverse domains, including electrical power systems [13]. For instance, evaluating and ranking
 the PFs for the components of a power grid can help reduce the grid model to focus more on
 highly participating components in order to accelerate power system simulations [33]. PFs can
 be estimated from both the model and responses of the grid. In this project, we developed a
 novel PF-based approach for partitioning the grid model into phasor and EMT zones.
 Power system dynamic hybrid simulation
 The phasor-domain dynamic simulation module only includes the positive-sequence network
 topologies in almost all commercial simulation tools. Several recent works started to model the
 negative- and zero- sequence power grid networks into the dynamic simulation [34] [35] [36].
 With the sequential components modeled in the transmission system, unbalanced faults can be
 simulated and more accurate hybrid simulation between the phasor and EMT domains can be
 developed.
 To combine the modeling techniques in those publications and the high-performance computing
 capability, this project team leveraged the modeling details from those references and developed
 sequential components models and the corresponding simulation capability in GridPACK, which
 is featured with high-performance computation and parallel simulation. With the developed
 capability, GridPACK incorporated a prototype of models that represented the zero- and
 negative- sequence impedances and admittances of transmission branches.
 Previously, the GridPACK was a standalone transmission-level dynamic simulation tool [8]. To
 enhance its features suitable for hybrid simulation and sensitivity analyses, the team improved
 GridPACK by adding the state perturbation functions for generators to facilitate the system
 sensitivity and partitioning analysis, interfacing external black-box models through dynamic

 8

 linked library (DLL) file, and developing an interface between GridPACK and HELICS to allow
 on-line data exchange.

 Project Objectives
 The objective of this project was to prove the feasibility of several techniques that can be used
 to accelerate the simulation of large-scale, IBR-dependent power systems with over hundreds
 of buses, while preserving the ability of the simulation to accurately capture the dynamics that
 are important to power system stability.

 Project Results and Discussion
 Overview

 To achieve the above objectives, the team focused on the following main technical tasks:
 1. Addition of three-sequence simulation capabilities to GridPACK.
 2. Development of the parallelizable EMT simulator, ParaEMT.
 3. Development of semi-analytical solutions for EMT simulations.
 4. Integration and validation of IBR models into ParaEMT and GridPACK.
 5. Development and validation of methods to decide where to partition a simulation between
 EMT and phasor zones.
 6. Development, implementation, and validation of a phasor-EMT hybrid simulation
 platform.
 Three-sequence phasor-domain modeling of power system dynamics

 Development of sequential components in GridPACK
 To enable GridPACK to simulate unbalanced power systems, we developed and tested three-
 sequence modeling and simulation capability in GridPACK. The entire development included the
 below efforts:
 • Implemented a data parser to import sequential data from PSS/E .seq file into the dynamic
 simulation application of GridPACK.
 • Implemented component-level (e.g., bus and branch) contributions to the diagonal and
 off-diagonal elements of positive-, negative- and zero- sequence Y matrices.
 • Developed an equivalent matrix module to calculate the equivalent impedance (or

 equivalent impedances for three-sequence networks seen from the fault bus.

 Additions to GridPACK to support system sensitivity analysis
 The goal of this effort was to implement a functionality in GridPACK to support system dynamic
 sensitivity analysis, which can further be used to decide partitioning of the system for hybrid
 simulation. This entailed perturbing a dynamic state, running a dynamic simulation, and
 observing the needed dynamic states. The perturbation was applied after initializing the dynamic
 simulation. Such a functionality did not exist in GridPACK before and was implemented for this
 project by making the following additions/modifications to the GridPACK code [8] [10]:
 • A capability for reading and modifying dynamic states of generators, governors, exciters, and
 IBRs (REGCA1, REPCA1, REECA1) was added to the dynamic simulation module. This
 functionality can also retrieve current states. There were two methods/functions added to
 GridPACK for this capability:
 o getState – Retrieves the value of a dynamic state for a particular device (generator,
 exciter, governor, IBR)
 o setState – Sets the value of a dynamic state for a particular device
 The getState and setState functionalities together can be used to perturb any state of the
 given dynamic device by a certain amount, by first retrieving the state value (getState) and
 then setting the perturbed value (setState).
 • Python wrapper functions for this capability were added to GridPACK.

 Parallelized electromagnetic transient modeling of power system dynamics
 A typical EMT simulation is an iteration between two major steps: network solution and device
 update. The work in this project covers the parallelization of both steps.
 It is widely recognized that a significant amount of time cost, typically up to 80%–97% for large
 systems, in EMT simulations is consumed by solving the network nodal equation [37].
 Traditionally, the sparse LU factorization of the network matrix has been used to solve the
 network nodal voltage vector through forward and backward substitutions; however, such a
 sequential approach is not easily parallelizable, leading to a bottleneck that limits the speed of
 EMT simulations. Conventionally, the parallelization of EMT simulations relied on exploiting the
 natural decoupling between subnetworks that arises from the traveling wave propagation delay
 across long-distance distributed parameter transmission lines [38] [39] and cables in a manual
 way [40] [41]. However, the performance of this approach is constrained by the number, location,
 and length of transmission lines, and it becomes infeasible when the delay is too small [42] [43].
 An alternative, which does not rely on the time delay of lines, is to automatically reformulate the
 network conductance matrix into a bordered block diagonal (BBD) form. With this form, the
 computations associated with N-1 out of the total N block matrices become completely
 decoupled and, therefore, can be naturally parallelized.
 Except parallelization of the network solution, parallelization of device updates is also helpful to
 reduce the overall simulation time cost. The device updates include mainly updating device
 current injections, updating device states, and updating branch historical currents. Those critical

 13

 simulation tasks are naturally independent and thus can be effortlessly parallelized to further
 improve the computational efficiency.
 To speed up EMT simulations, the NREL in-house Python-based EMT simulator, ParaEMT,
 which the project team started to develop under a laboratory-directed research and development
 (LDRD) project at NREL, has incorporated parallel computation to take advantage of the
 abundant computational resources that are available on HPCs. NREL’s Eagle supercomputer
 was the HPC used in this project. Note that ParaEMT is not limited to run on HPCs and can also
 run on standard laptop and desktop computers.

 Through the utilization of the BBD form-based network parallel computation, ParaEMT has

 parallelization is limited in many proprietary software packages.
 Additionally, the computational complexity of the BBD-based solver, as a special case of sparse
 LU-based solver, highly depends on the sparsity of the resulting factorized LU matrices, i.e., the
 number of non-zero matrix elements. The sparser these matrices are, the less complex the
 computation will be. It is noticed that the LU factors of the BBD matrix contain many non-zeros
 which are small in magnitude, particularly in the LU factors of the corner block. To increase
 simulation speed, ParaEMT can drop those non-zeros with magnitudes below a pre-defined
 threshold. It has been demonstrated that this introduces negligibly small errors into the
 simulation but can significantly decrease the time cost for the network solution.
 (2) Parallelization for the device updates.
 In addition to leveraging both shared and distributed compute nodes to accelerate EMT
 simulations for the network solution, ParaEMT is developed to further improve computational
 efficiency by effortlessly parallelizing natural independent tasks, including critical simulation

 15

 steps such as updating device current injections, updating device states, and updating branch
 historical currents across components, as marked by aqua boxes in Figure 8.
 ParaEMT is a Python-based EMT simulator. In addition to the parallel simulation strategies
 adopted in this project for acceleration, several other techniques have also been applied to
 improve the efficiency of the ParaEMT code, including the vectorization in Python by eliminating
 loops, use of Python 3.7 vs. 2.7, and just-in-time (JIT) compilation by Numba that translates part
 of ParaEMT into fast machine code. All of these can be synthesized to provide a combined
 acceleration effect.
 (3) Implementation and acceleration on HPC
 In this task, the parallelized EMT solver is implemented on Eagle, NREL’s HPC system [45], to
 accelerate the simulations.
 While the block LU factorization using the Schur complement as well as the forward and
 backward substitutions described in [44] were implemented in ParaEMT, the work in [44]
 explored only shared memory parallelism on a single machine. In comparison, a distributed
 memory paradigm using Message Passing Interface (MPI) [46] is exploited for EMT simulation
 on HPC in this project. This paradigm based on MPI allows computations to be distributed across
 a computer network while necessitating passing information between separate processes, i.e.,
 MPI ranks. Particularly, this design enabled us to use multiple compute nodes on a high-
 performance supercomputer.
 Specifically, the BBD matrix blocks of the network G matrix are distributed to MPI ranks in a
 round-robin fashion [47] and each LU factorization is computed in parallel. This is done before
 the time loop, and the factors are reused for each parallel forward and backward substitution
 phase.
 To access MPI in Python, the mpi4py package is utilized [48]. This package wraps an existing
 MPI library and enables the passing of arbitrary Python objects across MPI ranks. For computing
 the LU factors and block matrix solutions in the algorithm, the SuperLU solver [49] is
 implemented using the SciPy package [50].
 To exploit parallel simulation of device updates, the devices and branches are divided into
 blocks, and each MPI rank is assigned with one. The blocks are created to be as equal in size
 as possible for better performance. Each MPI rank computes the states and currents for the
 devices and branches in its assigned blocks. Immediately before solving the network nodal
 voltages, the current injections of devices and historical currents of branches are synchronized
 across all MPI ranks.

 Semi-analytical solution of power system dynamics
 The semi-analytical solution (SAS) approach has been successfully applied to the fast time-
 domain simulation of large-scale grids in the phasor domain in the past [12] [22] [23] [24] [25].
 This task applied a high-order SAS to the acceleration of EMT simulations that allow longer time
   steps [53].
 Unlike a traditional numerical simulation approach, the SAS-based approach decomposes
 computation tasks needed by a simulation into two stages as illustrated by Figure 15: Stage 1
 mathematically derives an approximate, analytical solution named an SAS, which retains
 accuracy for a long time interval thanks to its high-order approximation [54]; stage 2, which is
 the simulation stage, simply plugs values on the system condition and contingency into symbolic
 variables of the SAS over consecutive intervals until making up the desired simulation period
 [55]. Stage 1 is before simulation and will be independent of details of the contingency so that
 significant computations for the simulation can be finished ahead of time. During the simulation,
 the SAS will be evaluated recursively at long time intervals to provide simulation results like
 conventional EMT simulation. To derive such an SAS, a variety of mathematical methods can
 be applied, including the Adomian Decomposition, Pade Approximation, Continued Fractions,
 Holomorphic Embedding, and Differential Transformation [12] [22] [23] [24] [25].

 EMT solver acceleration by semi-analytical simulation methods
 Compared with ODE (ordinary differential equations) or DAE (differential algebraic equations)
 based power system phasor domain simulations [12], the EMT simulation is mainly conducted
 using the nodal approach, because it is more efficient for large systems. The first-order ODEs
 of fundamental grid components including resistors, inductors, and capacitors are discretized by
 the trapezoidal rule method to form the grid nodal voltage equations, and other components

 20

 including synchronous generators, exciters, governors, turbines, and stabilizers are represented

 Interfacing EPRI IBR model with GridPACK
 EPRI’s positive sequence (EPRIA1) IBR model was interfaced with GridPACK in this project.
 The goal of this endeavor was to “interface” the model, as opposed to “implement” the model in
 GridPACK. “Interfacing” here means that the model is written as a stand-alone code (user-
 defined model) that is linked with GridPACK at run-time via a dynamic linked library. Hitherto, all
 the existing dynamic models in GridPACK are native, i.e., their code is implemented in GridPACK
 and distributed as a part of GridPACK. So, the EPRIA1 model is the first instance of a user-
 defined model interfaced with GridPACK. The following modifications and additions were made
 to GridPACK code to link to this model:
 • A parser in GridPACK was written to read in the parameters of EPRIA1 given in the dyr
 file.
 • A new model named EPRIA1 was added to GridPACK. This model acts as a
 container/wrapper/shell for the EPRIA1 user-defined model. The EPRIA1 model in GridPACK
 does not do any numerical integration or any model evaluation itself, but rather calls the
 EPRIA1 shared library code at each time-instant for it to do the necessary calculations.
 • Since the EPRIA1 user-defined model is a stand-alone code, we modified GridPACK’s
 build system to compile the EPRIA1 GridPACK code. This addition allows GridPACK to be
 built even if the user does not have the EPRIA1 model available.

 phasor model or the EMT model of the grid. Here, the simulation period only needs to
 cover 2 to 3 oscillation cycles of the slowest mode of interest so as to estimate the PFs
 from the system response. For each mode, estimate linear or nonlinear PFs of each IBR
 and generator using both model and response-based methods. Response-based PFs
 estimated directly from the simulation results can complement the model-based PF
 estimation when the state-space EMT/phasor model of the grid is not available.
 3) According to the estimated PFs, decide whether the EMT model of each IBR needs to be
 retained or can be reduced to a phasor model in the final grid model for fast simulation,
 or in other words, determine the boundaries between EMT models and phasor models
 for the entire grid. Only those IBRs, generators and buses having PFs exceeding a
 threshold are necessarily represented in the EMT domain; otherwise, a nonlinear phasor
 model or a reduced phasor model will be used.
 This approach enables adaptive, intelligent partitioning and results in a speed-optimized hybrid
 phasor-EMT simulation that captures the relevant dynamics. When two adjacent regions are
 modeled in different domains, their interface model uses some techniques to preserve the
 dynamics of interest. The resulting partitioned phasor-EMT model will compute more rapidly
 because its internal dynamic models will include only the level of detail necessary to capture the
 dynamics of interest. A schematic flowchart of the designed PF-based approach for phasor-EMT
 partitioning is illustrated in Figure 35.

 In this project, we have developed a PF-based phasor-EMT partitioning approach to determine
 the boundary between models of the phasor and EMT domains. The approach can identify the
 minimum EMT zone required for hybrid simulations to accurately capture the dynamics of
 interest, such as electromechanical oscillations of generators and SSOs involving IBRs. We
 have designed and tested both model-based and response-based PF estimation methods to aid
 in boundary determination. The partitioning approach has undergone thorough validations on a
 small two-area system and has also been demonstrated on the 240-bus WECC system,
 considering SSO scenarios involving IBRs in the California area.
 More information on the partitioning approach developed under this project is available in [61].
 In addition, as a comparison between different types of partitioning, the team quantified the
 computation time between the SCR-based and PF-based partitioning approaches, by running a
 pseudo-co-simulation using a full EMT simulation to record the boundary current between the
 phasor and EMT zones. The recorded boundary bus data are used as external inputs for the
 EMT zone. The SCR cases for the 240-bus WECC system are:
 • SCR-Case 1: Lowest SCR at bus 2332, with boundary bus 2302, was considered as a
 single EMT zone, while the rest of the system is modeled in phasor.
 • SCR-Case 2: We select a threshold SCR of 7.0, that yields only 4 buses (2332, 3433,
 2533 and 3932) with SCRs lower than this threshold. These buses (and their boundary
 buses) are considered as EMT zones, while the rest of the system is modeled in the
 phasor domain.
 • SCR-Case 3: Given that most of the lowest SCRs are observed in the California region,
 we considered the entirety of California as an EMT zone. In the 240-bus WECC system,
 California is represented by 140 buses, and is connected to 9 boundary buses via 16
 lines.
 The results for a 60-s simulation with different partitioning cases between a full simulation and
 the EMT zones demonstrated speedup of 1.85 to 2.95 times in the above three cases
 The acceleration depends mostly on the size of EMT zone, given by the number of buses and
 devices included in that zone. The SCR-Case 1 only has a single device in its EMT zone that
 allows significant acceleration in comparison to the full simulation. Moreover, the greater number
 of boundary buses and buses inside the EMT zone implies two major slowdowns:

 44

 • In a full simulation, more signals in the boundary buses, such as voltages and currents,
 are required to be processed, recorded, and stored, that implies longer clock times.
 • In an EMT zone simulation, more boundary buses imply more computational time to
 transform phasor signals (from the phasor zone) into waveforms to connect boundary
 buses and the EMT zone, while more buses inside the EMT zone takes longer to compute
 the integration step.
 Initial results showcase how simulating the entire California via SCR-Case 3 is the one that
 results in less acceleration than the other cases. Given that this case is the largest EMT zone
 representing 140 of the 240 buses, with the most IBR resources, there is not enough
 improvements in comparison to the full simulation scenario, resulting in an acceleration of only
 1.85 times. Note that the above analyses are mainly for conceptual study using pseudo-co-
 simulations, and the accuracy and efficiency in real hybrid simulations remained to be tested in
 other tasks described below.

 Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
 Accomplishment #1: Published open-source, HPC compatible EMT simulator
 To benefit the entire power system community including both academia and industry, ParaEMT,
 an open-source, parallelizable, and HPC-compatible EMT simulator for large-scale IBR-rich
 power grids, is now publicly accessible on GitHub. To help more users better understand and
 use ParaEMT, we provided a detailed introduction and guidance. A summary is given as follows.
 (1) Main features of ParaEMT:

 49

 The initial motivation for developing ParaEMT is to provide a flexible environment for conducting
 EMT simulations of large-scale bulk power systems with high integration levels of IBRs. On the
 way to achieve this target, ParaEMT was developed with the following main features:
 a) Fully open source and transparent: Allows unrestricted access to the underlying source
 code and encourages active engagement and contributions from the community.
 b) User-friendly: Offers ease of use, readability, and efficient execution on personal
 computers for small to medium-sized power grids using the just-in-time compiler in
 Python. For example, under series (non-parallelized) simulation, it can run much faster
 than PSCAD on the 240-bus WECC system, as shown in the table below.
 Table XV: Time cost on the WECC 240-bus system for a 1-s simulation
 Simulator Series simulation
 PSCAD 90 s
 ParaEMT 29 s
 c) Cross-platform compatibility: Able to operate on most prevalent operating systems,
 including Unix, Linux, Windows, and macOS.
 d) Automated parallel computation: Provides fully automated parallel computation for
 solving network equations, which accelerates the simulations.
 e) IEEE/Cigre power system DLL compatibility: Seamlessly incorporates black-box
 component dynamic models developed by third parties. One case study is shown below.
 ParaEMT incorporated a C-compiled DLL file containing a grid-following IBR model from
 EPRI [7]. The analysis is conducted on a small three-bus test case containing an infinite

 f) Intelligent initialization: Automatically builds and initializes EMT cases from predefined
 PSSE power flow files to avoid reaching abnormal operating modes.
 g) Results down-sampling capability: Reduces computer memory requirements for storage
 of simulation results while maintaining data integrity.

 h) Snapshot functionality: Enables users to save simulation progress as a snapshot file and
 resume the simulation from that point, thereby facilitating various disturbance simulations
 starting from one converged steady state.
 i) Allows continuous development to incorporate additional models and functions,

 Accomplishment #2: Added functionality in GridPACK
 In this project, the team has developed negative- and zero- sequence network models in PNNL’s
 GridPACK software and integrated the network models with dynamic simulation application to
 enable the simulation of unbalanced faults in transmission systems. We also developed scripts
 to generate power flow and dynamic models for the phasor zones of the hybrid simulation
 systems, using the Kundur 2-area system and 240-bus WECC system. We also implemented a
 pseudo hybrid simulation platform to link GridPACK with pseudo EMT simulator through
 HELICS. This pseudo hybrid simulation platform allowed the time-varying values from the
 pseudo EMT simulator to be passed to GridPACK through HELICS, and served as a starting
 point to establish the full GridPACK-HELICS-ParaEMT hybrid simulation. Another new function
 was added in the GridPACK to obtain and perturb state variables of generator models, which

 53

 facilitates the capability of sensitivity analysis using GridPACK. Besides, an interface was
 developed in GridPACK to link EPRI’s black-box IBR model through a pre-compiled DLL file.
 This feature enables the integration of intellectual property protected IBR or dynamic control
 models into GridPACK without needing to supply the model source code to the development
 team.

 Accomplishment #3: Developed novel phasor-EMT partitioning method
 This project developed a novel method for deciding how to partition dynamics models between
 phasor and EMT zones to preserve the system dynamics of interest. The method is based on
 participation factors that can be calculated from the model itself (in the case of transparent
 models) or from dynamic simulation data. The latter capability is important because it can be
 applied even to black-box models (which are often all that is available for real-world IBR
 installations).

 Accomplishment #4: Demonstrated phasor-EMT hybrid simulation on a 240-bus system
 with only open-source software
 The team developed a phasor-EMT simulation platform leveraging open-source tools, including
 NREL’s ParaEMT, PNNL’s GridPACK, and also HELICS. This hybrid simulation platform was
 demonstrated to capture IBR-driven SSO dynamics that were not correctly captured in a phasor-
 domain simulator. Hence this platform could be useful for understanding the dynamics of large,
 high-IBR power systems in tractable simulation times.

 Budget and Schedule
 The total planned project budget was $1.5 million, and the total amount spent was also $1.5
 million. No cost share was planned or provided. The total planned project period was 24
 months. A three-month no-cost time extension was granted to allow the team to finalize the
 hybrid simulation given that two key contributors had changed organizations during the project,
 so the total project period lasted 27 months. The project started October 1, 2021, and ended
   December 31, 2023.

 Path Forward
 This project has proven the feasibility of several techniques to accelerate the dynamic simulation
 of large power systems with high levels of IBRs. This acceleration is achieved while preserving
 dynamics that are important to system stability. The techniques and tools developed under this
 project can be used by researchers, but likely require more refinement before they are ready to
 be used by industry engineers. The team is actively exploring avenues to bring the tools and
 techniques developed in this project to higher technology readiness levels. For example,
 ParaEMT would benefit from development of a larger library of dynamic models, development
 of additional documentation, and addition of a graphical user interface, among other
 improvements.
 Meanwhile, the key simulation engines used and developed in this project are available to the
 public at no cost, and hence can be adopted and used in their current states by anyone who
 finds them useful. Several researchers and industry engineers have expressed interest in
 ParaEMT. Integrating the tools with other simulation tools used by industry is an additional path
 to wider usage.

 54

 Interest is rising in cloud-based simulation tools. Both ParaEMT and GridPACK are candidates
 for cloud-based implementation. This would allow users to leverage commercially available
 cloud computing resources to simulate very large power systems in less time.
 To better support industry applications, the demonstrations conducted on the 240-bus WECC
 system in this project need to be further extended and investigated on large utility-scale systems
 with over thousands of buses, in which multiple EMT zones can be selected, making acceleration
 via parallel computations more promising, as demonstrated using artificially generated 10,000-
 bus systems in this project.
 In addition to accelerating the simulation speed, another important future application of the
 developed platform is helping investigate real-world system wide events. Replaying and
 analyzing real-world SSOs and other events can help utilities better schedule and improve
 system planning, and hence enhance the system stability.
 In addition, interest is also rising in the use of dynamic simulations in power systems operations
 applications (as opposed to planning applications, where dynamic simulation is more common
 today). For operations applications, simulation time is even more important than in planning; in
 fact, near real-time or even faster than real-time simulation speeds are often desired. Thus, it is
 likely that the acceleration techniques developed here will prove useful for such applications.
