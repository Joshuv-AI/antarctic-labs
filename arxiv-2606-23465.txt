Boolean degree one functions on the Grassmann scheme
Yuval Filmus

Abstract
Ferdinand Ihringer proved that Boolean degree one functions on the Grassmann scheme J q ​ ( n , k ) J_{q}(n,k) are trivial when min ⁡ ( k , n − k ) ≥ 2 \min(k,n-k)\geq 2 and n n is large enough. We provide a mostly self-contained exposition of this result.



1
Introduction

The Grassmann scheme
J_{q}(n,k)
consists of all
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. A function
f\colon J_{q}(n,k)\to\mathbb{R}
is
Boolean
if
f(V)\in\{0,1\}
for all
V\in J_{q}(n,k)
. It has
degree one
if it can be written as

f=\sum_{p}c_{p}x_{p},

where the sum goes over all points (one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
), and
x_{p}\in\{0,1\}
is the indicator of the input containing
p
. We could also allow a constant term, but this is unnecessary since

\sum_{p}x_{p}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{1}_{q}

over
J_{q}(n,k)
. (On the right-hand side we have a
q
-binomial coefficient, which in this case equals
q^{k-1}+\cdots+1
.)

Which degree one functions on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
are Boolean?

The obvious examples are
0,1,x_{p},1-x_{p}
. A less obvious example is the function
y_{r}\in\{0,1\}
, which is the indicator of the input being
orthogonal
to
r
:

y_{r}=1-\left(\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k}{1}_{q}-\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{k-1}{1}_{q}\right)^{-1}\sum_{p\not\perp r}x_{p}.

Incidentally, this shows that the property of having degree one is preserved by the duality of
J_{q}(n,k)
and
J_{q}(n,n-k)
. Given a function
f
on
J_{q}(n,k)
, we can define its dual
f^{\perp}
on
J_{q}(n,n-k)
by
f^{\perp}(V)=f(V^{\perp})
. Since
x_{p}^{\perp}=y_{p}
, it follows that
f
has degree one iff
f^{\perp}
has degree one.

We can also combine
x_{p}
and
y_{r}
if they do not “conflict”. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

A Boolean degree one function on
J_{q}(n,k)
is
trivial
if it belongs to the following list:

0,1,x_{p},1-x_{p},y_{r},1-y_{r},x_{p}+y_{r},1-x_{p}-y_{r},

where in the latter two cases,
p\not\perp r
.

We say that
J_{q}(n,k)
is
degree-one trivial (DOT)
if all Boolean degree one functions on
J_{q}(n,k)
are trivial.

If
k=1
then every function has degree one, and so in general
J_{q}(n,k)
is not DOT (unless
n
is very small). By duality, the same holds when
k=n-1
. When
q=2
, these are the only problematic cases: Filmus and Ihringer
[
FI19
]
showed that
J_{q}(n,k)
is DOT whenever
\min(k,n-k)\geq 2
.

Surprisingly, when
q>2
, there are Boolean degree one functions on
J_{q}(4,2)
which are not trivial. Here is an example for
q=3
, due to Bruen and Drudge
[
BD99
, Corollary 2.1]
.

1.1
Background

The study of Boolean degree one functions on the Grassmann scheme was initiated in a paper of Cameron and Liebler
[
CL82
]
on
J_{q}(4,2)
, and for this reason such functions are known as
Cameron–Liebler line classes
. A blog post by Ferdinand Ihringer
[
Ihr18
]
describes many other interpretations of the same concept.

Boolean degree one functions on various domains also arise naturally in Erdős–Ko–Rado (EKR) theory. The
Johnson scheme

J(n,k)
is the set of all subsets of
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
of size
k
. The classical EKR theorem
[
EKR61
]
states that if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq J(n,k)
is
intersecting
(any two sets in
\mathcal{F}
intersect) and
k\leq n/2
, then
|\mathcal{F}|\leq\binom{n-1}{k-1}
. This is known as the
EKR property
for
J(n,k)
. If furthermore
k<n/2
, then the only families achieving the bound are the
stars

\mathcal{F}_{i}=\{S:i\in S\}
, which is known as the
strict EKR property
.

There are many ways to prove the EKR theorem. Lovász
[
Lov79
]
gave a spectral proof based on his celebrated theta function. When
k<n/2
, the proof shows that every intersecting family of maximum size is in the linear span of the stars (identifying a family with its characteristic function), which is known as the
EKR module property
. Equivalently, every such intersecting family is a Boolean degree one function. Since the only degree one functions on the Johnson scheme (when
\min(k,n-k)\geq 2
) are
0,1,x_{i},1-x_{i}
, it follows that such intersecting families are stars.

The EKR theorem has been extended to various other domains, including the Grassmann scheme
[
FW86
]
. In many cases, including that of the Grassmann scheme, the only known proof is spectral, and the proof implies the EKR module property; see the monograph
[
GM16
]
for a thorough study. Understanding the extremal families thus reduces to understanding Boolean degree one functions on these various domains. Godsil and Meagher
[
GM09
]
developed a specialized technique to this end, which only applies to intersecting families.

In the case of
t
-intersecting families (every two objects in the family have at least
t
points in common), the spectral approach shows that extremal families are Boolean degree
t
functions. Boolean degree
t
functions over the Boolean cube
\{0,1\}^{t}
have a special structure: they depend on
O(2^{t})
coordinates
[
CHS20
,
Wel22
]
, and they can be computed by decision trees of depth
O(t^{3})

[
Mid04
]
. This follows from a well-developed theory of complexity measures
[
Bd02
]
, which has been extended to other domains
[
DFL+21
]
. Unfortunately, the relevant techniques do not seem to extend to the Grassmann scheme.

Isolating the coefficients

The goal of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every prime power
q

such that the following holds for all

If
f\colon J_{q}(n,2)\to\mathbb{R}

f=\sum_{p}c_{p}x_{p},\text{ where }c_{p}\in\left\{-\frac{q-1}{q},0,\frac{1}{q},1\right\}\text{ for all }p,

or the same holds for

The proof proceeds in four steps:

1.

By considering the restrictions of

to planes (three-dimensional subspaces), we show that the coefficients

are

2.

Using the Ramsey theorem for vector spaces, we find a large “reference” subspace

\frac{1}{q+1}
.

3.

For every point
p\notin U
, we use repeated applications of the Ramsey theorem for affine vector spaces to find a subspace of
U
which is “homogeneous with respect to
p

4.

We deduce that
c_{p}
has one of the claimed values.

The most difficult step is the third one.

We start by showing that the coefficients

Lemma 2.2

There exists a finite set

then

for all

.

Proof.

Every function on

has degree one. Indeed, if

then
g^{\perp}\colon J_{q}(3,1)\to\mathbb{R}
. Since

is a delta function on

,

g^{\perp}=\sum_{p\in J_{q}(3,1)}g^{\perp}(p)x_{p},

and so

g=\sum_{p\in J_{q}(3,1)}g(p^{\perp})y_{p}.

Every function

can thus be written in the form

g=\sum_{p\in J_{q}(3,2)}d_{p}x_{p}.

The space of real-valued functions on
J_{q}(3,2)
has dimension
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{3}{2}_{q}
and is spanned by the
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{3}{1}_{q}

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{3}{2}_{q}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{3}{1}_{q}
, we conclude that the functions

form a basis, and so the representation is unique.

There are finitely many Boolean functions on
J_{q}(3,2)
, and consequently, the coefficients

occurring in such functions belong to some finite set

.

We conclude the proof by showing that

Since

Appendix A
Restriction on the Johnson scheme

The Johnson scheme
J(n,k)
consists of all subsets of
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
of size
k
. A function on
J(n,k)
has
degree one
if it can be written as

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i},

where
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
indicates whether the input subset contains
i
.
We could also allow a constant term, but this is not necessary since
\sum_{i}x_{i}=k
on
J(n,k)
.

The analog of
Theorem

1.2
for the Johnson scheme is as follows.

Theorem A.1
.

If
\min(k,n-k)\geq 2
then every Boolean degree one function on
J(n,k)
is of one of the forms
0,1,x_{i},1-x_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}
be a Boolean function on
J(n,k)
.

For every distinct
i,j
we can find a set
S\in J(n,k)
containing
i
but not
j
. Then
f(S)-f(S\triangle\{i,j\})=c_{i}-c_{j}
, showing that
c_{i}-c_{j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
. If
c=\min_{i}c_{i}
, then this shows that
c_{i}\in\{c,c+1\}
for all
i
. Accordingly, we define
\delta_{i}:=c_{i}-c\in\{0,1\}
.

For every distinct
i,j,u,v
we can find a set
S\in J(n,k)
containing
i,j
but not
u,v
. Then
f(S)-f(S\triangle\{i,j,u,v\})=c_{i}+c_{j}-c_{u}-c_{v}=\delta_{i}+\delta_{j}-\delta_{u}-\delta_{v}
. Since the right-hand side belongs to
\{-1,0,1\}
, this rules out the possibility that
\delta_{i}=\delta_{j}=1
and
\delta_{u}=\delta_{v}=0
. Therefore either at most one
\delta_{i}
is equal to
1
, or at most one
\delta_{i}
is equal to
0
(and so exactly one
\delta_{i}
is equal to
0
, by definition of
c
).

If
\delta_{i}=0
for all
i
then
f
is constant, and so either
f=0
or
f=1
.

If
\delta_{i_{0}}=1
and
\delta_{i}=0
for
i\neq i_{0}
then choose a set
S_{0}
not containing
i_{0}
and a set
S_{1}
containing it. Since
f(S_{0})=kc
and
f(S_{1})=kc+1
, we see that
c=0
, and so
f=x_{i_{0}}
.

If
\delta_{i_{0}}=0
and
\delta_{i}=1
for
i\neq i_{0}
then choose a set
S_{0}
not containing
i_{0}
and a set
S_{1}
containing it. Since
f(S_{0})=k(c+1)
and
f(S_{1})=k(c+1)-1
, we see that
c+1=1/k
, and so
f=1-x_{i_{0}}
.
∎

While
Theorem

A.1
is easy to prove directly, we describe in this section an alternative proof based on an analog of
Proposition

4.1
.

Proposition A.2
.

Let
n>k\geq 2
. Suppose that every Boolean degree one function on
J(n,k)
is of one of the forms
0,1,x_{i},1-x_{i}
. Then the same holds for
J(n^{\prime},k)
for all

.

Given this
proposition
, we can conclude the proof as in
Section

4
given an appropriate base case.

The proof of
Proposition

A.2
follows the same steps as the proof of
Proposition

4.1
in
Section

4
. In order to reduce the number of cases, we will replace
0,1,x_{i},1-x_{i}
by
0^{+},0^{-},x_{i}^{+},x_{i}^{-}
, respectively.

The first step is to construct a “restriction table”. Given
S\subseteq[n^{\prime}]
of size
n
and
T\subset S
of size
n-1
, we consider the possible structures of
f|_{T}
given the structure of
f|_{S}
:

\begin{array}[]{c|ll}f|_{S}&f|_{T}\\
\hline\cr 0^{\pm}&0^{\pm}\\
x_{i}^{\pm}&0^{\pm}\text{ if }i\notin T,&x_{i}^{\pm}\text{ if }i\in T\end{array}

The graph on
J(n^{\prime},n)
in which we connect two sets
S,S^{\prime}
if their intersect has size
n-1
is known as the
Johnson graph

G(n^{\prime},n)
, and it is always connected.

The restriction table implies that if
S\sim S^{\prime}
and
f|_{S}
is positive (of the form
0^{+}
or
x_{i}^{+}
) then so are
f|_{S\cap S^{\prime}}
and
f|_{S^{\prime}}
. Since the Johnson graph is connected, we conclude that either all
f|_{S}
are positive, or all of them are negative. Without loss of generality, assume that all of them are positive.

If
f|_{S}=0
for all
S\in J(n^{\prime},n)
then
f=0
.

Next, suppose that
f|_{S_{0}}=x_{i_{0}}
for some
S_{0}
. Consider the subgraph
G_{i_{0}}
of
J(n^{\prime},n)
consisting of all sets containing
{i_{0}}
, which is a copy of the Johnson graph
G(n^{\prime}-1,n-1)
. The restriction table implies that if
S\stackrel{{\scriptstyle G_{i}}}{{\sim}}S^{\prime}
and
f|_{S}=x_{i_{0}}
then
f|_{S\cap S^{\prime}}=x_{i_{0}}
and hence also
f|_{S^{\prime}}=x_{i_{0}}
. Since
G_{i_{0}}
is connected and
f|_{S_{0}}=x_{i_{0}}
, we conclude that
f|_{S}=x_{i_{0}}
whenever
i_{0}\in S
.

The foregoing implies that
f|_{S}\in\{0,x_{i_{0}}\}
for all
S
. Indeed, suppose that
f|_{S_{1}}=x_{i_{1}}
for some
i_{1}\neq i_{0}
. Then
f|_{S}=x_{i_{1}}
whenever
i_{1}\in S
. Since

, we can find a set
S
containing both
i_{0}
and

. Then
f|_{S}=x_{i_{0}}

and
f|_{S}=x_{i_{1}}

Since

The proofs of

Proposition B.1
.

Suppose that the vectors in

are colored using

In this appendix we show how to derive

Proposition B.2

(Hales–Jewett)
.

Suppose that
[k]^{m}
is colored using
c

m\geq N_{\ref{pro:HJ}}(k,c)
, we can find a homogeneous
combinatorial line
: a word
w\in([k]\cup\{*\})^{m}
with at least one star such that the
k
words obtained by replacing all the stars by the same value have the same color.

Proof of
Proposition

B.1
.

Let
m=N_{\ref{pro:HJ}}(q^{r},c)
and
N_{\ref{pro:ramsey}}(q,c,r)=mr
. We will prove the result for
n=mr
; it then follows for larger
n
by considering a copy of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{mr}
inside
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Given a
c
-coloring of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we construct a
c
-coloring of
[q^{r}]^{m}
in the natural way, by interpreting
[q^{r}]
as
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{r}
.
Proposition

B.2
thus gives a homogeneous combinatorial line
w
.

Let
w_{0}
be the word obtained from
w
by replacing the stars with zeroes, and for

let
w_{i}
be obtained from

, where
