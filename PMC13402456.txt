Clozapine for Post-traumatic Stress Disorder: Redefining Treatment Resistance in Soldiers without Psychosis
Authors: Roshan Monaragala, Miyuru Chandradasa, KALA Kuruppuarachchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) often follows a chronic trajectory, with many patients failing to respond to first-line psychotherapy and SSRIs/SNRIs. This case series explores clozapine for treatment-resistant PTSD (TR-PTSD) without psychosis. Seven soldiers showed improved function and reduced aggression at doses of 125–300 mg, suggesting that clozapine warrants further investigation.Keywords: Antipsychotic agents, clozapine, military personnel, stress disorders, post-traumatic, treatment resistance

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), characterized by three core symptoms (i.e., re-experiencing the trauma in the present, deliberate avoidance of things reminiscent of the trauma, and persistent perceptions of heightened current threat), is associated with severe stressors such as combat exposure. It is a serious psychiatric disorder affecting 3.9% globally, reducing the quality of life, and affecting multiple psychosocial domains.1,2 Moreover, PTSD, despite receiving treatment, is shown to have a chronic trajectory, particularly among military veterans, which leads to lasting implications in occupational and social functioning, along with physical and mental health complications.1,2

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are recommended as first-line treatment for PTSD. 3 However, for first-line treatment, as few as 20%–30% achieve full remission, and 30%–40% experience partial response, and augmentation strategies with other types of medications and psychological approaches have yielded mixed results, underscoring the importance of treatment-resistant PTSD (TR-PTSD). 3 Failure to respond to one or more evidence-basedfirst-line treatments (including trauma-focused psychotherapy) despite adequate dosage and duration is considered TR-PTSD.1,2 To date, there is no empirically validated treatment algorithm for TR-PTSD, and we propose clozapine as a treatment option, highlighting our experience with it. Further, there are no previous studies on clozapine use for TR-PTSD in the absence of psychosis.

Case Series

A consultant psychiatrist clinically diagnosed all seven cases shown in Table 1 according to the ICD-11 criteria. Notably, all met the criterion D (i.e., Negative alterations in cognitions and mood associated with the traumatic events), which is a key feature to diagnose complex PTSD (CPTSD). It is noteworthy that none of the cases exhibited psychotic symptoms, and many had at least one co-morbidity. In addition to the first-line treatment options, second-generation antipsychotics (quetiapine) and other antidepressants (mirtazapine and amitriptyline) were instituted at the maximum tolerated doses, for approximately 12 months, but without any meaningful effect. Prolonged Exposure Therapy (Case 1) and Trauma-Focused Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (Case 3) were instituted for two cases without significant therapeutic benefit. In contrast, in the remaining cases, these were not applied due to severe emotional and/or behavioral disturbance that affected their occupational and social functioning (Table 1). Before clozapine initiation, the cases were selected to meet the proposed criteria for TR-PTSD (i.e., symptoms persisting with functional impairment and emotional dysregulation for over 12 months despite standard treatments, including the failure of psychotherapeutic measures).1,2

All responded to clozapine at daily doses between 125 and 300 mg. Although full remission was not achieved (except in Case 1), they became functional (i.e., resumed their occupational and family roles) and tolerated the residual symptoms. Moreover, the suicidal and aggressive tendencies were reduced, and interpersonal relationships improved. If clozapine was discontinued (Cases 5, 6, and 7), it was mainly due to intolerable side effects (i.e., undue drowsiness, marked weight loss, nocturia, and constipation). In 4 of 7 cases (Cases 1–4), they continue to function well on clozapine without significant adverse effects at present.

Discussion

Clozapine is a unique atypical antipsychotic because of its numerous receptor affinities, including serotonin (5-HT1C, 5-HT2), adrenergic (α1), muscarinic (M), histamine (H1), and dopamine (D1-D5) receptors. 4 Furthermore, it is demonstrated to be effective for psychiatric conditions resistant to conventional treatments (e.g., in schizophrenia), and in reducing suicidality, aggression, depression, and substance use. 5

There are empirically valid reasons to rationalize clozapine as a treatment option for TR-PTSD. Investigations suggest that PTSD symptoms arise from dysregulation of serotonergic, dopaminergic, and noradrenergic neurotransmitter systems, as well as dysfunction of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis; given its receptor affinity profile, clozapine may therefore alleviate these symptoms by modulating dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways and antagonizing α1-adrenergic receptors. 6 Sleep disturbance independently exacerbates daytime symptom severity, giving rise to treatment refractoriness in PTSD, and herein, the sedative properties of clozapine mediated by its antihistaminergic action could ameliorate the symptoms of PTSD.1,3 Comorbidities of PTSD (depression, anxiety, psychotic symptoms, and substance use disorders) are implicated in treatment resistance, and thereby, full remission requires concurrent improvement in both PTSD and comorbid symptoms. 7 Hence, given its established therapeutic versatility in schizophrenia with comorbidities, clozapine can be viewed as a rational treatment option for TR-PTSD. 8 In addition, complex PTSD, a subtype characterized by disturbances in affect regulation, self-concept, and interpersonal functioning, is shown to be refractory to standard treatment. Therefore, arguably, considering the mood-stabilizing, antidepressant, and calming effects, clozapine can be considered a rational therapeutic alternative.8,9

Compared to non-combat-related trauma, combat-related trauma is observed to incur a severe and chronic form of PTSD along with comorbidities, and often at a higher risk of developing TR-PTSD. 10 These findings converge with our hindsight experience, and hence, treatment of combat-related PTSD should be viewed differently, justifying our institution of clozapine.

Most side effects of clozapine are benign and manageable; nevertheless, they are a major reason attributed to poor drug compliance, which was so among three of our patients (Table 1). 4 However, it is noteworthy that the prevalence of serious negative adverse effects of clozapine, including agranulocytosis and cardiac complications, has been overestimated, leading to the false impression that clozapine is dangerous. 11 In fact, a prospective 11-year study on patients with treatment-resistant schizophrenia demonstrated that the risk of premature mortality is comparatively lower in those on clozapine than in those on other antipsychotics and not on medication. 12 The four cases that showed a significant improvement, and the other three, though discontinued due to intolerable side effects (non-fatal), displayed symptom reduction with clozapine, posit the therapeutic benefit of clozapine in TR-PTSD. Thereby, like treatment-resistant schizophrenia, we contend that PTSD elevates mortality risks (due to suicidal tendencies and cardiac complications) and impairs quality of life. 13 The benefits of clozapine treatment in TR-PTSD most likely outweigh its intrinsic risks in carefully selected cases with severe functional decline. Three patients (Cases 1, 2, and 7) had comorbid nicotine dependence and required higher therapeutic doses of clozapine (200–300 mg), which likely reflects the known inductive effect of tobacco smoking on CYP1A2 metabolism, a factor clinicians must closely evaluate during titration. 14

This is the first case series in the published literature to examine the clinical use of clozapine in a group of young soldiers with severe, combat-related treatment-resistant PTSD (TR-PTSD) who had no diagnosable psychotic symptoms. We emphasize the lack of a control group, the variable treatment responses, the lack of long-term post-discontinuation follow-up data to determine optimal treatment duration, and the low rate of concurrent trauma-focused psychotherapy due to severe initial behavioral disturbances and structural service constraints. The findings of this study are insufficient to determine the optimal duration of clozapine treatment in TR-PTSD due to variable responses; hence, a prospective study is warranted.

Conclusion

Due to the mounting societal and combat violence globally, the prevalence of PTSD would be higher in the future, and more robust and effective methods should be developed. As we have shown, clozapine is a promising alternative for carefully selected cases with significant functional impairment that should be empirically tested in future randomized controlled trials.
