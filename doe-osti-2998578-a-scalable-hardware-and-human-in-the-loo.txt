Executive Summary
This project developed a publicly available, high-ﬁdelity dataset about the interactions
among humans, homes, and heat pumps supporting grid interactive efficient buildings to
balance demand on the grid with comfort for occupants. Laboratory measurements and
simulations of the hardware capture the second-scale electric power dynamics of heat
pumps providing grid services like load shifting and load shedding. Field measurements,
behavior tracking, and qualitative surveys of people in their homes over multiple years—
including experimentally adjusting the heating and cooling system to provide grid
services—to capture the reciprocal effect of human behavior on grid services, and grid
services on human comfort. Taken together, these data capture the complete Hardware
and Human in the loop system for residential heat pumps, reducing large uncertainties in
simulation for design, and models for control of heat pumps, and grid-interactive buildings.
Hardware In the Loop: The laboratory experiments include a single stage, SEER 16, HSPF
9.5, 3-ton single-speed air source heat pump with 15 kW of backup auxiliary heating tested
in both cooling and heating mode, and a two-stage, SEER 21, 2-ton central air conditioner
tested in cooling mode for a set of outdoor temperatures and indoor setpoint temperatures
with indoor temperatures and cooling loads derived from a real-time building simulation
(i.e. hardware-in-the-loop, HwIL). Instead of running experiments for a day to produce a
day of data, the simulation runs faster than real-time when the heat pump is on or off for
extended periods (i.e. steady state). During these periods, the lab experiment is replaced
with well-studied standard models of heat pumps in steady state. Only when the heat
pump is repeatedly switching state are the simulated heat pump’s inputs and outputs into
the building simulation replaced with those from the lab experiment. This hardware-in-the-
loop data set offers unprecedented insight into the dynamic relationships of heat pumps’

Humans In the Loop: The ﬁeld study includes over ﬁfty participants in 25 homes in three
different climates around the United States, starting with a baseline detailed
documentation of the home, and coupled with in-depth surveys and interviews at the start
and end of study to capture household demographics, attitudes, and motivations. Sensors
record quantitative data like temperature, humidity, occupancy, air quality, occupancy
every few minutes, and participants respond to brief surveys of a few questions on a
smartphone. The longitudinal study followed each household for over two years, including
at least twelve months of baseline observation, followed by at least twelve months during
which researchers intervened by adjusting the home’s thermostats to simulate grid
interactions like demand reduction or load-shifting. Unlike laboratory studies in controlled
environmental chambers, these semi-controlled ﬁeld data capture real-life interactions
including responding to the experimentally adjusting the HVAC system to simulate grid
interaction like a demand response. Unlike many one-time post-occupancy surveys which
rely on occupants’ memory, the qualitative data are captured “right now” in the moment
and repeatedly many times over a long period. Further, the qualitative and behavior data
are coupled with the time-serries of quantitative measurements. This human-in-the-loop
dataset affords new insight into human comfort, discomfort, and behavior especially in
grid-interactive buildings. HuIL datasets and analysis code are publicly available online [2].

Impact: The ability of future buildings to shed, shift, and modulate their electricity demand
can prevent the need to build costly new transmission lines and power plants or operate
polluting, aged inefficient plants only to serve periods of peak demand. However, current
demand response programs see up to 30% of the automated attempts to change load
overridden by users because the automation does not account for changing user needs.
Further complicating these programs are the complex behavior of heat pumps when
operated cyclically during the short time periods to provide grid services such as ‘ramping’.
Catalyzed by the data and models produced in this project, future control algorithms in
smart thermostats could accurately predict their ability to change demand while keeping
occupants happy. At the same time, the data and models related to human behavior
developed through this project can reduce uncertainty for grid operators to design
programs that capture more load ﬂexibility while ensuring comfort and decreasing the risk
of overrides. Altogether, smart thermostats could provide ~100GW of load ﬂexibility,
potentially reducing peak electricity demand in the US by ~10% and thus reducing the need
for hard infrastructure investments to support these peaks.

Activities Supported by this Project
Budget Period 1 – HwIL and HuIL Platform Development
Task 1.1 – Develop Hardware in the Loop Capabilities
Hypothesis: Laboratory hardware-in-the-loop testing will accurately capture and
characterize transient and steady-state thermal and electrical behavior in heat pumps
controlled using simulated building physics, grid signals, and occupant behaviors.

 • Subtask 1.1.1 – HwIL Hardware Development: Developed physical hardware to
 measure and control relevant inputs and outputs of two residential heat pumps by
 designing a test rig that controls air enthalpy on interior and exterior of the heat
 pump, as well as locating the thermostat in a separately-controlled test chamber.

 • Subtask 1.1.2 – HwIL Software Development: Developed a co-simulation
 operating model (EnergyPlus) based on NREL's HwIL platform to integrate building
 physics, grid interactions, and occupant overrides into the testing.

 • Subtask 1.1.3 – HwIL Experiment Design: Designed experiments and developed
 test procedures to use hardware and software developed above to identify transient
 behavior of HPs and their effect on building grid services.

Findings: Successfully demonstrated HwIL HP transient testing with smart thermostats [3].
Speciﬁed transient HwIL experiments representing impactful transients for Grid-interactive
Efficient Buildings (GEBs). Completed demonstration of transient testing of heat pump and
air conditioner performance across a wide range of operating conditions, capturing high-
resolution power and temperature data that demonstrated expected cycling behavior
patterns. Speciﬁed experimental conditions for approximately HwIL experiments
representative Grid-interactive Efficient Buildings.

Task 1.2 – Develop Human in the Loop Capabilities
Hypothesis: Occupant behavior drives Heating, Ventilation, Air Conditioning (HVAC)
system operation, and changes in HVAC operation prompted by grid interaction will in turn
affect human behavior (e.g. overrides). Capturing the human-in-the-loop feedback among
HVAC systems, building physics, and human behavior requires long-term ﬁeld testing.

 • Subtask 1.2.1 – HuIL Hardware Development: Selected smart thermostats and
 other hardware to measure and control indoor temperature and conduct the study.
 The selected thermostat acts as the primary point of sensing and control to connect
 a suite of remote sensors, and additional instrumentation including occupancy
 sensors, window sensors. Plug load monitors for supplemental fans and heaters
 connect directly to the internet.

 • Subtask 1.2.2 – Occupant Behavior Modeling Framework: Developed multi-
 domain models of occupant behavior dynamics based on physiology, psychology,
 and sociology. Designed and validated Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA)
 questions and protocols for minimally burdensome data collection from occupants
 using smartphone, both during the baseline data collection and the subsequent
 experimental phase.

 • Subtask 1.2.3 – HuIL Software Development: Developed cloud and embedded
 software to interface between the home HVAC system, the study data repository,
 simulations, and occupant feedback loops. The software stack included thermostat
 and sensor integration using the open source Home Assistant app on smartphones.

 • Subtask 1.2.4 – HuIL Experiment Design: Designed experiments and procedures
 to use the hardware, software, and models developed above to conduct the HuIL
 experiments. This included protocols for protection of human subjects, recruiting
 participants, developing interview questions, standards for home documentation
 and instrumentation, and design of sampling and experimental interventions.

Findings: Developed integrated suite of hardware and software to support HuIL
experiments. Developed protocols for human research, secured IRB approval and
successfully recruited 41 participants in 20 homes in two climates. Documented homes,
installed sensors, conducted interviews, and connected homes to the cloud software to
support data collection and subsequent experiments. Further information on the
experiment design is detailed in Casavant, et al. 2022 [4].

Task 1.3 – Collect Dynamic Thermal Comfort Behavior Data
Hypothesis: Quantitative and Qualitative data collected under normal occupancy (i.e.
without simulated grid interaction) will reﬂect occupant thermal comfort behavior and
thermostat preferences. These baseline data enable dynamic models for occupant
behavior and systems performance; and serve as the control for the subsequent
experimental phase (see Task 2.2 below).

 • Subtask 1.3.1 – HuIL Deployment: Deployed thermostats and additional sensors in
 participant homes to track occupant behavior and building thermal dynamics.
 Conducted EMA both randomly and in response to user behaviors (e.g. thermostat
 adjustments).

 • Subtask 1.3.2 – Risk Assessment: Interviewed potential commercialization
 partners and industry experts to enumerate techno-economic and market risks of
 deploying occupant-centric controls for GEB grid services.

Findings: To better understand human-building interactions (HBIs) in residential buildings,
41 people in 20 homes in two climates participated in a 6-month study. Timestamps from
app-based thermal comfort surveys and thermostat interactions were synchronized to
time-series building systems data, resulting in the largest-of-its-kind HBI dataset. These
survey data are compared to predictions from industry-standard thermal comfort models.
Statistically signiﬁcant results show that these models, developed under steady-state
assumptions, yield greater error magnitudes and/or biases when spatiotemporal
temperature variations exceed 2°F. The mean spatial variation within homes in the dataset
was 4°F, thermostat DR control would commonly exacerbate such temporal variation. The
results highlight opportunities for improving DR load-control algorithms through a
paradigm shift to modeling discomfort rather than comfort, increasing the use of low-cost
sensors, and incorporating dynamic models of occupant behavior [5].

Budget Period 2 – HwIL HP Transient Modeling and HuIL Monitoring and
Demonstration
Task 2.1 – HwIL Transient HP Tests and Validation
Hypothesis: Heat pumps working to meet occupant needs in speciﬁc homes while
responding to grid signals will exhibit unique transient and steady-state thermal and
electrical behaviors, which can be modeled using appropriate parameters.

 • Subtask 2.1.1 – HwIL Test Design and Execution: Designed and executed 48
 distinct HP transient tests with controlled temperatures, enthalpies, home designs,
 and grid signals as deﬁned in Task 1.1.3 (above).

 • Subtask 2.1.2 – HwIL Modeling and Analysis: Analyzed the data HwIL data and
 identiﬁed transient conditions that were poorly characterized in standard building
 performance simulation heat pump models.

Findings: Completed comprehensive HwIL experiments at NREL's Energy Systems
Integration Facility (ESIF) using calibrated building models and controlled laboratory
conditions. Generated high-resolution datasets demonstrating heat pump cycling behavior
under various grid service scenarios. The data conﬁrmed expected patterns: in cooling
mode, as outdoor air temperature increases and setpoint decreases, cycle runtime
increases and time between cycles decreases. Similarly, in heating mode, as outdoor
temperature decreases and setpoint increases, cycle runtime increases and time between
cycles decreases. The heat pump runs continuously at extreme outdoor temperatures to
maintain indoor conditions. A primary ﬁnding not initially considered was the variability in
response dynamics on the same home, heat pump, and outdoor environment, but with a
different thermostat [6].

Task 2.2 – Passive (baseline) HuIL Data Collection and Modeling
Hypothesis: Year-long passive data collection will capture sufficient information to
develop dynamic behavior models and estimate internal occupant states under a range of
conditions for subsequent analysis.

 • Subtask 2.2.1 – HuIL Deployment: Deployed thermostats and sensors in all
 participant homes.

 • Subtask 2.2.2 – HuIL Data Collection: Conducted year-long data collection
 exercise across heating, cooling, and shoulder seasons to produce novel dataset
 capturing occupant behavior dynamics.

 • Subtask 2.2.3 – HuIL Modeling: Developed dynamic models of occupant thermal
 discomfort behavior separating physiological effects, psychological effects, and
 decision-making processes.

 • Subtask 2.2.4 – Curate and Deliver Results: Disseminated initial results in
 multiple conferences and workshops.

Findings: Successfully completed baseline HuIL data collection in 25 participating homes,
with adequate participant diversity across three climates (5 more homes were enrolled in
an additional climate since Task 1), and across heating, cooling, and shoulder seasons
(Milestone 2.2.1). The study was extended due to successful participation rates, with 20
out of the original 25 homes remaining in the research into a third year. Collected dozens of
sensor data points every ﬁve minutes over the year-plus in each home, and hundreds of
Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) responses with over a 25% mean response rate
(Milestone 2.2.2). Successfully demonstrated dynamic occupant behavior models,
quantiﬁed accuracy across ﬁeld data (Milestone 2.2.3) [7], and disseminated the results
through IEA Annex 79, ASHRAE MTG-OBB, and the Comfort at the Extremes communities
(Milestone 2.2.4) [8].

Task 2.3 – HuIL Thermal AR Development
Hypothesis: Thermal Augmented Reality (AR) can emulate heat pump performance by
controlling a non-heat pump HVAC system to demonstrate the effect on occupants of a
heat pump providing grid services as predicted by a digital twin simulation.

 • Subtask 2.3.1 – AR Temperature Control Validation: Deployed control algorithms
 in the Home Assistant platform demonstrating ability to implement long-term
 studies on human-building interactions.

 • Subtask 2.3.2 – AR Deployment: Deployed thermostat control algorithms to
 participant homes. Where the initial plan proposed temperature proﬁles to simulate
 speciﬁc heat pumps, we instead generated temperature proﬁles that produce and
 range of demand ﬂexibility conditions, designed to prompt occupant responses.

 • Subtask 2.3.3 – Risk Assessment: Interviewed commercial partners regarding
 techno-economic and market risks.

 • Subtask 2.3.4 – Market and Investment Assessment: Identiﬁed
 commercialization pathways and key stakeholders.

Findings: After initial data collection we determined that developing models of human
behavior demanded speciﬁc data: where the hypothesized Thermal AR approach would
reproduce the heat pump behavior, it would likely not produce high-quality human data,
because it would prompt relatively few occupant-building interactions. Based on
consultations with the DOE program officer, we determined that the ultimate goal of
modeling human interactions with homes was better served by collecting a richer dataset
including more interactions and focusing on areas where models have uncertainty, rather
than a few interactions in perfectly realistic conditions. Assessed value of occupant-
centric control for GEBs in terms of satisfaction costs and override ﬂexibility [7], [9].

Budget Period 3 – HwIL and HuIL Cross Validation
Task 3.1 – Demonstrate Occupant-Induced Heat Pump Effects
Research Question: How does occupant behavior affect the magnitude and reliability of
grid services provided by heat pumps in grid-interactive buildings?

 • Subtask 3.1.1 – HwIL Test Design and Execution: Reﬁned the experimental
 schedule to develop targeted data for heat pump model development under
 conditions of occupant override during demand response events. Analyzed initial
 HuIL data to design test conditions. Consulted with the team developing the
 OCHRE 1 software for modeling building portfolio load proﬁles due to their keen
 interest in improving dynamic models of heat pumps under demand response.
 Completed dozens of HP transient tests with controlled temperatures, enthalpies,
 home designs, and setpoints.

 • Subtask 3.1.2 – HwIL Modeling and Analysis: Rather than an internal modeling
 and simulation conﬁned to the objectives of this project and team, we collaborated
 closely with the NREL team developing OCHRE—an energy modeling tool focused
 on generating high resolution load proﬁles—to ensure the data developed in
 Subtask 3.1.1 would inform their models, even though that project’s timeline is not
 identical to this one.

Findings: The comparison of the HwIL data and the output of the OCHRE model under the
same condition showed power difference of a couple hundred Watts but the cycle timing
and duration is very similar between lab results and simulation. There are some differences
between the indoor temperatures from the lab and simulation outputs. These differences
could be attributed to differences in thermostat control logic. Additional areas of study
included auxiliary heat controls for second stage heating and defrost.

Task 3.2 – HuIL Thermal AR Experiments
Research Questions: Do occupants notice and change their behavior when HVAC
systems are providing grid services? What motivates occupant interactions with HVAC
systems via thermostat adjustments?

 • Subtask 3.2.1 – AR Deployment: Controlled temperatures in participant’s homes to
 simulate homes providing grid services, for example setting back the AC during the
 cooling season to reduce demand—with parameters based on the validated
 thermal models and occupant interfaces.

 • Subtask 3.2.2 – HuIL Experiment Testing: Conducted long-term testing (cooling,
 heating, and shoulder seasons) in which participants lived their daily lives in an
 ongoing tests receiving 1-2 demand ﬂexibility events per day and receiving 7-10 EMA
 requests per week.

 • Subtask 3.2.3 – HuIL Data preparation, Analysis, and Modeling: Analyzed
 quantitative and qualitative HuIL experimental data to understand drivers, needs,
 actions, and systems related to occupant behavior and began modeling work for
 occupant behavior during demand ﬂexibility.

Findings: Implemented longitudinal experiments building on the procedures developed in
task 2.3, and in to compare with data collected during passive collection phase in task 2.2.
(Milestone 3.2.1). Initiated demand response ﬁeld trials with innovative interface design
that hides setpoint changes from users to minimize habitual overrides and demonstrated
effectiveness of experimental protocol and infrastructure to capture behavior, comfort, and
motivation data from occupants experiencing simulated demand ﬂexibility events while
maintaining high rates of participant response and retention. (Milestone 3.2.2). Quantiﬁed
the impact of HPs on occupants and their effect on grid service delivery capability,
revealing that many manual thermostat adjustments during demand response events are
routine-based rather than comfort-driven. (Milestone 3.2.3) [10]. A data analysis pipeline
and open-source code templating package was developed to streamline data preparation
for release and future analysis [11], [12].

Future Work
Hardware in the Loop: While the experiments in this project advanced the understanding
of heat pump and control operations, it raised some additional areas for exploration. The
project had only limited time to evaluate the control of auxiliary heating, and only for one of
the tested thermostats. However, there are multiple situations which may trigger auxiliary
heat (e.g. compressor lock out, large setpoint changes, defrost cycles) and auxiliary heat
represents signiﬁcant energy use and possible discomfort. Grid interactions, for example
setbacks during and ramp ups after demand response events, exacerbate these
challenges. Additional hardware in the loop testing using the experimental designs
developed in this project could help close this information gap about how various
thermostats control auxiliary heating, and the possible connections with defrost cycles.

Humans in the loop: This is a ﬁrst-of-its-kind longitudinal residential human-building-
interaction study that combined environmental (e.g., temperature), systems (e.g., HVAC),
and occupant qualitative (e.g., EMAs) and quantitative (occupancy and interactions) data.
The methods developed here to reduce the overhead of sensors, rely on cloud services and
smart devices show the way to a truly scalable data collection method. The preliminary
data gathered here provide insights into the relative importances of various data and
parameters for modeling human behavior, which may streamline experimental design in
scaling future studies. Open challenges remain in data analysis where each household
generates sparse data, but rich data can be generated for the population.

Conclusion/Key Project Outcomes
The project successfully achieved all major milestones and end-of-project goals, delivering
comprehensive datasets and validated methodologies that advance the understanding of
human-building-grid interactions in the context of heat pump-based grid services.

 1. Novel Datasets: Created publicly available, high-ﬁdelity datasets capturing
 seconds-scale electric power dynamics of heat pumps providing grid services and
 occupant impacts.

 2. Validated Methodologies: Demonstrated feasibility of both hardware-in-the-loop
 and human-in-the-loop testing approaches for generating data necessary for model-
 based control of grid-interactive efficient buildings.

 3. Dynamic Models: Developed and validated dynamic models for both heat pump
 performance and occupant behavior that account for grid service provision.

 4. Science Education and Training: Multiple papers under review , several conference
 presentations have been accepted [8], [10]. One dissertation complete and
 graduated [7], one on track for graduation. One post-doctoral fellow placed in a full-
 time academic position. Funding secured from NSF to continue project at scale.
