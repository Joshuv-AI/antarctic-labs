 1. Summary
In partnership with Bergey Windpower, this project explored the use of recyclable and
recycled materials in small- to medium-wind turbine blades (WTBs). A recyclable epoxy
resin and infusible thermoplastic resin were investigated using identical fiber
reinforcements and manufacturing practices used as Bergey, and the substitution of
continuous virgin glass fiber (GF) layers with recycled nonwoven GF (rGF, NW) mats was
trialed. The alternative resins both showed very promising performance in terms of static
tensile and flexural behavior as well as thermal properties and fatigue behavior in
comparison with that of the incumbent materials used at Bergey. The NW rGF materials,
however, were found to be a poor substitute for the continuous GF materials for two
primary reasons. Firstly, the NW mats took up significantly more resin than their
continuous counterparts, which would increase both the weight and the cost of the WTBs.
Additionally, an unexpected reaction occurred between the rGF and epoxy materials that
resulted in a highly porous, foamed structure. The foaming phenomenon was not
observed with the rGF NW and infusible thermoplastic material, but the resin uptake was
still prohibitively high. Based on the results of this study, recyclable infusible resins could
be viable candidate materials for Bergey in the future. The foaming reaction with the rGF
NW mats could prove highly useful in other applications requiring structural foams and
has been used to produce demonstrative sandwich panels.
 2. Project Task
The original intention of this project was to trial different thermoplastic composites to
identify at least one that meets the requirements of small/medium WTBs. Upon discussing
the project with the partner, Bergey Windpower, it was apparent that thermoplastic
materials requiring melt processing are unlikely to be a viable replacement for their
incumbent epoxy due to the requirement and very high associated cost for heated molds.
Bergey currently manufactures their WTBs using vacuum assisted resin transfer molding
(VARTM) with a stack of unidirectional (UD) carbon fiber (CF) sandwiched between layers
of biaxial stitched GF in a matrix of an infusible and self-curing epoxy. This project
investigated whether the polymer matrix and/or the biaxial GF fabrics could be replaced
with more sustainable alternatives.
 3. Methods
 3.1. Materials

In the first portion of the project, two recyclable resin systems were first compared to
Bergey’s incumbent epoxy with a stack of identical GF and CF to that used in Bergey’s
wind blades. In the second phase, the continuous stitched glass fiber layers were
swapped for an equivalent weight of nonwoven recycled glass fiber, which was used with
each of the resins of interest.
   3.1.1. Resins
Three types of resin were compared in this study to create laminated stacks of GF-CF-
GF, in which the GF was either continuous or nonwoven, and the CF was continuous and
unidirectional (UD), which is discussed further in the next section. Bergey’s incumbent
epoxy, which consist of Pro-Set INF-114 resin and Pro-Set INF-210 hardener at a 3:1
ratio by volume, was purchased from Composite Envisions, LLC. The other resins
comprised a recyclable epoxy, Recyclamine, and an infusible thermoplastic, Elium.
The diamine hardener in Recyclamine epoxy systems is designed to include cleavable
crosslinks that enable recyclability. Recyclamines can be used in place of traditional
epoxies and are recycled by treatment in acetic acid at mild temperatures (~80 °C), which
dissolves the material. Sodium hydroxide can then be added to the solution, which results
in immediate precipitation of a thermoplastic compound. In this manner, reinforcing fibers
can be recovered from the epoxy laminate and a usable thermoplastic can be recovered
and used in a second application. An infusible Recyclamine system, Epotec YDL 5567
and Epotec THR 9369, was kindly provided by Aditya Birla Chemicals.
Elium systems are designed to mimic the process of mixing and infusing a traditional two-
part epoxy for use in the same applications. However, rather than mixing a resin and
crosslinker and crosslinking in place, Elium comprises a solution of monomers or
oligomers that is mixed with a solution of initiators and other polymerization agents such
as chain transfer agents or radical coupling agents. The two parts are mixed, infused into
fiber stacks, and polymerize in situ, resulting in a thermoplastic that can be subsequently
thermomechanically reprocessed. In this work, an accelerator was also added to aid in
rapid polymerization. The materials used, Elium 151 XOSA, Elium 151 SA, and Elium 151
XXO were kindly provided by Arkema.

 3.2. Material Processing
 3.2.1. Wet-Laid Nonwovens
The recycled GF fibers were processed to homogeneous,
isotropic mats using the wet-laid nonwoven technique. A simple
wet-lay setup was constructed comprising an acrylic tank affixed
to a drain separated by a metal mesh, as shown in Figure 3. A
tank solution was first prepared containing flocculant and
surfactant additives at concentrations of 0.11 g/L each. An
amount of rGF was weighed out to match the weight of an
equivalent size of the Biax-GF layers. As the inner dimensions of
the tank (and resulting mats) are approximately 12”x12”, this size
was used for all fabrics and mats in this work. Based on prior Figure 3. Wet-lay
experience in similar wet-lay systems of the same dimension, it nonwoven tank
was found that smaller batches containing 25-30 g of fiber
(corresponding to ~300gsm mats) produce the most homogeneous mats. For each rGF
layer, 3-4 mats were prepared and stacked to reach the target weight. To prepare a mat,
rGF was weighed out and placed in an industrial blender (Waring) fitted with a blunt blade
along with the tank solution. The fibers were mixed by pulsing on a low setting for up to
10 seconds. The tank of the wet-lay apparatus was then filled with the surfactant and
flocculant solution until it was approximately 2/3 full, and the suspension of fibers was
added. The tank was then agitated briefly by hand to disperse the fibers in the water, after
which the drain was opened and the tank solution emptied into a bucket below, while the
GF was gathered on the metal mesh. The mat was then removed from the tank and dried
in an oven at 60 °C overnight.
   3.2.2. VARTM

VARTM is a process used very commonly in the wind energy, aerospace, and marine
industries, among others. VARTM can produce laminated composites with a very high
fiber fraction and correspondingly high performance. However, the process is more costly
and time-consuming than other composite manufacturing techniques, so is usually
reserved for higher value applications. In a simple VARTM setup, a reinforcing fabric
stack is placed inside a vacuum-proof bag along with a tube connected to a resin reservoir
and a tube connected to a vacuum source. A vacuum is created within the bag that draws
resin inside the bag and through the reinforcing fabric, infiltrating the fabric while resulting
in a minimal resin content in the final material. Extreme care must be taken in setting up
a VARTM procedure to ensure no air leaks are present in the bagging or sealing
materials, a challenge which is exacerbated with increasing part size. A small VARTM
system was constructed to create test panels in this work in which fabrics were stacked
on a clean glass plate, as shown in Figure 4. Tubes are added on either side of the fabric
stack for resin feed and vacuum. The setup is then framed with sealant tape, with tape
also looped around the inlet and exit tubing. A layer of thin breather fabric, termed peel
ply, is then placed over the entire fabric stack extending from the resin tube to the vacuum
tube. The peel ply both encourages resin flow by separating the vacuum bag from the
 surface of the fabric and

 3.3. Characterization
Rectangular samples of various sizes were cut from each cured plaque using a water jet
cutter (WardJet).
 3.3.1. Static mechanical measurements
For flexural testing, 5”x0.5” samples were cut from each plaque in the 0 and 90° direction
with respect to the direction of the UD CF layer. Smples were tested using a 3-point bend
frame on an MTS hydraulic frame with a 200 lb-f load cell. The deformation rate of each
sample as well as the resulting flexural stress and strain were calculated according to
ASTM D790. Samples of the same dimensions were also used for tensile testing. Each
sample was tabbed at either end using 1”x0.5” Garolite pieces fixed to the samples with
JB Weld. Samples were tested according to ASTM D3039 using an MTS hydraulic frame
with a 25k lb-f load cell at a pull rate of 2 mm/min. Five specimens were tested of each
sample type for tensile and flexural measurements.
 3.3.2. Temperature Sweeps
The glass transition temperature of each material was measured using oscillatory
temperature sweeps on a dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA, TA Instruments) equipped
with a dual cantilever fixture (3-point bend with ends and center clamps). Samples were
cut to 2.4”x0.4” and tested at a strain amplitude of 15 µm and frequency of 1 Hz while the
temperature was ramped from room temperature to 200 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min. The
glass transition temperature was defined as the temperature at the peak of the tan(δ)
curve.
 3.3.3. Fatigue Tests
The flexural fatigue of each sample was measured in the 0 and 90° direction using a DMA
with a dual cantilever fixture. Each sample was oscillated at a strain amplitude of 0.01%
and a frequency of 10 Hz for a total of 100,000 cycles. The torsional fatigue of each
sample was measured in the 0° direction using 1.6”x0.4” samples on an oscillatory
rheometer (ARES G2, TA Instruments) equipped with a torsional fixture. A strain
amplitude sweep was first performed on each sample type to determine the material’s
linear viscoelastic region (LVR), and the fatigue behavior each sample was subsequently
measured at a strain amplitude within its LVR and a frequency of 5 Hz for a period of
20,000 sec (to reach 100,000 cycles). Each test was performed at room temperature.

 5. Final Product
The above data indicates that both Recyclamine and Elium resins are viable options to
replace the Proset epoxy currently used in Bergey’s small and medium WTBs.
Recyclamine is a thermoset epoxy system with crosslinks cleavable in mild acid solutions,
from which the reinforcing fibers can be reclaimed, and a usable thermoplastic can be
precipitated upon addition of an alkaline. Elium, on the other hand, is an infusible polymer
precursor system that can be infiltrated into a fiber stack and polymerized in situ. After
polymerization, Elium can be melt reprocessed as any typical thermoplastic. As such,
both Recyclamine and Elium represent recyclable systems that can be incorporated into
Bergey’s products. It was further found that the use of NW rGF resulted in deleterious
and sometimes unexpected effects when combined with the different resins such as
apparent foaming and high degrees of porosity. In their current state, the supplied rGF is
not suitable for replacement of the Biax GF in Bergey’s products when formed to NW

mats using wet-laying. However, the unexpected foaming phenomenon of the epoxy/rGF
could prove useful in numerous other applications.
 6. Challenges Encountered & Lessons Learned
As previously stated, the foaming observed in the epoxy samples with NW rGF was
unexpected, but could represent an opportunity for future research on new production
methods for fiber-reinforced structural foams, which have applications in a broad range
of industries. It is possible that rGF could still be useful in WTBs if either their surface
were modified to avoid foaming or if they were processed in a different manner.
Additionally, producing homogeneous mats of the rGF using the small-scale wet-lay tank
proved a significant challenge. The fibers were difficult to mix and disperse evenly, and
the mats themselves were very difficult to handle after removal from the tank and drying.
Working with loose GF in any form is challenging and can be considered a hazard if
proper PPE and containment procedures are not employed. Future NW production with
rGFs should include a binder to hold the mats together and avoid excessive shedding of
the loose fibers, making the mats both easier and safer for researchers to handle.
