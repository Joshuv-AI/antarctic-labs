Rapamycin Attenuates Acute Pancreatitis-Induced Acinar Injury in Mice With Pancreatic Loss of Gα13
Authors: Hidayatullah G Munshi, Anastasia E Metropulos, Khadijeh Karbalaei, Christina Spaulding, Nida Mubin, Yaning Xi, Apurba Majumder, Thao D Pham, Mario A Shields
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsWhile Gα13 transduces signals from G protein-coupled receptors to regulate inflammation, its role in pancreatitis remains poorly understood. This study aims to understand how Gα13 regulates pancreatitis-induced inflammation and acinar injury.MethodsExpression of Gα13 in mouse and human pancreas tissue was examined by immunostaining. Experimental pancreatitis was induced by administering cerulein to mice with wild-type and conditional knockout of Gα13 in the pancreas. The impact of Gα13 loss on pancreatitis was assessed by histology, immunostains, and cytokine proteome array. The Gα13-deficient mice were cotreated with the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor rapamycin during cerulein treatment to determine the effects of mTOR inhibition on pancreatitis.ResultsWe found reduced Gα13 expression in acute and chronic human pancreatitis samples. We show that induction of acute and chronic pancreatitis in mice with cerulein treatment reduces Gα13 expression. Moreover, we found worsening of acute and chronic pancreatitis-induced acinar injury in mice with pancreas-specific deletion of Gα13. While pancreatic deletion of Gα13 did not exacerbate acute pancreatitis-induced inflammation, we found that Gα13 loss mildly exacerbated chronic pancreatitis. Gα13 loss increased cerulein-induced mTOR signaling in the acute and chronic pancreatitis mouse models; however, the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin attenuated the effects of cerulein only in the acute pancreatitis model, whereas it exacerbated the effects of cerulein in the chronic pancreatitis model.ConclusionThis work demonstrates that Gα13 loss promotes tissue damage in acute and chronic pancreatitis, which can be attenuated by the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin in acute pancreatitis.Keywords: Gα13, Pancreatitis, Inflammation, Acinar Injury

Introduction

Gα proteins transduce signals from G protein-coupled receptors, the largest class of cell surface proteins that regulate varied biological processes.1 Since G protein-coupled receptors signal in part through Gα13,2 many studies have evaluated the role of Gα13 in physiological and pathologic processes.3, 4, 5, 6 For example, Gα13 regulates lysophosphatidic acid– and thrombin-mediated inflammation.7,8 Targeting Gα13 decreases thrombin-induced arachidonic acid expression in Chinese hamster ovary cells and sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P)-induced inflammatory genes in astrocytes.9,10 However, many studies evaluating Gα13 in inflammation were conducted in vitro, and studies evaluating the effects of epithelial targeting of Gα13 in vivo using genetically engineered mouse (GEM) models have been limited.

We recently showed that epithelial targeting of Gα13 in vivo in the KrasG12D/p53R172H/Pdx1-Cre (KPC) GEM model of pancreatic cancer promotes tumor development.11 We showed that loss of Gα13 increases mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signaling in the KPC GEM model, and that treatment with the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin can decrease the growth of KPC tumors with Gα13 loss. Our results with the GEM model contrast with the previously published cell line studies showing that Gα13 is pro-tumorigenic,12, 13, 14, 15 warranting employing GEM models to further understand the role of Gα13 in physiological and pathologic processes.

Importantly, Gα proteins can potentially regulate pancreatitis. Gα proteins in acinar cells mediate zymogen granule secretion downstream of cholecystokinin (CCK)-A receptors.16,17 Studies in isolated acinar cells have shown that the Gα13 protein functions downstream of CCK-A receptors to mediate amylase secretion.18,19 While previous studies have evaluated the effects of Gα13 loss in isolated acinar cells,18,19 the effects of targeting Gα13 on inflammation and pancreatitis have not been studied in vivo in mouse models. Here, we target Gα13 in the pancreas of a GEM model and evaluate the effects on pancreatitis-induced inflammation and acinar injury.

Materials and Methods

Animal Experiments

Study approval

All animal work and procedures were approved by the Northwestern University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Also, all animal experiments were performed per relevant guidelines and regulations.

Conditional knockout

Mice with loss of Gα13 in the pancreas were generated by crossing Pdx1-Cre mice (Jackson Laboratory #014647) to mice expressing the floxed allele of Gna13 (kindly provided by Stefan Offermanns, Max Planck Institute),7 to generate CGα13fl/+ and CGα13 fl/fl mice. All mice were bred on a C57/BL6 background.

Induction of pancreatitis

Pancreatitis was induced in age- and litter-matched mice between 8- and 12-week-old mice of both genders. For acute pancreatitis, mice were starved of food overnight, followed by 8 hourly intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (50 μg/kg). Chronic pancreatitis was induced by intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (250 μg/kg) 2 times daily for 14 days.

Blocking mammalian target of rapamycin in mice

Mice were treated with vehicle control (10% polyethylene glycol 400, 10% Tween80) or rapamycin (MedChemExpress) 60 mg/kg via intraperitoneal injections. For acute pancreatitis studies, mice were pretreated overnight followed by an additional dose 1 h before cerulein dosing. Once daily dosing of rapamycin was administered during chronic pancreatitis.

Serum amylase assay

Blood was collected from euthanized mice via cardiac puncture and allowed to clot at room temperature for 20–30 min. The coagulated blood was centrifuged at 2000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored in aliquots at −80 °C until use. The amylase activity in serum was determined using the EnzChek kit (Thermo Fisher E33651). Diluted serum samples or α-amylase standard from Bacillus species (Sigma-Aldrich A6380) were incubated with starch conjugated with boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY) dye substrate for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Fluorescent readings were acquired using Bio-Tek Synergy HT and the Gen5 software with excitation and emission wavelengths at 485 ± 20 nm and 520 ± 20 nm, respectively.

Cytokine array

Tissue samples were finely ground and lysed with cold radioimmunoprecipitation lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Tissue lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatant was used to detect cytokine levels using the proteome profiler mouse cytokine array kit (R&D Systems, ARY020).

Human pancreatitis samples

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue arrays containing human pancreatitis samples were obtained from US Biomax (PA691). The array panel has 68 cores with 6 mild/normal, 5 acute, and 57 chronic pancreatitis cases. We restricted our analysis to 18 of the 57 chronic samples that were indicated as nonneoplastic. The requirement for instructional review board approval was not required because this study only used deidentified human samples obtained from a commercial source, in accordance with the applicable regulations and institutional policies. The tissue array was stained according to the immunohistochemistry protocol below using the validated antibody (Human Protein Atlas https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000120063-GNA13/tissue/pancreas) for human Gα13 (Sigma-Aldrich HPA010087). The whole array was scanned using an Olympus VS120 slide scanner, and the analysis of positivity was done with QuPath software, which returned the percent positive areas of each core.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunostains, paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling for 10 minutes in sodium citrate (10 mM, pH 6.0) or tris-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (10 mM, pH 9.0) buffer using a pressure cooker. Endogenous peroxidase activity in tissue was quenched, and sections were blocked with a mixture of goat serum and bovine serum albumin. Tissue sections were incubated with antibodies: Gα13 (Sigma-Aldrich HPA010087) CK19 (Abcam #ab52625, 1:500), Amylase (Cell Signaling #3796, 1:2000), F4/80 (Cell Signaling #70076, 1:800), Ly6.2B (clone 7/4, BioRad # MCA771GT, 1:400), or p-Rps6 (Cell Signaling #4858, 1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. Antibody binding was detected using horse radish peroxidase-conjugated antirabbit secondary antibody and visualized using ImmPACT 3,3′-diaminobenzidine Peroxidase Substrate kit (VectorLabs, #SK-4105). For Ly6.2B, antibody binding was detected using the Vectastain Elite ABC-HRP Kit (VectorLabs, #PK6104) Photographs were taken on the Olympus VS120 or TissueGnostics slide scanner systems and analyzed by QuPath, ImageJ or Aperio Software (Leica Biosystems).

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling for 10 minutes in Tris buffer (10 mM, pH 9.0) using a pressure cooker. In select experiments, freshly isolated tissue samples were snap frozen in optimal cutting temperature compound and then sections were fixed in cold acetone:methanol (1:1) for 10 min. Tissue sections were stained using the mouse-on-mouse (MOM) immunodetection kit (Vector Laboratories [BMK-2202]). Tissue sections were blocked with MOM-blocking reagent for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing and incubation with MOM-diluent for 5 min. Tissue sections were then incubated with primary antibody for Gα13 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-293424, 1:100) for 1 h in MOM diluent. For multiplex immunofluorescence stains, tissue sections were incubated with a mix of antibodies for CK19 (DSHB Troma III 1:100), insulin (Cell Signaling # 3014 1:15,000) p-Rps6 (Cell Signaling #4858, 1:500), Gα13 (Sigma-Aldrich HPA010087 1:400) overnight at 4 °C, and then with antibody for Amylase (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-46657, 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Alexa fluor goat antimouse 647 #A32728, goat antirabbit 488 #A23731 and goat antirat 555 #A21434 1:250) for 30 min in the dark at room temperature. The tissue sections were washed and incubated with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Thermo Fisher D1306) for 10 min. Slides were mounted with fluorescence mounting media and images acquired using the EVOS M5000 microscope (Thermo Fisher) or Olympus VS120 slide scanning microscope. Fluorescence intensities were analyzed with Image J by averaging mean intensity values across at least 10 fields per sample.

Western blot

Tissue samples were finely ground and lysed with cold radioimmunoprecipitation lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The lysates were then clarified by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the protein concentration was determined using Precision Red solution (Cytoskeleton, Inc., Denver, CO). Equal amounts of protein were separated with a 10% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis gel. The separated proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane using the semi-dry transfer system (Bio-Rad). After blocking for 1 hour at room temperature with 5% bovine serum albumin, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Primary antibodies used include Gα13 (Santa Cruz sc-293424), p-Rps6 (Cell Signaling #4858), and Hsp90 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology sc-7947). Horse radish peroxidase-conjugated rabbit (A6667) or mouse (A4416) secondary antibody (Millipore-Sigma St. Louis, MO) was used with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for protein detection.

Quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction

RNA was isolated from tissue using the Rneasy kit (74,104, QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Complementary DNA was synthesized from 1–2 μg of RNA with random hexamers and M-MLV reverse transcriptase in a single 50 μL reaction. Quantitative PCR was performed using gene-specific TaqMan probes, TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix, and the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Hercules, CA). Probes used to detect Gna13 (Mm01250415_m1) using mouse Gapdh (Mm99999915_g1) or Rn18s (Mm03928990_g1) as endogenous controls, and their expression levels were used to calculate the relative expression using the comparative Ct method.20

Flow cytometry

At the experimental end point, mice were euthanized, and the pancreas was quickly removed and placed on ice in Hank balanced salt solution (HBSS) containing 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES). Tissue samples were finely minced with a scalpel and then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C. The tissue pellet was resuspended in HBSS digestion buffer containing 10 mM HEPES, 200 U/mL Collagenase 1A, 0.25 mg/mL soybean trypsin inhibitor, and 4U/mL of DNase I. Tissue samples were digested at 37 °C with shaking on a rocker platform and by mechanical dissociation by pipetting at 10-minute intervals for a total of 30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 mL cold HBSS wash buffer (5% FBS, 10 mM HEPES, and 0.25 mg/mL soybean trypsin inhibitor); the dissociated tissue was then filtered through 70 μm nylon mesh. The samples were centrifuged at 1200 rpm at 4 °C and then suspended in ammonium-chloride-potassium red blood cell lysis buffer for 3 min at room temperature. The lysis reaction was quenched with 10 mL of cold HBSS wash buffer, centrifuged, then resuspended in 2 mL of flow buffer (PBS with 2% FBS). Cells were counted, and viability recorded using trypan blue. One x 106 cells per well were dispensed in a 96-well round-bottom plate and blocked with TruStain FcX (101,320, BioLegend, San Diego, CA) at 1:50 dilution for 10 min on ice. The Fc block was removed by centrifugation, and cell pellets were resuspended and incubated in fluorophore-conjugated antibodies and LIVE/DEAD fixable blue viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L34962, 1:1000). Antimouse antibodies against CD45 (clone 30-F11 1:100), CD11b (clone M1/70 1:200), Ly6G (clone 1A8 1:300), and F4/80 (cone BM8 1:200) from Biolegend were used to stain cells. After 30 min of incubation in the dark and on ice, cells were washed 3 times with ice-cold flow buffer then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min. Samples were acquired on a 3C.A1 LSR Fortessa 1 Analyzer, and data were analyzed with FlowJo (Becton Dickinson).

Statistics

The in vivo results were compared using one-way analysis of variance and 2-tailed t-test analysis. Error bars represent the standard error of the mean or standard deviation as specified in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Reduced Gα13 Expression in Acute and Chronic Pancreatitis Samples

Initially, we evaluated Gα13 protein expression in human acute and chronic pancreatitis specimens. Compared to pancreas specimens with mild changes, Gα13 protein expression was reduced in specimens exhibiting acute and chronic pancreatitis (Figure 1A). To demonstrate that pancreatitis directly suppresses Gα13 protein expression, we treated mice with cerulein to induce acute or chronic pancreatitis. Mice were treated with 8 hourly intraperitoneal injections of the CCK analog cerulein to induce acute pancreatitis.21,22 For chronic pancreatitis, mice received intraperitoneal injections of cerulein twice daily for 14 days, and the pancreata were collected 1 day after the last cerulein injection. As expected, mice treated with cerulein to induce chronic pancreatitis demonstrated reduced amylase expression and increased CK19 expression (Figure 1C). Importantly, in agreement with the human data, we found reduced Gα13 protein expression in the acinar cells with induction of both acute and chronic pancreatitis (Figure 1B and C).

Pancreatic Deletion of Gα13 Exacerbates Acute Pancreatitis-Induced Injury to the Acinar Compartment

We next evaluated the effects of Gα13 loss on experimental pancreatitis in a GEM model. We crossed Pdx1-Cre mice to mice expressing the floxed allele of Gna13 to generate CGα13fl/+ and CGα13 fl/fl mice (referred to as CGfl/+ and CGfl/fl, respectively), which express reduced Gα13 levels in the pancreas (Figure 2A–C). Initially, we evaluated the effects of Gα13 loss on pancreas development. Gα13 loss did not affect the acinar, ductal, the islet compartments (Figure 2D), or the pancreas weights (Figure 2E). We then treated wild-type (CG+/+) and CGfl/fl mice with cerulein to induce acute pancreatitis.21,22 Blood was collected at 1 and 12 h after the last injection, and the pancreata were collected at 1, 12, and 48 h after the last injection. There was no significant difference in the body or pancreas weights between CG+/+ and CGfl/fl mice following cerulein treatment (Figure 2F and G). However, compared to the cerulein-treated CG+/+ mice, the cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice exhibit increased serum amylase levels at 1 h, with serum amylase levels decreasing in both CG+/+ and CGfl/fl mice at 12 h (Figure 2H). Moreover, the CGfl/fl mice demonstrated more pronounced histologic changes, including increased edema (evaluated by peri-acinar spacing), following cerulein treatment (Figure 2I), indicating increased acute pancreatitis-induced injury to the acinar compartment.

Pancreatic Deletion of Gα13 Mildly Exacerbates Chronic Pancreatitis

In parallel studies, we evaluated the effects of Gα13 loss on chronic pancreatitis.21,22 Chronic pancreatitis was induced by intraperitoneal injections of cerulein twice daily for 14 days, and the pancreata were collected 1 or 14 days after the last cerulein injection (Figure 4A). While mice treated with cerulein lost body weight, which they subsequently recovered, there was no significant difference in the body weights between the CG+/+ and the CGfl/fl mice (Figure 4B). However, there was a significant difference in pancreas weights between CG+/+ and CGfl/fl mice 1 day but not 14 days after the last cerulein injection (Figure 4C). There was decreased amylase staining in the 1-day samples in cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice compared to the control cerulein-treated CG+/+ mice (Figure 4D and E).

We also observed increased pancreatic infiltration by macrophages but not neutrophils in the cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice compared to the cerulein-treated CG+/+ mice (Figure 5A and B). In addition, there was no difference in the extent of fibrosis, as determined by trichrome staining (Figure 5C). Moreover, when we assessed the effects of Gα13 loss on cytokine expression 1 day after the last cerulein injection on the chronic pancreatitis protocol, we found variable effects on the cytokine expression. While there was increased Ccl5 expression in the pancreas of cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice compared to the pancreas of cerulein-treated CG+/+ control mice (Figure 5D), there was decreased expression of Cxcl1 in the pancreas of cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice compared to the pancreas of cerulein-treated CG+/+ control mice. Overall, these results indicate that loss of Gα13 mildly exacerbates chronic pancreatitis.

Rapamycin Exacerbates Chronic Pancreatitis-Induced Injury to the Acinar Compartment in Mice With Gα13 Loss

Since we found that loss of Gα13 increases mTOR signaling in the KPC GEM model,11 we next evaluated the effects of targeting mTOR using rapamycin on cerulein-induced histologic changes in the CGfl/fl mice treated with cerulein. Initially, we evaluated Rps6 phosphorylation, a downstream target of mTOR, in pancreatic specimens from mice treated on the chronic pancreatitis protocol. Compared to the control cerulein-treated CG+/+ mice, cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice demonstrate increased Rps6 phosphorylation in acinar cells (Figure 6A). The CGfl/fl mice were then treated with rapamycin daily during cerulein treatment, which was given twice daily for 2 weeks. As expected, treatment with rapamycin decreased Rps6 phosphorylation (Figure 6B). We found that rapamycin decreased amylase and CK19 levels in our model while not impacting the levels of macrophages and neutrophil in our model (Figure 6C–E). These results indicate that rapamycin exacerbates cerulein-induced injury to the acinar compartment mice with Gα13 loss.

Notably, CGfl/fl mice cotreated with rapamycin and cerulein regained body and pancreas weights to a similar extent as CGfl/fl mice treated with vehicle and cerulein once the cerulein treatment was stopped (Figure 6F and G). Histologic examination at Day 14 of recovery following cerulein treatment found no apparent difference in the pancreas between vehicle and rapamycin-treated CG+/+ and CGfl/fl mice (Figure 6H).

Rapamycin Attenuates Acute Pancreatitis-Induced Injury to the Acinar Compartment in Mice With Gα13 Loss

Finally, we evaluated the effects of rapamycin on cerulein-induced changes in the CGfl/fl mice treated with cerulein on the acute pancreatitis protocol. Compared to the control cerulein-treated CG+/+ mice, cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice demonstrate increased Rps6 phosphorylation in acinar cells (Figure 7A). The CGfl/fl mice were then pretreated with rapamycin 16 h and 1 h before 8 hourly injections of cerulein. Blood was collected 1 h after the last cerulein injection, and the pancreata were collected 1 h and 12 h after the last injection. As expected, rapamycin decreased Rps6 phosphorylation (Figure 7B). Rapamycin suppressed the increased serum amylase levels and pancreatic edema seen in the cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice (Figure 7C and D). Rapamycin had no effect on macrophages and neutrophils in the cerulein-treated CGfl/fl mice (Figure 7E). Notably, cotreatment with rapamycin did not affect the body weights of cerulein-treated mice during the recovery period (Figure 7F). There was preservation of the pancreas weights and no apparent difference in the pancreas between vehicle and rapamycin-treated CG+/+ and CGfl/fl mice at day 7 of recovery from acute pancreatitis (Figure 7G and H). These results demonstrate that rapamycin attenuates acute pancreatitis-induced injury to the acinar compartment in mice with Gα13 loss.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that Gα13 restrains pancreatitis-induced acinar cell injury, thereby maintaining pancreas homeostasis. Our data agree with the published studies showing that Gα13 restrains osteoclast-driven bone resorption in GEM models, thereby maintaining bone homeostasis.23,24 Loss of Gα13 in osteoclasts in GEM models increases NFATc1 signaling to cause bone resorption. Importantly, overexpression of Gα13 blocks bone resorption in a TNFα transgene-expressing mouse model of autoimmune arthritis and a lipopolysaccharides-injection mouse model of osteolysis.23 Also, mice with myeloid-specific deletion of Gα13 demonstrate that Gα13 restrains osteoclast function, thereby maintaining bone homeostasis.24

Importantly, while mTOR signaling functions in tissue repair and homeostasis,25,26 depending on the timing of activation, it can mediate either tissue injury or repair.27, 28, 29, 30 We found that rapamycin protects against acute pancreatitis-induced injury in mice with Gα13 loss but not against chronic pancreatitis-induced injury. Others have also shown that rapamycin ameliorates acute pancreatitis injury31, 32, 33 but worsens chronic pancreatitis-induced fibrotic changes.34 We have also found that rapamycin exacerbates chronic pancreatitis injury in mice with Gα13 loss. Our data suggest that mTOR signaling functions as a protective mechanism in chronic pancreatitis, limiting further pancreatic injury in mice with Gα13 loss.

Notably, we have found that Gα13 loss has minimal to no effects on pancreatitis-induced inflammation. Consistent with this observation, we show that the epithelial targeting of Gα13 in our GEM model has variable effects on several inflammatory cytokines following induction of acute and chronic pancreatitis, with Ccl5 increasing in both acute and chronic pancreatitis, but Cxcl1 and Il1ra decreasing in both acute and chronic pancreatitis. Others have also evaluated the role of Gα13 in regulating cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory mediators; however, these studies were conducted primarily using in vitro models. For example, Gα13 mediated the increase in IL-6 and COX-2 expression in astrocytes downstream of S1P receptor 3.9 Gα13 also mediated the increase in NLR family pyrin domain containing 3, interleukin-1 beta, and interleukin-18 in bone marrow-derived monocytes/macrophages following sphingosine-1-phosphate treatment.35 Thus, the differences between our findings and the in vitro data are likely due to the more complex in vivo microenvironment in the GEM models.

Conclusion

Overall, we show that Gα13 loss exacerbates pancreatitis-induced tissue damage, which can be attenuated by the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin in the context of acute pancreatitis. Thus, we demonstrate a protective role for Gα13 in maintaining pancreas homeostasis following acute and chronic pancreatitis-induced acinar cell injury.
