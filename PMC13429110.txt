Risk Factors for Urinary Tract Infection in Gynecological Malignant Tumor Inpatients: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Min Qiu, Heming Wu, Yun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveUrinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common infectious complications in hospitalized patients with gynecological malignancies and may lead to treatment interruption, prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and even severe systemic infection. To investigate the incidence of UTI and identify its associated risk factors among hospitalized patients with gynecological malignancies at Meizhou People’s Hospital, in order to provide evidence for targeted prevention and optimized clinical management.MethodsThis study conducted a retrospective analysis of 659 hospitalized patients with gynecological malignant tumors who were treated at Meizhou People’s Hospital in 2024. Clinical data were retrospectively collected, including general baseline data, tumor-related data, treatment-related data, and infection-related data. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to screen the risk factors for UTI.ResultsIn terms of tumor classification, cervical cancer accounted for the largest proportion, with 291 cases (44.2%), followed by endometrial carcinoma (201 cases, 30.5%), ovarian cancer (99 cases, 15.0%), and other types of gynecological malignancies (68 cases, 10.3%). Regarding treatment, 341 (51.7%) patients underwent ureteral stenting, 197 (29.9%) patients had an indwelling catheter for ≥7 days, 152 (23.1%) patients received chemotherapy, and 516 (78.3%) patients underwent surgical treatment. The incidence of UTI was 6.5% (43/659). Logistic regression analysis showed that indwelling catheter for ≥7 days (odds ratio (OR) = 4.48, 95% confidence interval (CI): 2.05–9.81, p<0.001), number of deliveries <2 (OR=2.46, 95% CI: 1.08–5.60, p=0.032), obesity (BMI≥28)(OR=3.32, 95% CI: 1.02–10.79, p=0.046) were associated with UTI.ConclusionIndwelling catheterization for ≥7 days, number of deliveries <2, and obesity are independent risk factors for UTI in patients with gynecological malignant tumors.

Introduction

Gynecologic malignant tumors are a category of diseases that pose a serious threat to women’s life and health, mainly including cervical cancer, ovarian cancer, and endometrial cancer.1 Their incidence ranks among the top in female malignant tumors and shows an increasing trend year by year.2 Gynecological malignant tumors, with high incidence and mortality, are common malignant tumors among women in China.3 The application of comprehensive treatment modalities such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy has significantly improved the survival rate of patients with gynecologic malignant tumors.4,5 However, the alterations in the body’s physiological functions caused by both the treatment process and the disease itself expose patients to the risk of various complications,6,7 among which urinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common infectious complications.8,9

The space-occupying effect of some gynecological malignant tumors (such as advanced cervical cancer and partial ovarian cancer) can directly compress the ureter or bladder, resulting in urinary tract obstruction and increased residual urine volume, creating conditions for pathogen colonization, and causing urinary tract infection in patients.10,11 In addition, surgical treatment may damage the urinary tract mucosa. Chemotherapy and radiotherapy can suppress the body’s immune function and reduce resistance to pathogens.12,13 Meanwhile, invasive procedures such as indwelling catheterization can also increase the risk of pathogen colonization and infection. Studies have shown that the incidence of UTI in gynecological cancer patients can reach 5–15%, which is an important factor leading to the interruption of treatment and causing sepsis.9–11 Moreover, factors such as patient age, underlying diseases, and variations in treatment regimens may also lead to significant individual differences in the risk of UTI occurrence. These pathological and treatment-induced urinary tract injuries collectively elevate UTI susceptibility, which underscores the necessity of our present retrospective analysis to quantify independent risk factors and guide localized infection prevention for hospitalized gynecological cancer patients.

UTI not only exacerbates patients’ physical distress and induces uncomfortable symptoms such as frequent micturition, urgent micturition, and dysuria,14 but also may result in treatment interruption, prolonged hospital stays, and increased medical cost burden.15,16 More seriously, if the infection fails to be effectively controlled in a timely manner, it may progress to severe complications such as pyelonephritis and sepsis, which further impairs patients’ quality of life and even endangers their lives.17,18 Therefore, the prevention and control of UTI in patients with gynecologic malignant tumors has become an important issue in clinical nursing and diagnosis and treatment management. Numerous international studies have confirmed that certain factors (such as prolonged indwelling catheterization and obesity) are recognized as risk factors for UTI in gynecological cancers.11,19 However, existing epidemiological data are mostly based on populations from other regions of China or overseas. There are substantial regional variations in microbial antimicrobial resistance profiles and clinical practices.20 Targeted strategies for urinary tract infection prevention should be implemented in different regions according to local patient characteristics.21 To date, there is a lack of localized clinical evidence focusing on gynecological malignant tumor inpatients in Meizhou, a southern Chinese city with unique regional dietary, living and obstetric characteristics.22,23 This study conducted a systematic analysis of the incidence of UTI and its related risk factors among gynecological cancer patients at Meizhou People’s Hospital in 2024. Clinically, this study’s local risk factor data facilitates targeted UTI intervention for gynecologic cancer inpatients; academically, it supplements regional epidemiological evidence absent in existing researches.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort

This study retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of patients with gynecologic malignancies admitted to Meizhou People’s Hospital between January 2024 and December 2024. Inclusion criteria: (1) female patients aged ≥18 years old, with pathologically confirmed gynecological malignant tumor; (2) patients scheduled to receive surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, or comprehensive anti-tumor treatment; (3) complete baseline data, laboratory tests (urinalysis, urine culture), medical history, and treatment records available for risk stratification; (4) written informed consent was obtained from all participants; and (5) patients with normal cognitive function and able to cooperate with sample collection and follow-up.

Exclusion criteria: (1) patients with active UTI or asymptomatic bacteriuria at admission (before anti-tumor treatment); (2) patients with primary urinary tract obstruction, urinary calculi, congenital urinary tract malformation and other intrinsic urinary system disorders were excluded (secondary urinary tract compression induced by gynecological malignant tumors were not excluded); (3) patients with other malignant tumors or severe systemic diseases: severe heart, liver, renal insufficiency, hematological diseases, autoimmune diseases, immunodeficiency disorders; (4) pregnant or lactating women; and (5) patients with incomplete clinical data, lost to follow-up, or unable to cooperate with the study protocol.

For this study, gynecological malignant tumors are defined as primary malignant neoplasms originating from the female reproductive system, with corresponding ICD-10 diagnostic codes specified as follows: cervical cancer (C53), endometrial cancer (C54), ovarian cancer (C56), vulvar cancer (C51), vaginal cancer (C52), fallopian tube cancer (C57), and gestational trophoblastic malignancies (O01, O02, D39).

UTI Diagnostic Criteria

The diagnostic criteria for UTI:24 (1) clinical manifestations: urinary frequency, urgency, dysuria, lower abdominal distension, lumbago, and fever (body temperature ≥38.5°C), consistent with urinary tract infection; (2) laboratory examinations: clean midstream urine bacterial culture with colony count ≥105 CFU/mL, or urine bacterial culture with colony count ≥104 CFU/mL for catheterized patients, accompanied by urine sediment microscopy showing ≥5 white blood cells per high-power field; and (3) urine specimen contamination and pseudobacteremia were excluded. The diagnosis was jointly determined by two obstetricians and gynecologists and one laboratory physician; any disagreement was resolved via group discussion to reach a final conclusion. For non-catheterized patients, clean midstream urine was collected. For patients with indwelling urinary catheters, specimens were aspirated through newly replaced catheters instead of drainage bags to avoid biofilm contamination. All samples were sent for bacterial culture and microscopic examination immediately after collection.

Data Collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system, nursing records, laboratory reports, and medical progress notes. The data were categorized into four groups: general baseline data, tumor-related data, treatment-related data, and infection-related data. Specific indicators were as follows: (1) general baseline data: age, body mass index (BMI), number of deliveries, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia), and menopausal status; (2) tumor-related data: pathological type of tumor, clinical stage, tumor differentiation grade, and metastasis status; (3) treatment-related data: surgical approach, duration of indwelling catheter, chemotherapy regimen and cycles, radiotherapy status, and antibiotic use; and (4) infection-related data: time of UTI occurrence, type of pathogenic bacteria, and infection outcome.

Statistical Analysis

Data were statistically processed and analyzed using SPSS 26.0. Univariate analysis was performed using Chi-square test (or Fisher’s exact test) to explore the relationship between each factor and the occurrence of UTI. Before multivariate logistic regression, multicollinearity of all candidate variables was evaluated using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF). All variables had VIF values less than 5, revealing no obvious multicollinearity. All potential confounding factors including age, BMI, parity, comorbidities, tumor type and treatment modalities were entered into univariate analysis. Variables with p<0.05 were included in the multivariate Logistic regression model to adjust for confounding effects. A forward stepwise regression approach was then performed with αin=0.05 and αout=0.10. p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For underlying comorbidities, 202 patients (30.7%) were complicated with hypertension, 107 patients (16.2%) had diabetes, 143 patients (21.7%) presented with anemia, and 79 patients (12.0%) suffered from hypoproteinemia. Stratified by age, 380 patients (57.7%) were over 60 years old, while 279 patients (42.3%) were aged 60 years or younger. According to BMI stratification, the majority of patients had normal BMI ranging from 18.5 to 28 (592 cases, 89.8%); 36 patients (5.5%) were underweight (BMI ≤18.5), and 31 patients (4.7%) were obese (BMI ≥28).

In terms of obstetric history, 562 patients (85.3%) had a pregnancy history of ≥2 times, whereas 96 patients (14.6%) had fewer than 2 pregnancies. Regarding interventional and therapeutic procedures, 341 patients (51.7%) received ureteral stent implantation, 197 patients (29.9%) had an indwelling urinary catheter for 7 days or longer, 152 patients (23.1%) underwent chemotherapy, and 516 patients (78.3%) received surgical treatment.

Discussion

This study focuses on the core issues of risk stratification and prevention and control of UTI in patients with gynecological malignant tumors. Through systematic analysis of clinically relevant factors, it identifies indwelling catheterization for ≥7 days, number of deliveries <2, and obesity as independent risk factors for UTI occurrence. These findings not only deepen the understanding of the pathogenesis of UTI in patients with gynecological malignant tumors, but also provide strong evidence-based medical support for formulating precise clinical prevention and control strategies.

Indwelling catheterization for ≥7 days was identified as the main independent risk factor for UTI, which is consistent with previous studies. Patients with gynecological malignancies often require long-term catheterization owing to surgical trauma and postoperative urinary dysfunction.25 As a foreign body, the catheter impairs the urethral mucosal barrier and facilitates bacterial ascension.11,26 Prolonged catheterization also promotes biofilm formation, which protects bacteria from antibiotics and immune clearance, and causes mucosal ischemia, further increasing infection risk.27,28 Therefore, shortening catheter indwelling time and enhancing catheter care are crucial for preventing UTI in clinical practice.

Chemotherapy, a core treatment for gynecological malignancies, is associated with an increased risk of UTI through complex mechanisms. Traditionally, chemotherapy is considered to be associated with an increased risk of infection due to myelosuppression, impaired immune function, and frequent invasive procedures. Chemotherapeutic agents, including platinum and taxanes, can severely impair the immune system, resulting in reduced neutrophil counts and compromised immune function, which in turn increases susceptibility to infection.29 Chemotherapy induces immunosuppression, markedly reducing patients’ resistance to pathogenic microorganisms.30 Meanwhile, the gastrointestinal toxicity of chemotherapeutic agents causes nausea, vomiting, diarrhea and other adverse reactions, leading to dehydration and electrolyte imbalance. These disturbances impair the normal physiological function of the urinary system and elevate infection risk.31 Furthermore, certain chemotherapeutic drugs can damage renal tubules, disrupt renal filtration and excretion, and further promote the progression of infection.32,33 In the present study, the incidence of UTI in patients who received chemotherapy was lower than that in patients without chemotherapy, which was contrary to the findings of some previous studies. Several potential explanations may account for this unexpected observation in our cohort. First, patients receiving chemotherapy in our study may have received more intensive perioperative or peri-procedural urinary tract care, infection surveillance, and prophylactic interventions (such as prophylactic antibiotics), which may have offset the infection risk related to immunosuppression. Second, the baseline characteristics, underlying diseases, and indications for chemotherapy differed between the two groups, which may have introduced confounding effects on the occurrence of UTI. Third, the relatively small sample size and single-center design may limit the comparability between groups and contribute to the inconsistent result. Therefore, the relationship between chemotherapy and the risk of UTI is complex and may be affected by multiple clinical factors. Finally, patients receiving chemotherapy may have lower rates of urinary catheter placement and shorter catheter indwelling time, a major risk factor for urinary tract infection. Regrettably, we cannot statistically verify these conjectures with internal study data within this retrospective cohort. Future prospective studies with complete relevant clinical documentation are warranted to validate the underlying drivers of this association.

Number of deliveries <2 was identified as an independent risk factor for UTI in patients with gynecological malignancies, and this finding carries important clinical implications. From an anatomical perspective, nulliparous and oligoparous women tend to have a relatively shorter urethral length and different anatomical configuration of the periurethral tissue compared with multiparous individuals, which may facilitate the ascending migration of periurethral flora into the urinary tract.34 In terms of voiding behaviors, women with fewer childbirths may have distinct habitual voiding patterns, such as infrequent urination or urinary holding, which increase residual urine and create a favorable environment for bacterial proliferation.35 These inherent anatomical characteristics and voiding behavioral differences collectively explain the higher UTI susceptibility observed among patients with fewer deliveries in our cohort. Notably, some studies have proposed that multiple deliveries might impair the physiological barrier of the genital tract, potentially increasing susceptibility to urinary tract invasion by pathogenic microorganisms.36,37 Collectively, these findings indicate that obstetric history should be incorporated into UTI risk stratification for patients with gynecological malignancies. For women with fewer than 2 deliveries, targeted evaluation and training of pelvic floor muscle function, together with education on proper voiding habits to reduce urinary stasis, may help decrease the risk of UTI. Nevertheless, the precise biological mechanism underlying the association between the number of deliveries and UTI risk remains to be fully elucidated. Further well-designed studies are warranted to verify this relationship and clarify its underlying pathogenesis.

This study confirms the significant correlation between obesity and UTI risk in patients with gynecological malignancies. Excessive adipose tissue accumulation alters pelvic anatomy, compresses the urinary tract, impairs urinary drainage, and increases residual urine volume.38 Obesity is also associated with metabolic disturbances including insulin resistance and chronic low‑grade inflammation.39,40 Chronic inflammation disrupts inflammatory pathways and compromises the urethral mucosal barrier, while insulin resistance impairs immune cell function and reduces microbial clearance. Moreover, abundant perineal fat in obese patients promotes bacterial colonization and facilitates pathogenic invasion of the urethra.41 These results indicate that weight control should be emphasized in the comprehensive management of these patients. Weight reduction via rational diet and moderate exercise to improve metabolic disorders, combined with enhanced perineal care to maintain local cleanliness and dryness, is critical for lowering UTI incidence.

Based on the identified independent risk factors in this study, a risk stratification system for UTI in patients with gynecological malignant tumors can be established. For patients with indwelling catheterization for ≥7 days, the priority is to optimize catheter care procedures and shorten the indwelling duration; and for obese patients, weight management and local care should be implemented. It can achieve precise prevention and control of UTI, thereby avoiding overtreatment and improving the efficiency of prevention and control.

This study still has certain limitations. Firstly, this study was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, and the patients included in the analysis were those who received treatment in our hospital and did not include patients who were managed solely through outpatient care. Additionally, this may have limited the generalizability of our research results. Secondly, in-depth analysis of the interaction between various risk factors was not conducted, and the impact of the combined effect of different risk factors on UTI occurrence risk still needs further investigation. Thirdly, due to the retrospective design, detailed information regarding the classification of urinary tract infections (upper versus lower tract) and the microbiological profile of uropathogens was not available in the medical records and could not be systematically analyzed. These data would have provided further insights into the clinical and etiological characteristics of UTI. Therefore, future prospective studies with complete microbiological and clinical documentation are warranted to validate and extend our findings. Meanwhile, based on the findings of this study, targeted prevention and control measures should be developed and verified to provide a more comprehensive UTI prevention and control protocol for clinical practice.

Conclusion

Indwelling catheterization for ≥7 days, number of deliveries <2, and obesity are independent risk factors for UTI in patients with gynecological malignant tumors. Clinically, a risk stratification system should be established based on these risk factors, and individualized prevention and control strategies implemented to reduce the incidence of UTI and improve patient prognosis. Furthermore, additional high-quality studies are warranted to further clarify the pathogenesis of UTI and optimize prevention and control measures, thus providing stronger evidence for the comprehensive clinical management of patients with gynecological malignancies.
