Abstract:
The DOE‐BER “EvoNet” project investigates the genetic and molecular basis of plant resilience
in extreme environments. We do this by identifying key genes that enable “extreme survivor”
species to thrive in the nitrogen-poor soils of Chile’s hyper-arid Atacama Desert. Our collections
focus on 32 Atacama extremophile species, including seven grass species with potential biofuel
applications. To identify genes-of-importance to survival we compared genomic and
transcriptomic profiles of extremophile species that thrive in the Atacama to those of closely
related “sister” species from nitrogen-rich arid and mesic regions of California. Deep RNA
sequencing and de novo transcriptome assembly across these triplet species sets supported a
phylogenomic framework for identifying positively selected genes associated with adaptive
divergence. Our integrative analysis combined ecological and environmental data,
metagenomics, evolutionary and systems biology, and metabolomics. This enabled us to create
an unprecedented framework for systematically understanding how non-model plants have
adapted to survive in extreme conditions. Our resulting database of positively selected ortholog
groups in the extremophile plants offers promising targets for engineering crop and biofuel
species with enhanced resilience to drought and extreme weather. Additionally, our newest
dataset explores and exploits a complementary metabolomic approach. This new aspect provides
innovative strategies to manipulate plant cell metabolism, further supporting efforts to improve
agricultural productivity in the face of extreme climates. Importantly, our combined
evolutionary- and metabolomic-based strategies focused on convergent patterns of adaptation,
providing a genetic and metabolomic toolkit for improving crop and biofuel resilience across
diverse plant species. Finally, our novel exploration of ecological and evolutionary dynamics
delivered to the community a phylogenomic computational pipeline called “PhyloGeneious.”
Our continued adaptations of this pipeline are publicly available to expedite evolutionary
genomic research for future scientific discoveries. In total, our DOE-BER has provided genomic,
metabolomic, and computational strategies to understand how extremophile plants provide
evolutionary and physiological targets for improving agricultural and biofuel production.

Introduction/Major Goals of the Project:
 The goal of our DOE‐BER “EvoNet” project is to identify the genetic basis of plant
adaptation to marginal nutrient poor soils, extreme drought, and extreme irradiance conditions.
This DOE BER project exploits the genomes of “extreme survivor” plants adapted to grow and
thrive in marginal, nitrogen (N) poor soils of the Andes mountain range in Chile’s Atacama
Desert. Despite living in the driest non-polar desert with the world’s highest levels of damaging
radiation, plants from across most Angiosperm clades have adapted to grow in these conditions.
Through DOE-BER funding for EvoNet, we employed and adapted our in-suite phylogenomic
pipeline “PhyloGeneious” [1]. This phylogenomic pipeline enables species phylogeny,
orthology, and positive-selection identification of genes using a novel “triplet‐species”
sampling strategy from our own collections and botanical identification of wild plants. This
pipeline enabled us to identify the genes and regulatory patterns that distinguish Atacama

“extreme survivors” from their closest drought‐adapted (but N‐replete) and non‐adapted sister
species from California. These “extreme survivors” which grow in arid, low‐N soils broadly
cover the main branches in the flowering plants, including 7 monocot species, whose physiology
is relevant to major plant biofuels.
We aimed to enrich studies of plant stress-resilience, which have previously relied on relatively
non-stress adapted plants like the model Arabidopsis thaliana. While studies focused on
N‐adaptation mechanisms within a single model species using traditional genetic approaches are
powerful, they miss an evolutionary perspective
of these adaptations ‐ which is the focus of the
EvoNet project [1]. We accomplished this by
investigating the transcriptomes, regulatory

extremophile plants in their  native

project (Fig 1) was developed by the co‐PIs of
this grant with expertise in plant systematics

Importantly, our phylogenomic pipeline has

of the PhyloGeneious phylogenomic pipeline
lies in its ability to discover evolutionary

Through EvoNet, we applied the
PhyloGeneious pipeline to a set of “triplet‐
sister species”. This included plants adapted to
low‐N and drought (Atacama) vs. two sister
species from California, one drought adapted,
and the other a mesic species, which grow
under N‐replete conditions. We note that Chile
and California are two of the six floristic

controls for seasonal fluctuations in rainfall and
temperature, specifically hot, dry summer and
cool, wet winters. This triplet‐species

phylogenomic approach could identify genes associated with adaptation to growth in marginal
low‐N and dry soils. Based on our current studies, this phylogenomic analysis has identified a
set of hundreds of genes that show the phylogenetic signature of adaptation to low‐N and
drought across multiple species triplets.

In a systems biology approach, we have also connected the genes underlying the divergence of
the Atacama species. This is based on their phylogenetic signature for adaptation to grow in
low‐N arid environments. We have also connected these genes into a gene interaction network
that includes metabolic, regulatory and protein interactions [4]. The strength of combining
network approaches with phylogenomic signatures is three‐pronged: First, it identifies modules
of genes that work together and will help discover new components of known pathways (e.g.,
N-assimilation). Second, it identifies key regulators of the gene network modules as network
hubs (e.g. transcription factors (TFs) regulating the genes required for the adaptation
mechanisms). Third, it is important to note that the vast majority of species included in this
project have never been studied at a genomic or molecular interaction level. Therefore, there is
little functional information on the roles of genes in these marginal survivors and even less on
the gene network connectivity. Thus, we will transfer extensive “network knowledge” from the
data rich model Arabidopsis to these poorly‐studied Atacama “extreme survivor” species, by
exploiting the ortholog grouping enabled by our PhyloGeneious phylogenomic pipeline [2].

We accomplished our goals in four related aims:

Aim 1. Plant collection of “Extreme survivors”: Species collection and sequencing: We use a
“triplet sampling” strategy to capture genes involved in adaptations of “extreme survivors”. This
involves comparing species which grow on dry, N‐poor soils (Atacama)to their drought‐adapted
“sister” species that grow on N‐replete soils (CA), or vs. “mesic” CA sister species not adapted
to drought.
Aim 2. Phylogenomic and Network Analysis: (A) Perform phylogenomic analysis of the
“extreme survivors” in a “triplet‐sister species sampling” strategy to identify genes supporting
evolution of adaptations to marginal, dry and low‐N soils. (B) Perform network analysis based
on gene expression and interactions.
Aim 3. Integrate Phylogenomic and Network signatures: Combine phylogenomic signals and
gene networks to prioritize candidate genes associated with adaptations to marginal, dry and
low‐N soils.
Aim 4. Functional validation of candidate genes: Functionally validate top‐ranked candidate
genes for low‐N adaptations in the model plant Arabidopsis. Future applications may transfer
genes from an extremophile Atacama tomato (Solanum chilense) to domesticated tomato
(Solanum lycopersicum) for improvement of crop resilience.

 Project outcome: This
 DOE EvoNet project
 addresses the challenge
 of generating biomass
 feedstock for biofuels
 on marginal, nutrient
 poor soils. By
 exploiting marginal
 soils for plant growth,
 this approach would not
 disrupt the use of fertile
 lands to increase supply
 of crops needed to feed
 worldwide food
 demand, and also
 conserve energy and
 protect the environment.
 An early study into the
 use of ‘marginal lands’
 for biofuel production
 [5], suggested an
 opportunity to
 supplement the current
 biofuel production
 model of monoculture
 biofuel crops, by
 generating substantial
biomass from marginal lands. That study [5] projected that such a model which exploits marginal
soils would also be advantageous because it would have low economic/agricultural input and a
net carbon offset. A counter argument to this claim, is that the fertilizer inputs needed for
continued production of biofuel crops in marginal soils would negate the economic and
environmental gains [6]. However, the main benefit of using marginal lands is that it avoids land
use competition with food crops, thus circumventing the food vs. fuel debate.
The two main challenges to plant growth in marginal soils are the low availability of water and
macronutrients, primarily nitrogen, (N) [7,8]. These two deficiencies tend to co‐localize in dry,
sandy soils and the combined effect of low‐N and drought is especially detrimental to yield [8].
In our EvoNet study, we identified key molecular players involved in the adaptation to
growth in marginal soils that are low in a key nutrient ‐N and within a water‐poor
environment. The genes identified in these “extreme survivor” species will be ideal transgenes
to generate transgenic biofuel crops that can thrive on marginal nutrient poor soils, with lower

 5

levels of applied fertilizer. Conversely, the identification of the role of these genes can be used
to fine‐map loci in existing natural populations of biofuel crops and thus hasten molecular
breeding in these crops. Therefore, by combining the knowledge of genes crucial to survival in
marginal low‐N soils and the adapted genes from the “extreme survivor” species this project will
usher in the development of biofuel crops that can thrive in marginal nutrient poor soils.

Outcomes:
Identification of Candidate Drought Resilience Genes:
In our field studies, we characterized climate, soil, plant, and soil–microbe diversity at 22 sites
(every 100 m of altitude) along the Talabre–Lejia transect (TLT) in the Atacama of Chile over a
10-year period, the results of which were published in Eshel et al. 2021 [1] (Fig. 1). We
quantified drought, nutrient deficiencies, large diurnal temperature oscillations, and pH gradients
that define three distinct vegetational belts along the altitudinal cline (Fig. 2).

Atacama plant species in the TLT span 10 major plant clades across the three vegetation belts
(Fig. 2). Analysis of environmental data found that overall, plants growing in Atacama cope with
prolonged droughts, while taking advantage of very short and unpredictable periods of increased
water availability. They also survive in high temperatures, both above- and below ground, and
are exposed to
extremely high
solar radiation
during the day, and
chilling to freezing
at night [9].
Nitrogen (N), the
quantitatively most
essential
macronutrient,was
extremely low in
all tested soil
samples. Total N
content fluctuated
between 8.51 and
22.56 g/m3 (5.32
and 14.1 mg/kg,
respectively),
which is about five
times lower than
the global mean N
content for warm

temperate deserts [10], and 1.4 times lower than the minimal agricultural reference range for this
nutrient [11].

We assessed soil microbes by metabarcoding sequencing by comparing root-zone soil to
proximal local soil of 32 species (Fig 3) . We found enrichment of C- and N-cycling functions of
soil bacteria associated with plant roots (Fig 3C). We detected N-fixing bacteria associated
exclusively in the root zone of 10 Atacama species.
Overall, we found more than a two-fold enrichment in N-fixing bacteria near plant roots, for 18
Atacama species. We validated this discovery through quantifying amplicons of a subunit of the

 7

nitrogenase gene (nifH) to confirm the existence of active N-fixation occurring in plant results
(Fig 3D). Excitingly, the majority of these Atacama species are not legumes, where N-fixing
associations have been well-known through the plants recruitment of bacteria to rhizobia. This
result indicates that some Atacama plant species across lineages developed an active N-fixing
bacteria recruitment/facilitation mechanism to optimize N acquisition under the extremely low N
Atacama soils, which can be applied to studies of monocots.

We deep sequenced transcriptomes of 32 dominant plant species spanning the major plant clades
in the Atacama, and found their transcriptomes were enriched in genes for stress responses,
metabolism, and energy production (Fig 4, 5). To identify genes associated with plant adaptation
to harsh environments, we compared 32 Atacama species with the 32 closest sequenced species,
comprising 70 taxa and 1,686,950 proteins (Fig 4). To perform phylogenomic reconstruction, we
concatenated 15,972 ortholog groups into a supermatrix of 8,599,764 amino acids. Using two
codon based methods, we identified 265 candidate positively selected genes (PSGs) in the
Atacama plants, 64% of which are located in Pfam domains, supporting their functional
relevance (Fig 5).

"Triplet Species" Transcriptome Assemblies: To further increase our precision to discover
adaptations of wild plants to extreme environments, we developed a “sister species” sampling
approach across Mediterranean floristic provinces. Our goal is to identify the genes that evolved
to enable “extreme survivor” species of the Atacama to thrive on dry, N-poor soils (Atacama) in
their actual and complex environments. To meet this goal, we have successfully collected 28
triplet “sister” species (a total 84 species) that span 10 major plant families composed of
Atacama species (adapted to drought & low-N) and related “sister” species in California (CA).
For each triplet group, one CA sister was collected in a drought (xeric) environment, while the
other was from a non-drought environment (mesic). Both CA sister sites however have higher
nutrition and less irradiance stress that is prevalent for these extremophiles in the Atacama. We
made RNA-seq libraries and assembled the transcripts into gene models for the 84 species to fuel

 10

our phylogenomic analysis. We have generated high‐quality and >80% complete transcriptomes
for all 84 plant species (Fig 7). In addition, we have also collected organ specific data for a
subset of Atacama species with an average BUSCO completeness of 92%. In total, our updated
transcriptome data expands the genetic resources of biologically-diverse non-model organisms in
the context of their natural environments, which will improve biological discoveries that cannot
be found in laboratory environments.

Improved Phylogenomic and Positive Selection Analysis: With our gene content data annotated
from our collected transcriptomes for the 84 triplet species, we performed large-scale
phylogenomic analysis. This enabled us to identify genes supporting evolution of adaptations to
marginal, dry and low-N soils in the Atacama species – compared to their close relatives in CA.
We performed this analysis for each of the major plant families, providing: Poaceae (Grasses,
whose valuable crops members include rice and maize), Fabaceae (Legumes), and Lamiids
(Solanaceae, including tomato), and Asteraceae (sunflower).

In order to accomplish this analysis , we developed the phylogenomic pipeline PhyloGeneious
[1] (Fig 8). In our current application of the PhyloGeneious pipeline, we identified genes whose
encoded protein sequences have evolved specifically in each of the three environment (Atacama,
CA Xeric and CA Mesic) with varying levels of adaptation to drought and Low N. Several of the
Atacama species are close relatives to current crops (e.g., tomato), which enable us to more
readily translate our discoveries to agricultural improvement. In fact, our latest collections and
investigations of Solanum chilense (close tomato relative growing in the Atacama) aims to
transfer stress-resilience from an extremophile wild plant to domesticated tomato. This would
enable us to create stress-resilient crops based on the systems biology analyses of this wild
relative. In the model plant Arabidopsis, we have been finalizing “field-to-bench" studies to test
if transferring the “Atacama” versions of these evolved genes to Arabidopsis will alter
stress-resilience regulatory networks and make plants more resilient to drought and low-N stress
in lab experiments. Furthermore, our future plans to transfer these Atacama “extremophile
genes” into tomato crops will impact crop production on marginal N-poor and dry soils. It will
also be useful for growing biofuel crops on marginal soils in the US, which will increase CO2
sequestration and ameliorate harmful effects of N-fertilizer production and overuse.

We identified lineage‐specific genes in our data set. We therefore decided to conduct separate
phylogenomic analyses within each of the five major plant groups in our data set: Poaceae,
Fabaceae, Caryophyllales, and to the two Asterids clades: Lamiids and Campanulids (Fig 9).
Using the PhyloGeneious pipeline, we identified 24,568, 22,284, 11,102, 18,858 and 49,969
ortholog groups for each of these plant groups that contain an ortholog from 4 or more species, in
each clade analysis, respectively. These ortholog groups were used to develop an aligned
supermatrix of ortholog sequences which was the input data to develop phylogenetic trees for
each plant family (Fig 8). The total evidence (TE) tree was reconstructed using both parsimony

 11

and Maximum Likelihood (using JTT, WAG and LG models) approaches, which recovered
consistent topologies for the Poaceae, Fabaceae, Caryophyllales, and the Lamiids clades (Fig 8).

Improved Positive Selection Analysis: To uncover the most relevant genes for adaptation to the
Atacama Desert, we calculated the following measures for each ortholog group: Positive
Selection (PS) Analysis (dN/dS) was determined using the HyPhy pipeline [12] which quantifies
evolutionary pressures acting on protein coding genes (Fig. 8). HyPhy utilized two methods to
test branches together and individually to identify positive selected ortholog groups (PSOGs) that
are exclusive and reoccurring (found in multiple genera). Studying recurring patterns of PS is
powerful for engineering plant resilience, as the same PSOGs show evidence of extremophile
adaptation across independent lineages. This increases their likelihood of improving stress
resilience in diverse phylogenetic contexts. We also tested for PS signals that occur at specific
amino acid sites, allowing us to pinpoint extremophile adaptations in the context of the protein
structure, which improves our hypotheses on protein mechanisms. Using the VirtualPlant
platform19 developed by the Coruzzi lab, network analysis of the Arabidopsis thaliana (“At”)
orthologs for each set of PSOGs was conducted to predict regulatory connections of the PSOGs.
VirtualPlant integrates At genome-wide data concerning the known and predicted relationships of

 12

genes, proteins and molecules to generate interaction networks. Furthermore, we developed
novel bioinformatic software to improve functional enrichment studies of orthologs for
non-model organisms. This was necessary since most Gene Ontology (GO) Enrichment Analysis
methods are designed for work with species with complete genomic references. To conduct GO
enrichment of recently assembled, non-model transcriptomes, we developed a pipeline that
generates ortholog databases for each non-model species using protein annotations from
InterProScan and EggNOG.20-21 By inventing a novel OrthoGAnno.R package, the Coruzzi lab
has integrated functional annotations with ortholog databases for each phylogenetic family.
PSOGs are then subject to enrichment analysis using the TopGO package in R with modification
to identify enriched biologically relevant GO processes.22-24 Thus, the OrthoGAnno.R script we
have developed is a powerful new tool for the field of evolutionary genomics since it extends
functional enrichment analyses from the classical approach of one-species at a time, to
comprehensive inclusion of orthologous species’ databases.

Integrate Phylogenomic and Network signatures: In this aim, we combined phylogenomic signals
and gene networks to prioritize candidate genes associated with adaptations to marginal, dry, and
low-N soils. These methods have allowed us to identify candidate drought tolerance genes to
functionally validate in planta including 3 transcription factors hubs with known stress tolerance

functions in Arabidopsis. An example network of PSOGs with Arabidopsis orthologs in Atacama
lamiids is shown in Fig. 10. Atacama versions of these TFs vs. their Arabidopsis orthologs are

Identify genomic and metabolomic adaptations of extremophile plants.
​ The plants from the Atacama pertain to various Angiosperm lineages, each with unique
relevance to agriculture and biofuel production. However, our discovery of convergently derived
metabolic strategies and compounds that confer generalized tolerance to harsh environments can
present universal strategies to improve agricultural productivity.

 Therefore, as a complementary
 approach to positive selection
 analysis, NYU, PUCC, and Purdue
 university collaborated to
 understand how extremophile
 Atacama plants coordinately
 rewired their metabolism and
 genomes to adapt to the extreme
 Atacama environment [13] This was
 conducted through metabolic
 pathway reconstruction and
 enrichment of the 32 Atacama
 species in comparison to (as in
 Eshel et al. 2021) the 32 publicly
 sister species that were not adapted
 to extreme environments and had
 publicly available transcriptomes.
5,006 annotated reactions were detected from the transcriptome data of the 64 species using a
metabolic function software called PathwayTools [14]. Then, copy number variation of the
different reactions assigned to each species’ annotated transcriptomes was used to find cases
where Atacama plants have expanded the number of genes encoding a specific reaction in
comparison to the non-adapted sisters. A set of 2,507 biochemical reactions were enriched at
>3-fold copy number in an Atacama vs. sister species. Of these, shared biochemical reaction
enrichments–or those that had expanded in >50% of the 32 Atacama vs. sister species
comparisons–represented well-known members of primary or secondary metabolism that play a
role in plant stress resilience (Fig 11). For instance, the converged reaction number expansions
included reactions related to biosynthesis of terpenes, flavonoids, and polyamines, all which

 14

have been previously associated with plant stress-tolerance pathways (Fig 11). Therefore, our
comparative approach of biochemical pathway annotation and gene copy number enrichment
indicated that multiple extremophile plants across diverse lineages evolved to expand the same
metabolic pathways to survive the harsh environments. This supports our claim that targeting
generalized metabolic pathways to rewiring plant metabolism may increase stress resilience
across diverse plant crops and biofuels.
 Furthermore, we improved the significance of biochemical pathway enrichment by
finding connections of metabolic pathway reaction expansion to in planta identified metabolites
of wild-collected plants. Researchers at PUCC collected 24 plant species from the Atacama TLT
field site and conducted untargeted metabolomics to identify compound patterns that conferred
stress-resilience across diverse lineages [13]. Using machine learning, PUCC and Purdue
University researchers identified 39 metabolites that predicted the environmental variation of the
 Atacama field site independent of
 species identity. The predictive
 environmental variation
 encompasses the precipitation and
 temperature gradients of the site,
 emphasizing these metabolites’
 importance for stress resilience
 [13].
 Next to link genomic and
 metabolomic innovations of
 extremophile plants, we
 incorporated the quantitative
 variation per species of metabolite

 environmental-predictive

 expanded reaction numbers (Fig

 across 16 Atacama species that
 contained both wild-sampled

 correlations (Pearson r > 0.7,

(Fig 12). 457 of these reaction-to-metabolite relationships included Atacama-enriched reactions,
with metabolites like D-alanyl-D-alanine, quercetin 3-(6''-ferulylglucoside), and cucurbitoside F
having the greatest number of Atacama-enriched reaction correlations (104, 85, and 69
correlations, respectively, Fig 12B). One of the most exciting aspects of this correlation analysis,
is that the expanded reactions-to-metabolite correlations often occurred convergently in unrelated

occurred due to the independent genomic expansions of genes in the triterpene biosynthesis
pathway. From these and the published reaction-to-metabolite correlation in our published paper,
we show how convergent extremophile evolution presents strategies for metabolomic rewiring
that can be applied to diverse plant families.

Conclusion:
​ Our integrative analysis of ecological & environmental data, metagenomics, and
evolutionary & systems biology created an unprecedented framework for systematically
understanding how several non-model Atacama plant species respond to and thrive in extreme
environments. The resultant database of positively-selected ortholog groups in these Atacama
extremophile plants presents future targets for engineering crop and biofuel species to be resilient
to extreme weather and drought events that greatly inhibit crop yields and farmer outcomes. The
convergence of positively selected genes and metabolic accumulation patterns across distinct
Atacama plant lineages increases the likelihood of success in engineering programs. This is
because certain ortholog groups and metabolites are likely to function as mechanistic sources of
resilience across diverse phylogenies. Furthermore, the metabolomic approach provides cutting
edge strategies to identify metabolic pathways whose manipulation will likely enhance
stress-resilience in diverse lineages. Based on our own discoveries from this DOE BER grant
cycle, we are continuing to explore the ability of Atacama-derived gene sequences to confer
stress tolerance in Arabidopsis and the crop tomato to present future avenues of enhancing
growth on marginal soils.
