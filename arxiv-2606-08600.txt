Crescents and the real variable Cesàro operator
Anil Belli, Ugur Gul, William T. Ross, Aristomenis G. Siskakis

Abstract
. This paper explores a version of the classical Cesàro integral operator for the Lebesgue space L p ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{p}(0,1) where we discuss its norm, spectral properties, cyclicity, and invariant subspaces. The spectrum of the Cesàro operator will be a crescent domain whose geometry depends on p p . An important tool will be semigroups of weighted composition operators on L p ​ ( 0 , 1 ) L^{p}(0,1) .

2.
Semigroups and boundedness

Sources for the properties of semigroups of bounded linear operators used below are
[
4
,
5
,
10
]
. In our setting, we explore a semigroup of weighted composition operators on
L^{p}(0,1)
.
Fix
t\geqslant 0
and define
\varphi_{t}
by

\varphi_{t}(x):=\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1},\quad 0\leqslant x\leqslant 1.

Each
\varphi_{t}
satisfies
\varphi_{t}(0)=0
,
\varphi_{t}(1)=1
,
\varphi_{t}
is strictly increasing and continuous and thus maps
[0,1]
bijectively onto
[0,1]
, and
\varphi_{t}(x)\leqslant x
for all
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
and
t\geqslant 0
(see Fig.
3
). When
t<0
, observe that
\varphi_{t}
still maps
[0,1]
bijectively onto
[0,1]
but
\varphi_{t}(x)\geqslant x
for all
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
(see Fig.
3
). One can verify that
\varphi_{s}\circ\varphi_{t}=\varphi_{s+t}
for all
s
and
t
.

For each
t\geqslant 0
define the
weighted composition operator

S_{t}
on
L^{p}(0,1)
by

(2.1)

(S_{t}f)(x):=\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{x}f(\varphi_{t}(x)),\quad 0<x<1.

A calculation shows that
S_{s}\circ S_{t}=S_{s+t}
and so
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
forms a semigroup.
We now prove that each
S_{t}
defines a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}(0,1)
as well as estimate
\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

For the semigroup
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
we have the following.

(a)

If
1<p<2
then
e^{-\frac{t}{p}}\leqslant\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{t}{q}}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

(b)

If
2\leqslant p<\infty
then
e^{-\frac{t}{q}}\leqslant\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{t}{p}}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Below use the change of variables

x=\varphi_{-t}(u)=\frac{e^{t}u}{(e^{t}-1)u+1},\quad dx=\frac{e^{t}}{((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{2}}du.

Thus, for any
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\|S_{t}f\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{x}f(\varphi_{t}(x))\right|^{p}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{p}}{(\frac{e^{t}u}{(e^{t}-1)u+1})^{p}}|f(u)|^{p}\frac{e^{t}}{((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{2}}\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{p}((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{p}}{u^{p}e^{tp}}|f(u)|^{p}\frac{e^{t}}{((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{2}}\,du,

\displaystyle=e^{t(1-p)}\int_{0}^{1}|f(u)|^{p}((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{p-2}\,du.

Now observe

(2.4)

1\leqslant(e^{t}-1)u+1\leqslant e^{t}\;\text{for all $t\geqslant 0$ and $0\leqslant u\leqslant 1$}.

which says that

(2.5)

e^{-t}\leqslant\frac{1}{(e^{t}-1)u+1}\leqslant 1.

When
1<p<2
, (
2
) now implies

e^{-\frac{t}{p}}\leqslant\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{t}{q}}\;\mbox{for all $t\geqslant 0$},

which proves (a).
When
2\leqslant p<\infty
, (
2
) and (
2.4
) give us

e^{-\frac{t}{q}}\leqslant\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{t}{p}}\;\mbox{for all $t\geqslant 0$},

which proves (b) and thus completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

For each
1<p<\infty
the semigroup
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is strongly continuous on
L^{p}(0,1)
in that

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\|S_{t}f-f\|_{p}=0,\quad f\in L^{p}(0,1).

Proof.

Fix
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
and
\varepsilon>0
. The density of the continuous functions on
[0,1]
in
L^{p}(0,1)
produce a continuous
g
on
[0,1]
with
\|f-g\|_{p}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Since
\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant 1
for all
t\geqslant 0
and
1<p<\infty
(Proposition
2.2
) we have

\displaystyle\|S_{t}f-f\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|S_{t}f-S_{t}g\|_{p}+\|S_{t}g-g\|_{p}+\|g-f\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\|f-g\|_{p}+\|S_{t}g-g\|_{p}+\|g-f\|_{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant 1\cdot\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}+\|S_{t}g-g\|_{p}+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\epsilon+\|S_{t}g-g\|_{p}.

Since
\varphi_{t}(x)\to x
as
t\to 0^{+}
for each
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
and thus
S_{t}g\to g
pointwise on
[0,1]
and

|S_{t}g(x)|\leqslant e^{-t}\max_{0\leqslant u\leqslant 1}|g(u)|

(note the use of (
2.5
)), we can use the continuity of
g
, along with the dominated convergence theorem, to see that

S_{t}g\to g
in the norm of
L^{p}(0,1)
as
t\to 0^{+}
. This detail and the estimate above gives us

\varlimsup_{t\to 0^{+}}\|S_{t}f-f\|_{p}\leqslant\varepsilon,

from which the result follows.
∎

Also important is the quantity

\omega_{0}=\omega_{0}(\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}):=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\|S_{t}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}}{t}

[
4
, p. 251]
. As a consequence of Proposition
2.2
,

(2.7)

\omega_{0}\leqslant\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{q}&\mbox{if $1<p<2$,}\\[4.0pt]
-\frac{1}{p}&\mbox{if $p\geqslant 2$.}\end{cases}

The semigroup
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
has an
infinitesimal generator

A_{p}
defined by

A_{p}f:=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{S_{t}f-f}{t}

whenever this limit exists in the norm of
L^{p}(0,1)
. Define
\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
, the
domain
of
A_{p}
, to be the set of all
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
for which this limit exists. Known theory of strongly continuous semigroups says that
\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
is a dense linear manifold of
L^{p}(0,1)
and
A_{p}
is a closed operator, meaning that
\mathcal{D}(A_{p})
, endowed with the norm
\|f\|_{p}+\|A_{p}f\|_{p}
, is a Banach space
[
5
, Ch. 2, §10]
. Moreover, for appropriately smooth
f
, we have

(2.8)

(A_{p}f)(x)=\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{x}\cdot f(\varphi_{t}(x))\Big)\Big)\Big|_{t=0}=-(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}

and hence

(2.9)

\mathcal{D}(A_{p})=\{f\in L^{p}(0,1):(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}\in L^{p}(0,1)\}.

The
resolvent set

\rho(A_{p})
is the collection of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which

R(\lambda,A_{p}):=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}

is a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}(0,1)
in that
\lambda I-A
is injective,
(\lambda I-A)\mathcal{D}(A_{p})=L^{p}(0,1)
, and
(\lambda I-A_{p})
has a bounded inverse.
Standard semigroup theory says that the
resolvent operator

R(\lambda,A_{p})
has the Laplace transform representation

(2.10)

R(\lambda,A_{p})f=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}(S_{t}f)dt.

From (
2.7
), this implies that whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>\omega_{0}
, the resolvent
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}(0,1)
. Since
\omega_{0}<0
we have

0\in\rho(A_{p})\;\;\mbox{for all $1<p<\infty$},

which says that
R(0,A_{p})
is a bounded linear operator for all
1<p<\infty
. Solving the resulting differential equation

-(1-x)(xf)^{\prime}(x)=g(x),

i.e.,
R(0,A_{p})f=g
, shows that

(2.11)

C_{p}=R(0,A_{p})=(-A_{p})^{-1}.

We summarize this discussion with the following result.

Theorem 2.12
.

The Cesàro operator
C_{p}

is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
for all
1<p<\infty
.

Proposition
2.2
, (
2.10
), and (
2.11
) yield the estimates

(2.13)

\|C_{p}\|\leqslant\begin{cases}q&\mbox{if $1<p<2$},\\
p&\mbox{if $2\leqslant p<\infty$.}\end{cases}

Corollary
5.1
below will show equality in both of these cases.

Proposition 2.14
.

The Cesàro operator
C_{1}
is unbounded on
L^{1}(0,1)
.

Proof.

Consider the sequence of functions
(f_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
on
[0,1]
defined by

(2.15)

f_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}n&\mbox{if $0\leqslant x\leqslant\frac{1}{n}$},\\
0&\mbox{if $\frac{1}{n}<x\leqslant 1$},\end{cases}

and observe that
\|f_{n}\|_{1}=1
. A calculation shows that

(C_{1}f_{n})(x)=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle\frac{n}{x}\log\frac{1}{1-x}}&\mbox{if $0\leqslant x\leqslant\frac{1}{n}$,}\\[10.0pt]
{\displaystyle\frac{n}{x}\log\frac{n}{n-1}}&\mbox{if $\frac{1}{n}<x\leqslant 1$}\end{cases}

and so

\displaystyle\|C_{1}f_{n}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}|(C_{1}f_{n})(x)|dx

\displaystyle=n\int_{0}^{1/n}\frac{1}{x}\log\frac{1}{1-x}dx+n\log\frac{n}{n-1}\int_{1/n}^{1}\frac{1}{x}dx

\displaystyle=n\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{k}k^{2}}+(n\log n)\log\frac{n}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}k^{2}}+\big(\frac{n}{n-1}\log n\big)(n-1)\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}k^{2}}+\log n\cdot(\frac{n}{n-1})\cdot\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\Big)^{n-1}.

Since

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n-1}\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{n-1}\Big)^{n-1}=\log e=1

and the sum

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}k^{2}}

is uniformly bounded above in
n
, we have

\|C_{1}f_{n}\|_{1}\sim\log n.

Thus,
C_{1}
is unbounded on
L^{1}(0,1)
.
∎

3.
Point spectrum

We now discuss the eigenvalues of the Cesàro operator
C_{p}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The point spectrum of
C_{p}
is empty when
p\geqslant 2
and is the open crescent domain

D(\frac{q}{2},\frac{q}{2})\setminus\overline{D(\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2})}
when
1<p<2
.

Proof.

The eigenvalue equation
C_{p}f=\lambda f
yields the integral equation

\frac{1}{x}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{f(t)}{1-t}dt=\lambda f(x)

which can be converted into a differential equation that can be solved in the standard way as

f(x)=c\frac{x^{\frac{1}{\lambda}-1}}{(1-x)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}},\quad c\in\mathbb{C}.

When
c\not=0
, the above
f
belongs to
L^{p}(0,1)
for
p\geqslant 2
only when

\frac{1}{q}<\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{\lambda}<\frac{1}{p}.

i.e., for
no

\lambda
(since
p\geqslant 2
implies
p^{-1}\leqslant q^{-1}
). Thus, in this case,
C_{p}
has no eigenvalues. A similar argument works when
1<p<2
but the above function
f
belongs to
L^{p}(0,1)
only when

\frac{1}{q}<\operatorname{Re}\frac{1}{\lambda}<\frac{1}{p},

which is
a nonempty vertical strip (since
1<p<2
implies
q^{-1}<p^{-1}
). The function
z\mapsto z^{-1}
maps this strip onto the desired crescent domain.
∎

Eventually we will need the point spectrum of the infinitesimal generator
A_{p}
. The proof is similar to that of the previous proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

The point spectrum of
A_{p}
is empty when
p\geqslant 2
and is the vertical strip
\{\lambda:-\frac{1}{p}<\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{q}\}
when
1<p<2
.

4.
Spectrum

Our analysis of the spectrum of
C_{p}
involves the spectrum of the infinitesimal generator
A_{p}
of the semigroup
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. Recall from (
2.8
) that

(A_{p}f)(x)=-(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}

We now compute the resolvent operator
R(\lambda,A_{p})
and prove the resolvent set
\rho(A_{p})
contains the union of two open half planes.

Theorem 4.1
.

(a)

For each
1<p<2
we have the following.

(i)

Whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\frac{1}{q}
,

(4.2)

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}}.

(ii)

Whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{p}
,

(4.3)

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=-\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}}.

Moreover,
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever

\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\tfrac{1}{q}\;\mbox{or}\;\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\tfrac{1}{p}.

(b)

For each
p\geqslant 2
we have the following.

(i)

Whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\frac{1}{p}
,

(4.4)

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}}.

(ii)

Whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{q}
,

(4.5)

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=-\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}}.

Moreover,
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is a bounded linear operator on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever

\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\tfrac{1}{p}\;\mbox{or}\;\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\tfrac{1}{q}.

Proof.

From (
2.7
), along with the discussion preceding it, we know that
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\frac{1}{q}
(when
1<p<2
) or whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda>-\frac{1}{p}
(when
p\geqslant 2)
.
Moreover, in both cases, we use the Laplace transform identity in (
2.10
) to see that

\displaystyle R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\lambda}S_{t}f(x)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{-t})^{\lambda}\frac{e^{-t}}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}f\bigg(\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}\bigg)dt.

With the substitution

u=\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1},

and checking

e^{-t}=\frac{u(1-x)}{x(1-u)}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;dt=-\frac{1}{u(1-u)}du,

the above integral transforms into

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{u^{\lambda}f(u)du}{(1-u)^{\lambda+1}},

which proves (
4.2
) and (
4.4
).

On the other hand, a formula for
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is found by solving

(\lambda I-A_{p})f(x)=\lambda f(x)+(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}=g(x)

for
f
in terms of
g
. The integrating factor

\mu(x):=\frac{x^{\lambda+1}}{(1-x)^{\lambda}}

transforms this differential equation into

(\mu(x)f(x))^{\prime}=\Big(\frac{x^{\lambda+1}}{(1-x)^{\lambda}}f(x)\Big)^{\prime}=\frac{x^{\lambda}}{(1-x)^{\lambda+1}}g(x).

Whenever
1<p<2
and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{p}
, observe that

\lim_{x\to 1^{-}}\frac{x^{\lambda+1}}{(1-x)^{\lambda}}f(x)=0

and, for each
0<x<1
, the integral

\int_{x}^{1}\frac{t^{\lambda}}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}}g(t)dt

converges.
Thus,

(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=-\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}},

which proves (
4.3
). A similar argument verifies (
4.5
) when
p\geqslant 2
and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{q}
.

It remains to prove
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{p}
(for
1<p<2
) or whenever
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{q}
(for
p\geqslant 2
). To this end, let

I_{\mu}f:=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\mu t}S_{t}fdt.

Formally substituting

u=\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}

in the above integral and
and checking that

e^{t}=\frac{x(1-u)}{u(1-x)}\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;dt=-\frac{1}{u(1-u)}du,

we have

\displaystyle I_{\mu}f(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}(e^{t})^{\mu}\frac{u}{x}\frac{f(u)}{u(1-u)}du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\bigg(\frac{x(1-u)}{u(1-x)}\bigg)^{\mu}\frac{1}{x}\frac{f(u)}{1-u}du

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{\mu-1}}{(1-x)^{\mu}}\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(1-u)^{\mu-1}}{u^{\mu}}f(u)du.

Now consider the formula

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=(\lambda I-A_{p})^{-1}f(x)=-\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{x}^{1}\frac{t^{\lambda}f(t)dt}{(1-t)^{\lambda+1}},

from (b).
Make the substitution
u=1-t
to get

R(\lambda,A_{p})f(x)=-\frac{(1-x)^{\lambda}}{x^{\lambda+1}}\int_{0}^{1-x}\frac{(1-u)^{\lambda}}{u^{\lambda+1}}f(1-u)du.

The “flip operator”

(\mathcal{F}f)(x):=f(1-x)

is isometric on
L^{p}(0,1)
and

R(\lambda,A_{p})=-\mathcal{F}\circ I_{\lambda+1}\circ\mathcal{F}.

Thus on
L^{p}(0,1)
,
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is bounded if and only if
I_{\lambda+1}
is bounded. We also have

\displaystyle\|I_{\lambda+1}f\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1)t}\|S_{t}f\|_{p}dt\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1-\frac{1}{q})t}dt\bigg)\|f\|_{p}

(note the use of Proposition
2.2
(a)). Hence, since

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1-\frac{1}{q})t}dt<\infty

whenever

\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-1+\tfrac{1}{q}=-(1-\tfrac{1}{q})=-\tfrac{1}{p},

I_{\lambda+1}
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever
1<p<2
and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{p}
. Conclusion:
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
.

In a similar way,

\displaystyle\|I_{\lambda+1}f\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1)t}\|S_{t}f\|_{p}dt\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant\bigg(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1-\frac{1}{p})t}dt\bigg)\|f\|_{p},

(note the use of Proposition
2.2
(b))
and since

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda)+1-\frac{1}{p})t}dt<\infty

whenever

\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-1+\tfrac{1}{p}=-(1-\tfrac{1}{p})=-\tfrac{1}{q},

I_{\lambda+1}
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
whenever
p\geqslant 2
and
\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{q}
. As before, this implies
R(\lambda,A_{p})
is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

When
1<p<2
,

\sigma(C_{p})=\overline{D(\tfrac{q}{2},\tfrac{q}{2})}\setminus D(\tfrac{p}{2},\tfrac{p}{2}).

Proof.

Note that

\sigma(A_{p})\subseteq\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{p}\leqslant\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{q}\}
(Theorem
4.1
)
and

\sigma_{p}(A_{p})=\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{p}<\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\tfrac{1}{q}\}
(Proposition
3.2
)
and thus

\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{p}<\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\tfrac{1}{q}\}\subseteq\sigma(A_{p})\subseteq\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{p}\leqslant\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{q}\}.

Since
A_{p}
is a closed operator and thus
\sigma(A_{p})
is a closed set
[
4
, p. 240]
, taking closures above gives us

\sigma(A_{p})=\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{p}\leqslant\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{q}\}.

The identity
C_{p}=(-A_{p})^{-1}
from (
2.11
) and the spectral mapping theorem for the resolvent
[
4
, p. 243]
, via
z\mapsto-z^{-1}
, yields the result.
∎

We now discuss the spectrum of
C_{p}
when
p>2
. As always, our path is through the infinitesimal generator
A_{p}
for the semigroup
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
.

The
group
of operators
\{S_{t}\}_{-\infty<t<\infty}
,

(S_{t}f)(x)=\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{x}f(\varphi_{t}(x)),\quad\varphi_{t}(x)=\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1},\quad-\infty<t<\infty,

(recall the discussion of
\varphi_{t}
for
t<0
from §
2
)
is strongly continuous on
L^{p}(0,1)
in that

\lim_{t\to 0}\|S_{t}f-f\|_{p}=0,\quad f\in L^{p}(0,1),

and has infinitesimal generator

(A_{p}f)(x)=-(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime},

with domain

\mathcal{D}(A_{p})=\left\{f\in L^{p}(0,1):\,(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}\in L^{p}(0,1)\right\}.

The group
\{S_{t}\}_{-\infty<t<\infty}
can be split into the two semigroups
\{S_{t}^{+}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
and
\{S_{t}^{-}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
defined by

(S_{t}^{+}f)(x)=\frac{\varphi_{t}(x)}{x}f(\varphi_{t}(x))=\frac{e^{-t}}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}f\left(\frac{e^{-t}x}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}\right),\quad t\geqslant 0,

and

(S_{t}^{-}f)(x)=\frac{\varphi_{-t}(x)}{x}f(\varphi_{-t}(x))=\frac{e^{t}}{(e^{t}-1)x+1}f\left(\frac{e^{t}x}{(e^{t}-1)x+1}\right),\quad t\geqslant 0.

Both are strongly continuous on
L^{p}(0,1)
. The infinitesimal generator of
\{S_{t}^{+}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is

(A_{p}^{+}f)(x)=-(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}=(A_{p}f)(x),

while the infinitesimal generator of
\{S_{t}^{-}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is

(A_{p}^{-}f)(x)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{S_{t}^{-}f(x)-f(x)}{t}=(1-x)(xf(x))^{\prime}=-(A_{p}^{+}f)(x)

with
\mathcal{D}(A_{p}^{-})=\mathcal{D}(A_{p}^{+})
. Thus,

(4.7)

A_{p}^{+}=A_{p},\quad A_{p}^{-}=-A_{p},\quad\mathcal{D}(A_{p}^{-})=\mathcal{D}(A_{p}^{+})=D(A_{p}).

The technical details involved in the discussion above (splitting the group into two semigroups) is found in
[
10
, Sec. 1.6]
.

For each
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
, Proposition
2.2
(b) yields

(4.8)

\|S_{t}^{+}f\|_{p}^{p}=e^{t(1-p)}\int_{0}^{1}|f(u)|^{p}((e^{t}-1)u+1)^{p-2}du,\quad t\geqslant 0,

and, in a similar way,

(4.9)

\|S_{t}^{-}f\|_{p}^{p}=e^{t(p-1)}\int_{0}^{1}|f(u)|^{p}((e^{-t}-1)u+1)^{p-2}du,\quad t\geqslant 0.

From (
4.8
), since
(e^{t}-1)u+1\leqslant e^{t}
for
u\in[0,1]
,
t\geqslant 0
, and
p-2>0
, we conclude

\|S_{t}^{+}f\|_{p}^{p}\leqslant e^{t(1-p)}e^{t(p-2)}\|f\|_{p}^{p}=e^{-t}\|f\|_{p}^{p},\quad f\in L^{p}(0,1),

and so

(4.10)

\|S_{t}^{+}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{-\frac{t}{p}}.

From
(e^{-t}-1)u+1\leqslant 1
for
u\in[0,1]
,
t\geqslant 0
, and
p-2>0
, (
4.9
) yields

\|S_{t}^{-}f\|_{p}^{p}\leqslant e^{t(p-1)}\|f\|_{p}^{p},\quad f\in L^{p}(0,1).

Hence

(4.11)

\|S_{t}^{-}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}\leqslant e^{t(\frac{p-1}{p})}=e^{\frac{t}{q}}.

Similarly from (
4.10
), the growth bound for
\{S_{t}^{+}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is

\omega_{0}(\{S_{t}^{+}\}_{t\geqslant 0})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\|S_{t}^{+}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}}{t}\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{p},

and so

\sigma(A_{p}^{+})\subseteq\{\lambda:\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{p}\}.

From (
4.11
) we also have

\omega_{0}(\{S_{t}^{-}\}_{t\geqslant 0})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\|S_{t}^{-}\|_{L^{p}\to L^{p}}}{t}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{q},

which yields

\sigma(A^{-})\subseteq\{\lambda:\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant\tfrac{1}{q}\}.

The identities in (
4.7
) give us

\sigma(A_{p})=\sigma(A_{p}^{+})=-\sigma(A_{p}^{-}),

and so

\displaystyle\sigma(A_{p})

\displaystyle\subseteq\{\lambda:\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{p}\}\cap\{\lambda:\operatorname{Re}\lambda\geqslant-\tfrac{1}{q}\}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{q}\leqslant\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{p}\}.

Proposition 4.13
.

For each
p>2
the spectrum of
A_{p}
is the vertical strip

V_{p}:=\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{q}\leqslant\operatorname{Re}\lambda\leqslant-\tfrac{1}{p}\}.

Proof.

The containment

\sigma(A_{p})\subseteq V_{p}
is from (
4.12
). To prove the reverse, it suffices to show that the open strip

V_{p}^{\circ}=\{\lambda:-\tfrac{1}{q}<\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\tfrac{1}{p}\}

is contained in
\sigma(A_{p})
. Indeed, since
A_{p}
is a closed operator, it has closed spectrum
[
4
, p. 240]
. Thus, taking closures, we have
V_{p}\subseteq\sigma(A_{p})
and hence equality.
To prove
V_{p}^{\circ}\subseteq\sigma(A_{p})
, let
\lambda\in V_{p}^{\circ}
and set

f_{\lambda}(x)=(1-x)^{\lambda}.
Observe that

(\lambda I-A)f_{\lambda}(x)=(\lambda+1)(1-x)^{\lambda+1}.

Since
-\frac{1}{q}<\operatorname{Re}\lambda<-\frac{1}{p}
we have

(4.14)

f_{\lambda}(x)=(1-x)^{\lambda}\notin L^{p}(0,1)\;\mbox{and}\;(1-x)^{\lambda+1}\in L^{p}(0,1).

In fact,
f_{\lambda}
is the unique solution to the differential equation

(\lambda I-A_{p}f)(x)=(\lambda+1)(1-x)^{\lambda+1}.

Towards a contradiction, assume
\lambda\in\rho(A_{p})
. Then

R(\lambda,A_{p})

is bounded on
L^{p}(0,1)
and so

R(\lambda,A_{p})((\lambda+1)(1-x)^{\lambda+1})=f_{\lambda}(x)=(1-x)^{\lambda}\in L^{p}(0,1).

This contradicts (
4.14
) and thus completes the proof.
∎

As in the proof of Corollary
4.6
, use the identity
C_{p}=(-A_{p})^{-1}
from (
2.11
) and the spectral mapping theorem for the resolvent
[
4
, p. 243]
to obtain the following.

Corollary 4.15
.

When
p\geqslant 2
,
\sigma(C_{p})=\overline{D(\tfrac{p}{2},\tfrac{p}{2})}\setminus D(\tfrac{q}{2},\tfrac{q}{2}).

When
p=2
, the spectrum of
C_{2}
becomes the circle
\partial D(1,1)
, which was already observed in
[
1
]
.

6.
Invariant subspaces

Analogous to our initial paper
[
1
]
on the real variable Cesàro operator, where we explored the invariant subspaces of
C_{2}
, in this section we explore the invariant subspaces for
C_{p}
,
1<p<\infty
. Their description, as well as the techniques used to examine them, become more complicated. We direct the reader to
[
6
,
7
]
for similar looking results in related settings. The main result of this section is the following.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. For a closed subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L^{p}(0,1)
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

C_{p}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
;

(b)

S_{t}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.

The proof of this theorem requires a few technical details. The first involves contractive semigroups on Banach spaces. Suppose that
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a strongly continuous contractive semigroup on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
in that
\|T_{t}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}}\leqslant 1
for all
t\geqslant 0
. If
A
is the corresponding infinitesimal generator for
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, then, as discussed in §
2
,
1\in\rho(A)
and so we can define the
cogenerator

V:=(A+I)(A-I)^{-1}.

This operator is bounded on
\mathcal{X}
.
This next result from
[
3
, Thm. 5.2]
estimates the norm of the powers of
V
.

Lemma 6.2
.

If
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a strongly continuous contractive semigroup on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, then

\|V^{n}\|=O(\sqrt{n}),\quad n\to\infty.

For Hilbert spaces, it turns out that
V
is contractive when
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is contractive and thus
\|V^{n}\|\leqslant 1
for all
n
. This is not always the case in the Banach space setting.
This next detail, proved for Hilbert spaces, is from
[
5
, p. 146]
. We follow their proof with an appropriate alteration needed for the Banach space case using the previous lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
be a strongly continuous contractive semigroup on a Banach space
\mathcal{X}
with cogenerator
V
. Then, for a closed subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

T_{t}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
t\geqslant 0
;

(b)

V\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Observe that

V=I+2(A-I)^{-1}

and from our discussion in §
2
,

(I-A)^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}T_{t}dt.

Hence, if
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for each
T_{t}
,
t\geqslant 0
, then
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
V
.

Conversely, suppose
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
V
. For each
\lambda>0
, define the operator
A_{\lambda}
on
\mathcal{X}
by

A_{\lambda}:=\lambda^{2}(\lambda I-A)^{-1}-\lambda I.

We now show that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
A_{\lambda}
by showing that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
(\lambda I-A)^{-1}
. Observe that

\displaystyle(\lambda I-A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\lambda I-(V+I)(V-I)^{-1}\Big)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(V-I)(\lambda(V-I)-(V+I))^{-1}

\displaystyle=(V-I)\frac{1}{\lambda+1}\Big\{\Big(\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda+1}\Big)V-I\Big\}^{-1}.

Since

-1<\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda+1}<1\;\;\mbox{and}\;\;\|V^{n}\|=O(\sqrt{n}),\quad\lambda>0,n\in\mathbb{N},

from Lemma
6.2
, the operator

\Big\{\Big(\frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda+1}\Big)V-I\Big\}^{-1}

can be expanded as a convergent geometric series in operator norm. By our assumption that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
V
, we see that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for each
A_{\lambda}
,
\lambda>0
.
The semigroup
\{T_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is contractive and so (
2.10
) yields

\|(\lambda I-A)^{-1}\|\leqslant\frac{1}{\lambda},\quad\lambda>0,

and hence
\|A_{\lambda}\|\leqslant 2\lambda
. Thus, since the exponential function is entire, the operator
e^{tA_{\lambda}}
defined by

e^{tA_{\lambda}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}\frac{A_{\lambda}^{n}}{n!}

is a well defined bounded operator and the series above converges in operator norm.
For each
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{M}
, the proof of the Hille–Yoshida theorem (which works for contractive semigroups on Banach spaces – see
[
5
, p. 144]
) implies that the semigroup
\{e^{tA_{\lambda}}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}e^{tA_{\lambda}}{\bf x}=T_{t}{\bf x},\quad{\bf x}\in\mathcal{X},t\geqslant 0.

Since,
A_{\lambda}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
\lambda>0
, it follows from the above series expansion that
e^{tA_{\lambda}}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
\lambda,t\geqslant 0
and so
T_{t}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
t\geqslant 0
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
6.1
is as follows: When
1<p<2
, define

\widetilde{S}_{t}:=e^{t}S_{qt}

and observe from Proposition
2.2
, that
\{\widetilde{S}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a contractive semigroup. Moreover, standard theory says that if
A
is the infinitesimal generator for
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, then

\widetilde{A}=qA+I

is the infinitesimal generator for
\{\widetilde{S}_{t}\}_{g\geqslant 0}
. In addition, the cogenerator
V
of
\{\widetilde{S}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{A}+I)(\widetilde{A}-I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(qA+2I)(qA)^{-1}

\displaystyle=I+\tfrac{2}{q}A^{-1}

\displaystyle=I-\tfrac{2}{q}C_{p}.

Thus, the operators
V
and
C_{p}
share the same invariant subspaces. Now apply Lemma
6.3
.
When
p\geqslant 2
, apply the same argument but with the contractive semigroup, via Proposition
2.2
, defined by

\widetilde{S}_{t}=e^{t}S_{pt},\quad t\geqslant 0.

This completes the proof.

Example 6.4
.

For
0<a<1
, define
\mathcal{M}_{a}
to be subspace of all
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
that vanish almost everywhere on
[0,a]
. One can check that
\mathcal{M}_{a}
is invariant for all
S_{t}
,
t>0
, and thus an invariant subspace for
C_{p}
.

Example 6.5
.

When
1<p<2
we have

f_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{x^{\frac{1}{\lambda}-1}}{(1-x)^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}}\in L^{p}(0,1)

whenever
\lambda
belongs to the crescent
D(\frac{q}{2},\frac{q}{2})\setminus\overline{D(\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2})}
. Moreover, since
f_{\lambda}
is an eigenvector for
C_{p}
(Proposition
3.1
), the one dimensional subspace
\mathbb{C}f_{\lambda}
is an invariant subspace for
C_{p}
. A calculation verifies that

S_{t}f_{\lambda}=e^{-\frac{t}{\lambda}}f_{\lambda},\quad t>0,

which confirms Theorem
6.1
. Worth exploring from here is the following idea. Suppose
E\subseteq D(\frac{q}{2},\frac{q}{2})\setminus\overline{D(\frac{p}{2},\frac{p}{2})}
and consider the subspace

\mathcal{M}_{E}:=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{f_{\lambda}:\lambda\in E\}.

Observe that
\mathcal{M}_{E}
is an invariant subspace for
C_{p}
. The only question is whether or not
\mathcal{M}_{E}=L^{p}(0,1)
. If
E
is a finite set, then clearly
\mathcal{M}_{E}\not=L^{p}(0,1)
. When
E
is infinite and sufficiently rich, is
\mathcal{M}_{E}=L^{p}(0,1)
?

To better understand the structure of the common invariant subspaces for
\{S_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
, we expand a discussion from
[
1
]
and relate them to the common invariant subspaces for a related semigroup on
L^{p}(-\infty,\infty)
. To see this, observe that the map

u\mapsto\frac{1}{1+e^{u}},\quad-\infty<u<\infty,

is a diffeomorphism of
(-\infty,\infty)
onto
(0,1)
and some calculus shows that

(\Phi_{p}f)(u):=\frac{e^{u/p}}{(1+e^{u})^{2/p}}f\big(\frac{1}{1+e^{u}}\big)

defines an isometric map from
L^{p}(0,1)
onto
L^{p}(-\infty,\infty)
. Moreover, another calculation, similar to the one in
[
1
]
, reveals that

(\Phi_{p}S_{t}\Phi_{p}^{-1}g)(u)=e^{-t/p}\Big(\frac{1+e^{t+u}}{1+e^{u}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{p}-1}g(u+t),\quad g\in L^{p}(-\infty,\infty).

This brings us to the following.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
1<p<\infty
. For a closed subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq L^{p}(0,1)
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
C_{p}
;

(b)

\mathcal{M}
is invariant for all
S_{t}
,
t\geqslant 0
;

(c)

\Phi_{p}\mathcal{M}\subseteq L^{p}(-\infty,\infty)
is invariant for each operator

(6.7)

g\mapsto\Big(\frac{1+e^{t+u}}{1+e^{u}}\Big)^{\frac{2}{p}-1}g(u+t),\quad t\geqslant 0.

When
p=2
, observe that (
6.7
) becomes the translation operator

g\mapsto g(\cdot+t)

on
L^{2}(-\infty,\infty)
and the common invariant subspaces for
t\geqslant 0
are known by a theorem of Helson
[
8
]
(see the discussion in
[
1
]
). When
p\not=2
, the common invariant subspaces of the semigroup (
6.7
) seem to be more complicated and do not, as of yet, have a tangible description as in the
p=2
case.

7.
Cyclicity

Another line of research is the cyclicity of
C_{p}
. Here we ask if there exist an
f\in L^{p}(0,1)
such that

\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{C_{p}^{n}f:n\geqslant 0\}=L^{p}(0,1)?

Such an
f
is called a
cyclic vector
for
C_{p}
. Can we characterize such
f
? Though we don’t know a complete characterization, we do know that cyclic vectors exist.

Theorem 7.1
.

The function
\chi\equiv 1
on
[0,1]
is a cyclic vector for
C_{p}
for all
1<p<\infty
.

Proof.

Fix
1<p<\infty
and let

\mathcal{M}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{C_{p}^{n}\chi:n\geqslant 0\}.
We want to show that
\mathcal{M}=L^{p}(0,1)
. Obviously
C_{p}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and thus

S_{t}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for all
t\geqslant 0
(Theorem
6.1
). Hence
S_{t}\chi\in\mathcal{M}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. If
g\in L^{q}(0,1)
annihilates
\mathcal{M}
, then

0=\int_{0}^{1}(S_{t}\chi\cdot g)dx=0,\quad t\geqslant 0.

Since

S_{t}\chi(x)=\frac{e^{-t}}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1},

such an annihilating
g
satisfies

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g(x)}{(e^{-t}-1)x+1}dx=0,\quad t\geqslant 0,

and thus

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g(x)}{1+\lambda x}dx=0,\quad-1<\lambda<0.

Hölder’s inequality shows that

\Big|\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}g(x)dx\Big|=O(n^{-1/p})

and thus

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{g(x)}{1+zx}dx=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}z^{n}\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}g(x)dx

defines an analytic function on
D(0,1)
that is equal to zero on the interval
(-1,0)
and thus zero on
D(0,1)
. By the uniqueness of power series coefficients,

\int_{0}^{1}x^{n}g(x)dx=0,\quad n\geqslant 0.

The Stone–Weierstrass theorem gives us
g=0
almost everywhere and the Hahn–Banach separation theorem implies that
\operatorname{span}\{S_{t}\chi:t\geqslant 0\}
is dense in
L^{p}(0,1)
. Thus
\mathcal{M}=L^{p}(0,1)
.
∎

8.
A fnal remark

A similar integral substitution, as in §
6
, shows that

(\Phi_{p}C_{p}\Phi_{p}^{-1}g)(u)=\int_{u}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1+e^{u}}{1+e^{s}}\Big)^{1-\frac{2}{p}}e^{\frac{u-s}{p}}g(s)ds,\quad g\in L^{p}(-\infty,\infty).

When
p=2
, the above becomes a convolution operator on
L^{2}(-\infty,\infty)
. Now apply a Fourier transform argument to see that
C_{2}
is unitarily equivalent to the multiplication operator

g(x)\mapsto\frac{1}{\frac{1}{2}-ix}g(x)

on
L^{2}(-\infty,\infty)

[
1
]
.
The known spectral properties of this multiplication operator, as well as its normality, will show that
