Executive Summary
In the previous works of Davis et al. (2013), Davis et al. (2022), and Bhatt et al. (2022); the
process design, mass/energy balance, and air pollutant emissions of an advanced biorefinery
that produces renewable diesel blendstock (RDB) from lignocellulosic biomass via aerobic
respiration was established. This study assesses the air quality impacts of an RDB biorefinery
by estimating fine particulate matter (PM2.5) concentrations from biorefinery air pollutant
emissions. It continues a prior analysis that used a geospatial assessment to identify source
regions for biomass feedstocks and studied the impact of feedstock production on air quality
(Thind et al. 2022). Thind et al. (2022) identified RDB biorefineries that can use corn stover
feedstocks of 2,000; 5,200; and 9,100 dry metric tons per day (DMT/day), based in Iowa, and
suggested 7 unique counties can serve as hosts for a biorefinery that draws biomass feedstock
from neighboring counties. A biorefinery input feedstock rate of 2,000 DMT/day is the optimal
rate considering economies of scale and feedstock delivery costs while 9,100 DMT/day
represents a small petroleum refinery based on diesel fuel output data from the Energy
Information Administration (Davis et al. 2022). These 7 counties and 3 feedstock rates lead to
131 unique county-biorefinery size combinations, which—combined with two on-site waste
lignin end uses at the biorefinery (lignin as a fuel for heat and electric power cogeneration [i.e.,
“lignin-for-electricity”2] and lignin for pellet coproduction [i.e., “lignin-for-pellet”])—leads to
26 scenarios or combinations. We assess the air-quality-related sustainability aspects of each of
these scenarios.

This report focuses on three distinct but related aspects of the sustainability of future
lignocellulosic biorefineries:

 • Are there any constraints on scale and location of biorefineries considering how air
 pollutant emissions from new RDB biorefineries could affect compliance with the U.S.
 National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) of PM2.5?
 • Which upstream supply chain stage in the life cycle of RDB leads to the largest portion
 of downwind pollutant concentration: feedstock production or the biorefinery?
 • What is the relative contribution of each unit process to total annual emissions of
 primary PM2.5 and secondary PM2.5 precursors within each upstream stage of the life

The 26 biorefinery design combinations help capture how a biorefinery’s emissions of air
pollutants and their resulting impact on county-level and regional air quality are influenced by
the magnitude of production scale, lignin utilization strategy, and location of a proposed
biorefinery. Emission estimates for the three biorefinery sizes are based on preliminary
potential-to-emit (PTE) calculations for the design of an RDB biorefinery using 100% corn
stover considering two lignin utilization strategies (Bhatt et al. 2022).

Using a novel reduced-complexity air quality model called the Intervention Model for Air
Pollution (InMAP), annual average impacts of biorefinery emissions on the dispersion and
formation of secondary PM2.5 in the atmosphere for each scenario are studied. Location-specific
biorefinery annual emission estimates of nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur dioxide (SO2), volatile
organic compounds (VOCs), ammonia (NH3), and primary PM2.5 serve as the main inputs to
InMAP. Incremental, additional PM2.5 concentrations caused by the emission of biorefining
corn stover into RDB are assessed and compared to those of corn stover production.

We apply methods developed in Thind et al. (2022) to estimate the constraints on PM2.5
precursor emissions based on compliance with U.S. Environmental Protection Agency’s
(EPA’s) annual primary PM2.5 NAAQS of 12.0 micrograms per cubic meter (µg/m3) at
downwind receptors of a biorefinery. To illuminate which upstream stage contributes most to
air quality impacts in the life cycle of renewable diesel, marginal PM2.5 concentrations are
compared at multiple downwind air quality monitor locations. In addition, through a hotspot
analysis, we identify the primary contributing factors of emissions within the feedstock
production and biorefinery stage operations. In doing so, we provide insights for improving the
air pollutant emission-related sustainability of advanced lignocellulosic biofuel production.

Key Findings
Following are the key findings from our analysis aligned with the questions we addressed.

Key Finding #1: A new biorefinery will add to air pollutant emissions in the region in which it
is located. The larger the biorefinery, the greater its emissions and potential to impact
downwind air quality. Even when the biorefineries reach the size of small petroleum refineries
(5,200 and 9,100 DMT/day), our modeling indicates their emissions should not cause
downwind air quality to worsen to the degree that additional areas would be considered out of
compliance with the annual PM2.5 NAAQS. This is true for both lignin utilization strategies
(either for “electricity” or pellets) and all Iowa biorefinery locations tested. However, it is also
true some downwind locations are already out of compliance with the NAAQS and will
continue to be so with any new sources. In addition, it should be noted this assessment is based
on the currently applicable annual PM2.5 standard of 12.0 µg/m3, which has recently been
updated by EPA to 9.0 µg/m3, and any change in results because of the reduction of the
concentration allowable under the new NAAQS level was not studied.

Key Finding #2: Emissions from the feedstock production stage contribute more to the
incremental change in PM2.5 concentrations at downwind monitors than the biorefinery with
either of the waste lignin use scenarios. This is illustrated in Figure ES-1, where it can be
noticed for a selected feedstock input rate and host county, emissions from the feedstock stage

and the relative contribution of different unit processes to those emissions are revealed through
a hotspot analysis (Figure ES-2). Figure ES-2 (top) shows that the highest emitting life cycle
stage varies by pollutant, and Figure ES-2 (bottom) reflects the same result at the unit process
level. These results suggest there is no single mitigation strategy in terms of biofuel supply
chain stage or unit process that could uniformly reduce emissions.

If a goal is to reduce emissions of a specific pollutant, our findings can help initially identify
promising targets. The feedstock production stage emits far more NH3 compared to the
biorefinery itself, which aligns well with the established understanding that agriculture and
allied activities are the largest contributors to NH3 emissions globally and in the United States
(Wyer et al. 2022). In the feedstock production stage, almost all ammonia emissions are from
fertilizer application, which is released in the atmosphere through volatilization of ammonia
contained in synthetic nitrogen fertilizers such as urea, anhydrous ammonia, and nitrogen-
phosphorous-potassium (NPK) (Behera et al. 2013). Emissions of NOx are largely from the
boiler for heat and electric power cogeneration at the lignin-for-electricity biorefinery, but there
are substantial NOx emissions from the feedstock stage and lignin-for-pellet biorefinery as well.
SO2 emissions are also primarily from the boiler for heat and electric power cogeneration at the
lignin-for-electricity biorefinery with very small emissions from the feedstock stage or
alternative use of lignin for pellet coproduction. Primary PM2.5 emissions are highest from the
feedstock stage, mostly attributable to fugitive dust emissions during harvesting. Primary PM2.5
emissions from a lignin-using biorefinery are about 50% of those from the feedstock stage.

1 Introduction
Petroleum products dominate energy consumption of the U.S. transportation sector as they
accounted for about 89% of the U.S. total energy consumption for transportation in 2023 (U.S.
EIA 2023). Gasoline is the single largest contributing fuel which accounted for 52% of the total
U.S. transportation sector energy use, distillates like diesel 22%, and jet fuel 12%, followed by
the next largest energy source of biofuels at 6%, natural gas 5%, and others like residual fuel oil
and electricity at 3% in total.

In the current bioeconomy for transportation, the primary liquid biofuels in use in the United
States are corn ethanol as well as biodiesel and renewable diesel derived from vegetable oils and
other fats, oils, and grease. Together, these three fuels account for about 95% of the total
currently used biomass resources of 157 million dry metric tons for transportation fuel and 43%
of 342 million dry metric tons considering all end use sectors (i.e., transportation fuel, heat and
power, bio-based chemicals, and wood pellets) in 2022 (Jacobson and Curran 2024). The U.S.
Department of Energy (DOE) projects more than 1 billion dry metric tons of biomass is
potentially available in a mature market, where a significant portion is from forestland and
agricultural resources (U.S. DOE 2024). Lignocellulosic biomass from these forestland and
agricultural resources can be converted to renewable diesel blendstock (RDB).

Renewable diesel derived from forestland and agricultural residue and other waste streams can
overcome many of the traditional limitations associated with corn ethanol and biodiesel such as
competition with land resource availability for food production and water use. While renewable
diesel has been shown to have lower life cycle GHG emissions than fossil derived fuels (Zoppi et
al. 2023; Okeke et al. 2020), there could still be significant emissions of criteria air pollutants
(CAPs) such as oxides of nitrogen (NOx) and sulfur (SOx), primary particulate matter (e.g.,
directly emitted particles with aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 µm or less, so-called “fine”
particulate matter, or PM2.5), volatile organic compounds (VOCs), and ammonia (NH3) as well as
hazardous air pollutants (HAPs) associated with their production and use. These gaseous
pollutants (collectively called precursors in the context of the subject of this sentence) can also
undergo physical and chemical transformations in the atmosphere to form secondary PM2.5,
which together with primary PM2.5 define total PM2.5 mass. These emissions occur at all stages
of the fuel’s life cycle, i.e., feedstock production, feedstock conversion to fuel at a biorefinery,
transportation of the finished fuel, and eventual use of the fuel in a combustion process.

Analysis related to life cycle GHG emissions from lignocellulosic biorefineries producing RDB
have been included in numerous prior studies (e.g., Zoppi et al. 2023; Larnaudie et al. 2020;
Okeke et al. 2020). However, there is a relative paucity of studies that have focused on CAP
emissions and air quality impacts from the production and use of second-generation biofuels
(i.e., biofuels produced from non-edible biomass) such as RDB compared to its first-generation
counterparts (i.e., biofuels produced from edible energy crops such as corn). Particulate matter
and ozone are two key CAPs whose exposure has been associated with long-term health effects
and whose concentrations are regulated by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)
through the national ambient air quality standards (NAAQS) (O. U.S. EPA 2024). Prior studies
that have focused on first-generation biofuels such as corn ethanol and second-generation
biofuels such as corn stover cellulosic ethanol quantified the life cycle CAP emissions and
assessed air quality impacts by simulating changes in concentrations of particulate matter and

 1

ozone. In the work by Tessum et al., the authors developed spatiotemporally explicit life cycle
inventories of several CAPs and found PM2.5 emissions from cellulosic ethanol are lower than
those from corn ethanol, reinforcing the findings in the aforementioned work by Hill et al.
(Tessum et al. 2012). A study focused on sustainable aviation fuel generation in the northwestern
region of the United States found harvesting of forest residue, residue transportation, production
of biojet fuel, and transportation of the finished fuel to blending terminals supplying the region’s
airports offered significant air quality and health benefits from PM2.5 and ozone compared to a
business-as-usual fate of the forest residue (Ravi et al. 2018). Clearly, there is a need to better
understand the emissions and air quality impacts from the production of second-generation
biofuels such as RDB as studies have focused on ethanol and aviation fuel for second generation
biofuel production.

Advanced biorefineries using waste biomass feedstock are still new, and although air pollutant
emissions have been calculated for the novel conversion processes of lignocellulosic biomass
(e.g., Davis et al. 2022; Bhatt et al. 2022), there is a lack of published research of the air quality
impacts from various sources of emissions at such biorefineries. A bottleneck in understanding
the requirements to meet air quality regulatory standards affects the deployment and scalability
of the biofuel industry. This study builds on prior research done at the National Laboratory of the
Rockies (NLR) both in the process modeling space focused on producing RDB and in analysis to
understand applicable federal air pollutant regulations that constrain allowable emissions from
such biorefineries using herbaceous biomass from residual agriculture feedstock and producing
renewable hydrocarbon fuels (Davis et al. 2013; Davis et al. 2022; Bhatt et al. 2022). In this
report, the analysis focuses on advancing our understanding of how future deployments of
herbaceous biofuel production can affect the air quality at a county-level and regional scale. We
use a subset of the design cases that vary feedstock type, biorefinery size, and use of coproducts
as described in Davis et al. (2022) (more details on this are provided in Section 2), and use air-
quality-related metrics developed previously to perform a screening-level assessment of the
biorefinery emissions impacts on concentrations of PM2.5 developed in NLR’s bioenergy air
quality workstream (Thind et al. 2022).

This report focuses on three distinct but related aspects pertaining to sustainability of future
lignocellulosic biorefineries:
 • Are there any constraints on scale and location of biorefineries considering how
 emissions from new RDB biorefineries could affect compliance with the U.S. National
 Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) of PM2.5?
 • Which upstream supply chain stage in the life cycle of RDB leads to the largest portion of
 downwind pollutant concentration: feedstock production or the biorefinery?
 • What is the relative contribution of each unit process to total annual emissions of primary
 PM2.5 and gaseous precursors to secondary PM2.5 within two supply chain stages of RDB
 production considered in this work?

We provide a data-driven assessment to answer these three questions using a case study centered
in the U.S. state of Iowa. Iowa was chosen because it has abundant corn production and thus
available biomass residue as a feedstock to the biorefinery. Note in this analysis we focus only
on the air quality impacts associated with PM2.5 (both primary and secondary), although there
would be additional impacts on concentrations of other pollutants such as ozone, nitrogen oxides,
and HAPs not considered here.

2 Models and Methods
In the following subsections, we provide a brief description of the biorefinery process modeling
that takes herbaceous biomass and biochemically converts it to renewable diesel, production of
the feedstock (herbaceous biomass), associated emission estimations, and air quality modeling
approach undertaken. A description of the assessment metrics used to answer the questions
raised in the introduction section is provided along with the case study used in the analysis. Note
several of the methodological approaches have been developed in previously published works,
which can be reviewed by any readers interested in more details.

2.1 Overview of Process Modeling and Scenario Identification
Conversion of lignocellulosic biomass into renewable diesel hydrocarbon blendstock via aerobic
respiration has been analyzed via process modeling and mass/energy balance using Aspen plus
modeling software in the prior works by Davis and coauthors (Davis et al. 2013; Davis et al.
2022). The work of Davis et al. (2022) forms the process modeling basis for this report, which
designed 12 biorefinery design permutations spanning two selected feedstock types (i.e., Idaho
National Laboratory uniform format blend and corn stover), three biorefinery input capacity
sizes (i.e., 2,000; 5,200; and 9,100 dry metric tons [DMT] per day), and two waste lignin
utilization methods (i.e., the coproduction of heat & electric power and solid pellets). As
cogeneration of RDB, the so-called “lignin-for-electricity” biorefinery design uses waste lignin
as a fuel for process heat and excess electric power via a steam boiler-generator to be exported to
the grid.3 The “lignin-for-pellet” design uses waste lignin to coproduce lignin pellets via a
pelletizer, which are exported as a solid fuel for off-site use. This report examines a corn-stover-
based RDB biorefinery at the three capacity sizes and two waste lignin uses whose various
physical system components represent the emission sources for which we estimate the resulting
incremental PM2.5 concentrations (Table 1). The scope of this study includes comparison of
biorefinery to feedstock production in terms of air quality impact and does not itself include
estimate emissions (instead using prior research). Prior research compatible with the research
here examined only corn stover as a feedstock (Thind et al. 2022); thus, this research is
constrained to likewise examining only corn stover as a feedstock.

2.3 Air Quality Modeling and a Description of Evaluation Metrics
The incremental PM2.5 concentrations resulting from emissions of the biorefining design
permutations considered in this study are modeled using a reduced-complexity air quality model
called the Intervention Model for Air Pollution (InMAP) (Tessum et al. 2017). InMAP predicts
the annual average PM2.5 concentration from any changes in pollutant emissions considering the
physical and chemical atmospheric processes that govern pollutant transport in the troposphere.
InMAP takes annual emission rates of NOx, SO2, primary PM2.5, VOCs, and NH3 from both
ground-based and elevated sources as an input. The model considers the influence of advection,
diffusion, chemical reactions, and removal from the atmosphere via wet and dry deposition.

InMAP’s spatial domain is discretized in horizontal and vertical grid cells whose resolution is
determined by pollutant concentration gradient and underlying population exposed to the
concentration, which leads to a variable horizontal grid cell size from 1 kilometer (km) by 1 km
to 48 km by 48 km. Vertical mixing extends beyond the planetary boundary layer so that the
model also predicts pollutant transport and associated concentrations at grid-cell levels above the
ground-level layer. PM2.5 concentrations outputted by the model and assessed in this study
pertain to the ground-level layer because all air quality monitors from the EPA’s air quality
system (AQS) repository are located at the ground level.

Given two waste lignin uses, three input biorefinery capacity sizes, and the 13 most suitable
locations for a biorefinery to take as input the three scales of feedstock production in Iowa
(discussed in Section 2.4), we execute 26 different InMAP simulations. Our Python-based
approach to prepare model inputs in a geospatially linked format to represent the biorefinery as a
point source with stack attributes can be found in Appendix A.2.

2.3.1 ARCIE Metric for Evaluating Compliance with the NAAQS
Methods developed in Thind et al. (2022) are applied to estimate the constraints on primary
PM2.5 and secondary PM2.5 precursor (e.g., NOx, SO2, VOC, NH3) emissions based on NAAQS
compliance at receptors downwind of the biorefineries. Specifically, we calculate the Available
Regulatory Capacity for Incremental Emissions (ARCIE) metric, which quantifies how much of
the difference between the maximum allowable ambient pollutant concentration under federal
regulation (NAAQS) and that of the measured pollutant concentrations at federal monitoring
locations would be used considering emissions from an additional source. ARCIE is used to
assess how much capacity is available at specific air pollutant monitoring locations to receive
additional concentration of a pollutant relative to the applicable regulatory limit of that pollutant.
It is especially useful to estimate the net effect of newly introduced source emissions on
surrounding areas in meeting ambient air quality standards. For a generic pollutant X with
concentration [PX], the ARCIE metric is represented by the following equation:

PM2.5 concentration limit of 12.0 microgram per cubic meter (µg/m3) as established through the
NAAQS primary standard to protect human health (O. U.S. EPA 2024). [PX]receptor is the sum of
two components:
 • Annual average baseline PM2.5 concentration at monitor locations (i.e., the existing
 baseline concentration before the introduction of additional emissions from any new or
 modified source), and
 • Annual average incremental PM2.5 concentration at monitor locations (i.e., the marginal
 increase in concentration after the introduction of additional emission from corn stover
 production and/or biorefining corn stover into RDB).

For instance, Figure 1 shows the selected EPA monitor locations considered when analyzing a

Appendix A.3. The relevant number and spatial extent of monitors to consider can be decided by
the analyst and may be limited to those within the state housing the potential new source or
further downwind states. In our case, we follow the selection of monitors made by Thind et al.
(2022), which considered an extensive set of states’ monitors.

A positive ARCIE value indicates for a given monitor, the sum of baseline concentration plus the
modeled concentration resulting from a new source remains below the primary NAAQS of
PM2.5. It is the average room left for a monitor to receive additional PM2.5 concentration while
remaining in compliance. A negative ARCIE value indicates the sum is greater than the PM2.5
NAAQS standard and thus the location surrounding the monitor would be in noncompliance of
the PM2.5 NAAQS. Some monitor locations are already out of compliance with the PM 2.5
NAAQS; these are labeled as “pre-NAA” (nonattainment area). Monitors with a negative ARCIE
value not labeled “pre-NAA” are labeled “now NAA” to differentiate between NAAs that were a
result of the newly introduced source emissions or caused by preexisting conditions.

The ARCIE metric allows an analyst to consider whether a potential new source could cause
noncompliance for a NAAQS or would be feasible to operate in terms of maintaining compliance
with the NAAQS for all evaluated monitors. If all considered monitors show positive ARCIE,
the new air pollutant emitting source is considered acceptable to operate. The new source might
lead to noncompliance if its additional emissions cause a selected monitor (that is not already out
of compliance) to yield an ARCIE that is negative (“now NAA”). This condition is called
“flipping” by Thind et al. (2022). In addition, if an evaluated monitor is already out of
compliance to the NAAQS (“pre-NAA”), any new source with additional emissions would only
make that situation worse, and the ARCIE would remain negative.

2.3.2 Methods Comparing Biorefinery Air Quality Impacts to Feedstock
 Production
We compare potential biorefinery air quality impacts to those from corn stover feedstock
production reported in Thind et al. (2022) to illuminate which supply chain stage contributes
most to air quality impacts. We implement this comparison by counting the number of monitors
commonly evaluated for both supply chain stages whose ARCIE metric is flipped because of the
emissions from each stage, respectively. We also assess the respective and cumulative effect of
emissions from both stages on the maximum and average incremental PM2.5 concentration
observed at the associated monitor locations. The differences in magnitude and relative
contribution of various pollutant species to the annual total emissions of primary PM2.5 and
precursors of secondary PM2.5 from each stage is discussed in the accompanying results section.
Their influence on the formation of secondary particulate species (e.g., particulate nitrate and
secondary organic aerosols), which sum to the total incremental PM2.5 concentration, is also
discussed.

2.3.3 Hotspot Analysis for Supply Chain Emissions Intensity
A hotspot analysis is used to rank the sources (unit processes) contributing to emissions of each
air pollutant within each upstream supply chain stage of RDB. Because different precursor
pollutants influence the formation of secondary PM2.5 in a unique way, we identify the main

source of emissions for each pollutant within each stage to provide insight into opportunities for
improving the air-emission-related sustainability of renewable diesel production from corn
stover.

For RDB biorefining, we investigate the PTE calculations for the design of the simulated RDB
biorefinery using 100% corn stover for each combination of input feedstock rate and waste lignin
use. Air pollutant emissions from a biorefinery are associated to its system components and the
conversion processes that occur within them. As further described in Appendix A.1, Davis et al.
(2022), and Bhatt et al. (2022); sources of emissions within the biorefinery include boiler
combustion, enzyme production, feed handling, and the cooling tower.

For corn stover production, we investigate the annual emissions from the 13 origin counties (and
neighboring source counties) identified within Iowa disaggregated by source category. In Thind
et al. (2022), source categories are associated to unit processes with direct emissions from corn
stover harvesting and local processing. These include emissions from fuel used by equipment
(e.g., agricultural machinery, on-farm transport vehicles) for harvesting corn stover, fertilizer
applications to compensate for lost soil nutrients, and fugitive dust emissions from machinery
operations (as shown in Figure 2 in Thind et al. [2022]).

3.2 Impact of Biorefinery Emissions on Marginal PM2.5 Concentrations
3.2.1 Regional Air Quality Impacts From Biorefinery Operations
Simulated annual average concentrations of PM2.5 attributable to biorefineries vary considerably,
depending on the input capacity and location of the facility. Although these biorefineries can be
large emission sources, the air quality impacts represented through annual average concentration

 12

of PM2.5 are largely in downwind regions of Iowa, Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Illinois as shown
in Figure 4 for the three origin counties in Iowa (Franklin, Kossuth, and Sioux) that can host all
three biorefinery sizes. Simulated incremental average concentrations in parts of these states can
exceed 0.025 µg/m3 as well as some grid cells experiencing concentrations exceeding 0.25 µg/m3
for the largest biorefinery with feed rates of 9,100 DMT/day when lignin is used for on-site
electricity cogeneration. Simulated annual average concentrations in most downwind regions
outside these states are likely to be below 0.0025 µg/m3.

The impact of biorefinery production scale is reflected in annual average PM2.5 concentrations,
and the smaller biorefineries of 2,000 DMT/day and 5,200 DMT/day are expected to have a
much smaller air quality impact footprint—especially for the smallest scale considered in this
report for which the highest PM2.5 concentration increase is localized to Iowa and almost always
below 0.0025 µg/m3. Pellet coproducing biorefineries have similar spatial pattern on PM2.5-
related air quality impacts, and the impacts generally are much smaller than those of the
corresponding biorefinery with on-site electricity cogeneration using lignin as a fuel. This
difference is attributable to lower emissions from a pellet coproducing refinery as opposed to
electricity cogenerating biorefineries.

Given the annual average wind patterns, the impacts of biorefinery emissions on PM2.5
concentration are greater eastward than in the states and regions west of the biorefinery host
counties. The largest incremental PM2.5 concentrations of an electricity cogenerating biorefinery
at 2,000 DMT/day range between 0.957 and 4.04 µg/m3 whereas those of the pellet coproducing
biorefinery range between 0.357 and 1.51 µg/m3. Similarly, at 5,200 and 9,100 DMT/day, the
largest simulated changes in PM2.5 concentration at any grid cell for the electricity cogenerating
biorefinery increase to 2.49–10.5 µg/m3 and 4.36–19.41 µg/m3, respectively whereas those of the
pellet coproducing biorefinery increase to 0.929–3.92 µg/m3 and 1.62–7.05 µg/m3, respectively.

Larger areas affected downwind of the emission sources and maximum PM2.5 concentrations at
the ground level for the waste lignin use of electricity are because of the increased on-site
emissions of combusting lignin. The lignin-for-electricity scenario uses lignin waste as a fuel
within the biorefinery’s boiler to cogenerate process heat and excess electric power which is
exported to the grid. While lignin is burned inside a boiler to cogenerate steam and electricity via
a steam turbine-generator, combustion by-products in the form of air pollutant emissions are
produced in excess of those from using the boiler to simply meet the facility’s steam and
electricity demands. On the other hand, the lignin-for-pellet scenario uses waste lignin to
coproduce lignin pellets via a pelletizer that are then exported as solid fuel.

elevated emission sources. County-level annual average PM2.5 concentrations rarely exceed 0.25
µg/m3, and when they do, they are localized to the county hosting the biorefinery. For any
location-size combination shown in Figure 7, no more than a dozen counties experience marginal
concentrations exceeding 0.025 µg/m3. It is not surprising the largest PM2.5 marginal impacts are
from the largest biorefineries with a feed rate of 9,100 DMT/day, which is roughly equivalent to
the size of a small petroleum refinery. These localized differences in PM2.5 concentration across
the span of locations and sizes are a function of variability in meteorological conditions across
different regions, which can influence the dispersion of emitted pollutants, as well as different
emission rates—which scales proportionately to the biomass feed rate to the refineries.

3.5 Biorefinery Operation versus Feedstock Production Unit Process
 PM2.5 Emissions
Emissions from the biorefinery for the two different lignin end uses and three input feedstock
sizes are described in Section 3.1. The emissions scale linearly with the size of biorefinery. In
this section, we compare the emissions from biorefining operations with those from feedstock
production and, given the linearity of emissions, we focus on just one size (9,100 DMT/day) of
biorefinery located in Franklin County, Iowa. Although the emissions from the biorefinery scale
linearly with size, corresponding feedstock operations may not scale linearly and can even vary
from one county to another for the same size. However, the variations are small (usually within
10%) and do not affect the qualitative results presented in this section (additional details and
results are included in Appendix A.10).

Among the CAPs considered here, feedstock production is the largest source of ammonia
emissions, with annual emissions of 2,500 tons/year with almost negligible emissions from the
biorefinery (for either of the end uses of lignin) (see top panel in Figure 11). Feedstock

 22

production is also the largest emissions source of primary PM2.5 at almost 160 tons/year. A pellet
coproducing biorefinery emits around 95 tons/year of PM2.5, and an electricity cogenerating
biorefinery contributes 75 tons/year of PM2.5. Emissions of SO2 and VOCs are almost entirely
from the biorefinery, with almost negligible emissions from the feedstock production stage, and
dominated by electricity end use of lignin. At the biorefinery, electricity cogeneration emits 500
tons/year of SO2 and VOCs, respectively, whereas a pellet coproducing biorefinery emits almost
2 orders of magnitude smaller emissions of SO2 and 70% less emissions of VOCs. Emissions of
NOx are also likely to be dominated by an electricity cogenerating biorefinery at about 2,000
tons/year, which are almost 5 times more than the emissions from either a lignin-for-pellet
biorefinery or the feedstock production stage.

Understanding the relative contributions of different processes to the emissions of specific
pollutants can provide insights into potential control technologies that can be used to reduce
emissions and subsequently decrease the adverse effects on human health and the environment.
The contribution of different unit processes at the two supply chain stages are also shown in
Figure 10 (bottom panel). The unit processes (e.g., deacetylation pretreatment, enzyme
production, and hydrotreating) contributing to emissions of different CAPs vary considerably
between the two end uses of lignin at the biorefinery. In the lignin-for-electricity scenario,
emissions of all pollutants besides NH3 are dominated by the boiler. As mentioned previously,
NOx and VOC emissions of the electricity scenario are nearly 3–5 times higher than those of the
pellet scenario and the SO2 emissions are more than 100 times higher. The contribution of the
boiler to emissions in the lignin-for-pellet scenario is much less than in the electricity scenario
because the pellet coproducing biorefinery does not combust hydrocarbon matter on-site to
export electricity.

Emissions of NH3 are dominated (~88% of the annual total) by cellulase enzyme production
required for the hydrolysis process that converts cellulose to hydrocarbons. The hydrolysis
process requires ammonia as a chemical input. Because the hydrolysis process is key to
hydrocarbon conversion, it is present for both lignin uses; this is also true for the acid and
conditioning pretreatment process, which accounts for most of the remaining ammonia emissions
at either biorefinery case. In the feedstock production stage, almost all ammonia emissions are
from fertilizer application. After land application of synthetic nitrogen fertilizers such as urea,
anhydrous ammonia, and nitrogen-phosphorous-potassium (NPK), ammonia can be released
through volatilization into the atmosphere (Behera et al. 2013). Globally, agriculture accounts for
81% of annual ammonia emissions in which fertilizer application (synthetic and others)
correspond to 40% of ammonia emissions in the United States (Domingo et al. 2021; Wyer et al.
2022). Given this outsized role of agricultural activities in ammonia emissions, it is not
surprising that fertilizer application in corn fields is the most dominant emission process.

In addition to ammonia emissions, nitrogenous fertilizer application is also the leading process
contributing to NOx emissions from the feedstock stage (~95% of total NOx emissions). Recent
studies have shown soil microbial activities can be a major and underestimated source of NOx
emissions from fertilized agricultural lands alongside operations of on-farm agricultural
machinery for harvesting and other activities (Oikawa et al. 2015; Almaraz et al. 2018). NOx
emissions at the biorefinery are almost entirely from the boiler, although the magnitude of NOx
emissions differs significantly between the two uses of lignin. NOx emissions from the boiler in
the lignin-for-pellet biorefinery design are ~75% less than those of the lignin-for-electricity

 23

biorefinery, but the boiler still contributes to nearly 98% of annual NOx emissions from the
biorefinery in the pellet scenario. NOx emissions from all other sources (e.g., preheater,
emergency generator, and fire pump) are much smaller for both lignin uses.

Primary PM2.5 is the other pollutant (besides ammonia) whose emissions are higher in the
feedstock stage than the biorefinery stage. Almost all primary PM2.5 from the feedstock
production stage is emitted as fugitive dust during crop harvesting. However, the lignin pelletizer
emits a significant amount of primary PM2.5, which is not present in the process design of the
electricity cogenerating biorefinery. The boiler and feedstock handling are the major processes
contributing to almost 95% of PM2.5 emissions collectively in the lignin-for-electricity scenario
whereas a similar contribution from pelletizer and feedstock handling is found in the lignin-for-
pellet scenario.

In the lignin-for-electricity scenario, the boiler is by far the largest source of SO2 emissions
(~98% of total SO2 emitted). Enzyme production is the primary contributing source of SO2
emissions in the lignin-for-pellet scenario (~98% of annual total emissions). At the electricity
cogenerating biorefinery, more than 90% of VOC emissions are from the boiler (~ 76% of total
VOC emitted), cooling tower (~10%), and wastewater treatment plant (~6%). The contribution
of different processes to VOC emissions is similarly distributed in the pellet coproducing
biorefinery as about 75% of the total emissions are due to the boiler (~25% of total VOC
emitted), wastewater treatment plant (~23%), and cooling tower (~22%). As mentioned
previously in this section, SO2 and VOC emissions in the feedstock production stage are very
small (i.e., cumulatively less than 1% of total emissions) and are caused by two major processes,
on-farm machinery use for harvesting and loading operations.

Although the PTE estimations of the simulated RDB biorefinery accounted for anticipated
pollution controls, including flue gas desulfurization and selective noncatalytic reduction
(SNCR), lignin combustion at the boiler can still have significant emissions of NOx, VOC, and
SO2 (Bhatt et al. 2022). On the other hand, Bhatt et al. (2022) do not assume any emission
control equipment for the process of drying and pelletizing lignin, leading to uncontrolled
emissions of primary PM2.5 emissions being almost 30% higher in the lignin-for-pellet scenario
compared to the lignin-for-electricity scenario. With appropriate control technologies, emissions
can be decreased compared to PTE estimates provided here, likely leading to smaller air quality
impacts in some regions.

4 Discussion
Potential-to-estimate for the corn stover to renewable diesel blendstock biorefineries with two
separate waste lignin use cases suggests the refineries considered here can be a major source of
emission (i.e., annual emissions > 100 tons) for one or more of the five regulated CAPs. We start
with these emission estimates and conduct analysis to understand their impacts on county-level
and regional air quality as well as regulatory implications of these changes in PM2.5
concentrations by considering the change at specific air quality monitoring sites (typically used
for nonattainment analysis by EPA). An analysis is also conducted to understand the relative
roles of feedstock production and biorefining stages of biofuel production on air quality. Lastly,
once the relative air quality impacts from the two biofuel production stages are assessed, we
explored the contribution of different unit processes to total and relative emissions to provide
preliminary guidance so that process-specific emission reduction strategies could be
implemented by practitioners in the biofuel industry to reduce emissions and air quality impacts.
Air quality impacts are expressed through changes in ambient concentrations of PM2.5 whereas
emissions analysis considers the mass emissions of relevant air pollutants (NOx, NH3, primary
PM2.5, SO2, and VOCs).

In the highest emitting scenario (the largest biorefinery size with lignin-for-electricity), the
feedstock production stage increases the mean concentration of PM2.5 across all monitor
locations by 0.0025 µg/m3. For a given scale and host location, PM2.5-related air quality impacts
from the feedstock stage are 2–10 times higher than from the biorefineries. Biorefinery
emissions’ air quality impacts analyzed here suggest the county-level and regional air quality
impacts are small. For example, the maximum of the mean of annual average change in PM2.5
concentration across all monitors from any of the county-size lignin use cases is <0.001 µg/m3,
which has order-of-magnitude similarity to the biorefinery-attributed concentrations predicted in
prior works. Ravi et al. (2018) studied two biorefinery locations in the Pacific Northwest region
of United States and found biorefinery emissions contributed to a change in PM2.5 concentration
by <0.5 µg/m3. However, differences in various factors such as emissions regions, feedstock
types, process design, and model resolutions make a direct comparison difficult. The impacts
analysis in this work that focuses on county-level averages indicates simulated increase in annual
average concentrations of PM2.5 in some counties immediately downwind of the biorefinery can
be as high as 0.025–0.25 µg/m3, with the change in some host counties at the largest biorefinery
size reaching 0.25–2.5 µg/m3. A recent study focused on biorefinery emissions in New York
State suggests the local impacts can be much higher at receptors immediately downwind of the
biorefinery. Lee et al. (2021) studied impacts of emissions from three biorefinery designs and
found the highest simulated marginal increase in concentrations can reach 3.97–11.3 µg/m3. The
air quality model used in this work, has an adaptive spatial grid cell sizing algorithm that can
provide analysis outcomes at 1 km. However, analysis and reporting at this spatial resolution
may be inadequate to understand true county-level impacts, which require even higher spatial
resolution (sub kilometer scale) and use of different numerical models such as AERMOD, an
EPA regulatory model used in Lee et al. (2021) to capture fine-scale impacts.

Our analysis using the ARCIE metric to assess the potential regulatory impact of biorefinery
sources suggests no additional monitors would have an incremental change because of
biorefineries that can lead them to exceed the EPA-stipulated primary NAAQS for annual PM2.5
concentration. There are three to six monitor locations (per scenario) where PM2.5 concentration

 26

currently exceeds the EPA NAAQS for annual average PM2.5, which will continue to be in the
same category after biorefinery sources are introduced. Note the ARCIE metric used here is for
PM2.5, which had a NAAQS annual average limit of 12.0 µg/m3 when we began this analysis.
EPA revised the PM2.5 standard to 9.0 µg/m3 in March 2024 (U.S. EPA 2024) and implemented
the change in May 2024 (National Archives and Records Administration 2024). However, our
analysis does not consider the implications of this change. Nevertheless, given the incremental
changes observed in simulated results, this change in PM2.5 NAAQS is unlikely to impact many
monitors. Although there will likely be many more monitors where annual average PM2.5
concentration will exceed 9.0, they will be out of compliance before the introduction of
biorefineries 5 such that results would yield the same count of monitors that are considered
“flipped.”

Neither of the biofuel supply chain stages analyzed here (feedstock production or biorefining)
has higher emissions across all criteria pollutants. Emissions of ammonia, sulfur, and nitrogen
oxides can lead to the formation of inorganic aerosols in the atmosphere (Seinfeld and Pandis
2016). The feedstock stage emits far more NH3 than the biorefineries, which aligns with the
established understanding that agriculture is the largest contributor to NH3 emissions globally
(and in the United States) (Wyer et al. 2022). In the feedstock production stage, almost all
ammonia emissions are from fertilizer application, which is released in the atmosphere through
volatilization of ammonia contained in synthetic nitrogen fertilizers (Behera et al. 2013).
Emissions of NOx are largely from the boiler for electricity cogeneration at the lignin-for-
electricity biorefinery, but there are substantial emissions from the feedstock stage and lignin-
for-pellet biorefinery as well. SO2 emissions are also primarily from the boiler for electricity
cogeneration at the lignin-for-electricity biorefinery with very small emissions from the
feedstock stage or alternative use of lignin for pellet coproduction. Primary PM2.5 emissions are
highest from the feedstock stage (mostly attributable to fugitive dust emissions during
harvesting) and the corresponding biorefinery emissions about 50% of those from the feedstock
stage.

Sulfate and nitrate aerosols form the bulk (90%) of incremental total PM2.5 mass at select
monitors in the electricity cogenerating biorefinery case whereas primary PM2.5 and nitrate
aerosols contribute to more than 90% of the incremental total PM2.5 mass from pellet
coproducing biorefineries (Figure A-11). The fraction of incremental total PM2.5 mass that is
secondary organic aerosols is very small. This compositional difference can suggest some
preliminary strategies about the unit processes that can be focused on to mitigate emissions,
which in this case suggests emissions of NOx and SO2 for the electricity cogenerating
biorefinery and NOx and primary PM2.5 for the pellet coproducing biorefinery. In this work,
however, we did not conduct sensitivity studies of how this compositional impact can vary at
receptors near the emissions source versus those far from the source. This can be an important
consideration given time and length scales for the formation of secondary aerosols can be hours
to days and hundreds to thousands of kilometers. Also, the analysis conducted here is based on

5 Conclusion and Limitations
This study assesses and compares PM2.5-related air quality impacts of a corn stover to renewable
diesel blendstock biorefinery supply chain, focusing on two key stages: feedstock production and
conversion to renewable diesel at a biorefining facility. We also analyze the regulatory
implications of “new” emissions from the biorefinery and whether these new emissions could
lead to potential noncompliance concerns at downwind PM2.5 monitors. In addition, we compare
the emissions intensities of individual unit processes to identify processes that contribute most to
potential emissions of CAPs. In this analysis, we study the impacts from two design cases of the
biorefinery. In the first design, the waste lignin is used for electricity cogeneration; in the second
design, waste lignin is pelletized. This detailed analysis explores 26 unique combinations of
feedstock rates, waste lignin uses, and hypothetical biorefinery locations sited in select corn
stover production counties across Iowa.6

Our findings suggest despite the biorefineries being a major source of emissions, the incremental
annual average PM2.5 impacts across a regional scale of monitor locations are orders of
magnitude below the NAAQS standard. The simulated RDB biorefineries coproducing either
electricity or pellets can still cause county-averaged increments in annual average PM2.5 between
0.25 and 2.5 µg/m3. However, the emission inputs used for our analysis are based on a
preliminary PTE estimate, so opportunities exist to further reduce these PM2.5 increments.
Despite these near-source increments reaching up to 20% of the standard, no new downwind
monitors are expected to become noncompliant because of biorefinery emissions. Unit process
level emissions analysis suggests a focus on reducing emissions of NOx and SO2 from the boiler
at the electricity cogenerating biorefinery and reducing primary PM2.5 emissions from the
pelletizer and SO2 from the boiler at the lignin-for-pellet biorefinery can help further mitigate air
quality impacts. The corresponding unit process at the feedstock stage is fertilizer application,
which leads to significant emissions of NH3 and NOx. Given the nature of this source type,
specific, place-based ammonia management and fertilizer application strategies must be
considered.

Though the analysis performed is detailed, it is not comprehensive. The following caveats and
limitations are important to note:

 • This work relies on PTE emissions, which are the maximum emissions a biorefinery can
 emit at its theoretical design. Therefore, the reported changes in marginal concentration
 of PM2.5 can be considered theoretical maximum changes attributed to the hypothetical
 biorefineries and scenarios considered in this report. Likely, an herbaceous biofuel
 facility could be designed and operated so that the emissions are lower, reducing the air
 quality impacts reported here (e.g., by using different emission control equipment and

 • EPA implemented a change in the primary standard of PM2.5 in the NAAQS from three-

 depends on PM2.5 baseline concentrations of EPA monitor locations recorded in 2017

 • The baseline and incremental concentrations at monitors used in the ARCIE metric are
 one-year annual average values as reported by the EPA and InMAP, respectively, rather

 • An important caveat of this analysis is the use of a reduced complexity model of air
 quality, which can provide only a screening-level assessment from the emission sources
 in any analysis. A biorefinery, as a chemical facility, will have to conduct detailed
 analysis using regulatory-grade models to assess impacts on the concentrations of one or
 more pollutants at different averaging periods of those pollutants. Given the model design
 of the reduced complexity model used in this report (InMAP), we report only annual
 average changes in the concentration of PM2.5. Nevertheless, the analysis is useful to

 can be helpful to strategize the air-emission-related sustainability of the bioenergy

 • Recent studies have concluded biorefineries can be a significant source of NOx, VOCs,
 and other criteria pollutants, which can significantly affect the air quality in the
 immediate vicinity and short downwind distance (a few to tens of kilometers). Detailed
 modeling and analysis can provide additional insights into any emission reduction
 strategies.

 • Marginal changes in ground-level pollutant concentrations serve as input to estimate the
 air-related health implications of exposed populations via chemical response functions.
 Although InMAP exhibits such capability, this study did not consider the health-related
 outputs of the model because they fall outside the established scope. However, such
 metrics are useful to consider the environmental justice implications from newly
 introduced emissions in biofuel production and help alleviate social concerns with the
 future citing of its supply chain emissions.

 • Pollutant emissions and associated PM2.5 concentrations because of corn stover
 transportation from the field to the preprocessing facility, preprocessing of corn stover,
 and transportation to the biorefinery input gate were not considered. To capture the
 complete air quality implications from the upstream stages of corn-stover-based

 30

 renewable diesel, the transportation of renewable diesel from the biorefinery output gate
 to the end user must also be considered. To establish the entire lifecycle air quality
 implications, the emissions from the end-use (e.g., combustion) stage of the fuel must
 additionally be included. Depending on the system boundary established by the LCA
 practitioner, the additional air quality implications of avoided emissions from the
 coproduction of electricity and lignin pellets may be considered.

 • Our analysis does not consider any impacts on the displacement of products and goods
 upstream or downstream of our system boundary. For example, cogeneration of
 electricity from an RDB biorefinery in Iowa can displace fossil-fired electricity
 generating units as well as the mining, processing, and transportation of coal or other
 fossil fuels whereas the coproduction of lignin pellets may necessitate additional
 transportation-related fuel consumption for supplying this finished product to respective
 markets. Some of these aspects are considered in Quiroz et al. (forthcoming), but a
 comprehensive, systems-level assessment is outside the scope of this analysis.

Description of biorefinery areas
Area 100 involves conveying incoming blended feedstock from trucks to storage domes and then
to pretreatment in Area 200. Conveyors are equipped with dust collection and baghouse systems.
The pretreatment in Area 200 involves deacetylation with sodium hydroxide, acid treatment with
sulfuric acid, and conditioning with ammonia.to break down the lignocellulosic cells. This
introduces evaporative emissions and chemical by-products. Cellulose is converted to glucose
and then to free fatty acids in Area 300 which also introduces VOC emissions as by-products of
various chemical pathways involved in enzymatic hydrolysis and bioconversion. The hydrolysis
takes as input cellulase enzyme which is produced in Area 400. Area 400 emits SO2 and NH3
because SO2 is used as a nutrient and ammonia as an ingredient of enzyme production (note that
cellulase enzyme production involves other ingredients such as glucose, water, and air).
Emissions from hydrotreating in Area 500 are not accounted for because its exhaust is:
combusted in the Area 500 pre-heater or the Area 800 boiler. A pre-heater is used to heat free
fatty acids in Area 500 prior to hydrotreating and assumed to combust natural gas. Hence,
emissions of NOx, SO2, PM2.5, and VOC are present. Areas 200, 300, 500, and 800 produce
wastewater that is sent to be treated in Area 600. Emissions of VOCs in Area 600 occur as
fugitive emissions from atmospheric exposure of the wastewater, vapor leaks through openings,
and ventilation from equipment. Similarly, storage tanks of renewable diesel and process
chemicals including the loading of renewable diesel in transport vehicles in Area 700 introduces
fugitive emissions of VOCs. Just as the natural gas boiler for pre-heating in Area 500 introduces
by-products of combustion, the boiler for process heat and electric power generation in Area 800
which is assumed to combust natural gas produces the same pollutants (i.e., NOx, SO2, PM2.5,
and VOC). The boiler is equipped with flue gas desulfurization, overfire air, and selective non-
catalytic reduction. The pollutants also apply to the emergency power production and fire water
pumping in Area 900 which is assumed to combust diesel. Additionally, the cooling tower in the
utilities area (i.e., Area 900) is assumed to emit PM2.5 and VOCs from leaking process
equipment. Unique to the lignin-pellet co-producing biorefinery design, waste lignin is diverted

pressing/pelletizing operations also emit PM2.5. Lastly, fugitive emissions of VOCs are a result

A.2 Generation of InMAP Ready Biorefinery Emission Files
Six potential-to-emit (PTE) estimates (i.e., annual emission rates for the CAPs) representing two
waste lignin uses (i.e., lignin-for-electricity and lignin-for-pellet) , and three input feedstock rates
(i.e., 2,000, 5,200, and 9,100 DMT/day), are calculated. These biorefinery annual emissions of
NOx, SO2, primary PM2.5, VOCs, and NH3 are converted into an ESRI shapefile using python’s
geopandas module, with the centroid for each of the relevant counties serving as the location of
hypothetical biorefinery site. Additional emission source attributes of stack height, stack exit
diameter, flue gas exit velocity, and flue gas exit temperature are based on central tendencies of
the corresponding data for ethanol biorefineries based on U.S. Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) National Emissions Inventory (NEI) unit-level data. With a total of 13 origin counties
identified within Iowa, we generate 26 different ESRI shapefiles for use as an input in the
Intervention Model for Air Pollution (InMAP).

A.3 Mapping InMAP Modeled Incremental PM2.5 Concentrations to the
Air Quality System Monitor Locations
The annual average incremental PM2.5 concentration at monitor locations is obtained by
geospatially overlaying the InMAP gridded concentration outputs corresponding to biorefinery
emissions with the latitude/longitude (lat/lon) locations of selected monitors. The locations of
such monitors are obtained by filtering the data directly obtained from EPA air quality system
(AQS) by the state, county, monitor site number, and parameter occurrence code for which
identified origin counties in the state of Iowa corn stover production air quality model results are
available. These results can be found in the supplementary information of Thind et al. (2022).
These tabular data are transformed to geospatial data via Python by defining geospatially linked
point geometries from the longitudinal and latitudinal coordinates obtained from raw EPA AQS
data. The native file format of InMAP model outputs is GIS-based so that they are directly
imported into Python as geodatabases. By reprojecting the point objects representing selected
EPA monitors from a three-dimensional Coordinate Reference System (CRS) into the two-
dimensional CRS for which InMAP outputs are available, both layers are now overlayed. From
there, InMAP grids that are not intersected by a monitor are eliminated because the concentration
results that pertain to these grids are deemed irrelevant for calculating the Available Regulatory
Capacity for Incremental Emissions (ARCIE) metric. The results consist of a unique geodatabase
per scenario containing the average background concentration directly measured by EPA and the
incremental concentration predicted by InMAP at EPA AQS monitoring locations.

A.10 Additional Details on Emissions From Biorefinery and Feedstock
Production
Emissions vary in magnitude depending on the production scale and/or the origin county location
because of the difference in agricultural mass and harvest land area required to meet a given
production scale. For example, at 2,000 DMT/day, the direct NOx and NH3 emissions from the
crops and land are constant at 111 and 552 tons per year, respectively, across the various origin
counties. Similarly, at 9,100 DMT/day, NOx and NH3 are constant at 505 and 2520 tons per
year, respectively, because the amount of ammonia-based fertilizer and nutrient losses through
direct emission from the crops and soil depends on the mass rather than area of biomass required
to be produced.

However, NOx and PM2.5 emissions from harvesting activities vary considerably and scale
according to the size of harvesting area required to produce a given corn stover rate. Depending
on the resource potential of corn stover production for counties in Iowa, the reach into
surrounding source counties (if necessary) and the proportion of their land area used to meet a
given production rate are determined. With more land area to cover for certain origin counties to
meet a given corn stover production rate, NOx emissions from fuel usage and fugitive PM2.5 dust
emissions from harvest machinery operating across the field increase. For example, at 2,000
DMT/day, NOx emissions vary from 1.53 to 2.62 tons per year whereas PM2.5 emissions vary
from 29.7 to 51.0 tons per year from harvesting between Sioux and Marion as the origin
counties, respectively. Though only 8.7% of corn stover production can be met by Marion
County because of its production capacity, the rest is sourced from three neighboring counties
(i.e., Marchall, Jasper, and Mahaska). At the same time, Sioux is a self-sufficient county, which
means it can produce 2,000 DMT/day alone without having to source from surrounding counties.
This results in 1.84 times more land required to produce 2,000 DMT/day of corn stover with
Marion as the origin county versus that of Sioux County (i.e., 5610 versus 1980 km2).

Similarly, at 9,100 DMT/day, NOx and PM2.5 emissions from harvesting range from 6.94 to 9.85
and 135 to 192 tons per year, respectively, between O’Brien and Scott as origin counties. Though
O’Brien can supply 21% of the production rate, only 7.5% can be met by Scott County. As a
result, 73.2% more land is used to produce 9,100 DMT/day of corn stover with Scott as the
origin county versus that of O’Brien County (i.e., 12,800 versus 7,370 km2). Because NOx is a
hydrocarbon combustion by-product and PM2.5 is suspended from loose grounds under the
influence of strong physical forces, the fuel use and transport distances of corn stover harvest
machinery directly influences NOx and PM2.5 emissions. Therefore, as the average land area
required for harvest increases with the corn stover production rate, NOx and PM2.5 emissions
also increase with production rate because of an increase in harvesting machinery activity. No
self-sufficient origin counties were identified in Iowa to meet 5,200 and 9,100 DMT/day of corn
stover production so that the physical reach of origin counties into neighboring counties to
supplement corn stover production is inversely proportional to the corn stover resource potential
of the neighboring source counties.
