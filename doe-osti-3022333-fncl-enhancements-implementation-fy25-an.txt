Executive Summary
The FNCL investigations team at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) has completed
research and development of hardware, signal processing, and analysis tools to enhance the
measurement capabilities of both the current CAEN SyS VeryFuel Fast Neutron Collar (FNCL)
instrument and a next-generation FNCL prototype. The team successfully built and commissioned
the LLNL Demonstrator System: a fully integrated, three-panel detector system featuring higher
segmentation, plastic scintillators (EJ-276D), Silicon Photomultipliers (SiPMs), no high-voltage
requirement, a reduced electronic footprint, and the LLNL-developed Gaussian Mixture Model Pulse
Shape Discrimination (GMM-PSD) signal processing.

An extensive experimental campaign was conducted at LLNL’s Inherently Safe Subcritical Assembly
(ISSA) facility using both the baseline FNCL and the LLNL Demonstrator. The campaign results
validated system performance, calibration stability, and the eaectiveness of advanced signal
processing and analysis algorithms in a relevant environment.

Key accomplishments for FY25 include:

  - Design, assembly, and commissioning of three LLNL Demonstrator panels featuring SiPM
 instrumentation (eliminating the need for high voltage), EJ-276D plastic scintillator, and a
 compact, deployable form factor.

  - Successful demonstration and calibration of both the baseline FNCL and Demonstrator
 systems using standard sources and the new VX2730 CAEN digitizer.

  - Deployment of the baseline FNCL at the ISSA facility with the capability to interrogate up to
 nine ISSA fuel elements (33.4 g/cm U-235 linear density).

  - Application of optimized GMM-PSD analysis, achieving equivalent relative uncertainty to the
 as-built FNCL data acquisition system (DAQ) with a 28% reduction in measurement time.

  - Benchmarking of the LLNL Demonstrator System at ISSA, achieving induced ﬁssion doubles
 rates with <1% statistical uncertainty, comparable to the baseline FNCL.

  - Active interrogation of ISSA fuel elements using both AmLi and DD neutron generator
 sources, demonstrating the ability to measure induced ﬁssion neutron doubles rates and
 supporting the feasibility of calibration curve assay approaches for fresh fuel assemblies
 (FFAs).

 9

  - Successful benchmarking of the FNCL – Fast Isotopic Fuel Assay (FIFA) analysis software
 using a wide range of mock FFAs, with and without burnable poisons, and validation of its
 predictive capability for U-235 and gadolinium content.

  - Initiation of formal technology transfer to CAEN for future commercialization, including both
 hardware and software developed at LLNL.

These advancements position the LLNL Demonstrator system for improved deployability, enhanced
measurement performance, and expanded analytical capabilities for nuclear safeguards
applications. The formal transfer of technology to CAEN began in FY25 and will continue through
FY26.

Introduction
A multidisciplinary team of researchers at LLNL is developing hardware, signal processing, and
analysis tools which can substantially enhance the measurement capabilities of the CAEN SyS
VeryFuel Fast Neutron Collar (FNCL) instrument, developed for the assay of fresh fuel assemblies
(FFAs) in IAEA safeguards applications. During FY24, the LLNL team completed the operational
testing of the LLNL development panels built in FY23 and assessed the performance of neutron
detection array instrumentation options for a next-generation FNCL panel prototype. Potential FNCL
next-generation panel design options were further informed by detailed instrument response models
implemented in both MCNP and Geant4 transport codes, both validated via extensive laboratory
experiments with the baseline FNCL instrument measuring multiple nuclear material samples. The
team leveraged conclusions from this analysis, with direct input from the IAEA on FNCL
instrumentation needs [1], to design and build an updated FNCL prototype panel. The baseline FNCL
was deployed at LLNL’s Inherently Safe Subcritical Assembly (ISSA) facility, and measurements were
made to conﬁrm the fuel elements would be satisfactory for testing the prototype system. The
groundwork was also laid for extended experimental measurements at ISSA with both systems.
These eaorts culminated in the completion of the updated FNCL prototype panel by the end of FY24.
Supporting analysis and hardware eaorts for improved pulse shape discrimination between gamma
and neutron signals also advanced. This includes the implementation of the GMM algorithm in
CAEN’s on-board Field Programmable Gate Array (FPGA) software, as well as the design and testing
of SiPM pulse-shortening electronics boards for improved PSD and reduced data throughput.

In FY25, the hardware and software enhancements previously developed have been propagated into
a full 3-panel LLNL Demonstrator System. This is a fully integrated detector system operating with an
updated CAEN digitizer for higher segmentation, no high-voltage requirement, a smaller electronic
footprint, and implemented with the LLNL-developed GMM-PSD signal processing. The team has
conducted a second set of measurements at the ISSA facility with extensive testing of all developed
technologies in a relevant environment. The ISSA deployment milestones included evaluating the
performance of a DD generator for rapid active interrogation measurements in comparison to the
standard AmLi source, benchmarking of the full 3-panel demonstrator with utilization of the GMM-
PSD algorithm and SiPM pulse shaping technology, and validating the FNCL - Fast Isotopic Fuel
Assay’s (FIFA) capability to measure U-235 content and to predict gadolinium poison content with
passive interrogation data.

The formal transfer of these developed technologies has required that CAEN and LLNL enter a mutual
nondisclosure agreement (executed mid-Q2); technical discussions started in FY25 Q2 and will
continue into FY26.

The LLNL Demonstrator System
Overview
The LLNL Demonstrator System design has been developed and completed in FY25 to address the
needs requested by the IAEA during the May FY24 project meeting. The IAEA requested several
technological enhancements to improve the deployability of the baseline FNCL system while
maintaining or improving on measurement performance, which included the following key elements:

- Reduction in the FNCL panel form factor for easier transport and setup between facilities,
 speciﬁcally through replacement of Photomultiplier Tubes (PMTs) with Silicon Photomultipliers
 (SiPMs) for scintillation light readout

- Replacement of the current-generation FNCL’s EJ-309 liquid scintillator with a solid scintillator
 alternative that eliminates liquid ﬂammability concerns, oaers comparable Pulse Shape
 Discrimination (PSD) performance, has a robust form factor, and is cost-eaective and
 commercially available

- Removal of the need for high voltage to power the FNCL PMTs, which removes high voltage
 safety concerns that could be a barrier to deployment in certain contexts

At the end of FY24, a prototype FNCL panel was designed and constructed to lay the groundwork for
this request. The panel utilized nine SensL ARRAYJ-60035-64P-PCB SiPM arrays, CAEN’s
commercially available readout boards, and Eljen EJ-276D plastic scintillator to detect fast neutrons
from ﬁssion. Performance of the panel was successfully demonstrated at end of FY24.

In FY25, a set of three SiPM-based FNCL detector panels, referred to as the LLNL Demonstrator
System, were designed, assembled, commissioned, and deployed at LLNL’s ISSA facility to
benchmark system performance. The three panels were operated using CAEN’s newest generation
VX2730 digitizer board, with all SiPMs powered by CAEN’s SiPM-focused bias supplies.

Several key design features have been incorporated into the panel design for improved performance
and deployability:

 - All cable feedthroughs were installed on a patch panel, which can be easily serviced or
 replaced by removing several screws. Any future electronics or feedthrough design changes
 would only require modiﬁcation to the patch panel.

 - Incorporation of HDPE spacers between each detector row, providing attenuation of external
 background neutrons while also avoiding excessive self-shielding of fast ﬁssion neutrons from
 the FFA.

 - Lead sheets and protective covers were placed at the top and bottom detector rows to reduce
 gamma-ray background rates produced by a fresh fuel assembly. This was not possible in the
 baseline FNCL panels due to the positioning of the PMTs.

 - Addition of a feedthrough for temperature monitoring and gain compensation of the SiPMs.
 The CAEN-supplied temperature sensor was installed in one of the cells to provide bias voltage
 compensation, stabilizing the SiPM charge response with temperature variation.

Performance Testing of the Completed System
Prior to deployment at the ISSA facility, the LLNL Demonstrator System needed to be operationally
tested in a laboratory setting. The individual panels were integrated with CAEN’s VX2730 digitizer and
SiPM bias supply hardware, then fully tested. The main steps needed to achieve a deployment-ready
system included:

 - Individual operation of each SiPM + EJ-276D detector cell to ensure SiPMs and readout
 boards were detecting scintillator light. This data was also used to set the digitizer
 conﬁguration settings, including the total waveform width (298 samples, or 496 ns), digitizer
 trigger type (a Constant Fraction discriminator setting of 75%), and each channel’s trigger
 thresholds. The thresholds were set at the lowest digitizer sample possible for all cells in
 order to maximize detection eaiciency at lower energy signatures and minimize excessive
 triggering on channel baseline noise.

 - Detailed studies on the required SiPM bias voltage compensation needed to maintain stable
 SiPM light response in varying operational temperatures. Temperature variations are
 primarily from changes in the facility’s ambient temperature or from the heat generated by
 the CAEN readout board ampliﬁers. See Appendix A for details on the temperature
 compensation studies and ﬁnal values used at the ISSA facility.

 - Energy response calibration of SiPM-scintillator cells. Each Demonstrator panel is powered
 with a single SiPM bias supply which was tuned using a Cs-137 point source. Bias values were
 chosen such that the SiPM pulses would saturate in the digitizer for energy signatures at
 approximately 1.25 MeV electron equivalent. Each channel’s digitizer gain was tuned to
 match the total charge measured across all channels in a single panel. Each panel’s bias
 voltage operated in the 29.5-30.0 V bias range, with the exact voltage being temperature
 dependent.

 - Veriﬁcation of GMM-PSD capability with the SiPM + EJ-276D detector cells using a Cf-252
 neutron and Cs-137 gamma-ray source. While this capability was already validated in
 previous studies, detector cells were individually checked to conﬁrm particle discrimination
 was performing as expected. Figure 4 shows the LLNL Demonstrator System during testing
 and examples of raw waveform data collected after tuning parameters in CoMPASS.

FNCL Operations at the ISSA Facility
Overview of the ISSA Facility
A key objective over the lifetime of the FNCL Enhancements project has been to demonstrate the use
of all developed hardware and software technologies in a relevant environment and compare to the
baseline FNCL system. To achieve this deliverable, the ISSA facility at LLNL was identiﬁed as a viable
choice to host the deployment of the baseline FNCL and the LLNL Demonstrator Systems in order to
exercise the new system capabilities.

The ISSA facility has several features which made it an attractive relevant environment for
deployment. The ISSA facility provides fuel elements composed of parallel plates of U-235-enriched
uranium oxide (93% enrichment) pressed between aluminum plates, providing 232 g of U-235 per
element. These fuel elements provide an elongated source geometry with a U-235 linear density of
3.71 g/cm per fuel element, encasement is 73.7 cm long, with internal parallel source plates of 62.6
cm length. These fuel elements can emulate the source proﬁle of a FFA more eaectively than a typical
point or can-sized source. From an operational standpoint, the ISSA facility was desirable because
the FNCL team was given full priority to take measurements with ISSA fuel elements and allowed to
work largely independently from other facility operations after all necessary trainings and job
brieﬁngs were conducted. Additionally, conducting operations at an LLNL-based facility reduced the
cost burden incurred from shipping equipment and operator travel.

A preliminary deployment of the baseline FNCL system was conducted in FY24 to validate the ISSA
fuel elements as a relevant source for conducting U-235 loading measurements. The baseline FNCL
system was used to measure up to four ISSA fuel elements in passive (no interrogation source) and

Scope of the FY25 ISSA Measurement Campaign
After the successful FY24 deployment, a second deployment with an extended scope of operations
was planned and executed in FY25. The key objectives identiﬁed for this deployment included:

 - Objective A: Deploy the LLNL Demonstrator System with the completed detector panels at the
 ISSA facility. Compare the performance of the LLNL Demonstrator System with the baseline
 FNCL system using both passive and active neutron interrogation.

 - Objective B: Compare the baseline FNCL system’s capability to characterize U-235 content with
 active interrogation using an AmLi source and a DD generator. Perform these measurements
 using up to nine ISSA fuel elements, more than doubling the total U-235 content measured in
 FY24.

 - Objective C: Conduct passive measurements of up to nine ISSA fuel elements using both
 systems with a minimum presence of cadmium. Utilize data for benchmarking the FNCL-FIFA
 algorithm’s U-235 content prediction capability.

Early FY25 eaorts focused on necessary preparations such as developing all needed changes to
facility documentation, conducting criticality safety evaluations, and scheduling the usage of AmLi
and DD source equipment for the deployment. Two DD generators were acquired, a Thermo

Validation of System Stability During ISSA Deployment
Prior to processing and analysis of any ISSA fuel data, all Cs-137 and Cf-252 datasets collected over
the ISSA deployment campaign were analyzed to conﬁrm stability of both detectors. When checking
stability of both systems, three key data features were monitored to ensure minimal variation
throughout the measurement campaign:

 - Compton shoulder peak position of Cs-137 calibration data in each individual channel. A stable
 detector cell should measure the same PMT or SiPM charge distribution throughout data
 collection. The Compton shoulder can also be used to calibrate the charge-to-energy response
 for each detector cell. Energy calibration was successful for both systems and produced similar
 distributions for both systems’ detectors, as shown below in Figure 8.

 - The neutron and gamma-ray waveform templates from the GMM-PSD algorithm should not vary
 considerably throughout the measurement campaign. A stable detector cell should measure
 similar neutron-like and gamma-ray-like pulses throughout the campaign. Both systems
 showed very consistent GMM template shapes across all channels throughout data collect. The
 average neutron and gamma-ray templates for both systems in a single channel are shown in
 Figure 9.

GMM-PSD for Improved Particle Identiﬁcation
Overview
The Gaussian Mixture Model based Pulse Shape Discrimination (GMM-PSD) algorithm was
developed at LLNL to discriminate between neutron and gamma interactions in organic scintillators
such as EJ-309. It has been previously demonstrated by this team to provide high performance
particle identiﬁcation for diaerent organic scintillators at low energies and in high gamma
environments [8]. The work performed this year has focused on implementing the GMM-PSD
algorithm as ﬁrmware for the onboard FPGA digitizer being used as the LLNL Demonstrator’s data
acquisition system. In addition, the team has applied this implementation to be used by the baseline
FNCL system. In current FNCL software, digitized waveforms from each cell are processed by
applying a series of "ﬁlters” (coincidence, energy, PSD, pile-up), after which the time-stamped events
are recorded as a list mode ﬁle for processing post-data collect. PSD is performed using the
conventional tail-to-total ratio method [11] and signals are reduced to the rates of single neutrons
and of coincident neutrons, called doubles. The goal this year was to use the GMM-PSD waveform
processing to streamline the identiﬁcation of neutron and gamma-ray pulses in order to maximize
the signal collection in high-rate environments such as those near an FFA. This section will give a
brief overview of the theory involved in the GMM-PSD algorithm followed by an in-depth discussion
on its implementation on to the LLNL Demonstrator DAQ and the resulting performance.

Theory and Motivation
The GMM-PSD method discriminates between neutron and gamma interactions in organic
scintillators such as EJ-309. It is based on a speciﬁc set of preprocessing steps along with shape and
variance templates. The class of algorithms known as Gaussian Mixture Models are used by a
statistical learning model to produce these templates. For more details regarding its development
see [7].

Statistical learning classiﬁers use measured quantities, called features, to group each item in a
dataset into labeled categories. Labeling is eventually discrete but can be based on a continuous
score compared to one or more thresholds. The number of categories the classiﬁer assumes the
dataset contains are called components. A two-component variant of GMM is utilized for this
application, gamma and neutron. The number of features in our model is equal to the number of
utilized waveform samples available after preprocessing. The algorithm also produces estimates of
the variability for each of the features. They are represented as the diagonal elements of a covariance
matrix, and the joint variability between features as the oa-diagonal elements. The covariance
estimate is assumed to be the same for each component (i.e. tied) with only the diagonal elements
utilized for classiﬁcation scoring. The model is trained using machine learning on neutron and
gamma signals from a Cf-252 point source. The trained model can then be used for experimental
data analysis.

During analysis each scintillator pulse is preprocessed, then compared utilizing the Mahalanobis
distance [7] to the templates. The results are combined to form a ﬁnal score, the GMM-parameter,
that varies from -1 to 1. A positive GMM-parameter indicates a pulse that is more “neutron like”,
whereas a negative value corresponds to one that is “gamma like”. If the GMM parameter value is
close to zero this indicates a poor match to either template or is indicative of pulse-pile up. See
Appendix for an in-depth description of the pre-processing, training, and pulse scoring steps used
to create the GMM-PSD analysis.

GMM-PSD Integration onto the CAEN Digitizer
To maximize the success of commercial support for GMM-PSD we have coordinated with CAEN to
optimize our software implementation and dependencies. We have used CAEN developed tools
such as Scikit-learn for integration into the current FNCL digitizer software, Sci-Compiler-based
ﬁrmware for transferring ﬁrmware, and maintained active communication with their team during
development and testing.

The VX2730 32-channel, 14-bit, 500 MS/s CAEN digitizer boards oaer the capability for FPGA-based
user implementation of particle discrimination algorithms for real-time pulse identiﬁcation. The
general implementation of the GMM algorithms on FPGAs was previously demonstrated at LLNL [9].
By replacing the four B1370D digitizers in the baseline FNCL we gain robust clock distribution and
synchronization, in addition to the ability to program the GMM-PSD algorithm into the ﬁrmware. The
LLNL Demonstrator DAQ electronics have signiﬁcantly reduced size, weight, and number of
components, with a data throughput that will match or exceed the speciﬁcations of the baseline
system DAQ.

The onboard GMM-PSD algorithm was developed using Sci-Compiler (OpenFPGA) modules to create
the ﬁrmware logic and readout. Sci-Compiler’s utility uses graphical block diagrams to generate and
compile ﬁrmware for CAEN FPGA-based digitizers. Sci-Compiler utilizes Xilinx Vivado internally to
convert block diagram code to gate-level hardware code, which sets up the FPGA to perform the
desired functionality, in this case onboard GMM-PSD.

The high-level logic of the GMM-PSD ﬁrmware is illustrated in Figure 11. The incoming waveform
undergoes polarity inversion, and then the trigger generation logic is used to create a trigger signal to
initiate the subsequent waveform processing steps. The waveform then undergoes the
preprocessing steps of baseline subtraction, variance normalization1 (or Anscombe), and shifted z-
score normalization. First, the preprocessed waveform is read out for neutron and GMM template
generation using Scikit-learn’s Gaussian Mixture library. Then, the templates are written back into the
RAM memory of the digitizer. During scoring mode acquisition, the preprocessed waveforms are
scored against the loaded templates for GMM-based PSD for neutron and gamma ray identiﬁcation.
We also developed the data I/O Sci-Compiler routines and implemented a readout code for the
digitizer. This uses the list mode component built into the ﬁrmware logic to write the time-stamped
events with the scored GMM parameter, integrated pulse charge, and either the raw waveform, or the
preprocessed waveform, to ﬁle.

An important note is that the training of the waveform templates using Scikit-learn must be
performed oaline. This is because an onboard GMM template training would require developing an
Expectation Maximization (EM) algorithm which is iterative and sequential in logic and requires
extensive memory and high precision ﬂoating-point arithmetic. As FPGAs are limited in memory
compared to CPUs/GPUs and mostly work on ﬁxed point arithmetic, implementing the GMM training
onboard is unfeasible.

Experimental Analysis and Results

Analysis Methodology for Experimental Data
The performance comparisons between the baseline FNCL and the Demonstrator system used
much of the same information that is generated in the standard exam report made by the FNCL DAQ
software. The key metric in this report is the rate of neutron coincidences called neutron doubles,
which is used with a calibration curve, to predict the linear loading of U-235 of the item being
interrogated. Neutron doubles are deﬁned as two consecutive neutron signals, in diaerent detector
cells, within a time window that is optimized to select ﬁssion neutrons from the same ﬁssion event.

The ﬁrst analysis step is to process interrogation data with the FCAna software package [18], which
applies all energy calibration constants, channel timing oasets, and GMM-PSD template scoring to
raw detector pulses. All calibration and GMM-PSD values are acquired from FCAna preprocessing
steps with Cs-137 and Cf-252 data, the same data used to validate detector stability throughout the
ISSA deployment. While the baseline FNCL data analysis was completed with the version of the
FCAna software package transferred to the IAEA in FY24, the LLNL Demonstrator System data was
processed with a slightly modiﬁed FCAna software in order to accommodate the diaerences in SiPM
pulse sizes as well as expanding to 27 detector cells from the baseline’s 12 cells.

After interrogation data is calibrated and scored using GMM-PSD, a selection criteria is applied to the
detector pulses to identify neutron doubles candidates. First, neutron candidates are selected from
detector pulses using a cut in GMM parameter vs. Energy space. Then, neutron coincidences are
selected using a timing cut while also rejecting neutron double-scatter background candidates. To
select neutron coincidence candidates in time, any two neutron-like pulses which occur within <
74 𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 of each other are accepted as a neutron coincidence pair consistent with timing expected from
a single ﬁssion of uranium. This is the same timing cut used to select neutron coincidence
candidates in previous results and in the FCAna software package provided to the IAEA.

 FY25 Annual Report FNCL Enhancements Implementation 34

There is an additional timing requirement applied to coincident neutrons detected in the same
detector panel in the baseline FNCL to reduce the rate of neutrons scattering from one cell to a
neighboring cell and producing a “double-scatter background” or “fake double”. This timing cut
rejects any two neutron pulses within < 14 𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 of each other as double-scatter background. The LLNL
Demonstrator System has an increased cell density per panel which allows for more ﬂexibility in how
these double-scatter neutron events are rejected. For this analysis, any two neutron pulses within <
14 𝑛𝑛𝑛𝑛 of each other in the same detector cell row are rejected as double-scatter background.

A signal optimization study was performed using the ISSA datasets to maximize the number of
induced ﬁssion doubles measured for each interrogation source type. See Appendix D for an in-depth
explanation of this study and its ﬁndings.

AmLi Interrogation with the LLNL Demonstrator System

To compare the baseline FNCL’s doubles count rate with the Demonstrator system, the standard
workﬂow was performed for both systems with the same fuel elements in the same environment.
Each system was initially energy calibrated with Cs-137 and PSD calibrated with a Cf-252 source.

DD Generator Interrogation with the Baseline FNCL system

Measurements of the ISSA fuel elements were conducted using the Starﬁre DD generator and the
Thermo Scientiﬁc DD generators. For DD generator-based measurements, the ﬁssion-based neutron
doubles count rate is estimated by taking a DD-interrogated dataset with fuel elements in the volume
and then taking a DD-interrogation dataset with no fuel elements in the interrogation volume. The
doubles rate measured with zero fuel elements is then subtracted from the doubles rate measured
with fuel elements in the interrogation volume.

Note that this background subtraction technique is not optimal, as the background doubles rate due
to the DD generator could change between when fuel elements are and are not present. In the case
of the ISSA fuel elements, this systematic eaect is also expected to increase as more fuel elements
are introduced to the interrogation volume. Fortunately, for actual application with FFAs, this
diaerence should only produce a constant oaset in the calibration curve and wouldn’t aaect the
curve’s shape. The magnitude of this systematic oaset was studied by operating the Starﬁre DD
generator with ISSA mock fuel elements, which have source plates made of aluminum oxide like the

FNCL – Fast Isotopic Fuel Assay (FIFA) Analysis Software
Overview of the Methodology
The alternative algorithm theory team has developed, over the last several years, a theoretical model
to predict an analytic waiting time distribution (WTD) for neutron arrival times out of FFAs using
passive measurements. This work was built from an original model developed by Richard Feynman
[15] but has been improved upon signiﬁcantly. We use a theoretical model to predict the evolution of
ﬁssion chains in FFA fuel. Fission chain evolution can be understood by analyzing WTDs, or the
distribution of arrival times of successive neutrons in the detector. This model predicts a WTD for a
FFA as well as several features unique to FFAs such as the low fuel density, and the ability of neutrons
to escape and return to the fuel after thermalizing or partially thermalizing. The fact that thermalized
neutrons re-enter the FFA is crucial to our work, as only thermalized neutrons have a high likelihood
to induce ﬁssion on U-235. As such, measuring that signal allows us to probe the U-235 content in
the FFA with only passive interrogation.

Summary
The FY25 FNCL Enhancements Implementation project successfully advanced both hardware and
software capabilities for the CAEN SyS VeryFuel Fast Neutron Collar (FNCL) system, targeting
improved fresh fuel assay performance and deployability for IAEA safeguards applications. The team
designed, built, and commissioned the LLNL Demonstrator System—a fully integrated, three-panel
detector array utilizing SiPMs, EJ-276D plastic scintillators, and a compact, low-voltage electronic
architecture. Enhanced signal processing was achieved through the implementation of the LLNL-
developed Gaussian Mixture Model Pulse Shape Discrimination (GMM-PSD) algorithm, both in
software and as on-board ﬁrmware for CAEN digitizers.

An extensive experimental campaign at LLNL’s Inherently Safe Subcritical Assembly (ISSA) facility
demonstrated the new system’s performance. The LLNL Demonstrator matched or exceeded the
baseline FNCL system’s measurement precision, achieving <2% statistical uncertainty in neutron
doubles counting for up to nine ISSA fuel elements. Both AmLi and deuterium-deuterium (DD)
neutron generators were validated as interrogation sources, with DD generators enabling faster
measurements at comparable accuracy.

The FNCL – Fast Isotopic Fuel Assay (FIFA) analysis software was benchmarked using simulated and
experimental data, showing strong predictive capability for U-235 and gadolinium content from
passive measurements. The team also validated system stability, calibration robustness, and the
eaectiveness of GMM-PSD for neutron/gamma discrimination in high-background environments.

Technology transfer activities with CAEN have commenced, this includes documentation and code
for hardware designs, GMM-PSD ﬁrmware, and analysis software. This collaboration is expected to
continue into FY26.

In summary, the project achieved all major milestones: delivering a next-generation FNCL prototype
with improved deployability, demonstrating enhanced measurement performance in relevant
environments, validating advanced analysis algorithms, and initiating formal technology transfer to
support future commercialization and deployment.

Appendix A
Temperature-Dependent SiPM performance Studies
During operation of the prototype panel in FY24, a shift in each SiPM detector’s energy response was
observed in the ﬁrst ~45 minutes of panel operation due to temperature variation. It was found that
the preampliﬁers of the CAEN readout boards produced heat within each SiPM detector enclosure,
raising each sensor’s temperature by approximately 7°C before reaching equilibrium. Temperature
compensation was incorporated into the demonstrator panel design to ensure stable operation in a
range of temperatures and to eliminate the need for the system to reach thermal equilibrium before
operation.

To compensate for energy response changes with temperature variations, each LLNL Demonstrator
Panel has a thermistor incorporated into the panel which interfaces with the CAEN DT5485P SiPM
Power Supply for providing real-time temperature corrections. The power supply uses a temperature
dependent gain correction function to vary the bias voltage via a look-up table, ensuring a stable
energy response across a range of temperatures. The gain correction function was calibrated using
a LLNL Demonstrator panel, providing <5% energy response variation across the 20 - 40°C range, as
shown in Figure 50. The required demonstrator warm-up time is ﬁve minutes, consistent with the
baseline FNCL initial setup time. If the temperature remains relatively stable throughout a
measurement the energy response variation should be a subdominant eaect relative to statistical or
systematic uncertainties. Stable operation was veriﬁed during the ISSA deployment using Cs-137
energy calibration measurements at the beginning and end of each day. Each channel’s energy
calibration constant varied at < 2%, which is consistent with variations observed for the baseline
FNCL hardware in the same environment.

Comparison of DD generator operation experience

total time to conduct measurements is directly aaected by the stability and operational convenience
of a DD generator.

The Thermo Scientiﬁc P-383 DD generator was generally a smooth operational experience and
resulted in quick data collects from start-up to shut-down. The neutron output is controlled by setting
the anode current and voltage in the vendor provided software package. After setting these
parameters and pressing start, the DD generator automatically ramped the power provided to the DD
source and would reach stable operation in under 30 seconds. The source was consistently reliable

generator also has an operator crash button and an indicator light for when neutrons are being
generated, all beneﬁcial safety features.

signiﬁcance factor rather than a large change in the signiﬁcance metric space proﬁle. As such, the
approach described above should be valid for ﬁnding the optimal GMM vs. energy cut relative to the
rest in the GMM vs. Energy proﬁle.

Appendix H
Background Doubles Rate: No Fuel vs. 9-Fuel Elements
When developing a calibration curve, the standard procedure for AmLi-based interrogations with the
FNCL DAQ is to subtract the passive doubles rate from the AmLi-interrogated active doubles rate.
However, DD generators produce a higher-energy neutron spectrum and have demonstrated a
presence of correlated neutron backgrounds; this suggests that a background subtraction procedure
should also somehow include the contribution of DD source neutron backgrounds. Unfortunately,
the background doubles rate produced by the DD generator in the FNCL system will be inherently
diaerent whether a FFA is installed or not, as the FFA’s bulk material will attenuate and scatter the
DD neutrons.

The ideal solution to quantifying the DD neutron background would be to measure the neutron
doubles candidate rate when a “Mock FFA” is installed in the FNCL volume. The mock FFA would
have the same geometry and material composition, only have no uranium loading in the FFA which
would produce ﬁssions. Although this is the most accurate solution to characterizing the DD neutron
doubles background rate, such a mock FFA is unlikely to be available at a facility.

The most practical solution for potential future deployments where an FFA is interrogated with a DD
source would be to measure the DD neutron background rate at a facility with no FFA installed, then
apply a correction based on the expected attenuation/shielding from the FFA. This correction would
be straightforward to quantify with simulation, and the correction could be validated/benchmarked
using the ISSA facility mock fuel data collected in the ISSA deployment.

Since the ISSA facility had a set of “mock ISSA fuel elements”, measurements of zero fuel elements
and nine mock fuel elements were conducted with the Starﬁre DD generator at the ISSA facility. The
systematic diaerence in measured doubles candidate rates after all analysis cuts are shown below.
The diaerence in measured neutron doubles suggests that the measured background rate is 26 ± 2%
lower when characterized with mock fuel in the interrogation volume. Although the correction factor
for actual FFAs would likely be diaerent, this gives some sense of what level of oaset would be
expected in the doubles vs. U-235 linear density calibration curve produced without mock fuel.

The FNCL-FIFA analysis requires data input from a series of FFAs that span the expected range of U-

VII.1. It was also cross compared with MCNP6.3 PTRAC transport results to ensure consistent results
between diaerent particle transport codes. A visual rendering of the geometry built in Geant4 and
MCNP6 is shown alongside a photo of the physical detector in Figure 67. All detector eaects speciﬁc
to the FNCL are applied post transport code to allow for experimentally based calibration for each.

Figure 67. Photo of the FNCL detector at LLNL alongside visual rendering of the simulated FNCL geometry in

The detailed response of the baseline FNCL was developed as a post-processing step, completed in
FY24, and bundled into the Predictive Response Modeling (PRM) Toolkit. The PRM-toolkit takes Monte
Carlo based truth information, which can originate from any particle transport code, and applies
realistic nuclear instrumentation response, speciﬁc to the FNCL instrument, to model changes to
the experimental setup or detector design and predict the corresponding change to detector
response. Detector response details were obtained from [2][13][14] and experimental results. The
toolkit incorporates detector eaects and systematic qualities such as proton quenching factor,
scintillation light production, timing uncertainty, signal length, dead time, and energy dependent
detection eaiciency. The primary goal of the PRM-toolkit is to provide a way to quickly compare the
impact of design changes, shielding additions, radioactive source variations, or the addition of new
sources, such as active interrogation. It allows a user to adjust parameters via transport modeling
software and accurately predict the resulting changes in neutron detection eaiciency and resulting
doubles rates of the experimental FNCL detector.
