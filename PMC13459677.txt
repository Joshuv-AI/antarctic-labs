Perinatal depression, and the perspective of individuals with legal backgrounds in India: Exploring policy gaps and human rights implications
Authors: Ritika Behl, Vivek Nemane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerinatal Depression (PND) remains an underrecognized public health concern in India, despite its myriad consequences for women, children and families. Unmet perinatal mental health (PMH) needs may have a multifaceted adverse impact on human rights advancements. However, in India, awareness about PND and PMH amongst healthcare providers and law enforcement agencies remains limited, which is critical for law and policy reforms.ObjectivesThis study assesses awareness and perceptions of PND and PMH among individuals holding Indian law degrees (IHLD), with a focus on the currently implemented mental health and rights (MHR) and the reproductive health and rights (RHR) frameworks, and their human rights implications.Study Design and MethodsTwo online, cross-sectional surveys were conducted with IHLD using purposive and snowball sampling. Separate surveys were conducted to effectively cover the implementation of the MHR and RHR frameworks, to avoid confusion among participants, given the absence of integrated PMH services. Surveys included multiple-choice questions, with a small number of open-ended questions. Quantitative data were analysed descriptively, while cross-survey comparisons were made at the aggregate level to identify trends.ResultsMost participants (90.6%, n=106) in the first survey perceived a positive role of legal interventions in improving health statuses, but only 38.5% (n=45) self-rated themselves as aware of the latest MHR reforms. Almost half of the participants in both surveys, covering MHR (49.6%, n=58/117) and RHR (40.6%, n=41/101) frameworks, were unsure about the availability of PMH services. The majority of participants in both surveys agreed that the existing policy gap results in potential rights violations for women and children, making heightened awareness imperative.ConclusionIHLD’s awareness levels and perspectives can strongly influence their representation of vulnerable population groups in the courts and in the dispensation of justice. Law and policy reforms can increase awareness about PND and PMH among IHLD. However, professional development programs for IHLD and their involvement in multisectoral collaborative approaches are critical.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) advocates adoption of the rights-based, multisectoral approach to PMH, and common perinatal mental disorders (PMDs) such as depression and anxiety, emphasizing their interconnectedness with human rights.1,2 There is an intrinsic relationship between reproductive health and rights (RHR) and mental health and rights (MHR) and PMDs, such as PND.3,4

In India, the absence of perinatal mental health (PMH)-focused health laws and policies, along with the non-integration of PMH services within the RHR frameworks, and the non-inclusion of PMH within the latest mental health legislation, the Mental Health Care Act, 2017, results in limited awareness among healthcare providers, the perinatal population, and law enforcement agencies, besides contributing to service delivery gaps.5–7 It is also associated with amplified impact of social determinants, including gender bias, intimate partner violence, unwanted pregnancies, and gender-based roles, exacerbating the vulnerabilities of women during the perinatal period.6,8,9 Thereby, resulting in indirect discrimination and systemic violation of women’s and children’s rights.6,8

Legal professionals, including individuals holding law degrees (IHLD), play a critical role in safeguarding human rights, yet their knowledge of PMH and PND remains underexplored in India. Assessing IHLD’s level of awareness can inform policy reforms and future developments, highlight gaps in justice delivery, and support integration of PMH in RHR and MHR frameworks.

Objectives of the study

The present study primarily assesses IHLD’s awareness of PMH and PND, and their perspective about effectiveness of India’s RHR and MHR frameworks in meeting the women's PMH needs. The study aims to understand IHLD’s perceptions about i. the importance of legal interventions in improving population-level health status, ii. self-awareness among IHLD about the latest MHR reforms, and iii. policy gaps related to PND and PMH in India and their implications for women’s and children’s rights.

Study design

The present study’s research design is based on the WHO’s multisectoral collaborative approach to mental health and realization of human rights, 1 making the adoption of a participatory research approach 10 imperative. Participatory research is a systematic inquiry that involves collaborative research through partnerships between the researcher and people affected by or responsible for undertaking actions on the subject matter of the study. 10 However, given the limited understanding of PND amongst the law enforcement agencies in India, such as the judiciary and advocates,11,12 apart from non-inclusion of PMH under the existing RHR and MHR frameworks,5–7 the adoption of a participatory research approach in this study was limited to assessing awareness levels and seeking insights through the perceptions of IHLD.

The authors deemed this methodology most suitable for meeting the objectives of this study, also considering the interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary nature of PND, PMH, and women’s rights,4,8 and stigma attached to mental health, especially PMH in Indian society.6,8 Furthermore, this approach was adopted to inform future law and policy reforms and to prioritize courses of action, such as professional development programs for IHLD.

This study used two online, cross-sectional, self-reported surveys to explore participants’ knowledge of the inclusion of PMH within the Indian MHR and RHR frameworks, separately, and their perceptions regarding policy gaps and their implications for women’s and children’s rights. This study was conducted in adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee for Biomedical and Health Research Studies of Symbiosis International (Deemed) University, Pune (Proposal No. SIU/IEC/603), in February 2024. The surveys were conducted in February and March 2024.

Sampling and recruitment of study participants

Eligible participants included individuals with Indian law degrees, including Doctorate in Law (PhD), Master of Laws (LL.M.), Bachelor of Arts and Bachelor of Laws (B.A. LL.B.), Bachelor of Business Administration and Bachelor of Laws (B.B.A. LL.B.), and Bachelor of Commerce and Bachelor of Laws (B.Com LL.B.), irrespective of their current occupation. Purposive and snowball sampling were employed to recruit study participants, using social platforms such as WhatsApp, LinkedIn, Facebook (Meta), and Google Mail (Gmail). No regional or graduation-year restrictions were applied to maximize participation. Participants were invited voluntarily, and no incentives were provided. A participant information sheet (PIS) explained the study objectives, data privacy and informed consent.

Given limited knowledge of PND amongst law enforcement agencies,11,12 existing policy gaps regarding PND and PMH,6,7 and cultural and political factors that significantly shape perspectives of motherhood,5,8 the authors employed a non-random, online recruitment approach. Hence, the sample cannot be considered statistically representative of all IHLD in India.

The first author contacted prospective participants. Both surveys were conducted using Google Forms, and the survey links were circulated using the above-mentioned online platforms. Only participants who completed the first survey were requested to participate in the second survey to ensure that responses to both surveys were received from the same participants and to ensure synchronised analysis of the collected data from both surveys.

The first survey included 6 MCQs and 4 open-ended questions, spread over 5 pages, including the PIS, and the second survey comprised 4 MCQs and 3 open-ended questions, spread over 3 pages. Adaptive questioning was utilised for the first survey. Participants could submit the Google Form only after answering all survey questions.

Since most questions for both surveys were MCQs, no minimum or maximum time frames for completing the surveys were established. Answers could not be modified once the two survey forms were submitted, and the survey settings were designed to prevent participants from filling out the surveys twice. Google Sheets was used to collect and store the data. The first author manually and consistently reviewed the collected data to avoid duplicate entries for both surveys.

Data analysis

Closed-ended survey responses were analysed using descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, to summarise participants’ awareness and perceptions regarding PMH-related legal frameworks. Cross-survey comparisons were conducted at the aggregate level to explore trends in participants’ responses across MHR and RHR framework surveys, particularly regarding awareness of PMH inclusion and perceived human rights implications. Simple subgroup analyses were performed using chi-square tests to examine potential differences in categorical response patterns across selected survey variables. These analyses were primarily descriptive and exploratory in nature, intended to identify broad trends rather than to establish causal associations.

The analyses were based on raw response counts obtained from both surveys. Graphical presentations and summary tables were generated using Google Sheets. Chi-square comparisons were conducted using the underlying categorical response frequencies derived from the survey datasets. Given the exploratory design, non-random sampling approach, and relatively small subgroup sizes, the findings were interpreted cautiously and are not intended to provide nationally generalizable inferences.

Results

A total of 119 responses were received for the first survey. After the first author deleted two duplicate entries, the analytical sample comprised 117 responses. A total of 101 were received for the second survey, cumulatively resulting in an 86% completion rate. Among participants in the first survey, 16 failed to participate in the second survey despite receiving reminders.

Self-awareness about the existing Indian MHR framework

Participants were asked to self-assess their knowledge and awareness about the Indian MHR framework, including the National Health Policy (2017), the National Mental Health Policy (2014), the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, and the MHCA, 2017. Less than half of the participants (38.5%, n=45) were aware of the existing Indian MHR framework, and an equal proportion (38.5%, n=45) reported having a limited understanding of it. However, 23.1% of the participants (n=27) reported being unaware of the Indian MHR framework.

PMH within the Indian MHR framework

Most participants (49.6%, n=58) were unsure whether PMH care is covered under the existing Indian MHR framework, while many (32.5%, n=38) affirmed its obscurity within the framework. However, some participants (17.9%, n=21) who believed that PMH care is provided within the existing MHR framework failed to identify such (relevant) legal provisions.

Next, the participants were asked their opinions regarding the future inclusion of PMH within the existing MHR framework. Almost all participants (90.6%, n=106) opined that it should be covered, while 9.4% (n=11) were unsure about the requirement of such inclusion.

The last question of the first online survey explored participants’ opinions on the existing policy gap regarding PMH within the MHR framework and whether it violates women’s rights and/or children’s rights in India. The majority of participants (83.6%, n=98) perceived that the existing policy gap regarding PMH care contributes to the violation of women’s rights and/or children’s rights. In comparison, 13.7% (n=16) of participants were unsure about these consequences for advancing women’s and children’s rights. Also, 2.6% (n=3) of the participants did not opine that women’s and/or children’s rights are violated due to the non-inclusion of PMH within the MHR framework.

Awareness of PND and PMH amongst individuals holding Indian law degrees

More than half of the participants (58.4%, n=59) reported being aware of PND and PMH before participating in this study, and 28.7% (n=29) stated that they were aware of PND but were unfamiliar with the medical terminology. However, a small number of the participants (12.9%, n=13) reported being unaware of PMH and PND.

Lastly, participants were asked about the need to raise awareness amongst the Indian legal fraternity members about PND and PMH, more so in the context of infanticide and suicide cases, which can result from severe PND. The vast majority of the participants (94.1% (n=95)) opined that it is imperative to raise such awareness. However, a small number of participants (3% (n=3)) specified that they were unsure if such enhanced awareness would be helpful, while the other 3% (n=3) signified that it was not required.

Discussion

This study, in an innovative manner, collected data on knowledge and awareness of PND and PMH among IHLD in India through two cross-sectional, online, self-reported surveys. The study informs about the criticality of raising awareness amongst the Indian legal fraternity members regarding PMDs, including PND and PMH. These findings support and expand upon the previous studies,6,11,12 which had raised similar concerns about the lack of adequate awareness of PND and its consequences, besides limited understanding of its interdisciplinary nature beyond psychiatric nosology, in India.

Given this background, the findings from this study are significant and suggest that the wider dissemination and discourse on the interdisciplinary nature of PND and PMH as a public health concern, and a precursor to human rights violations, among the IHLD in India are imminent. The WHO has also recommended that a multisectoral collaborative approach is necessary to reduce the treatment gap for PMDs and to bridge the gap regarding unmet PMH needs of women, which would facilitate advancement of women’s rights.2,3

The Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination Against Women and the WHO have emphasised the States’ obligation to meet the PMH needs of women since they are uniquely and disproportionately affected by PND.3,21,22 Infanticide. and extended suicide are the grave consequences associated with severe PMDs.11,23 Moreover, the suicide mortality rate among Indian women and girls is twice the prevalent global rate, and suicide accounts for the majority of deaths in the reproductive age group of 15-39 years for Indian women. 24 The high prevalence rate of PMDs amongst Indian women,5,25 coupled with high rates of suicide ideation during pregnancy,26,27 and completed suicides amongst Indian women of reproductive age, 28 make awareness levels of IHLD critical for policy reforms.

Also, since PMDs, including PND, compromise the physical and mental health of children,29,30 besides being intergenerational,31,32 the IHLD, if equipped, can help States identify unmet PMH needs as a risk factor for preventable maternal and child morbidity and mortality. The Committee on the Rights of the Child had underscored this responsibility of signatory States in the context of the right to health of women and children. 33

A persistent awareness gap among IHLD may affect their engagement with PMDs and PMH-related issues in legal contexts. Hence, in this situation, it is imperative to raise awareness among IHLDs about PMDs and their public health and human rights implications, since they need to function as human rights defenders.

Implications

Based on the survey findings, several implications emerge regarding awareness and perceptions of PMH, and PND amongst IHLD:a. Awareness gap amongst IHLD: Most participants had limited knowledge of PMH, and the enormity associated with severe PND, apart from higher-level unawareness about the MHR framework. This gap can potentially influence legal decision-making in cases involving perinatal women, highlighting the need for targeted education and professional development of IHLD, focusing on such medico-legal issues. An interdisciplinary and multicollaborative approach should inform actionable guidance for IHLD to facilitate the advancement of women’s and children’s rights in cases involving perinatal women, especially owing to the absence of PMH services.b. Policy gaps and rights violations: The majority of the participants opined that the absence of PMH care within existing frameworks has pertinent human-rights-based implications. This perspective should facilitate law and policy reforms providing for PMH care within the existing MHR and RHR frameworks.c. Future research: Larger, systematic surveys are needed to assess knowledge across the wider legal fraternity about medico-legal issues such as PND and PMH, considering their implications for Public Health Law and Human Rights Law.

Limitations of the study

This study provides preliminary insights into awareness of PMH and PND among IHLD. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The recruitment of the study participants relied on voluntary online participation, which may have introduced bias. Also, both surveys were conducted online (given practical barriers in reaching the large population of law graduates across India), which limits the representativeness of the study samples. Consequently, the findings cannot be generalized to all IHLD in India. The study findings provide preliminary exploratory insights rather than nationally representative conclusions. The study design was primarily cross-sectional and descriptive, without involving inferential statistical analysis or multivariate exploration of predictors of PMH awareness among IHLD. Furthermore, the study is designed to capture awareness levels and perceptions of IHLD, but did not include participants at different stages of the study for reasons explained previously. Future studies may focus on the practical inclusion of IHLD through or after awareness and professional development programs.

Conclusion

IHLD can strongly influence the impact of legal interventions because they represent vulnerable population subgroups, including women, children, and families, in the court of law, especially when recent laws and policy reforms continue to fail to address PND and PMH. Therefore, to meet the PMH needs and advance human rights, amending laws alone will not be sufficient; we need to strengthen human rights-based legal responses for women and children, which IHLD are optimally placed to facilitate. Furthermore, it is critical to involve IHLD in all multisectoral collaborative approaches that influence women’s and children’s health while facilitating their effective participation through training and professional development programs.
