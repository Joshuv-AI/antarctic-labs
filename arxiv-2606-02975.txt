Abstract




Diagonal Condition in Multiplication Table of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\alpha)

Abstract

Multiplication table of a ring with identity
\mathbf{1}
is said to have the diagonal condition if
\mathbf{1}
’s occur only on the main diagonal. In this paper, we study the diagonal condition in the ring of Gaussian integers
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Furthermore, we also find the Gaussian integers
\alpha
so that the rings of Gaussian integers modulo
\alpha
satisfy the diagonal condition.

Keywords:
diagonal condition, multiplication table, Gaussian integers, quotient ring

2020 MSC:
11R04, 11T30, 11N13.

Theorem 1.1
Let

\mathbb{Z}_{n}

1.

\mathbf{1}
’s occur only on the main diagonal (not off diagonal) of multiplication table of

If

n\mid a^{2}-1

4.

If
p

2.1
Basic knowledge of
\mathbb{Z}[i]

Gaussian integers are complex numbers is in the form

Definition
For

Norm

Remark 2.1.1
It is obvious that
\,\mathcal{N}(\alpha)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}=\mathbb{Z}^{+}\cup\{0\}

Theorem 2.1.2
For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

\mathcal{N}(\alpha\beta)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha)\,\mathcal{N}(\beta)

Definition

\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

Theorem 2.1.3
The only Gaussian integers that are units in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
are
1,-1,i,-i

Definition
For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

\beta\not=0

\alpha=\beta\gamma

\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

Theorem 2.1.4
For all
\alpha,\beta
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, if
\beta\mid\alpha
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
then
\mathcal{N}(\beta)\mid\mathcal{N}(\alpha)
in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 2.1.5

(

Given
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

\mathcal{N}(\rho)<\mathcal{N}(\beta)

\mathcal{N}(\rho)<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\,\mathcal{N}(\beta)

Proof.

We provide proof without words in Geometry: Division Algorithm in

2.2
Coset representatives of quotient ring
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)

This subsection gives concepts of congruence–modulo and coset representatives in
\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)
.

Definition
For Gaussian integers
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma
, we say that
\beta

congruence

\gamma

modulo

\alpha
denoted by
\beta\equiv\gamma\pmod{\alpha}
, when
\alpha\mid(\beta-\gamma)
.

Remark 2.2.1
It can be proved that Congruence–Modulo is an equivalence relation on
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]
. The equivalence classes are cosets in quotient ring
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)
.

Next, we provide methods to find coset representatives of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)
when
\alpha=a+bi
through multiples of Gaussian in grid as followed.

1.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider
(a+bi)(m+ni)=(a+bi)m+(-b+ai)n
.

2.

Plot points
(a,b)
and
(-b,a)
in the grid, then form two vectors from origin to these points.

3.

Extend the sided of two vectors to create a (main) square. Coset representatives of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)
are points located inside the square and on two main vectors including origin.

4.

Translate the square till it can be filled the grid. Then, we have (all) cosets.

Examples 2.2.2
Find coset representatives of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(2+2i)
.

First, consider
(2+2i)(m+ni)=(2+2i)m+(-2+2i)n
. Then follow steps 2. and 3. :

From the figure, the coset representatives of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(2+2i)
are
0,i,2i,3i,1+i,1+2i,\\
-1+i,-1+2i
equivalent with points
(0,0),(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(1,1),(1,2),(-1,1),(-1,2)
in grid.

Lemma 2.2.3
For
a,b,c,d,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

1.

a\equiv b\pmod{c}
in
\mathbb{Z}
if and only if
a\equiv b\pmod{c}
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

2.

a+bi\equiv c+di\pmod{m}
if and only if
a\equiv c\pmod{m}
and
b\equiv d\pmod{m}
.

Let
\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)
be the number of coset representatives of
\,\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)
i.e.
\mathfrak{N}(\alpha):=\lvert\mathbb{Z}[i]/\penalty(\alpha)\rvert
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Lemma 2.2.4
If
m\in\mathbb{Z}
is nonzero, then
\,\mathfrak{N}(m)=m^{2}
.

Lemma 2.2.5
If
\alpha\not=0
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, then
\,\mathfrak{N}(\overline{\alpha})=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)
.

Lemma 2.2.6
If
\alpha
and
\beta
are nonzero in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, then
\,\mathfrak{N}(\alpha\beta)=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{N}(\beta)
. In another word, function
\mathfrak{N}
is multiplicative.

Theorem 2.2.7
If
\alpha\not=0
in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, then
\,\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha)
.

Proof.

By Lemma 2.2.6,
\,\mathfrak{N}(\alpha\overline{\alpha})=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)\,\mathfrak{N}(\overline{\alpha})
. From Lemma 2.2.5, we have the right hand side of previous equation is
\,\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)^{2}
. Since
\alpha\overline{\alpha}=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)
is integer and Lemma 2.2.4, it can be concluded that
\,\mathfrak{N}(\alpha\overline{\alpha})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha)^{2}
. Thus,
\,\mathcal{N}(\alpha)^{2}=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)^{2}
. Hence, we have
\,\mathcal{N}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{N}(\alpha)
.
∎

2.3
Gaussian primes

In this subsection, we find all Gaussian primes from Gaussian integers which divide primes in
\mathbb{Z}
.

Definition
In
\mathbb{Z}[i]
, we say that
\alpha\sim\beta
if
\alpha=u\beta
or
\alpha=u\beta
where
u
is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Lemma 2.3.1
Let
\pi
be a Gaussian prime. Then, there exists a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\,\pi\mid p
.

We can find all Gaussian primes from Gaussian integers which divide primes in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Theorem 2.3.2
A prime in
\mathbb{Z}
is a factorized element (reducible element) in
\mathbb{Z}[i]
if and only if
p
can be written as a sum of two squares.

Remark 2.3.3
Any primes
p\in\mathbb{Z}
which cannot be written as a sum of two squares, then
p
is not a factorized element (reducible element). Thus,
p
is a Gaussian primes.

Corollary 2.3.4
If
p
is a prime in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, then
p
is a Gaussian prime.

Lemma 2.3.5
Given
p\in\mathbb{Z}
is a prime. Either
p=2
or
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
if and only if there exist integers
a,b
such that
p=a^{2}+b^{2}
.

Theorem 2.3.6
Let
p
be a prime in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
. Then
p=\pi\bar{\pi}
where
\pi=a+bi
and
\bar{\pi}=a-bi
whilst
\pi\not\sim\bar{\pi}
.

Proof.

By Lemma 2.3.4, there exist integers
a,b
such that
p=a^{2}+b^{2}
. So,
p=\pi\bar{\pi}
where
\pi=a+bi
and
\bar{\pi}=a-bi
. Assume that
a+bi\sim a-bi
. Then, there are 4 cases as followed

•

case

a+bi=a-bi
: we have
b=0
. Then
p=a^{2}
, a contradiction.

•

case

a+bi=-(a-bi)
: we have
a=0
. Then
p=b^{2}
, a contradiction.

•

case

a+bi=i(a-bi)
: we get
a=b
. Then
p=2a^{2}=2b^{2}
, a contradiction.

•

case

a+bi=-i(a-bi)
: we get
a=-b
. Then
p=2b^{2}=2a^{2}
, a contradiction.

Thus,
\pi=a+bi\not\sim a-bi=\bar{\pi}
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

\mathbb{Z}[i]/((1+i)^{n})
do not satisfy diagonal condition when
n\geq 3
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((1+i)^{n})
satisfy diagonal condition for some
n\geq 3
. Then, for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
s.t.
\gcd(\alpha,1+i)=\delta
where
\delta
is unit, we have
\alpha^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(1+i)^{n}}
. In
(\mathbb{Z}[i]/(1+i)^{3})^{\times}
, we get
(-2+i)^{2}=4-4i-1\equiv-1\pmod{(1+i)^{3}}
that makes
(-2+i)^{4}\equiv 1\pmod{(1+i)^{3}}
. Since
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((1+i)^{n})
satisfy diagonal condition for some
n\geq 3
,
(-2+i)^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(1+i)^{n}}
and hence
(-2+i)^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(1+i)^{3}}
. Thus,
-1\equiv 1\pmod{(1+i)^{3}}
so that
(1+i)^{3}\mid-2
. This leads a contradiction, the statement we supposed is wrong. Hence,
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((1+i)^{n})
do not satisfy diagonal condition for all
n\geq 3
.
∎

Remark 3.3
If the order of Gaussian integers modulo
\alpha
is greater than or equal to
3
, then multiplication table of
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\alpha)
do not have diagonal condition.

Lemma 3.4

\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p)
do not satisfy diagonal condition when
p
are primes in
\mathbb{Z}
s.t.
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

Since
p
are primes in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
,
p
are Gaussian primes. Then
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p)
is a finite field which has order
p^{2}
because
(p)
is the maximal ideal. Thus,
(\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p))^{\times}
is a cyclic group with its order is equal to
p^{2}-1\geq 3
. By Remark 3.3, the statement of lemma is proved.
∎

Theorem 3.5

\mathbb{Z}[i]/((p)^{n})
do not satisfy diagonal condition when
n\geq 2
and
p
are primes in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 3\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((p)^{n})
satisfy diagonal condition for some
n\geq 2
. Then, we have
\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[i]
s.t.
\gcd(\alpha,p)=\delta
where
\delta
is unit,
\alpha^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{n}}
. However, there exists
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}[i]/(p)
such that the order of
\beta
modulo
p
is
p^{2}-1\geq 3
. That makes
\beta^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{p}
, contradicts with
\beta^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{p}
by assumption. Hence, the statement of theorem is confirmed.
∎

Lemma 3.6

\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi)
and
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\bar{\pi})
do not satisfy diagonal condition when
\pi\bar{\pi}=p
and
p
are primes in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}
.

Proof.

By Theorem 2.3.7,
\pi\not\sim\bar{\pi}
without loss of generality. Since
\pi
is a Gaussian prime,
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi)
is a field because
(\pi)
is a maximal ideal. Thus,
(\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi))^{\times}
is a cyclic group with order

. Then, there exists

such that the order of
\alpha
modulo
\pi
is equal to

so that
\alpha^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{\pi}
. Hence,
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(\pi)
does not satisfy diagonal condition.
∎

Theorem 3.7

\mathbb{Z}[i]/((\pi)^{n})
and
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((\bar{\pi})^{n})
do not satisfy diagonal condition when
n\geq 2
and
\pi\bar{\pi}=p
where
p
are primes in
\mathbb{Z}
such that
p\equiv 1\pmod{4}

Proof.

By Theorem 2.3.7,
\pi\not\sim\bar{\pi}
without loss of generality. Suppose
\mathbb{Z}[i]/((\pi)^{n})
satisfy diagonal condition. Then,
\alpha^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(\pi)^{n}}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}[i]

, there exists

such that the order of

so that
\beta^{2}\not\equiv 1\pmod{\pi}
. By the assumption, we need to have
\beta^{2}\equiv 1\pmod{(\pi)^{n}}
