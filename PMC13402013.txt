Pharmacological Re‐Optimization With Venlafaxine in Two Elderly Patients With Severe Major Depressive Disorder Considered for Electroconvulsive Therapy: A Two‐Patient Case Report
Authors: Wataru Omori, Kenichi Oga, Yoshikazu Masuda, Go Okada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTElectroconvulsive therapy (ECT) effectively treats severe late‐life depression but is often approached with caution in very elderly patients with cardiac or cerebrovascular disease. Venlafaxine, a serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor with dose‐dependent transporter engagement, may be useful for anxious depression with psychomotor disturbance, yet data are limited. We describe two elderly inpatients with severe major depressive disorder for whom ECT was planned. Case 1 was a man in his late 70s with complete atrioventricular block and a permanent pacemaker who relapsed despite long‐term duloxetine‐based outpatient therapy. Case 2 was a woman in her late 80s with prior clipping of an unruptured middle cerebral artery aneurysm whose symptoms worsened despite outpatient mirtazapine and risperidone with the addition of duloxetine. In both cases, duloxetine was cross‐tapered to venlafaxine extended‐release while mirtazapine was continued, and venlafaxine ER was titrated to 150 mg/day as a closely monitored, time‐limited pharmacological re‐optimization step during ECT preparation. Symptoms improved; the 21‐item GRID‐Hamilton Depression Rating Scale total scores fell into the remission range. Appetite and oral intake improved, and planned ECT was canceled. These cases highlight the potential value of individualized pharmacological re‐evaluation, including SNRI switching, even when ECT is being considered.Keywords: case report, electroconvulsive therapy, late‐life depression, major depressive disorder, venlafaxine

Introduction and Background

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) is effective for severe or treatment‐resistant major depressive disorder in older adults and can be delivered safely with multidisciplinary planning [1]. However, in very elderly patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices or cerebrovascular disease, concerns persist despite reports supporting careful hemodynamic monitoring and multidisciplinary collaboration [2, 3].

Venlafaxine is a serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) with dose‐dependent transporter engagement: serotonergic effects predominate at lower doses, whereas norepinephrine transporter engagement becomes more prominent at higher doses [4]. PET data demonstrate measurable norepinephrine transporter occupancy with venlafaxine ER that increases with dose and may approach a plateau around 150 mg/day in some analyses [5]. Meta‐analyses and international guidelines support venlafaxine as an option for severe or treatment‐resistant major depressive disorder [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Guidelines also discuss combination strategies, including venlafaxine plus mirtazapine, for selected patients [9, 10]. Venlafaxine extended‐release at 75–225 mg/day has demonstrated efficacy and tolerability in Japanese patients with major depressive disorder [8].

Both patients described in this report were treated at the Department of Psychiatry, Hiroshima University Hospital, Hiroshima, Japan. We report these cases because ECT was being prepared; however, remission was achieved after individualized pharmacological re‐optimization, including a switch from duloxetine to venlafaxine ER in combination with mirtazapine. The report follows the CARE case report guidelines [11].

Case Presentation

Case 1

A man in his late 70s was involuntarily admitted to the psychiatric ward of Hiroshima University Hospital because of severe depressive symptoms and functional decline. He had experienced a first depressive episode about 27 years earlier after the accidental death of a junior colleague and had since had recurrent depressive episodes treated pharmacologically at a psychiatric clinic. In the years preceding the current admission, his mood had been stable at this clinic on duloxetine 30 mg/day, mirtazapine 30 mg/day, and olanzapine 10 mg/day. There was no family history of psychiatric disorders. His medical history included hypertension and complete atrioventricular block treated with permanent pacemaker implantation.

Approximately 1 month before admission, he developed dyspnea without an obvious trigger and repeatedly complained that “breathing is difficult.” He was evaluated at a general hospital, where an internal medicine assessment showed no acute cardiopulmonary abnormality. At the same psychiatric clinic, duloxetine was increased to 60 mg/day in response to presumed relapse of depression; however, anxiety intensified, appetite decreased, and he lost about 3 kg in 1 month. Because his symptoms and poor intake persisted, he was admitted to the internal medicine department of the general hospital, where no new somatic disease was found, and was then transferred to the psychiatric ward of Hiroshima University Hospital for management of severe depression.

On admission, he was mostly bedridden and required assistance to get out of bed. He showed marked psychomotor retardation, long response latencies, and difficulty sustaining attention in conversation. Anxiety was intense; minor stimuli provoked rapid, shallow breathing and hyperventilation. He expressed strong guilt about burdening his family but denied active suicidal intent. Depression severity was assessed with the 21‐item GRID‐Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (GRID‐HAM‐D); the total score was 29, indicating very severe depression. Routine blood tests were largely unremarkable other than mild hyponatremia, and brain magnetic resonance imaging showed mild frontal lobe atrophy and chronic white‐matter ischemic changes appropriate for age.

We initially continued duloxetine 60 mg/day and olanzapine 10 mg/day and increased mirtazapine from 30 to 45 mg/day, alongside supportive care, physiotherapy, and nutritional support. Lorazepam (up to 1 mg/day) was used for severe anxiety and hyperventilation; however, its effect was limited, and his anxiety, hyperventilation, psychomotor retardation, and poor oral intake persisted, with marked functional impairment. Given the severity of symptoms and the limited response to pharmacotherapy, we consulted cardiology and anesthesiology and began preparing for ECT, drawing on reports that ECT can be performed safely in patients with pacemakers when devices are appropriately managed [2].

During this preparation period, we selected venlafaxine ER as a final, time‐limited pharmacological re‐optimization step before proceeding to ECT. The rationale for choosing venlafaxine ER, rather than simply continuing duloxetine or switching to another antidepressant class, was based on the patient's persistent severe anxiety, hyperventilation, psychomotor retardation, and poor oral intake despite duloxetine‐based treatment, together with the need for once‐daily administration, gradual titration, and close cardiovascular monitoring in the setting of a permanent pacemaker. On hospital day 15, we began cross‐tapering from duloxetine to venlafaxine ER. Duloxetine was tapered from 60 to 40 mg/day and then 20 mg/day before discontinuation, while venlafaxine ER was initiated at 75 mg/day and titrated to 150 mg/day by hospital day 22, with careful monitoring of blood pressure, heart rate, and pacemaker function. Mirtazapine 45 mg/day and olanzapine 10 mg/day were continued. Before the medication change, the rationale for switching from duloxetine to venlafaxine ER, the expected benefits, potential adverse effects, including possible blood pressure and heart‐rate changes, and alternatives, including proceeding to ECT, were explained to the patient; consent for the medication change was obtained.

After venlafaxine ER was titrated to 150 mg/day, his anxiety, hyperventilation, and depressive mood improved steadily, and lorazepam was gradually tapered and discontinued. The planned ECT course was canceled on hospital day 24, and he became able to walk on the ward and participate in occupational therapy. Olanzapine was tapered (10 → 5 → 2.5 mg/day) because of daytime sleepiness. The GRID‐HAM‐D total score decreased to 1 by hospital day 36, indicating remission, and he was discharged on hospital day 42, receiving venlafaxine ER 150 mg/day, mirtazapine 45 mg/day, and olanzapine 2.5 mg/day. After remission, his Mini‐Mental State Examination score was 30/30, suggesting no clinically evident dementia. No clinically significant changes in blood pressure, heart rate, or pacemaker function were observed during venlafaxine ER treatment. Case 2

A woman in her late 80s was admitted to the psychiatric ward of Hiroshima University Hospital with severe depressive symptoms, anxiety, and weight loss. At age 64, she had undergone clipping of an unruptured right middle cerebral artery aneurysm. One year before the index admission, she developed a depressive episode after surgical resection of squamous cell carcinoma of the lower lip and was admitted to the Department of Psychiatry at Hiroshima University Hospital, where her symptoms remitted with inpatient treatment. She also had hypertension. There was no family history of psychiatric disorders.

In the months before the index admission, she was followed as an outpatient on mirtazapine 45 mg/day and risperidone 1.5 mg/day. About 4 weeks before admission, she again developed depressed mood, anxiety, anergia, and loss of appetite. Despite intensified outpatient care, including initiation of duloxetine 20 mg/day, her oral intake decreased and she lost around 3 kg in 1 month. She was involuntarily admitted to our psychiatric ward.

On admission, she appeared tense and reported that she was “too anxious to settle down.” She showed psychomotor agitation at times but also marked loss of energy and motivation, needing help with basic activities. She denied active suicidal intent but expressed strong guilt about troubling her husband. The GRID‐HAM‐D total score was 30, again indicating very severe depression. Laboratory tests were within normal limits. Brain computed tomography revealed age‐appropriate atrophy, chronic deep white‐matter ischemic changes, and postoperative clips in the right middle cerebral artery region.

We increased duloxetine (20 → 40 → 60 mg/day) and switched risperidone to olanzapine (2.5 mg/day, titrated to 5 mg/day), aiming to improve anxiety, sleep, and appetite. Despite these changes, her anxiety and depressive symptoms persisted, oral intake remained poor, and she lost an additional 3 kg during the first 4 weeks after hospital admission, in addition to her pre‐admission weight loss. High‐calorie parenteral nutrition via a peripherally inserted central catheter was initiated on hospital Day 25 to prevent further nutritional decline. Because of severe depression and malnutrition, we consulted neurosurgery and anesthesiology and began preparing for ECT, referring to literature suggesting that ECT can be delivered safely in patients with treated cerebral aneurysms under careful blood‐pressure control [3].

On hospital day 28, we initiated a cross‐taper from duloxetine to venlafaxine ER as a final, time‐limited pharmacological re‐optimization step before proceeding to ECT. Venlafaxine ER was chosen because anxiety, depressive symptoms, and nutritional decline persisted despite duloxetine escalation and antipsychotic switching, and because gradual titration of the extended‐release formulation allowed close monitoring in the context of advanced age, hypertension, and prior aneurysm clipping. Duloxetine was tapered from 60 to 40 mg/day and then 20 mg/day before discontinuation, and venlafaxine ER was started at 75 mg/day and titrated to 150 mg/day by hospital day 37, while mirtazapine 45 mg/day and olanzapine 5 mg/day were maintained. Blood pressure and heart rate were closely monitored because of her age and vascular history. Before the medication change, the rationale for switching from duloxetine to venlafaxine ER, the expected benefits, potential adverse effects, including possible blood pressure and heart‐rate changes, and alternatives, including proceeding to ECT, were explained to the patient; consent for the medication change was obtained.

After venlafaxine ER was titrated to 150 mg/day, her mood and anxiety improved, psychomotor agitation lessened, and she began to request food spontaneously. The planned ECT course was canceled on hospital day 43. Oral intake increased, allowing parenteral nutrition to be discontinued (hospital day 46) and the catheter to be removed. Olanzapine was later reduced to 2.5 mg/day because of concern about falls. The GRID‐HAM‐D total score decreased to 1 by hospital day 64, and she was discharged on hospital day 87, receiving venlafaxine ER 150 mg/day, mirtazapine 45 mg/day, and olanzapine 2.5 mg/day. At discharge, her Mini‐Mental State Examination score was 28/30 (−1 for serial sevens, −1 for recall). No neurological complications or sustained blood‐pressure elevations were observed during venlafaxine ER treatment. Patient Perspective

Both patients reviewed the case descriptions and agreed that the manuscript accurately reflected their clinical course. After clinical improvement, they expressed relief that their depressive symptoms and oral intake had improved during the period in which ECT was being prepared and that ECT was ultimately not required. They also indicated that the explanation of the medication change, including its rationale, possible adverse effects, and the alternative of proceeding to ECT, helped them understand and accept the treatment plan.

Discussion

These cases illustrate that individualized pharmacological re‐optimization may remain valuable even when ECT is being prepared. Both patients had prominent anxiety, insufficient response to duloxetine‐based regimens, and comorbidities warranting caution (pacemaker in Case 1; prior aneurysm clipping in Case 2). The selection of venlafaxine ER in these cases was not intended to imply that venlafaxine is generally superior to duloxetine or to other antidepressant strategies. Rather, the clinical decision was made after reassessing each patient's symptom profile, previous response to duloxetine‐based treatment, comorbidities, and the short interval available while ECT was being arranged. Venlafaxine ER was considered a reasonable option because it permits once‐daily administration and gradual titration, and the 150 mg/day dose was expected to provide a clinically relevant serotonergic–noradrenergic treatment step while allowing close monitoring for blood pressure, heart rate, neurological, and device‐related complications [4, 5, 8]. Other strategies, including proceeding directly to ECT, using another antidepressant, or pursuing augmentation, remained clinically available and would have been pursued if symptoms had worsened or if no meaningful improvement had emerged within the monitored re‐optimization period (Box 1).

At 150 mg/day, venlafaxine ER may provide greater norepinephrine transporter engagement than lower doses, although dose–occupancy relationships vary across methodologies [4, 5]. Meta‐analyses and guidelines support venlafaxine as an option in severe or treatment‐resistant major depressive disorder [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Our observations are consistent with these data; however, they do not imply superiority of venlafaxine over duloxetine.

Depressive phenotypes are heterogeneous, and some patients may respond better to duloxetine than to venlafaxine, and vice versa. Although both are SNRIs, their pharmacological profiles differ; thus, switching between SNRIs may be meaningful in selected patients rather than redundant.

Both patients were already receiving mirtazapine and antipsychotics at the time of switching. The venlafaxine–mirtazapine combination may support sleep and appetite while venlafaxine targets core depressive and anxiety symptoms [9, 10]. However, improvement cannot be attributed solely to venlafaxine ER, and spontaneous improvement over time cannot be excluded.

Venlafaxine ER can increase blood pressure and heart rate and therefore requires cautious use in elderly patients with cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease [8]. In these two cases, doses were increased under close monitoring, and no serious adverse events were observed. Nonetheless, this experience does not replace individualized risk–benefit assessment, nor does it argue against ECT when rapid response is essential or when pharmacological strategies fail.

This report is limited by the small number of patients, the lack of a control condition, and the use of combination pharmacotherapy. Despite these limitations, the cases highlight the importance of individualized pharmacological re‐evaluation and careful treatment planning, even when ECT is being considered.
