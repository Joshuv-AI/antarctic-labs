The Effect of Mobilization With Movement Techniques on Pain and Range of Elbow Extension in Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis, a Case Report
Authors: Fateme Bokaee, Tayebeh Roghani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTJuvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is one of the most prevalent chronic childhood inflammatory diseases. Patients with JIA often present with morning stiffness, swelling, pain, muscle weakness, and muscle atrophy around the affected joints and activity limitations. Fear of exercises results in functional limitations. High‐dose corticosteroids provide good effects in the short term but have no long‐term effect. Physical therapy focused on pain reduction and improvement of musculoskeletal function is an essential part of the management of JIA. Mobilization with movement (MWM) is considered a system of manual therapy techniques to return pain‐free range of motion. In the present case report, authors reported the effect of MWM techniques on pain and range of elbow extension in one case of Juvenile idiopathic arthritis. Result demonstrated that these techniques can be beneficial to improve pain and range of elbow extension in JIA.Keywords: arthritis, elbow, mobilization, movement, pain, physical therapy, rheumatic

Introduction

Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is considered the most common pediatric rheumatic disease. JIA is diagnosed if the arthritis of unknown etiology starts before the age of 16 and lasts for at least six weeks [1]. Symptoms of JIA include pain, joint stiffness, joint swelling, muscle weakness, muscle atrophy, and movement restrictions. The standard care for JIA involves pharmacological intervention (nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs and disease‐modifying agents) to prevent disease progression [2]. The aims of effective management of rheumatoid diseases are pain reduction, disease control, joint preservation, and improvement of function [3]. Pain and mobility limitation, as a consequence of disease, have been regarded as key contributors to reduced quality of life in patients with JIA [2].

The aim of joint mobilization techniques is to stretch joint capsules, tendons, ligaments, and improve normal joint movement. Mobilization with movement (MWM) technique is a type of manual therapy techniques involving the application of a sustained accessory glide while performing a painful movement. MWM techniques can help to achieve complete, pain‐free range of motion. It is proposed that MWM techniques correct joint position errors [3]. Previous studies evaluated the efficacy of MWM techniques on tennis elbow, but to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the effects of these techniques on patients with JIA [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. The objective of the current case report was to investigate the effects of MWM techniques on pain and range of elbow extension in JIA.

Case History/Examination

N.F was a 22 years old girl referred to physical therapy for left elbow. She reported pain and restriction of elbow extension as her chief complaint. In history taking she reported that she had been diagnosed with JIA (polyarticular subtype) when she was 3 years old. Disease duration was 19 years. Disease activity status was low according to clinical disease activity index. Functional status was 66/100 according to elbow functional assessment scale. She had X‐ray of left elbow (Figure 1).

X‐ray of elbow.

She had elbow pain from eight years ago and she took pain killers. Pain and restriction of elbow extension were increased in last month without any history of trauma. She had started medications from 19 years ago. Current medications for current JIA were prednisolone tablet (5 mg), diclofenac sodium capsule (100 mg) and methotrexate tablet (2.5 mg). She had history of physical therapy for JIA two months ago (electrotherapy, stretching and strengthening exercises), but she had no improvement in pain and range of elbow extension. She had night pain that woke her up from sleep. She was stressed about her disease. In observation, left elbow was in flexion and she had kept her left elbow on abdomen. Elbow extension movement was painful and 45° less than normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

At first visit, other sources of elbow pain and limitation of elbow extension (cervical or shoulder source) were ruled out and pain and limitation of elbow extension were attributed to the JIA. MWM techniques were applied by a physical therapist with 20 years of experience in orthopedic physical therapy. MWM techniques were lateral glide technique and olecranon medial tilt, lateral rotation technique. The aim of application of these techniques was pain reduction and improvement of range of elbow extension.

For lateral glide technique, position of patient was supine. Upper extremity was in relaxed position in lateral rotation of shoulder and forearm supination. Elbow was in flexion slightly before starting point of restriction of elbow extension. Therapist stand beside affected elbow. Palmar surface of stabilizer hand of therapist stabilized lateral surface of distal humerus. Palmar surface of mobilizer hand of therapist was placed on medial surface of proximal ulna, slightly distal to joint line to apply gliding force. Therapist applied a lateral gliding force to the proximal ulna. Therapist maintained lateral gliding force and patient was requested to extend elbow to starting point of pain (Figure 2) [9].

Lateral glide technique.

For olecranon medial tilt, lateral rotation technique, position of patient was standing. Left upper extremity was in relaxed position in slight shoulder flexion and supported by therapist. Elbow was in flexion before starting point of restriction of elbow extension. Therapist stand in front of the patient, beside affected elbow. Therapist stabilized medial surface of distal humerus by one hand. Thenar eminence of another hand was placed on the posterolateral part of the olecranon slightly distal to the joint line. Other fingers were placed on posteromedial part of the olecranon. Therapist applied a medial tilt force to proximal ulna. Patient was requested to extend elbow before starting point of pain (Figure 3) [9].

Olecranon medial tilt, lateral rotation technique.

It should be mentioned that MWM techniques were applied gently. These techniques were pain free both during and following application. At First session three set of three repetitions of MWM techniques was conducted. Pain and range of motion were improved after first session, so therapist applied five sets of ten repetitions of MWM techniques in next sessions. There was one set of overpressure (passive end‐range pain‐free pressure) as the last set of technique. There was one‐minute rest between sets. Patient participated in 10 sessions of physiotherapy (3 sessions each week). Patient was instructed to do home exercise (three times a day, 10 repetition) to maintain improvement of pain and increased range of elbow extension. Home exercise included repetitive movement of elbow extension, functional use of elbow extension in activities of daily living (ADL), and isometric elbow extension in new available range of elbow extension.

It should be mentioned that a goniometer was used to measure the range of elbow extension. For this purpose, the goniometer was centered on the lateral epicondyle, and the stationary arm of the goniometer was placed at the tip of the acromion process. The mobile arm of the goniometer was oriented along the midline of the wrist [10].

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The results revealed that after 10 sessions of application of MWM techniques, resting pain was decreased from 6 to 3 on a 10‐point visual analog scale. Patient reported no night pain. Range of elbow extension was increased significantly from −45° to near 0° (Figures 4 and 5). Functional status was improved from 66/100 (before treatment) to 77/100 (after treatment) according to elbow functional assessment scale. Interesting finding in present case was that improvement in pain and range of elbow extension was maintained between sessions of physical therapy. In follow up (one month after last session), patient had no resting pain or night pain and 90% of increased range of elbow extension was maintained.

Discussion

The aim of the present case report was to evaluate the effectiveness of MWM techniques on pain and range of elbow extension in JIA. The findings indicated an improvement in pain and range of elbow extension in JIA with the application of MWM techniques. To the best of our knowledge, this case report may be one of the first studies investigating the efficacy of MWM techniques in JIA. It seems that the current case report revealed preliminary data about the effectiveness of MWM techniques in JIA.

In MWM techniques a passive gliding force is applied to a joint and sustained while the patient performs a painful reduced movement. It was claimed that these techniques can improve pain and function immediately following application [11]. MWM techniques give the clinician immediate feedback about pain [12] and these techniques include the active participation of the patient and elimination of pain during application of techniques. These features warrant the safety of these techniques. No significant adverse effects following application of these techniques have been reported [13].

Increased range of elbow extension in present case can be attributed to correction of positional fault of the bony segments and pain reduction. Possible mechanism for pain reduction can be attributed to these points that evidence suggests that MWM techniques may induce hypoalgesic effects [3]. It is suggested that MWM techniques achieve rapid improvement in persistent musculoskeletal pain that have been resistant to other types of treatment. Postulated mechanism of action of these techniques has focused on the restoration of bony positional faults [11]. From neurophysiologic point of view, elbow pain may increase the level of facilitation of the motor‐neuron pool in the spinal cord. Elimination of elbow pain with MWM techniques would reduce abnormal facilitation and return the motor‐neuron pool to a lower level of excitation [14]. Other possible mechanism for pain reduction in present case report can be attributed to improvement of proprioception after application of MWM techniques. Swelling, pain, and damaged musculoskeletal tissues can deteriorate proprioception. Disrupted proprioception has negative impact on muscle stiffness modulation and feedback and feedforward motor control. The elbow contributes to fine and synchronized movements of the upper limb during ADL. Manual therapy can stimulate the mechanoreceptors in soft tissue, muscle and joint and enhance somatosensory data to the central nervous system. Studies have shown that joint mobilization can stimulate motoneurons, increase sensitivity of muscle spindle, improve proprioception, and resulti into increased muscle strength and control. Some previous research reported improvements of elbow proprioception following application of MWM techniques in healthy participants [15].

We have not found any similar study conducted on JIA to compare results with them. There are some studies that evaluated the effect of MWM techniques on osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or tennis elbow. The result of our study is similar to these studies. Alkhawajah et al. in their research investigated the effect of MWM techniques on function and pain compared to sham MWM techniques in 40 patients with knee osteoarthritis. Results demonstrated that MWM techniques provided superior benefits over sham MWM techniques in terms of pain, physical function, knee muscle strength, and knee flexion range of motion [16]. Özçelep et al. evaluated the effects of MWM technique on joint distance, effusion area, and inflammation in patients with rheumatoid arthritis. This study indicated that MWM techniques effectively reduce inflammation in the wrist and PIP joints, increase joint space, and improve quality of life [3]. Reyhan et al. assessed the effects of MWM techniques in lateral epicondylitis. Forty patients were randomly assigned to group 1 (MWM techniques, exercise, and cold therapy) or group 2 (exercise and cold therapy). Results indicated that MWM techniques in addition to exercise and cold therapy have positive effects on elbow pain, function, and grip strength [4]. In another study, syed et al. examined the effects of adding MWM techniques to eccentric exercise for the management of lateral epicondylitis. Thirty patients were assigned to two groups: 15 in the MWM group (MWM technique + eccentric exercise) and 15 in the eccentric exercise group. This study showed that adding MWM techniques to eccentric exercise resulted in greater improvement of function and pain reduction in lateral epicondylitis [5]. Bagcaci et al. compared the effects of MWM techniques and muscle energy technique (MET) on pain, grip strength, and function in patients with lateral epicondylitis. Results demonstrated greater improvement in pain, grip strength, and function in both MWM techniques and MET groups than in the control group. There was not a statistically significant difference between MWM and MET groups [6]. Uttamchandani et al. evaluated the efficacy of powerball versus MWM techniques on pain and function in patients with lateral epicondylitis. The findings indicated that in both groups pain and function have significantly improved [7]. Abbott et al. investigated the initial effects of elbow MWM techniques on grip strength in subjects with lateral epicondylitis. According to the results, authors concluded that MWM techniques are a promising intervention for management of patients with lateral epicondylitis [8].

It should be mentioned that although the results of this study are positive, current evidence is insufficient to conclusively support efficacy of these techniques. There are some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. Present case report conducted on only one patient. Large randomized controlled trials are recommended to provide more definitive conclusions. We did not compare effectiveness of MWM techniques with other techniques. Future studies are suggested to explore effectiveness of other techniques in JIA. Present study had one‐month follow‐up period. This short term follow‐up is another limitation of current study. Similar research with more extended periods of follow‐up are suggested to confirm the persistency of the clinical effects. Future studies could suggest the use of these techniques and prescribing the most appropriate MWM techniques in JIA.

Conclusion

The results of the present study suggest that MWM techniques are an effective strategy to decrease pain and improve the range of elbow extension in JIA. These results may offer patients with JIA an opportunity to improve daily function.
