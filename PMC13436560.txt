Best Evidence Summary for Improving Self-Awareness of Falls in Elderly Postoperative Patients with Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fracture
Authors: Yujia Huo, Xiaohong Wang, Yu Wang, Jianing Yang, Yuqi Zeng, Hongxia Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study aims to evaluate and synthesize the best available evidence for improving the self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF), so as to provide a reference for clinical practice.MethodsThis evidence summary was conducted in accordance with the reporting standards established by the Fudan University Center for Evidence-Based Nursing. Guided by the “6S” evidence pyramid model, we systematically searched relevant domestic and international databases and official websites to retrieve literature concerning the improvement of the self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF, covering the time frame from database inception to December 2025. Two researchers independently assessed the quality of all included literature, and subsequently performed evidence extraction, synthesis, and grading in strict compliance with established evidence-based criteria.ResultsA total of 15 studies were included, consisting of 1 clinical decision, 5 guidelines, 5 expert consensuses, 3 systematic reviews, and 1 evidence summary. From these, 28 best-evidence recommendations were identified and categorized into six domains: establishing a multidisciplinary team, fall-related assessment and screening, health education and guidance, osteoporosis self-management, rehabilitation therapy, and follow-up and evaluation.ConclusionThis study summarizes the best available evidence on improving the self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF, thereby providing guidance for clinical practice. However, high-quality direct evidence in this research field remains limited, and further rigorous studies are required to continuously update and enrich the evidence base. In clinical application, individualized intervention plans should be formulated based on personalized fall risk profiles and patient preferences, to effectively reduce fall incidence and prevent postoperative refractures.Keywords: aged, osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture, self-awareness of falls, evidence summary, evidence-based nursing

Introduction

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) is a serious complication of osteoporosis and the most common type of fragility fracture.1 With the progressive global population aging, the prevalence of OVCF continues to increase, affecting 20% to 30% of individuals aged over 50 years worldwide.2 Currently, percutaneous vertebral augmentation (PVA) has become a widely used surgical treatment for OVCF due to its advantages including minimal invasiveness, rapid postoperative recovery, short operative duration, and favorable patient tolerance.3

Falls constitute a major global public health concern among the aging population, potentially resulting in severe adverse outcomes, including fractures, traumatic brain injury, and restricted mobility.4 Notably, falls serve as the leading cause of osteoporotic fractures in older adults, among which thoracolumbar vertebral fractures are the most prevalent subtype.5 For elderly postoperative patients with OVCF, fall risk is attributable to multiple synergistic factors. First, OVCF patients commonly suffer from sarcopenia and frailty, which impair neuromuscular performance and compromise postural balance.6 Second, vertebral structural damage induced by compression fractures alters spinal biomechanics, triggers kyphotic deformity and anterior displacement of the body’s center of gravity, and consequently undermines postural regulation and gait stability.7 Third, postoperative weakness of the paraspinal and core muscles is common in this population, which persistently impairs dynamic balance capacity.8 Furthermore, unresolved low back pain and incomplete functional recovery of the lumbar spine further limit joint mobility and compromise activities of daily living.9 Critically, falls experienced by elderly postoperative patients with OVCF not only elevate the risks of refracture, surgical failure, and permanent functional disability, but also substantially increase the healthcare burden and mortality rates.10

Self-awareness of falls refers to a context-dependent, composite characteristic of older adults’ subjective risk perception and behavioral intention in response to existing or potential fall events. It is a multidimensional concept that encompasses both the perception of fall risk and a holistic judgment of whether one possesses the awareness to cope.11 A higher level of self-awareness of falls is closely associated with improved fall-preventive behaviors and reduced fall risk in older populations.12 Nevertheless, elderly postoperative patients with OVCF commonly exhibit impaired self-awareness of falls due to the combined effects of proprioceptive and cognitive decline, excessive fear of falling and anxiety, chronic pain, sedative medication use, and adverse environmental conditions. Inadequate improvement in self-awareness of falls, a critical cognitive and psychological factor influencing adherence to fall prevention strategies,13 may result in insufficient engagement in preventive behaviors, thereby creating a vicious cycle of “fall—fracture—re-fall” among this patient population.

Existing domestic and international studies on self-awareness of falls have predominantly targeted community-dwelling older adults, stroke patients, and hypertensive patients, with most adopting a cross-sectional design. However, empirical investigations focusing on self-awareness of falls among elderly postoperative patients with OVCF remain limited and preliminary, and targeted interventional studies for this specific population are scarce. Driven by increased physical activity after pain remission, persistent bone fragility, and residual psychological trauma related to falls, self-awareness of falls in this patient population follows a distinct trajectory compared to that of general older adult cohorts. Accordingly, systematic evidence synthesis focusing on the improvement of self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF is currently absent, making it difficult to provide effective practice guidance for healthcare professionals and caregivers. Multiple validated tools are currently available for fall risk evaluation among postmenopausal women and osteoporosis patients, including the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Fall Risk Assessment Tool (FRAT), Falls Efficacy Scale (FES), and Hendrich II Fall Risk Model,14 as well as the commonly used OAK system for fall risk screening.15 Nevertheless, these existing instruments mainly focus on general fall risk stratification, while neglecting targeted evaluation of self-awareness dimensions. Against this background, the present study systematically retrieves domestic and international literature focusing on self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF and synthesizes the best available clinical evidence. This study aims to provide an evidence-based foundation for clinical fall prevention practice, thereby helping to reduce the incidence of falls and postoperative refractures, and ultimately enhance patients’ quality of life. Different from conventional fall prevention strategies that merely target physical risk factors, this evidence synthesis takes self-awareness of falls as the core intervention focus and constructs a comprehensive management framework covering standardized assessment, targeted intervention, and long-term follow-up.

Materials and Methods

This study followed the evidence synthesis reporting standards established by the Fudan University Center for Evidence-Based Nursing, which include six core steps: problem formulation, literature retrieval, literature screening, literature quality appraisal, evidence synthesis, and evidence grading.16 The study was formally registered with the registration number ES20269724.

Problem Establishment

The evidence-based research questions were formulated based on the PIPOST model.17 P (population): Elderly postoperative patients with OVCF; I (intervention): Intervention measures targeting the improvement of self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF; P (professional): Clinicians, nurses, rehabilitation therapists, dietitians, and other members of the multidisciplinary care team responsible for implementing the evidence; O (outcome): Fall incidence, self-awareness of falls, fall risk, new-onset fractures, and alterations in bone mineral density; S (setting): Hospitals, communities, and families; T (type of evidence): Clinical decisions, guidelines, evidence summaries, systematic reviews, and expert consensuses.

Retrieval Strategy

According to the “6S” evidence pyramid model,18 a systematic literature search was performed across Chinese and international databases, as well as guideline platforms, using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text terms. The retrieved resources included Up To Date, BMJ Best Practice, JBI Evidence Synthesis, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), Guidelines International Network (GIN), Registered Nurses’ Association of Ontario (RNAO), Medlive, CNKI, Wanfang Database, VIP Database, SinoMed, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Embase, CINAHL, International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF), Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA), and American Physical Therapy Association (APTA). The search period spanned from the inception of each database to December 2025. The search terms used were: “Osteoporos*/Age-Related Osteoporos*/Age Related Osteoporosis/Age-Related Bone Loss*/Bone Loss, Age Related/Senile Osteoporos*” AND “Spinal Fracture*/Hangman* Fracture/Vertebral Compression Fracture/Vertebral Fracture/Spine Fracture/Thoracic Vertebral Fracture/Lumbar Fracture/Thoracolumbar Fracture/Compression Fracture/Osteoporotic Fracture*/Fragility Fracture/Percutaneous Vertebroplasty/Percutaneous Kyphoplasty/Percutaneous Vertebral Augmentation” AND “Fall*/Accidental Fall*/Stumble/Slip/Self-Awareness of Fall*/Fall Risk Perception”. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Study population comprising postoperative patients aged ≥ 60 years with OVCF; (2) Studies focusing on the improvement or assessment of self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF; (3) Study types including clinical decisions, guidelines, expert consensuses, evidence summaries, and systematic reviews; (4) Studies published in Chinese or English.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Unavailable full texts or duplicate publications; (2) Outdated versions of studies that have been updated; (3) Studies that failed quality assessment.

Quality Evaluation of the Literature

Guidelines were evaluated using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation II (AGREE II).19 The AGREE II system comprises six domains and 23 items, each scored on a scale of 1 to 7, whereby higher scores indicate greater conformity to the corresponding evaluation criteria. The standardized score for each domain was calculated using the following formula: [(actual score evaluated − lowest possible score)/(highest possible score − lowest possible score)] × 100%. The recommendation grade was determined based on its domain-specific standardized scores: Grade A if all six domains scored ≥ 60%; Grade B if ≥ 3 domains scored ≥ 30% and at least one scored < 60%; and Grade C if ≥ 3 domains scored < 30%. Expert consensus statements and systematic reviews were evaluated using the criteria of the Authentic Assessment Tool (2016)20 from the Australian JBI Evidence-Based Healthcare Center. Each item was rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not Applicable” for quality assessment. Evidence summaries and clinical decisions were evaluated with the Critical Appraisal for Summaries of Evidence (CASE) tool,21 which consists of four domains and ten items, with each item rated as “Yes,” “Not completely,” or “No.” The whole quality appraisal procedure was performed independently by two researchers trained in evidence-based nursing methodology, and all discrepancies were resolved through team discussions to reach a consensus.

Evidence Extraction and Summary

Information from the included literature was independently extracted by two trained researchers, covering basic characteristics including authors, publication years, research themes, literature resources, and literature types. Evidence was then summarized according to the topic. When multiple pieces of evidence were essentially consistent, the most concise and easily understandable expression was prioritized. If the evidence was complementary, it was merged. For contradictory evidence, we adhered to the principle of prioritizing recently published studies, high-quality literature, and authoritative evidence-based medicine resources. The 2014 version of the JBI Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation22 was adopted to stratify synthesized evidence into Levels 1 to 5. The strength of recommendation was determined as Grade A (strong recommendation) or Grade B (weak recommendation) based on the feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness of the evidence.22 A strong recommendation (Grade A) requires evidence that unequivocally demonstrates the benefits outweigh the harms (or vice versa) and is derived from high-quality studies. Moreover, such recommendations must reflect reasonable resource allocation and thoroughly incorporate patient values, preferences, and experiences. A weak recommendation (Grade B) is warranted when the evidence is of lower quality, the balance between benefits and harms is uncertain, the resource implications are unclear, or patient values, preferences, and experiences have not been comprehensively considered.

Discussion

Establish a Multidisciplinary Integrated Model and Implement Fracture Liaison Services (FLS)

The first two pieces of evidence recommend the establishment of a multidisciplinary team and the implementation of holistic patient management based on the Fracture Liaison Service (FLS) framework. FLS has been validated to reduce the risk of subsequent fractures among patients with fragility fractures.38 Existing research demonstrates that FLS intervention strategies applied to patients with spinal fragility fractures can effectively improve post-discharge medication adherence, mitigate postoperative fall risk, and prevent secondary fractures, ultimately optimizing patients’ long-term quality of life.39 Despite the promising clinical outcomes associated with FLS implementation, the current evidence base derives predominantly from observational studies rather than large-scale randomized controlled trials, which limits robust causal inference. Furthermore, effective FLS delivery relies on stable institutional support and sustained interdisciplinary collaboration, creating notable implementation barriers in resource-limited healthcare settings. Therefore, sufficient human and material resources should be allocated to develop adaptable FLS implementation models that suit varied resource settings, while promoting the standardization of FLS practice and strengthening cost-effectiveness research in China.

Systematically Conduct Fall-Related Assessment and Screening

Items 3 to 6 specify the core contents and corresponding assessment tools for fall-related evaluation in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF. Item 3 is supported by high-level evidence and can be directly applied to clinical practice. Fall risk arises from the complex interplay of multiple factors, including physiological status, psychological status, medication, environmental conditions, and social support.40 Comprehensive fall assessment for this patient population enables accurate identification of individual fall risk factors, thereby providing a scientific basis for the formulation of individualized nursing plans for fall prevention.24 Patients with vertebral fractures frequently develop fear of falling due to prior fall events or postoperative activity restrictions. Excessive fear of falling may cause self-restriction of daily activities, which in turn reduces muscle strength and impairs balance performance.34 This vicious cycle further elevates patients’ fall vulnerability and raises the risk of recurrent falls.41 Accordingly, fear of falling should be a key focus in assessment. Frailty is highly prevalent in elderly patients with OVCF and is closely correlated with increased risks of falls, secondary fractures, postoperative complications, and mortality.42 Early screening and systematic assessment of frailty are essential to slow frailty progression and optimize clinical outcomes in this patient population.43 Proactive evaluation of patients’ fall risk awareness and their willingness to participate in fall prevention facilitates the intensive implementation of targeted intervention strategies.37 Healthcare professionals should conduct dynamic postoperative assessments of patients’ pain severity, neurological function, and other relevant aspects.31 Nevertheless, Items 4 and 5 are supported by low-level evidence, which necessitates individualized adjustments according to real-world clinical practice. Additionally, no unified clinical standard has currently been established for the optimal timing of fall-related assessments in this patient population. The selection of efficient and accurate fall assessment tools is crucial for the effective prevention of falls and subsequent fall-related injuries.44 Different assessment tools are currently utilized to evaluate patients’ fall risk, fear of falling, and self-awareness of falls.29,34 Although the SAFE exhibits promising application potential, it has only been validated in general elderly inpatient populations,13 and its applicability to elderly postoperative patients with OVCF requires further verification. Moreover, high-quality clinical evidence regarding fall-related assessments in this specific patient population remains insufficient, highlighting the urgent need for further studies to establish a standardized assessment framework in this field.

Providing Diverse and Individualized Health Education and Guidance

Evidence items 7 to 12 highlight the indispensable role of health education in fall prevention. Clinical cases describing postoperative fall-induced refractures, coupled with visual risk stratification tools, enable patients to self-evaluate their fall risks and enhance self-awareness of falls.34 However, the use of real adverse clinical cases to warn patients may induce excessive fear of falling, resulting in unnecessary activity limitation and even functional disability.41 Current literature fails to establish an optimal balance between enhancing patients’ self-awareness of falls and avoiding excessive fear of falling. Patients with osteoporotic vertebral fractures are at significantly higher risk of secondary fractures, with a reported refracture rate of up to 10.3%.31 Targeted health education for patients and their families concerning the potential risks and adverse outcomes of subsequent fractures is essential to consolidate their awareness of refracture hazards.30 Fall-related anxiety can limit patients’ daily physical activity, which further leads to decreased muscle strength and impaired balance function. Furthermore, depressive symptoms may undermine patients’ self-efficacy, weakening their capacity to identify environmental fall hazards.45 Psychological counseling and cognitive behavioral therapy can effectively alleviate fear of falling, thereby improving patients’ mobility and quality of life.24–26,34 Supported by high-level evidence and demonstrating strong feasibility, this recommendation is conducive to widespread dissemination and implementation in clinical practice. Appropriate health education exerts a pivotal beneficial impact on osteoporosis management and the enhancement of patient treatment adherence.33 Health education should incorporate not only fall-relevant knowledge but also disease cognition regarding osteoporosis and OVCF, postoperative rehabilitation techniques, and standardized application of assistive devices including lumbar braces.33,37 Nevertheless, the optimal duration and intervention frequency of targeted health education for this specific patient population have yet to be clarified. Further high-quality prospective clinical trials are thus warranted to develop standardized, efficient health education protocols. Healthcare professionals serve as core participants in the fall prevention management of postoperative patients, which necessitates the reinforcement of targeted professional training for clinical staff.37 Research indicates that the implementation of multifaceted health education can effectively improve osteoporosis knowledge and treatment adherence among patients with fragility fractures.46 Accordingly, it is recommended that multimodal health education approaches, including video tutorials, printed educational brochures, and WeChat-based online platforms, be adopted to increase patients’ disease-related knowledge and self-management capacity.29,37 Despite the considerable clinical value of multimodal health education strategies, the relevant evidence is limited to expert consensus with a low level of evidence. Nevertheless, such educational interventions provide actionable clinical guidance and merit widespread clinical adoption.

Strengthen Comprehensive Interventions for Osteoporosis Self-Management

Postoperative osteoporosis self-management among patients encompasses multiple dimensions. Long-term standardized self-management programs can effectively enhance patients’ ability to follow individualized healthcare regimens and boost therapeutic compliance.10 Malnutrition has been shown to impair muscle strength and physical performance, delay fracture repair and postoperative rehabilitation, and elevate risks of secondary fractures and functional disability.47 Nutritional intervention constitutes an indispensable component of systematic osteoporosis management. Sufficient dietary protein intake attenuates bone loss and augments muscle strength, whereas vitamin D supplementation delivers substantial benefits for fall prevention among high-risk groups.48 Patients should be clearly instructed to maintain a well-balanced diet. Meanwhile, avoidance of unhealthy dietary behaviors is highly recommended, and healthcare professionals should guide patients to develop scientific and standardized nutritional perceptions and dietary habits.26 However, elderly postoperative patients with OVCF frequently suffer from a variety of chronic comorbidities, such as chronic kidney disease and cardiovascular disorders. Given this complexity, the existing evidence remains insufficient to support the formulation of individualized dietary intervention strategies for this patient population. Most people can rely on sun exposure to meet their vitamin D requirements, and bone mineral density increases significantly with longer sun exposure time.28 Strict patient adherence to standardized anti-osteoporosis medications during the perioperative stage and long-term post-fracture follow-up, in combination with routine calcium and vitamin D supplementation, can substantially reduce the risk of subsequent vertebral refracture.49 In addition, patients are advised to minimize or discontinue the use of fall-risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs), as well as pharmaceuticals that exert detrimental effects on bone metabolism.24,25,27,28,30,33 Patients with polypharmacy exhibit higher risks of falls and secondary fractures, since agents including sedatives and antidepressants compromise postural balance and motor coordination, further elevating fall susceptibility.50 Medication management for patients with OVCF faces substantial clinical challenges. Research indicates that long-term adherence to anti-osteoporosis pharmacotherapy is generally suboptimal, often due to cost, side effects, or forgetfulness.51 Additionally, deprescribing FRIDs is particularly difficult, as many patients are already dependent on sedative agents, and there is a lack of clear alternative treatments or structured tapering protocols. Future high-quality studies are recommended to provide more reliable and targeted evidence for medication management.

Advancing Systematic and Standardized Postoperative Rehabilitation

Postoperative rehabilitation serves as the cornerstone of comprehensive management for fragility fractures.47 Cumulative clinical evidence has demonstrated that postoperative pain is an independent risk factor for postoperative falls.52 Accordingly, standardized pain management protocols are highly recommended to reduce postoperative pain intensity.28,31 While diverse physical therapy modalities confer beneficial effects on postoperative rehabilitation, treatment parameters, energy output intensities, and relevant safety thresholds of these interventions remain to be systematically validated in clinical trials.28 The relatively high economic costs of physical therapy may impede its widespread clinical implementation, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings. Early initiation of postoperative rehabilitation training accelerates the recovery of joint mobility, shortens bed rest duration, and reduces the incidence of postoperative complications.31 Research indicates that multimodal exercise regimens substantially improve bone mineral density and overall physical function in patients with fragility fractures.53 Although multimodal exercise training is strongly endorsed in numerous guidelines and expert consensus statements, the detailed operational protocols of such training have not reached unified clinical standards; large-sample, high-quality randomized controlled trials are urgently warranted to generate robust clinical evidence. Timely implementation of targeted functional training for lumbar and paraspinal muscles enhances core stability and provides moderate mechanical stimulation to fractured vertebral segments, thereby accelerating bone healing following vertebral injury.54 Progressive resistance and balance training improves muscle strength, alleviates postoperative pain, and reduces fear of falling, collectively lowering fall risk in vulnerable patients.55 Current clinical evidence on postoperative rehabilitation interventions for OVCF remains limited and inconsistent, and no unified consensus has been reached regarding the intensity, frequency, and duration of rehabilitation protocols. Given inter-individual variability in age, bone quality, and neuromuscular function, personalized rehabilitation exercise regimens should be dynamically tailored to individual fracture healing progress and overall functional status. Building on these, proprioceptive and dual-task training can be further integrated into the existing rehabilitation framework. Although such training modalities have not been widely applied in the rehabilitation of elderly postoperative patients with OVCF, substantial evidence from neurorehabilitation has demonstrated that they can not only optimize gait parameters but also significantly improve balance control and reduce fall risk.56,57 Within the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) clinical framework, multidisciplinary teamwork has emerged as a core paradigm for postoperative orthopedic rehabilitation.58 A multidisciplinary rehabilitation model integrates assessment, treatment, and community support, building a hospital–community network to enhance fall prevention adherence, prevent subsequent fractures, and improve outcomes.

Establishment of Informatized and Dynamic Follow-Up Evaluation

Items 24 to 28 of the evidence summary elaborate on follow-up and evaluation strategies tailored for elderly postoperative patients with OVCF. Scheduled follow-ups and serial assessments form an indispensable component of long-term clinical management for patients with vertebral fragility fractures.32 Clinicians should conduct regular fall risk screening and comprehensive fall assessment throughout the entire postoperative period.30 However, the current evidence base remains weak, as most recommendations are derived solely from expert consensus. Importantly, unified time frames for repeated fall risk reassessment have not yet been established, and this deficiency impedes the translation of relevant recommendations into generalizable clinical pathways. Clinically validated fracture risk assessment tools are recommended to quantify refracture probability and identify patients at high risk of refracture.27 The calibration performance of the FRAX algorithm among patients with prior vertebral fractures remains controversial, given that this tool fails to incorporate fall risk, bone turnover markers, and other vital predictive indicators. Currently, bone mineral density (BMD) and bone turnover markers (BTM) act as core objective biomarkers for assessing therapeutic responses to anti-osteoporotic medications during postoperative follow-up. Moreover, serial BMD measurements conducted with identical equipment at the same medical institution are highly recommended to ensure reliable, comparable longitudinal outcomes.31 With the rapid advancement of information technology and ubiquitous mobile devices, digital health platforms, including WeChat and specialized mobile applications, have been widely integrated into the entire clinical workflow for fall risk screening, standardized health education, patient outcome reporting, and long-term postoperative follow-up.59 Nevertheless, low technology acceptance and poor usage adherence among elderly patients, coupled with concerns over data security and personal privacy, remain major barriers limiting the large-scale clinical implementation of digital rehabilitation management. These shortcomings warrant sufficient clinical attention and targeted intervention strategies to improve the applicability of digital health services. To validate the efficacy of fall management interventions, comprehensive assessment incorporating multiple outcome indicators is recommended, including fall incidence, level of self-awareness of falls, fall risk, new fracture occurrence, and changes in BMD.31,34,35

Conclusion

This study systematically synthesized 28 evidence-based recommendations from 15 studies, covering six domains: establishing a multidisciplinary team, fall-related assessment and screening, health education and guidance, osteoporosis self-management, rehabilitation therapy, and follow-up and evaluation. Furthermore, the results emphasize the multidimensional and systematic characteristics of fall prevention management, demonstrating that single and fragmented educational or training interventions yield unsatisfactory preventive outcomes. Therefore, it is crucial to integrate psychological intervention, medication management, nutritional support, and rehabilitation therapy organically to formulate a holistic and standardized fall prevention management system. These recommendations may help improve patients’ self-awareness of falls and proactive preventive behaviors, reduce the incidence of falls and postoperative refractures, and ultimately promote the recovery of postoperative functional independence in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only literature published in Chinese and English was included in the present study, which may restrict the geographical and cultural generalizability of the synthesized evidence. Second, the included literature predominantly comprised guidelines and expert consensus statements, and high-quality prospective studies directly examining self-awareness of falls in elderly postoperative patients with OVCF remain scarce. Furthermore, heterogeneity was observed across studies in assessment tools, intervention timing, and outcome measures, which may have constrained evidence synthesis and direct comparisons. In addition, most of the available evidence was generated from non-Chinese populations or general elderly groups, and its clinical applicability and effectiveness for Chinese elderly postoperative patients with OVCF require further verification. Therefore, although the evidence synthesized in this study provides a structured theoretical and clinical reference, the strength and scope of applicability of these recommendations should be interpreted with caution in clinical practice. Future research should focus on the following key directions. First, large-sample, multicenter, prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to systematically evaluate the efficacy of integrated intervention strategies for improving self-awareness of falls. Second, fall self-awareness assessment tools specifically designed for elderly postoperative patients with OVCF should be developed and validated to improve measurement standardization. Third, innovative personalized intervention models based on mobile health (mHealth) technologies, including mobile applications and wearable monitoring devices, should be further explored.
