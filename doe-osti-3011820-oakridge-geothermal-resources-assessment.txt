Executive Summary
This report is a resource assessment of potential geothermal energy applications in Oakridge,
Oregon, a rural community in the central Oregon Cascade Range. This assessment was prepared by
the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) under the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Communities Local Energy Action Program (LEAP). Oakridge had a previous geothermal assessment
completed nearly 50 years ago that indicated potential for geothermal energy technologies in the
area. This assessment builds on those results to assess the viability of those technologies for
Oakridge.
Concurrent with the findings of the previous assessment, this assessment finds the Oakridge region
in the central Oregon Cascade Range presents promising geothermal potential because of its unique
geological setting, characterized by active volcanism and structural lineaments that facilitate deep
geothermal fluid circulation. The geothermal resources in the central Oregon Cascade Range—
particularly in areas such as Newberry Volcano, Crater Lake, and Oakridge—represent significant
potential for energy production. These regions exhibit elevated heat flow, indicating a strong
geothermal gradient that is suitable for both electricity generation and direct-use applications. The
presence of thermal springs and high heat flow in areas such as McCredie and Kitson Hot Springs
further supports the viability of geothermal development in the region.
High-temperature hydrothermal systems associated with volcanic centers, such as Newberry Volcano
and Crater Lake, indicate substantial subsurface heat, where elevated heat flow values exceed 100
milliwatts per square meter, highlighting potential for electricity generation and direct-use
applications. The dominant sodium chloride-sulfate (Na-Cl-[SO₄]) water composition in the area,
combined with its structural geology (i.e., major faults and lineaments and their intersections),
indicates conditions exist for geothermal activity.

Geological assessments reveal the region encompasses both the Western Cascades and High
Cascades provinces, with volcanic rock formations ranging from Oligocene tuffs to Quaternary lavas.
Thermal modeling, using the Stanford Thermal Earth Model, predicts subsurface temperatures
reaching 175°C, or 347°F, at a depth of 3 kilometers (km), or 1.86 miles (mi), in the southern
Oakridge area, particularly near Kitson Hot Springs. Surface manifestations, including McCredie and
Kitson Hot Springs, further confirm the presence of geothermal resources, with silica and cation
geothermometers estimating reservoir temperatures between 100°C and 160°C, or 212°F and
320°F.

Generally, high-temperature geothermal resources (above 150°C or 302°F) are primarily used for
electricity generation, whereas lower-temperature resources support direct-use applications—
including heating and cooling for homes and businesses, industrial processes, agriculture, and
recreational activities. The temperature of geothermal resources in the Oakridge area has not been
confirmed, and further exploration—including through advanced thermal models, geochemical
analysis, and geophysical surveys—can help refine subsurface reservoir target predictions and
enhance the understanding of geothermal potential. For example, the Stanford Thermal Model and
the surface manifestations demonstrate some variability in predicted reservoir temperatures,
highlighting the value of further exploration.

Given the potential geothermal resources in the area, a combined heat and power (CHP) plant
similar to the one at the Oregon Institute of Technology could be a viable solution for the Oakridge
region. NREL conducted a simulation using the GEOPHIRES tool (GEOthermal energy for Production
of Heat and electricity [“IR”] Economically Simulated) and evaluated a combined heating and
electricity production system based on a cogeneration parallel cycle. The estimated outputs include
1.38 MW of electricity and 13.63 MW of heat, with a levelized cost of electricity of $13.52

 iv

cents/kWh and a levelized cost of heat of $4.14 USD/million British thermal units. The system
assumes a well depth of 2.8 km, or 1.74 mi; a geothermal gradient of 58°C/km, or 168°F/mi; and a
flow rate of 30 kilograms per second, or 66 pounds per second, per production well, using one
injection and two production wells. The total capital cost is estimated at $20.5M, including $5.81M
for drilling and completion, $1M for stimulation, $11.4M for the surface power plant, $1.7M for the
field gathering system, and $0.54M for exploration. Annual operating and maintenance costs are
projected at $1.4M, comprising $0.3M for wellfield maintenance and $0.9M for power plant upkeep.
Economic modeling follows a standard levelized cost approach, assuming a 5% interest rate, a 30-
year project lifetime, and an 80% capacity factor. The analysis indicates a net present value of
$3.28M, an internal rate of return of 6.42%, and a payback period of 13.6 years, with 50% of costs
allocated to the electrical plant. Although based on some assumptions, these estimates serve as a
general reference for capital and operational costs in geothermal CHP projects, aiding in feasibility
assessments and investment planning.

To make an informed decision for future geothermal investment, the community coalition could
consider a geophysical exploration in the Oakridge area, particularly near Kitson and McCredie Hot
Springs, using geological mapping, geochemical analysis, and geophysical surveys to refine
subsurface predictions and identify promising drilling locations. In addition, developing direct-use
applications such as district heating and industrial heating can present a near-term opportunity,
alongside power generation. The coalition can also evaluate the feasibility of connecting geothermal
resources to existing transmission infrastructure. Costs to site geothermal applications beyond 15–
30 km from the existing power grid can rise because of increased challenges related to terrain,
permitting, right-of-way issues, and infrastructure upgrades. Finally, it can be beneficial to streamline
the permitting process by working closely with local, state, and federal agencies to establish clear
guidelines for geothermal development that protect the environment while supporting economic
growth.

Geothermal Energy Fundamentals
“Geothermal energy” refers to the heat produced within the Earth that can be harnessed for human
use. This heat originates from the Earth’s initial formation, the radioactive decay of minerals in its
core, and solar accumulation (Turcotte and Schubert 2002). The geothermal heat at the Earth’s core
is estimated to reach temperatures as high as 5,000°C, comparable to the surface temperature of
the Sun. The temperature at the base of the Earth’s crust is approximately 1,000°C. The
temperature at the base of the mantle and the Earth’s core is estimated to be approximately
3,500°C and 5,000°C, respectively.
This geothermal gradient, or the temperature difference between the Earth’s core and surface,
generates a continuous flow of thermal energy from the core to the surface (Ejiga et al. 2022). The
geothermal gradient refers to the rate at which the temperature of the Earth’s crust increases in
depth. It represents the thermal gradient within the Earth’s crust and is a key concept in
understanding the distribution of geothermal energy (Turcotte and Schubert 2002). This gradient can
vary depending on geological conditions and the presence of certain factors such as groundwater
flow.
In typical continental crust, the geothermal gradient averages around 25°C per kilometer (km;
72.4°F per mile [mi]) within the first 5 km (approximately 2–3 mi) of the Earth’s surface. However, at
greater depths, such as around 40 km, this gradient decreases to about 16°C per km (Turcotte and
Schubert 2002). This decline happens because the heat transfer mechanism shifts from conduction
to convection and other processes as depth increases. Importantly, the geothermal gradient is not
constant everywhere. In regions with substantial groundwater flow, the temperature increase with
depth can drop significantly, become negligible, or even reverse. Groundwater flow can carry heat
away, disrupting the typical pattern of temperature increase—especially in aquifers or areas with high
permeability.
To better understand the significance of subsurface temperatures for geothermal energy, it’s
important to clarify the relationship between geothermal gradient, thermal conductivity, and heat
flow. Subsurface temperature is the primary factor in determining the suitability of a geothermal
resource for various applications. Subsurface temperature is influenced by the geothermal gradient,
which describes how quickly temperature increases with depth, and the thermal conductivity of the
surrounding rock, which affects how efficiently heat is transferred. Together, these two parameters
determine heat flow, or the rate at which thermal energy moves through the Earth’s crust. Although
the geothermal gradient gives a general sense of subsurface heating, thermal conductivity
determines how much of that heat is available and accessible. Understanding these relationships is
crucial to assess the development potential of a geothermal site, particularly when evaluating
whether a resource is suitable for power generation, district heating, or direct-use applications.
On the other hand, low-temperature geothermal energy is generated from solar heat absorbed and
stored near the Earth’s surface (Figure 1). This stored solar heat ensures surface temperatures
remain relatively stable year-round, providing a dependable resource for applications such as
geothermal heat pumps and direct-use systems.

Resource Requirements and Geothermal Applications
Resource Categories
The GeoVision report (2019) identifies three primary categories of geothermal resources: geothermal
heat pump (GHP) resources, hydrothermal resources, and enhanced geothermal systems (EGS).
Geothermal Heat Pump Resources
Shallow soil, rock, and aquifers across all 50 states provide a significant geothermal resource
because of their thermal storage capabilities. This thermal storage enables their use as a medium
for heat exchange, improving the efficiency of heating and cooling systems in residential and
commercial buildings (Figure 2). Geothermal heat pumps leverage these shallow-earth resources to
reduce energy consumption. GHPs can be installed wherever the ground can be accessed at depths
unaffected by seasonal temperature fluctuations.

Hydrothermal Resources
Hydrothermal systems rely on natural heat within the Earth combined with groundwater and rock
features, such as open fractures, which allow fluid flow for heat recovery. These resources vary in
temperature, from slightly above ambient conditions to over 375°C, or 707°F (Figure 2).
Temperatures above this threshold may require advanced technologies to effectively harness the
energy for various applications.
Enhanced Geothermal Systems
Enhanced geothermal systems comprise unconventional resources with significant heat but lack the
groundwater or rock characteristics needed for energy extraction without advanced engineering.
These systems can operate at any temperature above ambient that supports energy conversion
(Figure 2), although their practicality depends on the costs of the engineering required. EGS
resources have potential applications across geothermal technologies.
Resource Considerations
These three geothermal resources are applicable to various electric and nonelectric energy
applications (Figure 2). Although some rely on surface-level temperatures, others require deep
drilling. The resource’s temperature determines its application. High-temperature geothermal
resources (above 150°C or 302°F) are primarily used for electricity generation, whereas lower-
temperature resources support direct-use applications—including heating and cooling for homes and
businesses, industrial processes, agriculture, and recreational activities. In shallow-earth
environments, where temperatures remain consistent (below 30°C or 86°F), GHPs provide an

 3

efficient option for residential and commercial heating and cooling (U.S. Department of Energy
2019).
Although geothermal power plants have been providing renewable energy for more than a century,
recent advancements highlight the versatility of geothermal resources beyond reliable, constant
power generation. Geothermal energy also offers solutions for heating, cooling, and energy storage
while also supporting carbon capture, green hydrogen production, and the recovery of critical
minerals (Figure 3). Acknowledging its potential, both public and private sector stakeholders are
increasingly investing in and driving the development of geothermal technologies.

Geothermal Applications
GEOTHERMAL POWER TECHNOLOGY: CONVENTIONAL
Geothermal energy is widely used for electricity generation through three main types of power plants:
dry steam, flash steam, and binary cycle systems (Figure 4).
 • Dry steam plants. These systems use steam directly from underground reservoirs to drive
 turbines, making them the simplest form of geothermal power generation.
 • Flash steam plants. In this system, high-temperature geothermal fluids are brought to the
 surface. A sudden drop in pressure causes the fluid to “flash” into steam, which is then used
 to power turbines.
 • Binary cycle plants. Designed for lower-temperature geothermal resources, binary cycle
 plants transfer heat from geothermal fluids to a secondary fluid—such as isobutane or
 isopentane—in a heat exchanger. These secondary fluids, with lower boiling points than
 water, vaporize at moderate geothermal temperatures (typically 100–200°C or 212–
 392°F). The vapor drives the turbines to produce electricity. The secondary fluid is then
 condensed back into liquid form and recirculated in a closed-loop system.

 4

These technologies illustrate the versatility and efficiency of geothermal power generation,
leveraging Earth’s natural heat to produce renewable energy from conventional hydrothermal
geothermal systems.

Figure 4. Types of geothermal power plant configurations include dry steam, flash steam, and binary cycle systems. Image
 from GeoVision (2019).

GEOTHERMAL DIRECT USES
Geothermal direct-use applications include heating greenhouses and aquaculture facilities, food
processing (e.g., drying agricultural products and brewing beer), and industrial processes requiring
heat (e.g., pulp and paper production, drying cement, and treating lumber). These applications could
operate using a cascade system, where geothermal water is used sequentially for multiple purposes
to maximize energy efficiency. For instance, hot water from a geothermal source may first be used
for industrial processing, followed by greenhouse heating, and finally for aquaculture.
Another notable geothermal direct-use application highlighted in the GeoVision report (2019) is
district heating. These systems use geothermal resources with temperatures below 150°C or
302°F, where electricity generation has historically been less economically viable (McCabe et al.
2019). In geothermal district heating, hot water is drawn from geothermal reservoirs and delivered
through pipelines. The heat is then either transferred via heat exchangers or directly supplied to
residential and commercial buildings, effectively meeting heating and hot water demands across
entire communities.
One prominent example is the geothermal district heating system in Boise, Idaho, which has
operated since the 1890s. In 2012, it expanded to include 645,000 square feet (ft²) of floor space
from Boise State University (John W. Lund and Boyd 2016; Mink 2017). The United States has 23
operational geothermal district heating systems, with a total installed capacity of about 100
megawatts thermal (MWth) as of 2021 (NREL 2021). In comparison, Europe had 401 such systems
by 2023, with a total of 6 gigawattg thermal (GWth) of installed capacity across 29 countries (Cariaga
2024), accounting for nearly half of the global installed capacity for direct geothermal use.
Recently, The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) released the Pathways to Commercial Liftoff:
Geothermal Heating and Cooling report, outlining strategies to expand the adoption of geothermal
heating and cooling technologies across the nation (U.S. Department of Energy 2025a). DOE’s report
emphasizes increasing the adoption of geothermal heating and cooling technologies offers a
significant opportunity to reduce energy consumption, lower carbon emissions, and enhance energy
system reliability. By 2035, geothermal heating and cooling systems could be installed in the
equivalent of 7 million homes, tripling the current U.S. capacity—with potential investments ranging
from $100 billion to $150 billion.

GEOTHERMAL HEAT PUMPS OR GEO-EXCHANGE TECHNOLOGY
A geothermal heat pump system operates by transferring thermal energy between a building and the
Earth through a network of underground pipes. In winter, the system extracts heat from the ground,
which maintains a consistent temperature, and uses it to heat the building. In summer, the process
is reversed: The system removes heat from the building and transfers it to the cooler ground,
providing efficient cooling. This method takes advantage of the Earth’s stable underground
temperatures, making it an energy-efficient solution for year-round heating and cooling (U.S.
Department of Energy 2025b).
Geothermal heat pumps are widely used in both residential and commercial applications in the
United States to meet heating and cooling demands. These systems, often referred to as ground
source heat pumps (GSHP), rely on the thermal storage capabilities of the Earth’s shallow depths,
typically around 30 feet. At this depth, the temperature remains relatively constant, ranging between
10°C (50°F) and 15°C (59°F), which is typically warmer than the surrounding air in winter and
cooler in summer. By using this stable temperature, GHPs efficiently store and release excess heat
as needed (J. W. Lund 2001; Liu, Lu, and Yang 2019).
A typical GHP system comprises three main components: 1) a ground heat exchanger, which is a
network of pipes buried underground or immersed in water bodies or groundwater; 2) an energy
delivery system, such as a heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) system with ducts for
forced air or in-floor radiant heating; and 3) the heat pump, which circulates thermal energy between
the ground heat exchanger and the delivery system. The heat exchanger transfers heat to or from a
fluid, usually a water/antifreeze mixture (U.S. Department of Energy 2025b).
In the United States, most GHP systems (approximately 84%) use closed-loop ground heat
exchangers, with the majority installed in vertical configurations, followed by horizontal loops. A
smaller portion (16%) employs open-loop systems, which use groundwater or surface water to
transfer heat (see Figure 5). These systems are versatile and can efficiently heat, cool, and even
provide hot water for homes and buildings (J. W. Lund 2001; Liu, Lu, and Yang 2019).

Figure 5. Diagrams of frequently used closed-loop and open-loop ground heat exchangers. Image from GeoVision (2019).

DOE’s Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Geothermal Heating and Cooling report highlights the
transformative potential of GHP technologies. These systems offer impressive energy efficiency,
environmental benefits, and longevity, with underground components lasting more than 50 years.
Unlike other geothermal technologies, GHPs can be installed almost anywhere, making them
versatile and accessible for widespread adoption.

Oakridge Geothermal Resources Assessment
Overview of Geothermal Resources in Central Oregon Cascade
Range
The central Oregon Cascade Range is recognized for its significant geothermal potential, attributed
to its unique geological features and volcanic activity. The region’s geothermal resources are
primarily associated with high-temperature hydrothermal systems linked to volcanic centers and
deep fault zones (Figure 6).

 Figure 6. A map depicting geothermal resources across the United States. Image from National Renewable Energy
 Laboratory (NREL) (NREL 2018).

Studies have identified several promising areas for geothermal development, including the Newberry
Volcano and Crater Lake areas (Blackwell, Negraru, and Richards 2006; Priest and Vogt 1983).
These locations exhibit elevated heat flow and thermal springs, indicating substantial subsurface
heat. The geothermal gradient in these areas suggests temperatures suitable for both electricity
generation and direct-use applications.
Heat flow measurements in the central Oregon Cascade Range reveal significant variations between
the Western and High Cascade subprovinces. In the Western Cascades, heat flow values average

 7

approximately 40–50 milliwatts per square meter (mW/m²) or 3.7–4.6 mW/ft². In contrast, the High
Cascades exhibit higher heat flow values, averaging around 100 mW/m² or 9.3 mW/ft² (Priest and
Vogt 1983). This increase in heat flow from west to east correlates with the region’s volcanic activity
and geological structures (Figure 7). Notably, areas near Newberry Volcano and Crater Lake have
been identified as geothermal hotspots, with heat flow values exceeding 100 mW/m², indicating
substantial geothermal potential (Priest and Vogt 1983).

Figure 7. Map showing heat flow in the Cascade Range of Oregon with contour interval –20 mW/m2. Image from Priest, G.
 R., and Vogt, B. F. (Eds.). (Priest and Vogt 1983).

The central Oregon Cascade Range hosts several geothermal systems characterized by the presence
of thermal springs and wells. Notable examples include Breitenbush Hot Springs, McCredie Hot
Springs, and Belknap Hot Springs. These geothermal fluids are typically meteoric recharge, having
infiltrated the ground and been heated by the region’s elevated geothermal gradient. The
temperatures of these fluids at the surface vary, with some springs exhibiting temperatures suitable
for direct-use applications. The chemical composition of the geothermal fluids often includes
dissolved minerals such as sulfate, calcite, and silica, reflecting interactions with the surrounding
rock formations (Priest and Vogt 1983). Understanding the characteristics of these geothermal fluids
is essential for assessing the region’s geothermal energy potential and for the development of these
resources. Fluid chemistry plays a critical role in system design and long-term operation. The
chemical composition of geothermal fluids influences scaling and corrosion risks, which can
significantly impact the performance, maintenance needs, and lifespan of geothermal infrastructure.
In addition, it informs the selection of materials for wells and surface equipment, helps anticipate
environmental considerations such as gas emissions or fluid disposal, and supports decisions about
appropriate energy conversion technologies.

 8

However, the development of these resources requires careful consideration of environmental
impacts, particularly in regions overlapping with protected lands and recreational areas. Ongoing
research and exploration are a requirement to fully assess the viability and sustainability of
geothermal energy production in the central Oregon Cascade Range (Priest and Vogt 1983).

Geothermal Resource Assessment in Oakridge
GEOLOGICAL SETTINGS
The geology of the Oakridge area in Oregon is characterized by a diverse assemblage of volcanic and
sedimentary formations. According to Brown et al. (1980), the region features basalt and andesite,
indicative of its volcanic origins. In addition, sedimentary deposits—including tuffs and breccias—
reflect periods of volcanic activity and subsequent erosion.
The Willamette Pass area is in the rugged mountains immediately east of Oakridge, Oregon. This
area encompasses parts of both the Western Cascades and High Cascades geological provinces,
further highlighting the geological complexity. The western part is dominated by older Oligocene
tuffaceous rocks and Miocene lavas of the Western Cascades, whereas the eastern part is
characterized by Quaternary and late Tertiary age High Cascade lavas as well as Pliocene lavas (see
Figure 8 for more details).

GEOTHERMAL GRADIENT TEMPERATURE AND THERMAL CONDUCTIVITY
The National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) used the Stanford Thermal Model (STM) to
identify temperature gradients and thermal conductivity within the Oakridge region, leveraging its
advanced integration of geological, thermal, and geophysical data for accurate subsurface
predictions (Aljubran and Horne 2024). More details about the model can be found at the Stanford
Thermal Model Website.
The STM is a comprehensive temperature-at-depth model for the conterminous United States,
spanning depths from 0 to 7 km. Developed by Aljubran and Horne at Stanford University (2024),
this model uses a data-driven spatial interpolation algorithm based on physics-informed graph neural

 10

networks. It integrates various physical quantities—including bottomhole temperature, depth,
geographic coordinates, elevation, sediment thickness, magnetic anomaly, gravity anomaly, gamma-
ray flux of radioactive elements, seismicity, and electric conductivity—to predict subsurface
temperature, surface heat flow, and rock thermal conductivity. The model achieves mean absolute
errors of 4.8°C for temperature, 8.1 mW/m² for surface heat flow, and 0.07 W/(°C·m), or Watts per
meter Celsius, for thermal conductivity.
The temperature gradient values predicted by the Stanford Thermal Model for the southern area of
Oakridge town, near Kitson Hot Springs, indicate subsurface temperatures of approximately 71.6°C
(160.9°F) at 1 km depth, 124°C (254.7°F) at 2 km, and 175°C (345°F) at 3 km (see Figures 9 and
10). These findings highlight the region’s low to medium geothermal potential. The corresponding
thermal conductivities at these depths are 1.6 W/(°C·m) at 1 km and 2.0 W/(°C·m) at both 2 km and
3 km depths, which indicates moderate subsurface heat transfer properties. Although these values
may not support high-temperature geothermal power generation, they are well-suited for direct-use
applications such as district heating, greenhouse agriculture, aquaculture, or industrial processes. In
addition, the resource characteristics may be favorable for combined heat and power (CHP) systems,
which generate electricity at lower efficiencies while capturing and using the waste heat for thermal
applications. This integrated approach can significantly improve overall energy efficiency and make
geothermal development more economically viable in low to medium temperature settings.

GEOCHEMISTRY AND GEOTHERMOMETERS OF HOT SPRINGS
NREL used the Powell and Cumming spreadsheet for liquid-phase geothermal systems to estimate
reservoir temperatures (Powell and Cumming 2010). This tool applies various geothermometers,
which use the chemical composition of geothermal waters to estimate subsurface temperature
conditions. By analyzing the major elements and water chemistry parameters such as pH and
surface temperatures, NREL calculated various reservoir temperature estimates. These estimates
are essential for understanding the thermal characteristics of the geothermal system and for
evaluating its development potential.
The chemical data used for this analysis are summarized in Table 1 and are sourced from Brown et
al. (1980). Differences in elemental concentrations—such as silica, sodium, potassium, calcium, and
magnesium—can provide insights into subsurface temperature conditions, reservoir depth, fluid-rock
interactions, and potential scaling or corrosion issues. For example, high silica concentrations may
indicate elevated subsurface temperatures, supporting the potential for electricity generation or CHP
applications. Elevated calcium or carbonate levels can signal a greater risk of scaling, which could
impact the longevity and efficiency of geothermal equipment. Magnesium concentrations are often
used in geothermometry to evaluate equilibrium states and the reliability of temperature estimates;
higher values may suggest fluids have mixed with cooler shallow waters. Chloride is particularly
important because it behaves conservatively in geothermal fluids, meaning it doesn’t readily
precipitate or react with surrounding rock. As such, chloride levels can help identify the origin and
mixing of geothermal fluids. Higher chloride concentrations typically indicate deep, reservoir-derived
fluids with minimal dilution from surface water, suggesting a more productive and thermally mature

 12

system. In contrast, low chloride levels may suggest surface water influence or less developed
geothermal circulation.

The analysis of water chemistry using the Piper diagram (Figure 12) revealed dominant water facies
across most of the sampled hot springs and wells. Water facies refer to the distinct chemical “types”
or signatures of groundwater, based on the relative concentrations of major ions such as calcium,
magnesium, sodium, potassium, bicarbonate, sulfate, and chloride. Most of these waters are
classified as sodium chloride and sulfate (Na-Cl-[SO₄]) types, which are commonly associated with
geothermal systems. This water type reflects significant interaction between geothermal fluids and
the surrounding geological formations. Sodium chloride dominance typically indicates deep
circulation and prolonged contact with rock-forming minerals, which is often a positive indicator for
geothermal development because it suggests a mature, well-established geothermal system with
potentially elevated subsurface temperatures. The presence of sulfate suggests interactions with
more oxidized environments or shallow hydrothermal processes, such as the cooling and oxidation of
volcanic gases or the weathering of sulfide minerals, which may indicate some mixing with surface or
shallow groundwater.

The Na-Cl-[SO₄] composition indicates a transitional system where deep geothermal fluids are mixing
with shallower waters, which can provide important insights into fluid flow pathways, recharge areas,
and reservoir connectivity. From a development perspective, this mixed water type may still support
geothermal applications, especially direct-use or low- to moderate-temperature technologies, but it
also highlights the need for careful resource management. Mixing can influence temperature
stability, increasing scaling or corrosion potential.
The results of the K-Mg-Na diagram indicate all hot spring waters plot within the region of partial
equilibration (Figure 13). In geothermal geochemistry, partial equilibration suggests the fluids have
interacted with reservoir rocks at elevated temperatures but have not fully reached chemical
equilibrium, often because of mixing with cooler waters during their ascent to the surface or
relatively short residence times underground. Among them, McCredie Hot Springs exhibits the
highest estimated reservoir temperatures of approximately 160°C, whereas Kitson Hot Springs
shows reservoir temperatures around 120°C.

STRUCTURAL GEOLOGY AND PERMEABILITY
The geothermal systems in the Willamette Pass area are primarily associated with the Oligocene
volcanic sequence, which comprises highly altered volcanic rocks with generally low natural
permeability. All thermal springs in this region occur in topographic lows and are closely linked to
major lineaments or mapped faults, indicating structural features play a critical role in the circulation
and emergence of geothermal waters (Brown et al. 1980).
Kitson Hot Springs is located along the Eugene-Denio lineament, where east-west and northeast-
trending lineaments intersect. Similarly, McCredie Hot Springs lies near a major north-trending
lineament, whereas the Wall Creek warm springs occur at the intersection of west-northwest and
northeast-trending faults. These structural intersections likely enhance the permeability required for
geothermal fluids to flow and surface (see Figure 14; Brown et al. 1980).

 17

 displaying different magnitudes. Figure by Estefanny Davalos, NREL.

Although the bedrock host material (altered Oligocene volcanic rock) has low permeability, fracturing
along faults and lineaments creates pathways for geothermal water movement. This suggests
fracture permeability is critical for geothermal activity in the region. The enhancement of
permeability through pervasive fracturing may be necessary for the development of geothermal
aquifers, as evidenced by past drilling experiences in the area (Brown et al. 1980).
In this context, geothermal development faces the challenge that natural permeability may be
insufficient in some locations. As a result, accessing or improving fluid flow could require advanced
exploration techniques, targeted drilling near known structural features, or even reservoir stimulation
methods (e.g., hydraulic fracturing or other enhancement strategies). These additional technical
demands can significantly increase both the cost and complexity of development and highlight the
need for detailed subsurface characterization and strategic planning during project design and early-
stage exploration.
GEOTHERMAL LAND MANAGEMENT AND TRANSMISSION LINE
When selecting geothermal plant locations, proximity to key infrastructure is a crucial factor in
determining both the economic and logistical feasibility of a project. For example, McCredie Hot
Springs is approximately 11 km (7 mi) away, whereas Kitson Hot Springs is just 5 km (3 mi) from
potential transmission line connections (Figure 15). Geothermal plants located close to existing
transmission infrastructure (i.e., within 15–30 km) will have lower interconnection costs and energy

 18

losses during power transmission. Geothermal sites situated farther from grid infrastructure may
face significantly higher costs because of the need to construct access roads, substations, and
transmission lines. Building new transmission lines is expensive, with costs ranging from $1 to $8
million per mile, depending on the complexity of the terrain (U.S. EIA 2018).

Access to well-maintained roads streamlines the construction, maintenance, and operational phases
of geothermal projects. Poor access can delay development and increase costs; if the plant requires
water for cooling, proximity to reliable water sources or pipelines will likely increase operational
efficiency.
In addition, geothermal development often intersects with land management policies because
geothermal resources are frequently located in areas managed by various entities—including federal,
state, and private stakeholders. These land management policies may include environmental,
recreational, and commercial considerations. In the Oakridge community, for example, the land
surrounding McCredie Hot Springs and Kitson Hot Springs is managed by a combination of entities,
including private landowners, the U.S. Forest Service, and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Each of
these stakeholders has specific guidelines and objectives that project proponents must consider
during geothermal exploration and development. Federal lands managed by agencies—such as the

 19

U.S. Forest Service—often prioritize conservation, which may limit large-scale infrastructure
development or require additional environmental assessments. Similarly, areas under the
jurisdiction of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers may have specific mandates related to water
resource management and public safety. Private lands, however, may offer more flexibility for
geothermal development but require negotiation and collaboration with landowners to secure
access.
Navigating this complex landscape requires thorough planning and coordination to address land use
restrictions, permitting requirements, and stakeholder concerns. Integrating geothermal projects into
existing land management frameworks can help minimize environmental impacts, support local
economies, and align with broader local goals. In this context, the proximity of geothermal resources
to infrastructure and areas with diverse land management highlights the importance of collaborative
approaches to ensure the successful implementation of geothermal energy projects.

Geothermal Development in Oakridge: Next Steps
GEOTHERMAL EXPLORATION
Developing a geothermal site is a multiphase process that requires careful planning, resource
evaluation, and regulatory compliance. Each phase is described next.
1. Site Identification
The first step in geothermal development involves reviewing existing geological, hydrological, and
geophysical data to locate areas with high geothermal potential. This phase focuses on regions with
active tectonics, volcanic activity, or hydrothermal systems. Tools such as heat flow maps, satellite
imagery, and fault line analysis are often used to narrow down potential sites. This report addresses
a critical aspect of this foundational phase, contributing insights into site identification and resource
evaluation.
2. Surveys
Once a potential site is identified, detailed surveys are conducted to assess the resource further:
 a. Geophysical surveys. These include seismic studies, gravity surveys, and magnetotelluric
 methods to map subsurface structures and heat sources.
 b. Geochemical surveys. These include sampling and analyzing spring water, gas emissions,
 and soil for indicators such as elevated silica, CO₂, or other indicators that suggest
 geothermal activity.
These surveys provide baseline data on the site’s subsurface characteristics and the likelihood of
discovering a geothermal resource suitable for development.
3. Exploratory Drilling
Exploratory wells are drilled to confirm the presence of geothermal resources at depth. This phase is
high-risk and capital-intensive because drilling involves penetrating the Earth’s crust to access
geothermal reservoirs. Core samples, temperature logs, and pressure readings from the wells help
validate the resource’s temperature, flow rate, and chemical composition.
Repurposing oil and gas wells that have significant water coproduction presents a unique
opportunity for geothermal energy development. These wells often tap into deep formations with
elevated temperatures, making them potential candidates for geothermal resource extraction. Using
existing infrastructure can significantly reduce the upfront costs associated with drilling and site
preparation, which are typically among the most expensive aspects of geothermal development.
However, several challenges must be addressed to make this opportunity viable. The well integrity
must be thoroughly assessed because older wells may have degraded casings or cement that could

 20

limit their ability to sustain geothermal operations. Moreover, the water chemistry from these wells
can pose challenges—such as scaling, corrosion, or environmental management issues—which
require specialized treatment technologies.
4. Reservoir Assessment
Once the resource is confirmed, its capacity and sustainability are assessed. Reservoir engineers
use data from exploratory wells to develop models of the geothermal system. They estimate factors
such as the size of the reservoir, temperature gradients, recharge rates, long-term productivity, and
potential decline.
This evaluation determines whether the reservoir can support long-term energy production without
significant depletion or environmental harm.
5. Feasibility and Environmental Studies
This phase combines technical, economic, and environmental analyses to ensure the project is
viable:
 • Economic feasibility. Cost estimation, including drilling, construction, and operational
 expenses, is conducted to assess financial sustainability.
 • Environmental studies. Environmental Impact Assessments ensure compliance with
 regulations and address potential impacts on local ecosystems, water use, and
 greenhouse gas emissions. Community engagement is also critical during this phase to
 address concerns and gain support.
6. Development and Construction
If the project passes the feasibility studies, development begins. This includes the following:
 a. Drilling additional production and injection wells
 b. Constructing surface infrastructure, including pipelines, power plants, and cooling systems
 c. Connecting the geothermal plant to existing transmission lines for grid integration.
Efficient project management is essential during this phase to control costs and minimize
construction delays.
7. Operation and Maintenance
Once the geothermal plant is operational, it begins to produce electricity or direct-use heat.
Continuous monitoring increases the likelihood that the plant operates efficiently. Maintenance
involves periodic well servicing, equipment inspections, and reservoir management to prevent
overexploitation and maintain the balance between production and recharge rates.
Iterative Process and Future Expansion
Throughout these phases, ongoing data collection and analysis support the project’s
implementation. If the reservoir demonstrates surplus capacity, additional phases of expansion may
include drilling new wells or increasing plant capacity. Continuous innovation and adaptive
management increase the likelihood that the geothermal site remains a reliable energy source for
decades.

Opportunities and Challenges for Geothermal Energy
Geothermal energy is a baseload energy source that provides both heat and power, offering
advantages over other renewables. Its high capacity factor allows it to generate 2–4 times more
electricity than wind or solar plants of the same capacity (Figure 16). This makes it a reliable and
efficient solution, particularly for remote communities, where it could replace diesel for heat and
power. Geothermal systems are known for their resilience, reliability, and flexibility, with power plants
having a lifetime of more than 30 years and heating systems lasting 50 years (e.g., Assad et al.
2022).

The report by the Energy Sector Management Assistance Program (Gehringer and Loksha 2012)
highlights that geothermal energy, although requiring significant upfront investment, offers long-term

most significant costs and risks because identifying and confirming geothermal reservoirs involves
technical challenges. However, these risks can be effectively managed through innovative strategies
such as financial support, risk-sharing mechanisms, and public-private partnerships, which help
attract investments and ensure financial stability. Once a geothermal resource is successfully
developed, it provides reliable, low-carbon energy with a long operational lifespan—making it a
valuable solution for power generation.

DOE’s Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Geothermal Heating and Cooling report highlights the

peak electricity demand and improving energy efficiency. Key challenges include high upfront costs,

programs, streamlined permitting, and workforce expansion.

Conclusions and Next Steps
The geothermal resources in the central Oregon Cascade Range—particularly in areas such as
Newberry Volcano, Crater Lake, and Oakridge—represent significant potential for energy production.
These regions exhibit elevated heat flow, indicating a strong geothermal gradient that is suitable for
both electricity generation and direct-use applications. The presence of thermal springs and high
heat flow in areas such as McCredie and Kitson Hot Springs further supports the viability of
geothermal development in the region.
Focusing on the Oakridge study area, the analysis using the Stanford Thermal Model reveals
subsurface temperatures that indicate low to medium geothermal potential. For instance, the
temperature gradients predicted for the southern Oakridge area, near Kitson Hot Springs, show
subsurface temperatures of approximately 72°C (160.9°F) at a depth of 1 km, 124°C (255°F) at 2
km, and 175°C (347°F) at 3 km. These values are consistent with moderate geothermal potential,
with temperatures suitable for combined electricity and direct-use applications, but may be less
suitable for high-temperature electricity generation without further use of EGS technologies. These
findings demonstrate the possibility of exploring geothermal resources in Oakridge for electricity and
direct-use purposes such as district heating and industrial applications.
However, the successful development of geothermal resources in the region must consider several
challenges, including the geological complexity of the area, structural permeability, and potential
environmental impacts. The development of geothermal energy systems is closely tied to the location
of key infrastructure such as transmission lines and access roads. Proximity to this infrastructure

 26

contributes to minimized development costs and increased efficient energy transmission. In addition,
land management considerations—particularly in regions with protected lands and recreational
areas—must be carefully navigated to balance economic development with environmental
conservation.
The following next steps can help the community further understand geothermal potential:
 • Focused exploration. Continue exploration efforts in the Oakridge area, particularly in the
 southern region near Kitson Hot Springs and McCredie Hot Springs. Incorporating
 advanced thermal models, geochemical analysis, and geophysical surveys—such as
 magnetotellurics—will help refine subsurface reservoir target predictions and enhance
 the understanding of geothermal potential. These tools can also assist in identifying
 promising drilling locations, targeting areas with the most favorable conditions for
 geothermal development. This approach will optimize the exploration process and
 increase the likelihood of successful resource discovery.
 • Develop direct-use applications. Given the moderate geothermal gradients identified for
 Oakridge, direct-use applications such as district heating and industrial heating could be
 economically feasible near-term opportunities for geothermal development and
 combined with power generation.
 • Enhance infrastructure connectivity. Evaluate the feasibility of connecting geothermal
 resources in Oakridge to existing transmission infrastructure so potential geothermal
 sites are within 15–30 km (9–12 mi) of power grids.
 • Environmental and land management coordination. Collaborate with federal, state, and
 private landowners to develop geothermal resources while maintaining a balance with
 conservation and public use goals. Areas managed by the U.S. Forest Service and U.S.
 Army Corps of Engineers are subject to those agencies’ land use restrictions and
 regulations, which will require compliance by project developers.
 • Regulatory and permitting framework. Streamline permitting processes by working
 closely with local, state, and federal agencies to create clear guidelines for geothermal
 development, ensuring environmental protection while facilitating economic
 development.
