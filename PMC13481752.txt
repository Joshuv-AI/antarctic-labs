Neutralization of pathogenic PC-OxPL by AAV-delivered scFv as a therapeutic strategy for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Andreia Gomes-Duarte, Jamie K. Wong, Alessandro Moro, Svetlana Pasteuning-Vuhman, Wouter Pos, Rob Haselberg, Marina Sogorb-González, Sofia Pascoal, Linda Borst, Madhurima Chatterjee, Martin Giera, Sotirios Tsimikas, Saud A. Sadiq, Sander van Deventer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disorder defined by progressive motor neuron loss and TDP-43 proteinopathy, yet the upstream drivers of this pathology remain unclear. Oxidized phosphatidylcholines (PC-OxPL) have emerged as potent inducers of proteinopathy in the central nervous system (CNS), but their role in ALS has not been systematically explored. We identify a distinct PC-OxPL signature in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with sporadic ALS (sALS) and show that apolipoprotein E (apoE)–containing particles are the primary PC-OxPL carriers in this compartment. In human iPSC-derived motor neurons, PC-OxPL exposure triggered disease-relevant transcriptional alterations and TDP-43 pathology, establishing PC-OxPL as a mediator of ALS-like neurodegeneration in vitro. To counteract this toxicity, we engineered an Adeno-Associated Virus (AAV)-delivered single-chain antibody fragment (scFv), PC-OxPL-VecTab, targeting PC-OxPL neoepitopes. PC-OxPL-VecTab neutralized PC-OxPL-induced neurotoxicity, reduced TDP-43 aggregation, and prevented motor neuron death and behavioral deficits in a sALS CSF transfer mouse model. Intrathecal delivery of PC-OxPL-VecTab in minipigs achieved broad CNS biodistribution and transgene expression, supporting the feasibility of CNS delivery. These findings position PC-OxPL as a mechanistic contributor to ALS pathogenesis and establish PC-OxPL-VecTab as a therapeutic strategy for ALS with potential broader applicability to disorders associated with PC-OxPL accumulation.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive and ultimately fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by the selective loss of upper and lower motor neurons in the motor cortex, brainstem, and spinal cord.1,2 Most patients die within three to five years of diagnosis, predominantly due to respiratory failure, underscoring the urgent need for effective disease-modifying therapies. While a minority of cases are familial and caused by highly penetrant mutations in genes such as SOD1, C9orf72, or FUS, the majority of patients are classified as having sporadic ALS (sALS), for which the precise etiological triggers remain largely unknown.3

A converging body of evidence supports TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) proteinopathy as the central pathological hallmark in ALS, present in approximately 97% of all cases, including most sALS and non-SOD1/FUS familial forms. TDP-43 pathology is characterized by mislocalization of TDP-43 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, where it forms insoluble aggregates, accompanied by nuclear depletion and loss of its normal functions.4,5 TDP-43 is a multifunctional RNA- and DNA-binding protein involved in chromatin remodeling, RNA splicing and stability, proteostasis, stress granule dynamics, and mitochondrial homeostasis.5,6,7 Disruption of TDP-43 function adversely affects motor neuron health, but despite extensive research, the upstream mechanisms that initiate and sustain TDP-43 pathology in ALS remain incompletely understood, with genetic background, cellular stress, and metabolic alterations proposed as contributing factors.4

Oxidized phosphatidylcholines (PC-OxPL) are conserved danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) generated during lipid peroxidation that act as potent mediators of inflammation, tissue injury, and degeneration.8,9 Their role has been firmly established in cardiovascular, pulmonary, metabolic, and central nervous system (CNS) diseases, and accumulating evidence implicates PC-OxPL in neurodegenerative disorders.10,11 PC-OxPL can act as catalysts of proteinopathy,12,13,14,15,16 and directly activate innate immune pathways.8,9 In Alzheimer disease (AD), PC-OxPL levels increase early and correlate with cognitive decline,17 while ApoE4, the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, impairs neuronal efflux and clearance of PC-OxPL.18 Moreover, PC-OxPL have been associated with tau-19 and amyloid-like phenotypes,14,15,16 and have been shown to drive demyelination, axonal loss, and chronic neurodegeneration in multiple sclerosis models,20,21,22 highlighting their broad pathological potential in the CNS.

Emerging data indicate that PC-OxPL also contributes to the pathophysiology of ALS. Elevated levels of phosphatidylcholines (PCs), precursors of PC-containing oxidized phospholipids, have been detected in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of ALS patients.23 Histopathological analyses reveal accumulation of PC-OxPL in upper motor neurons in ALS,22 and high systemic levels of oxidized low-density lipoproteins (OxLDLs), which are enriched in PC-OxPL, associated with more rapid ALS progression.24 Moreover, TDP-43 has recently been identified as a lipid-binding protein whose RNA recognition motif (RRM) domain interacts with membrane phospholipids, with negatively charged membranes accelerating TDP-43 aggregation.12 Together, these observations suggest that PC-OxPL accumulation is a consistent feature of ALS and may mechanistically intersect with TDP-43 proteinopathy, although the causal pathways and cellular mechanisms remain undefined.

In peripheral tissues, endogenous defense mechanisms partially mitigate the detrimental effects of oxidized phospholipids. Both humans and mice possess natural IgM antibodies that specifically recognize the phosphocholine head group of oxidized, but not native, phospholipids.8 Transgenic overexpression of a single-chain variant of such an antibody (E06) in mice confers robust protection from a spectrum of OxPL-mediated pathologies, including atherosclerosis, hepatic steatosis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, bone loss, and nociceptive pain syndromes.25,26,27,28,29,30,31 In contrast, the CNS is not known to contain these innate antibodies, potentially rendering the brain particularly vulnerable to PC-OxPL accumulation and associated neurotoxicity in neurodegenerative diseases such as ALS.11 This apparent lack of intrinsic humoral neutralization of PC-OxPL within the CNS identifies a critical therapeutic gap and a rationale for targeted strategies to neutralize PC-OxPL in the brain.10

In this study, we investigated the mechanisms by which PC-OxPL contribute to ALS-relevant neurotoxicity and evaluated the therapeutic potential of neutralizing PC-OxPL using an AAV-delivered PC-OxPL-VecTab. We demonstrate that PC-OxPL species accumulate in the CNS of ALS patients, particularly in motor neurons, and identify apolipoprotein E (apoE) as the principal PC-OxPL carrier in the CNS, with apoE levels upregulated in ALS. Using wild-type (WT) and TDP-43M337V motor neurons, we show that PC-OxPL exposure elicits ALS-relevant phenotypes, including the activation of ALS-linked transcriptional programs and induction of pathological TDP-43 aggregation. Notably, PC-OxPL-VecTab effectively neutralizes PC-OxPL toxicity in vitro and prevents sALS CSF-induced pathology in WT mice, including motor neuron death and motor deficits. Biodistribution studies in minipigs further support the translational feasibility of PC-OxPL-VecTab for human use. Collectively, these results support the hypothesis that PC-OxPL contribute to CSF-mediated sALS pathology and suggest that PC-OxPL neutralization warrants further investigation as a therapeutic strategy for ALS.

Results

PC-OxPL accumulates in the diseased ALS brain and spinal cord

TDP-43 pathology is present in 97% of all ALS patients and spreads differentially throughout brain areas with disease progression.32 We assessed the presence of PC-OxPL across different human brain regions in patients with sALS and non-demented controls (NDC) (Table S1). Before characterization of NDC and sALS tissues, PC-OxPL detection using hE06 immunohistochemistry (IHC) was optimized and validated (Figures S1A–S1C), demonstrating the absence of nonspecific secondary binding and a concentration-dependent signal. Evaluation of the spinal cord, frontal cortex, thalamus, hippocampus, and occipital cortex revealed PC-OxPL, primarily within cell bodies, with variable signal intensities across brain areas and individuals (Figure 1A). An overall upward trend in PC-OxPL signal was observed in the spinal cord ventral horn and white matter areas of sALS patients in comparison to NDCs (Figure 1B). We examined the cell-type specificity of PC-OxPL accumulation by quantifying hE06, ChAT (motor neurons), and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, all nuclei) signals in the spinal cord using co-line profiling. This analysis revealed selective PC-OxPL enrichment in ChAT+ neurons within the ventral horn of both NDC and sALS tissue, demonstrated by the co-localization of hE06 with ChAT but not with DAPI (Figure 1C), in selected regions. Next, we profiled the discrete PC-OxPL species associated with ALS. To address this, targeted lipidomics analysis33 was performed on CSF and plasma collected from ALS patients and healthy controls (HCs). We focused on six precursor families (PC) and their corresponding PC-OxPL products. Analysis of CSF collected from ALS patients revealed an increase in 16/18 PC-OxPL-detected species, of which 10/18 were significantly changed (Figure 1D). This distinct pattern was replicated in the profiles of the respective non-oxidized precursor phospholipids relative to HC (Figure 1D). The same species showed an opposite profile in matched ALS plasma, with 12/18 significantly downregulated compared to HC (Figure 1E). PC-OxPL elicits ALS-like phenotypes in WT motor neurons

Building on the identification of a pathological PC-OxPL phenotype in ALS, we next examined the molecular mechanisms engaged by PC-OxPLs in brain cell types implicated in disease. Notably, 16:0/18:1, 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC), is found to be the most abundant PC species in the brain34,35,36 and its oxidation results in two main 16:0/09:0 PC-OxPL species: 1-palmitoyl-2-(9-oxononanayl)-phosphocholine (PONPC), and 1-palmitoyl-2-azelaoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (PAzPC). Hence, we compared the effects of PONPC and PAzPC on WT motor neurons and found that both PC-OxPL species were markedly neurotoxic compared with the non-oxidized PC, 1-hexadecanoyl-2-octadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (PSPC), with PAzPC inducing rapid cell death (Figures S2A and S2B), thereby prompting the selection of PONPC for subsequent in vitro assays.

To characterize early molecular responses to PC-OxPL, we initially conducted targeted transcriptomic analysis of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived motor neurons using the NanoString Neuropathology and Neuroinflammation panels. This strategy provided a focused assessment of disease-relevant pathways, mitigating the masking effects of lipid-toxicity-associated stress responses that can dominate bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets. Of the 770 transcripts, exposure of WT neurons to PONPC led to 173 (neuropathology, NP) and 138 (neuroinflammation, NI) differently expressed (DE) genes, accounting for approximately 25% transcriptome alterations in response to PONPC exposure (Table S4). We used commercially available iPSC-derived motor neurons harboring known ALS-associated mutations to replicate ALS pathophysiology in vitro—TDP-43M337V and SOD1G93A—and compared transcriptome changes in these motor neurons with those of WT motor neurons exposed to PONPC. Overlapping dysregulation in gene expression was observed between the TDP-43M337V and SOD1G93A transcriptomes (NP = 35.08%; NI = 34.35%), as expected, given that these genes are related to ALS pathogenesis (Figure 2A; Table S4). A considerable transcriptome overlap was found between WT motor neurons exposed to PONPC and SOD1G93A motor neurons (NP = 39.69%; NI = 50.31%) and, to a lesser extent, TDP-43M337V motor neurons (NP = 28.48%; NI = 50.31%) (Figure 2A; Table S4). To broaden these targeted findings, we performed unbiased bulk RNA-seq on PONPC-treated WT motor neurons, enabling a comprehensive view of metabolic and lipid-driven transcriptional programs to complement the ALS-like signatures detected by NanoString. In total, 514 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were detected in PONPC-treated motor neurons (270 upregulated, 244 downregulated) from a transcriptome of 43,213 genes (Figure 2B; Table S5), consistent with a robust shift in metabolic transcriptional programs. Gene Ontology (GO) and pathway enrichment analyses demonstrated that PONPC-responsive DEGs were heavily enriched in biological processes related to lipid metabolism, sterol and cholesterol biosynthesis, and regulation of Sterol Regulatory Element-Binding Protein (SREBP)-dependent pathways (Figure 2C; Table S6). These included core metabolic nodes such as the mevalonate pathway, cholesterol homeostasis, and sterol biosynthetic processes, consistent with activation of lipid-regulatory programs. Comparative transcriptomics between PONPC-treated WT motor neurons and referenced ALS datasets37,38,39 revealed a subtle overlap in dysregulated genes (Figure 2D; Table S7). The relevance of this overlap was assessed using pathway-level comparisons, which confirmed a strong shared enrichment for metabolic and lipid-related processes, including phospholipid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthetic pathways, cholesterol metabolic processes, and small-molecule metabolism (Figure 2E; Table S8).

We ultimately addressed the phenotypic consequences of motor neuron exposure to PONPC by focusing on TDP-43 proteinopathy, including aggregation and phosphorylation.4 Notably, PONPC exposure caused marked and clear TDP-43 aggregation both in WT and TDP-43M337V motor neuron-astrocyte co-culture (Figure 2F). The effects were comparable in both iPSC-derived motor neuron lines and more significant than the effect of the commonly used proteasome inhibitor carbobenzoxy-Leu-Leu-leucinal (MG-132) (Figure S3A). No significant alterations in TDP-43 phosphorylation were observed under similar conditions (Figure S3B). Finally, mitochondrial dysfunction is an essential marker of oxidative stress.40 Yet, we found no differences in the mitochondrial phenotypes of motor neurons following PONPC exposure according to selected readouts (Figures S3C and S3D).

In summary, PONPC exposure induced ALS-related lipid-metabolism signatures and robust TDP-43 aggregation in motor neurons, recapitulating key ALS hallmarks.

ApoE is the main carrier of PC-OxPL in the CNS

APOE was among the most DE genes in WT motor neurons in response to PONPC exposure, as determined by NanoString analysis (Figure 3A). This pattern was mirrored by increased protein expression, as shown by a time-dependent increase in the number of apoE + cells (Figure 3B, left) and an increase in average intensity (Figure 3B, right). To better understand the apolipoprotein: PC-OxPL profile in ALS, we analyzed biofluids from ALS patients and the respective PC-OxPL content across different apolipoproteins (apoB, apo(a), apoE, apoC-III, apoA-I). PC-OxPL were predominantly detected in apoE particles in CSF from ALS patients (Figure 3C), and this was replicated in HC biofluids (Figure S3E). A distinct profile was observed in plasma collected from the same patients, with a similar distribution of PC-OxPL across the different apolipoproteins, except for apoA-I (Figure 3C). We further investigated the apoE pattern and profile in ALS. Quantitative and qualitative analyses revealed increased apoE expression in white and gray matter regions of the ALS spinal cord compared with NDC (Figure 3D). Similar to the trend observed in the apolipoprotein: PC-OxPL profile, apoE concentration was significantly increased in the CSF, but not in plasma, of ALS patients compared with HC (Figure 3E). AAV5.2-delivered PC-OxPL-VecTab prevents PONPC-mediated neurotoxicity in WT motor neurons

We developed an AAV5.2-delivered vectorized single-chain antibody fragment (scFv), binding to the PC head group of oxidized phospholipids (PC-OxPL-VecTab) (Figure 4A), as well as an AAV5.2-delivered vector encoding green fluorescent protein (GFP). AAV5.2-GFP successfully transduced ALS-relevant cell types, including motor neurons, astrocytes, and co-cultures, and targeted 40%–60% of all motor neurons at an multiplicities of infection (MOI) of 1E6 (Figure S3F). We characterized the expression profile of AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab and corresponding variants (including HiBiT-tagged constructs) at 14 days post-transduction. All construct variants demonstrated robust vector DNA (vDNA) levels increased proportionally with MOI (1E4 to 1E6), indicating efficient and scalable neuronal transduction (Figure 4B). PC-OxPL-VecTab mRNA similarly rose with MOI, confirming dose-responsive transcriptional activity (Figure 4C). Importantly, vDNA and mRNA levels were tightly correlated (R2 = 0.9191) (Figure 4D), demonstrating a strong linear relationship between neuronal transduction and transgene expression. We next assessed PC-OxPL-VecTab protein expression using a HiBiT-tagged surrogate vector, enabling sensitive and reliable quantification of the encoded protein. At last, we investigated the effect of PC-OxPL-VecTab on TDP-43-related pathology. PC-OxPL-VecTab neutralizes PC-OxPL toxicity in a sALS CSF mouse model

While in vitro evaluation of PC-OxPL-VecTab provides an initial indication of its ability to neutralize PC-OxPL toxicity, assessing its activity in more complex biological models is essential for determining its therapeutic potential. Intrathecal injection with AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab and subsequent sALS CSF led to a normalization of PC-OxPL levels in the spinal cord (Figure 5A). At the same time, it prevented motor impairments, as evidenced by motor deficit scores and grip strength levels comparable to those in saline-injected animals (Figures 5B and 5C). Furthermore, it completely abolished motor neuron death caused by sALS CSF injection, as indicated by the number of ChAT+ motor neurons, which was similar to that in saline-injected mice (Figure 5D; Figure S4A). We assessed whether AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab would also be effective in protecting against the neuroinflammatory changes observed in sALS CSF-injected mice. Despite the mild differences between saline and sALS CSF-injected mice, AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab normalized GFAP and IBA1 expression toward saline levels (Figures S4B and S4C).

Although these results suggested a link between PC-OxPL and CSF-mediated toxicity in ALS, they do not exclude the possibility that sALS CSF toxicity may result from phospholipid oxidation products (e.g., 4-Hydroxynonenal [4-HNE] or Malondialdehyde [MDA])8 rather than direct PC-OxPL toxicity. Hence, we investigated the effect of preincubation of sALS CSF with a purified non-vectorized PC-OxPL-VecTab prior to intrathecal injection. Preincubation of sALS CSF with PC-OxPL-VecTab, but not with Control-VecTab, prevented sALS CSF toxicity, including PC-OxPL accumulation (Figure 5E), motor deficits (Figure 5F), grip strength decline (Figure 5G), and motor neuron degeneration (Figure 5H). Preincubation with PC-OxPL-VecTab also prevented TDP-43 mislocalization induced by sALS CSF treatment; however, the effects were not statistically significant, likely because only a small number of animals were tested (Figure S4D). No differences were found in GFAP/IBA1 expression upon sALS CSF preincubation with either Control- or PC-OxPL-VecTab (Figures S4E and S4F).

Collectively, these results indicate that PC-OxPL contributes to CSF-mediated sALS pathology and that PC-OxPL-VecTab robustly neutralizes this toxic activity.

To investigate the feasibility of intrathecal administration for targeting the spinal cord and motor cortex, we examined the biodistribution and expression of AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab in the CNS of large animals following a single dose. Five WT minipigs were injected with AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab or AAV5.2-GFP via intrathecal administration in the lumbar region of the spinal cord (Figure 6B). After injection, the animals were followed for 8 weeks before necropsy. The biodistribution of AAV5.2 was determined by vDNA and genome copy (gc) counts in different segments of the spinal cord (cervical, thoracic, and lumbar), in the motor cortex, temporal cortex, and occipital cortex. Consistent with the route of injection and CSF flow, high levels of vDNA were detected in the spinal cord, being the highest in the lumbar region, and in all cortical areas (Figure 6C). AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab and GFP mRNA expression levels were measured in the same regions and quantified as fold-change (FC) to the porcine housekeeping gene Hprt1 (Figure 6D). The highest levels of transgene expression (>1 FC to Hprt1) were detected in the lumbar spinal cord. Correlation analysis between vDNA levels and mRNA levels showed a significant positive correlation between transduction levels and transgene expression in both the spinal cord and the brain cortex (Figure 6E). To visualize the distribution of AAV in the brain, GFP immunohistochemical analysis was performed. The motor cortex, particularly large pyramidal neurons in layer V, showed GFP signal (Figure 6F). In the cervical and lumbar spinal cords, a large number of large cells in the ventral horn, potentially motor neurons, were positive for GFP staining. With CNS biodistribution and transgene expression established, we next examined whether intrathecal AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab delivery produced detectable systemic PC-OxPL-VecTab protein levels that could be linked to therapeutic activity. To determine whether a single dose of AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab, previously shown to be therapeutically effective in the sALS CSF mouse model, could generate measurable circulating protein levels, a separate cohort of mice received surrogate HiBiT-tagged AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab construct, enabling quantification of circulating protein levels in plasma (Figure 6G). Four weeks after AAV injection, plasma levels of HiBiT-tagged AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab ranged from 11 to 27 μg/mL (Figure 6H). Collectively, these results show that intrathecal AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab effectively targets ALS-relevant CNS regions in large animals and demonstrates durable transgene expression following intrathecal administration.

Discussion

Growing evidence implicates PC-OxPL as mediators of pathology across diverse diseases, including CNS proteinopathies.10,11 However, their role in sALS has remained poorly defined. Here, we show that PC-OxPL accumulate in ALS-affected motor system regions and directly perturb motor neuron homeostasis by inducing TDP-43 aggregation and activating lipid-repair and stress-response programs. These effects are reversed by AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab, which neutralizes PC-OxPL toxicity in human motor neuron cultures and prevents motor system degeneration in the sALS CSF-transfer model.

A central observation is the selective accumulation of PC-OxPL in motor neurons within vulnerable CNS regions. Signal within axon-like structures suggests that oxidized lipids extend into axonal compartments, consistent with the known propagation of TDP-43 pathology along motor networks.5,32 Together with a CSF (but not plasma) lipidomic signature, these findings support enhanced CNS lipid oxidation and/or insufficient PC-OxPL clearance in disease-vulnerable regions.

PC-OxPL in CSF map predominantly to apoE-containing particles, consistent with apoE’s role as the primary CNS apolipoprotein for lipid redistribution and membrane repair.54,55 ApoE is elevated in ALS spinal cord and CSF and emerged as one of the most strongly induced transcripts after PC-OxPL exposure in human motor neurons. These observations support a model in which apoE upregulation acts as a compensatory response to sequester and off-load oxidized membrane lipids. This aligns with mechanisms in which neuronal stress enhances apoE-dependent lipid efflux toward astrocytes for mitochondrial β-oxidation.56 Nonetheless, impaired clearance or disease-stage-specific constraints may render this response insufficient, particularly in the context of apoE variants that disrupt lipoprotein trafficking. For instance, recent evidence shows that apoE variants, particularly apoE4, disrupt normal apoE-mediated trafficking, thereby impairing PC-OxPL neuronal efflux/clearance.18 The compartmental divergence observed is also relevant as it likely reflects differences in lipoprotein origin and Blood-Brain Barrier (BBB) permeability,55,57 suggesting that elevated CSF PC-OxPL predominantly result from altered CNS metabolism rather than systemic redistribution. This is consistent with the increased susceptibility of the CNS to lipid oxidative damage, driven by its high lipid content, metabolic activity, and relatively limited antioxidant capacity,58 in contrast to peripheral compartments that benefit from more efficient clearance and immune-mediated handling of oxidized lipid species.59 Together, these features support a model of compartment-specific PC-OxPL dynamics, in which CNS accumulation reflects local production and/or insufficient clearance, rather than mirroring plasma levels.

A key contribution of this study is the identification of a mechanistic association between PC-OxPL and ALS-relevant pathological processes. Targeted NanoString profiling showed that PONPC altered NP- and NI-related transcripts while RNA-seq revealed enrichment of lipid-metabolism and sterol-biosynthesis pathways, consistent with attempts to restore membrane integrity following oxidized phospholipid injury.60 Of note, we observed limited gene-level overlap between NanoString and RNA-seq, a previously reported phenomenon reflecting inherent cross-platform differences.61,62,63 These findings support viewing the two approaches as complementary rather than directly comparable. We therefore emphasize the consistent pathway-level convergence, particularly in neuronal-like processes, lipid-metabolism, and membrane-repair programs, as the more reliable indicator of biological coherence, which largely overlapped with reference ALS datasets.37,38,39,64 In line with this, PONPC robustly induced TDP-43 aggregation in both WT and TDP-43M337V motor neuron-astrocyte co-cultures.

Conceptually, PC-OxPL exposure pushes motor neurons toward a lipid-repair and proteotoxic-stress state. The mechanism by which PC-OxPL promote TDP-43 pathology is likely multifactorial. One parsimonious model is that PC-OxPL alter membrane biophysical properties and surface charge, lowering the barrier for aggregation of phospholipid-binding proteins.9,65,66 Prior findings demonstrated that increased membrane anionic charge augments TDP-43 binding to the bilayer interface and facilitates aggregation of disease-associated TDP-43 fragments.12 In parallel, oxidized phospholipids can activate stress responses and inflammatory signaling, indirectly promoting TDP-43 pathology through impaired RNA metabolism, clearance pathways, or stress-granule dynamic.4,5,6,7,67 In ALS, where aging, oxidative stress, NI, and genetic susceptibilities intersect, oxidized lipid-driven metabolic strain may lower the threshold for TDP-43 dysfunction and neuron loss.4,40,68,69,70,71 Future studies should delineate the temporal sequence linking PC-OxPL accumulation to proteostasis defects, metabolic collapse, axonal transport failure, and synaptic dysfunction, and determine whether intervention timing influences reversibility. Prior work supports ferroptosis as biologically plausible in ALS,72,73 but our findings align more strongly with PC-OxPL as downstream products of lipid oxidative stress rather than a dominant ferroptosis signature. As a practical mechanistic follow-up, oxylipin profiling,74 together with GPX4/ACSL4/lipid peroxidation readout, could provide an activity-proximal test of which lipid-oxidation pathways associated with PC-OxPL engage ferroptotic vulnerability, and vice versa. In PONPC-exposed motor neurons, AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab expression overlapped with gene programs linked to cellular structure, biological regulation, and protein binding, consistent with its ability to prevent TDP-43 proteinopathy, likely through stabilizing TDP-43 RRM interactions with the lipid bilayer.12 Concurrently, AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab restored PONPC-sensitive transcripts, including those involved in pathways shared with PD and AD, reinforcing PC-OxPL as mediators of NI and neurodegeneration beyond TDP-43 pathology.

To connect mechanism to patient-relevant toxicity, we used the sALS CSF transfer mouse model, where intrathecal delivery of sALS CSF induces motor deficits and motor neuron loss, whereas CSF from familial ALS cases does not elicit this phenotype in foundational studies.53 In this model, AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab protected against motor dysfunction and motor neuron degeneration, consistent with neutralization of a toxic CSF component. Ex vivo neutralization strengthened these findings, where pre-incubating sALS CSF with purified PC-OxPL-VecTab, but not control scFv, eliminated motor neuron loss and motor deficits. Because PC-OxPL-VecTab selectively recognizes the neoepitope generated by phospholipid oxidation, while sparing non-phospholipid oxidized lipids, oxidized fatty acids, and secondary aldehydes such as 4-HNE and MDA8; these findings support a model in which CSF-borne PC-OxPL, elevated in ALS, function as active neurotoxic agents. These findings align with in vivo reports that specific PC-OxPL species induce spinal-cord pathology,20 supporting their capacity to drive neurodegeneration. Increasing cohort diversity and comparing ALS PC-OxPL profiles with those of other neuroinflammatory or neurodegenerative conditions may clarify whether distinct ALS subtypes or ALS itself display characteristic PC-OxPL fingerprints.

Prior observations that apoB mediates sALS pathology spread may at first seem at odds with the apoE-centric effects.53 This discrepancy may reflect challenges in replicating apoE-PC-OxPL interactions under non-physiological conditions, attenuated but not absent neurotoxicity of other CSF-present apolipoproteins, or apolipoprotein-selective depletion. Although our models capture PC-OxPL accumulation in ALS-relevant contexts, the ALS-CSF transfer model represents an acute, patient-derived neurotoxicity paradigm rather than the full spectrum of ALS pathogenesis. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted in the context of this model and do not establish that PC-OxPL are universal drivers of disease progression across all forms of ALS. Future studies in complementary model systems will be required to define the contribution of PC-OxPL throughout disease initiation and progression. Nevertheless, the present study integrates evidence from patient-derived biofluids, mechanistic in vitro models, and supportive in vivo findings, providing a multi-level framework supporting a contributory role for PC-OxPL in ALS pathology.64

The present large-animal study was designed primarily to evaluate CNS biodistribution and transgene expression following intrathecal administration. GFP expression was observed in upper and lower motor neurons in minipigs and circulating surrogate PC-OxPL-VecTab detected in mice, with CSF concentrations of up to 800 ng/mL (unpublished data), and measurable plasma levels, supporting effective secretion and CNS bioavailability. Due to the inherent challenges of CSF sampling, quantifiable CSF levels were obtained in a limited number of mice (n = 2), yet, these data provide proof-of-concept evidence of CNS expression following vector administration. Plasma concentrations were comparable to those achieved with an AAV8-delivered E06-scFv for hepatic PC-OxPL targeting,28 suggesting that AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab may potentially reach levels consistent with functional engagement of extracellular PC-OxPL. This distribution may be particularly relevant for patient subsets in which peripheral PC-OxPL dysregulation contributes to disease, as suggested by early dyslipidaemia in SOD1-associated ALS.64 In summary, the minipig data demonstrate robust CNS transduction across relevant regions, while the mouse data provides orthogonal evidence of CSF and systemic exposure.

Collectively, the present study identifies PC-OxPL as candidate contributors linking oxidative lipid stress to TDP-43 dysfunction, metabolic strain, and motor neuron vulnerability. By demonstrating that targeted PC-OxPL neutralization restores ALS-relevant cellular phenotypes and interrupts toxicity in the ALS-CSF transfer paradigm, we provide a foundation for PC-OxPL interception as a therapeutic strategy in sALS. Large-animal biodistribution supports the translational potential of PC-OxPL-VecTab and highlights opportunities to extend PC-OxPL-targeted strategies across neurodegenerative conditions marked by PC-OxPL-driven pathology.

Methods

Human samples and tissue characterization

Sample collection

Details on sample selection, demographic data, and disease characteristics are provided in Table S1.

IHC and imaging of human tissue samples

Formalin-Fixed Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) tissue samples were obtained from the Netherlands Brain Bank (NBB). The procedures have been approved by the ethics committee of the VU University Medical Center (Amsterdam, the Netherlands), where all autopsies take place. FFPE tissues were sectioned with a Leica RM2255 Rotary Microtome. Samples were trimmed until the entire section profile was visible. Sectioning continued until 30 consecutive 7 μm sections were obtained.

For deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were incubated in Tissue Clear for 10 min. Then, sections were incubated in Tissue Clear/100% Ethanol (ETOH) (1:1) for 5 min, 100% ETOH for 2 min, 96% ETOH for 2 min, 70% ETOH for 2 min, and 50% ETOH for 2 min. Finally, the sections were washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min and incubated in ddH2O for 2 min.

A Tris-based buffer was prepared by adding 2.5 mL of Antigen Unmasking Solution (Tris-based) to 250 mL of ddH2O and adjusting the pH to 9.0. Slides were incubated in a pressure cooker at 95°C for 40 min and then cooled for 20 min. Sections were washed twice in PBS for 3 min.

Sections were encircled with a grease pen, and slides were sorted into a damp chamber. Sections were incubated with 1× TrueBlack (Biotium, cat. No. 23007) in 70% ETOH for 30 s (s) (100–150 μL per section) and washed twice for five min in PBS. Sections were blocked for 30 min with 10% donkey-blocking serum solution at room temperature (RT). The blocking buffer was washed away, and sections were incubated overnight at RT with primary antibodies. Sections were washed three times for 5 min in PBS and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Sections were washed twice for 5 min in PBS, followed by a 20 min staining with a DAPI solution. Sections were washed twice for five min in ddH2O and finally embedded in Mowiol (stored in a briefcase). Antibody details and experimental conditions are listed in detail in Table S2.

Image processing and analysis

Images were initially obtained in.czi format. For image export, the original 16-bit format was converted to 8-bit. Images were processed using the following software: Zen Blue Edition (v.3.1 or higher), Image Pro Premier (v.9.3 or higher), and Fiji/ImageJ (v.1.54f).

Quantitative analysis of PC-OxPL and apoE staining was performed using infrared (IR) area (%), which represents the proportion of the region of interest (ROI) occupied by positively stained objects, and mean signal (lum), which represents the average pixel intensity of all detected objects. The line profile was generated in Zen Lite using distance and intensity measurements for the channels of interest.

apoE ELISA

Samples were assayed using the RayBio Human apoE enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit. The assay was run following the manufacturer’s instructions; kit lot 0301240061 was used for the analysis. Samples were diluted at 1:3,000 and 1:500 for plasma and CSF, respectively. In brief, after incubation with apoE standard or plasma/CSF samples, the wells were sequentially treated with biotinylated anti-apoE, streptavidin, and the 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) One-step substrate reagent, with buffer washes between each step. After adding the TMB substrate, the plate was incubated for 30 min at RT under gentle shaking (450 rpm), after which 50 μL stop solution was added. Signals were acquired after 5 min by using the EnVision Reader at 450 nm. To determine apoE concentrations, standard curve signals were log-log transformed and fitted to a 5-parameter logistic model using GraphPad Prism v.9.5.1. The back-calculated analyte amount was adjusted for the dilution factor to determine its concentration in the CSF samples. Relative apoE amount in ALS samples was calculated as a percentage of the average amount measured in HC (defined as 100%).

Quantitation of PC-OxPL in human CSF on lipoproteins

Established and novel chemiluminescent enzyme-linked assays were used to quantify PC-OxPL on apoB-100 (apoB), apo(a), apoE, apoC-III, and apoA-I in plasma. OxPL-apoB, OxPL-apo(a), and OxPL-apoA-I have been validated and previously described in detail.27,75,76 Briefly, antibodies MB47 binding to apoB, LPA4 binding to apo(a), and sheep anti-human apoA-I (The Binding Site, Birmingham, UK) are plated overnight to microtiter well plates, plasma is added at a 1:50 dilution, and OxPL-apoB and OxPL-apo(a) are detected by b-E06 IgM and measured by chemiluminescence as relative light units (RLUs) per 100 milliseconds. A standard curve of PC equivalents using a linear range of PC-BSA is then used to convert RLU to nmol/L PC-OxPL, which is additionally adjusted for the plasma dilution. To detect OxPL-apoE and OxPL-apoC-III, microtiter well plates were coated with polyclonal rabbit anti-human apoE (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) that recognizes all three apoE isoforms at 5 μg/mL and a rabbit monoclonal antibody to apoC-III (Abgent, San Diego, CA, USA) at 2 μg/mL after documenting that these concentrations were saturating. Plasma was added at a 1:50 dilution, and OxPL-apoE and OxPL-apoC-III were detected and reported in nmol/L as discussed earlier. For CSF, because the analyte concentrations are lower, a 1:10 dilution of CSF was used, and OxPL-apoB, OxPL-apo(a), OxPL-apoE, OxPL-apoC-III, and OxPL-apoA-I were measured and reported after appropriate dilution, in a similar fashion to the plasma assays.

Targeted lipidomics analysis of human CSF and plasma

A targeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) assay covering 31 PC-OxPL species and their precursors33 was used to quantify their levels in the plasma of ALS patients and HC. The complete list of lipid species monitored and detected is provided in Table S3. In short, lipids were extracted using a modified methyl tert-butyl ether-based protocol from 25 μL of CSF or 25 μL of plasma after the addition of 20 μL of an internal standard mix (100 ng/mL 1,2-Dinonanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine [DNPC] and d5-PC 17:0/22:4 in methanol). After drying and reconstitution, samples were analyzed on a Shimadzu Nexera LC40 system coupled to a Sciex 6500+ QTrap mass spectrometer, using the following settings. Lipid separation was achieved using a Kinetex C18 100 Å LC Column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) kept at 50°C. Mobile phase A consisted of acetonitrile: water (50:50, v/v), and mobile phase B was 2-propanol:acetonitrile: water (85:10:5, v/v/v), both containing 5 mM ammonium formate and 0.1% formic acid delivered at a flow rate of 400 μL/min. The gradient elution was 10% B at 0.0 min, 25% B at 2.0 min, 30% B at 3.5 min, 75% B at 5.0 min, 77% B at 8.5 min, 100% B at 9.0 min, 100% B at 10.0 min, and reverting to 10% B at 10.5 min. Peak area ratios of lipids to the internal standards (ISs) were used for further analysis. The Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) transitions are detailed in Table S3. Other mass spectrometry (MS) parameters include curtain gas 26 psi, ion source gas 1 and 2; 40 and 30 psi, “low” collision gas, an ion spray voltage of 4,500 V, a source temperature of 450°C, and an entrance, collision cell exit declustering potential of 10, 15, and 100 V, respectively.

In vitro experiments

Human iPSC-derived cultures

Axol MNPs

Healthy human iPSC-derived motor neuron progenitors (MNPs) (ax0078, Axol Bioscience) were purchased and used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, a T25 culture flask (734–2004, VWR) was coated with 0.1 mg/mL Poly-D-Lysine (PDL) (A38904-01-Gibco) for 2 h at 37°C, followed by three times washing with PBS (90–094, Gibco). Next, the culture flask was coated with 20 μg/mL Surebond (ax0041, Axol Bioscience) overnight at 37°C. Cryopreserved MNPs were thawed and resuspended in recovery medium (ax0071, Axol Bioscience) supplemented with 0.1 μM retinoic acid (R2625-50MG, Sigma) and 10 μM Y-27632 (10–2301, Focus Biomolecules). Cells were plated at a density of 1-1.5E5 cells/cm2 in the PDL/Surebond-coated T25 flask and cultured in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C. The day after seeding, the medium was replaced completely with recovery medium without Y-27632. Full medium changes were performed every 2–3 days. At a confluence of 70%, MNPs were passaged and expanded. For the dissociation, cells were incubated with Accutase (A11105-01, Gibco) and monitored until >90% of cells were detached. Cells were seeded in motor neuron maintenance medium completed with 0.2 μM Compound E (ab142164, Abcam), 0.5 μM retinoic acid, 5 ng/mL Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) (78133, STEMCELL Technologies), 10 ng/mL Ciliary Neurotrophic Factor (CNTF) (78170, STEMCELL Technologies), and 10 μM Y-27632 in a 96-well plate (165305, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The next day, the medium was replaced with complete motor neuron maintenance medium without Y-27632, and then half of the medium was replaced every 2–3 days.

Human iPSC-derived motor neurons and astrocytes

Human iPSC-derived cell lines (iCell Motor Neurons AHN, iCell Motor Neurons ALS TDP43, iCell Motor Neurons SOD1, and iCell Astrocytes) were purchased from Fujifilm Cellular Dynamics International (FCDI, Madison, WI, USA). Frozen vials were thawed and seeded according to the manufacturer’s instructions at a recommended density of 100,000 live cells/cm2, or as appropriate considering the designated assay and readout.

PSPC and PC-OxPL treatment

PONPC (870605 P-1MG, Avanti Polar Lipids), PAzPC (670600 P-1MG, Avanti Polar Lipids), and PSPC (850456 P-25MG, Avanti Polar Lipids) were dissolved in 100% Ethanol (EtOH) (437433 T, VWR) and heated to 35°C followed by sonication (37 kHz) for 30–60 s. Fresh PSPC and PONPC solutions were prepared before the start of the experiment and diluted in complete appropriate medium to 2× the final concentration. For treatment, half of the medium was removed and replaced with 2× concentrated PSPC/PONPC medium to achieve the final concentration. Cells were treated with 25 μM of PSPC/PONPC and incubated for the assigned time (24 h or 24 h followed by a 24 h washout, depending on the readout). 0.3% EtOH was used as a vehicle control. For washout experiments, all medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium.

Generation and transduction of AAV5.2 constructs

PC-OxPL-VecTab sequences (i.e., the mouse or humanized scFv sequence) were identical to a previously reported design26,77 with minor modifications, including a different promoter (chicken β-actin hybrid, CBh or VectorY’s proprietary constitutive human-derived promoter, promoter 1) and SV40 poly(A) tail.

AAV generation

AAV5.2 carrying the mouse PC-OxPL-VecTab sequence or the reporter gene GFP was produced in the Baculovirus-insect cell production system. To this end, two recombinant baculoviruses were generated. The first recombinant Baculovirus (Bac-CapRep) carried both the AAV5.2 Capsid (Cap5.2) and AAV2 Replication (Rep) sequence.77 The second recombinant Baculovirus (Bac-Transgene) carried a transgene cassette containing the mouse, humanized PC-OxPL-VecTab or GFP transgenes flanked by the AAV2 inverted terminal repeat (ITR) sequences. Recombinant AAV production was performed by co-infection of Sf9 cells with Bac-CapRep and Bac-Transgene. Following harvest, the AAV vectors were purified using two rounds of CsCl density ultracentrifugation or affinity chromatography. Purified AAV was subsequently buffer exchanged and sterilized by filtration through a 0.2 μm filter. AAV vectors were titrated using Stunner (Unchained Labs).

AAV transduction

Human iPSC-derived cultures were transduced with various AAV constructs at MOI of 1E4, 1E5, and 1E6 viral genomes (vg). Transduction was performed at 7–10 days post-seeding by removing 50% of the culture medium and replacing it with 50% fresh medium containing the AAV vectors. Four hours post-transduction, the culture was replenished with half of the original culture volume. Cultures were subsequently maintained with half-medium changes every 3 days.

Gene expression studies

RNA extraction and quality control

iCell motor neurons were at a cell density of 350,000–700,000/well using a 12-well plate. At the end of the experiment, lysates were collected in QIAzol lysis reagent (79306, QIAGEN). RNA isolation was performed using the miRNeasy Mini kit (217004, QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol for samples containing <1 μg total RNA.

RNA concentrations were determined using NanoDrop ONEC (Thermo Scientific). Quality control analysis was performed using the Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies RNA 6000 Nano) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All the samples analyzed contained high-quality RNA, with an RNA integrity number (RIN) above 8.5 and clear ribosomal RNA (rRNA) 18S/28 S peaks.

DNA extraction

Cell lysates were collected by incubating cells with Accutase and washing them with D-PBS. DNA isolation was performed using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (69504, QIAGEN). DNA concentrations were determined using the NanoDrop ONEC (Thermo Scientific).

nCounter gene expression (NanoString)

Two pre-designed NanoString Neuroscience panels (Assays and Panels for Neuroscience | NanoString) were selected for gene expression analysis: NP and NI.

Samples for the nCounter assay were treated as described in the NanoString Technologies Gene Expression Hybridization Protocol (Gene Expression CodeSet RNA Hybridization Protocol; MAN-10056-05), and 5 μL of sample (20 ng/μL) was used in each hybridization reaction. The entire hybridization reaction was used as input for the nCounter Prep Station and loaded onto an nCounter MAX cartridge. Each nCounter cartridge was read on the NanoString digital analyzer (nCounter MAX/FLEX) with 280 fields of view (FOV). Finally, digital count files were generated for each sample run.

Raw data were analyzed using the nSolver 4.0 Analysis Software. Raw data files were imported into nSolver 4.0. Datasets were processed according to a two-step method: (1) quality control (QC) and (2) Expression changes. All samples were verified regarding QC parameters using Basic nSolver analysis software (101), applying default settings recommended by NanoString for binding density, mRNA content, mRNA positive normalization factor, mRNA content normalization factor, and FOV. Only transcripts with average counts ≥80 reads were considered suitable for use as housekeeping genes. Negative control probe reads were used to define the minimum expression threshold (≥15 reads). Following QC, samples were clustered into the respective experiment groups. Pairwise ratio comparisons were applied to datasets on both panels to compare experimental groups. FC was used as a read-out for each possible combination. Regression analysis (coefficient of determination, R2) was used to analyze independent replicates, which showed R2 ≥ 0.95 across all datasets. Detailed information on the software, including recommended ranges of values for the different QC parameters, creating experiments, normalization, and analyzing data, can be found in the nSolver 4.0 Analysis Software User Manual (NanoString.com).

After processing the datasets, the percentage of transcripts with altered expression following PONPC treatment was calculated based on the initial number of transcripts analyzed. Transcripts exhibiting a FC of ≥10% were considered differentially regulated.

Bulk RNA-seq

Total bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed by Single Cell Discoveries (SCD, Utrecht, the Netherlands) using their standardized bulk RNA-seq workflow. Read quality was evaluated using FastQC and aggregated with MultiQC. Adapter trimming and removal of low-quality reads were performed by SCD using their standard processing pipeline. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38/hg38 human reference genome using STAR, and gene-level read quantification was obtained with featureCounts based on Ensembl annotation (GRCh38.p14). Raw gene-level counts provided by SCD were used as input for downstream analyses.

DGE analysis

Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v.1.50.2) in R (v.4.5). The raw counts matrix was then filtered for sample quality, and genes with fewer than 6 raw counts in at least 3 samples were excluded to reduce noise. Normalization and dispersion estimation were carried out using DESeq2’s internal modeling framework, and the design was optimized to account for the sample variability. To improve effect-size estimation for lowly expressed genes, log2 FC were shrinkage-corrected using lfcShrink with the adaptive shrinkage (ashr) method. Diagnostic plots, including log ratio versus mean average (MA) plots, were generated to assess data quality and ensure appropriate model fit.

GO analysis

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using gProfiler2 (g: Profiler, v.0.2.4; https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler). Significantly upregulated and downregulated genes from the DESeq2 analysis were analyzed independently to capture direction-specific biological processes. The background gene set was defined as all genes tested for differential expression, ensuring compatibility with DESeq2 filtering steps. Enrichment was conducted across multiple gProfiler sources, including GO Biological Process (GO: BP), GO Molecular Function (GO: MF), GO Cellular Component (GO: CC), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome pathways. Results were exported as tables and visualized using dot plots.

To visualize hierarchical relationships among enriched GO terms, the ancestry of each significant term was traced to the highest-level GO categories. The resulting hierarchical tree was converted into a radial layout using the ggplot2 + ggsunburst workflows, with node sizes representing the number of genes annotated to the term.

cDNA synthesis

Single- and double-stranded DNA was digested with the DNase I, RNase-free kit (EN0525, Thermo Scientific). The reaction was carried out by incubation with DNase I at 37°C for 30 min, and the reaction was stopped by incubation at 65°C for 10 min in the presence of EDTA. Reverse transcription was performed with the Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit for quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) (K1641, Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was carried out in a 20 μL reaction volume containing 8 μL of RNA, 2 μL of DNase I, and 10 μL of the Maxima mix. The following cDNA synthesis program was used: (25°C, 10 min), (50°C, 15 min), (85°C, 5 min).

RT-qPCR

A PCR master mix was created by adding the appropriate primers and probe. As an endogenous control for PC-OxPL-VecTab detection, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (4333764 T, Thermo Fisher) was used. The master mix was added to a MicroAmp Fast Optical 0.1 mL 96-Well Reaction Plate (4346907, Thermo Scientific). The qPCR reaction was carried out using the QuantStudio 5 (Thermo Scientific). The following qPCR program was used: hold (95°C, 20 s), denature (95°C, 1 s), repeated 2–3 (×40) and annealing-elongation (60°C, 20 s).

ApoE detection and analysis

Cells were initially fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) (11586711, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min at RT.

After washing with PBS (14190–094, Gibco), cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (M236-10ML, VWR) for 15 min at RT, washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS), and incubated with blocking solution (3% BSA [422371×, VWR]) for 30 min at RT. Cells were incubated with the primary antibody diluted in blocking solution overnight at 4°C, or for one h at RT. After three washes in TBS, cells were incubated with the secondary antibody diluted in blocking solution in the dark at RT for 1 h. Cells were washed twice with TBS and incubated with Hoechst (H3570, Invitrogen) diluted 1:10,000 in TBS in the dark at RT for 10 min. After one wash with TBS, cells were stored at 4°C until imaging. Further details of the antibodies and experimental conditions are provided in Table S2.

Images of the cells were captured using the ImageXpress Pico system (Molecular Devices) with 10×/20×/40× magnification and up to four detection channels (fluorescein isothiocyanate [FITC], Tetramethylrhodamine Isothiocyanate [TRITC], Cy5, and DAPI), depending on the secondary antibodies and dyes used in each experiment.

Analyses were carried out using predefined analysis protocols in the CellReporterXpress Software. The percentage of positive cells and all cell average intensities (shown graphically as “expression”) were quantified by running the cell scoring protocol.

HTRF TDP-43 aggregation assay

The HTRF kit (TDP-43 aggregation kit, Cisbio) was used to measure TDP-43 aggregation, which is defined as insoluble cytoplasmic inclusions, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100,000/20,000 or 125,000/25,000 iCell Motor Neurons/Astrocytes (Fujifilm) were seeded in a co-culture setting on a Cytoview 96- 48-well plate (Axion Biosystems), respectively. Cells were cultured in BrainPhys Neuronal Medium (05790, STEMCELL Technologies), supplemented with and without N-[N-(3,5-Difluorophenacetyl)-L-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine t-butyl ester (γ-secretase inhibitor) (DAPT) (weeks 1, 2, and 3, respectively). AAV transduction was performed on day seven post-cell seeding, and PSPC/PONPC treatment (25 μM) was performed on day 21 for 24 h, followed by 24 h washout. On days 21–22 in vitro, two cellular lysates (wells) were pooled for each condition, and the assay was run with three technical replicates per condition. For each sample, the ratio of acceptor and donor emission signals was calculated as follows: ratio = (Signal 665 nm/Signal 620 nm) × 10E4. The aggregation ratio was calculated for each sample as follows: aggregation ratio = ratio disaggregated sample/ratio control sample.

Animal experiments

sALS CSF mouse model

Experiments were performed as described in Wong et al.,53 with minor modifications. All mice were housed together throughout the study by assigned numerical order (maximum five mice per cage) with a mix of all experimental groups in each cage. Mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups. All observations, experimental procedures, and analysis were conducted by experimenters blinded to group allocation.

Patient demographics and CSF collection

CSF was obtained from one sALS patient diagnosed by board-certified neurologists with a subspeciality interest in ALS. CSF from one SALS patient seen at the Larry G. Gluck Division of ALS Research at the Tisch MS Research Center of New York (Tisch Center) was selected for this study. Information on patient demographics is available elsewhere.53

Institutional review board approval and informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, were obtained before CSF collection. Samples were collected using sterile techniques, either by lumbar puncture or by aspiration through a surgically implanted access port. CSF samples were centrifuged at 200 × g for 15 min to remove cells, confirmed to be free of red blood cell contamination by microscopy, and then stored in aliquots at −80°C.

Intrathecal injection into the cervical subarachnoid space

Adult female C57BL/6 J mice (aged 9 weeks at the time of first surgery) purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Mispro Biotech Services (New York). Before surgery, mice were anesthetized with a ketamine (110 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) cocktail and received subcutaneous (s.c.) injections of 0.1 mg/kg buprenorphine, 2.5 mg/kg Baytril, and 1 mL 0.9% saline. Laminectomies at cervical levels 4 (C4) and 5 (C5) were performed to expose the underlying spinal cord. A 32-gauge Hamilton syringe was inserted underneath the dura mater, and 5 μL of AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab (1.3E12 gc/mouse) or saline were slowly injected into the subarachnoid space. After 4 weeks, a second surgery was performed, and 3 μL of sALS CSF or saline were injected into the subarachnoid space. At least 3 mice were injected per group in each round, and three independent rounds were performed. Independently, a separate cohort of animals was injected with AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab-HiBiT at a dose of 8.5 × 1011 gc/mouse as a surrogate for protein detection and did not receive a second surgery after 4 weeks.

Preincubation of PC-OxPL-VecTab with sALS CSF prior to intrathecal injection

To allow preincubation of sALS CSF, Control-VecTab or PC-OxPL-VecTab were produced at a concentration >0.5 mg/mL. All buffers used were prepared with Versylene (endotoxin-free, sterile) water. Endotoxin was removed from instruments by incubation with 0.1 M NaOH for at least 16 h. HEK293E-253 cells were transfected with endotoxin-free plasmid DNA containing the sequences of interest using the rPEx technology (RPEXBIO). At 6 days post-transfection, conditioned medium containing recombinant protein was harvested by centrifugation, and the samples were stored at 4°C. Using Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC) purification, the recombinant proteins were bound in batch to 0.5 mL Nickel Excel Sepharose for 4–5 h at 20°C. Then, the Nickel Excel Sepharose containing bound protein was harvested by centrifugation and transferred to a gravity-flow column. A-specific bound proteins were removed by washing the column with IMAC buffer A containing 0 mM and 10 mM imidazole. The protein was eluted with IMAC buffer A containing 500 mM imidazole and collected into 2.5 mL fractions. Recombinant protein-containing fractions were pooled. The conditioned medium and the unbound IMAC fraction were analyzed by LabChip capillary electrophoresis. Next, the buffer was exchanged for PBS by desalting using a HiPrep 26/10 desalting column equilibrated in PBS. Recombinant protein-containing fractions were pooled. If necessary, the sample was concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-4 10 kDa spin filter. For formulation purposes, the pool was sterilized by filtration over a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and the product was stored in 0.5 mL aliquots at 4°C. Finally, both scFvs were analyzed by Labchip capillary electrophoresis.

Adult female C57BL/6 J mice (aged 13 weeks at the time of surgery) purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were used. Prior to intrathecal injection, 2 μg of purified recombinant scFv control-VecTab or PC-OxPL-VecTab was mixed with either saline or sALS CSF and kept on ice, then at RT for 10 min, before injection. The intrathecal procedures were performed as described previously. Two to five mice were injected per experimental group, and one round of experiments was performed.

Behavioral testing

Motor deficit score testing: following intrathecal CSF delivery, all mice underwent motor testing at 1 day post-injection (DPI). Forelimb reaching, gripping, and tail flaccidity were evaluated on a three-point scale. Mice were held by their tails above their cage bars and allowed to reach and grip the bars for five trials. Mice displaying no motor deficits were given a score of 0. Any deficits in either reaching or gripping were each given a score of 1. Specifically, inaccurate reach was considered a reaching deficit, and weakness in grip strength or clenched forepaws was scored as a gripping deficit. Tail flaccidity was also given a score of 1. All motor testing was performed blinded with respect to treatment groups.

Grip strength testing: mice were habituated to the grip strength meter (TSE systems) for 3 days prior to surgery. Each mouse was given 1 min to explore the grip strength meter, then held by its tail and allowed to grip the bar with both forelimbs for 5 consecutive trials. After a 30 s rest period, the mice were given another five trials to grip and then returned to their home cage. Baseline grip strength was measured 1 day prior to surgery, and grip strength was also measured at 1 DPI. The mean grip strength force was calculated from five trials. Normalized grip strength values were calculated by dividing the mean grip strength force on the post-injection testing day by the mean baseline grip strength force.

Tissue harvesting: mice were overdosed with ketamine (300 mg/kg) and xylazine (30 mg/kg) and then perfused transcardially with PBS followed by 4% PFA in 0.1 M PBS, pH 7.4. Spinal cords were dissected, postfixed in 4% PFA overnight, and then placed in 30% sucrose overnight for cryoprotection.

Cervical spinal cords were cut half a centimeter rostral and half a centimeter caudal to the injection site. The one cm segments were then embedded and frozen in Tissue Tek (VWR International, PA, USA). Spinal cords were sectioned sagittally at 20 μm thickness using a cryostat (Leica) and then slide-mounted onto Histobond slides (VWR International, PA). The anatomical orientation of tissue sections and the order and position in which they were mounted onto the slides were kept consistent to facilitate unbiased histological comparisons, as described in further detail below.

Mice injected with AAV5.2-PC-OxPL-VecTab-HiBiT were overdosed with ketamine (300 mg/kg) and xylazine (30 mg/kg). Terminal blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture in EDTA tubes and processed to plasma. The tubes were kept on ice, and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 g (4°C) within 30 min from the sample collection. The resulting plasma was collected in a single tube (Screw cap micro tube, 2 mL; Sarstedt, ref. 72.664). The sample was snap-frozen in a 1.5-mL safe-lock Eppendorf tube in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until sent for further analysis.

IHC sALS CSF mouse model: immunostaining was performed on a series of spinal cord sections at 100 μm intervals throughout the cervical spinal cord. Details on the antibodies used and experimental conditions are listed in Table S2.

ChAT, GFAP, and IBA1 detection: slides with spinal cord sections were washed three times in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (PBS/T), then incubated in 10% normal goat serum (NGS) or normal donkey serum (NDS) in PBS/T for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies were diluted in 10% NGS or NDS in PBS/T and incubation occurred overnight at 4°C. After incubation, slides were rinsed three times in PBS and incubated in the appropriate Alexa-Fluor secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) in 10% NGS or NDS in PBS/T for 1.5 h at RT. Slides were rinsed three times in PBS and then counterstained with 1:2,500 DAPI in PBS (Invitrogen) for 5 min. After two final washes in PBS, slides were mounted on slides and then mounted using Fluoromount (Sigma).

PC-OxPL detection: slides with spinal cord sections were washed three times in PBS, then incubated in blocking buffer (10% NGS in PBS/T) for one h at RT. The hE06 protein was used to detect PC-OxPL, which was diluted in blocking buffer and incubated overnight at 4°C. After incubation, slides were rinsed three times in PBS for 10 min and incubated in the appropriate dilution of goat anti-human IgG Alexa 647 (Invitrogen) in blocking buffer for 1.5 h at RT. Slides were rinsed three times in PBS and then counterstained with 1:2,500 DAPI in PBS (Invitrogen) for 5 min. After two final washes in PBS, slides were mounted using Fluoromount (Sigma).

Histological analyses: images were captured at 20× magnification using a Zeiss Axio Imager. Acquisition parameters and exposure times were kept consistent across all antibody stains. To ensure unbiased comparisons between experimental groups, spinal cord images were captured from slides with similar tissue section numbers, and the experimenters verified that the anatomical regions matched. The number of motor neurons and immunostaining intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Three images per mouse were quantified to calculate the mean number of motor neurons. Fluorescence intensities were measured as mean gray values in regions of interest.

HiBiT assay: HiBiT-tagged protein concentrations in plasma and cell culture supernatant were quantified using the Nano-Glo HiBiT Extracellular Detection System (Promega, cat no. N2421). Recombinant HiBiT control protein (Promega, cat no. N3010/N301A) was used to generate a standard curve by serial dilution in PBS or in fresh cell culture media. Mouse plasma samples were diluted in PBS and assayed in duplicate in white 96-well plates (Thermo Scientific, cat no. 136101) alongside standards and negative controls. A standard curve was prepared using a recombinant HiBiT control protein, starting at 300 ng/mL, with serial 3-fold dilutions to generate eight standard points. Standards were prepared in PBS or fresh cell culture media. Plasma samples were analyzed at dilution factors of 100×, 500×, and 1,000×, with each dilution measured in duplicate, and the mean value calculated for further analysis. Three different negative controls were used: reagent buffer (supplied by Promega), dilution matrix control (i.e., PBS), and plasma from vehicle-treated mice (diluted in the same manner as the treated mice’s plasma samples). Cell culture media was not further diluted.

Fresh HiBiT extracellular detection reagent was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For a 100 μl reaction mix per well, 50 μL of reagent buffer, 0.5 μL of LgBiT protein, 1 μL of substrate, and 50 μL of prediluted standard or sample were added. Plates were mixed by orbital shaking at RT and incubated for up to 10 min to allow HiBiT to complement LgBiT and reconstitute active NanoLuc luciferase. Luminescence was measured using a plate luminometer in luminescence mode without wavelength selection, with an integration time of 0.5 s per well.

Background-subtracted luminescence values were used to generate a standard curve using a four-parameter logistic fit, and HiBiT-tagged protein concentrations in samples were interpolated from this curve. For background subtraction, the average of the HiBiT-negative dilution matrix controls was subtracted from standard (STD), samples, and vehicle samples. Assay acceptance criteria included an R2 ≥ 0.99 for the standard curve and intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation ≤15% and ≤20%, respectively.

WT minipigs

Intrathecal delivery of AAV and tissue collection

All experiments were carried out in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of experimental animals and were approved by the State Veterinary. Ten nine-month-old miniature pigs were selected at the Institute of Animal Physiology and Genetics in Libechov (Czech Republic). For intrathecal administration, under deep sedation and after cleaning and disinfection of the skin in the lumbar region of animals, a polyethylene tubing (PE10, 427401, Intramedic Clay Adams) attached to a 5 mL syringe passed through a spinal needle (Spinocan; 4501195–13; 1.1 x 88 mm; B. Braun Melsungen) was used. After tubing insertion into the intrathecal space, approximately 1/2 mL of CSF was collected to ensure precise localization of the tubing. Next, 4 mL of AAV5.2-CBh-GFP (4.5E13 gc/mL) or AAV5.2-CBh-PC-OxPL-VecTab (2.9E13 gc/mL) were injected into five minipigs per group. After administration, all animals in all groups were under observation until awakening. For at least 3 days post-application, animals received daily analgesia to relieve pain.

Eight weeks after treatment, minipigs were euthanized under deep sedation, followed by anesthesia overdosing with Propofol and whole-body PBS perfusion of the animal. Once removed from the skull, the brain was coronally sliced into 3/4-mm blocks from frontal to caudal. The right hemisphere was used for biomolecular analyses, during which 38 brain punches were taken, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80°C. The left hemisphere was fixed overnight in 4% PFA and then transferred to 30% sucrose with sodium azide for sectioning. The spinal cord was separated into the cervical, thoracic, and lumbar segments, and each segment was divided into three pieces. Two were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis, and one was PFA-fixed and transferred to 30% sucrose with sodium azide for IHC.

DNA isolation and vDNA quantification

DNA was isolated from brain tissue punches using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). DNA concentrations were determined using the NanoDrop ONEc (Thermo Scientific). Primers specific to the CBh promoter sequence were used to quantify vector genome copies (gc) by TaqMan qPCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The amount of vDNA was calculated based on a plasmid standard curve. Results were reported as gc/µg genomic DNA.

RNA isolation and transgene mRNA quantification

RNA isolation was performed using the RNeasy Mini kit (74104, QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the RNA was eluted in 30 μL of RNase-free water. RNA concentrations were determined using NanoDrop ONEC (Thermo Scientific).

cDNA synthesis and qPCR were performed using the same protocol as for the cells (as described previously). Real-time PCR amplification was performed with customized TaqMan primers for GFP or PC-OxPL-VecTab expression and with TaqMan primers for Sus scrofa Hypoxanthine Phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (Hprt1) porcine housekeeping gene (Ss03388274_m1, Thermo Fisher). The transgene mRNA expression levels were calculated as FC to ss Hprt1 expression.

GFP IHC and analysis

PFA-fixed brain slices were cut into 30 µm sections (10–15 sections/slice), spread on a microscopic slide, and air-dried. For immunostaining analysis, the sections on glass were antigen-retrieved by treating them in citrate buffer (pH = 6) at 90°C for 30 min. Subsequently, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with a 0.3% hydrogen peroxide-methanol solution for 20 min. The brain sections were immunostained using the rabbit primary antibody anti-GFP (1:1,000), incubated with a biotinylated donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:400, RPN 1004 V; GE Healthcare), followed by an avidin-peroxidase complex (1:400, A3151; Sigma-Aldrich). The avidin-peroxidase complex was visualized by incubation with a solution containing a dissolved 3,30-diaminobenzidine tablet (4170; Kementec Diagnostics). The sections were dehydrated and mounted with DePeX (Sigma). Images were acquired using a histological scanner (VS120-5 Virtual Slide Microscope fluorescence; Olympus).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (v.9.2.0 and v.10.2.3).

Differences in PC-OxPL measured by LC-MS in CSF and plasma were determined using a two-tailed Welch’s t test.

Differences in PC-OxPL content on different apolipoproteins were assessed with a one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test.

Differences in the apoE profile as measured by IHC were evaluated using a two-way ANOVA with Šidák’s multiple comparison test. Normalized apoE concentrations in plasma, and CSF were determined according to a two-tailed Mann-Whitney test.

Differences in TDP-43 aggregation were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s or Dunnett’s multiple comparison test.

For the sALS CSF mouse model, motor deficit scores, normalized grip strength, motor neuron numbers, and standard immunostaining intensities were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc analyses. The sample size (n = 8 animals per group) for the sALS CSF mouse model was determined using a power analysis based on quantitative data from the pioneer study,53 selecting normalized grip strength and the number of ChAT+ motor neurons as the primary endpoints and using the reported means and variances within a two-sample design (α = 0.05; power = 0.80). For each independent in vivo cohort, at least 3 mice were injected per experimental group. Each independent study consisted of two rounds of surgeries, and the data from the three studies were combined.

All values are expressed as mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM), unless stated otherwise. For box-and-whisker plots, the median, interquartile range, and minimum/maximum values are shown. Statistical significance was considered for p values p ≤ 0.05 (∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ∗∗p < 0.01; ∗p < 0.05; ns, p ≥ 0.05).
