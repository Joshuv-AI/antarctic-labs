Defect‐Symmetry Engineered Gyroidal Photonic Crystals Achieve Monotonic Modulation of Bandgap Width With a Stable Mid‐Gap

Shuo Huang, Yang Zhou, Wenxin Ning, Hengliang Wang, Lu Han, Xueyan Feng

Abstract

Three‐dimensional (3D) photonic crystals with a photonic bandgap (PBG) facilitate strong control of light propagation. In many applications, PBG width needs to be systematically manipulated while the system needs to be operated at predetermined wavelengths. Conventional methods for such situations require the coordinated modulation of multiple factors, which complicates practical implementation. Achieving monotonic tuning of the PBG width while maintaining a stable central frequency through single‐parameter modulation remains challenging. Here, inspired by the twin grain boundary discovered in natural gyroidal networks, we construct a series of gyroid‐derived tubular networks incorporating twin mirror symmetry as a periodic design element with different gap distances between the twin mirror planes. Both calculations and experimental validation demonstrated that these structures exhibit monotonic tunability in PBG width with increasing gap distance between the twin mirror planes, while the central frequency of the PBG maintains stable. Electric field analysis indicates that the introduced mirror symmetry with different gap distances changes the distribution of electrical field from discrete mode into continuous mode gradually, contributing to the PBG monotonic phenomenon. This work opens a route for manipulating the spectral position and width of PBG in 3D photonic crystals separately and highlights the potential of structural defect engineering for photonic crystal design and fabrication.
Inspired by the twin grain boundary discovered in natural gyroidal networks, a series of gyroid‐derived photonic crystals are constructed based on defect‐symmetry engineering. The new series of structures enables monotonic tuning of the width of the photonic bandgap (PBG) while the central frequency of the PBG maintains stable. This study provides a new strategy for tailoring the PBG of three‐dimensional photonic crystals.

Introduction

Three‐dimensional (3D) photonic crystals are 3D periodic dielectric media with a photonic bandgap (PBG) []. In photonic crystals possessing a complete PBG, electromagnetic wave propagation within the forbidden frequency range is prohibited in all directions. Such light‐manipulation capability facilitates diverse applications, including high‐performance optical filters, passive radiative cooling, and the realization of photonic quantum computing, etc [, ]. Among the key characteristics of photonic crystals, the spectral position and width of the PBG are fundamentally important, as they determine both the operational frequency and functional bandwidth of the corresponding photonic devices. Tremendous efforts have been made to manipulate these two characteristics of PBG [, , ]. In many applications such as passive radiative cooling [, , ], optical communication [, ], and laser systems [], bandgap width needs to be systematically manipulated while the systems need to be operated at predetermined wavelengths. Conventional methods for such situations require the coordinated modulation of structural type/dimension, dielectric constant, dielectric filling fraction, etc. Such coupling complicates practical implementation. Realizing monotonic control of the PBG width with a stable central frequency through single‐parameter tuning is still challenging.

Structural type, structural size, and dielectric contrast are the key factors influencing the PBGs []. Different structures inherently possess distinct band structures. Such variability intrinsically makes simply changing the structure type is not an ideal way to systematically manipulate the PBG width and position, particularly in the large‐scale design and fabrication of photonic devices. For the same structures, monotonic tuning of the bandgap width at a stable central frequency through single‐parameter modulation is also challenging. Traditional approaches for modifying the PBG width typically induce a shift in its spectral position. Preserving a constant frequency generally necessitates coordinated variations of several structural and material parameters, such as the characteristic dimensions, dielectric filling fraction, and dielectric constant. Therefore, to achieve monotonic control of the bandgap width while remaining at a stable central frequency through single‐parameter tuning, the design and development of new systems is highly demanded.

Since the seminal proposal of the photonic crystal concept by Yablonovitch [], researchers have identified various structures with robust complete PBGs such as woodpile [], diamond [], gyroid [], and inverse opal structures [, , , ], etc. These designs are frequently inspired by natural minerals or biological organisms. Researchers are also exploring innovative perturbations of classical structures, including shifted double gyroid [], non‐affinely transformed gyroids [, ], and even amorphous diamond architectures []. These structural evolutions expand new degrees of freedom for light manipulation and next‐generation photonic materials. Beyond the classical morphologies and corresponding deformations, structural defect engineering, which is based on manipulating the features of structural defects within established structures, is increasingly being involved into structure design []. For example, the utilization of point defects to achieve the spatial localization of electromagnetic modes and the incorporation of line defects to facilitate waveguiding have demonstrated the ability to manipulate material photonic behaviors via structural defect engineering []. The conventional research in this area has mainly focused on the integration of such localized defects to break the symmetries of established and perfect crystalline frameworks [, , , , , ]. However, leveraging the intrinsic symmetry of the structural defects as a periodic design element to generate new photonic crystal structures has not been explored.

The gyroid is one of the most notable morphologies for photonic crystals [, , , , , , , ]. It is a 3D tubular network structure with a considerable PBG. Previous studies have indicated that twin boundaries, which are related to additional mirror symmetry, are frequently encountered during the self‐assembly of gyroid structures in different systems [, , ]. Here, we exploit such twin defect‐associated mirror symmetry as a periodic symmetry element to construct a series of gyroid‐derived tubular network structures with increasing gap distance between the parallel twin mirror planes (named GT1, GT2, and GT3). This defect‐symmetry‐driven design enables decoupled control of PBG width and frequency. Calculations based on the plane‐wave expansion (PWE) method reveal that these structures with the same size, same volume fraction, and same material dielectric contrast exhibit monotonic increases in relative PBG width (9.2%, 13.2%, and 17.9% for GT1, GT2, and GT3, respectively), while the central frequency of the PBG varies by less than 0.85% relatively, remaining nearly unchanged. Such photonic behavior is further experimentally validated by measurement of corresponding photonic crystal devices fabricated by two‐photon lithography (TPL) and atomic layer deposition (ALD). Electric field analysis indicates that the introduced twin mirror symmetry with different gap distances changes the distribution of electrical field from discrete mode into continuous modes gradually, which is the key factor contributing to the PBG behaviors. This work establishes a general framework for decoupled bandgap engineering in 3D photonic crystals and highlights the potential of defect‐symmetry‐derived architectures for photonic crystal design and fabrication.

Results and Discussion

Design of Photonic Crystals

In our previous study, a twin boundary along the (422) lattice plane in a block copolymer assembled double‐gyroid (DG) material was characterized via 3D electron microscopy tomography (Figure ). The detailed morphological and topological features of the twin boundary have been revealed, and the results match well with the twin model constructed based on the level‐set model []. Such twin boundaries provide additional mirror symmetry to the gyroid networks. In the real sample, the twins tend to appear in groups in the form of parallel 2D twin planes. Fabrication of Photonic Crystal Devices

To experimentally validate the photonic crystal phenomena observed above, corresponding infrared photonic crystal devices were fabricated (Figure ). Two‐photon lithography was first employed to print the initial cross‐linked acrylate‐based polymer scaffold. To open a PBG, materials with a high refractive index are necessary. Here, the atomic layer deposition method was used to coat high refractive index materials onto the low refractive index polymer. To ensure the high‐fidelity preservation of the printed structure, which is critical for the resulting optical response, low‐temperature atomic layer deposition for TiO2 deposition was employed. This approach effectively mitigates structural deformation during processing [, , , ], which makes the fabricated structures have a great agreement with the designed models, as shown in Figure , ensuring that the final photonic crystals accurately preserve the intended structural design.

Based on the unit cell parameters of GT1, GT2, GT3 introduced above, with an original single gyroid cubic unit cell length l = 2.50 μm, the unit cell length |a⃗|, |b⃗| were kept identical for GT1 to GT3 (|a⃗|= 2.17 μm, |b⃗|= 3.54 μm), and the unit cell length |c⃗| for GT1, GT2, and GT3 are 2.04, 4.08, 6.12μm, respectively. The polymer scaffold was first prepared (Figure ), and to maintain an identical overall device dimension of the printed polymer devices, the number of fabricated unit cells within the devices for GT1, GT2, and GT3 was 25 × 25 × 36, 25 × 25 × 18, and 25 × 25 × 12 (L × W × H), respectively. Then, a 100 nm TiO2 shell was deposited onto the polymeric scaffold via atomic layer deposition (Figure ). This thickness was thin but strong enough to prevent collapse or cracking during high‐temperature treatments. Subsequently, the boundary surfaces of the printed devices were milled via focused ion beam to expose the interior polymer (Figure ). The sample was then subjected to a controlled thermal treatment in air for calcination of the polymeric scaffold, retaining the TiO2 tubular structure, whose EDS analysis is shown in Figure . This procedure ensures the complete removal of the polymer template (Figure ) while serving as a high‐temperature annealing stage for TiO2, which further enhances its dielectric constant (Figure ). Finally, to increase the volume fraction of high dielectric materials, an additional 100 nm TiO2 was infiltrated into both the interior and outer surfaces. All the devices were fabricated on the same Si substrate (Figure ) to minimize the influence of the substrate in subsequent optical measurements.

The fabricated photonic crystal devices, GT1 to GT3, were characterized under an infrared microscope to collect their infrared spectra (Figure ), and the obtained spectra are shown in Figure . All three devices exhibit high reflectivity at 4.25 μm, with the reflectivity increasing progressively from 60.96% to 66.13% and 69.46%. At different sample rotation angles, the measured reflectance consistently exhibits a progressive increase from GT1 to GT3 (Figure ). The monotonic increase in experimental reflectance had a great agreement with the band structure analysis of the three structures. When electromagnetic waves with frequencies within the PBG propagate through the photonic crystal, they undergo exponential spatial decay. The attenuation rate reaches a maximum as the frequency approaches the PBG center []. Since the fabricated photonic crystals possess a finite size rather than infinite periodicity, a portion of the incident light within the PBG may transmit through the structure. A wider bandgap results in more rapid evanescent decay, yielding superior optical confinement and higher reflectance. To further verify the robustness of this approach and exclude potential fabrication‐induced effects on the measured results, further independent batches of samples were fabricated, which exhibited the same reflectance trend as above (Figure ).

Leveraging this experimental phenomenon, these architectures can be applied in advanced fields such as passive radiative cooling. The monotonic tunability of reflectance within a fixed frequency regime can correspond to high, medium, and low cooling power densities. The fact that both the fabrication scale and the dielectric materials remain unchanged means that the established process flow is compatible across different performance requirements, providing advantages for the streamlined and scalable industrial manufacturing of next‐generation photonic devices.

Mechanism Analysis

The characteristics of PBG are closely related to the distribution of electric‐field energy at different bands. In photonic crystals, the lower frequency modes tend to localize their electric field energy within the high‐dielectric regions. Moreover, owing to the Hermitian nature of the Maxwell eigenvalue problem, the eigenmodes in the upper band remain orthogonal to those in the lower frequency bands []. Therefore, the band below the PBG, usually called the Dielectric Band (DB), concentrates most of its energy within high‐dielectric regions, while the band above the PBG, known as the Air Band (AB), exhibits more energy localization in the low‐dielectric regions. The spatial distribution of the energy‐concentrated regions and the extent of localization have significant influence on the width and spectral position of the PBG [, ]. Here, we calculated the time‐averaged electric‐field energy density of DB of GT1 to GT3 at Γ k‐point (0, 0, 0) to study the mechanism of the PBG phenomenon, which of the other bands shown in Figures . To facilitate comparison of the energy distributions among GT1 to GT3, the normalized electric‐field energy density used is defined as the ratio between local voxel energy value to the maximum voxel energy value within each system, details are provided in the Field analysis section of the Methods. The Γ k‐point was selected because the Bloch phase factor is unity at the Γ k‐point and the electric field distribution directly reflect the global energy profile of the mode.

GT1, GT2, and GT3 exhibit different electric field distribution patterns (Figure ). For the tubular network‐like photonic crystals, multiple interconnected nodes and corresponding struts connecting them provide effective pathways to guide and redistribute the electric field throughout the dielectric network. In the design and fabrication of GT1 to GT3 structures, the additional mirror symmetry changes the gyroidal networks with only f3 nodes into tubular networks with f3 and f4 nodes, as mentioned above. Moreover, due to the mirror symmetry, the orientation of the electric field close to the mirror plane must be parallel to the plane. For GT1, all the nodes are located at the repeated mirror planes, and the electric field mainly converges in the struts near f4 nodes. Meanwhile, the repeated mirror planes are closely spaced and principally identical, making the converging electric field cannot be efficiently spread through the branching pathways (GT1 in Figure ). This leads to a discrete field pattern, which can also be directly reflected in the field energy distribution of the structure (GT1 in Figure ). As for GT2, with more gyroidal features preserved, the electric field can flow from the mirror planes into the gyroidal‐like portion, and the gyroidal f3 nodes can guide the electric field to spread into the network (GT2 in Figure ). But notable, in this situation, there is still restriction region of electric‐field distribution within the structure (GT2 in Figure ). For GT3, it has more gyroidal f3 nodes to guide the electric field into a more uniform and continuous distribution throughout the structure (GT3 in Figure ). GT1, GT2, and GT3 also have different normalized electric‐field energy density value histogram profiles (Figure ). GT1 exhibits a highly nonuniform distribution. GT3 displays a more homogeneous distribution compared with GT1. GT2 shows an intermediate case between GT1 and GT3.

Photonic crystals with more uniformly distributed electric field in the high‐dielectric region generally have wider photonic bandgaps []. As the electric‐field distribution patterns and electric‐field energy density value histogram profiles discussed above show, the distribution of electrical field from GT1 to GT3 changes from a nonuniform and discrete mode into a uniform and continuous mode gradually, resulting in a monotonic increase in the complete PBG width from GT1 to GT3. Meanwhile, the periodic twin mirror features as well as the similar morphology in GT tubular networks lead to analogous electric‐field propagation and similar electric‐field distribution patterns. And the identical volume fraction of networks and material composition minimize variations in the overall dielectric distribution. These factors work together to remain the central frequency of the PBG at a stable region.

Conclusion

The design of structures with different geometry, topology, and symmetry is the key basis for further development of photonic crystals with breakthrough properties. Structural defects, as deviations from the ideal, perfectly periodic structures, could provide a rich origin of new morphologies and symmetries. Here, based on the clear understanding of gyroid twin boundary morphology, we designed and constructed a series of gyroid twin tubular networks. Both calculations and experimental measurements demonstrated that they are good photonic crystal candidates. In addition, for this set of gyroid twin structures, by fine‐tuning the gap between two periodic twin mirror planes, monotonic tunability of the width of the PBG with a stable central frequency can be achieved. This study opens the door to introducing symmetries into classical structures to achieve extraordinary photonic performance based on structural defect engineering.

Methods

Generate Gyroid‐Twin Structure

The original gyroid structure is given by the level‐set equation as below

sin (2πx)*cos (2πy) + sin (2πy)*cos (2πz) + sin (2πz)* cos (2πx) = t.

To generate the epsilon files that the photonic band calculation requires and the stl files for the print, we used Mathematica, where the equation was modified for twinning by the (422) plane of the single gyroid structure. We rotated the structure acos(16) degree around the axis (1, ‐2, 0) first, then acos(225−35) degree around the axis (0, 0, 1) to gain the unit cell of GT1 to GT3. Through the adjustment of t value, the structure epsilon files of different volume fractions can be obtained.

Photonic Band Structure Calculation

The calculation is carried on the open‐source software MPB, with the plane‐wave expansion method (PWE). To get smooth and accurate bands, we interpolate 10 points between the k‐points on the boundary of the irreducible orthorhombic Brillouin zone, while the redundant paths are exhibited in Figure . The number of bands calculated was 20. The resolution was set to 80, which is high enough to get precise calculation results. The mesh size was 3 by default. The calculation results are expressed as normalized frequencies.

Sample Preparation

The polymer scaffold of the samples was prepared through TPL on the Nanoscribe Quantum X platform. The resin used was IP‐DIP2, which is suitable for the micro print set. Moreover, a 2‐photon‐polymerized IP‐DIP2 structure can be completely removed through calcination at 600°C in air. The resin was dripped on the polished silicon substrate, which had been silanized, and the 63x print head was immersed in it for printing. The stl files of GT1 to GT3 were imported in the DeScribe software and sliced at 0.1 μm for the accuracy of the printed structure. The laser power and scan speed were set as 25 mW and 65 000 μm/s. After printing, the sample was submerged into propylene glycol methyl ether acetate (PGMEA) solvent for 10 min, to clean the unpolymerized resin and to develop the 3D‐printed structure. Meanwhile, the sample was irradiated with UV light to accelerate the development of the printed structure and reduce shrinkage during the process. Then the sample was submerged in the isopropanol solvent for 8 min to finish the development and clean the PGMEA with the remaining resin.

The ALD system (Beneq TFS 200) was used to conformally grow TiO2 onto the polymer scaffold at 200°C. The H2O and TiCl4 were used as precursors, both with a pulsing time of 0.15s. After 2000 cycles, a 100nm TiO2 coat can be obtained. Then the boundaries of the structure were milled using FIB (Thermo Scientific Scios 2 DualBeam). The voltage and current of the ion beam were set as 30.00kV and 3.00nA. After exposure of the polymer scaffold, the sample was placed in the tubular furnace (OTF‐1200X, Hefei Kejing), where the temperature was slowly ramped to 600°C in an hour and held for 2 h to remove the polymer completely. Moreover, the tubular structure was treated with the same ALD process to grow another 100nm TiO2 both onto the surface and into the interior. SEM (Thermo Scientific Scios 2 DualBeam) was used to image the samples in the process, with an electron beam whose voltage and current were set to 10.00 kV and 0.80nA.

IR Spectra Acquisition

The reflection spectra were measured on the Fourier Transform infrared spectroscopy (PerkinElmer Spotlight 200i). The cassegrain would focus the infrared light from the source, then irradiated toward the sample with an angle range of 15°–36°, and the reflected light would be collected by an MCT detector. Reflection spectra were acquired from a 30 μm × 30 μm aperture region at the center of the sample. Therefore, the collected spectra were a weighted average of the reflected signal over this angular range. The monotonic bandgap width modulation of GT1 to GT3 is primarily manifested in the complete photonic bandgap. The reflectance spectra acquired in this way can effectively reveal the omnidirectional PBG.
