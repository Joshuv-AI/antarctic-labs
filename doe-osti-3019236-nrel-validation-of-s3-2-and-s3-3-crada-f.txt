  Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

  Original Agreement, Mod 0-1: Under this CRADA, NREL will work with First Solar to validate
  First Solar’s newly developed packaging technology, referred to as S3.2 and S3.3.

  Modification 2: As a follow-up to the accelerated testing done on First Solar’s new module design
  as the first part of this CRADA, NREL will next test the modules outdoors. A small system will be
  grid-tied with an inverter, and the performance will be carefully monitored to detect any evidence
  of module degradation. The period of observation is planned to extend over five years.

  Modification 3: NREL will assist in the investigation of performance shortfalls observed in certain
  products at some of First Solar’s test arrays.

  development of control algorithms and testing of photovoltaic (PV) systems operating with a

  system utilizing NREL’s 1 MW/1MWh battery system and FS-provided 350 kW PV array. The PV
  array will be installed at the NWTC test site and integrated into the NWTC test facility with
  interconnection to the 13.2kV grid and 7 megavolt-amperes (MVA) Controllable Grid Interface
  (CGI). The CRADA also includes work on the joint publication of results achieved from
  development and testing efforts. The consecutive phases 2 and 3 will be focused on analyzing the
  benefits of medium voltage direct current (MVDC) collector systems and economic aspects of
  PV/storage operation, respectively. In addition, expansion of the capacity of the FS-provided PV
  array up to the 1 MW level will also be considered.

  ORIGINAL AGREEMENT , MODIFICATION 0:

  Purpose

  First Solar, the world’s largest manufacturer of thin film photovoltaic modules, is seeking third-
  party validation of their module technology through accelerated lifetime testing. First Solar feels
  NREL would be an ideal partner for this work due to their extensive background, experience, and
  available facilities. The validation is intended to be focused on First Solar’s newly developed
  packaging technology, referred to as S3.2 and S3.3. Aside from validating the technology, results
  from this study may be used to improve safety and performance standards such as IEC 61730 and
  UL 1703. The validation will be done under an agreed- upon CRADA. Publication of results will
  only be done at the consent of First Solar.

  Scope of Work

  The scope of work for S3.2 and S3.3 validation includes cooperative planning, execution, and
  analysis of all recommended and agreed-upon test protocols to determine the suitability of the
  product for long-term deployment of the photovoltaic modules to a broad range of climates. The
  test plan will be established within two months of project initiation, and NREL will write monthly
  reports for the duration of the work.

  S3.2 is defined as a First Solar Series 3 PV module that incorporates a new packaging technology
  to improve robustness in some accelerated lifetime tests.

  S3.3 is defined as a First Solar Series 3 PV module that utilizes S3.2 packaging technology and
  incorporates a reduced isolation distance at the edge of the module. S3.3 should be validated for
  incorporation into 1500V systems.
 3

  The tests will be designed to provide useful information but will not comprise a full reliability
  assessment. For example, module failures may be dominated by glass breakage; this testing will
  not be designed to address this failure mode.

  We anticipate an extensive test program that may be difficult to complete in one year. The
  equipment at NREL is shared between multiple projects; prioritization will be applied when
  chambers are in high demand. NREL will discuss with First Solar when First Solar would prefer to
  pay the higher cost associated with earlier completion of the work.

  (1) Durability of Modules using ASTM E2188-10

  Background:

  Edge-seal testing is not typically relevant to a silicon module with a polymeric back sheet, and,
  historically, PV qualification tests have not included a test specifically designed to test the edge seal.
  However, today’s windows are often made with an edge seal that has challenging requirements, and
  ASTM E2188-10, Standard Test Method for Insulating Glass Unit Performance, was written to test
  the durability of the edge seal used in insulating glass units or windows. The ASTM E2188-10 was,
  thus, chosen to test the edge seal of a thin-film PV module. We note that this test is designed for
  windows, so it does not include pass-fail criteria appropriate for PV modules. This report documents
  the decrease in output and changes observed in imaging.

  Baseline testing was documented in a previous report, but some of the data are included here for
  reference.

  (2) Mocon Moisture Ingress Measurements

  This work included moisture ingress testing of materials with models to estimate the time for water
  to penetrate the package. The computer code and calculations were all sent to First Solar.
  Additionally, a publication was submitted to the peer reviewed Journal, M. D. Kempe, D. L. Nobles,
  L. Postak, and J. A. Calderon, "Moisture ingress prediction in polyisobutylene-based edge seal with
  molecular sieve desiccant," Progress in Photovoltaics: Research and Applications, vol. 26, no. 2, pp.
  93-101, 2018.

  (3) Summary

  In this work, we evaluated the interlayer film and the edge-seal material for their ability to keep
  moisture out of the module package. The interlayer film was found to have a high diffusivity,
  comparable to Energy Ventures Analysis (EVA), but with a significantly reduced solubility. This, in
  addition to not producing acetic acid, would lead one to expect to see reduced corrosion rates;
  however, this was not explicitly tested in this study. By itself, the interlayer film will not significantly
  reduce moisture ingress, requiring the use of a very low permeability edge seal.

  So long as good adhesion is maintained an edge seal width of 1 cm should be able to keep moisture
  out of a package for 25 years in almost any climate.

  Appendix A: Summary of ASTM E2188 test conditions.

  ASTM E2188 “Standard Test Method for Insulating Glass Unit Performance” is a standard used for
  windows that has been shown to differentiate the quality of windows with respect to the duration of
  their service life [1].

  Phase I: 14 days at 60±3°C and 95±5% RH.

  Phase II: 252 cycles of a duration of six hours. Temperature lowered to -29°C and held for 1h,
  returning to ambient over the course of 1h. Then they are exposed to ultraviolet (UV) light using
  fluorescent UV-A lamps were placed 45±20 mm from the sample’s surface using two lamps per
  module. The light intensity of the lamps is held above 10 W/m2 at the lamp surface. For comparison,
  UV-A lamps primarily emit light between 300 and 360 nm with a peak at about 340 nm, but the
  standard solar spectrum (ASTM D173) has 46.1 W/m2 between 300 and 400 nm. The temperature is
  ramped up to 60°C and held for 3 h prior to repeating the cycle.

  Phase III: 28 days at 60±3°C and 95±5% RH.

  For windows, the dew point of moisture in the air space is used as an indicator of performance, but
  for PV modules, we adapted it by looking at IV curves before and after exposure.

  Task 4: Ongoing monitoring and reporting in years 2-5: Quarterly reports

  Executive summary

  In a field with six c-Si systems, two CdTe systems, and one CIGS system, each CIGS and CdTe
  system had a higher performance ratio than every c-Si system. Average performance ratio for the
  two CdTe systems was 90% whereas the average of the six c-Si systems was 84%. Differences in
  temperature coefficient and spectral response are responsible for most of the performance ratio
  difference (3.5% out of 6%). Two of the c-Si systems showed symptoms of cell cracking and
  interconnect failure expected to reduce performance, but no other performance-reducing defects
  were seen in the field. We observed no system-level differences expected to affect performance.
  Part of the remaining disparity in performance between the systems (1% to 2%) is attributed to
  varying gaps between the nameplate rating and the actual STC performance. The remaining
  differences are unexplained and may be due to a variety of differences in module (temperature
  coefficient or degradation), system (wiring or combiner box configuration), or instrumentation
  (current and voltage monitoring or faulty meteorological information).

  Introduction
  In December 2016 we visited a PV array field, owned by First Solar, with six c-Si systems, two
  CdTe systems and one CIGS system. The site was in Homestead, Florida. The systems were all
  approximately 5 kW in capacity and had been installed in 2014. We assessed performance
  differences among the systems using monitoring data, visual inspection and thermal and
  luminescence imaging.

  Differences in array performance
  We calculated the DC performance ratio (PR) for nine systems at the site using the procedure
  described in the appendix and considering conditions when irradiance exceeded 10 W m−2. The
  results are included in Table 1. The average CdTe system had a 0.06 higher PR compared to the
  average c-Si system. The reasons for this difference might include

 1. Different temperature coefficients and operating temperatures
 2. Different spectral responses
 3. More severe degradation in c-Si modules compared to CdTe and CIGS modules
 4. System configuration differences
 5. Different gaps between nameplate and actual STC performance

  Temperature coefficient and operating temperature

  Although operating temperature differences may account for substantial performance differences,
  we concluded that logged module temperature data were not suitable for calculating these
  differences due to instrumentation malfunctions. To quantify performance differences due to
  temperature coefficient, we calculated the temperature-corrected performance ratio using the
  method described in the appendix. Temperature-corrected PR is included in Table 1.

  There remains a 0.05 difference in temperature-corrected PR between the average CdTe system and
  the average c-Si system.

 17

  Spectral response

  The narrower spectral response of CdTe compared to c-Si and CIGS means that certain spectral
  changes can have a large effect on broadband irradiance but a small effect on the output of a CdTe
  system. This is particularly true for changes in precipitable water vapor, which is typically much
  higher in Homestead than the 14.2 mm value used in the standard solar spectrum. Spectrum-
  corrected PR (PRTSC) is included in Table 1 along with the results using only the temperature
  correction (PRTC). Performance differences due to spectrum explain much of the performance gap
  between the c-Si and the CdTe systems.

  There remains a difference of 0.03 between the average CdTe PR and the average c-Si PR that is
  not explained by temperature or spectrum.

  Degradation

  We performed visual, infrared, and outdoor electroluminescence (EL) inspections of selected c-Si
  modules. We also collected outdoor I-V curves on selected modules.

  In the CanadianSolar MaxPower CS6X-P-305 system, we observed visual defects, including long
  cracks (4/9 modules) with corrosion, small cruciform cracks (3/9 modules) with discoloration, and
  grid-line corrosion adjacent to busbars (6/9 modules), shown in Figure 1. EL imaging revealed that
  the long cracks were not yet isolating large portions of the cells (Figure 2). This was confirmed by
  IR thermography, which showed that in sunny conditions no cell strings were being bypassed.
  Outdoor I-V measurements on selected modules did not reveal any detectable problems. We
  cannot quantify the performance loss from the cracks, but we expect it to be small based on the EL
  images. This loss is expected to worsen, however, as the cell metallization bridging the cracks
  fails.

  In the SunEdison/MEMC SE-330BTC-35 system, we observed visible cell cracking (8/10
  modules), shown in Figure 3. EL imaging revealed cracked cells, broken interconnects, and screen
  print defects (Figure 4). Thermal imaging did not show any bypassed cell strings in this system,
  but we expect the cell and interconnect problems in this system to cause a substantial loss of
  performance.

  Comprehensive EL imaging of the SunPower system revealed a total of six cracked cells out of 12
  96-cell modules (Figure 5). The cracks were thoroughly bridged by cell metallization, and we do

 18

  not expect them to be responsible for any measurable performance loss. Comprehensive outdoor I-
  V curve measurements on this system, while not usable to quantify degradation, did not show any
  pathological features indicative of diode failure or major mismatch.

  In the Trina PDG5-255W system, we ruled out major visual defects. A spot check using EL
  showed no major cell cracking or interconnect issues (Figure 6). Outdoor I-V measurements on
  selected modules did not reveal any problems.

  Every c-Si and CIGS system showed visual degradation that we do not anticipate affecting
  performance, such as algae growth on frames, minor permanent soiling at the bottom edges of
  frames, and corroded grounding hardware. No visual defects appeared in the CdTe systems.

  System configuration differences

  An overview of the site, showing the very similar physical deployment for each system, is shown
  in Figure 7. Although each system had slightly different nameplate capacity, this did not lead to
  clipping more than 0.1% of the time. Every system had the same model of inverter. We evaluated
  each combiner box visually and with IR thermography and established that no blown fuses or
  faulty electrical connections were present. Row-to-row shading, when present, affected all systems
  equally. We found no system configuration differences that were expected to cause performance
  differences between systems.

  Nameplate performance

  Some part of the difference in performance not explained by temperature, spectrum, or module
  degradation may be due to differences in module nameplate rating. A nameplate Pmp rating higher
  than the actual Pmp at STC would result in a lower PR.

  First Solar commissioned indoor STC measurements of a subset of modules installed in the
  systems we considered. Although the measurement uncertainty in module solar simulator
  measurements often exceeds 2%, we include them here for discussion purposes. The results,
  summarized in Figure 8, show that some of the c-Si products, including Canadian Solar, Trina, and
  Yingli modules, were indeed rated 1%–2% below actual measured Pmp. However, SunEdison and
  SunPower modules were rated up to 4% above the actual measured Pmp, which should have
  provided a boost to their PR compared to if they had been rated at the measured value.

  The c-Si products were measured after light exposure to eliminate effects of light-induced
  degradation. The thin-film products were measured after the same light exposure as the c-Si
  products, but this exposure was not done according to the manufacturers’ specified procedures for
  flash measurements. Performance measurements made on thin-film products without proper
  preconditioning can give results that have very high uncertainty and are uncorrelated to field
  performance. Therefore, we avoid drawing conclusions from the STC measurements on these
  products.

 Task 2: Controller development and testing for PV/BESS operation

 The aim of this multi-year task is to develop a controller and test various use cases for combined
 PV/BESS operation under both regular and simulated grid conditions. The diagram of the
 envisaged PV plants integration in the existing NWTC electrical and SCADA infrastructure is
 shown in . Many use cases for PV plant providing various types of ancillary services to the grid
 with and without energy storage can be tested in this configuration. The proposed project will be
 performed at a multi-MW scale utilizing NWTC’s existing unique testing capabilities that have
 been developed during recent years and is aimed at demonstrating performance and generating
 data sets to help address important questions and help utilities and system operators fully
 understand the benefits of energy storage for grid operations and the integration of variable solar
 PV generation. Questions that plan to be addressed include:

 27

 • What combined services can PV and BESS technologies provide to the grid for increased
 flexibility?
 • Can the proper grid services be provided at an affordable cost?
 • Do locations of BESS in respect to PV plant locations provide better grid services than
 others?
 • What is the role of BESS in firming the solar power?
 • How to operate energy storage for minimizing PV curtailment and emissions while
 maintaining adequate levels of reliability and reduced cost of energy?
 This collaboration will leverage the unique capabilities of the NWTC test site where the existing
 11 MW of wind and solar PV generation are in proximity and on the same MV electrical bus
 with the 8 MW energy storage testing facility, 7 MVA controlled grid, and multi-MW VFD-
 based dynamometers. The combination of these capabilities in one location creates a convenient
 test bed to demonstrate the multiple use cases for PV and BESS technologies under extremely
 variable resource conditions and controlled grid scenarios.

 The BESS, due to its tremendous range of uses and configurations, may assist PV integration in
 any number of ways by increasing power system flexibility. These uses include (1) matching
 generation to loads through time shifting, PV curtailment reduction, transmission congestion
 management, and elements of power flow control; (2) promoting higher levels of PV
 penetrations by balancing the grid through ancillary services, load-following, ramp limiting, and
 load-levelling; (3) managing forecast uncertainty and short-term variability in PV generation
 through reserves; (4) smoothing output from individual solar plants; and (5) island and microgrid
 applications. In addition, in distribution applications, BESS can play crucial roles in increasing
 the hosting capacities of distribution feeders for large levels of PV penetration by retail energy
 time-shifting, providing voltage support, and improving power quality. This collaboration will
 produce analyzed test data and comprehensive performance evaluation to understand the impacts
 of a combination of PV and BESS for all above uses. This will be the first and one-of-a kind
 demonstration of all types of active and reactive power controls by PV and BESS utilizing
 controlled grid conditions emulated by the 7 MVA power electronic grid simulator.

 a. NREL and FS will develop a controller allowing operation of the PV/BESS system for
 various use cases.
 b. NREL and FS will develop a detailed extended test plan for use case testing and
 demonstration.
 c. Testing of electric energy time-shift (arbitrage) use case: the ability of BESS to time-
 shift the PV-generated energy for different virtual load profile scenarios will be tested
 and demonstrated.
 d. Testing of load-following use case: PV/BESS will operate in a grid-connected mode
 following a simulated utility set point signal at different power levels.
 e. Testing ramp-rate limiting use case: the ability of the combined PV/BESS system to
 provide various power ramp rates during morning and afternoon hours will be tested and
 demonstrated.
 f. Testing of frequency regulation use case: PV/BESS will operate during extended
 periods of time in a grid-connected mode following simulated system operator AGC set
 point to participate in frequency regulation with zero or minimum PV curtailment
 g. Testing of primary frequency controls (PFC) use cases: PV/BESS will operate in a
 CGI- connected mode, providing a governor droop-like response with zero or minimum
 PV curtailment at different droop settings (e.g., 3%, 5%, etc.) under different simulated
 grid contingency scenarios.
 h. Testing fast frequency response (FFR) and synthetic inertial control use cases:
 PV/BESS will operate in a CGI-connected mode, providing FFR and inertia-like
 responses with zero or minimum PV curtailment under different simulated grid
 contingency scenarios.
 i. Testing stacked services: provision of all the above services at relevant time scales and
 transition from one service mode to another by the PV/BESS will be demonstrated.

 30

 j. Testing the virtual Dispatchable PV Plant use case: PV/BESS will operate as a
 dispatchable power plant following the simulated market dispatch while maintaining the

 k. Testing islanded mode use case: the ability of the PV/BESS system to operate in

 l. Testing voltage, reactive power, and power factor control use cases: PV/BESS will
 operate in a grid-simulator connected mode to test combined reactive power-related

 m. Testing fault performance characteristics: PV/BESS will operate in a grid-simulator

 PV/BESS system.
 n. Testing advanced ancillary services: controls will be developed and implemented for a
 combined PV/BESS to provide power system oscillations damping, sub-synchronous

 and other advanced services.

 Task 2.

 As part of First Solar’s effort to reduce manufacturing costs and improve performance, three
 different edge seal vendors were evaluated side by side. One was the current material being used
 in their modules, and the other two were alternatives from competitors. This evaluation was
 conducted in a manner like that conducted as part of the original CRADA [2-4]. This uses
 samples constructed with glass/100 nm Ca/edge seal/glass. These Ca-film samples were then
 exposed to controlled humidity environments and photographed periodically. As water diffuses
 through the edge seal, it reacts with the Ca metal, converting it to CaOH₂, which is transparent,
 giving a visual measurement of moisture ingress. Films were exposed to temperatures of 45°C,
 65°C and 85°C, at humidity levels around 10%, 25%, and 85% using saturated salt solutions of
 LiCl, MgCl₂, KCl or an environmental chamber at the desired set point. For each of the nine test
 conditions used with all three edge seal materials, at least two replicates were used. More if one
 of the samples proved to be bad in the early stages of exposure.

 Edge Seal adhesion:

 First Solar wanted to know if the edge seal material #1 would be able to maintain an adequate
 adhesion level for the expected lifetime of the modules. To make this assessment with reasonable
 confidence in the results, one must either do an exposure with an acceleration factor that is less
 than 10× which translates into a two or three year test, or do an experiment that can be
 extrapolated. For a single point 10× test, one must know how much a given test condition is
 accelerating the loss. Because of these considerations, we decided to run test conditions that
 could be extrapolated to the field use.

 36

 To do this, a customized Atlas Ci4000 Weather-Ometer was used, which is capable of producing
 4 separate conditions of UV light intensity and temperature [5, 6], Figure 3. The absolute
 humidity of the overall chamber is controlled, but the RH will be unique at each sample
 temperature. The lamps used were Xe-arc with a Right Light inner filter and a CIRA quartz outer
 filter to reduce the amount of infrared light, which is compliant with ASTM D7869 [7] to
 provide high fidelity to the outdoor UV solar spectrum. Sample conditions were chosen as
   85°C/10 UV Suns/3% RH, 85°C/5 UV Suns/3% RH, 85°C/1 UV Suns/3% RH,
   85°C/2.5 UV Suns/21% RH, 60°C/10 UV Suns/60% RH, and 65°C/2.5 UV Suns/3% RH. Here
 UV Suns is defined relative to a standard solar spectrum where the total irradiance between
 300 nm and 400 nm is 46.1 W/m² such that a 10-UV Sun exposure has 461 W/m² between
 300 nm and 400 nm. The light intensity was adjusted to this value at the center of the plates, like
 what was done by Kempe et. Al [5].

 A very interesting phenomenon was seen in these samples where the glass piece on the UV-light-
 facing side of the sample twisted under its own weight with no apparent deformation of the edge
 seal Figure 3 and Figure 4B. The PIB used in this study is not crosslinked and does not
 chemically bond to the glass substrates. All the adhesion strength comes from van der Waals or
 ionically derived factors. PIB is a difficult polymer to crosslink because the quaternary carbon in
 the backbone results in more stable radicals, which results in chain scission reactions upon
 exposure to radicals as would be produced with a peroxide- based crosslinker. We suspect that
 on the UV side, small molecule oligomers are formed in response to heat and UV exposure
 which lubricates the twisting of these samples but at a very slow rate. When tested after UV
 exposure, almost every sample failed, leaving a thin film of material on the UV-facing side
 Figure 4A. This would be an expected result if the UV light reduced the overall molecular
 weight of the polymer, which would reduce the tear strength of the polymer. The process by
 which the glass pieces can slide past each other happens on a much longer timeframe than the
 testing time of these samples. In this context, the aging of these samples can be understood as a
 reduction in the average molecular weight of the PIB in the vicinity of the glass on the
 UV-facing side of the samples.

 In this experimental setup, we are systematically varying temperature, UV light intensity, and
 relative humidity. This allows us to independently assess the impact of each of these stressors.
 For humidity, we have a pair of measurements at 85°C/2.5 Suns with 3% RH and 21% RH. Over
 4400 h of exposure, both samples degraded about the same amount, but counterintuitively, the
 21% RH sample had the higher maximum stress throughout the experiment. However, it only
 deviated by a little more than one standard deviation until the end when the ranges of
 measurements overlapped. The differences here are small enough that we concluded that the
 humidity level was not important in extrapolation to the field.

 Encapsulant Permeation

 Because CdTe-based devices are sensitive to moisture, understanding the permeation properties
 of the encapsulant materials is useful for understanding the extent of damage that will be
 incurred if the edge seal or the junction box hole is compromised. To do this, the transient water
 vapor transmission properties were measured for a number of polyolefin elastomer (POE)
 interlayer materials and some others associated with the package [10, 11]. In this method, the
 films were placed in our Mocon Permatran, and dry N₂ was passed over both sides of the film
 until it was completely dry, and no more moisture was detected. Then moisture is introduced into
 the chamber on the bottom side, and the transient permeation profile is fit to the curve to extract
 out the diffusivity (D), solubility (S) and permeability (P) parameters of the films. Then this
 procedure is repeated at temperatures ranging from 5°C up to 85°C, from which the data is fit to
 an Arrhenius model, and the activation energy and perfecter for these processes is determined.
 Some of the materials were found to have different permeation characteristics above and below
 the melt transition. This is unusual to find because it is an indication of significant crystallinity in
 the polymer, which is typically avoided in the choice of interlayer to maximize transmittance.
 But because CdTe is constructed as a superstrate on glass, interlayer transmittance is not
 important.
