 I. INTRODUCTION

 Most temperature measurement methods rely upon the assumption of a single uniform source as part of the
analysis in inferring an observed temperature T from the surface of a material. This is true for the radiometric
and spectroscopic methods we rely upon in shock physics. However, there are obvious cases where this is a poor
assumption that can cause large errors in analysis, such as the measurement of emission from a surface composed of
more than one material, such as a rusted surface of Fe and Fe2 O3 , or a multi-material surface that could be produced
through alloying methods (e.g., mokume gane) or 3D printing. Here we perform an initial examination of the effects of
varied surface properties upon the analysis using idealized test data to constrain when we may have better confidence
in the resulting calculations. We also examine the structures of the fits to test whether, in principle, comparing the
fit with ideal gray bodies would reveal such a structure and enable the researcher to identify when a two-temperature
fitting model should be used instead of a single-T model or a wavelength-dependent emissivity model.

 II. METHODS

  To simplify this problem while providing illustrations of its complexity, we constrained our systematic search to
2000≤ T ≤10000K and used the Planck equation to model spectral radiance L. Mathematica was used to create
radiance curves L[T, λ]test that are the weighted average of two ideal blackbodies (that is, no noise or background) at
temperatures Ta and Tb ,

                                        L[T, λ]test = aL[Ta , λ] + (1 − a)L[Tb , λ],                                  (1)

where Ta > Tb , and a is the fraction of emitting surface at Ta . These curves were then fitted in Mathematica to
find a single temperature Tf it and emissivity ϵf it , as would be performed in a typical analysis. The quality of fit was
compared by taking the ratio of the modeled two-T surface and the fitted graybody to determine the level of data
quality and noise control that would be needed to determine the difference between a two-T surface and a single T
surface, and to assess the conditions under which such a determination would be possible. The fitted results for all
cases were tabulated and are presented in Tables I- VIII.

 III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

 Light emitted by a 2000K blackbody surface in the visible region analyzed here is minimal. Unsurprisingly, it is
insignificant compared to light emitted by higher-T surfaces. As a result, the fitting analysis is dominated by the
higher Ta , as shown in Table I. For 4000K and higher Ta , the fitted ϵ is roughly equal to the area a at Ta , and
Tf it ∼ Ta . Even compared to a 3000K surface that only covers 5% of the surface, a λ-dependent factor of 2-20 in
emission is seen, as shown in Fig. 1, while at higher Ta it can be several orders of magnitude. As a result, the inferred
graybody Tf it is dominated by the higher Ta even at small areas, and the inferred graybody emissivity is roughly
equal to the area at Ta . In the case where we see a high calculated T and low emissivity, it is appropriate to consider
whether the source is a large area emitting at low efficiency, or alternately is composed of hot spots emitting against a
much cooler background. From the data analysis alone this may not be readily distinguishable and a time-dependent
model may be needed. However, it raises the question of whether a spectral method can be applied to resolve the
difference between these two cases.
 To answer this question, we assess the quality of fit through direct comparison as in Fig. 2. A simple visual
assessment is not particularly reliable, however, so we also calculate the ratio of the “true” simulated data to the
fitted data. This “model ratio” of the test curve to the best fitted parameter is shown in Fig. 3 for a combination of a

3000K blackbody (5% coverage) and a 2000K blackbody (95% coverage). This figure shows that the two curves agree
within about 15%, which is a typical noise level, over most of the spectrum from 430-700nm. Thus to distinguish
this type of ill fit would require high quality data from 400-430nm, or improved noise resolution over the rest of
the spectrum. This can pose a particular challenge as signal-to-noise ratios are often poor in the blue region of the
spectrum due to poor quantum efficiency of the detector. This is worsened with 3000K and 2000K surfaces as they
emit very little light in the blue region as well. At these temperatures it is crucial to extend the spectral range
to include the infrared region, where the error in the fitting model becomes more readily apparent. For example,
at 1500nm, the ratio of the “true” data to the graybody model is about a factor of two higher, which is readily
distinguishable even with substantial noise levels. As Ta increases and the light contributed by the hotter surface
dominates, the approximation of fitting the total surface as a low-emissivity, high-T surface improves, especially in
the visible region. This is observed as the model ratio remains closer to 1 over a broader spectral region, as shown
in Figure 4; in the visible region, the ratio shown in Fig. 3 was the most extreme of the fifty cases tested and shown
in Table I. If the IR region is included in the assessment a much larger fraction of curves show detectable deviation,
and the possibility of resolving a two-T surface increases.
 As the minimum temperature Tb increases, the ratio of luminance for Ta and Tb , and their area-dependent ratios for
the surface model, become more similar. This result is shown in Figs. 5- 7 for Tb =3000, 4000, and 5000K respectively.
Note in Fig. 7 the ratio of luminance can be less than 1 if the area of surface at Ta is small. In these cases, the fit is
dominated more by the lower (1 − a)Tb . For the case of Ta =6000K, Tb =5000K, and a=0.05, the modeled graybody
fit of ϵ=0.97 and Tf it =5099K agrees with the “true” modeled data within 2% in the spectral band of 400-2700nm
where we make all of our measurements. Thus this hot spot condition cannot be distinguished from a single graybody
fit; however, the hot spots are only 20% hotter, as opposed to the conditions observed for Tb =2000K. As the hot
regions become even hotter–e.g., for Ta =10000K, Tb =5000K, and a=0.1–ϵf it drops and discrepancies between the
modeled luminance and fitted luminance increase, especially in the IR (about 1.4 in this example; other examples are
shown in Fig. 8). The consistent use of IR detectors, and the addition of IR spectrometers, may be more powerful for
identification of two-T surfaces than data in the visible region alone.
 This result suggests that as the fitted emissivity decreases, we should attempt to assess the curvature of the fitted
graybody and the observed luminance to determine if hot spots are possible. If fitted emissivity remains high, it is
unlikely that hot spots can be detected unless luminance can be measured with extremely high precision or can be
resolved spatially. If the hot spots are close in T then determination from fit quality alone is unlikely to succeed,
and precise spatial measurement is recommended. Additional analysis of the data alone in these cases is unlikely
to yield a conclusive result, especially if noise is present. Therefore, it is crucial to know through careful surface
preparation such as rust removal that a two-T surface is unlikely to exist. If this is not feasible, as in composite or
layered materials, one may carefully characterize the placement of detector probes relative to material variation to
ensure that only one material is being sampled, or to ensure that the amount a and (1 − a) of each material being
sampled is known so that these parameters can be explicitly included in subsequent modeling and analysis. Spatially
resolved thermal imaging should be applied if available to reduce uncertainty and the number of fitting parameters
needed for later analysis, as well as teh maximum error that is likely to exist.
 It is promising that the maximum error between the fitted graybody and the model two-T surface for the cases
tested decreased as Tb increased. For a fixed Tb , the maximum error between the fit and the modeled data was
generally larger as Ta − Tb increased (for an example, see Fig. 8), though there were the occasional exceptions at
Tb =2000 and 3000K. At higher T the fitted graybody showed minor differences from the modeled emission of the
two-T surface, as shown in Fig. 9. However, these differences are unlikely to be resolved unless signal-to-noise is
exceptionally good, which would require substantial improvement to the detectors currently in use.
