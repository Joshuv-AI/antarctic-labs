2. INTRODUCTION

 The purpose of this engineering calculations and analysis report (ECAR) is to summarize
 Department of Energy (DOE) approved hazard evaluation methods and specific parameters to be
 used in qualitative and quantitative accident analysis for both reactor and nonreactor nuclear
 facilities. The Department of Energy-Idaho Operations Office (DOE-ID) has established
 evaluation guidelines (EGs) in correspondence CLN201105, “Department of Energy, Office of
 Nuclear Energy, Idaho Operations Office 2020 Documented Safety Analysis Review and
 Oversight Guidance,”1 for Idaho National Laboratory (INL) reactor and other nuclear facilities.

 These EGs are to be used for acceptance criteria in the hazard evaluation and for selection of
 safety class (SC) and safety significant (SS) structures, systems, and components (SSCs),
 specific administrative controls (SACs), and technical safety requirements (TSRs) in compliance
 with DOE G 423.1-1B, “Implementation Guide for Use in Developing Technical Safety
 Requirements,”2 and DOE-STD-1186-2016, “Specific Administrative Controls.”3 Additional
 guidance relevant to this summary was provided in CLN240813, “Unmitigated Analysis for New
 Department of Energy Reactors for Safety System Classification.”4

 Other applicable DOE and Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) specific guidance that is to
 be used when preparing hazards evaluations for safety analysis reports are identified. They are
 but not limited to; Orders (DOE O), DOE Reports, Standards (STD), Handbooks (HDBK), Code
 of Federal Regulations (CFR), Regulatory Guides (RG) and US Nuclear Regulatory Commission
 Regulation (NUREG) reports.

 The requirements found in DOE-STD-3009-2014, “Preparation of Nonreactor Nuclear Facility
 Documented Safety Analysis,”5 establish specific criteria for the important radiological dose
 consequence. The establishment of DOE-STD-3009 as the mandatory guidance is intended to
 ensure consistent compliance with DOE regulations and technical requirements for nonreactor
 and reactor facilities. There are specific differences between the types of dose consequence
 accident analysis that will be clarified in this document.

 This ECAR also summarizes existing INL technical basis documents that establish the
 methodology and default parameters used for calculating the consequences of a release of
 radionuclides to the atmosphere at INL Hazard Category (HC) 2 Category B Reactors and
 Hazard Category 2 or 3 nuclear facilities. Where DOE-STD-3009-2014 defines a nuclear facility
 as:

 Nuclear facility. A reactor or a nonreactor nuclear facility where an activity is conducted
 for or on behalf of DOE and includes any related area, structure, facility, or activity to the
 extent necessary to ensure proper implementation of the requirements established by 10
 CFR Part 830, “Nuclear Safety Management.”

3. SOFTWARE

 DOE-STD-30095 requires one of the followings options to be used in evaluating atmospheric
 dispersion and the resulting plume dispersion coefficient (X/Q):

 • Option 1: Follow a process based on NRC Regulatory Guide 1.145, “Atmospheric Dispersion
 Models for Potential Accident Consequence Assessments at Nuclear Power Plants.”9

 • Option 2: Use a DOE-approved toolbox code and apply the conservative parameters.

 • Option 3: Use site-specific methods and parameters as defined in a site/facility specific
 DOE-approved modeling protocol.

 Analysis Parameter 1 – Software

 Since 1964, the INL has used the Radiological Safety Analysis Computer (RSAC)10 program as
 the code of record for all of the nuclear facilities. From the earliest years of the National Reactor
 Test Station, RSAC has been used to evaluate accidents that were too complex to be evaluated
 by hand calculations. It has been used to evaluate over 50 reactors, numerous nuclear facilities
 and uncountable experiments.

 RSAC is approved by DOE-ID for calculating the dose consequences of a release of
 radionuclides to the atmosphere at the INL under Option 3.

 CCN244780, “Approval of the Sample Preparation Laboratory (PDSA-423),”11 clarifies DOE-ID
 position on Option 3. It states:

 “Also approved and provided by the SPL submittals is TEV-3543, "INL Nonreactor Nuclear
 Facility Radiological Dose Consequence Modeling Protocol." This dose consequence modeling
 protocol was used for the analysis supporting the PDSA for SPL, and will be used for future
 DSAs prepared using DOE-STD-3009-2014, "Preparation of Nonreactor Nuclear Facility
 Documented Safety Analysis." This protocol represents site specific parameters for the INL, and
 is consistent with modeling protocol using the Radiological Safety Analysis Computer (RSAC)
 Program.”

 Other software that meets the criteria outlined in Options 1 or 2 can be used. Care should be
 taken when comparing results between software packages to understand what the differences in
 the parameters are, such as dispersion coefficients and particle transport effects.

4. HAZARD CATEGORIZATION

 10 CFR 830 Subpart B, “Safety Basis Requirements,”12 NE-STD-1027-2025, “Hazard
 Categorization of DOE Nuclear Facilities,”13 DOE-STD-3009-2014,5 and DOE-STD-1237-2021,
 “Documented Safety Analysis for DOE Reactor Facilities,”14 establish the requirements and
 guidelines for performing hazard and accident analyses and provide a methodology for hazard
 categorization. 10 CFR Part 830, Nuclear Safety Management, Subpart B, Section 830.202,
 Safety Basis, (b)(3), which states that:

 Page 7 of 37

 “In establishing the safety basis for a hazard category 1, 2, or 3 DOE nuclear facility, the
 contractor responsible for the facility must…Categorize the facility consistent with
      DOE-STD-1027-2018.” (NE-STD-1027-2025 supersedes at INL)

 Nuclear facility hazard category is determined by application of NE-STD-1027-2025. Hazard
 categorization is key to determining the appropriate methodology used for accident evaluation.
 NE-STD-1027-2025 is applicable to all DOE-owned or DOE-leased, including the National
 Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA), nuclear facilities and activities located at INL.
 Specifically for reactor facilities there are two sub-categories as defined in 10 CFR 830 Section
 3. They are Category A and B.

 • Hazard Category 1 - Intended for Category A reactors or nonreactor nuclear facilities
 designated by Program Secretarial Officers (PSOs). The Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) is a
 HC-1 Category A reactor and the requirements for HC-1 Category A reactors is not
 addressed in this ECAR.

 Category A Reactor – Those production, test, and research reactors designated by DOE based
 on power level (i.e., design thermal power rating of 20 megawatts steady state and higher),
 potential fission product inventory, and experimental capability. Category A reactors are
 Hazard Category 1 nuclear facilities.

 • Hazard Category 2 – Threshold Quantities (TQs) for HC-2 are based on a 1 rem dose to a
 receptor at just less than 300 meters; using a X/Q value of 1 x 10-4 sec/m3 to generate the
 TQs; simplified release fraction bins; and categorized as HC-2, unless criticality is precluded.

 Category B Reactor – A reactor as defined by 10 CFR Part 830, Section 830.3, that is not a
 Category A Reactor. Category B reactors are Hazard Category 2 nuclear facilities.

 • Hazard Category 3 –TQs are based on a 10 rem dose to a receptor at 30 meters; using a X/Q
 value of 7.2 x 10-2 sec/m3; and comparison of the five exposure pathways and selection of the
 most limiting pathway to establish the TQs. There are no nuclear reactors categorized as
 Hazard Category-3.

 • Hazard Evaluation - A hazard evaluation is an organized effort to identify and analyze the
 significance of hazardous situations associated with a process or activity. Specifically, hazard
 evaluations are used to pinpoint weaknesses in the design and operation of facilities that
 could lead to hazardous material releases, fires, or explosions. These studies provide
 organizations with information to help them improve the safety and manage the risk of their
 operations. DOE-STD-3009-20145 states:

 “The hazard evaluation shall provide (a) an assessment of the facility hazards associated
 with the full scope of planned operations covered by the DSA and (b) the identification of
 controls that can prevent or mitigate these hazards or hazardous conditions. The hazard
 evaluation shall analyze normal operations (e.g., startup, facility activities, shutdown, and
 testing and maintenance configurations) as well as abnormal and accident conditions. In
 addition to the process-related hazards identified during the hazard identification process,

 Page 8 of 37

 the hazard evaluation shall also address natural phenomena and man-made external events
 that can affect the facility.”

 Analysis Parameter 3 – Inherently Safe Fuel

 Category B reactor dose consequence evaluation may credit the fuel design as a passive design
 feature when two conditions are met:

 1. There is a documented agreement between DOE/ID and the reactor designer/design

 be exceeded under design basis conditions.

 And

 2. Testing and manufacturing under a qualified process provides assurance that
 fundamental safety features regarding the properties of the fuel can be met.

6. ATOMOSPHERIC TRANSPORT AND DOSE CONSEQUENCE

 Two sets of input parameters are used to evaluate dose consequence in accordance with the basis
 standards. Those two sets are Meteorological Conditions and Dose Consequence Assessment.
 TEV-3543, “INL Nonreactor Nuclear Facility Radiological Dose Consequence Modeling
 Protocol,”22 as approved by DOE-ID demonstrates adoption of the DOE-STD-3009-2014
 specific guidance for the most relevant parameters for the radiological dose consequence
 modeling protocol: receptor location, atmospheric dispersion, and dose coefficients, as well as,
 technical bases for other derived parameters.

 6.1 Meteorology

 The INL uses a specific set of preapproved atmospheric conditions in DSA dose
 consequence analysis. Meteorologic conditions can be adjusted based on specific
 conditions and events as approved by DOE-ID.

 Additional sources of meteorological data for consideration in the description of airflow
 trajectories from the site may include National Weather Service stations or other
 meteorological programs that are well maintained and well exposed (e.g., other nuclear
 facilities, university and private meteorological programs).

 Regulatory Guide (RG) 1.23, “Meteorological Monitoring Programs for Nuclear Power
 Plants,”23 Revision 1, issued March 2007, includes guidance on the measurement and
 processing of onsite meteorological data for use as input to atmospheric dispersion
 models in support of plant licensing and operation. RG 1.23 describes a suitable onsite
 meteorological measurements program to collect the basic meteorological data needed to:

 6.1.1 Meteorologic Condition Basis

                       Per DOE-STD-3009-2014:

 For the calculation of off-site doses, five years of representative, recent
 meteorological data shall be used as input to the dispersion model. If five years
 of data are not available, justification for using a smaller data set shall be
 provided in the DSA. If representative meteorological data is not available,
 Pasquill stability class F and one meter/second wind speeds may be used for
 radiological dispersion consistent with NRC’s and DOE’s long-standing
 practice.

 Analysis Parameter 5 – Weather Conditions Data Set

 The NOAA/INL Mesonet online data set meets RG 1.23 and provides over 20
 years of continuous quality-controlled meteorological data across the INL and
 is the data set to be used at the INL is approved by DOE-ID.

 Meteorological measurements have been made at the INL since 1950. The
 National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s (NOAA) Air Resources
 Laboratory (with its local office in Idaho Falls) maintains the Mesonet database
 for the INL onsite and offsite meteorological towers.

 The results are summarized by NOAA in EGG-WM-11243, “Air Dispersion
 Parameters for Use in Idaho National Engineering Laboratory Department of
 Energy Safety Analyses.”24 This document establishes the INL meteorological
 monitoring program that complies with RG 1.23. This document provides the
 basis for selecting the appropriate weather class, wind speed, mixing layer depth
 and other dispersion parameters associated with dose consequence modeling.
 The values for the weather conditions approved by DOE-ID are found in
 TEV-354322 and are summarized in this document.

 Other classification schemes may be used to estimate atmospheric stability but
 the use of alternative methods to classify atmospheric stability may require
 modifications to the models described in Regulatory Guides 1.1459 and 1.194.25

 Where:

 RG 1.1459 provides guidance on appropriate dispersion models for estimating
 offsite relative concentrations (χ/Q values) as a function of downwind direction
 and distance (i.e., at the EAB and LPZ) for various short-term periods (up to 30
 days) after an accident.

 • The Exclusion Area Boundary (EAB) radiation receptor is located at the
 closest boundary of the designated exclusion area surrounding a nuclear
 facility, typically defined as the boundary where the licensee has the
 authority to remove personnel and property.

                                                                                           Page 14 of 37

 • The Low Population Zone (LPZ) isn't a single receptor but a defined
 geographical area surrounding a nuclear plant, with its outer boundary
 serving as a critical point for calculating public radiation doses during
 accidents, using receptors located at or beyond this boundary, often
 modeled via atmospheric dispersion codes like RSAC to assess potential
 health impacts.

 And

 RG 1.194, “Atmospheric Relative Concentrations for Control Room
 Radiological Habitability Assessments at Nuclear Power Plants,”25 discusses
 acceptable approaches for estimating short-term (i.e., 2 hours to 30 days
 postaccident) average χ/Q values in the vicinity of buildings at control room
 ventilation air intakes and at other locations of significant air inleakage to the
 control room envelope resulting from postulated accidental radiological airborne
 releases.

 Conditions other than those that are preapproved by DOE will require DOE-ID
 manager approval. Methods for the classification of atmospheric stability, or for
 direct determination of plume spread parameters, should be adequately
 described and substantiated for applicability to the site.

 6.1.6 Elevated vs. Ground Release

 RG 1.194 indicates that the dose associated with an elevated release (release
 from the stack) should not be evaluated unless the stack height is greater than
 2.5 times that of adjacent structures. NUREG-0800, “Standard Review Plan for
 the Review of Safety Analysis Reports for Nuclear Power Plants,”33 contains a
 section (2.3.4) with relevant guidance. Section 2.3.4 states,

 “Most accidental releases can be considered as continuous releases (i.e., on the
 order of several minutes or more). The general Gaussian diffusion model for
 continuous releases is used to evaluate releases on the order of several minutes
 or more. For puff releases, either instantaneous point-source Gaussian diffusion
 equations with a correction for initial source volume or hemispheric cloud
 configuration assumptions with the cloud traveling slowly downwind (e.g., at
 approximately 1 m/sec) can be used. Other modifications to the atmospheric
 dispersion model which should be considered include restrictions to horizontal
 or vertical plume spread (e.g., by narrow deep valleys, channeling of airflow,
 and by persistent low-level temperature inversions). Fumigation conditions
 should be considered for elevated releases to the receptor.”

 6.1.7 Mixing depth and Air Density

 The atmospheric mixed layer is a zone having nearly constant potential
 temperature and specific humidity with height above ground level. In the case of
 plume diffusion it acts a ceiling and reflects back the plume into the area of
 concern. A lower mixing depth will increase ground level concentrations from a
 radioactive release at farther distances from the release.

 The effect of air density on plume transport is significant, as it influences the
 buoyancy and momentum of the plume. When the plume's density is close to
 that of the ambient air, the effect of density variations on inertia may be ignored,
 and only the buoyancy driving force needs to be accounted for. However, for
 very buoyant plumes, the density differences are crucial, and the model must be
 adjusted accordingly. Air density is an input parameter for RSAC but is only
 used when a jet or buoyant plume release is specified. These options are only
 available when an elevated (Stack) release occurs.

 Analysis Parameter 12 – Mixing Depth and Air Density

 The 95% mixing depth at the INL is 400 meters. The air density at the INL is
 1099 grams/cubic meter. Both of these values are default in RSAC.

 6.1.8 Dry Deposition Velocities

 The dry deposition factor (DDF) accounts for the material that is removed from
 the plume via deposition on the ground and accounts only for plume depletion
 that is expected to occur, independent of weather conditions.

 An estimate of the amount of radioactivity that is deposited on the ground must
 be made to calculate ground surface, resuspension and ingestion doses. Dry

 be used: 0.1 cm/sec for unfiltered release of particles (1-10 um Aerodynamic

 6.2.2 Material-at-Risk (MAR)

 The modeling protocol needs to include sufficient information to allow for the
 establishment of the technical basis for the dispersion modeling result. By
 providing this level of information regarding the tools, methodologies, site
 characteristics, and data sources, the facility can ensure that any concerns
 regarding the final result are resolved early in the process.

 The MAR is the amount of radionuclides (in grams or curies of activity for each
 radionuclide) available to be acted on by a given physical stress. For facilities,
 processes, and activities, the MAR is a value representing some maximum

                                                                                             Page 25 of 37

 quantity of radionuclide present or reasonably anticipated for the process or
 structure being analyzed. The inventory for reactor fuel should include all
 radionuclides prior to the applications of release fractions based on inherently
 safe fuel or other passive design features.

 Different MARs may be assigned for different accidents as it is only necessary
 to define the material in those discrete physical locations that are exposed to a
 given stress. For example, a spill may involve only the contents of a tank in one
 glovebox. Conversely, a seismic event may involve all of the material in a
 building.

 Analysis Parameter 16 – Material at Risk (MAR)

 Bounding material-at-risk for given accidents are estimated as part of physical
 model development The MAR is the bounding quantity of radioactive material
 that is available to be acted upon by a given physical stress from a postulated
 accident. The MAR may be the total inventory in a facility or a portion of this
 inventory in one location or operation, depending on the event. MAR values
 used in hazard and accident analysis shall be consistent with the values noted in
 hazard identification/evaluation, and shall be bounding with respect to each
 accident being evaluated. While DOE-STD-1027-92 (NE-STD-1027-2025)
 excludes material in Department of Transportation Type B containers from
 consideration for the purposes of hazard categorization, the existence of such
 material shall be acknowledged in the DSA and the material excluded from the
 source term for a particular accident scenario only if the containers can be
 shown to perform their safety functions under accident conditions.

 6.2.3 Damage Ratio

 The damage ratio (DR) represents the fraction of MAR that could be affected by
 the postulated accident and is a function of the accident initiator and the
 operational scenario being evaluated. DRs are determined based on engineering
 judgment, best available information, and prior analyses.

 The DR is estimated based upon engineering analysis of the response of
 structural materials and materials-of-construction for containment to the type
 and level of stress/force generated by the event. Standard engineering
 approximations are typically used. These approximations often include a degree
 of conservatism due to simplification of phenomena to obtain a useable model,
 but the purpose of the approximation is to obtain, to the degree possible, a
 realistic understanding of potential effects.

 The DR for scenarios without passive design features credited or engineering
 analysis is generally evaluated at 1.0. It assumes that the events are significant
 enough such that 100% of the material at risk is sufficiently impacted from the
 event to be released.

                                                                                           Page 26 of 37

 Analysis Parameter 17 – Damage Ratio (DR)

 DR is the fraction of material that is actually affected by the accident-generating
 conditions. DOE-HDBK-3010 notes that some degree of ambiguity can result
 from overlapping definitions of MAR and DR. A given DSA should use one
 consistent definition throughout. A DR of 1.0 shall be used unless there is an
 applicable standard or technical basis for a different value. For example,
 DOE-STD-5506-2007 contains specific DRs (and associated MAR guidance)
 that may be used in transuranic waste operations.

 6.2.4 Airborne Release Fraction, Respirable Fraction and Particle Diameter

 The Airborne Release Fraction (ARF) is the coefficient used to estimate the
 amount of a radioactive material that can be suspended in air and made available
 for airborne transport under a specific set of induced physical stresses.

 The Respirable Fraction (RF) is the fraction of airborne radionuclide particles
 that can be transported through air and inhaled into the human respiratory
 system. The RF is commonly assumed to include particles of 10-μm
 Aerodynamic Equivalent Diameter and less.

 The particle diameter will affect the dose conversion factor used for a particular
 accident. The dose conversion factor for the facility worker in ICRP-68 assumes
 a median diameter of 5 μm, and for the Public in ICRP-72 assumes a median
 diameter of 1 μm.

 and RFs for a wide variety of MAR and release phenomena are presented in
 DOE-HDBK-3010. The bounding estimates shall be used unless a different
 value is provided in an applicable standard or is otherwise technically justified.

 6.2.5 Leak Path Factor

 The leak path factor (LPF) is the fraction of material in the aerosol transported
 through some confinement deposition or filtration mechanism. Several leakpaths
 may be associated with a specific accident, such as the fraction passing from a
 glovebox, the fraction passing from a room, or the fraction passing through a

                                                                                            Page 28 of 37

 leaking door. The LPF used in the common five-factor formula is the total
 fraction of respirable airborne material released during the accident that escapes
 from the building to the environment.

 Analysis Parameter 19 – Leak Path Factor

 For purposes of the unmitigated release calculation, the LPF shall be set to
 unity. For mitigated analysis, analytical tools used in calculating the LPF shall
 be appropriate to the physical conditions being modeled, including the use of
 input parameters, such that the overall LPF would be conservative. Typically,
 this factor accounts for the amount of the airborne MAR that escapes from a
 building or room.

 LPFs other than 1.0 are accident specific and approved by DOE-ID.

 6.2.6 Breathing rate (BR) and Absorption Types

 Breathing rate (BR) is a property of the receptor, and is independent of all
 radionuclides and sources involved in an accident.

 Absorption Types as defined in ICRP-68 is the absorption from the respiratory
 tract to the bloodstream is categorized into three default types based on how
 quickly the material dissolves:

 Type F (Fast): Materials that are readily absorbed into the blood with a fast rate
 of clearance. These have an approximate absorption half-time of 10 minutes
 (100% of the material).

 Type M (Moderate): Materials with intermediate rates of absorption. About 10%
 is absorbed with a 10-minute half-time, while the remaining 90% has a half-time
                       of 140 days.

 Type S (Slow): Materials that are relatively insoluble and stay in the lungs for a
 long time. Only 0.1% is absorbed quickly (10-minute half-time), and 99.9% has
 a half-time of 7,000 days.

 These types are based on the Human Respiratory Tract Model (HRTM) found in
 ICRP-66, “Radiological Protection, Human Respiratory Tract Model for
 Radiological Protection.”37

 Analysis Parameter 20 – Breathing Rate and Absorption Type

                       Per DOE-STD-3009-2014, a breathing rate of 3.3 × 10-4 m3/s, which

 maximally-exposed off-site individual (MOI) and collocated worker. INL

 6.2.7 Cloud Gamma

 Cloud Gamma radiation dose, or "cloudshine," is the external exposure from
 gamma-emitting radionuclides suspended in the atmosphere, typically following
 a nuclear accident or explosion. Doses are calculated using air concentration,
 specific radionuclide energies, and geometry (often half an infinite cloud at
 ground level). Key factors include plume altitude, air density, and distance from
 the source.

 Gaussian Plume Model: Commonly used to calculate the air concentration and,
 subsequently, the dose at ground level, assuming the receptor is exposed to half
 an infinite cloud due to the ground surface.

 Factors Affecting Gamma Gamma Dose:

 • Distance: The dose decreases with horizontal distance from the source,
 but overhead plumes at short distances can still cause significant dose
 rates.

 • Meteorology: Stable atmospheric conditions can keep the plume aloft,
 resulting in low ground-level doses but potentially high, unexpected
 overhead gamma doses.

 • Altitude: Gamma rays from lower, nearer parts of the cloud contribute
 more to the dose than higher, distant parts.

 • Dose Rate Components: The total dose rate depends on the radionuclide
 inventory, cloud concentration, and energy levels.

 Analysis Parameter 18 – Cloud Gamma

 Cloud gamma only needs to be evaluated if they cause an upward change in the
 consequence level as defined in Table 1 of DOE-STD-3009-2014. However, it is
 recommended to always calculate cloud gamma and include it in the total dose.

 In cases where direct shine may contribute significantly to dose, that
 contribution should be evaluated without the use of the RF, and without the use
 of the ARF if due to a spill release resulting in exposure to a pool.
