  1. Abstract summarizing the general topics addressed during the award.

 The comprehensive research endeavors, primarily supported by the U.S. Department of Energy
 Grant DE-SC0021320, focus on developing and implementing advanced machine learning
 techniques for scientific data compression within high-performance computing (HPC)
 environments. This work directly addresses the critical challenge of managing the massive,
 complex datasets generated by scientific simulations in domains such as computational fluid
 dynamics (CFD), climate modeling, and fusion energy. A core objective is to achieve substantial
 data reduction without compromising the scientific integrity and accuracy required for
 subsequent analysis. Across this body of work, we develop a coherent framework for learned
 scientific data compression with rigorous guarantees, bridging modern generative models and
 the strict reliability demands of HPC and simulation science. The papers collectively show how
 neural compression schemes—particularly autoencoder-based and hybrid generative models—
 can be augmented with explicit error bounds, instance-specific constraints, and fast algorithmic
 structures to ensure fidelity not only of raw fields but also of derived scientific quantities. Key
 contributions include nonlinear–by–linear decompositions that preserve provable distortion
 limits, constraint-satisfaction formulations that enforce per-instance guarantees, and scalable
 algorithms tailored to CFD and other large-scale simulation workloads. Furthermore, the research
 extends to the preservation of derived quantities of interest (QoI), leveraging techniques like
 nonlinear constraint satisfaction to maintain the validity of critical scientific metrics post-
 compression. Taken together, this work advances learned compression from a heuristic tool into
 a principled, controllable component of scientific workflows, aligning ML-based reduction with
 the correctness, performance, and trust requirements of high-performance computing.

  2. Brief technical description of most relevant projects/publications.

 The research program funded by DOE Grant DE-SC0021320 meticulously addresses the challenges
 of scientific data compression, focusing on high-fidelity, error-bounded lossy compression
 methods essential for modern scientific applications in high-performance computing (HPC)
 environments. Scientific simulations, particularly in fields like fusion energy, climate modeling,
 and computational fluid dynamics (CFD), generate enormous volumes of complex, high-
 dimensional data that pose significant storage, transfer, and analysis burdens. Traditional
 compression techniques often fall short in providing the necessary fidelity guarantees,
 particularly for derived quantities of interest (QoI), which are vital for scientific discovery. The
 methodologies developed through this grant introduce a paradigm shift by integrating advanced
 machine learning with rigorous error control, ensuring that data reduction does not compromise
 scientific validity.

 A cornerstone of this research involves the adaptation and enhancement of autoencoder
 architectures for scientific data. Autoencoders are neural networks designed to learn efficient,
 low-dimensional representations of input data by compressing it into a latent space and then
 reconstructing it. This process allows for significant data reduction while preserving key features.
 The studies combine these autoencoders with tensor decompositions and product quantizers to
 optimize the compression process. Tensor decompositions help exploit the intrinsic

 spatiotemporal and inter-variable correlations present in scientific datasets, providing a
 structured approach to data representation before neural network processing.

 A paramount feature across these works is the incorporation of guaranteed error bounds. Unlike
 generic compression methods, these approaches provide reliable error control, which is crucial
 for scientific applications where numerical precision is critical. The error-bounding mechanism is
 typically achieved through a multi-stage process. Initial compression by autoencoders might yield
 a primary reconstruction. The residuals—the differences between the original and reconstructed
 data—are then further analyzed and compressed. Methods like Principal Component Analysis
 (PCA) are applied to these residuals, retaining only a minimal number of PCA components to
 ensure the reconstruction error at the block level remains within a user-defined threshold. This
 PCA-based approach helps guarantee error bounds. This ensures that the reconstructed data
 deviates from the original within a specified, scientifically acceptable margin, validating the
 reliability of compressed data for subsequent analyses.

 A significant innovation highlighted in these publications is the focus on preserving derived
 quantities of interest (QoI) alongside bounding the primary data error. Scientific analyses often
 depend on complex metrics or physical laws derived from raw simulation outputs, and ensuring
 these QoI remain accurate after compression is non-trivial. The research employs a two-stage
 approach: first, reducing primary data with bounded errors, and second, applying constraint
 satisfaction post-processing to ensure QoI preservation. This is achieved by adjusting the
 compressed representation through nonlinear constraint satisfaction using instance-specific
 linear operators. This adaptive approach allows autoencoders to conform to specific scientific or
 physical constraints, improving the fidelity and robustness of reconstructed data, particularly
 when small deviations could lead to significant inaccuracies in scientific conclusions.

 The practical relevance of this research is underscored by its direct application to various grand
 challenge problems in HPC. The developed compression techniques are deployed in real-world
 scientific simulations, including computational fluid dynamics (CFD) applications, climate
 modeling, and fusion energy research. For instance, "Machine Learning Techniques for Data
 Reduction of CFD Applications" demonstrates how guaranteed block autoencoder with tensor
 correction methods (GBATC) can achieve two-to-three orders of magnitude data reduction while
 maintaining error bounds for complex flow simulations. Similarly, these techniques are applied to
 climate applications, exploiting spatial, temporal, and inter-variable tensor correlations to
 compress large-scale climate simulation outputs effectively.

 A critical aspect of their HPC deployment is the development of fast algorithms and GPU-
 parallelized post-processing modules. These components ensure that the advanced machine
 learning compression methods can operate at speeds commensurate with the high throughput
 demands of scientific workflows, without compromising the strict error bounds or QoI
 preservation. The integration of these features ensures that the research outcomes are not
 merely theoretical advancements but practical, scalable solutions that significantly mitigate the
 "data deluge" problem, enabling more efficient utilization of HPC resources and accelerating
 scientific discovery.
