 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would demolish Building 25-4314 in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS) in compliance with the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S. Department
of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on Historic
Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security Site, Nye
County, Nevada (NNSS PA). Building 25-4314 (State Historic Preservation Office [SHPO] Resource #
B19016) is a contributing element to the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District
(SHPO Resource # D424), which has been determined eligible for listing in the National Register of
Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A, B, C, and D (Reno et al. 2023).
The proposed demolition constitutes an adverse effect. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office (NNSA/NFO) consulted with the SHPO on an
adverse effect finding for the undertaking and notified the SHPO of its intent to use the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA on December 11, 2024 (Julian to Reed). The SHPO concurred on December
30, 2024 (Reed to Julian). Therefore, NNSA/NFO has prepared mitigation pursuant to the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible
districts.
 MITIGATION CONTENTS
The standard mitigation for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible districts consists of an
updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to document current conditions (Appendix
D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated
sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing mitigation contents and summarizing
the building’s historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for Building 25-4314 in accordance with
NNSS PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Appendix A). It provides additional information about the historic property, an individual NRHP
evaluation, and interior description, engineering drawings, and current and historical photographs. This
letter report fulfills NNSS PA Appendix D.I.B.
 DESCRIPTION OF BUILDING 25-4314
The Radiation Services Building is near the center of a large rectangular, bladed yard and surrounded by a
chain link and barbed wire fence (see Figures 1 and 2). The building is a 33 × 45-foot one-story building
with a simple rectangular plan. The walls are tan concrete block on a concrete foundation. The flat roof
supports air-handling equipment. Flush eaves reinforce the basic box appearance of the building. The
front façade has a centered flush steel door. The small metal hood over the door is a later addition. A
floodlight on the wall illuminates the entry. A galvanized steel equipment mount is bolted to the wall.
The rear has a door with a Yucca Mountain Project (YMP) Shelter location sign on it and a light above. A
later modification is the creation of a new wall opening for a sliding window to the right of the door with
a white aluminum rain deflector. Between them are surface mounted 110 and 220 volt outlets. Another
floodlight near the roof illuminates the rear yard.
The east side has a steel roof ladder. A lean-to equipment shelter has been added. Under the shelter are
two concrete equipment pads with bollards. One is empty, while the other has a transformer and electrical
equipment box. There are also two wall-mounted equipment boxes and another floodlight. The west side

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National
Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected
an area in the southwestern portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test reactors and
engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and other private contractors under the Rover and
NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall objective was to design an efficient and
reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the moon, Mars, and beyond.
Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly, and disassembly
buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
The Radiation Services Building was constructed in 1967 for the study and control of radiation associated
with testing at the NRDS. In addition to meeting and office space, the Radiation Services building also
had equipment and instrument storage areas and laboratory space. The Respirator Decontamination and
Check Out Room provided a place where equipment could be decontaminated after each use and then
stored. Present day decontamination procedures for respirators indicate that scrubbing the respirator with
soap and water will remove radiological and nuclear hazards from the equipment (Janssen et al. 2018). An
initial rinse is performed, followed by aggressive scrubbing using brushes and soap, then a final rinse. It
is likely similar standards were in place when Building 25-4314 was in use.
Of note within Building 25-4314 are the Sample Prep Lab and Counting Lab, which are conjoined via a
pass window or pass box, to ease the exchange of radiological test samples and lessen the potential for
contamination and exposure. Samples were collected and prepared for testing in the Sample Prep Lab
using shielding equipment like sample hoods, shield walls, and shield carts designed to keep lab workers
protected from contamination. Once the samples were ready for testing, they were transferred to the
Counting Lab using the pass window and then placed inside testing equipment designed to detect
radioactive materials. The only unshielded step was during the transfer through the pass window, the use
of which eliminated precious seconds of potential exposure time had samples needed to be walked
between rooms (RGEC 1979).
By 1973, the NRDS program was canceled and the building was no longer in use. It remained vacant until
the late 1990s when it was converted into a medical facility for use in the Yucca Mountain Project. At this
time the rooms were renumbered, and several were converted to office spaces. No major structural
modifications were made to the building to accommodate the medical facility, which was only in use for a
few years until it eventually closed. The storage cabinets were removed from all the rooms, but it is
unknown if that was part of the conversion to a medical facility or if that took place after the medical
facility closed. The building is currently vacant.
Individual Significance Evaluation
Building 25-4314 is not recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP, as it does not appear to be
significant as an individual property under any of the applicable significance criteria. It was directly used
for testing samples for radioactive particulates unique to a series of nuclear rocket propulsion tests and
decontaminating personal protective equipment exposed to those particulates. However, it was not the
only building within the NRDS district that did so. Furthermore, it is not directly or importantly
associated with any specific scientific developments. Therefore, Building 25-4314 does not appear to be
significant under Criterion A. It is also not considered significant under Criterion B as it cannot be

associated with the lives of persons significant in our past. Building 25-4314 does not represent any
distinctive characteristics of type, period, or method of construction or possess high artistic value, nor is it
the work of a master or a significant and distinguishable entity; therefore, it is not significant under
Criterion C. Building 25-4314 was only in use as a radiation services building for a short number of years
and all traces of equipment or physical material that might yield additional information about the building
are no longer present. No information can be found that relates to its use or importance as a medical
facility; therefore, it is unlikely Building 25-4314 could yield more information that could be considered
important, and it is not significant under Criterion D.
Integrity
Building 25-4314 is in good condition and retains all seven aspects of integrity to some degree to convey
its significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. The integrity of location,
materials, and workmanship remain intact, with integrity of design diminished slightly due to the removal
of storage shelves and cabinets from several of the rooms. It has lost some degree of integrity of setting,
association, and feeling due to the fact that the NRDS district is no longer used for nuclear rocket
development research.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Due to the limited scope of this investigation, the Radiation Services Building (25-4314, B19016) was not individually
evaluated for eligibility to the NRHP under any of the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria (36 CFR 60.4).
For the purposes of the present survey, resources identified within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development
Station (NRDS) Historic District were only evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district.
The NRDS Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing
center for the Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from 1956 to 1973.
Facilities such as this one for the study and control of radiation comprise one of the principal character-defining
elements of the NRDS Historic District. Built in 1965, this building was used for radiation services for the NRDS
program until it ended in 1973. Presently the building and all parts of the yard are vacant. With the conversion of the
old Medical Facility (25-4117, B19013) to offices, this building was eventually converted to a medical facility in the
late 1990s by Bechtel Nevada (Bechtel 1999). Air photos show the yard was still used for storage as late as June
2005, at which time what appears to be a row of trailers was parked in the rear area behind the fence. During the
NRDS era, this building was a critical radiation safety and control building. It retains all seven aspects of integrity to
convey its significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District.
The simple horseshoe pit (AR1) stands out as one of the two outside recreational facilities identified anywhere in the
NRDS, speaking to its strict mission focus (the other is the small park at Well J-11). Like the park, this pit is clearly
an unplanned initiative by site workers.
The Radiation Services Building is also a potential contributing element to the unevaluated Central Support Area
(CSA) subdistrict during the NRDS period of significance. This subdistrict is a concentration of buildings and
structures that convey the interrelated activities essential for operating the NRDS. Building functions included
administration, warehousing and storage, technical and maintenance shops, vehicle shop and fuel station,
communications, a medical facility, and radiation services. This subdistrict, like others at the NRDS, was intentionally
located three to four miles upwind from the testing areas to avoid potential fallout. The CSA was never close to a
self-sufficient settlement but was a specialized NRDS satellite of the main base camp of Mercury. After the
termination of the NRDS programs in 1973, the CSA facilities continued to be used to support various programs of
the Nevada Research & Development Area (NRDA), USAF programs, including the Peacekeeper (MX) Tests, the
field base for the Nevada Nuclear Waste Storage Investigations (NNWSI) (commonly known as the Yucca Mountain
Project), and others. However, these themes have not been defined as significant historic contexts in the history of
the local area, Nevada, or the nation.

1990s when it was converted into a medical facility for use in the Yucca Mountain Project (YMP). At this time the
rooms were renumbered, and several were converted to office spaces. No major structural modifications were made
to the building to accommodate the medical facility, which was only in use for a few years until it eventually closed.
The storage cabinets were removed from all the rooms, but it is unknown if that was part of the conversion to a
medical facility or if that took place after the medical facility closed. The building is currently vacant.
The Radiation Services building is a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District, but it is not recommended
individually eligible for listing in the NRHP. Though it retains integrity, it is not individually significant under any of the
Significance Criteria as outlined below.
Criterion A. To be individually eligible for the National Register under Criterion A, properties must be associated with
events or a series of events that have made a significant contribution to broad patterns of our history. The Radiation
Services building was directly used for testing samples for radioactive particulates unique to a series of nuclear rocket
propulsion tests and decontaminating personal protective equipment exposed to those particulates. However, it was
not the only building within the NRDS district that did so. Other radiation safety and services buildings were present
where additional radiological tests were conducted. Whether or not the purposes for radiological testing differed
substantially between buildings is unknown. Furthermore, the building is not directly or importantly associated with
any specific NRDS developments. Therefore, Building 25-4314 does not appear to be significant under Criterion A.
Criterion B. Properties may be eligible for the National Register if they are associated with the lives of persons
significant in our past. An archival review cannot associate use of the Radiation Services building with any significant
persons; therefore, the Radiation Services building does not appear to be significant under Criterion B.
Criterion C. Properties may be eligible under Criterion C if they embody the distinctive characteristics of a type,
period, or method of construction, or that represents the work of a master, or that possesses high artistic values.
Properties may also be eligible under this criterion if they represent a significant and distinguishable entity whose
components may lack individual distinction. As a purely utilitarian building, Building 25-4314 does not meet any of
these metrics. Therefore, Building 25-4314 does not appear to be significant under Criterion C.
Criterion D. Properties significant under Criterion D must have the potential to yield further information about human
history that can only be answered by the actual physical material of the resources. There are two requirements for a
resource to be found significant under Criterion D: the property must have or have had information to contribute to our
understanding of human history or prehistory, and the information must be considered important. Building 25-4314
was only in use as a radiation services building for a short number of years and all traces of equipment or physical
material that might yield additional information about the building are no longer present. No information can be found
that relates to its use or importance as a medical facility; therefore, Building 25-4314 does not appear to be significant
under Criterion D.

significance as a contributing element to the NRDS Historic District. The integrity of location, materials, and

 7. Narrative Architectural Description

 For a description of the exterior of the building, see Reno et al. 2023 and the building’s original ARA form.
 Interior:
 When it was built in 1967, the Radiation Services building was designed with eight rooms, four each on the east
 and west side, with a central corridor that ran north-south from the main entrance. The rooms were numbered 1-9,
 with 9 being the corridor itself. The rooms on the west side were numbered sequentially north to south beginning
 with Room 3, the Equipment and Instrument Storage Room. The east and west walls of Room 3 were lined with
 cabinets and metal storage shelves. The north and south walls each had a single smaller cabinet centered on the
 wall. There were two other floating storage cabinets in the center of the room. The entry door is on the east wall.
 Room 4 was the men’s bathroom. The west wall featured two stalls with toilets; a urinal was on the south wall. A
 partition divided the east wall with an entry door and sink on either side; the north wall was bare. The women’s
 bathroom, Room 5, had two stalls on the west wall, one with a toilet and the other with a water heater. The sink
 was on the north wall and both the south and east walls were bare, except for an entry door on the east wall.
 Room 6 was the Central Dosimetry and Office and Central Records Room. The entry door from the corridor is
 centered on the east wall but the south wall also features an external entrance. Two windows are present on the
 west walls and the north wall is bare.
 The rooms on the east side of the central corridor began with Room 1, the Sample Prep Lab, which is entered
 through a door on the west wall. The remainder of the west wall and the north wall were fully lined with storage
 cabinets. In the center of the cabinet on the north wall was a sink. The east wall had a smaller counter and sink
 with a ventilated hood. The south wall features a pass box or pass window to facilitate the movement of samples
 between the Sample Prep Lab and Room 2, the Counting Lab. The north wall of the Counting Lab, Room 2, was
 bare except for the pass window. The east and south walls featured counters with open shelving below. The west
 wall was bare except for an entry door. At present, the south wall is carpeted; it is unknown if this was a later
 modification.
 The next room was the Respirator Decontamination and Check Out Room (Room 7), followed by the Briefing and
 Training Room (Room 8). The walls of both rooms were bare except for entry doors on the west and north walls
 respectively. Room 7 had a gamma-ray spectrometer on the north wall; Room 8 features a window on the south
 wall.
 When the building was repurposed in the late 1990s, the rooms were renumbered in a clockwise rotation beginning
 with the northeast room (the former Sample Prep Lab) as Room 1. The men’s bathroom was converted into a
 unisex bathroom with a movable men’s/women’s sign on the door (see photos 14 and 15) and the women’s
 bathroom was converted into a shower. Storage cabinets and shelving units were removed from the rooms;
 however, it is not known if this occurred during conversion to the medical facility or sometime thereafter. Also
 present are wooden shelves in the former Briefing and Training Room and the Respirator Decontamination and
 Check Out Room, which were not present on the original engineering drawings and may have been added at a
 later date.
 An archival review did not locate any information that could indicate the purpose or function of any of the rooms
 once the building was converted into a medical facility. Magnetic name plates were added to the former Equipment
 and Instrument Storage Room and the Counting Lab. Presumably these became offices, but no other information
 could identify or confirm the functions of the other rooms.
