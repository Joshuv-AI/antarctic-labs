 1. ABSTRACT

The use of blended fuel sources in land based gas turbine engines drives variations in the resulting
operational profile (temperatures and pressures) which can impact engine reliability. Furthermore,
variability in the manufacture of components affects the resulting microstructure which directly impacts
material performance and reliability. Currently, data-driven models are typically used for maintaining and
inspecting fleets of engines. Without explicitly capturing material and operational sources of variability
conservatism must be used in developing component-level reliability models. Therefore, there exists an
opportunity to use information from materials-scale physics models to better inform reliability modeling
and reduce conservatism; the impact is more cost-efficient operation and maintenance of current and future
fleets. Specifically, this work establishes a computational framework for evaluating the probabilistic high
temperature creep performance of hot-section Ni-based superalloys where uncertainty comes from both
microstructural and operational variability. A novel high-fidelity physics model which phenomenologically
captures grain-boundary sensitive phenomena has been established. A probabilistic calibration procedure
was used to calibrate the model and capture uncertainty in the parameterized model coefficients. A design
of experiments methodology was established for identifying informative microstructural digital
representations for suitable for forward model evaluation. Results show that training a machine-learning
surrogate using this design criteria outperforms random selection of microstructural representations.
Finally, two surrogate models were developed: (1) a deterministic surrogate model which predicts the local
field response given microstructure, constitutive model parameters, and operating conditions (stress,
temperature) and (2) a probabilistic model, where uncertainty comes from constitutive law uncertainty,
built using denoising diffusion probabilistic models which samples responses given (1) microstructure and
(2) operating conditions. These surrogate models enable partner Siemens Energy to rapidly perform UQ
analysis specific to creep deformation across a range of microstructures and operating conditions. The
impact is that these ML and physics codes can be used to establish more advanced reliability models for
the inspection, servicing, and maintenance of land based gas turbine engines.

 2. STATEMENT OF OBJECTIVES

The goal of this work is to establish a framework for determining safe operating windows for mixed-fueled
gas turbine engines (natural gas, hydrogen, conventional jet fuel) by utilizing physics-informed physics and
machine learning models. Existing engine management reliability models impose narrow operating margins
to mitigate against risk from operational uncertainty. We propose that some of this risk can be mitigated by
incorporating information from hot section materials physics simulations. This will be performed within a
probabilistic framework; uncertainty from both microstructure and operational conditions (temperatures,
loads) will be explicitly captured via simulation. If successful, this work will enable Siemens Energy to
establish reliability tools for incorporating fuel blend and blade-variability information for effective
operation of the hydrogen gas turbine fleet.

 3. BENEFITS TO THE FUNDING DOE OFFICE'S MISSION

This work is focused on establishing a framework for quantifying sources of uncertainty (microstructure
and operating conditions) and propagating this information to perform risk assessments for turbomachinery.
The impact of this work is that risk in turbomachinery reliability modeling can be better quantified and,
hence, enables more efficient operation of fleets. This effort has been enabled by high fidelity
microstructure-scale physics simulations and computationally efficient machine learning and AI-based
surrogate models. This focus area is directly aligned with the Department of Energy’s stated goal of
maximizing domestic energy abundance. Additionally, this effort to bolster cheap and reliability domestic

 4. TECHNICAL RESULTS

The overall program focus is shown in Figure 1. The goal is to establish a numerical model than can be
used to quantify material creep performance variability coming from (1) microstructural and (2) operational
variability. Microstructural variability can include grain size and crystallographic texture. Operational
variability may include different temperature/load conditions which may arise due to variability in the
particular customer’s fuel blend (conventional fuel, natural gas, hydrogen blend, etc.). The approach is to
establish a high-fidelity physics model which can capture the relevant deformation modes, build a
computationally efficient machine learning (ML) surrogate that can be rapidly evaluated, and finally
provide predicted localized responses owing to the various sources of uncertainty (loading, microstructure,
model uncertainty).

 Figure 1. Establish physics-based and ML models, for reliability modeling, that can capture (1) microstructural variability and
 (2) operational variability.

This problem was divided into three key components shown in Figure 2: (1) establishment of a numerical
model capturing key deformation physics (2) efficient model calibration and (3) ML surrogate
development.

Grain-scale deformation behavior was modeled using open-source crystal plasticity finite element
(CPFEM) code DAMASK [1]. A schematic of the modeling strategy is shown in Figure 3. Each element is
assigned three euler angles which represent a specific grain’s crystal lattice orientation (relative to the global
reference coordinate system). Each element shares the same anisotropic elastic model. However, each
element is assigned a different plasticity model; grain interiors ‘core’ are assigned a CP model, grain
boundaries ‘mantel’ assigned a J2 model [2]. Our hypothesis was that grain boundaries, when
accommodating Coble creep, should behave viscously. The final image in Figure 3 shows the magnitude
of the inelastic velocity gradient clearly showing localized grain-boundary deformation (note that the
microstructures are not 1:1).

 Figure 3. (left) digital microstructural representation of local grains and their crystal lattice orientation (middle) core/mantel
 arrangement (right) response field.

One challenge faced by using this hybrid core-mantel model is that the contribution to accommodating
deformation along grain boundaries or within grain interiors need to vary as a function of applied stress
(and temperature). Naively utilizing one single term in the power law for the J2-mantel results in non-
physical deformation; at high stresses the grain boundaries become overly stiff constraining core
deformation. Hence, a two-term flow rule was adopted for describing the deformation behavior of the J2-
mantel elements. In this way at high stresses both the core and mantel can deform concurrently
(homogenous regime) while at low stresses only the grain boundaries contribute (composite regime). This
is schematically shown in Figure 4. Also shown are numerical results and some experimental data
confirming that the two-term flow rule in the mantel elements indeed provides flexibility for capture the
stress-dependent rate-sensitivity exponent variation. Note that the DAMASK source code was modified to
include a second term in J2 plasticity.

 Figure 4. (left) core-CP and two-term mantel-J2 flow laws (right) resulting simulated response and experimental data.
 Localization of deformation to GBs clearly observable at low stresses.

The established constitutive model, built using phenomenological model available in DAMASK, has 14
unknown constitutive parameters (power law flow law coefficients and hardening parameters for both CP-
core and J2-mantel). Introducing temperature dependency increases the number of calibration coefficients
to 22. A single evaluation of the DAMASK model on a 643 regular mesh using the DAMASK FFT solver
up to a strain of 2-4% (steady state creep) requires ~1hr of compute on 32 cores of a single node on ORNL
CADES. Iterating over various potential solutions in 22-dimensional space, with each function evaluation
requiring 1hr, is extremely computationally expensive. Therefore a computer-code calibration strategy was
instead used e.g. Kennedy and O’Hagan approach [3]. This strategy instead proposed the following strategy:
 1. Identify an experimental design over the unknown calibration coefficient values (space filling
         design [4])
 2. Run the computer-code simulation over the experimental design and postprocess the key response
 variables (steady state creep rate, time to 0.3% strain, time to 1.0% strain)
 3. Define a statistical model (likelihood of the experimental data combined with model response
 variables). Experimental data is assumed to be i.i.d. with log-rates and log-times being normally
 distributed. The numerical model response is typically assumed be a Gaussian process where
 correlation between model evaluations is determined by defining a correlation function which
 computes the distance between calibration coefficient inputs.
 4. Perform inference to estimate the unknown calibration coefficients.

The advantage of this approach is that the forward model only need be evaluated over a single ‘batch’.
Typically ~10X the number of dimensions in your problem (for reasonable dimensionality). In our case a

 4

few additional evaluations are needed (~30-40X number of dimensions in the problem) as we used an active
learning approach to incrementally converge on the ‘true’ constitutive model parameter distribution [5].
The process is (1) run the model 250 times over an initial design of calibration coefficients (2) perform
inference (3) identify an additional 100 calibration coefficient settings which are ‘near’ the solution (4) run
the model over these 100 sequential points (5) perform inference again, and (6) iterate until converged.
Typically, this approach results in far fewer function evaluations for identifying a calibrated model. This is
particularly true if gradient-based approaches were to be used for optimization (gradients are estimated
using finite differences e.g. each iteration requires 1 function evaluation + 22 function evaluations to
estimate df/dxi for each of the 22 calibration coefficient xi). Furthermore, since the inference is performed
using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling the statistical calibration approach yields
probabilistic densities of the unknown calibration coefficients. Typical mean-squared-error optimization
routines to fit the model only provide point solutions of the unknown calibration coefficients. Uncertainty
estimates of the point solution can also be provided however require assumptions regarding the asymptotic
convergence of the posterior distribution (usually – lots of experimental data is needed for this to be valid).
A schematic of the Gaussian process surrogate used in the constitutive model parameter calibration strategy
is shown in Figure 5. The inputs are constitutive model parameters and output is a scalar quantity of interest
(log-rate, log-time). Note that one of this limitations of this surrogate is that the microstructure, also an
input to the DAMASK CPFEM simulation, must be fixed! GP surrogates can only operate on parametric
problems (input scalars/vectors) and mostly can only handle simple outputs (single scalar). More advanced
GPs can generate multiple outputs but require some assumptions on how these outputs are correlated. More
recently deep learning based GPs have also been developed which offer some additional flexibility.
Nonetheless, for the purposes of simply calibrating the physics model, a standard GP was used with fixed
microstructure. This limits the flexibility available to calibrate to different microstructures but was deemed
sufficient for this specific problem. The model calibration procedure follows that found in previously
published work. Experimental data, testing conditions, and detailed calibration results are omitted here as
some of the information is proprietary.

 Figure 5. GP-surrogate input/output structure. Note that structure must be fixed in this case since it is difficult to parameterize
 the microstructure representation.

A single evaluation of the physics model up to 2-4% strain (typical deformation needed to achieve steady-
state creep deformation) requires ~1hr of compute on a single node 32-cores on ORNL’s CADES. Even
with the calibrated model available it is challenging to use the established model within a reliability
framework where Monte Carlo analysis over variations in structure and/or operation conditions may be
needed. Hence, there is a critical need to develop a machine-learning AI-based surrogate model. The input-

 5

output structure of the desired model is shown in Figure 6. The input consists of: structure representation
m, constitutive model representation 𝜽, and operational conditions 𝒛 = (𝜎,𝑇). Note that we include here
constitutive model parameters as an input since the calibrated model does contain uncertainty. Simply
‘picking’ one point solution may fail to capture behavior on the tail-ends of the distribution which is
important for reliability modeling. The desired output is the full-field response (stress tensor, strain tensor,
deformation-rate tensor) which may be useful for failure analysis. In fatigue, for instance, both plastic strain
and the stress state are known to influence crack initiation. Here a Unet architecture backbone is used to
train the surrogate. Convolutions within the Unet are well suited for capturing the spatial-structure of the
input microstructure. Vector/scalar inputs are included into the model by using an approach analogous to
positional encodings in transformers [6], [7]. At each layer inputs 𝜽 and z are are mapped to a higher-
dimension vector representation by simply multiplying against learnable weight matrix. That vector is then
added to the feature maps produced by convolutions. This operation enables the network to be sensitive to
constitutive model parameters (varying due to calibration uncertainty) and operational conditions
(stress/temperature varying due to engine operation preferences). Self-attention mechanisms were placed
after all convolution blocks.

 Figure 6. Deterministic ML surrogate input/output structure.

The model was trained on ~5000 simulated examples on multiple 80GB A100 GPUs using distributed
parallelism (DDP) in PyTorch. Here only the stress state at steady-state creep conditions is considered as
an output variable. For demonstration purposed data augmentation was performed by randomly sub-
sampling 323 volumes from the 643 dataset. Note that this is valid since the FFT solver in DAMASK
imposes volume-averaged deformation gradient or stress tensor. Hence, assuming that the subsampled
volume is indeed representative (not too small), the local imposed boundary conditions should match the
boundary conditions imposed on the larger 643 volume. Several 322 slices evaluated on a test data set are
shown in Figure 7. Results indicate that the model indeed performs well.

operational conditions. The loading direction stress state and one component of shear are shown.

In addition to the deterministic model this program has developed a probabilistic model which samples
material response fields corresponding to constitutive model parameter uncertainty. The model is built on
the now ubiquitous denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) [7]. The premise is that for a specified
input structure m and operational conditions z the response follows a response distribution where variability
comes from uncertainty in the calibrated constitutive parameters. DDPMs are models for sampling complex
forms of data (images, audio, tabular data, etc.) with flexibility for conditioning on external information. A
schematic of the input-output structure Figure 8.

 Figure 8. Probabilistic ML surrogate input/output structure.

Finally, early in the program a strategy was developed for intelligent selection of microstructural
representations for building of surrogates. The key problem is that training these ML models requires large
data sets which require significant computational resources to amass. In the field of computer experiments
space-filling design criteria are often used for identifying settings over which to evaluate computationally
expensive parameterized models (inputs are simply scalars). The goal is to ‘spread’ points efficiently in the
parameter space so that any surrogate trained on the response output is exposed to a diversity of data

 7

representative of the parameter space. The challenge in the micromechanical problem is that one of the
inputs to the model is a voxelized representation of the material structure; this is not easily parameterizable.
Hence, these approaches for designing diverse inputs for surrogate model training are not directly
applicable.

The approach used by this team was to perform a similar optimization over the input design and using
various ‘features’ to encode information present in the candidate representative volume elements (RVEs).
A schematic is shown in Figure 9. Full details can be found in a preprint manuscript prepared by the team
[8]. Results, shown in Figure 10, demonstrate that indeed when training with sufficient data intelligently
selected points (conditional maxi-min cMm) outperforms models trained using data corresponding to
randomly selected candidate RVEs. Note that one of the novelties of this work was establishment of a self-
supervised feature extraction method that extracts microstructural statistics (Figure 11). Spatial-orientation
data is manipulated via convolutions followed by extraction of mean/variance data to capture statistics.
Orientation-only data is manipulated by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) followed by computation of a
spatial mean. The data is concatenated and mapped to a latent vector representation. This approach is
suspected to be useful because microstructures are inherently stochastic and, hence, the computation of
summary statistics may be more useful than a VAE-based representation that explicitly encodes all details
of the stochastic structure.

 8. CONCLUSIONS

This program has achieved all three key objectives (1) establishment of a novel constitutive model capable
of capturing grain-boundary sensitive creep deformation behavior (2) a calibration framework making use
of computationally efficient strategies to fit high-fidelity model to experimental creep-deformation data (3)
distillation of the physics-model information into computationally efficient ML/AI-based surrogate models.
The impact to partner Siemens Energy Inc. is access to a suite of tools essential for both interpreting
experimental creep data and making predictions of the behavior of components subject to uncertain
conditions. Furthermore, these tools can be generalized towards analyzing the behavior of different
material systems or potentially different deformation mechanisms. These capabilities offer the potential
to better quantify risk in assessing component/material performance which benefits the safety of
operations, reduces costs associated with unnecessary repairs and maintenance, and offers customers
additional flexibility in how to run engines. These capabilities benefit the greater domestic energy landscape
by offering cheap and reliable energy
