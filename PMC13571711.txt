Acute Bilateral Diaphragmatic Weakness as a Presentation of Parsonage–Turner Syndrome
Authors: Stephany Ivonne Briones Alvarado, José Daniel Cordero Márquez, Melissa Sanjuanelo Muñoz, Alicia Cerezo Lajas, Alicia Oliva Ramos, Soledad López Martín
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeuralgic amyotrophy (NA), also known as Parsonage–Turner syndrome, is an uncommon inflammatory neuropathy characterized by acute neuropathic pain followed by muscle weakness and atrophy. Phrenic nerve involvement leading to diaphragmatic dysfunction is rare and may represent a diagnostic challenge, particularly in postoperative patients in whom alternative cardiopulmonary etiologies coexist.Keywords: Neuralgic, Amyotrophy, Parsonage–Turner, Dysfunction, Diaphragm, CPAP

A 62-year-old never-smoker who had recently undergone right total knee arthroplasty presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset dyspnoea and left shoulder pain. Laboratory investigations revealed an elevated D-dimer level of 4285 ng/mL, with no other relevant abnormalities on the initial work-up. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a small thrombus within a subsegmental branch of the left lower lobe pulmonary artery and elevation of both hemidiaphragms. However, the marked worsening of dyspnoea in the supine position, together with persistent left shoulder pain, could not be fully explained by pulmonary embolism alone. Diaphragmatic ultrasound revealed a bilateral reduction in craniocaudal diaphragmatic excursion, which was more pronounced on the left side (Fig. 1). Chest CT showed no obvious diaphragmatic thinning, and ultrasound demonstrated preserved diaphragmatic thickness bilaterally. Spirometry showed a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 3.1 L in the sitting position, equivalent to 91% of the predicted value. In the supine position, the FVC decreased by 27% to 2.26 L, equivalent to approximately 66% of the predicted value. The forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1) was 2.32 L, corresponding to 89% of the predicted value. Respiratory muscle strength testing demonstrated markedly reduced pressures (MIP: 27 cmH2O, MEP: 24 cmH2O, and SNIP: 17 cmH2O).

Baseline arterial blood gas analysis revealed no evidence of hypercapnia (pH 7.37, PaCO2 42 mmHg, PaO2 62 mmHg, bicarbonate 21 mmol/L, and oxygen saturation 91%). The patient denied any previous history of chronic respiratory symptoms, recent trauma, or potential toxic exposures. The only medications administered following arthroplasty were paracetamol, ibuprofen, and prophylactic heparin. An extensive diagnostic evaluation, including an autoimmune panel and serological testing, was unremarkable except for positive IgM serology for Mycoplasma spp. In light of these findings, continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), analgesic therapy, and respiratory rehabilitation were initiated. This approach enabled the patient to tolerate the supine position. Transcutaneous capnography and overnight respiratory polygraphy were performed while receiving CPAP because the patient was unable to tolerate the supine position without ventilatory support. Transcutaneous capnography did not reveal significant hypercapnia (TcCO2: 42 mmHg), whereas overnight respiratory polygraphy revealed an apnoea-hypopnoea index of 11.5 events/h.

After three months of treatment, the patient was able to remain in the supine position for approximately three minutes without CPAP assistance, with complete resolution of left shoulder pain. The follow-up spirometry at one month showed a sitting FVC of 3.3 L (93%), which decreased by 23% in the supine position, to 2.54 L, equivalent to approximately 72% of the predicted value. The FEV1 was 2.39 L (92%) (Fig. 1). Because of technical limitations, electromyography could not be performed within the first two weeks after symptom onset and was ultimately carried out five weeks later. The study was consistent with bilateral multilevel preganglionic axonal involvement affecting the C5-C7 roots. Brain and cervical magnetic resonance imaging revealed no structural abnormalities. Based on the clinical presentation and complementary investigations, a diagnosis of bilateral diaphragmatic weakness secondary to neuralgic amyotrophy (NA) was established. NA, also known as Parsonage–Turner syndrome, is a rare multifocal inflammatory neuropathy characterized by acute neuropathic pain involving the shoulder and/or upper limbs, followed by weakness and atrophy of muscles innervated predominantly by the C5–C7 roots.1

Pain is typically nocturnal and may persist for approximately 30 days; however, one-third of patients develop chronic pain refractory to conventional analgesics. Nevertheless, most patients experience substantial recovery, with more than 90% achieving functional recovery within three years.1 Idiopathic and hereditary forms have also been described. The latter are approximately ten times less common and are associated with mutations or duplications of the SEPT9 gene on chromosome 17q25 in up to 55% of cases.2 Respiratory manifestations are uncommon in NA and are usually related to the involvement of peripheral nerves other than the brachial plexus. Phrenic nerve involvement resulting in unilateral or bilateral diaphragmatic dysfunction has been reported in approximately 7% of patients, with symptoms including dyspnoea, orthopnoea and sleep disturbances.2 In these cases, the diagnosis relies on clinical suspicion after excluding more common causes of acute diaphragmatic weakness, such as acute cervical spinal cord lesions or transverse myelitis.3, 4, 5 In our patient, the pulmonary embolism was considered most likely to represent an incidental finding or, at most, a minor contributing factor rather than the primary cause of the patient's symptoms.

A potential interaction between mechanical factors (repetitive microtrauma or surgery) and immune-mediated mechanisms, including infections and vaccination, has been proposed as a trigger for NA.6, 7 The precise immunopathogenesis of NA remains incompletely understood, and it is still unclear whether the disease is predominantly mediated by T cells or B cells. The limited histopathological evidence available suggests a T-cell-mediated process, based on the detection of endoneurial T-cell infiltrates.8, 9 Nevertheless, antiganglioside antibodies can be detected in the serum of approximately 26% of affected patients.9 These antibodies are directed against components of the neuronal membrane and are also found in other inflammatory neuropathies. In our patient, positive Mycoplasma IgM serology may support the hypothesis that a recent infectious process triggered an aberrant immune response leading to NA. The recent arthroplasty may also have contributed to disease onset; however, the surgical procedures most frequently implicated as triggers of NA are those involving the cervical region and shoulder girdle.8

Microtrauma affecting the nerve roots in these regions may transiently increase blood-nerve barrier permeability, thereby allowing cellular and humoral components of the immune system to enter the endoneurial space and initiate an autoimmune-inflammatory process.8, 9 We acknowledge several diagnostic limitations. A dedicated phrenic nerve conduction study was not performed, and electromyography was obtained several weeks after symptom onset because of technical constraints. Despite these limitations, the close anatomical and functional relationship between the phrenic nerve and the brachial plexus, repeatedly described in patients with NA, together with the findings from the complementary investigations, strongly supports our diagnostic suspicion. Conventional treatment during the acute phase of NA consists of first-line analgesics and corticosteroids, although there is no conclusive evidence that these interventions modify disease progression.5 In cases of diaphragmatic involvement, treatment is based on respiratory rehabilitation and non-invasive ventilatory support.

We are aware that CPAP was not considered the first-line treatment in these cases, and we planned to switch to noninvasive ventilation in the event of hypercapnia, persistent symptoms, or clinical deterioration.10 The decision was made after presenting the case to a multidisciplinary committee, given that the patient did not present with daytime hypercapnia or any other disease affecting the respiratory system, had no central obesity (body mass index: 23.3 kg/m2), and that the diaphragmatic pathology was potentially reversible. CPAP improved tolerance of the supine position, demonstrated the ability to maintain TcCO2 at normal levels, showed satisfactory control on polysomnography, and resulted in serial clinical and functional improvements. Hence the decision to continue with it. Bilateral diaphragmatic dysfunction secondary to neuralgic amyotrophy is a rare but important cause of acute orthopnea that may be overlooked in the presence of concomitant conditions, such as pulmonary embolism. In patients presenting with shoulder pain, orthopnea, and elevated hemidiaphragms, a high index of suspicion, along with a diaphragmatic ultrasound and an evaluation of the respiratory muscles, can facilitate early diagnosis and appropriate supportive treatment.
