Executive Summary
This report presents the findings from Task 7 of the project, which focused on improving the performance
and reliability of wave energy converters (WECs) under real-world conditions, particularly in the
presence of marine growth (biofouling) and system faults. The work was conducted using the RM3 point
absorber WEC model, a marine current turbine based on the SHARKS project model, and the WEC-Sim
simulation platform, and it included both modeling and control system development.

Marine growth, such as barnacles and mussels, accumulates on submerged surfaces and alters the
hydrodynamic properties of marine energy systems. For point absorber WECs, the research developed a
drag coefficient model relating surface roughness from hard biofouling (mussels, barnacles) to
hydrodynamic losses. The model adapts experimental data for perpendicular flow to the axial flow
conditions relevant for heaving devices. WEC-Sim simulations of the RM3 device swept wave heights
from 1-4 m, periods from 4-16 s, and biofouling protrusion from 0 to 0.1 m. At H=2m and resonant
period (12s), heavy biofouling reduced PTO power by 15% while decreasing peak velocity and
displacement by approximately 10%. Optimal PTO damping was found to decrease with increasing
biofouling, but re-tuning control recovered only about 6% of lost power—demonstrating that biofouling
effects are largely irreducible through damping control alone. Notably, the greatest performance
sensitivity occurs with initial biofouling onset rather than continued growth.

For hydrokinetic turbines, a reinforcement learning approach was developed to maximize energy
generation as biofouling progressively degrades performance. A SARSA algorithm with Gaussian radial
basis function approximation learns optimal damping coefficients as turbine dynamics change over time.
Biofouling effects were modeled as shifts in the power coefficient curve, decreasing both optimal tip-
speed ratio and maximum Cp based on experimental data. The RL agent uses flow velocity as state,
damping coefficient as action, and instantaneous Cp as reward, following an epsilon-greedy exploration
policy. Tested on a year-long Mississippi River flow profile with progressive biofouling growth modeled
by a logistic function, the adaptive controller generated 0.92% more energy than a fixed optimal-TSR
baseline by continuously adapting to the changing plant without requiring explicit biofouling
measurement.

The fault reconnaissance study evaluated failure mechanisms across six WEC subsystems: generator,
power electronics, WEC body, mooring lines, seals, and PTO. Each failure mode was ranked for
probability and severity, with costs quantified based on work time, crew, vessel costs, and lost revenue for
a 100 kW reference device. Biofouling emerged as a high-risk item at $204,000/year due to marine
growth causing up to 45% power reduction. PTO failures present $254,000/year risk due to system
complexity and drydocking requirements. Corrosion was prevalent across components, with seal
degradation at $96,000/year risk. Extreme sea states posed $169,000/year risk to mooring systems.

The outcomes of Task 7 provide actionable insights for the marine energy community. They demonstrate
the need for adaptive control strategies and robust modeling approaches that account for environmental
degradation. The work has been disseminated through technical reports and conference presentations,
including the 2024 American Control Conference, and offers a foundation for future research and industry
adoption.

Introduction
Task 7 of the project aimed to investigate the effects of marine growth and system faults on the
performance and control of marine energy systems. The task was motivated by the need to improve the
reliability and efficiency of wave and current energy converters operating in harsh ocean environments.

The task was divided into three main efforts toward the deliverables: (1) modeling and simulation of
biofouling effects on the RM3 Wave Energy Converter (WEC), (2) development of adaptive control
strategies for the SHARKS Marine Current Turbine (MCT), and (3) a fault reconnaissance study to
identify common failure modes in WEC systems.

In summary, the literature review establishes that biofouling is problematic for marine energy because
high plankton levels—a biofouling indicator—correlate geographically with areas of greatest wave
energy density. Prior studies have found varying impacts: experimental work on the Oyster800 showed up
to 15% power reduction depending on kelp growth location, simulations of a Waves4Power buoy
predicted up to 25% power loss over 25 years with 40% occurring in the first year, while a Lysekil buoy
study found no power impact due to its high damping and mass ratio. The report also summarizes
research on how biofouling affects drag coefficients and adds mass (ranging from 5–40 kg/m² depending
on species).

The simulation study uses WEC-Sim to model biofouling effects on the RM3 reference model,
developing a drag coefficient model based on surface roughness for axially-oriented cylinders. Results
show that increasing roughness from 1 mm to 1 m reduces average PTO power by approximately 7%
under regular seas (T=10 s) and by 11–22% under irregular seas with a Pierson-Moskowitz spectrum,
with the system being most sensitive to initial small perturbations in roughness. These findings have

important implications for WEC controller design, as both surface roughness and added mass must be
accounted for to maintain optimal power extraction over the device's operational lifetime.

The report for Deliverable 7.3.1 as originally submitted is appended.

In summary, the fault reconnaissance study systematically evaluated failure mechanisms across six major
WEC subsystems: generator, power electronic switches, WEC body, mooring lines, O-rings/seals, and
PTO. Each failure mode was ranked on a 1-5 scale for both probability and severity, with severity
quantified in dollars per year based on work time, crew requirements, vessel costs, and lost revenue for a
100 kW reference WEC. Biofouling on the WEC body emerged as a high-risk item at approximately
$204,000/year, driven by inevitable marine growth reaching 100% coverage within 500 days and causing
up to 45% power reduction. PTO failures are a roughly $254,000/year risk due to system complexity
requiring extensive repair time and potential drydocking. The analysis also identified corrosion as a
prevalent failure mechanism across multiple components, with O-ring and seal corrosion presenting
approximately $96,000/year in risk due to accelerated degradation in saltwater environments. Extreme sea
states posed $169,000/year risk to mooring systems.

Deliverable 7.4.1: Report quantifying the difference in performance
between model-based and model-free control in the presence of faults
The report for Deliverable 7.4.1 as originally submitted is appended.

The report summarizes two American Control Conference 2024 papers from Oregon State University
addressing biofouling impacts on marine energy systems and control-based mitigation strategies. The first
paper (Skrovanek & Brekken) uses WEC-Sim to model how biofouling-induced drag affects the RM3
point absorber, finding that at H=2m and the resonant period of 12 seconds, PTO power decreases by
15% and peak velocity/displacement decrease by 10% when roughness increases from zero to 0.1m.
Importantly, the study shows that optimal damping control can recover 5–10% of the lost power,
demonstrating that adaptive control tuning can partially compensate for biofouling effects without
physical cleaning. The second paper (Barton, Brekken, Cao) applies reinforcement learning to marine
hydrokinetic turbines, using a SARSA algorithm with Gaussian RBF features to adaptively adjust
damping coefficients as biofouling shifts the Cp(λ) curve. In a Mississippi River case study, the RL
controller outperformed a fixed non-adaptive controller by nearly 1% annually, approaching the
performance of perfect biofouling compensation.

The deliverable also includes extended work from Sam Barton's MS thesis on life-extending control for
HKT power electronics, which are identified as weak points due to thermal cycling failures. This work
uses a monetary-based reward function balancing energy revenue against converter replacement costs,
with an RL agent adjusting the feedback torque gain k to optimize the tradeoff. Case studies using real
tidal flow and LMP pricing data from 2019 and 2024 showed the RL method reduced power electronics

life consumption by 77–85% with energy generation impacts of 6–25%, resulting in revenue impacts
ranging from -1.3% to +45.4% depending on energy prices.

Deliverable 7.4.2: Submit journal or conference publication on model-
mismatch and fault-tolerant control
There are two main papers summarizing the Task 7 results:
 • D. Skrovanek and T. Brekken, “Impact of biofouling on point absorber wave energy converter
 performance and control,” in 2024 American Control Conference (ACC), pp. 3328–3333, 2024.
 • S. Barton, T. K. Brekken, and Y. Cao, “Reinforcement learning control for enhancing marine
 hydrokinetic turbine energy generation,” in 2024 American Control Conference (ACC), pp.
        1044–1050, 2024.

Those papers are appended to this report.

The Skrovanek and Brekken paper investigates how biofouling affects the performance of point absorber
wave energy converters using WEC-Sim simulations of the RM3 reference model. The authors develop a
drag coefficient model based on experimentally-derived relationships from the literature, relating surface
roughness (average biofouling protrusion k divided by characteristic dimension D) to drag coefficient Cd.
While existing models were developed for flow perpendicular to cylinders, the authors apply the percent
increase in drag to the axial flow case relevant to heaving WECs. The drag force is modeled as
proportional to the square of velocity, and the study examines how this nonlinear effect impacts WEC
dynamics by sweeping wave heights from 1–4 m, periods from 4–16 s, and biofouling protrusion from 0
to 0.1 m.

The simulation results demonstrate that biofouling has significant and quantifiable effects on WEC
performance. At H=2 m and the resonant period of 12 seconds, average PTO power decreases by 15%
when biofouling reaches k=0.1 m, while peak velocity and displacement decrease by approximately 10%.
The study also reveals that optimal damping conditions shift with biofouling: as roughness increases, both
the optimal period and optimal damping coefficient decrease. For example, at H=2 m, the optimal
damping drops from 12,000 kN/m/s with no biofouling to 9,602 kN/m/s at k=0.1 m—a 20% reduction.
Importantly, the power reduction effects are more dramatic at higher wave heights, with losses becoming
increasingly severe as H increases from 1 m to 4 m.

The authors examine whether adjusting control parameters can recover lost power due to biofouling. They
find that re-tuning the PTO damping to the new optimal value for k=0.1 m recovers only about 2 kW,
representing no more than 6% of the overall power reduction—indicating that biofouling's physical
effects dominate over what control adjustments can compensate. The paper notes that while anti-fouling
coatings require impractical yearly application, a more practical approach may be to set the PTO damping
to optimal values for expected maximum biofouling levels after initial deployment years. The authors also
note that biofouling affects factors beyond drag, such as added mass from organisms like mussels (40
kg/m²), which could increase the RM3's mass by over 7% if fully covered, potentially creating additional
performance impacts not captured in this drag-focused study.

 6

The Barton, Brekken, and Cao paper presents a reinforcement learning approach to maximize power
generation from marine hydrokinetic turbines (HKTs) while accounting for the degrading effects of
biofouling. The authors address a key challenge for marine energy adoption: high levelized cost of energy
(LCOE), which can be reduced by maximizing energy extraction. The paper develops a SARSA (state-
action-reward-state-action) algorithm using Gaussian radial basis functions for function approximation,
which learns the optimal damping coefficient to apply to a direct-drive 5 kW turbine based on incoming
flow velocity. The HKT model is derived from experimental data, and biofouling effects are characterized
using a logistic growth model that predicts 100% surface coverage in approximately 400 days for
calcareous fouling (barnacles, mussels). The biofouling shifts the Cp versus TSR curve, reducing
maximum Cp by 0.08 and shifting optimal TSR downward by 0.7.

The RL framework uses flow velocity as the state, damping coefficient as the action, and coefficient of
power (Cp) as the reward signal. The agent employs an ε-greedy policy for balancing exploration and
exploitation, with exploration constrained to within 5 Nm·s/rad of the current damping coefficient to
prevent damaging torque spikes. The SARSA agent was first trained on a 100-day sinusoidal flow profile
without biofouling, converging to a stable policy in approximately 50 days with mean Cp of 0.4419.
Unlike Q-learning methods commonly used in renewable energy applications, SARSA was chosen
because Q-learning tends to diverge when applied with linear function approximation methods.
The trained agent was then tested on a year-long case study using Mississippi River discharge data from a
USGS station in Baton Rouge, with biofouling effects gradually increasing throughout the simulation.

Three control cases were compared: perfect control with complete knowledge of biofouling (Case 1),
optimal TSR control without biofouling compensation (Case 2), and the adaptive SARSA controller
(Case 3). Results showed that the SARSA method generated 0.92% more energy (approximately 58 kWh)
than the optimal TSR controller over the year. The SARSA agent successfully adapted to the changing
plant dynamics caused by biofouling, maintaining higher Cp values especially in the latter half of the case
study when biofouling effects became more severe. The authors note that future work may include other
RL algorithms such as actor-critic methods, hardware-in-the-loop testing, and system-level control for
HKT arrays.

Conclusions
Improved Modeling of Marine Growth Impacts
The project developed quantitative models linking biofouling characteristics to WEC performance
degradation. Using WEC-Sim, researchers simulated the RM3 point absorber under varying degrees of
hard biofouling, establishing a drag coefficient model that relates surface roughness to hydrodynamic
losses. For a cylinder with fineness ratio L/D in axial flow, the drag coefficient follows a sigmoidal
relationship with roughness k/D, allowing prediction of drag forces from measurable biofouling
characteristics. Simulations at H=2m and resonant period showed PTO power decreasing by 15% with
heavy biofouling (k=0.1m), while peak velocity and displacement each decreased by approximately 10%.
For marine hydrokinetic turbines, researchers modeled biofouling's effect on the power coefficient curve
using experimental data showing that blade roughness decreases both optimal tip-speed ratio and
maximum Cp. A logistic growth model captured time-dependent surface coverage, enabling year-long
simulations demonstrating cumulative energy losses for LCOE projections.

Quantitative Assessment of Failure and Fault Modes
The fault reconnaissance study systematically evaluated failure mechanisms across six major WEC
subsystems: generator, power electronic switches, WEC body, mooring lines, O-rings/seals, and PTO.
Each failure mode was ranked on a 1-5 scale for both probability and severity, with severity quantified in
dollars per year based on work time, crew requirements, vessel costs, and lost revenue for a 100 kW
reference WEC. Biofouling on the WEC body emerged as a high-risk item at approximately
$204,000/year, driven by inevitable marine growth reaching 100% coverage within 500 days and causing
up to 45% power reduction. PTO failures are a roughly $254,000/year risk due to system complexity
requiring extensive repair time and potential drydocking. The analysis also identified corrosion as a
prevalent failure mechanism across multiple components, with O-ring and seal corrosion presenting
approximately $96,000/year in risk due to accelerated degradation in saltwater environments. Extreme sea
states posed $169,000/year risk to mooring systems.

Fault Mitigation and Control Strategies
Both studies explored adaptive control to compensate for biofouling-induced model mismatch. For point
absorbers, re-optimizing PTO damping for biofouled conditions recovered 2 kW of a 32 kW loss
(approximately 6% of the degradation), demonstrating that biofouling effects are largely irreducible
through damping control alone. For hydrokinetic turbines, a SARSA reinforcement learning controller
using Gaussian radial basis function approximation was developed that continuously learns optimal
damping coefficients as plant dynamics change. Tested against Mississippi River flow data over one year
with progressive biofouling, the RL controller generated 0.92% more energy than a fixed optimal-TSR
baseline by adapting to the shifting Cp curve without requiring explicit biofouling measurement. This
demonstrates that adaptive control strategies can partially mitigate performance degradation from marine
growth.

 8

Recommendations
Future work should explore more detailed biofouling models that account for species-specific growth
patterns and spatial distribution. Additionally, the RM3 model could consider ballasting, to change the
hydrodynamic behavior of the system in response to the impacts of biofouling. This is a good topic for
future research. Experimental validation of simulation results would strengthen confidence in the models.

Adaptive control strategies, such as reinforcement learning, should be further developed and tested in
hardware-in-the-loop or field environments. These methods show promise for improving performance
under fault conditions.

Design guidelines should incorporate fault reconnaissance findings to improve system resilience.
Maintenance strategies should consider the trade-offs between physical cleaning and control adaptation.

Dissemination of these findings through technical publications and collaboration with industry partners
will help accelerate the adoption of robust control methods in marine renewable energy systems. The two
main published products from the work are the American Controls Conference papers by Skrovanek and
Barton. Both were presented at a special session of the 2024 ACC meeting.

 The effect of biofouling-induced drag forces on the
performance of a point absorber WEC was studied.
Because these drag forces are nonlinear, the dynamics
of the WEC were simulated using the tool WEC-Sim.
In general, biofouling was found to reduce the WEC’s
optimal damping point, power, peak displacement, and
peak velocity. For the operating point of H = 2 m and
at the WEC’s resonant period (12 s), the average PTO
power was found to decrease by 15% after growing a
10−1 m layer of hard biofouling. It was shown that
decreasing the PTO damping by 20% recovers some of
the inevitable power loss due to biofouling but is limited
to only a small percentage of that power loss. At this
same operating point, the peak velocity and peak position
were found to decrease by approximately 10%, with that
decrease being greater at larger wave periods.
