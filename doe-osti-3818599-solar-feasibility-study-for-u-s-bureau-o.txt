Executive Summary
At the request of the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Reclamation), a feasibility study was
conducted to evaluate solar photovoltaics at the Reclamation facilities and lands at the Glen
Canyon National Recreation Area in Page, Arizona. This assessment evaluates photovoltaic (PV)
options to offset energy use at the Reclamation warehouse and to explore larger-scale PV
potential on adjacent federal land.

The solar resource in Page, Arizona offers strong potential for developing solar PV, supported by
high average peak sun hours. Approximately 6.1 hours/day for fixed-tilt systems, increasing to
about 8.3 hours/day with single-axis tracking and 8.6 hours/day with dual-axis tracking reflect a
notably high solar radiation potential due to the region’s clear skies and elevated terrain. Arizona
ranks top among U.S. states in solar irradiance, averaging around 6.54 peak sun hours/day,
making it one of the most favorable locations nationwide for PV deployment. These high peak
sun hours support high solar energy capture. Fixed-tilt systems in Page would perform
comparably to other high-performing systems in Arizona, while single- or dual-axis tracking
could significantly boost energy yield, by around 35–40% more than fixed systems.
These metrics establish Page as an excellent candidate for solar PV development: even
moderately sized systems can deliver substantial annual energy generation, supporting both
commercial and utility-scale applications.

For the Reclamation warehouse in Page, the existing commercial electricity rate structure is
dominated by energy charges, making a simple payback analysis appropriate. A recommended
61-kWDC carport PV system is projected to generate approximately 96,440 kWh annually, or
about 65% of the building’s electricity consumption. With an estimated installed cost of about
$112,000 and annual operations and maintenance costs of roughly $1,050, the first-year total cost
is projected at $113,050. Expected annual energy cost savings of about $8,680 result in an
estimated simple payback period of approximately 13 years. More detailed analysis, such as
incorporating demand charge reductions or conducting life-cycle cost analysis using tools like
NLR’s energy technology techno-economic optimization model, REopt, could further refine
these results.

At the larger land parcel shared by Reclamation and the National Park Service, approximately 25
acres could support a 6.25-MWDC single-axis tracking PV system capable of producing roughly
12,800 MWh per year. With an estimated installation cost of about $10 million and annual O&M
of about $150,000, first-year costs total approximately $10.15 million. Economic performance
was not calculated due to the lack of defined energy valuation for the generated electricity.
Future analysis may also consider ownership and financing structures, including trade-offs
between direct federal purchase and third-party financing models.

This report details the methods used to assess the solar PV potential within the Reclamation
facilities and lands in Page, Arizona, as well as the potential given the existing electricity rates
and available space.

1 Introduction
The City of Page, Arizona and the surrounding federal lands present strong potential for solar
photovoltaic (PV) development due to the region’s high solar irradiance, clear-sky conditions,
and consistently high peak sun hours. Typical fixed-tilt systems in the Page area can access an
average of more than six peak sun hours per day, while single-axis tracking technologies can
achieve significantly higher solar capture. These conditions position Page as an excellent
candidate for both behind-the-meter and larger-scale solar installations capable of delivering
substantial annual energy generation.

Given this context, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Reclamation) requested assistance from
engineers at the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) to conduct a feasibility study to
evaluate solar PV at the Reclamation facilities and lands at Glen Canyon National Recreation
Area, located in Page, Arizona, to support Reclamation energy goals and maximize energy
generation on Reclamation lands. NLR initiated an evaluation of local facilities and land
holdings to identify feasible locations for PV deployment and to determine the economic and
operational benefits of various system configurations. As part of this assessment, NLR and
Reclamation staff conducted a structured review of multiple federal sites in the vicinity of Glen
Canyon Dam. The evaluation included the Reclamation Glen Canyon Warehouse and adjacent
parking structure, lands near the East Gate of the dam, the aggregate supply site, and National
Park Service (NPS) lands west of the dam. Additional areas consisting of narrow right-of-way
“fingers” southwest of the Western Area Power Administration (WAPA) switchyard and
tunnel-access lands east of the Colorado River were also reviewed. These latter sites were
determined unsuitable for PV development due to limited land area, difficult access, and
configurations incompatible with commercial solar arrays.

Staff also examined the potential for floating PV on Lake Powell directly in front of the dam;
however, this concept was deemed impracticable because unrestricted boat access to the dam
face must be maintained. Previous analysis of the Carl Hayden Visitor Center, conducted by
NLR in 2015, similarly concluded that the site offers poor PV viability due to restricted roof
access, shading, and the small usable area available.

As part of a site visit conducted in November of 2024, NLR staff collected information, which
was then used for desktop studies to estimate the PV potential on Reclamation property. This
report summarizes the methodology, assumptions, and results of the analysis for consideration by
Reclamation staff.

2 Methodology
NLR and Reclamation staff evaluated the Reclamation Glen Canyon Warehouse and parking
structure, lands near the East Gate of Glen Canyon Dam, and aggregate supply site and National
Park Service (NPS) lands west of the Dam. Staff also evaluated lands consisting of narrow right
of way “fingers” of land southwest of the Western Area Power Administration (WAPA)
switchyard, and tunnel access land and facilities east of the Colorado River; these sites were
found to be unsuitable for PV. The “fingers” sites are too narrow for commercial PV and have
difficult access, while the tunnel access lands and facilities are too small for commercial PV.
Staff also evaluated floating PV on the water of Lake Powell in front of the dam and found it

 1

unsuitable for floating PV because boat access is required to the front of the dam. The Carl
Hayden Visitor Center was evaluated for PV by NLR in 2015 and found to be a poor application
for PV because of poor roof access, shading, and small available area (950 square feet [ft2]), as
reported in a non-public September 2015 report.

3 Assumptions
This section summarizes assumptions about the site, PV technology, and economics used in this
analysis.

3.1 Site Electricity Consumption and Costs
Electricity consumption and cost data are essential inputs for a solar PV feasibility study because
they determine the baseline energy use the system is intended to offset and establish the
economic value of generated solar energy. Understanding loads, usage patterns, and utility rate
structures, including energy and demand charges, allows engineers to estimate potential bill
savings, size PV systems appropriately, and evaluate the economic performance of different
system configurations. Electricity consumption and evaluation assumptions are listed in this
section.

3.1.1 Electricity Consumption
The warehouse building is located at 640 Haul Road, Page Arizona, 86040. Electricity for the
warehouse building is provided by Page Utility Enterprises (PUE), a not-for-profit, public power
entity owned and operated by the City of Page. The annual energy use of the Warehouse building
was 147,200 kilowatt-hours (kWh) in Fiscal Year 2023.

3.1.2 Warehouse Building Photovoltaic Evaluation
The warehouse building consists of a high bay section with an east-west-facing roof at
approximately 5° pitch with excellent solar exposure and two lower roofs on the east and west
sides of the building with poor solar exposure, as shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. Warehouse building cross-section
 Image from Shane Mower, Reclamation

 2

The roof on the building was installed in 2020 and is in excellent condition. The roof has few
obstructions and would be good for the installation of PV (see Figure 2). The building structure
is robust and could easily support the 4–6 pounds per square foot of a ballasted PV system. A
partially ballasted PV system would require minimal roof penetrations for anchors and the panels
could be arranged to be sloped at 5 degrees facing south.

 Figure 2. Upper warehouse roof looking south

The upper roof is about 48 feet wide by 386 feet long. However, PV panels would need to be set
back 6 feet from the edge per the Occupational Safety and Health Administration’s (OSHA’s)
fall protection requirements. So, the actual OSHA-approved area subtracts 12 feet from each
direction, totaling 13,464 ft2. Accounting for a few obstructions, the usable area for PV is about
13,000 ft2.

The expected annual energy production of a 5-degree-sloped PV system facing south on a roof in
Page, Arizona is 1,581 kWh/kWDC. A 93-kWDC PV system will generate about 147,200
kWh/year, which means the warehouse has net-zero annual energy production and use. For this
site, a 100-kWDC (70-kWAC) PV system should be planned, which will allow the site to cover net
loads even after several years of degradation. The power density of a 5-degree-sloped PV system

 3

is about 12 W/ft2. The area required for a 100-kWDC PV is about 8,333 ft2. The upper roof has
more than enough room for a 100-kWDC PV system. The warehouse has an existing ladder to the
upper roof for PV operation and maintenance (O&M), but a crane would be required to get the
PV system to the upper roof.

The electrical power system in the warehouse building has adequate capacity (and may have
spare breakers) for a 100-kWDC PV system. The warehouse receives power from a 20.78-kilovolt
(kV) to 208-volt (V) PUE step-down transformer. Panel PC1 is the most likely location to feed
the output from the ~70-kWAC inverter. The National Electric Code (NEC) requires that breaker
size must be 125% of the maximum inverter output. A ~250-ampere (A) breaker in a 208-V
three-phase panel will be required.

3.1.3 Warehouse Building Carport Photovoltaic Evaluation
The warehouse building has an existing carport that is ideal for PV installation. Images of the
existing carport are shown in Figure 3.

 Figure 3. Warehouse building carport, looking east

The carport has a heavy-duty metal roof sloped to the south at 5 degrees. The lower roof is 12 ft
9 inches (in.) tall on the high side, and the upper roof is 19 ft 7 in. tall on the high side. The
lower roof is 26 ft wide and 68 ft long (with an area of 1,768 ft2). The upper roof is 35 ft wide
and 47 ft long (with an area of 1,645 ft2). The entire upper roof area can be used to host PV, but
only the ~61-ft west side of the lower roof area is usable for PVs as it typically experiences
morning shading from the upper roof to the east. The total available area for PV is 3,230 ft2.

The PV panels can be directly attached to the metal deck that is sloped at 5 degrees to the south.
Modern PV panels are 22% or more efficient, which is more than 20 W/ft2. Allowing for space
between panels for mounting hardware (19 W/ft2) is reasonable, so the maximum total maximum
power would be 61 kWDC (40 kWAC). A 61-kWDC system would produce about 96,440
kWh/year, which is about 65% of the annual energy use of the warehouse building.

A spare conduit is available from the carport to the warehouse building. Panel PC1 is the most
likely location to feed the output from the ~40-kWAC inverter. The NEC requires that breaker

 4

size must be 125% of the maximum inverter output. A ~140-A breaker in a 208-V three-phase
panel will be required.

3.1.4 Electric Utility Rate
The PUE commercial service with demand meter is used with monthly charges of $35, $5.25/kW
for demand, and $0.0874/kWh for energy. The annual average blended rate is $0.09/kWh. PUE
has a simple net metering rate for PV where all energy that is supplied to the PUE distribution
system is fully credited to the account at the retail rate. A simple solar interconnection
agreement1 is required.

3.1.5 Aggregate Supply Site West of the Glen Canyon Dam
When the hydroelectric dam was being built, the aggregate supply site west of the dam provided
a lot of aggregate for the concrete in the dam. The site is mostly flat and has excellent solar
exposure. The total site area is about 14.7 acres (Figure 4-7). A zero-tilt single-axis tracking PV
is assumed because of the large, flat area available. The power density is about 4 acres/megawatt
(MW). So, a 3.7-MWDC (2.8 MWAC) PV system could be installed. The annual energy
production of the system would be about 7,600 MWh.

Reclamation’s intent is to install a PV system and feed the energy to the dam to offset the dam’s
internal load of about 4 MW, which is currently being fed from hydroelectricity generated by the
dam. All the energy that is generated by the PV can easily be used on site and will free up the
hydroelectricity to be supplied to the grid by Reclamation and WAPA, leveraging the existing
interconnection agreement for the hydroelectricity. A service road connects the site to the dam;
new wire would need to be run along the service road from the aggregate supply site to the dam.

4 Results and Discussion
The PUE commercial service with demand meter is used with monthly charges of $35, $5.25/kW
for demand, and $0.0874/kWh for energy. The annual average blended rate is $0.09/kWh. A
simple payback analysis is adequate for the Reclamation warehouse PV system because net-
metering is available and costs are dominated by the energy charge.

The carport PV system is recommended. A 61-kWDC system would produce about 96,440
kWh/year, which is about 65% of the annual energy use of the warehouse building. The expected
installed cost would be ~$1,832/kWDC, which is ~$112,000. The annual O&M is expected to be
about $17/kW, which is ~$1,050/year. The total first year cost would be about $113,050. The
expected annual energy cost saving is 96,440 kWh/year at $0.09/kWh, which is ~$8,680/yr. The
simple payback is therefore calculated by dividing $112,000 by $8,680/year, which totals 12.9
years. We assume about 13 years.

More complex economics, including demand savings, could be calculated using tools such as
REopt®.5 Life cycle costs include the present value of electric grid purchases as well as PV
capital and O&M costs. The net present value for a particular scenario represents the difference
in life cycle cost savings for that scenario relative to the business-as-usual scenario.

Reclamation and NPS’s available land has an area of about 25 acres. A 6.25-MWDC single-axis
tracking PV system would produce about 12,800 MWh/year. The expected installation cost
would be about $1,576/kWDC, which is ~$10 million. The annual O&M is expected to be about
$24/kW, which is ~$150,000/year. The total first year cost would be about $10.15 million.
Economics are not calculated because the value of energy generated is unknown.

Additional future analysis could also consider the trade-offs between direct purchase and third-
party financing ownership models.
