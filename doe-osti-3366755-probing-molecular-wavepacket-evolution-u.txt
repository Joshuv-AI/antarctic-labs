1 Project Scope
The goal of this project was to develop and demonstrate multidimensional coherent spectroscopy (MDCS)
techniques to fully map wave-packet dynamics in simple molecules. MDCS correlates excitation and emis-
sion frequencies, enabling continuous tracking of wave-packet evolution even when multiple wave-packets
evolve simultaneously. The initial experiments were to be performed using the MDCS apparatus in the PI’s
laboratory to record the evolution of the wave-packet’s energy in molecular iodine. A longer-term goal was
to develop a version compatible with using an X-ray Free Electron Laser (XFEL) as a probe, which would
allow the evolution of the internuclear distance of the wave-packet—not just its energy—to be determined.
 The scientific questions motivating the project were:

 • Can the evolution of the energy of a wave packet in a diatomic molecule be fully mapped using
 multidimensional coherent spectroscopy?
 • Can wave packets on different potential energy surfaces be separated using coherent techniques?
 • Can these methods be adapted to using an XFEL as a probe to map the internuclear distance of the
 wave packet?
 • Do these methods open the door to studying wave packet dynamics in more complex molecules?

2 Summary of Accomplishments
This project began in August 2020, at the height of the COVID-19 pandemic. Progress was significantly
impacted by laboratory access restrictions and, more persistently, by supply chain disruptions that affected
procurement and repair of critical equipment throughout the project period. Despite these challenges, sub-
stantial infrastructure development and preliminary measurements were achieved.

2.1 Laser System Replacement and Commissioning
A critical first step was to replace the existing, outdated laser system with one suited to the proposed exper-
iments. A ytterbium-based bulk optic oscillator/amplifier combined with a non-collinear optical parametric
amplifier (NOPA) was identified as the optimal solution. This system operates at a 1 MHz repetition rate,
matching the planned repetition rate for the future LCLS-II upgrade, and provides greater optical bandwidth
than the previous system.

 Procurement was initiated early in the project; however, delivery was delayed by several months due
to the system being manufactured in Europe and the vendor experiencing supply chain issues. Installation
was further delayed by travel restrictions on vendor field-service personnel. Final installation and commis-
sioning were completed in May 2021, approximately six months behind the original timeline. The system
operated for approximately two years before suffering damage to the nonlinear crystals in June 2023. De-
lays in obtaining replacement crystals (sourced from Lithuania via Austria) meant the system was not fully
functional again until mid-September 2023, resulting in a three-month gap during a critical period of the
project.

2.2 Iodine Cell Design and Fabrication
In parallel with the laser system acquisition, a custom iodine vapor cell was designed and procured. Standard
off-the-shelf iodine cells used for laser stabilization are several centimeters long, which is incompatible
with the crossed-beam geometry of the MDCS experiment. The optimum cell thickness was determined by
balancing the beam crossing region length, vapor density as a function of temperature, and the requirement
of approximately 50% peak absorption to minimize distortion of MDCS spectra. A 0.5 mm thickness was
determined to be optimal; however, fabrication constraints led to the use of a 1 mm cell operated at lower
vapor density controlled by temperature.
 A custom sample holder with integral heater was designed and fabricated in-house. Nichrome wire
wrapped around the cell body near each window provided reliable heating while keeping the windows
hotter than the side arm to prevent condensation. A temperature controller was assembled from off-the-shelf
components. Absorption spectroscopy measurements confirmed that an optical density near 0.3 could be
achieved at a cell temperature of approximately 200◦ C, with the optical density tunable via cell temperature.

2.3 Spectrometer Modifications
The change in ultrafast light source required a separate CW laser for the reference beam used to implement
active path-length locking in the Multidimensional Optical Nonlinear SpecTrometer (MONSTR). A 50 mW
CW frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser was acquired for this purpose. Both the new ultrafast light source and
the reference laser were aligned into the MONSTR, and time-zero for the ultrafast pulses was determined.
 Additional dispersion compensation beyond that built into the NOPA was required to achieve short
pulses at the sample. A home-built prism compressor was implemented to provide reasonably well-compressed
pulses, though residual spatial chirp from alignment issues in the compressor remained under investigation
at the conclusion of the project.

2.4 Preliminary Four-Wave-Mixing Measurements
Initial attempts were made to observe a three-pulse four-wave-mixing signal from the iodine cell. Spectra
measured in the signal direction (ks = −kA + kB + kC ) showed contributions from both the desired non-
linear signal and scattered light from the pump beams. A subtraction procedure was developed to estimate
and remove the scattered light contribution. At the conclusion of the project, work was ongoing to mini-
mize scatter and optimize the four-wave-mixing signal strength and purity to levels sufficient for generating
multidimensional coherent spectra.

3 Technical Challenges and Remaining Issues
Several technical issues were identified that degraded signal quality and prevented the acquisition of full
multidimensional coherent spectra during the project period:

 • The additional path length through the pulse compressor caused beam expansion, resulting in a focal
 spot smaller than designed and hindering optimization of beam overlap at the sample. A telescope to
 reduce and recollimate the transverse beam size was being installed.
 • Residual spatial chirp attributed to the pulse compressor alignment required further optimization.
 • The pulse width at the sample remained longer than transform-limited, necessitating characterization
 via FROG and compensation using additional elements such as chirped mirrors.

4 Impact of COVID-19 Pandemic
The COVID-19 pandemic had a profound and lasting impact on this project. Initial laboratory access restric-
tions delayed early work, but the most significant and persistent effect was on the supply chain. The laser
system delivery was delayed by approximately six months. When the laser system failed in 2023, replace-
ment crystals required months to obtain from international suppliers. These cumulative delays consumed a
substantial fraction of the project period and prevented the project from reaching its primary science goals
within the funded timeline.

5 Outlook
Although the project did not achieve its full scientific objectives due to the extraordinary circumstances de-
scribed above, the infrastructure developed—including the new laser system, custom iodine cell, modified
MONSTR apparatus, and preliminary signal detection—positions the laboratory to pursue these measure-
ments in future work. The technical issues identified at the project’s conclusion are well-understood and
have clear solutions. Once resolved, the apparatus will be capable of producing 2D and 3D coherent spectra
of molecular iodine to map wave-packet dynamics, which remains a compelling scientific goal. The longer-
term vision of combining MDCS with XFEL probes at LCLS to directly measure internuclear distances
during wave-packet evolution remains a promising future direction.
