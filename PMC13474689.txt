Insights into glucomoringin biosynthesis in moringa sprouts through integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses
Authors: Yi Liu, Dejian Huang, Shao Quan Liu, Yuyun Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlucomoringin (GMG) is a distinctive glucosinolate rich in moringa seeds and sprouts. GMG has gained considerable attention as a precursor of moringin (RBITC), a unique isothiocyanate with anticancer potential. However, studies on GMG biosynthesis in moringa sprouts remain scarce, highlighting the needs for investigation. In this study, a tyrosine-associated GMG biosynthetic pathway was proposed based on transcript annotation and metabolite prediction. The candidate genes potentially associated with GMG biosynthesis were tentatively identified, such as CYP79A1, CYP83B1, SUR1, UGT74B1, and SOT16. Two additional genes TGG4 and TGG5 were tentatively identified as candidates possibly involved in the bioconversion of GMG to RBITC. These findings offer valuable insights into GMG biosynthesis and may facilitate the development of strategies to enhance GMG production in moringa sprouts.Keywords: Moringa seed germination, Metabolomics, Transcriptomics, Biosynthesis

Introduction

Glucosinolates (GSLs) are a class of sulfur-rich secondary metabolites omnipresent in Brassicaceae vegetables, including broccoli, cabbage, and cauliflower (Halkier & Gershenzon, 2006). They share a common chemical structural homogeneity characterized by an S-β-d-glucopyranosyl unit anomerically connected to an O-sulfated (Z)-thiohydroximate function, along with a variable side chain (Galuppo et al., 2014). GSLs have been widely recognized for their crucial roles in plant defense and human health (Dinkova-Kostova & Kostov, 2012). Their biological activities are primarily associated with the corresponding isothiocyanates (ITCs) formed through myrosinase-catalyzed hydrolysis (Hopkins et al., 2009; Traka, 2016).

Most ITCs occur as volatile, pungent oils and are chemically unstable (Tumer et al., 2015). However, moringin (4-(α-L-rhamnosyloxy)-benzyl ITC, RBITC), an atypical rhamnose-substituted benzyl ITC derived from its glycosylated precursor glucomoringin (4-(α-L-rhamnosyloxy)-benzyl GSL, GMG) predominantly found in Moringa oleifera Lam. (moringa) seeds, has emerged as a relatively stable analogue (Bennett et al., 2003; Waterman et al., 2014). The retained rhamnose sugar moiety likely contributes to its comparatively high stability and solid appearance (Tumer et al., 2015; Waterman et al., 2014). Apart from its inherent stability, RBITC exhibits strong inhibitory activities against multiple cancer cell lines (e.g., MCF-7, HePG-2, and HCT-116), outperforming the commonly studied benzyl ITC and its derivative, 4-hydroxybenzyl ITC (Lu et al., 2020). RBITC also exhibits a stronger antitumoral activity against myeloma cells than the well-studied ITC sulforaphane, possibly through inhibiting nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) expression (Brunelli et al., 2010).

Given the good stability and promising anticancer effects of RBITC, increasing its level and that of its precursor GMG in moringa seeds is of research interest and practical significance. Controlled germination has been shown to increase GMG content in moringa sprouts and promote myrosinase-mediated conversion to RBITC (Coello et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024). However, the potential biosynthetic pathways of GMG remain largely unexplored. It has been reported that tyrosine may serve as the precursor that initiates GMG biosynthesis in moringa sprouts (Liu et al., 2025). On one hand, tyrosine contains a hydroxyl group at the para (C4) position of the benzene ring, which may correspond to the site of rhamnosyloxy substitution in GMG. On the other hand, tyrosine treatment was found to markedly enhance GMG production and accumulation relative to untreated moringa sprouts (Liu et al., 2024). In another study, Guo and coauthors proposed an alternative pathway, in which phenylalanine serves as the precursor, contributing directly to the formation of GSL core structure, followed by side-chain modification specific to GMG (Guo et al., 2025). The introduction of the rhamnosyloxy group likely involves the hydroxylation at the C4 position of the benzene ring, followed by glycosylation with rhamnose via glycosyltransferases. However, these proposed pathways remain to be verified.

Ultraperformance liquid chromatography quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC-QTOF-MS) is a powerful analytical tool well suited for untargeted plant metabolomic analysis, enabling comprehensive profiling and reliable identification of diverse plant metabolites, which is also helpful for understanding the biotransformation of GMG intermediates during moringa seed germination. Additionally, although more than 110 genes associated with GSL biosynthesis have been identified in Arabidopsis thaliana (Harun et al., 2020), genes associated with GMG biosynthesis in moringa sprouts remain largely unknown. The present study aimed to integrate transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets to identify candidate genes and putative metabolic intermediates associated with GMG accumulation, thereby providing insights into GMG biosynthesis in moringa sprouts.

Materials and methods

Plant material and sprouting conditions

Moringa seeds (PKM-1 variety) were bought from Grenera Nutrients Pvt. Ltd. (Tamil Nadu, India). Moringa seeds were sterilized, soaked in water with/without tyrosine treatment (1 mM) and germinated at 30 °C for 6 days in the absence of light (Liu et al., 2024). Moringa seeds (day 0) and sprouts (day 2, 4, and 6) were randomly collected for RNA extraction.

Total RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

On each sampling day, moringa sprouts were randomly collected and frozen in liquid nitrogen. One gram of each sample was used for total RNA extraction by the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). RNA quality and integrity were evaluated using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 5.5 were used for library construction and RNA sequencing (Fakhrah et al., 2025). Messager RNA (mRNA) was enriched from total RNA using the Poly(T) oligo-attached magnetic beads. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the Fast RNA-seq Library Prep Kit V2 (ABclonal Technology, Woburn, MA, USA), quantified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus-PE150 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Library preparation and sequencing were conducted by NovgeneAIT Genomics Singapore Pte Ltd.

De novo assembly and gene functional annotation

Raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.4) to remove adapter sequences, reads containing more than 10% ambiguous nucleotides, and low-quality reads in which more than 50% of the bases had a quality score below 5. Clean reads were subsequently de novo assembled using Trinity (v2.15.2). To facilitate gene-level expression analysis, assembled transcripts were clustered into gene-level groups using Corset (v1.04) based on shared read alignments and expression patterns.

Gene functional annotation was performed by aligning assembled transcripts against seven public databases, including the NCBI non-redundant protein (NR), NCBI non-redundant nucleotide (NT), Swiss-Prot, Protein Families (Pfam), Eukaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Sequence similarity searches against the NR, Swiss-Prot, and KOG databases were performed using DIAMOND (E-value ≤1e–5), while NT annotation was conducted using BLASTN. Protein domains were identified using HMMER against the Pfam database (E-value ≤1e–2). GO annotations were assigned using Blast2GO based on BLAST results (E-value ≤1e–6), while KEGG Orthology (KO) assignments and pathway mapping were obtained using the KEGG Automatic Annotation Server (KAAS) (E-value ≤1e–5).

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in moringa seeds and sprouts

DEGs between pairwise comparisons were identified using the DESeq2 R package. The Benjamini-Hochberg approach was used to adjust the resulting p values (Zhao et al., 2021). Genes with an adjusted p-value <0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Among these, genes with log2 (fold change) > 1 were regarded as significantly upregulated, whereas those with log2 (fold change) < −1 were regarded as significantly downregulated.

Analysis of GO enrichment and KEGG pathways

GO term enrichment was analyzed using the clusterProfiler R package, considering those with an adjusted p-value <0.05 as significantly enriched. KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using KOBAS to determine significantly enriched pathways associated with DEGs.

Metabolite identification and analysis

Moringa seed germination was conducted following the procedure described in Section 2.1. Both ungerminated and germinated moringa seeds were lyophilized, ground into flour, and stored at −20 °C for untargeted metabolomic analysis.

The methanol-extracted metabolites from moringa seeds and sprouts were analyzed using an ACQUITY UPLC I-Class PLUS system (Waters, Milford, MA) paired with a Waters Xevo G2-XS QTOF-MS. The procedures of metabolite extraction, conditions of metabolite analysis, raw data analysis, and metabolite identification were conducted according to our previous study (Liu et al., 2024). Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to identify the metabolic variations in pairwise comparisons.

Results and discussion

Transcriptome assembly and functional annotation

A total of approximately 493 million raw reads with an average of 23 million reads per sample, were generated from transcriptome sequencing of moringa seeds and sprouts. After quality filtering, approximately 475 million high-quality clean reads were obtained for downstream analysis. Owing to the lack of a publicly available reference genome for moringa, de novo transcriptome assembly was performed using Trinity.

Functional annotation of the assembled unigenes was performed by sequence similarity searches against several public databases. As shown in Fig. S1A, approximately 91% of the annotated unigenes shared more than 60% sequence similarity with known sequences, whereas those with lower similarity may represent distant homologs or previously uncharacterized genes. The E-value distribution of the top hits revealed that approximately 64% of the annotated unigenes exhibited highly significant sequence similarity (E-value <1e–45) (Fig. S1B), indicating a high level of confidence in the functional annotation of the assembled transcriptome.

To characterize the functional composition of the moringa transcriptome, GO terms were assigned to the assembled unigenes based on sequence similarity. As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 26,931 unigenes were assigned 99,550 GO terms, which were classified into 40 functional groups with the three principal GO categories, including biological process (48,982, 49.20%), molecular function (34,215, 34.37%), and cellular component (16,353, 16.43%). Within the biological process category, cellular process (14,846 terms) and metabolic process (13,226 terms) were the two most abundant functional groups, accounting for 57.31% of all biological process annotation, indicating extensive cellular and metabolic activities during moringa sprouting.

Analysis of DEGs and KEGG pathway enrichment

Volcano plots of DEGs were constructed to visualize the overall transcriptomic differences between non-treated moringa sprouts and either ungerminated seeds or tyrosine-treated sprouts. Genes exhibiting significant upregulation and downregulation are shown as red and green dots, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2A–C, germination triggered extensive transcriptional changes in moringa sprouts relative to seeds, with over 8000 genes upregulated and more than 7000 genes downregulated. These results indicate that germination stimulates transcription activities in moringa sprouts, underpinning some key processes during their early development, such as cell division, reserve mobilization, organ formation, and stress protection (Bewley et al., 2012; Núñez-Gastélum et al., 2023). In addition, tyrosine supplementation induced differential expression of approximately 430–600 genes in sprouts harvested on day 2, 4, and 6, compared to their respective counterparts without tyrosine treatment (Fig. 2D–F), which indicates that exogenous tyrosine exerted a comparatively modest impact on the sprout transcriptome, likely fine-tuning genes involved in specific metabolic or signaling pathways, such as the biosynthesis of tyrosine-derived secondary metabolites. For example, a previous study reported that tyrosine treatment significantly increased total GSL content of moringa sprouts in comparison to that of ungerminated seeds (Liu et al., 2024). Similar results have also been reported elsewhere that foliar application of methionine to the cotyledons boosted GSL accumulation in broccoli and rutabaga sprouts by 19% and 84%, respectively (Baenas et al., 2014).

The 20 most significantly enriched pathways ranked by adjusted p-values were selected and are presented in Fig. 3. In the d2-ck vs d0, d4-ck vs d0, and d6-ck vs d0 comparisons, pathways like ribosome, starch and sucrose metabolism, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism were significantly enriched among the upregulated DEGs (Fig. 3A–C). These findings reflect the dynamic and intensive biological processes that occur during germination, where mRNA translation is rapidly activated to synthesize proteins essential for cellular activities, such as cell division and metabolism. Both stored mRNA and newly transcribed ones are translated into enzymes and structural proteins that drive some key processes, including reserve mobilization, energy production, and organ formation, transitioning the seeds from a quiescent to a metabolically active state (Bewley et al., 2012). Additionally, the number of DEGs associated with GSL biosynthetic pathways, phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan metabolism, beta-alanine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, sulfur metabolism, and glutathione metabolism, increased during moringa seed germination. These findings suggest that germination promotes GSL metabolism in moringa sprouts possibly through the activation of genes that regulate amino acid metabolism, which provide precursors for GSL biosynthesis.

Moreover, in the d2-tyr vs d2-ck, d4-tyr vs d4-ck, and d6-tyr vs d6-ck comparisons, tyrosine treatment further promoted the expression of DEGs involved in GSL biosynthesis, tyrosine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 3D–F). In addition, the overexpression of DEGs associated with glutathione metabolism and cysteine metabolism suggests that glutathione and cysteine may serve as sulfur donors for the formation of thiohydroximate, a critical intermediate for the synthesis of GSLs. The enrichment of these pathways partially accounts for the enhanced GSL biosynthesis observed in moringa sprouts (Liu et al., 2024). Given that GSLs are synthesized from amino acids (Andini et al., 2019; Sønderby et al., 2010), identifying candidate genes involved in these metabolic processes is crucial for providing insights into the molecular mechanism governing GSL metabolism in moringa sprouts.

Expression patterns of candidate genes potentially involved in GMG biosynthesis

Pathway enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs involved in tyrosine, phenylalanine, and cysteine metabolism were significantly upregulated. These transcriptional changes may be associated with enhanced precursor availability for secondary metabolite biosynthesis, particularly GSLs, for which amino acids serve as primary biosynthetic precursors. Given that GMG is the dominant GSL in moringa seeds and sprouts (Fahey et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2024), candidate genes potentially associated with its biosynthesis were screened out, and their relative expression levels are shown in Fig. 4.

Expression profile of several key genes potentially involved in glucomoringin biosynthesis and hydrolysis. d0 represents moringa seeds; d2-ck, d4-ck, and d6-ck represent moringa sprouts harvested on day 2, 4, and 6, respectively; d2-tyr, d4-tyr, and d6-tyr represent tyrosine-treated moringa sprouts harvested on day 2, 4, and 6, respectively.

The tentatively identified candidate genes include CYP79A1, CYP83B1, SUR1, UGT74B1, and SOT16, which have also been reported in other plant species, such as A. thaliana and Brassica rapa (Harun et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2023; Zang et al., 2009). Interestingly, these candidate genes were markedly upregulated in moringa sprouts following both germination and tyrosine treatment. Their elevated expression levels possibly resulted in the increased contents of total GSLs and GMG in moringa sprouts, as reported previously (Liu et al., 2024). Moreover, tyrosine treatment generally upregulated the expression of candidate genes potentially involved in the formation of GMG core structure, suggesting a possible contribution to the elevated GMG content in tyrosine-treated sprouts relative to the control (Liu et al., 2024). Therefore, exogenous tyrosine supplementation during moringa seed germination offers a simple and efficient strategy for producing GMG-enriched moringa microgreens.

Furthermore, three myrosinase-regulating genes (e.g., TGG4, TGG5, and TGG6) were tentatively identified in this study. TGG4 and TGG5 are likely functional and may contribute to the enzymatic conversion of GMG to RBITC, whereas TGG6 is considered a pseudogene in A. thaliana and likely produces myrosinase without catalytic activity (Wang et al., 2009). The elevated expression of TGG4 and TGG5 (Fig. 4) is consistent with enhanced GMG hydrolysis and the increased accumulation of RBITC (Liu et al., 2024). These two candidate genes are typically expressed in Arabidopsis roots, where they have been implicated in auxin biosynthesis and root development (Fu et al., 2016). It is implied that they may also play a crucial role in the early developmental processes of moringa sprouts. However, in the present study, RNA was extracted from whole seeds and sprouts rather than from specific tissues. Therefore, tissue-specific RNA extraction and expression analysis are required to confirm whether TGG4/5 are also expressed in moringa roots.

A further consideration is that despite its wide use in non-model organisms, de novo transcriptome assembly still faces some challenges, such as fragmented assemblies, limited functional annotation, and potential biases in transcript quantification (Martin & Wang, 2011). Therefore, the putative roles of these candidate genes involved in GMG biosynthesis and hydrolysis remain to be confirmed. Further studies employing quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and other molecular approaches are needed to validate their functions and to elucidate the effects of tyrosine treatment on their expression patterns.

Proposed biosynthetic mechanism of GMG in moringa sprouts

Untargeted analysis was also conducted to profile metabolic alterations during germination, identify metabolites potentially involved in GMG biosynthesis, and facilitate the integration of metabolomic and transcriptomic datasets for providing insights into GMG biosynthesis. As shown in Figs. 5 and S2, OPLS-DA score plots revealed clear metabolic variations between ungerminated moringa seeds and sprouts, as well as between sprouts with and without tyrosine treatment. It was observed that, in each pairwise comparison, samples were distinctly clustered both along the predictive and orthogonal component, suggesting that both germination and tyrosine treatment led to significant changes in the metabolite profiles. The high R2Y (cum) and Q2 (cum) values (Table S1) indicate the strong discriminatory capacity and reliability of the model. Furthermore, with reference to KEGG metabolic pathways, several intermediate compounds possibly involved in GMG biosynthesis were tentatively screened out (e.g., p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate, p-hydroxybenzyldesulfo-GSL, and p-hydroxybenzyl GSL). These findings strongly suggest that GMG originates from tyrosine, although two other intermediate compounds, including (E)-4-hydroxyphenylacetaldehyde oxime and S-(hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximoyl)-L-cysteine, were not identified in the present study.

Based on the integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses, a tentative biosynthetic pathway of GMG in moringa sprouts is proposed (Fig. 6). Tyrosine serves as the primary amino acid precursor for GMG biosynthesis. The biotransformation from tyrosine to GMG may involve several candidate genes, such as CYP79A1, CYP83B1, SUR1, UGT74B1, and SOT16. Based on the characterized GSL biosynthetic pathways in A. thaliana, CYP79A1 is proposed to initiate the pathway by converting tyrosine to (E)-4-hydroxyphenylacetaldoxime. The resulting aldoxime is subsequently proposed to be oxidized by CYP83B1 to generate either a nitrile oxide or an aci-nitro intermediate, which undergoes glutathione conjugation and is further processed to form S-(hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximoyl)-L-cysteine. SUR1 is then predicted to catalyze C—S bond cleavage, producing p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate and thereby facilitating the downstream biosynthesis of GMG.

Proposed pathways of glucomoringin biosynthesis and hydrolysis in moringa sprouts, with candidate genes included based on transcriptomic analysis. Abbreviations: Glc, glucose; R, rhamnose sugar moiety.

Notably, two putative glycosylation routes leading to GMG formation are proposed. In the first proposed route, the thiohydroximate intermediate is predicted to be converted to p-hydroxybenzyldesulfo-GSL by UDP-glucotransferases of the UGT74 family. The resulting p-desulfo-GSL is subsequently sulfated by sulfotransferases to produce glucosinalbin, which may then undergo side-chain rhamnosylation to form GMG (Müller et al., 2015). However, the genes responsible for this putative side-chain modification step have not yet been identified in moringa. An alternative route may involve the direct rhamnosylation of the thiohydroximate intermediate to generate 4-(α-L-rhamnosyloxy)-benzyldesulfo-GSL, which is subsequently converted to GMG through sulfation. The transcriptomic data presented here provide candidate genes associated with these putative biosynthetic steps. However, the proposed pathways require further validation through gene expression analysis and functional characterization.

Gene-metabolite correlation analysis was further performed to investigate the associations between candidate gene expression and metabolite accumulation during moringa sprouting. As shown in Table 1, GMG was positively correlated with the expression of CYP79A1, TGG4 and TGG5 (p < 0.001), suggesting that these genes are associated with GMG metabolism. Similar positive correlations were observed between RBITC and these candidate genes, supporting their potential involvement in GMG biosynthesis and hydrolysis. In particular, the positive correlation between glucosinalbin and CYP79A1 (p < 0.001) is consistent with previous studies, demonstrating that heterologous expression of CYP79A1 in transgenic A. thaliana resulted in the accumulation of the tyrosine-derived GSL glucosinalbin (Bak et al., 1999; Petersen et al., 2001). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that CYP79A1 may play an important role in the early steps of the proposed GMG biosynthetic pathway. Likewise, the positive correlations of RBITC with TGG4 (p < 0.001) and TGG5 (p < 0.01) are consistent with the proposed involvement of these genes in the conversion of GMG to RBITC. Nevertheless, these associations do not establish casual relationships, and the functions of these candidate genes require further validation through RT-qPCR and functional studies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, untargeted metabolomic analysis revealed that both germination and tyrosine treatment induced significant biochemical changes in moringa sprouts, as evidenced by the clear separation of samples in the OPLS-DA score plots. Several putative metabolic intermediates potentially involved in GMG biosynthesis, including p-hydroxyphenylacetothiohydroximate, p-hydroxybenzyldesulfo-GSL, and p-hydroxybenzyl GSL, were tentatively identified through untargeted metabolomic analysis. Based on these tentatively identified intermediates and integrated transcriptomic data, a putative GMG biosynthetic pathway originating from tyrosine was proposed. Transcriptomic analysis further identified several candidate genes including CYP79A1, CYP83B1, SUR1, UGT74B1, and SOT16 that may be associated with the formation of the GMG core structure. In addition, TGG4 and TGG5 were identified as candidate genes potentially associated with the conversion of GMG to RBITC. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the molecular basis of GMG metabolism in moringa sprouts. Nevertheless, the putative metabolic intermediates identified in this study require structural validation, and the candidate genes associated with GMG biosynthesis and hydrolysiswarrant further functional validation.
