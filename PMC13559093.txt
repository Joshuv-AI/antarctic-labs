Clinical characteristics and prognosis of infant onset inflammatory bowel disease in Chinese children: a 10-year single-center experience
Authors: Junye Du, Dexiu Guan, Xiaolin Ye, Tianzhuo Zhang, Jie Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInfant-onset inflammatory bowel disease (IO-IBD) is a rare, severe subtype of pediatric IBD with insufficient clinical and genetic data in northern China. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics, gene mutation spectrum, treatment responses and long-term prognosis of IO-IBD in children in northern China, and to provide evidence-based basis for the standardized diagnosis and treatment of this disease.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the clinical data of 98 children diagnosed with IO-IBD in Beijing Children’s Hospital from January 2016 to December 2025. According to clinical phenotypes, the patients were divided into Crohn’s disease (CD) phenotype and ulcerative colitis (UC) phenotype. The clinical features, laboratory examinations, treatment regimens and prognoses were compared between the two groups. Whole-exome sequencing or IBD gene panel testing was performed on 77 children to analyze the differences in clinical characteristics and prognoses between monogenic and non-monogenic IO-IBD.ResultsAmong the 98 children with IO-IBD, 64 (65.3%) had CD phenotype and 34 (34.7%) had UC phenotype. The median age of onset in the entire cohort was 4 months. There was no significant difference in the median age of onset between CD and UC phenotype children (5.0 vs. 4.0 months, P=0.16), but the incidence rates of perianal lesions and penetrating disease behavior were significantly higher in the CD group. Among the 77 children who underwent genetic testing, 39 (50.6%) were identified with definite monogenic mutations, of which IL-10RA gene mutations accounted for 84.6% (33/39). Children with monogenic IO-IBD had more severe disease conditions. The median Pediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (PCDAI) score in CD phenotype (60, 49.0–67.5 vs. 32.5, 22.5–42.5; P<0.01), median Pediatric Ulcerative Colitis Activity Index (PUCAI) score in UC phenotype (47.5, 35.0–65.0 vs. 35, 25.0–45.0; P=0.02) and mortality rate (28.21%, 14.7–45.3% vs. 1.72%, 0.04–9.2%; P<0.01) were significantly higher than those in non-monogenic children. Among the 33 children with IL-10RA gene mutations, 6 successfully underwent allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), with an overall survival rate of 100% (54.1–100%). For the remaining 27 patients who did not receive HSCT, 14 died during follow-up, corresponding to a mortality rate of 51.9% (32.0–71.3%).ConclusionsIO-IBD in children in northern China is predominantly CD phenotype with a high proportion of monogenic causes, among which IL-10RA mutations are dominant. Children with IL-10RA mutations have more severe disease and higher mortality rates, and early HSCT can significantly improve their prognosis. It is recommended that all children with IO-IBD should routinely undergo IL-10RA gene testing, and HSCT indications should be evaluated as early as possible after diagnosis.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This 10-year study enrolled 98 infant-onset inflammatory bowel disease (IO-IBD) cases, the largest well-characterized cohort in northern China.

• Monogenic variants account for 50.6% of genetically tested cases, with IL-10RA mutations as the dominant etiology (84.6% of monogenic cases).

• Monogenic IO-IBD presents more severe phenotypes and significantly higher mortality than non-monogenic disease.

• Early hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) achieves 100% overall survival in IL-10RA-deficient patients, versus 51.9% mortality in non-transplant cases.

What is known and what is new?

• IO-IBD is a severe early-onset subtype with strong genetic predisposition; IL-10RA mutation is prevalent in East Asian populations, and HSCT is curative for IL-10 pathway deficiency.

• This study first systematically presents 10-year clinical data of IO-IBD in northern China, confirms predominant Crohn’s disease phenotype, and identifies perianal lesions and penetrating behavior as clinical warning signs for monogenic etiology.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Routine IL-10RA genetic testing should be performed for all infants with IBD onset before 2 years of age.

• Patients with confirmed IL-10RA deficiency should receive early HSCT eligibility assessment to improve prognosis.

• A national multicenter IO-IBD registry is recommended to optimize diagnosis and treatment strategies.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of chronic idiopathic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract with a multifactorial etiology that affects individuals across all age groups. It can involve the ileum, rectum, colon, and even the entire digestive tract, and includes Crohn’s disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC), and IBD-unclassified (IBD-U) (1). Currently, pediatric IBD with onset before 6 years of age is defined as very early-onset IBD (VEO-IBD), among which cases with onset before 2 years of age are classified as infant-onset IBD (IO-IBD), accounting for only 1% of all pediatric IBD patients (2). Accumulating evidence indicates that compared with late-onset IBD (LO-IBD) (onset between 6 and 16 years of age), VEO-IBD is characterized by predominant colonic involvement and distinct epidemiological features, with a significantly stronger genetic predisposition. However, epidemiological data on VEO-IBD, particularly IO-IBD, remain scarce, especially in developing and newly industrialized countries (3-5). Further elucidation of the clinical characteristics and disease course of patients across different regions and ethnicities would help delineate the full clinical spectrum of this condition, and the accumulation of comprehensive data would improve the accuracy of diagnosis and treatment. Therefore, the present study aimed to analyze the clinical characteristics and disease progression of pediatric IO-IBD patients in China to provide evidence-based insights for its diagnosis and management. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0540/rc).

Methods

Study design and patients

This was a single-center retrospective study that enrolled children diagnosed with IO-IBD in Beijing Children’s Hospital from January 2016 to December 2025. The diagnosis was established based on endoscopy, histopathology and comprehensive clinical evaluation.

Inclusion criteria: (I) meeting the diagnostic criteria of the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Inflammatory Bowel Disease [2019] (6); (II) age at onset <2 years; (III) complete clinical data available.

Exclusion criteria: (I) intestinal inflammation caused by infectious, eosinophilic or other non-IBDs; (II) incomplete clinical data; (III) refusal of participation by patients or their guardians.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2026-E-149-R), which waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of the study. Clinical data of the enrolled children at disease onset were obtained by reviewing medical records. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

According to the Montreal classification, CD was classified by disease location into ileal (L1), colonic (L2), ileocolonic (L3) and upper gastrointestinal (UGI) tract (L4); and by disease behavior into non-stricturing non-penetrating (B1), stricturing (B2), penetrating (B3) and perianal disease (P). Disease activity severity was graded as mild (S1) or moderate/severe (S2) using the Pediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (PCDAI). UC was classified by disease extent into proctitis (E1), left-sided colitis (E2) and pancolitis (E3). Disease activity severity was graded as mild (S1), moderate (S2) or severe (S3) using the Pediatric Ulcerative Colitis Activity Index (PUCAI) (7-9). Disease severity was defined by PCDAI for CD phenotype and PUCAI for UC phenotype, respectively.

IO-IBD with definite pathogenic or likely pathogenic gene mutations (classified according to the ACMG 2015 standards) identified by genetic testing was defined as monogenic IO-IBD. For genetic subgroup comparisons between monogenic and mutation-negative IO-IBD, patients without genetic testing and those carrying only variants of uncertain significance (VUS) were excluded, as they could not be definitively assigned to either group. Intestinal-related surgeries included intestinal resection, enterostomy and total colectomy, but excluded perianal abscess incision and drainage or seton placement.

Conventional therapy was defined as first-line standard treatment regimens for IO-IBD in this cohort, exclusively consisting of exclusive enteral nutritional support, 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) preparations, and systemic corticosteroids for induction and maintenance of remission.

Long-term follow-up was conducted through outpatient reviews and telephone interviews to record treatment regimen adjustments, complications and final outcomes. Prognostic endpoints: (I) stable disease: no corticosteroid treatment required, only 5-ASA or thalidomide for maintenance of remission; (II) active disease: requiring continuous corticosteroids to control inflammation, or PCDAI score ≥10 or PUCAI score ≥10 despite treatment with immunosuppressants, biologics and 5-ASA; (III) lost to follow-up; (IV) death.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. For descriptive statistics, all effect estimates are presented with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Normally distributed data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR), with 95% CIs for medians derived using the non-parametric percentile method. Categorical data are described as frequencies and percentages (n, %), with 95% CIs for proportions calculated via the Clopper-Pearson exact binomial method, which is the gold standard for small-sample studies and provides robust interval estimates. For comparative analyses, normally distributed continuous variables between two groups were compared using the independent samples t-test; non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test; and categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This was a single-center retrospective study. A total of 98 children diagnosed with IO-IBD were enrolled, who received comprehensive clinical, endoscopic and histopathological evaluations at Beijing Children’s Hospital between January 2016 and December 2025. All participants met the diagnostic criteria specified in the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Inflammatory Bowel Disease [2019], and their age at onset was younger than 2 years.

Genetic testing was performed in 77 of the 98 enrolled IO-IBD patients. Thirty-nine patients (39.8% of the entire cohort; 31 CD, 8 UC) were diagnosed with monogenic IO-IBD, with IL10RA mutations being the predominant cause (33 cases, 84.6% of monogenic cases, 33.7% of the entire cohort; 29 CD, 4 UC). Other monogenic mutations included TTC37 (n=2), LRBA (n=1), XIAP (n=1), and TTC7A (n=1). Two additional CD patients harbored compound genetic alterations: one with concurrent NFKB1, IL10RA, and DGAT1 variants, and another with an MT-ND mutation plus a suspected IL10RB mutation.

Thirty patients (30.6% of the entire cohort; 13 CD, 17 UC) had no detected monogenic mutations, while 21 patients (21.4%; 18 CD, 3 UC) did not undergo genetic testing. The remaining 8 patients (8.2%) carried other genetic variants, comprising 2 CD patients (1 with NOD2 mutation, 1 with IL23R abnormality plus TSC2 heterozygous mutation) and 6 UC patients (1 each with BCL11B/IRF5, NR3C2 heterozygous mutation, JAK3 variant, AIRE plus AR variants, ITGB3/TNFRS/NFKB2 variants, and IL23R heterozygous mutation).

Among the entire cohort, 11 patients were lost to follow-up, and the remaining 87 patients had a median follow-up duration of 2.38 years (range, 0.1–9.94 years). Stratified by phenotype, the median follow-up duration was 2.42 years (range, 0.10–9.94 years) for the CD group and 2.19 years (range, 0.21–8.76 years) for the UC group, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P=0.33). The median age at disease onset was 4 months (range, 0–23 months), with 78 patients (79.6%) presenting with disease onset before 1 year of age. The median diagnostic delay was 3 months (range, 0.13–36 months).

Regarding gender distribution, there were 52 males (53.1%) and 46 females (46.9%) in the entire cohort, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.13:1. The male-to-female ratio was 1.21:1 (35/29) in the CD group and 1:1 (17/17) in the UC group, and no statistically significant difference in gender distribution was observed between the two groups (P=0.66). With respect to family history, 8 patients (8.2%) had a family history of IBD in first- or second-degree relatives, including 6 patients (9.4%) in the CD group and 2 patients (5.9%) in the UC group, with no significant intergroup difference (P=0.71). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference in age at disease onset between the CD and UC groups (median 5.0 vs. 4.0 months, P=0.16).

Clinical manifestations

The most common clinical manifestations in the entire cohort were diarrhea (93/98, 94.9%), bloody stool (81/98, 82.7%), and fever (76/98, 77.6%). The incidence of bloody stool was slightly higher in the UC group than in the CD group (29/34, 85.3% vs. 52/64, 81.3%). Other common manifestations included failure to thrive/weight loss (12/98, 12.2%), growth retardation (6/98, 6.1%), abdominal pain (6/98, 6.1%), vomiting (4/98, 4.1%), and abdominal distension (1/98, 1.0%). Perianal lesions were exclusively observed in CD patients (35/64, 54.7% of CD group, 35.7% of entire cohort), while recurrent infections occurred only in UC patients (2/34, 5.9% of UC group, 2.0% of entire cohort). Less common manifestations included oral ulcers (22/98, 22.4%), skin rash (31/98, 31.6%), arthritis (6/98, 6.1%), liver function impairment (1/98, 1.0%), and skin ulcer (1/98, 1.0%), with arthritis affecting 4 CD patients (6.3%) and 2 UC patients (5.9%).

In the CD subgroup, monogenic CD patients had significantly higher incidences of perianal lesions (90.3% vs. 15.4%, P<0.001) and skin rash (61.3% vs. 15.4%, P=0.008) compared with non-monogenic CD patients. The incidence of oral ulcers was also higher in monogenic CD patients (48.4% vs. 15.4%), with a borderline statistically significant difference (P=0.050, exact P=0.0496). No significant differences were observed in diarrhea, bloody stool, fever, abdominal pain, vomiting, failure to thrive/weight loss, growth retardation, or arthritis between the two CD subgroups. In the UC subgroup, monogenic UC patients had a significantly higher incidence of anemia than non-monogenic UC patients (50.0% vs. 0.0%, P=0.006). Although growth retardation was more common in monogenic UC patients (37.5% vs. 5.9%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.08). No significant differences were found in other clinical manifestations between the two UC subgroups.

Endoscopic findings and clinical assessment

Ninety-seven patients underwent gastroscopy and colonoscopy.

Disease location

According to the Paris classification, among CD patients, 49 (76.6%) had isolated colonic involvement (L2), 13 (20.3%) had ileocolonic involvement (L3), 1 (1.6%) had isolated UGI tract involvement, and 1 (1.6%) had no documented intestinal segment involvement. Thirty-six CD patients (56.3%) had concomitant UGI involvement, including stomach (29/64, 45.3%), duodenum (19/64, 29.7%), and esophagus (6/64, 9.4%). Terminal ileum involvement was observed in 13 patients (20.3%). The most commonly involved colonic segments in CD were the sigmoid colon (61/64, 95.3%), rectum (58/64, 90.6%), and descending colon (57/64, 89.1%), followed by the transverse colon (53/64, 82.8%) and ascending colon (48/64, 75.0%).

Among UC patients, 29 (85.3%) had extensive colitis (E3), 4 (11.8%) had left-sided colitis (E2), and no patients had proctitis only (E1). One UC patient did not undergo endoscopy due to young age and severe illness. All 31 UC patients (91.2%) had involvement of the rectum, sigmoid colon, and descending colon; 28 (82.4%) had transverse colon involvement, and 24 (70.6%) had ascending colon involvement.

Disease behavior and severity

In the CD group, 37 patients (57.8%) had inflammatory disease behavior (B1), 4 (6.3%) had stricturing disease (B2), and 23 (35.9%) had penetrating disease (B3). Thirty-five CD patients (54.7%) had concomitant perianal disease (P).

Regarding disease severity, based on the PCDAI, 8 CD patients (12.5%) had mild disease, 14 (21.9%) had moderate disease, and 42 (65.6%) had severe disease. Based on the PUCAI, 13 UC patients (38.2%) had mild disease, 18 (52.9%) had moderate disease, and 3 (8.8%) had severe disease.

Genetic subgroup analysis

The median follow-up duration was comparable between the monogenic and non-monogenic IO-IBD subgroups included in the prognostic analysis (1.97 vs. 2.56 years, P=0.17). Regarding overall survival outcomes, monogenic IO-IBD was associated with a significantly elevated mortality rate compared with non-monogenic IO-IBD. A total of 11 deaths occurred among 39 monogenic patients (28.21%, 14.7–45.3%), while only 1 death was recorded in 58 non-monogenic patients (1.72%, 0.04–9.2%), with a statistically significant intergroup difference (P<0.01).

In the CD subgroup, monogenic CD patients (n=31) had L2 and L3 involvement in 24 (77.4%) and 6 (19.4%) cases, respectively, while non-monogenic CD patients (n=13) had L2 and L3 involvement in 11 (84.6%) and 1 (7.7%) cases, respectively, with 1 patient (7.7%) having isolated UGI involvement. Monogenic CD patients had a significantly higher proportion of penetrating disease behavior (18/31, 58.1% vs. 2/13, 15.4%; P=0.02) and more frequent perianal disease (28/31, 90.3% vs. 2/13, 15.4%; P<0.001) compared with non-monogenic CD patients. No significant difference was observed in PCDAI severity distribution between the two CD subgroups (P=0.79).

In the UC subgroup, monogenic UC patients (n=8) had E3 and E2 involvement in 6 (75.0%) and 1 (12.5%) cases, respectively, while non-monogenic UC patients (n=17) had E3 and E2 involvement in 15 (88.2%) and 2 (11.8%) cases, respectively. No significant differences were found in disease extent distribution or severity between the two UC subgroups.

Laboratory parameters

CD patients had significantly higher C-reactive protein (CRP) levels than UC patients (median, 32.5 vs. 10.0 mg/L; P=0.03). Elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), white blood cell count and platelet count were also observed in the CD group (23.0 vs. 14.0 mm/h, P=0.048; 13.59×109/L vs. 10.57×109/L, P=0.02; 517×109/L vs. 417×109/L, P=0.003). No significant differences were found in hemoglobin (98.5 vs. 96.5 g/L, P=0.98) and albumin (33.55 vs. 33.95 g/L, P=0.73) between the two groups.

Fecal calprotectin was tested in 63 patients, among whom 57 (90.5%) had elevated levels, and 44 (69.8%) exceeded the upper detection limit (>1,800 µg/g). Fecal calprotectin was significantly higher in CD patients than in UC patients (median, 1800 vs. 953 µg/g; P=0.001).

Stratified analysis by genetic status showed that monogenic CD patients presented with higher CRP (median, 47.0 vs. 10.0 mg/L; P=0.01) and lower hemoglobin (96.0 vs. 112.0 g/L; P=0.01) compared with non-monogenic CD patients. No intergroup differences were detected in ESR, white blood cell count, platelet count, albumin and fecal calprotectin.

In the UC cohort, monogenic cases had higher CRP levels than non-monogenic counterparts (median, 35.6 mg/L vs. 10.0 mg/L; P=0.03), while all other laboratory markers were comparable between the two subgroups.

Treatment and outcomes

In this study, the children received a tiered treatment including basic nutritional support, 5-ASA, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, biologics, and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), with overlap between different treatment methods.

Treatment regimens

Overall, 45 patients (45.9%) received conventional therapy (27 CD, 18 UC; P=0.40). 5-ASA was used in 28 patients (28.6%), with a higher but non-significant rate in UC than CD (41.2% vs. 21.9%, P=0.06). Corticosteroids (34.7%, 34/98) and thalidomide (30.6%, 30/98) were used with comparable rates between groups (both P>0.05). Azathioprine/6-mercaptopurine and tacrolimus/cyclosporine were each administered to 3 patients (3.1%).

Biologic therapy was given to 27 patients (27.6%, 17 CD, 10 UC; P=0.81). Infliximab was the first-line biologic in 25 patients (92.6%), followed by adalimumab and vedolizumab (1 each, 3.7%). Nine patients (33.3%) switched biologics due to allergy (n=3), loss of response (n=4), or inadequate efficacy (n=2), with no intergroup difference (P>0.99). Seven patients (7.1%, 6 CD, 1 UC) underwent allogeneic HSCT.

Genetic subgroup analysis

Monogenic CD patients had significantly lower rates of conventional therapy (22.6% vs. 69.2%, P=0.006), including 5-ASA (3.2% vs. 38.5%, P=0.006) and corticosteroids (22.6% vs. 61.5%, P=0.02), but higher thalidomide use (41.9% vs. 7.7%, P=0.04) compared with non-monogenic CD patients. Biologic use was comparable between the two CD subgroups (P=0.65). No significant differences in treatment patterns were observed between monogenic and non-monogenic UC patients.

Treatment outcomes

Among 45 patients on conventional therapy, 23 (51.1%) achieved stable disease, 9 (20.0%) had active disease, 6 (13.3%) were lost to follow-up, 6 (13.3%) died, and 1 (2.2%) discontinued treatment. For 30 thalidomide-treated patients, 19 (63.3%) had stable disease, 4 (13.3%) active disease, 4 (13.3%) lost to follow-up, and 3 (10.0%) died. Of the 27 biologic recipients, 22 (81.5%) had stable disease, 3 (11.1%) active disease, and 2 (7.4%) lost to follow-up, with no deaths. All 7 patients who underwent or were scheduled for HSCT had no deaths or loss to follow-up, with 6 achieving stable disease (85.7%, 42.1–99.6%) and 1 remaining with active disease (14.3%, 0.4–57.9%).

Surgical interventions

Eight patients (7.8% of the entire cohort) underwent intestinal surgeries. Among them, 5 had monogenic VEO-IBD, 7 had CD, and 1 had UC. Surgical procedures included 4 ileostomies, 1 colostomy, 1 intestinal resection, 1 total colectomy, and 1 surgery for intestinal obstruction in a UC patient. The indications for surgery were intestinal obstruction (n=3), intestinal perforation (n=3), and massive intestinal bleeding (n=2).

Discussion

IO-IBD, defined as IBD with onset before 2 years of age, represents the earliest and most genetically enriched subgroup within VEO-IBD. Compared with later-onset pediatric IBD, IO-IBD is more likely to present with severe intestinal inflammation, atypical extraintestinal manifestations, poor response to conventional therapy and underlying inborn errors of immunity (10). Data from Asian populations remain limited, and clinical characteristics may differ across ethnic and geographic backgrounds. This single-center cohort included 98 children diagnosed from January 2016 to December 2025 and analyzed their clinical phenotype, genetic spectrum, treatment pattern and outcomes, providing evidence for the diagnosis and management of IO-IBD in northern China.

Phenotypic characteristics of IO-IBD in northern China

The disease phenotype in this cohort was dominated by CD. Among 98 children, 64 (65.3%) had a CD phenotype and 34 (34.7%) had a UC phenotype. This distribution was close to that reported in a Japanese single-center cohort (CD 64.8% vs. UC 35.2%) (11), but differed from reports from Southeast Asia and India, where UC accounted for a larger proportion of VEO-IBD cases (12-14). These differences suggest that the phenotype distribution of early-onset IBD may be affected by both genetic background and regional environmental exposure. The similarity between the present cohort and the Japanese cohort may reflect shared East Asian genetic susceptibility, whereas South and Southeast Asian cohorts may have different host and environmental determinants.

The median age at onset was 4.0 months, and 78 children (79.6%) developed symptoms before 1 year of age. No significant difference in age at onset was observed between CD and UC phenotypes (5.0 vs. 4.0 months, P=0.16), indicating that very early presentation was common in both groups. CD lesions were mainly colonic: isolated colonic disease (L2) was present in 49/64 patients (76.6%), while ileocolonic disease (L3) was present in 13/64 (20.3%). UGI involvement was recorded in 36/64 CD patients (56.3%), and perianal disease was observed in 35/64 (54.7%). UC was also characterized by extensive involvement, with extensive colitis (E3) in 29/34 patients (85.3%) and no case limited to proctitis alone. CD patients showed a stronger inflammatory burden than UC patients, including higher rates of fever (84.4% vs. 64.7%, P=0.041) and higher CRP, ESR, white blood cell count, platelet count and fecal calprotectin levels. Penetrating disease behavior was observed in 23/64 CD patients (35.9%). These findings indicate that IO-IBD in this cohort often involved a wide intestinal extent and required careful evaluation for perianal disease, penetrating complications and systemic inflammation from the earliest stage.

High proportion of monogenic IO-IBD and predominance of IL-10RA mutations

A systematic review of 750 monogenic IBD patients worldwide published in 2022 showed that IL-10RA mutation is the most common cause of monogenic IBD worldwide, but the proportion varies greatly among different regions, with East Asia having a significantly higher proportion than other regions (15,16).

Among the 77 children who underwent genetic testing in our research, 39 (50.6%) were diagnosed with monogenic IO-IBD; this accounted for 39.8% of the entire cohort. The proportion was higher than that reported in many Western and other Asian cohorts (17,18), supporting the concept that the earlier the disease onset, the greater the likelihood of a monogenic etiology. IL-10RA mutations were the leading genetic cause, accounting for 33/39 monogenic cases (84.6%) and 33.7% of all enrolled patients. Other definite monogenic causes included TTC37, LRBA, XIAP and TTC7A mutations, but each was rare. The marked predominance of IL-10RA mutations highlights a genetic pattern that appears particularly important in Chinese children with IO-IBD.

Genetic status was associated with distinct clinical features, especially in the CD phenotype. Compared with mutation-negative CD patients, monogenic CD patients had markedly higher rates of perianal lesions (90.3% vs. 15.4%, P<0.001), penetrating behavior (58.1% vs. 15.4%, P=0.02), and skin rash (61.3% vs. 15.4%, P=0.008). Oral ulcers were also more frequent in monogenic CD (48.4% vs. 15.4%), with borderline significance (P=0.050). Laboratory data showed higher CRP (47.0 vs. 10.0 mg/L, P=0.01) and lower hemoglobin (96.0 vs. 112.0 g/L, P=0.01) in monogenic CD. In UC phenotype, monogenic cases had a higher rate of anemia (50.0% vs. 0.0%, P=0.006) and higher CRP levels (35.6 vs. 10.0 mg/L, P=0.03). These findings indicate that perianal disease, penetrating complications, rash, anemia and elevated inflammatory markers should raise suspicion for a monogenic cause.

For genetic subgroup comparisons of CD phenotype, 31 monogenic CD cases and 13 mutation-negative CD cases were included, while 18 patients without genetic testing and 2 patients with only VUS were excluded, accounting for 31.3% of the entire CD cohort. To assess potential selection bias, we compared clinical features between genetically tested (n=46) and untested (n=18) CD patients. No significant difference was observed in median age at onset (5.0 vs. 4.5 months, P=0.62) or sex distribution between the two groups. However, tested patients had significantly higher rates of perianal lesions (67.4% vs. 22.2%, P<0.001), penetrating disease behavior (43.5% vs. 11.1%, P=0.02), and severe disease assessed by PCDAI (82.6% vs. 22.2%, P<0.001), indicating a selection bias toward more severe cases undergoing genetic testing. But this does not affect the clinical significance of the advantage of IL-10RA mutation. IL-10 signaling is essential for intestinal immune homeostasis; loss-of-function mutations in IL-10RA can lead to uncontrolled activation of intestinal immune cells and severe colitis in infancy. Because conventional anti-inflammatory or immunosuppressive therapies cannot correct the underlying immune defect, early genetic testing should be incorporated into the diagnostic workflow for all children with IO-IBD (19). In Chinese infants, IL-10RA testing should be prioritized, particularly in those with CD phenotype, neonatal or early infancy onset, perianal lesions, penetrating disease behavior, recurrent systemic inflammation, skin manifestations or family history.

Treatment response, prognosis and the role of HSCT

The treatment data reflected both the severity of IO-IBD and the challenge of medical management in this age group. Conventional therapy was used in 45/98 patients (45.9%), including 5-ASA in 28 (28.6%) and corticosteroids in 34 (34.7%). Thalidomide was used in 30 patients (30.6%). Biologic therapy was administered to 27 patients (27.6%), mostly infliximab as the initial biologic agent (25/27, 92.6%). Biologic switching occurred in 9/27 patients (33.3%) because of allergy, loss of response or inadequate efficacy, with no significant difference between CD and UC phenotypes. A study by Kerur et al. showed that the conversion rate of biologics in children with CD type is higher than that in those with UC type, suggesting that CD type VEO-IBD is more prone to treatment resistance (20). Among biologic-treated patients, 22/27 (81.5%) achieved stable disease and no deaths were recorded, although interpretation should consider the retrospective design, treatment overlap and possible selection bias.

Outcomes differed substantially according to genetic etiology and access to HSCT. Seven patients underwent or were scheduled for allogeneic HSCT, including 6 CD patients and 1 UC patient; no deaths or loss to follow-up occurred in this group, and 6/7 (85.7%) achieved stable disease. Among 33 children with IL-10RA mutations, 6 successfully underwent HSCT and all survived, whereas the mortality rate among those who did not receive HSCT reached 51.9%. These data support HSCT as the key disease-modifying treatment for IL-10RA-deficient IO-IBD and suggest that delayed referral may expose patients to irreversible complications, severe infections, intestinal perforation, bleeding or death.

Taken together, the clinical pathway for IO-IBD in China should emphasize early recognition, early genetic testing and early treatment stratification. A retrospective study conducted in 2013 included 40 patients with VEO-IBD, of whom 9 had IL-10/IL-10R deficiency. The transplant group had a good prognosis in all 3 cases, while 1 of the 6 non transplant cases died and the remaining 5 did not achieve clinical remission (21). Children without a confirmed monogenic cause may be managed with conventional step-up or biologic-based strategies according to disease severity and response. Children with confirmed IL-10RA deficiency require prompt multidisciplinary evaluation for HSCT rather than prolonged ineffective immunosuppression. Establishing a multicenter IO-IBD registry integrating phenotype, genotype, treatment and long-term outcomes would help refine risk stratification and develop diagnostic and therapeutic recommendations tailored to Chinese children.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective study. All enrolled patients were recruited from our national children’s medical center, which may lead to referral bias. Accordingly, the findings cannot fully reflect the characteristics of IO-IBD in the general pediatric population across China. Second, genetic testing was not performed in some children due to economic constraints, parental refusal, or incomplete early workflow, and 31.3% of CD patients were excluded from genetic subgroup analysis. Clinical prioritization of genetic testing for patients with more severe disease and typical monogenic phenotypes introduced a selection bias, which may have led to overestimation of the proportion of monogenic etiology in the overall IO-IBD cohort. However, this bias does not affect the core conclusions regarding the clinical features and prognostic significance of confirmed monogenic cases. Third, the follow-up duration for a subset of patients was relatively short; thus, their long-term prognosis requires further long-term observation.

Conclusions

This study represents the largest single-center cohort study of IO-IBD conducted in northern China to date. Our results demonstrate that IO-IBD in this region is predominantly of CD phenotype, characterized by early disease onset and more extensive intestinal involvement. Monogenic IO-IBD accounts for a substantial proportion of cases, with IL-10RA mutations being the most prevalent genetic etiology. Children harboring IL-10RA mutations exhibit an extremely poor response to conventional medical therapies and a significantly elevated mortality rate, whereas early HSCT can markedly improve their long-term prognosis. The findings of this study provide critical evidence-based support for the early diagnosis, optimized genetic testing strategies and individualized treatment of IO-IBD in China, and strongly underscore the necessity of universal IL-10RA gene testing in all children presenting with IO-IBD.
