Nasopharyngeal carcinoma with osseous metastases in an 8-year-old girl: a case report
Authors: Alaa Eid Alfarhan, Doha Mohammed Alhawarnh, Mouhammed Sleiay, Mohammad Kokash, Mhd Omran Khedr, MHD Ali Othman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is exceptionally rare in young children, particularly those under 10 years of age. Presentation with widespread osseous metastases at diagnosis is even more uncommon, posing diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.Case presentation:An 8-year-old girl presented with an 8-month history of a gradually enlarging right cervical mass associated with fever, weight loss, night sweats, generalized bone pain, and fatigue. Examination revealed a firm, nontender neck mass of size 10 × 8 cm with bilateral cervical lymphadenopathy. Laboratory findings showed elevated inflammatory markers and LDH. Imaging demonstrated a primary nasopharyngeal tumor with extensive nodal involvement and osseous metastases confirmed by bone scan. Biopsy revealed metastatic nonkeratinizing nasopharyngeal carcinoma.Clinical discussion:The patient received three cycles of cisplatin/5-FU induction chemotherapy, followed by concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cisplatin 30 mg/m2 weekly for 7 weeks). This approach achieved disease control despite the advanced stage at presentation.Conclusion:This case highlights the importance of considering nasopharyngeal carcinoma in children with cervical lymphadenopathy and systemic symptoms. Multicenter studies are needed to establish standardized pediatric protocols and to investigate molecular biomarkers.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is an uncommon epithelial malignancy characterized by marked geographic variation in incidence. The disease is particularly prevalent in East and Southeast Asia and North Africa, with reported annual incidence rates ranging from 4 to 25 cases per 100 000 individuals[1–3].

Radiological imaging plays a crucial role in disease staging. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is generally preferred because of its superior soft-tissue resolution and ability to detect skull-base and intracranial extension, whereas computed tomography (CT) is useful for evaluating osseous involvement[3–5].

Because of the complex anatomy of the nasopharynx and the difficulty of obtaining adequate surgical margins, surgery is rarely used as a first-line treatment. Consequently, radiotherapy, alone or combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, remains the cornerstone of management[3,6].

We report a rare case of metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma in an 8-year-old girl, highlighting both the unusually young age at presentation and the presence of extensive skeletal metastases.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old girl presented with a progressively enlarging right-sided neck swelling (Fig. 1) that had increased in size over more than 8 months. Associated symptoms included intermittent fever, weight loss, night sweats, generalized bone pain (particularly in the right thigh), fatigue, and malaise. She denied epistaxis, hearing loss, and tinnitus, but had persistent snoring and mouth breathing suggestive of upper-airway obstruction. Family history was negative for NPC and other malignancies. Physical examination revealed a firm, nontender, mobile right cervical mass measuring approximately 10 × 8 cm. Multiple enlarged cervical lymph nodes were palpable bilaterally in the submandibular and sternocleidomastoid regions. Cardiopulmonary examination was otherwise unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated a white blood cell count of 16 000/µL with 82% neutrophils, urea 12.7 mg/dL, creatinine 0.22 mg/dL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 20 mm/h, C-reactive protein 127 mg/L, and lactate dehydrogenase 541 U/L.

Contrast-enhanced CT of the brain, neck, chest, abdomen, and pelvis revealed irregular thickening of the right nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal walls (Fig. 2), measuring approximately 45 × 50 mm. The lesion extended toward the skull base and infiltrated the retropharyngeal space. Multiple enlarged retropharyngeal, cervical, and supraclavicular lymph nodes were identified. Mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy was also present. Several bilateral pulmonary nodules measuring up to 8 mm raised suspicion for metastatic disease. In addition, a right hip joint effusion and a periosteal reaction involving the inferior aspect of the right ilium were noted. Whole-body bone scintigraphy demonstrated increased radiotracer uptake in the sixth thoracic vertebra, first lumbar vertebra, right iliac wing, right pubic bone, left iliac wing, and proximal right tibia, consistent with multifocal skeletal metastases.

Flexible nasopharyngeal endoscopy and biopsy of the primary lesion could not be performed because of financial constraints. Therefore, the diagnosis relied on imaging findings and histopathological examination of an excised cervical lymph node.

Histopathological evaluation revealed sheets and nests of large undifferentiated epithelial cells with vesicular nuclei, prominent nucleoli, and indistinct cytoplasmic borders within a dense lymphoid stroma (Fig. 3). Keratinization was absent. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for cytokeratin (CK) and p40, confirming metastatic non-keratinizing nasopharyngeal carcinoma involving the cervical lymph nodes. Given the established association between Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) and nonkeratinizing NPC, additional viral testing was performed. Serological testing for EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA) IgM and IgG as well as EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA) was negative. Epstein–Barr virus–encoded RNA (EBER) in-situ hybridization was also negative. Screening for cytomegalovirus and human immunodeficiency virus was unremarkable.

The patient received induction chemotherapy consisting of cisplatin 100 mg/m2 on day 1 and continuous-infusion 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) 1000 mg/m2/day on days 1–5. The regimen was repeated every three weeks for three cycles. Treatment was administered only when the absolute neutrophil count was ≥1000 cells/mm3, the platelet count was ≥100 000 cells/mm3, and the serum creatinine was ≤1.5 times the upper normal limit.

Three weeks after induction chemotherapy, concurrent chemoradiotherapy was initiated. Weekly cisplatin at 30 mg/m2 was administered alongside external-beam radiotherapy for 7 weeks, resulting in a cumulative cisplatin dose of 510 mg/m2.

After treatment, the patient demonstrated marked clinical improvement, including a reduction in the cervical mass and lymphadenopathy. Constitutional symptoms improved substantially. Follow-up CT, approximately three months later, showed regression of the primary lesion, decreased mediastinal and cervical lymphadenopathy, and a reduction in pulmonary nodules. Repeat bone scintigraphy demonstrated decreased activity within previously identified skeletal lesions, indicating a favorable partial response.

The patient remained in oncologic follow-up for 12 months. No local progression or new metastatic lesions were identified. She maintained good functional status, with gradual resumption of daily activities. Mild xerostomia and intermittent fatigue were observed, but no severe late toxicities developed.

Discussion

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma is an uncommon epithelial malignancy with a highly variable geographic distribution. Incidence rates reach approximately 20 cases per 100 000 population in endemic regions but remain near 1 case per 100 000 in non-endemic areas[1]. This disparity is attributed to interactions among environmental factors, genetic susceptibility, and viral oncogenesis, particularly EBV infection[1,2].

Although NPC predominantly affects adults, pediatric cases are rare and account for only 1–2% of all reported cases in China[1–3]. Pediatric NPC is most commonly diagnosed between 12 and 15 years of age and frequently presents with advanced locoregional disease and cervical lymphadenopathy[3,4]. The present patient was diagnosed at 8 years of age, making the case particularly unusual.

The diagnostic gold standard remains nasopharyngeal endoscopy with biopsy of the primary lesion[2]. In the current case, the diagnosis was established from cervical lymph-node histopathology because a biopsy of the primary tumor was not feasible. Histological findings were consistent with metastatic non-keratinizing undifferentiated NPC (WHO type III). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated positivity for cytokeratin and p40. Numerous studies have demonstrated a strong association between EBV infection and WHO types II and III NPC[6]; however, EBV studies were negative in this patient.

Imaging is essential for disease staging and evaluation of tumor extent. MRI is considered the most sensitive modality for assessing skull-base invasion and cervical nodal metastases, whereas CT remains useful for identifying cortical bone destruction[2]. In our patient, CT demonstrated extensive local disease involving the nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal regions.

Distant skeletal metastases at diagnosis are relatively uncommon, occurring in approximately 7% of NPC cases[7]. The vertebral column, ribs, sternum, pelvis, and femur are among the most frequently affected sites[8,9]. In the present case, bone scintigraphy revealed metastatic involvement of the lumbar vertebra, iliac wings, pubic bone, and proximal tibia. Such extensive skeletal dissemination at presentation is exceptionally rare in children.

Radhakrishnan et al described a 14-year-old boy with NPC and widespread metastases involving the liver, spleen, and bone marrow at diagnosis, emphasizing the aggressive nature of pediatric metastatic NPC[10]. Another report documented multifocal skeletal metastases detected by FDG PET/CT, highlighting the value of advanced imaging for identifying occult osseous disease and improving staging accuracy[11].

Current treatment strategies rely primarily on radiotherapy combined with platinum-based chemotherapy, with some protocols incorporating maintenance immunotherapy, such as interferon-beta[10–15]. Nevertheless, pediatric-specific treatment guidelines remain limited because most prospective studies have focused on adults. Ohguri et al reported prolonged survival in a pediatric patient with recurrent bone, liver, and nodal metastases following multimodal treatment, suggesting that aggressive combined therapy may provide meaningful benefit even in disseminated disease[16].

Conclusion

This report describes a rare case of metastatic nasopharyngeal carcinoma in an 8-year-old girl who presented with cervical lymphadenopathy and systemic B symptoms. Staging demonstrated extensive nodal disease and multiple osseous metastases. The patient achieved a favorable response following induction chemotherapy and concurrent chemoradiotherapy.

Given the rarity of NPC in young children, multicenter studies are needed to establish standardized pediatric treatment protocols. Future research should focus on molecular profiling, identification of prognostic biomarkers, optimization of chemoradiotherapy regimens, and evaluation of emerging therapies, such as immunotherapy, for advanced pediatric disease.
