Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation with Commutator Scaling
Junaid Aftab, Dong An, Konstantina Trivisa

Abstract
The Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation (LCHS) framework simulates dissipative linear dynamics by representing time evolution as an integral over unitary operators, which is discretized by quadrature and implemented via Hamiltonian simulation. While existing analyses achieve near-optimal scaling in time and precision using norm-based quantities of the dissipative generator, we show that implementing the Hamiltonian simulation steps with Multi-Product Formulas (MPFs) yields commutator-sensitive error and complexity bounds. We demonstrate that the quadrature rule affects not only discretization error but also commutator structure and query complexity. This dependence is quantified through post-quadrature analysis for abstract MPF error profiles and for general time-independent and local Hamiltonians using known commutator-sensitive MPF error estimates. We compare uniform trapezoidal and free-scale sinh–sinh quadrature, showing improved quadrature-cardinality scaling for the latter, and illustrate the framework with applications to fractional diffusion, advection–diffusion, and open quantum systems.

From a broader perspective, LCHS is a special case of the Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU) framework of Childs and Wiebe

Specifically, MPFs construct these high-order approximations by linearly combining sequences of low-order Trotter steps with carefully chosen weights. [

This limitation naturally motivates the central question of our work: can we design an efficient quantum algorithm for general non-unitary systems with both near-optimal scaling (in time and precision) and commutator scaling?

In this work, we answer this question affirmatively in a quadrature-dependent sense. We develop an LCHS–MPF algorithm for non-unitary dynamics that applies to both time-independent and time-dependent settings, and illustrate its use in applications arising from linear differential equations and open quantum systems. In this algorithm, LCHS reduces non-unitary simulation to a family of Hamiltonian simulations, while MPFs implement these simulations with commutator-sensitive complexity. Our key observation is that the outer LCHS quadrature does not merely control discretization error. Rather, the quadrature nodes also determine the Hamiltonians passed to the inner simulation routine, and therefore affect the commutator structure, step-size restrictions, LCU normalization, weighted error accumulation, and final query complexity. We track this dependence explicitly and obtain a quantum algorithm for non-unitary simulation whose complexity reflects both the quadrature structure of LCHS and the commutator scaling of MPFs.

e^{-AT}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)e^{-iG_{k}T}\,dk.

Here

\mu_{Q}

\{G_{k_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}

1.2
Related Works

Several recent works study when non-unitary quantum simulation can inherit structural advantages from unitary Hamiltonian simulation. Wang et al.
[
72
]
recently derived commutator-based Trotter error bounds for Lindbladian simulation. Their work is close in motivation to ours. Both analyses ask whether the favorable commutator scaling of Hamiltonian product formulas persists in dissipative dynamics. The technical settings, however, are distinct. Their bounds apply directly to product-formula approximations of Lindblad generators and use Richardson extrapolation for observable estimation, whereas our approach first uses the LCHS representation to reduce dissipative linear evolution to a quadrature-indexed family of Hamiltonian simulations. Thus, the commutators appearing in our analysis are those of the Hamiltonians generated by the LCHS quadrature, and the main issue is to track how this quadrature dependence enters the MPF error and complexity estimates.

A second related direction concerns quantum algorithms for non-unitary dynamics based on matrix-function and eigenvalue-transformation representations. An et al.
[
3
]
extended the LCHS approach from matrix exponentials to broader matrix eigenvalue transformations represented through Laplace transforms. Low and Su
[
53
]
introduced quantum eigenvalue processing, an eigenvalue transformation framework for non-normal matrices that addresses related non-Hermitian phenomena from a different algorithmic perspective. These works complement the present paper by broadening the class of matrix functions that can be implemented on quantum computers. By contrast, the focus of the present work is not to enlarge the class of admissible matrix functions, but to analyze how the post-quadrature LCHS representation interacts with a commutator-sensitive Hamiltonian simulation primitive.

Contour-integral methods provide another point of comparison. Takahira et al.
[
65
]
used Cauchy’s integral formula and block encoding to implement matrix functions as linear combinations of shifted matrix inverses. More recently, Jiang and An
[
40
]
analyzed the complexity of contour-integral-based quantum eigenvalue transformations, with applications to Hamiltonian simulation, matrix polynomials, and linear differential equations. Wang et al.
[
70
]
developed a contour-based matrix-decomposition framework for non-unitary dynamics using a finite decomposition derived from Cauchy’s residue theorem. These approaches are related to LCHS through analytic matrix-function representations, However, their elementary operations are typically shifted resolvents or Hermitian transformations, rather than Hamiltonian evolutions of the form
e^{-i(H+kL)t}
whose commutator structure is tracked in the present work.

Other transform-based approaches have also been developed in the literature.
Schrödingerization

[
43
,
44
,
41
,
42
]
converts non-unitary linear dynamics into unitary dynamics in one higher dimension, achieving optimal or near-optimal matrix-query dependence after suitable smooth initialization. Jin et al.
[
45
]
proposed a transmutation-based method for dissipative diffusion generated by positive semi-definite operators, expressing the diffusion semigroup via the Kannai transform as a Gaussian-weighted superposition of unitary wave propagators. A recent Poisson-summation framework by Wang et al.
[
71
]
further emphasizes the role of spectral aliasing and discretization in non-unitary matrix transformations. These works illustrate that non-unitary simulation can often be reduced to superpositions of unitary or resolvent-type primitives.

1.3
Discussion and Open Questions

Our main result shows that the query complexity is polylogarithmic in time and inverse precision, and at the same time achieves commutator scaling. This matches our previous analysis of commutator scaling for MPF. It remains an open question whether one can obtain optimal scaling in both time and inverse precision while preserving commutator scaling.

Another natural question is whether the quadrature dependence can be further optimized beyond the quadrature rules analyzed here. Although the present analysis accommodates fairly general quadrature rules, the resulting complexity bounds still depend on quantities such as
\Lambda_{m,Q}
,
R_{Q}
,
\alpha_{Q}
, and the discrete moments
M^{Q}_{\nu}
. It would be interesting to determine whether there exist quadrature rules that simultaneously improve discretization accuracy, LCU normalization, moment growth, and the post-quadrature MPF cost. A related question is how to optimize the LCHS kernel profile jointly with the quadrature rule. The kernel parameters influence the approximation error, truncation error, quadrature radius, LCU normalization, and the discrete moment bounds entering the MPF error profiles. A more systematic kernel–quadrature optimization may therefore improve the overall complexity of the algorithm.

The LCU implementation introduces an overhead governed by the normalization of the linear combination and by the norm of the desired output state. Although amplitude amplification can boost the success probability, the resulting state-preparation cost still contains normalization-dependent factors. It would be interesting to determine whether alternative block-encoding constructions, quantum signal processing techniques, or dissipative embeddings can reduce this overhead while preserving the commutator-sensitive advantages of the LCHS–MPF framework.

Several extensions of the time-dependent framework remain open. The analysis in
Section

D.2
assumes sufficient regularity of the time-dependent generator on each subinterval. It would be interesting to determine whether similar complexity guarantees can be obtained under weaker assumptions, such as piecewise-smooth, discontinuous, or rapidly varying dissipative generators within the LCHS-compatible setting.

Finally, it would be useful to better understand the practical performance of MPF-based dissipative simulation. The present work focuses primarily on asymptotic complexity bounds, but concrete implementations may exhibit additional structure that is not visible at the worst-case level. Numerical studies could clarify which parameter regimes are most favorable for near-term or fault-tolerant quantum architectures.

1.

f(n)=\mathcal{O}(g(n))
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0

such that

for all

2.

f(n)=\Omega(g(n))
if and only if there exists a constant
C>0
and an integer

such that

The symbol

denotes a product in which the elements are arranged with increasing indices from right to left. Similarly, the notation

is defined to denote a product with elements ordered with increasing indices from left to right. Specifically, we have

\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{i=1,\cdots,k}}A_{i}=A_{k}\cdots A_{1},\quad\overset{\longrightarrow}{\prod_{i=1,\cdots,k}}A_{i}=A_{1}\cdots A_{k}.

\|f\|_{L^{1}[a,b]}

2.1.2
Linear Algebra Notation

All vector spaces considered are finite-dimensional and defined over
\mathbb{C}
. Vectors are denoted either by lowercase Roman letters with a

symbol on top or by ket notation, depending on context. Linear operators—represented as matrices—are denoted by uppercase Roman letters. The linear algebra notation used throughout this paper is summarized below:

1.

denotes the conjugate transpose of

2.

\|\vec{a}\|_{1}
denotes the

\|\vec{a}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{i}|
.

3.

\|A\|

4.

\|A\|_{L^{1}[a,b]}
denotes the

-norm of a time dependent matrix

on

5.

The notation

6.

For two matrices

The formal solution to the matrix-valued linear differential equation

denotes the time-ordered exponential operator. We also denote the time-ordered exponential operator as

[

for more details. In what follows, we set

2.2
Product Formulas

Consider a time-dependent Hamiltonian
H(t)
for
t\in[0,T]
, decomposed as
H(t)=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma}(t)
. For each
\gamma
, let
U_{\gamma}(T)
denote the time-ordered exponential generated by
H_{\gamma}(t)
on
[0,T]
. Product-formula methods assume that each
U_{\gamma}(T)
can be implemented efficiently. Under this assumption, a generic product formula approximates the exact time-evolution operator
U(T)
as

(2.3)

U_{p}(T)=\prod_{\xi=1}^{\Xi}\prod_{\gamma=1}^{\Gamma}U_{\pi_{\xi}(\gamma)}(\beta_{\xi\gamma}T,(\beta_{\xi\gamma}+\alpha_{\xi\gamma})T),

where
\pi_{\xi}
is a permutation in
S_{\Gamma}
and
\alpha_{\xi,\gamma},\beta_{\xi,\gamma}\in[0,1]
such that
\beta_{\xi\gamma}+\alpha_{\xi\gamma}\in[0,1]
. We say that
U_{p}
is a
p
th-order product formula if its single-step approximation error satisfies

(2.4)

\|U_{p}(T)-U(T)\|=\mathcal{O}(T^{p+1}).

In practice, the interval
[0,T]
is partitioned into
r
segments, and the evolution on each segment is approximated by a product formula. The parameter
r
is referred to as the Trotter number. The resulting approximation is
(U_{p}(T/r))^{r}
.

Example 2.1
.

Product formulas can be constructed using the Suzuki recursion
[
63
,
64
]
, which yields, in particular, the following first-order and second-order product formulas

(2.5)

U_{1}(0,T)=\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{\gamma=1,\cdots,\Gamma}}U_{\gamma}(0,T),\quad U_{2}(0,T)=\overset{\longrightarrow}{\prod_{\gamma=1,\cdots,\Gamma}}U_{\gamma}(T/2,T)\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{\gamma=1,\cdots,\Gamma}}U_{\gamma}(0,T/2).

We do not discuss higher-order product formulas in any detail, as these are not used in this work.

When
H(t)\equiv H
is time-independent, the error scaling of product formulas is well understood. In particular,
[
28
]
showed that, for a general
p
th-order product formula, ensuring the global error bound
\lVert U_{p}(T/r)^{r}-U(T)\rVert\leq\epsilon
requires choosing
r
such that

(2.6)

r=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},p+1}(H)^{1/p}T^{1+1/p}}{\epsilon^{1/p}}\right),\quad\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},p+1}(H)=\sum_{j_{1},\dots,j_{p+1}=1}^{\Gamma}\|[H_{j_{1}},\dots,H_{j_{p+1}}]\|,

where
\epsilon
is the target precision. The dependence on
\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},p+1}(H)
is commonly referred to as
commutator scaling
. This property enables product-formula Hamiltonian simulation algorithms to achieve favorable system-size scaling for physically local Hamiltonians (see, e.g.,
[
10
,
73
,
27
]
). By contrast, the error scaling of product formulas for time-dependent Hamiltonians is less completely understood. Early work of Wiebe et al.
[
74
]
derived bounds for smoothly varying Hamiltonians based on
1
-norm scaling. Subsequent studies
[
19
,
5
,
39
]
analyzed low-order time-dependent product formulas, some of which exhibit commutator scaling. A general treatment was later given by Childs et al.
[
29
]
, who established commutator scaling for smoothly varying Hamiltonians with finite-range interactions. More recently, Mizuta et al.
[
57
]
analyzed generic smoothly varying time-dependent product formulas and proved commutator-scaling bounds. In this work, we use only their results on time-dependent multi-product formulas, which are recalled in
Section

2.4
.

.

Throughout the remainder of the paper, we restrict attention to second-order product formulas, i.e.,
p=2

2.3
Linear Combination of Unitaries

The Linear Combination of Unitaries (LCU) algorithm
[
30
]
provides a method for implementing linear combinations of unitary operators on a quantum computer. Let
M\in\mathbb{N}
, let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{M}\in\mathbb{C}
, and let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{M}
be unitary operators. The LCU algorithm aims to implement the operator
U=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}U_{j}
. The algorithm assumes that two operators can be efficiently implemented. First, it requires access to state preparation (
\operatorname{PREP}
) oracles:

(2.7)

\displaystyle\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\|\vec{a}\|_{1}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\sqrt{a_{j}}\ket{j},\quad\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{L}}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\|\vec{a}\|_{1}}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\overline{\sqrt{a_{j}}}\ket{j}

where
\sqrt{\cdot}
denotes the principal branch of the square root. Second, it assumes access to a multi-qubit controlled operation, commonly called a select oracle, of the form

(2.8)

\operatorname{SEL}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}U_{j}\otimes\ket{j}\bra{j}.

Assuming access to the required operators, the LCU algorithm implements the unitary operator
U_{\text{LCU}}=(I\otimes\operatorname{PREP}^{\dagger}_{\operatorname{L}})\cdot\operatorname{SEL}\cdot(I\otimes\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}})
. If
\ket{\psi}
denotes an input state, we have

(2.9)

U_{\text{LCU}}\ket{\psi}\ket{0}^{\log M}=\frac{1}{\|\vec{a}\|_{1}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}U_{j}\right)\ket{\psi}\ket{0}^{\log M}+\ket{\perp},

where
\ket{\perp}
denotes a potentially non-normalized state satisfying
(I\otimes\ket{0}^{\log M}\bra{0}^{\log M})\ket{\perp}=0
. Constructing
U_{\operatorname{LCU}}
requires a single query to each of the oracles
\operatorname{PREP}^{\dagger}_{\operatorname{L}}
,
\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}}
, and
\operatorname{SEL}
, with the implementation of
\operatorname{SEL}
typically dominating the computational cost. The ancilla register, initially prepared in the state
\ket{0}^{\log M}
, must be measured to obtain
\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}U_{j}\ket{\psi}
. To achieve a constant success probability,
\mathcal{O}(\|\vec{a}\|_{1})
rounds of amplitude amplification
[
16
]
are required.

2.4
Multi-Product Formula

The Multi-Product Formula (MPF) for time-independent Hamiltonian simulation constructs a linear combination of time-independent product formulas

(2.10)

U_{\operatorname{MP}}(T)=\sum_{j=1}^{M}a_{j}\,U^{b_{j}}_{2}(T/b_{j}),\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{R},b_{j}\in\mathbb{N}.

Low, Kliuchnikov, and Wiebe
[
51
]
showed that, for
M=m
, the coefficients
a_{j}
and exponents
b_{j}
can be chosen such that
\|\vec{a}\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\log m)
and
\|\vec{b}\|_{1}=\mathcal{O}(m^{2}\log m)
, ensuring that
\|U_{\operatorname{MP}}(T)-e^{-iHT}\|=\mathcal{O}(T^{2m+1})
. Consequently, although an MPF is constructed from product formulas of fixed order, it can achieve arbitrarily high orders of convergence. It can be implemented using the LCU algorithm as follows:

1.

Consider implementing the MPF over a short time-step,
\Delta
. Define the state-preparation oracles as in
Equation

2.7
, and let the select oracle

(2.11)

\operatorname{SEL}=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}U_{2}\left(\Delta/b_{j+1}\right)^{b_{j+1}}\otimes\ket{j}\bra{j}

encode the MPF. Then
(I\otimes\operatorname{PREP}^{\dagger}_{\operatorname{L}})\cdot\operatorname{SEL}\cdot(I\otimes\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}})
implements the MPF via LCU, with the ancilla state
\ket{0}
encoding the target operation and initial success probability
\mathcal{O}(1/\|\vec{a}\|_{1}^{2})
.

2.

Consider simulating evolution over total time
T
. Partition
[0,T]
into
r
equal segments. On each segment, apply the MPF for
\Delta=T/r
using LCU. Robust oblivious amplitude amplification is applied at each step to maintain a constant success probability.

The cost of the implementation above is dominated by the oracle
\operatorname{SEL}
, which uses
\mathcal{O}(\|\vec{b}\|_{1})
queries to controlled-
U_{2}
operations. Amplitude amplification contributes an additional factor of
\mathcal{O}(\|\vec{a}\|_{1})
. Thus, each time step requires
\mathcal{O}(\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1})
queries to
U_{2}
, and
r
steps require
\mathcal{O}(r\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1})
queries in total. Although
\|\vec{a}\|_{1}
and
\|\vec{b}\|_{1}
are specified in
Section

2.4
, a rigorous choice of
r
was previously unavailable and was expected to exhibit commutator scaling because the construction uses
U_{2}
. This gap was recently closed by Aftab et al.
[
1
]
and Mizuta
[
58
]
. We recall the relevant short-time simulation results below, with time-step size denoted by
\Delta
.

Proposition 2.3

(Theorem 8 in
[
1

)
.

Let
H=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\Gamma}H_{\gamma}
be a time-independent Hamiltonian. Suppose there exists a

such that

. If

is chosen such that

(2.12)

\|U_{\operatorname{MP}}(\Delta)-U(\Delta)\|\leq\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}\\
j\geq 2m\end{subarray}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\frac{\Delta^{j+l}}{l!}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1},\cdots,j_{l}\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+},\\
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{l}=j\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{\kappa=1}^{l}\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},j_{\kappa}+1}(H)\right)\right).

(2.16)

2.5
Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation

The Linear Combination of Hamiltonian Simulation (LCHS) algorithm simulates
non-unitary dynamics
by expressing them as linear combinations of
unitary dynamics
.
LCHS formulation was first introduced in
[
8
]
and improved in
[
4
]
.
This approach generalizes the Fourier representation of the exponential function on the positive real axis,

(2.21)

e^{-x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)e^{-ikx}\,dk,\quad x\geq 0.

Specifically, for a possibly time-dependent operator
A(t)=L(t)+iH(t)
, with Hermitian
L(t)\succeq 0
and
H(t)
, they show that

(2.22)

\mathcal{T}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}A(s)\,ds}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)U_{k}(t)dk,\quad t\in[0,T]

solves
Equation

1.2
in the case
b(t)\equiv 0
.
The case
b(t)\neq 0
is then derived via an application of Duhamel’s principle.
The LCHS algorithm then proceeds by truncating the integral to
[-K,K]
for some
K>0
and implementing it via the LCU algorithm, where each

(2.23)

U_{k}(t):=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{t}(H(s)+kL(s))\,ds}

is implemented via a time-dependent Hamiltonian simulation algorithm. The function
\hat{f}(k)
is called the LCHS kernel function and should be the inverse Fourier transform of the function
f(x)
which takes the value of
e^{-x}
for
x\geq 0
.
Previous works have suggested the choices of
\hat{f}(k)
to be
\frac{1}{\pi(1+k^{2})}

[
8
]
or
\frac{1}{2\pi e^{-2^{\beta}}e^{(1+iz)^{\beta}}},\beta\in(0,1)

[
4
]
, where the LCHS algorithm with the latter kernel function can solve
Equation

1.2
in near-optimal time and precision complexity. Recently, Low and Somma
[
52
]
introduced an approximate LCHS framework that further improves upon the previous formalism.
Their key observation is that the requirement that
Equation

2.21
hold exactly can be relaxed so that it need only hold approximately. That is, it suffices to find a function
f
such that, for
\epsilon>0
, we have
|e^{-x}-f(x)|\leq\epsilon
for
x\geq 0
, and such that
Equation

2.21
holds only approximately.
This weaker condition admits a broader class of kernels and leads to a flexible and more efficient LCHS algorithm with optimal complexity.
Their main result is summarized below.

Proposition 2.6

(Theorem 1 in
[
52
]
)
.

For
T\geq 0
and
n\geq 1
, let
A(t)=L(t)+iH(t)\in\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}
be a time-dependent matrix for
t\in[0,T]
, where
L(t)
and
H(t)
are Hermitian operators such that
\|L\|_{L^{1}[0,T]}
and
\|H\|_{L^{1}[0,T]}
are finite. Consider the uniform strip

(2.24)

S_{[-y_{0},0]}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\operatorname{Im}(z)\in[-y_{0},0]\}

for some
y_{0}>1
. On
S_{[-y_{0},0]}
, assume that the complex-valued kernel function
\hat{f}(z)
is such that
\lim_{|z|\to\infty}\hat{f}(z)=0
,
(z+i)\hat{f}(z)
is analytic and the residue at
z=-i
is
\operatorname{Res}(\hat{f},-i)=i/\sqrt{2\pi}
. For
t\in[0,T]
, we have

(2.25)

\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)U_{k}(t)dk-\mathcal{T}e^{-\int_{0}^{t}A(s)\,ds}\right\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]}|\hat{f}(k)|dk+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(k-iy_{0})|dk.

In what follows, we use the improved approximate formulation of Low and Somma
[
52
]
.

3
Pre-Quadrature Error Analysis

In this section, we give a pre-quadrature error analysis of the algorithm in the time-independent homogeneous case. Throughout, let
A=L+iH
, where
L
and
H
are Hermitian and
L\succeq 0
. In the pre-quadrature regime, the algorithm has three main sources of error:

1.

(Approximation) The approximation error,
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})
, arising by replacing
e^{-AT}
with the LCHS representation. That is,
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0}):=\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)U_{k}(T)\,dk-e^{-AT}\|
. By
Equation

2.25
, after taking the limit
R\to\infty
, it is bounded by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\hat{f}(k-iy_{0})|\,dk
.

2.

(Truncation) The truncation error,
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)
, arising from restricting
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)\,U_{k}(t)\,dk
to
[-R,R]
. That is,
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R):=\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(k)U_{k}(T)\,dk-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)U_{k}(T)\,dk\|
. By
Equation

2.25
, since
U_{k}(T)
is unitary, it is bounded by
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus[-R,R]}|\hat{f}(k)|\,dk
.

3.

(Inner Simulation) The simulation error
E_{\mathrm{MPF}}(T)
, arising from replacing each
U_{k}(T)
by its MPF approximation
\widetilde{U}_{k}(T)
.

In what follows, we use the four-parameter kernel family introduced by Low and Somma
[
52
]
:

(3.1)

\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)=\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{d(1-ik)}e^{-\frac{k^{2}+1}{4c^{2}}}}{(1-ik)(b+ik)^{a-1}},\quad a\in\mathbb{N},\;b,c>0,\;d\in\mathbb{R}.

We call
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
the kernel profile and write
\hat{f}(k)
for
\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)
when clear.

Section

3.1
discusses the approximation error,
Section

3.2
the truncation error,
Section

3.3
the inner simulation error, and
Section

3.4
discusses the coupling between the truncation and inner simulation errors.

3.1
Approximation Error

We first analyze the approximation error
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})
. Low and Somma analyzed
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})
for the subclass
\hat{f}_{2,1}(k;c,d)
. Here, we analyze
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})
for the full family
\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)
. Most proofs are relegated to
Appendix

A
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
and
y_{0}>1
. We have

(3.2)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{2\pi}e^{\,d(1-y_{0})+\frac{y_{0}^{2}-1}{4c^{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4c^{2})}\,dk}{\sqrt{(y_{0}-1)^{2}+k^{2}}\,((b+y_{0})^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}.

The proof of
3.1
can be found in
Section

A.1
. The integral in
3.1
has an exact representation in terms of the confluent hypergeometric function, but that form is less transparent. We instead use the bound
((b+y_{0})^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}\geq(b+y_{0})^{a-1}
to obtain the simpler upper bound

(3.3)

E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{(b+y_{0})^{a-1}}\frac{e^{\,d(1-y_{0})+\frac{y_{0}^{2}-1}{4c^{2}}}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4c^{2})}}{\sqrt{(y_{0}-1)^{2}+k^{2}}}\,dk.

This bound is explicit and retains the essential dependence on
y_{0}
and
a,b,c,d
.
3.2
, which is proved in
Section

A.2
, evaluates this integral form.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
s,\sigma>0
, we have

(3.4)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4\sigma^{2})}}{\sqrt{k^{2}+s^{2}}}\,dk=e^{s^{2}/(8\sigma^{2})}K_{0}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}\right),

where
K_{0}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the second kind.

Using
3.2
with
s=y_{0}-1
,
\sigma=c
and
K_{0}(x)\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2x}}e^{-x}
for
x>0
, we obtain

(3.5)

E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{(y_{0}-1)(b+y_{0})^{a-1}}e^{d(1-y_{0})+\frac{y_{0}^{2}-1}{4c^{2}}}.

Equation

3.5
motivates minimizing the right-hand side and identifying kernel profiles for which this bound is at most
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. In
3.3
, which is proved in
Section

A.3
, we first study existence and uniqueness of minimizers with respect to
y_{0}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
. Define the surrogate cost functional

(3.6)

B(y_{0}):=\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{(y_{0}-1)(b+y_{0})^{a-1}}e^{d(1-y_{0})+\frac{y_{0}^{2}-1}{4c^{2}}},\quad y_{0}>1.

Then
\frac{d}{dy_{0}}\log B(y_{0})=-d+\frac{y_{0}}{2c^{2}}-\frac{1}{y_{0}-1}-\frac{a-1}{b+y_{0}}
.
Consequently, any stationary point
y_{0}^{*}
of
\log B
satisfies

(3.7)

\frac{y_{0}^{*}}{2c^{2}}-d=\frac{1}{y_{0}^{*}-1}+\frac{a-1}{b+y_{0}^{*}}.

Moreover, we have
\frac{d^{2}}{dy_{0}^{2}}\log B(y_{0})=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}+\frac{1}{(y_{0}-1)^{2}}+\frac{a-1}{(b+y_{0})^{2}}>0
.
Therefore,
\log B
is strictly convex on
(1,\infty)
and has at most one stationary point. Any such stationary point is the unique global minimizer of
B
. Moreover,
B
admits a global minimizer.

3.3
implies that, for a fixed kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, the surrogate cost
B(y_{0})
admits a unique minimizer
y_{0}^{\ast}
. If
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}>0
denotes the approximation error budget, then

(3.8)

\text{there exists }y_{0}\in(1,\infty)\text{ such that }\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}\iff\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}.

Equation

3.8
gives a sufficient condition for
E_{\operatorname{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. It remains to determine when
y_{0}^{\ast}
satisfies
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
.
3.4
gives a condition in terms of the parameter
d
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}>0
. For
s>1
, define

(3.9)

\displaystyle\Phi(s)

\displaystyle:=\frac{s}{2c^{2}}-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{a-1}{b+s},

(3.10)

\displaystyle\Psi(s)

\displaystyle:=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}(s-1)}\left(\frac{b+1}{b+s}\right)^{a-1}\exp\left(1-\frac{(s-1)^{2}}{4c^{2}}+\frac{(a-1)(s-1)}{b+s}\right).

\Phi
is strictly increasing on
(1,\infty)
,
\Psi
is strictly decreasing on
(1,\infty)
, and there exists a unique
s_{\epsilon}\in(1,\infty)
such that
\Psi(s_{\epsilon})=\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. Moreover, for a fixed kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, let
y_{0}^{\ast}
be the unique minimizer of
B
from
3.3
. Then

(3.11)

\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}\iff d\geq\Phi(s_{\epsilon}).

Proof.

By
3.3
,
B
admits a unique minimizer
y_{0}^{\ast}\in(1,\infty)
satisfying
d=\Phi(y_{0}^{\ast})
. From
Equation

A.7
,
\Phi^{\prime}(s)>0
, so
\Phi
is strictly increasing on
(1,\infty)
. Moreover,

(3.12)

\Phi(s)\to-\infty\quad\text{as }s\to 1^{+},\quad\Phi(s)\to+\infty\quad\text{as }s\to\infty.

Hence,
\Phi
is a bijection from
(1,\infty)
onto
\mathbb{R}
. Using
d=\Phi(y_{0}^{\ast})
in
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})
gives
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})=\Psi(y_{0}^{\ast})
. We now show that
\Psi
is strictly decreasing. Differentiating
\log\Psi
gives

(3.13)

\frac{d}{ds}\log\Psi(s)=-\frac{1}{s-1}-\frac{s-1}{2c^{2}}-\frac{(a-1)(s-1)}{(b+s)^{2}}<0.

Thus
\Psi
is strictly decreasing on
(1,\infty)
. Moreover, we have

(3.14)

\Psi(s)\to+\infty\quad\text{as }s\to 1^{+},\quad\Psi(s)\to 0\quad\text{as }s\to\infty.

Hence, there exists a unique
s_{\epsilon}\in(1,\infty)
such that
\Psi(s_{\epsilon})=\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. Finally, since
\Psi
is strictly decreasing and
d=\Phi(y_{0}^{\ast})
, with
\Phi
strictly increasing, we obtain

(3.15)

\displaystyle\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0}^{\ast})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}

\displaystyle\iff\Psi(y_{0}^{\ast})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}\iff y_{0}^{\ast}\geq s_{\epsilon}\iff d=\Phi(y_{0}^{\ast})\geq\Phi(s_{\epsilon}).

This completes the proof.
∎

3.4
shows that taking
d
above an explicit lower bound ensures
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
. The threshold is determined by the unique solution of
\Psi(s)=\epsilon_{\mathrm{approx}}
, which can be computed numerically.
3.5
, which is proved in
Section

A.4
, gives explicit sufficient conditions on
c
and
d
and determines a corresponding choice of
y_{0}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}\in(0,1)
and let
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
be a kernel profile. Then the following statements hold.

1.

Define
\omega_{\epsilon}:=W(e^{2a}/(2\pi\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}^{2}))
and
\widetilde{x}_{0}:=1+c\sqrt{2\omega_{\epsilon}}
, where
W
denotes the Lambert
W
-function. If

(3.16)

d\geq\frac{\widetilde{x}_{0}}{2c^{2}}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{x}_{0}-1}-\frac{a-1}{b+\widetilde{x}_{0}},

then there exists
y_{0}>1
such that
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. Consequently,
E_{\operatorname{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
.

2.

Define
\overline{x}_{0}:=1+2c\sqrt{a+\log(1/\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}})}
. If

(3.17)

d\geq\frac{\overline{x}_{0}}{2c^{2}}-\frac{1}{\overline{x}_{0}-1}-\frac{a-1}{b+\overline{x}_{0}},

then there exists
y_{0}>1
such that
\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}B(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. Consequently,
E_{\operatorname{approx}}(y_{0})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
.

3.

The sufficient lower bound on
d
in
Equation

3.17
is obtained by evaluating
\Phi(s)
at
\overline{x}_{0}
. In particular,

(3.18)

\Phi(\overline{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{a+\log(1/\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}})}-\frac{1}{\overline{x}_{0}-1}-\frac{a-1}{b+\overline{x}_{0}}.

For fixed
b
and
c
, as either
a\to\infty
or
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}\to 0
, or both, we have

(3.19)

\Phi(\overline{x}_{0})=\frac{1}{2c^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{a+\log(1/\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}})}\right).

Therefore, a sufficient lower bound on
d
grows at most on the order of
\frac{1}{2c^{2}}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{a+\log(1/\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}})}
.

3.2
Truncation Error

We now compute the truncation error in
3.6
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
and
R>0
. We have

(3.20)

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{b+1}{b}\right)^{a-1}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}}\operatorname{erfc}\left(\frac{R}{2c}\right).

The proof of
3.6
can be founnd in
Section

A.5
. The bound in
3.6
shows that
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)\to 0
as
R\to\infty
. This motivates considering the set of radii for which the truncation error is below a fixed tolerance
\epsilon^{\prime}>0

(3.21)

\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}:=\{R>0:E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)<\epsilon^{\prime}\}.

3.7
gives a computable lower bound ensuring
R\in\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. The set
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
is non-empty for every
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. For a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, define

(3.22)

\vartheta(\vec{\theta}):=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{c}\left(\frac{b}{b+1}\right)^{a-1}\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}}}.

If
\vartheta(\vec{\theta})<1
, then every
R>2c\,\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(\vartheta(\vec{\theta}))
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
. If
\vartheta(\vec{\theta})\geq 1
, then every
R>0
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}
.

Proof.

\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
since
\lim_{R\to\infty}E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)=0
by
3.6
. If
\vartheta(\vec{\theta})<1
, then
3.6
shows that
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)<\epsilon^{\prime}
is guaranteed whenever
\operatorname{erfc}(R/(2c))<\vartheta(\vec{\theta})
. Since
\operatorname{erfc}
is strictly decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, this is equivalent to
R>2c\,\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(\vartheta(\vec{\theta}))
. If
\vartheta(\vec{\theta})\geq 1
, then
\operatorname{erfc}(R/(2c))<1\leq\vartheta(\vec{\theta})
, and the same estimate gives
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)<\epsilon^{\prime}
for all
R>0
.
∎

3.3
Inner Simulation Error

We next compute the inner simulation error. For
k\in\mathbb{R}
, define
G_{k}:=H+kL
. Since
G_{k}
consists of
H
and
L
, each nested commutator is formed by choosing either
H
or
L
at each position, motivating the following definition.

Definition 3.8
.

For integers
j\geq 2
and
0\leq\ell\leq j
, define

(3.23)

\mathcal{C}_{j,\ell}(H,L):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}M\in\{H,L\}^{j}\\
N_{L}(M)=\ell\end{subarray}}\|[M_{1},M_{2},\dots,M_{j}]\|,

where
N_{L}(M)
denotes the number of occurrences of
L
in the word
M=(M_{1},\dots,M_{j})\in\{H,L\}^{j}
.

3.9
formalizes the fact that the nested commutators of
G_{k}
depend polynomially on
k
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Fix
j\geq 2
. The
j
-fold nested commutator profile associated with the decomposition
G_{k}=H+kL
satisfies

(3.24)

\alpha_{\mathrm{comm},j}(G_{k}):=\sum_{M_{1},\ldots,M_{j}\in\{H,kL\}}\|[M_{1},\ldots,M_{j}]\|=\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}|k|^{\ell}\mathcal{C}_{j,\ell}(H,L).

In particular,
\alpha_{\mathrm{comm},j}(G_{k})
is a polynomial in
|k|
of degree at most
j
, with coefficients determined by nested commutators of
H
and
L
.

Proof.

By multilinearity of nested commutators, each word in the decomposition
G_{k}=H+kL
containing exactly
\ell
copies of
L
contributes a factor
k^{\ell}
. Hence its norm contributes
|k|^{\ell}
. Grouping the resulting commutator terms by the number
\ell
of occurrences of
L
gives
Equation

3.24
. ∎

We approximate
U_{k}(t)=e^{-iG_{k}t}
by a
2m
-th order MPF built from the second-order product formula, denoted
U_{k}^{(2m)}
. We state the error bound abstractly, which is instantiated later using either
2.3
or
2.4
.

Assumption 3.10
.

For
R>0
, consider the time-independent family
\{G_{k}\}_{|k|\leq R}
. Fix
m,N\geq 1
and
\delta>0
, and let
U_{k}^{(2m)}(t)
denote the order-
2m
MPF approximation to
U_{k}(t)
. Assume there exist:

1.

a non-negative function

(3.25)

\Phi_{m}:[-R,R]\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},

based on nested commutators of
G_{k}
, the system size
N
and satisfying

\Phi_{m,R}^{\ast}(N):=\sup_{|k|\leq R}\Phi_{m}(k,N)<\infty
,

2.

a step size
\Delta(R,N,\delta)>0
,

3.

a remainder term
R_{m}(\delta)\geq 0
,

such that, for all
|k|\leq R
and
0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)
,

(3.26)

\|U_{k}^{(2m)}(\Delta)-U_{k}(\Delta)\|\leq C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Phi_{m}(k,N)+R_{m}(\delta)=:\eta_{k}(\Delta,k,N,\delta,m),

where
C_{m}>0
is constant. For simplicity, we write
\eta_{k}(\Delta)
for
\eta_{k}(\Delta,k,N,\delta,m)
.

Remark 3.11
.

3.10
captures different MPF error scalings through
\Phi_{m}(k,N)
. Under
2.3
, expanding
G_{k}
gives a convergent series in
|k|
. In contrast,
2.4
involves commutator products followed by a
(j+l)
-th root. This yields non-polynomial dependence on
k
. Thus,
3.10
covers both polynomial and root-type commutator scalings. Also,
R_{m}(\delta)=0
for
2.3
, while
R_{m}(\delta)=\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1}\delta
for
2.4
.

We now quantify how the abstract error bound in
3.10
propagates through the LCHS representation. We first define the quantities needed for the short-time analysis in
3.12
, prove a one-step MPF error bound for step size
\Delta
in
3.13
, and extend it to
[0,T]
in
3.14
. Since the argument does not use specific properties of
\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)
, we write
\hat{f}(k)
unless the parameter dependence is needed.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
\hat{f}
be a kernel (as in
Equation

3.1
). For
R>0
, define the truncated kernel norm by
\alpha_{\hat{f},R}:=\|\hat{f}\|_{L^{1}[-R,R]}/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Under
3.10
, define the truncated profile average

(3.27)

\Lambda_{m}(R,N):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\,\Phi_{m}(k,N)dk.

We first establish the error bound for a short time step
\Delta
in
3.13
. The proof can be found in
Section

B.1
.

Lemma 3.13
.

Fix
R,\delta>0,\;m,N\geq 1
.
For all
0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)
, we have

(3.28)

E_{\mathrm{MPF}}(\Delta)=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)\big(U^{(2m)}_{k}(\Delta)-U_{k}(\Delta)\big)dk\right\|\leq C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Lambda_{m}(R,N)+\alpha_{\hat{f},R}R_{m}(\delta).

We now extend this short-time error bound to the full simulation over
[0,T]
in
3.14
.

Proposition 3.14
.

Fix
R,\delta>0,\;m,N\geq 1
. Let
T>0
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Set
\Delta:=T/r
. Assume that
0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)
. Then

(3.29)

E_{\mathrm{MPF}}(T)=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)\Big(\big(U_{k}^{(2m)}(\Delta)\big)^{r}-U_{k}(T)\Big)dk\right\|\leq\frac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\eta_{k}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1}dk,

where
\eta_{k}(\Delta):=C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Phi_{m}(k,N)+R_{m}(\delta)
. In particular, if

\eta_{R}(\Delta)=\sup_{|k|\leq R}\eta_{k}(\Delta)
,
then

(3.30)

\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)\left((U_{k}^{(2m)}(\Delta))^{r}-U_{k}(T)\right)dk\right\|\leq\frac{C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m}(R,N)+r\alpha_{\hat{f},R}R_{m}(\delta)}{(1+\eta_{R}(T/r))^{1-r}}.

Proof.

Fix
k\in[-R,R]
and write
A_{k}:=U_{k}^{(2m)}(\Delta)
and
B_{k}:=U_{k}(\Delta)
. Since
U_{k}(T)=U_{k}(\Delta)^{r}=B_{k}^{r}
, the telescoping identity gives

(3.31)

A_{k}^{r}-B_{k}^{r}=\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}A_{k}^{\,r-1-j}(A_{k}-B_{k})B_{k}^{j}.

Taking norms and using that
B_{k}
is unitary, so
\|B_{k}^{j}\|=1
, we obtain

(3.32)

\|A_{k}^{r}-B_{k}^{r}\|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\|A_{k}\|^{\,r-1-j}\|A_{k}-B_{k}\|.

By
3.10
,
\|A_{k}-B_{k}\|\leq\eta_{k}(\Delta)
. Hence,
\|A_{k}\|\leq\|B_{k}\|+\|A_{k}-B_{k}\|\leq 1+\eta_{k}(\Delta)
, and therefore

(3.33)

\|A_{k}^{r}-B_{k}^{r}\|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1-j}\eta_{k}(\Delta)\leq r\eta_{k}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1}.

Multiplying by
|\hat{f}(k)|/\sqrt{2\pi}
and integrating over
[-R,R]
yields

(3.34)

\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}\hat{f}(k)\Big(\big(U_{k}^{(2m)}(\Delta)\big)^{r}-U_{k}(T)\Big)dk\right\|\leq\frac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\,\eta_{k}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1}dk,

which proves
Equation

3.29
. Noting that for
|k|\leq R
,
\eta_{k}(\Delta)\leq\eta_{R}(T/r)
implies that

(3.35)

\frac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\,\eta_{k}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1}dk\leq\frac{r}{\sqrt{2\pi}}(1+\eta_{R}(T/r))^{r-1}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\,\eta_{k}(\Delta)dk.

Using the definition of
\eta_{k}(\Delta)
and the quantities
\Lambda_{m}(R,N)
and
\alpha_{\hat{f},R}
, we find

(3.36)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-R}^{R}|\hat{f}(k)|\,\eta_{k}(\Delta)dk=C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Lambda_{m}(R,N)+\alpha_{\hat{f},R}R_{m}(\delta).

Substituting
\Delta=T/r
gives

(3.37)

r\left(C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Lambda_{m}(R,N)+\alpha_{\hat{f},R}R_{m}(\delta)\right)=C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m}(R,N)+r\,\alpha_{\hat{f},R}R_{m}(\delta).

This completes the proof.
∎

3.4
Coupling of Truncation and Inner Simulation Errors

The key point of
Section

3.3
is that, after integrating over
k\in[-R,R]
, the inner simulation error depends on
\Lambda_{m}(R,N)
. Hence,
R
and the inner simulation error are coupled. Increasing
R
reduces truncation error but, when
\Phi_{m}(k,N)
grows with
|k|
, also increases
\Lambda_{m}(R,N)
and amplifies simulation error. We capture this trade-off through a feasibility problem. Define

(3.38)

\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}}:=\left\{(R,\delta,r)\in(0,\infty)^{2}\times\mathbb{N}:E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)+E_{\mathrm{MPF}}(T)\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}},\frac{T}{r}\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)\right\}.

Here
\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}
is the total error budget for the truncation and inner simulation errors. If
E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)\geq\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}
, then
(R,\delta,r)\notin\mathcal{F}_{\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}}
for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
. Hence a positive error budget for the inner simulation is available only when
R\in\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}}
. For such
R
, define
\epsilon_{R}:=\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}-E_{\mathrm{trunc}}(R)>0
. After fixing
R\in\mathcal{H}_{\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}}
, the problem therefore reduces to determining pairs
(r,\delta)\in\mathbb{N}\times(0,\infty)
such that

(3.39)

E_{\mathrm{MPF}}(T)\leq\epsilon_{R},\quad\frac{T}{r}\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)

The choice of
r
depends on whether
2.3
or
2.4
instantiates
3.10
. We defer this discussion to
Section

4
.

4

4.1
Post-Quadrature Error Bound

The analysis in
Section

3.4
was carried out before discretizing the LCHS integral. We now show that the same coupling persists after applying a quadrature rule. Let
Q=\{(k_{i},w_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}
be a quadrature rule, with
k_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
w_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. Define its radius by
R_{Q}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|k_{i}|
.
The corresponding ideal quadrature operator is

(4.1)

W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}U_{k_{i}}(T),\qquad v_{i}:=\frac{w_{i}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k_{i};c,d)

and the associated quadrature mass is
\alpha_{Q}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|v_{i}|
. Under
3.10
, we also define the discrete weighted MPF profile by

(4.2)

\Lambda_{m,Q}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|v_{i}|\Phi_{m}(k_{i},N).

This is the post-quadrature analogue of
\Lambda_{m}(R,N)
. Define
\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\Phi_{m}(k_{i},N)
. Let
U_{k_{i}}^{(2m)}(T)
denote the
2m
-th order MPF approximation to
U_{k_{i}}(T)
over
[0,T]
, and define

W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}U^{(2m)}_{k_{i}}(T)
.
Specific quadrature rules are discussed in
Section

4.6
. We have the following discrete analog of
3.14
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
Q=\{(k_{i},w_{i})\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}
be a quadrature rule and set
R_{Q}:=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|k_{i}|
. Fix
m,N\geq 1
,
T>0
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\delta>0
. Set
\Delta:=T/r
. Assume that
0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta)
. Then

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|v_{i}|\|U_{k_{i}}(T)-U^{(2m)}_{k_{i}}(T)\|\leq r\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|v_{i}|\eta_{k_{i}}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k_{i}}(\Delta))^{r-1}.

In particular, if
\eta_{Q}(\Delta):=\max_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\eta_{k_{i}}(\Delta)
, then

(4.4)

\|W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}\|\leq(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\left(C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}+r\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\right).

Proof.

The result follows by the same telescoping argument as in
3.14
, followed by the definitions of
\alpha_{Q}
and
\Lambda_{m,Q}
.
∎

4.1
shows that, after fixing the quadrature rule, the inner simulation error couples to the quadrature error through
R_{Q}
,
\alpha_{Q}
, and
\Lambda_{m,Q}
. This is the post-quadrature analogue of
Section

3.4
. We end with a total error bound, which is the starting point for the complexity analysis in
Section

4.3
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
such that
d
and
y_{0}
are chosen as in
3.4
or
3.5
. Let
Q
be a quadrature rule,
T,\delta>0
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\Delta=T/r
. If
0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta)
, then

(4.5)

\displaystyle\|e^{-AT}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}\|

\displaystyle\leq E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})+E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)

(4.6)

\displaystyle+(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\left(C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}+r\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\right).

Proof.

Let
W_{\infty}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)U_{k}(T)dk
, and define the quadrature error by
E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q):=\|W_{\infty}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}\|
. By the triangle inequality, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\|e^{-AT}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|e^{-AT}-W_{\infty}\|+\|W_{\infty}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}\|+\|W_{Q}^{\mathrm{ideal}}-W_{Q}^{\mathrm{MPF}}\|.

The first term is bounded by
E_{\mathrm{approx}}(y_{0})
, the second term is
E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)
, and the third term follows from
4.1
. Since the nodes of
Q
lie in
[-R_{Q},R_{Q}]
, the MPF step-size condition is imposed with
R_{Q}
, yielding a bound involving the quadrature-induced quantities
\alpha_{Q}
,
\Lambda_{m,Q}
, and
\eta_{Q}
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The quantity
E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)
in
4.2
measures the total outer discretization error from the infinite LCHS integral to the finite quadrature rule. For concrete quadrature rules, this error may itself be decomposed into truncation and quadrature components (see
Section

4.6
).

4.2
shows that the quadrature rule affects the final error through
E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)
and through
R_{Q}
,
\alpha_{Q}
, and
\Lambda_{m,Q}
. Thus, quadrature couples discretization error to quantum implementation error. This yields the post-quadrature problem. Choose an admissible rule
Q
with
E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)<\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}
, leaving residual budget
\epsilon_{Q}:=\epsilon_{\mathrm{comb}}-E_{\mathrm{quad}}(Q)>0
. Then choose
(r,\delta)\in\mathbb{N}\times(0,\infty)
such that

(4.8)

(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\left(C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}+r\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\right)\leq\epsilon_{Q},\quad\frac{T}{r}\leq\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta).

This is the post-quadrature analogue of the discussion in
Section

3.4
.

4.2
Quantum Implementation

The complexity estimates below address two tasks in a fully quantum implementation. The first task is to construct a block-encoding of the post-quadrature approximation

(4.9)

W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}U^{(2m)}_{k_{i}}(T)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\left[U_{2,k_{i}}\left(\frac{T}{rb_{j}}\right)\right]^{b_{j}}\right)^{r}.

Here
v_{i}
denotes the quadrature coefficients, while
a_{j},b_{j}
denote the coefficients from
Section

2.4
. Given
Q
, define the quadrature preparation oracles

(4.10)

\operatorname{PREP}^{Q}_{\operatorname{R}}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{Q}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\sqrt{v_{i}}\ket{i},\quad\operatorname{PREP}^{Q}_{\operatorname{L}}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_{Q}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\overline{\sqrt{v_{i}}}\ket{i},

where
\alpha_{Q}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}|v_{i}|
. For each
i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}
, let
\mathcal{U}_{Q}
be a unitary block-encoding of
U_{k_{i}}^{(2m)}(T)
, normalized to one after amplitude amplification at each of the
r
time steps. Hence, we have

(4.11)

(\bra{0}\otimes I)\mathcal{U}_{Q}(\ket{0}\otimes I)=U_{k_{i}}^{(2m)}(T).

Define the controlled unitary
\operatorname{SELECT}_{Q}:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\ket{i}\bra{i}\otimes\mathcal{U}_{Q}
. By the outer LCU construction, we obtain a block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}

(4.12)

(\bra{0}\bra{0}\otimes I)(\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{L}}^{Q\dagger}\otimes I)\operatorname{SELECT}_{Q}(\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}}^{Q}\otimes I)(\ket{0}\ket{0}\otimes I)=\frac{W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}}{\alpha_{Q}}.

Writing
K_{m}:=\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1}
, one controlled implementation of
U_{k_{i}}^{(2m)}(T)
with
r
time steps costs
\mathcal{O}(rK_{m})
controlled second-order product-formula queries. Hence, one use of
\operatorname{SELECT}_{Q}
has the same cost. One block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}/\alpha_{Q}
uses
\operatorname{SELECT}_{Q}
, the quadrature preparation oracles
\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{L}}^{Q}
,
\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}}^{Q}
, and their required inverses in the outer LCU construction.

Remark 4.4
.

The implementation of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}
is a nested LCU construction. The outer LCU is the quadrature sum over
q
, with normalization
\alpha_{Q}
, while the inner LCU implements each selected
U_{k_{i}}^{(2m)}(T)
by an MPF. We do not flatten this into one LCU over
(q,j_{1},\ldots,j_{r})
, since that would introduce normalization
\alpha_{Q}\|\vec{a}\|_{1}^{r}
, which is not the implementation model used here.

The second task is to prepare the normalized output state
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)/\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\|
. Write
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}:=u(0)/\|u(0)\|
. Applying the block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}/\alpha_{Q}
to
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}
and post-selecting the ancillas on
\ket{0}
gives the un-normalized state

W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}/\alpha_{Q}
, whose norm is

(4.13)

\frac{\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}\|}{\alpha_{Q}}=\frac{\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\|}{\alpha_{Q}\|u(0)\|}.

Hence, amplitude amplification requires
\chi_{Q}(u(0)):=\frac{\alpha_{Q}\|u(0)\|}{\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\|}
uses of the block-encoding, up to absolute constants. Therefore, normalized state preparation costs
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q}(u(0))rK_{m})
controlled second-order product-formula queries,
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q}(u(0)))
uses of the quadrature preparation oracles and their inverses, and
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q}(u(0)))
uses of the state-preparation oracle for
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}
and its inverse.

Remark 4.5
.

Let
u(T):=e^{-AT}u(0)
. If
\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}-e^{-AT}\|\leq\epsilon
and
\epsilon\|u(0)\|\leq\|u(T)\|/2
, then

(4.14)

\|W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\|\geq\|u(T)\|-\epsilon\|u(0)\|\geq\frac{\|u(T)\|}{2}.

Consequently,
\chi_{Q}(u(0))\leq 2\alpha_{Q}\|u(0)\|/\|u(T)\|
, so the state query complexity is
\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{\|u(0)\|}{\|u(T)\|}\alpha_{Q}rK_{m}\right)
, which becomes
\mathcal{O}(\alpha_{Q}rK_{m}/\|u(T)\|)
when
\|u(0)\|=1
. This is the standard form in which normalized-state query complexity is presented in the literature.

4.3
Overall Query Complexity

Split the error tolerance as
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}\leq\epsilon
, where
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
controls
E_{\operatorname{approx}}(y_{0})
and
\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}
controls the combined quadrature and inner-simulation errors.
3.4
and
3.5
gives sufficient conditions on
c,d
to ensure the approximation error is bounded blow by
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}
. Choose a quadrature rule
Q
such that
E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q)<\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}
, and define
\epsilon_{Q}:=\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}-E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q)
. Specific quadrature rules are discussed in
Section

4.6
. We first prove an abstract fixed-
2m
-order overall complexity estimate, based on
3.10
, for arbitrary
Q
. For
\delta>0
, define

(4.15)

r_{Q}(\delta):=\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{T}{\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta)},\left(2C_{m}T^{2m+1}\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}\right)^{1/2m},\left(\frac{2e\,C_{m}T^{2m+1}\Lambda_{m,Q}}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)^{1/2m}\right\}\right\rceil.

Theorem 4.6
.

Fix
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
and
T>0
.
Let
\epsilon,\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}},\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}>0
satisfy
\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}\leq\epsilon
. Let
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
be an admissible kernel profile, and let
Q
be a quadrature rule such that
E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q)<\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}
. Set
\epsilon_{Q}:=\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}-E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q)>0
. Suppose there exists
\delta>0
such that

(4.16)

r_{Q}(\delta)R_{m}(\delta)\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad r_{Q}(\delta)\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\leq\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}.

Choose
r=r_{Q}(\delta)
. Then
\|e^{-AT}-W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\|\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, a block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}/\alpha_{Q}
can be implemented using

(4.17)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}\left(r_{Q}(\delta)K_{m}\right)

controlled second-order product-formula queries. If
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\neq 0
, the normalized output state proportional
to
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\ket{u(0)}
can be prepared using

(4.18)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{state}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{Q}(u(0))r_{Q}(\delta)K_{m}\right)

controlled second-order product-formula queries. Equivalently, this uses
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q}(u(0)))
applications of the block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}/\alpha_{Q}
, hence
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q}(u(0)))
calls to
\operatorname{SELECT}_{Q}
, the quadrature preparation oracles, their inverses, and the state-preparation oracle for
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}
.

Proof.

Let
r=r_{Q}(\delta)
. By
Equation

4.15
, we have

(4.19)

\frac{T}{r}\leq\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta),\quad C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}.

Together with
Equation

4.16
, this gives

(4.20)

r\eta_{Q}(T/r)=C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}+rR_{m}(\delta)\leq 1.

Hence, we have

(4.21)

(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\leq(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r}\leq e^{r\eta_{Q}(T/r)}\leq e.

Using this estimate, the third condition in
Equation

4.15
, and
Equation

4.16
, we obtain

(4.22)

\displaystyle(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\left(C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}+r\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\right)\leq e\left(\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}+\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}\right)=\epsilon_{Q}.

By the estimate in
4.2
and the admissibility of the kernel and quadrature rule, we have

(4.23)

\displaystyle\|e^{-AT}-W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}+E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q)+\epsilon_{Q}=\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}\leq\epsilon.

The block-encoding and normalized-state query estimates follow from the discussion
Section

4.2
.
∎

In what follows, define the
m
-dependent post-quadrature effective commutator scale

(4.24)

\mu_{m,Q}(\delta):=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\Delta(R_{Q},N,\delta)},(C_{m}\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast})^{1/2m},\left(C_{m}\Lambda_{m,Q}\right)^{1/2m}\right\}.

It can then be checked that
Equation

4.15
implies that

(4.25)

r_{Q}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\mu_{m,Q}(\delta)\max\left\{T,\,T^{1+\frac{1}{2m}},\,T^{1+\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2m}}\right\}\right),

for
\epsilon_{Q}>0
. Under the additional assumption
0<\epsilon_{Q}<1
, this simplifies to

(4.26)

r_{Q}(\delta)=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\mu_{m,Q}(\delta)\max\left\{T,\,T^{1+\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2m}}\right\}\right).

In what follows, assume
0<\epsilon_{Q}<1
. In
4.7
, we allow mild MPF-order dependence in the effective commutator scale and optimize over the order to obtain complexity logarithmic in
T/\epsilon_{Q}
, up to the growth envelope
F
.

Corollary 4.7
.

Assume the error budget, kernel profile, and quadrature hypotheses of
4.6
. Let
K_{m}:=\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1}
and
0<\epsilon_{Q}<1
. Suppose there exist
m_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu_{Q}\geq 1
, and a non-decreasing function
F:[m_{0},\infty)\to[1,\infty)
such that one can choose
\delta_{m}>0
satisfying

(4.27)

\mu_{m,Q}(\delta_{m})\leq\mu_{Q}F(m).

for
m\geq m_{0}
. Assume also that, for every
m\geq m_{0}
, we have

(4.28)

r_{Q}(\delta_{m})R_{m}(\delta_{m})\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad r_{Q}(\delta_{m})\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta_{m})\leq\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}.

Choose
m=\max\left\{m_{0},\left\lceil\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right\rceil\right\}
and set
\delta=\delta_{m}
and
r=r_{Q}(\delta_{m})
. Then
\|e^{-AT}-W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}\|\leq\epsilon
. Moreover, we have

(4.29)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\Bigg).

If
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}u(0)\neq 0
, we have

(4.30)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{state}}(u(0))=\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\chi_{Q}(u(0))\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\Bigg).

If
T\geq 1
and
\mu_{Q}TF(m)\geq 1
, then
Equation

4.29
and
Equation

4.30
simplify to

(4.31)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\mu_{Q}TF(m)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\Bigg),

and

(4.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{state}}(u(0))=\mathcal{O}\Bigg(\chi_{Q}(u(0))\,\mu_{Q}TF(m)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\Bigg).

Proof.

The admissibility conditions in
Equation

4.28
allow us to apply
4.6
with
\delta=\delta_{m}
, so the error is at most
\epsilon
. By
Equation

4.17
, we have
\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}(r_{Q}(\delta_{m})K_{m})
. Using
Equation

4.26
and
Equation

4.27
, we obtain

(4.33)

r_{Q}(\delta_{m})=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\left\{T,\,T^{1+\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2m}}\right\}\right).

Since
T^{1+\frac{1}{2m}}\epsilon_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2m}}=T\left(\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)^{1/2m}
, the choice
m=\max\left\{m_{0},\left\lceil\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right\rceil\right\}
implies
(T/\epsilon_{Q})^{1/2m}=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Therefore, we have

(4.34)

r_{Q}(\delta_{m})=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right).

Consequently, we have
\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}\left(K_{m}\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right)\right)
.
Since
K_{m}=\mathcal{O}(m^{2}(\log m)^{2})
as in
Section

2.4
, the choice of
m
gives

(4.35)

m^{2}(\log m)^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\right),

with constants depending at most on
m_{0}
. Hence, we have

(4.36)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{block}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\right).

This proves
Equation

4.29
. Multiplying by
\chi_{Q}(u(0))
gives
Equation

4.30
. If
T\geq 1
and
\mu_{Q}TF(m)\geq 1
, then

(4.37)

1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}=1+\mu_{Q}TF(m)=\mathcal{O}(\mu_{Q}TF(m)).

The derivation establishes
Equation

4.31
and
Equation

4.32
.
∎

4.4
General Commutator Scaling Specialization

The analysis in
Section

4.3
allowed arbitrary quadrature under
3.10
. We now specialize to
2.3
, still with arbitrary quadrature. We first discuss the applicability of
2.3
. For each
G_{k_{i}}=H+k_{i}L
, we must compute
\inf_{j\geq J}\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},j}(G_{k_{i}})^{-1/j}
. Define
\mathcal{C}_{j}(H,L):=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq j}\mathcal{C}_{j,\ell}(H,L)
. We have

(4.40)

\displaystyle\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},j}(G_{k_{i}})\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{j}|k_{i}|^{\ell}\mathcal{C}_{j,\ell}(H,L)\leq\mathcal{C}_{j}(H,L)(j+1)\max\{1,|k_{i}|\}^{j}.

Let
\chi_{J}(H,L):=\inf_{j\geq J}\mathcal{C}_{j}(H,L)^{-1/j}
. For
J\geq 1
, we have

(4.41)

\displaystyle\inf_{j\geq J}\alpha_{\operatorname{comm},j}(G_{k_{i}})^{-1/j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\inf_{j\geq J}((j+1)^{-1/j}\mathcal{C}_{j}(H,L)^{-1/j})}{\max\{1,|k_{i}|\}}

(4.42)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\inf_{j\geq J}(j+1)^{-1/j})(\inf_{j\geq J}\mathcal{C}_{j}(H,L)^{-1/j})}{\max\{1,|k_{i}|\}}

(4.43)

The last inequality follows from

\displaystyle\Phi_{m,\nu}(H,L):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}\\

Lemma 4.8

\displaystyle\|U_{\operatorname{MPF},k_{i}}(\Delta)-e^{-iG_{k_{i}}\Delta}\|\leq

\displaystyle\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in 2\mathbb{Z}_{+}\\

Lemma 4.9

For

We now fix a quadrature rule

4.6.1
Uniform Trapezoidal Rule

We first specialize to the uniform trapezoidal rule. Following the Low–Somma discretization strategy
[
52
]
, we compare the infinite LCHS integral with the infinite trapezoidal sum using strip analyticity, and then truncate the lattice to obtain the quadrature rule used in the LCU implementation. We use the notation

(4.63)

F_{T}(z):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(z;c,d)U_{z}(T).

Then
W_{\infty}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)U_{k}(T)\,dk=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{T}(k)\,dk
. We first state
4.12
, which is proved in
Section

B.5
, a basic estimate used in the complexity analysis of the uniform trapezoidal quadrature rule.

Lemma 4.12
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, and define

(4.64)

\rho_{\ast}:=\begin{cases}1,&a=1,\\
\min\{1,b\},&a>1.\end{cases}

For every
\rho\in(0,\rho_{\ast})
,
F_{T}
is analytic on an open set containing the closed strip
\overline{S}_{\rho}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\ |\operatorname{Im}z|\leq\rho\}
. Moreover,
F_{T}(x+i\beta)
decays uniformly to zero on
\overline{S}_{\rho}
as
|x|\to\infty
, and

(4.65)

\sup_{|\beta|\leq\rho}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|F_{T}(x+i\beta)\|\,dx\leq M_{a,b}(\rho),

where
D_{a,b}(\rho)=(b-\rho)^{a-1}
and

(4.66)

M_{a,b}(\rho):=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{(1-\rho)D_{a,b}(\rho)}\exp\left(d+\frac{\rho^{2}-1}{4c^{2}}+\rho\max\{-d,d+T\|L\|\}\right).

We use the uniform trapezoidal rule because it converges exponentially for functions analytic in a strip around the real axis
[
52
]
. This is formalized below.

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
\rho>0
. Suppose that
g
is analytic on an open set containing
\overline{S}_{\rho}
and that
g
decays uniformly to zero on
\overline{S}_{\rho}
as
|z|\to\infty
. If
M>0
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|g(x+i\beta)\|\,dx\leq M

for every
\beta\in(-\rho,\rho)
, then, for every
h>0
, we have

(4.67)

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(x)\,dx-h\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}g(qh)\right\|\leq\frac{2M}{e^{2\pi\rho/h}-1}.

Proof.

This is the exponentially convergent trapezoidal estimate of Trefethen–Weideman
[
68
, Theorem 5.1]
, in the matrix-valued form used by Low–Somma
[
52
, Lemma 10]
.
∎

We now analyze the uniform trapezoidal rule in our setting. Fix
R>0
and
h>0
, and set
n_{h}:=\left\lceil\frac{R}{h}\right\rceil
and
R_{h}:=n_{h}h
. Then
R_{h}\geq R
and
R_{h}<R+h
. Define the finite uniform trapezoidal rule

(4.68)

Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}:=\{(k_{i},w_{i}):k_{i}=qh,\ w_{i}=h,\ q=-n_{h},\ldots,n_{h}\}.

For this rule,
R_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}=R_{h}
. The LCHS quadrature coefficients are
v_{q}=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)
. The corresponding ideal quadrature operator is

(4.69)

W_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap},\operatorname{ideal}}:=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)U_{qh}(T)=h\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}F_{T}(qh).

The associated LCU normalization and discrete moments are

(4.70)

\alpha_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}:=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)|,\quad M_{\nu,R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}:=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)|\,|qh|^{\nu},

for
\nu\geq 0
. Moreover, the omitted lattice tail is

(4.71)

E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h}):=\frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{|q|>n_{h}}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)|.

4.14
bounds the quadrature errors
E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h})
and
E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)
, the latter arising from
4.13
.

Proposition 4.14
.

Fix a kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, and let
\rho\in(0,\rho_{\ast})
, where
\rho_{\ast}
is defined in
Equation

4.64
. Let
R>0
,
h>0

(4.72)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}})=\|W_{\infty}-W_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap},\operatorname{ideal}}\|\leq E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)+E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h}),

where
E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho):=\frac{2M_{a,b}(\rho)}{e^{2\pi\rho/h}-1}
. Moreover, we have
E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h})\leq E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R_{h})\leq E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R)
.
Consequently, we have

(4.73)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}})\leq E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)+E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R).

Proof.

Applying
4.13
to
g=F_{T}
, and using
4.12
with
M=M_{a,b}(\rho)
, gives

(4.74)

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{T}(x)\,dx-h\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{T}(qh)\right\|\leq E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho).

Therefore,

(4.75)

\displaystyle\left\|W_{\infty}-W_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap},\operatorname{ideal}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{T}(x)\,dx-h\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}F_{T}(qh)\right\|+h\sum_{|q|>n_{h}}\|F_{T}(qh)\|

(4.76)

\displaystyle\leq E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)+E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h}).

It remains to compare the lattice tail with the continuous truncation error. For real
k
,
U_{k}(T)
is unitary and

(4.77)

|\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)|=\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4c^{2})}}{\sqrt{1+k^{2}}(b^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}.

This function is even. For
k>0

(4.78)

\frac{d}{dk}\log|\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)|=-\frac{k}{2c^{2}}-\frac{k}{1+k^{2}}-(a-1)\frac{k}{b^{2}+k^{2}}<0.

Hence
k\mapsto|\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)|
is decreasing on
[0,\infty)
. Since
R_{h}=n_{h}h
, monotonicity gives

(4.79)

h\sum_{q=n_{h}+1}^{\infty}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(qh;c,d)|\leq\int_{R_{h}}^{\infty}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)|\,dk.

The same argument applies on the negative half-line. Multiplying by
1/\sqrt{2\pi}
gives
E_{\operatorname{tail},h}(R_{h})\leq E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R_{h})
. Finally, since
R_{h}\geq R
, monotonicity of

gives
E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R_{h})\leq E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R)
.
∎

4.14
bounds the quadrature error by mesh and tail errors. Then
4.15

(4.80)

\displaystyle C_{\operatorname{tail}}:=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{b+1}{b}\right)^{a-1}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}},\quad C_{\operatorname{ker}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{b+1}{b}\right)^{a-1}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}},\quad\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}:=\frac{(b+1)^{a-1}}{2\pi}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}}.

4.15
bounds the quantities that determine the post-quadrature parameters for the trapezoidal quadrature rule.

Corollary 4.15
.

Let
\rho\in(0,\rho_{\ast})

\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}},\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}>0
. Define

(4.81)

\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}:=\max\left\{1,\log\left(\frac{C_{\operatorname{tail}}}{\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}}\right)\right\},\quad\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}:=\log\left(1+\frac{2M_{a,b}(\rho)}{\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}}\right).

Choose
h=\min\left\{1,2c,\frac{2\pi\rho}{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}}\right\}
and
R=2c\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}
, and set
n_{h}=\lceil R/h\rceil
,
R_{h}=n_{h}h
. Then
E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}
. Moreover, we have

(4.82)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}|

\displaystyle=2n_{h}+1=\mathcal{O}\left(1+(1+c)\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}+\frac{c}{\rho}\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}\,\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}\right),

(4.83)

\displaystyle R_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}

\displaystyle=R_{h}=\mathcal{O}\left(c\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}+\min\left\{1,2c,\frac{\rho}{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}}\right\}\right).

For a fixed admissible kernel profile and balanced budgets
\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}\asymp\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}\asymp\epsilon_{q}
, we obtain

(4.84)

|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{c}{\rho}\log^{3/2}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\right),\quad R_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(c\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)}\right),

for fixed
\rho
and
c
. For
\nu\geq 0
, define

(4.85)

\mathfrak{M}_{\nu,a,b}(c):=\begin{cases}1,&\nu<a-1,\\
1+\log(1+c),&\nu=a-1,\\
1+c^{\nu-a+1},&\nu>a-1.\end{cases}

Then there exists a constant
C_{\nu,a,b}^{\operatorname{trap}}>0
, depending only on
\nu,a,b
, such that

(4.86)

M_{\nu,R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}C_{\nu,a,b}^{\operatorname{trap}}\mathfrak{M}_{\nu,a,b}(c).

In particular,
\alpha_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}=M_{0,R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}C_{0,a,b}^{\operatorname{trap}}\mathfrak{M}_{0,a,b}(c)
. Equivalently, the trapezoidal moments satisfy

(4.87)

M_{\nu,R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}),&\nu<a-1,\\
\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}\log(1+c)),&\nu=a-1,\\
\mathcal{O}(\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}c^{\nu-a+1}),&\nu>a-1.\end{cases}

Proof.

The choice of
h
controls the mesh error. By
4.13
,
E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}
. It remains to control the truncation error. By
3.7
,
E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R)\leq C_{\operatorname{tail}}\operatorname{erfc}(R/(2c))
. Since
\operatorname{erfc}(x)\leq e^{-x^{2}}
, the condition
R\geq 2c\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}
implies
E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R)\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}
. Combining these estimates with
4.14
gives

(4.88)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}})\leq E_{\operatorname{trap}}(h,\rho)+E_{\operatorname{trunc}}(R)\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}.

Since
n_{h}=\lceil R/h\rceil
, we have
|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}|=2n_{h}+1\leq 2R/h+3
. Moreover,

(4.89)

\frac{1}{h}=\max\left\{1,\frac{1}{2c},\frac{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}}{2\pi\rho}\right\}=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\frac{1}{c}+\frac{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}}{\rho}\right).

Substituting
R=2c\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}}
gives the bound on
|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}|
in
Equation

4.82
. Moreover,
R_{h}=n_{h}h\leq R+h
, giving the bound on
R_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}
in
Equation

4.83
. The balanced-budget estimates follow from
\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}=\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\epsilon_{q}))
, for fixed
\rho
and
c
. It remains to bound the LCU normalization and discrete moments. For real
k
,
Equation

4.77
implies

(4.90)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}|\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)|=\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}/(4c^{2})}}{\sqrt{1+k^{2}}(b^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}.

Therefore,

(4.91)

M_{\nu,R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}h\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu}}{\sqrt{1+(qh)^{2}}(b^{2}+(qh)^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}.

For
k>0
, we have

(4.92)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+k^{2}}(b^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{b^{a-1}},\frac{1}{k^{a}}\right\}.

Indeed, the first bound follows from
\sqrt{1+k^{2}}\geq 1
and
(b^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}\geq b^{a-1}
, while the second follows from
\sqrt{1+k^{2}}\geq k
and
(b^{2}+k^{2})^{(a-1)/2}\geq k^{a-1}
. Let

(4.93)

\kappa_{b}:=\begin{cases}1,&a=1,\\
b^{(a-1)/a},&a>1.\end{cases}

We split the full-line sum in
Equation

4.91
into the regions
|qh|\leq\kappa_{b}
and
|qh|>\kappa_{b}
. Since
h\leq 1
, the first contribution is bounded by a constant depending only on
\nu,a,b
. More precisely,

(4.94)

h\sum_{|qh|\leq\kappa_{b}}\frac{e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu}}{\sqrt{1+(qh)^{2}}(b^{2}+(qh)^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}\leq C_{\nu,a,b}.

On the complementary region,
Equation

4.92
gives

(4.95)

h\sum_{|qh|>\kappa_{b}}\frac{e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu}}{\sqrt{1+(qh)^{2}}(b^{2}+(qh)^{2})^{(a-1)/2}}\leq h\sum_{|qh|>\kappa_{b}}e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu-a}.

The latter Riemann sum has three regimes. If
\nu<a-1
, then
|k|^{\nu-a}
is integrable at infinity, and hence

(4.96)

h\sum_{|qh|>\kappa_{b}}e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu-a}\leq C_{\nu,a,b}.

If
\nu=a-1
, then the summand behaves like
1/|qh|
until the Gaussian cutoff scale
|qh|\sim c
, and therefore

(4.97)

h\sum_{|qh|>\kappa_{b}}e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{-1}\leq C_{a,b}\bigl(1+\log(1+c)\bigr).

If
\nu>a-1
, comparison with the corresponding Gaussian integral gives

(4.98)

\displaystyle h\sum_{|qh|>\kappa_{b}}e^{-(qh)^{2}/(4c^{2})}|qh|^{\nu-a}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\nu,a,b}+C_{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-k^{2}/(4c^{2})}k^{\nu-a}\,dk\leq C_{\nu,a,b}+C_{\nu}c^{\nu-a+1}.

Combining these three cases with
Equation

4.91
proves
Equation

4.86
. The normalization bound is the case
\nu=0
, and the asymptotic statement follows directly from the definition of
\mathfrak{M}_{\nu,a,b}(c)
.
∎

3.5
shows that, when the kernel parameters satisfy

, one may take

. Assuming that all error-tolerance parameters are of the same order as

,

implies

(4.99)

|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\log^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\right),\quad R_{Q_{R,h}^{\operatorname{trap}}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\right).

Moreover,

4.6.2
Sinh–Sinh Trapezoidal Rule

We next consider a non-uniform quadrature rule obtained by applying the trapezoidal rule after the change of variables
k=\phi_{\eta}(x):=\eta\sinh x
, where
0<\eta\leq 2c
is a free scale parameter. Set
\lambda_{\eta}:=\eta/(2c)
. Under this change of variables, the Gaussian factor in
\hat{f}_{a,b}
becomes
e^{-\phi_{\eta}(x)^{2}/(4c^{2})}=e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}x}
, so the transformed integrand has double-exponential decay along the real axis. The choice
\eta=2c
gives the Gaussian-normalized case
e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}x}=e^{-\sinh^{2}x}
. See
[
68
, Section 15]
for more on this quadrature rule. Define

(4.100)

G_{T,\eta}(z):=F_{T}(\phi_{\eta}(z))\phi_{\eta}^{\prime}(z)=\eta\cosh z\,F_{T}(\eta\sinh z).

Then, by the change of variables
k=\phi_{\eta}(x)
, we have
W_{\infty}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{T}(k)\,dk=\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{T,\eta}(x)\,dx
. The exponential convergence of the sinh–sinh rule follows by applying the infinite-line trapezoidal estimate to the transformed integrand
G_{T,\eta}
. The relevant analyticity strip is now an
x
-strip rather than the original
k
-strip. Let

(4.101)

\beta_{\ast,\eta}:=\sup\left\{\beta>0:G_{T,\eta}\text{ is analytic on an open set containing }\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq\beta\}\right\}.

Equivalently,
\beta_{\ast,\eta}
is limited by the nearest preimage of the poles
k=-i
and
k=ib
under
z\mapsto\eta\sinh z
, and by the requirement that the transformed Gaussian decay on horizontal translates. In particular, one may restrict to
\beta<\pi/4
, since

(4.102)

\operatorname{Re}(\sinh^{2}(x+i\beta))=\sinh^{2}x\cos(2\beta)-\sin^{2}\beta

has positive quadratic growth in
|x|
whenever
|\beta|<\pi/4
.
For
\beta\in(0,\min\{\beta_{\ast,\eta},\pi/4\})
, define

(4.103)

\delta_{1,\eta}(\beta):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}\\
|\omega|\leq\beta\end{subarray}}\left|1-i\phi_{\eta}(x+i\omega)\right|,\qquad\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta):=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}\\
|\omega|\leq\beta\end{subarray}}\left|b+i\phi_{\eta}(x+i\omega)\right|.

Here
\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)
is only needed when
a>1
. Since
\beta<\beta_{\ast,\eta}
, these quantities are strictly positive. Set

(4.104)

\rho_{\beta}:=\cos(2\beta)>0,\qquad A_{\eta,\beta}:=\eta\sin\beta\,\bigl(|d|+T\|L\|\bigr).

Define

(4.105)

M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta):=\frac{\eta(b+1)^{a-1}}{2\pi\,\delta_{1,\eta}(\beta)\,\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)^{a-1}}\exp\left(d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}+\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sin^{2}\beta+A_{\eta,\beta}+\frac{A_{\eta,\beta}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}\right)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}},

with the convention
\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)^{a-1}=1
when
a=1
.

Lemma 4.16
.

Let
0<\eta\leq 2c
and
\beta\in(0,\min\{\beta_{\ast,\eta},\pi/4\})
. Then
G_{T,\eta}
is analytic on an open set containing the closed strip
\overline{S}_{\beta}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|\operatorname{Im}z|\leq\beta\}
. Moreover,
G_{T,\eta}(x+i\omega)
decays uniformly to zero on
\overline{S}_{\beta}
as
|x|\to\infty
, and

(4.106)

\sup_{|\omega|\leq\beta}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|G_{T,\eta}(x+i\omega)\|\,dx\leq M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta).

Consequently, for every
h>0
,

(4.107)

\left\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{T,\eta}(x)\,dx-h\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{T,\eta}(qh)\right\|\leq\frac{2M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta)}{e^{2\pi\beta/h}-1}.

Proof.

Let
z=x+i\omega
, with
|\omega|\leq\beta
, and write
\phi_{\eta}(z)=\eta\sinh z=u+iv
, where
u=\eta\sinh x\cos\omega
and
v=\eta\cosh x\sin\omega
. Since
\beta<\beta_{\ast,\eta}
, the transformed contour stays a positive distance from the pole
k=-i
, and, when
a>1
, from the pole
k=ib
. Hence
|1-i\phi_{\eta}(z)|\geq\delta_{1,\eta}(\beta)
and, for
a>1
,
|b+i\phi_{\eta}(z)|\geq\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)
. Moreover,
|e^{d(1-i\phi_{\eta}(z))}|=e^{d(1+v)}\leq e^{d}e^{|d||v|}
. Using the time-independent bound for
U_{k}(T)
as in
4.12
, we have
\|U_{\phi_{\eta}(z)}(T)\|\leq e^{T\|L\|\,|v|}
. Since
|v|\leq\eta\sin\beta\,\cosh x
, the combined non-Gaussian exponential growth is bounded by
\exp(A_{\eta,\beta}\cosh x)
. Next, we have

(4.108)

\operatorname{Re}(\sinh^{2}(x+i\omega))=\sinh^{2}x\cos(2\omega)-\sin^{2}\omega\geq\rho_{\beta}\sinh^{2}x-\sin^{2}\beta.

Therefore, the Gaussian factor satisfies

(4.109)

|e^{-(\phi_{\eta}(z)^{2}+1)/(4c^{2})}|=e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\operatorname{Re}(\sinh^{2}z)-1/(4c^{2})}\leq\exp\left(-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}\sinh^{2}x+\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sin^{2}\beta-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}\right).

Also
|\cosh(x+i\omega)|\leq\cosh x
. Combining these estimates gives

(4.110)

\|G_{T,\eta}(x+i\omega)\|\leq\frac{\eta(b+1)^{a-1}}{2\pi\,\delta_{1,\eta}(\beta)\,\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)^{a-1}}\exp\left(d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}+\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sin^{2}\beta\right)\cosh x\exp\left(-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}\sinh^{2}x+A_{\eta,\beta}\cosh x\right).

Using
\cosh x\leq 1+|\sinh x|
and
A_{\eta,\beta}|\sinh x|\leq\frac{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}{2}\sinh^{2}x+\frac{A_{\eta,\beta}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}
, we obtain

(4.111)

-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}\sinh^{2}x+A_{\eta,\beta}\cosh x\leq-\frac{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}{2}\sinh^{2}x+A_{\eta,\beta}+\frac{A_{\eta,\beta}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}.

Hence,

(4.112)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|G_{T,\eta}(x+i\omega)\|\,dx\leq\frac{\eta(b+1)^{a-1}}{2\pi\,\delta_{1,\eta}(\beta)\,\delta_{b,\eta}(\beta)^{a-1}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}+\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sin^{2}\beta+A_{\eta,\beta}+\frac{A_{\eta,\beta}^{2}}{2\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}\right)

(4.113)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cosh x\,e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}{2}\sinh^{2}x}\,dx.

With the substitution
u=\sinh x
,
du=\cosh x\,dx
, we get

(4.114)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\cosh x\,e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}{2}\sinh^{2}x}\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-\frac{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}{2}u^{2}}\,du=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\rho_{\beta}}}.

This proves
Equation

4.106
with
M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta)
. The same bound also implies uniform decay as
|x|\to\infty
. Finally, applying
4.13
to
g=G_{T,\eta}
gives
Equation

4.107
.
∎

4.16
gives exponential mesh convergence in
1/h
. The advantage of the sinh–sinh transformation is that the lattice truncation error decays double-exponentially in the
x
-truncation length. Fix
Y>0
and
h>0
. As in
Section

4.6.1
, set
n_{h}:=\left\lceil\frac{Y}{h}\right\rceil
and
Y_{h}:=n_{h}h
. Define the finite sinh–sinh quadrature rule

(4.115)

Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}:=\left\{(k_{i},w_{i}):k_{i}=\eta\sinh(qh),\ w_{i}=\eta h\cosh(qh),\ q=-n_{h},\ldots,n_{h}\right\}.

For this rule,
R_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}=\eta\sinh(Y_{h})
.
The corresponding ideal quadrature operator is

(4.116)

W_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta,\operatorname{ideal}}:=h\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}G_{T,\eta}(qh)=\frac{\eta h}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}\cosh(qh)\,\hat{f}_{a,b}(\eta\sinh(qh);c,d)U_{\eta\sinh(qh)}(T).

The LCHS quadrature coefficients are
v_{q}=\frac{\eta h\cosh(qh)}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(\eta\sinh(qh);c,d)
. The associated LCU normalization is
\alpha_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}:=\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}|v_{q}|
, and the discrete moments are
M_{\nu,Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}:=\sum_{q=-n_{h}}^{n_{h}}|v_{q}|\,|\eta\sinh(qh)|^{\nu}
, for
\nu\geq 0
. Moreover, the omitted transformed-lattice tail is
E_{\operatorname{tail},h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(Y_{h}):=h\sum_{|q|>n_{h}}\|G_{T,\eta}(qh)\|
.

4.17
is the analogue of
4.14
.

Proposition 4.17
.

Let
0<\eta\leq 2c
and
\beta\in(0,\min\{\beta_{\ast,\eta},\pi/4\})
. Let
Y>0
,
h>0
. Then

(4.117)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta})=\left\|W_{\infty}-W_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta,\operatorname{ideal}}\right\|\leq E_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(h,\beta)+E_{\operatorname{tail},h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(Y_{h}),

where
E_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(h,\beta):=\frac{2M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta)}{e^{2\pi\beta/h}-1}
. Moreover, if
\lambda_{\eta}\sinh Y_{h}\geq 1
, then

(4.118)

E_{\operatorname{tail},h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(Y_{h})\leq 2c\sqrt{\pi}\,C_{\operatorname{ker}}\,\operatorname{erfc}(\lambda_{\eta}\sinh Y_{h})\leq 2c\sqrt{\pi}\,C_{\operatorname{ker}}\,e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}Y_{h}},

where
C_{\operatorname{ker}}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{b+1}{b}\right)^{a-1}e^{d-\frac{1}{4c^{2}}}
.

Proof.

The mesh contribution follows from
4.16
. For the tail, use the real-axis bound
\|G_{T,\eta}(x)\|\leq\eta C_{\operatorname{ker}}\cosh x\,e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}x}
, which follows from the Gaussian majorant for
|\hat{f}_{a,b}|
and unitarity of
U_{k}(T)
for real
k
. The function
x\mapsto\cosh x\,e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}x}
is decreasing whenever
\lambda_{\eta}\sinh x\geq 1
. Hence, if
\lambda_{\eta}\sinh Y_{h}\geq 1
, then monotonicity gives

(4.119)

\displaystyle E_{\operatorname{tail},h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(Y_{h})

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{Y_{h}}^{\infty}\eta C_{\operatorname{ker}}\cosh x\,e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}\sinh^{2}x}\,dx

(4.120)

\displaystyle=2\eta C_{\operatorname{ker}}\int_{\sinh Y_{h}}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{\eta}^{2}u^{2}}\,du=2c\sqrt{\pi}\,C_{\operatorname{ker}}\,\operatorname{erfc}(\lambda_{\eta}\sinh Y_{h}).

The final inequality follows from
\operatorname{erfc}(x)\leq e^{-x^{2}}
.
∎

Corollary 4.18
.

Let
0<\eta\leq 2c
,
\beta\in(0,\min\{\beta_{\ast,\eta},\pi/4\})
, and
\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}},\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}>0
. Consider the following quantities:

(4.121)

\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}:=\log\left(1+\frac{2M_{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}(\beta)}{\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}}\right),\quad\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}:=\max\left\{1,\log\left(\frac{2c\sqrt{\pi}C_{\operatorname{ker}}}{\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}}\right)\right\}.

Choose

h=\min\left\{1,\frac{2\pi\beta}{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}\right\},\qquad Y=\max\left\{1,\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}}{\lambda_{\eta}}\right)\right\}=\max\left\{1,\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\frac{2c}{\eta}\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}\right)\right\},

and set
n_{h}=\lceil Y/h\rceil
,
Y_{h}=n_{h}h
. Then
E_{\operatorname{quad}}(Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta})\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}
. Moreover, we have

(4.122)

\displaystyle|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}|

\displaystyle=2n_{h}+1=\mathcal{O}\left(1+\log\left(1+\frac{\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}}{\lambda_{\eta}}\right)\left(1+\frac{\ell_{\operatorname{mesh}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}{\beta}\right)\right),

(4.123)

\displaystyle R_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}

\displaystyle=\eta\sinh(Y_{h})=\mathcal{O}\left(c\sqrt{\ell_{\operatorname{tail}}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}+\eta\right).

For a fixed admissible kernel profile and balanced computational budgets
\epsilon_{\operatorname{mesh}}\asymp\epsilon_{\operatorname{tail}}\asymp\epsilon_{q}
, we obtain

(4.124)

|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\beta}\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\log\left(1+\frac{2c}{\eta}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)}\right)\right),\quad R_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}=\mathcal{O}\left(c\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)}+\eta\right).

Define
\mathfrak{M}_{\nu,a,b}(c)
as in
Equation

4.85
. Then there exists a constant
C_{\nu,a,b}^{\operatorname{sinh}}>0
, depending only on
\nu,a,b
, such that

(4.125)

M_{\nu,Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}C_{\nu,a,b}^{\operatorname{sinh}}\mathfrak{M}_{\nu,a,b}(c).

In particular,
\alpha_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}=M_{0,Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}\leq\widetilde{C}_{\operatorname{ker}}C_{0,a,b}^{\operatorname{sinh}}\mathfrak{M}_{0,a,b}(c)
. Equivalently, the sinh–sinh moments satisfy the same scaling as in
Equation

4.87
.

a,d=\mathcal{O}(1)
,

, then

shows that, for a fixed free-scale choice

|\mathcal{I}_{Q_{Y,h}^{\operatorname{sinh},\eta}}|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\log\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon_{q}}\right)\right),

which is asymptotically smaller than

.

Fractional Diffusion Equation

Let

5.3
No-Jump Dynamics for a Dissipative Ising Model

We next consider the no-jump regime of a dissipative transverse-field Ising model with local spontaneous emission, formulated as a Lindblad dynamics. The no-jump regime is obtained from the standard quantum-trajectory formalism
[
32
,
61
,
48
,
62
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E(V))
be a finite graph. The closed-system transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian is

(5.51)

H=\sum_{j\in V}h_{j}X_{j}+\sum_{\langle j,\ell\rangle\in E(V)}J_{j\ell}Z_{j}Z_{\ell},

where
h_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are transverse-field strengths and
J_{j\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
are Ising couplings. Local spontaneous emission is modeled by Lindblad jump operators
L_{j}=\sqrt{2\gamma_{j}}\,\sigma_{j}^{-}
, where
\gamma_{j}\geq 0
and
\sigma_{j}^{-}=\ket{0}\!\bra{1}
. Thus
L_{j}
represents local decay at site
j
with rate
\gamma_{j}
. The associated Lindblad equation for
\rho
is

(5.52)

\frac{d\rho}{dt}=-i[H,\rho]+\sum_{j\in V}(L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j},\rho\}).

Equation

5.52
gives the unconditional evolution averaged over all emission histories. In the quantum-trajectory picture,
L_{j}\rho L_{j}^{\dagger}
represents a detected jump at site
j
. Conditioning on records with no detected emissions gives the no-jump evolution, generated by the effective Hamiltonian

(5.53)

H_{\operatorname{eff}}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j\in V}L_{j}^{\dagger}L_{j}=\sum_{j\in V}h_{j}X_{j}+\sum_{\langle j,\ell\rangle\in E(V)}J_{j\ell}Z_{j}Z_{\ell}-i\sum_{j\in V}\gamma_{j}n_{j},

where
n_{j}=\sigma_{j}^{+}\sigma_{j}^{-}=|1\rangle\langle 1|=(I-Z_{j})/2
.
The final term reflects the no-jump conditioning, where configurations more likely to emit are suppressed when no photon is detected. The corresponding dynamics are given by

(5.54)

i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=H_{\mathrm{eff}}\ket{\psi(t)}.

Let
L=\sum_{j\in V}\gamma_{j}n_{j}=\sum_{j\in V}\gamma_{j}\frac{I-Z_{j}}{2}
. Then
L\succeq 0
and
H_{\operatorname{eff}}=H-iL
. Multiplying
Equation

5.54
by

(5.55)

\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=-i(H-iL)\ket{\psi(t)}=-(L+iH)\ket{\psi(t)}.

In what follows, take
h_{j}\equiv h
,
J_{j\ell}\equiv J
, and
\gamma_{j}\equiv\gamma
, and assume
\mathcal{G}
has maximum degree
D
, independent of
|V|
. We first verify locality and extensivity. The Hamiltonian

is
2

is supported on on one site and

is supported on two sites. For each site

D

-extensive with

\|A\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}:=\max\left\{\max_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}\left(1+|p-q|_{\operatorname{per}}\right)^{a}|(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}|,\max_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}\sum_{p\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}\left(1+|p-q|_{\operatorname{per}}\right)^{a}|(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}|\right\}.

It can be checked that
Equation

C.7
indeed defines a norm. For
a=0
, this norm controls the spectral norm. Indeed, since
F_{N}
is unitary and
\|A\|\leq\sqrt{\|A\|_{1}\|A\|_{\infty}}
for any matrix
A
, we have

(C.8)

\|A\|=\|F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger}\|\leq\sqrt{\|F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger}\|_{1}\|F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger}\|_{\infty}}\leq\|A\|_{0,\mathcal{F}}.

We first record the two commutator-type operator estimates used below.

1.

Recall from
Equation

5.11
that, for every matrix
A
, we have

(C.9)

(F_{N}\operatorname{ad}_{L_{s}}(A)F_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}=(\lambda_{p}^{s}-\lambda_{q}^{s})(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}.

Applying the weaker consequence of
5.2
from
Equation

5.16
, we obtain

(C.10)

\|\operatorname{ad}_{L_{s}}(A)\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq C_{s,d}N^{\theta_{s}/d}\|A\|_{a+\beta_{s},\mathcal{F}}.

Therefore,
\operatorname{ad}_{L_{s}}
maps the Fourier-weighted norm with exponent
a+\beta_{s}
into the corresponding norm with exponent
a
, at the cost of the factor
C_{s,d}N^{\theta_{s}/d}
.

2.

Next, since
F_{N}HF_{N}^{\dagger}
has entries
\widehat{V}_{p-q}
, multiplication by
H
becomes convolution in the Fourier basis. We use the submultiplicative weight estimate

(C.11)

1+|p-q|_{\operatorname{per}}\leq\bigl(1+|p-r|_{\operatorname{per}}\bigr)\bigl(1+|r-q|_{\operatorname{per}}\bigr),

which follows from the triangle inequality for the periodic distance. We claim that

(C.12)

\|HA\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\|A\|_{a,\mathcal{F}},\qquad\|AH\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\|A\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}.

Indeed, we have

(C.13)

(F_{N}HAF_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}=((F_{N}HF_{N}^{\dagger})(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger}))_{p,q}=\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}\widehat{V}_{p-r}(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger})_{r,q}.

Hence, by
Equation

C.11
we have

(C.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}(1+|p-q|_{\operatorname{per}})^{a}|(F_{N}HAF_{N}^{\dagger})_{p,q}|

(C.15)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}(1+|p-r|_{\operatorname{per}})^{a}|\widehat{V}_{p-r}|\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}_{M}^{d}}(1+|r-q|_{\operatorname{per}})^{a}|(F_{N}AF_{N}^{\dagger})_{r,q}|\leq\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\|A\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}.

Taking the maximum over
p
gives the row estimate for
HA
. The column estimate is identical. The bound for
AH
follows in the same way. Thus
Equation

C.12
holds. Consequently, we have

(C.16)

\|\operatorname{ad}_{H}(A)\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}=\|[H,A]\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq\|HA\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}+\|AH\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\|A\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}.

Therefore,
\operatorname{ad}_{H}
is bounded on each weighted Fourier norm and contributes no explicit
N^{\theta_{s}/d}
factor.

We now apply these two estimates to the nested word. Write
W_{j,\ell}(H,L_{s})=[A_{j},[A_{j-1},\dots,[A_{2},A_{1}]\cdots]]
for
A_{i}\in\{H,L_{s}\}
. For
m=1,\dots,j-1
, define the partial commutator

(C.17)

W^{(m)}:=[A_{m+1},[A_{m},\dots,[A_{2},A_{1}]\cdots]].

Equivalently,
W^{(m)}=\operatorname{ad}_{A_{m+1}}(W^{(m-1)})
for
m=2,\dots,j-1
. Let
n_{m}:=m+1
,
\ell_{m}
be the number of occurrences of
L_{s}
among
A_{1},\dots,A_{m+1}
, and let
h_{m}:=n_{m}-\ell_{m}
be the number of occurrences of
H
among the same operators. We prove by induction on
m
that, for every
a\geq 0
,

(C.18)

\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2^{h_{m}}C_{s,d}^{\ell_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m}\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{h_{m}}N^{\ell_{m}\theta_{s}/d}.

1.

Let
m=1
. We have
W^{(1)}=[A_{2},A_{1}]
. If
A_{2}=A_{1}
, then
W^{(1)}=0
, so the estimate is immediate. Otherwise,
W^{(1)}=\pm[L_{s},H]=\pm\operatorname{ad}_{L_{s}}(H)
. Using
Equation

C.10
, we obtain

(C.19)

\|W^{(1)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq C_{s,d}N^{\theta_{s}/d}\|H\|_{a+\beta_{s},\mathcal{F}}.

Since
F_{N}HF_{N}^{\dagger}
has entries
\widehat{V}_{p-q}
, we have

(C.20)

\|H\|_{a+\beta_{s},\mathcal{F}}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a+\beta_{s}}^{(N)}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a+2\beta_{s}}^{(N)}.

In the non-zero base case,
n_{1}=2
,
\ell_{1}=1
, and
h_{1}=1
. Hence, we obtain
Equation

C.18
. This proves the base case.

2.

Assume
Equation

C.18
holds for
W^{(m-1)}
, where
2\leq m\leq j-1
. Since
W^{(m)}=\operatorname{ad}_{A_{m+1}}(W^{(m-1)}),
there are two cases.

(a)

If
A_{m+1}=L_{s}
, then
Equation

C.10
gives

(C.21)

\displaystyle\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}=\|\operatorname{ad}_{L_{s}}(W^{(m-1)})\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq C_{s,d}N^{\theta_{s}/d}\|W^{(m-1)}\|_{a+\beta_{s},\mathcal{F}}.

Applying the induction hypothesis to
W^{(m-1)}
with weight
a+\beta_{s}
, we obtain

(C.22)

\displaystyle\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{s,d}N^{\theta_{s}/d}2^{h_{m-1}}C_{s,d}^{\ell_{m-1}}(\mathcal{V}_{a+\beta_{s}+n_{m-1}\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{h_{m-1}}N^{\ell_{m-1}\theta_{s}/d}.

Because
A_{m+1}=L_{s}
, we have
\ell_{m}=\ell_{m-1}+1,h_{m}=h_{m-1}
and
n_{m}=n_{m-1}+1
. Thus,
a+\beta_{s}+n_{m-1}\beta_{s}=a+n_{m}\beta_{s}
. Hence, we have

(C.23)

\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2^{h_{m}}C_{s,d}^{\ell_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m}\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{h_{m}}N^{\ell_{m}\theta_{s}/d}.

(b)

If
A_{m+1}=H
, then
Equation

C.16
gives

(C.24)

\displaystyle\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}=\|\operatorname{ad}_{H}(W^{(m-1)})\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\|W^{(m-1)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}.

Applying the induction hypothesis gives

(C.25)

\displaystyle\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}2^{h_{m-1}}C_{s,d}^{\ell_{m-1}}(\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m-1}\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{h_{m-1}}N^{\ell_{m-1}\theta_{s}/d}.

Because
A_{m+1}=H
, we have
\ell_{m}=\ell_{m-1}
,
h_{m}=h_{m-1}+1
and
n_{m}=n_{m-1}+1
. Moreover,
\mathcal{V}_{b}^{(N)}
is non-decreasing in
b
. Therefore, we have
\mathcal{V}_{a}^{(N)}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m}\beta_{s}}^{(N)}
and
\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m-1}\beta_{s}}^{(N)}\leq\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m}\beta_{s}}^{(N)}
. Hence, it follows that

(C.26)

\|W^{(m)}\|_{a,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2^{h_{m}}C_{s,d}^{\ell_{m}}(\mathcal{V}_{a+n_{m}\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{h_{m}}N^{\ell_{m}\theta_{s}/d}.

This completes the induction.

Taking
m=j-1
and
a=0
, we have
n_{j-1}=j
,
\ell_{j-1}=\ell
and
h_{j-1}=j-\ell
. Thus
Equation

C.18
gives

(C.27)

\|W_{j,\ell}(H,L_{s})\|_{0,\mathcal{F}}\leq 2^{j-\ell}C_{s,d}^{\ell}(\mathcal{V}_{j\beta_{s}}^{(N)})^{j-\ell}N^{\ell\theta_{s}/d}.

The claim follows since
\|W_{j,\ell}(H,L_{s})\|\leq\|W_{j,\ell}(H,L_{s})\|_{0,\mathcal{F}}
.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

For
0\leq p,q\leq r
, we have
(S^{T})^{p}P_{0}S^{q}=e_{p}e_{q}^{T}
.
Indeed, since
S=\sum_{j=0}^{M-1}e_{j}e_{j+1}^{T}
, we have
Se_{j+1}=e_{j}
and
S^{T}e_{j}=e_{j+1}
. Thus,
(S^{T})^{p}e_{0}=e_{p}
. Also, we have
e_{0}^{T}S^{q}=e_{q}^{T}
. Since
P_{0}=e_{0}e_{0}^{T}
, it follows that

(C.28)

(S^{T})^{p}P_{0}S^{q}=(S^{T})^{p}e_{0}e_{0}^{T}S^{q}=e_{p}e_{q}^{T}.

Similarly, we have
S^{p}P_{M}(S^{T})^{q}=e_{M-p}e_{M-q}^{T}
. Each non-zero matrix of the form
e_{p}e_{q}^{T}
has operator norm one. Therefore, for every representation
X=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}E_{\eta}
the triangle inequality gives
\|X\|\leq\sum_{\eta}|c_{\eta}|
. Taking the infimum over all such representations proves
\|X\|\leq\|X\|_{\mathcal{B}}
. We now show that for every
X\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
, we have
[A_{1},X]\in\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
,
[B_{1},X]\in\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
. It suffices to check it on the generators of
\mathcal{B}_{r}
. First consider
(S^{T})^{p}P_{0}S^{q}=e_{p}e_{q}^{T}
for some
0\leq p,q\leq r
. Since
Se_{k}=e_{k-1}
for
1\leq k\leq M
and
Se_{0}=0
, while
S^{T}e_{k}=e_{k+1}
for
0\leq k\leq M-1
and
S^{T}e_{M}=0
, we have

(C.29)

Se_{p}e_{q}^{T}=\begin{cases}e_{p-1}e_{q}^{T},&p\geq 1,\\
0,&p=0,\end{cases}\quad\quad S^{T}e_{p}e_{q}^{T}=\begin{cases}e_{p+1}e_{q}^{T},&p\leq M-1,\\
0,&p=M.\end{cases}

Similarly, using
e_{q}^{T}S=e_{q+1}^{T}
for
q\leq M-1
,
e_{M}^{T}S=0
,
e_{q}^{T}S^{T}=e_{q-1}^{T}
for
q\geq 1
, and
e_{0}^{T}S^{T}=0
, we obtain

(C.30)

e_{p}e_{q}^{T}S=\begin{cases}e_{p}e_{q+1}^{T},&q\leq M-1,\\
0,&q=M,\end{cases}\quad\quad e_{p}e_{q}^{T}S^{T}=\begin{cases}e_{p}e_{q-1}^{T},&q\geq 1,\\
0,&q=0.\end{cases}

Therefore each of
Se_{p}e_{q}^{T},e_{p}e_{q}^{T}S,S^{T}e_{p}e_{q}^{T},e_{p}e_{q}^{T}S^{T}
is either zero or a matrix unit
e_{p^{\prime}}e_{q^{\prime}}^{T}
with
0\leq p^{\prime},q^{\prime}\leq r+1
, and hence belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
. The case
S^{p}P_{M}(S^{T})^{q}=e_{M-p}e_{M-q}^{T}
is similar. Since every
X\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
is a linear combination of the generators
(S^{T})^{p}P_{0}S^{q}
and
S^{p}P_{M}(S^{T})^{q}
, we have that
SX,\;XS,\;S^{T}X,\;XS^{T}\in\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
. Using the definitions of
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
, we have

(C.31)

\displaystyle[A_{1},X]

\displaystyle=-SX-S^{T}X+XS+XS^{T},

(C.32)

\displaystyle[B_{1},X]

\displaystyle=SX-S^{T}X-XS+XS^{T}.

Hence,
[A_{1},X]
and
[B_{1},X]
belong to
\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
for each
X\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
. If
X=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}E_{\eta}
is any representation of
X
, then

(C.33)

SX=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}SE_{\eta},\quad S^{T}X=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}S^{T}E_{\eta},\quad XS=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}E_{\eta}S,\quad XS^{T}=\sum_{\eta}c_{\eta}E_{\eta}S^{T}.

By the argument above, each
SE_{\eta},S^{T}E_{\eta},E_{\eta}S,E_{\eta}S^{T}
is either zero or in
\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
. Hence each of the four shifted matrices admits a representation in
\mathcal{B}_{r+1}
with coefficient cost at most
\sum_{\eta}|c_{\eta}|
. Taking the infimum over all representations of
X
gives

(C.34)

\|SX\|_{\mathcal{B}},\ \|S^{T}X\|_{\mathcal{B}},\ \|XS\|_{\mathcal{B}},\ \|XS^{T}\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\|X\|_{\mathcal{B}}.

Using the sub-additivity of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{B}}
gives
\|[A_{1},X]\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4\|X\|_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\|[B_{1},X]\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4\|X\|_{\mathcal{B}}
. By
5.6
, we have
[A_{1},B_{1}]=2(P_{0}-P_{M})
. Since
P_{0},P_{M}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
, this implies
\|[A_{1},B_{1}]\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4
and
\|[B_{1},A_{1}]\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4
. Every non-zero right-nested mixed commutator of length
j\geq 2
is obtained from
[A_{1},B_{1}]
or
[B_{1},A_{1}]
by
j-2
additional commutations with
A_{1}
or
B_{1}
. Applying the argument above inductively gives

(C.35)

\|W_{j}(A_{1},B_{1})\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4\cdot 4^{j-2}=4^{j-1}.

Hence, we have
\|W_{j}(A_{1},B_{1})\|\leq\|W_{j}(A_{1},B_{1})\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 4^{j-1}
. This completes the proof.

Appendix D
Extension to Non-Homogeneous and Time-Dependent Cases

In
Section

3
and
Section

4
, we analyzed the time-independent homogeneous case in detail. In this section, we explain how the same framework extends to more general cases, namely the non-homogeneous case in
Section

D.1
and the time-dependent case in
Section

D.2
.

D.1
Non-Homogeneous Case

We first consider the time-independent non-homogeneous problem

(D.1)

\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=-Au(t)+b(t).

As before, write
A=L+iH
, with
L,H
Hermitian and
L\succeq 0
. By Duhamel’s principle, the solution of
Equation

D.1
is

(D.2)

u(T)=e^{-AT}u(0)+\int_{0}^{T}e^{-A(T-s)}b(s)\,ds.

Equation

D.2
shows that the non-homogeneous term is a superposition of homogeneous evolutions over times
T-s
. Applying the LCHS representation to each homogeneous propagator in the Duhamel term gives the two-variable representation

(D.3)

\int_{0}^{T}e^{-A(T-s)}b(s)\,ds\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)U_{k}(T-s)b(s)\,dk\,ds.

Thus, the non-homogeneous contribution involves two discretizations: the LCHS quadrature
Q
in the Fourier variable
k
, and the time quadrature
P
in the Duhamel variable
s
. This viewpoint is used below. For
\tau\in[0,T]
, define the ideal and MPF-implemented post-quadrature operators by

(D.4)

W_{Q}^{\operatorname{ideal}}(\tau):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}U_{k_{i}}(\tau),\qquad W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(\tau):=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}v_{i}U^{(2m)}_{k_{i}}(\tau),

The quantities
\alpha_{Q}
,
\Lambda_{m,Q}
, and
\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}
are defined as before. Let
P=\{(s_{p},\omega_{p}):p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}\}
be a quadrature rule on
[0,T]
for the integral in
Equation

D.3
. We write

(D.5)

\tau_{p}:=T-s_{p},\qquad B_{P}:=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}|\omega_{p}|\,\|b(s_{p})\|.

We assume
B_{P}<\infty
. The assumption
b\in L^{1}([0,T])
suffices for
Equation

D.2
, but does not define point values
b(s_{p})
. The quadrature implementation is therefore stated under the additional source-access assumption that the selected values
b(s_{p})
are well-defined and accessible via a state-preparation oracles. Discretizing
Equation

D.3
by
Q
in
k
and
P
in
s
, and then replacing each
U_{k_{i}}(\tau_{p})
by its MPF approximation, gives

(D.6)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)U_{k}(T-s)b(s)\,dk\,ds\approx\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{Q}}\omega_{p}v_{i}U^{(2m)}_{k_{i}}(\tau_{p})b(s_{p}).

Equivalently, the non-homogeneous LCHS–MPF approximation is

(D.7)

u_{Q,P}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T):=W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T)u(0)+\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}\omega_{p}W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(\tau_{p})b(s_{p}).

We state the error estimate in terms of two quadrature errors. First, define the uniform-in-time LCHS quadrature error by

(D.8)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}^{[0,T]}(Q):=\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq T}\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}_{a,b}(k;c,d)U_{k}(\tau)\,dk-W_{Q}^{\operatorname{ideal}}(\tau)\right\|.

The quadrature estimates in
Section

4.6
also bound
E_{\operatorname{quad}}^{[0,T]}(Q)
. Indeed, the strip estimates used there are monotone in the final time. Replacing
T
by any
\tau\in[0,T]
only decreases the factor involving
T\|L\|
. Hence the same quadrature rule
Q
, chosen using the endpoint time
T
, satisfies

(D.9)

E_{\operatorname{quad}}^{[0,T]}(Q)\leq E_{\operatorname{mesh}}(Q;T)+E_{\operatorname{tail}}(Q;T),

with the right-hand side given by the corresponding estimates in
4.15
or
4.18
. Second, define the time-quadrature error by

(D.10)

E_{\operatorname{time}}(P):=\left\|\int_{0}^{T}e^{-A(T-s)}b(s)\,ds-\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}\omega_{p}e^{-A(T-s_{p})}b(s_{p})\right\|.

The quantity
E_{\operatorname{time}}(P)
is kept abstract below. Once
P
and the regularity of the representative
b
are specified, it can be bounded by the corresponding quadrature estimate.

Lemma D.1
.

Fix an admissible kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq\tau\leq T}\left\|e^{-A\tau}-W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(\tau)\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\eta_{Q}(T/r))^{r-1}\left(C_{m}\frac{T^{2m+1}}{r^{2m}}\Lambda_{m,Q}+r\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\right).

The proof is the same as that of

, with this value of
\epsilon_{Q}
, and if the admissibility conditions

(D.21)

r_{Q}(\delta)R_{m}(\delta)\leq\frac{1}{2},\qquad r_{Q}(\delta)\alpha_{Q}R_{m}(\delta)\leq\frac{\epsilon_{Q}}{2e}

hold, then
\|u(T)-u_{Q,P}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T)\|\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

For
r=r_{Q}(\delta)
, the same argument used in
4.6
gives
\mathcal{E}_{Q}^{[0,T]}(r_{Q}(\delta),\delta)\leq\epsilon_{\operatorname{approx}}+\epsilon_{\operatorname{comb}}
. Combining this bound with
D.2
and
Equation

D.20
proves the claim.
∎

We now describe the implementation cost. Assume state-preparation oracles for
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}:=u(0)/\|u(0)\|
, when
u(0)\neq 0
, and for
\ket{\widetilde{b}(s_{p})}:=b(s_{p})/\|b(s_{p})\|
, for each
p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}
with
b(s_{p})\neq 0
. Terms with zero vector coefficients are omitted from the outer LCU, and the phases of
\omega_{p}
are included in the outer preparation. Let
\beta_{P}:=\|u(0)\|+B_{P}
. Define the source-preparation oracle

(D.22)

\operatorname{PREP}^{P}\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta_{P}}}\left(\sqrt{\|u(0)\|}\ket{\star}+\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}\sqrt{|\omega_{p}|\,\|b(s_{p})\|}\ket{p}\right),

with the phases of
\omega_{p}
included in the corresponding left-preparation state.
The vector in
Equation

D.7
is then implemented by an outer LCU over
\{\star\}\cup\mathcal{I}_{P}
, with normalization
\beta_{P}
. Since each term applies a block-encoding of
W_{Q}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(\tau)
with normalization
\alpha_{Q}
, the total normalization is
\alpha_{Q}\beta_{P}
. Thus, the amplitude amplification factor is
\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b):=\frac{\alpha_{Q}\beta_{P}}{\|u_{Q,P}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T)\|}
.
D.4
gives the complexity estimates.

Corollary D.4
.

Under the assumptions of
D.3
, the normalized state proportional to
u_{Q,P}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T)
can be prepared using

(D.23)

\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{state}}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b)\,r_{Q}(\delta)K_{m}\right)

controlled second-order product-formula queries, together with
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b))
calls to the data-preparation oracles for
\ket{\widetilde{u}(0)}
,
\ket{\widetilde{b}(s_{p})}
, and their inverses, as well as to
\operatorname{PREP}^{P}
,
\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{L}}^{Q}
, and
\operatorname{PREP}_{\operatorname{R}}^{Q}
. Moreover, under the optimized-order assumptions of
4.7
, we have

(D.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\operatorname{state}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b)\left(1+\mu_{Q}F(m)\max\{1,T\}\right)\left(\log\left(e+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\left(\log\log\left(e^{e}+\frac{T}{\epsilon_{Q}}\right)\right)^{2}\right).

Proof.

The implementation uses the nested LCU construction from
Section

4.2
, with an additional LCU combining the initial-condition and discretized Duhamel terms. One block-encoding costs
\mathcal{O}(r_{Q}(\delta)K_{m})
controlled second-order product-formula queries, and amplitude amplification uses
\mathcal{O}(\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b))
such applications. The optimized estimates follow by substituting the optimized bound for
r_{Q}(\delta_{m})K_{m}
from
4.7
. The factor
F(m)
is inherited from
Equation

4.27
.
∎

Remark D.5
.

Let
u(T)
denote the exact solution of
Equation

D.1
. If
E_{\operatorname{time}}(P)+\mathcal{E}_{Q}^{[0,T]}(r,\delta)\beta_{P}\leq\frac{\|u(T)\|}{2}
, then

(D.25)

\|u_{Q,P}^{\operatorname{MPF}}(T)\|\geq\|u(T)\|-E_{\operatorname{time}}(P)-\mathcal{E}_{Q}^{[0,T]}(r,\delta)\beta_{P}\geq\frac{\|u(T)\|}{2}.

Consequently,
\chi_{Q,P}(u(0),b)\leq 2\alpha_{Q}\beta_{P}/\|u(T)\|
, and the state query complexity is
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\alpha_{Q}\beta_{P}}{\|u(T)\|}\,r_{Q}(\delta)K_{m}\right)
. If the time-quadrature rule satisfies the source-normalization estimate

(D.26)

B_{P}=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{I}_{P}}|\omega_{p}|\,\|b(s_{p})\|\leq C_{\operatorname{src}}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0,T]},

with
C_{\operatorname{src}}>0
independent of the target precision, then
\beta_{P}\leq\|u(0)\|+C_{\operatorname{src}}\|b\|_{L^{1}[0,T]}
. In particular, when
C_{\operatorname{src}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the normalized state-preparation cost has the usual non-homogeneous normalization factor
(\|u(0)\|+\|b\|_{L^{1}[0,T]})/\|u(T)\|
, up to the LCHS normalization
\alpha_{Q}
and the MPF simulation cost. This is the standard form in which normalized-state query complexity is presented in the literature.

We see that no new commutator quantities arise. The source
b(t)
affects only source-state preparation, the time-quadrature error
E_{\operatorname{time}}(P)
, and the normalization
B_{P}
. The post-quadrature quantities
R_{Q}
,
\alpha_{Q}
,
\Lambda_{m,Q}
,
\Phi_{m,Q}^{\ast}
,
r_{Q}
,
\mu_{Q}
are inherited from the homogeneous analysis.

D.2
Time-Dependent Case

We now extend the analysis to the time-dependent case for the special case local and extensive Hamiltonians. For simplicity, we consider the homogeneous problem.

(D.27)

\frac{d}{dt}u(t)=-A(t)u(t),\qquad A(t)=L(t)+iH(t),

where, for every
t\in[0,T]
, the matrices
L(t)
and
H(t)
are Hermitian and
L(t)\succeq 0
. The extension to the non-homogeneous case follows as in
Section

D.1
. Let
U_{A}(s,t):=\mathcal{T}\exp(-\int_{s}^{t}A(\tau)d\tau)
denote the non-unitary propagator from
s
to
t
. As before, for
k\in\mathbb{R}
, define
G_{k}(t):=H(t)+kL(t)
and let

(D.28)

U_{k}(s,t):=\mathcal{T}\exp\left(-i\int_{s}^{t}G_{k}(\tau)d\tau\right).

The approximation and truncation estimates from
Sections

3.1
and
3.6
continue to apply after replacing
e^{-AT}
by
U_{A}(0,T)
and
U_{k}(T)
by
U_{k}(0,T)
. In particular, for the kernel profile
\vec{\theta}=(a,b,c,d)
, the same admissible choices of
d
and
y_{0}
from
3.4
and
3.5
continue to hold. Motivated by
2.5
, define

(D.29)

C_{m}:=4^{2m+1},\quad R_{m}(\delta):=\|\vec{a}\|_{1}\|\vec{b}\|_{1}\delta,\quad\Phi_{m}(k,N,\delta):=\mu_{k,p_{0}}^{2m+1},\quad\mu_{R,p_{0}}:=\sup_{|k|\leq R}\mu_{k,p_{0}},

where
\mu_{k,p_{0}}
denotes the quantity
\mu_{p_{0}}
from
2.5
applied to the Hamiltonian
G_{k}(t)
,
p_{0}
is defined as in
2.5
, and
\|\vec{a}\|_{1}
and
\|\vec{b}\|_{1}
are the
1
-norms of the MPF coefficient vectors discussed above. Throughout this subsection, we assume that the family
\{G_{k}(t):|k|\leq R\}
admits a decomposition into
q_{R}
-local,
g_{R}
-extensive terms, and that the derivative bound in
2.5
holds with a constant
f_{R}
, uniformly in
k
and in the initial time of the sub-interval. Define

(D.30)

\Delta(R,N,\delta):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{8e^{3}p_{0}(2q_{R}g_{R}+2\Gamma f_{R})},\frac{1}{8\mu_{R,p_{0}}}\right\}.

We first record the pointwise short-time estimate obtained from
2.5
.

Lemma D.6
.

Fix
R>0
,
m,N\geq 1
, and
\delta\in(0,1)
. Let

0<\Delta\leq\Delta(R,N,\delta)
. For
|k|\leq R
and

s\in[0,T-\Delta]
, let
\widetilde{U}_{k,s}^{(2m)}
denote the order-
2m

time-dependent MPF approximation to
U_{k}(s,s+\Delta)
. Then

(D.31)

\|\widetilde{U}_{k,s}^{(2m)}-U_{k}(s,s+\Delta)\|\leq\|\vec{a}\|_{1}C_{m}\Delta^{2m+1}\Phi_{m}(k,N,\delta)+R_{m}(\delta).

Proof.

By assumption, the constants
q_{R}
,
g_{R}
,
f_{R}
, and
\mu_{R,p_{0}}
control the estimate uniformly for every
|k|\leq R
and every initial time
s
. Now apply
2.5
to the shifted Hamiltonian
\tau\mapsto G_{k}(s+\tau)
for
\tau\in[0,\Delta]
.
∎

The passage from short-time to long-time simulation is not identical to the time-independent case. This is because the exact propagator on
[0,T]
is an ordered product of generally different interval propagators
U_{k}(t_{j},t_{j+1})
, rather than a power of a single fixed short-time propagator. Let
t_{j}=j\Delta
, where
\Delta=T/r
. Then

(D.32)

U_{k}(0,T)=\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq r-1}}U_{k}(t_{j},t_{j+1}).

The factors in
Equation

D.32
are generally distinct, so one cannot use the
identity
U_{k}(0,T)=U_{k}(0,\Delta)^{r}
. We instead use the following telescoping estimate for
non-stationary products.

Lemma D.7
.

Let
r\geq 1
, and set
t_{j}=j\Delta
for
j=0,\ldots,r
, where
\Delta=T/r
. For fixed
k
, define
U_{k,j}:=U_{k}(t_{j},t_{j+1})
and
\widetilde{U}_{k,j}:=\widetilde{U}_{k,t_{j}}^{(2m)}
. Set
U_{k}^{(2m)}(0,T):=\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq r-1}}\widetilde{U}_{k,j}
. If
\|\widetilde{U}_{k,j}-U_{k,j}\|\leq\eta_{k}(\Delta)
for some
\eta_{k}(\Delta)>0

for every
j=0,\ldots,r-1
, then

(D.33)

\|U_{k}^{(2m)}(0,T)-U_{k}(0,T)\|\leq r\eta_{k}(\Delta)\left(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta)\right)^{r-1}.

Proof.

Using the ordered-product convention from
Section

2.1
, we have

(D.34)

\displaystyle\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq r-1}}\widetilde{U}_{k,j}-\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq r-1}}U_{k,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}\left(\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{\ell+1\leq j\leq r-1}}\widetilde{U}_{k,j}\right)(\widetilde{U}_{k,\ell}-U_{k,\ell})\left(\overset{\longleftarrow}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq\ell-1}}U_{k,j}\right).

Empty products are interpreted as the identity. Since
U_{k,j}
is unitary,
\|U_{k,j}\|=1
. Moreover, we have

(D.35)

\|\widetilde{U}_{k,j}\|\leq\|U_{k,j}\|+\|\widetilde{U}_{k,j}-U_{k,j}\|\leq 1+\eta_{k}(\Delta).

Taking norms gives

(D.36)

\|U_{k}^{(2m)}(0,T)-U_{k}(0,T)\|\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{r-1}\eta_{k}(\Delta)\left(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta)\right)^{r-1-\ell}.

The right-hand side is bounded by
r\eta_{k}(\Delta)(1+\eta_{k}(\Delta))^{r-1}
. This proves
Equation

D.33
.
∎
