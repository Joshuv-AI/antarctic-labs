 Executive Summary
The Department of Energy (DOE) funds a diverse and distributed ecosystem of repositories that
steward scientific data, publications, and software across its research programs, user facilities,
and national laboratories. While significant progress has been made in standardizing dataset-
level metadata, the metadata describing repositories themselves (their identity, governance,
access interfaces, policies, and technical capabilities) remains inconsistent and fragmented across
DOE-funded systems. This variability limits discoverability, interoperability, automated
validation, and AI-driven analysis, all of which are increasingly essential for modern scientific
workflows. To address this gap, the DOE Data Curation Working Group (DCWG) developed
the DOE Repository Metadata Profile (DRMP). The DRMP is a practical, community-driven
framework that defines how repositories can describe themselves in a consistent, machine-
actionable, and scalable manner.

The DRMP is not a new metadata schema. Instead, it is a mapping profile and structured
element set capturing the essential characteristics of DOE repositories. It harmonizes
repository-level metadata across six widely adopted community schemas: RE3Data; DCAT-US
v3; Schema.org; Dublin Core; DataCite 4.6; and PREMIS 3.0. This harmonization eliminates
reinvention and enables interoperability within DOE and across the broader scientific ecosystem.
A core objective of the DRMP is to reduce burden on repositories by allowing them to reuse
their existing metadata through a Rosetta-style crosswalk rather than redesigning local
implementations.

The profile introduces a three-level conformance model that supports incremental adoption:

 • Level 1 – Minimum Viable Record (MVR): foundational identification elements
 required for workflows, project registration, and basic repository presence.
 • Level 2 – Interoperable: structured metadata enabling alignment with national and
 international discovery systems.
 • Level 3 – AI-Ready: enhanced provenance, policy transparency, fixity, semantic
 context, and capabilities that support automated reasoning, model training governance,
 and machine-assisted curation.

To support implementation, the DRMP includes JSON Schema definitions, OpenAPI
patterns, and MCP templates that allow repositories to publish machine-readable metadata
directly within existing platforms. These resources are modular and lightweight, enabling
adoption without major architectural change.

Adopting the DRMP enables repositories to:

 • Enhance discoverability and interoperability by aligning identifiers, classifications, and
 descriptive elements across widely used schema standards.
 • Support federated discovery and cross-registration across DOE systems, Data.gov, and
 international catalogs.

 7

 • Enable AI agents and workflow orchestration systems to interpret repository-level
 metadata within the American Science Cloud (AmSC) through Model Context Protocol
 (MCP)-based context publication.
 • Demonstrate alignment with DOE’s open science, stewardship, and FAIR data priorities.

This guidance represents a community-driven step forward. Through voluntary adoption and
continued feedback, the DRMP advances a cohesive, machine-actionable description of DOE
repositories that supports FAIR data practices, preparing the infrastructure for AI-enabled
research, and strengthening the discoverability and reuse of DOE’s scientific outputs.

 1. Introduction
The Department of Energy (DOE) supports an expansive network of repositories that preserve,
manage, and disseminate scientific data, software, and publications generated across its national
laboratories, user facilities, and research programs. Although DOE repositories share common
stewardship goals, they vary widely in purpose, scope, technical architecture, and metadata
practices. This diversity reflects the scientific breadth of DOE’s mission, but is also creates
challenges for discovery, interoperability, automation, and AI-enables analysis.

Over the past decade, significant progress has been made in harmonizing dataset-level metadata
through community schemas such as DataCite, Dublin Core, DCAT, and schema.org; however,
the metadata describing repositories themselves remains unevenly implemented and
inconsistently expressed across the DOE ecosystem. Repository metadata includes a repository’s
identity, governance, access interfaces, policies, and supported capabilities. This gap in
repository metadata complicates efforts to integrate repositories into large-scale scientific
workflows, federated catalogs, and the emerging American Science Cloud (AmSC). They also
limit the ability of automated agents and AI systems to reason about repository trustworthiness,
access pathways, and policy constraints.

The DOE Data Curation Working Group (DCWG) initiated the DOE Repository Metadata
Profile (DRMP) to address these repository metadata gaps. The DRMP establishes a unified,
machine-actionable set of repository-level descriptive elements that DOE repositories can adopt
incrementally. It is not a new schema, but rather a structured, harmonized metadata profile that
maps directly onto six widely adopted standards-RE3Data, DCAT-US v3, Schema.org, Dublin
Core, DataCite 4.6, and PREMIS 3.0. By aligning with existing standards rather than replacing
them, the DRMP reduces implementation burden while enabling repositories to reuse metadata
they already maintain.

A key design goal of the DRMP is scalability; supporting lightweight adoption for small or
project-level repositories, while providing richer semantic and policy detail for mature
repositories integrating with AI and workflow automation. To support this, the DRMP
introduces a three-tiered conformance model:

 • Level 1 (Minimum Viable Record): establishes a repository's identity, ownership, and
 presence within DOE systems.
 • Level 2 (Interoperable): enables alignment with national and international metadata
 catalogs through structured descriptions and declared policies.
 • Level 3 (AI-Ready): provides the provenance, fixity, semantic context, AI governance
 metadata, and capability declarations needed for machine reasoning and AI-assisted
 workflows.

The DRMP also includes implementation resources: JSON Schema; OpenAPI patterns; and
MCP-based context publishing templates. These resources will allow repositories to express
profile elements in a standardized, machine-readable form without major system redesign.

Collectively, these efforts position the DRMP as a foundational component of DOE's broader
open science, FAIR data, and AI-readiness initiatives. By adopting consistent repository-level

 9

metadata, DOE repositories can increase discoverability, improve automation and cross-system
integration, and strengthen the transparency and stewardship of scientific outputs.
1.1 Scope of this Document
This guidance focuses exclusively on metadata about repositories themselves, not the datasets,
publications, or digital objects they host. The DOE Repository Metadata Profile (DRMP)
defines an interoperability framework that:

 • Harmonizes repository-level metadata across RE3Data, DCAT-US v3, Schema.org,
 Dublin Core, DataCite 4.6, and PREMIS 3.0.
 • Provides examples of machine-actionable publication using JSON-LD, RDF, and
 OpenAPI-aligned patterns.
 • Introduces a three-level conformance model supporting incremental repository adoption
 and validation.
 • Supplies appendices containing crosswalk mappings, example payloads, policy
 templates, OpenAPI examples, and MCP patterns to support consistent implementation.

The DRMP does not define a new ontology or replace existing standards. Instead, it offers a
harmonized mapping profile and implementation guide that transforms repository-level metadata
from narrative description into machine-actionable infrastructure suitable for automation,
workflow orchestration, and AI-enabled analysis.
1.2 Intended Audience
This document is intended for:
 • Repository developers and technical professionals responsible for implementing metadata
 services, APIs, and integration workflows.
 • Librarians, data curators, metadata specialists, and information scientists who design
 repository metadata models and ensure alignment with FAIR principles.
 • DOE program managers, data stewards, and repository operators seeking a consistent
 framework for assessing repository readiness, interoperability, and compliance with
 federal policy.
 • AI, ML, and workflow automation professionals who require structured repository
 descriptions to integrate repositories into automated pipelines, federated search platforms,
 and the AmSC.

The guidance assumes familiarity with common metadata standards (e.g., DCAT, Dublin Core,
Schema.org) and provides a concise, actionable framework for aligning repository descriptions
with DOE’s open-science, stewardship, and AI-readiness goals.

 2. Background and Context
The DRMP builds upon established community standards that already describe research
repositories, catalogs, and preservation systems. Rather than defining a new schema, the DRMP
harmonizes existing repository-level metadata across widely adopted standards to enable
consistent, machine-actionable, and interoperable metadata within the DOE ecosystem. This
approach reduces implementation burden, supports reuse of existing metadata, and provides a
scalable pathway toward interoperability and AI readiness. The remainder of this chapter
provides the background needed to understand the DRMP, including its interoperability
foundations, mapping logic, and the role of repository-level metadata in DOE’s open science and
AI-readiness goals.
2.1 Interoperability Foundations
The DRMP draws on the complementary strengths of six mature metadata frameworks, each
describing different aspects of repository identity, governance, access, and technical capabilities:

 • RE3Data [4]: The authoritative international registry of research data repositories,
 defining attributes for repository identity, governance, funding, access rights, and
 technical interfaces.
 • DCAT-US v3 [5]: The U.S. federal profile of the W3C Data Catalog Vocabulary,
 enabling repositories to expose structured metadata for integration with Data.gov and
 other federal discovery platforms.
 • Schema.org [6]: A web-scale vocabulary widely used for structured metadata embedded
 in HTML pages and APIs, improving discoverability through commercial search engines
 and machine agents.
 • Dublin Core [7]: A foundational general-purpose vocabulary for resource description,
 providing stable, widely recognized predicates for identity, rights, subject classification,
 and relationships.
 • DataCite 4.6 [8]: A schema widely used for persistent identifiers and dataset-level
 metadata, offering compatible concepts for contributors, publishers, subjects, and funding
 bodies.
 • PREMIS 3.0 [9]: A preservation metadata standard describing provenance events, object
 characteristics, fixity information, and preservation actions essential for long-term
 stewardship.

These six schemas form the basis of the DRMP Rosetta-style crosswalk presented in Appendix
B:

 • RE3Data provides the baseline descriptors.
 • DCAT-US v3 enables federal interoperability.
 • Schema.org improves web and agent discoverability.
 • Dublin Core and DataCite offer general-purpose descriptive interoperability.
 • PREMIS contributes provenance, fixity, and preservation semantics required for AI-
 ready curation.

 11

Together, these mappings establish a layered, standards-aligned foundation supporting machine-
actionable repository-level metadata across DOE and the broader scientific ecosystem.
2.2 Mapping Logic and Structure
The DRMP organizes repository-level metadata into conceptual groups that represent functional
dimensions shared across the source schemas. These include:

 • Identity and Classification
 • Organizational and Funding Context
 • Access, Interfaces, and Technical Capabilities
 • Policies and Governance
 • Provenance, Preservation, and Fixity
 • Semantic Context and AI Governance

Within each dimension, DRMP fields are mapped to their nearest conceptual equivalents in the
six source schemas. This harmonization accounts for naming differences, semantic equivalence,
and machine-actionable structure. The mappings appear in:

 • Appendix A: JSON Schema for machine-readable DRMP implementation
 • Appendix B: Rosetta crosswalk mapping DRMP elements into six standards
 • Appendix C: OpenAPI and XML examples for API-based metadata exchange
 • Appendix F: Policy templates for Level 2 and Level 3 compliance

By following this structure, repositories can generate metadata that remains interoperable across
RE3Data, DCAT-US, Schema.org, Dublin Core, DataCite, and PREMIS, while maintaining
flexibility in local implementation. The profile also supports automated validation and self-
assessment through the three conformance levels defined in Chapter 3: Minimum Viable Record
(Level 1); Interoperable (Level 2); and AI-Ready (Level 3).
2.3 Why Repository Metadata Matters
Repository-level metadata is foundational to DOE's open science, FAIR data, and AI-readiness
objectives. When expressed in a machine-actionable form, this metadata enables:

 • Transparency and trust: Clear declarations of governance, policies, access models, and
 repository capabilities allow users and automated systems to evaluate repository
 trustworthiness and appropriateness for reuse.
 • Discoverability and interoperability: Harmonized metadata ensures that DOE
 repositories can be integrated into RE3Data, Data.gov, FAIRsharing, and other national
 and international discovery ecosystems.
 • Automation and AI integration: Structured metadata enables workflow systems, AI
 agents, and the American Science Cloud (AmSC) to identify repositories, understand
 their interfaces, and automate metadata harvesting, validation, and policy-aware
 reasoning.

 12

 • Policy alignment and reporting: Machine-actionable metadata supports compliance
 with DOE's Public Access Plan 2.0, FAIR metrics initiatives, and broader federal
 mandates for transparency and stewardship.

In this context, repository-level metadata operates as infrastructure, not documentation. By
aligning DOE repositories around a common mapping profile and a scalable conformance model,
the DRMP provides the foundation for automated, federated, and AI-assisted interoperability
across DOE's scientific knowledge ecosystem.
2.4 Summary
Taken together, these interoperability foundations, mapping structures, and policy considerations
establish the conceptual basis for the DRMP. They demonstrate why consistent, machine-
actionable repository-level metadata is essential for FAIR practices, workflow integration, and
AI-enabled research. This background provides the necessary context for the conformance
model introduced in the next chapter.

 3. The DRMP Conformance Model
The DRMP defines a three-level conformance model that provides repositories with a structured,
progressive path toward machine-actionability, interoperability, and ultimately AI readiness.
Each level introduces specific metadata, technical capabilities, and governance signals that
enable repositories to function as identifiable, harvestable, and eventually AI-integrable
components of DOE’s scientific data ecosystem. The model is intentionally incremental: Level
1 establishes a minimum machine-actionable identity record; Level 2 adds the interfaces and
policies required for automated harvesting and federated discovery; and Level 3 introduces the
semantic alignment, access-control metadata (accessModel and authorizationModel), and AI-
governance policies (such as aiUsePolicy) needed for agentic orchestration and participation in
the AmSC.

Rather than enforcing a uniform standard, the DRMP recognizes that DOE repositories vary in
maturity, design, and technical capacity. The conformance levels therefore offer an achievable,
additive roadmap that repository teams can adopt over time, ensuring that each level supports
clear operational, technical, and compliance outcomes.

The conformance model allows repositories to:

 • Evaluate their current metadata coverage and technical interfaces against a standardized
 benchmark.
 • Identify specific actions needed to progress toward full interoperability and semantic
 integration.
 • Support operational readiness for automated harvesting, compliance monitoring, AI-
 governance signaling, and integration within agent-based environments.

Together, these levels form a roadmap that elevates repository metadata from static
documentation to an extensible, machine-actionable infrastructure that supports discovery,
compliance, and cross-domain integration. This chapter presents each conformance level in
detail, outlining the required elements, technical capabilities, and validation steps that constitute
the DRMP maturity model.
3.1 Level 1 – Minimum Viable Record (MVR)
Objective: Establish a minimal, machine-actionable identity record that allows a repository to
be uniquely referenced, validated, and registered within DOE data ecosystems.

Level 1 defines the baseline metadata sufficient for a repository to exist as a uniquely
identifiable, citable, and trackable component of the DOE scientific enterprise. It intentionally
excludes descriptive, policy, API, or semantic metadata. Instead, if focuses on essential identity
attributes require to:

 • Anchor the repository to a global or local persistent identifier.
 • Identify the operating organization.
 • Provide a minimal trust and contact context.
 • Allow automated systems to ingest, route, and validate the repository’s identity.

 14

 A Level 1 record contains exactly five required fields, plus a required conformsTo
 declaration:

 1. repositoryIdentifier – a persistent, globally unique identifier.
 2. repositoryName – a human-readable name (e.g., ORNL Constellation).
 3. repositoryType – a controlled vocabulary term describing the repository category
 (e.g., institutional, disciplinary).
 4. hostingInstitution – the responsible organization.
 5. contactPoint – a support or administrative email address.
 6. conformsTo – must equal “Level-1-MVR”.

These fields form the minimum machine-actionable footprint. They allow any DOE system such
as registries, workflow engines, metadata validators, or AmSC components to recognize the
repository, validate its existence, and anchor additional metadata layers.

Optional fields such as fundingBody or repositoryDescription may be included but
are not required at Level 1.

Level 1 therefore represents the “registration1 tier” where a repository can be indexed, cited, and
managed within DOE infrastructure, but is not yet interoperable or semantically enriched.
3.2 Level 2 – Interoperability
Objective: Provide machine-readable interfaces, technical metadata formats, and operational
policies required for federated discovery, automated harvesting, and cross-enclave integration.

Level 2 introduces interoperability capabilities that move a repository beyond simple
registrability and into DOE-wide discovery and data lifecycle ecosystem. At this level, metadata
exposure becomes machine-actionable, enabling automated harvesting, validation, monitoring,
and version-aware ingestion.

A Level 2 repository must implement the following required metadata elements, in addition to all
Level 1 fields:

API Exposure and Formats

 • apiEndpoints – network-accessible machine interfaces (e.g., REST, JSON, OAI-
 PMH).
 • metadataFormats – describes the repository-level metadata formats or schemas that
 the respository exposes through its interfaces and exports, not the per-dataset internal
 metadata formats (e.g., Dublin Core, DCAT-US).

1
 Here, “registered” refers to establishing a machine-actionable identity record within DOE-managed metadata

 15

These fields allow aggregators, search services, and automated agents to retrieve repository and
dataset-level metadata in predictable formats.

Policy Surfaces for Automation
 • licensePolicy – a machine-readable URI referencing terms of use or licensing
 conditions.
 • retentionPolicy – a machine-readable URI referencing retention or disposition
 documentation.
 • harvestPolicy – a machine-readable URI describing harvesting permissions.

Declaring these policies enables compliant, automated harvesting across DOE and external
systems such as Data.gov, FAIRsharing, RE3Data, OpenAIRE (Guidelines 4.0), and European
Open Science Cloud (EOSC).

Temporal Versioning and Trust Elements
  • issued – timestamp when the repository description was created.
  • modified – timestamp for the most recent update.
  • stewardshipCommitment – a machine-readable statement of the repository’s
 curation scope (short-term access only, medium-term curation, or long-term preservation)

These allow monitoring tools and metadata pipelines to detect changes, generate deltas, and track
repository evolution over time.

Technical Capabilities
 • persistentIdentifierPolicy – declares which PID systems the repository
 routinely uses (pidSystems)and may optionally point to a human-readable, versioned
 PID governance policy (pidPolicy)
 • capabilities – machine-actionable declaration of supported repository features,
 including Level 2–relevant operations such as:
 o apiAccess
 o bulkDownload
 o datasetVersioning
 o webhooks
 o schemaBrowse

These enable DOE systems to reason about whether a repository supports the operational
requirements of integration pipelines (e.g., whether it supports dataset-level versioning for
incremental harvesting).

A Level 2 repository must set “Level-2-Interoperable” within the conformsTo field.

At this level, repositories become interoperable across systems. Their metadata can be
automatically harvested, validated, and federated, enabling participation in:

 • DOE facility catalog services

 16

 • Data.gov (DCAT-US v3) pipelines
 • FAIRsharing and RE3Data crosswalks
 • cross-enclave metadata brokers
 • knowledge graph construction
 • schema.org–indexed search environments

Optional but recommended interoperability metadata:
 • accessModel - open, authenticated, or restricted
 • authorizationModel - none, API key, OAuth2, federated

Level 2 is the operational bridge between a repository’s local implementation and the broader
interconnected infrastructure of the DOE scientific enterprise. It becomes a technically
interoperable node within DOE where its metadata can be harvested, parsed, validated, and
federated by automated systems.
3.3 Level 3 – AI Readiness
Objective: Enable repositories to participate in AI-driven workflows by exposing semantic,
provenance, authorization, and policy metadata required for automated reasoning, trust scoring,
and agentic orchestration.

Level 3 represents the highest maturity tier in the DRMP conformance model. At this level,
repositories publish the rich machine-actionable information that allow AI systems, reasoning
engines, and MCP-based agents to safely interact with the repository.

A Level 3 repository must implement all Level 1 and Level 2 requirements, plus the following
AI-readiness elements:

Semantic Grounding
 • ontologyAlignment – URIs of ontologies, controlled vocabularies, or schemas used
 by the repository. Implementers may also include versioned URIs in
 ontologyVersion corresponding to those ontologies where available.
 • preservationPolicy – a machine-readable statement of long-term preservation
 commitment.
 • semanticContext – JSON-LD or RDF @context URIs that provide machine-readable
 mappings from DRMP and local fields to ontology or schema URIs. Repositories that
 implement MCP should include their MCP context URL (e.g., /.well-
 known/mcp/context.jsonld) in this list.

These enable semantic enrichment, knowledge graph alignment, and trust-aware AI systems.

Provenance and Integrity
 • provenance – PREMIS event records describing key repository actions (validation,
 transformation, updates).
 • fixity – PREMIS fixity metadata to enable integrity verification.

 17

These support confidence scores and automated trust evaluations.

AI Use and Governance
 • aiUsePolicy – machine-readable policy controlling AI/ML use of the repository’s
 content or metadata (training allowed, derivative models allowed, commercial use
 allowed). Repositories may also provide an aiPolicy URI that links to a human-
 readable AI use or AI governance policy document.

This ensures that AI agents respect legal, ethical, and contractual constraints. Also note that
since the DRMP is a repository-level profile, the aiUsePolicy sub-elements describe the
repository’s default stance. For general-purpose repositories where permissions vary by dataset,
sub-elements may use the dataset-specific value to indicate that AI tools must consult
dataset-level rights for precise guidance.

Access and Authorization Models
 • accessModel — required for ML agents determining usability
 • authorizationModel — allows AI agents to autonomously request tokens

These allow AI agents to determine whether they can access repository APIs and how to
authenticate safely.

MCP Tools and Agent Interfaces
  • mcpTools – machine-readable MCP tool descriptors exposing repository metadata to
 AI agents and orchestration systems.

This is the critical interface enabling “describe,” “query,” and “inspect” operations in the Model
Context Protocol (MCP) ecosystem.

A Level 3 repository must set "conformsTo": "Level-3-AIReady".

At this level, repositories become AI-ready nodes capable of supporting:

 • Autonomous metadata discovery
 • Agentic curation workflows
 • Trust evaluations and safety constraints

3.4 Progression and Validation
The DRMP conformance model is incremental. Repositories may begin at Level 1 and
progressively adopt Level 2 and Level 3 capabilities as they mature. Validation is performed
using the DRMP JSON Schema described in Appendix A, which enforces level-specific
requirements through conditional schema logic.

Validation steps include:

 • Schema validation for required fields, type correctness, and controlled vocabularies.
 • API endpoint validation (Level 2 and Level 3): checking endpoint availability, metadata
 formats, error handling, and declared capabilities.
 • Policy validation: ensuring harvestPolicy, licensePolicy,
 retentionPolicy, and aiUsePolicy are machine-readable URIs.
 • Access/authorization model evaluation to confirm that Level 3 repositories clearly
 declare authentication constraints.
 • MCP tool validation for Level 3 repositories to ensure all MCP descriptors are complete
 and well-formed.
 • Provenance and fixity validation for PREMIS elements.
 • Generation of machine-actionable validation reports that may be published or integrated
 into DOE compliance workflows.

Conformance levels are additive; achieving Level 3 implies full compliance with Level 1 and
Level 2.
3.4.1 Conformance Validation Workflow
To support consistent adoption, the DRMP defines a unified validation workflow applicable to

Step 1 — Assemble a Repository-Level Description

 • RDA anchor consistency (where applicable)

 4. Integration Mechanisms: API and MCP Enablement
The DRMP’s conformance model becomes operational through a set of integration mechanisms
that translate metadata standards into actionable interoperability. Where Chapter 3 defines what
repositories must expose at each maturity level, this chapter describes how those elements are
used to enable machine-actionable discovery, harvesting, semantic exchange, and AI-driven
orchestration. At Level 2, repositories surface structured metadata through standard APIs along
with capability declarations, harvest policies, and machine-readable governance signals. At
Level 3, repositories publish semantic context and agent-interaction descriptors using the Model
Context Protocol (MCP), enabling AI agents in the AmSC to reason over repository policies,
capabilities, and trust attributes.

Together, these mechanisms ensure that repositories do not operate as isolated metadata silos,
but as interoperable, machine-negotiable components of the DOE scientific data ecosystem.

This chapter details how repositories implement these capabilities in practice, describing the
API conventions required for Level 2 interoperability and the MCP-based semantic context
mechanisms required for Level 3 AI readiness.

4.1 API-Based Interoperability
APIs provide the first operational layer of interoperability under the DRMP. They allow
repositories to expose metadata in structured, machine-readable formats suitable for automated
harvesting, validation, monitoring, and integration into discovery platforms.

For repositories achieving Level 2 conformance, API-based interoperability requires the
following practices and supporting metadata:

Core API Principles
Repositories SHOULD:

 • Provide REST or OAI-PMH endpoints for repository-level (and dataset-level) metadata.
 • Support JSON, JSON-LD, or RDF serializations, enabling semantic clarity and
 transformation.
 • Align API responses with DCAT-US v3, Schema.org Dataset, DataCatalog, and
 Organization classes.
 • Propagate persistent identifiers (PIDs) such as DOIs, ARKs, and ROR IDs at all
 appropriate entity levels.
 • Publish API documentation using OpenAPI 3.1, enabling automated validation by
 external agents.
 • Ensure all endpoints support consistent pagination, rate limiting, and error reporting
 conventions.
 • Required Level 2 Metadata for API Interoperability

In addition to core DRMP Level 2 fields, the schema requires several elements that directly
support API interaction:

 22

 • apiEndpoints - Machine-readable URIs exposing metadata.
 • metadataFormats - Serialization formats supported by the API.
 • licensePolicy - Machine-readable license or terms-of-use URL.
 • retentionPolicy - Documented retention and disposition policy.
 • harvestPolicy - A required declaration specifying whether and how external
 systems may legally or operationally harvest metadata.
 • stewardshipCommitment - A repository's declared scope of curation or
 preservation.
 • capabilities - Machine-readable declarations of supported technical operations, such as:
 o apiAccess
 o bulkDownload
 o datasetVersioning
 o webhooks
 o schemaBrowse

Operational Impact
These fields enable interoperable behavior across DOE and public registries:

 • Data.gov, RE3Data, FAIRsharing, and OSTI can harvest, validate, and classify repository

 • Automated validation frameworks (Appendix A) can test endpoint completeness, policy

API-based interoperability establishes a robust foundation on which Level 3 semantic and AI-
facing capabilities can be built.
4.2 Semantic Context and the Model Context Protocol (MCP)
While APIs provide access to metadata records, MCP enables exchange of context, meaning
structured information describing how repositories manage, expose, and interrelate their
resources. MCP defines a lightweight, machine-interpretable framework that allows repositories
to communicate their operational capabilities, interfaces, and governance logic directly to AI
agents and workflow systems.

In the DRMP, MCP integration is required for Level 3 conformance. The following metadata
and descriptors must be available in machine-actionable form:

Required Level 3 Metadata Fields
 • ontologyAlignment - Links to domain ontologies or controlled vocabularies
 relevant to repository content.
 • preservationPolicy - A machine-readable preservation commitment.
 • provenance - PREMIS event metadata describing validation, updates, and operational
 change history.
 • fixity - Checksums enabling integrity verification of metadata representations.

 23

 • accessModel - Declares whether APIs are open, authenticated, or
 restricted.
 • authorizationModel - Specifies mechanism (none, api-key, oauth2, or
 federated).
 • aiUsePolicy - Machine-readable AI-governance metadata including training,
 derivative models, commercial use, and other constraints.
 • capabilities - Expanded list for AI-facing systems, including:
 o semanticSearch
 o vectorSearch
 o embeddings
 o reasoningSupport
 o mcpTools

Distinguishing “capabilities” from “mcpTools”
The DRMP intentionally separates declaring support for MCP from describing the MCP tools
themselves:
 • capabilities includes a lightweight flag (e.g., "mcpTools") indicating that MCP
 integration is available. This allows AI agents to triage repositories quickly.
 • mcpTools contains the actual MCP tool descriptors that are detailed, structured
 definitions of operations that an agent may invoke.

A Level 3 repository must therefore expose:
 • mcpTools - A list of MCP tool descriptors that define agent-accessible operations (e.g.,
 search, describe, validate, resolve).

Example MCP tool descriptor:
{
  "toolName": "list-datasets",
  "endpoint": "https://repo.example.org/mcp/list-datasets",
  "description": "Returns an array of dataset identifiers available in the
repository."
}

MCP in AI-enabled ecosystems
Through these capabilities and descriptors, MCP enables:
 • autonomous selection of compatible repositories
 • reasoning over access rules, provenance integrity, and ontology alignment
 • integration with AI pipelines that require trust, validation, and policy signals
 • dynamic orchestration within the AmSC

By exposing MCP context manifests and tool definitions, repositories become first-class
participants in AI workflows, capable not only of being harvested, but of being queried,
validated, and utilized by autonomous systems.

 5. Schema Alignment and RDA-Informed Maturity Ratings

single, machine-actionable framework for describing repositories. The DRMP crosswalk forms
the operational backbone of this framework: it harmonizes established schemas so that
repository-level metadata across DOE systems is both interoperable and progressively AI-ready.
Beginning at Level 2, the alignment incorporates machine-actionable access and authorization
metadata; by Level 3, it integrates AI-governance and capability signals required for agent-
driven workflows.

The DRMP also draws on the Research Data Alliance (RDA) Common Descriptive Attributes,

Together, these components provide a coherent framework for aligning DOE repository metadata
with community standards, enabling interoperability, policy compliance, and AI readiness across
the DOE scientific knowledge ecosystem.

 6. Applying the DRMP Crosswalk: Implementation Guidance

To recap, Chapters 3 - 5 established the conceptual foundations of the DRMP by defining its
conformance levels, describing the API and MCP integration mechanisms, and outlining the
schema-alignment strategy. This chapter focuses on practical adoption. It describes how
repository operators can implement the DRMP using metadata they already maintain, map
existing schemas onto the profile, and validate their repository-level records through machine-
actionable tools.

Rather than prescribing a new metadata system, the DRMP is designed to wrap around and
harmonize existing repository descriptions. Most DOE repositories already expose metadata
using RE3Data fields, DCAT distributions, Dublin Core tags, DataCite elements, or custom
schemas embedded in Exchange, CKAN, Dataverse, IDEAS, or homegrown repositories.

Chapter 6 therefore demonstrates how the DRMP can be implemented without restructuring
internal systems, by using the crosswalk mappings and validation scaffolding provided in this
chapter and in Appendices A and B. We introduce the full Rosetta-Stone crosswalk matrix,
describe an implementation process for generating DRMP records from existing metadata,
discuss security and access expression, and finish with a practical implementation checklist.

6.1 Rosetta-Stone Matrix
Chapter 5 introduced the standards underlying the DRMP, which are RE3Data, DCAT-US v3,
Schema.org, Dublin Core, DataCite 4.6, PREMIS 3.0, and the RDA Common Descriptive
Attributes; it only provided a snippet, but did not present the full crosswalk. This section
provides that crosswalk in operational form.

The Rosetta-Stone Matrix is the central implementation tool of the DRMP. It aligns each DRMP
metadata element with its equivalent representations across the six community schemas and
specifies the conformance level (Level 1, 2, or 3) at which the element becomes required.

Rather than functioning as a new schema, the matrix demonstrates that implementers rarely need
to restructure existing metadata systems. Most repositories already maintain functionally
equivalent fields under RE3Data, DCAT, DataCite, Dublin Core, or Schema.org naming
conventions. The Rosetta-Stone Matrix simply makes these equivalences explicit so repositories
can generate DRMP-conformant metadata with translation, not reinvention.

The matrix supports five core implementation tasks:
 • Mapping: identify which existing metadata fields already satisfy DRMP requirements.
 • Gap detection: determine which elements must be added to achieve Level 2 or Level 3.
 • Normalization: standardize field names, data formats, and PID expressions across
 repositories.
 • Federation readiness: ensure compatibility with OSTI, Data.gov, RE3Data, and
 integrated ecosystems like IRI or AmSC.

 33

 • Progress tracking: use RDA Gap ratings to estimate effort and maturity progress.

The full Rosetta-Stone Matrix is presented in Appendix B.

Together, these mappings show that the DRMP is not a new metadata standard but a
harmonization layer. Repositories can generate DRMP-conformant metadata without
restructuring their internal systems by simply aligning existing fields to the DRMP element set
and progressively adding the few missing attributes required for Level 2 and Level 3 AI
readiness.

This crosswalk provides the operational foundation for the implementation steps that follow in
Sections 6.2–6.5.
6.2 Implementing the DRMP Using Existing Metadata Schemas
Most DOE repositories already maintain structured metadata that aligns with at least one of the
six community schemas used in the DRMP crosswalk. The implementation process is
therefore transformational rather than generative where repositories map existing fields into the
DRMP element set and add only the few missing fields required at higher conformance levels.
6.2.1 Identify Your Source Schemas
Determine which community schemas your repository currently uses, directly or indirectly:

 • Many institutional repositories expose Dublin Core fields.
 • DOE-funded repositories often use DCAT-US through CKAN or custom REST APIs.
 • DOI-issuing systems automatically generate DataCite 4.6 metadata.
 • Preservation-oriented repositories maintain PREMIS event records.

Knowing which schemas are already in use makes the DRMP mapping almost entirely
mechanical.
6.2.2 Map Internal Fields to DRMP Elements
Using the Rosetta-Stone Matrix:

 • repositoryName ← dct:title or dc:title or RE3Data repositoryName
 • repositoryIdentifier ← DOI, ARK, HANDLE, or OSTI ID
 • apiEndpoints ← DCAT endpointURL
 • ontologyAlignment ← Schema.org about[] or DCAT theme taxonomy entries
 • provenance ← PREMIS event records

This mapping is one-to-one and does not require repositories to modify internal storage
structures.

6.2.3 Add Fields Needed for Level 2 or Level 3 Progression
Some repositories may not currently expose:

 34

 • license or retention policies
 • access or authorization declarations
 • harvest policies
 • ontology integrations
 • fixity or provenance summaries
 • MCP tool descriptors (Level 3)

These can be added incrementally and published alongside existing metadata without system
redesign.

By leveraging existing schemas and augmenting only where needed, repositories can achieve
DRMP conformance with minimal overhead.
6.3 Building a DRMP Record from Local Metadata
Once mappings have been identified, repositories can construct a DRMP-compliant record using
the JSON Schema in Appendix A. The process involves three steps:

Step 1: Gather Internal Metadata Fields
Pull values from internal databases, API outputs, repository configuration files, or published
system documentation.

Step 2: Translate and Normalize
Apply the Rosetta-Stone Matrix to transform each field into the corresponding DRMP name and
structured value. Normalization includes:

 • converting dates to ISO 8601
 • converting identifiers to resolvable PID forms
 • listing metadata formats as strings
 • using controlled vocabularies (e.g., ROR IDs, SPDX licenses) where applicable

Step 3: Generate the Record Automatically
Most repositories can implement a lightweight export script (Python, JavaScript, Go, or CLI)
that:

 • Pulls required metadata from internal systems
 • Applies the mapping in a config file
 • Outputs a JSON file that validates against the DRMP schema

This script can run as part of a scheduled build, deployment cycle, or metadata refresh pipeline.
The outcome is a stable, machine-actionable representation that can be harvested by data and
other orchestration services.

Repositories must populate the following fields to describe how access is governed:

 36

 • accessModel - Declares whether interfaces are open, authenticated, or restricted.
 • authorizationModel - Specifies supported mechanisms (e.g., none, api-key,
 oauth2, federated identity).
 • accessMechanisms[] - Documents available user and machine interfaces (REST
 API, OAI-PMH, WebUI, etc.).
 • authnMethods[] - Lists recognized authentication workflows (OAuth2, DOE SSO,
 SAML, etc.).

These fields allow downstream systems such as including harvesters, workflows, and AI agents
to correctly negotiate access requirements and handle restricted resources.

AI-Use and Harvest Policies
Two additional governance fields communicate usage boundaries:

 • aiUsePolicy - Machine-readable constraints describing whether repository metadata
 and content may be used for training, derivative models, commercial purposes, or other
 AI-related activities.
 • harvestPolicy - Declares machine-actionable rules for automated metadata
 harvesting and redistribution.

These fields prevent unintentional policy violations and enable automated systems to determine
whether a repository can be used in training, inference, or federated search. Sample policies are
provided in Appendix F.

Restricted Endpoints
For endpoints requiring authentication or role-based access, repositories should include a
conformsTo[] reference pointing to publicly documented security profiles, access policies, or
usage terms. This informs consuming systems about constraints without revealing sensitive
internal configuration.

Handling Secrets
DRMP records must never contain API keys, passwords, tokens, or any operational credentials.
Such information must be managed through standard cybersecurity practices outside of the
metadata layer.

Thus, by representing access controls, authorization pathways, and AI-use rules in structured,
machine-readable form, repositories provide transparency to users and automated agents while
preserving security boundaries and supporting DOE-wide policy alignment.

 7. Governance and Change Management
Chapters 3–6 described how repositories interpret, implement, and validate DRMP conformance.
This chapter turns to the governance of the DRMP itself and how the profile will be maintained,
updated, and stewarded as DOE research practices evolve.

The DCWG is a volunteer community of practice without formal oversight authority; its
governance approach emphasizes transparency, collaborative review, and incremental
improvement rather than prescriptive or mandatory requirements. The objective is to provide a
stable, referenceable interoperability framework while allowing repositories full flexibility in
local implementation.
7.1 Stewardship Model
• The DCWG serves as the steward of the DRMP, maintaining documentation, evaluating
 proposed changes, and coordinating input from across DOE laboratories, OSTI, and other
 stakeholder groups.
• Repositories and practitioners are encouraged to submit feedback, identify gaps, and propose
 extensions through the mechanisms described below.
• Significant revisions will be informed by broad consultation across DOE laboratories,
 program offices, OSTI, librarians, curators, technical professionals, and information science
 communities.
• Public copies of the specification, JSON schemas, examples, and crosswalk materials will be
 hosted on the DCWG website and GitHub repository.
• Major releases or formal versions may also be archived to OSTI to ensure persistent access,
 citation, and historical traceability.
7.2 Versioning
The DRMP follows semantic versioning to indicate the scope of change:

 • Patch (1.0.x): editorial corrections, clarifications, or typographical fixes.
 • Minor (1.x.0): additions of optional fields, new crosswalk alignments, or expanded best-
 practice guidance.
 • Major (x.0.0): structural changes to conformance levels, revised definitions, or element
 deprecations.

Each release will include:

• A public changelog summarizing modifications and their rationale
• Notes on interoperability implications.
• Updated schemas, validation tools, and examples.

Version control ensures that repositories, tool developers, and federated services can track
evolution of the DRMP and maintain alignment with specific profile version.
7.3 Deprecation Policy
• Elements identified for removal or major modification will be marked deprecated for at least
 one full release cycle before removal.

 40

• Repositories should maintain backward compatibility during this period while preparing to
 adopt updated mappings.
• Deprecation warnings will appear in the DRMP documentation, crosswalk tables, JSON
 schemas, and example metadata records.
• Machine-actionable artifacts (e.g., JSON-Schema validators or metadata checklists) will
 include explicit annotations to assist implementers in migration and compliance.
7.4 Extensions and Community Contributions
The DRMP supports controlled, community-driven extensibility:

 • Repositories may define local extensions using "other" enumerations or x--prefixed
 custom fields, provided semantic meaning is documented.
 • Extensions demonstrating broad utility or alignment with DOE-wide priorities may be
 considered for inclusion in future releases.
 • Contribution proposals should include clear use cases, example metadata, and evidence
 of semantic alignment with established ontologies, controlled vocabularies, or crosswalk
 targets.
 • Extensions with substantial adoption across sites may be promoted to optional or
 recommended DRMP elements.
7.5 Change Request Process
The DCWG maintains a public issue tracker (e.g., GitHub) to coordinate updates:

 • Submitters may propose clarifications, corrections, new fields, vocabulary updates, or
 crosswalk enhancements.
 • Issues are triaged on a regular cadence and incorporated into the next appropriate release
 (patch, minor, major).
 • Approved changes are propagated to all relevant artifacts, including the master mapping
 document, JSON schemas, examples, and validation tools, ensuring alignment between
 human-readable and machine-actionable representations.
7.6 Alignment with External Standards
To maintain interoperability, the DRMP is periodically reviewed against updates to major
community and federal frameworks, including:

• RE3Data, DCAT-US v3, Schema.org, DataCite 4.6, Dublin Core (DCMI)
• FAIRsharing, OSTI metadata schemas, and emerging federal interoperability profiles
• Ontology services, provenance standards, and preservation frameworks relevant to AI-
 readiness

When upstream standards evolve, the DCWG evaluates impact on DOE practice and updates
crosswalks accordingly. Major alignment changes will be documented with example adaptation
strategies.

 8. Conclusion
The DCWG developed this profile to provide practical, community-driven guidance for
describing repositories across the DOE. By focusing on repository-level metadata (rather than
dataset-level descriptions), the DRMP addresses a long-standing structural gap in discovery,
interoperability, and machine-actionability within DOE’s scientific data ecosystem.

The guidance presented here is advisory, not prescriptive. It is intended to support voluntary,
incremental adoption across diverse repositories, enabling implementers to align with DOE’s
strategic direction while maintaining full flexibility in local system design.

We grounded the profile in widely used community schemas: RE3Data; DCAT-US v3;
Schema.org; Dublin Core (DCMI 1.1); DataCite 4.6; and PREMIS 3.0. Thus, the DRMP avoids
reinvention and strengthens interoperability with global data-exchange and FAIR-enabling
infrastructures. These alignments ensure that DOE repositories can participate meaningfully in
interconnected, multi-agency scientific knowledge environments.

We introduced three conformance levels in this report: Minimum Viable Record; Interoperable;
and AI-Ready. This established a clear vocabulary and progression pathway for repositories to
self-assess and enhance their metadata practices. Together with the JSON Schema, Rosetta-
Matrix crosswalk, OpenAPI specification, and MCP templates provided in the appendices, the
DRMP offers a coherent, implementation-ready framework that repositories can adopt at their
own pace.

Data repositories operate with distinct missions, data types, stakeholder communities, and
operational constraints. This diversity has naturally produced a patchwork of metadata practices
and standards which, while locally optimized, often hinder broader discovery, access, and
interoperability. Adoption of the DRMP can help introduce greater consistency across
repositories; however, implementation also requires repositories to conduct self-assessments,
identify conformance gaps, and establish improvement plans. These activities demand time,
expertise, and resources, and in the absence of formal mandates may be perceived as optional,
potentially slowing adoption. In practice, repositories frequently require clear justification for
funding allocated to metadata enhancement efforts. Endorsement of the DRMP by DOE-level
programs and initiatives, including the Genesis Mission and the AmSC, can provide important
strategic validation and funding justification, analogous to policy-level drivers that have
historically accelerated alignment efforts across the DOE complex.

Widespread, voluntary adoption of the DRMP will position DOE repositories to meet the goals
of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (2025), integrate into the AmSC, and maintain alignment with
DOE’s Public Access Plan 2.0. More importantly, it will strengthen the Department’s long-term
stewardship of scientific data by ensuring that repository metadata remains transparent,
interoperable, durable, and AI-ready for emerging research paradigms.

The DCWG invites repositories across the DOE complex to engage with this guidance, propose
enhancements, and contribute extensions. Through sustained collaboration and iterative
improvement, the DOE community can collectively advance a resilient metadata framework, one

 43

that supports discovery, accelerates access, and enables responsible innovation across the
Department’s scientific mission.

Implementation Notes

A. Validation Tools
DRMP conformance can be validated using any JSON Schema validator that fully
supports draft-2020-12, including jsonschema (Python), ajv (Node.js), and spectral.

Repositories should validate both:
 • baseline schema conformance, and
 • conformance-level–specific requirements (L1, L2, L3).

 54

Level 3 repositories should additionally validate MCP tool descriptors and ensure all MCP
endpoints return well-formed responses.

B. Continuous Integration and Deployment
Integrating DRMP validation into CI/CD pipelines ensures that:
 • repository metadata updates do not break conformance,
 • API endpoints remain reachable and machine-actionable,
 • MCP tool descriptors stay synchronized with repository capabilities.

Recommended CI checks include:
 1. JSON Schema validation (all levels)
 2. API health checks (Level 2 and above)
 3. MCP tool descriptor validation (Level 3)
 4. Fixity and provenance updates (where applicable)

C. Schema Versioning and Compatibility
Future revisions of the DRMP should adopt clear semantic versioning:
 • MAJOR: Breaking changes to fields, required elements, or conformance-level meaning
 • MINOR: Additive extensions, optional fields, new capabilities
 • PATCH: Error fixes, clarifications

Each schema must publish a unique $id and include a schemaVersion field in its metadata to
ensure AI agents and MCP tooling can negotiate compatibility across versions.

Version stability is particularly important for Level 3 repositories that expose MCP interfaces.

D. Governance, Stewardship, and Change Control
The DRMP schema and associated conformance model should be governed by the DCWG with
transparent community change control and public review.

A central, publicly accessible schema registry should be maintained to publish:
 • stable schema versions,
 • deprecation notices,
 • MCP tool updates,
 • implementation patterns,
 • and crosswalk/mapping profile revisions.

Repositories should maintain internal governance processes for:
 • metadata provenance,
 • fixity tracking,
 • AI use policy management, and
 • stewardship commitments (especially for long-term preservation claims).

 Appendix F – Repository Policy Starter Kit
This appendix provides short example policies that repositories can use as starting points when
developing their own public-facing documentation. These templates illustrate the kinds of
license, retention, harvest, preservation, and PID policies expected for DRMP Level 2 and Level
3 readiness.

These examples are not official DOE or institutional policies, and they should not be adopted
verbatim. Any repository implementing or adapting this language must follow its institution’s
review and approval process, including legal or compliance vetting as required. The examples
here are intended to spark discussion and provide practical scaffolding, not to replace local
policy guidance.

Example policies provided:

 • License Policy
 • Retention Policy
 • Harvest Policy
 • Persistent Identifier Policy
 • Preservation Policy
 • AI Use Policy

F.1 License Policy (Example Page)
Title: Repository License and Use Policy
URL: https://example-repo.org/policies/license

1. Overview
The Example Research Repository (“the Repository”) makes datasets, metadata, and
documentation available for public use under standardized open licenses. This policy describes
how licenses are assigned and how users may reuse repository content.

2. License Assignment
Datasets are assigned a license by the depositing researcher or project.
The repository strongly recommends the use of:
 • Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC-BY-4.0) for general research data
 • Open Data Commons Open Database License (ODbL) for structured databases
Metadata generated by the repository staff is released under CC0 1.0.

3. User Rights & Responsibilities
Users may download, reuse, and redistribute materials according to the terms of the license
attached to each dataset. Users must:
 • Retain attribution where required
 • Note any modifications
 • Comply with downstream community or journal requirements

4. Repository Responsibilities
The repository ensures that:
 • License information is included in dataset records
 • Machine-readable license metadata (dct:license) is exposed through APIs
 • Users can filter datasets by license type

5. Contact
For questions or requests to update a license, email support@example-repo.org.

F-2 Retention Policy (Example Page)
Title: Data Retention and Lifecycle Policy
URL: https://example-repo.org/policies/retention

1. Purpose
This policy outlines how long datasets and associated materials are retained, how long metadata
is preserved, and what conditions apply to removal or deprecation.

2. Retention Timeframes
Published datasets: retained for a minimum of 10 years after publication.
Metadata records: retained indefinitely to support discovery and citation.
Embargoed datasets: embargo duration is determined by the depositor (max. 3 years).

3. Removal and Deprecation
Datasets may be withdrawn when:
 • Legal or privacy concerns are identified
 • The depositor requests removal for justified reasons
 • A dataset is superseded by a corrected or curated version

Withdrawn datasets leave a tombstone page containing:
 • Citation
 • DOI
 • Reason for withdrawal
 • Contact information

4. File Formats and Obsolescence
The repository monitors high-risk formats and may repackage or migrate files to ensure future
accessibility.

5. Review Cycle
This policy is reviewed every two years.

F-3 Harvest Policy (Example Page)
Title: Metadata Harvesting and API Access Policy
URL: https://example-repo.org/policies/harvest

1. Purpose
This policy defines how external systems may harvest metadata and interact with repository
APIs.

2. Harvesting Standards Supported
The repository supports:
 • OAI-PMH 2.0 at https://example-repo.org/oai
 • DCAT-US v3 JSON export at https://example-repo.org/api/dcat
 • Search API at https://example-repo.org/api/v1/search

3. Conditions of Use
External harvesting systems must:
 • Respect a maximum frequency of one request per second
 • Include a valid User-Agent header
 • Avoid high-volume full-content scraping of file downloads

4. Access Restrictions
Metadata is openly harvestable.
Full datasets may require authentication.
Rate limits may apply during periods of heavy system load.

5. Termination of Access
The repository may suspend hosts that violate rate limits or misuse the service.

F-4 Persistent Identifier (PID) Policy (Example Page)
Title: Persistent Identifier Assignment and Management Policy
URL: https://example-repo.org/policies/pids

1. Overview
This policy describes the use of persistent identifiers (PIDs) for datasets, researchers,
organizations, and projects within the repository.

2. PID Systems Supported
The repository uses the following PID systems:
 • DOI (DataCite) for datasets, software, and documentation
 • ORCID iD for authors
 • ROR ID for organizations
 • Handle for internal references and legacy records
 • LocalID for temporary objects during ingestion workflows

3. DOI Assignment Rules
A DOI is minted when a dataset is approved for publication.
DOIs are never re-assigned or deleted.
Updated versions receive new DOIs, with version links exposed.

4. Responsibility and Registration
The repository is the authoritative registration agency for DOIs minted under its prefix. All
DOIs are registered with DataCite including:
 • Metadata record
 • Landing page
 • Version chain
 • Funding references

5. PID Governance
The repository maintains:
 • A public PID namespace documentation page
 • A commitment to maintain DOI landing pages for at least 10 years

F-5 Preservation Policy (Example Page)
Title: Digital Preservation and Integrity Policy
URL: https://example-repo.org/policies/preservation

1. Purpose
This policy outlines the repository’s long-term preservation strategy, including fixity checking,
format sustainability, and migration processes.

2. Preservation Scope
The repository preserves:
 • Published datasets
 • Accompanying documentation
 • Metadata records
 • Software needed for interpretation (when possible)

3. Fixity and Integrity
Checksums (SHA-256) are generated for all files upon ingest.
Fixity is revalidated every 90 days.
Any mismatches trigger an automatic preservation audit.

4. Format Sustainability
Preferred formats include:
 • CSV, NetCDF, HDF5, Parquet
 • JSON, XML, YAML for metadata
 • PNG/JPEG for images (archival TIFF when available)

5. Migration and Refresh Strategy
Files in deprecated formats may be migrated to preservation formats after curator review.
Original bitstreams are always retained unless legally restricted.

6. Storage Strategy
Data is stored across:
 • A primary high-availability disk tier
 • A secondary cloud replica
 • A cold long-term archival tape tier

7. Policy Review
Updated every two years, or sooner when technology or sustainability needs change.

F-6 AI Use Policy (Example Page)
Title: AI Use and Machine Interaction Policy
URL (example): https://example-repo.org/policies/ai-use

1. Overview
This policy describes how artificial intelligence (AI) systems, machine agents, and automated
tools may interact with the Repository. It defines the conditions under which repository content
may be used for model training, evaluation, derivative model creation, or automated access.

2. Training Permission
Unless otherwise stated in individual dataset records:
 • Metadata-only training is permitted.
 • Full dataset training requires approval from the depositing researcher or the
 Repository’s data governance office.
 • Datasets with restricted, sensitive, or export-controlled content may not be used for
 training.
 • Dataset-level restrictions always override repository-wide defaults.

3. Derivative Models
Models trained using Repository metadata or datasets must:
 • Cite the Repository and any relevant dataset DOIs.
 • Clearly document whether training inputs included full data, metadata, or mixed sources.
 • Not imply endorsement by the Repository or its host institution.
Derivative model publication may be restricted for sensitive datasets.

4. Commercial AI Use
Commercial AI use is permitted only when dataset-specific license terms allow it.
If a dataset’s license prohibits commercial applications, machine agents must enforce those
restrictions.

5. Model Evaluation Permission
AI models may evaluate or benchmark against Repository metadata or datasets under the
following conditions:
 • Evaluation tasks must not attempt to re-identify individuals from anonymized datasets.
 • Benchmark logs should record dataset versions used for reproducibility.

6. Automated Agents and Interactions
Machine agents accessing the Repository (including crawlers, MCP tools, AI agents, and
workflow bots) must:
 • Identify themselves via User-Agent headers
 • Respect documented rate limits
 • Avoid scraping restricted or embargoed files

 • Comply with authentication requirements where applicable
 • Violations may result in temporary or permanent access restrictions.

7. AI Governance and Compliance
AI use must comply with:
 • Repository license terms
 • DOE public access and data management policies
 • Institutional guidelines for responsible AI and automated decision-making

8. Policy Review
This policy is reviewed every two years or when changes in AI governance, privacy
requirements, or funding agency guidance require updates.
