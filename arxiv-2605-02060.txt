DR-SNE: Density-Regularized Stochastic Neighbor Embedding
Maksim Kazanskii

Abstract
Dimensionality reduction methods such as t-SNE are designed to preserve local neighborhood structure but do not explicitly account for how probability mass is distributed, often leading to distortions of data density. We reformulate dimensionality reduction as the joint alignment of two components: (i) conditional structure, capturing local relationships, and (ii) relative density structure, captured via local density statistics. Based on this perspective, we introduce Density-Regularized SNE (DR-SNE), which augments the stochastic neighbor embedding objective with a density regularization term derived from normalized log-density estimates. Unlike prior approaches such as DensMAP and DenSNE, which rely on local scale consistency, DR-SNE directly aligns normalized density estimates, providing a simple and scale-invariant mechanism for preserving relative density variations. Empirically, DR-SNE improves density preservation while maintaining competitive neighborhood fidelity, and yields gains on density-sensitive tasks such as anomaly detection across multiple datasets. These results suggest that incorporating density information complements geometry-focused objectives in dimensionality reduction.



1
Introduction

Dimensionality reduction is typically formulated as a geometric problem: given high-dimensional data, the goal is to preserve local neighborhood relationships in a lower-dimensional embedding. Methods such as t-SNE achieve this by aligning conditional similarity distributions, effectively preserving local structure while allowing substantial freedom in global organization. However, this formulation implicitly ignores a fundamental aspect of the data: how probability mass is distributed locally across the dataset. In practice, dimensionality reduction methods severely distort density, expanding dense regions and compressing sparse ones, leading to embeddings that misrepresent the underlying data distribution. This limitation has been widely observed in practice
[
27
]
and analyzed in related theoretical work
[
10
]
, yet it is typically treated as a side effect rather than a core modeling issue. In this work, we argue that this limitation arises from an incomplete objective. We propose that dimensionality reduction should be understood as the joint alignment of two components of a data distribution:

conditional structure
(local relationships) and

relative density structure
(variation of probability mass via local density statistics).

This perspective can be formalized via a decomposition of the Kullback–Leibler divergence between joint distributions into conditional and marginal components, where standard SNE objectives optimize only the conditional term. In this work, we introduce a complementary objective that aligns spatial density statistics via normalized local density estimates. Existing methods primarily optimize the first component, while leaving the second unconstrained. This leads to embeddings that are locally faithful but globally misleading in terms of density. While this decomposition is standard in information theory, its implications for dimensionality reduction have not been explicitly exploited. We use it to motivate a separation between conditional structure and marginal density.

We introduce Density-Regularized SNE (DR-SNE), a simple and principled extension of the stochastic neighbor framework that explicitly incorporates a local approximation to marginal density alignment via normalized density estimates. By augmenting the standard objective with a density consistency term based on normalized log-density estimates, DR-SNE directly penalizes local volume distortion, yielding a scale-invariant and interpretable objective. Unlike prior density-aware methods such as DensMAP and DenSNE, which enforce consistency of local neighborhood scales (e.g., radii or bandwidths), the proposed approach explicitly aligns
globally normalized density estimates
. This formulation preserves relative density structure across the dataset via normalized local density estimates, rather than relying on indirect scale-based proxies.

Related Work

Classical dimensionality reduction methods such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
[
3
]
and manifold learning approaches including Isomap
[
21
]
and Locally Linear Embedding (LLE)
[
18
]
focus on preserving global or local geometric structure, but do not explicitly address density preservation.

t
-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE)
[
22
]
is widely used due to its strong local neighborhood preservation and ability to reveal cluster structure, with several extensions improving scalability and optimization
[
9
]
.
However, t-SNE is known to distort global geometry and can distort variations in data density, often producing embeddings with misleading cluster sizes and spatial relationships
[
27
,
10
]
.
Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)
[
12
]
provides an alternative graph-based formulation that often yields improved global organization.
Other recent dimensionality reduction methods such as TriMap
[
1
]
and PaCMAP
[
26
]
aim to better preserve global structure through alternative objective formulations.
Building on this, density-aware variants such as DensMAP and DenSNE
[
13
]
incorporate density information by encouraging consistency between local neighborhood scales (e.g., radii or bandwidths) across spaces.
While these approaches improve qualitative density representation, they rely on scale-based proxies that are only indirectly related to local sampling density and can be sensitive to embedding scale and neighborhood definition.
While kNN-based density estimates are themselves local approximations, they directly estimate relative sampling density and become scale-invariant after normalization, unlike radius-based constraints which depend on embedding scale and neighborhood definition. In contrast, we propose a formulation based on
explicit alignment of density-derived local statistics
.
We estimate local densities using
k
-nearest neighbor statistics in both spaces and minimize discrepancies between normalized log-density estimates, yielding a scale-invariant objective that explicitly aligns relative density variations.
This provides a more direct and interpretable mechanism for controlling density preservation than proxy-based approaches. Our formulation is also related to distribution alignment approaches such as optimal transport, which aim to match probability measures across spaces
[
16
,
15
]
. However, these methods typically focus on global distribution matching, whereas DR-SNE enforces alignment of local density statistics compatible with stochastic neighbor embedding. We note connections to recent work leveraging embedding-space density for tasks such as out-of-distribution detection
[
4
]
, where density can serve as a signal for confidence and distributional shift. While these approaches do not explicitly address density preservation in dimensionality reduction, they highlight the importance of density as a meaningful statistical quantity in learned representations. Overall, our approach provides a simple and principled mechanism for preserving
relative density variations
, enabling an explicit trade-off between neighborhood fidelity and density consistency.

3
Methods

3.1
Density-Regularized Stochastic Neighbor Embedding

Given data
X=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, we learn an embedding
Z=\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\ll D
) that preserves local structure and relative density.

We define high-dimensional affinities as in
[
22
]
:

p_{ij}=\frac{p_{j|i}+p_{i|j}}{2n},\quad p_{j|i}\propto\exp(-\beta_{i}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|^{2}),

(1)

with
\beta_{i}
set by perplexity. Low-dimensional similarities use a Student-
t
kernel:

q_{ij}=\frac{(1+\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2})^{-1}}{\sum_{k\neq l}(1+\|z_{k}-z_{l}\|^{2})^{-1}}.

(2)

The SNE objective can be interpreted as minimizing a KL divergence between joint distributions
P_{ij}=P_{i}P_{j|i}
and
Q_{ij}=Q_{i}Q_{j|i}
:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij}\,\|\,Q_{ij})=\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}\,\|\,Q_{i})+\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{j|i}\,\|\,Q_{j|i})\right].

(3)

Since SNE fixes
P_{i}=Q_{i}=1/n
, the marginal term vanishes, and the objective reduces to aligning conditional distributions only.

We optimize a sparse KL objective over
k
-nearest neighbors:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(i)}p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}.

(4)

To incorporate spatial density information not captured by the SNE objective, we estimate local densities via
k
-NN distances:

\rho_{i}^{\mathrm{high}}=\frac{k}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(i)}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|},\quad\rho_{i}^{\mathrm{low}}=\frac{k}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(i)}\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|},

(5)

and normalize them to unit mean. We then minimize a log-density discrepancy:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\rho_{i}^{\mathrm{high}}-\log\rho_{i}^{\mathrm{low}}\right)^{2}.

(6)

The final objective is

\mathcal{L}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\mathrm{early}},&t<T_{\mathrm{warmup}},\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(7)

where early exaggeration is applied during a warm-up phase. Optimization is performed with Adam and gradient clipping, with Gaussian initialization. The density regularization term is theoretically motivated via local volume distortion and marginal alignment (Appendix
A
).

3.2
Comparison Methods

We compare DR-SNE against widely used dimensionality reduction methods, including both classical approaches and density-aware extensions:
t-SNE

[
22
]
, which preserves local neighborhood structure via a Kullback–Leibler divergence objective;
UMAP

[
12
]
, a graph-based method that balances local and global structure;
PaCMAP

[
26
]
, which improves global organization through multiple pairwise relationships; and density-aware variants
DensMAP
and
DenSNE

[
13
]
, which incorporate density information via local scale constraints. We omit other global-structure methods such as
TriMap

[
1
]
and
LargeVis

[
20
]
, as our focus is on density-aware formulations; preliminary experiments indicated similar trends in density distortion and did not affect the overall conclusions. In addition, we include a
high-dimensional baseline (HIGH-D)
, where all evaluations are performed directly in the original feature space without dimensionality reduction. This baseline provides an upper reference for methods that rely on the original geometry and density structure, allowing us to assess the extent to which embeddings preserve information relevant for downstream tasks. All methods are evaluated under comparable settings, with hyperparameters selected using standard practices or grid search.

To enable a controlled comparison, we adopt a constrained evaluation protocol. For each dataset, hyperparameters are selected to maximize density correlation subject to a fixed trustworthiness threshold. This compares all methods at a similar level of local neighborhood preservation and isolates differences in density fidelity. In effect, we analyze the trade-off between topology and density by asking:
given the same trustworthiness, which method better preserves relative density?
This corresponds to comparing methods along a Pareto frontier between neighborhood preservation and density alignment.

Under the constrained evaluation protocol (fixed trustworthiness), DR-SNE consistently attains the highest density correlation across all datasets in Table
1
. The improvements are notable (e.g., MNIST:
0.991
vs.
0.987
for DensMAP; PBMC:
0.974

1
. Geometry-focused methods such as t-SNE, UMAP, and PaCMAP emphasize cluster separation but substantially distort density, often fragmenting continuous structures (e.g., Shuttle) or producing artificially uniform clusters (PBMC, Fashion-MNIST). Density-aware variants (DensMAP, DenSNE) partially mitigate these effects, yet still tend to either over-compress dense regions or disrupt global organization. In contrast, DR-SNE yields embeddings with smoother transitions and more continuous structure: dense regions remain compact, while sparse regions expand and connect more naturally. This comes at the cost of reduced cluster sharpness and increased class overlap relative to methods optimized for visual separation. Overall, DR-SNE provides a more faithful representation of the underlying distribution, particularly in hybrid datasets such as PBMC and Shuttle, where both discrete clusters and continuous variation are present.

We then apply a set of anomaly scoring functions directly in the embedding space.
Specifically, we consider:
(i)
k
-nearest neighbor (kNN) distance, defined as the sum of distances to the
k
nearest neighbors;
(ii) Local Outlier Factor (LOF), which measures deviations in local density relative to neighboring points;
(iii) Isolation Forest (IF), a tree-based method that isolates anomalies via random partitioning; and
(iv) a centroid-based score defined as the Euclidean distance to the global mean of the embedding. Performance is evaluated using the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), which is more informative in imbalanced anomaly detection settings.
All metrics are averaged over 5 random seeds to account for stochasticity in the embedding procedures. We compare DR-SNE against standard dimensionality reduction methods, including
t
-SNE, UMAP, and PaCMAP, as well as density-aware baselines such as DensMAP and DenSNE.
For each method, we perform a grid search over key hyperparameters (two per method), covering a broad range of configurations.
Full hyperparameter grids for each method can be found in Appendix
D
. This evaluation framework mitigates some confounding factors by applying identical anomaly scoring functions across all embeddings. While downstream detectors depend on both geometry and density, this setup allows us to probe how differences in local density structure induced by the embedding affect anomaly detection performance.

Results are summarized in Table

. The left panel reports the best configuration per method and detector, while the right panel shows a fair comparison with hyperparameters selected by maximizing Isolation Forest (IF) performance, isolating the role of density structure. Additional results are provided in Appendix

. Across datasets, performance is regime-dependent. On
CIFAR
, DR-SNE is competitive but not dominant: DenSNE performs best for kNN, UMAP for LOF, and PaCMAP for centroid scoring, while IF results are similar across methods. This reflects differing inductive biases favoring different detectors. On
Thyroid

, a clear split emerges: PaCMAP excels for IF and centroid scoring, while DR-SNE performs best for kNN and LOF, highlighting the benefit of density preservation for local detectors. Under the Fair protocol, HIGH-D becomes competitive and DR-SNE remains stable but no longer dominant, indicating sensitivity of local methods to hyperparameter selection.
Overall, these results support the view that explicit alignment of relative density can be beneficial for downstream tasks that depend on local distributional structure, particularly for kNN- and LOF-based detection. At the same time, geometry-focused methods may be preferable for detectors such as IF or centroid scoring that benefit from strong global separation. DR-SNE has
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time complexity per iteration due to dense pairwise interactions, similar to standard t-SNE. While this limits scalability in the current implementation, existing acceleration techniques (e.g., Barnes–Hut or interpolation-based methods) can be applied directly. Full complexity analysis is provided in Appendix

.

5
Discussion

The results support the central hypothesis of this work: dimensionality reduction can be understood as the joint alignment of conditional structure and marginal density. Classical methods focus primarily on conditional alignment, which explains their strong neighborhood preservation but also their systematic distortion of density. By explicitly introducing a marginal alignment term, DR-SNE provides a controlled mechanism for preserving relative density variations. Empirically, this leads to a consistent and well-behaved trade-off. As the density regularization strength
\lambda
increases, density correlation improves monotonically, while neighborhood-based metrics such as trustworthiness degrade gradually. Importantly, small non-zero values of
\lambda
often improve both density preservation and local structure, indicating that mild density regularization acts as a stabilizer rather than a competing objective. This trade-off is not specific to the proposed method, but reflects a fundamental limitation of dimensionality reduction. As shown in the theoretical analysis, preserving density corresponds to constraining local volume distortion, which is generally incompatible with exact preservation of neighborhood geometry in low dimensions. The role of
\lambda
is therefore to interpolate between these two objectives, rather than to eliminate the trade-off. From a practical perspective, the results demonstrate that DR-SNE is particularly effective in density-driven and hybrid regimes, where preserving relative density is critical for downstream tasks such as anomaly detection. In contrast, in purely clustered or manifold-dominated settings, geometry-focused methods may remain preferable. This highlights the importance of selecting dimensionality reduction objectives based on the underlying data structure. Finally, we observe that DR-SNE exhibits stable behavior across a broad range of hyperparameters. Both
\lambda
and the neighborhood size
k
admit wide intervals with consistent performance, reducing sensitivity to precise tuning. This robustness, together with the explicit and interpretable objective, makes the method practical for real-world applications where both structure and distributional fidelity are important.

6
Conclusion

We introduced a principled reformulation of dimensionality reduction as the joint alignment of conditional structure and marginal density, addressing a key limitation of widely used methods such as t-SNE and UMAP, which often distort the underlying data distribution. By explicitly aligning normalized log-density estimates between high- and low-dimensional spaces, DR-SNE preserves relative density variations and more faithfully captures continuous structure, rather than imposing artificial cluster geometry. Empirically, this leads to improved density fidelity while maintaining competitive neighborhood preservation, with gains in downstream tasks such as anomaly detection. The objective is simple, interpretable, and provides a direct control over the trade-off between topology and distributional alignment. More broadly, this work suggests that dimensionality reduction should be viewed not only as a geometric problem, but as the alignment of complementary components of the data distribution, opening new directions for statistically grounded embedding methods.

Appendix A
Theoretical Analysis of Density Alignment

We analyze the density regularization term in DR-SNE and show that it corresponds to controlling
local volume distortion
induced by the embedding. We further provide complementary interpretations in terms of marginal distribution alignment and information-theoretic decomposition.

A.1
Density Transformation and Estimation

Let
X=\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
be samples from a distribution supported on a smooth
m
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\ll D
) be a smooth mapping, with
z=f(x)
.

Under the change-of-variables formula restricted to
\mathcal{M}
(see, e.g.,
[
8
]
), densities transform as:

\rho^{(Z)}(f(x))=\frac{\rho^{(X)}(x)}{J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)},

(9)

where

J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)=\sqrt{\det\left(J_{f}(x)^{\top}J_{f}(x)\right)}

(10)

is the Jacobian determinant restricted to the tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
.

Taking logarithms yields:

\log\rho^{(X)}(x)-\log\rho^{(Z)}(f(x))=\log J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x).

(11)

Thus, differences in log-density correspond directly to local volume distortion induced by the mapping. In practice, densities are not observed and must be estimated. We use a
k
-nearest neighbor estimator:

\rho_{i}=\frac{k}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}(i)}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|+\epsilon},

(12)

consistent under standard assumptions
[
11
,
19
]
.

To remove global scaling effects, we normalize:

\tilde{\rho}_{i}=\frac{\rho_{i}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}}.

(13)

Taking logarithms eliminates multiplicative constants and yields scale-invariant quantities.

A.2
Connection to the DR-SNE Objective

The density term in DR-SNE is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(X)}-\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(Z)}\right)^{2}.

(14)

Using the density transformation relationship and ignoring estimation noise, we obtain the approximation:

\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(X)}-\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(Z)}\approx\log J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\log J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x_{j}).

(15)

Substituting into the objective gives:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}\approx\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}[\log J_{\mathcal{M}}f(x)]\right)^{2}.

(16)

Interpretation.

This shows that minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}
encourages the embedding to equalize local volume changes across the data manifold, up to a global scaling factor.

The density term penalizes variability in local volume distortion. This has several important consequences:

•

Promotes uniform expansion:
regions of the manifold are scaled more evenly, reducing extreme stretching or compression.

•

Discourages collapse of dense regions:
high-density areas are prevented from collapsing into overly compact clusters.

•

Prevents artificial equalization:
unlike standard methods that tend to produce clusters of similar size, density regularization preserves relative differences in sampling density.

Overall, the effect is to preserve
relative density structure
rather than enforcing geometric separation alone.

A.3
Distributional Interpretation

The density term can also be interpreted as aligning marginal distributions between the original and embedding spaces.

Let
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Z}
denote the empirical distributions in the original and embedding spaces. The difference in log-densities corresponds to differences in log-likelihood under these distributions.

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}
reduces discrepancies between local log-density estimates, which can be viewed as a relaxed, local approximation to matching marginal distributions. Unlike global divergence measures such as KL divergence between
\mu_{X}
and
\mu_{Z}
, the proposed objective operates on
local statistics
, making it tractable and compatible with stochastic neighbor embedding.

This provides a complementary perspective:

•

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}
aligns conditional distributions (local neighborhoods),

•

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}
aligns marginal distributions (density of points).

We further provide an information-theoretic view of DR-SNE as aligning complementary components of the data distribution.

Density as marginal information.

Although index marginals are uniform, the underlying data density is not. This information is captured by local density estimates
\rho_{i}
.

The DR-SNE density term:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dens}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left(\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(X)}-\log\tilde{\rho}_{i}^{(Z)}\right)^{2}

(17)

enforces consistency between log-density estimates across spaces, acting as a local proxy for marginal alignment.

Unified objective.

The full objective can be written as:

\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[\mathrm{KL}(P_{j|i}\,\|\,Q_{j|i})\big]}_{\text{conditional}}+\lambda\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{i}\big[(\log\rho_{i}^{(X)}-\log\rho_{i}^{(Z)})^{2}\big]}_{\text{marginal}}.

(18)

Thus, DR-SNE jointly aligns conditional structure and marginal density. In contrast, standard methods perform only partial alignment, explaining their density distortions.

Information-theoretic decomposition.

Let
P_{ij}
and
Q_{ij}
denote the joint similarity distributions in the original and embedding spaces, respectively. Assume they factorize as

P_{ij}=P_{i}\,P_{j|i},\quad Q_{ij}=Q_{i}\,Q_{j|i}.

(19)

The Kullback–Leibler divergence between joint distributions admits the decomposition:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij}\,\|\,Q_{ij})=\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}\,\|\,Q_{i})+\mathbb{E}_{i\sim P_{i}}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{j|i}\,\|\,Q_{j|i})\right].

(20)

In stochastic neighbor embedding methods, the marginal distributions are fixed and uniform, i.e.,
P_{i}=Q_{i}=\frac{1}{n}
. As a result, the marginal term
\mathrm{KL}(P_{i}\,\|\,Q_{i})
vanishes, and the objective reduces to optimizing only the conditional component:

\mathrm{KL}(P_{ij}\,\|\,Q_{ij})=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\mathrm{KL}(P_{j|i}\,\|\,Q_{j|i})\right].

(21)

This explains why standard SNE-based methods focus exclusively on preserving local neighborhood structure while leaving the distribution of probability mass unconstrained.

A.4
Properties and Trade-offs

The density regularization term exhibits several useful invariance properties.

•

Scale invariance:
since densities are normalized to unit mean and compared in log-space, the objective is invariant to global multiplicative scaling of the embedding, i.e.,
z_{i}\mapsto\alpha z_{i}
.

•

Translation invariance:
density estimates depend only on pairwise distances and are therefore invariant to global shifts of the embedding.

As a result, the objective focuses on
relative density variations
across the dataset, rather than absolute scale.

Here we present additional ablation studies on PCA dimensionality and neighborhood size.
We analyze the effect of PCA dimensionality on embedding quality under fixed density regularization (
\lambda=0.01
) (Table
5
). The results reveal a clear trade-off between local structure preservation and global density alignment.

Lower-dimensional representations (PCA = 10–20) achieve the highest trustworthiness and continuity, indicating strong preservation of local neighborhoods. However, these representations exhibit higher stress and limited global expressiveness, suggesting that aggressive dimensionality reduction constrains the embedding’s ability to capture large-scale structure.

Interestingly, density correlation remains remarkably stable across a wide range of PCA dimensionalities, exhibiting only weak dependence on the number of retained components. This highlights a key strength of the proposed approach: density structure is robustly captured even under substantial dimensionality reduction. In contrast to local metrics such as trustworthiness and continuity, which degrade as dimensionality increases, density preservation remains consistently high. This suggests that the method effectively decouples global density alignment from the specific choice of feature representation, making it less sensitive to preprocessing.

In addition to these observations, we note that the transition between low- and high-dimensional regimes is smooth rather than abrupt. As the number of principal components increases, local neighborhood structure is gradually relaxed in favor of improved global consistency, without introducing significant instability in the embedding. This behavior suggests that the method does not rely on a narrow optimal range of PCA dimensionality, but instead operates robustly across a continuum of representations. From a practical perspective, this reduces the need for careful tuning of preprocessing parameters, as reasonable performance can be achieved under a wide range of dimensionality choices.

Appendix D
Downstream tasks: anomaly detection

Experimental setup.

All methods are evaluated under a unified pipeline: inputs are standardized, embeddings are computed in 2D, and anomaly scores are obtained using kNN, LOF, Isolation Forest (IF), and centroid-based scoring. Results in the main text and Appendix follow the same evaluation pipeline but are reported on different datasets.
Both
Best
(detector-specific tuning) and
Fair (IF)
selection protocols are considered. The same setup is applied consistently across all datasets and methods to ensure a controlled and comparable evaluation.

Datasets

We evaluate on a combination of real-world and synthetic datasets designed to probe density-aware anomaly detection.
PBMC

[
29
]
is a single-cell RNA-seq dataset where rare cell populations are treated as anomalies; cells are normalized, log-transformed, reduced via PCA, and clustered using Leiden, with the smallest clusters defining anomalies.
MNIST

[
7
]
and
Fashion-MNIST

[
28
]
are used in a one-class anomaly detection setting, where a single class is treated as normal and all remaining classes are considered anomalous, providing high-dimensional benchmarks with semantic rather than density-based anomalies.
CIFAR-10

[
5
]
is included as a more complex natural image dataset, where we adopt the same one-class protocol, treating one class as inliers and the rest as anomalies, resulting in a challenging high-dimensional setting with less separable structure.
Shuttle

[
2
]
is a tabular dataset with a well-defined anomaly class, commonly used in anomaly detection benchmarks; it represents a density-driven regime where anomalies correspond to rare system states. Synthetic datasets explicitly control density variations. In the
density spiral dataset
, samples are generated along a spiral manifold
(x,y)=(t\cos t,t\sin t)
with sinusoidal density modulation. Points are resampled according to this density, perturbed with small Gaussian noise, and embedded into higher-dimensional space via random linear projections followed by standardization. Anomalies are defined as samples below a density percentile threshold.

Grid Search

All methods are evaluated in 2D with inputs standardized prior to embedding (after dataset-specific preprocessing) and identical anomaly scoring pipelines (kNN, LOF, Isolation Forest, centroid distance). Hyperparameters are selected via grid search over Cartesian products of method-specific parameter grids, and the same search protocol is applied across all experiments, including both main text and Appendix results. Results.

Table
7
summarizes anomaly detection performance across embeddings.

On
Fashion-MNIST
, DR-SNE is competitive but not top-performing in the Best setting. Methods such as DensMAP and UMAP achieve higher peak scores (e.g., UMAP for IF and LOF, DensMAP for kNN), indicating that strong cluster structure can favor geometry-driven embeddings. However, under the Fair (IF) protocol, DR-SNE maintains relatively stable performance across detectors, whereas several methods (notably UMAP and PaCMAP) exhibit substantial drops for kNN. This highlights a trade-off between peak performance and cross-detector robustness.

On
PBMC
, results are more heterogeneous. DR-SNE achieves the best centroid-based performance in both protocols and remains competitive for IF, but is outperformed in kNN and LOF by methods such as UMAP, PaCMAP, and DensMAP in the Best setting. Under the Fair protocol, DR-SNE retains stable IF and centroid performance, while some competing methods degrade significantly (e.g., DenSNE for centroid). Overall, no single method dominates across all detectors, suggesting that in biological data with mixed cluster and density structure, different inductive biases favor different detectors.

On the
synthetic density spiral
, where anomalies are explicitly defined by low-density regions, DR-SNE shows clear advantages for IF and centroid scoring, achieving the best results in both Best and Fair settings. The optimal configuration consistently selects strong density regularization (
\lambda=1.0
), confirming that explicit density alignment improves anomaly separability when density directly defines anomalies. In contrast, other density-aware methods (DenSNE, DensMAP) do not consistently translate density modeling into improved downstream performance, particularly under the Fair protocol.

The
HIGH-D
baseline performs strongly for specific detectors (notably kNN on the synthetic dataset and IF on Fashion-MNIST), indicating that the original space can already encode useful structure. However, its performance is inconsistent across detectors, reinforcing the need for embeddings that provide more uniform behavior. Across methods, a consistent pattern emerges: approaches such as t-SNE, UMAP, and PaCMAP often achieve strong results for individual detectors but exhibit high variability across scoring functions and sensitivity to the selection protocol. Density-aware methods (DenSNE, DensMAP) can reach high peak performance, but tend to be less stable under Fair selection. Overall, DR-SNE provides a balanced trade-off: while not always achieving the highest peak scores, it delivers consistently strong performance across detectors and protocols, particularly in settings where density plays a meaningful role.

Effect of density regularization.

We sweep
\lambda
while keeping other parameters fixed (Figure
7
) and evaluate AUPRC across detectors. Performance typically peaks at small but non-zero values (
\lambda\approx 10^{-4}-10^{-3}
), indicating that mild density regularization provides the best trade-off. However, on some datasets (e.g. Fashion-MNIST, Synthetic, Shuttle) the local maxima are achieved at large values (
\lambda\approx 1
), suggesting a second competitive regime where strong density alignment is beneficial. Which regime dominates appears to be dataset-dependent. The second local maximum indicates that, beyond moderate regularization, enforcing strong density alignment can again improve separability, likely by enhancing global structure at the expense of local flexibility. Appendix E
Time Complexity

The computational complexity of DR-SNE is dominated by the construction and normalization of the low-dimensional affinity matrix and the computation of pairwise distances. Computing the distance matrix
D_{ij}=\|z_{i}-z_{j}\|^{2}
requires
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
operations, and normalization of the affinity matrix involves summation over all pairs, resulting in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
complexity per iteration.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence and density regularization terms are evaluated over
k
-nearest neighbor graphs and scale as
\mathcal{O}(nk)
, and therefore do not dominate the overall complexity. As a result, DR-SNE has
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
time complexity per iteration, matching that of standard t-SNE.

Runtime as a function of the number of samples
n

\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
scaling (red dashed line). The shaded region indicates
\pm 1
standard deviation over 7 independent runs. All experiments were conducted with single-threaded execution to ensure consistent timing.

This scaling is confirmed empirically in Figure
8
, where runtime exhibits approximately quadratic growth with respect to the number of samples.

Importantly, this limitation is not specific to DR-SNE, but is inherited from the original t-SNE formulation. As shown in prior work, the complexity can be reduced to
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
using tree-based approximations such as Barnes–Hut t-SNE
[
23
]
, or to near-linear time using interpolation-based methods such as FIt-SNE
[
9
]
.

The density regularization term operates only on local neighborhoods and introduces an additional
\mathcal{O}(nk)
cost, which does not affect the asymptotic complexity. Since it does not alter the pairwise interaction structure in the embedding space, existing acceleration techniques can be applied directly. Thus, DR-SNE is fully compatible with scalable t-SNE implementations.

All runtime measurements were performed on a single CPU using single-threaded execution.

Appendix F
Problem Regimes and Evaluation Perspective

Dimensionality reduction methods are often evaluated using a fixed set of metrics across datasets, implicitly assuming that all datasets pose similar structural challenges. However, real-world data exhibit fundamentally different types of structure, and no single method can optimally preserve all of them simultaneously. In this section, we formalize a taxonomy of problem regimes and position DR-SNE within this landscape.

F.1
Problem Regimes

We identify four common regimes that capture the dominant structural properties encountered in practice:

(1) Clustered Structure.

Data consist of well-separated groups with relatively homogeneous internal density.
Examples include image classification datasets such as MNIST and Fashion-MNIST.
In this regime, the primary objective is to preserve neighborhood structure and cluster separation.

(2) Density-Driven Structure.

Data exhibit significant variation in sampling density, often without clear cluster boundaries.
Low-density regions often correspond to rare or potentially anomalous samples, although this relationship depends on the data-generating process. Examples include anomaly detection datasets such as Shuttle and Thyroid.

(3) Continuous Manifolds.

Data lie on smooth low-dimensional manifolds with continuous variation and no discrete clusters.
Examples include synthetic datasets such as Swiss roll or spiral structures.
Here, preserving global geometry is more important than density or clustering.

(4) Hybrid Structure.

Data combine cluster structure with meaningful density variation within and across clusters.
Examples include single-cell RNA-seq datasets (e.g., PBMC, Shuttle), where both discrete cell types and continuous transitions are present.

F.2
Evaluation Implications

These regimes impose different and often conflicting requirements on dimensionality reduction methods:

•

Clustered structure
favors methods that strongly emphasize local neighborhood preservation and cluster separation (e.g., t-SNE, UMAP).

•

Density-driven structure
requires methods that explicitly incorporate density information, either via local scale constraints or direct density alignment (e.g., DensMAP, DR-SNE).

•

Continuous manifolds
require methods that better preserve global geometry and long-range relationships (e.g., Isomap, TriMap, PaCMAP).

•

Hybrid regimes
require methods that jointly balance topology and density. Approaches that explicitly incorporate density information can be advantageous in this setting, although different methods may perform well depending on the dataset (e.g., DensMAP, DR-SNE).

As a result, standard metrics such as trustworthiness or silhouette score capture only partial aspects of embedding quality. In particular, they do not reflect how well the embedding preserves the underlying data distribution.

To address this limitation, we explicitly evaluated
density preservation
using correlation between log-density estimates in the original and embedding spaces. This metric directly measures alignment of marginal distributions, complementing existing topology-based metrics.

F.3
Positioning of DR-SNE

The proposed DR-SNE method is designed to explicitly preserve relative density structure, which is largely overlooked by existing dimensionality reduction approaches.

Strengths.

DR-SNE performs particularly well in:

•

Density-driven regimes
, where preserving low-density regions is critical (e.g., anomaly detection),

•

Hybrid regimes
, where both cluster structure and density variation are present.

Trade-offs.

These gains come at the cost of reduced performance in regimes where density is not the primary signal:

•

In purely clustered datasets, DR-SNE remains competitive but does not outperform methods optimized for cluster separation.

•

In continuous manifold settings, enforcing density consistency can distort global geometry.

Interpretation.

These observations are consistent with the theoretical analysis in Appendix
A
: density preservation corresponds to constraining local volume distortion, which inherently competes with geometric embedding objectives under dimensionality reduction.

F.4
Implications for Practice

These results suggest that dimensionality reduction should be treated as a
task-dependent
problem rather than a single objective.

•

For visualization and clustering, topology-preserving methods remain appropriate.

•

For statistical analysis, anomaly detection, and distributional interpretation, density-preserving embeddings provide a more faithful representation.

DR-SNE provides a simple mechanism to interpolate between these regimes via a single parameter, allowing practitioners to adapt the embedding to the structure most relevant for their application.
