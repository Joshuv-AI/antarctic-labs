Center for Bio-Inspired Energy Science (CBES)
 Final Report

The goal of the EFRC on Bio-Inspired Energy Science (CBES) has been to create and understand
materials and systems inspired by biology, seeking to innovate on energy utilization and its environmental
implications. In the most recent two-year funding cycle (2022–2024), we extended strategic projects from
each of the five tasks from the previous cycle. This work offers important advances in how soft matter
can be used to convert and store energy. We briefly summarize selected key accomplishments from each
task below.

Task 1: “Multi-Scale Synthesis of Artificial Muscles”
In the previous phase of CBES, we designed magnetoelastic hydrogel objects that actuate in response to
light. By embedding nickel nanowires in the hydrogels, the materials could “walk” in programmed paths
in response to an applied magnetic field in acidic water. Interestingly, we observed that adding
supramolecular nanofibers could enhance the photoactuation. During this funding cycle, we reported that
the high-aspect-ratio morphologies not only play a significant role in providing mechanical reinforcement
to the matrix but also enhance photoactuation for both light-driven volumetric contraction and expansion
of spiropyran hydrogels.1 Molecular dynamics simulations indicate that water within the high-aspect-ratio
supramolecular polymers exhibits a faster draining rate as compared to those in spherical micelles, which
suggests that the high-aspect-ratio supramolecular polymers effectively facilitate the transport of trapped
water molecules by functioning as channels and therefore enhancing actuation of the hybrid system.
More recently, we reported that adding ionizable acrylic acid (AA) co-monomers into the networks
enables photoactuation at nearly neutral pH. This, in turn, led to a new mechanism to accelerate the
response by triggering the bundling of supramolecular nanofibers by rapid proton exchange reactions.2
Inspired by natural pedicellariae, defensive organs present in echinoderms like starfish and sea urchins,
we also used the rapid response and large shape transformations to create hydrogels capable of
spontaneously tracking external light sources (positive phototaxis). We found that the hybrid bonding
hydrogel (covalent network plus supramolecular nanofibers) tracks light significantly faster
(approximately six times faster to reach the maximum bending angle) compared to the covalent network.
Based on this high-performing material, we also designed a hydrogel system that can swim toward a light
source (positive phototaxis).3 This hydrogel “swimmer” can autonomously sense and move toward a light
source, a phenomenon reminiscent of phototactic behavior found in nature. By combining experimental
techniques and theoretical modeling, we analyzed the observed swimming mechanism and the emergence
of phototactic properties. This work highlights how the synergistic interplay of covalent and
supramolecular networks in soft materials can be used to emulate the complex behaviors of living
systems.
We have also developed photo- and electro-activated hydrogel robots that can autonomously capture and
deliver cargo, avoid obstacles, and return without external, stepwise control. By applying two spiropyran
molecules with different substituents, photo-regulated charge reversal functionalities are obtained and
enable autonomous behaviors under a constant electric field. The photo- and electro-active soft robot can
autonomously perform complex tasks using constant external stimuli without human intervention, thus
providing new opportunities to develop intelligent soft robotics.4

Task 2: “Magnetic Morphogenesis”
CBES researchers have studied the equation of state that arises in a system of colloidal rotors driven by a
magnetic field on an inclined surface, which shows unusual behavior with changes in viscosity and
frequency of rotation. Stokesian dynamics is a well-established computational method for simulating
dynamics of many particles suspended in a conventional passive fluid medium. Active fluids composed of
self-driving particles with broken time-reversal symmetry permit the emergence of a so-called “odd
viscosity.” We have extended the conventional Stokesian dynamics formalism to incorporate the
additional hydrodynamic effects due to odd viscosity, which enables simulating collective behavior of
many particles suspended in an active fluid medium with both even viscosity and odd viscosity.5
The dynamic motion produced by precessing magnetic fields can drive matter into far-from-equilibrium
states. We predict 1D periodic ordering in systems of precessing rods when magnetic interactions
between rods remain insignificant. The precession angle of the rods is completely determined by the
field’s precession angle, the ratio of the field’s precession frequency, and the characteristic response
frequency of the rods. We have developed a molecular dynamics model that explicitly calculates
magnetic interactions between particles, and we also simulate rods in the limit of a strong and fast
precessing magnetic field where inter-rod magnetic interactions are negligible, using a purely steric
model.6 Our simulations show how steric interactions drive the rods from a positionally disordered phase
(nematic) to a layered (smectic) phase. As the rod precession angle increases, the nematic–smectic
transition density significantly decreases. The minimization of unfavorable steric interactions also induces
phase separation in binary mixtures of rods of different lengths. We propose that this effect is general for
any force that produces precession in elongated particles and this work therefore helps advance the
understanding and control of out-of-equilibrium soft matter systems.

Task 3: “Autonomous Soft Microrobots”
Growth in the complexity of biological systems typically involves the aggregation of basic structural units
into stable colonies and communication among the units to develop collective responses. The evolution of
early cells into multicellular organisms may have been facilitated by simple physicochemical processes in
solution, which simultaneously promoted aggregation and communication. CBES researchers modeled
such synergistic behavior in a system of synthetic microcapsules.7 Analogous to cells, the capsules
“communicate” with each other through the exchange of signaling chemicals and development of local
chemical gradients that drive fluid flows. The induced flows in turn can transport and assemble capsules
into large colonies. Demonstrating this interdependence between aggregation and communication
provides new insights into the role of the chemical networks in the evolution of early cellular assemblies
and yields new guidelines for designing synthetic active matter with lifelike properties.

In our previous CBES renewal proposal, we proposed a high-risk project to use DNA base pairing to store
energy that could be used to power small-scale devices. Based on this idea, we recently reported on an
approach that enhances both the speed and force of DNA-powered machines and artificial hinge
machines.8 A microheater, with millisecond response, heats or re-cools DNA origami constructs,
hybridizing or dehybridizing sticky ends. Because anything within 20 µm of the heater equilibrates to a
programmed temperature change in milliseconds, sticky ends of a compound DNA origami machine can
open and close synchronously and operate cooperatively, in phase, additively increasing the drive force
compared to single pair of sticky ends DNA machine (the six-helix bundle DNA origami hinge machine).
In these experiments, we fold and unfold two square origami with 10 pairs of complementary sticky ends
to drive a bead on the end of a rod like origami to speeds exceeding 30 µm/s. It may also prove useful for
experiments on machines using other temperature-sensitive active materials such as enzymes.
Furthermore, the system’s capacity for reversible and repeatable actuation under thermal control enables

broader application scenarios, including as a building block for soft microrobotic systems that perform
periodic mechanical tasks at the microscale or for active DNA-based materials that exhibit dynamic
structural transformations. While this project was not fully completed under CBES, the Center provided
initial support for preliminary experiments that were critical for development of the project.

Task 4 “Hierarchical Structure-Mediated Photocatalysis”
One of the key accomplishments from this funding period includes the study of large colloidal crystals
engineered with DNA. In brief, unprecedentedly large (> 100 µm) single crystalline colloidal crystals
engineered with DNA were synthesized and their actuatable and dynamic properties were investigated.9
We discovered that the DNA bonds in these crystals recover from severe deformation upon dehydration
and rehydration and are thus self-adapting. As a result, these crystals also maintain tunable optical
properties based on internal and external structure changes during deformation. These complicated
amorphous type-deformations and subsequent recovery were further explored using molecular dynamics
(MD) simulations of intact and deformed model crystals with flexible DNA bonds. This work showed
that the programmability of DNA bonding allows for the creation of hybrid shape memory materials
capable of recovering from large perturbations and accompanying shape and property changes.
In a related area of research, we also leveraged plasmonic nanoframes (NFs) and their tendency to
assemble on surfaces to prepare ultrathin broadband metasurface absorbers.10 Inspired by natural systems
such as butterfly wings, chameleon skin, and photosynthetic membranes, our materials are composed of
simple nanoscale building blocks autonomously organized at an interface into hierarchical, ordered
structures whose collective optical response are distinct from those of their individual building blocks.
Indeed, metasurface properties arise from the geometry, packing, and cooperative interactions within
them. In this study, a simple, bottom-up lithography-free approach allows for precise control over lattice
symmetry and packing density while remaining scalable over large areas. As a result, a single ultrathin
monolayer exhibits high, polarization-independent optical absorption that would not be achievable from
isolated nanostructures alone. Furthermore, systematically varying the shapes of assembled NFs revealed
that the arrangement and ordering of the assembled monolayers are dictated by nanoframe shape-
dependent steric hindrance and surfactant-induced depletion forces, which controlled whether edge-to-
edge or corner-to-edge contacts dominate during surface assembly.

Task 5: “Mechanical Enhancement of Photocatalysis”
Mechanical expansion and contraction of pores within photosynthetic organisms regulate a series of
processes that are necessary to manage light absorption, control gas exchange, and regulate water loss.
These pores, known as stoma, allow the plant to maximize photosynthetic output depending on
environmental conditions such as light intensity, humidity, and temperature by actively changing the size
of the stomal opening. Despite advances in artificial photosynthetic systems, little is known about the
effect of such mechanical actuation in synthetic materials where chemical reactions occur. We recently
discovered a novel self-assembled system that photocatalytically produces hydrogen peroxide without the
need for a metal catalyst.11 By integrating this supramolecular polymer into a temperature-responsive
poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) scaffold, we found that the photocatalytic reaction rates can be controlled
by changing temperature as an external stimulus. At room temperature, the hydrogel is swollen and the
photocatalysis is faster. Upon heating, the hybrid contracts and the photocatalysis slows down. With
heating and cooling cycles, we found that the photocatalysis rate increases with the actuation rates, due to
enhanced flow of water and solutes in and out of the hydrogel. The demonstration that actuation can
regulate and even enhance the photocatalytic rates provides a framework for designing future lifelike
artificial photosynthetic systems.

Building on the molecular design used above, we discovered a dissipative supramolecular system that
reversibly absorbs external energy under anaerobic conditions, causing the system to switch from small
aggregates of molecules to ribbon-shaped supramolecular polymers. In this work, the monomer was a
chromophore amphiphile based on aminonaphthalene conjugated to methyl viologen moieties that
absorbs energy from visible light, chemical fuels, an electrochemical bias, or x-rays to store an electron
per monomer molecule and trigger formation of a polymeric “pimer.” The supramolecular polymerization
is synergistically triggered by p-p stacking and p-radical pimerization, which stores electrons in the
assembly and releases them upon exposure to oxygen as reactive oxygen species (ROS). The ROS can
then oxidize an organic substrate in the absence of visible light, thus providing a model system for “dark
photocatalysis.” The fully reversible process converts a yellow solution of aggregates into a black
hydrogel, enabling transient microscale photopatterning which dissipates as electrons are released and de-
pimerization occurs. A manuscript based on this work is currently in revision and will be resubmitted
soon.12 The results of this task suggest interesting opportunities for the development of stimuli-responsive
materials that can enhance catalytic function to produce liquid fuels or other useful chemical products.
