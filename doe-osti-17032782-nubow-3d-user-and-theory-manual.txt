 Abstract

NUBOW-3D is a specialized Fortran computer code developed for the three-dimensional struc-
tural analysis of hexagonal ducted reactor core assemblies in liquid metal cooled fast reactors. It
predicts assembly bowing (displacements) and inter-assembly contact forces using a beam theory
based representation of reactor ducts, which are treated as the primary structural members of the
core. NUBOW-3D accounts for axial variations in duct geometry and material properties, multiple
assembly types within the same core, and loading effects arising from thermal expansion, thermal-
induced bowing, irradiation-induced creep and swelling, and interactions with restraint systems
such as rings or yokes.

 The code models reactor assemblies as discretized beam elements with uncoupled bending in
two orthogonal directions and evaluates contact at specified load points using linearized stiffness
representations for hexagonal load pads and circular supports. Because contact and gap closure
introduce strong nonlinearities, NUBOW-3D employs an iterative equilibrium solution strategy
using artificial soft springs and shim adjustments to obtain stable and converged displacement
and force solutions. There are also models for inelastic strain accumulation, pressure-induced
duct dilation, power ramping with temperature scaling, and time-history analysis for pseudo-static
operating transients.

 The primary purpose of NUBOW-3D is to support evaluation of core restraint system perfor-
mance and to generate assembly displacement fields for subsequent reactivity feedback calculations
associated with radial core expansion. This manual presents the theoretical basis, numerical for-
mulation, contact and inelastic strain models, solution procedures, input structure, and example
applications needed for accurate and informed use of the software in reactor core structural analysis.

1 Introduction

NUBOW-3D is a special purpose computer code developed in Fortran for the three-dimensional
structural analysis of reactor core systems to determine the bowing (displacement) and contact forces
for hexagonal ducted assemblies in a liquid metal-cooled fast reactors [1]. The code incorporates
a beam theory model for the out-of-plane bending of ducted reactor assemblies where the ducts
are the primary structural components of the assemblies. These ducts may have axial variations in
geometric properties and may include different types within the same system (e.g., fuel assemblies
may be different from control assemblies, shields, reflectors, etc.). Loading of the system can occur
from thermal expansion and bowing (generated from temperature data provided as input), inelastic
bowing of ducts caused by irradiation-induced creep and swelling (calculated using the flux and
temperature data provided as input to material property correlations), and displacement/contact
from restraint rings or yokes as the outer boundary constraints for the system.

 The purpose of NUBOW-3D is to calculate the deflections (bowing) and contact forces between
neighboring contacting ducts in order to evaluate the core restraint system design and to provide
displacements for follow-on reactivity feedback calculation of core radial expansion due to assembly
duct bowing. The outputs can be independently reviewed and assessed with the option of generating
displacement files that can be used for reactivity change calculations. For an example of this
implementation, see the PERSENT manual [2].

 The primary goal of this manual is to educate the reader on the theory behind NUBOW-3D
so that accurate analysis results can be obtained when using the NUBOW-3D software to model
reactor problems.

 This manual has 10 major sections. Section 3 describes the bending model and duct displacement
assumptions, starting from deriving the beam bending theory, beam element and stiffness matrix
formulation, and the duct discretization model and inputs. Section 4 describes the basic contact
model for load pads and support points along with the duct clearance definition and assumptions.
Section 5 describes the iterative solver for the force equilibrium and displacement model. Section 6
presents the inelastic strain model for creep and swelling displacement induced by flux gradients
as well as pressure-induced duct dilation. Section 7 discusses the power ramping and temperature
scaling feature which can be useful for quickly evaluating ramped power conditions or standby state
at refueling conditions. Section 8 discusses the time history analysis feature and necessary inputs
and calculation steps for force equilibrium, inelastic strain calculation, and power ramping. Section
9 presents the input structure for the code. Finally, Section 10 presents an example problem with a
brief description of the output results.

2 Reactor Overview

This section describes the overview for the reactor assembly model that NUBOW-3D assumes to
determine the duct displacements and contact forces. The assumed model is a hexagonal array of
hexagonal ducted assemblies, shown in Figure 2.1, with the ducts oriented with the flat face of the
hex-ducts facing in the positive global Y -axis. The ducts are assumed to have a uniform centerline
spacing but can vary in size axially and in the radial plane. NUBOW-3D was originally designed for
30◦ symmetric models and full core models were added later. Symmetric models have a restricted
duct number scheme, whereas full core layouts follow a standard counter-clockwise hexagonal ring
pattern where no ducts are skipped in the input stream (ducts can be treated as not-present, but
all ducts in a ring are required for input).

 For 30◦ symmetric sectors, a structured numbering system is assumed to correctly account for
symmetry boundaries. This numbering system, which is depicted in Figure 2.2 for a 7-ring sector,
starts with duct number 1 at the core center and ducts are numbered consecutively outward on
the global Y -axis to the outermost ring. Thus, for a 7-ring reactor, ducts 1 through 7 should all
be located in the first column as shown. Numbering continues consecutively, starting at the 30◦
symmetry line and proceeds outward, parallel to the global Y -axis as depicted.

 To identify neighboring ducts for contact detection and contact force calculation, the duct
neighbors or adjacency needs to be defined. Each duct must be assigned an adjacent duct (or
neighbor) for each of its six faces. The adjacency is comprised of a formatted input file which
contains a column of each duct number and 6 columns for each of the duct faces with the duct
number adjacent to that face, orientated with Face 1 at the top of the duct, and counting around
the duct clockwise, as shown in Figure 2.3. An example of the layout of the adjacency file is shown
in Table 2.1. For ducts along the core periphery which neighbor the restraint ring, a duct value of
2000 is assumed to represent the restraint. Ducts along the symmetry line are adjacent to ducts
which are symmetric reflections of ducts within the sector. These symmetry conditions are identified
by “phantom” ducts with numbers set to the negative of the ducts within the sector. Thus, the 6
adjacent ducts to duct number 4 in Figure 2.2 are 5, 10, 9, 3, -9, and -10 are shown in Table 2.1.

 To account for the fact that the outer ring of ducts may not be full, such as corner ducts
removed to make the hexagonal array more “circular”, ducts can be excluded from the neighbors list
to be skipped over when counting through the ducts. Generally these ducts will then be replaced in
the adjacency file with an ID of 2000 to represent them as part of the restraint. However, the full list
of ducts must remain as they are simply skipped over while solving for displacements and contact
forces, but they remain in the list of counted ducts. These ducts are defined in the adjacency file as
having all faces adjacent to the restraint, or all faces assigned a value of 2000 so they can be added
to the exclusion list.

3 Bending Model and Duct Displacements

3.1 Elastic Beam Bending Theory

To find the assembly deformations in the reactor, each assembly is treated as an elastic beam, with
the axial length direction oriented along the z−axis, and subjected to bending displacements in the
uncoupled x- and y-axis directions with a thin-walled hexagonal duct cross-section. The equations
which govern the elastic beam bending deformation are treated from mechanics of deformable
solids and are presented from [3]. To derive the displacement relationships, the most common case
of a straight beam elastically deformed with loads perpendicular to the cross-section is used, as
shown in Figure 3.1. In general, the deformation of a beam would be cause by internal shear forces
and bending moments, but when the length is much larger than the cross-sectional depth, the
deformation is dominated by bending and the shear forces can be ignored.

3.2 Beam Element Definition

The theory presented in Section 3.1 applies for a prismatic beam, which has constant cross-sectional
parameters and material properties along the entire length. To mathematically treat the deformation
for beams with axially varying cross-sections and variable material properties (as a function of
axially varying temperature), each duct is broken up (discretized) into multiple elements and the
formulation is applied to each individual element. This method to treat the individual elements of a
beam and formulate the matrix approach to solving for displacements is call the Stiffness Method
and is duplicated from [4] with various other derivation forms found in [5], [6], and [7]. An element
of a beam is defined as a section of the beam identified by two nodes (or vertices), one at either
end of the element.The beam is assumed to have a constant cross-section and material property
definition between the two nodes. This definition is shown in Figure 3.3, showing a single beam
element, with generalized displacements at the end nodes made up of the end displacements (vi and
vj ) and the end rotations (θi and θj ). The corresponding generalized forces are (pxi , pxj , mi , and
mj ).

3.3 Duct Discretization

The Stiffness Method formulation described in Section 3.2 is used and applied to the ducts in the
reactor model by discretization of each duct into multiple axial elements to predict the displacements
and contact forces under multiple operating conditions. Each duct is treated as a beam element,
oritented vertically with the beam z-axis pointed upward and the xy-plane in the direction of the
radial plane as shown in Figure 3.4 with the duct flat-to-flat direction pointed in the y-axis direction.

 Assemblies (ducts) are assumed to be beams, subdivided at specified axial locations into a
number of uniform axial elements which may have different geometric properties. Elements are
assumed to have cross-sections that are hexagonal for the ducted regions or circular for the inlet
nozzle at the grid plate support location. A stiffness matrix is generated for each beam element
using input data for the element lengths and cross-sectional geometry and material properties.
These matrices, discussed below, relate the bending (elastic) displacements of beam centerlines, at

specific axial points (load points), and the forces acting on the beams at these points.

 Individual ducts are subdivided into a number of axial segments at points termed geometric
nodes as shown in Figure 3.5. These nodes may be anywhere along the duct, and define axial
locations of geometric changes, contact points, and other points of interest. Different ducts may
have different numbers of nodes at different axial locations, with the axial coordinates specified
for each node. A sub-set of these nodes is identified as a set of load points. Load points are the

number at the same axial coordinates for all ducts. The load pads are the designed contact locations
at various axial positions along the ducts within SFR cores which are meant to control the contact
and bowing behavior of the core. Usually there is one at or near the top of the duct, called the Top

3.4 Duct Displacement

The displacement of each duct is defined in terms of the position of the duct centerline relative to
its local coordinate axis system located at the thermally expanded pitch center. The convention for
displacement is shown in Figure 3.7 where the local xyz coordinate system is oriented parallel with
the global XY Z coordinate system. The duct displacement vector v(z) is the displacement of a
point on the duct centerline measured in a plane parallel to the XY plane.

 The vector displacement of the duct centerline v(z) is described in terms of two components,
vx (z) and vy (z), which are calculated at the axial node locations described in Section 3.3. Relative
to this local axis system, the displacements vx and vy are the total beam displacement, excluding
only the initial rigid body translation resulting from grid plate thermal expansion. Thus, a zero

displacement for a point would mean that at that axial location, the duct centerline coincided with
the vertical z-axis through the expanded pitch center. Non-zero displacements include the effects of
mechanical bending, bowing due to thermal gradients, inelastic strains (creep and swelling), and
shifting and tilting (rigid body translation and rotation). These latter effects can occur because
of clearances which can exist at all axial locations. Thus, the final position of a duct will include
bending and bowing with respect to a line in the beam, and any rigid body movement that occurs
before a determinate loading position is achieved, as shown in Figure 3.8. To elaborate this point,
consider the case of two originally loose beams which begin to bow into contact, as in a power ramp.
As the beam contacts, it rotates and/or translates in opposite directions with zero contact force,
until both beams have moved to positions where at least three contacts have been established for
each beam, so that both the contact and restraint forces can begin to grow.Then any additional
bowing would cause the forces to increase and cause beam bending. In this respect, it is significant
to note that for some actual reactor systems, thermal expansion and bowing effects may not be
sufficient to load all ducts into a determinate loading position. Such ducts can remain free, or
be loaded at one or more axial locations with self-equilibrating forces. For such situations, the
position of the duct is indeterminate, however they do not present any major complications within
NUBOW-3D.

are a subset of nodes defined from the full list geometric node points along each duct. Presently,

5 Iterative Solver

The presence of gaps at contact points results in the force equilibrium equations to be non-linear
with respect to displacement and a direct solution is not practical. Thus, the equilibrium solution
for forces and displacements is obtained via an iterative process. The iterative process starts with
an assumed displacement state for all load points. For this assumed displacement state, the beam
shear forces FB are evaluated along with the contact forces described in Section 4. They are shown
in Figure 5.1 for a typical hexagonal load point, where it should be noted that the contact forces
satisfy the gap criterion that tensile forces are set to zero.

 Figure 5.1: Contact Force and Residual Force

 If the forces equilibrate at all load points for the assumed displacement state, then this would
be an equilibrium solution for the system. For a general displacement, these forces would not
equilibrate but leave a residual force R, as shown in Figure 5.1, where the individual contact forces
F1 . . . F6 have been replaced by their vector resultant FC . For the entire system, these residual
forces can be expressed in vector form as

where the size of each force vector is two times the number of load points to account for the
decoupled forces in x- and y-axis directions. The beam shear forces can be written as

 This direct solution is not applicable for an actual system because the stiffness matrix K
and gap vector ∆ are displacement dependent. Therefore, a basic iteration procedure for solving
Eq. (5.5) would be to evaluate the components of the residual force for an assumed displacement
state, and then adjust the displacements to reduce the residuals until they all approach zero. In
doing so, it would be necessary to evaluate the individual components of contact force for each
displacement state so that the appropriate values for the elements of K and ∆ would occur in
Eq. (5.5).

 For a typical reactor system, the preceeding has two major problems: 1) contact stiffness
coefficients in K may be three to four orders of magnitude greater than elements of KB thereby
yielding an ill-conditioned system that requires small increments in displacement to maintain
mathematical stability, 2) the occurrence of gap closures at points with large contact stiffness can
result in very large changes in the components of the residual force which would yield unrealistically
large components of displacement.

 To overcome these difficulties, a two-level iterative procedure was developed in which the actual

 26

(stiff) springs are replaced by a set of artificial (soft) springs and a set of artificial constraints. This
soft system is well-conditioned such that gap closures do not result in gross perturbations of the
system. In physical terms, this process consists of replacing the actual system of stiff springs by
an artificial system with soft springs and a set of “shims” which act at those points where the
stiff springs have been replaced. After a number of iterations are performed for the soft system to
establish trends for displacements and the contact state, the sizes of these artificial “shims” are
adjusted to reduce the relative displacements at the artificially soft contacts. Calculating the size of
these shims from the magnitude of the contact forces and the actual and artificial spring stiffnesses
allows this scheme to converge to an equilibrium state in which the beam displacements and forces
are correct. The forces and displacements for the soft system with shims are the same as those for
the real system without shims. This approach avoids a rigorous contact search algorithm as the
differential axial expansion between adjacent heated assemblies is assumed to be small, so the axial
positions of load points do not change relative to nearby assemblies.

6 Inelastic Strain Calculation

Inelastic creep and swelling strains are calculated for each duct at each timepoint in the problem.
These strains are calculated from input material property correlations and are integrated over
each beam to give an inelastic “bowed” shape. These bowed shapes are used in the subsequent
equilibrium calculation to find duct forces and displacements.

 In modeling this inelastic behavior, the ducts are considered to act like beams, with basic
beam-theory assumptions that the beam deforms with plane cross-sections remaining plane, and

their treatment of thermal stress. A somewhat different development of this beam theory model is
presented here for completeness, and as an aid in interpreting the calculated results.

 Equation 6.25 has been developed by making use of only the conventional beam theory

beam or the mechanism for developing inelastic strains. Likewise, no assumptions have been made
concerning the magnitude or presence of other components of strain, except for the assumption that
plane cross-sections remain plane before and after deformation.

 It is also significant to note that because of the assumption that plane sections remain plane
before and after deformation, stresses due to inelastic strains result only from the non-linearity of
the strain distribution. That is, if the inelastic strain is broken into parts which include a constant
term, terms linear in x and y, and all non-linear terms, then the constant and linear parts will
cancel in Eq. (6.25) after performing the integrations. This fact is significant in interpreting the
stress results. In addition, it should be noted that in general, since the elastic strain distribution
will be non-linear, the stress will not be zero at the centroid of the cross-section and will not be
zero on the neutral line MN in Figure 6.2 where the total strain is zero. It should also be noted
that the development, even for the case of symmetric cross-sections (Ixy = 0), is not equivalent to
the superposition of results obtained by treating beam bending in the xz- and yz-planes separately.
This can be illustrated by considering the curvatures give in Eqs. (6.21) and (6.22) with Ixy = 0. In
Eq. (6.21), the y variation in εi must be included in evaluating the integral terms. Thus, y variations
in the inelastic strain effect the curvature in the xz-plane, and treatment of these effects separately
is not valid for a three dimensional case.

6.1 Duct Strain Cell Discretization

Individual ducts are assumed to be subdivided by the axial nodes into to As discussed below, these
strain nodes can be distributed differently for different ducts in the system, which are characterized
as different strain types in a manner analogous to the definitions of geometric nodes and geometric
types, and thermal modes and thermal types.

 At each axial strain node, the cross-section is subdivided into a number of equal area strain
“cells” uniformly distributed over all duct faces, as shown in Figure 6.3, so that stress and strain
distributions over the cross-section can be evaluated. The strain calculation model assumes the duct
geometry to be a thin-walled, regular hexagonal structure and segments the entire cross-sectional
area into a number of equal width and length rectangular cells with evenly distributed centroids
along each duct-wall face. This imposes a small a error of overlapping regions at the duct corners,
which remains small for thin-walled structures, and decreases further with larger amounts of cells
per face.

 At each cell, values for the local temperature, flux, fluence, creep strain, and swelling strain
are calculated at the cell centroids. For cell centroids which fall between the input points for
temperature and flux, the values are linearly interpolated to the cell centroid and assumed constant
over each cell. The creep and swelling strains in each cell are used to evaluate the stress at the
cell using Eq. (6.25). This cell stress, along with the local temperature, flux, fluence, etc., is used
to calculate the time incremental changes in the creep and swelling strains for the cell with the
material correlations for each cell.

6.3 Duct Dilation Model

NUBOW-3D calculates the duct dilation, or bulging, resulting from irradiation-enhanced creep and
material swelling. The duct dilation in the core region can cause duct-to-duct contact where it
otherwise would not occur, or change clearance gaps at load points where creep and swelling is
significant. To capture this effect, the clearance gaps at user-specified load points are recalculated
to account for changes in duct geometry. These changes include increases in duct dimensions due
to material swelling and creep relaxation of stresses induced by internal fluid pressure, as well as

when present.

7 Power Ramping and Temperature Scaling

One useful feature for evaluating transients in a pseudo-static scenario, without having to re-
calculate new temperature inputs, NUBOW-3D can perform a power scale calculation to core-wide
temperature values at specific time points. The ramps in power or flow are considered as pseudo-
static transients which occur over a zero-time duration (in terms of full-power reactor days), but
are considered sufficiently slow to keep the system in static equilibrium. This approach does not
account for changing in interassembly heat transfer which may occur due to the localized changes in
power.

 For any given time step, a power ramp calculation can be specified by the user which calculates
the equilibrium forces and displacements for the system using input values for a sequence of different
relative power values. Relative power is defined to mean the percentage of full power which can be
any real value greater than or equal to 0. To specify power ramps, the user must supply the values
and times at which they occur as pairs, first indicating the power fraction coefficient, and the time
(in full power reactor days) at which it occurs. The number of ramps is presently limited to six and
the number of points per ramp is limited to five. Two or more ramps may occur at the same time
to provide more than five steps, if needed.

 During execution, a calculation plan is created automatically based on the time points that are
included. If needed, a strain increment calculation is inserted before a power ramp to update the
inelastic bowed shapes.

 To perform power ramp calculations, the provided temperature data at a give time point is
simply scaled. This scaling is done in a bi-linear scheme as a function of the reactor power and
flow, and four parameters which specify the reactor inlet conditions during a power/flow-ramp. The
model assumes a variable power-to-flow ratio from a zero power level to some relative power level
Xp , which is less than or equal to unity. Above Xp it is assumed that the power to flow ratio is
unity. Any given input value for temperature is scaled by the relation:

8 Time Incrementing

NUBOW-3D can perform a time history analysis made up of combinations of three different types
of pseudo-static calculations at user-specified time points including: (1) solve the static equilibrium
forces and displacements (procedure detailed in Section 5), (2) solve the domain inelastic strain
(prodecure detailed in Section 6), and (3) power (or flow) ramp calculations (procedure detailed
in Section 7). The sequence in which these calculations are performed is determined from three
time point inputs: EVAL FORCE, EVAL STRAIN, and POWER RAMP. These time points are
provided by the user as input and can include any list of real values greater than or equal to zero,
in full power days, listed in monotonically increasing order.

 These input times are used to determine a plan of times step calculations, which are printed as
output and are identified by a calculation step number to reference the progress of the calculations.
The calculation steps follow a specific order at each time point. First, an equilibrium calculation
is performed at a given time point to determine the duct deflections and hex face contact points.
Second, a strain increment calculation over a given time interval is performed to determine the
change in deflection and contact force due to inelastic effects of irradiation swelling, creep, and duct
dilation due to pressure. Finally, a power ramp calculation is performed based on the previous
equilibrium or strain calculation, and the results are scaled based on the temperature profile change.
Although the input times for these different types of calculations can be defined independently, the
actual steps required are not independent, as discussed below, and the necessary steps to perform
consistent calculations are automatically included when developing the calculation step procedure.

 To perform a force equilibrium calculation at any time point after the initial time, as discussed
in Section 5, the bowed shapes of the assemblies at that time must be recalculated to account for
incremental strain due to swelling and creep. Thus, a strain calculation step must be performed
for the time interval beginning at the time point of the previoius strain calculation and ending at
the requested time point of the strain calculation. Because the time points in the three inputs
(EVAL FORCE, EVAL STRAIN, and POWER RAMP) can be defined independently, if a force
calculation or power ramp step is specified at a time point at which a strain calculation step is
not, the strain step is automatically included to generate the required bowed shapes before force
equilibrium. Similarly, equivalent steps are deleted. For example, if a force equilibrium step is
specified followed by a power ramp starting at full-power, the force calculations are not repeated,
and the redundant step in the ramp will be eliminated.

 For power ramp calculations, it is also necessary to specify the ratio of the power to flow level
for each ramp step and the time point at which to apply the ramp, as discussed in Section 7.

 Starting at the specified initial simulation time, the specified times are used to calculate results
for solution steps. A typical problem run could be to perform a power ramp or full-power equilibrium
calculation at the first timepoint and then a sequence of strain increment calculations and full-
power equilibrium calculations at later times EVAL STRAIN and EVAL FORCE. It is possible to
specify repeated strain increment calculations without calculating new equilibrium positions at the
intermediate times. This can be accomplished by specifying EVAL STRAIN time points which fall
between time points for EVAL FORCE. In such cases, the existing values for displacements at the
contact points are used and the beam bending forces are updated to account for the changing bowed

 45

shapes. These updated beam bending forces are then used to calculate bending stresses, which
are used in incrementing the inelastic strains. In this analysis approach, the inelastic strains are
updated assuming fixed displacement boundary conditions for the beams. This process is repeated
for each successive value of EVAL STRAIN, until the time reaches the next value specified for
EVAL FORCE or POWER RAMP, at which point new equilibrium forces and displacements will
be calculated. The calculation step progression is detailed in the flowchart in Figure 8.1.

9 Input Structure

The NUBOW-3D input is keyword based and processed via a free format input processor. The basic
restriction of that input processor is that the line length is less than 1024 characters. For comment
lines or comments in lines, the user may use ‘!’ or ‘#’ characters. All of the NUBOW-3D keyword
input is case insensitive. The keywords NUBOW-3D presently recognizes are listed in Table 9.1
through Table 9.4 along with descriptions and examples of those inputs.

 Table 9.1 provides list of keywords to specify the main inputs for the model setup and input files.
The INPUT NAME keyword is for the name of the model simulation for identification in the main
output file. The INPUT GEOM keyword specifies the file path and name for the duct layout and
adjacency file, which details the locations of each of the ducts by associating them as a neighbor (or
adjacent to) a duct and its face. The TIME DAYS keyword specifies the list of time points in days
for each of the temperature and fast flux input files, for use in evaluating time history deflections
and contact forces with multiple sets of inputs, usually beginning of equilibrium cycle (BOEC)
and end of equilibrium cycle (EOEC). The keywords INPUT TEMPS and INPUT FASTFLUX are
associated with the list of input files for temperature and fast flux, respectively, which correspond
to the time points in TIME DAYS.

 Table 9.2 provides the list of keywords to specify the inputs associated for the reactor description
setup. The AXIAL OFFSET keyword is used to provide an offset value to the NUBOW-3D model
for the temperature and fast flux data which comes from upstream software results that do not model
the entire duct or nozzle region; the value would be subtracted from temperature and fast flux axial
point assignment to line the values up with the NUBOW-3D axial mesh. The ASSEMBLY PITCH
keyword is to define the duct-to-duct centerline space. The keyword FLUX USAGE is a relic of
an older fast flux assignment method, which determines the fast flux assignment and the axial
and azimuthal distribution; a value of 3 is the default to assume individual ducts have their own
assignment values and are linearly interpolated between axial nodes. The FAB TEMP keyword is
used to assign the fabrication temperature, or the initial temperature, of the model, and should be
different from full power operating temperature. The INLET ZERO keyword is used to assign the
inlet temperature of the reactor core at zero power condition, while the INLET TEMP keyword is
used to assign the inlet temperature at hot full power operating condition. The difference of these
values is used to scale reactor temperatures for use in evaluating the duct shapes and residual contact
for zero power condition at EOEC for estimating withdrawal forces. The keywords INLET BREAK
and BREAK POINT are used for defining the inlet temperature at a specific full flow breakpoint,
usually set to 1.0, for scaling of temperatures during a power ramp calculation. The GRID TEMP
keyword is for assigning the grid plate temperature at full power condition. The GRID MAT
keyword is used to assign the material to the grid support structure. The GRID STIFFNESS
keyword is used to assign a value of the stiffness of the grid plate for use in calculating contact of
the nozzle with the grid support receptacle. The keyword MEAN DUCT TEMP is used to assign a
mean value for the ducts at full power operating temperature for use in material model evaluation
that fall outside of temperature range for the correlation. The keywords PRESSURE INLET and
PRESSURE OUTLET are used to define the pressure drop across the assembly from the inlet
point to the top load pad to determine the internal fluid pressure at the contact points for the
calculation of the duct dilation during irradiation creep. The keywords COMPL PRESSURE and

 48

COMPL CONTACT are used to assign the creep compliance factors for determining the duct wall
pressure and contact force change in duct wall dimensions due to irradiation creep.

 Table 9.3 provides the list of keywords used to specify the axial mesh and time point calculation
steps. The AXIAL MESH keyword is used to describe the element meshing for the geometric nodes
of all ducts in the model. Every duct will be assigned the same axial mesh, which must be a list
of real numbers, and include the locations for any geometric cross-section change and the contact
points and is limited to a total number of 47 nodes. The EVAL FORCE keyword assigns the time
points for each of the equilibrium calculation steps to evaluate the equilibrium contact forces and
duct displacements, the EVAL STRAIN keyword assigns the time points for each of the inelastic
strain calculation steps to evaluate the irradiation induced creep and swelling effects on the duct
displacements, and the POWER RAMP assigns the value of the power ramp to calculate a ramped
equilibrium force and displacement step. The force and strain lists are simply lists of the time
points, while the ramp list is a pair of values including the time point to apply the ramp and the
ramp value associated with the time.

 Table 9.4 provides the list of keywords used to define the duct geometry for each of up to
five different geometry types. For all of the keywords in this list, except RESTRAINT RING, the
first item specified is the name of the duct type associated with the geometric features, which is
presently limited to five unique identifiers. While only one duct type is required to assign values for
an entire calculation model, if multiple names are specified, every keyword must have a definition
for that name. The DUCT SIZE keyword is used to assign the duct flat-to-flat dimensions and
duct wall thickness to a given duct type, along with the axial range for that geometry definition
from the lower axial point to the higher axial point and the material property identifier. The
DUCT NOZZLE keyword is used to assign the inlet nozzle support points to the lower portion of
the assembly, along with the contact stiffness value and the lower and upper nozzle-to-receptacle
clearance values. The DUCT LOADPAD keyword is used to assign a load pad dimension to an
existing axial node defined in AXIAL MESH. This identifies the node as a contact point and assigns
the geometry associated with the load pad dimension such as the thickness of the pad on top of
the duct and the contact stiffness value, for contact point clearance and force calculation. The
DUCT INERTIA keyword assigns the bending stiffness value to portions of the duct specified by a
lower and upper axial value. The DUCT TCELLS keyword is used to specify the number of strain
cells across each of the geometric nodes for a given duct type for use in calculating the irradiation
creep and swelling strains. The DUCT ASSIGN keyword is used to assign each of the duct types
named in the previous keywords to each of the ducts in a list. The method used to assign the
ducts is to give the name of the duct type and the list of all the ducts to assign to that type. The
RESTRAINT RING keyword is used to assign the gap and the dimension values for each of the
restraint rings, associated with each of the contact points defined by DUCT LOADPAD. To define
a contact point without a restraint ring, the convention would be to assign a large gap value so that
contact does no initiate during the solve.

 Table 9.5 provides the list of keywords used to define the solver criteria used for the contact
force iteration solve and the output file description. The MAXF RESIDUAL keyword is used to
define the maximum residual force criteria for the hard and soft contact convergence iteration solves.
The SOFT STIFFNESS keyword allows the user to assign the soft problem stiffness values for the
beam bending and contact force. The SIM START STOP allows the user to specify the starting

 49

calculation step number and the ending calculation step number. The starting value can be any
number greater than 1 but less than the total number of calculation steps, which allows the solution
to start from an intermediate calculation result. The ending step, for a full analysis, would be
the summation of all the steps defined in EVAL FORCE, EVAL STRAIN, and POWER RAMP.
The PREFIX VTK allows the user to specify the file prefix identifier for the vtk output files used
for visualization of the output data. The outputs will be generated for each equilibrium time
step and will contain “prefix Displ 000x.vtk” and “prefix Force 000x.vtk” files for outputting the
displacement and contact force plots.

10 Example Problem

The output from NUBOW-3D is rather extensive with many individual output tables which get
repeated for each time point calculation for force equilibrium, inelastic strain, and if specified, power
ramps. To display the connection between input and the various outputs, this section will focus
on a single test case which is detailed below. This problem is derived from the fourth verification
problem (VP4) in [11] which corresponds to Test 7 included with the NUBOW-3D distribution. The
verification report specifies a full core, 7-ring reactor, with a ring of assemblies outside the active
core region bowing outwards due to an induced thermal gradient. The full core model is shown in
Figure 10.1 with the black ring around the reactor representing the restraint ring at the ACLP and
TLP elevations along the duct, with a load pad to restraint ring clearance value of 0.5 mm. The
blue arrows indicate the direction of assumed free-bowing based on the thermal gradients applied to
the ducts, which is shown in Figure 10.2. The ducts in the ring are separated into 3 heated regions:
(1) below the active core, (2) the active core, and (3) above the active core. In the region below
the active core, all the assemblies are heated to 400 ◦ C. In the region above the active core, the
corner of the ducts facing the center of the rector model are heated to 550 ◦ C while the opposite
corners are heated to 500 ◦ C, with a linearly varying temperature across the face. In the active core
region, the temperatures develop along the axial direction linearly, from 400 ◦ C to either 550 ◦ C
or 500 ◦ C, with a linear radial gradient. All other assemblies outside this ring are heated only to
400 ◦ C along their entire axial height. The assembly model geometric parameters defined at 20 ◦ C
are shown in Table 10.1; column 1 denotes the assembly item (i.e. duct, load pad, etc.), column 2
defines the specific geometric parameter, column 3 gives the value assigned to the parameter as
defined by the reference report in mm, and column 4 provides the values converted to in. Because
NUBOW-3D expects all input units to be in lbs, in, and ◦ F, multiple decimal values are carried so
the unit conversion can precisely reflect the original input values.

 After successful completion of the program, a number of output tables are prepared for the
user to view both processed input values and outputs for displacements and contact forces. The
main output starts with the system description showing the mapping of the geometry and adjacency
(neighbors) for all the ducts, as shown in an excerpt in Figure 10.8. The next table, shown in
Figures 10.9 & 10.10, details the mapped temperatures from the thermal node input to the geometry
and strain node locations, interpolating the values where necessary and converting the units from
◦ C to ◦ F. Column 1 gives the duct number, column 2 gives the axial position (axial node number

counted from the bottom of the duct), and column 3 gives the axial position (in). Columns 4 through
9 give the temperatures at each of the duct corners, starting from the top left and counting clockwise
around the duct, as described in Section 3.5. As shown, at duct 1, all the values are constant at
752◦ F (400◦ C), while at duct 43, the values are changing based on the mapped temperatures being
interpolated to the geometry nodes. It also provides a value for the duct flat-to-flat (Diameter)
and the duct wall thickness (Wall). A table is prepared from each input file for each of the time

 65

steps specified in the input. The same information is also provided for the fast flux data, but is not
used in this example. Figure 10.11 is an excerpt of the main output which shows the details of the
calculations steps defined by the user, giving the total number of calculation steps, and a detail of
each calculation per step at the time (in days) at which it occurs. In this example, Step 1 is a force
equilibrium calculation at 0 days (full power), and Step 2 is a Power Ramp calculation at 0 days
with a power fraction of 0. Figure 10.12 is an excerpt of the main output which shows the initial
free thermal bow values, the initial inelastic bowed shapes, and the initial clearances at the start of
the calculation step for each of the contact points, with all displacement and gap values in units of
inches. Columns 1 and 2 denote the duct number and contact point index, respectively, counting
upward axially from the lower point on the nozzle as the first contact point. Thus, levels 1 and
2 are the nozzle lower and upper points, respectively, and levels 3 and 4 are the ACLP and TLP
points, respectively. Columns 3 and 4 give the value of the free thermal bow displacement, without
contact interference. Columns 5 and 6 give the inelastic displacement value. Columns 7 through 12
give the values of the clearance between the nozzle and receptacle, adjacent ducts, and the restraint
ring, based on the adjacency information provided (and also output in Figure 10.8). The numbering
is given by DEL(1), through DEL(6), where DEL(1) is the gap at Face 1 and DEL(6) is the gap at
Face 6. This output table is repeated for every force equilibrium and inelastic strain step, where
the inelastic strain values and clearances are adjusted to account for irradiation creep and swelling,
and the thermal bowed shapes are adjusted for different input temepratures. An excerpt of the
converged contact solution for the force equilibrium step is presented in Figure 10.13. The table
gives the values of the contact forces for each face of each duct’s contact points (lb), the total bowed
deformation values (in), and the force residual. Columns 1 and 2 denote the duct number and
contact point index, respectively. Columns 3 through 8 give the values of the contact force at each of
the faces, numbered F1 (face 1) through F6 (face 6). Columns 9 and 10 give the total displacement
of the duct at the contact point, including contributions from all restraints, duct-to-duct contact,
and inelastic effects, with VX representing the x-axis displacement and VY representing the y-axis
displacement. Columns 11 and 12 give the nodal force residual value for the x- and y-axis direction,
respectively. The final main output is a table showing the full axial deformation results for each duct
at every time step, with an excerpt shown in Figure 10.14, with all displacement presented with
units in inches. The header for each table includes the calculation step number, the time (in days)
at which the step takes place, and the power ramping fraction. Column 1 gives the duct number,
column gives the axial node index, and column 3 gives the axial position (in inches). Columns 4 and
5 provide the x- and y-axis total restrained displacement, respectively. Columns 6 and 7 provide
the x- and y-axis inelastic contributions to the displacement.
