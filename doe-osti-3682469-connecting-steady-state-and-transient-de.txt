 ABSTRACT
 Loss of coolant accident testing of zirconium alloy fuel cladding utilize a rupture temperature –
cladding stress correlation, such as NuReG-0630 to determine failure during postulated accident
scenarios. While aspects of such correlations are physics-based, the developed trends are empirical in
nature. Cladding deformation and eventual rupture trends are often linked to creep behavior of the
material. In this work, a systematic evaluation of rupture tests was performed on Zirclaoy-4 fuel cladding
within and above the hoop stress ranges typically evaluated for light water reactor applications. Hoop
direction creep tests were performed in similar temperature ranges as transient rupture tests. Both in situ
data (digital image correlation) and ex situ markers (rupture temperature and rupture pressure) were
compared against high temperature tensile data. It was shown in this work that a failure onset point for
fuel cladding during temperature transients can be related to the time-independent, isothermal ultimate
tensile strength of the cladding, rather than only being related to time-dependent creep-driven failure.
While intermediate-temperature creep is necessary for temperature transient modeling, the
implementation of high-temperature yielding and ultimate tensile strength trends into a mechanistic model
are likely required for accurate prediction of the balloon and burst phenomena.

 1. BACKGROUND
 1.1 Motivation
 Light water reactors are licensed to mitigate severe effects from select accident conditions, i.e. design
basis accidents (DBAs). Licensing criteria is most often defined for a specific initiated event, such as a
loss of coolant accident (LOCA), where parameters such as peak cladding and fuel centerline
temperatures, fuel stability, system pressure, and risk of radiological release are metrics used for defining
safety envelopes [1]. LOCA events are designated by size and severity, where the size of the coolant leak
dictates response time and fuel cladding degradation. Designation of a large break LOCA as the DBA of
interest ensures that all LOCA events are appropriately bounded and accounted for, despite large break
LOCAs being a highly rare incident with predicted occurrences near 10-5 – 10-6 per reactor year.

 Typical LOCA analyses involve single rod or small bundle fuel rod exposure to a pressure differential
and a temperature ramp until cladding rupture. Oak Ridge National Laboratory has been performing
research on Zr alloy cladding LOCA events since at least the 1960’s [2,3], in response to successful
implementation of Zr alloy fuel rods within the USS Nautilus in the 1950’s [4]. Chapman conducted a
large LOCA test campaign in collaboration with the Nuclear Regulatory commission in the 1970’s [5,6]
with a focus on understanding empirical relationships between rod internal pressure and rupture
temperature. Powers and Meyers continued Chapman’s approach by quantifying trends witnessed in lab
scale LOCA testing [7,8], which will be utilized throughout this work as a bounding LOCA rupture
metric. Chung et al. similarly performed LOCA testing on Zr alloys with varied gaseous environments [9]
to emphasize allowable oxygen content for fuel handling purposes post LOCA rupture [10]. Similar
testing plans and targeted research goals around cladding performance during LOCA transients occurred
internationally around the same time [11–14]. Ultimately, empirical correlations were all similarly
established, where cladding rupture characteristics showed complex dependences on both material
properties (i.e. soluble oxygen and hydrogen, Zr alloy phase stability, energy barrier for surface
oxidation) and testing control parameters (i.e. internal pressure, heating rate, and test gas environment).
Clear needs remain present to mechanistically understand interactions between different Zr alloy types
and their rupture properties with various testing conditions, as mechanism identification can accelerate
improvements in model simulations for predictive performance and safety analysis.

 1.2 Objectives
 The objective of this work is to more clearly showcase the failure mechanism of fuel cladding under
LOCA type conditions. The identification of a failure mechanism is expected to support higher fidelity
modeling and simulation efforts of design basis accidents and may increase the margin to failure. To
accomplish this objective, LOCA tests were performed at 1, 5, and 28 °C/s within a typical LOCA
pressurization range as well as pressure ranges much higher than expected for LWR large break LOCA’s.
LOCA test data was compared against isothermal isobaric creep tests and high-temperature tensile
datasets. Digital image correlation was utilized for select test cases to provide further insight into failure
mechanisms. All testing was completed with unirradiated, stress relief annealed Zircaloy-4 cladding.

 2. METHODOLOGY
 2.1 Overview
 Accident testing was emulated within the Severe Accident Test Station (SATS) at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL). Zircaloy-4 cladding segments in the unirradiated state were exposed to
internal gas pressurization and elevated temperatures for assessment of both cladding rupture and
intermediate temperature creep. Stereo angle digital image correlation provided in situ deformation
measurements throughout select tests. The experimental methodology is provided below in detail.

2.1.1 Material Description and Specimen Geometry

 Zircaloy-4 cladding used in this work was in the stress relief annealed (SRA) state with an outer
diameter (OD) of 9.5 mm and a wall thickness of 0.575 mm. The material chemistry is reported in Table
1. Electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) produced the image quality map and inverse pole figure of
the as-received material state, shown in Figure 2 [17]. The material consisted of a bimodal grain structure,
where recrystallized 1-10 µm grains were surrounded by a matrix of uncrystallized regions with high
dislocation density and sub-micron grains.

 Cladding segments of 305 mm were used for loss of coolant accident (LOCA) testing, while a 152

2.1.2 Experimental Setup
2.1.2.1 Loss of Coolant Accident Testing

 LOCA testing was performed in two furnaces. One furnace consisted of a 12 lamp infrared heating
furnace, utilized for standard LOCA testing [18]. A second 4-lamp infrared heating furnace was utilized
when digital image correlation was implemented for in-situ strain monitoring (DIC will be discussed in a
later section) [19]. A breakdown of all 38 LOCA tests conducted can be found on Table 2, with summary
rupture performance metrics in the Appendix. The heating rates were chosen to bound the correlations
showcased in NuReg-0630. Internal pressure conditions were chosen to span the entire range of pressure
cases typically assessed in the literature, and additionally, expand the pressurization range higher than
what has been assessed for cladding materials.

 For both furnace test styles, specimens underwent the same thermal profile, as described here. A
standard SATS LOCA test train was assembled using a 6 mm alumina filler rod. Four thermocouples wire
wrapped to the cladding OD at the following locations: control thermocouple 25.4 mm above cladding
centerline at 0°, 25.4 mm above cladding centerline at 180°, 76.2 mm above cladding centerline at 0°, and
76.2 mm below cladding centerline at 0°. The test train was loaded into a quartz reaction tube and
internally pressurized with Ar (g) to generate the targeted hoop stress. The internal pressure was held
constant to a regulated gas cylinder. The assembly was heated at 5 °C/s to 300 °C. Steam was injected
into the reaction tube at 300 °C and allowed five minutes to stabilize. Then, the assembly was heated at 1
°C/s until cladding rupture. 1 °C/s was chosen as the primary ramp rate to provide the most control for
highest quality laboratory data, rather than to perfectly emulate a large break LOCA condition. Select
tests were performed at both 5 and 28 °C/s to give additional comparison against legacy datasets. Power
to the furnace was stopped upon rupture and the gas line was closed. Diagnostic data such as rupture
temperature and rupture pressure were extracted. Post LOCA test, the cladding rupture size, shape, and
balloon size were measured via white light profilometry.

2.1.2.2 In Situ Strain Monitoring

 Two LOCA tests with 3.3 and 6.6 MPa internal pressure, resulting in 25 and 50 MPa hoop stresses,
were performed with in-situ strain monitoring, as noted on Table 2. Additionally, isothermal, isobaric
tube pressurization tests were performed to assess the impacts of creep and plastic deformation in a more
controlled setting than LOCA testing. Based on prior high temperature tensile test data on the same
material [20], test conditions were defined such that data could be collected in the following regimes:
below the cladding yield stress, above the cladding yield stress, and above the cladding ultimate tensile
strength. A hoop stress of 50 MPa was chosen for three test temperatures; 550, 650, and 750 °C. 50 MPa
hoop stress was chosen for direct comparison to the 6.6 MPa LOCA test with DIC.

 Testing involved heating an unpressurized cladding segment to 300 °C, upon which steam flow was
stabilized. The cladding was then brought up to the target temperature and internal pressurization was
then applied to generate the hoop stress. The cladding was held for enough time to acquire adequate strain
data, or until rupture. Digital image correlation was used to track strains throughout the test.

Implementing Digital Image Correlation

 Digital image correlation has been utilized at ORNL to evaluate in-situ deformation during fuel
cladding accident tests [21,19,15,22]. Select testing in this work was performed with DIC to showcase
high value and need of the in situ data collection regarding LOCA transient effects. Two 12 megapixel
cameras were positioned outside of the furnace at a ~10° stereo angle. Lenses were focused on the
specimen, which was illuminated with blue lights. Blue light filters ensured that radiation light during the

 5

test was not saturating the camera sensors. The cameras were calibrated to a commercial calibration grid,
followed by a second calibration to a rigid plate with a high-temperature paint speckle pattern, similar to
what was used on the specimens. A white base coat was initially applied, followed by a random speckle
pattern of black paint for high contrast.

 Data collection was initiated at room temperature at the start of the test at a frame rate of 10 frames
per second. Frame rate collection was decreased during temporary holds at intermediate temperature, such
as at 300°C when steam injection was stabilized. All thermocouple and pressure data were linked to each
image via data acquisition system. Post test analysis included calculation of Hencky strain and
instantaneous strain rates. Elastic response and Zircaloy thermal expansion were subtracted from the
datasets to provide true plastic strain evolution throughout each test. Strain data was compiled from all
visible regions of the cladding and averaged, rather than analyses of each individual nodal datapoint from
the strain maps.

 3. RESULTS
 3.1 Loss of Coolant Accident Testing
 A series of LOCA burst tests were conducted across burst stress ranging from 10 to 400 MPa at
heating rates of 1, 5, 28 °C/s to study the burst behavior and heating rate effects. Figure 3 provides a
visual reference to the cladding rupture characteristics for a majority of the 1 °C/s dataset. Cladding
balloon and rupture was typically found near the cladding centerline above or below the control
thermocouple. The control thermocouple position can be ascertained by the visible constraint from wire
wrapping the thermocouples to the cladding. Figure 4 represents the burst temperature plotted against the
burst stress along with the trendline obtained using the NUREG-0630 empirical correlation for 1 °C/s
tests (a), 5 °C/s tests (b), and 28 °C/s tests (c) together with arbitrary heating rates using equation 2.

For 1 °C/s dataset, the experimental burst temperatures agree well with the NUREG-0630 trendline up to
100 MPa. Above this stress level, the burst temperature deviates from the empirical correlation, and the
deviation becomes more significant as burst stress increases. In the high burst stress region, the
correlation tends to overpredict the reduction in burst temperature because the relationship was
established in a stress range where the creep is dominant deformation mechanisms, which is not valid at
such high stress regime. The original empirical relationship was established based on experimental data
collected within a limited hoop-stress range, thus extrapolation above that range is not reliable. However,
the agreement between the experimental data and NUREG-0630 is exceptional at lower stress level. The
slow heating rate (1 °C/s) provides uniform heat and deformation to the cladding, resulting in small data
scatter and consistent rupture temperatures.

 The 5 °C/s dataset shows a similar trend to the 1 °C/s results. The experimental burst temperature
agrees well with the NUREG-0630 trendline below 100 MPa, but the burst temperature generally lies
above the empirical predictions.

 For 28 °C/s burst tests, the experimental burst temperatures from this work align well with the
trendline up to 65 MPa. At higher burst stress, the deviation between the experimental data and the
empirical correlation tends to be smaller for the higher heating rate tests than the lower heating rate tests.
The NUREG-0630 correlations are constructed primarily from the burst stress range where creep
deformation is dominant. At high heating rates, the time to rupture becomes short, minimizing the creep
deformation with the stress range used to generate the correlation. As a result, the effect of burst stress is
less dominant compared to slow heating. Overall, the test results show that a different model is needed to
describe burst temperature behavior in the high-stress, pressure-dominated regime for heating rates of 1,
5, and 28 °C/s.

 Post-test characterizations were performed to examine the cladding behavior at rupture, including the
diametric strain and burst opening measurements. Figure 5 shows the maximum diametric strain,
averaged from the measurements taken at 0° (burst opening facing upward) and 90° (burst opening facing
sideways), plotted against the burst temperature for 1, 5, and 28 °C/s heating rate. The maximum average
diametric strain was selected as the representative strain parameter because the ballooning and rupture
morphology vary between tests due to the several factors such as alignment of the test train, uniformity of
the temperature profile, and the rupture location on the sample. By averaging the maximum diametric
strain from 0 and 90°, the value better represents the overall extent of ballooning region. Generally, the
results here are in agreement with legacy data that showcase maximum balloon strains are associated with
rupture near 700 – 900 °C [9,23–25].

Figure 5. Maximum diametric strain averaged from 0 and 90 ° as a function of burst temperature for 1, 5,
28 °C/s heating rates.

 It is difficult to establish a clear trend for diametric strain as a function of burst temperature because
the data show noticeable scatter, even among duplicate tests. For 1 °C and 5 °C/s tests, the diametric
strain increases with burst temperature up to 650-750 °C, after which it decreases as burst temperature
increases further up to ~950 °C. Although the data is limited, the diametric strain appears to increase
again above 950 °C. The 28 °C/s tests show a similar tendency, despite the small number of available
data: diametric strain increases up to 800 °C, reaches a local minimum around 950 °C, and then increases
again at higher temperature. These behaviors are consistent with deformation behavior described in
NUREG-0630, where three superplastic peaks are observed (though only two peaks fall within the

 9

temperature range of this study). Below 800 °C, Zircaloy-4 strength decreases and additional slip systems
become active, allowing higher creep deformation before rupture, especially under slower heating rate.
The maximum peak near 800 °C corresponds to superplastic behavior in the α-phase. The reduced
ductility in the α+β phase region leads to a local minimum in strain around 950 °C. Another local
maximum is observed around 1000 °C due to the superplastic behavior in the β-phase. NUREG-0630
used circumferential strain as a representative strain parameter, but the overall trend is comparable. The 5
°C/s tests show a slight shift in the α-phase superplastic peak toward higher temperature relative to the 1
°C/s results. The maximum strains from 1 °C/s tests are higher than those from the 5 °C/s tests, and 5 °C/s
strains are higher than the 28 °C/s strains. This is reasonable as lower heating rates allow longer time for
creep-driven, uniform deformation up to rupture, though it is premature to draw firm conclusions as more
data is needed.

 In addition to diametric strain, the burst opening morphology was characterized. Burst opening size
and shape play an important role in evaluating potential fuel dispersal, fragmentation, and relocation
during LOCA conditions. The burst opening morphology varies significantly due to several factors
including heating rate, temperature gradient, internal pressure, localized strain, and rupture mechanisms.
To reduce the shape-related bias, burst opening area was selected as the representative metric. Figure 6
shows the burst opening area plotted against burst temperature for 1, 5, and 28 °C/s heating rates.

Figure 6. Burst opening area as a function of burst temperature for 1, 5, 28 °C/s heating rates.

 The burst opening area follows a similar trend to the diametric strain. For 1 °C/s tests, the burst
opening area increases with burst temperature and reaches the maximum near 650 °C, consistent with the
diametric strain behavior which also shows a maximum at 650 °C. For 5 °C/s tests, the maximum burst
opening area observed near 700 °C, approximately 50 °C higher than the peak observed in 1 °C/s tests.
Unlike the diametric strain profiles, a second peak near 1000 °C was not observed in the burst opening
area. This difference is expected, as burst opening is related to the localized fracture mechanisms at the
rupture while diametric strain is governed by the deformation mechanisms during transient heating.

 3.2 In-Situ Strain Monitoring
 Two LOCA tests were performed at 1 °C/s with 25 or 50 MPa hoop stress while strain monitoring via
stereo DIC. Elastic strains were removed, resulting in the plastic hoop strain data plotted in Figure 7 for
both datasets. Plastic strain rate was also calculated. Damage accumulation initiated around 500 °C – 550
°C for both applied stresses, and any plastic strain below this temperature was within the dataset noise
levels. A distinct transition towards acceleration of the creep rate occurred around 750 °C for the 25 MPa
test (Figure 7b) and 650 °C for the 50 MPa test (Figure 7d). Each cladding test reached 1% plastic hoop
strain at 801 and 689 °C, respectively.

 Figure 10 compares the strain rates from the in-situ data collection testing. The LOCA strain rates
were pulled at specified temperatures of 550, 650 and 750 °C throughout the 1 °C/s temperature ramp.
The isothermal and isobaric data columns were calculated in two ways. The 650 and 750 °C strain rates

temperatures. At 550 °C, the primary creep regime shows better agreement with the LOCA data. The
current comparison implies that primary creep dominates a lower temperature deformation regime. Yet,
creep deformation when below the material yield stress represents only a minor component of cladding
damage during rapid thermal transients such as LOCAs.

 4. DISCUSSION
 4.1 Connecting Steady State to Transient Deformation
 NuReg-0630 provides a robust prediction method for qualitative assessment of fuel cladding rupture
during a LOCA type event. In this work, it was shown that this empirical model cannot be extrapolated
beyond its initial rupture temperature and pressure ranges. Elevated pressure rupture testing outside the
traditional scope for LWR accident conditions showed that new correlations would be necessary. Of
particular interest, the heating rate dependence derived by Chapman, Powers, and Meyer [23,16] is not
consistent across all temperature and pressure ranges. Instead, there exists multiple regimes that should be
separated within future work scope for further investigation. First, it is expected that high-temperature
exposure enhances thermally activated creep processes; slip planes become active, materials
fundamentally increase in their ductility, and dislocations can be annihilated rapidly. This high-
temperature and low-pressure regime would be expected to have a heating rate dependence due to its
thermally activated deformation modes. In the high-pressure and low-temperature rupture regime, the

 13

same deformation modes are less active or possess increased barriers for deformation. Microstructure
stability, defect density, and macroscopic cladding anomalies are expected to be rate limiting for
dislocation motion. The results of this work suggest that the two mechanistic regimes may have different
heating rate dependencies, implying a graded heating rate dependency may exist across the entire rupture
temperature and pressure space.

 Common modeling approaches for LOCA type events rely on creep deformation to effectively
capture strain evolution upon heating. In agreement with the approach, this work utilized digital image
correlation to showcase cladding creep at a 550 °C 50 MPa isothermal isobaric condition. Yet, direct
comparison to the 50 MPa LOCA test at 1 °C/s highlighted how primary creep is likely governing strain
evolution during a temperature. This qualitatively makes sense due to the relatively short time scales that
fuel cladding experiences at a single temperature during a LOCA event. Future modeling efforts must
consider compounding primary creep evolution, rather than utilizing purely steady state experimental data
in model development.

 Prior work performed high-temperature tensile testing of the same batch Zircaloy-4 cladding in the
axial direction [20]. The ultimate tensile strength trendline from that work is shown in Figure 11
alongside the 1 °C/s LOCA rupture data, 28 °C/s LOCA rupture data, and NuReg-0630 curve. Through
this data visualization, it becomes clear that creep in the traditional sense is not the dominant failure mode
for fuel cladding during a LOCA event. Rather, the results of this work suggest that LOCA cladding
failure is initiated when the local hoop stress exceeds the cladding ultimate tensile strength. Unless the
pressure or temperature ramp are immediately alleviated, cladding can be expected to fail once this plastic
instability initiates. As such, exceedance of UTS represents a time-independent failure threshold for fuel
cladding. Cladding rupture was not evident immediately after the exceedance of UTS. Instead, there
exists some time-to-failure that is likely dependent on the material microstructure, heating rate,
temperature history, and local cladding defects. One particularly valuable aspect of the ultimate tensile
strength (UTS) is that it can serve as a conservative criterion for predicting non-failure. Specifically,
cladding burst is not expected to occur if the stress in the cladding remains below the material UTS. This
approach is expected to be most useful for evaluating cladding behavior under BWR LOCA conditions
and for fuel rods located on the periphery of PWR cores.

 Digital image correlation of both 1 °C/s LOCA tests provided strong evidence of strain evolution
prior to the rupture event. The previously noted strain rate transition points (Figure 7b, Figure 7d) were
also plotted in terms of the transition temperature and hoop stress on Figure 11. It can be inferred that the
transition to an exponentially increasing strain rate within the cladding aligns with the onset of plastic
instability, or “necking,” when UTS is exceeded. While some heating rate dependence remains present
between 1 and 28 °C/s across the pressure range tested, the heating rate dependence appears to be most
prevalent for high-temperature rupture events. Here, we propose that hoop direction UTS can be
estimated from DIC strain data during transient testing conditions such as a LOCA.

 Figure 11 showed that the LOCA data across a wide pressurization regime qualitatively resembles the
UTS temperature dependence. Yet, there exists a clear offset between both the LOCA rupture data and the
UTS data along the x-axis temperature space. The time and temperature delta was calculated for each
LOCA test dataset and is shown on Figure 12. The temperature delay between exceeding UTS and the
rupture event showcased unique findings. The results appear bounded by the 1 °C/s data trends, and
literature comparisons with SRA Zry-4 cladding LOCA testing also show similar behavior [14,25]. At
lower temperature regimes, claddings experienced a longer amount of time deforming after crossing UTS.
Thermally activated deformation processes are inhibited in this regime. A local minimum was present
when UTS was exceeded around 525 °C. As such, it can be assumed that a mechanism surrounding this
intermediate temperature regime is accelerating deformation during localized failure after exceeding UTS,
and potential reasons are hypothesized here.

 First, prismatic and basal slip can become noticeably active above 550 °C [26,27], and this transition
from lower temperature twinning behavior [28,29] could be reasons for the differing trends in the time-to-
rupture above and below ~525 °C. This Zr alloy anisotropy is often modeled using anisotropic Hill
parameters (F, G, and H), yet a constant degree of anisotropy is typically utilized across the entire α-Zr
temperature range. Rosinger et al. performed creep testing on Zircaloy-4 cladding at 800 °C and reported
near isotropic Hill parameters (F = G = H = 0.5) [30], later confirmed by Choi et al. with HANA cladding
[31]. Accelerated creep testing on Zircaloy-4 cladding has more recently implied that a transition to

 15

isotropic deformation behavior can initiate closer to 700 °C [17]. Such changes in anisotropy may provide
insights on the time-to-rupture shape as a function of material exposure temperature.

 Additionally, SRA Zircaloy-4 fuel cladding has also been shown to undergo rapid recrystallization
processes around 525 °C, although accumulated time at temperature must be appropriately taken into
account [32,33]. The above mechanisms are worth further investigation regarding impacts on cladding
rupture. The smallest time-to-rupture is present at elevated temperatures, where rupture occurred in the α-
β phase transition regime, in addition to being a regime where the most deformation modes are thermally
activated. Claddings which traverse through 800 °C with reasonably high hoop stresses are also expected
to have a delayed rupture event due to superplastic flow [24], which agrees with what is shown in Figure
12.

 Targeted characterization, in particular electron backscattered diffraction and transmission electron
microscopy, should be pursued across all relevant regimes to improve insights. Particular attention should
be given to regimes with major microstructural evolution, such as recrystallization or α-β transformation,
as these areas are likely to showcase the largest impact on the time or temperature delay between UTS
and cladding rupture. Further, a statistical analysis on the time or temperature delay from UTS until
rupture is necessary. The cleanest datasets came from the more controlled nature of 1 °C/s testing, and
additionally the 28 °C/s dataset showcases the smallest degree of change in the UTS-to-rupture
temperature delta. Therefore, statistical assessments should initiate with lower ramp rate testing.

 While conventional creep approaches remain valuable for modeling deformation during LOCA
transients, clear gaps remain in the efficacy of models to appropriately predict localized balloon and
rupture events. Therefore, this work provides clear direction towards mechanistic model development
with emphasis on a time-independent UTS failure criterion. Uniform to local deformation transitions can
be experimentally assessed via in situ strain monitoring, thus providing direct comparison datasets to
expedite modeling capabilities. Utilization of a UTS-based failure criterion has clear impacts on the safety
envelope. Due to the microstructurally dependent time-to-rupture gap after exceeding UTS, a UTS failure
criterion inherently decreases the failure temperature of fuel cladding. Yet, the accuracy and reliability of
a time-independent failure criterion could be expected to dramatically decrease risk. Further margin can
be gained via exploiting the time-to-rupture gap with novel fuel cladding chemistries, heat treatments, and
microstructural features, where materials assessments can be made to control how much localized work
hardening competes with cladding wall thinning.

 5. CONCLUSION
 LOCA testing has represented a long-time experimental case to determine safety limits during design
basis accident conditions. Yet, a mechanistic understanding of the cladding deformation throughout the
LOCA transient has in many regards remains unknown. Here, the LOCA testing methodology was
extended to a high-pressure regime not typically considered applicable for LWR accident testing. The
extension of the pressure regime, in tandem with in situ strain monitoring, provided clear evidence that
cladding failure during a LOCA can be initiated by exceeding the material’s ultimate tensile strength.
Cladding deformation above 1% can be prevented in most thermal transients by maintaining hoop stresses
below the cladding UTS. The time or temperature delay between exceeding UTS and the physical rupture
event appears to be most heavily impacted by microstructural features and heating rate, which represent
potential avenues to exploit for future increases in burst margin. Here, digital image correlation provided
a method for estimating the cladding hoop direction UTS during high-temperature thermal transients such
as a LOCA. Mechanistic model efforts should consider combination of compounding intermediate
temperature primary creep relationships, time independent UTS failure initiation, and a time/temperature-
to-failure metric for improved predictions against available experimental datasets.
