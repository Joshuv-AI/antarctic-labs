Case Report: Two rare cases of aggressive fibromatosis originating in the intestinal tract
Authors: Xinsheng Zhang, Xiping Yu, Danni Dong, Jibo Hu, Houyun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aggressive Fibromatosis (AF) is a rare mesenchymal tumor characterized by nuclear β-catenin positivity. Its exact etiology remains unclear but is thought to be associated with hormonal factors, trauma, or prior surgery. Clinically, AF is uncommon and demonstrates nonspecific imaging features, frequently complicating preoperative diagnosis. This is particularly true for intra-abdominal AF presenting with atypical features, which can be easily mistaken for other abdominal space-occupying lesions. Herein, we report two rare cases of primary intestinal AF, one of which was complicated by tumor rupture and subsequent intra-abdominal abscess formation. Through an integrated analysis of clinical presentation, imaging findings, and pathological results, this report discusses the diagnostic considerations and management strategies for AF.

Introduction

Aggressive Fibromatosis (AF), also known as Desmoid-type Fibromatosis (DF), is a rare mesenchymal tumor that comprises approximately 3% of all soft tissue sarcomas and less than 0.03% of all tumors (1). Its annual incidence is approximately 5–6 cases per million population, with a peak occurrence between 30 and 40 years old (2). According to the World Health Organization (WHO) definition, AF is described as “a clonal fibroblast proliferation occurring in the deep soft tissues, characterized by infiltrative growth and a tendency to local recurrence, but not capable of metastasis” (3). Based on anatomical location, AF can be classified into three types: extra-abdominal (60%), abdominal wall (25%), and intra-abdominal (8-15%) (4). Intra-abdominal AF commonly occurs in the mesentery, retroperitoneum, and pelvic cavity, while primary origin in the intestinal tract is extremely rare (5). Clinical presentation is highly variable, depending on lesion size and anatomical site, and often mimics that of more common abdominal conditions, posing a diagnostic challenge (6).

Here we present two rare cases of AF originating primarily within the intestinal tract. Both cases were initially misdiagnosed as other abdominal tumors preoperatively. By analyzing their imaging and pathology characteristics, along with a review of the relevant literature, we aim to improve the recognition of this entity and offer clinical insights to aid in its diagnosis and management.

Case report

Case 1

A 25-year-old male presented with a 3-day history of abdominal pain and fever. The pain, localized predominantly around the umbilicus, was severe and of sudden onset, accompanied by fever and headache. Although temporary relief was achieved with self-administered anti-pyretics and antibiotics, the symptoms recurred intermittently. The patient’s condition subsequently worsened, with his temperature spiking to 41 °C, accompanied by chills, rigors, dizziness, headache, and reduced urine output. After receiving symptomatic management in the emergency department, including antibiotic therapy, antispasmodics, and analgesics, his symptoms improved significantly.

Physical examination revealed a soft, non-distended abdomen with tenderness in the periumbilical and left upper quadrants, but no rebound tenderness. Laboratory investigations showed mildly elevated serum tumor markers: carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA-125, 35.1 U/mL), protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II (PIVKA-II, 41.59 mAU/mL), and pro-gastrin-releasing peptide (ProGRP, 66.57 pg/mL). Complete blood count demonstrated leukocytosis (white blood cell count 17.9×109/L) with neutrophilia (absolute neutrophil count 16×109/L, neutrophil percentage 89.3%). Biochemistry revealed a markedly elevated C-reactive protein level (CRP, 263.5 mg/L). On computed tomography (CT), a slightly hypodense mass was noted at the inferior border of the pancreatic body and tail, containing internal patchy areas of low attenuation. The lesion demonstrated mild to moderate heterogeneous enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans. Its margins with the pancreas were ill-defined, with obscuration of the surrounding fat planes and associated thickening and haziness of the adjacent peritoneum and omentum. The mass abutted the gastric wall and duodenum, displacing adjacent bowel loops and the left kidney (Figures 1A–D). The initial radiological impression was a possible solid pseudopapillary neoplasm of the pancreas. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a large, well-demarcated mass in the left abdomen with heterogeneous signal intensity. The solid components appeared isointense on T1-weighted images (T1WI), slightly hyperintense on T2-weighted images (T2WI), and isointense on both diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps. These components showed progressive contrast enhancement, which was most pronounced on delayed-phase images. The lesion also contained cystic areas exhibiting low T1WI signal, heterogeneously high T2WI signal, high DWI signal, and low ADC signal, with internal septations. The cyst wall was irregularly thickened with localized papillary projections. The DWI and ADC findings suggested restricted diffusion. Post-contrast images revealed marked enhancement of the septa and cyst wall. A localized communication between the lesion and the peritoneal cavity was noted. The mass had indistinct borders with the adjacent bowel and pancreatic body/tail, causing compression of the duodenum and resultant proximal bowel dilatation with fluid accumulation. The surrounding fat planes were blurred, with streaky and flocculent exudative changes (Figures 1E–L). Preoperative diagnosis was a tumor likely origin from either the bowel (suggestive of a stromal tumor) or the pancreas (suggestive of a solid pseudopapillary tumor, SPT). The patient had no family history of familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP). Preoperative colonoscopy was not performed given the emergent presentation with tumor rupture and intra-abdominal abscess. Genetic testing for APC and CTNNB1 mutations was also not carried out.

Following intensive anti-infective therapy, the patient underwent a partial colectomy on the third hospital day. Intraoperatively, a tumor measuring approximately 10.0 × 10.0 cm was found at the splenic flexure of the colon. The tumor had ruptured, with a surrounding abscess that extensively involved the colon, spleen, pancreas, and small intestine, along with multiple peritoneal adhesions. The colon was transected 2 cm from the tumor margin to achieve complete resection. The distal stump was oversewn, and a proximal diverting ostomy was created. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of AF. The tumor displayed infiltrative growth within the intestinal wall and adipose tissue. The tumor cells were arranged in fascicular or storiform patterns, composed of spindle cells with abundant cytoplasm and elongated, wavy nuclei interspersed among collagen bundles, accompanied by a significant infiltrate of acute and chronic inflammatory cells (Figure 2A). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated β-catenin (nuclear +), CD34 (vascular +), SMA (vascular +), Desmin (vascular +), Ki-67 (1% +), SDHB (+), Calponin (+), CD117 (-), DOG-1 (-), S-100 (-), STAT6 (-), SOX-10 (-) (Figure 2B). On postoperative 20 days, the patient developed a small bowel obstruction, which was managed conservatively and subsequently resolved. The patient showed no evidence of tumor recurrence on a contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen performed 5 months after surgery.

Case 2

A 36-year-old female presented with a left abdominal mass that she had self-palpated one month prior. Ultrasound performed at an outside hospital subsequently reported a ‘large left abdominal mass’. The patient reported subjective enlargement of the mass over time. Physical examination revealed a soft, non-distended abdomen. A palpable, ill-defined, soft-textured mass was present in the left abdomen, without associated tenderness or rebound tenderness. Laboratory data was notable only for an elevated CRP level (11.3 mg/L). Serum tumor markers and complete blood count were within normal limits. Imaging Findings: Ultrasonography revealed a well-demarcated, relatively regular hypoechoic mass with heterogeneous internal echotexture was visualized in the left upper quadrant (Figure 3A). Color Doppler flow imaging (CDFI) showed scant intralesional blood flow (Figure 3B). CT revealed a slightly hypodense mass with ill-defined margins and heterogeneous internal attenuation was seen in the left upper abdomen. Contrast-enhanced scans demonstrated a progressive enhancement pattern. Branches of the superior mesenteric artery were seen coursing through the mass. The lesion had an indistinct interface with adjacent bowel loops and the small bowel mesentery, causing mass effect with displacement and partial luminal narrowing of the neighboring small intestine (Figures 3C–F). Preoperative CT diagnosis was most likely a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST). MRI revealed a relatively well-defined mass in the left upper abdomen, which appeared slightly hypointense on T1-weighted images and heterogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted images, with internal flow voids. DWI showed scattered patchy hyperintensities, with corresponding low signal on the ADC map. Post-contrast images demonstrated progressive, markedly heterogeneous enhancement with patchy non-enhancing areas. The adjacent left kidney and small bowel were displaced due to compression (Figures 3G–N). The MRI impression also strongly suggested a GIST. The patient denied any family history of FAP or related neoplasms. Routine preoperative colonoscopy and molecular testing for APC and CTNNB1 mutations were not performed in this case.

On the sixth hospital day, the patient underwent duodenal tumor resection. Intraoperative exploration revealed an exophytic, firm mass measuring approximately 14 cm × 12 cm originating from the duodenal wall, located about 5 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz. The tumor was adherent to adjacent small bowel segments and the omentum, with distinct nutrient vessels identified. Adhesiolysis was performed, feeding vessels were ligated, and the bowel was transected 3 cm proximal and distal to the tumor to achieve complete resection of the tumor and the involved intestinal segment. Continuity was restored via a side-to-side duodenojejunostomy, with the anastomosis reinforced by additional sutures. The procedure was uneventful, and the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit for postoperative monitoring. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis of AF. The tumor infiltrated the muscularis propria of the intestinal wall and the surrounding adipose tissue, with both proximal and distal resection margins negative for tumor involvement (Figure 4A). Immunohistochemical results: β-catenin (nuclear +), LEF1 (partial +), Ki-67 (+, approximately 2%), SDHB (+), CD34 (vascular +), CD117 (-), SMA (-), Desmin (-), S-100 (-), DOG-1 (-), STAT6 (-) (Figure 4B). The patient recovered well following the surgery. A follow-up contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen performed 6 months postoperatively revealed no evidence of tumor recurrence. Discussion

AF is a rare fibroblastic tumor that originates from the connective tissues of muscles, fascia, or aponeuroses (7). First described by MacFarlane in 1832 (8), the pathogenesis of AF has not been fully elucidated to date. However, existing studies suggest potential associations with factors such as hormone levels, trauma, and surgical procedures (9–11). Clinically, AF can be classified into sporadic and familial types (12). The sporadic type typically demonstrates monoclonal origin and occurs predominantly in extra-abdominal sites or the abdominal wall, whereas the familial type is commonly associated with FAP and often presents as intra-abdominal lesions, particularly in the mesentery (12–15). Regardless of subtype, primary intestinal AF is rare, and intestinal AF complicated by abscess formation is even more uncommon. Previous literature has documented AF in intestinal segments including the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure of the colon, transverse colon, and sigmoid colon (Table 2) (13, 16–26). Reported cases, however, are scarce, and to our knowledge, no previous report has described its occurrence at the splenic flexure of the colon. This report presents two cases of AF originating from the intestinal tract — one in the duodenum and the other at the splenic flexure of the colon. The colonic case was complicated by tumor perforation and abscess formation, a distinct clinical manifestation that further highlights the rarity and complexity of this disease. This report aims to contribute to the accumulation of cases of intestinal AF at rare sites and to provide a reference for future clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Macroscopically, AF appears as a firm, grayish-white, scar-like mass. On microscopy it is characterized by heterogeneous, poorly defined and uniform proliferation of spindle cells that resembling myofibroblasts surrounded by a stroma of abundant collagen and a vascular network lacking capsule (2). The histological differential diagnosis of AF is broad, encompassing a wide range of spindle cell lesions from scarring to GIST and low-grade liposarcomas. However, a confident diagnosis may be made by virtue, as most mimicking neoplasms exhibit specific histological diagnostic features and lack nuclear β-catenin immunoreactivity (5).

The clinical presentation of AF is highly heterogeneous, varying with tumor location, size, and growth rate. Generally, AF involving the limbs or abdominal wall is locally invasive and may cause pain, paresthesia, or functional impairment that significantly affects quality of life, yet carries a low mortality rate (27). In contrast, intra-abdominal AF often presents as a painless mass. However, with progression, its infiltrative growth can lead to serious complications such as bowel obstruction, perforation, or ischemia, whereas abscess formation remains exceedingly rare (2, 27, 28). The two cases presented here illustrate this clinical spectrum. The duodenal AF, despite its large size, was asymptomatic and detected only as a poorly defined abdominal mass on physical examination. In contrast, the colonic AF at the splenic flexure presented with signs of infection—fever, leukocytosis, and markedly elevated CRP—secondary to tumor rupture and abscess formation. This variability complicates the preoperative diagnosis of intestinal AF, which is often mistaken for a GIST. Thus, both radiologists and clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in their diagnostic approach.

The radiological features of AF are heterogeneous in the literature, a diversity primarily dictated by its histological composition and vascular characteristics. Moreover, AF is frequently misdiagnosed due to its overlapping imaging characteristics with malignancies, such as similar signal intensity/attenuation, tumor margin morphology, and a tendency to encase adjacent structures (29, 30). Such misdiagnosis may lead to unnecessary overtreatment. Multimodal imaging tools including ultrasound, CT, and MRI are not only crucial for diagnosis but also play a central role in staging, therapeutic planning, and follow-up (2, 7). Ultrasound is useful for initial assessment, particularly in abdominal wall and extremity AF, where it typically reveals a well- or ill-defined soft-tissue mass with heterogeneous echogenicity (31, 32). CT is the preferred modality for evaluating intra-abdominal AF, clearly delineating lesion location, extent, and anatomical relationships, thereby providing key information for surgical planning and resectability assessment. It also serves as the primary tool for monitoring postoperative recurrence and complications (33). On CT, AF most commonly appears as a soft-tissue mass with attenuation similar to skeletal muscle. Areas rich in myxoid components are hypodense, whereas collagen-dense regions may show slightly increased density (34). Lesions usually enhance to varying degrees, while necrosis and calcification are exceedingly rare (33, 34). An imaging study of 102 AF cases from different sites reported enhancement patterns on CT as mild (10.8%), moderate (67.6%), or marked (21.6%), underscoring the considerable heterogeneity in tumor enhancement (29). A recent large-series study of 96 AF patients undergoing contrast-enhanced CT found the arterial penetration sign—defined as an artery traversing the lesion without luminal stenosis—in 26% of cases. Among intra-abdominal lesions, this sign was observed in 42.5%, indicating it is a characteristic but not pathognomonic finding of AF (35). In the present report, contrast-enhanced CT of the duodenal lesion also demonstrated branches of the superior mesenteric artery penetrating the mass without luminal narrowing (Figure 3D), suggestive of the arterial penetration sign and providing important imaging support for the diagnosis. Owing to its superior soft-tissue contrast, MRI holds particular value in imaging AF, especially for extra-abdominal and abdominal wall lesions (7). Signal intensity variations across MRI sequences reflect the proportional composition of collagen fibers, spindle cells, myxoid stroma, and extracellular matrix within the tumor (34). On T1WI, AF shows signal intensity similar to muscle. On T2WI, areas with higher collagen and fibrous content appear hypointense, while regions richer in cellular or myxoid components display heterogeneous hyperintensity (2). Approximately 90% of AF lesions exhibit moderate to marked enhancement, especially in cellular-rich, less fibrotic areas (36). The fibrous components may show persistent contrast uptake over time, resulting in a progressive enhancement pattern (37). In both intestinal AF cases presented here, the tumors demonstrated pronounced delayed enhancement, consistent with the above enhancement characteristics. This enhancement pattern is common in AF but lacks diagnostic specificity and cannot independently confirm the disease (33). Furthermore, densely packed fibrous tissue within AF restricts water diffusion, leading to high signal on DWI. Previous studies indicate that the mean ADC value of AF is significantly higher than that of malignant soft-tissue tumors, offering useful discriminatory information. Lee et al. reported a mean ADC of 1493 × 10–6 mm2/s for AF, significantly higher than the 873 × 10–6 mm2/s for malignant soft-tissue tumors (P < 0.001) (30). Similarly, Oka et al. found AF ADC values (1360 × 10–6 mm2/s) to be significantly greater than those of malignant controls (880 × 10–6 mm2/s, P < 0.01) (38). In our two cases of intestinal AF, the mean ADC values (1496 and 1467 × 10–6 mm2/s, respectively) were measured on ADC maps derived from DWI (b = 0 and 800 s/mm2). Three 100 mm2 ROIs were positioned in the solid tumor parenchyma following previously described methods (30, 38), with necrotic, cystic-degenerative, and hemorrhagic areas carefully excluded. These values fell within the reported ADC range for AF. These findings further support the diagnosis and aid in distinguishing AF from malignancies. However, the definitive diagnosis of AF depends primarily on pathology and immunohistochemistry.

Preoperatively, both cases in this report were initially misdiagnosed as GIST. This misdiagnosis likely stems from two factors. Firstly, the intestinal location of the lesions, combined with internal cystic change, necrosis, and abscess formation, resulted in heterogeneous density and signal intensity, creating imaging overlap with GIST (23). Secondly, GIST is the most common mesenchymal tumor of the gastrointestinal tract, whereas primary intestinal AF is exceedingly rare, with only scattered case reports in the literature. This disparity naturally leads to a higher diagnostic consideration for common entities over rare ones during image interpretation. Although abscess and cystic degeneration are uncommon in AF, they should still be included in the differential diagnosis (39). This was primarily because the tumor, located at the splenic flexure, had ruptured and formed a surrounding abscess that engulfed adjacent structures including the pancreas, obscuring their boundaries. Furthermore, its imaging features—cystic-solid composition, septations, and progressive enhancement—shared similarities with SPT. The key distinctions, however, lie in the fact that SPT typically presents as a well-circumscribed, encapsulated mass prone to internal hemorrhage or necrosis, while AF often demonstrates infiltrative growth with dense fibrous adhesions to surrounding tissues (37). Beyond GIST and SPT, the differential diagnosis for intestinal AF also includes gastrointestinal clear cell sarcoma-like tumor, gastrointestinal schwannoma, gastrointestinal leiomyoma, extranodal follicular dendritic cell sarcoma, and solitary fibrous tumor, among others. The combination of nuclear β-catenin positivity in tumor cells and characteristic CT/MRI findings can aid in distinguishing AF from these entities (40, 41). A thorough understanding of the imaging features of AF is therefore crucial for accurate preoperative diagnosis, which in turn provides a reliable imaging foundation for formulating precise clinical management strategies.

The management of AF must account for its heterogeneous clinical presentation and biological behavior, underscoring the importance of an individualized approach to achieve optimal outcomes. For patients where R0 resection with organ preservation is feasible, surgical excision remains the cornerstone of therapy (42). However, the locally infiltrative nature of AF often makes it challenging to obtain adequate surgical margins, which is crucial for minimizing recurrence risk. A large, two-center retrospective imaging study of AF reported an overall postoperative recurrence rate of 38.6% (22/57) among patients who underwent complete resection. Recurrence rates varied by anatomical site: 8.3% for abdominal wall, 23.8% for intra-abdominal, and 66.7% for extra-abdominal lesions (35). In recent years, the treatment paradigm has gradually shifted toward conservative, non-surgical strategies, particularly for asymptomatic patients at initial presentation. In such cases, a “wait-and-see” or active surveillance approach is now considered first-line management, given that a substantial proportion of lesions remain stable or even regress spontaneously over time (43, 44). One study of 83 AF patients under active surveillance reported a 5-year progression-free survival rate of 50%, with a median time to progression of 14 months (44). Another study noted spontaneous tumor regression in 29 of 102 patients (28.4%) (45). If disease progresses during surveillance, surgery or systemic therapy may be considered. Medical therapy is particularly relevant for lesions involving critical structures or those deemed unresectable (46). Systemic options include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), anti-hormonal agents, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and traditional low-dose chemotherapy regimens such as liposomal doxorubicin (2). Radiotherapy may be an option for unresectable or recurrent disease in patients at high surgical risk (47), but due to its anatomical constraints, intra-abdominal AF is generally not amenable to radiotherapy (48, 49). For intestinal AF, preoperative diagnosis is often difficult, and surgery remains the most commonly recommended treatment (13). Given the potential for life-threatening complications such as bowel obstruction or perforation, intestinal lesions typically warrant more aggressive surgical intervention, as demonstrated by the tumor rupture and secondary abscess in Case 1favor. The second patient with duodenal AF underwent en bloc resection of the tumor and involved bowel segment six days after admission due to adhesions to adjacent small intestine and omentum. Neither patient received adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy, and both have been free of recurrence during the follow-up period.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the follow-up period has been extended to 5 months for Case 1 and 6 months for Case 2 based on recent imaging, these durations are still relatively short for AF, which is known for late local recurrence. Long-term surveillance is essential to confirm sustained remission. Second, genetic testing for APC or CTNNB1 mutations and preoperative colonoscopy were not routinely performed, limiting our ability to completely exclude the influence of FAP or explore the molecular pathogenesis of these specific cases. Third, both patients underwent surgical resection without preoperative biopsy or conservative management, so we could not assess the efficacy of non-surgical treatment strategies for intestinal AF.

Conclusion

AF is a rare, locally invasive mesenchymal tumor. This report presents two cases of intestinal AF, one of which was complicated by tumor rupture and abscess formation, underscoring the clinical variability and potential severity of this condition. Imaging is pivotal for delineating disease extent, aiding differential diagnosis, and clinical management. Definitive diagnosis relies on histopathological examination with nuclear β-catenin immunohistochemistry. While surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment for intestinal AF, the substantial risk of local recurrence necessitates long-term postoperative surveillance.
