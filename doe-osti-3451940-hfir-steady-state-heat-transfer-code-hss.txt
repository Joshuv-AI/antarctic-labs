 ABSTRACT
HFIR Steady State Heat Transfer Code (HSSHTC), the safety basis steady state thermal hydraulics (TH)
code for the High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR), uses a highly conservative approach in which all input
and calculation uncertainties are resolved simultaneously at their most limiting setting. This results in ex-
cessive conservatism which does not account for the high unlikelihood of such simultaneous worst-case
conditions. The present study explores an alternative approach, best estimate plus uncertainty (BEPU), in
which reasonable working assumptions for the probability distribution of each input uncertainty are used
to determine a relationship between burnout power margin and core fuel failure probability. The study ad-
hered to a philosophy of perturbing uncertainty parameters already defined within the HSSHTC method-
ology while preserving the HSSHTC calculation approach and solution methodology. Based on the as-
sumptions herein, based on a 95/99 confidence interval, the BEPU approach resulted in a 0.29 increase in
burnout power ratio (25 MW increase in burnout power) compared to the latest HSSHTC calculations of
C-HFIR-2026-001. The study can be refined in the future by employing fuel fabrication data to provide
more realistic input distributions. Future changes to the HSSHTC methodology could allow a more com-
prehensive treatment of uncertainties and further increase the burnout power ratio as a result.

 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 MOTIVATION
The HFIR Steady State Heat Transfer Code (HSSHTC) is used to support the determination of thermal
and fluid safety limits (SLs) and limiting control settings (LCSs) for High Flux Isotope Reactor (HFIR)
operation. It applies conservatism primarily through deterministic uncertainty factors (i.e., “U-factors”).
U-factors account for fabrication tolerances, operating-condition uncertainty, measurement uncertainty,
and model/correlation uncertainty, and are applied within a compounded hot channel and hot spot frame-
work to obtain limiting thermal margins. This approach is intentionally conservative. However, because
the factors are treated as simultaneously adverse, the resulting limiting condition represents an unlikely
combination of uncertainties and can severely underestimate the physically realistic safety margin.
The purpose of the present statistical approach is to retain a conservative safety demonstration while re-
placing the fully stacked deterministic treatment with a best estimate plus uncertainty (BEPU) approach:
 • Each U-factor is treated as an uncertain input and assigned a probability distribution or range based
 on the available technical basis (e.g., fabrication/measurement tolerances, model/correlation uncer-
 tainty) and engineering judgment.
 • Sets of U-factors are sampled to generate many statistically independent trials, and each trial is eval-
 uated with HSSHTC to produce a corresponding burnout power ratio.
 • The resulting set of trials is used to estimate the burnout power ratio distribution and establish an
 updated burnout margin corresponding to a sufficiently high probability that the fuel SLs will not be
 exceeded.
The intent of this study is to provide a scoping analysis that demonstrates an application of BEPU methods
to HSSHTC safety-basis calculations and illustrates the potential increase in safety margin when using this
method.

1.2 BACKGROUND AND PURPOSE OF HSSHTC
SLs are defined in the HFIR safety analysis report (SAR) and technical safety requirement (TSR) for pri-
mary thermal and hydraulic process variables to reasonably protect the fuel cladding under quasi steady-
state conditions. Corresponding LCSs are also established for these variables based on the following crite-
rion:
 “With any given variable at its SL, all other variables at their limiting safety system setting
 (LSSS) (LCS), and all uncertainties in the technical knowledge of the process resolved unfa-
 vorably, no hot spot burnout can occur.”
This is a conservatively bounding approach in which each SL is satisfied while,simultaneously, all other
process variables are at their most limiting allowed values (LCS) and not at nominal values. For Mode 1
operation (i.e., normal power operation), burnout is taken as the physical limit at which cladding failure is
assumed. Table 4.4-2 of Chapter 4 of the HFIR SAR lists these SLs and LCSs.
The HSSHTC is a steady-state thermal-hydraulic safety analysis tool used to support these quasi steady-
state SLs. It calculates the burnout power, defined as the lowest core operating power level at which burnout
occurs. The burnout power ratio (i.e., burnout power divided by nominal core power of 85 MW) is com-
pared to the relevant SL or LCS flux-to-flow ratio to determine the relative burnout margin.
The HSSHTC burnout power calculation is based on a 1D, steady-state thermal-hydraulic evaluation along
several vertical “streaks” within a representative flow channel and adjacent fuel plate. The calculation ac-
counts for the following:

 2

 • Hydraulic conditions (e.g., core flow rate or channel flow, pressure, inlet temperature)
 • Geometric and material definitions (e.g., fuel-plate and coolant-channel geometry, thermal conduc-
 tivities, etc.)
 • Spatial power distribution information for the plate
 • Heat transfer and friction correlations applicable to HFIR plate-type fuel
 • Uncertainties in the above inputs and models
1.3 REGULATORY FRAMEWORK
The existing HFIR safety-basis modeling philosophy intentionally resolves model, fabrication, and oper-
ating uncertainties in the most unfavorable direction simultaneously. In the HSSHTC implementation, this
conservatism is applied through multiple uncertainty factors which are often multiplicative. Physically,
they represent effects which in many cases may be coupled (e.g., flow resistance and heat transfer affect
cladding temperature; cladding temperature affects oxide growth, which then feeds back on heat transfer
and hydraulics). As a result, the overall conservatism is not simply the product of independent margins,
and the degree of bounding in the combined result is difficult to interpret quantitatively without a statistical
treatment of the uncertainties and their interactions. (Ruggles 2022) This simultaneous worst-case stacking
of conservatisms does not account for the unlikelihood of such a simultaneous worst-case occurrence.
Statistical methods, by contrast, can reduce this excess conservatism by providing a statistically supported
demonstration that cladding failure will not occur below an acceptable probability threshold. This ap-
proach has been widely adopted in recent decades by the commercial nuclear power industry as a means
of recovering additional margin in safety calculations. The approach has also been explored for poten-
tial inclusion within the safety bases of the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), Mas-
sachusetts Institute of Technology Reactor (MITR), and Advanced Test Reactor (ATR) high-performance
research reactors.
For HFIR, the applicable approval framework is US Department of Energy (DOE) authorization under
10 CFR Part 830 rather than Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) licensing under 10 CFR Part 50.
Nevertheless, NRC BEPU practice provides a directly relevant precedent because 10 CFR 830 Appendix A
identifies NRC Regulatory Guide 1.70, or a successor document, as an acceptable documented safety anal-
ysis (DSA)/SAR methodology for DOE reactors, and DOE’s safety-basis framework uses DSA, TSR, un-
reviewed safety question (USQ), and safety evaluation report (SER) concepts that were developed with
NRC subject matter expert input and are similar in structure to NRC reactor licensing practice. Under
10 CFR 830.204(a), a DOE contractor must obtain DOE approval for the methodology used to prepare
the DSA unless a listed Appendix A methodology is used; DOE then reviews the DSA for adequacy, doc-
uments its approval in a SER, and approves TSRs derived from the DSA. Accordingly, NRC BEPU prece-
dent should be treated as persuasive technical and regulatory precedent for structuring an HFIR BEPU
methodology, while the acceptance decision rests with DOE through the HFIR safety-basis approval pro-
cess.
NRC precedent is particularly relevant for the statistical interpretation of thermal-margin acceptance crite-
ria. NRC Standard Review Plan Section 4.4, “Thermal and Hydraulic Design,” identifies the conventional
thermal-margin criterion for departure from nucleate boiling ratio (DNBR), critical heat flux ratio (CHFR),
or critical power ratio (CPR) correlations as demonstrating at least a 95% probability at a 95% confidence
level that the limiting hot fuel rod does not experience departure from nucleate boiling (DNB) or boiling
transition during applicable normal-operation or anticipated-operational-occurrence conditions (U.S. Nu-
clear Regulatory Commission 2007). For HFIR, the analogous BEPU formulation would define the rele-
vant safety metric for the limiting fuel plate or hot channel, propagate justified input and model uncertain-
ties through the thermal-hydraulic analysis, and demonstrate that the applicable HFIR safety limit is not

 3

exceeded with a specified high probability, such as a 95% nonexceedance probability, with an appropriate
confidence level. In this sense, the NRC 95% probability precedent provides a defensible statistical struc-
ture for replacing deterministic stacking of uncertainty factors.
It is worth noting that this 95% probability of core failure avoidance applies to the limiting operating con-
ditions considered in the HSSHTC safety analyses, with all process variables resolved unfavorably includ-
ing additional margin for instrumentation and other uncertainties (Oak Ridge National Laboratory 2025).
The statistical analysis demonstrates that, even under these bounding conditions, the core non-failure prob-
ability remains 95% or greater.
The specific application of this 95% precedent to HFIR, including failure definition, uncertainty distribu-
tions, and confidence treatment, is provided in Section 2.

 2. STATISTICAL METHODOLOGY IMPLEMENTATION
2.1 BASELINE HSSHTC INPUT FILES
The use of HSSHTC to support the HFIR SLs and LCSs is described in Chapter 4 of the SAR (Oak Ridge
National Laboratory 2025). These calculations were updated in (C-HFIR-2019-011, n.d.) to support the
reintroduction of fuel-plate spacing combs at the core inlet. C-HFIR-2026-001, Rev. 0 (C-HFIR-2026-
001, n.d.), performed a sensitivity study to support unreviewed safety question determination (USQD) in
response to potential inadequacy of the documented safety analysis (PISA), which identified a potential
nonconservatism in the treatment of U8 (heat transfer coefficient uncertainty factor (U-factor)). This sen-
sitivity study determined that increasing the U8 value from the 0.9 currently used in the referenced safety
calculations to higher values (e.g., 1.0 or 1.1) reduces the burnout margin calculated by HSSHTC.
The calculation inputs from C-HFIR-2026-001 with U8 = 1.1 are used as the baseline inputs for the
present statistical analysis. Specifically, the “Case 2 (Flux-to-Flow)” inputs at beginning of cycle (BOC)
produced the lowest burnout power margin relative to the relevant SL/LCS. The present study applied
statistical analysis to this BOC burnup point as well as the end of cycle (EOC) burnup point to examine
whether the impact of U-factors differs across the cycle.
In the C-HFIR-2026-001 analyses, separate “inner fuel element (IFE)” and “outer fuel element (OFE)”
cases were analyzed in which either the IFE or OFE U25 factors were applied but not both. This effectively
forced the hot spot to occur in the element where the U25 factors were applied. Additional “assembly”
cases were analyzed which applied both sets of U25 at once. These “assembly” input files were used as
the baseline input files for the present study because it was desirable to allow the code to determine the hot
spot location and burnout power margin based on both elements simultaneously.
2.2 WILKS NONPARAMETRIC ORDER STATISTICS
The statistical objective of this BEPU analysis is to demonstrate, to a probability p, that the figure of merit
(FOM) (the burnout power ratio Qratio ) for Case 2 (Flux-to-Flow) is above the SL of 1.36. This is expressed
as a tolerance statement: with confidence level γ, at least a fraction p of the population of all possible
HSSHTC realizations satisfies the acceptance criterion.
Wilks’ formula (Wilks 1941) provides a purely nonparametric method for constructing such a one-sided
tolerance interval directly from random samples, without assuming any particular distribution for the FOM.
This ensures that p is appropriately satisfied even if the FOM distribution deviates from, say, a normal dis-
tribution. The cost is a larger required sample size compared with a parametric approach; effectively, the
nonparametric approach must take an additional "penalty" in terms of increased sample size in order to
ensure the desired level of confidence is met generically, independent of the distribution shape (Croarkin
and Tobias 2002). However, for BEPU regulatory acceptance, this conservatism in the statistical method is
generally desirable because its validity does not depend on the distribution shape matching a pre-assumed
shape. Furthermore, parametric approaches require well-defined values with known distributions, which
is not the case in the present application due to lack of detailed enough knowledge of the input uncertainty
distributions. A non-parametric approach is more suitable given this lack of knowledge as well.

2.3 NONFAILURE PROBABILITY
The HFIR core contains a total of 540 fuel plates (171 in the IFE and 369 in the OFE). As discussed in
Section 1, there is strong NRC precedent for a 95% nonfailure probability criterion. However, this should
not be interpreted as the single-plate probability, but rather as the probability that none of the fuel plates in
the core fail the SL.
In most reactor designs, the fuel plates or rods in the core are heterogeneous in terms of power peaking
factors, flow rates, and others, experienced by each rod or plate. This means that, by design, a small pop-
ulation of rods/plates can be identified as the most limiting (i.e., the most likely to fail first if a fuel failure
occurs). In this type of core design, the statistical analysis can be limited to only these rods/plates or even a
single most limiting rod/plate, as sufficiently encapsulating the overall failure probability of all rods/plates
in the core.
However, because of the unique azimuthal symmetry of the HFIR core design, all 171 IFE plates have
identical nominal power and hydraulic conditions and can all be considered equally likely to fail. The
same is true for the 369 OFE plates. This hinders the ability to reduce the population of potentially lim-
iting plates which is possible for other reactors.
If all 540 plates are assumed to have a nominally equal chance of being limiting, then a per-plate nonfail-
ure probability pplate must be defined that corresponds to the desired overall core failure probability pcore
(e.g., 95%). The probability that zero plates fail is

Each HSSHTC run calculates the burnout response of a single plate. Therefore, a probability criterion of
p = 0.9999 in the statistical analysis provides a conservatively bounding means of satisfying pcore = 0.95
for all 540 plates. This highly restrictive p = 0.9999 criterion could be relaxed in future BEPU HSSHTC
applications through the following considerations:
 • A 9% neutron flux tilt allowance is incorporated into the U3 factor. Sections 4.4.2.11 and 4.4.4.3.2
 of the HFIR SAR discuss that, although the fuel grading and control element design of HFIR min-
 imizes radial or azimuthal flux tilts, the 9% flux tilt allowance provides flexibility in experimental
 loading which has the potential to perturb the spatial flux distribution. This flux tilt allowance is ap-
 plied as a 9% hot-plate power penalty in the HSSHTC SL analyses, and has been retained as a fixed
 9% penalty in the statistical analyses as well (Section 3.2.1). However, in reality, only a subset of
 fuel plates (e.g., those in the vicinity of certain experiments) would be expected to experience this
 +9% overpower condition even in a worst-case experimental loading. This could be credited to re-
 duce the population of potentially limiting fuel plates (N in Eq. 4), which would reduce pplate . In
 addition to radial flux tilts, experiments can induce axial flux tilts as well; such an approach should
 consider both effects when justifying the pplate assumptions.
 • The IFE and OFE plates are exposed to different conditions (e.g., power distributions, geometry, bur-
 nup, etc.). Therefore, one element is nominally more limiting than the other at a given core burnup
 point. Therefore, it may be justifiable to reduce N to only the plates in the nominally limiting ele-
 ment (N = 171 or N = 369) depending on the burnup point.
Either method could result in a p criterion between 0.95 and 0.9999. Results are presented at both of these
endpoints to explore the effect of p on the final burnout power ratio result.

2.4 CONFIDENCE LEVEL
Although NRC SRP Section 4.4 recommends 95% as the confidence level (in conjunction with 95% prob-
ability), this confidence criterion should be understood as a minimum statistical demonstration rather than
an upper bound on conservatism. A higher confidence level, such as 99% confidence, would generally
be more conservative because it requires greater statistical assurance that the same population-level ac-
ceptance criterion is satisfied. The practical limitation is that higher confidence levels require additional
samples or higher-order tolerance-limit statistics, which can increase computational cost. Therefore, if the
computational burden is acceptable, the use of a higher confidence level can provide additional statistical
margin while remaining consistent with the intent of the NRC precedent.
HSSHTC calculations execute extremely quickly, and the statistical samples in this study were executed in
parallel (this is possible because all samples are independent). On the Kayway cluster, using 48 processor
cores, the case with the largest sample size (p = 0.9999, γ = 0.99, r = 6, N = 131081) required approx-
imately 20 min of wall clock time to complete. This makes even these highly restrictive statistical settings
tractable for analysis.
Based on limited testing, γ = 0.99 appeared to provide a small increase in burnout power ratio over γ =
0.95, and therefore γ = 0.99 was chosen for this study. Further study could more thoroughly quantify the
impact of γ on the final burnout power ratio result.

2.5 NONPARAMETRIC ORDER
In this context, order r, often called the Wilks order or nonparametric order, controls which upper-tail sam-
ple value is used as the one-sided tolerance bound: r = 1 uses the single maximum, while r > 1 uses the
r-th largest value Y(N−r+1) (Section 2.2). Increasing r also increases the minimum sample size (N), mean-
ing that more samples are required to achieve the desired probability and confidence level.

 7

Using r = 1 makes the tolerance limit depend on the single most extreme realization, thus anchoring the
bound entirely to the worst observed outcome. Although this might appear conservative, the (p, γ) cover-
age and confidence guarantees are equivalent across all valid choices of r satisfying Eq. (1); what r = 1
actually maximizes is sensitivity to chance behavior in one sample (e.g., an unusually pessimistic com-
bination of inputs or a numerical artifact in one run), thereby making the reported bound more variable
across repeated sampling campaigns rather than systematically more or less restrictive.
The benefit of a higher order is stability (reduced variance) of the final FOM result. As with the confidence
interval, the fast execution time of HSSHTC ensures that higher r values (therefore higher N) remain prac-
tical in terms of execution time. The choice of r = 6 in this study (Table 1) was arbitrary to try and balance
between FOM stability and runtime; future work could explore the impact of r more rigorously.

2.6 TREATMENT OF FAILED HSSHTC CASES
A small number of statistical samples failed during HSSHTC execution. The failure rate was less than
0.1% in all analyses performed. These failures involved a fatal exception (i.e., code failure) during HSSHTC
execution; the precise cause could not be immediately determined because no useful error message was
provided by the code. Inspection of the failed samples did not reveal a clear trend with respect to sampled
input values, and there was no observable evidence that the failed cases were more likely to be limiting
(i.e., low power ratio) vs. nonfailed cases. An extremely conservative assumption could have been made
that all failed cases would have produced limiting power ratio results (e.g., less than the safety limit), but
this was not considered to be appropriate for the reason given above and would have produced an unreal-
istic and dramatically skewed final Wilks result. Instead, these cases were simply omitted from the Dakota
uncertainty quantification toolkit (Dakota) statistical post-processing, and the statistical results presented in
this report are only based on nonfailed cases.
Refactoring efforts will be performed on the HSSHTC code in the near future and will include more robust
error handling and more comprehensive code testing to potentially reduce or eliminate such failures or, at
the very least, make it clearer as to why such failures occur. This may alleviate potential nonconservatism
concerns related to omitting failed samples from the statistical analysis. However, in the meantime, the
observed failure frequency of less than 0.1% is considered unlikely to significantly impact the statistical
results.

high-performance computing platforms, Dakota has evolved into a general-purpose toolkit encompassing

surrogate modeling. It is released under the GNU Lesser General Public License and is widely used across

Dakota provides a layered architecture: a set of iterators (algorithms) communicates with an application
code through a standardized file-based or library interface, making it largely agnostic to the physics code
being analyzed. This loose coupling allows Dakota to drive virtually any simulation code—including For-

2.8 HSSHTC HOT-SPOT SEARCH METHODOLOGY
The HSSHTC hot-spot result is not based on a single, preselected location on a fuel plate. Rather, the code
performs a set of thermal-hydraulic and hot-spot calculation in which each 2D radial–axial location is as-
sumed to be the hot-spot location in turn. Hot streak and hot spot U-factors are applied accordingly for
each candidate location. The code then determines which candidate location resulted in the most limit-
ing (lowest) margin to burnout. This is then reported in the output file as the final hot spot location and
burnout power for that code iteration. Additional code iterations are then performed by adjusting the total
core power level until the most limiting location reaches the point of burnout within a specified conver-
gence tolerance. (McLain 1967).
Several U-factors (see discussion in Section 3) apply conservative multipliers to the hot spot location as
well as the streak and/or radial stripe containing the hot spot. By sweeping over all possible hot spot loca-
tions, the most severe co-location of unfavorable parameters–hot spot/streak factors, power factors, axial
offset factors– will always be considered, regardless of how unlikely such a co-location actually may be.
This is appropriate for the current conservative methodology because it ensures the worst-possible scenario
is accounted for.
However, this hot-spot approach is not well-suited to a statistical methodology. The statistical methodol-
ogy seeks to characterize the realistic variation in plate conditions accounting for random uncertainties,
including spatial randomness. An ideal statistical approach would, for example, apply random perturba-
tions in fuel loading and other parameters to each 2D radial–axial location on the plate independently. A
single thermal-hydraulic calculation could then be carried out to find the heat flux and burnout margin at
each 2D location, and the most limiting location would be reported as the hot spot. This would allow for
realistic characterization of the hot spot and burnout margin variation from plate to plate, without artifi-
cially applying hot spot/streak factors to the worst possible 2D location.
This more ideal statistical approach is not possible in the current HSSHTC executable. Significant code
restructuring and modification would be required to accomplish this. Instead, the present analysis consti-
tutes essentially a hybrid of statistical and conservative or legacy assumptions in terms of how hot spot and
hot streak factors are applied. This will impact the burnout power and hot spot location results presented
in this study, and it is recommended to explore code modifications to allow a more statistically suitable
spatial randomization approach in the future.

Normal (Gaussian) distributions govern many uncertain or random physical processes and may be particu-

For a two-sided normal distribution, approximately 95% of the area lies within 1.96 standard deviations of
the mean. The standard deviation can be chosen to satisfy Assumption 2. Each tail contains 2.5% of the
total area prior to truncation. After truncation, the remaining 95% of the distribution is renormalized to a

Most of the fuel fabrication U-factor distributions were defined using this approach. Several required spe-

3.1.1 Segregation factors
The fuel segregation factors U18 and U19 refer to spatial clustering of fuel particles within the fuel meat
and the increase in local surface heat flux that can result from this clustering. A perfectly homogeneous
fuel particle distribution would correspond to a segregation factor of 1.0. Per the HFIR fuel specifica-
tions (RRD-FE-3, n.d.), the maximum local fuel concentration is 27% above the nominal fuel concentra-
tion within any single “spot” (i.e., inspection area) along the fuel surface. McLain (McLain 1967) per-
formed an analysis demonstrating that a given percentage of local segregation produces, conservatively,
the same percentage increase in maximum heat flux on the fuel surface. This led to setting U18 and U19
conservatively to 1.27 in the current safety calculations.
The segregation factor cannot physically be below 1.0 (i.e., perfect particle homogeneity), and its distribu-
tion is better described by a one-sided distribution vs. a two-sided normal distribution. The natural cluster-
ing of particles randomly placed throughout a medium, ignoring packing-factor effects and other factors,
should approach a lognormal distribution as the number of particles approaches infinity. This was selected
as the most natural choice for describing the segregation U-factor distributions in the absence of fabrica-
tion and inspection data.
As was assumed for normal distributions, 95% of fabricated components were assumed to satisfy the ac-
ceptance criterion, with the other 5% being rejected. The lognormal distribution was truncated accord-
ingly, then renormalized to increase the total area of the probability distribution from 0.95 to 1.0. This as-
sumption corresponds to P(U ≤ 1.27) = 0.95, or equivalently a failure rate of f = P(U > 1.27) = 0.05.

3.1.5 Nonbonds
Nonbonds (i.e., blisters) can form during the fuel fabrication process and indicate localized detachment of
the cladding and fuel meat layers, which reduces thermal conductance. The current conservative HSSHTC

 14

methodology assumes a worst-case scenario in which the largest allowable nonbond area, based on fuel
specifications, occurs on the cold side of the plate. It is also assumed to occur at the limiting hot spot loca-
tion. The outcome would result in the maximum possible localized diversion of heat flux to the hot side.
By design, the fuel meat thickness varies along the radial arc of the involute fuel plates for flux shaping.
When the fuel meat thickness is reduced, material is removed from the outer (cold) side of the plate and
replaced with a filler. Because of this asymmetric fuel positioning across the plate thickness, the hot- and
cold-side heat fluxes differ even in the absence of nonbonds. This difference depends on the arc position s
along the plate. When a nonbond is present, its impact on the hot/cold heat-flux split is also s dependent.
The HSSHTC code implements the nonbond U-factors (U20 and U21 ) as third-order polynomials in s.
In the HSSHTC executable used for safety analyses, these polynomial coefficients are hard-coded in the
source. These coefficients are derived from thermal calculations using the HEATING code.
ORNL/Technical Memo (TM)-2024/3192 (Hizoum, Borowiec, and Jain 2024) performed COMSOL ther-
mal calculations that verified the HEATING results under equivalent modeling assumptions for the HEU
fuel design. A particularly relevant result for this study is the COMSOL calculation of the baseline hot/-
cold heat-flux split in the absence of nonbonds. Since the fuel meat is closer to the hot side of the plate
than the cold side (due to asymmetric fuel meat tapering), a larger fraction of heat is transferred to the hot
side. This is represented by the η = 0 curves in Figure 3, considering that U20 and U21 represent multipli-
ers on the hot-side and cold-side heat flux, respectively. The presence of nonbonds at the hot spot location
will further impact these distributions by driving heat away from the nonbond toward the opposite side of
the plate. It was found by the authors of ORNL/TM-2024/3192 that the U20 and U21 curves used in the
current safety calculations (the η = 1 curves in Figure 3) include both effects (asymmetric fuel positioning
and nonbonds) simultaneously.
For the present study, it was assumed that the nonbonds size and its location relative to the hot spot can
vary, resulting in U20 and U21 distributions between the η = 0 (no nonbonds) and η = 1 (worst-case non-
bonds) curves described above. The distributions were also extrapolated to negative η values, which repre-
sents the possibility of nonbonds occurring on the hot side of the plate rather than the cold side; this would
suppress rather than enhance the hot spot surface heat flux value.

inspection of historical datasets or through analysis of HFIR operating data, but this approach was not in-
vestigated in the present study.
Ruggles further identifies specific model areas where uncertainty quantification is particularly challeng-
ing for BEPU application of HSSHTC, including oxide growth behavior and the flow split/flow response

used by the steady-state model. This observation supports treating oxide-related and flow-related uncer-
tainty factors, along with any derived parameters that strongly influence those phenomena, as priority tar-

bration. (Ruggles 2022)

corresponds to 0.95 core failure probability, as discussed in Section 2.
Burnout power results for a 0.95 plate probability are shown as well, requiring 259 samples (Table 1). This

analysis rather than all 540 fuel plates being considered as potentially limiting. Section 2.3 discussed ap-

sult is included to illustrate an upper limit on how much additional burnout margin could be gained by pur-

Both the BOC and EOC burnup state points were analyzed for the Case 2 (flux-to-flow) HSSHTC analysis

The prevalence of axial cell 29 can be attributed to the U25 factor, which modifies the power applied specif-

This indicates that the bottom cell is frequently limiting even without a U25 -driven increase in local power.
This is partially due to the bottom cell having the highest coolant temperature and lowest pressure in the
channel.
It is important to note that the prevalence of this specific hot spot location is also a consequence of the
HSSHTC hot spot methodology discussed in Section 2.8. As discussed in that section, the current HSSHTC
executable does not allow specifying truly realistic 2D spatial variations in fuel loading and other factors,
which would be necessary to properly quantify the true expected distribution of hot spot locations. Rather,
the current hot spot methodology remains geared toward a conservative worst-case hot spot location ap-
proach. Recommendations will be given in the Conclusions for code modifications which may allow a
more realistic distribution of hot spot locations–and resulting burnout power levels–to be determined in
future work.

4.4 PARTIAL RANK CORRELATION COEFFICIENTS
Figure 7 shows the partial rank correlation coefficient (PRCC) values for each input variable. The PRCC
between an input variable and the output (burnout power ratio FOM) quantifies the monotonic association
between that input and the output after removing the influence of all other inputs. It ranges from −1 to +1,
wherein values near ±1 indicate a strong influence of that input parameter on the output, values near zero
indicate little or no association and the sign indicates the direction of the relationship (positive: output in-
creases with the input; negative: output decreases with the input). Because it operates on ranks rather than
raw values, the PRCC is robust to nonlinear, but monotonic, input–output relationships and is relatively
insensitive to outliers, thus making it a reliable nonparametric sensitivity measure for this application.
The PRCC values show that most of the highest-impact parameters relate to as-fabricated fuel character-
istics, especially U18 (hot side segregation), U20 (hot side nonbonds), U24 (hot streak factor), U25 (axial
positioning), and enmin (minimum narrow channel thickness). Although the hot side factors U18 and U20
were highly influential, the cold side factors U19 and U21 had almost no impact.

 5. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK
This study provides a proof of concept for application of BEPU to HSSHTC. A key accomplishment was
to develop a calculation framework and scripting capability to automate this process, including defining
the statistical parameters, performing random sampling on the input factors, modifying the HSSHTC input
file, driving the simulations in parallel, and collecting and processing the results. Although future improve-
ments are required to apply this analysis to safety calculations, this initial effort also demonstrated the clear
potential of BEPU methods for increasing the safety margin of the HSSHTC calculations.
In some cases, decades of HFIR operational data and high-resolution simulation data (e.g., from COMSOL
or other sources) could be used to establish and justify more precise probability density function (PDF)
shapes and ranges. Manufacturing data (e.g., quantifying PDF distributions of fuel loading, particle segre-
gation, fuel length, etc.) could also be used, leveraging decades of fabrication experience for the U3 O8 – Al
fuel currently used in HFIR.
The method used by HSSHTC to apply hot streak and hot spot factors is geared toward a traditional un-
certainty mindset of finding the worst combination of possible options, that is, applying worst-case condi-
tions to each r–z location in turn. This approach could be replaced with a more probabilistic treatment that
would, for example, apply randomly sampled fuel-loading variations to each r–z cell while enforcing the
relevant restrictions on plate-, streak-, and spot-concentration limits from the manufacturing specifications.
This would allow the failure probability for a given plate to be evaluated more realistically, rather than re-
taining legacy conservatisms inherent to the current hot spot method. Such a change would better align
with the statistical uncertainty framework and could further reduce excess conservatism.
Improvements to the statistical implementation could be made in future analyses. This may include use of
a self-normalized importance sampling strategy to more efficiently and robustly target the tails of the out-
put probability distributions. This technique would be especially suitable to the present application which
targets a very high probability level (99.99%) and would lessen the number of samples required while pre-
dicting the final value more consistently. Another statistical aspect not considered in the present work is
potential correlation between input parameters. For example, the hot- and cold-side segregation factors
(U18 and U19 may be considered directly correlated; a high value of one implies a low value of the other
and vice versa. Other input parameters may be directly or loosely correlated as well. This should be ac-
counted for in the sampling process to better characterize the expected population of plate behaviors. A
third improvement would be to use a secondary feature importance method (e.g. Sobol’ indices) to double-
check the partial rank correlation coefficient results, as the later on its own may provide misleading results
for some (e.g. non-monotonic) input-output relationships.
The RELAP5-based HFIR transient safety analyses rely on the effective hot channel gap width from the
HSSHTC calculation output as well as several of the same U-factors. Future work could apply statisti-
cal analysis to the RELAP5 calculations as well, either as a separate calculation or as a second step after
HSSHTC within the current calculation process.

APPENDIX A. STATISTICAL CALCULATION IMPLEMENTATION

 A-1

 APPENDIX A. STATISTICAL CALCULATION IMPLEMENTATION
The statistical calculations were driven by a series of Python scripts that were generated for the purposes
of this study. The scripts are contained in a private Git repository located at https://code-int.ornl.gov/awa/
pyHSSHTC which can be made available upon request. The primary steps include the following:
 • Reading of a JSON settings file containing U-factor distribution information and statistical settings
 • Preparation of Dakota input files
 • Execution of Dakota in "pre-run" mode
 • Preparation of HSSHTC input files for all statistical samples
 • Execution of all HSSHTC cases in parallel
 • Processing of HSSHTC output files
 • Execution of Dakota in "post-run" mode
 • Tabulation and plotting of results
The workflow is built on two Python packages developed specifically for this project: pyDakota, which
manages all interactions with the Dakota executable and handles input distribution parameterization, and
pyHSSHTC, which handles HSSHTC input file generation, execution, and output parsing.

A.1 PREPROCESSING: INPUT PREPARATION AND SAMPLE GENERATION
The preprocessing stage is managed by the dakotaProcessor class in pyDakota. Each HSSHTC uncer-
tainty case is defined by two input files: a HSSHTC base input template (.base.inp) containing <PARAM>
placeholders for uncertain parameters, and a JSON specification file (.dakota.json) describing the prob-
ability distributions of each uncertain variable. The JSON specification file for the results in Section 4 is
shown in Figure 13.

 Figure 13. JSON specification file for this study

The JSON file uses an engineering-friendly schema in which normal uncertain variables are specified by a
nominal mean, 95% coverage interval bounds, and a truncation flag, rather than by the means and standard

 A-2

deviations required by Dakota directly. The pyDakota preprocessing layer (the distributionSpecs
module) resolves these engineering specifications into Dakota-native parameters—computing the implied
standard deviation from the coverage probability and bounds using the inverse normal CDF—and writes
the completed Dakota input file. Lognormal variables are specified by a shift value and a log-standard de-
viation, with a limit value and failure rate used to define the tail of the distribution.
With the Dakota input file fully populated, dakotaProcessor.dakotaPreprocess() invokes Dakota in
pre-run mode:
  dakota -i normal.dakota.in -pre_run ::params.in
Dakota generates all N sample parameter vectors and writes them to a single flat tabular file (params.in).
The dakotaProcessor reads the file, creates a numbered subdirectory for each sample (testDir/1/,
testDir/2/, . . . , testDir/N/), and copies the base input template into each subdirectory. The Wilks or-
der r and confidence level γ are configurable at runtime and are injected into the Dakota input file before
the pre-run step, allowing the minimum sample size to be recomputed automatically for each study config-
uration.

A.2 PARALLEL HSSHTC EXECUTION
The N HSSHTC samples are executed concurrently using Python’s concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor.
A configurable number of worker processes (up to max_processes, bounded by the number of available
CPU cores minus one) consume samples from a bounded queue. The queue maintains up to 4×max_processes
in-flight tasks at any time, preventing memory exhaustion while keeping all cores saturated.
Each worker invocation calls process_sample(), which performs three steps:
 1. Input generation. The hfirPhysics class reads the Dakota-supplied parameter vector from the
 per-sample params.i.in file and writes a complete HSSHTC input deck (code.inp) into the sam-
 ple subdirectory. Lognormal variables sampled by Dakota in the shifted space (V = U −δ) are shifted
 back to physical space (U = V + δ) before being written to the input file. Additional fixed parame-
 ters (mesh resolution nr, nz) are injected at this stage. U25 isotopic data—fuel loading distributions
 required for HSSHTC power-shape calculations—are read from external data files and interpolated
 onto the HSSHTC mesh; the processing mode (simple or regular) and data file paths are config-
 urable.
 2. Code execution. runHSSHTC.runHSSHTC() changes to the sample subdirectory and launches the
 HSSHTC executable via a shell command, piping a name file to its standard input and redirecting all
 console output to a per-sample log file (hsshtc_i.log). A nonzero exit code raises a RuntimeError
 that is caught by the worker and classified as an ABORTED failure.
 3. Result extraction. HSSHTC_post_processor_simple.writeHSSHTCResultsToDakota() parses
 the HSSHTC output file (code.out), extracts the converged values of QRATIO, VVFR, the hot spot
 element index, and the (i, j) spatial indices, and writes a Dakota format results file (resultsToDakota.out)
 in the sample subdirectory. If the output file is absent or the execution completion marker is not
 found, then the case is classified as NOT_RUN or ABORTED, respectively, and the fallback FOM val-
 ues (configurable via failedCaseValues) are written in place of FAIL.
Completion is tracked by a progress counter that emits timing checkpoints at configurable percentage in-
tervals and writes a machine-readable timing log (progress_timing.log) to the run directory for post-
hoc throughput analysis.

 A-3

A.3 POST-PROCESSING AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS
Upon completion of all samples, dakotaProcessor.dakotaPostprocess() collates the individual
resultsToDakota.out files into the tabular results file expected by Dakota’s post-run mode before in-
voking the following:
  dakota -i normal.dakota.in -post_run results.dat::
Dakota computes moment statistics (i.e., mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis) for each FOM,
constructs empirical CDFs, and evaluates the Wilks tolerance bounds at all requested probability levels.
The Python layer parses the Dakota output file to extract the mean, standard deviation, and Wilks upper
bounds for each probability level, prints a runtime statistics summary—including total samples, successes,
aborts, elapsed time and rate—and writes a compact text summary (dakotaSummary.txt) to the run di-
rectory.

A.4 OUTPUT AND VISUALIZATION
If matplotlib is available, the plotMaker utility generates scatter plots of each uncertain input against
each FOM to support sensitivity screening, histograms of the FOM distributions with vertical lines mark-
ing the sample mean and Wilks bounds, and normal distribution overlays for comparison. All plots are
saved to the run directory alongside the Dakota tabular results file, the timing log, and the Dakota standard
output and error files. The entire run directory tree is organized under a hierarchy that mirrors the input di-
rectory structure (testDir/Dakota/year/burnStep/runName/), making it straightforward to compare
results across burn steps, uncertainty configurations, and Wilks order settings.

Decreasing the hot-plate average loading to 0.99 in the U4 distribution did not further reduce the power
ratio; the power ratio increased slightly (by 0.00023) in this case. A similar trend was observed for U5 , in
which an average loading of 0.99 was more limiting than 1.01. This implies that minimizing the lower-half
U4 and maximizing the lower-half U5 does not necessarily produce the most limiting result; the average
plate loading has a separate (smaller) effect that should be considered as well.
