1. Abstract

This research program established a transformative framework for the discovery and design of
mechanical metamaterials, which are architected structures engineered to control physical phenomena
like sound and vibration in ways natural materials cannot. To overcome the traditional reliance on
trial-and-error, the project developed an interpretable Artificial Intelligence (AI) framework that moves
beyond "black box" models to reveal the specific geometric patterns—such as "unit-cell templates"—that
govern a material’s performance. A major breakthrough was the development of a hierarchical design
method, which allows a single material to block vibrations across multiple frequency ranges
simultaneously by layering patterns at different scales without them interfering with one another. This was
further expanded to include irregular, graph-based designs that use spanning tree algorithms to ensure
structural connectivity while allowing for customized, direction-dependent properties like stiffness and
acoustic impedance.

Beyond design, the project addressed the practicalities of real-world production by developing uncertainty
quantification techniques that account for manufacturing defects and material variability, reducing the
need for expensive physical testing by orders of magnitude. To speed up the discovery process, the team
implemented Gaussian Process Regression and other surrogate models that provide accurate
performance predictions at a fraction of the traditional computational cost. The AI-generated designs were
successfully validated through fabrication of physical samples and wave propagation experiments,
confirming their ability to accurately guide or reflect waves as predicted. By contributing these tools and
high-quality FAIR benchmark datasets to the wider scientific community, this work provides a scalable
foundation for advancing technologies in aerospace vibration control, medical imaging, and noise
reduction.

2. Brief technical description of findings and outcomes.

The project established a comprehensive framework for the discovery, design, and characterization of
mechanical metamaterials by integrating interpretable machine learning (ML) with physics-based
simulations and experimental validation. The primary hypothesis driving the work was that hidden,
discoverable patterns exist within material structures that can be distilled into simple, transferable rules to
govern the relationship between geometry and bulk physical properties. To address this, the team
proposed the Learning Refined Compositional Rules (LRCR) framework, a coarse-to-fine sampling
method designed to bridge the gap between length scales while overcoming the exponential
computational costs associated with high-resolution material design.

Key Computational Approaches and Interpretable ML: A cornerstone of the project was the
development and application of the Generalized Optimal Sparse Decision Trees (GOSDT) algorithm
and the shape-frequency feature representation. Unlike traditional "black box" neural networks, which
often performed poorly on these scientific datasets, GOSDT provided transparent if-then rules that
achieved over 97% accuracy in predicting bandgaps—frequency ranges where vibrations are

blocked—using simple geometric motifs like "unit-cell templates". The team extended this to the Unit Cell
Template framework, which distinguishes between "fixed" regions necessary for performance and "free"
regions that allow for design flexibility, such as weight reduction through "void" inclusions.

High-Speed Surrogate Modeling for Dispersion Relations: To address the immense computational
cost of traditional physics simulations, the team developed a specialized Gaussian Process Regression
(GPR) surrogate model. This model acts as a high-speed approximation for computing dispersion
relations—the mathematical map of how waves travel through a material. Notably, this GPR model is
resolution-independent and representation-invariant, meaning it can learn from simple designs and
accurately predict the performance of complex, finely detailed structures nearly eight times faster than
traditional methods. Unlike standard AI, it also provides uncertainty estimates and is differentiable,
making it a powerful tool for gradient-based design optimization.

Hierarchical Design, Irregular Materials, and Uncertainty: The research achieved significant
breakthroughs in multiscale engineering and manufacturing reliability:

 ●​ Hierarchical Scaling: The project demonstrated a novel multiscale technique where
 coarse-scale bandgap objectives remained robust even when fine-scale features were
 introduced. This "scale-separation effect" allowed for the design of materials capable of blocking
 vibrations across multiple, distinct frequency ranges simultaneously.
 ●​ Graph-Based Irregular Designs: To move beyond periodic grids, the team implemented a
 framework using graph algorithms and spanning trees. This ensured structural interconnectivity in
 irregular designs, facilitating customized anisotropy (direction-dependent properties) while
 guaranteeing manufacturability.
 ●​ Uncertainty Quantification (UQ): Recognizing the sensitivity of metamaterials to manufacturing
 defects, the team used Polynomial Chaos Expansion to develop surrogate models. This approach
 reduced the sampling requirements for characterizing material performance by two to three
 orders of magnitude compared to traditional brute-force testing.

Experimental Validation and FAIR Data: The project results were rigorously validated through physical
sample generation and laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV) experiments. Physical tests of hierarchical steel
structures confirmed that numerically predicted bandgaps were accurately realized in real-world finite
structures. Finally, to support the broader scientific community, the project developed the MetaMine portal
and an extensible metamaterials ontology to ensure all generated data is Findable, Accessible,
Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR). Significant datasets were also contributed to the UCI Machine
Learning Repository to serve as benchmarks for future AI research in materials science.
