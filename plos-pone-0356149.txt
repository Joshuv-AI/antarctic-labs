A single-aortotomy end-to-side technique simplifies murine aortic transplantation

Hao Dun, Maura Sticco-Ivins, Gianni Bonnici, Hannah Beatty, Benjamin J Kopecky

ABSTRACT
Background: Transplant arteriosclerosis remains a major cause of late graft failure after solid organ transplantation. Murine aortic transplantation models have provided important insights into the mechanisms underlying chronic allograft rejection, but the surgical procedure remains technically demanding. We developed a modified end-to-side aortic transplantation technique utilizing a single recipient aortotomy to simplify vascular anastomosis in mice. Methods: Recipient infrarenal aortas were occluded proximally and distally using microvascular clamps. A single longitudinal aortotomy was created in the recipient infrarenal aorta. The proximal and distal ends of a donor thoracic aortic graft were independently anastomosed end-to-side to this single opening. Following completion of the anastomoses, a central ligature was placed between the donor graft inlet and outlet tracts to direct blood flow through the graft. Operative outcomes were compared with those of the conventional (two-aortotomy) end-to-side technique. Results: Donor harvest times were similar between groups. Recipient preparation time was shorter with the single-aortotomy technique (10.07 ± 0.95 vs. 10.87 ± 1.17 min, p = 0.033). Anastomosis time was significantly reduced compared with the conventional technique (14.73 ± 1.24 vs. 20.40 ± 1.57 min, p < 0.0001). Graft patency was 93% versus 87%, thrombosis occurred in one animal per group, and survival was comparable between techniques. Conclusion: A single-aortotomy end-to-side configuration significantly reduces anastomosis time while maintaining graft patency, survival, and the ability to reproduce transplant vasculopathy. This technique provides a simplified and reproducible approach for murine aortic transplantation.

Introduction The technique of abdominal heterotopic aortic transplantation in mice, as a model of chronic transplant rejection, was first described by Koulack et al. [ 1 ] in 1995. This method involves performing two distinct end-to-end vascular anastomoses, replacing a segment of the recipient infrarenal aorta with a 1.0 cm donor thoracic aortic graft ( S1 Fig ). However, its widespread application has been limited by the technical complexity of the vascular anastomoses, a challenge further exacerbated by size discrepancies between the donor thoracic aorta and the recipient abdominal aorta [ 2 ]. To overcome these technical challenges, the sleeve [ 3 ] and cuff [ 4 ] techniques were developed and have been increasingly adopted as alternative approaches. A comparative study demonstrated that both the sleeve and cuff techniques are superior to the conventional end-to-end suture method in murine aortic transplantation [ 5 ]. Nevertheless, despite these modifications, the procedures remain technically demanding and are particularly susceptible to thrombosis formation due to the small vessel diameter in mice. Moreover, a major limitation of the end-to-end anastomosis approach is the requirement for complete separation of the recipient infrarenal aorta from the inferior vena cava (IVC). This step constitutes one of the most technically challenging aspects of the procedure and carries a high risk of vascular injury, which may lead to severe intraoperative bleeding. Thus, the end-to- side technique emerged as a simplified approach for performing aortic transplantation in mice [ 2 , 6 ]. In this method, two-aortotomies are created in the recipient infrarenal aorta and subsequently anastomosed to the proximal and distal ends of the donor aortic graft ( S2 Fig ). The segment of the recipient infrarenal aorta between the two anastomoses is then transected [ 2 ] or ligated [ 6 ], thereby directing blood flow through the donor aortic conduit and ensuring graft perfusion. Compared with the traditional end-to- end technique, the end-to- side approach offers several advantages . By clamping the recipient infrarenal aorta and IVC together, it eliminates the need for vessel separation, thereby minimizing the risk of bleeding. In addition, this technique permits implantation of a relatively longer donor aortic segment, providing more tissue for subsequent histological and molecular analyses. More importantly, it overcomes the challenge of size discrepancies between donor and recipient vessels, making the anastomosis technically less demanding. Consequently, the end-to-side model has been reported to achieve a success rate exceeding 90% [ 6 ]. However, the conventional end-to-side technique requires the creation of two aortotomies in the recipient infrarenal aorta ( S2 Fig ). An intact segment of aorta must be preserved between the two aortotomies, which substantially limits the available working length of the recipient infrarenal aorta, particularly when two vascular clamps are applied. To conserve space, some investigators [ 6 ], including our group, have used tie ligation instead of clamps to occlude the recipient aorta. Despite this modification, the improvement in available aortic length remains limited. Moreover, tie ligation may cause the aorta and IVC to collapse together when they are not separated, further complicating the anastomosis and increasing technical difficulty. Whether recipient reconstruction can be simplified further while preserving biological fidelity of the model remains unknown. Herein, we describe a modified single-aortotomy end-to-side aortic transplantation technique that addresses several limitations of conventional approaches. Rather than creating two separate recipient aortotomies, a single-aortotomy is used to accommodate both the proximal and distal ends of the donor graft. Following completion of the anastomoses, a central ligature separates the inlet and outlet tracts and directs blood flow through the donor graft ( S3 Fig ). This configuration conserves recipient aortic working length, simplifies the operative field, and reduces the complexity of vascular reconstruction. We hypothesized that this modification would decrease operative time while maintaining graft patency, survival, and the capacity to support development of transplant vasculopathy. Materials and methods Animals Male C57BL/6J and C57BL/6J-BM12 mice, aged 8–12 weeks, were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). Animals were housed under standard conditions at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus Animal Facility. Food and water were provided ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus (Protocol #01386). Animals were monitored daily during the first postoperative week and at least three times weekly thereafter for body weight, activity, grooming behavior, mobility, wound healing, and signs of distress. Humane endpoints included >15% body weight loss, persistent hindlimb paralysis, severe lethargy, labored breathing, inability to obtain food or water, or other conditions judged by veterinary staff to compromise animal welfare. Experimental design Fifteen syngeneic B6 → B6 aortic transplants were performed using the single-aortotomy end-to-side technique and compared with fifteen syngeneic B6 → B6 transplants performed using the conventional two-aortotomy end-to-side technique. Operative outcomes including donor harvest time, recipient preparation time, anastomosis time, graft patency, thrombosis, bleeding complications, and survival were compared between groups ( S1 Table ). To evaluate whether the modified technique supported development of transplant vasculopathy, four allogeneic B6 → BM12 transplants and four syngeneic B6 → B6 transplants were performed using the new technique and analyzed 100 days after transplantation. All procedures were performed by a single microsurgeon (HD). Anesthesia Animals were administered subcutaneous extended-release buprenorphine (1 mg/kg), followed by intraperitoneal ketamine hydrochloride (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (8 mg/kg) dissolved in saline. The depth of anesthesia was assessed using a toe-pinch reflex test. Animals that withdrew their foot in response to the stimulus were considered inadequately anesthetized and were not subjected to the procedure until an adequate anesthetic depth was achieved. All mice were continuously monitored throughout anesthesia and placed on a temperature-controlled heating-pad to maintain body temperature during the procedure . Donor operation Donor operation was performed as previously described [ 7 ]. Briefly, after laparotomy, the IVC was injected with 2.0 mL of cold saline containing heparin (100 U/mL). The inferior mesenteric artery was then transected to facilitate blood decompression. Subsequently, bilateral thoracotomy was performed along both sides of the thoracic spine, and the anterior chest wall was removed. The thymus was resected to expose the aortic arch and pulmonary artery. The heart was reflected to the right, and the left lung was removed to allow full visualization of the aortic arch and descending aorta. The parietal pleura overlying the descending aorta was bluntly dissected. A 6−0 silk ligature was placed around the descending aorta proximal to the diaphragmatic hiatus and secured. The descending aorta was transected distal to the ligature and gently elevated to permit careful dissection of the surrounding connective tissue using fine scissors, proceeding proximally toward the aortic arch. For aortic arch procurement, the three major branches of the left subclavian artery, left common carotid artery, and innominate artery were individually isolated and transected from the arch. The ascending aorta was separated from the pulmonary artery by sharp dissection of the connective tissue and was then transected near its origin from the heart. The harvested donor aorta was immediately placed in ice-cold saline solution and stored until the recipient procedure was completed. Recipient operation After adequately being anesthetized, the recipient mouse was placed in a supine position with the tail oriented toward the operator. A midline laparotomy was performed from the pubis to the xiphoid process, and the abdominal cavity was exposed using a micro-retractor. The intestines were gently exteriorized to the left side and wrapped in saline-moistened gauze. The infrarenal aorta and IVC were carefully dissected free from the surrounding tissues using blunt dissection. Based on anatomic variability, one to two groups of lumbar vessels beneath the infrarenal aorta and IVC were identified and ligated en bloc . An aortotomy was created as previously described [ 7 ] ( Fig 2A ). Briefly, the tip of a 4 mm (3/8) needle was passed longitudinally through the anterior wall of the recipient aorta and partially brought out, then held in place with a needle holder. By gently elevating the needle, the segment of the anterior aortic wall overlying the needle was easily excised by cutting beneath it with fine scissors, creating an approximately 1 mm elliptical aortotomy with smooth, well-trimmed edges ( S2 Video ). The donor aortic graft was positioned on the left side of the recipient abdominal cavity ( Fig 2B ). Postoperative care Immediately after surgery, recipient mice received 1.0 ml of sterile saline subcutaneously for fluid resuscitation. The animals were then placed in an incubator (Pet Brooder 90, Curadle ICU MXB90N) for 24 hours, maintained at 29–30°C with 50% relative humidity. Standard chow and water were provided ad libitum . Surgical success was defined as survival without evidence of hindlimb paralysis throughout the designated observation period. A subset of animals were followed for 100 days after transplantation, a time point previously shown to permit development of cardiac allograft vasculopathy in murine aortic transplant models [ 8 ]. Ultimately, all animals were euthanized and underwent autopsy, followed by histological analysis of the aortic grafts and relevant tissues. Euthanasia methods were approved by IACUC and included isoflurane inhalation and an approved secondary method (exsanguination). All attempts to minimize animal discomfort were taken. Histology Freshly explanted donor aortas were carefully dissected to capture the anastomotic sites. Aortas were flushed with 5 mL ice cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C. Fixed aortas were thrice rinsed with PBS and immediately processed for paraffin embedding by the histology core. For paraffin embedded tissues, 4 µm thick slices were placed on glass slides and stained for Verhoeff-Van Gieson stain (Cancer Diagnostics #SSC1135) per manufacturer’s protocol. Images were obtained on the Olympus VS200 high throughput slide scanning microscope with the 20x objective through the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus Imaging Core. Images were processed and regions of interest selected on the Olyvia software. Histological evidence of vasculopathy was defined as neointimal hyperplasia associated with luminal narrowing within donor graft segments. Statistical analysis All data were presented as mean with 95% confidence interval of the mean. Differences between groups were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s T -test. Discussion The principal finding of this study is that a single-aortotomy end-to-side configuration simplifies murine aortic transplantation while preserving the essential biological characteristics of the model. Compared with the conventional two-aortotomy end-to-side technique, this approach significantly reduced anastomosis time without compromising graft patency, survival, or the development of allograft vasculopathy. These findings suggest that the single-aortotomy model represents a practical refinement of existing murine aortic transplantation techniques. Murine aortic transplantation has evolved through successive technical modifications aimed at reducing operative complexity while maintaining experimental utility. Conventional end-to-end transplantation is limited by donor-recipient size mismatch and the need for extensive dissection to separate the aorta from the inferior vena cava, increasing the risk of bleeding. The subsequent end-to-side approach addressed many of these challenges by permitting simultaneous clamping of the aorta and IVC and avoiding complete vessel separation. However, it still requires creation of two recipient aortotomies and preservation of an intervening aortic segment, which constrains the available operative field and increases reconstructive complexity. Our modification advances this evolution by eliminating the need for two separate recipient arteriotomies and replacing them with a single shared aortotomy. Several technical innovations contribute to the simplification achieved by the single-aortotomy model. The first innovation is the creation of a single recipient aortotomy that accommodates both the proximal (inlet) and distal (outlet) ends of the donor aortic graft. In our experience, generation of the aortotomy is most reliably performed using a 4-mm (3/8) needle passed longitudinally through the anterior wall of the recipient infrarenal aorta and secured with a needle holder. Gentle elevation of the needle creates a stable platform beneath the vessel wall, allowing the intervening tissue to be excised with fine scissors. This maneuver consistently produces a smooth elliptical opening with well-trimmed edges. Because a single opening must ultimately accommodate both donor graft ends, the initial aortotomy may occasionally require extension. When enlargement is necessary, the proximal and/or distal corners can be carefully lengthened to match the dimensions of the donor graft. However, enlargement should be performed conservatively. Excessive extension creates an oversized opening that increases the technical difficulty of the anastomosis, reduces tissue support for suturing, and may increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding. A second innovation is the substantial reduction in vascular reconstruction time achieved by eliminating the need for two separate aortotomies. In the conventional end-to-side technique, two independent arteriotomies must be created, carefully positioned, and subsequently incorporated into two distinct anastomotic fields while preserving an intervening segment of recipient aorta. In contrast, the single-aortotomy configuration consolidates the reconstruction into a single operative field. This eliminates the time required to create and optimize a second recipient opening, reduces repeated vessel manipulation, simplifies graft orientation, and decreases the overall amount of vascular suturing required to complete the reconstruction. These technical advantages translated into a marked reduction in anastomosis time, with reconstruction completed approximately 28% faster than with the conventional two-aortotomy approach. Importantly, this improvement was achieved without compromising graft patency, thrombosis rates, or long-term survival, indicating that procedural simplification does not occur at the expense of surgical reliability. A third innovation is preservation of recipient aortic working length. In the conventional two-aortotomy technique, an intact segment of recipient infrarenal aorta must be maintained between the proximal and distal arteriotomies. Because the available infrarenal aortic segment in mice is inherently short, this requirement significantly restricts the operative workspace, particularly when vascular clamps are present. By utilizing a single-aortotomy, no intervening recipient aortic segment must be preserved. Consequently, a greater proportion of the infrarenal aorta remains available for graft implantation and surgical manipulation. This expanded working space facilitates graft positioning, improves visualization of the operative field, and reduces technical difficulty during vascular reconstruction. A fourth innovation involves the method of vascular occlusion. We apply the distal microvascular clamp first, followed by placement of the proximal clamp. This sequence allows decompression of the IVC during the clamping process. As venous blood drains from the isolated segment, the IVC progressively collapses, creating a substantially clearer operative field around the infrarenal aorta. The resulting improvement in exposure facilitates both aortotomy creation and vascular suturing. Importantly, successful decompression of the IVC requires interruption of lumbar venous inflow. One or two groups of lumbar vessels located beneath the aorta and IVC should therefore be identified and ligated before vascular clamping. Failure to interrupt these tributaries permits continued venous filling of the isolated caval segment, resulting in persistent IVC distension that complicates microsurgical manipulation. Thus, lumbar vessel ligation represents a critical technical step that contributes substantially to visualization and ease of reconstruction. A fifth innovation is the adoption of a fully everting suture technique for both anterior and posterior walls of the anastomosis. In our previous murine transplantation models, posterior walls were commonly reconstructed using inverting sutures placed from within the vessel lumen, whereas anterior walls were completed using an everting approach. Although effective, this method relies on secure exposure of the posterior wall and typically requires two opposing anchor stitches for each anastomosis. In the present configuration, a single-aortotomy serves simultaneously as the anastomotic site for both donor graft ends. Because each donor segment is secured by only one anchor stitch, exposure of the posterior wall for intraluminal suturing becomes technically more challenging. We therefore adopted a fully everting approach in which both anterior and posterior walls are sutured from the external vessel surface. Continuous suturing is performed with the needle entering through the cut edge of the donor aorta and exiting through the cut edge of the recipient aorta. This orientation provides excellent visualization of both vessel edges and facilitates precise placement of each stitch while minimizing the risk of inadvertently catching the opposite vessel wall. The anterior wall is reconstructed from the proximal to distal apex, after which the animal is rotated so that its head faces the operator, allowing the posterior wall to be completed from distal to proximal under direct visualization. This maneuver eliminates the need to flip the graft repeatedly during reconstruction and provides consistent exposure throughout the procedure. In our experience, the combination of dual anchor sutures and a fully everting continuous technique results in accurate vessel alignment, improved visualization, and efficient completion of the anastomosis within the constrained operative field created by the murine infrarenal aorta. A critical consideration for any surgical modification is whether it preserves the biological utility of the model. Histological analysis demonstrated the expected development of allograft vasculopathy in B6 → BM12 transplants, whereas syngeneic controls maintained normal vascular architecture. Furthermore, graft patency and long-term perfusion were maintained throughout the study period. These findings indicate that the modified configuration retains the key biological features necessary for investigations of transplant arteriosclerosis and chronic allograft rejection. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, all procedures were performed by a single experienced microsurgeon, and the learning curve associated with the technique was not formally assessed. Second, quantitative morphometric analysis of vasculopathy was not performed. Future studies will be required to determine whether disease severity is identical between the single- and two-aortotomy configurations. Third, the hemodynamic consequences of the shared anastomotic site were not evaluated. Alterations in local flow patterns or wall shear stress could potentially influence vascular remodeling and warrant further investigation. Conclusion In conclusion, the single-aortotomy end-to-side technique represents a meaningful surgical advance in murine aortic transplantation. By simplifying recipient reconstruction and significantly reducing anastomosis time while preserving graft function and vasculopathy development, this model provides an efficient and reproducible platform for studies of chronic allograft rejection and transplant vasculopathy.
