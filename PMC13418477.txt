Prevention is better than cure: Awareness and acceptance of the HPV vaccine for condyloma acuminata (CA) among college students who self-report no sexual experience
Authors: Qingjing Shao, Min Peng, Xuechun Lin, Yuying Zheng, Yutang Feng, Wujian Ke, Huachun Zou, Xuqi Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) types 6 and 11 cause 90% of condyloma acuminata (CA). Timely vaccination reduces CA-related disease burdens, especially among sexually inexperienced college students, a critical catch-up group for vaccination. Few studies investigate sexually inexperienced college students’ views on CA and HPV vaccination, despite research on general student HPV awareness. This cross-sectional study was conducted in Guangdong in May 2017, and recruited college students who self-identified as having no prior sexual relations. Participants completed a WeChat-based online questionnaire to assess their knowledge of HPV, CA, and acceptance of HPV vaccination for CA. A total of 2037 valid questionnaires were collected and analyzed. Knowledge of CA (26.3%) and HPV (18.9%) was low among college students who self-reported no sexual experience, as was the HPV vaccination rate (2.6%). However, their acceptance of the HPV vaccine for CA was relatively high (54.7%). Respondents were more likely to have received the HPV vaccine for CA if they had heard of CA (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.05–1.85), had actively learned about CA (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.10–2.38), considered the vaccine safe (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.03–3.65), and believed the vaccine effective in preventing CA (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.05–12.59). Sexually inexperienced college students demonstrated insufficient knowledge of CA and HPV vaccines. Higher awareness of the safety and efficacy of the HPV vaccine, as well as knowledge about CA, were positive associated with the willingness to receive the HPV vaccine.KEYWORDS: Condyloma acuminata (CA), sexually transmitted infection (STI), human papillomavirus (HPV), vaccines, college students without sexual experience, vaccine hesitancy, knowledge, acceptance

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a very common sexually transmitted pathogen and pose a significant public health concern. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV strains is the leading cause of cervical and anal malignancies.1 Conversely, sexual transmission of low-risk HPV types 6 and 11 cause 90% of condyloma acuminata (CA).2 Although most of condyloma acuminatum are benign lesions, studies have shown that the risk of progression to cervical cancer in patients with recurrent CA is significantly higher than that in the general population. It is suggested that CA is not a simple skin lesion, but closely related to the occurrence of HPV-related malignant tumors.3–5 In addition, CA can also facilitate the transmission of other sexually transmitted infections such as HIV and syphilis.6,7 Although not as dangerous as cancer, CA is a common sexually transmitted infection (STI) among young men and women (0.36% to 12% among women, 0.27% to 7.9% among men, and even higher among Young men who have sex with men (YMSM)).8–10 Nevertheless, due to the insidious asymptomatic phase and high recurrence rate of CA, the actual infection rate and socioeconomic burden are still underestimated.11–14 Over the years, researchers and the general public have placed a greater emphasis on the ability of HPV vaccination to prevent cancer, whereas the vaccine’s ability to prevent CA has frequently been overlooked or given secondary importance.

Studies have demonstrated that quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines have excellent efficacy in preventing HPV infection and almost complete CA protection in the youngest vaccinated groups,14–16 but price considerations still constrain their use. In light of this, developing a lower-priced HPV vaccine for CA is crucial for protecting younger people. It would be preferable to reduce the prevalence of CA. Phase 1 (NCT02405520) and Phase 2 (NCT02710851) clinical trials for a novel HPV 6/11 vaccine have been completed. This vaccine’s safety and potent immunogenicity have the potential to improve the CA community’s immunity in the future.17

Once the immune response elicited by vaccination is complete, it is difficult for uninfected individuals to be diagnosed with the appropriate HPV or to exhibit CA signs after future sexual behavior.18 Research results from basic studies also suggest that immune system regulation will become an important treatment method in the treatment of tumors such as cervical cancer.19,20 The results of a clinical cohort study from the Czech Republic found that HPV vaccination has a beneficial long-term effect on reducing the risk of CIN2+ recurrence.21 On this basis, the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices internationally recommends HPV vaccination for adolescents before engaging in frequent sexual activity to reduce the risk of HPV infection.22 As a pioneer of this proposal, the Australian government’s HPV vaccine program for adolescents was initiated in 2007 and has led to a substantial decline in CA among YMSM and women aged 15–20. China’s 2025 announcement to advance the national HPV immunization strategy from a regional pilot to inclusion in the national immunization program, marks a milestone in cervical cancer control, however, great challenges remain. HPV vaccination coverage in adolescents is still very low, and HPV vaccine hesitancy is still very serious.23

Therefore, it is essential to investigate the population of college students without sexual experience who could benefit tremendously from the vaccine yet have limited willingness to vaccinate due to cognitive constraints.24 Studies focusing on individuals without sexual experience are still scarce in China. However, due to the characteristics of CA (difficulty of treatment, high recurrence rate, and excellent vaccination protection), prevention must precede treatment. Therefore, we deemed it important to assess the awareness and vaccination willingness of college students without sex experience, aiming to improve the HPV vaccine for CA and optimize the promotion strategies. This study investigated the knowledge regarding CA and HPV vaccines among sexually inexperienced college students from various institutions in Guangdong, China. Furthermore, we analyzed the association between their willingness to receive the HPV vaccine for CA prevention and key contributing factors.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design involving college students in Guangzhou, China. The questionnaire was developed by dermatologists, public health researchers, sociologists, and experts in adolescent sexual health education from grade A tertiary hospitals in Guangzhou, China. In February 2017, a pilot test of the questionnaire was administered among the target population. Based on the pilot test results, the instrument was refined to create the final version, which was subsequently deployed online using Questionnaire Star software. The formal questionnaire was distributed to participants by Lingnan Partners (a local social organization in Guangzhou with influence in the field of adolescent sexual health) utilizing college student volunteers from various universities in Guangdong Province. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Hospital of Dermatology in Guangzhou, China (Ethics No. GDDHLS-2016–112102). Electronic Written Informed Consent informed consent was obtained from each participant before filling out the questionnaire. No private identifiable information was collected to protect participant privacy.

Data collection

This survey questionnaire is divided into six sections, covering informed consent, demographic characteristics (such as gender, age, grade, method of study, monthly expenditure), personal health status and behaviors (such as alcohol consumption, smoking, screening for sexually transmitted diseases), knowledge related to CA and HPV vaccine, behaviors related to HPV vaccine, and the respondents’ attitudes toward the HPV vaccine for CA. The researchers ensured the validity of the questionnaire through the following methods: 1. They established contact with student volunteers from various universities within Guangzhou through the organization Lingnan Partners, which mainly focuses on the prevention of adolescent sexual health. These student volunteers served as the questionnaire distribution liaisons for each university and distributed the questionnaires to their classmates through WeChat to ensure the identification of the research subjects. 2. To minimize the occurrence of selection bias, volunteers from each university were recruited by grade, with 1–2 volunteers per grade, to facilitate even distribution across all university grades. 3. The researchers implemented restrictions in the electronic questionnaire system, allowing only one person per IP address to submit a single response, and each complete respondent could receive 10 RMB (equivalent to 1.4 USD) as compensation to minimize invalid responses. Before distributing the questionnaires, the partner of Lingnan Partners first selected the volunteers from each university and conducted online training on the survey methods for the selected volunteers. After the training, the volunteers sent the electronic questionnaires one-on-one through WeChat. In March 2017, volunteers from various universities distributed this research questionnaire through the WeChat platform and completed data collection within 48 hours. The researchers excluded invalid questionnaires (those with incomplete filling, logical errors in filling, inconsistency, and filling time significantly lower than the time threshold determined based on the pre-survey results) and questionnaires where the respondents reported having had sexual behavior before, and only retained the questionnaire data where the respondents reported no previous sexual behavior in the questionnaire for statistical analysis (Figure 1).

Questionnaire data cleaning flowchart.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics and their health-related behavioral characteristics, awareness of CA and HPV vaccine (cervical cancer vaccine), and attitudes toward the HPV vaccine for CA. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables and examine differences between male and female college students who self-report no sexual experience based on these items. Univariate analysis initially presented crude odds ratios (OR). Univariate logistic regression analysis began with the complete set of sociodemographic, behavioral characteristics, awareness of CA and HPV vaccine/cervical cancer vaccine, attitudes toward the HPV vaccine for CA, and vaccination behavioral intention to assess associations with vaccination intention and attitudes toward the HPV vaccine for CA. Variables with P values < .05 in the univariate model were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to explore factors associated with acceptance of the HPV vaccine for CA. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to evaluate the results of the regression model. Data processing and analysis were performed using SPSS software version 22.0, with a significance threshold of P value .05.

Results

Demographics and personal health

In this study, a total of 2037 questionnaires were collected from 30 colleges in Guangdong, China. The demographic statistics and health status of the target group are summarized by sex. The sample consisted of 41.5% males (N = 846) and 58.5% females (N = 1191); the average age was 19.88 ± 1.41 y old. The majority of participants were in lower grades (29.5% in the first year and 10.5% in the second), of Han Chinese ethnicity (98.5%). Living expenses were concentrated in the ranges of 500–999 RMB (33.5%) and 1000–1499 RMB (47.7%). In addition, 51.5% of the reported consuming alcohol occasionally or rarely, with women having a lower proportion of nondrinking (55.0% vs. 35.7%) than men. 97.1% of the sample reported they did not smoke. The proportions of female college students who drink alcohol and smoke are both lower than those of male college students (P < .001 for both). Knowledge of CA and HPV vaccine, vaccine behaviors

Table 2 reveals that, regarding knowledge about CA and HPV vaccine, 26.3% of students had heard of CA, and even fewer knew its transmission routes (11.8%), symptoms (9.2%), risks (9.3%), and treatment (3.6%). In addition, 7.0% of students acknowledged that the HPV vaccine could prevent CA, and 2.6% (N = 53) had received the HPV vaccine. The proportion of female college students who had heard of the HPV vaccine (23.3%) were significantly higher than male college students (12.8%) (P < .001). Similarly, the proportion of female college students who had friends or family members who had received the HPV vaccine was higher than that of male college students (P < .02).

Attitudes toward the HPV vaccine for CA

Regarding attitudes toward the vaccine (Table 3), more than half (54.7%, N = 1115) of students reported that they were willing to receive the vaccine for CA, and there was no significant difference between the sexes. A total of 15.2% of students believed the HPV vaccine for CA was safe; however, 50.0% of the students were uncertain about the safety of HPV vaccine for CA and 3.9% believed unsafe. Nearly half of the students (44.0%) said they were unsure as to whether the vaccine could prevent CA, whereas 21.4% believed it to be effective and only 1.1% disagreed. 77.0% of students were more likely to be vaccinated if the vaccine cost less than 600 RMB, and 62.5% stated they would be more willing to choose a Chinese-made vaccine if the price was low and the efficacy was not diminished. Those who did not want to be vaccinated expressed uncertainty about the preventive effect (77.7%) and fear of adverse reactions (60.7%). 93.5% of the students willing to be vaccinated expected the vaccine to prevent CA and 7.0% reported being at high risk for contracting CA. Association of demographics, perceptions, behaviors and attitudes with willingness to receive the HPV vaccine for CA

As shown in Table 4, college students who self-reported no sex experience and possessed greater vaccine knowledge had a higher willingness to receive the HPV vaccine for CA (P < .001). Respondents who received HPV/cervical cancer education, had active knowledge of CA, had discussed CA or were surrounded by vaccinated people were more willing to receive the HPV vaccine for CA (P < .001). Different attitudes toward vaccines also affected the willingness to be vaccinated (P < .001). A total of 84.5% (234/277) of students who regarded the vaccine as safe and 83.3% (363/436) who believed it effective in preventing CA were more willing to be vaccinated. Furthermore, there was no link between psychological price and willingness.

Factors associated with the intention to receive the HPV vaccine for CA

The analysis results of the multivariate binary logistic regression model indicated that being a full-time student (OR = 1.95, 95% CI: 1.05–3.61, p < .05), having heard of CA (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.05–1.85, p < .05), having actively learned about CA (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.10–2.38, p < .05), considering the vaccine safe (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 1.03–3.65, p < .05), and believing the vaccine to be effective in preventing CA (OR = 5.08, 95% CI: 2.05–12.59, p < .001) were all factors associated with the willingness to receive the HPV vaccine for CA. It should be noted that the variable “Know the routes of CA transmission” was positively associated with vaccination willingness in univariate analysis (OR = 1.38), but the direction of the association reversed in the multivariate model (OR = 0.54). Regarding this reversal, we determined that the variable may be influenced by confounding factors or collinearity with other knowledge and attitude variables; therefore, as shown in Table 4, it should not be interpreted as an independent protective or risk factor.

Discussion

The college student population is a high-risk group for HPV infection and also a key target group for HPV vaccination. A systematic review and meta-analysis showed that the global awareness rate of HPV among college students is about 68.3%, and the awareness rate of HPV vaccines is about 53.5%, but the actual vaccination rate is only 10.4%.10 Globally, significant disparities exist in the HPV vaccination rates among the youth population. The vaccination rates are generally higher in high-income countries, while relatively lower in middle- and low-income countries. For example, in Australia, the implementation of school-based vaccination programs has enabled the median HPV vaccination rate among adolescents to reach 85%.25 A study involving female college students at a health college in Jordan found that although 75% of participants expressed willingness to vaccinate if the vaccine were provided free of charge, the actual vaccination rate was only 3.6%; notably, “perceiving the risk of HPV infection as low” was the most frequently cited reason for vaccine refusal.26 A large-scale, multi-center survey in China demonstrated that the HPV vaccination rates among Chinese college students remain generally low, far from reaching the ideal herd immunity level. In this demographic, the completion rate for the full HPV vaccine series is typically below 30%.27

Similarly, regional economic conditions significantly influence HPV vaccination rate. A study conducted in Hangzhou, found that the vaccination rate among female college students was 56.08%.28 Conversely, a survey of college students in Zhengzhou, China, reported that only 4.2% had received the HPV vaccine.29 Studies from the United States also suggest that HPV vaccine acceptance and uptake among young people aged 18–21 in rural areas are significantly lower than in urban areas.30 These findings indicate that vaccine hesitancy remains a critical concern for HPV vaccination among adolescents, especially in low – and middle – income countries and economically underdeveloped regions.

Theoretical research suggests that vaccine hesitancy is driven by multi – dimensional factors such as knowledge deficiency, negative attitudes, and behavioral barriers. The knowledge – attitude – practice theory provides a classic theoretical framework for understanding this complex phenomenon. This theory holds that the acquisition of knowledge is the foundation for establishing positive beliefs and attitudes, and positive beliefs and attitudes, in turn, are the driving force for promoting changes in healthy behaviors. Therefore, cognitive factors are one of the core reasons for vaccine hesitancy among young people toward the HPV vaccine. The findings indicate that college students who self-reported no sexual experience had significantly limited awareness regarding the HPV vaccine’s prevention of STIs. Moreover, this lack of awareness is correlated with HPV vaccine hesitancy. A study conducted in Jiangsu, China, also found that although more than 90% of students could correctly identify the transmission routes of HPV, there were still significant knowledge gaps regarding the relationship between HPV and AIDS and its treatment.31 Similarly, another mediation model analysis based on female college students in China showed that knowledge indirectly affects vaccine hesitancy through the mediating effects of trust and risk perception. The higher the knowledge level, the stronger the trust and risk perception, and the lower the degree of vaccine hesitancy. This indicates that the lack of knowledge directly affects students’ risk perception and vaccination willingness.32 Therefore, filling the knowledge gap among the youth population regarding HPV-related knowledge is the primary task for eliminating vaccine hesitancy.

Gender is also an important factor contributing to HPV vaccine hesitancy. In this study, nearly the same proportion of male (27.1%) and female (25.7%) sexually inexperienced college students had heard of CA. However, compared to male students (12.8%), a significantly larger proportion of female students (23.3%) were aware that quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines could prevent CA. Other similar studies have found that male college students generally have lower awareness and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine than female students. This disparity may be partly attributed to the longstanding characterization of the HPV vaccine as a “female vaccine.” A study conducted in France found that only 36.6% of male middle – school students and 37.8% of parents were willing to receive or have their children receive the HPV vaccine. “Believing that their children are at no risk” was one of the important reasons for parents to refuse vaccination. In Switzerland, a study on youths aged 15–26 found that the HPV vaccination rate among females (58%) was much higher than that among males (15%).33 According to a study of seven colleges in China, 39.5% of male students and 57.3% of female students had heard of the HPV vaccine.34 Male students in the Wenzhou region also reported significantly lower levels of HPV vaccine awareness than female students (53.7% vs. 78.2%).35 In a study among male college students in Jiangsu, China, 24.3% of respondents reported having good knowledge of HPV and the vaccine.36 The sex difference between men and women in these reports regarding HPV vaccines aligns with the findings of this study.

Taken together, these data indicate that young women are generally more aware of the vaccine than adolescent males, as the current marketing efforts mainly target women and emphasizes their ability to prevent cervical cancer. In January and April 2025, the Chinese government also approved the use of Merck’s quadrivalent and nonavalent HPV vaccines for men aged 9–26 and 16–26, respectively. However, hesitation regarding about HPV vaccination among the male population in China remains a significant concern.23 HPV vaccination rates among adolescent males remain similarly low. HPV vaccines also demonstrate significant clinical value by effectively preventing CA and genital malignancies in men. Multiple epidemiological studies suggest that the promotion of HPV vaccines among the male population should be strengthened.37,38

Sexual experience is also considered an important factor in influencing vaccine hesitancy. Survey data on students without sexual experience revealed that both men and women were significantly less knowledgeable about the HPV vaccine than college students with sexual experience.39,40 A study from the University of Iowa in the United States also shows that women and students with sexual experience have a stronger willingness to receive the HPV vaccine.24 The reason may be that students with sexual experience usually have a more intuitive risk perception of HPV infection, so their willingness to receive the vaccine may be higher. Sexual history status may be a key factor affecting college students’ awareness and acceptance of HPV vaccines. However, if the vaccine is administered after sexual activity, it is likely to result in suboptimal protective efficacy. Therefore, it is of great significance to promote and administer the HPV vaccine to adolescents who have not yet started having sexual intercourse.

Another issue that deserves attention is that the COVID-19 pandemic has also led to a negative attitude among parents toward the HPV vaccine in a short period. The acceptance of the HPV vaccine among adolescents mainly depends on the strong support of parents and appropriate healthcare professionals and vaccination services. Travel restrictions, lockdowns, school closures, and social distancing have led to more people being hesitant about the HPV vaccine.41 Some college students may confuse the side effects of the HPV vaccine with those of the COVID-19 vaccine. This generalized concern about vaccine safety has become an important trigger for the escalation of vaccine hesitancy.42 This underscores the need to emphasize the safety and protective effects of the vaccine in subsequent public health education and communication efforts, thereby enhancing vaccine acceptance among young individuals without sexual experience.

The strength of this study stems from the fact that the subjects surveyed were young college students who self-reported having no prior sexual experience. This group is a key catch-up population for HPV vaccination. CA more frequently manifests at a younger age in the young population, whereas malignant tumors are perceived as more remote risks and are easily overlooked. Furthermore, this population is at high risk for STIs and significant psychological distress, reproductive burden, and economic burden of CA.43,44 Therefore, if the 6/11 HPV vaccine for CA is administered to this population in a timely manner prior to sexual activity, even if they are temporarily unable to obtain access to quadrivalent or nonavalent vaccines for financial reasons, it may reduce the risk of contracting CA in the short term, during the period when sexual debut is anticipated. The vaccine can protect the physical and mental health of the young population and lessen the disease burden.

This study has several limitations. First, the research used self-administered electronic questionnaires, so it cannot completely rule out potential biases from self-reporting and inaccurate responses. Second, participants completed the questionnaires via social media platforms, necessitating caution in interpreting the results. Finally, in recent years after the data collection for this study, there have been significant changes in China’s policies related to the HPV immunization program and the eligible population and age for HPV vaccination. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to the current context may be limited. However, for the non-sexually active adolescent population investigated in this study, our findings retain significant relevance. Firstly, it was not until 2025 that China approved the use of the HPV vaccine for adolescent males on the Chinese mainland.23 However, due to the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and the COVID-19 vaccine, the hesitancy in HPV vaccination in China and even globally is still quite serious at present.41,42 Our research results suggest that the awareness of condyloma acuminata and the views on the safety and protective efficacy of the vaccine are the core factors influencing the willingness to receive the condyloma acuminata – preventive HPV vaccine. Therefore, priority should be given to implementing health education on the prevention of HPV – related diseases and disseminating information regarding vaccine safety and efficacy among this population, aiming to address vaccine hesitancy and optimize strategies for more equitable public health outcomes. Our research findings still have certain theoretical reference value for formulating the promotion strategy of the HPV vaccine among adolescent populations who have not had sexual intercourse.
