EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Geologic hydrogen (H₂)—naturally occurring in the Earth’s crust or generated in situ through
engineered pathways—has emerged as a promising energy resource with the potential for near-
term deployment. This report synthesizes the state of knowledge on geologic hydrogen systems,
drawing parallels with petroleum systems to illustrate how H₂ is generated (e.g., by
serpentinization, radiolysis, mantle degassing, and in-situ hydrocarbon conversion), migrates
along faults or fractures, and accumulates in reservoirs sealed by low-permeability rocks. Although
only a few fields—most notably Bourakébougou in Mali—have reached commercial production,
research indicates that large portions of the United States, including the Midcontinent Rift System,
banded iron formations in the north-central region, and ultramafic complexes on the East and West
Coasts, may host untapped hydrogen resources.

Also examined are early-stage, “orange” hydrogen concepts in which H₂ generation is enhanced
or induced through fluid injection into iron-rich or hydrocarbon-bearing formations. These
anthropogenic approaches, while less explored in the literature, align closely with established
subsurface engineering technologies—such as hydraulic fracturing, enhanced oil recovery, and
carbon sequestration—and may provide a pathway for large-scale hydrogen production and
accompanying co-benefits (e.g., geothermal energy extraction and carbon mineralization). The
report further discusses prospectivity analysis tools that integrate geophysical, geochemical, and
reservoir characterization methods, as well as the growing role of large language models (LLMs)
for managing and synthesizing diverse data sources. Building on these insights, this report
identifies critical research directions—from lab-based reaction kinetics experiments to pilot-scale
field demonstrations—that will help delineate resource potential, address containment challenges,
and streamline economic viability for geologic hydrogen as a versatile and reliable long term
energy option.

1. INTRODUCTION

Naturally sourced geologic hydrogen occurrences have been identified globally (Zgonnik, 2020),
including within the United States. The potential of geologic hydrogen, both naturally occurring
and anthropogenically induced, as a novel energy source is increasingly recognized. It holds
promise as a critical addition to the U.S. energy portfolio and may contribute significantly to
achieving national objectives for affordable, reliable, and secure energy resources.

Despite its potential, comprehensive scientific investigation remains limited, and several
fundamental questions are yet to be addressed. These include:

 • What mechanisms are responsible for the generation of geologic hydrogen?
 • Under what in situ geologic conditions—such as iron-rich, water-saturated formations—
 can these mechanisms operate at technically accessible depths?
 • How does hydrogen migrate from its source through overlying geologic formations and
 ultimately escape to the atmosphere?
 • Which geologic systems are capable of trapping and retaining hydrogen over geologic
 timescales?
 • How can hydrogen generation be initiated or enhanced through anthropogenic means under
 in situ or stimulated conditions?
 • What are the current technological capabilities for safely extracting geologic hydrogen?
 • What ancillary benefits could arise from the injection of fluids (e.g., water, liquid carbon
 dioxide) into hydrogen-generating source rocks—such as geothermal energy development,
 critical mineral extraction, or carbon sequestration?

Answering these questions is essential for assessing the potential volume of technically and
economically recoverable geologic hydrogen resources. The U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE)
National Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL) is uniquely positioned to address these
challenges, leveraging its expertise in engineered subsurface systems research and development
(R&D).

This report provides a comprehensive literature review that aims to:

 • Synthesize the fundamental concepts underlying geologic hydrogen systems.
 • Evaluate the current state-of-the-art in geologic hydrogen exploration and production.
 • Identify critical knowledge gaps and outline research questions that could be addressed
 through the development of targeted research portfolios at NETL.

2. BACKGROUND

This section introduces geologic hydrogen within the broader landscape of hydrogen production
pathways. It then highlights the relevance of geologic hydrogen in meeting projected global
hydrogen demand, primarily due to its potentially low cost of production.

2.1 TYPES OF HYDROGEN (COLOR CLASSIFICATION)

Hydrogen production is often described using a color-based nomenclature, where each color is
associated with a particular generative mechanism. However, this color-coding system is
inconsistently applied across the literature, occasionally assigning the same color to different
production pathways. As such, it can lead to confusion. For completeness and reader awareness, a
brief overview of the most commonly used color terms is provided here. Beyond this section, this
report avoids using color-based terminology and instead refers to hydrogen types by their
production mechanisms.

Currently, most of the world's hydrogen is produced from natural gas or coal through reforming
processes. This form of hydrogen is referred to as “grey” (from natural gas) or “black” (from coal),
with the dark color referencing the feedstock's appearance. In the United States, approximately
95% of hydrogen is classified as grey and is produced via steam methane reforming (SMR), a
thermal catalytic process where methane reacts with steam to produce hydrogen and other gases.

If the carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions from the reforming process are captured and stored at a rate
exceeding 95% of the otherwise unabated emissions—and if methane (CH₄) emissions remain
below 0.2%—the resulting hydrogen is referred to as “blue.” “Green” hydrogen is produced via
electrolysis of water powered by regenerable energy sources. The term “blue” and “green” reflect
relatively low ecological impacts associated with these production pathways.

“White” or “gold” hydrogen refers to hydrogen that occurs naturally in the subsurface and can be
extracted without synthetic conversion processes. This form of hydrogen is believed to originate
primarily from geologic reactions, such as serpentinization—where reduced water reacts with
oxidized iron at elevated temperatures. The white/gold designation reflects both the natural origin
of this hydrogen and its perceived value, particularly due to its potential for low-cost production.

If serpentinization is anthropogenically induced—for instance, by injecting water into iron-rich
rock formations—the resulting hydrogen is commonly referred to as “orange” hydrogen, with the
color symbolizing oxidized iron. Additionally, in situ hydrogen extraction from hydrocarbon
reservoirs or in situ hydrocarbon conversion (e.g., via SMR-like processes within the subsurface)
also falls under the category of orange hydrogen. Together, white/gold and orange hydrogen are
collectively referred to as geologic hydrogen.

Figure 1 illustrates these hydrogen types within a geologic cross-section. Less common hydrogen
classifications not depicted include “pink” hydrogen (electrolysis powered by nuclear energy) and
“yellow” hydrogen (electrolysis powered by solar energy).

Each hydrogen type presents unique challenges:

 • Black/grey hydrogen is associated with challenges associated with capture of byproducts.
 • Blue hydrogen faces significant cost barriers due to the need for carbon capture, transport,
 and permanent storage, along with the added cost of minimizing CH₄ leakage.
 • Green hydrogen remains economically uncompetitive relative to black/grey hydrogen due
 to high capital and operational costs.
 • White/gold hydrogen (i.e., natural hydrogen) occurrences are rare, and production rates
 have generally been below commercial thresholds. For instance, the only known producing
 natural hydrogen well, located in Bourakébougou, Mali, yields approximately 5–50 tonnes
 per year—equivalent to just 0.3–3 barrels of oil per day (Everts, 2024). No other discovery
 has yet demonstrated commercial viability. Given this scarcity, significant uncertainty
 remains regarding the abundance and accessibility of natural hydrogen, especially at the
 greater depths where higher concentrations are presumed to exist. Moreover,
 considerations such as emissions, water use, and surface disturbance—may mirror those
 observed in the oil and gas exploration and production (E&P) industry.
 • Orange hydrogen (i.e., anthropogenic geologic hydrogen) remains largely conceptual.
 Like natural hydrogen, it may face high energy input requirements, elevated costs, and
 trade-offs related to ecosystem impact—depending on the technologies developed for
 subsurface hydrogen generation (Osselin et al., 2022).

Global demand for hydrogen across all sources was approximately 70 million metric tons per

electrolysis to become cost-competitive (Yedinak, 2022).

2.3 GLOBAL OCCURRENCE

Global in-place estimates of geologic hydrogen range from billions to trillions of metric tons. A
recent estimate suggests the annual flow of geologic hydrogen (excluding diffusive seepage and
deep-sourced emissions) is approximately 23 Mtpa (Ellis, 2023).

Occurrences of geologic hydrogen have been reported in a wide range of geologic settings,
including:

 • Precambrian basement rock (Guélard et al., 2017; Lollar et al., 2014; Newell et al., 2007;
 Sherwood Lollar et al., 2007)
 • Ophiolite belts (Vacquand et al., 2018)
 • Ultrabasic springs (e.g., in ophiolitic terrain) releasing hydrogen-rich bubbles (Morrill et
 al., 2013)
 • Midcontinent rift systems (Coveney et al., 1987: Guélard et al., 2017;)
 • Mid-ocean ridges, such as the Lost City and Rainbow hydrothermal systems (Cannat et al.,
          2010; Vacquand et al., 2018)
 • Hydrothermal vents (Allen & Seyfried, 2004; Mayhew et al., 2013)
 • Fault zones (Johnsgard, 1988; Prinzhofer et al., 2024; Ware et al., 1984)

Additional geologic settings are listed in the legend of Figure 4, which maps global geologic
hydrogen occurrences (exceeding 10% H₂ purity), categorized by geologic setting, and further
classified into free gas, gas in fluid inclusions, and dissolved gas.

Geologic hydrogen systems typically feature hydrogen as a major component, often accompanied
by methane (CH₄), nitrogen (N₂), and various other gases (e.g., O₂, CO₂, He, Ar, H₂S). These
systems are frequently associated with active or paleo-serpentinization processes (Headlee, 1962;
Moore & Sigler, 1987; Newcombe, 1935).

Well-documented examples of geologic hydrogen occurrences include:

 • Turkey – Gas seeps associated with low-temperature serpentinization in the Tekirova and
 Kızıldağ ophiolites, notably near Chimaera and Tahtaköprü (D’Alessandro et al., 2018;
          Etiope, 2023; Hosgörmez, 2007; Wang et al., 2023).
 • New Caledonia – Bubbling alkaline springs and other seeps near Baie du Carénage and
 Source des Kaoris, associated with serpentinization of ultramafic rocks and possibly
 microbial activity (Vacquand et al., 2018).
 • Albania – Gas seeps along fault zones in the Bulqizë ophiolite, observed within chromite
 mines (Truche et al., 2024).
 • Oman – Hydrogen-rich springs associated with both low- and high-temperature
 serpentinization, and possibly the release of ancient fluid inclusions from the Samail
 ophiolite (Leong et al., 2023; Vacquand et al., 2018).
 • Philippines – Seeps linked to low- and high-temperature serpentinization of ophiolites in
 the Zambales region (Abrajano et al., 1988; Vacquand et al., 2018).

 • Mali – Conventional well production from carbonate reservoirs in Bourakébougou,
 sourced by serpentinization of ultramafic protolith interacting with meteoric water and
 sealed by dolerite dykes (Hutchinson et al., 2024).

Hydrogen purities at these sites vary widely, from 7.5–11.3% in the Tekirova ophiolite (Turkey),
to 30% in New Caledonia, 40% in the Philippines, 60% in the Kızıldağ ophiolite (Turkey), 84%

3. GEOLOGICAL HYDROGEN SYSTEM (VS. PETROLEUM SYSTEM)

A geologic hydrogen system is a conceptual framework that defines the critical elements and
processes required for the formation, accumulation, and potential commercial extraction of
geologic hydrogen. As a useful analog, the petroleum system provides a long-established
framework used to understand hydrocarbon accumulations and serves as a structural reference for
organizing the current state of knowledge regarding geologic hydrogen systems.

In the context of petroleum exploration, a petroleum system includes the following
key elements associated with a specific hydrocarbon accumulation:

 • Hydrocarbon source rock and generation,
 • Migration pathway connecting the source to the reservoir,
 • Reservoir with adequate porosity and permeability to store hydrocarbons, and
 • Containment (or seal) structure capable of preventing hydrocarbon escape.

When considering extraction, anthropogenic aspects are often superimposed onto the natural
petroleum system to account for engineered interventions aimed at improving resource recovery.
These include:

 • Stimulation inputs at the source (e.g., thermal or chemical treatments to initiate or enhance
 hydrocarbon generation), and
 • Reservoir stimulation techniques to mobilize hydrocarbons and enhance flow (e.g.,
 hydraulic fracturing, gas injection).

Similarly, the geologic hydrogen system can be defined using analogous elements and processes,
encompassing both natural and anthropogenic system types. Natural systems occur without human
intervention, while anthropogenic systems rely on engineered inputs to initiate or enhance
hydrogen generation or mobilization.

Exploration and production strategies for geologic hydrogen may differ significantly from those
used in petroleum systems, primarily due to fundamental differences in the relative
stability and regenerability of the two resources. These differences are illustrated in Table 1,
particularly in the context of resource regenerability at the project scale.
Petroleum is a chemically and physically stable fluid, with residence times in the subsurface that
span geologic timescales—typically hundreds of thousands to millions of years. As such,
petroleum accumulations form slowly and are considered effectively depleted within the
timeframe of anthropogenic production activities.
In contrast, geologic hydrogen is comparatively unstable, both chemically and physically, and is
therefore characterized by higher mobility and shorter residence times in the subsurface—on the
order of years to decades. Critically, certain geologic hydrogen systems may be capable of ongoing
generation at rates commensurate with production, rendering them effectively self-regenerating on

anthropogenic timescales. This distinction has significant implications for exploration, resource
assessment, and long-term production strategy development.

3.1 GEOLOGIC HYDROGEN SYSTEM PROCESSES
This section provides further detail on the processes within the geologic hydrogen system—
specifically, the mechanisms of generation, migration, consumption, and containment. These
processes, briefly introduced in Table 1, are critical to understanding the formation and behavior
of geologic hydrogen accumulations and identifying key knowledge gaps that affect exploration
and production strategies.

3.1.1 Generation Mechanisms

Geologic hydrogen can be generated through a range of chemical, physical, and biological
processes. The most commonly cited mechanisms are summarized in Table 2, and include:

 • Serpentinization,
 • Core and mantle degassing,
 • Radiolysis,
 • Biogenesis,
 • Water-rock interactions with freshly exposed mineral surfaces,
 • Decomposition of hydroxyl groups within mineral lattices, and
 • Thermal decomposition of organic matter.

These mechanisms can be categorized along several conceptual axes. One commonly used
classification distinguishes between primary and secondary hydrogen sources. Generation
occurring within the Earth's mantle or core is considered primary, while processes occurring in
the crust are considered secondary (Zgonnik, 2020). Another axis distinguishes
between abiogenic (non-biological) and biogenic (biologically mediated) generation mechanisms.

While these taxonomies are conceptually useful, this report adopts a classification framework
better suited for resource assessment and prospectivity analysis. Specifically, generation
mechanisms are evaluated based on:

 • Potential to support commercial-scale hydrogen generation, and
 • Potential to be induced or enhanced through anthropogenic intervention.

These two criteria are central to identifying viable geologic hydrogen systems and to formulating
strategies for future research and development.

Several geologic hydrogen generation mechanisms exhibit potential for commercial-scale
production (Table 1). These include serpentinization, core and mantle degassing, radiolysis, and
biogenesis. Among these, the only documented analog for commercial-scale hydrogen generation
is the Bourakébougou field in Mali, where hydrogen is generated primarily through

 24

serpentinization (Maiga et al., 2023, 2024). The remaining mechanisms—core and mantle
degassing, radiolysis, and biogenesis—remain theoretical with respect to commercial viability.

Of the mechanisms listed, serpentinization is widely accepted to have a clear potential to be
anthropogenically induced. This process could be initiated or enhanced through injection of hot
water or steam, with potentially chemical catalysts, into appropriate source rocks under suitable
pressure and temperature (P–T) conditions.

Serpentinization is a geochemical process wherein reduced (ferrous) iron-bearing minerals react
with water via hydrolysis, producing ferric iron minerals (such as oxides) and molecular hydrogen.
The reaction typically occurs in ultramafic rocks that are rich in ferrous minerals, such as olivine
(particularly the iron-rich endmember fayalite), pyroxene, and magnetite. Candidate source rocks
for serpentinization include basalts, ophiolites, peridotites, and banded iron formations (BIFs).
Mineral assemblages such as lizardite, chrysotile, antigorite, and magnetite are indicative of
ongoing or past serpentinization.

Successful serpentinization requires:

 • A flux of water through the reactive rock matrix, and
 • Pressure-temperature conditions conducive to hydrogen generation.

According to the literature, high-temperature (HT) serpentinization occurs optimally between
200°C and 310–400°C (McCollom & Bach, 2009; Osselin et al., 2022), with the upper range
generally associated with peridotites beneath oceanic crust and the lower range typical of ophiolite-
peridotite massifs. Low-temperature (LT) serpentinization has been observed at temperatures as
low as 80°C (Templeton et al., 2024); however, the rate of H₂ generation under LT conditions
remains uncertain. As such, the viability of LT serpentinization as a commercially relevant or
anthropogenically inducible process remains unclear. Furthermore, the pressure conditions
favorable for either HT or LT serpentinization are not well constrained in the existing literature.
On the other hand, emerging research focuses on using natural catalysts that include nickel and
other platinum group elements to stimulate hydrogen production at low temperature and shallow
depth (Kortsha, 2024; Schwander et al., 2023).

Given the water requirements and subsurface stimulation potential, serpentinization may be a
strong candidate for anthropogenic hydrogen generation. Subsurface engineering techniques
derived from petroleum industry practices, such as hydraulic fracturing, may be leveraged to
enhance permeability and increase the surface area of rock-water contact, facilitating more
efficient serpentinization reactions (Hand, 2023).

Radiolysis involves the ionization and dissociation of water molecules by radioactive decay,
producing molecular hydrogen and oxygen (Lin et al., 2005). This process occurs in rocks enriched
in radioactive elements, such as uranium, thorium, and potassium. Due to its slow reaction kinetics,
radiolysis typically requires geologic timescales to produce appreciable quantities of hydrogen.
Consequently, significant accumulations of radiolytically generated hydrogen are most likely to
be found in Precambrian and Cambrian-aged formations. The slow rate of reaction renders

radiolysis unsuitable for anthropogenic hydrogen generation or near-term commercial
development.

Core and mantle degassing is a hypothetical primary hydrogen generation mechanism, wherein
unstable iron hydrides (e.g., iron monohydride) in the Earth's core and mantle disassociate to
release molecular hydrogen. Previous study refers to hydrogen from this source as “primary”
geologic hydrogen (Zgonnik, 2020). The hypothesis is supported by several lines of indirect
evidence:

 • Anomalous core density calculations, suggesting that Earth's core is less dense than a pure
 nickel-iron composition would predict—potentially due to the presence of light elements
 such as hydrogen bound in hydrides.
 • Hydrogen emissions observed along deep-seated faults, implying a mantle or core origin.
 • Isotopic signatures, particularly hydrogen with low deuterium content, consistent with
 deep-Earth sourcing (Prinzhofer et al., 2018).

Despite these indicators, the mechanistic details of degassing remain poorly understood. The P–T
conditions, spatial domains, and geologic controls that would govern efficient hydrogen release
from the core or mantle are not yet well defined in the literature. As such, core and mantle
degassing remain a theoretical process with uncertain potential for resource development or
anthropogenic enhancement.

In addition to such non-hydrocarbon-sourced geologic hydrogen generation mechanisms, one
avenue that can be utilized to generate hydrogen directly is in-situ hydrocarbon conversion, which
is classified in Table 1 as an anthropogenic hydrogen system subtype. This process involves the
generation of hydrogen directly from subsurface hydrocarbon resources, through mechanisms
such as biogenesis and thermochemical decomposition of organic matter, as detailed in Table 2.

In many cases, it may be more economical to extract and utilize hydrocarbon resources directly
for energy production, rather than converting them in situ to hydrogen. However, under certain
conditions—such as where direct hydrocarbon extraction is technically, economically, or
ecologically constrained—in-situ hydrocarbon conversion may represent a viable anthropogenic
hydrogen strategy.

3.1.2 Migration Mechanisms

For natural hydrogen deposits to accumulate, migration from a source to a reservoir rock is
required. Geologic hydrogen migration can occur as a free gas, as gas trapped in fluid inclusions,
or dissolved in groundwater. Two primary mechanisms govern hydrogen migration: diffusion and
advection (Lodhia & Peeters, 2024).

Diffusion, governed by Fick’s Law (Jacobs & Jacobs, 1935), is a molecular process driven by
concentration gradients. Hydrogen moves from areas of high concentration to areas of lower
concentration through media such as crystal lattices and pore spaces, including across low-
permeability clayey sediments and caprocks (Liu, 2024). Notably, hydrogen is approximately 2.8
times more diffusive than methane (Hutchinson et al., 2024). While this high diffusivity enables
hydrogen to migrate easily, it also reduces the effectiveness of conventional seal rocks—such as
mudstones—making long-term containment more challenging.

Advection refers to hydrogen transport along discrete features such as fractures, faults, and
bedding planes, or when dissolved in groundwater. In the gas phase, hydrogen advection is driven
by pressure gradients, including buoyancy, and can be described by Darcy’s Law. In aqueous
solution, hydrogen is transported with groundwater flow, again driven by pressure differences.
Compared to diffusion, advection is generally a more efficient mechanism for bulk transport of
hydrogen.

The rate and variability of diffusion are strongly influenced by physical and geochemical
conditions, including pressure, temperature, and the mineralogical and textural properties of the
host rock (Lodhia & Peeters, 2024). Diffusivity typically increases with temperature but varies
considerably based on mineral structure. For example, hydrogen can diffuse almost
instantaneously in fine-grained, microcrystalline rocks, while coarser-grained or aggregated
rocks—such as granites—can trap hydrogen in fluid inclusions for geologic timescales, including
billions of years (Figure 7) (Hutchinson et al., 2024; Lodhia & Peeters, 2024).

In sedimentary settings, higher salinity can reduce hydrogen diffusivity, likely due to increased
brine connectivity within pore networks relative to empty or gas-filled pore spaces. Increasing
pressure also reduces diffusivity, as elevated fluid density at constant temperature decreases the
available free space for gas movement (Lodhia et al., 2024).

3.1.3 Consumption Mechanisms

Hydrogen loss in the subsurface can occur through two primary pathways: physical migration,
which results in spatial and temporal losses driven by diffusion and advection, and chemical or
biological consumption. The latter category includes both abiotic geochemical reactions and biotic
activity involving subsurface microbial communities.

Previous study conducted one-dimensional geochemical batch modeling experiments to
investigate hydrogen loss in a sandstone formation (Dodangoda, 2024). The experiments were
performed under controlled subsurface conditions at a pressure of 20 MPa and a temperature of
80°C, intended to simulate a plausible hydrogen storage or generation condition. The results
demonstrated that hydrogen loss can occur under a variety of subsurface scenarios, depending on
changes to physical and chemical parameters (Figure 8).

Over a modeled 100-year period, 6.07 percent of hydrogen was lost due to aqueous diffusion
through pore spaces, while 2.31 percent was consumed through mineral reactions. Among the
various geochemical processes, carbonate dissolution contributed significantly to hydrogen loss.
Interestingly, the presence of methane in the system was associated with the lowest hydrogen loss
across all scenarios, suggesting that methane-rich conditions may be favorable for hydrogen
retention.

It is important to note that the modeling study did not incorporate microbial processes, nor did it
consider variations in mineralogical composition or other dynamic subsurface conditions. As such,
the results represent a simplified system and actual hydrogen loss in natural settings may differ
substantially, especially in the presence of bacterial activity or heterogeneous mineral
assemblages.

Abiotic reactions can lead to petrophysical changes in both the storage reservoir and the overlying
caprock over time. These changes may include alterations in porosity and permeability, typically
resulting from mineral precipitation or dissolution processes. One previous study (Hemme & Van
Berk, 2018), using a one-dimensional diffusive mass transport model, found that aqueous diffusion
through the caprock over a 30-year period did not significantly alter porosity, primarily due to the
inherently slow nature of the diffusion process. However, according to previous study, over a 100-
year timescale, porosity in sandstone decreased slightly, while porosity in claystone declined more
substantially (Dodangoda, 2024). These findings suggest that although slow, aqueous diffusion
into the caprock could compromise its sealing capacity, particularly over extended geologic
periods—a likely scenario for many natural hydrogen accumulations.

Microbial processes that consume hydrogen as an energy source are known as biotic consumption
mechanisms. These processes occur in both subsurface conditions and near-surface soils and can
significantly alter the composition of gas mixtures over time (Zgonnik, 2020). Biotic hydrogen
consumption is closely coupled with hydrogen production, often playing a regulatory role in
maintaining hydrogen availability in natural systems. It is estimated that approximately 80 percent

of atmospheric hydrogen loss occurs due to microbial uptake in surface soils, where hydrogen
production rates are negligible in comparison to consumption (Zgonnik, 2020).

In subsurface settings, biotic processes are generally more active in shallower settings where
microbial life is more abundant. These zones are typically characterized by temperatures below
135°F and pressures less than 35 MPa. The primary microbial reactions responsible for hydrogen
consumption in these settings include methanogenesis, acetogenesis, sulfate reduction, and iron
reduction (Perera, 2023).

Although direct quantification of biotic hydrogen loss in geologic settings remains limited in the
literature, several case studies provide insights. In one notable example, a town gas storage facility
in deep aquifers in Lobodice, Czechia, experienced 45 to 50 percent hydrogen loss over a period
of seven months, with hydrogen being converted primarily to methane and hydrogen sulfide
(Amigáň et al., 1990; Muhammed et al., 2024). Another study identified methanogenesis,
homoacetogenesis, and sulfate reduction as the three dominant biotic processes contributing to
hydrogen loss in depleted oil and gas reservoirs. Recent research efforts have increasingly focused
on characterizing these reactions and their implications for subsurface hydrogen storage, and a
summary of these findings is provided in Table 4 (Muhammed et al., 2024; Thaysen et al., 2021).

Similar to abiotic processes, biotic reactions are influenced by physicochemical conditions during
hydrogen storage and can induce rock–fluid interactions that may compromise the long-term
integrity of both the caprock and wellbore infrastructure (Perera, 2023). For example, sulfate-
reducing microbial activity can lower the pH of the formation fluid, creating an acidic condition
that promotes sulfide precipitation. These conditions may result in corrosion of wellbore materials
and a reduction in caprock permeability, thereby affecting the effectiveness of the seal and
increasing the risk of hydrogen leakage over time.

3.1.4 Containment Mechanisms

In conventional petroleum systems, the chemical stability of hydrocarbon molecules and long
geologic residence times facilitate the accumulation of significant hydrocarbon volumes. In
contrast, hydrogen's high diffusivity, chemical reactivity with oxidants, and susceptibility to
microbial consumption make long-term containment more challenging. As a result, stable, long-
lived accumulations of geologic hydrogen analogous to conventional petroleum reservoirs have
not yet been definitively documented.

Current understanding suggests that geologic hydrogen accumulations are sustained when the rate
of hydrogen generation from a continuously emitting source exceeds the rate of loss through
migration, reaction, or biological activity. In such a dynamic system, if the regeneration rate
exceeds commercial extraction, the hydrogen resource can be considered effectively regenerating
(Hand, 2023). However, the existence of traditional, non-regenerating accumulations with long-
term stability remains uncertain.

A notable example of hydrogen accumulation is the Bourakébougou field in Mali (Maiga et al.,
2023, 2024). In this system, hydrogen is trapped within an anticlinal structure hosted in carbonate
and sandstone formations that are intruded by dolerite, a medium-grained mafic intrusive rock
(Figure 9). Although estimates suggest the field may have once contained up to 60 billion cubic
feet (approximately 5 million tonnes) of recoverable hydrogen, evidence points to ongoing

 36

hydrogen generation, likely driven by continuous meteoric water recharge and associated
serpentinization of ultramafic source rocks. Additionally, intra-reservoir hydrogen generation may
also be contributing to the system’s regenerability.

Several factors may contribute to successful hydrogen containment at Bourakébougou. The
dolerite caprock is notably thick, and formation microimager (FMI) logs indicate that the upper
dolerite layer is unfractured, enhancing its sealing capacity. Furthermore, in the upper carbonate
and sandstone reservoirs, hydrogen exists as a free gas phase, which limits microbial activity by
restricting the gas’s dissolution into water—a necessary condition for microbial metabolism. In
deeper sandstone units, hydrogen is largely dissolved in formation fluids and is observed to degas
during drilling operations.

These deeper reservoirs are also associated with banded iron formations (BIFs) and magnetite,
both of which are known to act as hydrogen source materials. BIFs are commonly recognized as
geologic hydrogen source rocks, and magnetite has been shown experimentally to contribute to
hydrogen generation (Geymond et al., 2023). This suggests that active hydrogen generation may
be occurring within the deeper portions of the system, further supporting the hypothesis that
Bourakébougou represents a self-regenerating geologic hydrogen resource.

3.2.1 Source

Geologic hydrogen can be generated through multiple mechanisms, each involving different
source components. This section focuses on source rocks associated with serpentinization, as it is
currently considered the most commercially viable and potentially anthropogenically inducible
hydrogen generation pathway. Unlike in-situ hydrocarbon conversion, serpentinization does not
depend on the transformation of extractable hydrocarbon resources.

Serpentinization occurs when mafic or ultramafic rocks (see global map in Figure 10), containing
reduced iron-rich minerals, react with water under favorable temperature and pressure conditions.
The reaction produces iron oxides and molecular hydrogen. Suitable source rocks for this process
include ultramafic lithologies such as peridotites and tholeiitic basalts, which are commonly
associated with greenstone belts and ophiolite complexes.

Greenstone belts are metamorphosed volcanic sequences that originated at oceanic spreading
centers or island arcs and are composed predominantly of mafic and ultramafic rocks. These belts
often host extensive iron-rich mineral assemblages and are considered favorable conditions for
serpentinization (Cannat et al., 2010). Ophiolites are fragments of oceanic crust and upper mantle
that have been emplaced onto continental crust during orogenic events. These assemblages are
often exposed in mountain belts and commonly contain serpentine minerals formed through
previous serpentinization episodes (Vacquand et al., 2018).

Serpentinization may also occur in banded iron formations (BIFs), which are typically older than
one billion years and consist of alternating layers of iron oxides and silica-rich chert (Geymond et
al., 2023). BIFs are interpreted to have formed from chemical reactions between dissolved iron in
ancient oceans and oxygen produced by cyanobacteria, resulting in the precipitation of insoluble
iron oxides. These formations serve as both hydrogen source rocks and indicators of past reactive
conditions conducive to serpentinization.

Iron-rich ore bodies associated with these lithologies often host economically important minerals,
including iron, gold, mercury, nickel, copper, and polymetallic deposits such as niobium-tantalum,
tungsten-molybdenum, and loparite. These ore systems may offer opportunities for co-production
of hydrogen and critical minerals or provide additional context for identifying favorable source
rock conditions (Osselin et al., 2022).

For source rocks to undergo serpentinization, a subsurface plumbing system must be present to
facilitate water–rock interaction. This system typically involves a network of fractures, faults,
permeable layers, or other discrete boundaries that allow the ingress of water—whether
groundwater, meteoric water, or seawater—to reach and react with the reactive ultramafic
lithologies. In certain geologic settings, hydrogen generation may be further enhanced by natural
processes, such as hydrothermal circulation. In hydrothermal systems, water heated by proximity
to a magmatic source can circulate at temperatures between 200°C and 300°C, conditions
favorable for elevated hydrogen generation rates (McCollom et al., 2016; Templeton et al., 2024).
Additionally, high concentrations of metals and low pH conditions commonly found in
hydrothermal systems may act as catalytic agents, further enhancing hydrogen production.

In the case of the Bourakébougou field in Mali, the source of natural hydrogen is serpentinized
cratonic basement rock composed of olivine-rich ultramafic protolith (Maiga et al., 2023, 2024).
Hydrogen generation is thought to be driven by freshwater recharge, which initiates and sustains
the serpentinization process.

3.2.2 Migration Pathways

As discussed in the earlier section on “Migration Mechanisms,” hydrogen transport occurs
primarily through diffusion and advection. This section focuses on advective migration,

 39

 Geological Hydrogen: From Natural Occurrences to Anthropogenic Generation – A Critical Review of Potential,

emphasizing the role of deep-seated discontinuities, fractures, and other discrete structural features
that facilitate hydrogen flow from source rocks to potential reservoir formations.

Deep-seated discontinuities are critical not only for hydrogen migration but also for primary
hydrogen generation through mantle degassing. Features such as basement faults are commonly
located in tectonic settings associated with failed or active rift zones, orogenic belts resulting from
plate collisions, or subduction-driven accretionary complexes. These geodynamic settings offer
vertical and lateral migration pathways that allow mantle-sourced hydrogen to move into the upper
crust. Other structural features, such as kimberlite pipes, also act as vertical conduits linking the
mantle to the surface. These pipes are typically found in stable cratonic regions with deep
lithospheric roots but may also occur in orogenic belts, at terrane boundaries, along rift zones, in
extensions of oceanic fracture zones, or within dyke swarms (Jelsma et al., 2009).

Migration pathways must possess sufficient permeability to facilitate hydrogen movement from
source rocks to reservoir formations. At the macro scale, structural features such as faults, natural
or induced fractures, unconformities, bedding planes, and foliations serve as discrete migration
conduits. Each of these pathways influences migration efficiency and the resulting volume of
hydrogen that may accumulate. For example, fractures are generally smaller-scale and often
discontinuous, leading to localized accumulations; however, they typically exhibit higher
permeability than faults and can be artificially enhanced through techniques such as hydraulic
fracturing. Faults, in contrast, may span larger distances and offer more continuous migration
pathways, but their permeability is often variable due to the presence of fault gouge or sealing
minerals.

At finer scales, diffusion through pore networks also interacts with these larger-scale structures.
Modeling efforts aimed at predicting hydrogen migration and accumulation must therefore
consider both micro-scale diffusion and macro- or continental-scale structural discontinuities to
accurately represent system behavior.

Hydrogen-rich gases may reach the surface in various forms, including bubbling springs, diffuse
seeps, or even visible flames emanating from fractures and faults. The nature of the surface
expression depends largely on the efficiency of the migration pathway and the depth and
concentration of the hydrogen source. Lateral migration of hydrogen can also occur through pore
spaces, driven by buoyancy forces and pressure gradients. For example, in the Bourakébougou
field in Mali, hydrogen-rich wells are located near the crest of an anticline, while wells along the
flanks exhibit lower hydrogen concentrations. This distribution likely reflects structural control,
where lateral migration causes hydrogen to accumulate at the apex of the fold due to the geometry
of the dipping limbs.

3.2.3 Reservoir

Geologic hydrogen storage typically relies on porous and permeable sedimentary rocks, such as
sandstones and carbonates, which possess sufficient primary porosity to accommodate free-phase
or dissolved hydrogen. These formations serve as natural traps where hydrogen, once generated
and migrated from the source, can accumulate and be stored.

 40

Unconventional or low-permeability reservoirs, including certain carbonates and shales, may also
act as hydrogen storage units. However, in these settings, hydrogen is primarily held within micro-
or nano-scale pore spaces, and the low matrix permeability limits natural flow. In such cases,
hydraulic fracture stimulation may be required to create effective permeability pathways to enable
hydrogen extraction.

In some geologic contexts, the reservoir rock itself may also serve as a source of hydrogen. This
occurs when serpentinization or radiolysis reactions take place within the reservoir, provided the
necessary mineralogical and geochemical conditions are present. An example of this is the
Bourakébougou field in Mali, where the reservoir contains ultramafic lithologies capable of
generating hydrogen in situ, likely sustained by ongoing serpentinization and freshwater recharge.

3.2.4 Containment (Trap / Seal)

An effective seal rock, or impermeable barrier that prevents vertical migration of hydrogen from
the reservoir, is typically composed of low-permeability lithologies and/or influenced by
subsurface conditions that enhance the sealing effect. Common seal lithologies include shales,
mudstones, evaporites, volcaniclastic rocks, and igneous intrusions—such as the dolerite sill that
acts as the seal at the Bourakébougou field in Mali.

In addition to lithologic properties, low permeability may be enhanced by diagenetic cements or
by subsurface conditions characterized by high pressure and low temperature. These conditions
reduce hydrogen diffusion rates and help maintain hydrogen in the free gas phase, where it is less
susceptible to biotic consumption. Such characteristics are observed at Bourakébougou, where the
effectiveness of the seal is further supported by its significant thickness and lack of fracturing.

In theory, a seal rock could also serve as an active source of hydrogen, particularly if
serpentinization is ongoing in the overlying formation and pressure gradients favor downward
migration into an underlying reservoir. While no natural hydrogen systems demonstrating this
configuration have been documented to date, similar source-over-seal behavior has been observed
in hydrocarbon systems, such as those in the Williston Basin and the Gulf of Mexico (Marra et al.,
2021; Snedden et al., 2020).

Salt formations offer another proven seal type. Salt is known for its extremely low permeability
and plastic behavior, making it an ideal seal for hydrogen. Artificially produced hydrogen is
currently stored in salt caverns at four known locations globally, three of which are located in the
United States (Lord et al., 2014). Additionally, salt has been a critical sealing element in many
major hydrocarbon systems worldwide, with classic examples of salt-trapped oil fields found in
the Gulf of Mexico.

In addition to a seal, successful containment requires a trap—a structural or stratigraphic
configuration that provides geometric closure and prevents upward or lateral escape of buoyant
hydrogen. Structural traps include anticlines, which are among the most effective and widely
documented trap types in hydrocarbon systems. Other trap types include fault traps, where an
impermeable fault juxtaposes a permeable reservoir, and pinch-out traps, where lateral facies
changes seal the reservoir on all sides. Combination traps, which involve both structural and

stratigraphic elements, are also common. For example, a reservoir may be bounded by a fault on
one side and a facies change or unconformity on another.

3.3.1 Natural Systems

The drilling and extraction of natural hydrogen from porous media or conventional reservoirs—
classified under the “Natural Serpentinization” and “Mantle-Source” system subtypes in Table 1—
closely parallels the operational practices of conventional oil and gas development. These
operations typically involve the drilling of one or more production wells. A substantial body of
knowledge exists both in the United States and internationally on oil and gas drilling technologies,
well completions, and hydrogen-compatible infrastructure. This includes materials designed to
resist hydrogen embrittlement, which are directly applicable to natural hydrogen production.

In Mali’s Bourakébougou field, for example, 24 wells—similar in configuration to the conceptual
well illustrated in Figure 9—have been drilled to extract hydrogen generated through natural
serpentinization (Maiga et al., 2023, 2024). This project provides the only documented case of
commercial hydrogen extraction from a conventional geologic reservoir, confirming the feasibility
of natural hydrogen production under suitable conditions.

The “Migration Pathway Target” subtype, also identified in Table 1, is analogous to
unconventional oil and gas plays where the primary production target is not a conventional porous
reservoir but rather structurally controlled features such as fractures or fault zones. In fractured
hydrocarbon plays, directional drilling is employed to intersect productive fracture networks with
sufficient permeability and saturation. A broad knowledge base from oil and gas operations
focused on fracture plays is directly transferable to the exploration and development of hydrogen
accumulations within similar structural migration pathways.

Although the production aspects of natural hydrogen systems are similar to those in the petroleum
industry, exploration remains less developed due to the limited number of confirmed hydrogen
plays. To date, the Bourakébougou field remains the only commercial project that has successfully
produced hydrogen from a conventional reservoir, and notably, it was discovered serendipitously
during a groundwater drilling operation (Maiga et al., 2023, 2024).

3.3.2 Anthropogenic Systems

The drilling and extraction processes for anthropogenic hydrogen systems are less well-defined
than those for natural systems, but they offer greater operational flexibility. Unlike natural systems,
which require the alignment of multiple elements—source, reservoir, seal, generation mechanism,

 44

 Geological Hydrogen: From Natural Occurrences to Anthropogenic Generation – A Critical Review of Potential,

temporal stability, and migration pathways—anthropogenic systems primarily require a source
rock and an engineered method to induce hydrogen generation.

Anthropogenic hydrogen extraction involves stimulating in-situ hydrogen generation from source
rocks. These may include iron-rich lithologies in the case of serpentinization or hydrocarbon-rich
lithologies in the case of in-situ hydrocarbon conversion. In many instances, permeability must be
created or enhanced in the source rock to allow generated hydrogen to flow toward the wellbore
for extraction. A substantial body of knowledge exists in the United States regarding enhanced oil
recovery techniques (e.g., waterflooding and CO₂-EOR), as well as unconventional extraction
methods such as horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing. These methods may be directly
applicable to anthropogenic hydrogen production.

Hydrocarbon conversion is a promising avenue, given the abundance of hydrocarbon resources
worldwide. These processes are conceptually analogous to serpentinization-based hydrogen
generation. In-situ hydrocarbon conversion, although still emerging, seeks to convert
hydrocarbons directly within the reservoir into hydrogen, leaving carbon dioxide and other
byproducts in place. Established thermal methods include in-situ gasification and in-situ
combustion, which operate via hydrocarbon cracking, aquathermolysis, the water-gas shift
reaction, and coke gasification (Hanfi et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024). Gasification involves
injecting air and/or oxygen along with water at elevated temperatures to thermally process heavy
oil reservoirs. The resulting reactions oxidize hydrocarbons and water, releasing heat and
producing hydrogen.

In-situ combustion, a more established method, is primarily aimed at enhancing oil recovery and
produces hydrogen as a byproduct (Hassanpouryouzband et al., 2025). Air or oxygen is injected
to oxidize hydrocarbons, generating heat that reduces the viscosity of unburned oil and mobilizes
it toward production wells. Hydrogen may be recovered using downhole membranes that separate
the gas mixture and bring hydrogen to the surface. Compared to gasification, in-situ combustion
may be less resource-intensive, as it uses compressed air and does not require external water or
steam input (Hanfi et al., 2024). Additional heating methods under investigation include radio-
frequency electromagnetic and microwave technologies (Song et al., 2024).

Steam methane reforming (SMR), the most common hydrogen production method, is typically
conducted in surface reactors but could be adapted for in-situ application, particularly in gas fields
(U.S. Department of Energy, 2025). In SMR, methane reacts with steam in the presence of a
catalyst, often nickel-based, to produce hydrogen and other gases. A subsequent water-gas shift
reaction converts carbon monoxide and steam into additional hydrogen and CO₂. The CO₂ can be
sequestered in the reservoir, while hydrogen is separated and produced at the surface.

These and other in-situ conversion techniques are described in greater detail by previous studies
(Hassanpouryouzband et al., 2025; Salahshoor & Afzal, 2022), and are summarized in Table 3.
Catalysts play an important role in improving the efficiency of thermal processes, particularly by
lowering the energy required for combustion and increasing hydrogen yield. Natural materials
already present in the reservoir, such as water, quartz, clay, and metallic minerals, can act as
catalytic agents (Hanfi et al., 2024). Synthetic catalysts, including water-soluble metal salts or oil-

 45

soluble formulations, may also be injected, although further research is needed to understand their
long-term behavior and effectiveness (Hanfi et al., 2024; Hassanpouryouzband et al., 2025).

One major challenge of thermal conversion processes is their low efficiency, which stems from
limited control over chemical reactions and heat loss to the surrounding reservoir (Ikpeka & Ugwu,
2023). Formation damage is also a concern, especially when aggressive reactions alter reservoir
rock properties(Hanfi et al., 2024).

Biological conversion is another emerging technique for in-situ hydrogen production. In one
example, a biotechnology firm used bacteria to convert residual oil in a depleted well into hydrogen
and carbon dioxide (Baraniuk, 2022). This approach may be applicable to the thousands of
abandoned wells across the United States and could provide a low-cost opportunity for hydrogen
recovery in legacy oil fields.

Economic considerations remain critical in evaluating anthropogenic hydrogen strategies. In many
cases, it may be more viable to extract hydrocarbons directly than to convert them in situ. However,
non-commercial hydrocarbon accumulations or depleted fields may offer favorable conditions for
hydrogen generation. Although research is currently focused on using depleted oil and gas fields
for hydrogen storage, these same formations may also be viable for anthropogenic hydrogen
production.

Anthropogenic hydrogen generation by serpentinization involves directly targeting iron-rich
source rocks to induce hydrogen-producing reactions. This requires injecting water-based
stimulants under elevated temperatures (typically 60 to 260 degrees Celsius) and pressures
exceeding 50 bars (Gates & Wang, 2022). Candidate injectants include water, CO₂ (Osselin et al.,
2022), and catalysts such as nickel and cobalt (Goren et al., 2025). After allowing sufficient
reaction time, formation fluids containing hydrogen and other gases (e.g., methane, CO₂, nitrogen)
can be recovered through the wellbore. Hydrogen can then be separated using membranes or
density-based techniques, while remaining fluids may be reprocessed. In cases where CO₂ is used,
it may offer ancillary benefits such as geologic carbon sequestration, similar to enhanced oil
recovery processes.

Similarly to serpentinization reducing water molecules in the presence of iron-rich mineral like
olivine, a new study found that ammonia (NH3) can be generated by reacting natural olivine
minerals with nitrate-source water at temperatures between 130°C and 300°C, and even at ambient
conditions (Gao et al., 2025). The hydrogen in NH3 comes from H2O reduction as in
serpentinization. The process requires no external hydrogen or electric current and emits no direct
CO2, Using Cu2+ as a catalyst, 1.8 kg NH3/t olivine was produced at 300°C in 21 hours. It is
estimated that the Earth's subsurface could theoretically produce enough NH3 for 2.42 million
years (Gao et al., 2025).

Hydraulic fracturing can improve both hydrocarbon conversion and serpentinization processes by
enhancing connectivity and reactive surface area in low-permeability formations. A common well
configuration includes a horizontal leg drilled into the source rock, which is then hydraulically
fractured using high-pressure water and proppant to keep the induced fractures open. The “huff
and puff” technique, traditionally used in enhanced oil recovery, may be adapted for cyclic

 46

hydrogen production. In this method, a single well is used for both injection and production: water,
steam, or catalyzed fluids are injected to stimulate reactions (huff), followed by a soak period, then
gas production (puff). The cycle is repeated to maximize recovery (Wan et al., 2024).

Despite the potential of anthropogenic hydrogen generation, significant knowledge gaps remain.
These include a limited understanding of how best to induce and expedite serpentinization in iron-
rich rocks, uncertainties related to reaction kinetics under various subsurface conditions, and
optimization of production processes. Materials safety and techno-economic assessments for both
processes and materials also require further study.

of reported hydrogen finds are primarily clustered in the midcontinent region, particularly near the
Midcontinent Rift System in Kansas, Iowa, and Nebraska. Additional isolated occurrences have
been reported in Utah, as shown in Figure 12.

One notable exception is a well drilled intentionally for hydrogen exploration. In 2019, Natural
Hydrogen Energy drilled an exploratory well in Nebraska specifically targeting subsurface
hydrogen accumulations (Pearce et al., 2024). This marks one of the first purpose-drilled hydrogen
wells in the U.S. and highlights a growing interest in identifying commercially viable hydrogen
resources.

Despite these efforts, geologic hydrogen remains significantly underexplored within the U.S., and
large uncertainties persist regarding the scale and recoverability of potential hydrogen
accumulations. Continued geologic mapping, data integration, and targeted drilling will be
essential to reduce these uncertainties and assess the commercial potential of U.S. geologic
hydrogen systems.

4.1 OPHIOLITES

Global occurrences of geologic hydrogen frequently coincide with ophiolitic complexes, as
previously discussed in the Global Occurrence section. Examples from Turkey, Albania, Oman,
New Caledonia, and the Philippines consistently show that ophiolites are favorable hosts for
hydrogen generation, particularly through low- to high-temperature serpentinization. This global
context strongly suggests that ophiolites in the U.S. should be considered prime targets for
geologic hydrogen prospectivity analyses.

While fewer data are available from U.S. ophiolite systems, there are notable cases. Previous study
documented hydrogen production in serpentinized peridotites near Austin, Texas, where measured
hydrogen concentrations reached up to 50.9 percent, accompanied by methane, nitrogen, and light
hydrocarbons (Morrill et al., 2013). This example highlights the potential for active hydrogen
generation in U.S. ultramafic systems and supports the application of similar geochemical and
isotopic investigation methods used internationally.

In this study, two ophiolite regions are examined in greater detail. The first is the greenstone belt
extending along the U.S. East Coast, which is spatially associated with the so-called "Carolina
Bays" or fairy circles—enigmatic surface features hypothesized to mark subsurface gas emissions.
The second is the Cedars Peridotite in northern California, a site known for peridotite-hosted hot
springs and active serpentinization processes. These regions offer promising opportunities for
future exploration of anthropogenic hydrogen generation, supported by analogs from globally
studied ophiolite-hosted systems.

Precambrian ophiolite complexes along the eastern seaboard of the United States have been
proposed as potential sources of ongoing geologic hydrogen generation. This hypothesis is based
in part on their spatial association with hydrogen-enriched soil gas measurements taken within the

The Carolina Bays—sometimes referred to colloquially as “fairy circles”—are shallow, elliptical
geomorphic features characterized by unconsolidated granular sediments, elevated rims, marshy
interiors, and a relative absence of large vegetation. One hypothesis for this lack of vegetation is
that microbial activity in the bay centers consumes hydrogen, thereby suppressing microbial
processes such as nitrogen fixation that typically support vegetation growth (Zgonnik et al., 2015).

Hydrogen flux from these features has been estimated to reach up to 2,700 cubic meters per day
in some bays (Zgonnik et al., 2015). Soil gas samples taken from the coarse sand rims of the bays
have shown hydrogen concentrations exceeding 1,200 parts per million (ppm), while the wetland
centers exhibit lower concentrations. This pattern suggests that the hydrogen is not biogenically
produced in situ, but rather is migrating upward along deep-seated pathways, with the Carolina
Bays potentially representing the surface terminations of those pathways.

One hypothesis for bay formation proposes that the upward migration of hydrogen causes
diagenesis and the development of secondary porosity, weakening overlying lithologies and
resulting in localized surface collapse and formation of the elliptical bay structures. This
hypothesis, if validated, would link deep geologic hydrogen generation and migration with a
distinct geomorphic surface expression.

An alternative and competing hypothesis suggests that the Carolina Bays are impact features
formed during the late Pleistocene, as part of the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis. In this model,
the bays are interpreted as secondary impact structures formed from debris ejected during a bolide
strike on the Laurentide Ice Sheet. Supporting evidence includes (1) the orientation of long axes

the “Nebraska Bays”), appear to converge on a region in Canada, potentially identifying a central
impact point (Figure 14), and (2) analysis of quartz grains within bay sediments indicating rapid
formation during the late Pleistocene. While this hypothesis does not explain the elevated
hydrogen levels, it raises the possibility of unique lithologic or morphological conditions that may
facilitate biotic decomposition of organics or hydrogen migration, which could be explored
through isotopic analysis.

However, evidence of recent bay formation observed by the previous study (Zgonnik et al., 2015)
contradicts the impact origin theory. They documented the present-day formation of an ovoid
structure within the bounds of an existing bay, marked by vegetation dieback and elevated
hydrogen soil gas readings (Figure 15), suggesting active geologic or microbial processes remain
ongoing.

The correlation of elevated hydrogen in soil gas with ophiolitic source rocks in this region suggests
a deep geologic hydrogen generation mechanism, likely serpentinization. The Carolina Bays may
represent surface expressions of migration pathways associated with basement thrust zones, where
ultramafic oceanic crust has been emplaced onto the continental margin (Figure 16). If confirmed,
the ophiolitic source rocks and associated migration features could represent viable targets for
natural hydrogen production via drilling, or for anthropogenic hydrogen generation through
stimulation and engineered recovery.

The ultramafic complexes in this region have been previously described in the literature. Previous
study characterizes areas such as the Asheville, North Carolina mining district as ultramafic
mineral carbonation zones (O’Connor et al., 2005), while another study describes ultramafic suites

hydrogen in this region.

4.1.2 Cedars Peridotite in California

Ophiolites are sections of oceanic crust and upper mantle that have been tectonically emplaced
onto continental margins through subduction and accretionary processes. These assemblages
frequently contain ultramafic rocks such as peridotite, which are rich in ferrous iron (Fe²⁺). When
these minerals—particularly olivine and pyroxenes—undergo serpentinization, a redox reaction
occurs in which ferrous iron is oxidized to ferric iron (Fe³⁺), and water is simultaneously reduced
to form molecular hydrogen. This process is a key mechanism for natural hydrogen generation in
ultramafic terrains.

The United States hosts several major ophiolite complexes, largely concentrated along the western
margin, reflecting its history of oceanic crust accretion. Notable examples include the California
Coast Range, Josephine, and Trinity ophiolites. Among these, the Cedars peridotite body in
northern California represents a particularly well-characterized example of a serpentinization-
prone ultramafic system with documented hydrogen emissions (Figure 17).

The Cedars peridotite is located in the Coast Ranges of northern California and is part of an
obducted ultramafic body associated with the Franciscan Subduction Complex (FSC). The rock
mass is composed primarily of olivine, orthopyroxene, and clinopyroxene. The exposed peridotite
body at The Cedars measures approximately 3.5 kilometers in width and 6.4 kilometers in length,
with an estimated vertical extent of 1–2 kilometers beneath the surface (Morrill et al., 2013).

Deep groundwater, originating from the FSC, flows upward through the peridotite and discharges
at the surface via more than 15 ultrabasic springs near creeks and rivers. These springs are
concentrated in the headwaters of Austin Creek. The geochemical composition of the discharging
groundwater reflects interaction with both the overlying peridotite and the marine sedimentary
units of the FSC. Gas samples collected from several of these springs revealed active degassing of
nitrogen (N₂), hydrogen (H₂), and methane (CH₄). The presence of hydrogen in these gases is
attributed to serpentinization reactions occurring within the ultramafic rock, where hydration of
iron-bearing minerals leads to hydrogen generation. Specifically, one of the key serpentinization
reactions involves the alteration of iron (II)-hydroxide to produce hydrogen gas:

The presence of hydrogen gas, iron-bearing minerals acting as natural catalysts, and elevated
temperatures associated with serpentinization creates a favorable geochemical condition for the
abiotic reduction of carbon to methane and potentially other hydrocarbons. This process can
proceed through Fischer–Tropsch-type (FTT) synthesis reactions, in which molecular hydrogen
reacts with inorganic carbon compounds to form methane and longer-chain alkanes (Sleep et al.,
2004). Such reactions are well-documented in other serpentinization systems globally and are
likely occurring in U.S. ophiolitic terrains as well.

In the case of the Cedars peridotite, geochemical conditions are highly favorable for both hydrogen
generation and secondary hydrocarbon formation. Serpentinization occurs under ultrabasic
conditions, with spring water pH values ranging from 11 to 12, and strongly reducing redox
potentials (Eh values between –656 and –585 mV). These conditions are consistent with those
observed in other actively serpentinizing ultramafic systems known to support hydrogen and
methane production.

Although direct seepages of hydrogen in U.S. ophiolites are not as extensively documented as in
global analogs, multiple lines of evidence point to ongoing in-situ hydrogen generation. These
include the presence of hyperalkaline springs, characteristic mineralogical transitions associated
with serpentinization, and microbial communities adapted to hydrogen- and methane-rich
conditions. Together, these features support the geologic feasibility of hydrogen generation in U.S.
ophiolites. Despite this potential, these systems remain relatively understudied compared to

banded iron formations or industrial hydrogen sources. Further field studies are needed to quantify
production rates and evaluate their economic potential.

Gas samples collected from springs at the Cedars provide insight into hydrogen generation and
associated gas compositions. In the headwaters of Austin Creek, gas compositions were measured
as 63.1 percent nitrogen (N₂), 15.7 percent hydrogen (H₂), and 15.8 percent methane (CH₄) by
volume. Even higher hydrogen concentrations were observed at nearby Camp Spring and Barnes
Spring, where hydrogen accounted for 50.9 percent and 34.0 percent of the total gas volume,
respectively. These measurements indicate significant in-situ hydrogen generation, most likely
derived from serpentinization reactions within the peridotite body.

The combination of favorable lithology, active geochemical processes, and measurable hydrogen
fluxes makes the Cedars peridotite a compelling target for further geologic hydrogen research. It
also serves as a valuable domestic analog to better-characterized systems overseas, offering
opportunities for both scientific investigation and early-stage exploration and development of
natural or anthropogenic hydrogen resources.

4.2 MIDCONTINENT RIFT SYSTEM (KANSAS-NEBRASKA)

The Midcontinental Rift System (MRS) is a 1.1-billion-year-old failed rift extending across the
Midwest United States from Minnesota to Kansas (Figure 18; (Coveney et al., 1987)). This system
transects approximately 2,500 km of the North American craton, stretching northeastward from
Kansas through the axis of Lake Superior and then southeastward into Michigan (Hinze &
Chandler, 2020). The rifting event thinned the continental crust in the region, bringing iron-rich
ultramafic mantle rocks (peridotite) closer to the surface. The normal faulting and horst-graben
structures associated with this rift system, such as the Nemaha uplift in Kansas, potentially provide
deep-seated fault pathways facilitating upward migration of mantle-generated hydrogen (H2) from
serpentinization and mantle degassing processes and allowing downward migration of meteoric
waters towards iron-rich source rocks (Figure 19). Basement fracture systems produced during the
failed rifting likely facilitate this bidirectional exchange of fluids. Kimberlite intrusions associated
with the rift system represent another plausible source and migration pathway for geological
hydrogen.

4.3 BANDED IRON FORMATION (MINNESOTA-MICHIGAN BIF RANGES)

4.3.1 Genesis of Banded Iron Formation

Banded iron formations (BIFs) are Precambrian marine sedimentary rocks found within cratonic
regions on all continents (Klein & Beukes, 1993). They represent major global reserves of iron
ore, accounting for over 60 percent of global iron production, and typically contain 20–40 weight
percent total iron with low Fe³⁺/Fetotal ratios. BIFs formed primarily during two key periods in
Earth's history: the Archean to Paleoproterozoic (approximately 3,500 to 1,800 million years ago)
and the Neoproterozoic (approximately 900 to 500 million years ago) (Ilyin, 2009).

The widely accepted model for BIF genesis involves the precipitation of ferric iron (Fe³⁺) from
ocean waters originally rich in dissolved ferrous iron (Fe²⁺). This oxidation is believed to have
been driven by oxygen produced by early cyanobacteria during photosynthesis, or by
photoferrotrophic microorganisms capable of oxidizing Fe²⁺ using light energy (Bekker et al.,
2010). For Neoproterozoic BIFs, deposition may be associated with transient ocean oxygenation
events following long periods of anoxia during Snowball Earth glaciations (Cox et al., 2013).

Four principal types of BIFs have been identified: Algoma, Superior, Rapitan, and Ediacaran
(Figure 21) (Aftabi et al., 2021). These types differ in terms of depositional setting, age, structure,
and geochemical characteristics. For example, the presence of micro- to nanobanding, diamictites,
dropstones, and cap carbonates are distinguishing structural features. Geochemically, BIFs exhibit
variability in immobile element concentrations (e.g., SiO₂, Al₂O₃, TiO₂), degrees of oxide
enrichment or depletion, and levels of hydrothermal, detrital, or hydrogenous contamination. The
source of phosphorus may vary, originating from exhalative hydrothermal systems or deep-sea
diagenetic-biogenic sources. Magnetite within BIFs can reflect varying crystallization
temperatures and hydrothermal origins, while the broader tectonic setting can include intraoceanic
island arcs, oceanic rift zones, intracontinental rift settings, or submarine volcano-sedimentary
settings.

Globally, significant BIF deposits are located in Archean and Proterozoic terranes in South Africa,
Australia, Brazil, China, and India. Notable examples include the Kaapvaal Craton in South Africa
(Moretti et al., 2022), the São Francisco Basin in Brazil (Geymond et al., 2022), and the Pilbara
and Yilgarn cratons in Western Australia (Rezaee, 2020). These regions host actively mined iron
deposits and have been associated with natural hydrogen occurrences. For instance, well samples
from Precambrian rocks in South Africa have yielded measurable concentrations of hydrogen, and
hydrogen emissions have been reported near iron mines situated in Proterozoic terranes.

In Brazil, remote sensing analyses have identified circular to sub-circular depressions—also
referred to as “fairy circles”—in proximity to BIF-hosted iron mines, particularly within the São
Francisco Basin. Similar geomorphic features have been mapped in the Hamersley Province of
Western Australia and the Kaapvaal Craton in South Africa. These sub-circular depressions
(SCDs) exhibit morphologies, dimensions, and spatial distributions that are consistent with
localized gas seepage. When found near BIF outcrops or iron mines, these features may indicate
underlying hydrogen emissions, although further field validation and isotopic studies are necessary
to confirm their origin (Moretti et al., 2021).

4.3.2 Hydrogen Generation from Banded Iron Formations

Iron oxidation coupled with water reduction is a well-established mechanism for hydrogen
generation, most notably observed in serpentinization systems. Recent research has extended this
concept to banded iron formations (BIFs), exploring their potential to generate natural hydrogen
through low-temperature weathering and water–rock interactions. Systematic analyses of BIFs,
incorporating petrological, mineralogical, and geochemical data, have suggested that the oxidation
of ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) in minerals such as magnetite can release H₂ under near-surface, low-
temperature conditions (Geymond et al., 2023).

 63

 Geological Hydrogen: From Natural Occurrences to Anthropogenic Generation – A Critical Review of Potential,

Experimental studies have demonstrated that magnetite can generate significant quantities of
hydrogen at temperatures below 100°C—conditions much milder than those associated with high-
temperature hydrothermal processes such as serpentinization (Geymond et al., 2023; Mayhew et
al., 2013). The hydrogen production mechanism involves a redox reaction in which water acts as
the oxidant. In anoxic conditions, water oxidizes Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ while itself being reduced to H₂. This
reaction can be summarized as follows:

Fe²⁺ (in magnetite) + H₂O → Fe³⁺ (in hematite or maghemite) + H₂↑

The oxidation of magnetite to hematite or maghemite through water–rock interaction plays a
central role in this process. As deeply circulating meteoric water enters reducing zones in the
subsurface—where conventional oxidants such as O₂, NO₃⁻, and SO₄²⁻ have been consumed by
organic matter oxidation—conditions become favorable for this redox-driven hydrogen
generation. Laboratory experiments simulating low-temperature burial conditions have
demonstrated measurable hydrogen production over timescales of days to weeks.

Based on these findings, a conceptual vertical reactive profile has been proposed (Figure 22), in
which hydrogen is generated at depth within BIF units through the progressive oxidation of
magnetite. This hydrogen may then migrate upward through permeable rock layers to reach the
surface. In this model, maghemite (γ-Fe₂O₃) forms as an intermediate oxidation product of
magnetite (α-Fe₃O₄) when exposed to oxygen-rich waters, and this transformation may represent
a newly recognized mechanism for subsurface hydrogen generation (Geymond et al., 2023).

While additional experimental and field validation is required, the existing data support the
hypothesis that magnetite-rich lithologies—such as those found in BIFs—should be considered
promising targets in the exploration for natural hydrogen resources. Furthermore, magnetite may
serve as a viable source for both naturally occurring and anthropogenically stimulated hydrogen
production.

To estimate the hydrogen generation potential from magnetite in BIFs, it is necessary to quantify
the abundance and oxidation state of iron species present. Direct estimation of hydrogen release
potential can be achieved through mineralogical and geochemical characterization, including the
identification and quantification of Fe²⁺-bearing phases and the extent of magnetite alteration. This
information will be critical for resource assessment and the evaluation of BIFs as hydrogen-
generating lithologies.

4.3.3 Global Occurrence of Banded Iron Formation

Australia
The potential for natural hydrogen generation from banded iron formations (BIFs) in Western
Australia (WA) is promising. The Archean Yilgarn Craton hosts abundant iron-rich mafic–
ultramafic rocks, fault systems, and overlying Eocene sedimentary units—all of which contribute
to favorable geological conditions for hydrogen production (Rezaee, 2020). Solid–liquid redox
reactions between iron-bearing minerals and groundwater in deep basement rocks could facilitate
hydrogen generation. The Yilgarn Craton’s complex assemblage of metamorphosed granites,
volcanic rocks, and sedimentary units—combined with evidence of hydrothermal alteration—
supports the plausibility of ongoing subsurface hydrogen production.

Brazil

Brazil contains some of the largest and most significant BIF deposits globally, notably within the
Quadrilátero Ferrífero (Iron Quadrangle) in Minas Gerais and the Carajás Mineral Province in Pará
(Geymond et al., 2022). These iron formations, dominated by hematite and magnetite, often
contain 20–40 wt% total iron, with localized enrichment exceeding 60 wt%. Mineralogical
transformations due to weathering, particularly under Brazil’s tropical climate, may contribute to
hydrogen generation through oxidation of Fe²⁺ phases.

Satellite imagery near BIF-hosted iron mines has revealed sub-circular depressions (SCDs),
morphological features also associated with hydrogen seepage in other regions. These SCDs have
been documented near BIF outcrops in the Carajás region and the São Francisco Basin. While
direct measurements of hydrogen emissions are lacking, the structural and climatic setting is
conducive to water–rock reactions that could produce hydrogen. The Archean BIFs in Carajás,
hosted in the Amazonian Craton, and the Proterozoic BIFs of the Iron Quadrangle on the São
Francisco Craton are particularly promising targets for further investigation.

South Africa
In South Africa, BIFs occur predominantly within the Transvaal Supergroup, including the
Griqualand West region, which hosts major iron ore deposits such as Sishen and Kolomela. These
formations, rich in iron oxides and hosted on the Kaapvaal Craton, are comparable in composition
and structure to BIFs found in other hydrogen-generating regions. The region’s Proterozoic
sedimentary history, combined with present-day oxidative weathering and groundwater
circulation, supports the possibility of hydrogen generation. Although hydrogen has been detected
in Precambrian rocks, direct emissions associated with BIFs have yet to be fully characterized
through field-based gas measurements.

China
China’s BIFs are primarily located in the Wugang (North China Craton) and Hengyang (South
China Craton) regions (Kim et al., 2025). Formed between 3.8 and 1.8 billion years ago, these
BIFs are composed mainly of magnetite and hematite. Studies suggest that BIFs rich in Fe²⁺
phases—especially magnetite—may produce hydrogen through low-temperature water–rock
reactions. Magnetite-rich BIFs with low Fe³⁺/Fetotal ratios are considered favorable. One study
estimated that a high-Fe²⁺ magnetite sample could generate up to 630 mmol H₂ per kilogram of
rock under optimal conditions. These findings highlight the importance of mineralogical
characterization in assessing BIF-related hydrogen potential.

USA
Due to similarities in geological setting observed globally, the potential for hydrogen production
from banded iron formations (BIFs) in North America refers to the capacity to extract hydrogen
gas from naturally occurring BIFs found throughout the continent. These iron-rich sedimentary
rocks may react with water under favorable subsurface conditions to generate hydrogen. Potential
regions of interest include the Upper Peninsula of Michigan, parts of the Midwest such as Iowa,
and areas in Wyoming.

BIFs in the Lake Superior region, particularly in Michigan’s Upper Peninsula, are of special
interest due to their extensive areal coverage and significant vertical thicknesses—up to 3,000 feet
(Figure 23). These characteristics provide a range of subsurface conditions (e.g., temperature,
water-rock interaction depth) favorable for sustained hydrogen generation. Similar to BIFs in
Australia and Brazil, sub-circular depressions (SCDs), sometimes referred to as “fairy circles,”
have been identified near these formations (Figure 24), and may be surface expressions of
hydrogen migrating from depth (Lévy et al., 2023).

However, distinguishing these features in the Lake Superior region is complicated by glacial
geomorphology. In particular, Minnesota—known as the “land of 10,000 lakes”—hosts numerous
depressions and surface features attributed to past glaciation. Additionally, sinkholes formed in
carbonate bedrock due to acidic groundwater may obscure or mimic the morphology of hydrogen-
related SCDs.

The BIF ranges of the Lake Superior region are intruded by the Midcontinent Rift System (MRS),
a failed Proterozoic rift that cuts across the central part of the North American plate (Duncanson
et al., 2024). The MRS, previously targeted for oil and gas exploration, exposes thick sequences
of igneous and volcanic rocks that may serve as hydrogen-generating source rocks. During rift
formation, tectonic uplift and fracturing may have brought deeper BIF horizons closer to the
surface or increased permeability for water-rock interaction. Of the seven major BIF belts in the
region, two remain active iron mining districts producing magnetite-rich Taconite. This iron oxide
resource, concentrated within distinct horizons of BIFs, is of direct relevance to hydrogen
generation through redox reactions.

To adequately assess the geologic hydrogen potential of BIFs in the Lake Superior region, a
comprehensive program of systematic sampling is needed. This includes:

 • Core sampling and groundwater collection from multiple locations across BIF outcrops
 and at depth
 • Detailed geologic, mineralogical, and geochemical analysis of the samples
 • Laboratory batch experiments to evaluate hydrogen generation pathways and kinetic
 behavior

Through such efforts, it will be possible to improve our understanding of hydrogen-producing
redox processes within BIFs, to determine whether viable natural hydrogen reservoirs exist, to
identify potential migration pathways for drilling, and to evaluate the feasibility of stimulating
anthropogenic hydrogen production from in-situ BIFs.

5. PROSPECTIVITY ANALYSIS METHODS FOR GEOLOGIC HYDROGEN

Prospectivity analysis, traditionally used in the oil and gas and mineral exploration industries, is a
systematic approach to evaluating the potential for undiscovered resources in an area. It involves
integrating geological, geochemical, and geophysical data to identify areas with the highest
likelihood of containing occurrences of a target resource. Beyond these data types, however, the
prospectivity analysis also incorporates the key processes and components resulting in resource
occurrences. While initially developed for hydrocarbons and mineral systems, the fundamental
principles of prospectivity analysis can be adapted and applied to geologic hydrogen exploration.
The cornerstone of this adaptation lies in a modified "petroleum system" concept, shifting from
source rock, reservoir, seal, and trap to encompass key elements vital for a hydrogen prospect.

5.1 GEOGRAPHIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS GIS-BASED ANALYSIS

Central to the approach is integration of all available data into a Geographic Information Systems
(GIS) to create spatially references layers, facilitating visualization, analysis, and modeling of
spatial relationships. For geologic hydrogen, this translates into defining a "hydrogen system"
encompassing all geological elements and processes controlling hydrogen generation, migration,
accumulation, and preservation. This system emphasizes the hydrogen source, migration
pathways, reservoir, trap, seal, and the timing of events. Unlike petroleum systems where source
rock dominates, hydrogen can arise from diverse mechanisms, necessitating careful identification
of the dominant source within a specific area and its quantitative characterization. Event charting
and timing analysis would be important as well (e.g., constructing timelines of geological events
such as ultramafic rock emplacement, faulting onset, hydrothermal activity, and seal development
and analyzing their temporal relationships).

USGS has recently released their first map of geologic hydrogen prospectivity and the data that
comprise of geologic and geophysical inputs used to create the geologic hydrogen prospectivity
map (USGS, 2025a). The maps are based on the geologic hydrogen system model, which includes
three primary components: source of natural hydrogen, reservoir to store hydrogen in the
subsurface, and seal/trap to retain the hydrogen and prevent leakage to the Earth’s surface (Gelman
et al., 2025).

In addition to petroleum systems concepts, insights from mineral systems concepts and mineral
prospectivity mapping can be highly valuable for exploration and discovery of potential
occurrences. The mineral systems concept focuses on the geological processes and controls that
lead to the concentration of valuable elements in ore deposits. Analogously, geologic hydrogen
can be considered as a "hydrogen deposit" formed through the concentration of hydrogen gas via
specific geological processes.

5.2 EXPERIMENTAL METHODS FOR CHARACTERIZING ROCK MASS
VALUES

A wide range of experimental methods (Table 5) is available to characterize the mineralogical and
chemical attributes of geological samples when assessing the prospectivity of banded iron
formations (BIFs) and ultramafic rocks—both relevant for geological hydrogen production and

 70

potentially hosting other economically valuable minerals. Optical microscopy, incorporating both
transmitted and reflected light, remains foundational for initial mineral identification and textural
analysis. Thin-section petrography clarifies the distribution of silicate and carbonate phases, while
reflected-light observations are critical for examining opaque minerals, including iron oxides and
sulfides, which frequently exhibit varying iron oxidation states (Fe²⁺ versus Fe³⁺).

For higher resolution, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) clarifies fine-grained textures and
compositional contrasts via backscattered electron (BSE) detection, whereas energy-dispersive X-
ray spectroscopy (EDS) permits rapid, semi-quantitative spot analyses of key minerals. For
ultramafic rocks, where serpentinization processes can generate molecular hydrogen, SEM-EDS
aids in identifying serpentine, brucite, magnetite, and other phases indicative of fluid-rock
reactions and alteration pathways. Electron microprobe analysis (EPMA), employing wavelength-
dispersive spectroscopy, delivers precise quantitative elemental concentrations, including total
iron content and associated cations in silicates and oxides. Although EPMA alone does not directly
differentiate iron oxidation states, it provides critical compositional data that, when integrated with
spectroscopic methods such as Mössbauer spectroscopy or XANES, robustly characterizes redox
conditions in BIF minerals and serpentinized ultra-mafics, thus contributing significantly to
evaluating hydrogen production potential. In scenarios requiring nanometer-scale resolution—
such as identifying clay minerals, micro-inclusions, or fine exsolution textures—transmission
electron microscopy (TEM), coupled with electron diffraction and EDS, enables detailed
examination of subtle mineral transformations relevant to hydrogen-generating reactions.

At a broader scale, X-ray diffraction (XRD) remains a standard technique to determine the
mineralogical makeup of BIF and ultramafic samples, especially where cryptocrystalline or fine-
grained phases are intergrown. In BIFs, XRD can distinguish between magnetite, hematite, and
other iron-bearing phases (e.g., siderite), while in ultramafic rocks it identifies serpentine varieties,
residual olivine, and accessory phases (e.g., chromite). Although less routine in economic
evaluations, single-crystal XRD and neutron diffraction reveal structural details important for
advanced characterization of accessory minerals, particularly crystallographic arrangements
influencing hydrogen generation processes.

For bulk chemical compositions, X-ray fluorescence (XRF) is often employed to measure major
and minor elements cost-effectively, providing an overview of total iron content and
concentrations of other economically relevant elements (e.g., Cr, Ni, or PGE in ultramafic
systems). Alternatively, inductively coupled plasma–optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) or
inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analyzes complex elemental suites
(trace and major) at higher sensitivities, clarifying the abundance of valuable trace components
alongside total iron. Techniques such as laser ablation ICP-MS (LA-ICP-MS) and secondary ion
mass spectrometry (SIMS) enable in situ microanalysis of mineral grains, often revealing
significant trace metal enrichments (e.g., Ni in serpentinized ultra-mafics, rare earth elements in
accessory minerals, or precious metals in sulfides), contributing to the economic valuation of the
rock mass.

Spectroscopic methods are powerful tools for investigating oxidation states—particularly the
Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ ratio—and other structural characteristics in BIF and ultramafic contexts. Raman
spectroscopy rapidly identifies mineral species in situ, including carbonaceous matter or minor

 71

 Geological Hydrogen: From Natural Occurrences to Anthropogenic Generation – A Critical Review of Potential,

phases in BIFs, and serpentine polymorphs in ultra-mafics. Fourier transform infrared
spectroscopy (FTIR) detects hydroxyl groups and carbonates, aiding in the interpretation of
hydration or carbonation processes affecting hydrogen potential. Mössbauer spectroscopy is
uniquely valuable for determining Fe²⁺ versus Fe³⁺ states, elucidating redox conditions central to
hydrogen-generating mineral-fluid interactions. Advanced structural analyses via synchrotron-
based X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure
(EXAFS) refine understanding of local coordination structures and oxidation states, informing
hydrogen generation and capture mechanisms.

Thermal analysis methods, including thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), differential scanning
calorimetry (DSC), and differential thermal analysis (DTA), further inform on mass-loss events
(e.g., dehydration of serpentine forming magnetite and H₂), phase transitions, and thermal stability
of mineral assemblages. Coupled with mineralogical data, thermal behavior elucidates reaction
pathways and temperatures favorable for hydrogen generation.

Imaging techniques such as X-ray computed tomography (CT) and micro-CT enable non-
destructive, three-dimensional visualization of internal rock structures, beneficial for quantifying
porosity, fracture networks, and accessory mineral distributions. In hydrogen generation contexts,
these 3D data illustrate reaction surface connectivity and potential fluid pathways. Physical
property measurements—density, magnetic susceptibility, and electrical conductivity—
complement chemical and mineralogical datasets by highlighting geophysical signatures; for
example, magnetic susceptibility and density data discriminate high-iron layers in BIFs or
magnetite-rich serpentinized zones, aligning with geophysical surveys identifying large
prospective targets at depth.

Combining isotopic and geochronological methods illuminates fluid and thermal histories
associated with mineralization and serpentinization. U-Pb dating of zircons constrains ultramafic
rock emplacement timing, while stable isotopes (O, H, C, S) indicate fluid sources, mineral-
forming temperatures, and conditions conducive to hydrogen production. Fluid inclusion
microthermometry, particularly in fracture fillings or altered zones, provides direct evidence of
fluid salinity, temperature, and composition—essential for evaluating hydrothermal processes
influencing hydrogen generation.

Specialized techniques such as cathodoluminescence (CL) microscopy and electron backscatter
diffraction (EBSD) further refine textural interpretations, revealing zoning or deformation
microstructures affecting reaction rates and mineral intergrowths.

Integrating these multi-scale analytical methods—ranging from macroscopic petrography to
nanoscale chemical analyses—enables economic geologists to conduct rigorous, data-driven
evaluations of BIF or ultramafic rock masses, effectively assessing hydrogen potential and broader
economic value. Studies of ophiolites from Albania, Oman, Turkey, the Philippines, New
Caledonia, and beyond highlight how careful gas sampling, isotopic analysis, and water chemistry
studies converge to yield a nuanced understanding of these systems. Integrating such approaches
into U.S. ophiolite research can illuminate domestic occurrences, help quantify their significance,
and ultimately place American hydrogen resources in a broader global context.

6. RECENT RESEARCH FOCUS, PENDING QUESTIONS AND NETL’S ROLE

This report references a total of 32 publications from the years 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025, as
summarized in Table 6. These publications were reviewed to assess the current state of knowledge
and identify research gaps across various aspects of geologic hydrogen systems.

Eleven publications focused on geologic hydrogen broadly. Among these, five provided general
overviews, one addressed prospectivity analysis, one focused on source rock characteristics, one
examined generation mechanisms, two discussed hydrogen migration, and one explored microbial
consumption processes. Additionally, two analog studies were included for their relevance to
geologic hydrogen prospectivity—these studies focused on rare earth elements and critical mineral
exploration techniques, which may offer transferable methodologies.

Nine publications focused primarily on natural geologic hydrogen systems. These included four
case studies on specific occurrences, one publication addressing both generation and consumption
mechanisms, one on reservoir characterization, two on containment and seal properties, and one
additional study on prospectivity. Two further analog studies addressed intermediate hydrogen
storage in geologic formations, offering conceptual parallels for reservoir and seal performance in
natural geologic H₂ systems.

Only eight publications were found to focus primarily on anthropogenic geologic hydrogen. Of
these, two were general overviews of the topic, and six focused on the generation or recovery of
hydrogen from in-situ hydrocarbons. Notably, none of the reviewed publications focused in detail
on anthropogenic hydrogen generation from iron-rich source rocks, despite these lithologies being
well-documented sources of natural hydrogen accumulations and surface emanations.

This relative lack of recent literature focused on anthropogenic hydrogen production from iron-
rich rocks highlights a clear research gap. It suggests that this topic may be well-positioned for
impactful and original contributions through focused R&D—particularly from institutions like
NETL with subsurface engineering expertise and experience in geologic energy systems.

Table 7 outlines the key knowledge gaps and associated outstanding research questions related to
geologic hydrogen, organized to align with NETL’s technical capabilities and research priorities.
The knowledge gaps are divided into two major thematic areas: exploration and production.

Within the exploration domain, several subtopics are identified. The first is detecting migration
pathways, which focuses on identifying the terminal expressions of subsurface migration systems
that could signal the presence of a geologic hydrogen system. This includes research questions
related to surface geomorphology (e.g., sub-circular depressions) and appropriate instrumentation
or sensing technologies to verify gas fluxes. The second subtopic is detecting source rocks, which
encompasses methodologies for locating and characterizing hydrogen-generating lithologies
through geochemical, geophysical, and petrophysical approaches. A third area of interest
is detecting reservoirs, which involves identifying porous and permeable formations capable of
hosting accumulations of natural hydrogen. The fourth subtopic, detecting hydrogen, includes both
the direct measurement of molecular hydrogen in the subsurface and the indirect assessment of its
potential generation. This includes the development and application of geological, geochemical,

geophysical, and petrophysical methods. Containment is another critical exploration subtopic,
focused on identifying and evaluating seal integrity to ensure that hydrogen accumulations can be
trapped over relevant timeframes. Finally, the subtopic of prospectivity addresses how to evaluate
the potential technical and economic viability of proposed natural or anthropogenic geologic
hydrogen systems at the project, field, or play level.

In the production domain, several additional subtopics are presented. Anthropogenic
generation focuses on inducing hydrogen production in laboratory and field settings, particularly
from iron-rich source rocks or hydrocarbon-rich reservoirs. Anthropogenic extraction addresses
the engineering methods and material requirements necessary for hydrogen production, including
well design and separation technologies. Another subtopic is anthropogenic ancillary benefits,
which refers to additional advantages associated with hydrogen generation processes—such as
carbon sequestration, geothermal energy recovery, or critical mineral mobilization—derived from
the injection of water, CO₂, or other fluids. The management and optimization of both natural and
anthropogenic hydrogen projects is also an important area of research, encompassing reservoir
engineering, performance monitoring, and predictive modeling. Lastly, the subtopic

conditions under which in-situ hydrogen generation from subsurface hydrocarbons may provide
greater economic value than conventional hydrocarbon extraction, particularly when factoring in
ecological and infrastructure considerations.

7. AI LLM APPROACH TO IMPROVING LITERATURE REVIEW METHODOLOGY

In recent years, document analysis and review has been accelerated by advancement in Artificial
Intelligence (AI) and Large Language Models (LLM). The potential for increased efficiency and
accurate research by utilizing these tools can change how to approach a literature
review. Document summarization uses LLMs to condense large documents into key points, saving
time and improving comprehension. Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) enhances LLM
capabilities by grounding them in external knowledge sources, improving accuracy and relevance
of generated content. LLMs also enhance search capabilities by understanding context and
semantics, retrieving more relevant results than traditional keyword searches.

Keeping these tools in mind, it was necessary to develop a new workflow and python tools to
explore how AI can enhance literature reviews. The steps included within this process include
collecting and filtering the research papers, processing them into text, embedding the text, and then
adding the data to a vector database. Once this is complete, the data collected in the vector database
is accessible any open-source data model for querying and processing. This process is called
Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and is a way to enhance an LLM to answers questions
while minimizing hallucinations and return references to the answers given.

Document Corpus Collection

The development of the document corpus involved a multi-faceted approach to gather relevant
publications. A few documents were initially identified and sourced from the comprehensive
review paper by Zgonnik (2020), which provides a broad overview of the field. To add to this
collection, additional references cited within the Zgonnik paper were retrieved, and supplementary
web searches were conducted to capture recent advancements and diverse perspectives. A team of
subject matter experts then filtered the collected documents based on their relationship and quality
to this study. This filtering ensured that the final corpus contained only the most relevant and high-
quality information. Ultimately, a total of 76 documents were included in the final corpus, which
then underwent further processing in order to include the documents in the Retrieval Augmented
Generation (RAG) Pipeline.

Technical Detail of the Pipeline
The technical pipeline for this project was designed to ingest, process, and prepare geologic
hydrogen research papers from various sources for analysis using a local Large Language Model
(LLM). The source documents were primarily in PDF format, representing the most common
format for published papers. A smaller subset consisted of scanned documents, typically older
papers that had been printed and subsequently digitized. Word files were also included,
representing a common format for developing documents. These diverse formats necessitated a
flexible and robust processing strategy to ensure accurate text extraction.

To accommodate the variety of document types, different methods of text extraction were
employed. PDF files were processed using the open-source PyPDF2 library
(https://pypi.org/project/PyPDF2/), a Python package specifically designed for extracting text and
metadata from PDF documents. Word documents were converted to plain text using the docx2txt

(https://pypi.org/project/docx2txt/) Python library, creating simplified text for subsequent
analysis. Scanned documents underwent optical character recognition (OCR) using Tesseract
(https://github.com/tesseract-ocr/tesseract), a powerful open-source OCR engine. The pytesseract
(https://pypi.org/project/pytesseract/) Python API was used to interface with Tesseract, enabling
the conversion of scanned images into machine-readable text.

Following text extraction, the resulting data underwent embedding and storage in a vector database
for efficient retrieval by the LLM. LangChain (https://pypi.org/project/langchain/) and LangChain

building applications powered by language models, were used to organize the embedding process.
The text was embedded using a local instance of Ollama (https://ollama.com/) hosting the nomic-
embed-text embedding model (https://ollama.com/library/nomic-embed-text).

The resulting vector representations were then stored in a ChromaDB
(https://pypi.org/project/chromadb/) vector database, enabling fast and accurate similarity searches

Additional data can be added to the database by following the same process and adding the

A system prompt is a set of instructions or guidelines provided to the model at the beginning of a

responses. The System Prompts for this effort were designed to improve the accuracy of the LLM's

8. DISCUSSION

The potential of geologic hydrogen as both a naturally occurring and an anthropogenically induced
energy resource is steadily drawing increased attention, largely due to its low carbon footprint and
alignment with broader energy transition goals. This report has highlighted the fundamentals of
geologic hydrogen systems, the analogy with petroleum systems, and the color-based classification
scheme that situates geologic hydrogen among more conventional hydrogen types. Yet the path
from early field observations—such as those in Mali, Kansas, and various ultramafic terrains—to
large-scale commercial development remains relatively uncharted. Several key discussion points,
underscored by the need for rigorous future research, emerge.

8.1 BRIDGING COLOR CLASSIFICATION TO SUBSURFACE REALITIES

While “white/gold” hydrogen (natural hydrogen) and “orange” hydrogen (anthropogenically
induced geologic hydrogen) offer compelling pathways, the diversity of color nomenclature often
obscures deeper geologic principles. Equally important, the transition from hydrogen color labels
to site-specific geologic processes (e.g., serpentinization or in-situ hydrocarbon conversion)
highlights both opportunities and complexities. For instance, “orange” hydrogen in iron-rich rocks
could theoretically evolve into a net-negative carbon solution if coupled with carbon dioxide
injection and mineralization. Future research should clarify these interplays between geology,
geochemistry, and carbon management, rather than rely primarily on the simplistic color-based
branding.

8.2 PETROLEUM SYSTEM ANALOGY AND ITS LIMITS

Conceptualizing hydrogen generation, migration, trapping, and containment under a “petroleum
system” framework helps to organize exploration targets—defining the source rock, pathways,
reservoir, and seal. However, hydrogen’s high diffusivity, reactivity, and proneness to microbial
consumption pose novel challenges for this analogy. Key differences include shorter residence
times, the potential for self-regenerating accumulations if generation outpaces loss, and unique
concerns over seal integrity. As such, integrated modeling must go beyond standard petroleum
approaches to account for faster fluxes, biotic factors, and new thermal-chemical pathways.
Research on how to adapt well-logging techniques for hydrogen detection, as well as the viability
of established seal rocks (e.g., salt, unfractured dolerite, or thick claystone), is urgently needed.

8.3 U.S. PROSPECTS: FROM NATURAL SYSTEMS TO ENGINEERED
PATHWAYS

This report identifies three particularly promising geologic settings in the United States for
advancing both natural and engineered hydrogen production:

Midcontinent Rift System: The occurrence of hydrogen in historical wells suggests iron-rich
basement and deep-seated faults may actively release hydrogen through serpentinization or mantle
degassing.

 90

 Geological Hydrogen: From Natural Occurrences to Anthropogenic Generation – A Critical Review of Potential,

Banded Iron Formations (BIFs) of the North-Central U.S.: Laboratory evidence indicates that
low-temperature oxidation of ferrous iron in magnetite can produce hydrogen; major BIF belts in
Minnesota, Michigan, and Wisconsin therefore warrant closer examination.

Ultramafic Complexes Along the East and West Coasts: Ophiolitic formations, in places
associated with unusual geomorphic features (“fairy circles”), may already be releasing hydrogen.
Deliberate stimulation (e.g., water/CO₂ injection) could enhance serpentinization, mirroring small-
scale field tests conducted overseas.

Going forward, site-specific pilot projects in these regions could test different stimulation
strategies, leveraging the petroleum sector’s experience with well completions and reservoir
stimulation.

8.4 PROSPECTIVITY ANALYSIS AND MULTI-PHYSICS EXPERIMENTAL
TECHNIQUES

Effective exploration for geologic hydrogen will depend on refined prospectivity analyses that
blend geology, geochemistry, geophysics, and advanced laboratory testing. Multi-physics
approaches—such as combining seismic surveys with electromagnetic, gravimetric, and
magnetotelluric data—can help delineate iron-rich source rocks and identify fault networks
facilitating hydrogen migration. Laboratory experiments are equally important: pressure-core
testing, geochemical kinetics under controlled temperature and pressure, and microbial interaction
studies are all critical to understanding H₂ generation and loss. These techniques can be further
integrated into numerical models to project system behavior under different injection or extraction
scenarios.

8.5  ANTHROPOGENIC HYDROGEN GENERATION: EMERGING PATHWAYS
AND CHALLENGES

Although the literature on “orange” hydrogen is sparse, it is precisely in this domain that
multidisciplinary R&D could deliver the greatest near-term advances.

Serpentinization Enhancement: Direct injection of water, catalysts, or CO₂ in ultramafic rocks
(e.g., ophiolites, peridotites, BIFs) to accelerate hydrogen generation. Key uncertainties concern
reaction kinetics, reservoir clogging from mineral precipitation, and overall energy balance (i.e.,
comparing injection costs versus hydrogen yield).

In-Situ Hydrocarbon Conversion: Adaptations of known enhanced oil recovery methods—such as
in-situ combustion or steam-assisted gravity drainage—to deliberately crack hydrocarbons into
hydrogen. Some pilot projects show promise but highlight operational complexities (heat
management, reservoir damage, carbon disposal) that must be addressed.
Each anthropogenic pathway raises new techno-economic questions, including whether the
incremental energy inputs, capital costs, and risk profiles can compete with other hydrogen
sources. Evaluating potential synergies—for example, carbon sequestration in serpentinites or co-
production of critical minerals—remains an important research direction.

8.6 DATA MANAGEMENT AND THE ROLE OF LARGE LANGUAGE MODELS

Given the nascent state of geologic hydrogen research, the field can quickly benefit from robust
data-sharing platforms and advanced AI tools. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) using large
language models (LLMs) has shown promise in accelerating literature reviews, distilling vast
technical repositories, and highlighting cross-disciplinary insights. Future efforts might see such
AI tools integrated directly into prospectivity workflows—analyzing well logs, published case
studies, sensor data, and lab results in near real time. Investments in open-source data
infrastructures (e.g., an “EDX4GH2”) would further enable collaborative, multi-agency research,
lowering barriers to entry for new investigators.

8.7 RESEARCH DIRECTIONS AND CROSS-CUTTING OPPORTUNITIES

Pilot Projects and Field Demonstrations: Small-scale field tests in each major geologic setting
(rift systems, BIFs, ultramafic belts) would help validate lab findings, confirm resource potential,
and illuminate best practices for stimulation, monitoring, and containment.

Wellbore Materials and Integrity: Hydrogen embrittlement remains a persistent concern,
particularly if downhole temperatures and pressures vary widely. Material science studies are
needed to ensure safe injection, production, and storage operations.

Hydrogen detection and monitoring: Long-term detection and monitoring of hydrogen
concentration in potential interested regions is important in terms of hydrogen product site
evaluation and geological events prediction such as earthquakes; yet the current techniques
available in the market are not sufficient to detect hydrogen accurately. More efforts are suggested
to spend in the development of effective detecting equipment for hydrogen detection and
monitoring.

Hybrid Energy Systems and Carbon Integration: Coupling hydrogen production with CO₂
sequestration, geothermal extraction, or critical mineral recovery could drastically improve
economics. These synergies warrant multi-disciplinary pilot designs that address both subsurface
and infrastructure constraints.

8.8 TOWARD COMMERCIAL AND POLICY RELEVANCE

As green and blue hydrogen still grapple with cost barriers, geologic hydrogen—whether sourced
naturally or produced via engineered processes—could serve as an important bridge toward
broader hydrogen adoption. Policymakers and industry stakeholders, however, will need clearer
data on resource size, production stability, reservoir life, and other pertinent safeguards.
Coordinated, well-funded research portfolios—spearheaded by institutions like the National
Energy Technology Laboratory (NETL)—are essential to de-risk early field pilots and accelerate
commercial readiness.

fundamental geoscience, cutting-edge reservoir engineering, advanced materials research, and AI-
driven data integration. By learning from both promising analogs (e.g., Mali’s Bourakébougou)

9. CONCLUSION

Interest in geologic hydrogen has expanded considerably in recent years due to its potential role
as a reliable, long term energy source. Although many efforts still focus on conventional “black”
and “grey” hydrogen—which rely on fossil fuel reforming and emit substantial carbon
emissions—there is growing recognition of alternative hydrogen “colors” with varying ecological
footprints. “Blue” hydrogen leverages carbon capture to reduce emissions, “green” hydrogen uses
regenerably powered electrolysis, and “white/gold” hydrogen emerges naturally in subsurface
systems. Additionally, “orange” hydrogen—anthropogenically induced geologic hydrogen—has
gained attention for its potential to tap iron-rich or hydrocarbon-bearing formations, further
diversifying the hydrogen supply landscape.

Drawing on analogies with petroleum systems (which comprise source, migration pathways,
reservoir, and seal), geologic hydrogen can similarly be conceptualized through “hydrogen
system” elements and processes. Like oil and gas, hydrogen requires a mechanism for generation,
a route for migration, a reservoir for accumulation, and a seal for containment—but differs
profoundly in its higher reactivity and diffusive loss. Natural hydrogen accumulations are best
exemplified by the Bourakébougou field in Mali, yet engineered pathways—injecting fluids,
catalysts, or heat into iron-rich rocks or hydrocarbon reservoirs—could substantially increase
hydrogen yields and offer a new paradigm analogous to enhanced oil recovery.

Several U.S. regions show strong indications of geologic hydrogen prospectivity, both for natural
accumulations and engineered systems:

 1. Midcontinent Rift System – Deep-seated faults and iron-rich mafic/ultramafic basement
 rocks may generate hydrogen via serpentinization or mantle degassing.
 2. Banded Iron Formations in the north-central U.S. – Magnetite-rich lithologies have
 documented potential for hydrogen generation through low-temperature redox reactions.
 3. Ultramafic complexes along the East and West Coasts – Ophiolites and other iron-rich
 terrains, associated in some places with “fairy circles,” suggest active or relict
 serpentinization processes.

Advancing the state of knowledge and ensuring safe, economic production of geologic hydrogen
will require robust prospectivity analyses that integrate a range of multi-physics experimental
techniques (e.g., geophysical surveys, well logging, isotopic studies, and laboratory-based
petrophysical tests). These methods will help confirm hydrogen origin and quantify key
parameters—such as generation rates, diffusion pathways, and seal integrity—across varied
geologic settings.

Finally, the large language model (LLM) approach presented in this report demonstrates how
advanced natural language processing, combined with retrieval-augmented generation, can aid in
synthesizing large volumes of technical literature. By offering rapid, context-aware searches and
concise document summarization, this LLM workflow can accelerate the identification of research
gaps and consolidate interdisciplinary findings. When paired with traditional field, laboratory, and
analytical data, it can help build a more cohesive, data-driven roadmap for realizing geologic
hydrogen’s promise in a low-carbon energy future.
