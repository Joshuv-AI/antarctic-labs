Ratio-based Loss Functions
Lena Helgerth, Andreas Christmann

Abstract




Abstract

Algorithms in machine learning and AI do critically depend on at least three key components: (i) the risk function, which is the expectation of the loss function, (ii) the function space, which is often called the hypothesis space, and (iii) the set of probability measures, which are allowed for the specified algorithm.
This paper gives a survey of a certain class of loss functions, which we call ratio-based.
In supervised learning, margin-based loss functions for classification tasks depending on the
product
of the output values
y_{i}
and the predictions
f(x_{i})
as well as distance-based loss functions depending on the
difference
of
y_{i}
and
f(x_{i})
for regression are common.
Distance-based loss functions are in particular useful, if an additive model assumption seems plausible, i.e. the common signal plus noise assumption.
However, in the literature, several loss functions proposed for regression purposes have a multiplicative error structure in mind and pay attention to relative errors, i.e. to the
ratio
of
y_{i}
and
f(x_{i})
.
In this survey article, we systematically investigate such ratio-based loss functions and propose a few new losses, which may be interesting for future research.
We concentrate on investigating general properties of ratio-based loss functions like continuity, Lipschitz-continuity, convexity, and differentiability, because these properties play a central role in most machine learning algorithms.
Therefore, we do not focus on some specific machine learning algorithm to derive universal consistency, learning rates, or stability results.
Instead, we want to enable future research in this direction.

1
Introduction

Machine learning (ML) and AI are well established in modern life.
Besides choosing an appropriate function space or hypothesis space
\mathcal{F}
and a set of probability measures
\mathcal{P}
, the choice of the loss function
L
is a crucial component of ML. Of course, many algorithms also need a good determination of hyperparameters such as a regularization parameter or the depth of a decision tree or a random forest, or the depth and the structure of
a deep neural network.
Two main goals of ML methods are (i) minimizing the risk or the regularized risk over
\mathcal{F}
and (ii) finding the optimal function
f^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
.
Here, we focus on the loss function for supervised learning.
Denote the given data set by
D:=((x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n}))\in(X\times Y)^{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
is the sample size,
x_{i}
denotes an input value in the set
X
, and
y_{i}
denotes the corresponding output value in the set
Y\subset\mathbb{R}
.
As predictive accuracy plays a key role when comparing ML methods, loss functions, i.e. measurable mappings
L:X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
, quantify how good a prediction
t\in\mathbb{R}
captures an input’s
x\in X
output value
y\in Y
.
The higher the loss, the poorer the prediction.
In many machine learning methods minimizing the expected loss, i.e. the risk, is a central component.
The input space
X
is typically required to be a complete separable metric space or even a Polish space, whereas the output space
Y
is often assumed to be a subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
Because loss functions are a critical component of ML methods, there is a variety of survey articles (see e.g.
[
28
,
27
,
CiampiconiEtAl2024
]
).

It is a useful and well-known fact that general properties of the loss function like continuity, Lipschitz continuity, convexity, or differentiability are inherited by the risk, i.e. the expectation of the loss function, under suitable conditions, see e.g.
[
25
, Chapter 2.2]
.

In binary classification, we have
Y=\{-1,1\}
and common loss functions for such methods are
margin-based
, i.e. there exists a representing function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
that fulfills

L(x,y,t)=\varphi(y\cdot t),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}.

Popular examples for margin-based loss functions are hinge loss
L_{\mathrm{hinge}}(x,y,t)=\max\{0,1-y\cdot t\}
, logistic loss
L_{\mathrm{c-logist}}(x,y,t)=\ln(1+\exp{(y\cdot t)})
, and least squares loss
L_{\mathrm{LS}}(x,y,t)=(1-y\cdot t)^{2}
.

In regression, it is common to assume
Y=\mathbb{R}
and use a
distance-based
loss function, i.e.
L
can be written as

L(x,y,t)=\psi(y-t),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R},

where the representing function satisfies
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
with
\psi(0)=0
.
Distance-based loss functions are of particular usefulness if an
additive
error structure is assumed, i.e. if the classical signal
plus
random noise assumption seems plausible. Obviously, distance-based loss functions satisfy
L(x,y,t)=L(x,y+c,t+c)
for all
(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, such loss functions and their risk functions are invariant w.r.t. additive shifts by some constant
c
.
Examples for distance-based loss functions are the least squares loss
L_{\mathrm{LS}}(x,y,t)=(y-t)^{2}
and the absolute error loss
L_{\mathrm{abs}}(x,y,t)=|y-t|
both for regression and the pinball loss
L(x,y,t)=\max\{\tau(y-t),-(1-\tau)(y-t)\}
for
\tau
-quantile regression, where
\tau\in(0,1)
is fixed.

However, in many real-world regression tasks, the output space
Y
is often a subset of
\mathbb{R}
, e.g.
Y=(0,\infty)
.
Furthermore, a
multiplicative
error model, i.e. signal
times
random noise, is often at least as plausible as a signal
plus
random noise assumption.
This gives the motivation to systematically investigate loss functions which depend on the
ratio
of predicted and observed values instead of their difference.
Especially for multiplicative models, relative errors are widely used (see e.g.
[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
,
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
,
31
]
).
A perfect ratio-based approach gives a scale-invariant version of predictive accuracy.
Note that this property cannot be ensured through an appropriate distance-based approach as long as a constant loss function
L\equiv 0
(which is totally uninteresting in ML) is neglected.
For this, assume
L:X\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a distance-based loss function satisfying
L(x,y,t)=L(x,cy,ct)
for all
(x,y,t)\in X\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
L
is scale-invariant.
Let
\psi
denote its representing function.
Then,

\psi(y-t)=\psi(c(y-t)),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R},c\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore,
\psi\equiv 0
is a constant function.

In a scale-invariant approach, evaluation becomes independent of absolute output values and its units.

[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
, (2)]
and
[
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
, (1)]
consider
“accelerated failure time models”
, which reduce to additive regression models after a logarithmic transformation.
Therefore, these models only handle positive data (cf.
[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
, p. 2]
).
Later, the question arises whether transformation to the multiplicative case is necessary.
We will see that back-transformation to the additive scenario is possible but does not give any additional information.
Since results also need to be transformed back, one either gets information about the difference or about their ratio, but not both.

Let us now briefly motivate why ratio-based loss functions can be of interest in applications. Medical fields as well as financial modeling or quality control are just a few examples in which a ratio-based error can be relevant and useful. This is one reason why logistic regression, odds ratio estimation in dose-response studies, and Poisson regression to model count data are often used in practice.
E.g., when predicting a person’s weight, absolute deviations often provide limited interpretation compared to relative ones.
If the aim of prediction is to calculate a dose of medicine, a deviation of 2 kg from the true value may be irrelevant for a 80 kg grown-up man’s dose. But in case of an 8 kg child, whose weight was measured as 10 kg, the medication dose was 25 % too high.
Here, the ratio is probably of greater interest than the difference.

Speaking of inflation, for a daily newspaper, which cost €3 before, an increase of €5 would be unacceptable for most customers and have a major impact on their buying decision.
For a car instead, lifting the price by €5 would not make a noticeable impact on the customer’s buying decision.

Therefore, ratio-based methods are an important addition to traditional distance-based techniques to measure predictive accuracy.

Speaking of ratios, one usually intuitively assumes positive values because of interpretability issues, although mathematically observed values and predicted values may both be negative in some applications.
Trivially, if both the observed values and the predicted values are negative, we can just multiply by
-1
.
Moreover, we have already seen before that in many real-world applications only positive outputs are possible, thinking of body height, body weight, measuring of time, prices, damage costs, fuel consumption of cars, etc.
This is why we do not use
Y=\mathbb{R}
as an output space in this paper, but a real subset of it.
To guarantee the former and to avoid dividing by zero, we take
Y
as a part of the positive real line.
Examples for positive outputs are also used to explain the significance of log-normal distribution or rather its advantages over the normal distribution in scenarios where only positive outputs can be expected (cf.
[
19
,
20
]
).

The paper is organized as follows.
Section
2
introduces the class of ratio-based loss functions.
Section
3
presents the main results explaining and systematically analyzing how certain properties of the loss function can be achieved.
Section
4
transfers these properties to the risk.
In Section
5
, we give some examples for ratio-based losses from the literature as well as some new ones and investigate their properties.
At the end, in Sections
6
and
7
we shortly discuss ratio-based loss functions’ connection to distance-bases loss functions and a further approach to ratio-based.
Some basic proofs are given in the appendix.

2
Definition

Let us first define ratio-based loss functions.
If not otherwise specified, in the remainder of the paper
(X,\mathcal{A})
denotes a measurable space and
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, while
\mathbb{R}
and all of its subsets are equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebras denoted by
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
and

\mathcal{B}(Y)
, respectively.

Definition 2.1
.

A supervised loss function
L:X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is called
ratio-based (rb)
if there exists a representing measurable function
\ell:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
as well as a constant
c\geq 0
, and a link function
u:\mathbb{R}\to Y
that is measurable, surjective, and monotonic, such that
\ell(1)=0
and

L(x,y,t)=\ell\biggl(\frac{u(t)+c}{y+c}\biggr),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R},

(1)

hold, where
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b\leq\infty
.
Moreover, we call
L

strictly ratio-based
if
c=0
.

The constant
c
helps to guarantee that the ratio is in
\bigl(\frac{a+c}{b+c},\frac{b+c}{a+c}\bigr)
.
Since many statistical learning algorithms use Hilbert spaces or Banach spaces of functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
to predict
\hat{y}
for
y
, one can in general not prevent the third variable
t=f(x)
from being negative.
Therefore, it is necessary to transform the predicted values to
Y
to be able to compare them accurately.
The link function transforms real values appropriately to
Y
.
Some examples for such link functions are given in Table
1
.

:

u
depending on output space

The idea to define a loss function via this relation has also been used before.
For example,
[
JadonEtAl2022
, Chapter III.D]
define
Relative Absolute Error (RAE)
through

RAE=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl|y_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\Bigr|},

where
\hat{y}_{i}=u(f(x_{i}))
.
In this context, the question arises whether RAE is well-defined.
In case
y_{i}=K
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, RAE can be undefined (
[
JadonEtAl2022
, Tab. IV]
).

[
27
]
also considered loss functions which use certain ratios, e.g.
Mean Absolute Relative Error (AbsRel)

AbsRel=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|\hat{y}_{i}-y_{i}|}{y_{i}}

(
[
27
, Chapter 8.2.1]
) and
Logarithmic RMSE (LRMSE)

LRMSE=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(\log(\hat{y}_{i})-\log(y_{i})\bigr)^{2}}

(
[
27
, Chapter 8.2.3]
) as well as
Mean Log10 error

MeanLog10=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl|\log_{10}(\hat{y}_{i})-\log_{10}(y_{i})\bigr|

(
[
27
, Chapter 8.2.5]
).
The last ones can be expressed through a ratio because of the logarithm’s rules.
A similar version of LRMSE is also discussed in
[
LiEtAl2025
, Chapter 3.1.8]
.
Aside from that,
[
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
]
discuss ratio-based approaches for their
least absolute relative error (LARE)
(
[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
,
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
]
),
least product relative error (LPRE)
(
[
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
, (3), (4)]
), and
general relative error (GRE)
(
[
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
, (8)]
).

Speaking of distance-based loss functions, symmetry is sometimes of concern.
Considering a distance-based loss with representing function
\psi
, symmetry is expressed via
\psi(r)=\psi(-r)
, see e.g.
[
Steinwart2007
, Def. 4.11 (ii)]
. Such functions penalize overestimation (
\hat{y}=y+c
) and underestimation (
\hat{y}=y-c
) of
y
, where
\hat{y}=f(x)
, by some
c\geq 0
equally, because
L(x,y,t-c)=L(x,y,t+c)
for all
(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}
and
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, in a ratio-based setting, it makes sometimes sense to use a loss function such that overestimation
\hat{y}=\lambda y
and underestimation
\hat{y}=\frac{y}{\lambda}
, where
\hat{y}=u(f(x))
and
\lambda\geq 1
, yields the same loss.
This can be obtained by requiring

\ell(r)=\ell(r^{-1}),\qquad r\in(0,\infty).

This idea was already discussed in
[
19
]
.
We call this property
ratio-symmetry
.
Roughly speaking, a ratio-based loss function is defined to realize the idea
L(x,y,t)=L(x,\lambda y,\lambda t)
for all
(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
(modulo modification of the prediction through the link function), whereas ratio-symmetry ensures
L(x,y,\lambda t)=L(x,y,\frac{1}{\lambda}t)
for all
(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda>0
.

[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
,
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
]
already mentioned that a loss without one type of relative error could lead to biased estimation and one should, therefore, take both errors into account, one relative to the response and one relative to its prediction.
Clearly, ratio-symmetry for ratio-based loss functions can be useful in some applications and not be useful in others, as is true also for symmetric distance-based loss functions.
Thinking of loans, for the lending bank, it is worse to not get their money back, once they gave someone credit, than to not give someone credit, who would have paid all his money back (cf.
[
10
, credit-scoring]
).
Moreover,
“it is […] worse to predict that a person will not have a heart attack when he or she actually will, than vice versa”
(
[
13
, Chapter 9.2.4]
).
In these cases, one does not wish a ratio-symmetric loss.

In binary classification with output space
Y=\{-1,1\}
, a margin-based loss function

L(x,y,t)=\varphi(yt),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R},

with representing function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
can be expressed in a ratio-based way.
Since
yt=\frac{t}{y}
holds for
y\in\{-1,1\}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

L(x,y,t)=\varphi\Bigl(\frac{t}{y}\Bigr),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}.

For that reason, all margin-based loss functions can be presented through a ratio-based approach if we do not take every specification from Definition
2.1
into account.

3
Properties of ratio-based loss functions

In this section, we systematically investigate properties of ratio-based loss functions and give some basic examples. These results will be used to investigate properties of the
corresponding risk functional in Section
4
.

For distance-based loss functions, it is well known that properties of the representing function
\psi
determine properties of the loss function and the risk function to a great extent. Let us now investigate, whether this is true for ratio-based loss functions, too.

If not otherwise mentioned,
L
is a ratio-based loss function (for regression) in this section.
As is common in the literature, we say that a rb loss function
L
has property

A
if
L
has this property w.r.t. the third argument uniformly for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
(cf.
[
25
, Chapter 2.2]
).

3.1
Continuity

A distance-based loss function is continuous if and only if the representing function
\psi
is continuous.
For ratio-based loss functions it is in general necessary to also claim continuity of
u
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
L
be a ratio-based loss function. If the functions
\ell
and
u
are continuous, then
L
is a continuous loss function.

Of course, this is only sufficient, as e.g. if
u
is measurable but discontinuous and
\ell\equiv 0
, then
L
is still a continuous loss function.

3.2
Differentiability

If all of the considered derivatives exist, with the common notation
L^{\prime}(x,y,t)
for the partial derivative
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}L(x,y,t)
, a straightforward calculation yields that

L^{\prime}(x,y,t)=\ell^{\prime}\biggl(\frac{u(t)+c}{y+c}\biggr)\frac{u^{\prime}(t)}{y+c},\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
L
be a ratio-based loss function.
Given two differentiable functions
\ell
and
u
,
L
is a differentiable loss function.

3.3
Convexity

Convexity is a strong, but mathematically very nice condition for loss functions, as it implies convexity of the risk functional independent of the probability measure, see e.g.
[
25
, Lem. 2.13]
.
The convexity of the risk functional is often useful to show uniqueness of the learning algorithm, as is well-known e.g. for many regularized kernel based methods.

A twice differentiable ratio-based loss function with twice differentiable
\ell
and
u
has second derivative (w.r.t.
t
)

L^{\prime\prime}(x,y,t)=\ell^{\prime\prime}\biggl(\frac{u(t)+c}{y+c}\biggr)\cdot\biggl(\frac{u^{\prime}(t)}{y+c}\biggr)^{2}+\ell^{\prime}\biggl(\frac{u(t)+c}{y+c}\biggr)\cdot\frac{u^{\prime\prime}(t)}{y+c}\penalty\ .

Hence, the assumption that
\ell
and
u
are convex functions is – even under this smoothness assumption on
\ell
and
u
– in general
not
sufficient to guarantee a convex rb loss, as
\ell(r)
should decrease for
r<1
.
From an applied point of view, it does not make sense to assume an increasing function
\ell
, since
\ell(1)=0
and underestimation, i.e.
\ell(r)
with
r\ll 1
, should in general yield positive losses.
Furthermore, the assumption of
u
being convex is not automatically fulfilled, e.g. for logistic distribution function.
In fact, for
Y=(0,1)
with corresponding link function
u
no matter of
c
, a non-constant convex ratio-based loss
L
is not even possible.
The main reason for that is the following.
Since
u
is monotone and surjective, either
\lim_{t\to\infty}u(t)=1
or
\lim_{t\to-\infty}u(t)=1
holds.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the first equation holds.
Then, for continuous
\ell
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}L(x,y,t)=\ell\Bigl(\frac{1+c}{y+c}\Bigr)<\infty
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
,
c\geq 0
.
Under these assumptions, if
c>0
,
\lim_{t\to-\infty}L(x,y,t)=\ell\Bigl(\frac{c}{y+c}\Bigr)<\infty
, too.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
L
be a non-constant rb loss function.
We put that into concrete terms assuming additionally the existence of
\tilde{t}<t<t^{\prime}
with
L(x,y,t)<L(x,y,t^{\prime})
and
L(x,y,t)<L(x,y,\tilde{t})
.
Let
Y=(0,1)
and consider logistic link function
u
.
Then,
L
is not a convex loss function.

The same holds true for intervals
Y=(a,\infty),a>0
, or
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b
, and continuous functions
\ell
as either
\lim_{t\to-\infty}L(x,y,t)
or
\lim_{t\to\infty}L(x,y,t)
is bounded which gives a contradiction to the loss function’s convexity no matter whether
c
equals zero or not.
Note that convex functions on
\mathbb{R}
are continuous automatically (
[
23
, Cor. 10.1.1]
).
Moreover, when
Y=(0,\infty)
with
c>0
, again, a convex loss function is not possible since
\lim_{t\to-\infty}L(x,y,t)
is bounded for
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.

When using parameter
c=0
and link function
u(t)=\e^{t}
from
\mathbb{R}
to
Y=(0,\infty)
, a convex loss is, at least, possible.
Therefore, we will focus on such strict rb loss functions for the moment.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
L
be a ratio-based loss function.
Let
Y=(0,\infty),u(t)=\e^{t}
, and
c=0
.
Define

\ell(r):=\widetilde{\ell}(r)+\widetilde{\ell}(r^{-1})-2\widetilde{\ell}(1)

with
\widetilde{\ell}:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
being a twice differentiable function satisfying

\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}(r)+r\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(r)\geq 0

(2)

for all
r\in(0,\infty)
.
Then,

L(x,y,t)=\ell\Bigl(\frac{\e^{t}}{y}\Bigr)=\widetilde{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{\e^{t}}{y}\Bigr)+\widetilde{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{y}{\e^{t}}\Bigr)-2\widetilde{\ell}(1)

(3)

is a convex ratio-based loss function.

Note that
u^{\prime}=u
yields easy calculations here and that the statement can be improved to strict convexity by claiming strict inequality in (
2
).

Example 3.5
.

In the context from Proposition
3.4
,
\widetilde{\ell}(r):=r^{\alpha},\alpha\geq 0
satisfies

\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}(r)+r\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(r)=\alpha r^{\alpha-1}+r\alpha(\alpha-1)r^{\alpha-2}=\alpha^{2}r^{\alpha-1}\geq 0

for all
r\in(0,\infty)
.
Thus, using Proposition
3.4
, we get the convex ratio-based loss function

L_{\alpha}(x,y,t):=\Bigl(\frac{\e^{t}}{y}\Bigr)^{\alpha}+\Bigl(\frac{y}{\e^{t}}\Bigr)^{\alpha}-2.

If
\alpha=0
, the loss function is constantly zero.
This is, of course, the trivial case and not interesting for applications.
The special case
\alpha=1
will be discussed in Chapter
5
.
Interestingly,
\ell
itself does not need to be convex.
Especially, if
\alpha<1
,

\ell^{\prime\prime}(r)=\alpha r^{-2}\bigl((\alpha-1)r^{\alpha}+(\alpha+1)r^{-\alpha}\bigr),\;r\in(0,\infty).

Choosing
r=\biggl(2\sqrt{\frac{1+\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\biggr)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}>0
gives
\ell^{\prime\prime}(r)<0
.
This means,
\ell
is not convex for
\alpha<1
.
Instead,
\ell
is convex if
\alpha\geq 1
.
Nevertheless, the corresponding loss function
L
is always convex.

We are now interested in a certain choice of
\widetilde{\ell}
to ensure the claims of Proposition
3.4
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
L
be a ratio-based loss function.
Let
Y=(0,\infty),u(t)=\e^{t}
, and
g\in C^{1}((0,\infty))
with
g
being an increasing function.
Then,

\widetilde{\ell}(r):=C+\int_{r_{0}}^{r}\frac{g(t)}{t}\;\mathrm{d}t,\qquad r\in(0,\infty)

with
r_{0}\geq 0
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\widetilde{\ell}
is well-defined, non-negative, and finite, fulfills (
2
) for all
r\in(0,\infty)
.
Thus,

L(x,y,t)=\widetilde{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{\e^{t}}{y}\Bigr)+\widetilde{\ell}\Bigl(\frac{y}{\e^{t}}\Bigr)-2\widetilde{\ell}(1)

is a convex loss function.

Example 3.7
.

In the notation from Proposition
3.6
,
\widetilde{\ell}(r):=\log(1+r)
gives a convex rb loss function.
Integrating
g(t)=\frac{t}{t+1}
on the open interval
(0,r)
yields

\int_{0}^{r}\frac{g(t)}{t}\;\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{r}\frac{1}{t+1}\;\mathrm{d}t=\log(1+r)=\widetilde{\ell}(r).

Here,
g
is a differentiable and increasing function on
(0,\infty)
.
Because of Proposition

3.4
Lipschitz Continuity

For the sake of convexity, one could think about replacing
Y=(0,1)
by its superset
Y=(0,\infty)
and using
c=0
.
However, choosing such output space can be disadvantageous in terms of Lipschitz continuity.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
L
be a ratio-based loss function.
Let
\ell
and
u
be Lipschitz-continuous and
c\geq 0
.
Consider the output space
Y=(a,b)
,
0\leq a<b\leq\infty
with

.
Then, rb loss (
1

|L|_{1}\leq\frac{|\ell|_{1}|u|_{1}}{a+c}
.

Here, both of the critical values

and

are possible.
In case

, we only require

.
Another possibility to get a Lipschitz continuous loss is to bound its derivative.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
L
be a differentiable (ratio-based) loss function and

. Suppose,

\sup_{(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R}}|L^{\prime}(x,y,t)|\leq M<\infty.

Then,
L
is a Lipschitz continuous loss function.

Proof.

Any differentiable function with bounded first derivative is Lipschitz continuous (cf. e.g.
[
18
, p. 147]
).
∎

Indeed, there is a equivalence (
[
4
, Chapter 1, Thm. 7.3]
).
Note that we do not need a

loss function here.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
L
be a differentiable (ratio-based) loss function such that
|L^{\prime}|
is not bounded. Then,
L
is not a Lipschitz continuous loss function.

(Continuation of Example

3.5
Finite Risk

Let
P
be a probability measure on
X\times Y
.
The
L
-risk of a measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by

\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f):=\int_{X\times Y}L(x,y,f(x))\;\mathrm{d}P(x,y).

Note, that this integral is always well-defined, because
L
is non-negative, but it can happen that
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)=\infty
for some
P
and some
f
.

As
P
is unknown in machine learning, many ML algorithms approximate
the true risk
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)
by the empirical risk

\mathcal{R}_{L,D}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(x_{i},y_{i},f(x_{i})),

where
D:=D_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{(x_{i},y_{i})}
denotes the empirical measure for the data set
\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{n},y_{n})\bigr)
.

To avoid the danger of overfitting, many ML algorithms minimize a regularized risk, i.e. one minimizes

\mathcal{R}^{\mathrm{reg}}_{L,P,\lambda}(f):=\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)+\lambda R(f)

over a suitable subset
\mathcal{F}
of (all) measurable functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
with some regularization parameter
\lambda>0
and regularization term
R(f)
.
One special case are support vector machines, see e.g.
[
29
,
30
,
25
]
with a general loss function
L
,
\mathcal{F}
chosen to be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space
H
, and
R(f)=\|f\|_{H}^{2}
.
There are too many generalizations to mention them here in detail.
E.g.
\mathcal{F}
chosen as a reproducing kernel Banach space and other regularization functions like
\|f\|^{p}
with
p\geq 1
or
\alpha_{1}\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{1}+\alpha_{2}\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
have been proposed, see e.g.
[
ZhangXuZhang2009
,
7
,
RosascoEtAl2013
,
6
]
and the references therein.
For mathematically even more advanced function spaces used in deep distributed convolutional neural networks, we refer to

[
32
]
,
[
21
]
, and
[
ZhangShiZhou2024
]
.

Remark 3.22
.

Let
L
be a rb loss function.
If
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b<\infty
(note that

), and

, but

,

So even the closed interval is contained in the domain of

Thus, if

is continuous,

is contained in
[0,M]
for some

(cf. e.g.
[

, p. 90]
).
Hence,

\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)\leq M<\infty

holds for any measurable function

.

Considering

, the upper fraction can become arbitrarily large and arbitrarily close to zero.

.

Let

Using a bounded function

whose (regularized) risk is finite such that the minimal (regularized) risk is finite. Often this is easily checked for the function

.
Let
L
be a rb loss function.
Then,

\frac{u(0)+c}{y+c}\in\biggl(0,\frac{u(0)+c}{a+c}\biggr)\subseteq(0,\infty),

where

is a certain value, that gives an upper bound for the quotient.

\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(0)=\int_{X\times Y}\biggl|\ell\biggl(\frac{u(0)+c}{y+c}\biggr)-\ell(1)\biggr|\;\mathrm{d}P(x,y).

.

Let

and

3.6
Nemitski Loss Functions

For distance-based loss functions and their risks, the Nemitski property plays an important role. The reason is that this property can help to achieve a finite
L
-risk, which is necessary for many ML methods.
Additionally, it helps to transfer continuity and differentiability from the loss function to
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}
.
The
Nemitski
property separates the influence of variables
(x,y)
and
t
:

L(x,y,t)\leq\tilde{b}(x,y)+\tilde{h}(|t|),

with increasing
\tilde{h}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
and measurable
\tilde{b}:X\times Y\to[0,\infty)
.
Furthermore, such loss functions are called
\boldsymbol{P}
-integrable Nemitski
if and only if
\tilde{b}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(P)
with
P
being the probability measure on
X\times Y
; for details, we refer to
[
25
, Section 2.4]
.
On the other hand, if

L(x,y,t)\leq\tilde{b}(x,y)+\lambda|t|^{p}

is valid for some
p\in(0,\infty)
and
\lambda>0
,
L
is a
Nemitski loss of order

\boldsymbol{p}
.
We apply these definitions also to ratio-based loss functions.

Remark 3.26
.

A locally Lipschitz continuous loss function
L
is a
P
-integrable Nemitski loss if and only if
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(0)<\infty
, see e.g.
[
25
, p. 31]
.

Example 3.27
.

With a monotonically increasing function
u
and
a+c\geq 0
,
\ell(r)=|r-1|
defines a
P
-integrable Nemitski loss function for all probability measures
P
on
X\times Y
.
A rough calculation yields

L(x,y,t)\leq\frac{u(|t|)+c}{a+c}+1.

4
Risk

For loss functions, it is common to consider the output space
Y
to be a closed subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
This property ensures that one can write risks

\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f):=\int_{X\times Y}L(x,y,f(x))\;\mathrm{d}P(x,y)

of measurable functions
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
as double integrals

\int_{X}\int_{Y}L(x,y,f(x))\;\mathrm{d}P(y|x)\;\mathrm{d}P_{X}(x).

Nevertheless, the same conclusion holds in our situation (recall
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b\leq\infty
), as can be seen by using results e.g. from
[
1
,
5
,
8
]
.
This requires a measurable Polish space
(X,\mathcal{A})
.
Since
\mathbb{R}
(endowed with the standard topology) is a Polish space (
[
1
,
5
]
), its open subset
Y
(endowed with its subspace topology) is Polish as well (
[
1
,
5
]
).
In particular,
X\times Y
is Polish (
[
1
,
5
]
).
Endowing
Y
with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(Y)
induced by the subspace topology,

with its product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}(Y)
is a measurable space.
Letting
P
be a probability measure on
X\times Y
,
(X\times Y,\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}(Y),P)
is probability space.
Define the projections

\pi_{X}:X\times Y\to X,\pi_{Y}:X\times Y\to Y
, which are measurable functions.
Let
\mathcal{C}:=\pi_{X}^{-1}(\mathcal{A})
denote the smallest sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{A}\otimes\mathcal{B}(Y)
such that
\pi_{X}
is measurable.
At last, we denote the marginal distribution of
P
on
X
by
P_{X}:=P\circ\pi_{X}^{-1}
.
Hence, the conditional probability distribution
P_{\pi_{Y}|\mathcal{C}}
on
\mathcal{B}(Y)\times(X\times Y)
exists (
[
8
, Thm. 10.2.2]
).
Therefore, there also exist conditional distributions

for
P
and

(
[
8
, Thm. 10.2.1 (I)]
),
which imply

\int_{X\times Y}g(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}P(x,y)=\int_{X}\int_{Y}g(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}P(y|x)\;dP_{X}(x)

for all
P
-integrable functions
g
(
[
8
, Thm. 10.2.1 (II)]
).
From this, we see that the upper equality holds not only for integrable functions

, but for all functions whose integral

is defined.
That is whenever
\int g_{+}\;\mathrm{d}P<\infty
or
\int g_{-}\;\mathrm{d}P<\infty

and

be locally Lipschitz continuous and let

Then,
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}:L_{\infty}(P_{X})\to[0,\infty)
is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

This follows directly from the combination of Lemmata

,
3.18
, and
3.19
with

5.9
General Relative Loss

Generalizing the concept of the previous loss functions, one gets the rb loss which is known as the

(

\ell(r)=g(|1-r|,|r^{-1}-1|).

The function

[

, Chapter 2.3]

that are not further specified.
For our purposes, we assume a measurable function

Some of the prior examples already discussed are sum, product, and maximum as possible mappings
g

Some obvious additional examples are
g(a,b)=a^{2}+b^{2}
,
g(a,b)=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
, and
g(a,b)=\sqrt{a+b}
,
which give the representing functions

\displaystyle\ell(r)

\displaystyle=(1-r)^{2}+(r^{-1}-1)^{2},

(26)

\displaystyle\ell(r)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{(1-r)^{2}+(r^{-1}-1)^{2}},

(27)

\displaystyle\ell(r)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{|1-r|+|r^{-1}-1|}.

These representing functions penalize underestimation and overestimation in the same manner.
An extreme version

[

Thus, we get (recall

\ell(r)=|1-r|+\exp(|r^{-1}-1|)-1.

(29)

Note that using a general relative error does not automatically imply ratio-symmetry.
Applying both error terms,
|1-r|
and
|r^{-1}-1|
, does not indicate that they are treated equally.

5.12
Smooth Robust Relative Loss Functions

Instead of clipping the loss function at some point,
[
11
]
give a general framework to (smoothly) flatten general unbounded loss functions.
Taking some of the previous unbounded functions
\ell
, one can flatten these by using

\widehat{\ell}(r):=\frac{1}{\lambda}\biggl(1-\frac{1}{1+b\ell(r)}\biggr)

for some
\lambda,b>0
.
Most properties still hold after this transformation, i.e. if
\ell
is ratio-symmetric, continuous, differentiable or Lipschitz continuous (either locally or globally),
\widehat{\ell}
is as well.
Note that convexity of
\ell
does in general not transfer to
\widehat{\ell}
.
Using an unbounded loss
\ell
,
\lim_{r\to 0,r>0}\ell(r)=\infty=\lim_{r\to\infty}\ell(r)
holds.
However,
\widehat{\ell}
is bounded as

\lim_{r\to 0,r>0}\widehat{\ell}(r)=\frac{1}{\lambda}=\lim_{r\to\infty}\widehat{\ell}(r).

As (
12
) provides some nice properties (see Tables
2
and
3
, respectively), we modify this one to keep most of them.
We get

\ell(r)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\biggl(1-\frac{1}{1+b\log(\cosh(\log(r)))}\biggr)

(34)

with parameters
\lambda,b>0
.
Here,
\lambda
can reduce the impact of noise whereas
b
pays attention to usual points and especially controls the loss function’s compactness and growth (see
[
11
]
).
Indeed,
[
11
]
not only used some parameter
b
but a nonnegative function
b(r)
.
One could think about expanding the examples to nonnegative functions
b(r)
for future research.
Since we have used differentiable log-cosh-log loss function before,
[
11
]
’s robust version was already smooth.

However, the choice of parameters and a case-by-case analysis can lead to higher computation time.

6
Connection to Distance-based Loss Functions

The question arises whether ratio-based loss functions are part of the class of distance-based loss functions or vice versa.
The constant function
L(x,y,t)=0
, for sure, is an element in both classes.
Furthermore,
L\equiv 0
is the only
constant
function, which can be both, ratio-based and distance-based, as both definitions require a certain value to be mapped to zero.
Obviously,
L\equiv 0
can be noted in a distance- and in a ratio-based way using representing functions
\psi\equiv 0
and
\ell\equiv 0
.

Next, we consider a strict ratio-based loss (i.e.
c=0
).
Because
\frac{u(t)}{y}\in(0,\infty)
,
y\in Y\subseteq(0,\infty)
, and
u:\mathbb{R}\to Y
is measurable, we obviously have

\ell\biggl(\frac{u(t)}{y}\biggr)=\ell\biggl(\exp\Bigl(\log\Bigl(\frac{u(t)}{y}\Bigr)\Bigr)\biggr)=\ell\Bigl(\exp(\log(u(t))-\log(y))\Bigl).

Define

\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty),\;\psi:=\ell\circ\exp.

For this function,
\psi(0)=0
is satisfied.
Therefore,
\psi
can serve as a representing function of a distance-based loss.
Together with
\tilde{y}:=-\log(y)
and
\tilde{t}:=-\log(u(t))
,

\ell\biggl(\frac{u(t)}{y}\biggr)=\ell\Bigl(\exp(\log(u(t))-\log(y))\Bigr)=\psi(\tilde{y}-\tilde{t}).

This also holds for
c>0
using transformations
\tilde{y}:=-\log(y+c)
and
\tilde{t}:=-\log(u(t)+c)
:

\ell\biggl(\frac{u(t)+c}{y+c}\biggr)=\psi(\tilde{y}-\tilde{t}).

Conversely, starting with a distance-based loss, whose representing function is
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty),\psi(0)=0
, one can write

\psi(y-t)=\psi(\log(\exp(y-t)))=\psi\biggl(\log\Bigl(\frac{\exp(y)}{\exp(t)}\Bigr)\biggr).

Additionally, we define

\ell:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty),\;\ell:=\psi\circ\log,

fulfilling
\ell(1)=0
.
Hence,
\psi
is a representing function of a distance-based loss.
Therefore,
\tilde{y}:=\exp(-y)
and
\tilde{t}:=\exp(-t)
in
(0,\infty)
satisfy

\psi(y-t)=\psi\biggl(\log\biggl(\frac{\exp(y)}{\exp(t)}\biggr)\biggr)=\ell\biggl(\frac{\exp(-t)}{\exp(-y)}\biggr)=\ell\biggl(\frac{\tilde{t}}{\tilde{y}}\biggr).

Even though, we can find an obvious connection between ratio-based and distance-based loss functions, one can in general
not
write a ratio-based loss as a distance-based loss in its
original
arguments
(x,y,t)
, but only in its transformed arguments and vice versa.
Additionally, as
[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
, Chapter 2]
emphasized,
“a transformation is a reasonable choice in some cases due to its theoretical simplicity. However, a linear relationship in the transformed model is not linear in the original one. And one need[s] to transform the analysis results back to the original measurement scale”
.
Recalling the medical example from the introduction, a 2 kg deviation does not give any hint about the ratio of true and predicted weight.
Remember the comparison between adult and child here.
The same holds true if it is only known that 80% of the true weight was captured by the prediction.
Then, one can conclude neither the true weight nor how far the prediction derives from the true value.

In the first chapter, we also saw that distance-based loss function cannot handle a ratio-based approach.
Conversely, assuming a ratio-based loss function which fulfills the distance-based property
\ell(\frac{\hat{y}}{y})=\ell(\frac{\hat{y}+\lambda}{y+\lambda})
for all
\hat{y},y\in Y
and
\lambda\geq 0
, i.e. under- or overestimation by a certain constant is penalized equally, implies a constant function.

Lastly, we also highlight these differences with some examples.
For this, at first, consider a systematic error of the predictions
\hat{y}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, which estimates the
\xi
-th of the observed value (
\xi>0,\xi\neq 1
), i.e.
\hat{y}_{i}=\xi y_{i}
.
Then,
y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}=(1-\xi)y_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Hence,
\psi(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})
does depend on
y_{i}
in distance-based setting, whereas
\ell\Bigl(\frac{\xi y_{i}}{y_{i}}\Bigr)=\ell(\xi)
in (strict) ratio-based setting is constant for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Second, we think of the systematic error
\hat{y}_{i}=y_{i}+\xi
for
\xi\in\mathbb{R},\xi\neq 0
.
In this case, the prediction either over- or underestimates the true value by a certain amount.
Therefore,
\psi(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})=\psi(\xi)
is constant, but the ratio-based expression
\ell\Bigl(\frac{y_{i}+\xi}{y_{i}}\Bigr)=\ell\Bigl(1+\frac{\xi}{y_{i}}\Bigr)
is not.

From this short comparison of ratio-based loss functions and distance-based loss functions, it is clear that
both
classes of loss functions are interesting and that the real-life application should be taken into account to decide which type of loss function is more appropriate.

7
Alternative Definition

One can ask why we defined ratio-based losses in the proposed way.
Instead of (
1
) we could have also considered

L(x,y,t):=\ell\biggl(\frac{y+c}{u(t)+c}\biggr),\qquad(x,y,t)\in X\times Y\times\mathbb{R},

(40)

using the inverse quotient.
In this short section, we argue that in most cases our slightly different notion of an rb loss function (
1
) yields the same properties, but sometimes the results need slightly weaker assumptions and the proofs are shorter. Of course, this does not matter if the rb loss function is based on a representation function
\ell(r)=\widetilde{\ell}(r)+\widetilde{\ell}(r^{-1})-2\widetilde{\ell}(1)
, see
Proposition
3.4
.

If not otherwise mentioned, the loss function
L
will be defined
in this section only
via (
40
).

Lemma 7.1
.

For a loss function
L
defined through (
40
), let
\ell
and
u
be continuous. Then
L
is a continuous loss function.

If
L
is twice differentiable w.r.t. to its last argument, for all
fixed values of
(x,y)
, we obtain

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime}\biggl(\frac{y+c}{u(t)+c}\biggr)\frac{y+c}{(u(t)+c)^{2}}u^{\prime}(t),

\displaystyle L^{\prime\prime}(x,y,t)

\displaystyle=-\ell^{\prime\prime}\biggl(\frac{y+c}{u(t)+c}\biggr)\!\biggl(\frac{(y+c)u^{\prime}(t)}{(u(t)+c)^{2}}\biggr)^{\!2}+\ell^{\prime}\biggl(\frac{y+c}{u(t)+c}\biggr)\frac{(y+c)}{(u(t)+c)^{3}}\bigl(u^{\prime\prime}(t)(u(t)+c)-2(u^{\prime}(t))^{2}\bigr).

Remark 7.2
.

Let
L
be a loss function defined via (
40
).
If
\ell
and
u
are (
n
times) differentiable functions, then
L
a (
n
times) differentiable loss function,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Looking at the second derivative, one can hardly derive a criterion for convexity.
However, as with the first definition, we refer to Proposition
3.4
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b<\infty
,
c\geq 0
with
a+c>0
.
Let
\ell
and
u
be Lipschitz continuous functions.
In that case,
L
defined by (
40
) is a Lipschitz continuous loss function.

Compared to the result in Chapter
3
, we have an additional restriction here.
Whereas Chapter
3
allows
b=\infty
, this is not possible here.
In contrast, Lemmata
3.12
and
3.13
as well as Proposition
3.15
still hold.
Additionally, for
Y=(0,1)
we only need a locally Lipschitz continuous function
\ell
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
L
be a loss function of type (
40
).
Let
Y=(0,1),u(t)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-t)}
, and
c>0
.
Moreover, let
\ell
be locally Lipschitz continuous.
Then,
L
is a Lipschitz continuous loss with Lipschitz constant
|L|_{1}\leq\frac{|\ell|_{I,1}|u|_{1}(1+c)}{c^{2}},I:=\Bigl(\frac{c}{1+c},\frac{1+c}{c}\Bigr)
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Assume
L
like in (
40
).
Let
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b<\infty
, and
a+c>0
.
Assume
\ell
and
u
to be locally Lipschitz continuous.
Then,
L
is a locally Lipschitz continuous loss function.

Altogether, most results from Chapter
3
apply to loss functions defined by (
40
) as well.
Only the result about global Lipschitz continuity has a certain restriction.
Hence, our original definition (
1
) of a ratio-based loss function has some minor advantages when compared with the definition in
(
40
).
Calculations are easier and shorter in Chapter
3
.

Furthermore, the former definition helps to control the risk in an easier manner. Indeed, the ratio

\frac{y+c}{u(t)+c}\in\biggl(\frac{a+c}{b+c},\frac{b+c}{a+c}\biggr)\subsetneq(0,\infty)

is bounded if
Y=(a,b),0\leq a<b<\infty
, and
c\geq 0
such that
a+c>0
.
Thus, a continuous representing function
\ell
bounds the risk
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(f)
of a measurable function
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover, if
Y=(0,\infty)
and
\ell
is bounded, the risk of a measurable function is finite.

When it comes to the calculation of the risk
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(0)
, where
Y=(0,\infty)
, we, however, have to consider the ratio

\frac{y+c}{u(0)+c}\in\Bigl(\frac{a+c}{u(0)+c},\infty\Bigr)

which has a lower, but no upper bound.
Even assuming a globally Lipschitz continuous
\ell
gives

\biggl|\ell\Bigl(\frac{y+c}{u(0)+c}\Bigr)-\ell(1)\biggr|\leq|\ell|_{1}\biggl|\frac{y-u(0)}{u(0)+c}\biggr|,

which can become arbitrarily large when taking the supremum over
y\in Y=(0,\infty)
.
In general, we can therefore not conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{L,P}(0)<\infty
.
Hence, using the inverse ratio does in this setting in general not bound the
L
-risk.

8
Conclusion

Many machine learning methods and AI algorithms are based on three cornerstones: (i) an appropriate function space
\mathcal{F}
often called hypothesis space, (ii) the set of probability measures
\mathcal{P}
, and (iii) a loss function
L
which is used to define the risk functional.

In supervised learning, margin-based loss functions for classification and distance-based loss functions for regression and quantile regression have been investigated in great detail by many authors. Distance-based loss functions are – not only but of particular – interest, if the loss between output values and its predictions depends on their
difference
.
This is obvious under the classical signal plus noise assumption.

This paper focused on loss functions for supervised learning,
which depend on the
ratio
of output values and its predictions.
Such ratio-based loss functions are of particular interest if a multiplicative error structure seems to be plausible.
Though, relative errors have been of concern for several years (see e.g.
[
ChenEtAl2010LARE
,
ChenEtAl2016LPRE
,
JadonEtAl2022
,
27
]
) for multiplicative models, a systematic investigation of ratio-based loss functions has not been done in the literature to our best knowledge.
Therefore, the goal of this survey article was to put ratio-based loss functions into a more general framework.
We proposed a general definition, investigated their properties and those of the corresponding risk functionals, and collected several examples from the literature and proposed some new ratio-based loss functions.
We also showed that in general neither ratio-based loss functions are a subset of distance-based loss functions nor vice versa, although constant loss functions (which are completely uninteresting for practical applications) obviously belong to both sets of loss functions.

The goal of this paper was not to deduce learning rates, robustness results, etc. for a certain machine learning method.
Instead, we hope that our survey paper will stimulate research on several machine learning algorithms based on ratio-based loss functions in various directions.
Such research may include finding additional convex and Lipschitz continuous ratio-based loss functions as well as deducing learning rates, representer theorems, or statistical robustness results (such as qualitative robustness, influence functions, and bounds for the maxbias) for certain machine learning methods including kernel based methods and CNNs.
Moreover, research in terms of non-convex loss functions can be crucial for some ML methods, too.
One can also think about mitigating the assumptions on the link function
u
as well as expanding the ratio-based definition to other output spaces.
We are currently focusing on applying ratio-based loss functions to kernel-based ML methods and want to describe the set of all convex and Lipschitz continuous rb loss functions.
However, this is beyond the scope of this paper.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Assume, that
L
is a convex rb loss function.
Let
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.
Then, for all
\hat{t}\in\mathbb{R}
,

s\mapsto\frac{L(x,y,s)-L(x,y,\hat{t})}{s-\hat{t}}=\frac{L(x,y,\hat{t})-L(x,y,s)}{\hat{t}-s}

(41)

is increasing.
Furthermore, there are
t<t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
L(x,y,t)<L(x,y,t^{\prime})
.
Define

q:=\frac{L(x,y,t^{\prime})-L(x,y,t)}{t^{\prime}-t}>0.

Let
t^{\prime\prime}>t^{\prime}
and
\lambda=\frac{t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}}{t-t^{\prime\prime}}\in(0,1)
. Then
t^{\prime}=\lambda t+(1-\lambda)t^{\prime\prime}
.
Since
L
is a convex loss function,

\displaystyle L(x,y,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\lambda L(x,y,t)+(1-\lambda)L(x,y,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad\frac{L(x,y,t^{\prime})-L(x,y,t^{\prime\prime})}{t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{L(x,y,t)-L(x,y,t^{\prime\prime})}{t-t^{\prime\prime}}\overset{\eqref{monotoneAbb}}{\geq}\frac{L(x,y,t)-L(x,y,t^{\prime})}{t-t^{\prime}}=q.

Hence,

\displaystyle L(x,y,t^{\prime})-L(x,y,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\leq q(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\qquad L(x,y,t^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\geq L(x,y,t^{\prime})+q(t^{\prime\prime}-t^{\prime}).

As
q>0
and
t^{\prime\prime}>t^{\prime}
,
\lim_{t\to\infty}L(x,y,t)=\infty
follows, giving a contradiction to
\lim_{t\to\infty}L(x,y,t)<\infty
discussed in advance of Proposition
3.3
.
Therefore, there is no convex loss
L
in this setting.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

Before proving the result, we show that
\ell
is well-defined, i.e.
\ell\geq 0
.
As each
r\in(0,\infty)
can be written as
r=\e^{t}=u(t)
with
t=\log(r)\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
f(t):=\widetilde{\ell}(\e^{t})
.
Then,

f^{\prime\prime}(t)=\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(\e^{t})\cdot(\e^{t})^{2}+\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}(\e^{t})\cdot\e^{t}=\e^{t}\biggl(\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}(\e^{t})+\e^{t}\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(\e^{t})\biggr).

As (
2
) holds,
f^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
f
is convex.
Therefore,

\widetilde{\ell}(1)=f(0)\leq\frac{1}{2}f(t)+\frac{1}{2}f(-t)=\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\ell}(r)+\frac{1}{2}\widetilde{\ell}(r^{-1})

gives the assertion.

For
y\in Y
, we now define
q(t):=\frac{\e^{t}}{y}
, then
q^{\prime}(t)=q(t)
and the derivative of
\frac{1}{q(t)}
is
-\frac{1}{q(t)}
.
Hence,

L^{\prime\prime}(x,y,t)=q(t)\Bigl(\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}(q(t))+q(t)\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(q(t))\Bigr)+\frac{1}{q(t)}\biggl(\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime}\Bigl(\frac{1}{q(t)}\Bigr)+\frac{1}{q(t)}\widetilde{\ell}^{\prime\prime}\Bigl(\frac{1}{q(t)}\Bigr)\biggr).

Since
q(t),\frac{1}{q(t)}\in(0,\infty)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
and (
2
) holds,
L^{\prime\prime}(x,y,t)\geq 0
.
Thus,
L
is a convex loss.
∎

Proof of Lemma
