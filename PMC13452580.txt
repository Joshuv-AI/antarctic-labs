Hyperkisspeptinemia and hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis uncoupling in hemodialysis patients: clinical evidence suggestive of central desensitization
Authors: Naiying Lan, Dan Ye, Maoting Li, Fanzhou Zeng, Cheng Xue, Zewei Chen, Qing Shao, Jun Liu, Nanmei Liu, Bo Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundReproductive and sexual dysfunction (uremic hypogonadism) are pervasive in maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients, yet the underlying neuroendocrine mechanisms remain poorly understood. Kisspeptin is the master upstream regulator of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis. We hypothesized that uremia-induced kisspeptin accumulation is associated with HPG axis uncoupling through central desensitization.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, 69 MHD patients and 20 age-matched healthy controls (HCs) were recruited. All participants were rigorously screened to exclude active inflammation and primary gonadal disease. Serum kisspeptin, gonadotropins, and sex steroids were quantified. Mediation analysis was performed to elucidate the pathophysiological pathway between kisspeptin and erectile dysfunction (ED) in males.ResultsSerum kisspeptin was profoundly elevated in MHD patients versus HCs (median, 30.35 vs 9.02 ng/ml; P < .001). In uremic females, despite profound hypoestrogenism and massive kisspeptin stimuli, compensatory gonadotropin surges were blunted (follicle-stimulating hormone: P = .138; luteinizing hormone: P = .149), suggesting potential pituitary desensitization. In uremic males, kisspeptin independently predicted decreased testosterone (β = −0.045, P = .046) after adjusting for age, C-reactive protein, and diabetes. Mediation analysis revealed a mediation pattern consistent with testosterone-mediated effects of kisspeptin’s impact on erectile function (indirect effect P = .046; direct effect P = .081), suggesting that kisspeptin accumulation may be associated with ED exclusively by suppressing the HPG axis. Serum kisspeptin predicted moderate-to-severe ED (area under the curve 0.734, 95% confidence interval: 0.596–0.872).ConclusionsThe marked hyperkisspeptinemia and concurrent HPG axis uncoupling observed in uremic patients are clinically suggestive of central neuroendocrine desensitization. Serum kisspeptin may serve as a novel exploratory functional proxy for the severity of uremic endocrine disruption, offering a potential target for therapeutic intervention.Keywords: erectile dysfunction, hemodialysis, hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, kisspeptin, uremic hypogonadism

INTRODUCTION

Reproductive and sexual dysfunction are highly prevalent yet often under-recognized complications in patients with end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) undergoing maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) [1, 2]. These disturbances, collectively termed uremic hypogonadism, manifest as erectile dysfunction (ED) and impaired spermatogenesis in men [3, 4], and menstrual irregularities or premature menopause in women [5–7]. Beyond the profound impact on quality of life, uremic hypogonadism is increasingly recognized as a contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and all-cause mortality [8], likely through its association with chronic inflammation and metabolic derangements.

The pathogenesis of uremic hypogonadism is classically considered multifactorial, involving primary gonadal failure, systemic uremic toxicity. However, the specific disruption of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis remains the most elusive component [9]. In the physiological state, the HPG axis is governed by the pulsatile release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus, which stimulates the pituitary gland to secrete follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). Emerging evidence identifies kisspeptin, a neuropeptide encoded by the KISS1 gene, as the “master upstream regulator” of this axis [10]. By binding to its receptor [Kiss1 receptor (Kiss1R)] on GnRH neurons, kisspeptin initiates the reproductive cascade and mediates the feedback loops of sex steroids.

Given that kisspeptin is a small peptide primarily cleared and degraded by the kidneys [11], its accumulation in the setting of severely impaired renal function is theoretically expected. However, the exact pathophysiological consequences of this hyperkisspeptinemia remain a critical knowledge gap. While some experimental models suggest that kisspeptin could be a target for treating subfertility [12, 13], it remains unknown whether this dramatically elevated kisspeptin actively drives HPG axis hyperactivity or, paradoxically, is associated with a state of central neuroendocrine desensitization—a phenomenon we conceptualize as “HPG axis uncoupling.” To date, the precise decoupling point between upstream kisspeptin signals and the downstream pituitary-gonadal response has not been systematically verified in a clinical cohort.

In the present study, we aimed to characterize the serum kisspeptin profile in a rigorously phenotyped cohort of MHD patients compared to healthy controls (HCs). Furthermore, we sought to test the hypothesis of uremia-induced central desensitization by elucidating the associations between kisspeptin and the HPG axis hormones. Finally, utilizing mediation analysis, we evaluated the potential of serum kisspeptin as a functional surrogate for identifying clinical reproductive dysfunction in the MHD population.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

Unlike large-scale epidemiological registries that are often limited by unmeasured confounders, our study deliberately prioritized a rigorously phenotyped and highly controlled cohort. Strict exclusion criteria were employed to cleanly dissect the precise neuroendocrine disruptions without the interfering effects of active inflammation, primary gonadal failure, or exogenous hormone therapies. This observational, cross-sectional case–control study was conducted at the hemodialysis center of Naval Medical Center of PLA, Shanghai, China between November 2025 and March 2026. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Naval Medical Center of PLA (Approval No. AF-HEC-092) and published elsewhere [14]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This trial is registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500106627) (Registration Date: 28 July 2025).

Initially, 190 patients undergoing MHD and 20 healthy volunteers were screened. The inclusion criteria for the MHD group were: (i) age ≥18 years; and (ii) receiving regular hemodialysis (three times a week, 4 hours per session) for at least 3 months. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (i) acute infection or systemic inflammatory disease within the past month; (ii) primary gonadal disease or history of gonadectomy; (iii) active malignancy; or (iv) current use of hormone replacement therapy, oral contraceptives, or medications known to interfere with the HPG axis. After applying the exclusion criteria and accounting for consent withdrawal or sample handling issues, a final cohort of 69 MHD patients and 20 age-matched HCs was included in the final analysis.

Clinical and demographic data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical data, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), dialysis vintage, and comorbidities (e.g. hypertension and diabetes mellitus), were systematically collected from electronic medical records. For male participants, erectile function was quantitatively assessed using the International Index of Erectile Function-5 (IIEF-5) questionnaire [15]. For female participants, detailed menstrual histories were recorded to determine menopausal status, categorized as regular menses, oligomenorrhea/irregular menses, or amenorrhea/menopause.

Laboratory measurements

Fasting venous blood samples were collected from all participants in the morning (between 07:00 and 09:00 a.m.) to minimize diurnal neuroendocrine variations. For MHD patients, samples were drawn prior to the start of the mid-week dialysis session. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes at 4°C and stored at −80°C until batch analysis.

Serum levels of kisspeptin were quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Shinnybio Corp., Shanghai, China), strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer’s assay validation, the sensitivity (minimum detectable concentration) is <0.1 ng/ml, the intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) is <10%, and the inter-assay CV is <15%. Additionally, the assay exhibits no cross-reactivity with other soluble structural analogs. Routine reproductive hormones, including FSH, LH, total testosterone (T), and estradiol (E2), as well as C-reactive protein (CRP) and routine biochemical parameters, were measured using an automated electrochemiluminescence immunoassay analyzer in the verified central laboratory (DIAN DIAGNOSTICS Group Co., Ltd).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.95). Continuous variables were assessed for normal distribution using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because the endocrinological data (including kisspeptin, FSH, and LH) exhibited skewed distributions, continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and comparisons between the HC and MHD groups were conducted using the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Given the exploratory nature of this observational study, we did not apply stringent corrections for multiple comparisons (e.g. Bonferroni correction), which should be taken into account when interpreting the reported P-values to avoid Type I error inflation.

To eliminate the confounding physiological differences between sexes, all subsequent HPG axis analyses were stratified by gender. Bivariate correlations between kisspeptin and clinical variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. To identify independent predictors of gonadal hormone levels, multivariate linear regression models were constructed, adjusting for clinically relevant covariates, including age, dialysis vintage, CRP, and diabetes mellitus. To further explore the pathophysiological pathways linking hyperkisspeptinemia to ED, a mediation analysis was conducted. In this theoretical model, serum kisspeptin was set as the independent variable (X), T as the mediator (M), and the IIEF-5 score as the dependent variable (Y). The direct, indirect (mediation), and total effects were estimated using Maximum Likelihood to determine whether testosterone mediates the impact of kisspeptin on sexual dysfunction. Finally, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to evaluate the clinical utility of kisspeptin in predicting the presence of moderate-to-severe ED (defined as IIEF-5 <12) in the male cohort. The optimal cut-off value was determined by maximizing the Youden index. A two-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Marked accumulation of serum kisspeptin in uremia

Overall serum kisspeptin levels were profoundly elevated in MHD patients compared to HCs (median: 30.35 vs 9.02 ng/ml, P < .001) (Fig. 2A). To eliminate the potential confounding effect of gender on the HPG axis, a prespecified subgroup analysis was conducted. The extreme elevation of kisspeptin in the MHD group remained highly significant in both males (P < .001) and females (P < .001) (Fig. 2B), suggesting that kisspeptin accumulation is a universal feature of the uremic milieu, independent of sex.

Decoupling of the HPG axis and central desensitization

To investigate the downstream effects of this upstream kisspeptin surge, we analyzed the corresponding pituitary and gonadal hormone responses (Fig. 3). In females, despite the presence of profound hypoestrogenism in both groups (Fig. 3F) and a three-fold higher kisspeptin stimulus in the MHD group, the compensatory surge of gonadotropins typically observed in physiological menopause was markedly blunted. There was no significant difference in the levels of FSH (P = .138) and LH (P = .149) between MHD and HC females (Fig. 3D and E).

Correlation heatmap analysis (Fig. 3A and B) further illustrated this endocrine paradox. In uremic females, the expected strong correlations between upstream kisspeptin and downstream FSH/LH were absent. In uremic males, serum kisspeptin exhibited significant negative correlations with both T levels and clinical erectile function scores (IIEF-5). Collectively, this pronounced absence of a compensatory gonadotropin surge, despite the dual presence of profound hypoestrogenism and massive upstream kisspeptin stimuli, provides observational evidence supporting the hypothesis of central pituitary desensitization (or HPG axis uncoupling) in the uremic milieu.

Independent predictors of gonadal dysfunction

To rigorously exclude the influence of critical confounders such as age, systemic inflammation, and dialysis vintage, gender-stratified multivariate linear regression models were constructed (Table 2). All enrolled MHD patients were in a state of oligoanuria, defined as a daily urine output of <400 ml. Consequently, the contribution of residual kidney function (RKF) to kisspeptin clearance was considered negligible and was not included as a separate covariate in the multivariate analysis. In males (Model 1), after adjusting for age, CRP, diabetes, and dialysis vintage, serum kisspeptin (β = −0.045, P = .046) and CRP (β = −0.015, P = .026) emerged as the only independent risk factors for decreased testosterone levels. Conversely, in females (Models 2 and 3), age independently predicted FSH and LH levels (P = .011 and P = .040, respectively), whereas kisspeptin completely lost its predictive value (P > .05). This statistical loss of association is consistent with disruption of the kisspeptin–GnRH–gonadotropin signaling pathway.

Predictive value of kisspeptin for clinical reproductive dysfunction

Beyond characterizing the neuroendocrine profile, we evaluated whether the magnitude of kisspeptin elevation functionally correlates with the severity of HPG axis disruption. ROC curve analysis was employed to evaluate the clinical utility of kisspeptin. In the male cohort, serum kisspeptin showed potential as an exploratory surrogate for predicting the presence of moderate-to-severe ED (IIEF-5 <12) with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.734 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.596–0.872]. An optimal cut-off of 17.62 ng/ml yielded a sensitivity of 85.2% (Fig. 4A), suggesting that kisspeptin concentrations may serve as a quantitative clinical surrogate for the degree of central endocrine failure in uremic patients.

Mediation analysis of the kisspeptin–testosterone–ED axis

To further elucidate the pathophysiological pathway linking hyperkisspeptinemia to ED, a mediation analysis was performed (Fig. 4B). The total effect of kisspeptin on IIEF-5 scores was highly significant (Estimate = −0.288, P = .005). However, when accounting for testosterone as a mediator, the direct effect of kisspeptin on IIEF-5 became nonsignificant (Estimate = −0.189, P = .081). In contrast, the indirect effect through testosterone was significant (Estimate = −0.098, P = .046). Analysis of the specific path coefficients revealed that kisspeptin was significantly associated with decreased testosterone (Path A: Estimate = −0.056, P = .002), which in turn strongly predicted lower IIEF-5 scores (Path B: Estimate = 1.750, P = .010). These results establish a statistical mediation model, indicating that kisspeptin accumulation is not directly linked to erectile impairment, but is statistically associated with sexual dysfunction exclusively through its negative impact on testosterone production. This statistical pathway supports the hypothesis regarding the functional consequence of central desensitization.

DISCUSSION

Main findings

To the best of our knowledge, this is one of the first clinical studies to systematically characterize the disruption of the kisspeptin–gonadotropin signaling pathway in patients undergoing MHD. The major findings are three-fold: (i) serum kisspeptin levels are profoundly elevated in the uremic milieu; (ii) despite this potent upstream stimulus, there is a marked “uncoupling” of the HPG axis characterized by a blunted pituitary response and subsequent gonadal failure, suggesting a hypothesis of central neuroendocrine desensitization; and (iii) mediation analysis reveals that hyperkisspeptinemia is statistically linked to severe ED exclusively through the suppression of testosterone, making it a potential exploratory clinical proxy for uremic sexual dysfunction.

Mechanisms of kisspeptin accumulation in uremia

The dramatic surge of kisspeptin in MHD patients likely stems from a combination of impaired renal clearance and potential hyperproduction. Kisspeptin-54, the primary circulating form, is a small peptide that is presumably filtered and degraded by the kidneys [16, 17]. In the setting of ESKD, the loss of functional nephrons leads to its significant accumulation, a phenomenon mirrored by other neuropeptides [18–20]. Furthermore, the chronic micro-inflammatory state (as evidenced by elevated CRP in our cohort) and metabolic disturbances associated with uremia may act as persistent triggers for the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus to upregulate kisspeptin expression, creating a state of “neuroendocrine congestion.” Additionally, alternative explanations for elevated kisspeptin concentrations must be considered; increased kisspeptin production could also be secondary to altered endocrine feedback loops and uremic metabolic disturbances, such as insulin resistance, rather than solely due to impaired renal clearance.

The “central desensitization” paradox: HPG axis uncoupling

The most striking observation of our study is the absence of a compensatory gonadotropin surge in the face of extremely high kisspeptin levels. In physiological menopause (as seen in our HC group), the withdrawal of estrogen negative feedback leads to a robust rise in FSH and LH. However, in our MHD patients, the FSH/LH levels remained inappropriately low.

A seemingly paradoxical finding in our male cohort is the independent negative correlation between serum kisspeptin and testosterone levels. In normal physiology, kisspeptin powerfully stimulates the HPG axis through pulsatile secretion [10, 21]. However, we hypothesize that the continuous, nonpulsatile accumulation of kisspeptin in uremia may trigger a profound downregulation and internalization of the Kiss1R on pituitary gonadotropes. This sustained hyperstimulation leads to receptor desensitization, a phenomenon hypothesized to be mechanistically analogous to the “chemical castration” observed with continuous GnRH agonist therapy. If such desensitization exists, the combination of this central desensitization and the loss of peripheral negative feedback due to uremic testicular impairment creates a vicious cycle, driving kisspeptin levels to pathological highs while testosterone production collapses.

Clinical implications and the mediation pathway

A pivotal finding of our study is derived from the mediation analysis in the male cohort. We demonstrated a statistical mediation model where the impact of kisspeptin on ED is entirely mediated by testosterone deficiency. This provides statistical support suggesting that kisspeptin accumulation does not exert direct peripheral toxicity on penile vasculature. Instead, it is associated with sexual dysfunction exclusively by suppressing the central HPG axis.

From a clinical perspective, this mediation pathway suggests that monitoring kisspeptin levels may potentially help clinicians quantify the severity of central endocrine failure. Furthermore, it implies that therapeutic strategies targeting the restoration of Kiss1R sensitivity or exogenous testosterone replacement (bypassing the central blockade) warrant future investigation for uremic patients with hyperkisspeptinemia-driven hypogonadism.

Emerging roles of kisspeptin in uremic metabolism and renal pathophysiology

Beyond its classical role in the reproductive axis, emerging evidence highlights the multifaceted functions of kisspeptin in metabolic regulation and renal pathophysiology, which warrants further investigation in the context of ESKD. Recent studies have demonstrated that kisspeptin regulates energy expenditure and glucose-stimulated insulin secretion [22], suggesting that the extreme hyperkisspeptinemia observed in our MHD cohort might be intricately linked to the complex metabolic derangements (e.g. insulin resistance and energy wasting) highly prevalent in uremia [23, 24]. Furthermore, animal models have shown profound alterations in local kisspeptin and Kiss1R expression within renal tubular and vascular cells during chronic renal failure [25] and acute ischemia/reperfusion injury [26]. These findings raise a compelling hypothesis: the surge of systemic kisspeptin in dialysis patients may not merely represent passive accumulation due to reduced renal clearance, but could also reflect an active, albeit potentially maladaptive, systemic or local compensatory response to renal injury and metabolic stress. Future longitudinal studies and animal models are eagerly awaited to dissect the causal relationship between the kisspeptin system, uremic metabolic inflexibility, and residual renal function.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this investigation is the focus on central neuroendocrine uncoupling, supported by a rigorously controlled cohort and sophisticated mediation analysis, offering a novel pathophysiological framework for uremic hypogonadism.

However, several crucial limitations must be acknowledged. First, the modest sample size, inherently limited by our strict single-center inclusion criteria (designed to minimize inflammatory and primary gonadal confounders), may reduce statistical power. Second, the cross-sectional design inherently precludes definitive causal inference. We cannot absolutely ascertain whether hyperkisspeptinemia is the primary active driver of HPG axis suppression or a parallel surrogate marker of severe uremic toxicity. Consequently, our mediation pathway cannot establish strict causality, and reverse causation and residual unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Future longitudinal studies are required to determine whether hyperkisspeptinemia is a causal contributor to endocrine dysfunction. Third, the diagnosis of true pituitary resistance typically requires dynamic endocrine stimulation testing (e.g. GnRH or kisspeptin challenge). Because our study relied on steady-state fasting hormone levels, the observed uncoupling can only be interpreted as an association suggestive of central desensitization rather than definitive resistance. Furthermore, our proposed mechanism involving Kiss1R downregulation, receptor internalization, and reduced responsiveness to chronic kisspeptin exposure remains highly speculative, as no direct measurements of Kiss1R expression, receptor activity, or GnRH secretion pathways were conducted. Fourth, due to the demographic characteristics of our cohort (median age >55 years), the vast majority of female participants were in a menopausal or amenorrheic state. The limited number of premenopausal women precluded a meaningful evaluation of kisspeptin fluctuations across specific menstrual phases and its direct correlation with uremic menstrual irregularities, thereby limiting the generalizability of these findings to physiological HPG-axis regulation in premenopausal women. Fifth, several key endocrine regulators that may contribute to reproductive dysfunction, such as inhibin B, sex hormone-binding globulin, dynamic GnRH activity, and other metabolic mediators, were not evaluated in this study, which could substantially influence interpretations of HPG-axis function. Sixth, the claim that serum kisspeptin is a clinically useful biomarker should be interpreted with caution; the ROC analysis demonstrated only moderate predictive performance (AUC = 0.734), and these findings were derived from a relatively small single-center cohort lacking external validation. Finally, although all patients presented with oligoanuria, more detailed quantitative information regarding residual kidney function (e.g. formal residual glomerular filtration rate assessments) was not available, which limits our ability to perfectly differentiate the proportion of kisspeptin elevation resulting from impaired renal clearance versus hyperproduction. Future longitudinal studies utilizing dynamic stimulation tests and animal models are essential to validate the molecular mechanisms of Kiss1R desensitization in uremia.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, by leveraging a rigorously phenotyped clinical cohort, our study demonstrates that serum kisspeptin levels are profoundly elevated in patients undergoing MHD. Furthermore, our findings provide novel evidence that uremia is associated with a pattern of HPG-axis dysregulation that may reflect central neuroendocrine desensitization to this massive kisspeptin stimulus. The application of mediation analysis further establishes that the association between hyperkisspeptinemia and ED is predominantly mediated by the suppression of testosterone, supporting the hypothesis that kisspeptin accumulation may lack direct peripheral toxicity. These pathophysiological insights challenge the traditional peripheral-centric view of uremic hypogonadism and establish the kisspeptin–GnRH signaling pathway as a crucial exploratory functional proxy for uremic endocrine disruption. Our findings suggest that targeting central receptor sensitivity warrants future investigation as a potential therapeutic strategy for addressing reproductive and metabolic failure in the hemodialysis population.
