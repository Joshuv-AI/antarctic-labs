It does what it says on the tin: safe synthetic data from coarsened margins.
Gillian M Raab

Abstract
This paper proposes a method of creating synthetic data (SD) that will have two important advantages for the user compared to other methods currently available. The first is transparency; unlike other methods, the person in receipt of the SD will know which of the relationships between variables in the original data will be approximately maintained in the SD. The second is a guarantee that the SD is derived from information that has already been judged to be free of disclosure risk. This is achieved by first defining and calculating the margins where relationships between variables will be maintained in the SD. Each margin will then be subject to statistical disclosure control (SDC) to the standards defined by the data custodian, e.g. top-coding and bottom-coding, combination of small categories and/or modifying small counts. Further adjustment of the curated margins is advised by coarsening all counts in the table to multiples of the disclosure limit. These adjusted margins are used to create SD by the Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF) algorithm. The practical steps involved in creating such SD are illustrated using data from the 1901 Census of Scotland.



1
Introduction

1.1
Overview

The creation of fully synthetic data (FSD) was first proposed as a privacy enhancing technology by Rubin
[
25
]
in 1993. The term “fully” is here taken to mean that all records for all variables in the potentially disclosive ground truth (GT) data are replaced by values generated from statistical models. It was hoped that the creation of synthetic versions of administrative data sets held by national statistics agencies (NSAs) might help to widen access to the information that can be obtained from such data and thus allow public sector data to be used to influence policy for the public good.

This paper considers the ways that SD can help this goal to be achieved, while assuring the public and data custodians in NSAs that releasing SD will not risk any breaches of the privacy of individuals whose records are held in the GT.

1.2
How can SD help to widen access?

Data bases of administrative data are generally held in secure environments where they can only be accessed by staff of the NSAs who analyse them to create national statistics and reports. Many agencies now have trusted research environments (TREs) where policy analysts, social scientists and others can access the data under strict supervision, without internet access and where research results can only be taken outside the safe setting after TRE staff have checked the output for disclosure risk
[
24
,
11
,
26
]
. Processes of obtaining access to TREs can be long and difficult. A TRE is a difficult work environment as it inhibits joint working and interpretation of data. There are a variety of possible ways that SD can be used to overcome these difficulties.

1.

REPLACEMENT
Use of the FSD instead of the GT to draw policy conclusions, possibly with the option of having results confirmed by running them on the GT before results are published.

2.

PLANNING
To use the SD to understand the variables in the data and formulate research plans.

3.

TEACHING
FSD can be used to create data sets for teaching researchers about the data held in the TRE and how to use it.

4.

CODE WRITING
Giving potential users of TREs access to a file with the same structure (variable names, types and details) as the original data, to allow code to be developed that they can use when access to the GT is permitted.

An additional aspect is whether the SD can be accessed outside the TRE, and if so under what conditions; freely available, only to identified individuals or under an end-user license agreement (EULA) where the recipient of the FSD signs up to conditions of use. The UK Data Service defines these three levels of access as

1.5
Creating FSD without access to the original GT records

The FSD created from univariate distributions is one example of this approach. It can be used for developing code and does allow the user to get some experience of the GT data, but its value for research planning or for creating teaching data sets is limited. Another approach, designed for creating teaching data sets, is to use the results of published analyses of GT data to generate FSD with genetic algorithms, described by
[

and that has been used to create a teaching dataset from the ONS Annual Survey of Hours and Earnings, linked to 2011 Census data.
[

. The method we propose for creating FSD has much in common with these two approaches. Knowledge of the summary data used to create the SD allows tells the user the relationships between variables that are expected to be retained in the SD.

2
Synthetic data from published coarsened margins.

2.1
The method

The proposed method consists of the following steps.

•

carry out SDC procedures in the GT data, such as top- and bottom-coding for numeric variables and and grouping them into ranges, form merged categories of any level of a variable with a small count.
14
14

14

these steps are often done for data that will be available in the TRE
.

•

Decide on the margins to be preserved in the SD and create them from the GT data.

•

Check the margins for small cells below a
disclosure limit specified by the TRE, often the value of 5 or 10, as suggested in
[
24
,
11
,
26
]
. If no tables require adjustment then proceed to the last two steps.

•

Adjust the margins. Values in the tables that are below the disclosure limit should be replaced and other cells may need to be be adjusted to prevent disclosure from multiple tables. One option discussed for the example is to coarsen the counts in the table so that the counts are changed to multiples of the disclosure limit.

•

Create a cross tabulation of all the variables that appear in the adjusted marginal tables and obtain a fit to this table by Iterative Proportional Fitting (IPF)
[
8
,
5
]

•

Generate one or more synthetic tables as samples of any desired size from a multinomial distributions with proabilities given by the fitted IPF proportions and create SD(s) from these tables.

When any of the margins have been modified to conform to disclosure rules the margins will no longer be compatible with each other, but this does not prevent the IPF algorithm from converging, as we show from the example.

The decision on which margins to fit can be taken in various ways. An analysis of the GT with log-linear models might be carried out to decide on important relationships between variables. Another approach, that we illustrate below, is to start with fitting all two-way margins and then explore whether there are any three-way margins that have not been fitted adequately.

When a table has two or more cells below the disclosure limit, then it will be considered non-disclosive. But if only one cell is below the disclosure limit then the table remains disclosive, since the number in the small cell can be determined by subtraction, and some further adjustment must be made

Where any of the margins contain zero counts, a decision needs to be made as to whether these are structural zeros: a case that could never arise, such as answering “yes” to “do you have a driving license” for people under 18. Non-structural zeros should be counted as disclosive and suppressed by replacing them with the disclosure limit. Similarly, some missing values are determined by the structure of the data; for example number of years smoked must be missing for non-smokers. These structural aspects are essential for any useful SD, but we do not illustrate them here.

The replacement of random zeros with the disclosure limit also avoids the problem of the random zero cells being reproduced in the SD. The methods
ipf
and
catall
(for saturated models) implemented in the synthpop package
[
18
]
add a small quantity to all the cells in the fitted probabilities. This is equivalent to a prior belief in a non-zero probability for every cell in the table. A similar approach was used saturated models by Jackson et al.
[
15
]
.

Should the NSA publish the tables that have been used to create the SD sets? This would be recommended to increase the transparency of the method. But it could mean publishing a large number of tables. Depending on which tables are used and how they have been modified, a disclosure risk may still be possible by combining information from tables, each of which would be considered non-disclosive by itself. This is discussed further in the next section, and a possible method of overcoming it is suggested.

2.2
Limitations

The two limitations of the IPF algorithm are the need to create a large cross-tabulation of all the variables, and the large number of iterations that can sometimes be needed for the algorithm to converge. The eleven variables in the example discussed below generated a table with just over 50 million cells, analysed on modest Windows laptop
15
15

15

Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-6300HQ CPU @ 2.30GHz (2.30 GHz) processor with 12Gbytes RAM
. but adding more variables would go beyond the memory available. With better computational resources this method could be applied to data sets with more variables.

If the margins used to create the SD are to be published, there is the possibility of a small cell being identified by combining information from different tables. Here is a very simple example: two-way tables with two or more values below the disclosure limit are generally considered to be safe because we cannot deduce the original values by subtraction from the known total of original records. However, if we know the one-way margins of the GT data, we can discover the true counts for any tables where any row or column where there is only a single cell below the disclosure limit. Even if the one-way tables are not published, they could be derived from any tables without modified cells. One way to overcome this would be for the disclosure control staff to
examine all possible combinations of published tables to check them for disclosure by differencing, a very tedious task if there are many tables. A more practical solution would be to coarsen the data by expressing all the counts in the margins as multiples of the disclosure limit. For a disclosure limit of 10, all the counts in the margins could be replaced as follows: counts 0-9 to 10, counts 10-19 to 20, counts 20-29 to 30… etc. Differencing such tables would not correspond to the differences in the original tables.

SD set(s) of any desired size could then be generated by IPF from proportions calculated from these counts. The sum of the counts in each margin will exceed the number of records in the original by a different number for each margin. A fixed number could be subtracted from the cells in every margin to ensure that the average total counts in all margins is close to the original sample size.

When IPF is carried out with higher order margins, it is important to include the univariate margins for any variables that do not appear in any of other margins. Another possibility would be to include one-way margins along with higher-order margins even when they do appear in the higher-order margins. This was attempted with the example below, but did not improve the utility and may increase disclosure risk.

3.3
Comparing the utility of the three SD sets

To compare each of the SD sets with the GT we start by evaluating how well they reproduce tables. The measure used is the standardised propensity score mean square error
SpMSE

[
27
]
. The propensity score is calculated by combining the rows of the observed and SD and assigning an indicator variable with the value
1
for synthetic rows and zero for original rows. The propensity score is then calculated from the predicted value of the indicator variable from the combined data, as the mean value of the squared difference of the predicted values from 0.5 for the case when SD and the GT have the same number of records,
N
19
19

19

when numbers are unequal more complicated formulae are needed
[
27
]
. For a table with
n
cells containing GT counts of
y_{i}
and synthetic counts
s_{i}
,
i=1,...n
, the predicted value from the combined data is just
(s_{i}+y_{i})/2
for all the observations in that cell. Hence we get

pMSE=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{(s_{i}+y_{i}){(s_{i}/(s_{i}+y_{i})-0.5)^{2}}}

(1)

reducing to

pMSE=\frac{1}{8N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\frac{(s_{i}-y_{i})^{2},}{(s_{i}+y_{i})/2}}.

(2)

We can see that equation 2 has a form similar to Perarson’s
\chi^{2}
statistic, but with the denominator replaced by the average of the observed and the expected. It was proposed as a measure for assessing SD by Voas and Williamson in 2001

The results so far suggest that the SD based on coarsened and adjusted margins performs the best of these three methods, although they are all rather similar. It is also the method that will allow the data for the margins used in the fitting to be released with minimal disclosure risk. Further results will be given for just this method alone.

The agreement between the GT and the SD was also assessed for threeway tables. No table gave an
SpMSE
value over 10, but a few tables had values between 5 and 10, most of which involved sex and age. Examining female heads of households in the GT revealed a different age structure by sex and marital status. To examine this for the SD synthetic ages would need to be created from the grouped values in the SD. This could easily be done by sampling from the ranges of the groups. If such analyses were of interest to those using the SD some threeway margins could be included in the IPF fit used to create the SD.

A final check on the SD is to use it to investigate how it might be used to fit a statistical model to predict an outcome. The outcome selected was whether a household employed any servants, predicted by logistic regression. Such modelling usually proceeds by first examining the univariate between candidate variables and the outcome (any servants). As expected from the results above the SD gave exactly the same picture as the GT. Then a series of models could be fitted to find one with the best explanatory power and helpful interpretation. None of the models explored showed a different picture for the SD compared to the GT.

A comparison between the fit for the SD and the GT is shown in Figure 3 for a model including sex, age group, marital status and numbers of rooms in the house. Houses with 5+ rooms (baseline was 1 room) were more likely to have servants, as were single people and widows. Households headed by men were more likely to have servants than those headed by women. The mean confidence interval overlap was 68%.
