Revisiting creeping viscoelastic cross-slot flow: Global linear stability and structural sensitivity analyses
Kun Zhang \aff 1, Zhanwen Wang \aff 1, Lailai Zhu \aff 1

Abstract
The viscoelastic instability of cross-slot flow was first observed experimentally almost half a century ago and reproduced numerically two decades ago, yet its physical origin remains unresolved. We revisit this problem for two-dimensional creeping flow of Oldroyd-B fluid by combining direct numerical simulations, global stability analysis, structural sensitivity analysis, and energy-budget analysis. Our simulations reproduce the canonical pitchfork bifurcation, and the stability analysis consistently predicts the threshold and perturbation growth rates. The leading eigenmode consists of a chiral velocity–stress perturbation that tilts and rotates the birefringent strand generated by the extensional flow. Structural sensitivity and energy-budget analyses identify narrow high-extension-rate ridges within the extensional flow as both the spatial core and energetic source of the instability. In these ridges, the stress-based wavemaker co-localizes with large positive disturbance polymeric stress power density, indicating localized transfer of stored elastic energy to the disturbance flow. Analyses of cross-slot variants with rounded corners and with a centered cylinder further reveal that neither sharp corners nor a free central stagnation point is the essential destabilizing ingredient; rather, the instability originates from elastic-energy release in extension-dominated regions characteristic of cross-slot flow.

Introduction

The addition of dilute polymers to a Newtonian solvent gives rise to a wide variety of complex flow phenomena
(
Datta et al. 2022
)
. The resulting non-Newtonian fluid typically possesses viscoelasticity, an essential rheological property underlying the emergence of diverse behaviors such as
rod-climbing (the Weissenberg effect)
(
Weissenberg 1947
;
Ruangkriengsin et al. 2025
)
, die swell
(
Tanner 2005
;
Tang et al. 2020
)
, turbulent drag reduction
(
White & Mungal 2008
;
Graham 2014
;
Choueiri et al. 2018
;
Wang et al. 2023
)
, and elastic
(
Groisman & Steinberg 2000
;
Steinberg 2021
;
Sasmal 2025
)
and elasto-inertial turbulence
(
Samanta et al. 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.
A particularly significant subset of these phenomena involves the alteration of hydrodynamic stability by polymer-induced stresses. Unlike Newtonian instabilities, which typically stem from inertial effects
(
Schmid & Henningson 2001
)
, polymeric flows are influenced by the interplay between inertia and fluid elasticity, leading to the emergence of elasto-inertial instabilities
(
Dubief et al. 2023
)
. Remarkably, these flows can also become unstable at vanishing Reynolds number via a pure elastic route
(
Shaqfeh 1996
)
. The ensuing elastic instability arises when the elasticity-indicating parameter, the Weissenberg (
Wi
) number (or Deborah number)—ratio of the polymer relaxation time to the characteristic flow convection time—exceeds a threshold.

Extensive studies have analyzed both elastic and elasto-inertial instabilities of viscoelastic flows in canonical settings: Taylor–Couette geometry
(
Muller et al. 1989
;
Larson et al. 1990
;
Shaqfeh et al. 1992
;
Graham 1998
;
Liu & Khomami 2013
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Beneitez et al. 2025
)
and parallel, rectilinear configurations including Kolmogorov
(
Boffetta et al. 2005
;
Bistagnino et al. 2007
;
Berti & Boffetta 2010
;
Lewy & Kerswell 2025
)
, planar Couette
(
Gorodtsov & Leonov 1967
;
Renardy 1992
;
Morozov & van Saarloos 2005
;
Binns & Wynn 2024
)
, planar Poiseuille
(
Grosch & Salwen 1968
;
Sureshkumar & Beris 1995
;
Wilson et al. 1999
;
Khalid et al. 2021
;
Lellep et al. 2021
)
, and pipe flows
(
Garg et al. 2018
;
Chaudhary et al. 2021
;
Dong & Zhang 2022
)
.
These geometric configurations admit analytical base-state solutions, which facilitate modal
(
Renardy & Renardy 1986
)

and non-modal
(
Jovanović & Kumar 2011
)
linear stability analyses, as well as weakly nonlinear analyses
(
Sánchez et al. 2022
)
.
These theoretical studies have provided deep insights into the mechanisms of center-mode instabilities
(
Garg et al. 2018
)
, the energy budgets of the eigenmodes
(
Sadanandan & Sureshkumar 2004
;
Hoda et al. 2008
;
Spyridakis et al. 2024
)
,
and the subcritical nature of the transition in shear-dominated parallel flows
(
Morozov & van Saarloos 2007
;
Garg et al. 2018
;
Buza et al. 2022
)
.

Despite extensive stability analyses of viscoelastic flows with analytical base states, far fewer efforts have addressed those without such base states—typically arising in
non-rectilinear geometries. By contrast, a surprisingly large body of experimental and numerical work has reported unstable viscoelastic flows in a broad range of complex geometric domains, including cross-slot
(
Arratia et al. 2006
;
Canossi et al. 2020
)
, pore–throat
(
De et al. 2017
;
Browne et al. 2020
;
Ekanem et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2021
)
, channels with a cylinder
(
Kenney et al. 2013
;
Peng et al. 2023
)

or cylinder arrays
(
Khomami & Moreno 1997
;
Grilli et al. 2013
;
Shi & Christopher 2016
)
, porous media
(
Walkama et al. 2020
;
Browne & Datta 2021
;
Haward et al. 2021
;
Kumar et al. 2022
)
, and so on.
This apparent gap between abundant experimental and numerical observations and limited theoretical understanding motivates systematic stability analyses of viscoelastic flows with and without inertia in complex geometries.

Such studies remain scarce, most likely owing to the notorious numerical difficulty of simulating viscoelastic flow, namely the so-called high Weissenberg number problem. To the best of our knowledge, and surprisingly, they have been restricted to
global linear stability analyses for
two-dimensional (2D) inertialess flows past a single cylinder
(
Sureshkumar et al. 1999
;
Smith et al. 2000
;
Spyridakis et al. 2024
)
or an array of cylinders
(
Sureshkumar et al. 1999
;
Smith et al. 2000
;
Smith et al. 2003
;
Sahin & Wilson 2008
)
in a channel.
Most of these works used the finite element method (FEM) for spatial discretization, whereas
Sahin & Wilson 2008
used a finite volume method.
These works identified distinct instability mechanisms.

Smith et al. 2000
established that flow of an Oldroyd-B fluid past a single cylinder remains linearly stable for all Weissenberg numbers accessible to their analysis,
while
Spyridakis et al. 2024
examining a Phan-Thien–Tanner fluid identified strain-rate thinning and elasticity as jointly important for the symmetry-breaking bifurcation. For periodic cylinder arrays, the dominant instability arises from perturbations to the shearing velocity-gradient component interacting with base-flow polymeric stresses
(
Smith et al. 2003
)
, or from elastic stresses in inter-cylinder recirculation regions producing spiral vortices
(
Sahin & Wilson 2008
)
.
In these configurations, flow instabilities are typically associated with strong shear or curved streamlines, leaving extension-dominated viscoelastic flows in complex geometries largely unexplored.

A representative example is the elastic-instability-induced symmetry breaking of low-Reynolds-number polymeric flows through a nominally symmetric cross-slot microfluidic device. As shown in figure
1
(a), it consists of a central square junction connected to four straight side channels arranged orthogonally. Fluid enters through the two opposing horizontal channels and is redirected towards the two vertical outlet branches, producing a stagnation point and
an extensional flow downstream of that point.
Creeping flow of Newtonian fluids in this device, as expected, preserves the geometric symmetry. By contrast, polymeric flow can break this symmetry through elastic instability: it remains symmetric at low strain rate (low
Wi
), transitions to a steady asymmetric state when
Wi
exceeds a critical value, and then becomes periodic or even chaotic through a secondary instability as the strain rate, and hence
Wi
, increases further. Notwithstanding being first observed nearly half a century ago
(
Gardner et al. 1982
)
and later rediscovered and attributed to elasticity instability by
Arratia et al. 2006
, the mechanism by which elasticity breaks symmetry remains elusive and controversial. Possible mechanisms include compressional flow upstream of the stagnation point
(
Poole et al. 2007
;
Rocha et al. 2009
)
, extensional flow downstream of it
(
Cruz et al. 2014
)
, geometrical singularity and strong shear associated with corners
(
Davoodi et al. 2019
;
Davoodi et al. 2021
)
,
and the interplay between fluid elasticity and shear-thinning
(
Yokokoji et al. 2024
)
.

Using the upper-convected Maxwell model,
Poole et al. 2007
first numerically reproduce steady symmetry breaking in cross-slot viscoelastic flow and attribute the instability to compressional flow upstream of the stagnation point. This view is later indirectly supported by
Rocha et al. 2009
,
who find for finite extendable nonlinear elastic fluids that small corner rounding barely affects the instability onset, thus excluding corner’s geometric singularity as the cause.

Cruz et al. 2014
attribute the steady symmetry breaking to the central extensional flow, which strongly stretches the polymers and generates a birefringent strand with substantial normal stresses and stress gradients.
The innovative optimized-shape cross-slot extensional rheometer (OSCER)
(
Alves 2008
;
Haward et al. 2012
)
eliminates sharp corners
and enables the generation of a nearly homogeneous extensional flow. Experiments
(
Haward & McKinley 2013
;
Haward et al. 2016
)
and simulations
(
Cruz & Alves 2018
)
in this configuration nevertheless still reported steady asymmetric flows via instability, thereby excluding corner singularity or near-corner shear as the essential instability driver.
Combining experiments and simulations,
Davoodi et al. 2019
argued that this instability is driven by high streamline curvature and shear rates near corners upon showing the peak values of the Pakdel–McKinley parameter therein. Their placement of a small cylinder at the cross center indicated minimal impact on the instability, thus excluding the stagnation point as the most critical destabilizing mechanism. The recent work by
Yokokoji et al. 2024
, combining experiments and simulations, demonstrated that the instability arises from a critical coupling between extensional elasticity at the stagnation point and shear-thinning near boundaries.
While fluid elasticity triggers the instability, shear-thinning is essential for locking the flow into a steady asymmetric state once the instability occurs.

While these numerical and experimental studies phenomenologically characterize how instability depends on various aspects of cross-slot flow, they have also led to diverse and sometimes conflicting mechanistic interpretations. A first-principles theoretical prediction of the instability onset and mode structure remains unavailable
(
Davoodi et al. 2021
)
, leaving the physical origin of symmetry breaking unresolved nearly half a century after the first experiment
(
Gardner et al. 1982
)
. This difficulty has been recognized as an open problem in non-Newtonian fluid mechanics
(
Wilson 2012
;
Cruz et al. 2014
)
. Indeed, even for this 2D inertialess flow,
Xi & Graham 2009
described linear stability analysis as “an exceedingly demanding task”.

In this work, we address this long-standing challenge by integrating global linear stability analysis (LSA), structural sensitivity and energy-budget analyses, and direct numerical simulations (DNS) for a creeping flow of Oldroyd-B fluid in a standard 2D cross-slot channel.
Through these analyses, we identify the physical mechanism underlying the first symmetry-breaking instability of the cross-slot flow.
The paper is organized as follows. We first describe the problem setup and numerical methods in §
2
and §
3
, respectively. After numerically reproducing the classical instability and bifurcation, we present the main results in §
5
, including the direct eigenmodes from LSA, the adjoint eigenmodes and wavemaker region from structural sensitivity analysis, and the energy budget. Finally, we summarize our findings and discuss their implications in §
6
.

2
Problem Formulation

We consider a canonical, 2D cross-slot flow of polymeric fluids at vanishing Reynolds number. As shown in figure
1
,
the flow domain comprises a square block of size
\tilde{D}
connected to four side channels of length
10\tilde{D}
; from hereafter,
\tilde{\left(\bcdot\right)}
denotes dimensional variables.
Fluid enters the slot through the two horizontal channels, each at an average velocity of
\tilde{U}
, and exits from the two vertical branches. We address the polymeric flow using the incompressible Stokes equations coupled with the diffusive Oldroyd-B constitutive law,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bnabla}\bcdot\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}=0,

(1a)

\displaystyle-\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{p}+\mu_{s}\tilde{\bnabla}\bcdot\left[\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\left(\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\tilde{\bnabla}\bcdot\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}={\boldsymbol{0}},

(1b)

where
\mu_{s}
denotes the solvent viscosity out of the total viscosity
\mu
,
\tilde{p}
and
\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
represent the pressure and fluid velocity, respectively. The polymeric stress tensor
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
satisfies

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+\lambda\overset{\triangledown}{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}=\mu_{p}\left[\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\left(\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\tilde{\epsilon}\tilde{\nabla}^{2}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},

(2)

where
\mu_{p}=\mu-\mu_{s}
is the polymer viscosity,
\lambda
is the relaxation time of polymers,
\tilde{\epsilon}
is the polymer-stress diffusivity, and
\overset{\triangledown}{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}
is the upper-convective derivative of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
,

\overset{\triangledown}{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}{\partial\tilde{t}}+\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}-\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\bcdot\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}-(\tilde{\bnabla}\tilde{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}})^{{\rm T}}\bcdot\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},

(3)

where
\tilde{t}
denotes time.

By choosing
\tilde{D}
,
\tilde{U}
,
\tilde{D}/\tilde{U}
,
and
\mu\tilde{U}/\tilde{D}
as the characteristic scales of length, velocity, time, and pressure/stress, respectively, we derive the dimensionless governing equations:

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4a)

\displaystyle-\bnabla p+\beta\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{0}},

(4b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+Wi\left[\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\partial t}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}-\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\beta)\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\epsilon\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}},

(4c)

where
\beta=\mu_{s}/\mu

At both inlets, we impose the analytical velocity and polymeric stress profiles of a fully developed channel flow of Oldroyd-B fluids

At the outlets, zero pressure, zero streamwise velocity gradient, and zero transverse velocity component are prescribed. No-slip boundary conditions are applied at all walls. When polymer-stress diffusion is present (

5
Global Linear Stability, Structural Sensitivity, and Energy–Budget Analyses

5.1
Global linear stability analysis

Having revealed the DNS results, we conduct global LSA to better understand the symmetry-breaking instability.
As commonly done, we decompose the state variable
{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=[p,{{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathsfbi{G},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
into a steady base state
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and an infinitesimal perturbation
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
,

{\boldsymbol{q}}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)={\boldsymbol{q}}^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}})+{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}},t),

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}
is the spatial position vector and
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{b}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is obtained by solving the steady-state governing equations via the Newton–Raphson method, see Appendix
8
for more details.
We linearize the DEVSS-G system (
5
) about the base state and seek perturbations in the normal-mode form

{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=e^{\sigma t}\hat{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(7)

where
\sigma

is the complex eigenvalue,
\hat{{\boldsymbol{q}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=[\hat{p},\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}},\hat{\mathsfbi{G}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}]({\boldsymbol{x}})
is the corresponding eigenmode, and
\Real(\sigma)
and
\Imag(\sigma)
denote its temporal growth rate and oscillation frequency, respectively. From hereafter, we refer to
\hat{{\boldsymbol{q}}}
as the direct eigenmode, in contrast to the adjoint eigenmode introduced later in §
5.2
. Substituting (
7
) into the linearized perturbation equations yields the direct global stability eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}=0,

(8a)

\displaystyle-\bnabla\hat{p}+\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\left(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+(\beta-1)\bnabla\bcdot\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}+\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\right)+\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}={\boldsymbol{0}},

(8b)

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+Wi\overset{\square}{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}=\left(1-\beta\right)\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}+\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\right)+\epsilon\nabla^{2}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},

(8c)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}=\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}},

(8d)

where
\overset{\square}{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}
is the linearized upper-convective derivative of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
,

\overset{\square}{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}}=\sigma\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{b}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}-\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}.

(9)

The system (
8
) is subject to the following boundary conditions: zero velocity and stress perturbations at the inlets, vanishing pressure and transverse velocity perturbations at the outlets, and zero velocity perturbation at all
walls. Derivation of the eigenvalue problem (
8
) and its numerical implementation are provided in Appendix
8
.

Using FreeFem++, we solve the discretized form of equation (
8
) for the complex eigenvalues
\sigma
and the corresponding eigenmodes. Each eigenmode is normalized such that the associated eigenvector of the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem (
39
) has unit
\ell^{2}
-norm.
We then assess the LSA prediction by comparing the real part
\sigma_{r}=\Real(\sigma)
of
the leading eigenvalue
\sigma
with the temporal growth rate extracted from DNS.
To achieve so, we monitor the time evolution of the
x
-velocity-component,
u_{A}
, at a probe

A:(x,y)=(0,1)
placed downstream of the stagnation point [see figure
2
(b)]; the magnitude
|u_{A}|
exhibits an initial exponential growth before saturating at a steady value
|u^{ss}_{A}|
.
Following the Landau amplitude equation
(
Landau 1944
;
Stuart 1960
;
Drazin & Reid 2004
)
, we fit the DNS data through

u_{A}(t)=\frac{u_{A}^{ss}}{\sqrt{1+e^{-2\sigma_{r}(t-t_{h})}}},

(10)

treating
\sigma_{r}
and the half-saturation time
t_{h}
when
u_{A}=u_{A}^{ss}/\sqrt{2}
as fitting parameters. The temporal signals
|u_{A}|(t)
and their corresponding fitting curves for four unstable
Wi
values are depicted in figure
4
(a). Its inset further indicates that the data collapse under the rescaling

\frac{u_{A}(T)}{u_{A}^{ss}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+e^{-2T}}},

(11)

with
T=\sigma_{r}(t-t_{h})

5.2
Structural sensitivity analysis

While the eigenmodes reveal where the instability is mostly active and hence provide useful insight into the instability mechanism,
they cannot pinpoint which regions of the flow are most receptive to external forcing or structural modifications. This complementary information is inherently encoded in the adjoint eigenmode
\hat{{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\left[\hat{p}^{\dagger},\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger},\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}\right]({\boldsymbol{x}})
.
As detailed in Appendix
9
, we derive the adjoint linearized equation (
57
) for the adjoint state variable
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=\left[p^{\dagger},{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger},\mathsfbi{G}^{\dagger},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\dagger}\right]({\boldsymbol{x}},t)
by adapting the framework of
Kim 2023
, originally developed for drag-sensitivity analysis in viscoelastic flow past a cylinder.
Substituting the normal-mode form
{\boldsymbol{q}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}},t)=e^{-\sigma^{\dagger}t}\hat{{\boldsymbol{q}}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}})
into equation (
57
) yields the adjoint eigenvalue problem:

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}=0,

(13a)

\displaystyle\bnabla\cdot\left[\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}+\left(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+(\beta-1)\bnabla\bcdot\left[\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger}+\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\bnabla\hat{p}^{\dagger}+Wi\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}{\boldsymbol{:}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quad+2Wi\bnabla\bcdot\left({\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}\right)+2\left(1-\beta\right)\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}={\boldsymbol{0}},

(13b)

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}+Wi\left[\sigma^{\dagger}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}-{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}-\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}\bcdot\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger}+\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\epsilon\nabla^{2}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}={\boldsymbol{0}},

(13c)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{\dagger}=\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}.

(13d)

The direct and adjoint eigenmodes exhibit markedly different spatial structures, so either mode alone provides limited insight into the instability mechanism. Following
Giannetti & Luchini 2007
;
Pralits et al. 2010

\displaystyle\eta_{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\left|\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|\left|\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|}{\left|\int_{\Omega}\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger*}\,\differential\Omega\right|},

(14)

which measures the spatial overlap between the direct velocity eigenmode
\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
and the adjoint velocity eigenmode
\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}^{\dagger}
. Here,
\Omega
denotes the computational domain, and
*
denotes complex conjugation. The classical structural sensitivity analysis, originally developed for inertia-induced instabilities in Newtonian flows, identifies the region where the leading eigenvalue is most sensitive to localized velocity feedback—equivalently—where both the direct and adjoint velocity eigenmodes are large. This region is referred to as the wavemaker often interpreted as the core or origin of instability
(
Giannetti & Luchini 2007
;
Luchini & Bottaro 2014
;
Schmid & Brandt 2014
)
, since it marks where the global mode has both strong local amplitude and high receptivity to feedback.
For the viscoelastic flow instability considered here, the relevant perturbation dynamics involve not only velocity perturbations but also polymeric stress perturbations. Hence, we introduce an analogous stress structural sensitivity field,

\displaystyle\eta_{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{\left|\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|\left|\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger}({\boldsymbol{x}})\right|}{\left|\int_{\Omega}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}^{\dagger*}\,\differential\Omega\right|}.

(15)

5.3
Energy-budget analysis

To identify the energetic origins of the symmetry-breaking instability
and further substantiate its physical mechanism, we perform a perturbation energy-budget analysis following
Sadanandan & Sureshkumar 2004
;
Spyridakis et al. 2024
.
By integrating over the domain
\Omega
, the inner product of the linearized momentum equation with the perturbation velocity
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}
, we derive the evolution equation for the perturbation energy
E
associated with polymeric stress as detailed in Appendix
10
:

E=\frac{Wi}{2}\int_{\Omega}{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\differential\Omega.

(16)

Strictly speaking,
E
is the perturbation energy per unit
out-of-plane length, but is referred to simply as the perturbation energy for brevity throughout this 2D study.
Notably, its rate of change
{\rm d}E/{\rm d}t
quantifies whether
perturbations are amplified or attenuated: positive rates indicate temporal growth of the perturbation energy and therefore instability.
To gain a deeper understanding, we further decompose this instability indicator into four energy-budget terms,

\frac{{\rm d}E}{{\rm d}t}=\phi_{p}+\phi_{vis}+\phi_{\epsilon}+\phi_{coup},

(17)

with each representing the rate of energy production/dissipation rooted in a specific physical mechanism.
In particular,
\phi_{p}
is the rate of work done by the perturbation pressure,
\phi_{vis}
represents the viscous dissipation rate that is consistently negative, while
\phi_{\epsilon}
accounts for the energy contribution due to polymer-stress diffusion, with their expressions provided in Appendix
10
.
The remaining term,
\phi_{coup}
, standing for the rate of energy exchange arising from the coupling between the base state and the perturbation,
is defined as

\phi_{coup}=-Wi\int_{\Omega}{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\prime}\differential\Omega,

(18)

where

{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{\prime}={{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{b}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}-\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}.

(19)

Figure
10
(a) shows the
perturbation-energy growth rate
\differential E/\differential t
and its four energy-budget terms versus
Wi
, where we have normalized all terms by
|\phi_{vis}|
such that
\phi_{vis}=-1
by construction.
The net growth rate
{\rm d}E/{\rm d}t
becomes positive from negative when
Wi>0.36
,
consistent with the critical Weissenberg number of the bifurcation identified earlier (see figure
3
). Among all the energy-budget terms,
\phi_{coup}
is the dominant positive one, and therefore, represents the major energy source.

). (b) Distribution of the polymeric power density,

. Positive values indicate the energetic source that feeds the instability.

To investigate the spatial distribution of the energy production rate, we first rewrite the integrand on the right-hand side of equation (
16
) as

{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}=\gradient\bcdot\left({\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)-\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\gradient{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+(\gradient{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime})^{{\rm T}}\right].

(20)

Using the divergence theorem, we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\gradient\bcdot\left({\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)\differential\Omega=\int_{\partial\Omega}{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\differential\Gamma=0,

(21)

since
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
at all boundaries (as discussed in Appendix
10.1
).
Hence, this integral makes no net contribution to the perturbation energy growth. Then, we focus on the linear growth stage, when the perturbations
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}
and
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}
can be represented by the leading normal mode,

{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\sim e^{\sigma_{r}t}\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}},\quad{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\sim e^{\sigma_{r}t}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},

(22)

where
\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
represent the leading direct eigenmodes of the velocity and polymeric stress, respectively. Substituting into the perturbation energy and calculating its derivative leads to

\frac{\differential E}{\differential t}\sim\sigma_{r}e^{2\sigma_{r}t}Wi\,\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{P}\differential\Omega,

(23)

where

\mathcal{P}=-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\,{\boldsymbol{:}}\,\frac{\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\left(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}}{2}

(24)

denotes the perturbation polymeric power density (per unit volume), which characterizes the local rate of energy exchange between disturbance kinetic energy density (ED) and polymer elastic ED.
With this sign convention,
\mathcal{P}
represents the rate at which the disturbance polymeric stress performs work on the disturbance flow. Thus, negative
\mathcal{P}
corresponds to transfer from the kinetic ED of the disturbance fluid motion to the elastic ED stored in deformed polymer chains; whereas positive
\mathcal{P}
indicates the release of stored elastic ED back into the disturbance kinetic ED, which destabilizes the flow.

In the Newtonian limit,
Wi\to 0
, the perturbation polymeric stress becomes proportional to, and aligned with, the perturbation strain-rate tensor,

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\to(1-\beta)\left[\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}})^{{\rm T}}\right]
. Consequently,
\mathcal{P}
is proportional to the negative of the disturbance viscous-dissipation density and is therefore non-positive throughout the domain, becoming strictly negative wherever the perturbation strain rate is nonzero. At larger
Wi
, the finite polymer relaxation time can introduce a local misalignment, or phase shift, between
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
and
\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}})^{{\rm T}}
. Their inner product may then become negative in localized regions, corresponding to
\mathcal{P}>0
, where stored elastic energy is released back to the disturbance flow and thus promotes instability.

As depicted in figure
10

\mathcal{P}
attains its largest positive values along a vertical ridge
aligning with the central zone of the extensional flows downstream of the stagnation point.
This region marks the energetic source of the instability. Importantly, this ridge closely matches the stress-based wavemaker shown in figure
9
(b), hence confirming the spatial origin of the elastic instability.

6
Conclusion and Discussion

In this work, we revisit the stability of two-dimensional creeping viscoelastic flow in a cross-slot channel, to clarify the mechanism underlying its first, steady symmetry-breaking instability. Although this instability was first observed experimentally nearly half a century ago
(
Gardner et al. 1982
)
and reproduced numerically nearly two decades ago
(
Poole et al. 2007
)
, its physical origin remains debated. To address this long-standing issue, we study inertialess cross-slot flow of a diffusive Oldroyd-B fluid by combining DNS, global LSA, structural sensitivity analysis, and energy-budget analysis, all implemented within the FEM library FreeFem++.

Our DNS captures the canonical symmetric-to-asymmetric flow transition through a pitchfork bifurcation at
Wi_{cr}\approx 0.36
. The LSA gives a consistent prediction of the critical Weissenberg number and of the temporal growth rates obtained from DNS. Moreover, the base state used in LSA and the leading direct eigenmode are recovered by the dominant zero-frequency and second DMD modes, respectively. These results collectively provide a stringent validation of our LSA implementation.

The leading velocity eigenmode from the LSA forms a centered, diamond-shaped vortical structure with closed streamlines, while the corresponding normal-polymeric-stress eigenmode exhibits antisymmetric parity. Together, they constitute a chiral linear perturbation that drives spontaneous symmetry breaking in the viscoelastic cross-slot flow by tilting and rotating the birefringent strand generated by the vertically oriented extensional flows. This mechanistic picture is further highlighted by visualizing the perturbation conformation tensor via spectral decomposition.

Beyond DNS and LSA, structural sensitivity analysis reveals a stress-based wavemaker region that marks the spatial core of the instability. This region, where the flow is highly sensitive to localized polymeric-stress perturbations, lies with the extensional-flow core and comprises vertical filaments arranged symmetrically about the stagnation point. This spatial configuration coincides with regions of strong perturbation conformation, naturally linking the wavemaker to the polymer-stretching/compression response.

We examine the growth of perturbation energy through a decomposition into four energy-budget contributions, each rooted in a distinct physical mechanism. The coupling between the base state and the perturbation fields is found to provide the dominant contribution.
It can be further characterized by the disturbance polymeric power density
\mathcal{P}
measuring the rate at which the disturbance polymeric stress performs work on the disturbance flow. Notably, positive
\mathcal{P}
is most pronounced in a vertical ridge along the centerline extensional flows downstream of the stagnation point, identifying the energetic source of the elastic instability. Its spatial overlap with the stress-based wavemaker further establishes this narrow, extension-dominated zone as the instability core.
We further test this insight by studying two geometric variants of the baseline setting: a cross-slot channel with rounded corners and another containing a centered cylinder eliminating the stagnation point.
In both cases, peak power density
\mathcal{P}
appears along vertical ridges aligned with the central
high-extension-rate zones, reinforcing the proposed instability mechanism.

These findings help reconcile the debate over whether the instability originates from sharp corners or from the
stagnation point. Studies on the OSCER variant of the cross-slot channel
(
Haward & McKinley 2013
;
Haward et al. 2016
;
Cruz & Alves 2018
)
suggest that sharp corners are not necessary for the instability, whereas the cylinder-inserted, stagnation-point-free setup investigated in
Davoodi et al. 2019
points instead to corner-induced streamline curvature and high deformation rates. We find that neither the corner nor the free central stagnation point
is the essential ingredient; rather, the spatial core of the instability lies in narrow high-extension-rate regions within the extensional flows. This explanation is more consistent with the instability mechanism of “large normal stresses and gradients in the birefringence strand” in extensional flow proposed by
Cruz et al. 2014
. As a side note, we find no evidence that upstream compressional-flow disturbances are relevant to the instability onset, contrary to earlier hypotheses.

In summary, our results offer insights into the long-standing
controversy over the physical origin of the creeping viscoelastic-flow instability in the cross-slot channel. More broadly, this study demonstrates the potential of integrating global stability, structural sensitivity, and energy-budget analyses to understand instability in viscoelastic flows through complex geometries for which analytical base states are unavailable. Future work will extend the current framework to systematically investigate related viscoelastic flow instabilities involving other ingredients, including 3D perturbations and flows, other complex geometries, finite inertia, realistic rheological models, unsteady base states, capillary interfaces, and deformable structures.

\breve{(\bcdot)}
to denote the test functions for the corresponding variables.
Multiplying the momentum equation (
5b
) by the velocity test function
\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}=\left(\breve{u},\breve{v}\right)
,
integrating over the domain
\Omega
, and applying integration by parts to the pressure and viscous-stress terms gives:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\{-p\bnabla\bcdot\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\bnabla\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left[\left(1-\beta\right)\bnabla\bcdot\left(\mathsfbi{G}+\mathsfbi{G}^{\rm T}\right)\right]\right\}\differential\Omega

\displaystyle+\int_{\partial\Omega}\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left\{{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left[p\mathsfbi{I}-[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{\rm T}]\right]\right\}\differential\Gamma=0,

(25)

where
\Omega
denotes the computational domain,
\partial\Omega
denotes its boundary,

\differential\Omega
is the area element in
\Omega
,
\differential\Gamma
is the line element on
\partial\Omega
,
and
{\boldsymbol{n}}=(n_{x},n_{y})
denotes the unit normal on
\partial\Omega
, pointing from the fluid toward the exterior.

The boundary term vanishes on the walls and inlet, where Dirichlet conditions are imposed on the velocity and the corresponding velocity test function is zero.
We consider a fully developed flow for the outlet boundary. For the upper outlet in the cross-slot,
u=0,p=0
and
\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0
. Then the integral on the outlet boundary becomes:

\int_{\partial\Omega}\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left\{{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left[p\mathsfbi{I}-[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{\rm T}]\right]\right\}\differential\Gamma=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left\{{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{\rm T}\right]\right\}\differential\Gamma.

(26)

For the upper outlet boundary featuring
n_{x}=0,n_{y}=1
,
we apply the boundary condition
u=0
and hence its associated test function

\breve{u}=0
.
Then the boundary term there vanishes:

-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left\{{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{\rm T}\right]\right\}=-\breve{u}_{\kappa}n_{\zeta}\left(\frac{\partial u_{\kappa}}{\partial x_{\zeta}}+\frac{\partial u_{\zeta}}{\partial x_{\kappa}}\right)=-2\breve{v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial y}=0.

(27)

So as it does at the lower outlet.

The weak form of the velocity gradient approximation
\mathsfbi{G}=\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}
is

\int_{\Omega}\breve{\mathsfbi{G}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left(\mathsfbi{G}-\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)\differential\Omega=0.

(28)

The weak form of the continuity equation is

\int_{\Omega}{\breve{p}}\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\differential\Omega=0.

(29)

The weak form of the constitutive equation is

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+Wi\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\partial t}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}-\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)+\left(\beta-1\right)\left(\mathsfbi{G}+\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\right)\right]+\epsilon\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\differential\Omega

\displaystyle\quad-\epsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left(\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right)\,\differential\Gamma=0.

(30)

We first note that the boundary integral involving stress diffusion in equation (
7
)
vanishes on each part of
\partial\Omega
.
At the walls, we have set
\epsilon=0
following
Beneitez et al. 2025
,
leading to zero integral.
At the inlets,
we impose the analytical polymeric stress profile as Dirichlet boundary conditions, so the test function
\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, again rendering the integral zero.
At the outlets, the natural boundary condition of the weak formulation implicitly enforces
{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, so that

-\epsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}{\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\left(\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right)\,\mathrm{d}A=-\epsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}\left({\boldsymbol{n}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right){\boldsymbol{:}}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\,\mathrm{d}A=0.

As the boundary contribution vanishes entirely, we obtain the following weak form of the constitutive equation:

\int_{\Omega}\left\{\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+Wi\left(\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\partial t}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}-\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)+\left(\beta-1\right)\left(\mathsfbi{G}+\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\right)\right]+\epsilon\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}:\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right\}\differential\Omega=0.

(31)

For unsteady DNS, we advance the solution from time level
t_{n}
at step
n
to
t_{n+1}
at step
n+1
, where
\Delta t=t_{n+1}-t_{n}
denotes the step size. We employ a decoupled time-stepping scheme consisting of two substeps.
In the first substep, given the stress field
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}
at time level
t_{n}
, we solve a coupled system comprising the continuity equation, the momentum equation, and the auxiliary equation for the velocity-gradient tensor to obtain
p_{n+1}
,
{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n+1}
, and
\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Big\{-p_{n+1}\bnabla\bcdot\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\bnabla\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n+1}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n+1}\right)^{\rm T}\right]-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}+\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left[\left(1-\beta\right)\bnabla\bcdot\left(\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}+\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}^{\rm T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-{\breve{p}}\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n+1}+\breve{\mathsfbi{G}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left(\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}-\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n+1}\right)\Big\}\differential\Omega=0.

(32)

In the second substep, the constitutive equation is solved to update
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}
by a characteristic-Galerkin method
(
Pironneau 1982
;
Machmoum & Esselaoui 2001
;
Lukáčová-Medvid’ová et al. 2016
)
, namely,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\{\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}+Wi\left(\frac{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}\circ{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t_{n})}{\Delta t}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}-\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad+\left(\beta-1\right)\left(\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}+\mathsfbi{G}_{n+1}^{{\rm T}}\right)\right]+\epsilon\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n+1}\Bigr\}\differential\Omega=0,

(33)

where
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}
denotes the trajectory (or characteristics) of a fluid particle.

{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})
is obtained by solving

\begin{cases}\frac{\differential}{\differential t}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}\left(t,{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)={{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n}\bigl({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t,{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr),\quad t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1}],\\
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}\left(t_{n+1},{\boldsymbol{x}}\right)={\boldsymbol{x}}.\end{cases}

(34)

Approximating this trajectory by a first-order scheme gives the upwind point
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}})\approx{\boldsymbol{x}}-{{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{n}({\boldsymbol{x}})\,\Delta t
.
Thus the composition term is written as
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}\circ{\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{n}\bigl({\boldsymbol{X}}_{n}(t_{n},{\boldsymbol{x}})\bigr)
, which is the previous-step stress evaluated at the upwind point.

Finally, we spatially discretize the weak forms using the
finite-element spaces introduced in
§
3
: triangular Taylor–Hood elements (
P_{2}
–
P_{1}
) for the velocity–pressure pair and continuous piecewise-linear elements (
P_{1}
) for
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and
\mathsfbi{G}
. We then assemble and solve the resulting discrete systems in FreeFem++.
Within this discrete setting, we further stabilize the stress update with an SUPG scheme
(
Baaijens 1998
)
, replacing the stress test function
\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}
by

\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}_{SUPG}=\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+\alpha\,{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}},

(35)

where
\alpha=c_{s}h/|{{\boldsymbol{u}}}|
,
h
is the characteristic element size, and
c_{s}=0.3
is a tunable constant.

8
Global LSA: derivation and implementation

Here, we detail the derivation for the global LSA and describe the associated numerical implementation.
We begin with the base state, whose governing equations are obtained by dropping the temporal derivative of equation (
5
):

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}=0,

(36a)

\displaystyle\bnabla p^{b}-\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}+\left(1-\beta\right)\bnabla\bcdot\left[\mathsfbi{G}^{b}+\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]={\boldsymbol{0}},

(36b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}+Wi\left[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{b}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\right]=\left(1-\beta\right)\left[\mathsfbi{G}^{b}+\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\epsilon\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b},

(36c)

\displaystyle\mathsfbi{G}^{b}=\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}.

(36d)

Subtracting equation (
36
) from equation (
5
), we obtain the perturbation equation:

\displaystyle\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}=0,

(37a)

\displaystyle\bnabla p^{\prime}-\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}+\left(1-\beta\right)\bnabla\bcdot\left[\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}+\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]={\boldsymbol{0}},

(37b)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}+Wi\left[\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}{\partial t}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{b}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\left(1-\beta\right)\left[\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}+\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\epsilon\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime},

(37c)

\displaystyle\mathsfbi{G}^{\prime}=\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}.

(37d)

Substituting the normal-mode form (
7
) into equation (
37
),
we obtain the direct global stability eigenvalue problem (
8
).
The corresponding coupled weak formulation is

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\sigma\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\differential\Omega=\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\{-\hat{p}\bnabla\bcdot\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\bnabla\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\left(\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+\left(1-\beta\right)\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}+\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\right)-{\breve{p}}\bnabla\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}

\displaystyle+\breve{\mathsfbi{G}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}-\bnabla\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\right)+\frac{1}{Wi}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[(1-\beta)\left(\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}+\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\right)-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\right]-\frac{\epsilon}{Wi}\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\bnabla\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}

\displaystyle-\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}+\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{b}-\left(\mathsfbi{G}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}-\hat{\mathsfbi{G}}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\right]\Bigl\}\differential\Omega.

(38)

Upon finite-element discretizations,
we obtain the generalized matrix eigenvalue problem

\mathsfbi{A}{\boldsymbol{z}}=\sigma\mathsfbi{B}{\boldsymbol{z}},

(39)

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is the discrete eigenvector,

\mathsfbi{A}
is the stiffness matrix assembled from the terms on the right hand side of equation (
8
),
and
\mathsfbi{B}
is the mass matrix arising from the term
\int_{\Omega}\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\differential\Omega
in the weak form.
To solve the generalized eigenvalue problem (
39
), we apply the shift-invert Arnoldi method that computes a limited set of eigenvalues near a specified shift value
\sigma_{shift}
, using the recurrence relation

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k}=\left(\mathsfbi{A}-\sigma_{shift}\mathsfbi{B}\right)^{-1}\mathsfbi{B}\,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k-1}
, where
k=1,2,\ldots
denotes the iteration count.
We set
\sigma_{shift}=0

to target eigenvalues near the origin of the complex plane.
We solve the eigenvalue problem in the framework of FreeFem++
(
Hecht 2012
)
through its interface to the Scalable Library for Eigenvalue Problem Computations (SLEPc)
(
Hernandez et al. 2005
)
.
The resulting eigenvector
{\boldsymbol{z}}
is normalized by its
\ell^{2}
-norm, resulting in the direct eigenmodes presented in the main text.

We solve the steady-state nonlinear system
F\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b},p^{b},\mathsfbi{G}^{b},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\right)=0
for the base state using Newton–Raphson method.
The nonlinear residual
F
is defined as

\displaystyle F\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}},p,\mathsfbi{G},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\{-p\bnabla\bcdot\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\bnabla\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\left(1-\beta\right)\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left(\bnabla\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}+\bnabla\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}^{\rm T}\right)

\displaystyle-{\breve{p}}\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\breve{\mathsfbi{G}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left(\mathsfbi{G}-\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)

\displaystyle+\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+Wi\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}-\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)+\left(\beta-1\right)\left(\mathsfbi{G}+\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\right)\right]+\epsilon\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\Bigl\}\differential\Omega,

(40)

and the Jacobian
\delta F
is

\displaystyle\delta F\left(\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}},\delta p,\delta\mathsfbi{G},\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\{-\delta p\bnabla\bcdot\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}+\bnabla\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\bnabla\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{\rm T}\right]-\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\bnabla\bcdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+\left(1-\beta\right)\breve{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}\bcdot\left[\bnabla\bcdot\delta\mathsfbi{G}+\bnabla\bcdot\left(\delta\mathsfbi{G}\right)^{\rm T}\right]

\displaystyle-{\breve{p}}\bnabla\bcdot\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\breve{\mathsfbi{G}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left(\delta\mathsfbi{G}-\bnabla\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)+\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle+Wi\,\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\delta{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\mathsfbi{G}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\delta\mathsfbi{G}-\left(\delta\mathsfbi{G}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\mathsfbi{G}^{{\rm T}}\bcdot\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]

\displaystyle+\left(\beta-1\right)\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\left[\delta\mathsfbi{G}+\left(\delta\mathsfbi{G}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\epsilon\bnabla\breve{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\boldsymbol{:}}\bnabla\delta{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\Bigl\}\differential\Omega.

(41)

Discretizing on the same finite-element spaces as in Appendix
7
, equations (
40
)–(
41
) yield the algebraic residual system and its Jacobian, which we solve by Newton iterations in FreeFem++
(
Hecht 2012
)
through the Scalable Nonlinear Equations Solvers (SNES) module of PETSc
(
PETSc Development Team 2026
)
.
At each SNES iteration, the Jacobian
\delta F
in equation (
41
) is assembled and passed to a preconditioned Krylov linear solver, and a
line-search procedure is applied to ensure convergence. The Newton iteration is terminated according to the default convergence criteria of PETSc’s SNES module, namely, the relative residual tolerance is less than
10^{-8}
.
The initial guess can be seeded from DNS results.
To compute high-
Wi
base states, we employ a continuation strategy in which the converged solution at a lower
Wi
provides the initial guess at the next
Wi
, thereby facilitating convergence.

8.1
Dynamic mode decomposition (DMD)

To characterize the instability directly from the simulation data, we apply DMD
(
Schmid 2010
)
to the early-time transient of the DNS.
DMD is a data-driven, equation-free technique that decomposes a sequence of flow snapshots into spatial modes, each evolving at a single complex rate
(
Rowley et al. 2009
)
.
The temporal growth rate extracted purely from the data can provide an independent benchmark
(
Grilli et al. 2013
)
for the
LSA predictions.

Specifically, we collect
k+1
equally spaced snapshots of the state variable
{\boldsymbol{q}}=[p,{{\boldsymbol{u}}},\mathsfbi{G},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}]
during the initial exponential growth.
Each snapshot is flattened into the discretized state vector
\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, sampled at
t^{s}_{i}=t^{s}_{1}+(i-1)\Delta t^{s}
(the superscript
s
denotes sampling), where
m
is the total number of degrees of freedom and
\Delta t^{s}
is the sampling interval.
The snapshots are arranged into two time-shifted data matrices

\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{1}&\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{2}&\cdots&\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{k}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}=\begin{bmatrix}\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{2}&\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{3}&\cdots&\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{k+1}\end{bmatrix},

(42)

so that the columns of
\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}
are those of
\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}
advanced by one sampling interval
\Delta t^{s}
.
DMD assumes that a linear operator
\mathsfbi{K}
approximately maps each snapshot to the next,
\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{i+1}\approx\mathsfbi{K}\,\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{i}
, or

\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}\approx\mathsfbi{K}\,\check{\mathsfbi{Q}},\qquad\mathsfbi{K}=\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}\,\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}^{+},

(43)

where
\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}^{+}
is the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse.

Because
m
is large,
\mathsfbi{K}
is not formed explicitly.
Instead, using the rank-
l
truncated singular value decomposition
\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}\approx\mathsfbi{U}\,{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\,\mathsfbi{V}^{{\rm T}}
, with
\mathsfbi{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times l}
,
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times l}
and
\mathsfbi{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times l}
,

\mathsfbi{K}
is represented in the rank-
l
proper orthogonal decomposition subspace:

\tilde{\mathsfbi{K}}=\mathsfbi{U}^{{\rm T}}\,\mathsfbi{K}\,\mathsfbi{U}=\mathsfbi{U}^{{\rm T}}\,\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}\,\mathsfbi{V}\,{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}.

(44)

We then solve the eigenvalue problem for
\tilde{\mathsfbi{K}}
,

\tilde{\mathsfbi{K}}\,\mathsfbi{W}=\mathsfbi{W}\,{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},

(45)

to obtain the eigenvector matrix
\mathsfbi{W}=[{\boldsymbol{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{l}]
and the diagonal matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}=diag(\Lambda_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{l})
of discrete-time DMD eigenvalues.
Next, we construct the matrix of exact-DMD modes as

{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}=\check{\mathsfbi{Q}}_{s}\,\mathsfbi{V}\,{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}\,\mathsfbi{W},

(46)

where
{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}=[{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{l}]
is the matrix whose columns are DMD eigenvectors
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j}
.
Introducing the matrix of continuous-time DMD eigenvalues
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{DMD}=diag(\omega^{DMD-1},\ldots,\omega^{DMD-l})
with

{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{DMD}=\frac{\ln{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\Delta t^{s}},

(47)

the state is reconstructed as

\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}(t)\approx{\boldsymbol{\Xi}}\,e^{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{DMD}(t-t^{s}_{1})}\check{{\boldsymbol{b}}},

(48)

where
\check{{\boldsymbol{b}}}={\boldsymbol{\Xi}}^{+}\check{{\boldsymbol{q}}}_{1}
is the vector of DMD mode amplitudes obtained by a least-squares fit to the initial condition.

We use the exact-DMD implementation of the open-source PyDMD package
(
Ichinaga et al. 2024
)
to compute the DMD modes and their eigenvalues.
The real and imaginary parts of the continuous-time eigenvalue
\omega^{DMD-j}
of the
j
th mode indicate the temporal growth rate and angular frequency, respectively.
The comparison with the LSA eigenmodes and eigenvalues is presented in §
5.1
.

9
Adjoint problem

We derive the adjoint linearized equation for the sensitivity analysis presented in §
5.2
.
We start from the governing equations written compactly as
\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}=0
, where
{\boldsymbol{Q}}=(p,{{\boldsymbol{u}}},{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
collects the state variables and
\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}
is defined as:

\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}=\begin{cases}\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}&\\
\beta\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\bnabla p&\\
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}+Wi\left[\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}{\partial t}+\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}}\bcdot\bnabla\right){\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\bcdot\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right]-\left(1-\beta\right)\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]-\epsilon\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}.&\end{cases}

(49)

Linearizing
\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}
about the base state
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{b}=\left(p^{b},{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\right)
and retaining only first-order terms in the perturbation
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\prime}=\left(p^{\prime},{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\right)
yields:

\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\\
\beta\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}-\bnabla p^{\prime}\\
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}+Wi\left[\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla\right){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right]\ldots\\
+Wi\left[\left({{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\bcdot\bnabla\right){\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}-\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\right)^{{\rm T}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}-{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{b}\right]-\left(1-\beta\right)\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]+Wi\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}{\partial t}-\epsilon\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(50)

The adjoint state
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\dagger}=\left(p^{\dagger},{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\dagger}\right)
is defined by requiring that the inner product
\langle{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}\rangle=0
for every admissible perturbation
{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\prime}
, with the
L^{2}
inner product defined as

\langle{\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{a}}\bcdot{\boldsymbol{b}}\ \differential\Omega,\quad\langle\mathsfbi{P},\mathsfbi{S}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\mathsfbi{P}{\boldsymbol{:}}\mathsfbi{S}\ \differential\Omega,

(51)

where
{\boldsymbol{a}}
,
{\boldsymbol{b}}
are vectors, and
\mathsfbi{P}
,
\mathsfbi{S}
are second-order tensors.

Expanding
\langle{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}\rangle
explicitly produces the weak form:

\displaystyle 0=\langle{\boldsymbol{Q}}^{\dagger},\mathcal{N}_{{\boldsymbol{Q}}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta\langle{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger},\bnabla\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]\rangle+\langle{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger},\bnabla\bcdot{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\rangle-\langle{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger},\bnabla p^{\prime}\rangle+\langle p^{\dagger},\bnabla\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle(1-\beta)\langle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\dagger},\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\rangle+Wi\langle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\dagger},\frac{\partial{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}}{\partial t}\rangle-\epsilon\langle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\dagger},\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\rangle.

\displaystyle B.I.=

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\partial\Omega}{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\dagger}\bcdot\left[\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}+\left(\bnabla{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime}\right)^{{\rm T}}\right]\bcdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\,\differential\Gamma

(55)

Regarding
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\bcdot\hat{{{\boldsymbol{u}}}}={\boldsymbol{0}}
at all boundaries

In §
5.3
, equation (
21

\bnabla\bcdot({\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\prime}\bcdot{{\boldsymbol{u}}}^{\prime})
