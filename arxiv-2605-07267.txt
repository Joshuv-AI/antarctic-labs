PerCaM-Health: Personalized Dynamic Causal Graphs for Healthcare Reasoning
Elahe Khatibi, Ziyu Wang, Saba A. Farahani, Di Huang, Hung Cao, Ramesh Jain, Amir M. Rahmani

Abstract
Personalized healthcare decisions require reasoning about how physiological and behavioral variables influence an individual patient over time. Existing temporal causal discovery methods are poorly matched to this setting: cohort-level models provide stable but non-personalized structures, while per-patient discovery is unreliable because individual trajectories are short, noisy, irregular, and non-stationary. This creates a fundamental gap between population-level causal modeling and the patient-specific, time-varying mechanisms needed for intervention reasoning. We introduce PerCaM-Health , a framework for learning personalized dynamic causal graphs from longitudinal health data. The framework learns a knowledge-guided population temporal graph, then conservatively adapts and evolves it using patient-specific temporal evidence and rolling-window updates, producing interpretable and auditable graph sequences. By coupling these graphs with temporal structural equations, the framework enables patient-level counterfactual queries, such as estimating short-horizon outcome changes under hypothetical behavioral interventions. Experiments on a semi-synthetic dynamic health benchmark show that PerCaM-Health improves graph recovery, dynamic edge tracking, and intervention direction accuracy compared to cohort-level, per-patient, and non-personalized temporal baselines. These results demonstrate that jointly modeling personalization and temporal evolution yields more reliable causal structure and intervention reasoning.

Healthcare data are inherently heterogeneous and longitudinal. A patient’s trajectory
can combine structured electronic health records, laboratory tests, medication
exposure, wearable signals, nutrition logs, images, and clinical text

,

. Large-scale
deep learning systems have demonstrated that such records can support accurate
prediction across clinical tasks

[
22
,

,

,

]
. However, high predictive
accuracy alone is often insufficient for clinical reasoning
[

,
34
,
35
]
. A model used to support
treatment planning or behavior change should also help answer mechanistic questions:
which variables appear to drive a patient’s current risk, how do these dependencies
vary across individuals, and how might an outcome change under a candidate
intervention?

Most modern healthcare predictors are associational
[
13
]
. Sequence and representation
models can exploit temporal context and cross-source signals, but their internal
representations rarely correspond to explicit causal mechanisms. This limits their
usefulness for intervention reasoning, pathway-level explanation, and reliable
decision support
[
13
,
34
]
. Causal representation learning and individual treatment effect
estimation provide complementary tools for counterfactual reasoning from
observational data
[
12
,
27
]
, while temporal
causal discovery methods model lagged dependencies in time series

[
7
,
20
]
. Yet many such methods remain
cohort-level: they estimate a single structure or treatment-response function and
therefore struggle to represent heterogeneity in physiology, behavior, comorbidity,
and treatment response.

The core challenge is to learn causal structure at the level at which healthcare
decisions are made: the individual patient, at the current point in time. Learning a
separate graph for every patient is statistically fragile because individual
trajectories are short, noisy, and irregular. Learning only a population graph is more
stable but ignores patient-specific mechanisms. We therefore ask:
how can we
learn individualized, time-evolving causal structures from longitudinal healthcare
data while preserving the statistical strength and clinical plausibility of
cohort-level learning?

pathway can differ across patients.
PerCaM-Health
bridges this gap by
adapting a shared population prior using patient-specific longitudinal evidence.

We propose
PerCaM-Health
, a personalized dynamic causal graph framework for healthcare reasoning. Motivated by this example, the framework first maps heterogeneous observations into interpretable patient-state variables, including measured variables and clinically meaningful inferred states when available. It then learns a knowledge-guided population temporal graph using lagged prediction, sparsity, and a smooth acyclicity penalty
[
41
]
. Rather than treating this graph as final,
PerCaM-Health
uses it as a causal scaffold: patient-specific temporal evidence conservatively reweights population-supported edges, while rolling-window updates track non-stationary changes over time. The resulting graph sequence is designed to be interpretable and auditable, because each edge can be decomposed into population, knowledge-prior, patient, and temporal-window contributions.

Unlike prior personalized causal graph methods,
PerCaM-Health
does not learn patient graphs independently from sparse trajectories. Instead, it formulates personalization as conservative adaptation of a knowledge-guided population scaffold using temporally directed patient evidence. In contrast to population-level knowledge-guided or dynamic causal discovery approaches, the framework jointly combines population anchoring, patient-specific temporal calibration, and rolling dynamic evolution within a unified interpretable graph-learning pipeline.

This design supports three forms of healthcare reasoning. First, it yields patient-specific pathways that expose which behavioral and physiological variables are most connected to outcomes such as glucose or risk state. Second, it provides a dynamic view of mechanisms, allowing edges to strengthen or weaken as a patient’s regime changes. Third, by coupling the graph with learned temporal structural equations, it enables model-based intervention queries such as the predicted effect of changing activity, adherence, or sleep over a short horizon. These queries are not substitutes for randomized evidence; rather, they are structured counterfactual hypotheses grounded in longitudinal data and explicit assumptions
[
21
,
24
]
. Overall, our contribution is a unified formulation that connects patient-state construction from heterogeneous data, knowledge-guided temporal structure learning, personalized graph adaptation, and dynamic graph evolution while remaining anchored to a shared population prior.

Contributions.

Our contributions are threefold: (i) we formulate personalized dynamic causal graph learning as population-prior adaptation for longitudinal healthcare data; (ii) we introduce conservative patient-level and rolling-window updates that personalize graph weights while maintaining cohort-level support; and (iii) we conduct controlled semi-synthetic evaluation with known dynamic causal mechanisms, enabling direct assessment of graph recovery, patient-specific adaptation, temporal edge tracking, and intervention-direction accuracy under heterogeneous and non-stationary patient trajectories.

3
Method

We propose
PerCaM-Health
, a modular framework for learning patient-specific
dynamic causal graphs from longitudinal healthcare data. The method is
designed for settings in which each individual contributes a short, noisy, and
potentially irregular time series, while the cohort as a whole contains enough
repeated structure to identify stable physiological and behavioral dependencies.

PerCaM-Health
separates graph construction and use into five stages:
(i) construction of patient-state trajectories from heterogeneous health data,
(ii) learning a knowledge-guided population graph, (iii) patient-specific adaptation
of the population graph, (iv) rolling-window evolution of the personalized graph, and
(v) intervention queries and counterfactual reasoning. This decomposition gives a
strong cohort-level inductive bias while allowing clinically meaningful heterogeneity
and non-stationarity. Rather than learning a fragile graph from each patient in
isolation, the population graph supplies a shared temporal scaffold, clinical
knowledge biases the search toward plausible directions, patient- and window-level conditional temporal evidence provides local calibration, and the resulting graph sequence supports
short-horizon intervention analysis. Figure
2
summarizes
the full workflow.

3.1
Problem Setup

Let
\mathcal{V}=\{1,\ldots,d\}
denote the set of observed health variables, such as
activity, sleep, adherence, stress, inflammation, and glucose. More generally, patient

i
may have modality-specific observations

\mathcal{O}_{i,t}^{(m)}
from structured records, laboratory measurements, wearable
signals, nutrition logs, text-derived features, or image-derived features. A
modality-specific encoder or aggregation map
\phi_{m}
converts these observations into
a common state vector,

x_{i,t}=\mathrm{concat}_{m=1}^{M}\left(\phi_{m}(\mathcal{O}_{i,\leq t}^{(m)})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(1)

The components of
x_{i,t}
may be directly measured variables or clinically
meaningful inferred state variables, such as adherence, stress, inflammation, or
disease activity scores. The resulting trajectory for patient
i
is

X_{i}=\{x_{i,t}\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}}
, where
t
indexes a fixed temporal resolution after
preprocessing. Our objective is to estimate a sequence of weighted directed graphs

\mathcal{G}_{i}^{(q)}=(\mathcal{V},W_{i}^{(q)})
, with

W_{i}^{(q)}\in[0,1]^{d\times d}
. Each entry
W_{i,uv}^{(q)}
measures the strength of
the directed dependence from variable
u
to variable
v
for patient
i
in temporal
window
q
, and self-loops are disallowed. Unlike a single static causal discovery
model, this formulation distinguishes three sources of structure: stable
population-level dependencies, patient-level deviations, and within-patient temporal
drift.

We interpret the learned edges as causal candidates under the standard assumptions for
longitudinal observational causal analysis: temporal precedence is meaningful, the
selected state variables capture the major time-varying confounders for the modeled
subsystem, and interventions do not alter the measurement process itself

[
21
,
24
]
. When these assumptions are not
warranted, the same estimates should be read as directed predictive dependencies.
This explicit interpretation is important in health settings, where the graph is
intended to support hypothesis generation and downstream intervention analysis rather
than replace prospective clinical validation.

3.2
Patient-State Construction

Raw health data can contain event-level observations, repeated measurements, wearable
signals, nutrition logs, and static covariates. We map these heterogeneous
observations into a patient-state table with one row per patient and time step. For
event-level datasets, measurements are aggregated to the configured temporal
granularity using summary statistics for physiological, behavioral, nutrition, and
event-count variables when available.

Let
\bar{X}_{i}
denote the resulting patient-state trajectory. Missing values within a
selected dynamic variable are imputed by forward and backward filling within patient;
remaining missing entries are filled by the cohort median for that variable. Before
population learning, each dynamic variable is standardized using cohort-level moments,
which prevents high-scale variables such as steps from dominating the temporal
prediction objective. Before patient adaptation and dynamic updates, variables are
standardized within the relevant patient or window so that the personalization terms
reflect association structure rather than measurement scale. Static descriptors such as age, sex, or baseline body mass index can be used for
stratification or evaluation, but are excluded from the dynamic adjacency unless
explicitly promoted to time-varying state variables. In the present instantiation,

\phi_{m}
is deterministic temporal aggregation and feature construction; learned
encoders can also be used, provided their outputs are calibrated to clinically
meaningful state dimensions before graph learning.

3.3
Knowledge-Guided Population Graph

The second stage learns a cohort-level directed graph that acts as a population prior. We parameterize the adjacency matrix with unconstrained logits
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and define

A=\sigma(\Theta)\odot(\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-I),

(2)

where
\sigma
is the logistic sigmoid and the diagonal mask removes self-loops. To capture temporal dependence, the model additionally learns lag-specific coefficient matrices
B^{(1)},\ldots,B^{(L)}
. The effective lag coefficient is

C^{(\ell)}=A\odot B^{(\ell)}.

Given a cohort tensor
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times T\times d}
, the model predicts each state from the previous
L
states:

\hat{x}_{i,t}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}x_{i,t-\ell}C^{(\ell)},\qquad t=L+1,\ldots,T.

(3)

This formulation follows the Granger-style principle that directed variables should improve prediction of future targets
[
7
]
. We optimize a population objective with reconstruction, sparsity, acyclicity, and clinical-prior regularization:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{rec}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rec}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sp}}\|A\|_{1,\mathrm{mean}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{dag}}h(A)+\lambda_{\mathrm{prior}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prior}},

(4)

where

h(A)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\exp(A\odot A)\right)-d

(5)

is the smooth acyclicity penalty from continuous DAG learning
[
41
]
.

Clinical prior masks encourage plausible dependencies and penalize forbidden directions. After optimization, the population graph
W^{(0)}
is exported by thresholding
A
and serves as the causal scaffold for patient-specific adaptation and dynamic evolution.

3.4
Patient-Specific Graph Adaptation

Patients can differ substantially in how strongly cohort-level dependencies manifest. We therefore adapt only population-supported edges: directions are inherited from the population temporal graph, while patient-specific temporal evidence adjusts edge strength.

For patient
i
, let
\tilde{X}_{i}
denote the within-patient standardized trajectory and let
\mathrm{Pa}^{(0)}(v)
denote the population-supported parent set of target variable
v
. We estimate patient-specific temporal effects using graph-constrained autoregressive regression:

x_{i,v,t}=\sum_{k\in\mathrm{Pa}^{(0)}(v)}\beta_{i,k\rightarrow v}\,x_{i,k,t-1}+\epsilon_{t},

(6)

where
\beta_{i,k\rightarrow v}
measures the conditional temporal influence of source variable
k
on target variable
v
.

The personalized edge update is

\delta_{i,uv}=\mathrm{clip}\left(\eta\,\beta_{i,u\rightarrow v}|W^{(0)}_{uv}|+\xi(\nu_{i,u}-1)|W^{(0)}_{uv}|,-\Delta_{\mathrm{pat}},\Delta_{\mathrm{pat}}\right),

(7)

where
\eta
controls temporal adaptation,
\xi
controls an optional variability correction, and
\nu_{i,u}
is the normalized variability of source variable
u
.

The personalized edge weight is

W_{i,uv}=\mathrm{clip}\left(W^{(0)}_{uv}+\delta_{i,uv},0,1\right),

(8)

and edges below
\tau_{\mathrm{pat}}
are removed. This produces a sparse patient-specific graph anchored to the population prior while reflecting individualized temporal evidence.

To further quantify edge importance, we evaluate a Granger-style predictive contribution by comparing the full autoregressive model against a reduced model with the source variable removed. Edges that improve future prediction receive stronger patient-specific support.

3.5
Rolling Dynamic Graph Evolution

Health behavior and physiology are non-stationary: sleep, activity, stress, adherence, and glucose regulation can change over time. We therefore convert each personalized graph into a sequence of dynamic graph snapshots using rolling temporal windows.

For patient
i
, we slide a window of size
m
over the trajectory with step size
s
. In window
q
, we estimate graph-constrained conditional temporal effects using a local ridge-regularized autoregressive model:

x_{i,v,t}=\sum_{u\in\mathrm{Pa}_{i}(v)}\beta^{(q)}_{i,u\rightarrow v}x_{i,u,t-1}+\epsilon_{t},

(9)

where
\beta^{(q)}_{i,u\rightarrow v}
measures the time-varying conditional influence of source variable
u
on target variable
v
within window
q
.

For each personalized edge
(u,v)
, the dynamic update is

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i,uv}^{(q)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{clip}\left(\gamma\beta^{(q)}_{i,u\rightarrow v}|W_{i,uv}|+\rho(\nu_{i,u}^{(q)}-1)|W_{i,uv}|,-\Delta_{\mathrm{dyn}},\Delta_{\mathrm{dyn}}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{i,uv}^{(q)}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{clip}\left(W_{i,uv}+\epsilon_{i,uv}^{(q)},0,1\right),

where
\nu_{i,u}^{(q)}
is a window-level volatility ratio.

To reduce instability from short windows, we apply exponential smoothing:

W_{i,uv}^{(q)}=\begin{cases}\hat{W}_{i,uv}^{(q)},&q=1,\\
\alpha W_{i,uv}^{(q-1)}+(1-\alpha)\hat{W}_{i,uv}^{(q)},&q>1.\end{cases}

(11)

Edges below
\tau_{\mathrm{dyn}}
are removed. The resulting graph sequence captures patient-specific temporal evolution while remaining anchored to the personalized causal scaffold. The update can also be interpreted as a localized Granger-style predictive contribution estimate, where edges that improve future prediction receive stronger dynamic support.

3.6
Intervention Queries and Counterfactuals

The learned graph sequence supports local, model-based intervention analysis through
the same temporal structural model used for population learning. For a patient and
window
q
, we obtain window-specific signed lag coefficients by rescaling the learned
population coefficients with the dynamic graph,

B_{i}^{(q,\ell)}=W_{i}^{(q)}\odot B^{(\ell)}
. The corresponding one-step predictor is

f_{i}^{(q)}(x_{t-1:t-L})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}x_{t-\ell}B_{i}^{(q,\ell)}
.
To evaluate a candidate intervention
\mathrm{do}(x_{u,t}=a)
, we replace the
intervened component in the history with
a
and recursively roll out

f_{i}^{(q)}
while holding the local graph fixed over the query horizon. The estimated

h
-step effect on target variable
v
is

\Delta_{u\rightarrow v}^{(h)}(a,a^{\prime})=\left[\hat{x}_{t+h}^{\,\mathrm{do}(x_{u,t}=a)}\right]_{v}-\left[\hat{x}_{t+h}^{\,\mathrm{do}(x_{u,t}=a^{\prime})}\right]_{v}.

(12)

This quantity is a model-based counterfactual contrast, not a randomized clinical
effect estimate. Its reliability depends on the causal assumptions in
Section
3.1
; under weaker assumptions it should be interpreted
as an intervention-sensitive predictive contrast.

3.7
Optimization and Complexity

The population prior is optimized end-to-end with first-order gradient methods using sparse initialization, prior-edge boosting, and gradient clipping for stability. The personalization and dynamic modules are deterministic post-training updates without additional neural parameters, which improves stability on short patient trajectories. Thresholds and update magnitudes are selected on validation data and reported in the appendix.

The computational cost of one population training epoch is
O(NTLd^{2})
, dominated by lagged matrix multiplication. Patient adaptation costs
O(\sum_{i}T_{i}d^{2})
, and dynamic evolution costs
O(\sum_{i}Q_{i}md^{2})
for rolling-window updates. The framework remains computationally lightweight and interpretable compared with large end-to-end neural temporal models.

4
Experiments

We evaluate
PerCaM-Health
on personalized dynamic causal graph learning,
temporal edge tracking, counterfactual intervention estimation, and graph-grounded
recommendation. Because complete patient-level dynamic causal graphs are rarely
available in real longitudinal healthcare data, we use a controlled semi-synthetic
benchmark with known mechanisms for evaluation. Our experiments therefore test
whether anchoring patient-specific dynamic graphs to a knowledge-guided population
prior improves recovery of known structure and known intervention directions under
controlled patient heterogeneity and temporal drift.

4.1
Experimental Setup

We evaluate
PerCaM-Health
on an author-constructed semi-synthetic dynamic
health benchmark with known ground-truth causal structure. The benchmark is generated
from temporal structural equations rather than downloaded as an existing public
dataset, because our evaluation requires access to the true directed graph,
patient-specific edge strengths, temporal edge trajectories, and intervention-effect
directions. Such complete causal ground truth is rarely available in real
observational healthcare datasets. The benchmark contains six longitudinal behavioral and physiological variables:
activity, sleep, adherence, stress, inflammation, and glucose. For each synthetic
patient, we sample patient-specific parameters around a shared population graph and
generate a multivariate time series using lagged structural equations with stochastic
noise. To model non-stationary health trajectories, selected edge strengths vary
across temporal regimes. The ground-truth graph includes clinically plausible pathways
such as sleep
\rightarrow
stress, activity
\rightarrow
stress,
adherence
\rightarrow
glucose, stress
\rightarrow
glucose,
inflammation
\rightarrow
glucose, sleep
\rightarrow
glucose, and
activity
\rightarrow
glucose.

We compare against static causal discovery, temporal causal discovery, fully dynamic
causal graph, knowledge-guided causal discovery, and personalized causal graph or
recommendation-oriented baselines. For graph recovery, we report directed Precision,
Recall, F1, and Structural Hamming Distance (SHD). For dynamic graph quality, we
report direction accuracy, temporal correlation, temporal MSE, and dynamic SHD. For
counterfactual and recommendation evaluation, we report intervention-direction
accuracy, mean glucose change, coverage, and explanation faithfulness. Full details of
the structural equations, parameter ranges, regime-shift process, baseline
implementations, and metric definitions are provided in
Appendix
A
.

4.4
Ablation Study

We ablate the main components of
PerCaM-Health
to quantify their
contributions. Figure
4(b)
reports the performance drop from the
full model. The full model achieves F1
0.94
, temporal correlation
0.80
, direction
accuracy
0.96
, mean effect
-0.31
, and faithfulness
0.88
. Removing the population
prior degrades graph recovery and temporal tracking, confirming that the population
scaffold is necessary for stable structure learning. Removing personalization causes
the largest drop in graph recovery, indicating that patient-specific adaptation is
essential for individualized causal modeling. Removing dynamic graph evolution eliminates temporal tracking and weakens intervention
performance, showing that static patient graphs are insufficient for non-stationary
health trajectories. Removing latent states preserves graph-level metrics but reduces
direction accuracy, effect magnitude, and faithfulness. This suggests that latent
states mainly improve intervention ranking and explanation quality rather than
structural recovery. The pattern also shows that the components affect different
failure modes: population anchoring reduces unstable edge discovery, personalization
calibrates shared pathways to individual trajectories, and dynamic evolution captures
within-patient regime changes that static graphs miss. Overall, the ablation study
shows that the population prior, personalization, dynamic evolution, and
latent-state-aware reasoning contribute complementary benefits rather than redundant
improvements.

5
Conclusion

This paper addresses the problem of learning personalized, time-evolving causal
structures from longitudinal healthcare data, where cohort-level models are stable
but non-personalized, while fully patient-specific discovery is unreliable under
short and noisy trajectories. We propose
PerCaM-Health
, a framework for
population-anchored personalized dynamic causal graph learning that combines a
knowledge-guided population scaffold, conservative patient-specific adaptation, and
rolling temporal evolution. Empirical results on a controlled semi-synthetic
benchmark with known dynamic causal mechanisms show improved recovery of known
graph structure, temporal edge tracking, and intervention-direction accuracy
compared with static, temporal, dynamic, knowledge-guided, and personalized
baselines. The resulting graph sequence provides an interpretable and auditable
representation of patient-specific pathways and temporal regime changes under
explicit causal assumptions. Future work will extend the framework to richer
multimodal clinical data, uncertainty-aware reasoning, and prospective validation
in real healthcare settings.

A.2
Evaluation Protocol

Graph recovery is evaluated against the directed ground-truth edge set using
precision, recall, F1, and SHD. Dynamic tracking is evaluated over rolling windows
using direction accuracy, temporal correlation, temporal MSE, and dynamic SHD.
Intervention evaluation checks whether each method predicts the expected sign of the
glucose response for the candidate actions. Recommendation evaluation reports
coverage, mean estimated target improvement, and explanation faithfulness, where an
explanation is faithful when it references the model-supported edge, effect direction,
and latent state used to rank the recommendation.

The baselines in the main paper are grouped by capability: static graph discovery
methods estimate a single cohort graph; temporal methods estimate lagged structure
without personalization; dynamic methods allow time variation but keep a shared
structure across patients; knowledge-guided methods use prior constraints but remain
population-level; and personalized baselines adapt to individuals without jointly
combining population anchoring, temporal evolution, and latent-state-aware
intervention reasoning.

B.3
Patient Adaptation and Dynamic Evolution

Patient adaptation standardizes each patient’s selected dynamic variables before estimating patient-specific temporal effects. For each target variable, the implementation performs graph-constrained conditional autoregressive regression using population-supported parent variables at time
t-1
. Population-supported edges are reweighted using personalization strength
\eta=0.20
, optional variability strength
\xi=0
, maximum absolute update
\Delta_{\mathrm{pat}}=0.05
, and patient-edge threshold
\tau_{\mathrm{pat}}=0.12
.

To provide additional temporal evidence for edge importance, the implementation also evaluates a Granger-style predictive contribution by comparing the prediction error of the full autoregressive model against a reduced model with the candidate source variable removed. This preserves population-supported structure during adaptation and does not introduce edges absent from the population prior.

The dynamic graph module uses a graph-constrained conditional time-varying VAR update. For each target variable in a rolling window, the module regresses the target at time
t
on the personalized-graph parent variables at time
t-1
using ridge regression with penalty
0.10
. The resulting conditional coefficient rescales the base personalized edge, the window-specific update is clipped to
0.08
, and adjacent windows are smoothed with coefficient
\alpha=0.60
. The rolling window length is 14 time steps, the step size is 3, and dynamic edges below
0.05
are omitted from the window snapshot.

This implementation allows the dynamic graph sequence to capture evolving patient-specific temporal mechanisms while maintaining structural consistency with the population causal scaffold.

B.4
Latent States, Counterfactuals, and Recommendations

Latent states are inferred at the same rolling-window resolution as the dynamic
graphs. For each window, the implementation computes means, standard deviations, and
slopes for glucose, stress, inflammation, adherence, activity, and sleep, and augments
these features with graph summaries such as incoming glucose edge weight and selected
edge strengths. Continuous latent scores are converted to rank-normalized burden
scores, combined into an overall risk score, and clustered into four discrete latent
states.

Counterfactual intervention simulation evaluates a fixed set of candidate actions:
increase activity by 1000 steps, improve adherence by 0.08, improve sleep by 0.5
hours, reduce stress by 0.05, and reduce inflammation by 0.05. Raw intervention
deltas are standardized by the within-window standard deviation of the intervened
variable. The estimated target change combines the intervention delta, the relevant
dynamic edge weight, the expected direction of the source-target relationship, and a
latent-risk multiplier. The recommendation module keeps beneficial interventions
whose absolute estimated effect is at least
0.05
, ranks up to three
recommendations per patient-window, and generates graph-grounded explanations from
the dynamic edge, latent state, and counterfactual estimate.

Appendix D
Discussion and Limitations

D.1
Discussion

PerCaM-Health
is designed for settings in which population-level causal discovery is statistically stable but insufficiently personalized, while fully independent per-patient discovery is unreliable under short, noisy, and non-stationary trajectories. The framework addresses this trade-off by using the population graph as a clinically plausible causal scaffold and adapting only population-supported edges using graph-constrained conditional temporal estimation. This preserves structural stability while allowing individualized causal calibration grounded in temporally directed predictive evidence.

An important implication is that personalization and temporal evolution address different sources of variation. Patient-specific adaptation captures relatively stable heterogeneity across individuals, such as differences in dominant behavioral or physiological pathways. Rolling-window dynamic evolution then captures changes within the same individual, including shifts caused by adherence, stress, activity, sleep, or evolving physiological regimes. Separating these stages improves interpretability compared with learning a single unconstrained black-box dynamic graph and allows the graph sequence to be interpreted as a calibrated temporal evolution rather than a sequence of independently rediscovered structures.

The learned graph sequence also serves as an auditable bridge between longitudinal measurements and downstream recommendations. Each recommendation can be traced to population support, patient-specific temporal evidence, local dynamic context, and a counterfactual estimate. This traceability is particularly important in healthcare because it exposes which mechanism the model relies on and allows clinicians or domain experts to inspect whether the inferred pathway is plausible for the patient. The outputs should therefore be interpreted as structured patient-specific hypotheses for review and prioritization rather than direct clinical actions.

The ablations further support this design. Removing personalization mainly harms structural recovery, suggesting that a shared graph alone cannot represent patient-level mechanistic differences. Removing dynamic evolution mainly harms temporal tracking and intervention reasoning, indicating that static personalized graphs fail to capture clinically relevant regime changes. Overall, the results suggest that the primary benefit of
PerCaM-Health
comes from combining population anchoring, conditional temporal personalization, Granger-style predictive evidence, and graph-constrained dynamic evolution within a unified interpretable framework.

D.2
Limitations

The main evaluation uses a single author-constructed semi-synthetic benchmark with
known dynamic causal structure. This controlled setting is necessary for measuring
graph recovery, temporal edge tracking, and intervention-direction accuracy against
known ground truth, which is rarely available in real longitudinal healthcare data.
However, semi-synthetic results do not establish clinical effectiveness or real-world
deployment readiness. In particular, the benchmark contains a limited number of
variables, patients, and temporal mechanisms, and does not capture the full
heterogeneity, missingness, intervention complexity, or distribution shift present in
real healthcare environments. Public longitudinal datasets could provide additional
partial validation of personalization and temporal adaptation behavior, even when
complete causal ground truth is unavailable. We therefore view the current evaluation
as controlled validation of the graph-learning and intervention-reasoning mechanism,
rather than as evidence that the learned effects are ready for clinical use.

The causal interpretation also depends on standard longitudinal observational
assumptions, including meaningful temporal precedence and sufficient measurement of
time-varying confounders. When these assumptions are only partially satisfied, the
graphs and counterfactuals should be treated as intervention-aware predictive
hypotheses rather than definitive causal effects. This distinction is important for
healthcare applications: the model can help prioritize plausible mechanisms, but
causal conclusions still require domain review and external evidence.

The current adaptation rule only reweights population-supported edges. This improves
stability and precision on short patient trajectories, but may miss rare mechanisms
absent from the cohort scaffold. Future work can add expert-approved edge expansion,
subgroup-specific priors, and uncertainty-gated patient-specific edge proposals. This
would preserve the conservative behavior of the current method while allowing the
model to represent strong patient-specific evidence that falls outside the initial
population graph.

Finally, the reference implementation uses a compact set of interpretable variables,
lagged linear prediction, and rolling-window updates. These choices support
auditability, but richer multimodal encoders, nonlinear structural equations,
continuous-time dynamics, uncertainty estimates, and prospective recommendation
evaluation are important next steps. In particular, deployment-facing studies should
measure whether graph-grounded recommendations are understandable, safe, and useful
to clinicians and patients. These limitations bound the current scope without
changing the central finding: population-anchored personalization improves dynamic
causal graph reliability in the evaluated setting.
