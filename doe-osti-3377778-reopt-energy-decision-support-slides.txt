 REopt Optimizes Integrated Energy Systems
• The National Laboratory of the Rocky's (NLR’s) REopt® platform optimizes the
 planning of generation, storage, and controllable loads to maximize the value of
 integrated systems.
• REopt considers electrical, heating, and cooling loads and technologies
 simultaneously to identify the optimal technology or mix of technologies.
• It transforms complex decisions into actionable results for building owners, utilities,
 developers, and industry.
• REopt analysis guides investment in economic, resilient energy technologies.

 What Kinds of Questions Can REopt Answer?

• What is the most cost-effective sizing of solar photovoltaics (PV) and battery storage
 for my site?
• How can I optimally operate my solar PV and battery to maximize their value and
 minimize my site’s energy costs?
• What is the most cost-effective system to operate as a microgrid for a grid outage
 spanning two hours? Two days? Two weeks? How much would it cost to install a
 completely off-grid system?
• What localized energy generation sources are available to my community, and what
 are the trade-offs of each of these sources, both individually and collectively?
• How could I achieve x% (solar, geothermal, hydropower, wind, and so on) energy at
 least cost?
• Where do market opportunities exist for DERs, both now and in the future?

Getting Started with REopt

 Accessing REopt

REopt is accessible to users with basic to advanced computing ability,
increasing in complexity from a web-based tool to an application programming
interface and open-source code. More advanced skillsets allow model
customization and running REopt programmatically.

• REopt Web Tool: accessible web interface that does not require programming
skills

• REopt API uses Python and Julia; leverages NREL computational resources

• REopt.jl uses Julia; offers more speed and flexibility to run locally

 Results

• Optimal system size and dispatch: The capacity of an energy system to best meet specific
 goals—that is, the process of determining which power generation units or energy resources
 should be operated at a given time to meet electricity demand in the most efficient and reliable way
       (NREL n.d.).
• Capital costs: The upfront costs required to purchase, build, or install an energy system or
 infrastructure. These include equipment, construction, engineering, land acquisition, permitting,
 and installation costs and are one-time expenses incurred before the system becomes operational
 (U.S. Energy Information Administration [EIA n.d.]).
• Operations and maintenance costs: The ongoing expenses required to keep an energy
 system running safely, efficiently, and reliably over its lifetime. These include labor, routine
 inspections, repairs, spare parts, insurance, and administrative overhead (EIA 2023).
• Net present value: A financial metric used to evaluate the profitability of a project by calculating
 the difference between the present value of cash inflows and the present value of cash outflows
 over the project's lifetime, using a specified discount rate (NREL n.d.).

                                                                                       National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   14

 Results (cont.)

• Energy bill savings: The reduction in a customer’s utility bills as a result of implementing energy
 efficiency measures, installing renewable energy systems, or using demand-side management
 strategies. These savings can be achieved through lower energy consumption or by reducing peak
 demand (U.S. Department of Energy [DOE n.d.]).
• Levelized costs of energy: A summary metric that combines the primary technology cost and
 performance parameters: capital expenditures, operating expenditures, and capacity factor (NREL
 n.d.).
• Internal rate of return: A metric used in financial analysis to estimate the profitability of potential
 investments and one that makes the net present value (NPV) of all discounted cash flows equal to
       zero (NREL 2023).
• Simple payback period: The minimum time it takes to recover an investment. The simple
 payback period for an energy system is the total investment cost divided by the first year’s
 revenues from energy saved, displaced, or produced (NREL 2000).
• Resilience metrics: Metrics to evaluate energy system resilience, including the probability of
 surviving outages of various durations.

Residential Solar-Plus Technologies: Behind-the-Meter
Description: REopt® can determine the optimal mix and size of
solar PV and “solar-plus” technologies to lower the life-cycle cost of
energy for several post-net-metering residential utility tariffs
(including time-of-use [TOU] and demand tariffs).

Technology: For Hawaii’s TOU residential tariff—where no energy
export is allowed—the solar-plus approach increases NPV by a
factor of 3 relative to the stand-alone solar PV approach: The smart
air conditioning unit and smart domestic water heater are
dispatched each day to precool the home and preheat water with
solar PV output. Any excess output remaining after precooling and
water heating is delivered to a battery, which is dispatched to
further reduce TOU rates.
