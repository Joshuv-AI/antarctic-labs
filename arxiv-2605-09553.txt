Prescribed Angle Surfaces Associated with Torse-Forming Vector Fields in Riemannian Manifolds
Muhittin Evren Aydin 1, Esra Dilmen 2, Büşra Karakaya 3

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce the notion of a prescribed angle hypersurface in a Riemannian manifold associated with a pair ( 𝒱 , θ ) (\mathcal{V},\theta) , where 𝒱 \mathcal{V} is a unit vector field along the hypersurface and θ \theta denotes the angle between 𝒱 \mathcal{V} and the unit normal vector field of the hypersurface. We study such hypersurfaces in case 𝒱 \mathcal{V} is a torse-forming vector field. In the particular 3 3 -dimensional case, we determine the intrinsic and extrinsic curvatures of these hypersurfaces in terms of the prescribed angle and the potential function of 𝒱 \mathcal{V} . Using this, we classify prescribed angle surfaces under suitable assumptions.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider orientable surfaces in Riemannian manifolds making a prescribed angle with a vector field. Although this is a classical topic in differential geometry, we introduce a new perspective, as described below.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable surface in a Riemannian manifold
M
and
\mathcal{N}
denote its unit normal vector field. Let
\mathcal{V}
be a nowhere-vanishing unit vector field along
\Sigma
. By a vector field along the surface
\Sigma
, we mean a section of the restricted tangent bundle
TM|_{\Sigma}
. Such vector fields may be tangent, normal or have both nonzero tangential and normal components with respect to
\Sigma
.

The angle function
\theta
between
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{V}
along
\Sigma
is defined by
\cos\theta=\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{V}\rangle
. The surface
\Sigma
is called a constant angle surface with respect to
\mathcal{V}
if the function
\theta
is constant along
\Sigma
. In this case, the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is sometimes called the axis of
\Sigma
.

Such surfaces in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
were studied in
[
6
]
, where
\mathcal{V}
is considered as a direction field on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. When the ambient space is a product manifold such as
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
16
]
,
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}

[
17
]
or
I\times_{\rho}\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
18
]
, constant angle surfaces were classified. In each case, the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is assumed to be tangent to the
\mathbb{R}
-direction. If
\mathcal{V}
is the position vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, then the class of constant slope surfaces was determined in
[
32
]
.

When
\mathcal{V}
is a Killing vector field, constant angle surfaces were also studied. For instance, the corresponding classifications in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
were obtained in
[
33
]
. In the
3
-dimensional Berger sphere, these surfaces were characterized in
[
30
]
. See also
[
35
]
for the case where the ambient space is
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

In higher-dimensional cases, a different perspective on the constant angle property is to require that the angle between the tangent space of a submanifold and a given vector field be constant. In this setting, and in the Euclidean case, such submanifolds are also called helix submanifolds
[
19
]
. Helix hypersurfaces were also described in the cited article by assuming
\mathcal{V}
to be a fixed direction. A recent characterization of helix surfaces was obtained in
[
28
]
so that isogonal lines become generalized helices and pseudogeodesic lines.

Another perspective was introduced by Chen
[
9
]
, defined rectifying submanifolds in Euclidean spaces as those for which the position vector field is orthogonal to the first normal space. Afterwards, Chen also generalized this notion to arbitrary Riemannian manifolds
[
10
]
.

Before proceeding with the literature review, we recall the notion of a torse-forming vector field. Let
\nabla
denote the Levi-Civita connection on a Riemannian manifold
M
. A vector field
\mathcal{V}\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
is said to be
torse-forming

[
38
]
if there exist a smooth function
f
on
M
and a
1
-form
\omega
such that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(M),

(1)

where
f
and
\omega
are called the potential function and the generating form of
\mathcal{V}
, respectively (see also
[
5
,
31
]
). In particular, the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is said to be concircular if
\omega
vanishes identically
[
37
]
. Otherwise, that is, when
\omega\neq 0
, it is called a torqued vector field provided that
\omega(\mathcal{V})=0
. Moreover, if
\omega=-f\nu
, where
\nu
is the
1
-form dual to
\mathcal{V}
, then the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is said to be anti-torqued
[
7
,
14
,
36
]
.

These vector fields provide a useful tool for studying the geometry and topology of ambient spaces. However, not every Riemannian manifold admits such vector fields globally. For results concerning the global existence of these vector fields on the ambient space, see
[
2
,
8
,
12
,
14
,
15
]
.

Continuing the literature review on constant angle hypersurfaces, a new approach to their study was initiated in
[
26
,
27
]
. Let the ambient space be one of the space forms
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
or
\mathbb{S}^{n}
. In this setting, a concircular hypersurface
\Sigma
is defined as a hypersurface for which
\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{V}\rangle
is equal to a constant
\theta_{0}
on
\Sigma
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a globally defined concircular vector field on the ambient space. Following this idea, the first author, together with Mihai and Özgür
[
4
]
, defined and characterized anti-torqued and torqued hypersurfaces in arbitrary Riemannian manifolds.

Notice that if
\mathcal{V}
is a globally defined concircular vector field, then for a concircular hypersurface the angle function
\theta
between
\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{V}
is given by
\cos\theta=\theta_{0}|\mathcal{V}|^{-1}
. This angle function is constant if and only if
|\mathcal{V}|
is constant. In this case, the hypersurface is totally umbilical (see Proposition
3.1
and
[
27
, Proposition 4]
). Otherwise, the angle function is non-constant along the hypersurface. This situation also occurs for torqued hypersurfaces. However, since every anti-torqued vector field can be assumed to be unit (see
[
14
]
), an anti-torqued hypersurface is also a constant angle hypersurface with anti-torqued axis.

When the ambient space is an arbitrary Riemannian manifold, constant angle surfaces were also investigated in
[
13
]
by assuming that
\mathcal{V}
is parallel transported along any curve on the surface. In addition, submanifolds in arbitrary Riemannian spaces having constant ratio and principal direction properties with respect to an ambient vector field were also studied in
[
29
]
.

In view of the above discussion, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable hypersurface in a Riemannian manifold
M
and
\mathcal{V}
be a nonzero vector field defined along
\Sigma
. Let
\theta:\Sigma\to[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
be a smooth function. We say that
\Sigma
is a prescribed angle (PA) hypersurface in
M
associated with the pair
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
if
\mathcal{V}
is unit along
\Sigma
and satisfies
\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{N}\rangle=\cos\theta
, where
\mathcal{N}
denotes the unit normal vector field of
\Sigma
. Such hypersurfaces will be briefly called PA hypersurfaces with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
.

Since the ambient space in Definition
1.1
is an arbitrary Riemannian manifold, unlike the usual approach in the literature, we no longer assume the existence of a globally defined vector field. Indeed, such an approach involves two separate problems: the existence of a globally defined vector field and the existence of PA hypersurfaces with respect to such a vector field. Instead, by Definition
1.1
, these two problems are reduced to a single problem. See Fig.

.

In this paper, we restrict our study to those PA hypersurfaces for which
\mathcal{V}
is a torse-forming vector field. Therefore, whenever we refer to a PA hypersurface, we assume the existence of a unit torse-forming vector field along the hypersurface.

By a torse-forming vector field
\mathcal{V}
along
\Sigma
, we mean a vector field
\mathcal{V}
satisfying (
1
), where the covariant differentiation is taken with respect to the ambient Levi–Civita connection and
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
.
In this case, as we will see in Lemma
3.3
, if
\mathcal{V}
is a unit torse-forming vector field, then it must be anti-torqued along
\Sigma
. Definition
1.1
allows us to study PA hypersurfaces even when the ambient space does not admit a globally defined anti-torqued vector field (see Example
3.4
and Remark
3.7
). This is because a torse-forming vector field defined on an open subset cannot, in general, be extended to the whole ambient space
[
2
,
15
]
. Therefore, assuming the existence of vector fields along hypersurfaces does not impose restrictions on the ambient space, in contrast to assuming the existence of globally defined ambient vector fields.

Moreover, as stated in Definition
1.1
, we do not impose the angle function
\theta
to be constant; instead, we allow it to be an arbitrary function. This constitutes a distinguishing feature of our paper compared to the existing literature. Indeed, for example, on a totally geodesic PA hypersurface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, the condition
\theta\notin\{0,\pi/2\}
implies that
\theta
is constant if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is parallel along the hypersurface (see Remark
3.7
). Apart from this, no further information about the hypersurface can be concluded. However, when
\theta
is allowed to vary, the intrinsic and extrinsic curvatures of a PA surface in a Riemannian manifold can be expressed in terms of the potential function of
\mathcal{V}
and the angle function
\theta
(see Proposition
4.1
). Therefore, it enables us to classify totally geodesic PA surfaces with constant intrinsic curvature (see Theorem
4.4
). Moreover, we also obtain classification and characterization results for non-totally geodesic PA surfaces when
\theta
is constant (see Proposition
3.9
and Theorems
3.8
and
4.5
).

Notice that our idea of allowing the angle function to vary is also inspired by the study of surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
34
]
, where the tangential component of
\Phi/|\Phi|
with respect to the surface is a principal direction and
\Phi
denotes the position vector field. In this setting, the angle between
\Phi/|\Phi|
and the unit normal vector varies. Recall that the vector field
\Phi/|\Phi|
is an example of an anti-torqued vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

[
14
]
. Therefore, the considered surfaces in
[
34
]
can be regarded as PA surfaces with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
such that the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
is a principal direction.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we give the preliminaries on hypersurfaces in Riemannian manifolds. In Section
3
, we establish basic lemmas and results for PA hypersurfaces. Moreover, we study the particular cases of PA hypersurfaces with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
when the function
\theta
is constant or the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is parallel along the hypersurface. In the final section, we obtain classification results for PA surfaces with constant intrinsic curvature. We also provide nontrivial examples corresponding to the obtained results.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
(M^{n+1},\langle,\rangle)
be a Riemannian manifold of dimension
n+1
. When the dimension is not necessary to specify, we will denote the manifold simply by
M
.

Let
\pi\subset T_{p}M
be a plane section at a point
p\in M
and
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be a basis of
\pi
. The sectional curvature of
\pi
is defined by

K(\pi)=\frac{\langle R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{2},e_{1}\rangle}{\langle e_{1},e_{1}\rangle\langle e_{2},e_{2}\rangle-\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle^{2}},

where
R
is the Riemannian curvature tensor of
M
associated with the Levi–Civita connection. The manifold
M
is called a space form if
K
is a real constant for all plane sections and points.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable hypersurface in
M^{n+1}
and
\mathcal{N}
its unit normal vector field. The shape operator
A
of
\Sigma
is a symmetric
(1,1)
-tensor field on
T\Sigma
defined by
A(X)=-\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}
,
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, where
\nabla
is the Levi–Civita connection on
M^{n+1}
. If
M^{n+1}
and
\Sigma
are orientable and equipped with chosen orientations, then, since
A
is symmetric, there exists, at each point
p\in\Sigma
, an orthonormal basis of principal directions
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{n}\}\subset T_{p}\Sigma
with corresponding real principal curvatures
\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n}

[
20
]
.

The induced Levi-Civita connection
\nabla^{\Sigma}
on
\Sigma
is obtained from the Gauss formula:

\nabla_{X}Y=\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}Y+\langle A(X),Y\rangle\mathcal{N},\quad X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

The hypersurface
\Sigma
is said to be totally geodesic if
A=0
and totally umbilical if
A=\delta\mbox{Id}
, for a smooth function
\delta
on
\Sigma
. For every
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, the Gauss equation is given by

R^{\Sigma}(X,Y)Z=\big(R(X,Y)Z\big)^{\top}+\langle A(Y),Z\rangle A(X)-\langle A(X),Z\rangle A(Y),

where
(\cdot)^{\top}
denotes the tangential component along
\Sigma
.

In particular, if
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is an orthonormal frame on a surface
\Sigma\subset M^{3}
, then the Gauss equation yields

K^{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{2})=K(e_{1},e_{2})+\langle A(e_{1}),e_{1}\rangle\langle A(e_{2}),e_{2}\rangle-\langle A(e_{1}),e_{2}\rangle^{2},

where
K^{\Sigma}(e_{1},e_{2})
denotes the intrinsic curvature of the surface and
K(e_{1},e_{2})
is the sectional curvature of the tangent plane
T_{p}\Sigma\subset T_{p}M^{3}
. Consequently, the Gauss equation reduces to
K_{\mathrm{int}}=K_{\mathrm{sec}}+K_{\mathrm{ext}},
where
K_{\mathrm{ext}}=\det A
is the extrinsic curvature of
\Sigma
. We call
\Sigma
is extrinsically (resp. intrinsically) flat if
K_{\mathrm{ext}}
(resp.
K_{\mathrm{int}}
) vanishes identically.

A surface
\Sigma\subset M^{3}
is said to be ruled if it admits a foliation by curves which are geodesics of the ambient space
[
25
]
.

3.
PA hypersurfaces with torse-forming vector fields

In this section, we study PA hypersurfaces Riemannian manifolds associated with torse-forming vector fields. We first characterize totally-umbilical hypersurfaces using concircular vector fields.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a unit concircular vector field
\mathcal{V}
along an orientable hypersurface
\Sigma
with nonzero potential function if and only if
\Sigma
is totally umbilical with normal vector field
\mathcal{V}
.

Proof.

Denote by
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connection of the ambient space. Assume that
\mathcal{V}
is a unit concircular vector field along
\Sigma
, that is,
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=fX
for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
. Since
\mathcal{V}
has unit length, we have

0=X\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle=2\langle\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle=2f\langle\mathcal{V},X\rangle,

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
. Since
f\neq 0
, it follows that
\langle\mathcal{V},X\rangle=0
and hence
\mathcal{V}
is normal to
\Sigma
. Therefore,
\mathcal{V}=\pm\mathcal{N}
, where
\mathcal{N}
is the unit normal vector field. Consequently,
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}=\pm fX,
which implies
A=\mp f\operatorname{Id}
, namely,
\Sigma
is totally umbilical.

Conversely, let
\Sigma
be a totally umbilical hypersurface and
\mathcal{N}
its unit normal vector field. Since
\Sigma
is totally umbilical, we have
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}=-\lambda X
,
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
, for some nonzero smooth function
\lambda
. This yields that
\mathcal{N}
is a unit concircular vector field along
\Sigma
with potential function
f=-\lambda
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Proposition
3.1
generalizes
[
27
, Proposition 4]
, where the result was established for real space forms, to arbitrary Riemannian manifolds.

Let
\Sigma
be a hypersurface in a Riemannian manifold
M
and
\mathcal{V}
be a unit torse-forming vector field along
\Sigma
. Denote by
\nabla
the Levi-Civita connnection on
M
. Then, for any
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
we write

0=X\langle\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle=2\langle\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle=2\langle fX+\omega(X)\mathcal{V},\mathcal{V}\rangle,

or equivalently

\omega(X)=-f\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle,\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

(2)

This yields the following result:

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a unit torse-forming vector field along a hypersurface
\Sigma
. Then, the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is anti-torqued along
\Sigma
. Moreover, the potential function of
\mathcal{V}
vanishes along
\Sigma
if and only if
\mathcal{V}
is parallel along
\Sigma
.

By Lemma
3.3
, in our setting, natural candidates for PA hypersurfaces are those in ambient spaces admitting globally defined anti-torqued vector fields. Indeed, if
M
is such a Riemannian manifold, then every orientable hypersurface in
M
is a PA hypersurface.

More generally, once the ambient space admits a totally umbilical hypersurface, one obtains an immediate example of a PA hypersurface, as shown below.

Example 3.4
.

Let
M
be a Riemannian manifold admitting a totally umbilical hypersurface
\Sigma
and
\mathcal{N}
its unit vector field. Then,
\Sigma
is an example of a PA hypersurface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}=\pm\mathcal{N}
and
\theta=0
. Indeed, since
\Sigma
is totally umbilical, we have
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{N}=\lambda X
for some smooth function
\lambda
on
\Sigma
and for all
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
. Hence,

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=\lambda X=\lambda(X-\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle\mathcal{V}),\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma),

which means that the normal vector field
\mathcal{V}=\pm\mathcal{N}
acts as a unit anti-torqued vector field along
\Sigma
.

Remark 3.5
.

Example
3.4
shows that a PA hypersurface can be considered in a Riemannian manifold even if it does not admit a globally defined anti-torqued vector field; for instance, when the ambient space is a sphere (see
[
14
, Example 1]
).

Assume that
\Sigma
is a PA hypersurface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
in a Riemannian manifold
M
. Denote by
\mathcal{N}
the unit normal vector field to
\Sigma
. By Definition
1.1
, the vector field
\mathcal{V}
admits a decomposition into its tangential and normal components along
\Sigma
, given by

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{T}+\cos\theta\mathcal{N},\quad\mathcal{T}=\sin\theta e_{1},

(3)

where
e_{1}
is a unit tangent vector field to
\Sigma
and
\theta:\Sigma\to[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
is a smooth function.

Let
\nabla
and
\nabla^{\Sigma}
denote the Levi-Civita connections of
M
and
\Sigma
, respectively. By the Gauss formula, it follows that

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}\mathcal{T}-\cos\theta A(X)+\big(\langle A(\mathcal{T}),X\rangle+X(\cos\theta)\big)\mathcal{N},

(4)

for every
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.3
,
\mathcal{V}
is anti-torqued along
\Sigma
, i.e.,
\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=f\big(X-\langle X,\mathcal{V}\rangle\mathcal{V}\big).
Using the decomposition (
3
) gives

\nabla_{X}\mathcal{V}=f\big(X-\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle(\mathcal{T}+\cos\theta\mathcal{N})\big),\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

Comparing with (
4
), we derive

\left\{\begin{aligned} f\big(X-\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T}\big)&=\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}\mathcal{T}-\cos\theta A(X),\\[4.0pt]
\langle A(\mathcal{T}),X\rangle+X(\cos\theta)&=-f\cos\theta\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

Therefore, by the second equation of (
5
), we have the following result

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a PA hypersurface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field with potential function
f
and
\theta\neq 0
. Let also
\mathcal{T}
be the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
with respect to
\Sigma
. Then,

(i)

\mathcal{T}
is a principal direction if and only if the function
\theta
is constant along the orthogonal distribution to
\mathcal{T}
.

(ii)

\mathcal{T}
is a principal direction with principal curvature
\kappa_{1}=-f\cos\theta
if and only if
\theta
is constant.

Proof.

Since
\theta\neq 0
, we have
\mathcal{T}\neq 0
. Let
Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
be orthogonal to
\mathcal{T}
. Then
the second equation of (
5
) gives
\langle A(\mathcal{T}),Y\rangle-\sin\theta Y(\theta)=0.
Therefore
\mathcal{T}
is a principal direction if and only if
\theta
is constant along the orthogonal disribution to
\mathcal{T}
, completing the proof of the first item. For the second item, assume that
\mathcal{T}
is a principal direction of
\Sigma
, say
A(\mathcal{T})=\kappa_{1}\mathcal{T}
. Then, the second equation of (
5
) yields

X(\cos\theta)=-(\kappa_{1}+f\cos\theta)\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle,\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

(6)

It follows that
\theta
is constant on
\Sigma
only if
\kappa_{1}=-f\cos\theta
.
∎

This result extends
[
34
, Proposition 2.1]
, obtained in the Euclidean space, to arbitrary Riemannian manifolds, where the position vector field of the Euclidean space is replaced by an arbitrary anti-torqued vector field. Moreover, in the following remark, we indicate the differences between working with torse-forming vector fields and parallel vector fields, as in
[
13
]
.

Remark 3.7
.

By Proposition
3.6
, the condition that
\mathcal{T}
is a principal direction is not sufficient by itself to ensure that
\theta
is constant. In addition, the corresponding principal curvature must satisfy
\kappa_{1}=-f\cos\theta
. This indicates the difference between working with torse-forming vector fields and parallel vector fields (see
[
13
, Proposition 5]
). Another difference can be seen as follows. To clarify this, consider the simplest case where
\Sigma
is totally geodesic. Then
A=0
and the second equation in (
5
) reduces to

\sin\theta\,X(\theta)=f\cos\theta\sin\theta\langle X,e_{1}\rangle.

This implies that
\theta
must be constant as long as
f
vanishes. Otherwise, i.e., if
f\neq 0
, the function
\theta
is not necessarily constant.

In the last results of this section, we study the particular cases where
\mathcal{V}
is parallel and where either the tangential or the normal component of
\mathcal{V}
vanishes identically. The first result extends
[
13
, Proposition 5]
to arbitrary dimensions. For the second, see also
[
3
, Proposition 4.1, Corollary 4.2]
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-totally geodesic PA hypersurface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is a parallel vector field.
If
\theta
is constant, then the integral curves of the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
are ambient geodesics. Moreover, the shape operator of
\Sigma
satisfies
\operatorname{det}(A)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{V}
is a parallel vector field along
\Sigma
, by (
5
) it follows

\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}\mathcal{T}=\cos\theta A(X),\quad\langle A(\mathcal{T}),X\rangle=-X(\cos\theta),\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

If
\theta
is constant, then we get
A(\mathcal{T})=0
and
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}=0
. Hence by the Gauss fomrula, we derive
\nabla_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}=0
, completing the result of the first part. To complete the proof, let
\{e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{n}\}
be a local orthonormal frame of principal directions on
\Sigma
. In terms of this frame, the matrix of the shape operator takes the form
A=0\oplus\operatorname{diag}[\kappa_{2},\ldots,\kappa_{n}]
, where
\oplus
denotes the direct sum of matrices. Hence its determinant is zero.
∎

.

Let
\Sigma

\mathcal{V}

(i)

If
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, then the integral curves of the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
are ambient geodesics. Moreover, the shape operator of
\Sigma
satisfies
\operatorname{det}(A)=0
.

(ii)

If
\theta=0
, then
\Sigma
is totally umbilical.

Proof.

(i)

Writing
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
in (
3
) yields
\mathcal{T}=e_{1}
. Considering in (
5
), we find

\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}\mathcal{T}=f\big(X-\langle X,\mathcal{T}\rangle\mathcal{T}\big),\quad\langle A(\mathcal{T}),X\rangle=0,\quad X\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma).

This gives
A(\mathcal{T})=0
and
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}=0
. The proof is completed by following that of Theorem
3.8
.

(ii)

If
\theta=0
, then by (
3
) it follows that
\mathcal{T}=0
. The conclusion follows from the first equality in (
5
).

∎

Considering item (ii) of Proposition
3.9
together with Example
3.4
and comparing them with Proposition
3.1
, an important difference emerges. The existence of a unit concircular vector field
\mathcal{V}
along a hypersurface forces the hypersurface to be totally umbilical and
\mathcal{V}
becomes normal to the hypersurface. However, the existence of a unit anti-torqued vector field on a hypersurface does not, in general, imply that the hypersurface is totally umbilical. For instance, as will observe in the items of Example
4.6
, the surfaces admitting a unit anti-torqued vector field are not totally umbilical.

4.
Constant curvature PA surfaces

In this section, we study the curvature properties of PA surfaces in
3
-dimensional Riemannian manifolds.

Under the assumption that the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
is a principal direction (or, in the sense of
[
29
]
, that the PA surface has the principal direction property with respect to
\mathcal{V}
), we first establish an auxiliary result for computing the Levi-Civita connection and the intrinsic curvature of such surfaces.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field with potential function
f
and
\theta\neq 0
. Let also
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be a local orthonormal frame on
\Sigma
of principal directions and
\kappa_{i}
the corresponding principal curvatures.
If the tangential component of
\mathcal{V}
satisfies
\mathcal{T}=\sin\theta e_{1}
, then

(i)

e_{1}(\theta)=\kappa_{1}+f\cos\theta,\quad e_{2}(\theta)=0,

(7)

(ii)

\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=0,\quad\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=Be_{2},\quad\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{2}=-Be_{1},\quad[e_{1},e_{2}]=-Be_{2},

(8)

(iii)

there is a coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\Sigma
such that its metric is given by

g^{\Sigma}=du^{2}+\big(pe^{\int Bdu)}\big)^{2}dv^{2},\quad e_{1}=\partial_{u},\quad e_{2}=\big(pe^{\int Bdu)}\big)^{-1}\partial_{v},

(9)

(iv)

the curvatures of
\Sigma
with respect to
(u,v)
are

K_{\mathrm{int}}=-\left(\frac{\partial B}{\partial u}+B^{2}\right),\quad K_{\mathrm{ext}}=\left(\frac{d\theta}{du}-f\cos\theta\right)\kappa_{2},

(10)

where
B:=\frac{f+\kappa_{2}\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}
and
p=p(v)
is some positive function.

Proof.

(i)

Writing
X=e_{1}
and
X=e_{2}
into (
6
) yields the derivatives
e_{i}(\theta)
in (
7
).

(ii)

We compute the covariant derivatives
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{i}}e_{j}
. By plugging
\mathcal{T}=\sin\theta e_{1}
and
A(e_{i})=\kappa_{i}e_{i}
into (
5
) we obtain

\left\{\begin{aligned} f\big(X-\sin^{2}\theta\langle X,e_{1}\rangle e_{1}\big)&=X(\sin\theta)e_{1}+\sin\theta\nabla^{\Sigma}_{X}e_{1}-\cos\theta A(X),\\[4.0pt]
\kappa_{1}\sin\theta\langle e_{1},X\rangle+X(\cos\theta)&=-f\sin\theta\cos\theta\langle X,e_{1}\rangle.\end{aligned}\right.

(11)

Substituting
X=e_{1}
in the first equation of (
11
) and considering (
7
), we conclude that
\sin\theta\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{1}=0
. From the identity
0=e_{1}\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle
, it follows that
\langle e_{1},\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{2}\rangle=0
. Since
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{2}
is orthogonal to both
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
, we derive
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{2}=0
. In addition, writing
X=e_{2}
in the first equation of (
11
), we obtain
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{1}=Be_{2}
. The value of the covariant derivative
\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{2}
follows from the identity
\langle\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{1},e_{2}\rangle=-\langle\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{2},e_{1}\rangle.
Since
\nabla^{\Sigma}
is the Levi-Civita connection, the Lie bracket
[e_{1},e_{2}]
is concluded by the torsion-free property. Therefore, we have obtained the expressions in (
8
).

(iii)

Using the items (i) and (ii), the curvature tensor is computed by

\displaystyle R^{\Sigma}(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}e_{1}-\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{1}}\nabla^{\Sigma}_{e_{2}}e_{1}+\nabla^{\Sigma}_{[e_{1},e_{2}]}e_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\big(e_{1}(B)+B^{2}\big)e_{2},

which yields
K_{\mathrm{int}}=-\big(e_{1}(B)+B^{2}\big)
. Again by using
[e_{1},e_{2}]=-Be_{2}
in (
8
), we may choose a local coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\Sigma
such that
e_{1}=\partial_{u}
and
e_{2}=\frac{1}{\beta(u,v)}\partial_{v}
, where
\beta(u,v)
is a smooth positive function. Since
[e_{2},e_{1}]=Be_{2}
, it follows that
B=\frac{\beta_{u}}{\beta}
. Integrating, we obtain
\beta(u,v)=p(v)e^{\int B(u,v)du}
for some positive function
p(v)
.

(iv)

K_{\mathrm{int}}
is already been computed in the previous item. The computation of
K_{\mathrm{ext}}
follows from the first equality in (
7
) together with the product of the principal curvatures.

∎

In the next two subsections, we separately investigate the cases where
\Sigma
is totally geodesic or not.

4.1.
Totally geodesic PA surfaces

Assume that
\Sigma
is totally geodesic and
\theta\neq 0,\frac{\pi}{2}
. As emphasized in Remark
3.7
, if
\mathcal{V}
is not parallel then the prescribed angle
\theta
is not necessarily constant. In this case, Proposition
4.1
yields the following result.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a totally geodesic PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field with potential function
f
and
\theta
is non-constant. Then there is a local coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\Sigma
such that

\theta(u)=\cos^{-1}\big(\sech F(u)\big),\quad K_{\mathrm{int}}=-\big(F^{\prime\prime}(u)\coth F(u)+F^{\prime}(u)^{2}\big).

(12)

where
F(u):=\int^{u}f(t)dt+c
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Since
\Sigma
is totally geodesic, its principal curvatures vanish and hence every tangent direction is principal. Then, by (
7
) and (
9
), we have
\theta_{u}=f\cos\theta
. Integrating this equation yields the first identity in (
12
). On the other hand, the function
B
introduced in Proposition
4.1
is now
B=f\csc\theta
. Due to
\csc\theta=\coth F
, it follows that
B=f\coth F
. Substituting this expression into (
10
), we obtain the formula for
K_{\mathrm{int}}
, completing the proof.
∎

Notice that the potential function
f
and the intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
can be also expressed in terms of
\theta
by

f=\theta^{\prime}\sec\theta,\quad K_{\mathrm{int}}=-\big((\log\tan\theta)^{\prime\prime}+(\log\tan\theta)^{\prime 2}\big).

We now provide an example in the hyperbolic ambient space illustrating Proposition
4.2
.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}

\mathbb{H}^{3}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z>0\}

endowed with the conformal metric
\langle,\rangle_{h}=\frac{1}{z^{2}}\langle,\rangle_{e}
, where
\langle,\rangle_{e}
is the Euclidean metric. It is known from
[
2
]
that
\mathcal{V}=-z\partial_{z}
is an anti-torqued vector field on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
whose potential function satisfies
f=1
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
denote the hemi-sphere contained in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
given by

\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=r^{2},z>0\}.

The principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}^{h}
of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
are related to the Euclidean principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}^{e}
by
\kappa_{i}^{h}=z\kappa_{i}^{e}+\langle\mathcal{N}_{e},\partial_{z}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{N}_{e}
denotes the Euclidean unit normal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see
[
22
]
). Since
\kappa_{i}^{e}=-\frac{1}{r}
and
\langle\mathcal{N}_{e},\partial_{z}\rangle=\frac{z}{r}
, we have
\kappa_{i}^{h}=0
. Hence,
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
is totally geodesic in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

The angle
\theta
between the unit normal
\mathcal{N}_{h}
of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
and the anti-torqued vector field
\mathcal{V}
along
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
is given by
\cos\theta=\frac{z}{r}
. In addition, since
\mathbb{H}^{3}
has constant sectional curvature
-1
, the intrinsic curvature of
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
is
K_{\mathrm{int}}=-1
.

Now choose a local coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
given by

x=r\cos g(u)\cos v,\quad y=r\cos g(u)\sin v,\quad z=r\sin g(u),

where
g(u)=\big(\sin^{-1}(\sech u)\big)
. Due to
f=1
and, up to an integration constant, the function
F
given in (
12
) is
F(u)=u
. Consequently, the angle function satisfies
\cos\theta(u)=\sech u
, verifying the first expression in (
12
). Moreover, since
F^{\prime\prime}=0
,
K_{\mathrm{int}}=-1
which also verifies the intrinsic curvature formula in (
12
).

In Example
4.3
, since
\mathbb{H}^{3}
has consant sectional curvature, the intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
of the totally geodesic surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}_{+}
becomes directly
-1
. However, this is not the case in general. Notice that the second equality in (
12
) is indeed an ODE and the existence of solutions is guaranteed by the standard theory of ODEs.

In the following result, we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for a totally geodesic PA surface to have constant intrinsic curvature, expressed in terms of the potential function.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a totally geodesic PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field with nonzero potential function
f
and
\theta
is non-constant. The intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
is some constant
K_{0}
if and only if there is a coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\Sigma
such that

F(u)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sinh^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}-K_{0}}{-K_{0}}}\,\sinh\big(\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)\right),&K_{0}<0,\,c_{1}\geq 0,\\[14.0pt]
\displaystyle\sinh^{-1}\big(\pm\sqrt{c_{1}}\,u+c_{2}\big),&K_{0}=0,\,c_{1}>0,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\sinh^{-1}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}-K_{0}}{K_{0}}}\,\sin\big(\sqrt{K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)\right),&0<K_{0}<c_{1},\end{cases}

(13)

where
c_{1},c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and the potential function is given by
f=F^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Assume that
K_{\mathrm{int}}=K_{0}
. Then, by (
12
), we obtain

F^{\prime\prime}\coth F+F^{\prime 2}+K_{0}=0.

(14)

Since
f\neq 0
, the function
F
is non-constant. If
F^{\prime\prime}=0
, then (
14
) reduces to
F^{\prime 2}+K_{0}=0
, which implies that
K_{0}<0
and
F^{\prime}(u)=\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}
. This corresponds to a particular solution of the form
F(u)=\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}u+c
in (
13
), where
c_{1}=0
and
c_{2}=c
.

We now assume that
F^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
and proceed to solve (
14
). Since
F^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
, we may treat the function
F^{\prime}
as a variable. Therefore, we set
F^{\prime}=P
. By the chain rule, differentiating this equality yields

F^{\prime\prime}=\frac{dF}{du}\frac{dP}{dF}=P\frac{dP}{dF}.

Substituting into (
14
), we conclude

P\frac{dP}{dF}\coth F+P^{2}+K_{0}=0.

We change the variable again and set
Q=P^{2}
. Differentiating, we obtain
\frac{dQ}{dF}=2P\frac{dP}{dF}
, and thus

\frac{1}{Q+K_{0}}\frac{dQ}{dF}=-2\tanh F.

Integrating,

P^{2}=c_{1}\sech^{2}F-K_{0},

(15)

where
c_{1}>0
is some constant.

Finally, we use another change of variable. Introduce
S(u)=\sinh F(u)
. Then
S^{\prime}=F^{\prime}\cosh F
. Multiplying (
15
) by
\cosh^{2}F=1+\sinh^{2}F
, we obtain

S^{\prime 2}=c_{1}-K_{0}(1+S^{2})=c_{1}-K_{0}-K_{0}S^{2}.

(16)

The solutions depend on the sign of
K_{0}
.

Case
K_{0}<0
.
Solving (
16
), we obtain

S(u)=\sqrt{\frac{K_{0}-c_{1}}{K_{0}}}\sinh\big(\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}u+c_{2}\big),\quad c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Since
S(u)=\sinh F(u)
, this yields the expression for
F(u)
in the case
K_{0}<0
.

Case
K_{0}=0
.
Equation (
16
) reduces to
S^{\prime 2}=c_{1}
and so
S(u)=\pm\sqrt{c_{1}}u+c_{2}
,
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.
Thus
F(u)=\sinh^{-1}(\sqrt{c_{1}}u+c_{2})
.

Case
K_{0}>0
.
In this case, (
16
) implies
c_{1}>K_{0}
and we derive

S(u)=\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}-K_{0}}{K_{0}}}\sin\big(\sqrt{K_{0}}u+c_{2}\big),\quad c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}.

Again, using
S(u)=\sinh F(u)
, we obtain the required expression for
F(u)
in the case
K_{0}>0
.
∎

Once Theorem
4.4
has been proved, we may conclude from (
13
) that the potential function
f
is given by

f(u)=F^{\prime}(u)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\pm\sqrt{c_{1}-K_{0}}\,\cosh\big(\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)}{\left(1+\frac{c_{1}-K_{0}}{-K_{0}}\sinh^{2}\big(\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)\right)^{1/2}},&K_{0}<0,\,c_{1}\geq 0,\\[16.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\pm\sqrt{c_{1}}}{\left(1+\big(\pm\sqrt{c_{1}}\,u+c_{2}\big)^{2}\right)^{1/2}},&K_{0}=0,\,c_{1}>0,\\[16.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{c_{1}-K_{0}}\,\cos\big(\sqrt{K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)}{\left(1+\frac{c_{1}-K_{0}}{K_{0}}\sin^{2}\big(\sqrt{K_{0}}(u+c_{2})\big)\right)^{1/2}},&0<K_{0}<c_{1}.\end{cases}

(17)

Returning to Example
4.3
, the case
K_{\mathrm{int}}=-1
corresponds to the choice
c_{1}=0
in the family of solutions with
K_{\mathrm{int}}<0
in (
17
).

Next, consider the horizontal plane
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
given by
\Sigma:\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:z=1\},
which is a totally geodesic surface. A polar coordinate system on
\Sigma
is given by
(x,y)=u(\cos v,\sin v)
. Let
\Phi
be the position vector. Then,
\mathcal{V}=\frac{\Phi}{|\Phi|}
is an anti-torqued vector field along
\Sigma
with potential function
f=\frac{1}{|\Phi|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+u^{2}}}
, which corresponds to the case
K_{\mathrm{int}}=0
in (
17
), where
c_{1}=1
and
c_{2}=0
.

4.2.
Non-totally geodesic PA surfaces

Even if
\Sigma
is not totally geodesic, it is still possible to characterize such surfaces under the assumption of constant intrinsic curvature. Let
\Sigma
be a PA surface satisfying the conditions of Proposition
4.1
. If the intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
is a constant
K_{0}
, then from (
10
) we obtain

B_{u}+B^{2}+K_{0}=0.

According to the sign of
K_{0}
, its solutions are given by

B(u,v)=\frac{f+\kappa_{2}\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}=\begin{cases}\sqrt{K_{0}}\,\tan\big(q(v)-\sqrt{K_{0}}\,u\big),&K_{0}>0,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{u+q(v)},&K_{0}=0,\\[10.0pt]
\sqrt{-K_{0}}\,\tanh\big(q(v)+\sqrt{-K_{0}}\,u\big)\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}},&K_{0}<0,\end{cases}

(18)

where
q(v)
is an arbitrary smooth function.

Particularly, it is worth investigating the case
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, because in this case the potential function
f
coincides with the function
B
. This leads to the following result.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\theta)
, where
\mathcal{V}
is an anti-torqued vector field with nonzero potential function
f
. If
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, then
\Sigma
is an extrinsically flat ruled surface. Moreover, the intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
is a constant
K_{0}
if and only if there is a coordinate system
(u,v)
on
\Sigma
such that

f(u,v)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{K_{0}}\,\tan\big(q(v)-\sqrt{K_{0}}\,u\big),&K_{0}>0,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{u+q(v)},&K_{0}=0,\\[10.0pt]
\sqrt{-K_{0}}\,\tanh\big(q(v)-\sqrt{-K_{0}}\,u\big)\quad\textnormal{or}\quad\pm\sqrt{-K_{0}},&K_{0}<0,\end{cases}

(19)

where
q(v)
is an arbitrary smooth function.

Proof.

Due to
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, it follows from (
3
) that
\mathcal{V}
is tangent to
\Sigma
and defines a principal direction with vanishing principal curvature. This yields that
K_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
and that the hypothesis of Proposition
4.1
is satisfied. Moreover, by Proposition
4.1
, we have
B=f
. Therefore, the result follows from (
18
).
∎

Assume that
\Sigma
is a PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\frac{\pi}{2})
. From Theorem
4.5
, it follows that
K_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
. By the Gauss equation, if the ambient space is a space form, then the sectional curvature
K_{\mathrm{sec}}
is constant and consequently the intrinsic curvature
K_{\mathrm{int}}
is also constant. For instance, we have the following:

Example 4.6
.

(i)

Let
\Phi
be the position vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\mathcal{V}=\frac{\Phi}{|\Phi|}
defines an anti-torqued vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Consider a cone
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with vertex at the origin. The vector field
\mathcal{V}
is tangent to
\Sigma
. In our setting,
\Sigma
is a PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\frac{\pi}{2})
. A local parametrization of
\Sigma
is given by
X(u,v)=u\beta(v)
, where
\beta\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
is an arclength parametrized curve. Since the potential function of
\mathcal{V}
is
f=\frac{1}{|\Phi|}
, its restriction to
\Sigma
satisfies
(f\circ X)(u,v)=\frac{1}{u}
. This is consistent with (
19
) in the case
K_{0}=0
.

(ii)

Similar to Example
4.3
, we consider the upper half-space model of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Let
\mathcal{V}=-z\partial_{z}
be an anti-torqued vector field on
\mathbb{H}^{3}
with potential function equal to
1
. Then a cylinder
\Sigma
, whose rulings are parallel to
\partial_{z}
, is a PA surface with
(\mathcal{V},\frac{\pi}{2})
. This corresponds to the case
K_{0}=-1
in (
19
).

As a final observation, we consider the case where the vector field
\mathcal{V}
is parallel along
\Sigma
. If
\Sigma
is totally umbilical, then every tangent direction to
\Sigma
is principal and the principal curvatures coincide, say
\kappa\neq 0
. Hence, the assumption of Proposition
4.1
is satisfied. It follows from (
7
) that
\kappa=\theta^{\prime}
and
B=\theta^{\prime}\cot\theta.
Substituting into (
10
), we get

K_{\mathrm{int}}=\theta^{\prime\prime}\cot\theta+\theta^{\prime 2},\quad K_{\mathrm{ext}}=\theta^{\prime 2},

and therefore
K_{\mathrm{sec}}=\theta^{\prime\prime}\cot\theta.
In particular, if
K_{\mathrm{ext}}
is constant, then
\theta^{\prime}
is constant and hence
\theta^{\prime\prime}=0
. Consequently,

K_{\mathrm{sec}}=0\quad\text{and}\quad K_{\mathrm{int}}=K_{\mathrm{ext}}.

A basic example illustrating this situation is the standard sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of radius
r
, whose principal curvatures are constant and equal to
1/r
.
