Propensity Score Methods in Rhinologic Observational Research: Principles, Applications, Extensions, and Practical Considerations
Authors: Jae Hoon Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for evaluating treatment efficacy because randomization minimizes measured and unmeasured confounding. In rhinology, however, RCTs are often impractical because of ethical constraints, patient preferences, financial limitations, and logistical challenges related to surgical interventions or long-term follow-up. Consequently, observational studies based on real-world data have become increasingly important for comparative effectiveness research. Despite their practical advantages, observational studies are inherently vulnerable to confounding by indication, selection bias, prevalent-user bias, and immortal time bias. Propensity score (PS) methods were developed to mitigate measured confounding and improve the internal validity of non-randomized comparisons. Since their introduction, PS approaches have expanded beyond traditional matching to include advanced weighting strategies, such as inverse probability of treatment weighting, overlap weighting, and doubly robust estimation. This review provides a clinician-oriented overview of PS methods, including their conceptual foundations, practical implementation, study design considerations, target population implications, diagnostic tools, strengths, limitations, and modern methodological extensions. The review emphasizes the application of these methods to rhinologic research, in which treatment allocation is frequently influenced by disease severity and patient characteristics.Keywords: Propensity score matching, Inverse probability of treatment weighting, Overlap weighting

INTRODUCTION

Observational research has become increasingly important in rhinology because many clinically relevant questions cannot readily be addressed through randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Surgical interventions, biologic therapies, and long-term disease management strategies are often evaluated using retrospective cohort studies, multicenter registries, administrative claims databases, or electronic medical records. Compared with RCTs, these real-world data sources can offer larger sample sizes, broader patient representation, and longer follow-up periods [1,2].

Unlike RCTs, however, observational studies do not allocate treatment randomly. In clinical practice, treatment selection is influenced by disease severity, symptom burden, prior treatment failure, comorbidities, anatomical considerations, socioeconomic factors, and institutional preferences. These baseline differences may independently influence outcomes and thereby introduce confounding [3].

This problem is particularly relevant in rhinology. Patients who undergo endoscopic sinus surgery generally have more severe chronic rhinosinusitis and greater symptom burden than medically managed controls. Similarly, biologic therapies for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps are often prescribed for patients with refractory disease, asthma, or eosinophilic inflammation. Therefore, direct comparisons between treatment groups may reflect baseline differences in disease severity rather than treatment effects.

Propensity score (PS) methods were introduced by Rosenbaum and Rubin [4] in 1983 as a statistical strategy to reduce confounding in observational research. Since then, PS methods have become central to comparative effectiveness research across multiple medical specialties. More recently, the causal inference literature has emphasized that appropriate study design—including active-comparator and new-user frameworks—is equally important for valid causal estimation [5–7].

This review aims to provide a clinically accessible yet methodologically rigorous overview of PS methods for rhinologists and clinician researchers.

Observational study design before PS approaches

Contemporary causal inference frameworks emphasize that statistical adjustment cannot compensate for fundamental flaws in study design. Observational studies should therefore be designed, whenever possible, to emulate hypothetical randomized trials [5]. One major source of bias in observational research is confounding by indication: patients who receive a particular therapy often differ systematically from those who do not. For example, comparing biologics for severe nasal polyposis with untreated controls may yield biased estimates because the decision to prescribe biologics is influenced by disease severity.

To reduce this bias, the contemporary pharmacoepidemiologic literature strongly recommends active-comparator designs [6]. Rather than comparing treatment with non-treatment, investigators compare two clinically plausible therapeutic alternatives used for similar indications. This approach improves comparability between groups and more closely approximates randomization. Another key design feature is the new-user design. Including prevalent users may introduce survivor bias because patients who have already tolerated therapy may differ systematically from newly treated patients. Prevalent-user cohorts may also generate immortal time bias when part of the follow-up period is incorrectly classified [8]. Therefore, whenever feasible, observational studies should include only patients at treatment initiation. The active-comparator, new-user framework combines these principles and is increasingly regarded as the preferred design for real-world comparative effectiveness research [5–7]. Although this approach may reduce sample size, it substantially improves internal validity and causal interpretability.

Conceptual basis of propensity scoring

A PS is the probability that a patient receives a specific treatment conditional on observed baseline covariates [4].

where e(X) denotes the estimated PS for a patient with characteristics X, P denotes probability, T=1 indicates receipt of the treatment of interest, and X represents observed baseline covariates, such as age, gender, disease severity, and comorbidities.

In practical terms, the PS summarizes multiple confounding variables into a single balancing measure. Patients with similar PS values should have similar measured baseline characteristics, regardless of the treatment they received. The central principle underlying PS methods is the balancing property [4]. Conditional on the PS, measured baseline covariates are similarly distributed between treatment groups. PS methods therefore attempt to mimic selected features of randomization by creating more comparable treatment groups.

Importantly, PS methods adjust only for measured confounders. Unmeasured variables, such as physician judgment, patient motivation, frailty, and unrecorded disease severity, may still bias results. Consequently, PS methods should not be interpreted as equivalent substitutes for RCTs.

Variables selected for PS estimation should reflect clinically meaningful factors associated with treatment assignment and outcomes. PS modeling is not intended simply to maximize predictive accuracy; its primary objective is to achieve covariate balance between treatment groups. Accordingly, variable selection should prioritize clinical relevance over automated statistical selection procedures.

More recently, machine learning methods, such as generalized boosted models, random forests, and Bayesian additive regression trees, have been introduced for PS estimation [9]. Although these approaches may improve predictive performance and more effectively model nonlinear relationships, their reduced interpretability may limit their practical appeal for clinicians.

Propensity score matching

Propensity score matching (PSM) remains the most widely recognized and clinically intuitive PS method. In PSM, treated patients are matched with untreated patients who have similar PS values, usually through nearest-neighbor or caliper matching algorithms [3,10]. Matching is often appealing to clinicians because the resulting cohorts resemble traditional matched clinical studies and are relatively straightforward to interpret. In many settings, matching yields excellent covariate balance and permits transparent presentation of baseline characteristics.

However, matching fundamentally changes the study’s target population. Patients who cannot be adequately matched are excluded from the analysis. Methodological studies have demonstrated that matching may exclude substantial portions of the original cohort, particularly when baseline differences between groups are large [10,11]. This issue has important clinical implications. In rhinology, for example, patients with the most severe disease may have no comparable control patients and may therefore be excluded during matching. Consequently, the resulting treatment effect estimate may apply primarily to patients in clinical equipoise rather than to the entire eligible population. Although matching improves interpretability and often reduces bias effectively, it may also reduce statistical precision and external validity because unmatched patients are discarded [11].

Inverse probability of treatment weighting

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) uses a fundamentally different approach. Rather than excluding patients, IPTW retains the entire cohort and weights each patient by the inverse probability of the observed treatment assignment [12].

where Wi denotes the weight assigned to patient i, Ti indicates treatment assignment (Ti=1 for treated patients and Ti=0 for untreated patients), and e(Xi) represents the PS, or the estimated probability that patient i receives the treatment based on baseline characteristics.

Conceptually, IPTW creates a pseudo-population in which treatment assignment is independent of measured baseline covariates. The main advantage of IPTW is that it preserves all study participants and estimates average treatment effects for the broader sampled population. This approach may improve generalizability compared with matching-based analyses [10].

However, IPTW can be problematic when extreme PS values are present. Patients who receive treatments that are unexpected given their baseline characteristics receive disproportionately large weights, which may destabilize estimates and inflate variance. Comparative methodological studies have demonstrated that IPTW may perform poorly when the overlap between treatment groups is limited [2].

For example, a very frail patient who unexpectedly undergoes aggressive surgery may exert disproportionate influence on the analysis despite representing a highly atypical clinical scenario. This instability may reduce precision and increase susceptibility to residual bias.

Overlap weighting

Overlap weighting (OW) has recently emerged as an attractive PS method for observational clinical research [13,14]. Unlike IPTW, OW emphasizes patients with a substantial probability of receiving either treatment and down-weights individuals with extreme PS values.

In OW, treated patients are weighted by 1–PS, whereas untreated patients are weighted by PS [14].

where Wi denotes the weight assigned to patient i and e(Xi) represents the PS, or the estimated probability that patient i receives the treatment based on baseline characteristics.

This strategy creates a study population centered on patients in clinical equipoise—that is, patients who could realistically have received either treatment in routine practice. Several advantages follow from this approach. First, OW often achieves nearly exact covariate balance for measured variables [13]. Second, because observations with extreme PS values receive minimal weight, variance and statistical instability are substantially reduced. Third, all patients remain in the analysis, preserving sample size while avoiding many limitations associated with IPTW. Recent reviews have suggested that OW may best mimic the characteristics of pragmatic randomized trials by simultaneously optimizing target population relevance, covariate balance, and precision [10,13]. Nevertheless, clinicians may find the concept of an “overlap population” less intuitive than traditional matched cohorts. In addition, the boundary of the clinical equipoise population is determined statistically by the observed PS distribution and may therefore vary across datasets and study settings.

In rhinology, OW may be particularly useful when comparing interventions subject to strong baseline treatment selection bias, such as surgery versus biologic therapy for severe chronic rhinosinusitis.

Stratification and doubly robust methods

PS stratification divides patients into strata according to the PS distribution, commonly into quintiles. Treatment effects are then estimated within each stratum [15]. Although stratification is relatively simple to implement, it may leave residual confounding within strata and may perform less well when outcome events are infrequent [2].

Doubly robust approaches combine PS adjustment with multivariable outcome regression [16]. These methods provide additional protection against model misspecification because valid estimation may still be possible if either the PS model or the outcome model is correctly specified. However, doubly robust methods are statistically more complex and less intuitive for many clinicians.

Target population and clinical interpretation

A key but often overlooked consideration in applying PS methods is that each approach estimates treatment effects for a distinct target population [10,13]. PSM generally estimates treatment effects among patients who can be successfully matched. IPTW estimates effects in the broader eligible sampled population. In contrast, OW estimates effects among patients in clinical equipoise. Investigators must therefore carefully consider which target population is most clinically relevant. No single PS method is universally superior; rather, the optimal approach depends on study objectives, baseline imbalance, overlap characteristics, and desired clinical interpretation [2].

Diagnostic assessment

Diagnostic assessment is essential for all PS methods. Simply reporting that a PS method was performed is insufficient. Standardized mean difference is the preferred metric for evaluating covariate balance because it is independent of sample size [17]. Values below 0.1 are generally considered acceptable, whereas values below 0.05 indicate excellent balance. Visualization tools, including Love plots, PS histograms, and density distributions, are also strongly recommended because they allow intuitive assessment of overlap and residual imbalance [17]. Notably, poor overlap between treatment groups may indicate that meaningful causal comparison is fundamentally difficult regardless of statistical adjustment [2].

Strengths and limitations of PS methods

PS methods offer several advantages for observational rhinologic research: they improve covariate balance, reduce measured confounding, facilitate comparative effectiveness studies using real-world data, and enhance transparency in observational analyses. Nevertheless, important limitations remain. PS methods cannot adjust for unmeasured confounders, physician preference, coding inaccuracies, frailty, or unknown biological variables. Incorrect model specification may also introduce additional bias [2,9]. Therefore, even sophisticated PS methods should be interpreted cautiously and should not be regarded as complete substitutes for randomized evidence.

Applications in rhinology

Applications of PS methods in rhinology continue to expand, particularly in comparative effectiveness studies that use real-world observational data. Because treatment allocation in rhinology is often strongly influenced by baseline disease severity, physician judgment, anatomical complexity, and comorbid conditions, observational studies in this field are especially vulnerable to confounding by indication and selection bias. Consequently, PS methods may substantially improve comparability between treatment groups and strengthen causal inference in retrospective analyses.

One of the most common applications of PS methods in rhinology is the comparative effectiveness analysis of endoscopic sinus surgery versus continued medical therapy for chronic rhinosinusitis. Patients selected for surgery generally have more severe symptoms, worse radiological findings, and more refractory disease after prior medical therapy than nonsurgical controls. Direct outcome comparisons are therefore highly susceptible to baseline imbalance. In such settings, PSM or OW may improve comparability between groups and reduce confounding related to disease severity.

Comparative studies of biologic therapy versus surgery for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps represent another important application. Patients receiving biologics frequently have severe eosinophilic inflammation, comorbid asthma, aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, and prior surgical failure. Because treatment allocation strongly reflects disease severity and inflammatory phenotype, OW may be particularly useful for focusing analyses on patients in clinical equipoise who realistically could have received either intervention.

PS methods may also be useful for evaluating risk factors associated with revision sinus surgery. For example, patients undergoing extensive surgery may differ systematically from those receiving more limited procedures with respect to baseline disease severity, polyp burden, prior surgical history, or inflammatory characteristics. PS adjustment may therefore reduce selection bias and improve the validity of retrospective comparisons evaluating long-term surgical outcomes.

Similarly, in studies of septoplasty or turbinate reduction, surgeons may select operative techniques according to anatomical complexity, concomitant nasal valve pathology, or severity of nasal obstruction. This non-random treatment allocation may substantially influence postoperative outcomes. In these situations, PS methods may help reduce baseline imbalance and improve the interpretability of comparative observational studies.

PS methods may also be valuable in sleep-related nasal surgery research, in which patient selection frequently depends on baseline obstructive sleep apnea severity, body mass index, anatomical findings, and physician preference. As healthcare databases and multicenter rhinologic registries continue to expand, familiarity with PS methodology will become increasingly important for rhinologists who conduct and interpret contemporary real-world comparative effectiveness research.

CONCLUSION

PS methods have become an essential framework for contemporary observational research in rhinology. Although RCTs remain the gold standard for causal inference, carefully designed observational studies that incorporate active-comparator, new-user designs and appropriately selected PS methods can substantially improve the validity of causal inference. Traditional matching remains highly interpretable and clinically intuitive, whereas IPTW provides broader population inference. More recently, OW has emerged as a particularly attractive strategy because it improves balance, precision, and clinical relevance simultaneously. Ultimately, the successful application of PS methods requires thoughtful study design, rigorous diagnostic assessment, careful interpretation of target populations, and recognition of residual limitations. As rhinologists increasingly engage in real-world evidence research, understanding these principles has become essential.
