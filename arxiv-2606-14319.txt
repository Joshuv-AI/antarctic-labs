Three subgroup common transversal in abelian groups
Maria Loukaki, Emmanouil Spyridakis

Abstract
. We completely characterize when three equal-order subgroups of a finite abelian group share a common transversal. Consequently, we determine when three full-rank lattices of equal volume with a discrete sum in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} admit a common fundamental domain, answering, for n = 3 n=3 , an open question from 1997.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
20K01, 20D60, 05D15, 52C22, 52B20

1.
Introduction

In the 1950s, Steinhaus
[
12
]
asked whether there exists a planar set that
tiles
the plane when
translated
by any full-rank lattice obtained by rotating the integer lattice around the origin.
In the language used in this paper, Steinhaus asked if there exists a common transversal
E
of all full-rank lattices
L_{\theta}=R_{\theta}\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
where
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
R_{\theta}
denotes rotation by
\theta
around the origin. That is, whether there exists a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
that covers all cosets modulo each
L_{\theta}
exactly once.
An affirmative answer to the Steinhaus problem was given only in this century
[
8
]
. But the variation of the problem where the subset of the plane is asked to be Lebesgue measurable
has sparked much more interest and is still open. The best result up to date for the measurable problem can be found in
[
10
]
.

The Steinhaus problem extends naturally to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d>2
. A full-rank lattice
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Every full-rank lattice
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be expressed as
A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for some invertible matrix
A
, with volume
\text{vol}(L)=|\det(A)|
. A
transversal
(or a
fundamental domain
) of
L=A\mathbb{Z}^{d}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set containing exactly one representative from each coset of
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
A common example of a fundamental domain of
L
is the so-called fundamental parallelepiped of
L=A\mathbb{Z}^{d}

P_{L}=A[0,1)^{d}

Recent results in
[
5
]
and
[
13
]
demonstrate the existence of a bounded and measurable common fundamental domain for two full-rank lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of equal volume.

This raises the question: Does a bounded and measurable common fundamental domain exist for three full-rank lattices of equal volume? Let us remark here that the question of finding a set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is a common transversal for a finite number of full-rank lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
first appeared in
[
9
]
. In the same paper Kolountzakis gave an example of three full-rank lattices of equal volume that do not admit a common fundamental domain. That being said, these full-rank lattices are not rotations of the full-rank lattice of the integers.
Furthermore, in
[
9
]
the following problem was first posed:

When does a finite set of subgroups
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
of the same finite index in an abelian group
G
admit a common transversal?

It is worth mentioning that when
n=2
, Hall’s Marriage Theorem guarantees the existence of a common transversal for two subgroups of the same index in
G
. But when more than two subgroups are involved common transversals do not always exist. Indeed take
G=\mathbf{Z}_{p}\times\mathbf{Z}_{p}
, for any prime
p
, and consider all its subgroups of order
p
. These are
p+1
in total and their union is equal to
G
. They cannot share a common transversal because no element of
G
lies outside their union. Thus, any common transversal (which, after normalization, can be assumed to contain the
0
element of
G
) would contain at least one more element from one of the subgroups.

Nevertheless, it was proved
[
9
, Thm. 1]
that if the sum
A_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}
of the subgroups, is direct then they always share a common transversal in
G
. (We say that a sum of subgroups
A_{1}+\ldots+A_{n}
is direct when every element
a\in A_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}
can be written in a unique way as
a=a_{i}+\cdots+a_{n}
, with
a_{i}\in A_{i}
). Consequently it was shown
[
9
, Thm. 4]
that if the full-rank lattices
L_{1},\cdots,L_{n}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
have the same volume and both
\sum L_{i}
and
\sum L_{i}^{*}
(the sum of their duals) are direct sums then they have a Lebesgue measurable common transversal, while if only the sum of
L_{i}
is direct then a bounded (but not necessarily measurable) common transversal was constructed
[
9
, 3.2]
.
The question that was also posed in
[
9
, 3.1]
, and is of interest in this paper, is whether full-rank lattices
L_{1},\cdots,L_{n}
sharing a common transversal can be characterized when they do not sum directly.

Partial results for the first question were given in
[
1
]
under the hypothesis that the subgroups
X,Y,Z
are all cyclic (see
[
1
, Thm. B]
) or that
X,Y,Z
are all complemented in
G
(see
[
1
, Thm. A]
).
In particular, after factoring out by their intersection, it was proved in
[
1
, Thm. B]
that three cyclic subgroups
X,Y
, and
Z
lack a common transversal in their sum
G
if and only if they exhibit a structure analogous to the Klein group. That is, precisely when their Sylow
2
-subgroups
X_{2},Y_{2},Z_{2}
are all cyclic, isomorphic to some
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
, and the Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
is
G_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}}
, the direct sum of any two of
X_{2},Y_{2}
and
Z_{2}
.

The main result of this paper fully resolves the problem of the existence of a common transversal for three subgroups of equal order inside a finite abelian group. In particular, we prove the the only problematic scenario where three subgroups
X,Y,Z
lack a common transversal in
G
is precisely the one that appeared in the cyclic case, i.e. when (after factoring out by their intersection) the Sylow
2
-subgroups
X_{2},Y_{2},Z_{2}
are all non-trivial cyclic and
G_{2}
is the direct sum of any two of them. Hence, the existence of a common transversal for
X,Y,Z
in
G
depends solely on the nature and position of their Sylow
2
-sugroups within
G
.
Our main result reads:

Theorem A
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group with subgroups
X,Y,Z
of equal index, and let
N=X\cap Y\cap Z
. Then
X,Y,Z
have no common transversal in
G
if and only if
G/N
has even order and the Sylow
2
-subgroup
(G/N)_{2}
of
G/N
satisfies

(G/N)_{2}=(X/N)_{2}\oplus(Y/N)_{2}=(X/N)_{2}\oplus(Z/N)_{2}=(Y/N)_{2}\oplus(Z/N)_{2},

where
(X/N)_{2}
,
(Y/N)_{2}
, and
(Z/N)_{2}
are cyclic Sylow
2
-subgroups of
X/N
,
Y/N
, and
Z/N
, respectively.

If the index
[G:X]
is odd, then
G_{2}\leq X_{2}
and similarly
G_{2}\leq Y_{2},Z_{2}
. Hence
G_{2}\leq N
, so
G/N
has odd order and the obstruction in
A
cannot occur. Thus

Corollary 1
.

Subgroups
X,Y,Z
of a finite abelian group
G
with equal odd index always share a common transversal in
G
.

The proof of the theorem above reduces to a perfect matching problem, utilizing abelian group maps that share characteristics similar to those of a
complete map
. A bijection
\theta:G\to G
is a complete mapping of
G
if the mapping
x\to x\theta(x)
is also a bijection. It was shown by Hall and Paige in 1955 (
[
6
]
)
that any finite group with nontrivial cyclic Sylow
2
-subgroup does not admit complete mappings and by Wilcox-Evans-Bray in 2009 (
[
14
]
,
[
3
]
) that the converse also holds.
The primary challenge with the maps needed for the proof of our Theorem A, lies with the prime
p=2

as detailed in Section
2
.

Let us now turn our attention to full-rank lattices and see how our result on finite groups translates.
Consider three full-rank lattices
K,L,M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of equal volume such that
L+M+K=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, via a non-singular linear transformation. In other words,
K,L,M
are three full-rank lattices of equal volume with a discrete sum in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Observe that in this case the sublattice
H:=K\cap L\cap M
is also a full-rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Indeed, the map

F:\mathbb{Z}^{d}/H\to(\mathbb{Z}^{d}/K)\times(\mathbb{Z}^{d}/L)\times(\mathbb{Z}^{d}/M)

defined as
F(n+H)=(n+K,n+L,n+M)
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is injective. Thus

[\mathbb{Z}^{d}:H]=|\mathbb{Z}^{d}/H|\leq|\mathbb{Z}^{d}/K||\mathbb{Z}^{d}/L||\mathbb{Z}^{d}/M|=\text{vol}(K)\text{vol}(L)\text{vol}(M)<\infty

since
K,L,M
are full-rank lattices.

Using
A
now we get

Theorem B
.

Let
K,L,M
be three full-rank lattices on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of equal volume such that
K+L+M
is a full-rank lattice. Denote their intersection by
H=K\cap L\cap M
and the quotient groups
X:=K/H
,
Y:=L/H
,
Z:=M/H
inside
G:=(L+M+K)/H
. Then
K,L,M
do not share a common fundamental domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if the Sylow
2
-subgroups of
X,Y,Z
are non-trivial cyclic, pairwise disjoint satisfying

G_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Y_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Z_{2}=Y_{2}\oplus Z_{2}
.

Finally, let us remark that if
K,L
, and
M
share a common fundamental domain within the full-rank lattice
K+L+M
, then a common fundamental domain for
K,L
, and
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be constructed as a finite union of polytopes. Specifically, if
F
is a finite common transversal of
K,L
, and
M
in
K+L+M
, then
F+P
, where
P
is a fundamental parallelepiped of the full-rank lattice
K+L+M
, serves as such a common fundamental domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and is a finite union of polytopes.

Throughout this paper,
G
denotes a finite abelian group, and
X,Y,Z
are subgroups of
G
of the same order, whose common transversals are the subject of investigation. We denote by
\mathbf{T}_{G}(X,Y,Z)
the set of common transversals of
X,Y,Z
in
G
. We also write
G_{p}
for the (unique as
G
is abelian) Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
for any prime
p
that divides the order
|G|
.

This paper is structured as follows: Section
2
establishes necessary bijections for subsequent proofs. We make some reductions and provide the notation we will be using in Section
3
. Sections
4
,
5
, and
6
focus on proving first some special cases and ultimately the general case of our main result (Theorem A) for abelian
p
-groups. This approach, on
p
-groups instead of general abelian groups, leverages the bijections from Section
2
and allows order inequalities to translate into divisibility relations, ensuring the existence of subgroups of the desired order. Specifically, Theorem A is proven for the special case of an abelian
p
-group
G=X\oplus Y
in Section
4
, and for
G=X+Y
in Section
5
. The proof of Theorem A for a general
p
-group
G=X+Y+Z
is presented in Section
6
. In Section
7
we complete the proofs of Theorem A and B by demonstrating that three subgroups
X,Y,Z
in a finite abelian group
G
share a common transversal if and only if their Sylow
p
-subgroups
X_{p},Y_{p},Z_{p}
share a common transversal in
G_{p}
for every prime divisor
p
of
|G|
. Finally, Section
8
provides examples of full-rank lattices that do not share a common transversal.

2.
Bijections

While common transversals exhibit algebraic structure in special cases, such as when the underlying subgroups are complemented, their existence generally reduces to a perfect matching problem, and as such have been treated in this paper.
This section serves exactly this purpose; to
provide all the required machinery, through bijections, that will be needed in the
forthcoming proofs.
The starting point, being Hall’s Theorem
[
7
]
according to which if
A=\{a_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is an abelian finite group of order
n
and
\{b_{1},\cdots,b_{n}\}
are elements of
G
(not necessarily distinct ) with
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}=0

then there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{n}
with

\{a_{\sigma(i)}+b_{i}\mid i\leq n\}=A=\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n}\}.

Additionally, we recall Hall-Paige Theorem (we only need it here for abelian groups) according to which every finite group
G
admits a complete map (i.e., a bijection
\theta:G\to G
with
x+\theta(x)
being also a bijection) if and only if
G
has a trivial or non-cyclic Sylow
2
-group.
For a detailed analysis and the history of the Hall-Paige Theorem one might see
[
4
]
.
The maps presented in this section, are based on Hall’s or Hall-Paige theorem and share a similar structure. But they are weaker forms of complete maps, with cyclic
2
-groups often presenting a challenge.

Let us note that for odd order groups, all the required maps constructed below could simply replaced by the bijection
\phi:G\to G
where
\phi(x)=-x
. However, the prime 2 necessitates more complex constructions. Therefore, while keeping in mind the simplified bijection available for odd order groups, we state and prove the lemmas and propositions of this section in a general way, regardless of parity.

Our first result of this type, that is actually a straightforward application of Hall’s theorem, is the following.

Lemma 1
.

Assume
G
is an abelian
p
-group and let
1\neq H<G
be a proper subgroup of
G
and
S
a transversal of
H
in
G
, so that every
g\in G
is written uniquely as
g=s+h
for some
s\in S
and
h\in H
. Then there exists a bijection
\psi:G\to G
so that
\psi(s+h)-h
is also a bijection of
G
.
If
G
is not a cyclic
2
-group, then there exists a bijection
\phi:G\to G
so that
\phi(s+h)-s
is also a bijection of
G
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{s_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
and
H=\{h_{j}\mid j\leq|H|\}
. For every element in
g_{i,j}\in G
, we write
g_{i,j}=s_{i}+h_{j}
with
s_{i}\in S
and
h_{j}\in H
uniquely determined. Let
B=\{b_{i,j}=-h_{j}\mid i\leq n,j\leq|H|\}
be the sequence where each
-h_{j}\in H
appears
[G:H]
times. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq n,\\
j\leq|H|\end{subarray}}{b_{i,j}}=[G:H]\sum_{j\leq|H|}(-h_{j})=[G:H]\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=0.

The last equality holds because
\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=0
, unless H is a cyclic
2
-group, in which case the sum is its unique involution. But then
[G:H]\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=0
since
[G:H]\geq 2
is an even number.
Now we apply Hall’s Theorem (Main Theorem in
[
7
]
) to the set
B
. Hence there exists a bijection
\psi:G\to G
so that

\psi(g_{i,j})+b_{i,j}=\psi(g_{i,j})-h_{j},

is a bijection of
G
(where
g_{i,j}=s_{i}+h_{j}
).

Assume now that
G
is not a cyclic
2
-group, and consider the sequence
B^{\prime}=\{b^{\prime}_{i,j}=s_{i}\mid i\leq n,j\leq|H|\}
. Observe that
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq n,\\
j\leq|H|\end{subarray}}g_{i,j}=0
, because
G
is not a cyclic
2
-group. Hence

0=\sum_{i,j}g_{i,j}=\sum_{i,j}s_{i}+h_{j}=|H|\sum_{i\leq n}s_{i}+[G:H]\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=|H|\sum_{i\leq n}s_{i}.

The last equality holds because
\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=0
unless
H
is a cyclic
2
-group, in which case the sum is its unique involution. In the latter case,
[G:H]\sum_{j\leq|H|}h_{j}=0
since
[G:H]\geq 2
is even.
Therefore,

\sum_{i\leq n,j\leq|H|}b^{\prime}_{i,j}=|H|\sum_{i\leq n}s_{i}=0.

Now we apply the main Theorem in
[
7
]
to the set
B^{\prime}
. Hence there exists
a bijection
f^{\prime}:G\to G
so that

f^{\prime}(g_{i,j})+b^{\prime}_{i,j}=f^{\prime}(g_{i,j})+s_{i}

is a bijection of
G
(where
g_{i,j}=s_{i}+h_{j}
).

Then the function
\phi:G\to G
defined as

\phi(g_{i,j})=f^{\prime}(g_{i,j})+s_{i},

is clearly the desired bijection.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Let us note that the requirement of the second part of the lemma that wants
G
to be a non-cyclic
2
-group is essential; if
G
is a cyclic
2
-group, there is no bijection
\phi:G\to G
with
\phi(s+h)-s
being also a bjection of
G
.
Indeed, a necessary condition for such a bijection to exist is that

\sum_{g\in G}g=\sum_{s\in S,h\in H}(\phi(h+s)-s)=\sum_{s\in S,h\in H}\phi(h+s)-\sum_{s\in S,h\in H}s=\sum_{g\in G}g+|H|\left(\sum_{s\in S}s\right)

where the last equality holds because
\phi
is a bijection on
G
. Thus, we must have that

|H|\left(\sum_{s\in S}s\right)=0

Since
G
is a cyclic
2
-group and
H
a proper subgroup,
G/H
is a non-trivial cyclic
2
-group. Consequently,
\sum_{s\in S}s
, where
S
is a complete system of representatives, represents the unique coset of order
2
in
G/H
. Every element in this coset has order
2|H|
, thus
|H|\left(\sum_{s\in S}s\right)\neq 0
. This contradiction establishes that the requirement of a non-cyclic 2-group
G
is necessary for the second part of the lemma.

Our next result reads:

Lemma 2
.

Assume
G
is an abelian group
p
-group and
1<A\leq B\leq G
subgroups of
G
. Let
\{g_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
a transversal of
B
in
G
. Assume further that
\{b_{j}\mid j\leq r\}
is a transversal of
A
in
B
and
A=\{a_{t}\mid t\leq s\}
. Then there exists a bijection
f:G\to G
so that for any fixed

we have

f(g_{i}+b_{j}+a_{t})-b_{j}\not\equiv f(g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}}+a_{t})-b_{j^{\prime}}\pmod{A}

for all
(i,j)\neq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Assume first that
A=B
and so
r=1
and
\{b_{j}\}=\{0\}
.
Then the identity map
f:G\to G
with
f(x)=x
satisfies the lemma. Indeed for any fixed

we get
f(g_{i}+a_{t})=g_{i}+a_{t}\equiv g_{i}\pmod{A}
. Since
\{g_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
is a transversal of
A=B
in
G
, the cosets

are distinct. Hence the required inequivalence holds for all
i\neq i^{\prime}
.

Hence we may assume henceforth that
A\neq B
.
If
B/A
is not a cyclic
2
-group, denote its elements by
\{\bar{b}_{j}\mid j\leq r\}
. Then
B/A
satisfies the Hall Paige theorem and thus there exists a permutation
\sigma\in S_{r}
that simultaneously satisfies
\{\bar{b}_{\sigma(j)}\mid j\leq r\}=B/A
and
\{\bar{b}_{\sigma(j)}-\bar{b}_{j}\mid j\leq r\}=B/A
.
If
A=\{a_{t}\mid t\leq s\}
then every element of

g_{i}+b_{j}+a_{t}
for some

. We define
f:G\to G

f(g_{i}+b_{j}+a_{t})=g_{i}+b_{\sigma(j)}+a_{t},

for all
i\leq n,j\leq r
and

.
Clearly

is a bijection of

, as
\sigma
is a permutation. Furthermore,
if
f(g_{i}+b_{j}+a_{t})-b_{j}\equiv f(g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}}+a_{t})-b_{j^{\prime}}\pmod{A}

g_{i}+b_{\sigma(j)}-b_{j}\equiv g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{\sigma(j^{\prime})}-b_{j^{\prime}}\pmod{A}
forcing

(because
g_{i}-g_{i^{\prime}}\in B
). Hence we get
b_{\sigma(j)}-b_{j}\equiv b_{\sigma(j^{\prime})}-b_{j^{\prime}}\pmod{A}
and thus
\bar{b}_{\sigma(j)}-\bar{b}_{j}=\bar{b}_{\sigma(j^{\prime})}-\bar{b}_{j^{\prime}}
contradicting the choice of
\sigma
. So the lemma follows in this case.

We now consider the remaining case where
B/A
is a non-trivial cyclic
2

B/A
is cyclic of order
r=2^{m}
, generated by
\bar{b}_{0}
for some
b_{0}\in B
. So

B/A=\{j\bar{b}_{0}\mid j\leq r\}
and
jb_{0}
are all distinct modulo

, for
j\leq 2^{m}
, while
d:=2^{m}b_{0}\in A
(so

is one of the
a_{t}
). Next, we partition
A

A_{1}

A_{2}
, each of size

(since

is even). Let
\theta:A_{1}\to A_{2}

Consider the map
f:G\to G

f(g_{i}+jb_{0}+a_{t})=\begin{cases}g_{i}+2j\,b_{0}+\theta(a_{t})+d,&\text{for }1\leq j\leq 2^{m-1},\,\,a_{t}\in A_{1}\\

g_{i}+(2j-1)\,b_{0}+a_{t},&\text{for }2^{m-1}+1\leq j\leq 2^{m},\,\,a_{t}\in A_{2}\end{cases}

for every

.
We show that

So let
g\in G
. Since the set
\{g_{i}+jb_{0}\>|\>i\leq n,j\leq r\}

A

for some

(1)

for exactly one

Let’s first assume that

If

Similarly we work for
j

a_{t}\in A_{1}
we get

(2)

\displaystyle f(g_{i}+\frac{j+1}{2}b_{0}+\theta(a_{t}))

\displaystyle=g_{i}+(2\frac{j+1}{2}-1)b_{0}+\theta^{-1}(\theta(a_{t}))+d

(3)

\displaystyle=g_{i}+jb_{0}+a_{t}+d=g_{i}+jb_{0}+a=g.

While if
j
is odd, and
a_{t}\in A_{2}
then

f(g_{i}+(2^{m-1}+\frac{j+1}{2})b_{0}+a_{t})=g_{i}+(2^{m}+j)b_{0}+a_{t}=g.

Hence

The function

.

To prove the stated inequivalence modulo

∎

Let us remark that it is crucial in

that

is a non-trivial subgroup of

. For example, if

and

is a cyclic

-group the function sought by the lemma is a complete map. However, cyclic

Furthermore, the assumption that

is a

and
\mathbf{Z}_{3}\cong A<B
. In this case

and the conclusion of the lemma states that for every

we should have

for some bijection

. But

has order 2 and so the conclusion of the lemma equivalently translates to:

f(a)\in A
if and only if

.
As

.

We complete the section with the following proposition.

Proposition 1
.

Let
G
be an abelian
p
-group. Assume
A\oplus B\leq G
with
|A|=|B|=r>1
and
B=\{b_{j}\mid j\leq r\}
. Assume further that
\{g_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
a transversal of
A\oplus B
in
G
. Then there exists a function
f:G\to G
such that

\displaystyle f(g_{i}+b_{j})

\displaystyle\not\equiv f(g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}})\pmod{A}

\displaystyle f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}

\displaystyle\not\equiv f(g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}})-g_{i^{\prime}}\pmod{B}

for all
(i,j)\neq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
A=\{a_{j}\mid j\leq r\}
and
B=\{b_{j}\mid j\leq r\}
the elements of
A
and
B
in an arbitrary but fixed order. We write
\bar{g}_{i}
for the elements of the quotient group
G/(A\oplus B)
and we also fix their order and their representatives
\{g_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
(that is, the enumeration of
g_{i}
is also fixed).

If
G=A\oplus B
, then
n=1
(that is,
\{g_{i}\}=\{0\}
), and the required map is
f:G\to G
defined by
f(b_{j}+a_{t})=f(b_{j})=b_{j}+a_{j}
for all
j,t\leq r
. Clearly,
f(b_{j})\equiv b_{j}\pmod{A}
, so
f(b_{j})
are distinct for different values of
j
. Likewise, since
f(b_{j})-0=f(b_{j})\equiv a_{j}\pmod{B}
, they are also distinct modulo
B
for different
j
. This proves the proposition in this case. Therefore, from now on we assume
G\neq A\oplus B
.

Define
H:=G/(A\oplus B)\times A
. Then the elements of
H
can be written as

H=\{h_{i,j}=(\bar{g}_{i},a_{j})\mid i\leq n,\ j\leq r\}.

The group
H
is evidently non-cyclic,
so the Hall–Paige theorem applies. Hence there exists a bijection
\phi:H\to H
so that
\phi(h)+h
is also a bijection of
H
. Set

(\bar{g}_{\phi_{1,{i,j}}(i)},a_{\phi_{2,{i,j}}(j)}):=\phi((\bar{g}_{i},a_{j})),

for indices
\phi_{1,i,j}(i)\leq n
and
\phi_{2,i,j}(j)\leq r
.
That
\phi
is a bijection forces

(4)

\{(\phi_{1,{i,j}}(i),\phi_{2,{i,j}}(j))\mid i\leq n,j\leq r\}=\{(i,j)\mid i\leq n,j\leq r\}.

We now want to reformulate the fact that
\phi(h)+h
is a bijection in terms of indices. To begin, for each
i\leq n
and
j\leq r
we set

(5)

\displaystyle(\bar{g}_{\theta_{{1,i,j}}(i)},a_{\theta_{2,i,j}(j)})

\displaystyle:=\phi((\bar{g}_{i},a_{j}))+(\bar{g}_{i},a_{j})

(6)

\displaystyle=(\bar{g}_{\phi_{{1,i,j}}(i)},a_{\phi_{{2,i,j}}(j)})+(\bar{g}_{i},a_{j})=(\bar{g}_{\phi_{{1,i,j}}(i)}+\bar{g}_{i},\,a_{\phi_{{2,i,j}}(j)}+a_{j}),

for suitable indices
\theta_{1,i,j}(i)\leq n
and
\theta_{2,i,j}(j)\leq r
. That the map
\phi(h)+h
is a bijection is equivalent to

(7)

\{(\theta_{1,i,j}(i),\,\,\theta_{2,i,j}(j))\mid i\leq n,\;j\leq r\}=\{(i,j)\mid i\leq n,\;j\leq r\}.

Moreover, (
5
) yields
\bar{g}_{\theta_{1,i,j}(i)}=\bar{g}_{\phi_{1,i,j}(i)}+\bar{g}_{i}
, and hence

(8)

g_{i}+g_{\phi_{1,i,j}(i)}=g_{\theta_{1,i,j}(i)}+\widehat{a}_{i,j}+\widehat{b}_{i,j}

for some
\widehat{a}_{i,j}\in A
and
\widehat{b}_{i,j}\in B
.

Define a map
f:G\to G
by

f(g_{i}+b_{j})=g_{\theta_{1,i,j}(i)}+b_{\theta_{2,i,j}(j)}+\widehat{a}_{i,j}+a_{\phi_{2,i,j}(j)},

and extend it to all of
G
, for instance, by setting
f(g_{i}+b_{j}+a_{t})=f(g_{i}+b_{j})
.
We claim that
f
fulfills the hypotheses of the proposition.

First, modulo
A
we have

f(g_{i}+b_{j})\equiv g_{\theta_{1,i,j}(i)}+b_{\theta_{2,i,j}(j)}\pmod{A},

and so, by (
7
), the elements
f(g_{i}+b_{j})
are pairwise distinct modulo
A
for different pairs
(i,j)
.

To verify the condition modulo
B
, note that, using (
8
), we obtain

f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}=g_{\theta_{1,i,j}(i)}+b_{\theta_{2,i,j}(j)}+\widehat{a}_{i,j}+a_{\phi_{2,i,j}(j)}-g_{i}\equiv g_{\phi_{1,i,j}(i)}+a_{\phi_{2,i,j}(j)}\pmod{B}.

Now (
4
) implies that

f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}\not\equiv f(g_{i^{\prime}}+b_{j^{\prime}})-g_{i^{\prime}}\pmod{B}

whenever
(i,j)\neq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
. This completes the proof of the proposition.
∎

We remark that the proposition does not generalize to arbitrary abelian groups because it relies on the Hall-Paige Theorem applied to the group
H
(as it appears in the proof). In particular if
H
contains a cyclic Sylow 2-subgroup, which can occur when
|G|
involves more than one prime the proof above fails and the proposition itself is not true. For instance, consider
G=\mathbf{Z}_{2}\times\mathbf{Z}_{3}\times\mathbf{Z}_{3}
,
A=\{0\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{3}\times\{0\}
, and
B=\{0\}\times\{0\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{3}
. Here
\{g_{i}\mid i\leq 2\}=\{g_{1}=(0,0,0),g_{2}=(1,0,0)\}
and the conclusion of the proposition states that there exists
f:G\to G
so that the six elements
f(g_{i}+b_{j})
are all distinct modulo
A
and thus cover the 6 cosets of
A
in
G
, where
B=\{b_{j}\mid j\leq 3\}
. Similarly
f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}
cover all the 6 cosets of
B
in
G
. If
f(g_{i}+b_{j})=(x_{i,j},y_{i,j},z_{i,j})\in G
then looking at the cosets of
A
in
G
we conclude that exactly three elements (out of the 6 distinct elements
\{f(g_{i}+b_{j})\}_{i,j}
) have first coordinate
x_{i,j}=0
and the other three have
x_{i,j}=1
. So

(9)

\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}=3\equiv 1\pmod{2}.

Similarly, for the elements
f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}=(\hat{x}_{i,j},\hat{y}_{i,j},\hat{z}_{i,j})
we have three of them having their first coordinate
\hat{x}_{i,j}
equal to
0
and the other three equal to
1
. So

(10)

\sum_{i,j}\hat{x}_{i,j}=3\equiv 1\pmod{2}.

Now notice that

(\hat{x}_{i,j},\hat{y}_{i,j},\hat{z}_{i,j})=f(g_{i}+b_{j})-g_{i}=\begin{cases}f(g_{i}+b_{j})=(x_{i,j},y_{i,j},z_{i,j}),\,\,\text{ if }i=1\\
f(g_{i}+b_{j})-(1,0,0)=(x_{i,j}-1,y_{i,j},z_{i,j}),\,\,\text{ if }i=2.\end{cases}

Hence, (
9
) implies

\sum_{i,j}\hat{x}_{i,j}=\sum_{i,j}x_{i,j}-3\equiv 0\pmod{2}.

This clearly contradicts (
10
), proving that
1
does not extend to arbitrary finite abelian groups.

3.
Reductions and notation

This section presents initial reductions to the general problem and introduces the main players of the proofs that will follow.

3.1.
Reductions

The next two lemmata are well known (see for example
[
9
]
and
[
1
]
) and we only mention them here. The first one shows that it suffices to assume that
G=X+Y+Z
(for its proof one might see
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
).

Lemma 3
.

Let
R
an abelian group (not necessarily finite) and
K,L,M
subgroups of
R
then
\mathbf{T}_{R}(K,L,M)=\emptyset
if and only if
\mathbf{T}_{K+L+M}(K,L,M)=\emptyset
.

The next result enables us to work with quotient groups (see
[
1
, Lemma 2.3]
).

Lemma 4
.

Let
R
be an abelian group and
K,L,M\leq R
with
N\leq K\cap L\cap M
. Then

\mathbf{T}_{R}(K,L,M)\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\mathbf{T}_{R/N}(K/N,L/N,M/N)\neq\emptyset
.
In particular,

\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}\in\mathbf{T}_{R}(K,L,M)
if and only if
\{g_{1}+N,\ldots,g_{m}+N\}\in\mathbf{T}_{R/N}(K/N,L/N,M/N)
.

An immediate consequence of the above lemma is that we can always assume that
X\cap Y\cap Z=\{0\}
.

The first simple but interesting result in the direction of our main theorem is the special case where the (common) index of
X
(and
Y
,
Z
) in
G
is a prime
p
.

Lemma 5
.

Assume
G
is finite abelian
p
-group and
X,Y,Z
subgroups of index
p
and trivial intersection. Then
T_{G}(X,Y,Z)=\emptyset
if and only if
p=2
and
G
is isomorphic to the Klein group
V_{4}
.

Proof.

Clearly the Klein group
V_{4}
satisfies the lemma. For the other direction assume first that
G\neq X\cup Y\cup Z
. Then any
g\in G\setminus(X\cup Y\cup Z)
generates a common transversal
\{i\,g\mid 1\leq i\leq p\}
for
X,Y
, and
Z
. If
G=X\cup Y\cup Z
then it is well known (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
2
]
) that
G
has a factor group isomorphic to
V_{4}
, so
p=2
. Moreover,
X,Y,Z
are distinct maximal subgroups of
G
, hence
G=X+Y=X+Z=Y+Z
, implying
|X\cap Y|=|X\cap Z|=|Y\cap Z|=|G|/4
. Since
|X\cap Y\cap Z|=1
, the inclusion-exclusion principle yields

|G|=|X\cup Y\cup Z|=\frac{3|G|}{2}-\frac{3|G|}{4}+1=\frac{3|G|}{4}+1.

Therefore
|G|=4
and so
G\cong V_{4}
, as desired.
∎

3.2.
Notation

Let us now introduce the notation that will be used henceforth.
G
is always assumed to be a finite abelian
p
-group with subgroups
X,Y,Z
of the same order
m
that intersect trivially, i.e.,
X\cap Y\cap Z=\{0\}
. We also assume that
G=X+Y+Z
.
We write
\hat{X}=X\cap(Y+Z)
,
\hat{Y}=Y\cap(X+Z)
, and
\hat{Z}=Z\cap(X+Y)
and assume that their orders are
l_{1},l_{2}
, and
l_{3}
, respectively.
Moreover we write

A:=X\cap Y
,
B:=X\cap Z
and
C:=Y\cap Z
, and their orders as

and
r_{3}
respectively. These subgroups are central to the proofs that follow and we collect their definitions, orders and relations, for easy reference, below;

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{X}=X\cap(Y+Z),\,\,\hat{Y}

\displaystyle=Y\cap(X+Z),\,\,\hat{Z}=Z\cap(X+Y)

(12)

\displaystyle l_{1}=|\hat{X}|,\,\,l_{2}

\displaystyle=|\hat{Y}|,\,\,l_{3}=|\hat{Z}|

(13)

\displaystyle A=X\cap Y,\,\,\,B

\displaystyle=X\cap Z,\,\,\,C=Y\cap Z

(14)

\displaystyle r_{1}=|A|,\,\,r_{2}

\displaystyle=|B|,\,\,r_{3}=|C|.

We also define

(16)

\displaystyle T_{X}:=A\oplus B

\displaystyle\leq\hat{X}\leq X

(17)

\displaystyle T_{Y}:=A\oplus C

\displaystyle\leq\hat{Y}\leq Y

(18)

\displaystyle T_{Z}:=B\oplus C

\displaystyle\leq\hat{Z}\leq Z

So
l_{i}\mid m
for all
i
, while
r_{1}r_{2}\mid l_{1}
,
r_{1}r_{3}\mid l_{2}
and
r_{2}r_{3}\mid l_{3}
.
Furthermore, the sum of
A=(X\cap Y),B=(X\cap Z)
and
C=(Y\cap Z)
is easily checked to be direct and we denote it as

(19)

T=A\oplus B\oplus C.

Let us also point that any of the three "hat"-groups is contained within the sum of the other two, i.e.,

(20)

\hat{X}+\hat{Y}=\hat{X}+\hat{Z}=\hat{Y}+\hat{Z},

because any relation
x+y=z
with
x\in X,y\in Y
, and
z\in Z
implies
x\in\hat{X},y\in\hat{Y}
, and
z\in\hat{Z}
. It is also straightforward that

(21)

\hat{X}\cap\hat{Y}=A,\,\,\hat{X}\cap\hat{Z}=B,\,\,\hat{Y}\cap\hat{Z}=C.

Because
G=X+Y+Z
, we can compute the order of
G
as a function of
m,l_{i}
and
r_{i}
as

(22)

|G|=|X+Y+Z|=\frac{m^{3}}{l_{1}r_{3}}=\frac{m^{3}}{l_{2}r_{2}}=\frac{m^{3}}{l_{3}r_{1}}

and thus

(23)

l_{1}r_{3}=l_{2}r_{2}=l_{3}r_{1}.

This in turn implies

(24)

[\hat{X}:T_{X}]=[\hat{Y}:T_{Y}]=[\hat{Z}:T_{Z}]=\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}=\frac{l_{2}}{r_{1}r_{3}}=\frac{l_{3}}{r_{2}r_{3}}.

The relationship between the "hat" groups (see (
20
)) allows us to simultaneously choose transversals for
T_{X},T_{Y},
and
T_{Z}
in
\hat{X},\hat{Y},
and
\hat{Z}
, respectively. We prove this in the next lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\{\hat{z_{i}}\mid i\leq\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}\}
be a transversal of
T_{Z}
in
\hat{Z}
. Then
\hat{z_{i}}=\hat{x}_{i}+\hat{y}_{i}
for some
\hat{x}_{i}\in X
and
\hat{y}_{i}\in Y
, where
i\leq\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}
. Moreover,
\{\hat{x}_{i}\mid i\leq\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}\}
and
\{\hat{y}_{i}\mid i\leq\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}\}
are transversals of
T_{X}
in
\hat{X}
and
T_{Y}
in
\hat{Y}
, respectively.

Proof.

As
\hat{Z}\leq\hat{X}+\hat{Y}
, we can express each
\hat{z}_{i}
for
i\leq\frac{l_{1}}{r_{1}r_{2}}
as
\hat{z_{i}}=\hat{x}_{i}+\hat{y}_{i}
, where
\hat{x}_{i}\in X
and
\hat{y}_{i}\in Y
. Thus, it remains to show that
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}
and
\{\hat{y}_{i}\}
are inequivalent modulo
T_{X}
and
T_{Y}
, respectively. As the problem is symmetric for
\hat{X}
and
\hat{Y}
, is suffices to prove
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}
is a transversal of
T_{X}
in
\hat{X}
.

Assume that
x_{i}-x_{i^{\prime}}\in T_{X}
for some

. Then
z_{i}-z_{i^{\prime}}=x_{i}-x_{i^{\prime}}+y_{i}-y_{i^{\prime}}\in\hat{Z}\cap(T_{X}+\hat{Y})
. Let us now observe

(25)

\hat{Z}\cap(T_{X}+\hat{Y})=\hat{Z}\cap(A\oplus B+\hat{Y})=\hat{Z}\cap(B\oplus C+\hat{Y})\\
=B\oplus C+(\hat{Z}\cap\hat{Y})=B\oplus C+C=B\oplus C=T_{Z},

where the second equality uses

, the third follows from Dedekind’s modular law since

, and the fourth uses (
21
). Thus,
x_{i}\equiv x_{i^{\prime}}\pmod{T_{X}}
implies
z_{i}\equiv z_{i^{\prime}}\pmod{T_{Z}}
. Since
\{z_{i}\}
is a transversal, we conclude that
\{x_{i}\}
are all inequivalent modulo
T_{X}
and so it is a transversal for
T_{x}
in
\hat{X}
, because it has the right order.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

4.
The case
G=X\oplus Y

This section addresses the special case where
G
is a finite abelian
p
group and is the direct sum of two subgroups,
X
and
Y
. This case is crucial because (as we will see) is the only instance of three subgroups lacking a common transversal. Our first theorem states:

Theorem 7
.

Assume
G
is an abelian
p
-group. Let
X,Y,Z
be proper subgroups of the same index in
G
such that
G=X\oplus Y
. Then
\mathbf{T}_{G}(X,Y,Z)=\emptyset
if and only if
p=2
the groups
X,Y,Z
are all cyclic and
G=X\oplus Y=X\oplus Z=Y\oplus Z
.

Proof.

In our notation (see (
11
), (
23
)), we have
r_{1}=1
,
\hat{Z}=Z
,
l_{3}=m=|X|
and
|G|=|X\oplus Y|=m^{2}
. Because
G
is the direct sum of
X
and
Y
every element of
Z
can be uniquely written as an element of
X
plus an element of
Y
, so that

(26)

Z=\{z_{i}=x_{i}+y_{i}\>|\>i\leq m\}

for some
x_{i}\in X
and
y_{i}\in Y
not necessarily distinct. Now we distinguish cases, depending on the values of
r_{2}
and
r_{3}
.

Case 1.
(
r_{1}=r_{2}=r_{3}=1
).

In this case all
A,B,C
are trivial groups. So
l_{1}=l_{2}=l_{3}=m
(by (
23
)) and thus, not only
\hat{Z}=Z
, but also
\hat{X}=X
and
\hat{Y}=Y
. Furthermore,
G=X\oplus Y=X\oplus Z=Y\oplus Z
and all the groups
X,Y,Z
are isomorphic;

Y\cong(X\oplus Y)/X=(X\oplus Z)/X\cong Z\cong(Y\oplus Z)/Y=(Y\oplus X)/Y\cong X

Furthermore,
6
implies that the elements
\{z_{i}\}
of
Z
can be expressed as
\{z_{i}=x_{i}+y_{i}\mid i\leq m\}
, where
\{x_{i}\mid i\leq m\}=X
and
\{y_{i}\mid i\leq m\}=Y
. We fix this enumeration for the elements of
X
,
Y
, and
Z
.

We first claim that a set
F
of cardinality
m
is a common transversal of
X
and
Y
if and only if there exists a bijection
\phi:Y\to Y
so that

(27)

F=\left\{x_{i}+\phi(y_{i})\>|\>i\leq m\right\}

The "only if" direction is clear; we now prove the "if" direction.
Let
F^{\prime}
be a common transversal of
X
and
Y
in
G
. As
F^{\prime}
is a transversal of
X
in
G
, we can enumerate its elements as
f_{i}
,
i\leq m
, such that
f_{i}\equiv x_{i}\pmod{Y}
, implying
f_{i}=x_{i}+y^{i}
for some
y^{i}\in Y
, for all
i\leq m
. Because
F^{\prime}
is also a transversal of
Y
in
G
, we have
\{y^{i}\mid i\leq m\}=Y
. Defining
\phi:Y\to Y
such that
\phi(y_{i})=y^{i}
, we observe that
\phi
is a bijection of
Y
and
F^{\prime}
has the form given by equation
(
27
)
.

Now, a transversal of
X,Y
in
G
, that we may assume to be of the form
(
27
)
for some bijection
\phi
, is a transversal of
Z
in
G
if and only if

x_{i}+\phi(y_{i})\not\equiv x_{i^{\prime}}+\phi(y_{i^{\prime}})\pmod{Z}

for all distinct
i,i^{\prime}\leq m
.

Since
x_{i}\equiv-y_{i}\pmod{Z}
(because
z_{i}=x_{i}+y_{i}
), a common transversal
F=\{x_{i}+\phi(y_{i})\>|\>i\leq m\}
of
X
and
Y
in
G
is also a transversal of
Z
if and only if
-y_{i}+\phi(y_{i})-(-y_{i^{\prime}}+\phi(y_{i^{\prime}}))\notin Z
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}\leq m
. This is equivalent to requiring that
\phi
apart from being a bijection of
Y
should also satisfy

\phi(y_{i})-y_{i}\neq\phi(y_{i^{\prime}})-y_{i^{\prime}}

for all
i\neq i^{\prime}\leq m
. In other words, the map

as they are isomorphic) is not a cyclic

-group. Therefore, a common transversal for

, which holds if and only if

is not cyclic.

This completes Case 1 of our theorem. Thus, may assume that not all

are trivial. Observe though that to complete the proof, it suffices to show that a common transversal always exists in the remaining cases.
Without loss we may assume that

.

Case 2.

)

By

in
\hat{Z}

\hat{X}

, respectively. Here,

, where

Fix an arbitrary enumeration of the elements of

\hat{X}
in

\hat{Y}
in

and

(i,j)\neq(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})

(28)

When G = X+Y

We are now ready to prove our next theorem. Let us observe that the following theorem along with
Theorem 7
, implies that if

is a

Theorem 8
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be proper subgroups of an abelian
p
-group
G
with equal index such that
G=X+Y
and
X\cap Y\cap Z=\{0\}
. Then
\mathbf{T}_{G}(X,Y,Z)=\emptyset
if and only if
p=2
, the groups
X,Y,Z
are cyclic and
G=X\oplus Y=X\oplus Z=Y\oplus Z
.

Proof.

If
X,Y,Z
are cyclic, the result follows from Theorem B in
[
1
]
. Otherwise, assume
X,Y,Z
are not all cyclic. We will show that (in this non-cyclic case)
\mathbf{T}_{G}(X,Y,Z)\neq\emptyset
, from which the theorem follows.

We proceed by induction on
[G:X]
, with the base case
[G:X]=p
covered in
5
initiating the induction.

Since
Z\leq X+Y
, we have
\hat{Z}=Z
, thus
l_{3}=m
, and (
23
) simplifies to

l_{1}r_{3}=l_{2}r_{2}=mr_{1}.

Also
l_{3}\geq l_{1},l_{2}
as
l_{i}\leq m
and thus
r_{1}\leq r_{2},r_{3}
and
r_{1}^{2}\leq r_{1}r_{2}\leq l_{1}\leq m
. In addition, equation (
22
) implies
|G|=\frac{m^{2}}{r_{1}}
and the index of
X
in
G
is
\frac{m}{r_{1}}
. At this point, where the index
[G:X]
is fixed, let us state the inductive hypothesis for clarity; assume
H=H_{1}+H_{2}
is an abelian
p
-group and
H_{1},H_{2},H_{3}
are subgroups of the same order, that intersect trivially and are not all cyclic, then they share a common transversal in
H
as long as their index is strictly less than
\frac{m}{r_{1}}
.

Because
G
is a
p
-group, the fact that
r_{1}\leq r_{2},r_{3}
ensures the existence of subgroups
L\leq B
and
K\leq C
of order
r_{1}
. Then

(32)

\displaystyle|X+K|

\displaystyle=|X\oplus K|=mr_{1}

(33)

\displaystyle|Y+L|

\displaystyle=|Y\oplus L|=mr_{1}

(34)

\displaystyle|Z+A|

\displaystyle=|Z\oplus A|=mr_{1}.

The group

is in fact a direct sum that we denote by

. This can be verified by noting, for example, that
K\cap(L+A)\leq C\cap(B+A)\leq C\cap X=\{0\}
(the other intersections are similar). Moreover,
|N|=r_{1}^{3}
, and
Figure 4
illustrates the selected subgroups.

Case 1.
(

In this case, we can apply the inductive hypothesis twice to get a common transversal for

On the one hand, the subgroups

are

(since

) of the same order

and

remains the sum of two of them. In addition, their index is
\frac{m}{r_{1}^{2}}

We can easily verify that

\widebar{X}:=(X\oplus K)/N,\widebar{Y}:=(Y\oplus L)/N
, and
\widebar{Z}:=(Z\oplus A)/N
, each with index
\frac{m}{r_{1}^{2}}
in
\widebar{G}
. Clearly

, as

and

. If

\widebar{X},\,\widebar{Y},\,\widebar{Z}

applies to this smaller triple. Therefore,
\widebar{X},\widebar{Y},
and

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=A\oplus B\oplus C

\displaystyle=T_{Y}=A\oplus C

This completes the proof of the lemma.

a_{i}\equiv a_{j}\pmod{X_{2}}
. Then
t_{i}-t_{j}=a_{i}-a_{j}+b_{i}-b_{j}\equiv b_{i}-b_{j}\pmod{X}
, as
X_{2}\leq X
. Since
t_{i}\not\equiv t_{j}\pmod{X}
, we have
b_{i}\not\equiv b_{j}\pmod{X_{q}}
. Because there are
n_{q}
inequivalent elements of
G_{q}
modulo
X_{q}
, there are at most
n_{q}
distinct elements
t_{i}
with
a\equiv a_{i}\pmod{X_{2}}
. Thus,
\left|\{i\leq n\mid a_{i}\equiv a\pmod{X_{2}}\}\right|\leq n_{q}
, and the claim follows.

But
X_{2}
is no more special than
Y_{2}
and
Z_{2}
and thus our claim really applies to all three subgroups, i.e.,

n_{q}=\left|\{i\leq n\mid a_{i}\equiv a\pmod{Y_{2}}\}\right|=\left|\{i\leq n\mid a_{i}\equiv a\pmod{Z_{2}}\}\right|
.

Let us next observe that any coset of
X_{2}
in
G_{2}
must be represented by an element of
T
because
T
is a transversal of
X
in
G
. As
G_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Y_{2}
,
Y_{2}
is a transversal of
X_{2}
in
G_{2}
. Thus, for every
y\in Y_{2}
, there exists
t_{i}\in T
of the form
t_{i}=a_{i}+b_{i}
so that
a_{i}\equiv y\pmod{X_{2}}
, and by our claim, there are exactly
n_{q}
such
t_{i}\in T
. (Indeed, consider the coset
y+X
in
G
. Since
T
is a transversal, there exists exactly one element in
T
that lies in
y\pmod{X}
call it
t=a+b
for some
a\in A,b\in G_{q}
. Then
y\equiv t\pmod{X}
implying that
a\equiv y\pmod{X_{2}}
.)

Similarly, there are exactly
n_{q}
elements
t_{i}\in T
of the form
t_{i}=a_{i}+b_{i}
with
a_{i}\equiv z\pmod{X_{2}}
for every
z\in Z
, and similarly for every other
combination of
X_{2},Y_{2},Z_{2}
.

Since
a_{i}\in G_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Y_{2}
, we can write
a_{i}=x_{i}+y_{i}
with
x_{i}\in X_{2}
and
y_{i}\in Y_{2}
. The observation above implies that the sequence
\{x_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
contains each element of
X_{2}
exactly
n_{q}
times, and similarly for
\{y_{i}\mid i\leq n\}
and
Y_{2}
. Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}=n_{q}x_{0}+n_{q}y_{0},

where
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
are the unique involutions in
X_{2}
and
Y_{2}
, respectively.

Similarly, since
G_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Z_{2}
, we can write
a_{i}=x^{\prime}_{i}+z_{i}
. Applying the same argument, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\prime}_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}=n_{q}x_{0}+n_{q}z_{0},

where
z_{0}
is the unique involution of
Z_{2}
. But now we should have
n_{q}y_{0}=n_{q}z_{0}
. Since
Y_{2}\cap Z_{2}=\{0\}
, this forces
n_{q}y_{0}=n_{q}z_{0}=0
. As
n_{q}
is odd and
y_{0},z_{0}
are involutions, we reach a contradiction. Thus, the lemma is proven.

∎

The proof of our main theorem is now immediate.

8.
Examples of Lattices with no common transversal

In this section we will provide examples of full-rank lattices (of equal volume) as subgroups of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that do not admit a common fundamental domain.

Our first such example is about full-rank lattices
K,L,M
whose quotients with their common intersection
H=K\cap L\cap M
are cyclic groups. Consider
m\geq 1
an odd integer and define

K=\begin{pmatrix}2^{n}m&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&2^{n}m\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad M=\{(k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}\times m\mathbb{Z}\>\mid\>k\equiv\frac{l}{m}\pmod{2^{n}}\}.

We note that
M=\langle(1,m),\,(0,2^{n}m)\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Now it is easy to see that

H=2^{n}m\mathbb{Z}\times 2^{n}m\mathbb{Z}

and

G:=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}}{H}=\frac{\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}}{2^{n}m\mathbb{Z}\times 2^{n}m\mathbb{Z}}\cong\mathbf{Z}_{2^{n}m}\times\mathbf{Z}_{2^{n}m}.

Furthermore, the quotient subgroups corresponding to
K,L,M
are all cyclic isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2^{n}m}
;

(48)

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle:=K/H\cong\{0\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{2^{n}m},

(49)

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=L/H\cong\mathbf{Z}_{2^{n}m}\times\{0\},

(50)

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=M/H=\{k(1,m)+H\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cong\mathbf{Z}_{2^{n}m}.

The Sylow
2
-subgroups of
X,Y
are
X_{2}=\langle(0,m)+H\rangle
and
Y_{2}=\langle(m,0)+H\rangle
, respectively. Moreover the Sylow
2
-subgroup of
Z
is
Z_{2}=\langle m(1,m)+H\rangle
. Thus, the requirements of
B
are met, confirming that
K,L,M
do not share a common fundamental domain.

The special case where
m=1
(under the convention that
\mathbf{Z}_{1}=\{0\}
) yields the following full-rank lattices:

K=\begin{pmatrix}2^{n}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad L=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&2^{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad M=\{(k,l)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\>|\>k\equiv l\bmod(2^{n})\}.

We remark here that for
n=1
this is exactly the construction given in
[
9
, 3.1]
.

For our next example we consider full-rank lattices
K,L,M
where the quotients by their intersection
H=K\cap L\cap M
are not all cyclic subgroups. We define

K:=\begin{pmatrix}18&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad L:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&18\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{2},\quad M:=\{(k,l)\in 3\mathbb{Z}\times 3\mathbb{Z}\>|\>\frac{k}{3}\equiv\frac{l}{3}\pmod{2}\}.

Observe that
M=\langle(3,3),\,(6,0)\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
and is

H=18\mathbb{Z}\times 18\mathbb{Z}.

So the quotient group is

G:=\mathbb{Z}^{2}/H\cong\mathbf{Z}_{18}\times\mathbf{Z}_{18}.

and the subgroups corresponding to
K,L,M
are

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle:=K/H=\{0\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{18},

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle:=L/H=\mathbf{Z}_{18}\times\{0\},

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=\{(3k,\,3l)\mid k,l\in\mathbf{Z}_{6},\,\,k\equiv l\pmod{2}\}\cong\mathbf{Z}_{3}\times\mathbf{Z}_{6}.

While
X
and
Y
are cyclic,
Z
is not; however, the Sylow
2
-subgroups of all three are cyclic of order
2
. Indeed

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(0,9)+H\rangle,

\displaystyle Y_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(9,0)+H\rangle,

\displaystyle Z_{2}

\displaystyle=\langle(9,9)+H\rangle.

Clearly
G_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Y_{2}=X_{2}\oplus Z_{2}=Y_{2}\oplus Z_{2}
and thus
K,L,M
do not share a common fundamental domain in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, by
B
.
