Executive Summary

Project 26-ERD-026 investigates functionally graded transitions between refractory metals and structural
steels for inertial fusion energy, using tungsten–steel as the initial material system. The objective is to
reduce cracking and improve thermomechanical performance through compositional grading and
architectural design. Year 1 established fabrication methods for bulk specimens and thin-film stacks,
identified candidate interlayer compositions, and initiated interface measurements and material-response
calculations.

The LLNL–Penn State team fabricated HT-9 steel specimens by directed energy deposition (DED) and
spark plasma sintering. Penn State also produced seven DED HT-9/W composition coupons spanning
nominally 10–70 wt% W. Surface cracking was observed in the 70 wt% W coupon. Because deposition
parameters varied across the series, the effects of composition and processing cannot yet be separated.
Initial thermodynamic screening using CALPHAD (Calculation of Phase Diagrams) identified candidate
interlayer compositions for experimental evaluation.

Magnetron sputtering process development reduced compressive tungsten film stress from approximately
2.0 to 0.52 GPa and enabled adherent coatings thicker than 1 µm on silicon substrates. Thin-film stacks
incorporating tungsten, vanadium, and 316 stainless steel provided controlled specimens for interface
studies. Nanoindentation paired with microscopy distinguished deformation, cracking, and delamination
responses, supporting the development of quantitative interface measurements for evaluating candidate
interlayers and graded transitions.

Preliminary one-dimensional calculations examined X-ray attenuation and initial temperature rise in
layered W/HT-9 structures and will inform interlayer design. Together, these activities established
experimental baselines and initial feedback between characterization and fabrication, with porosity and
residual stress informing processing choices. In FY27, measured properties and phase observations will
support model assessment and transition-material selection. Controlled comparisons of direct joints,
interlayers, and graded transitions will test whether grading reduces cracking and improves
thermomechanical performance and guide the integration of compositional grading with architectural
design.

Background and Research Objectives

Joining tungsten armor to structural steel remains a major materials challenge for inertial fusion energy
(IFE), particularly under repeated thermal loading. These materials have substantially different thermal
expansion and mechanical properties, which can lead to residual stress and cracking during fabrication and
thermal cycling. Brittle phases can also form at the interface, making both material selection and control of
processing history important. Recent U.S. fusion-materials planning identifies robust joining of tungsten to
structural alloys without brittle intermetallic formation as a continuing research need and emphasizes
coordinated alloy, process, and component design (Ferry et al., 2026).

 1

To address these challenges, this project investigates functionally graded transitions between refractory
metals and structural steels, using tungsten–steel as the initial material system. Compositional grading and
architectural design offer complementary ways to accommodate thermal expansion mismatch and tailor
mechanical response. Our hypothesis is that controlling the composition and structure of the transition
region can reduce cracking and improve thermomechanical performance compared with a direct tungsten–
steel interface. Previous work has demonstrated the use of computational thermodynamics to design
interlayers between these materials (Gräning et al., 2023). However, subsequent observations of ordered
phases in related interlayer interfaces highlight the need to assess phase predictions experimentally
(Gräning et al., 2025). Rapid solidification and repeated reheating during directed energy deposition (DED)
further motivate comparing equilibrium and Scheil-based predictions with measured phases and phase
fractions in the as-deposited condition.

Cui et al. (2021) demonstrated a complementary geometric approach, using patterned steel substrates and
field-assisted sintering to produce segmented tungsten coatings that survived at least 50 thermal cycles
between 300 and 800 °C. Their results motivate investigating how structural features can complement
compositional grading to improve strain accommodation.

Year 1 objectives, aligned with the project milestones, were to:

 • Processing development: Establish processing methods for the steel and refractory constituents
 and prepare specimens for characterization.
 • Geometry and materials for Functionally Graded Structures (FGS): Develop models to
 evaluate material configurations and guide geometry and material selection under loading
 conditions relevant to IFE.
 • Composition refinement: Screen candidate interlayers and composition paths using CALPHAD,
 which is a computational thermodynamics method used to predict which phases may form, with
 attention to brittle phase formation and crack susceptibility.
 • Mechanical behavior development: Develop methods to characterize mechanical response and
 distinguish cracking within the constituent materials from interfacial failure.

Processing Development and Baseline Specimens

Bulk specimens were produced using directed energy deposition (DED) and spark plasma sintering (SPS),
while magnetron sputtering provided controlled film stacks for investigating individual material interfaces.
These approaches address complementary aspects of the joining problem: bulk processing reveals
challenges associated with deposition, consolidation, and mixing, while thin films allow layer composition,
thickness, residual stress, and relevant defects to be varied for interface studies.

For this project, HT-9 provides a practical steel surrogate for developing processing methods and studying
tungsten–steel interface behavior. It is a nominal 12Cr-1Mo ferritic/martensitic steel containing W, V, and
a small Ni addition. HT-9 is conventionally used in a normalized and tempered martensitic condition,
providing an established reference for evaluating how fabrication affects microstructure and mechanical
response (Sridharan and Field, 2018). Its microstructural characteristics are relevant to ferritic/martensitic
structural steels, although its Mo and Ni additions distinguish it from reduced-activation compositions.
Applying the findings to a specific reduced activation ferritic martensitic (RAFM) steel will therefore
require evaluating the effects of composition and thermal history (Goodall et al., 2025).

 3

The Pennsylvania State University (Penn State) team members investigated DED conditions through a
single-bead parameter study, using optical cross sections and image analysis to examine how laser power
and scan speed affected porosity. Multilayer HT-9 specimens were also fabricated for microstructural and
mechanical characterization. Optical microscopy revealed cellular solidification structures, while Vickers
microhardness measurements provided an initial assessment of the deposited material. The reported mean
hardness values for DED processed HT-9 ranged from approximately 537 to 610 HV, with variation among
specimens and within some specimens (Table 1). The contributions of local microstructure, thermal history,
texture, and defects to this variation remain under investigation.

SPS provided a second route for producing HT-9 specimens and a basis for examining how processing
history, including thermal exposure and cooling rate, affects the resulting material. Hardness and porosity
measurements will support comparison with the DED specimens once test conditions and specimen states
are established. Two-dimensional image analysis of the polished cross-section indicated an estimated
relative density of 91 ± 1%. Microhardness testing of this SPS HT-9 sample yielded hardness values of 620
± 80 HV. Indents were placed away from visible pores, although subsurface porosity may have affected the
measured hardness.

To extend the bulk processing study toward tungsten–steel transitions, the Penn State DED effort fabricated
seven HT-9/W composition coupons containing nominally 10–70 wt% W. These individual compositions
provide specimens for examining mixing, microstructure, and defects along the direct transition between
the two materials. Surface cracking was observed in the 70 wt% W coupon, identifying a condition requiring
further investigation. Because processing parameters also varied across the series, the cracking cannot yet
be attributed solely to composition or to a particular phase. These specimens establish an experimental
baseline for evaluating whether selected interlayers and graded paths improve fabrication outcomes.

In parallel, magnetron-sputtering process development established thin film stacks for investigating
interlayer and transition compositions. In situ residual-stress measurements guided changes in deposition
conditions, reducing the measured compressive stress from approximately 2.0 to 0.52 GPa and enabling
adherent tungsten coatings thicker than 1 µm on silicon substrates (Figure 2). Thin film stacks incorporating
W, V, and 316 stainless steel were subsequently prepared for indentation studies to develop quantitative
measurements of resistance to interfacial separation. Initial diffraction measurements also showed that as-
deposited phases could differ from those of the source materials, including metastable β-W in early tungsten
films and as-deposited 𝛼-iron (ferrite) in lieu of bulk 𝛾-iron (austenite) in 316 stainless steel. These
observations motivate experimental assessment of phase formation in CALPHAD-selected interlayer
compositions.

CALPHAD Guided Selection of Transition Compositions

The processing studies establish which materials can be fabricated and where defects emerge, but a gradual
change in composition does not necessarily avoid brittle phases. A direct transition from steel to tungsten
passes through transition compositions whose phase stability can differ substantially from either endpoint.
CALPHAD calculations therefore support the experimental work and compositions selected for fabrication
by identifying alternative composition paths for investigation.

The team used equilibrium, Scheil, and hybrid Scheil–equilibrium calculations to screen candidate
transition paths between tungsten and steel. Initial calculations identified regions susceptible to brittle phase
formation and candidate interlayer compositions for experimental evaluation. HT-9 was approximated as
Fe–12Cr, so the calculations do not capture the effects of carbon and other alloying additions. Further
selection will consider measured chemistry and phases, processing behavior, and thermal properties.

Interface Characterization and Mechanical Measurements

Quantifying the benefits of an interlayer requires measuring resistance to separation at the intended
interface while accounting for film thickness, residual stress, and competing deformation or fracture
mechanisms. The sputtered film stacks provide controlled specimens for developing these measurements.

Nanoindentation coupled with scanning electron microscopy was used to assess deformation and damage
in sputtered thin-film specimens. Representative images of a W-based stack show a localized indentation
impression at lower load and cracking, film separation, and fragmentation at higher load (Figure 3). These
observations help identify test conditions for quantitative delamination analysis. Confirming the failure
location and accounting for film thickness, residual stress, and delamination geometry will be necessary to
interpret the measurements and compare interface behavior.

Modeling of Material Response

Material-response modeling is being developed as a screening tool to compare candidate configurations
and guide fabrication and testing under defined IFE loading conditions. Figure 4 outlines the intended
workflow, linking source conditions and material properties to calculations of energy deposition,
temperature rise, ablation, and thermomechanical response. A longer-term objective is to assess interfacial
stresses, layer thickness, and the conditions that may lead to failure. Year 1 demonstrated preliminary one-
dimensional calculations of X-ray attenuation and initial temperature rise in a layered W/HT-9 system, with
heating concentrated near the exposed surface. Implementations of ion and neutron heating are underway.

Figure 4. Intended workflow linking fusion-source conditions and material properties to energy deposition,
temperature rise, and thermomechanical response in layered first-wall materials. Year 1 demonstrated preliminary
one-dimensional X-ray attenuation and initial temperature-rise calculations in W/HT-9. Subsequent development will
incorporate ion and neutron heating and coupling to thermomechanical finite element analysis.

Mission Impact
This work supports LLNL’s IFE Institutional Initiative and Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Core
Competency by connecting processing, manufacturing, and advanced characterization. The capabilities
established in Year 1 provide a basis for selecting graded structures for further development. Collaboration
with Penn State also develops expertise in thermodynamic modeling, additive manufacturing, and
characterization for future LLNL materials programs.

Future Work
Year 2 (FY27) will build on Year 1 specimens and capabilities to select transition materials, fabricate
graded specimens, and compare direct joints, interlayers, and graded transitions. Comparisons of measured
chemistry and phases with CALPHAD predictions will guide material selection, while defect
characterization and heat-treatment studies will inform processing choices. Repeatable interface and
mechanical measurements, including confirmation of failure location, will test whether grading reduces
cracking and improves performance under controlled conditions. Measured properties and defined IFE
loading cases will support thermal and mechanical model development and guide the selection of layer
thicknesses and candidate architectures. These activities will assess the feasibility of combining
compositional grading with architectural design and establish reference configurations, performance
measures, and test conditions for FY28 thermomechanical evaluation.
