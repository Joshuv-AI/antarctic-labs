Developing a prognostic signature with cancer-associated fibroblasts for predicting the prognosis and immune landscape of prostate cancer
Authors: Mei Yi, Qin Liu, Jin Yang, Ruyi Wang, Jiangshu He, Bo Chen, Hanchao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProstate cancer (PCa) is the common malignant disease in older men. Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are a vital components of the tumor microenvironment (TME)and the major subpopulation of cells that promote tumor heterogeneity. However, there are still few studies on the correlation between PCa and CAFs. Thus, we predicted the prognosis of PCa by investigating CAFs characteristics in PCa and constructing a prognostic model with CAFs-related features. Firstly, we obtained the scRNA-seq and clinical data on PCa from the GEO and TCGA databases. We adopted a survival analysis to evaluate the impact of three distinct CAFs subtypes on the prognosis of PCa patients. Besides, we identified different CAFs by integrated univariate Cox regression analysis, LASSO analysis, and multivariate Cox regression analysis. Based on the cancer-associated fibroblast-related genes (CAFRGs), we built a prognostic model to exhibit PCa prognostic relevance and validated the prognostic signature. We also screened the drugs for PCa. Furthermore, we explored the correlation between malignant features and PCa. We revealed that apCAFs and myCAFs were significantly correlated with PCa patient prognosis. 5 prognostic CAFRGs (SYNM, NR4A1, MSMB, HOPX, and GJC1) were screened by integrated analysis. We found that the low-risk group patients had significantly higher survival rates. And validation analyses targeting the prognostic model indicated that the high-risk group patients were more to exhibit higher BCR across external validation sets. The ssGSEA algorithm indicted that the majority of the immune cells had increased levels of infiltration and higher immune function scores in the high-risk group. In addition, CAFRG scores were correlated with angiogenesis, EMT, and cell cycle pathway activity. In conclusion, we build a prognostic model with CAFs prognostic characteristics for PCa to offer further prediction of PCa prognosis and immunotherapy response, which ultimately guides the clinical management of PCa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63436-9.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Cancer-associated fibroblasts, Prognosis, Immunotherapeutic response, ScRNA-seqSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most common malignant tumor in elderly men, with biochemical recurrence (BCR) being a major driver of poor prognosis1,2. Current prognostic tools such as prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and Gleason score lack specificity in stratifying high-risk patients, and tumor microenvironment (TME)-mediated heterogeneity further compromises treatment efficacy3. Given that cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are heterogeneous, with three main subtypes: inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs, characterized by high IL-6 and LIF expression), myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs, marked by α-SMA and FAP expression), and antigen-presenting CAFs (apCAFs, defined by MHC-II and CD74 expression)4,5. apCAFs can present tumor antigens to CD4 + T cells to modulate immune responses, while myCAFs secrete TGF-β and remodel the extracellular matrix to promote tumor invasion and drug resistance6,7, suggesting their potential roles in PCa recurrence and treatment failure. However, the prognostic value of CAF subtypes and their related genes (CAFRGs) in PCa remains unclear.

Tumor progression and metastasis are tightly linked to malignant epithelial cells and rely heavily on complex crosstalk between tumor cells and stromal compartments within tumor mesenchyme8. The TME is the internal environment where tumor cells are located, and it has a significant impact on tumor development and causes widespread effects on host tissues9. TME is mainly composed of multiple cytokines, growth factors, immune cells, and CAFs. Cells in the TME are characterised by high plasticity, rapid differentiation, and constant change10. As the tumor grows and invades, the TME will respond rapidly to synergize or inhibit further tumor development. Based on the alterations in TME, various immunotherapies have been applied to the treatment of cancer. However, tumor heterogeneity and the complexity of TME caused severe resistance to immunotherapy and suboptimal therapeutic effects11. Thus, further exploration of the characteristics of TME is essential to optimizing drug resistance in immunotherapy and improving the prognosis and survival of cancer patients.

CAFs are one of the most essential parts of the TME and the main cell subpopulation contributing to tumor heterogeneity, having a pivotal role in tumor progression12. CAFs could regulate TME through the production of inflammatory factors and modulation of immune cells, thereby achieving immunosuppression and reducing the efficacy of tumor therapy13. Besides, CAFs modulate the extracellular matrix, creating barriers to drug and immune cell infiltration and promoting tumor invasion and metastasis14. CAFs were found to promote the occurrence, development, and invasive metastasis of PCa15. Further studies revealed that treatment outcomes and survival prognosis of PCa patients were closely linked to CAFs16. Presently, targeted regulation of CAFs has become a research hotspot in tumor therapy. Specifically, apCAFs express MHC-II molecules and CD74 to present tumor antigens to CD4 + T cells, potentially modulating anti-tumor immune responses and affecting immunotherapy efficacy17. MyCAFs secrete transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and remodel the extracellular matrix (ECM), which promotes tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and drug resistance—key drivers of BCR and poor treatment outcomes18. These mechanistic roles suggest that apCAF and myCAF-related features could serve as prognostic indicators for PCa.Nevertheless, the lack of knowledge of the heterogeneity of cellular subpopulations of CAFs makes it difficult to identify the specific subpopulations and hampers the search for specific biomarkers, thereby rendering clinically targeted therapies for CAFs ineffective. Hence, investigating the functional heterogeneity of CAFs and mining potential biomarkers aim to provide novel targets and ideas for the treatment of PCa.

Several recent studies have investigated the prognostic relevance of CAF-related signatures in PCa19–21. However, several issues remain insufficiently addressed. First, many previous models were based on global CAF abundance, broad CAF marker sets, or unsupervised CAF-related molecular subtypes, whereas the prognostic contribution of canonical functional CAF subtypes, particularly myCAFs and apCAFs, has not been fully clarified. Second, some existing signatures include relatively large numbers of genes or are derived from heterogeneous CAF definitions, which may limit their clinical translation. Third, few studies have integrated CAF-subtype-derived prognostic genes with BRFS validation, immune landscape characterization, malignant pathway analysis, and therapeutic sensitivity prediction within a unified framework.

Accumulating evidence indicates that CAF heterogeneity plays a pivotal role in shaping the tumor microenvironment and contributing to prostate cancer progression and biochemical recurrence. However, the prognostic implications of CAF subtypes and their related genes remain incompletely defined, and current clinical indicators, including PSA and Gleason score, still lack sufficient accuracy for individualized risk stratification. Here, we hypothesized that CAF subtype-derived signature genes could serve as prognostic biomarkers for predicting BRFS, characterizing the immune landscape, and reflecting malignant biological features of PCa. To test this hypothesis, we integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data to identify key CAF-related genes, construct and externally validate a prognostic signature, and further explore its immune relevance, malignant pathway associations, and potential therapeutic implications. This study aimed to provide a refined CAF-subtype-informed tool for recurrence risk stratification and personalized management of prostate cancer.

Methods

Preparation of data

The scRNA-seq data of PCa were downloaded from GEO-GSE141445 (platform: Illumina NovaSeq 6000), including 36,424 single cells from 13 PCa samples22. Bulk RNA-seq and clinical data were obtained from TCGA-PRAD (platform: Illumina HiSeq 2000) and GEO datasets (GSE46602: Affymetrix HG-U133A; GSE70768: Illumina HiSeq 2000; GSE70769: Illumina HiSeq 2000; GSE116918: Illumina HiSeq 4000)23,24. Detailed information of each dataset is summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Furthermore, RNA-seq and clinical information were obtained from 8,739 samples across 32 different tumor types from the UCSC Xena portal25.

The primary endpoint was BRFS (defined as the time from treatment to first BCR), and the secondary endpoint was OS (time from diagnosis to death) and BCR status. TCGA-PRAD and GSE116918 contained complete BRFS information and relatively complete clinical covariates, and were therefore used for model construction and internal validation. GSE46602, GSE70768, and GSE70769 also provided BCR status and recurrence-free follow-up time, allowing external BRFS validation by Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses. However, these external cohorts lacked complete OS information, so they were not used for OS analysis or fully adjusted multivariate clinical modeling. Batch effects were corrected using parametric ComBat with “dataset” as the batch covariate and “tumor stage” as the biological covariate. For bulk RNA-seq, data were normalized to log2-transformed TPM, with gene mapping via Ensembl ID to gene symbol (duplicate symbols removed). For scRNA-seq, quality control was performed as follows: cells with nFeature_RNA < 200 or > 6000, nCount_RNA < 500, or mitochondrial gene percentage > 10% were filtered out; doublets were removed using DoubletFinder v2.0 (pK = 0.09, nExp = 0.075*nCells).

Processing of scRNA-seq data

Key parameters and software versions for scRNA-seq analysis are summarized in Supplemental Table e2. Briefly, the “Seurat” R package (v[4.3.0.1](4.3.0.1)) was used to construct the Seurat object. After QC and doublet removal, data were normalized using LogNormalize (scale.factor = 10000), and variable genes were selected via vst method (nfeatures = 2000). Cell cycle effects were regressed out using G2M/S phase markers26, followed by PCA dimensionality reduction (npc = 30) and clustering (resolution = 1.3, seed.use = 1234). Fibroblasts were extracted and re-clustered, with CAF subtypes annotated based on canonical markers (iCAFs: IL-6, LIF; myCAFs: α-SMA, FAP; apCAFs: MHC-II, CD74). Highly variable genes for each CAF subtype were identified using FindAllMarkers (logFC ≥ 0.5, min.pct = 0.3, diff.pct ≥ 0.2). GSVA was performed using MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (v7.5.1) to assess pathway activation. SCENIC was used to evaluate transcription factor (TF) activation with the hg38 database. Monocle 2 was used to construct CAF differentiation trajectories, with the root cell selected as the COL1A1-high cluster (myCAF precursor). All analysis code has been deposited in GitHub (https://github.com) for reproducibility. All analyses were performed using R software, and all reproducible analytical codes and input data preprocessing scripts have been uploaded to a public GitHub repository for full reproducibility (https://github.com/JYfantast/CAF).

Development of prognostic model

Briefly, 289 CAFRGs identified from the single-cell RNA-seq analysis were used as candidate genes for prognostic modeling in the combined cohort consisting of TCGA-PRAD and GSE116918 samples. The combined cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort and an internal testing cohort at a ratio of 7:3 using the caret R package. The distribution of major clinical variables, including age, tumor stage, Gleason score, and PSA level, was compared between the two cohorts. In the training cohort, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression was first performed to identify CAFRGs associated with BRFS. Genes with P < 0.05 were retained as candidate prognostic genes for subsequent LASSO Cox regression. LASSO Cox regression was then conducted using the glmnet R package, with 10-fold cross-validation used to determine the optimal penalty parameter lambda. Genes with non-zero coefficients at lambda.min were selected for further modeling.

The genes selected by LASSO were then entered into a multivariate Cox regression model. Stepwise model selection was performed to obtain the final prognostic model. Therefore, the final regression coefficients used for risk-score calculation were derived from the refitted multivariate Cox model rather than from the penalized LASSO model. The risk score for each patient was calculated as follows: Risk score = Σ βi × Expi. where βi represents the multivariate Cox regression coefficient of gene i, and Expi represents the normalized expression level of gene i. The median risk score in the training cohort was used as the fixed cutoff to classify patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. This cutoff was then applied unchanged to the internal testing cohort and external validation cohorts to avoid outcome-dependent cutoff optimization.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, log-rank tests, hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals, time-dependent ROC curves, and C-index analysis were used to evaluate the prognostic performance of the CAFRG signature. External validation was performed in independent GEO cohorts using the same model coefficients and the same training-derived cutoff whenever the required genes were available. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All analyses were performed in R, and the complete reproducible code, and input data processing scripts, have been deposited in the revised public repository: https://github.com/JYfantast/CAF.

Validation of prognostic model

Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to compare BRFS between high- and low-risk groups, with numbers-at-risk, events, HR and 95% CIs reported.Time-dependent ROC curves (R package “timeROC”27 were generated to assess predictive performance at 3, 5, and 7 years, with optimism-corrected AUC (1000 bootstrap samples). C-index (95% CIs) evaluated discriminative ability, and calibration was assessed via calibration plots (Hosmer-Lemeshow test) to verify consistency between predicted and observed BRFS probabilities.Proportional hazards assumptions were validated using Schoenfeld residuals (all p > 0.05, no violation). Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses included risk score and clinical covariates (age, tumor stage, Gleason score, PSA level). Missing data (≤ 5% for all covariates) were imputed via multiple imputation (m = 5). Subgroup robustness was tested by interaction terms between risk score and each covariate (age: ≤65 vs. > 65; tumor stage: I-II vs. III-IV; Gleason score: ≤7 vs. > 7; PSA: ≤10 vs. > 10 ng/mL), with interaction p-values reported.GSVA pathway enrichment analysis was performed in the combined training set (TCGA-PRAD + GSE116918). Multiple testing was adjusted by FDR (FDR < 0.05 as significant), and enriched pathways’ prognostic relevance was explained based on their roles in tumor progression (e.g., cell cycle pathways linked to proliferation and BCR risk).Somatic mutation comparisons were conducted in TCGA-PRAD (n = 499) due to available data. Multiple testing was controlled by FDR, and mutation burden differences between risk groups were associated with prognosis (higher burden linked to increased immunogenicity and potential treatment response)28.

Exploration of the immune landscape

To investigate the peculiarities of the immune microenvironment among various groups, we applied the ssGSEA method to comprehensively assess the immune cell composition and immune function in each individual sample29,30. We utilized the Wilcox test to assess differences between groups, specifically examining variations in ICG expression levels. Additionally, we computed TMB scores and conducted comparative analyses to forecast response to immunotherapy within each risk group31.

Identification of drugs

The “calcPhenotype” function in the “oncoPredict” R package v0.2.0 was used to predict drug responsiveness using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (v8.4). Input data for oncoPredict included log2-transformed TPM values of the 5 CAFRGs from the combined training set (TCGA-PRAD + GSE116918). Gene matching between the study dataset and GDSC cell lines was performed using gene symbols, with only genes present in both datasets retained (all 5 CAFRGs were matched)32. Data normalization was performed by scaling each gene to have a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 across samples.The Ridge regression model implemented in oncoPredict was used to predict IC50 values for 50 chemotherapeutic drugs and 52 targeted therapeutic drugs. For each drug, we report: (1) distribution of predicted IC50 values in high- and low-risk groups; (2) effect size (mean difference in IC50 between high- and low-risk groups) with 95% CIs; (3) FDR-corrected p-values (FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant).Drug-class enrichment analysis was performed using the CLUE database (v2.0), with drug classes defined based on mechanism of action (MoA). Enrichment p-values were corrected using FDR, and significantly enriched drug classes (FDR < 0.05) were reported, along with their potential relevance to CAFRG-mediated pathways.

Correlation with malignant features

Gene sets associated with angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cell cycle were obtained from MSigDB Hallmark gene sets (v7.5.1) and Reactome Pathway Database (v82). GSVA was implemented using the R package “GSVA” v1.46.0 with parameters: method="gsva”, kcdf="Gaussian”, [min.sz](min.sz) = 10, [max.sz](max.sz) = 50025,33.Pathway activity scores were normalized to z-scores to facilitate cross-sample comparisons. Multiple testing across pathways was controlled using FDR (FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant)34,35. To demonstrate independence from the risk score and clinical covariates (age, tumor stage, Gleason score, PSA level), partial correlation analysis was performed, adjusting for these variables. Effect sizes (Pearson correlation coefficients, R) with 95% CIs were reported for the associations between CAFRG z-scores and pathway z-scores (angiogenesis, EMT, cell cycle).

Results

Identification and differentiation trajectory of CAF subtypes in prostate cancer

Figure 1 illustrates the overarching workflow of the current study. We employed the R package “clustree” to visually represent the interactions between various clusters at different resolutions, facilitating the selection of an appropriate resolution for downstream analysis (Fig. 2A)36. Subsequently, we conducted normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, following which the t-SNE plot depicted the distribution of distinct clusters (Fig. 2B). Cell annotations were then carried out for each of these clusters (Fig. 2C). Further, we utilized bubble plots to visualize the expression levels of marker genes for each cell type (Fig. 2D). We extracted fibroblasts, repeated the aforementioned investigation, and obtained the distribution of six distinct clusters through the t-SNE plot (Fig. 3A and B). We performed cell annotations on every CAF (Fig. 3C). Using the “FindAllMarkers” function, we identified a total of 289 genes for each high variant gene in every CAF (Supplemental Table 3). There are significant differences in mechanisms and pathways between different CAF clusters (Fig. 3D). Monocle 2 trajectory analysis was based on the lineage assumption that myCAFs represent a precursor state (supported by high COL1A1 expression, a known marker of quiescent fibroblasts), with apCAFs and iCAFs as differentiated states. The root cell was selected as the COL1A1-high cluster (myCAF precursor) due to its early position in the pseudotime ordering and expression of stemness-related genes (Fig. 4A). SCENIC analysis used the hg38 database (v10x) with regulon thresholds: minimum 2 target genes per TF, minimum 0.8 AUC score for regulon activation. Key TFs (e.g., STAT3 for iCAFs, SMAD3 for myCAFs, IRF1 for apCAFs) were validated by independent evidence showing their role in CAF subtype differentiation (Fig. 4B). Sensitivity analysis using Slingshot v2.0 (an alternative trajectory inference tool) confirmed the same lineage structure, with myCAFs as the root and apCAFs/iCAFs as differentiated branches, strengthening confidence in the trajectory results. Taken as a whole, these results suggest that CAFs exhibit distinct functions and states, thereby validating the reliability and validity of our analytical methods and findings.

Prognostic relevance of CAF subtypes to biochemical recurrence-free survival

To explore the potential prognostic impact of the distinct CAFs on patients diagnosed with PCa, we performed a comprehensive survival analysis that revealed a significant association between apCAFs and myCAFs and patient prognosis (Fig. 5A). Taken the latter finding into consideration, we subsequently analyzed both CAFs in depth. The entire set of PCa samples was randomly split into a training set and a testing set in a 7:3 ratio. We consequently employed univariate Cox analysis to screen 12 prognostic CAFRGs (Fig. 5B), followed by LASSO analysis, which further narrowed down the candidates to 10 CAFRGs (Fig. 5C). Finally, we utilized multivariate Cox analysis to build a model consisting of five prognostic CAFRGs (Fig. 5D). Nested cross-validation (NCV) results showed consistent performance across outer folds: mean optimism-corrected C-index = 0.78 (95% CI: 0.73–0.83) in the training set, and mean optimism-corrected AUC at 5 years = 0.79 (95% CI: 0.74–0.84). External validation in held-out cohorts using fixed coefficients from the training subset confirmed the model’s robustness: GSE46602 (HR = 2.15, 95% CI: 1.38–3.35, p < 0.001), GSE70768 (HR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.51–3.57, p < 0.001), GSE70769 (HR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.32–3.28, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6B). The cut-point was chosen as the median risk score from the training subset of the first outer fold (median = 1.24), which was applied consistently across all folds and external cohorts to ensure comparability.

Screening of prognostic CAFRGs and construction of the prognostic signature

Figure 6A demonstrated that the low-risk group patients were significantly higher survival rates across various internal sets, with statistically significant differences noted. These findings suggest that the model had high internal stability. Additionally, Fig. 6B illustrated that patients belonging to the high-risk group were more to exhibit higher BCR across various external groups (such as the TCGA, GSE116918, entire, GSE46602, GSE70768, and GSE70769 sets), with statistically significant differences noted. These results indicate that the model was capable of predicting both the prognosis and BCR for patients diagnosed with PCa. Furthermore, all AUC values associated with the signature exceeded 0.7 and surpassed those of clinical features (Fig. 7). Head-to-head performance comparisons confirmed the superiority of our 5-gene CAFRG signature over full transcriptome, proliferation pathway and immune-related gene models built with the same analytical framework. The CAFRG signature achieved the highest C-index and time-dependent AUC, with fewer genes and better clinical translatability for PCR-based clinical detection (Supplemental Table 4). The signature provided incremental prognostic value independent of all control models.

Time-dependent ROC comparison between CAFRG signature and conventional clinical features. Time-dependent ROC curves at 3-, 5- and 7-year BRFS demonstrated that the CAFRG signature achieved higher AUC values than single clinical indicators, reflecting superior predictive accuracy.

Internal and external cohort validation of the CAFRG signature

Moreover, the generalizability and applicability of the established prognostic signature were assessed in various patient populations in this study. Notably, the lower-risk group patients consistently demonstrated better survival outcomes across different clinical subgroups, which emphasizes the utility and versatility of the model. These observations were depicted in Fig. 8A. Both univariate and multivariate Cox analyses confirmed that the signature was an independent predictor for predicting prognosis, as depicted in Figs. 8B and C. The CAFRG signature outperformed all conventional clinical prognostic factors in discriminative ability, with a higher C-index and time-dependent AUC at 3, 5 and 7 years (Fig. 9A). Decision curve analysis confirmed the signature provided a higher net benefit than individual clinical factors and the combined clinical model. Adding the CAFRG signature to clinical parameters significantly improved prognostic reclassification for BCR risk. Notably, the different groups were found to have the most significant impact on prognosis, as depicted in Fig. 9B.

Pathway enrichment and somatic mutation landscape across risk subgroups

Furthermore, this study utilized the GSVA method to identify differentially enriched pathways in groups. The outcomes suggested that the high-risk group was enriched in pathways related to the cell cycle, DNA replication, mismatch repair, pathogenic Escherichia coli infection, and viral myocarditis. In contrast, the low-risk group was considerably enriched in pathways associated with other glycan degradation, GPI anchor biosynthesis, N-glycan biosynthesis, peroxisome, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis (Fig. 9C). Moreover, Fig. 10 showed gene mutations in various groups, with significantly more mutations noted in the high-risk group, which may partly explain the inferior prognosis associated with the high-risk group.

Somatic mutation landscape differences between high-risk and low-risk PCa patients. Distribution and burden of somatic mutations were compared, showing significantly higher mutation loads in the high-risk group relative to the low-risk group.

Immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression and TMB features

ssGSEA showed increased immune cell infiltration (e.g., CD8 + T cells, macrophages) and higher immune function scores (except APC co-stimulation and MHC class I) in the high-risk group (FDR < 0.05 for all comparisons, Fig. 11A-B). The expression of several immune checkpoint genes (ICGs), including CD274 (PD-L1) and CTLA4, was significantly upregulated in the high-risk group (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 11C). TMB was calculated as the number of non-synonymous somatic mutations per megabase (MB) of coding DNA, using TCGA-PRAD mutation data (n = 499). The high-risk group had significantly higher TMB scores (median = 3.2 mutations/MB) compared to the low-risk group (median = 1.8 mutations/MB, p < 0.001, Fig. 11D). These observations indicate that the high-risk subgroup exhibited a more immune-active microenvironment, characterized by increased immune cell infiltration, higher immune checkpoint gene expression, and elevated TMB. However, because treatment response data from an immune checkpoint inhibitor-treated PCa cohort were unavailable, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating evidence suggesting potential immunological relevance rather than direct evidence of improved immunotherapy responsiveness. To account for potential confounders (age, tumor stage, Gleason score), a multivariable linear regression model was used to adjust for these variables, confirming that the association between risk score and TMB/ICG expression remained significant (all p < 0.001). Multiple testing was controlled using FDR (FDR < 0.05 for all immune-related comparisons).

Drug sensitivity prediction as an extended translational analysis

To further explore translational therapeutic value, we performed systematic drug sensitivity prediction using the R package oncoPredict based on the GDSC database, covering 50 chemotherapeutic agents and 52 targeted drugs. We set FDR < 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance, and the corresponding results are visualized in Supplemental Fig.1A and 1B. This rigorous screening process allowed us to identify potentially efficacious treatment options, shedding light on the intricate landscape of therapeutic interventions for PCa.

Correlation of CAFRGs with angiogenesis, EMT and cell cycle malignant phenotypes

To further explore the biological relevance of the CAFRG signature, we evaluated its association with malignant phenotype-related pathway activity, including angiogenesis, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), and cell cycle pathways. The CAFRG z-score was significantly correlated with angiogenesis (R = 0.53, p < 0.001), EMT (R = 0.47, p < 0.001), and cell cycle pathway scores (R=−0.1, p < 0.001) across the TCGA pan-cancer cohort (Supplemental Fig. 2). These findings suggest that the CAFRG signature may reflect malignant biological states associated with stromal activation and tumor progression. Nevertheless, these results are based on transcriptomic correlation analyses and do not demonstrate that CAFRGs directly regulate angiogenesis, EMT, or cell cycle activity. Functional experiments are required to clarify the causal roles of these genes in PCa progression.

Discussion

PCa ranks as the most common malignant urinary tumor in elderly males, and BCR remains the primary cause of poor survival and clinical treatment failure37. Early PCa is often asymptomatic, and most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, accompanied by high drug resistance and unsatisfactory long-term prognosis38. Conventional clinical indicators, including preoperative prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and Gleason score, are routinely applied for prognostic evaluation; however, they lack sufficient specificity and accuracy for precise risk stratification, and cannot fully reflect the molecular heterogeneity of the TME. Therefore, it is urgently necessary to explore novel molecular biomarkers and robust prognostic models to guide individualized prognostic assessment and therapeutic decision-making for PCa patients.

We acknowledge that several CAF-related prognostic studies in PCa have been published in recent years19–21. Compared with these previous studies, the present work provides several incremental differences. First, instead of relying only on global CAF abundance or broad CAF marker sets, we focused on canonical functional CAF subtypes and found that apCAFs and myCAFs were significantly associated with BRFS. This allowed the model to be derived from prognostically relevant CAF subtype features rather than from CAFs as a single undifferentiated stromal population. Second, the final signature consisted of only five genes, namely SYNM, NR4A1, MSMB, HOPX, and GJC1, which is more compact than several previously reported CAF-related models and may be more feasible for future PCR- or panel-based clinical detection. Third, we evaluated the signature across cohorts with available BRFS information and further explored its association with immune infiltration, immune checkpoint expression, TMB, angiogenesis, EMT, cell cycle activity, and predicted drug sensitivity. These analyses provide a more focused view of how CAF subtype-derived genes may reflect recurrence risk and tumor microenvironment states.

With the rapid advancement of transcriptome and single-cell sequencing technologies, it has become feasible to dissect cellular heterogeneity, TME composition, and molecular mechanisms underlying tumor progression39. Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) exhibits unique advantages in identifying cell subpopulation heterogeneity40, specific molecular markers, and cell differentiation trajectories, which provides a new perspective for exploring TME stromal cells in PCa41. Accumulating studies have confirmed that transcriptomic characteristics and cellular ecosystem diversity are closely linked to malignant progression and poor prognosis of PCa42. Nevertheless, most current studies mainly focus on tumor epithelial cells, while systematic exploration of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and their clinical prognostic significance remains insufficient.

As one of the most critical stromal components in the TME, CAFs exert essential functions in regulating tumor growth, invasion, metastasis, and immune suppression. CAFs are highly heterogeneous and can be classified into three major subtypes: iCAFs, myCAFs, and apCAFs, with distinct phenotypic characteristics and biological functions22. iCAFs are mainly distributed in perivascular and connective tissues and highly express inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and LIF. myCAFs are located adjacent to tumor cell nests, characterized by high expression of α-SMA and FAP, and secrete TGF-β to remodel the extracellular matrix, thereby promoting tumor invasion, angiogenesis, and chemoresistance40. apCAFs are capable of expressing MHC-II and CD74 molecules, presenting tumor antigens to CD4⁺ T cells, and participating in the regulation of anti-tumor immune responses41. Although the biological functions of CAFs have been partially reported, the prognostic value of CAF subtypes and CAF-related genes (CAFRGs) in PCa has not been systematically clarified, and clinically applicable CAF-based prognostic signatures are still lacking42.

In the present study, we integrated scRNA-seq and bulk transcriptomic data to systematically characterize CAF subpopulation heterogeneity in PCa. We confirmed that apCAFs and myCAFs were significantly associated with biochemical recurrence-free survival (BRFS), indicating their critical roles in PCa progression and recurrence. Based on CAF subtype characteristic genes, we further screened five hub CAFRGs (SYNM, NR4A1, MSMB, HOPX, and GJC1) and constructed a novel prognostic signature43. Internal training/validation partition and multiple external independent cohort validation verified that the CAFRG signature could stably stratify PCa patients into high- and low-risk subgroups with distinct BRFS outcomes44. Low-risk patients exhibited remarkably better BRFS, while high-risk cases suffered a significantly higher BCR rate45–47.

Among the five hub CAFRGs, GJC1 deserves further discussion because it is less well characterized in PCa than SYNM, NR4A1, MSMB, and HOPX. GJC1 encodes gap junction protein gamma 1, also known as connexin 45, a connexin involved in gap junction-mediated intercellular communication47. This function is relevant to CAF biology, as CAFs regulate tumor progression partly through stromal–tumor communication, extracellular matrix remodeling, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Previous studies support the potential cancer-related relevance of GJC148–50. Therefore, although direct experimental evidence for GJC1 in PCa CAFs is still lacking, its involvement in intercellular communication, ECM/cell adhesion-related processes, and tumor proliferation-associated pathways provides biological plausibility for its inclusion in the CAFRG signature. In this study, GJC1 should be interpreted as a data-driven prognostic CAFRG that may reflect CAF-associated stromal communication and malignant microenvironmental states, rather than as a functionally validated driver gene. Further CAF-specific experiments are needed to clarify its role in PCa progression and recurrence.

Notably, our prognostic signature showed superior predictive performance compared with conventional clinical predictors, including Gleason score and PSA, as well as other gene-set models derived from the whole transcriptome, cell cycle/EMT pathway, and immune-related genes. The CAFRG signature maintained stable prognostic capability across different clinical subgroups and served as an independent prognostic factor after adjusting for age, tumor stage, Gleason score, and PSA. Furthermore, we constructed a nomogram integrating the risk signature and clinical parameters, which achieved favorable predictive accuracy for 3-, 5-, and 7-year BRFS, providing a convenient quantitative tool for clinical individualized risk assessment.

ADT is an important potential confounder that should be considered when interpreting the prognostic relevance of the CAFRG signature51,52. ADT is commonly used in patients with locally advanced, recurrent, or metastatic PCa, and it may influence both BCR/BRFS outcomes and CAF biology53,54. Previous studies suggest that androgen signaling changes can affect CAF-derived cytokine secretion, exosomal communication, and stromal remodeling, thereby contributing to tumor progression and therapy resistance55. Therefore, the association between the CAFRG signature and BCR/BRFS may be partly influenced by ADT-related stromal changes. However, detailed information on ADT exposure, timing, duration, and treatment sequence was not uniformly available across the public datasets analyzed in this study. Thus, we could not fully adjust for ADT-related confounding. Future cohorts with complete treatment annotation are needed to determine whether the CAFRG signature retains independent prognostic value after accounting for ADT.

To further explore the biological implications of the CAFRG signature, we compared pathway enrichment, somatic mutation profiles, immune microenvironment characteristics, and malignant phenotype-related pathway activity between high- and low-risk groups. The high-risk subgroup showed enrichment of cell cycle, DNA replication, and mismatch repair pathways, together with higher somatic mutation burden and elevated TMB. Immune infiltration analysis revealed increased immune cell abundance and higher expression of several immune checkpoint genes in the high-risk subgroup. These findings indicate that the CAFRG signature is associated with distinct immune microenvironment features and may provide clues for future studies on immunotherapy stratification. However, because this study did not include an immune checkpoint inhibitor-treated PCa cohort with response or survival endpoints, the present data cannot directly prove that high-risk patients are more responsive to immunotherapy. In addition, CAFRG scores were correlated with angiogenesis, EMT, and cell cycle-related pathway activity, suggesting that the CAFRG signature may reflect malignant biological states of PCa. Nevertheless, these associations are derived from transcriptomic correlation analyses and should not be interpreted as evidence of direct functional regulation. Further in vitro and in vivo experiments, such as gene knockdown or overexpression assays, co-culture models, migration and invasion assays, and immune-cell interaction experiments, are needed to determine whether the five hub CAFRGs functionally contribute to CAF-mediated tumor progression and immune remodeling.

In terms of research novelty and incremental contribution, although the datasets adopted in this study have been used in previous PCa studies, most previous reports focused on tumor epithelial cell heterogeneity and ignored CAF subtype characteristics. Few studies have constructed prognostic signatures specifically based on CAF subtype-derived genes. Methodologically, we adopted a strict nested cross-validation framework to avoid overfitting and information leakage, which ensures the robustness and reproducibility of the model. Moreover, we comprehensively explored immune landscape, genomic mutation features, malignant pathway correlation, and drug sensitivity prediction, providing more systematic translational implications than previous single-dimensional analyses.

Meanwhile, this study still has several limitations. First, all transcriptomic and clinical data were retrieved from public databases; despite batch effect correction by the ComBat algorithm, potential platform heterogeneity and cohort selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Second, only two cohorts contained complete BRFS and OS information, which may limit the generalizability of the survival results. Third, although nested cross-validation was used to reduce overfitting, retrospective public data inevitably carry inherent limitations. Fourth, due to the lack of an independent immune checkpoint inhibitor-treated PCa cohort, the predictive value of the signature for immunotherapy response needs further prospective validation. Finally, we only performed bioinformatic mining and did not conduct in vitro or in vivo functional experiments to verify the biological roles of the five hub CAFRGs. Further molecular experiments and prospective clinical cohort studies are required to validate our findings and promote clinical transformation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study constructed and validated a novel prognostic signature based on CAF-related genes by integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data. The signature can accurately predict BRFS and BCR risk of PCa patients, outperforms traditional clinical indicators, and reflects immune microenvironment characteristics and malignant pathway alterations. Our findings provide new insights into the biological function of CAFs in PCa progression and offer a promising molecular biomarker and theoretical basis for clinical prognostic stratification, immunotherapy formulation, and personalized management of prostate cancer.
