Integrative pan-cancer and COAD-focused multi-omics analysis of non-histone acetylation substrate-related transcriptional landscapes
Authors: Jiahao Zhu, Jiyun Shi, Peipei Shen, Rui Lou, Bin Zhang, Erwen Bao, Danqi Qian, Shengjun Ji, Ke Gu, Yong Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-histone acetylation regulates protein stability, transcription, and immune signaling, yet its substrate-specific roles in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) remain unclear. We performed an integrative multi-omics analysis to characterize acetylation substrate-related transcriptional signatures and their clinical relevance. Expression patterns and prognostic impacts of 33 acetylation-related enzymes were evaluated pan-cancer, and substrate-related networks were computationally inferred. A 15-gene substrate expression-based acetylation substrate-related imbalance score (AIScore) stratified COAD patients into prognostically distinct groups. Single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics revealed AIScore-high malignant cells preferentially interacting with SPP1-positive macrophages, forming immunosuppressive niches with reduced predicted immunotherapy response. Exploratory deep learning-based pathomics of whole-slide images, including ResNet-50 feature extraction, PCA, clustering, and Grad-CAM visualization, highlighted AIScore-associated morphologic hotspots at tumor–immune interfaces. These findings indicate that non-histone acetylation substrate-related transcriptional programs in COAD are linked to poor prognosis and spatial immune heterogeneity, while pathomics provides supportive morphology-linked evidence requiring further validation.

Introduction

Acetylation is a prevalent and versatile post-translational modification of proteins. Using acetyl-CoA as the acetyl group donor, acetyltransferases and deacetylases catalyze the addition and removal of acetyl groups from protein compounds, respectively. In histone acetylation, histone acetyltransferases and deacetylases modify the electrostatic interactions between histones and DNA, leading to a relaxed chromatin structure that facilitates transcriptional activation1.

Besides histones, non-histone proteins also undergo lysine acetylation. Non-histone acetylation encompasses a wider array of enzymes and target proteins and is involved in signaling, DNA damage repair, autophagy, mRNA stability, and protein-protein interactions2,3. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that these substrates of lysine acetyltransferases (KATs) and lysine deacetylases (KDACs) play critical roles in tumorigenesis and tumor progression. SKP2 was observed to be acetylated at K68 and K71 by EP300, a modification that can be counteracted by Sirtuin 3 (SIRT3). This acetylation disrupts Cadherin 1-mediated proteolysis and partially enhances cellular migration by promoting the proteasomal degradation of E-cadherin, thereby increasing the oncogenic activity of SKP24. AGO2 acetylation by EP300/CREBBP was found to promote cancer progression by increasing the biogenesis of the oncogenic miR-19b, which could be inhibited by Histone deacetylase 7 (HDAC7)5. Besides the regulation of oncoproteins, acetylation plays a role in tumor suppression. TP53 was one of the first non-histone proteins identified to undergo acetylation. The C-terminal domain of p53 is acetylated by EP300, which enhances its DNA-binding activity and activates TP536. In terms of immune response, EP300 and HDAC2 regulate the acetylation and deacetylation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), respectively. This modulation influences PD-L1’s nuclear localization and alters the transcriptional expression of various immune response-related genes7. In recent years, the expansion of acetylome databases has led to the emergence of numerous computational tools for identifying acetylation sites, such as DeepAcet8 and TransPTM9. However, current in silico protein acetylation tools, whether based on traditional machine learning or deep learning models, are primarily applied to histone acetylation. Despite the identification of a substantial number of lysine-acetylated proteins, the elucidation of the interactions between acetylation enzymes and their substrates has lagged behind.

In this study, we systematically characterized acetylation-related enzymes and substrate-associated transcriptional programs in cancer by integrating multi-omics data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) along with relevant knowledge resources to evaluate their pan-cancer effects. We assessed mutation, expression, and prognostic patterns of acetylation enzymes across various cancer types. To investigate the potential clinical relevance of acetylation enzyme-substrate interaction networks, we developed predictive models for both acetyltransferases and deacetylases to identify additional cancer-related enzyme-substrate interactions (ASI) and analyze their perturbation in specific cancer types. Furthermore, we constructed a prognostic model in colorectal cancer (COAD) using genes encoding acetylation-related substrates and explored AIScore-associated tumor microenvironment features using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) data. We hypothesized that these transcriptional programs are functionally relevant to tumor progression and patient prognosis, and that a computationally derived acetylation substrate-related imbalance score (AIScore) can serve as a robust biomarker for patient stratification. This hypothesis guided all subsequent computational analyses, including multi-cohort transcriptomic profiling, survival modeling, spatial transcriptomics, and cell-cell communication inference.

Results

Multiomics analysis of acetylation enzymes in pan-cancer

Thirty-three acetylation enzymes, including 15 acetyltransferases and 18 deacetylases were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1A). Analysis of somatic mutations in the TCGA dataset revealed that mutations in acetylation enzymes are infrequent. Even for frequently mutated enzymes like EP300 and CREBBP, mutation frequencies were around 3% (Fig. 1B). The highest mutation rates were observed in bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) (Fig. 1C). EP300 and CREBBP, two homologous lysine acetyltransferases, are crucial for tumor cell transformation and maintaining an oncogenic phenotype. Significant changes in expression of acetylation enzymes between tumor and normal samples were observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 1D). ESCO2, HDAC8, HDAC10, and SIRT7 were significantly upregulated across various cancers, while the lysine acetyltransferase KAT2B was significantly downregulated. This indicates consistent expression changes of the same acetylation enzyme in different tumors and corresponding normal tissues.

Most acetylation enzymes had prognostic effects on overall survival (OS), though the influence varied by cancer type (Fig. 1D). Despite coherent expressional alterations of enzymes like ESCO2 and HDAC10 across various cancers, they are associated with opposite prognostic outcomes in different cancers. This may be because these enzymes target different substrates in different cancer types.

Transcriptomic analysis of TCGA-breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA) and proteomic data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) showed that the protein expression of 33 acetylation enzymes closely correlated with their mRNA levels (Fig. 1E). Proteomic differential expression analysis revealed that most acetylation enzymes were differentially expressed between tumors and normal tissues, with these differences being consistent with mRNA expression differences (Fig. 1F).

Pathway analysis of acetylation enzymes in various cancers

A comprehensive pan-cancer landscape of acetyltransferases (Fig. S1A, Data S1) and deacetylases (Fig. S2A, Data S2) pathway interactions was constructed, revealing strong correlations with several cancer-relevant pathways including those involved in the immune system, signal transduction, and cell growth and death. The cell cycle emerged as the most common signaling pathway involving acetyltransferases across various tumors, while the Apelin signaling pathway was the most common for deacetylases. Further investigation of high-confidence acetylation enzyme pathway correlations (P < 0.05) in the ten cancer types with the largest sample sizes highlighted a series of pathway interactions for both acetyltransferases (Fig. S1B) and deacetylases (Fig. S2B).

Acetylation enzyme-substrate interaction prediction models prioritize candidate cancer-relevant acetylation enzyme substrates

We developed two acetylation enzyme-substrate interaction (ASI) prediction models to prioritize potential candidate substrates for acetyltransferases and deacetylases, as illustrated in Fig. 2A. A literature review identified 57 acetyltransferase-substrate pairs (Data S3) and 113 deacetylase-substrate pairs (Data S4). These interactions showed higher normalized mutual information-based expression associations and greater biological function similarities than random protein interactions. Random protein pairs were used as computational negative/background samples for model training, but they should not be interpreted as experimentally confirmed non-interacting pairs. Reported acetylation enzyme-substrate interactions showed tighter protein-protein interaction (PPI) network connections and higher domain-pair enrichment compared with randomly generated protein pairs. Both models for acetyltransferases (Fig. 2B) and deacetylases (Fig. 2C) exhibited similar trends in multidimensional feature differences. Internal computational evaluations, including random split validation and enzyme-exclusion validation, showed favorable discriminative performance of both models, with receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) values exceeding 0.85 (Fig. 2D, E, Data S5). Positive samples received significantly higher support vector machine (SVM) prediction scores than randomly generated negative samples (Fig. 2F–I). A number of tumor-related candidate ASIs were computationally prioritized. Out of 37,080 candidate protein pairs for acetyltransferases, 864 candidate pairs involving 289 candidate substrates received SVM prediction scores greater than 0.9 (Data S6). Similarly, for deacetylases, 532 candidate pairs involving 256 candidate substrates were prioritized by the SVM model from 44,496 candidate pairs (Data S7). CREBBP and EP300 had more substrates among acetyltransferases (Fig. 2J), while SIRT1 had more substrates among deacetylases (Fig. 2K). To account for the reversibility of acetylation and facilitate future research, we merged reported substrates with computationally predicted candidate substrates prioritized by our models. We then selected candidate substrates potentially associated with both acetyltransferases and deacetylases. Finally, 110 computationally prioritized candidate substrates and 676 corresponding candidate interaction pairs were retained for downstream exploratory analyses (Fig. 2L, M, Data S8). Given that these candidates were derived from computational prioritization rather than external biochemical validation, subsequent AIScore construction, tumor classification, and pathway analyses were interpreted as hypothesis-generating analyses based on acetylation substrate-related transcriptional programs.

Tumor subtype identification based on acetylation substrate-related expression profiles

To characterize substrate expression-associated heterogeneity across various cancer types, we categorized tumor samples into distinct groups by analyzing the mRNA and protein expression profiles of the 110 substrates within the computationally inferred candidate ASI network. We clustered nine cancer types, including bladder cancer (BLCA), breast cancer (BRCA), COAD, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), lower grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), sarcoma (SARC), and uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), into subtypes with significantly different prognoses (Fig. 3A). To uncover the molecular differences underlying substrate expression-associated heterogeneity, we identified differentially expressed substrates between clusters. In BLCA, COAD, KIRC, and UCEC, most substrates were upregulated in poor prognosis clusters, while in BRCA, LGG, LIHC, LUAD, and SARC, most substrates were downregulated (Fig. 3B). Functional heterogeneity was associated with pathways such as the cell cycle, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and TNF signaling (Fig. 3C). COAD, KIRC, LIHC, and LUAD shared 12 pathways, including extracellular matrix (ECM) receptor interaction, phosphoinositide 3-kinase-protein kinase B (PI3K-AKT) signaling, and ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation (Fig. 3D, Data S9).

Acetylases and their substrates are associated with tumor prognostic heterogeneity

Various substrates showed differential expression in COAD, KIRC, LIHC, and LUAD (Fig. S3A), indicating that precise modulation could enhance treatment outcomes. These substrates were linked to corresponding acetylases in the computationally inferred ASI network. We assessed the prognostic effects of acetylases and their substrates, finding that co-high expression of certain acetylases and their substrates can lead to either unfavorable or favorable prognosis (Fig. S3B).

Inhibiting specific acetylases associated with poor prognosis (e.g., CREBBP for COAD-C2, HDAC4 for LUAD-C2) may improve outcomes by reducing overexpression of subtype-driver substrates (e.g., SNAI1 for CREBBP in COAD, SFPQ for EP300 in LIHC, SNAI1 for HDAC4 in LUAD). Conversely, multiple favorable ASIs were identified for KIRC (e.g., ARNT and ACLY for CREBBP in KIRC), suggesting that these acetylase-substrate pairs may warrant further investigation as potential therapeutic targets.

Acetylation substrate-related imbalance score predicts prognosis and immunotherapy response in COAD

To analyze the prognostic relevance of genes encoding substrates potentially modifiable by both acetyltransferases and deacetylases, we performed univariate analysis on 110 genes in the TCGA-COAD cohort, identifying 35 genes associated with prognosis (26 linked to worse, 9 to better outcomes; Fig. 4A). These genes were used to construct an acetylation substrate-related imbalance score (AIScore) using established machine-learning and survival modeling approaches, with a leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework applied to evaluate 101 model combinations. The TCGA-COAD cohort served as the training dataset, with GSE29621 and GSE17536 for validation. To determine the best parameters for the model, we identified the optimal λ when the penalized likelihood deviation reached its minimum value in the TCGA-COAD training cohort (Fig. 5C, left panel). For the RSF component, the error rate was used as a function of the classification tree (Fig. 5C, middle panel), and the out-of-bag importance values of the predicted genes were calculated to determine their contributions (Fig. 4C, right panel). AIScore was calculated using the regression coefficient-weighted expressions of these genes. AIScore-high patients had significantly lower OS in TCGA-COAD (P = 0.008), GSE29621 (P = 0.011), and GSE17536 (P = 0.002) cohorts (Fig. 4D-F). AIScore-low patients had better disease-free survival (DFS) in GSE17536 (P = 0.012; Fig. 4G). Chi-square tests confirmed significant survival benefits for AIScore-low COAD patients across cohorts (P < 0.05; Fig. 4H–J). To further determine whether AIScore provided prognostic information independent of clinicopathological variables, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses in the TCGA-COAD, GSE29621, and GSE17536 cohorts. In the TCGA-COAD cohort, AIScore remained significantly associated with unfavorable overall survival after adjustment for age, gender, pathological stage, and microsatellite instability (MSI) status. Consistent results were also observed in the GSE29621 and GSE17536 validation cohorts after adjustment for available clinical variables, including age, gender, pathological stage, and histological grade. These results support AIScore as an independent prognostic indicator for COAD patients (Data S10). To assess the prognostic performance of AIScore, we performed time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) analysis, calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis. The time-dependent AUCs at 12, 36, and 60 months demonstrated stable discriminative performance of AIScore (Fig. S4A). Calibration curves showed acceptable agreement between predicted and observed survival probabilities at 12, 36, and 60 months (Fig. S4B–D). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed involvement in cell-cell communication, receptor tyrosine kinase signaling, and T cell factor (TCF)-dependent Wingless/Integrated (WNT) signaling (Fig. 4K). Low AIScore was associated with a positive programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor response, while high AIScore correlated with ineffectiveness, according to the Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm (Fig. 4L). AIScore was also positively correlated with the myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) score (Fig. 4M). To further compare AIScore with established immunotherapy-related indicators, we evaluated the prognostic associations of AIScore, MSI status, CD274 expression, CD8 T cell infiltration score, T cell exhaustion score, and tumor mutation burden (TMB) in the TCGA-COAD cohort. AIScore showed the strongest association with overall survival, with a hazard ratio of 3.23 and a 95% confidence interval of 1.96–5.33 (P = 4.42 × 10−6). In contrast, MSI status, CD274 expression, CD8 T cell infiltration score, T cell exhaustion score, and TMB were not significantly associated with overall survival (Data S11). These findings suggest that the AIScore may serve as a prognostic indicator and a potential marker of predicted immunotherapy response; single-cell analysis was then performed to explore AIScore-associated tumor microenvironmental features.

Heterogeneity of malignant epithelial cells in COAD and cell communication in the 5-cohort dataset

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from colon cancer tissues were extracted from a 5-cohort datasets comprising 42 COAD patients and 163,225 cells (Fig. 5A). Major cell clusters were annotated based on marker genes, identifying T/natural killer (T/NK) cells, B cells, plasma cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, epithelial cells, and glial cells (Fig. 5B). The proportional composition of these nine cell types across these samples is shown in Fig. 5C. Cancer cells in COAD, derived from epithelial cells, were identified by assessing copy number variation (CNV) inferred from single-cell gene expression data, confirming the malignant nature of the epithelial cells (Fig. 5D).

We re-clustered 26,932 malignant epithelial cells to differentiate those with high and low AIScores. Eleven clusters of malignant epithelial cells were identified, with clusters 2 and 5 classified as the AIScore-high malignant epithelial state (Epi_AIScoreH) and the remaining clusters classified as the AIScore-low malignant epithelial state (Epi_AIScoreL) (Fig. 5E, F). The Epi_AIScoreH classification was used as an exploratory malignant epithelial cell-state annotation. Cell-level analyses were used to identify transcriptional and communication patterns, whereas biological interpretation focused on sample-level trends and consistency with spatial transcriptomics rather than treating individual cells as independent clinical observations. Cell–cell interaction analyses were conducted to investigate ligand-receptor interactions between malignant epithelial cells with high or low AIScores and other cell types. Results from Cellchat demonstrated varying interactive strengths among the 10 cell subtypes, with the greatest interactions between Epi_AIScoreH and Epi_AIScoreL, followed by myeloid cells (Fig. 5G).

Given the significant association between AIScore and MDSC score, we focused on interactions between malignant epithelial cells and myeloid cells. Differential interaction pathways included midkine (MK) and secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) signaling pathways (Fig. 5H). Cell-cell communication analysis predicted that Epi_AIScoreH cells interacted with myeloid cells through the MK signaling pathway, while myeloid cells interacted with Epi_AIScoreH via the SPP1 signaling pathway. Myeloid cells were re-clustered into 13 subtypes (Fig. 5I, J, Data S12)10.

Further analyses revealed that Epi_AIScoreH cells were primary senders of the MK signaling pathway, with SPP1-positive macrophages (Macro_SPP1) as significant receivers (Fig. 5K). Epi_AIScoreH interacted with all myeloid cells primarily through the midkine-nucleolin (MDK-NCL) ligand-receptor pair, except neutrophils (Fig. 5L, M). Macro_SPP1 cells were identified as primary senders of the SPP1 signaling pathway, with Epi_AIScoreH as major receivers compared to AIScore low malignant epithelial cells (AIScoreL_Epi). Other notable receivers included CXCL10-positive macrophages (Macro_CXCL10), SLC40A1-positive macrophages (Macro_SLC40A1), monocytes, and proliferating myeloid cells (Fig. 5N). The interaction predominantly occurred via the SPP1-CD44 ligand-receptor pair (Fig. 5O, P).

Dynamic changes in the proportion of Epi_AIScoreH cells and signaling pathways during immunotherapy

The GSE236581 dataset provides scRNA-seq data from 22 intestinal cancer patients who received neoadjuvant immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based immunotherapy, with tissues collected before, during, and after treatment. This dataset allows for the analysis of changes in cell type proportions and signaling pathways throughout immunotherapy. Primary cell types were annotated based on specific gene markers (Fig. S5A, B), and malignant epithelial cells were identified using CNV analysis and subsequently reclustered (Fig. S5C). Epi_AIScoreH and Epi_AIScoreL in the GSE236581 dataset were determined using the previously described scoring method (Fig. S5D, E). Myeloid cells were annotated into 11 subtypes (Fig. S5F, G). We compared the proportions of Epi_AIScoreH cells before treatment, after fewer than three cycles, and after three cycles of neoadjuvant ICI treatment between patients with complete response (CR) and those with non-complete response (nonCR). NonCR patients exhibited a significantly higher proportion of Epi_AIScoreH before treatment (P = 0.039), but no significant differences were observed after treatment (P = 0.281) and post-surgery (P = 0.099) (Fig. 5Q).

We also investigated changes in signaling pathways between malignant epithelial cells with different AIScores and Macro_SPP1 in non-CR patients during neoadjuvant ICI treatment. Before ICI treatment, the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway was the main interaction between Epi_AIScoreH and Macro_SPP1, but it was inactive after treatment. The chemokine signaling pathway between Epi_AIScoreH and Macro_SPP1 remained active throughout immunotherapy, and adherens junctions between all malignant epithelial cells and Macro_SPP1 were consistently active (Fig. 5R). Regarding the impact of Macro_SPP1 on malignant epithelial cells with different AIScores, the influence of the MAPK signaling pathway on Epi_AIScoreH increased with more immunotherapy cycles. Additionally, the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kappa B) signaling pathway between Macro_SPP1 and all malignant epithelial cells remained active throughout the immunotherapy process (Fig. 5R).

Discussion

In this study, we systematically characterized acetylase alterations across 33 cancer types and identified widespread transcriptional dysregulation despite relatively low mutation frequencies. By constructing computationally inferred candidate ASI networks, we identified acetylation substrate-related expression patterns associated with tumor heterogeneity and prognostic divergence. In COAD, we established the AIScore signature based on dual-modifiable substrates, which robustly predicted overall survival and immunotherapy response. Importantly, integrative analyses combining single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics, and digital pathology–based pathomics revealed that AIScore-associated heterogeneity is not only molecular but also spatially and morphologically manifested. Deep learning–driven histomorphological annotation demonstrated that high-AIScore regions were enriched at tumor–stromal interfaces. Together, these findings indicate that acetylation substrate-related transcriptional signatures are reflected across multiple data layers, extending from transcriptomic profiles to spatial tissue architecture in COAD.

Protein acetylation is a critical post-translational modification. Despite numerous machine learning tools for predicting acetylation sites, benchmarks for non-histone sites are scarce. Wu et al. presented DeepAcet, using an MLP to model lysine acetylation, achieving accuracies of 84.95% and 84.87% in 10-fold cross-validation and independent testing, respectively8. However, it lacked structural feature consideration and systematic hyperparameter optimization. Recently, TransPTM, a transformer-based model, was developed using ProtT5-derived features and a Graph Neural Network (GNN) with TransformerConv layers, followed by a multilayer perceptron (MLP), showing robust performance in predicting non-histone acetylation sites9. Nevertheless, it did not employ synthetic minority over-sampling and lacked integration of biological data. Given the paucity of known non-histone acetylation substrates and large-scale data, our model employed synthetic over-sampling and incorporated biological function information, providing a computational framework for prioritizing candidate non-histone acetylation-related substrates for future validation.

Interestingly, the expression profiles of computationally prioritized candidate ASIs revealed clinically relevant cancer subtypes, where pivotal acetylases and their substrates collectively led to notable variations in prognosis across these subtypes. The clinical relevance of acetylase-associated expression patterns may depend, at least in part, on their linked candidate substrates. We then constructed an AIScore using established machine-learning and survival modeling approaches within a LOOCV framework. This comparative modeling strategy allowed us to evaluate multiple established algorithm combinations and select the model with the best average performance across the training and validation cohorts. The model’s performance was validated with an external dataset, confirming the robustness of the AIScore across different experimental settings. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses further supported the prognostic relevance of AIScore. After adjustment for available clinicopathological variables, AIScore remained significantly associated with overall survival in the TCGA-COAD, GSE29621, and GSE17536 cohorts, suggesting that AIScore may provide prognostic information beyond conventional clinical factors. The resulting 15-gene acetylation substrate-related expression signature showed favorable performance in predicting OS in COAD patients. Additionally, this AIScore may hold potential for estimating reduced likelihood of response to anti-PD-1 therapy in COAD, although further clinical studies are required to validate this application.

This study identified intercellular communication between Epi_AIScoreH and Macro_SPP1 via MK and SPP1 signaling pathways. SPP1 (osteopontin) is a multifunctional protein and potential cancer biomarker, with elevated levels linked to poor colorectal cancer (CRC) prognosis13. Advances in scRNA-seq revealed that Macro_SPP1 contributes to tumor progression and immune escape by expressing high levels of matrix metallopeptidase, which degrades the basement membrane to facilitate tumor invasion14. SPP1/CD44 signaling is associated with metastasis initiation15 and tumor growth promotion16. SPP1, expressed perivascularly with CD44, promotes tumor stem cell-like phenotypes through the CD44 intracellular domain, enhancing tumor aggressiveness and stem cell-like traits via CREBBP/EP300-mediated HIF-2α activity17. Our cell-cell communication analysis suggested that SPP1/CD44 signaling may act as a major ligand-receptor pair in SPP1 pathways, potentially worsening survival for COAD patients with high AIScore. Interaction between Macro_SPP1 and tumor epithelial cells activates nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/Akt) pathways, promoting tumor proliferation17. Consistently, we observed that the NF-kB pathway played a significant role in non-CR intestinal cancer patients throughout ICI treatment. Spatial transcriptome analysis revealed that extracellular matrix signaling was enriched in both Macro_SPP1 and Epi_AIScoreH spots. Qi et al. showed that Macro_SPP1 interacts with fibroblasts via TGFB1, leading to secretion of MMPs and collagen, remodeling the extracellular matrix, and forming a desmoplastic microenvironment in CRC18. This barrier hinders lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor core, reducing the efficacy of ICIs. Additionally, adherens junction signaling between Macro_SPP1 and Epi_AIScoreH persisted throughout immunotherapy in nonCR patients. Epi_AIScoreH levels were higher in nonCR patients before ICIs treatment, potentially enhancing the barrier. These factors may be associated with immune escape features in high-AIScore tumors and reduced predicted ICI response.

Although single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed AIScore-associated malignant epithelial states and their spatial association with SPP1-positive macrophages, these findings remain correlative and hypothesis-generating. Although five single-cell scoring algorithms were used to define the Epi_AIScoreH state, these algorithms have different score distributions and may be affected by shared malignant transcriptional programs such as cell cycle, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), hypoxia, and stress response. Therefore, Epi_AIScoreH should be interpreted as an AIScore-associated malignant epithelial transcriptional state rather than a specific acetylation-driven cell state. In addition, although the association between Epi_AIScoreH and Macro_SPP1 was supported by spatial transcriptomics and by analysis of an intestinal cancer neoadjuvant immunotherapy single-cell dataset, larger independent single-cell cohorts will be required to confirm the stability of patient-level Epi_AIScoreH proportions and the reproducibility of Epi_AIScoreH–Macro_SPP1 interactions.

Some limitations existed in the present study. First, the AIScore was derived from mRNA expression levels of genes encoding known or computationally predicted candidate acetylation-related substrates. mRNA abundance does not necessarily reflect protein abundance and cannot determine the fraction of a protein that is acetylated. Therefore, the AIScore should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of non-histone acetylation activity or acetylation stoichiometry. Instead, it represents an acetylation substrate-related transcriptional signature. Although single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses revealed AIScore-associated malignant epithelial states and their spatial association with SPP1-positive macrophages, these findings remain correlative and hypothesis-generating. Second, the ASI network in this study was mainly derived from computational prediction. Although the SVM model integrated normalized mutual information, functional similarity, PPI network proximity, and structural domain associations, these features do not provide direct biochemical evidence of enzyme-substrate binding or site-specific acetylation. In addition, because negative samples were generated from random protein pairs outside the curated ASI set, some negative samples may represent undiscovered true substrates, which could introduce label noise and affect model calibration. The newly predicted acetyltransferase-substrate and deacetylase-substrate pairs were not validated by co-immunoprecipitation, acetyl-proteomics, site-specific acetylation assays, or targeted mass spectrometry in the current study. Third, due to the limited number of known non-histone acetylation substrates, the background dataset used for model training is not sufficiently large, which may affect the stability and generalizability of the prediction model. Fourth, although our study offers valuable insights using scRNA-seq and ST, the spatial transcriptomics analysis included only four tissue sections from two public datasets. Spatial transcriptomics studies often include a limited number of tissue sections because of the high cost, tissue availability, and technical requirements of spatial profiling. Therefore, these spatial analyses should not be interpreted as population-level validation or as definitive evidence of a robust spatial niche. Instead, they were designed as proof-of-concept and hypothesis-generating analyses to investigate whether AIScore-high malignant epithelial states identified from transcriptomic analyses were spatially associated with SPP1-positive macrophage-enriched niches. In addition, although SpaCET-based deconvolution and COMMOT-based signaling inference provided useful spatial information, these results were computationally inferred and were not validated by pathologist-annotated spot-level labels, multiplex immunofluorescence, or spatial proteomics. Future studies using larger spatial transcriptomic cohorts, formal spatial statistical approaches such as Moran’s I, Ripley’s K, permutation testing, or neighborhood enrichment analysis, and orthogonal validation methods will be required. This interpretation is further supported by complementary evidence from scRNA-seq-based cell-cell communication analysis and digital pathology-based histomorphological mapping. In addition, variations and biases in single-cell protocols may affect low-expression gene detection, and the current resolution of ST limits the decoding of complex tissue structures. Fifth, although multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed, KRAS and BRAF mutation status could not be consistently incorporated into all cohorts because these molecular annotations were not uniformly available in the external validation datasets. Future studies using cohorts with complete clinical and molecular annotations are required to determine whether AIScore remains independently prognostic after adjustment for KRAS and BRAF mutation status. Sixth, we did not directly compare AIScore with established colorectal cancer molecular classification systems, such as consensus molecular subtypes (CMS) and colorectal cancer intrinsic subtypes (CRIS), because standardized subtype labels were not consistently available across all validation cohorts. Further studies integrating AIScore with CMS, CRIS, and other transcriptomic classifiers will be necessary to determine its incremental prognostic value. In addition, this study lacks direct functional, biochemical, or orthogonal experimental validation. The predicted acetylation substrate-associated transcriptional programs, spatial co-localization patterns, and deep learning-derived histomorphological regions were inferred from computational analyses. Although we integrated multiple independent data layers, including bulk transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and digital pathology, these approaches do not establish causal mechanisms. Future studies incorporating acetyl-proteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, targeted mass spectrometry, immunofluorescence or multiplex immunohistochemistry, and in vitro or in vivo functional assays will be necessary to validate the biological mechanisms suggested by our computational findings. Finally, although AIScore is related to acetylation substrate-associated transcriptional features, we did not evaluate its association with sensitivity to acetylation enzyme inhibitors, such as HDAC inhibitors, using Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC), PRISM, or experimental models. Improved data processing and analytical methods are needed to maximize the potential of these technologies. Our analyses highlight the need for future prospective studies and comprehensive in vivo and in vitro validations.

In conclusion, non-histone acetylation substrate-related transcriptional signatures are associated with prognostic heterogeneity and immune microenvironmental differences in COAD. The AIScore may help stratify COAD patients with distinct clinical outcomes and characterize substrate expression-associated tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity. The computationally inferred ASI network provides a candidate framework for prioritizing acetylation-related substrates, but key enzyme-substrate relationships require future biochemical validation.

All computational analyses, including differential gene expression, substrate prediction, AIScore construction, and integration of single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data, were performed in a stepwise manner directly aligned with this hypothesis. Ethics statement

This study primarily analyzed publicly available datasets. No new human samples were collected for this study. Analyses of publicly available data were conducted in accordance with the original publications and institutional guidelines.

TCGA data preparation and basic analysis

Somatic mutation data were obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) and analyzed using the “maftools” R package19. The mutation load for each cancer type was calculated based on the average number of mutations per sample. Normalized bulk mRNA sequencing data, along with clinical information from TCGA, were downloaded using the “TCGAbiolinks” R package20. Proteomics data were obtained from CPTAC21. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the “limma” R package22. For the survival analysis, patients were stratified into two groups based on the median expression. The log-rank test was then employed to compare survival times between the groups.

Pathway correlation analysis of acetylation enzymes

To clarify the relationships of acetylation enzyme pathways across different cancer types, we constructed a pan-cancer interaction landscape for these pathways. Within each cancer type, we employed the “WGCNA” R package23 to calculate transcriptomics-based biweight midcorrelations (using the bicor function) between each acetylation enzyme and other genes, ranking them by their absolute values. For each acetylation enzyme, we utilized the signaling pathway impact analysis (SPIA) algorithm24 to identify pathways significantly impacted by the top 500 genes.

Substrate prediction model construction

To prioritize potential ASIs, we constructed two computational prediction models for acetyltransferase-substrate and deacetylase-substrate candidate interactions. Experimentally reported acetylase-substrate pairs were manually curated from PubMed literature and were used as positive samples for model training. Negative samples were defined as randomly generated protein pairs that were not included in the curated set of experimentally validated acetylation enzyme-substrate interactions. We acknowledge that some randomly selected negative pairs may represent undiscovered true substrates, which could introduce label noise. Therefore, the prediction model was used only to prioritize candidate interactions for exploratory downstream analyses rather than to define confirmed biochemical enzyme-substrate relationships.

Association of biological function

The Gene Ontology (GO) is organized into three main categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). For each category, we employed the “GOSemSim” R package to compute the semantic similarity between the GO terms associated with two proteins26.

Association of network

The PPI network-based neighborhood similarity (PPI.NS) between proteins X and Y was calculated based on the p-value obtained from the Fisher exact test on the overlap between the network neighbors of X and Y. The PPI.NS was defined as: PPI.NS = -log10(P).

Association of structure

Structural associations were calculated from the domain pair reoccurrence score (DS):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$AS(X,Y)=\frac{\sum _{k,DDPk\in (X,Y)}w(DDPk)}{N}$$\end{document}AS(X,Y)=∑k,DDPk∈(X,Y)w(DDPk)N

Protein structure domains were obtained from Pfam27. The denominator represents the total number of possible domain-domain pairs (DDPs) between proteins X and Y, where the first domain belongs to protein X and the second to protein Y. The numerator is a weighted sum of these DDPs, with each weight corresponding to the frequency of the DDP in all background ASIs. Background ASIs refer to benchmark ASIs. If a tested protein pair is part of the benchmark ASIs, it is excluded from the background ASIs during the calculation of its domain pair reoccurrence score to avoid repetition. Experimentally verified acetylase substrates were identified through a manual search of PubMed literature and defined as the background ASIs for the prediction model.

Prediction model construction and validation

The Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) was used to address class imbalance during model training, and a SVM classifier was then trained to generate prediction scores for candidate ASIs28. To avoid data leakage, SMOTE was performed only within the training set after data partitioning. The independent testing set was kept completely separate and was not used during oversampling, hyperparameter tuning, or model training. For independent computational validation, the dataset containing both positive and negative ASIs was split into training and testing sets. Two rounds of validation were conducted. In the first round, 85% of the samples were randomly selected for training, with the remaining 15% used as an independent testing set. In the second round, samples involving two acetylation enzymes were randomly excluded from the training set and used as an independent testing set to evaluate model generalizability to enzymes not included during training. These two validation strategies were designed as internal computational validation procedures and do not substitute for validation using an independent external acetylation enzyme-substrate database.

The SVM model was trained using a radial basis function kernel. Hyperparameters were optimized within the training set using cross-validation. The penalty parameter C and kernel parameter γ were searched over a predefined grid, and the parameter combination with the best cross-validation performance was selected for final model training. Given the highly imbalanced nature of the ASI prediction task, model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics rather than ROC-AUC alone, including ROC-AUC, precision-recall AUC, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. Reported substrates and computationally predicted candidate substrates with SVM prediction scores greater than 0.9 were retained for downstream exploratory analyses. The threshold of 0.9 was used as a stringent computational cutoff to prioritize top-ranked candidate interactions rather than as a calibrated probability threshold. The SVM prediction score should therefore be interpreted as a computational prioritization score rather than the probability that a given protein pair represents a true biochemical enzyme-substrate interaction.

Genes from the COSMIC database were gathered29. The “KEGGgraph” R package was used to parse the “Pathways in cancer” KGML file and extract “map” objects30. Genes from these pathways were compiled. Multidimensional features were calculated, and SVM prediction scores for candidate ASIs were generated using the SVM classifier. We integrated the reported substrates and computationally predicted candidate substrates with SVM prediction scores greater than 0.9. Considering the reversibility of acetylation modification, we took the intersection of acetyltransferase candidate substrates and deacetylase candidate substrates from the integrated dataset to form the final acetylation enzyme substrate-related candidate dataset. It should be noted that the SVM-derived prediction score represents a computational prioritization score based on multidimensional features rather than direct biochemical evidence of physical interaction or acetylation modification. Therefore, newly predicted ASIs were considered computationally prioritized candidate interactions for downstream exploratory analyses and require future validation using independent acetylation databases, updated literature curation, acetyl-proteomics, co-immunoprecipitation, site-specific acetylation assays, or targeted mass spectrometry.

Tumor subtype identification based on ASIs

The “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package was used to identify cancer subtypes based on computationally prioritized candidate ASIs31. According to the results of clustering, limma was applied to identify acetylation enzymes with significantly different expression and determine the regulation direction in the poor prognosis group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) was utilized to identify the pathways based on the corresponding substrates.

Acetylation imbalance score construction with machine learning in TCGA-COAD cohort

Ten established machine-learning and survival modeling methods with 101 algorithm combinations were utilized to construct and evaluate AIScore in the TCGA-COAD cohort32. The algorithms employed in our analysis encompass CoxBoost, Cox Partial Least Squares Regression (plsRcox), Elastic Net (Enet), Generalized Boosted Regression Model (GBRM), Lasso, RSF, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, Supervised Principal Component (SuperPC), and SVM. Acetylation enzyme substrates were incorporated into 101 algorithm combinations to develop prediction models within the TCGA-COAD cohort via LOOCV. The external validation cohorts GSE2962133 and GSE1753634 were not involved in feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, model fitting, or algorithm selection. After the final model was fixed using the TCGA-COAD training cohort, its prognostic performance was evaluated in GSE29621 and GSE17536 as independent external validation cohorts. The model with the best internal cross-validated performance in the TCGA-COAD training cohort was selected as the final AIScore model. The C-index was subsequently calculated for both the training and validation sets across all models. The model exhibiting the highest average C-index was selected as the optimal model. Reactome enrichment was conducted with genes used to construct the AIScore35. Because transcriptomic data do not directly measure protein abundance or acetylation stoichiometry, the AIScore was constructed as a substrate gene expression-based signature rather than a direct indicator of protein acetylation levels. Genes included in the AIScore encode reported or computationally prioritized candidate acetylation-related substrates, and the score was used to characterize transcriptional programs associated with acetylation substrate networks. Part of the substrate set was derived from computationally prioritized candidate ASIs; the AIScore should be interpreted as an acetylation substrate-related transcriptional signature rather than a direct readout of validated acetylation enzyme-substrate activity.

Patients were categorized into two AIScore high (AIScoreH) and AIScore low (AIScoreL) groups based on the median AIScore value. This cohort-specific cutoff was used in the main analysis to reduce the influence of cross-platform differences between TCGA RNA-seq data and GEO microarray datasets. The prognostic significance of the risk groups was evaluated using Kaplan-Meier curves and the log-rank test. The TIDE score was calculated using the online tool (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/)36.

Preprocessing of external GEO validation cohorts

The external validation cohorts GSE29621 and GSE17536 were obtained from GEO. GSE29621 was generated using the GPL570 platform, Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. Platform information and probe annotation files were retrieved from GEO. Raw or processed microarray expression matrices were processed independently for each cohort before AIScore calculation. Probe identifiers were mapped to official gene symbols according to the corresponding platform annotation files. Probes without valid gene-symbol annotation were removed. When multiple probes mapped to the same gene symbol, the median expression value of these probes was used to represent the gene-level expression value. For normalization, processed GEO series matrix files were used when available. Expression values were log2-transformed when necessary after inspection of the expression distribution. Quantile normalization was applied to microarray expression matrices when raw probe-level data were reprocessed. Because GSE29621 and GSE17536 were used as independent external validation cohorts and were not merged for model training or model selection, no cross-cohort batch correction was applied between TCGA and GEO datasets or between the two GEO cohorts. Within each GEO cohort, AIScore was calculated using the genes included in the final TCGA-derived model.

scRNA-seq data processing

Two single-cell datasets were utilized in this study, including 5-cohorts37 obtained from Synapse Storage (https://www.synapse.org/Synapse:syn26844071/wiki/615389) and the GSE236581 cohort38 available in GEO. scRNA-seq data were integrated using the Seurat package (version 4.4). The two processed datasets, which had initially undergone quality control by the original authors, were independently analyzed for this study. We normalized the expression data of all cells using the “LogNormalize” function with a scale factor of 10,000. We then identified the top 2000 highly variable genes based on their mean and dispersion metrics. We also zero-centered and scaled each gene to unit variance. This step was crucial for minimizing potential batch effects that could arise during the integration of the datasets. Following this, we performed principal component analysis, a dimensionality reduction technique, to capture the most significant sources of variation within the data. Preliminary clustering and cell type annotation were achieved using the “FindClusters” function, which operated with 50 principal components and a resolution of 0.8 to ensure a detailed and meaningful clustering of the cells. To visualize the clustering results, we employed the uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) method. UMAP is a nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique that effectively captures and represents the complex structure of high-dimensional data in a two-dimensional space, facilitating intuitive visualization of cell clusters.

“InferCNV” was applied to identify malignant epithelial cells39. Specifically, 2000 B cells and T cells were randomly selected. Among these, 500 B cells and T cells were designated as the reference group. The remaining cells were designated as the test group.

scRNA-seq data scores calculating

Five algorithms, including AddModuleScore40, AUCell, ssGSEA, singscore, and UCell41, were used to evaluate AIScore-related gene-set activity in malignant epithelial cells. Because these algorithms generate scores with different scales and distributions, the integrated score was used only for relative cell-state annotation rather than as an absolute quantitative measure. For each scoring method, malignant epithelial cells were ranked according to the corresponding AIScore-related signature activity, and the results from the five methods were integrated to identify cells consistently showing high AIScore-associated transcriptional activity. Cells supported by more than one scoring method were annotated as Epi_AIScoreH, whereas the remaining malignant epithelial cells were annotated as Epi_AIScoreL. This threshold was used as an operational consensus rule for exploratory cell-state annotation rather than as a fixed biological cutoff.

Cell communication analysis

“Cellchat”42 and “CellCall”43 were used to infer cell-to-cell interactions. The “CellChat” R package features a comprehensive signaling interaction database, accounting for structural compositions of receptor-ligand interactions, including multimeric complexes, soluble agonists and antagonists, and stimulatory and inhibitory membrane-bound coreceptors42. CellCall is a toolkit that compiles ligand-receptor-transcription factor axis datasets from KEGG pathways to infer intercellular communication networks and internal regulatory signals by integrating both intracellular and intercellular signals. We used “CellCall” to further elucidate the specific pathway.

Spatial transcriptomic data processing

In this study, we analyzed spatial transcriptomics data generated using the 10× Genomics Visium platform from two public datasets, including the Wang et al. cohort11 and GSE22585712. The spatial transcriptomic sections used from GSE225857 were derived from primary colon cancer tissues and were therefore considered primary colorectal cancer-related spatial transcriptomic evidence. Raw count matrices and corresponding spatial image information were loaded into Seurat objects using the Seurat package. Spot-level quality control was performed before normalization. Spatial spots with fewer than 200 detected genes or fewer than 500 total transcript counts were excluded from downstream analysis. Mitochondrial transcript percentage was calculated for quality assessment but was not used as a hard filtering criterion. After quality control, 1516, 1758, 2054, and 3330 spatial spots were retained for Wang et al. (P1), Wang et al. (P2), GSE225857 (P1), and GSE225857 (P3), respectively. The quality-controlled spatial transcriptomics data were normalized using the SCTransform function in Seurat with default parameters, with the Spatial assay used as input. Principal component analysis was then performed using the RunPCA function based on the SCTransform-normalized data. The first 30 principal components were used for downstream dimensionality reduction and visualization with RunUMAP. These processed spatial transcriptomics objects were subsequently used for cell-type deconvolution, AIScore signature enrichment analysis, spatial co-localization analysis, and spatial signaling inference. These spatial analyses were designed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating analyses. The inferred spatial co-localization and signaling patterns were based on computational deconvolution and spatial inference rather than direct experimental validation, and therefore were interpreted as correlative spatial evidence. Future studies using larger spatial transcriptomic cohorts and formal spatial statistical methods, such as Moran’s I, Ripley’s K, permutation testing, or neighborhood enrichment analysis, will be required to further validate the spatial association between Epi_AIScoreH and Macro_SPP1.

Re‑localization for spatial transcriptome data

We employed the “SpaCET” package44 to identify the cell types present in each spot of the ST dataset. SpaCET estimated the proportion of malignant cells by initially predicting CNV in each spot, leveraging a dictionary of gene-specific features. Additionally, SpaCET utilized a linear regression model to hierarchically resolve the percentage of non-malignant cells in each spot. scRNA-seq datasets could be integrated into SpaCET to obtain reference expression profiles of various cell types necessary for the regression calculations. Tumor, stromal, and border spots were defined based on SpaCET-inferred malignant and stromal cell proportions, spatial location, and consistency with the corresponding H&E morphology. Spots with high inferred malignant cell abundance were annotated as tumor-enriched spots, spots with high inferred non-malignant stromal abundance were annotated as stromal-enriched spots, and spots located at the interface between tumor-enriched and stromal-enriched regions were annotated as border spots. Because matched pathologist-annotated spot-level ground truth was not available, these annotations were interpreted as computationally inferred spatial regions rather than experimentally validated pathological labels.

Spatial signaling analysis

The “COMMOT” package45 was applied to investigate the direction of signaling in the spatial transcriptome data. COMMOT, which stands for COMMunication analysis by Optimal Transport, was developed to infer cell-cell communication by simultaneously considering numerous ligand-receptor pairs. COMMOT allows for the summarization and comparison of the directions of spatial signaling, and it identifies the downstream effects of cell-cell communication on gene expression using an ensemble of tree models. COMMOT-inferred signaling directionality was based on the spatial distribution of ligand and receptor expression and optimal transport-based computational inference. Therefore, the inferred MDK-NCL and SPP1-CD44 signaling patterns were interpreted as computationally predicted spatial communication patterns rather than experimentally validated directional ligand-receptor activity.

In silico pathological region annotation

H&E-stained WSIs from 389 TCGA-COAD patients were subjected to an in silico pathological region annotation framework based on unsupervised deep feature clustering. WSIs were processed at 20× magnification and tessellated into non-overlapping tiles of 224 × 224 pixels, corresponding to approximately 112 × 112 μm² under an assumed resolution of 0.50 μm/pixel. Tiles with insufficient tissue content, excessive background, severe blur, or artifacts were excluded before downstream analysis. For each retained tile, 2048-dimensional deep features were extracted from the global average pooling layer of a ResNet-50 backbone pretrained on ImageNet. The extracted features were then reduced to 32 principal components using PCA, following a feature reduction strategy similar to previously reported histopathology-based deep learning workflows46. K-means clustering was subsequently applied to the PCA-reduced features to partition tiles into morphologically distinct pathological regions. The number of clusters was set to k = 6 based on visual assessment of histopathological interpretability after mapping cluster assignments back to the original WSIs. Cluster distributions were visualized using t-SNE, and tile-level cluster assignments were projected back to their original WSI coordinates to generate electronic annotation maps. Representative tiles from each cluster were selected based on proximity to the corresponding cluster centroid, adequate tissue content, and absence of major artifacts, followed by histopathological review to annotate the dominant morphological patterns.

For AIScore-associated histomorphological visualization, a weakly supervised strategy was used because tile-level transcriptomic labels were not available. Patient-level AIScore groups derived from matched bulk RNA-seq data were assigned to tiles from the corresponding WSI for AIScore-related visualization. Tile-level AIScore-related prediction scores were mapped back to WSI coordinates, and a majority-voting strategy was used to summarize tile-level outputs at the WSI level. Model interpretability was assessed using Grad-CAM. Grad-CAM was used to explain the AIScore-related weakly supervised classification model rather than the unsupervised K-means clustering process. Grad-CAM was performed on representative tiles from correctly classified AIScore-high and AIScore-low cases. Tiles were selected based on adequate histological quality, while tiles dominated by background, necrosis, tissue folds, or out-of-focus regions were excluded. Heatmaps were overlaid on the original H&E tiles to visualize image regions contributing to AIScore-related predictions. The deep learning-based histopathological analyses were performed as exploratory computational analyses. Unsupervised tile clustering and Grad-CAM visualization were used to support morphological interpretation of AIScore-associated regions, but they do not constitute independent pathological or functional validation. Because the AIScore labels were derived from patient-level bulk RNA-seq rather than tile-level ground truth, the Grad-CAM heatmaps should be interpreted as exploratory visualizations of histological regions contributing to AIScore-related predictions, rather than definitive tile-level validation of AIScore biology. Therefore, these results were interpreted together with transcriptomic and spatial findings rather than as stand-alone mechanistic evidence.

Statistics

For the statistical analyses, we utilized R version 4.2.1 and Python version 3.8. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon test or Kruskal-Wallis test was employed. These non-parametric tests are robust against deviations from normality and are suitable for comparing medians across groups. For variables that conformed to a normal distribution, we used two-tailed t-tests or one-way ANOVA to assess the clinical characteristics of subgroups with quantitative differences. To evaluate correlations, we conducted Spearman correlation analysis, which is a non-parametric measure of rank correlation. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the prognostic value of AIScore and clinicopathological variables for overall survival. Variables included in the multivariate Cox models were selected based on data availability in each cohort. In the TCGA-COAD cohort, AIScore, age, gender, pathological stage, and MSI status were included. In the GSE29621 and GSE17536 cohorts, AIScore and available clinicopathological variables, including age, gender, pathological stage, and histological grade, were included where applicable. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model. To further evaluate the prognostic performance of AIScore, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves were generated at 12, 36, and 60 months. Calibration curves were plotted to compare predicted and observed survival probabilities at 12, 36, and 60 months. Decision curve analysis was performed at the same time points to assess the potential clinical net benefit of AIScore across different threshold probabilities. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, including pan-cancer differential expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, gene-level prognostic screening, single-cell interaction comparisons, and spatial correlation analyses, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method where applicable. An adjusted P value or false discovery rate < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for multiple-testing analyses. For pre-specified comparisons, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, Cox regression models, and selected validation analyses, two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.
