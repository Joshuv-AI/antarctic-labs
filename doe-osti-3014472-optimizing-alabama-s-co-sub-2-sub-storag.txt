Executive Summary

Optimizing Alabama’s CO2 Storage in Shelby County (Project OASIS) is a CarbonSAFE Phase II
designed to support the U.S. Department of Energy’s goals of reducing project risks and costs for
future carbon dioxide (CO2) capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) projects, bringing more
storage resources into commercial classifications that support business and financial decisions,
and encouraging more rapid growth of a vibrant, geographically widespread industry for geologic
carbon storage. The goal was to establish the foundation for a commercial-scale geologic storage
complex for CO2 captured from Plant Gaston and surrounding industrial sources of CO2 located
in Shelby County, Alabama (Figure 1). Due to limited regional oil and gas exploration, these plants
have no known economically viable option for geological storage of CO2 in the area. The Project
Team established seven objectives to achieve its goal, including (1) demonstrate that the
subsurface saline formations at the storage complex can store commercial volumes of CO2 safely
and permanently; (2) develop a comprehensive Community and Stakeholder Engagement Plan
that supports local engagement and workforce development; (3) develop an infrastructure
framework for a CO2 storage hub; (4) develop a rigorous risk registry and conduct a
comprehensive risk assessment; (5) develop a monitoring plan; (6) develop a comprehensive site
characterization plan to support an Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Underground
Injection Control Class VI Permit in Phase III; and (7) evaluate project commerciality.

Plant Gaston is a major 1.88 GW (nameplate capacity) natural gas and coal fired power plant and
the site of the DOE’s National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC). If proven, the storage complex
would additionally serve as a central CO2 storage hub for multiple large industrial plants that are
located within the Project area. Project OASIS addressed its objectives by first drilling and
rigorously characterizing a deep stratigraphic test well to reduce the geologic and storage
resource uncertainty associated with the development of a storage complex in this area and
advance the Project’s development following the Society of Petroleum Engineer’s Storage
Resource Management System (SRMS). The target CO2 storage complex is the Cambro-
Ordovician section of the Alabama Valley and Ridge Province. These geological data were
incorporated into static earth models and numerical simulations to establish injectivity and better
understand the areal extent of the CO2 plume and help design the storage site and its monitoring
system. Simulations show limited opportunity, in the absence of development strategies that are
currently atypical for CCUS projects, such as perforating the entire Knox Group. Further, cashflow
models illustrate significant challenges associated with (1) the number of injection wells required
to achieve CarbonSAFE metrics based on existing data (27 injectors to achieve 1.6 million metric
tons of CO2) and (2) the cost to capture CO2 from a natural gas combined cycle facility. Taken
together, the two result in a project that is currently uneconomic. However, there are opportunities
for further exploration that may yield better porosity and permeability in the deep Cambrian Rome
Formation. If proven, this, along with N th-of-a-kind improvement to capture technologies (cost
reductions) would likely dramatically improve the viability of CO2 storage in the region. Importantly,
Project OASIS provided invaluable lessons learned, data, and best practices for project
developers evaluating CO2 storage opportunities in similar terrain while engaging with local
industry and sharing Project information readily with those interested in CCUS opportunity in the
region.
Work on this project was conducted under the purview of the Southern States Energy Board
(SSEB, the Recipient), Advanced Resources International, Inc., (ARI), Alabama A&M University
(AAMU), Auburn University (AU), Crescent Resource Innovation (CRI), Oklahoma State
University (OSU), Southern Company and Alabama Power Company, Westervelt, and Baker
Hughes. This report endeavors to provide a comprehensive overview of Project activities across
all tasks. Where discussed, Project Deliverables and Milestones are only summarized with
appropriate reference and access details included. For more detailed discussions on specific
topics, the reader is encouraged to retrieve individual files referenced throughout this report.

Project Schedule

Project OASIS was formally awarded on October 1, 2023, with an 18-month period of
performance concluding on March 31, 2025. A six-month no-cost time extension was requested
for Project OASIS (DE-FE0032267) on November 4, 2024, extending the total period of
performance to 24 months and shifting the end date from March 31, 2025, to September 30, 2025.
The extension request was approved and a formal modification to the award was received on
December 2, 2024. This extension supported ongoing activities at AU and AAMU, as well as the
broader objectives of the Project.
At AU, the extension facilitated efforts to characterize sidewall core samples through microscopy
(including scanning electron microscopy and X-ray computed tomography) were critical for
improving understanding of the bulk geochemistry, porosity, and permeability of the target
Cambro-Ordovician Knox Group and associated confining intervals (Task 4.0). In addition,
geochemical studies evaluated the impact of CO₂-brine interactions on reservoir porosity and
permeability. These experiments were essential not only for assessing the localized impacts of
CO₂ injection into carbonate reservoirs, but also for informing work in other regions, including the
Valley and Ridge Province of the Appalachian fold-and-thrust belt.
For AAMU, the extension enabled additional student participation in the ongoing internship
program designed to build student capacities in CCS-related topics and subsurface image
processing (Task 2.0).

Task 1.0 – Project Management and Planning

SSEB received the Project OASIS award on October 1, 2023. Pre-award costs dating back to
April 1, 2023, were approved by NETL on August 29, 2023. SSEB personnel coordinated with
Project OASIS sub-recipients and hosted a preliminary project kickoff meeting on September 28,
2023. Given the tight scheduling associated with drilling operations, SSEB oversaw pre-award
activities related to site access, permitting, materials procurement, and scheduling for the drilling
program. This work required frequent coordination with ARI and the site host, Westervelt.
Following the formal award, SSEB continued oversight of field activities, including invoice review
and schedule tracking. On October 19, 2023, SSEB staff visited the Project OASIS site to discuss
the drilling program with ARI and the on-site company representative, Doug Kilgore.
In addition to field activities, SSEB coordinated an open house with the National Carbon Capture
Center (NCCC) and Project OASIS partners on November 3, 2023. The event was attended by
approximately 30 individuals and included an overview of carbon, capture, utilization and storage
(CCUS), a tour of the NCCC, and a site visit to the OASIS drilling location. SSEB also held
recurring discussions with the Project OASIS Federal Project Manager (FPM), Johnathan Moore,
on October 19, 2023; November 2, 2023; November 16, 2023; November 30, 2023; and
December 14, 2023. These discussions focused on field program status and contractual

meeting on December 21, 2023.

Task 2.0 – Community Benefits Plan

SSEB led the coordination and oversight of the Project OASIS Community Benefits Plan (CBP)
from its inception, emphasizing inclusive stakeholder engagement, workforce development, and
community-focused carbon management awareness across Alabama and the southeastern
region. SSEB initiated CBP activities immediately following project award, culminating in
participation in the DOE CBP Assistance and Validation (A&V) meeting on December 21, 2023.
This meeting established the foundation for Project OASIS’s community engagement strategy,
defining milestones for transparency, workforce opportunity, and educational outreach.

OASIS Final Report                                                      DE-FE00332267 | Page 11

To foster local engagement early in the Project, SSEB developed and distributed a drilling
program informational flyer and co-hosted the November 3, 2023, open house NCCC and ARI.
The event introduced community members, regulators, and industry to CCUS concepts, drilling
operations, and the Project’s potential benefits to the state’s economy and environment.
During Q1 2024, SSEB advanced partnerships with educational institutions to strengthen
workforce and community relationships. Key meetings were held with Bishop State Community
College and Tuskegee University, establishing pathways for student participation through
internships and technical collaborations. SSEB also met with Cemex’s Hydrogen and CCUS
Director to align regional decarbonization objectives with the Project OASIS goals.
Through spring and summer 2024, SSEB expanded its outreach by meeting with the American
Petroleum Institute and distributing a Carbon Management Program newsletter to over 4,000
recipients, including legislators and industry leaders. SSEB staff also represented OASIS during
the SSEB 64th Annual Meeting in Chattanooga (September 2024), where they briefed Governor
Ivey’s (Alabama) Deputy Chief of Staff on project progress.
SSEB further pursued partnerships with the City of Birmingham’s EPA Climate Pollution
Reduction Grant program, seeking opportunities to integrate CCS topics into public meetings and
community dialogues. Dr. Ben Wernette represented SSEB at the July 29, 2024, CBP Interim
A&V meeting, presenting updates on ongoing community engagement and workforce
development activities.
Under Subtask 2.2 (Investing in Job Quality and Workforce), SSEB provided 11 high-powered
computers and 22 monitors to AAMU and facilitated installation of Baker Hughes’ JewelSuite
subsurface modeling software, creating a dedicated CCS learning laboratory. AAMU played a
critical role in the Project OASIS Workforce Development scope. In spring 2024, AAMU launched
a special topics course (EE490: Seismic Modeling and Migration) for six students, four of whom
were paid OASIS interns. The course covered seismic data acquisition, processing, and
interpretation in CCUS applications. Students engaged with OASIS project data and, in April
2024, participated in the NCCC field event, presenting their findings and networking with carbon
management professionals. Through the summer and fall of 2024, AAMU expanded the OASIS
internship program to additional students, integrating research on CO₂ interactions with rock
properties, seismic modeling, and reservoir characterization. The program emphasized practical
training, academic advancement, and workforce readiness. Plans for additional site visits to Plant
Gaston and the NCCC were established to further reinforce hands-on learning. Indeed, SSEB
collaborated with Southern Company, AAMU, and ARI to organize a student engagement visit to
the NCCC and Plant Gaston on April 19, 2024.

Drilling Campaign. The purpose of the Westover Stratigraphic Test #2 was to gather geologic

allow for a second casing string if wellbore stability was compromised at a greater depth. Finally,
directional tools were utilized from the surface, to maintain a vertical wellbore and reduce the risk
of sticking drill pipe, logging tools, or coring tools due to sloughing of weak shale lithologies or
steeply dipping and fractured units into the hole.

Regional Data Incorporation. A regional geologic and geophysical evaluation of the OASIS site
was developed using an expanded seismic and well data set. ARI acquired an additional 50 line-
miles of vintage 2D seismic data in raw field SEG-Y format from Seismic Exchange (SEI), along
with the associated field records. These data were reprocessed into post-stack time migration
(PSTM) panels in collaboration with Carbon Seismic, LLC and integrated with the 2D seismic lines
collected in 2022 during the execution of Project OASIS. Interpretation of the combined dataset
allowed ARI personnel to map key formation boundaries and structural elements within the OASIS
thrust-ramp system, ultimately producing an approximately 180-square-mile static earth model.
These results were subsequently incorporated into updates of the regional reservoir simulation
model.
As part of the broader regional assessment, a detailed evaluation was conducted on all three
wells in the Westover area (Westover #1 and #2 wells and the Arco/Anschutz #1). Drilling results
confirmed that Westover #2 penetrated the upper thrust sheet as expected and encountered the

OASIS Final Report                                                       DE-FE00332267 | Page 18

upper thrust fault beneath the ramp anticline at a depth of roughly 4,000 ft. Below this fault, the
well intersected a significantly thicker-than-expected section of Copper Ridge Dolomite, an
interval not predicted by the initial seismic interpretation.
This result indicates that the Copper Ridge has been structurally duplicated, in places up to triple
its original thickness, within a duplex formed adjacent to an oblique thrust ramp. This duplex
occupies structural space initially interpreted as the Newala Limestone. Revised seismic analysis
indicates that the upper portion of the Newala was translated across the oblique ramp and
preserved beneath the frontal edge of the shallow ramp anticline. The base of the Copper Ridge
marks a major thrust fault, below which the well transitions back into stratigraphy that aligns with
the original seismic model.
Formation Micro-Imager (FMI) logs reveal abundant large-aperture fractures within the Copper
Ridge duplex, indicating potential for CO₂ storage in the fracture system. Routine core analysis
data of sidewall core show that the carbonate matrix is tight, with peak porosity of 2.5% and peak
permeability of 1.5 mD; thus, the primary storage potential is fracture-dominated, with some
additional storage resource accessible through long-term diffusion of CO₂ into the more porous
carbonate intervals.
Regionally, the duplex appears to be well sealed by approximately 2,000 ft of tight Cambrian–
Mississippian carbonate within the overlying ramp anticline and an additional 1,500 ft of Floyd-
Parkwood Shale near the surface. Structural relationships suggest the fractured duplex covers an
area of roughly 6 mi² and has an estimated thickness of about 2,900 ft. These observations
informed the development of the static earth model and reservoir simulations detailed in Task 4.0
below.

Task 4.0 – Geologic Data Analysis

Core Analysis and Sample Evaluation. ARI initiated the core analysis phase by shipping 17.7
feet of whole core from the Westover #2 Stratigraphic well to the Geological Survey of Alabama
(GSA) in Tuscaloosa for storage and preparation. In December 2023, GSA slabbed the core for
detailed study. AU students travelled to Tuscaloosa to describe the whole core and collect
samples for thin-section analysis. Concurrently, 30 of the 39 rotary sidewall core samples were
sent to Core Labs in Houston for routine and special core analysis. Core Labs completed high
resolution photography and assessed porosity and permeability and generated a report that was
later forwarded to Auburn University for further evaluation.
During the first quarter of 2024, Core Labs finalized routine core analysis and plain light/UV
photography of the 30 intact sidewall samples. ARI incorporated these results into updates of the
geologic and reservoir models for the Cambro-Ordovician section to refine characterization of the
storage reservoirs and sealing intervals. AU received the whole core, thin sections, and other
geologic materials were transferred to AU to support continued evaluation of rock properties.
By mid-2024, ARI coordinated with AU and GSA to identify samples suitable for tri-axial testing
at NETL. This testing was critical for understanding fracture pressure in local reservoirs and
confining systems, improving assumptions for dynamic reservoir models.
Petrographic and Mineralogical Studies. AU advanced the analysis by preparing thin sections
from 29 sidewall cores and four additional cores from the whole core samples of Westover #2.
These thin sections confirmed limited porosity and permeability, consistent with the results from
open-hole wireline testing. Although many samples exhibited significant fracturing, most fractures

activity (see Figure 10). One sample displayed noteworthy vuggy porosity.
Additional thin sections were prepared from cuttings obtained from the adjacent Arco/Anschutz

showed similar characteristics, such as low porosity and permeability, with fewer fractures overall.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis characterized the Copper Ridge Dolomite samples from depths
of 3,000 to 4,000 feet, at 100-foot intervals. Software for mineralogical calculations was acquired
to determine bulk mineralogy from this data. Because the Ketona Dolomite was not penetrated
during drilling Westover #2, samples were sourced from Arco/Anschutz #1 and local outcrops. AU

Westover #2. Petrographic microscopy of cuttings and outcrop samples revealed largely
homogeneous, coarsely crystalline dolomite that had undergone complete replacement. The rock
is highly fractured and crushed, with some intercrystalline and vuggy porosity and calcite
precipitation along fractures. XRD results showed minimal compositional variation by depth or
location, dominated by dolomite with minor calcite and trace quartz.
Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis was conducted on powdered and polished lithic

samples to liquid CO₂ to assess dissolution, precipitation, mineral formation, and porosity
changes as part of a master’s thesis at AU. By mid-2025, SEM post-treatment scans were
completed, but results were ambiguous.

Task 6.0 – CarbonSAFE Phase III Readiness

Overview. The evaluation of CarbonSAFE Phase III Readiness relied on work completed in the
prior tasks and focused on developing the scientific, engineering, and permitting foundation
necessary for a future EPA UIC Class VI carbon storage site, including subsurface
characterization, injection well design, and early monitoring, verification, and accounting (MVA)
planning.
Early work focused on leveraging the comprehensive data collected from the Project OASIS
stratigraphic test well and supporting seismic studies. The team used the data sets to expand the
understanding of the storage potential beyond the immediate OASIS site. Specifically, the team
acquired and analyzed geophysical and petrophysical logs from the William Smith 9-21 well,
located approximately 20 miles southwest of Alabama Power’s Plant Gaston. These data were
compared with logs from the Arco/Anschutz well, revealing limited reservoir storage potential in
that southwestern region of the study area. This finding was significant in refining the geographic
boundaries of viable CO₂ storage formations in and around the OASIS site. While detailed in the
Task 6.1 Evaluation of EPA UIC Class VI Readiness Milestone and the Task 6.0 CarbonSAFE
Phase III Readiness Deliverable, several key observations are included below.
USDWs. No underground sources of drinking water (USDW) or shallow water zones were
identified during the drilling of Westover #1 or Westover #2. As a result, no water samples were
collected. It is possible that regional shallow water wells could be monitored as an alternative.
Confining Zone. As described elsewhere, the Project OASIS site enjoys a robust confining
interval above the target storage interval of interest. The Floyd-Parkwood Shale is thick and
laterally extensive, serving as an appropriate sealing horizon. Further, the upper Knox Group
consists mainly of impermeable dolomite, serving as a secondary confining system.
Characterized Injection Zone (the Knox Group). Project OASIS evaluated the suitability of the
Knox Group as a saline storage reservoir. Locally, the Knox Group is approximately 3,700 ft thick,
with porosity range from 2 to 5% and permeability ranging from 0.2 to 2 mD. Discrete fracture
networks offer potential opportunities for storage volume, though their geometry in space is
difficult to discern based on current data. Dynamic simulations of the Knox Group indicate that
injectivities of 0.06 MTPY CO2 per well are possible in a scenario wherein the entire 3,700 ft
interval is perforated. A scenario targeting only the fractured zones within the Knox Group reveal
much lower injectivities, on the order of 0.02 MTPY CO2 per year per well.
CO2 plume and pressure modeling ( 50 million metric tons over 30 years). The dynamic
simulation base case, utilizing the stratigraphy and reservoir properties described in preceding
sections and targeting only the fractured sections of the Knox Group stabilizes at approximately
0.02 MTPY CO2 per year per well. The scenario that explores the entire Knox Group stabilizes at
approximately 0.06 MTPY CO2 per year per well (Figure 15).

The simulated plume radius is approximately 3,250 ft while the pressure plume for the two
scenarios ranges from approximately 0.5- to 1-mile in radius, or slightly larger in area than the
CO2 plume. The limited spatial extent of the pressure plume is a result of the tight nature of these
lithologies. An important observation is the fact that perforating the entire Knox Group, which
could be accomplished using a screen completion, provides maximum access to the tight
formation, and increases injectivity by as much as 3 times when compared to the scenario that
explores just the fractured section of the Knox Group.
Seismic risk and modeling. Much of the seismic activity in Alabama is associated with the
Southern Appalachian Seismic Zone (also called the East Tennessee Seismic Zone). This zone
is moderate in risk. Based on the location of Project OASIS (approximately 30 miles east-
southeast of Birmingham, Alabama), the complex geologic setting, regional seismic zones, and
historical earthquake records, seismic risk is likely moderate.
Wellbore penetrations. The study area has only one existing wellbore penetration of interest
within 10 miles of the study area. Indeed, existing deep wellbore penetrations are limited to the
ARCO Oil and Gas Co. well (Arco/Anschutz #1; -86.52885, 33.28967). The well was drilled to
17,005 ft and logged to 16,890 ft below surface.
Theoretical injection zone (the Cambrian Rome Formation). While not sampled directly as
part of Project OASIS, theoretical injectivity estimates for the deep Rome Formation (14,000 ft
below surface) were developed based on data from the Arco/Anschutz #1 well logs, which indicate
porosity on the order of 2 to 10% and permeability ranging from 1 to 10 mD.
Injection well design, and initial monitoring, verification, and accounting plan. Engineering
design work began with the use of PipeSim and Tubular Design Analysis Software (TDAS) to
model multi-phase CO₂ flow and establish appropriate tubular specifications for both injection and
monitoring wells. Early simulations evaluated maximum injection rates and identified conditions
that could lead to undesirable wellbore or formation responses. These assessments provided key

inputs for ensuring regulatory compliance and maintaining well integrity under anticipated EPA
UIC Class VI operating scenarios.
By spring 2025, optimized designs were completed for the Deep Rome and Knox Formation
injection wells. The Deep Rome design was refined to accommodate higher CO₂ injection
volumes through increased tubing and casing dimensions, while the Knox design was intentionally
reduced in scale to align with lower flow requirements and minimize construction costs, resulting
in a technically robust and economically efficient system. TDAS was used to verify that the
updated tubular configurations deliver adequate strength and mechanical stability across all
project phases, from injection through eventual well abandonment.
In parallel with the engineering efforts, an initial internal review of the preliminary MVA plan was
conducted to identify monitoring challenges associated with the OASIS site’s complex geology.
The review evaluated how seismic, pressure, and geochemical monitoring techniques could be
integrated within both the Deep Rome and Knox reservoirs. Work also began on defining the
number and placement of injection and monitoring wells based on evolving geologic and reservoir
models, ensuring that the overall project design supported long-term monitoring objectives and

Task 8.0 – Commercialization Plan

Technical and Economic Evaluation. The Project OASIS team led a comprehensive evaluation
of potential development pathways for the commercialization plan, focusing on the engineering
and economic feasibility of future CCUS operations at and surrounding the OASIS site. In early
2025, the team developed a detailed series of AFEs to quantify expected costs associated with
well construction, monitoring, and operations under multiple development scenarios. AFEs were
prepared for injection wells, in-zone monitoring wells, and above-zone monitoring wells to assess
two geological and operational models, targeting CO₂ injection in the Knox Formation and the
Rome Formation.
These analyses represented a critical step in advancing the Project from research toward
commercialization readiness, providing granular insight into capital and operational costs.
Considerations included well design, drilling depth, tubular requirements, and monitoring
configurations, enabling a direct link between engineering decisions and project economics. This
work established a framework for comparing alternative storage configurations and understanding
the financial implications of injectivity, storativity, and overall reservoir performance.

By spring 2025, the AFEs were refined to incorporate updated reservoir and geological modeling
results from previous OASIS tasks, ensuring technical and economic consistency across the

Throughout this effort, the Project OASIS team collaborated internally to interpret modeling results

Commercialization Strategy and Integration. The Project OASIS team also led the
development of the commercialization strategy, integrating technical findings with broader
economic, logistical, and policy considerations. Planning for this work began in early 2024 and
progressed through detailed scenario development in 2025.
An initial outline for the Task 8.0 Commercialization Plan was developed to structure the
deliverable around field program insights, updated geologic models, and evolving project
economics. Recognizing that injectivity and storativity are central to feasibility, the team closely
coordinated with the subsurface modeling group to ensure integration of technical parameters
from Task 4 (Geologic Modeling and Characterization) into commercial planning.
Throughout 2024, conceptual models were iteratively refined to evaluate the commercial
implications of emerging technical findings. Analysis indicated that limited storage capacity at the
OASIS site could constrain economic potential, shaping development pathways and partnership
considerations. This insight emphasized the importance of scalable storage access and multi-
partner collaboration in future CCUS deployment within the Valley and Ridge province.
Under Subtask 8.2, the team conducted a conceptual-level design study for CO₂ transportation.
This included mapping power transmission line rights-of-way near the OASIS site to identify
potential pipeline corridors connecting regional CO₂ emitters to suitable storage zones. Co-
locating new infrastructure with existing utility corridors minimized potential environmental and
social impacts, providing a practical foundation for permitting and community engagement. By
2025, the Project OASIS team had integrated cost data, modeling results, and infrastructure
assessments into a working commercialization framework. This framework outlined multiple
commercial and technical scenarios, including capital investment requirements, potential
partners, policy incentives, and market conditions for CCUS deployment. These scenarios form
the basis of the final Task 8.0 Commercialization Plan, presenting a suite of practical options for
scaling carbon storage projects in Alabama and the broader Southeast.

Impact
The OASIS project advanced applied understanding of subsurface CO₂ storage and risk
assessment in the southeastern United States, particularly in geologically complex regions.
Through site characterization, modeling, and validation of storage formations, the project
generated new data, methodologies, and analytical tools that reduce uncertainty and improve
confidence in site selection, system design, and long-term monitoring for commercial-scale
carbon storage. Its integrated approach supported innovation across geology, engineering, data
analytics, and economics, producing insights relevant to industrial emissions management,
energy infrastructure development, and regional resource planning while complementing DOE’s
Fossil Energy and Carbon Management portfolio.
Beyond technical advances, OASIS strengthened workforce capacity and regional infrastructure
by engaging engineers, scientists, and students in hands-on research and collaboration with
universities, state agencies, and industry partners. The project produced durable institutional and
information resources, including validated geologic models and regional datasets, and actively
transferred modeling tools, cost analyses, and risk management frameworks to public- and
private-sector stakeholders to support accelerated deployment. Collectively, these outcomes
enable private-sector investment, job creation, and improved U.S. energy reliability and
competitiveness. All project funds were expended domestically, ensuring federal investments
supported U.S.-based institutions, jobs, and supply chains.

Conclusions
Currently, large-scale commercial CCUS development in the Valley and Ridge region of Alabama
has limited viability. Project OASIS was able to provide invaluable lessons learned, data, and best
practices for project developers evaluating CO2 storage opportunities in similar geology. This
project highlighted several key challenges: difficult drilling conditions, constrained storage
capacity, and a large AOR that necessitates numerous wells to accommodate

OASIS Final Report                                                       DE-FE00332267 | Page 36

economically/practically viable volumes. These geologic limitations make development of a
regional storage facility particularly challenging.
While the regional geology proved to be difficult, the OASIS team was able to identify opportunities
to overcome some of the challenges to commercial scale development. Drilling challenges were
greatly reduced by applying lessons learned from the Westover #1 Stratigraphic well and utilizing
newly acquired 2D seismic data to identify an optimal location to drill Westover #2 Stratigraphic
well. While a large-scale CarbonSAFE project would be difficult to execute in the Knox Formation,
regional data suggests that the Rome Formation may be characterized by better porosity and
permeability which could potentially improve commercial-scale economics. As such, further
evaluation of the Rome Formation may prove beneficial for emitters in this area of Alabama.
Smaller emitters may have success in the Knox Formation as the number of wells and the
associated well development cost is significantly lower than those of a CarbonSAFE-scale (~ 1.6
MTPY CO2) project. Additional advancements in capture technology and cost reductions
associated with Nth-of-a-kind capture systems may provide necessary cost savings to allow future
opportunities for local storage in the Valley and Ridge Province of Alabama. Despite the
numerous development challenges inherent to the Valley and Ridge Province, Project OASIS
identified viable pathways for safe and secure CO₂ storage in the region. The Project also
established best practices for siting and developing wells in similar geological settings, providing
valuable guidance that will support future development both within the region and in areas with
comparable geological characteristics.
