The Local Effects of Systemic Therapies in Urological Oncology: A Systematic Review
Authors: Sharon Nordheimer, Ofer N Gofrit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSystemic therapies can impact local disease progression in genitourinary malignancies, potentially reducing the necessity for extensive surgery. Understanding these effects is crucial for optimizing treatment approaches in urologic oncology.MethodsThrough PubMed and Scopus databases search up to May 2026, we identified studies focusing on surgical outcomes after systemic therapy in prostate, kidney, testicular, and bladder cancers.ResultsIn prostate cancer (PC), neoadjuvant hormonal therapy improved margin status and organ confinement but did not enhance biochemical‐free survival rates. Chemotherapy or immunotherapy provided no clinical benefit. In kidney cancer, tyrosine kinase inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors reduced tumor diameter by 13.5%–35% but did not improve recurrence‐free survival. Certain therapeutic combinations decreased vena cava thrombus length, lessening surgical complexity. Post‐chemotherapy, testicular cancer specimens revealed viable tumors in 8.3%–25% of cases, teratomas in 18.2%–50.0%, and scar or in situ neoplasms in 31.1%–60.0% of specimens. In muscle‐invasive bladder cancer, cisplatin‐based chemotherapy resulted in a pathological complete response (pCR) in approximately one‐third of patients, and the addition of immunotherapy or antibody‐drug conjugates further improved pCR rates. Molecular markers such as circulating tumor DNA and urine tumor DNA are being investigated as potential markers for predicting pCRs.ConclusionsCurrent systemic therapies do not eliminate the need for radical orchiectomy, nephrectomy, or local treatment in PC. The ability to avoid radical surgery in bladder cancer is still investigational. Therefore, attempts to avoid local treatment in patients with genitourinary malignancies should be pursued within the framework of a clinical trial.Keywords: biomarkers, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, neoadjuvant, response, surgery, uro‐oncology

Introduction

Local treatments in genitourinary malignancies often include complex and mutilative surgeries in patients with multiple co‐morbidities, and are associated with a decrease in quality of life and often with post‐treatment complications. If systemic treatment could decrease the extent of local treatment or even totally eliminate it, this would be highly beneficial. This concept justifies review of the local effects of systemic therapies. Most information comes from studies on neoadjuvant treatments administered prior to extirpative surgery in patients with muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) and high‐burden testicular cancer. Efforts have also been made to improve the prognosis of patients with localized kidney and prostate malignancies. The primary goal of these treatments is the elimination of micrometastatic disease, but a local effect is also expected. This review addresses the following questions:

Materials and Methods

A review of preoperative systemic treatments for malignancies of the kidneys, bladder, prostate, and testes was conducted through PubMed and Scopus databases, covering studies published until May 2026. Search terms included “kidney cancer,” “bladder cancer,” “testis cancer,” “prostate cancer (PC),” “neoadjuvant,” and “local effect,” in different combinations. The combination yielded 860 articles in kidney cancer, 2404 in prostate cancer, 2722 in bladder cancer, and 59 in testicular cancer. All prospective studies were included. Phase I, Phase II, and retrospective studies were considered if they provided results that could potentially influence future patient management. Negative studies were also included if they offered insights into the pathogenesis of the malignancy or the mechanism of neoadjuvant tumor response. Studies focusing solely on patients with metastatic disease or on tolerability and adverse events (AEs) were excluded. The initial search identified 6045 potentially relevant manuscripts and abstract presentations, of which 72 studies were included in this review.

Studies With Antiandrogens

The concept of inhibiting the androgenic axis before radical prostatectomy (RP) is supported by studies showing superior outcomes with radiotherapy following hormonal therapy, including improved PSA progression‐free survival, local and distant progression‐free survival, cancer‐specific survival, and overall survival (OS) rates [14]. Based on these findings, trials utilizing neoadjuvant hormonal therapies before RP were conducted. To reduce the need for long follow‐up periods to evaluate survival outcomes, surrogate markers such as margin status and organ confinement were used (Table 1). Several studies found a statistically significant reduction in both positive surgical margins and extracapsular invasion rates [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. However, these surrogates did not correlate with clinically meaningful outcomes such as biochemical‐free survival. The rarity of pathological complete response (pCR) after antiandrogen treatment further suggests the limited benefits of this approach. For instance, Schulman et al. randomized 402 patients to undergo either upfront RP or receive 3 months of treatment with luteinizing hormone‐releasing hormone (LHRH) agonist plus flutamide, followed by RP. Although the negative margin rate was significantly higher in the treatment group (47.5% vs. 26.2%, p = 0.01), there was no difference in the biochemical‐free recurrence rate at 4 years (32.5% vs. 26.4%, p = 0.19) [3]. As expected, neoadjuvant hormonal therapy was associated with significant side effects, including hot flashes, diarrhea, and loss of libido and erectile function. Aside from these adverse effects, this approach seems to primarily obscure tumor margins from pathologists. Nevertheless, recruiting low‐risk patients could have diluted any potential beneficial effect of this treatment.

Modern hormonal treatments, including LHRH antagonist (degarelix), signal transduction blockers (enzalutamide), and cyp17 inhibitors (abiraterone), have introduced a new era in neoadjuvant treatments. Modern trials have focused on intermediate and high‐risk patients. These treatments are generally well tolerated, with rare Grade 4–5 adverse events (AEs); however, the absence of control groups with upfront surgery complicates the evaluation of their true contribution. Evidence suggests that enzalutamide and abiraterone, either alone or in combination with leuprolide, offer no significant benefit in margin status, organ confinement, or pCR rate. A small study evaluating 3 months of treatment with degarelix, alone or combined with bicalutamide, showed no improvement in pathological outcomes compared to the LHRH agonist plus bicalutamide [7].

Studies With Chemotherapy Alone or in Combination With Antiandrogen

The improved OS rates in patients with metastatic hormone‐refractory PC treated with docetaxel—a taxane that disrupts microtubules—led to its testing in neoadjuvant trials. However, pCR was not achieved in any participants across several small Phase II trials [15, 16, 17]. Additionally, combinations of docetaxel with bevacizumab, estramustine, or etoposide also failed to demonstrate significant improvements [18, 19]. Combining hormonal blockade with docetaxel resulted in significantly better organ‐confined and negative surgical margins rates compared to upfront surgery but did not improve 3‐year biochemical‐free survival rates (89%, vs. 84%, p = 0.11) [8]. Paclitaxel, carboplatin, or estramustin did not improve biochemical‐free recurrence, disease‐specific survival, or OS rates compared to neoadjuvant androgen deprivation [20]. Similarly, cabazitaxel plus abiraterone and leuprolide did not improve the pCR or biochemical‐free relapse compared to abiraterone plus leuprolide alone (12‐month biochemical‐free survival of 78% vs. 76%, p = 0.9) [9]. Finally, among 72 high‐risk patients with localized PC treated with buserelin, nilutamide, and docetaxel for 6 months, only two patients (3%) achieved a pCR [21].

Studies With Immunotherapeutic Agents

Modern immunotherapeutic agents have demonstrated significant success in advanced metastatic disease, with several trials exploring their use preoperatively in surgically resectable cases. Potential advantages of neoadjuvant immunotherapy include tumor shrinkage, improved tolerability compared to the adjuvant setting, early eradication of micro‐metastatic disease, and an enhanced antigen‐specific response. PC is considered an immunogenically “cold” tumor; yet several immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) were investigated at the neoadjuvant setting. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD20 antigen (highly expressed on B‐cells found in PC), was administered to eight high‐risk patients with PC before surgery. While pathological evaluation revealed a significant decrease in CD20 density in the tumor region, no notable effect on the tumor itself was observed [22]. Ipilimumab, a monoclonal antibody that blocks CTLA‐4, demonstrates modest activity in castrate‐resistant PC. Its local effect, combined with androgen deprivation, was tested in 17 patients prior to RP. Ipilimumab‐treated patients exhibited a significantly higher frequency of CD4, CD8, and ICOS+ T cells in their prostates. However, there were no cases of pCR, and eight patients developed Grade 3/4 toxicity [23]. More recently, Ager et al. randomized 24 patients to receive either neoadjuvant antiandrogen treatment alone or in combination with BMS‐986218, a next‐generation non‐fucosylated anti‐CTLA‐4 antibody. Their results demonstrated that anti‐CTLA‐4 antibody reduced Treg accumulation; however, the clinical significance of this finding remains unexplored [24]. The goals of systemic therapies in locally advanced kidney cancer include shrinking and downstaging the tumor, enabling conversion from radical to partial nephrectomy, reducing tumor thrombus extension, and potentially rendering previously unresectable tumors operable. Kidney cancer has traditionally been resistant to systemic therapies, and neoadjuvant treatments were not pursued until the introduction of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and, more recently, of ICIs. When used in the metastatic setting, these agents demonstrated measurable effects on the primary tumor. For instance, pazopanib led to a 14.4% median reduction in tumor size [39]. The primary tumor may serve as a source of antigens, enhancing the systemic immune response. Table 2 summarizes trials of TKIs, ICIs, or their combinations administered preoperatively. Most of the available information was obtained from small Phase I or II studies involving patients with nonmetastatic, high‐risk clear cell carcinomas.

Effects on the Major Mass

The following TKIs were administered to patients before nephrectomy: sunitinib, sorafenib, cabozantinib, axitinib, sitravatinib, and pazopanib. These agents resulted in a 13.5%–35% reduction in tumor diameter [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. Most studies reported good tolerability, with no Grade 4–5 AEs. For example, in a Phase II trial, 21 patients were treated with the TKI pazopanib for 8 weeks, with partial response in 8 patients (38%), and stable disease in the others. No Grade 4 or 5 treatment‐related AEs were noted [40]. However, concerns were raised regarding a potential delay in surgical wound healing due to the significant anti‐angiogenic activity of these agents. As a result, most studies required a minimum 4‐week presurgical window between the last TKI dose and surgery. Theoretically, sorafenib and axitinib are more suitable than sunitinib due to their shorter half‐lives. Most trials, however, did not report post‐surgery complications attributable to TKIs.

Studies examining the effects of ICIs on the major mass included nivolumab (PD‐1 inhibitor), a combination of toripalimab (humanized anti‐PD‐1 IgG4 antibody) and axitinib (inhibitor of VEGF1‐3), and a combination of avelumab (PD‐L1 inhibitor) and axitinib. These studies reported a tumor diameter reduction of 26.7%–30% with no Grade 4–5 AEs [28, 33]. In a Phase III trial (PROSPER EA8143), Allaf et al. randomized 819 patients with locally advanced RCC who received either preoperative nivolumab or upfront surgery. After a median follow‐up of 30 months, the recurrence‐free survival in both groups was 33%; consequently, the trial was stopped due to its futility [34]. Effects on Renal Vein/Vena Cava Thrombus

The presence of thrombus in the renal vein (RV) or inferior vena cava (IVC) increases surgical complexity, depending on the IVC level, and worsens prognosis. Preoperative thrombus reduction with systemic treatment is desirable, and several case reports indicate a favorable response to TKIs. In the NAXIVA trial, a Phase II a single‐arm study, 8‐week treatment with the TKI axitinib was given to 20 patients with tumor thrombus. Mayo level reduction was observed in seven (35%), and thrombus length reduction in 15 (75%), leading to reduction of surgery invasiveness in 7/17 operated patients (41/2%). No Grade 4 or 5 AEs were observed [41]. Sunitinib did not reliably reduce IVC thrombus height, with 44% of patients showing a decrease and 66% showing either an increase or stability in thrombus size [36].

ICIs yielded no better outcomes. Khene et al. reported on 39 patients who received ICIs prior to nephrectomy and IVC thrombectomy, compared with 62 who underwent surgery without prior systemic therapy. After a median follow‐up of 19 months, no differences in disease‐free or OS rates were observed between the groups. Unfortunately, the authors did not report on changes in thrombus length or level [42]. The combination of tisleizumab (PD‐1/PD‐L1 inhibitor) and axitinib, administered to seven patients with IVC thrombus, resulted in a reduction in thrombus length in four patients (mean reduction of 5.8 mm, range of 1.8–7.2 mm) [43]. Torpalimab, a humanized anti‐PD‐1 IgG4 antibody, combined with axitinib, reduced mean thrombus length by a median of 2.3 cm, lowered thrombus level in 44% of patients, and modified the surgical strategy in 61.9% of patients. Common Grade 3 AEs included hypertension, dysphonia, stomatitis, fatigue, and proteinuria; no Grade 4–5 AEs were reported [37]. Bigot et al. retrospectively studied the effects of preoperative sunitinib or sorafenib on IVC thrombus height and showed a decrease in 43% of the patients, stable or increase in height in 57% [38]. Ng et al. went further and retrospectively compared the results of neoadjuvant TKI (mainly axitinib) or combination of TKI and PD‐1 inhibitor (mainly toripalimab, tisleizumab, or sintilimab) for 3–5 months before surgery in 185 patients (132 of them had tumor thrombus). The combination showed significantly better objective response rate over TKI alone (30.9% vs. 16.3%, p = 0.036), shortening of the thrombus (18.2% of the patients vs. 5.7%, p = 0.001), pCR (14.8% vs. 0%) and lower estimated blood loss (200 mL vs. 300 mL, p = 0.004) with no significantly higher incidence of adverse events or treatment discontinuations [44].

Non‐seminomatous germ cell tumors only.

Radical orchiectomy is the standard initial intervention for most patients suspected of having testicular germ cell tumors (TGCTs), as it provides local tumor control and definitive histopathological diagnosis. However, in patients with a testicular mass and widespread metastatic disease, chemotherapy is often initiated prior to orchiectomy. Cancer cells are thought to be protected from chemotherapy by the blood‐testis barrier (BTB), formed by tight junctions between Sertoli cells. Despite this, orchiectomy is still recommended after systemic chemotherapy [53, 54]. Clinical observations have raised questions regarding the integrity of the BTB. For example, patients who received cisplatin‐based chemotherapy for non‐seminomatous germ cell tumors (NSGCTs) showed a threefold reduction in the incidence of TGCTs in the contralateral testis compared to those who did not receive chemotherapy [55]. This suggests that cisplatin‐based chemotherapy may be effective in eradicating early testicular malignancies. A study on testis preservation after chemotherapy in 78 patients found that initial conservation was successful in 28 (36%) cases, and an additional 25 (32%) had no viable cancer on subsequent orchiectomy. However, the late relapse rate among patients who retained their testis was 26%, with all relapses successfully managed with delayed orchiectomy [56].

Retrospective studies of post‐chemotherapy orchiectomy offer insights into the local effects of systemic therapy on the primary tumor. Table 3 shows that the reported rates of residual viable germ cell tumors, teratomas, and necrotic or scar tissues vary widely across studies (49–56). Viable tumors were detected in 8.3%–25% of cases, teratomas in 18.2%–50.0%, and necrotic tissue, scar, or in situ neoplasms in 31.1%–60.0% of the resected testes after systemic chemotherapy.

Urothelial carcinoma is chemo responsive. Since Grossman et al.'s landmark study, the standard treatment for muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) has been neoadjuvant chemotherapy with three cycles of methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (M‐VAC), followed by a radical cystectomy (RC). This regimen showed an OS benefit and pCR rate of 38% compared to 15% in the surgery‐only group, indicating a significant local effect of the systemic therapy [57]. Subsequent trials have aimed to optimize neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens. The VESPER trial compared dose‐dense methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (dd‐MVAC) with the more commonly used gemcitabine‐cisplatin (GC) combination. The study reported a pCR rate of 42% with dd‐MVAC and 36% with GC (p = 0.2) [58].

Given the demonstrated efficacy of ICIs in metastatic urothelial carcinoma, their use in perioperative settings was evaluated. NIAGARA randomized patients with MIBC to receive either GC or GC plus durvalumab (PD‐L1 inhibitor) preoperatively, followed by completion of 1 year of durvalumab therapy. The addition of durvalumab significantly improved the 24 months event‐free survival (from 59.8% to 67.8%, p < 0.001), the 24 months overall survival (from 75.2% to 82.2%, p = 0.01) and resulted in a pCR of 33.8% compared to 25.8% in the control group (p = 0.04) [59]. In the HCRN GU 16–257 trial, cisplatin‐eligible patients with cT2–T4aN0M0 received four cycles of GC and nivolumab, followed by clinical restaging. Patients achieving clinical complete response (cCR) were eligible for bladder preservation with continued nivolumab for 4 months, followed by active surveillance (AS). Among 72 eligible patients, 33 (43%) achieved a cCR. After 2 years, 97% of these patients remained metastasis‐free, and 72.2% of them preserved their bladders [62].

A key question in bladder cancer management is whether tailoring systemic therapy to individual tumor biology can increase the rate of pCR. The RETAIN BLADDER trial investigated this approach. The hypothesis was that mutations in ATM, ERCC2, FANCC, or RB1 increase sensitivity to platinum‐based chemotherapy. Among 70 patients with MIBC who received platinum‐based chemotherapy, 25 with one or more of these mutations and no clinical evidence of residual disease (based on post‐chemotherapy restaging via transurethral resection, urine cytology, and cross‐sectional imaging) were assigned an AS protocol. After 2 years, 12 patients (48%) remained metastasis‐free with an intact, nonirradiated bladder [63]. RETAIN‐2 is an ongoing Phase II trial evaluating bladder preservation in patients with cT2–T3N0M0, treated with dd‐MVAC chemotherapy and nivolumab [64]. Pretreatment transurethral resection specimens were sequenced for mutations in ATM, ERCC2, or RB1. Patients with mutations who demonstrated a cCR following neoadjuvant therapy were placed on AS. After a median follow‐up of 21.7 months, 60% of patients were metastasis‐free and retained a nonirradiated bladder.

Antibody‐drug conjugates (ADCs) deliver cytotoxic payloads directly to tumor cells through antigen‐specific monoclonal antibodies targeting molecules such as nectin‐4, HER2, and Trop‐2. Enfortumab vedotin (EV), an ADC targeting nectin‐4, has been evaluated in two Phase II trials as a monotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting. Both studies administered three cycles of EV prior to RC, demonstrating pCR in approximately one‐third of the patients [65, 66]. Disitamab vedotin, an ADC targeting HER2, combined with the PD‐1 inhibitor toripalimab, reported an interim pCR rate of 80% (8/10 patients) after three treatment cycles [67]. Sacituzumab govitecan, an ADC directed against Trop‐2, was studied in the SURE‐1 trial, showing a 36.4% pCR among 11 patients [68]. Recently, the results of two pivotal Phase III trials evaluating perioperative enfortumab vedotin (EV) in combination with pembrolizumab have been reported, establishing a new benchmark for treatment efficacy in muscle‐invasive bladder cancer and specifically regarding the effect on the primary tumor.

In the EV‐303/KENOTE‐905 trial, patients with cisplatin‐ineligible or cisplatin‐declining MIBC were randomized to receive perioperative EV plus pembrolizumab followed by radical cystectomy or radical cystectomy alone. The combination significantly improved both event‐free survival and overall survival compared with surgery alone. Furthermore, the pCR rate was 57.1% in the EV–pembrolizumab arm versus 8.6% in the control arm [60]. EV‐304/KEYNOTE‐B15 trial enrolled patients with cisplatin‐eligible MIBC and randomized them to receive perioperative EV plus pembrolizumab or standard cisplatin‐based neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by radical cystectomy. Treatment with EV plus pembrolizumab resulted in statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements in event‐free survival, pCR, and overall survival compared with standard chemotherapy [61]. Collectively, these landmark trials represent a major advance in the perioperative management of MIBC. Beyond demonstrating significant survival benefits, they achieved unprecedented pathological complete response rates in randomized Phase III studies, raising expectations for the potential of highly active systemic therapy to enhance local tumor eradication before definitive surgery.

The next critical question is when it is safe to omit local treatment after an apparently successful systemic treatment. This remains a major challenge, as a substantial proportion of patients who achieve a cCR are found to have residual disease upon RC [69, 70]. Multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) and liquid biopsies may help bridge the gap between cCR and pCR. Assays measuring circulating tumor cells (CTCs), cell‐free DNA (cfDNA), plasma circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and urine tumor DNA (utDNA) are being explored as biomarkers for tumor burden and treatment response. In one study, 84% (42/52) of ctDNA‐negative patients after neoadjuvant chemotherapy achieved a pCR at RC, compared to 0% among ctDNA‐positive patients [71]. utDNA is also emerging as a potential biomarker for detecting residual disease. In a cohort of 42 patients assessed on the day of RC, median utDNA levels were 4.3% among 16 patients with residual disease and 0% among 26 patients who achieved pCR [72].

Discussion

The manuscript reviews the local response to systemic treatments in patients with malignancies of the testis, kidney, prostate, and bladder. The ultimate goal is to safely omit local therapy, which is associated with morbidity, complications, mutilation, and a decrease in the quality of life. The two main challenges are selecting the most effective treatment for the individual patient and confirming pCR.

Prostate Cancer

Research efforts to enhance the RP outcomes in locally advanced PC have focused extensively on preoperative neoadjuvant treatments. Preoperative hormonal interventions, including LHRH agonists, antagonists, and androgen receptor blockers, reduced positive surgical margins and increased organ‐confined disease rates, but did not improve biochemical recurrence‐free survival. Why does neoadjuvant hormonal therapy that works so well before radiation is useless prior to surgery? The answer lies in the synergism between antiandrogens and radiation and the absence of such synergism with surgery. Hormonal therapy shrinks the prostate, making simulation more precise, arrests cancer cells at G1 phase (through decreasing the expression of cyclin D1 and increasing the expression of p21 and p27), and importantly impairs DNA repair mechanisms. All these functions improve response to radiation but are not relevant before surgery. Chemotherapeutic agents and immune‐based interventions have similarly failed to demonstrate clinical benefit in PC. Currently, none of these approaches have shown clinical efficacy, emphasizing the need for caution in using neoadjuvant therapies before the RP clinical trial. Better understanding of the role of the immune system in PC may provide novel neoadjuvant treatments.

Kidney Cancer

Effective systemic therapy before surgery could simplify procedures, potentially converting radical nephrectomy to partial nephrectomy and improving prognosis. This is especially significant in patients with IVC thrombus. Moreover, since kidney cancer is a slow‐growing tumor, delaying surgery by a few weeks may not adversely affect surgical outcomes.

Most reports on kidney cancer are small Phase I or II studies. TKIs and ICIs have demonstrated a reduction in tumor diameter by 13.5%–35%. While the clinical significance of this finding is questionable. A well‐conducted Phase III trial that randomized patients to either perioperative nivolumab or upfront surgery found no improvement in recurrence‐free survival [33]. Studies of the effects of systemic therapy on IVC thrombus showed unpredictable outcome, with approximately 40% of patients experiencing a decrease in thrombus length and 60% exhibiting stable or increased thrombus size. A recent promising study reported that combining torpalimab and axitinib reduced median thrombus length by 2.3 cm and altered the surgical strategy in 61.9% of patients [37]. A retrospective study showed the superiority of combination of neoadjuvant TKI and ICI over TKI alone in shortening tumor thrombus and shrinking the tumor [44]. This combination should be considered in patients with locally advanced disease.

Testis Cancer

Chemotherapy administered to patients with widespread germ‐cell cancer metastases, who later underwent orchiectomy, revealed viable tumors in about 20% of patients, teratomas in 40%, and scars or CIS in 40% of specimens. These findings emphasize the heterogeneity of treatment responses between primary and metastatic sites, reflecting both the complexity of testicular cancer biology and the influence of the BTB. Additionally, the multiclonality of NSGCTs, characterized by the coexistence of multiple histologic components within the same tumor, definitely contributes to this variability [53]. The presence of viable tumors in the testis after chemotherapy may have prognostic significance [45, 47, 51]. All reports on the local response of testis cancer to systemic therapy are retrospective, and thus their impact on clinical practice is limited.

Muscle Invasive Bladder Cancer

Local treatments for MIBC are associated with high morbidity, complications, mutilation, and a reduced quality of life. Effective systemic treatments that would avoid these outcomes would be highly beneficial. The observation that three cycles of M‐VAC resulted in a 38% pCR rate has sparked interest in systemic therapies as a means to eliminate the need for local treatment [57]. Recently, the incorporation of immune checkpoint inhibitors and antibody‐drug conjugates into the perioperative setting has resulted in unprecedented pCR rates exceeding 50% in randomized Phase III trials, substantially surpassing those achieved with cisplatin‐based chemotherapy alone and further fuelling interest in organ‐preserving approaches guided by systemic therapy [60, 61]. Also, “patient‐oriented treatment” tailored to specific tumor mutations [63, 64] is a promising approach. The use of molecular markers in urine and blood may increase confidence that a cCR truly reflects a pCR [71, 72].

Conclusions

Studies on the local effects of systemic treatments deepen our understanding of carcinogenesis and hold potential for improving local cancer control. However, thus far, systemic therapies do not eliminate the need for radical orchiectomy, nephrectomy, or local treatment of PC. While promising results have been observed in bladder cancer, selecting the optimal treatment and verifying pCR remain imprecise. Therefore, currently, attempts to avoid local treatment in patients with any of the four common genitourinary malignancies should be pursued solely within the context of a clinical trial.
