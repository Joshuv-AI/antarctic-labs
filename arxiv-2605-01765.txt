Distributional Causal Mediation via Conditional Generative Modeling
Jinlun Zhang, Haoneng Huang, Zishu Zhan, Chun-quan Ou

Abstract
Mediation analysis has traditionally focused on outcome-level summary contrasts, such as mean effects, which may obscure substantial distributional changes induced by complex and nonlinear causal mechanisms. We propose Distributional Causal Mediation Analysis (DCMA), a generative learning framework for identifying and estimating treatment effects on entire outcome distributions transmitted through multiple mediators. DCMA learns conditional generative models for the mediators and the outcome, recovering the relevant conditional distributions from observational data. Leveraging the identification formulas, it reconstructs interventional outcome distributions via Monte Carlo forward simulation by noise resampling, enabling the capture of both classical summary effects and rich distributional contrasts such as energy distance and the Wasserstein distance. Analytical error bounds are derived to decompose how estimation errors in the learned conditional models propagate to the reconstructed interventional outcome distributions. The empirical effectiveness of DCMA is demonstrated through numerical experiments and real-world data applications.

However, existing approaches often rely on parametric models and focus on summary effects such as mean contrasts, which may obscure important nonlinear mechanisms and distributional heterogeneity. Interventions with similar average effects can yield very different outcome distributions, for example by affecting subgroups differently. A tracking program, through differences in teacher support and resources, may leave the class average unchanged while widening the gap between high and low performers, resulting in a bimodal distribution. Therefore, it is essential to move beyond summary-based mediation analysis toward a full distributional characterization of interventional effects. Moreover, in practice there are typically multiple mediators, raising the question of how to capture the path-specific distributional effects of an exposure on an outcome that operate through distinct mediators.

Motivated by above considerations, we extend interventional mediation analysis to a distributional framework that characterizes how interventions reshape the entire outcome distribution, both directly and through multiple mediators. We further introduce a generalized notion of distributional mediation effects that unifies a broad class of causal targets, including mean and quantile effects, exceedance probabilities, and distributional distances such as the energy distance (ED)
(
12
)
.

We derive identification results that express distributional interventional effects in terms of conditional distributions, reducing estimation to learning the conditional laws of mediators and outcomes under appropriate interventions. Building on this characterization, we develop a deep generative learning framework that flexibly models these conditional distributions via a conditional generator
g(X,\varepsilon)
, enabling efficient Monte Carlo approximation of interventional outcome distributions without explicit likelihood specification
(
23
;
16
;
15
)
.

Leveraging these insights, we propose the Distributional Causal Mediation
Analysis (DCMA) framework. Our contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce distributional interventional mediation estimands that
characterize causal mediation effects at the level of full outcome
distributions, generalizing classical mean-based mediation effects.

•

We develop a generative mediation framework that models the conditional
distributions of multiple mediators and outcome via noise-based conditional
generators, enabling flexible distributional effect estimation.

•

We establish identification results and derive analytical error bounds that quantify how estimation errors in the learned mediator and outcome conditional models propagate to the reconstructed interventional outcome distributions.

Interventional mediation was formally introduced to enable effect decomposition

mediation effects may be ill-defined or non-identifiable

(
18

Building on this formulation, subsequent work extended interventional mediation

. More recently,

Along related lines,

3.2
Target Estimands

We extend interventional mediation effects to a distributional framework, where the primary objects of interest are the interventional outcome distributions themselves. Given a bivariate functional
\Psi:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\text{ITE}^{\Psi}

\displaystyle:=\Psi\left(P_{Y_{1\tilde{{\bm{M}}}_{1}}},P_{Y_{0\tilde{{\bm{M}}}_{0}}}\right),

\displaystyle\text{IDE}^{\Psi}

\displaystyle:=\Psi\left(P_{Y_{1\tilde{{\bm{M}}}_{0}}},P_{Y_{0\tilde{{\bm{M}}}_{0}}}\right),

3.3
Identification

To identify the interventional mediation effects, we invoke the standard
assumptions of consistency, positivity, and sequential ignorability.

Assumption 3.1

(Consistency)
.

For any

\bm{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{S})

(A,\bm{M})=(a,\bm{m})

, then

.

Assumption 3.2

(Positivity)
.

For all covariate values
\bm{z}
with positive probability,

\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid\bm{Z}=\bm{z})>0

for the treatment levels of interest. In addition, for each mediator

s=1,\dots,S

M_{s}
given

(A=a,\bm{Z}=\bm{z})
has support covering the values induced by the
interventions under consideration.

Assumption 3.3

(Sequential ignorability)
.

\displaystyle\text{(1)}\quad Y_{a\bm{m}}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid\bm{Z},

\displaystyle\text{(2)}\quad Y_{a\bm{m}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\bm{M}\mid(A,\bm{Z}),

\displaystyle\text{(3)}\quad\bm{M}_{a}\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid\bm{Z}.

Assumption

states that there is no unmeasured confounding
for the exposure–outcome and exposure–mediator relationships conditional
on baseline covariates
\bm{Z}

(Identification)

Under Assumptions

4
DCMA Framework

4.1
Overall Pipeline

Figure
2
summarizes the proposed DCMA framework, which consists
of four main stages.

(i) Data preprocessing.

The observed data are split into training and validation sets, and the mediators

\bm{M}
, outcome
Y
, and covariates
\bm{Z}
are standardized for numerical
stability.

(ii) Conditional generative modeling.

DCMA learns a mediator generator
f_{M}(A,\bm{Z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{M})
and an outcome generator
f_{Y}(A,\bm{Z},\bm{M},\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y})
to approximate the conditional mediator and outcome distributions. During training, independent noise variables
\bm{\varepsilon}_{M}
and
\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y}
are repeatedly sampled and injected into the generators, and the model parameters are optimized using distributional learning objectives.

(iii) Interventional distribution generation.

Interventional mediator and outcome samples are generated by forward simulation (Algorithm details can be found in Appendix
D
)
from the trained generators under the corresponding treatment assignments,
implementing the identification formulas via noise resampling.

(iv) Causal effects estimation.

Because the model is generative, it can be used to sample counterfactual outcomes under user-specified interventions. Distributional causal estimands are then obtained by applying the corresponding functionals (e.g., means, quantiles, exceedance probabilities) to the resulting Monte Carlo draws.

4.2
Conditional Generators for Mediators and Outcome

We model each conditional distribution using a noise-driven generator that maps
exogenous randomness to samples following the desired conditional distribution.

For the mediators, let
\bm{\varepsilon}_{M}
be a noise vector independent of

(A,\bm{Z})
with distribution
P_{\bm{\varepsilon}_{M}}
.
We consider a generative function

f_{M}(a,\bm{z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{M})

such that, for every
(a,\bm{z})
in the support of
(A,\bm{Z})
,

f_{M}(a,\bm{z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{M})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}P_{\bm{M}\mid A=a,\bm{Z}=\bm{z}}.

That is, the generator
f_{M}
induces the conditional distribution of the
mediators given
(A,\bm{Z})
. Conditional samples from

P_{\bm{M}\mid A=a,\bm{Z}=\bm{z}}
are obtained by drawing

\bm{\varepsilon}_{M}
and evaluating
f_{M}(a,\bm{z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{M})
.
We denote by
P_{f_{M}(\cdot\mid a,\bm{z})}
the distribution induced by the
mediator generator.

The outcome generator is defined analogously.
Let
\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y}
be independent noise with distribution

P_{\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y}}
, independent of
(A,\bm{M},\bm{Z})
.
We consider a generative function

f_{Y}(a,\bm{m},\bm{z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y})

such that

f_{Y}(a,\bm{m},\bm{z},\bm{\varepsilon}_{Y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}P_{Y\mid A=a,\bm{M}=\bm{m},\bm{Z}=\bm{z}}.

We denote by
P_{f_{Y}(\cdot\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z})}
the distribution induced by the
outcome generator.

4.3
Population Objective via Distributional Discrepancy

We train the generators by minimizing a conditional distributional discrepancy.
Let
\mathcal{D}(P,Q)
denote any distributional discrepancy satisfying that,
for any probability distributions
P
and
Q
,

\mathcal{D}(P,Q)\;\geq\;\mathcal{D}(Q,Q),

with equality if and only if
P=Q
. The population risks are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{M}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A,\bm{Z}}\Big[\mathcal{D}\!\big(P_{f_{M}(\cdot\mid A,\bm{Z})},P_{\bm{M}\mid A,\bm{Z}}\big)\Big],

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{Y}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{A,\bm{M},\bm{Z}}\Big[\mathcal{D}\!\big(P_{f_{Y}(\cdot\mid A,\bm{M},\bm{Z})},P_{Y\mid A,\bm{M},\bm{Z}}\big)\Big].

The population-optimal generators satisfy

f_{M}^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{L}_{M},\qquad f_{Y}^{\ast}\in\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathcal{L}_{Y},

where
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{Y}
denote sufficiently rich function classes.

Implications of the population objective.

Under the distributional discrepancy
\mathcal{D}
defined above and sufficiently rich function classes
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, any population-optimal generators
f_{M}^{\ast}
and
f_{Y}^{\ast}
recover the true conditional data-generating mechanisms in the following sense:

\displaystyle P_{f_{M}^{\ast}(\cdot\mid a,\bm{z})}

\displaystyle=P_{\bm{M}\mid A=a,\bm{Z}=\bm{z}},

\displaystyle P_{f_{Y}^{\ast}(\cdot\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z})}

\displaystyle=P_{Y\mid A=a,\bm{M}=\bm{m},\bm{Z}=\bm{z}},

for almost all relevant conditioning values.
This implies that, at the population optimum, DCMA recovers the exact conditional interventional distributions without requiring likelihood specification, parametric assumptions, or explicit density modeling. A proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

5
Error Analysis

5.1
ED and its RKHS representation

We use the ED
(
12
)
to quantify
distributional estimation error as it is closely aligned with the ES used
for training. Specifically, the expected ES of a predictive distribution
differs from its ED to the true distribution only by a constant that
depends on the true distribution.
For any
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
the ED is defined as

\mathrm{ED}(P,Q)=2\,\mathbb{E}\|X-Y\|-\mathbb{E}\|X-X^{\prime}\|-\mathbb{E}\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|,

where
X,X^{\prime}\sim P
and
Y,Y^{\prime}\sim Q
are independent.
The ED is nonnegative and equals zero if and only if
P=Q
.

The Euclidean distance appearing in the definition of ED
is of negative type.
A fundamental result of
14
establishes that
for any positive definite kernel
k
inducing a negative-type semimetric

\rho(z,w)=k(z,z)+k(w,w)-2k(z,w)
, the corresponding RKHS

\mathcal{H}_{k}
satisfies

\mathrm{ED}(P,Q)=2\big\|\mu_{k}(P)-\mu_{k}(Q)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}^{2},

where
\mu_{k}(P)=\int k(\cdot,x)\,P(dx)
denotes the kernel mean embedding.
This representation allows distributional errors to be analyzed using Hilbert
space techniques.

5.2
Errors decomposition

Building on the RKHS representation of the ED established above,
we now study how estimation errors from the mediator and outcome models
propagate to the reconstructed interventional outcome distribution.

For notational simplicity, we write

\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}

for the estimated conditional mediator and outcome distributions, and

P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}

for their true counterparts.
Let

\displaystyle\widehat{R}

\displaystyle:=\int\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\,\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}),

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle:=\int P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\,P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m})

denote the estimated and true interventional outcome distributions.
We quantify their discrepancy by

B:={\rm ED}\big(\widehat{R},R\big).

To separate the contributions of the mediator and outcome models,
we introduce the intermediate distribution

R^{\mathrm{med}}=\int\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\,P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}),

which corresponds to evaluating the learned outcome model under the true
mediator distribution.

Using the Hilbert space norm inequality

\|u-v\|^{2}\leq 2\|u-w\|^{2}+2\|w-v\|^{2}

implied by the RKHS representation of the ED, we obtain

B\leq 2\Big[\underbrace{\mathrm{ED}\big(\widehat{R},R^{\mathrm{med}}\big)}_{B_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathrm{ED}\big(R^{\mathrm{med}},R\big)}_{B_{2}}\Big].

These two terms admit a clear interpretation.
The term
B_{1}
captures how errors in estimating the mediator distribution
propagate through the outcome mechanism, while
B_{2}
reflects the
approximation error of the conditional outcome model when evaluated under the
true mediator distribution.

5.3
Regularity conditions

Let
k_{Y}
be a positive definite kernel on the outcome space that induces the
ED, and let
\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}
denote its associated RKHS.
We impose the following regularity conditions to control the two error terms
arising in the decomposition of interventional distributional errors.

Condition 1

For all relevant
(a,\bm{m},\bm{z})
, the conditional outcome distributions

\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}
and
P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}
belong to

\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}})
.
Moreover, the mapping

\bm{m}\mapsto\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)

is strongly measurable and Bochner integrable with respect to both the estimated
and true interventional mediator distributions

\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
.

Condition 2

The mediator distributions

\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}

belong to
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}})
.

Condition 3

Define the estimated conditional outcome embedding

\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})=\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}.

There exists a constant
L_{\psi}<\infty
such that

\big\|\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})-\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq L_{\psi}\,\|\bm{m}-\bm{m}^{\prime}\|,\quad\forall\,\bm{m},\bm{m}^{\prime}.

Conditions 1 and 2 impose mild moment assumptions on the conditional outcome and
mediator distributions, ensuring that EDs and kernel
mean embeddings are well defined and that the relevant Bochner integrals exist.
Condition 3 is a technical regularity assumption on the estimated conditional
outcome embedding, requiring Lipschitz continuity with respect to the mediator
argument. It is imposed to facilitate the theoretical analysis of error
propagation to the induced interventional outcome distributions.

5.4
Error bounds

Lemma 5.1

(Mediator distribution error propagation)
.

Let
W_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the 1-Wasserstein distance.
Under Conditions 1–3,

B_{1}\leq 2L_{\psi}^{2}\,W_{1}^{2}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right).

Lemma 5.2

(Outcome model error contribution)
.

Under Conditions 1–2,

B_{2}\leq\int\mathrm{ED}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

Combining the above two lemmas immediately yields the following structural error bound for the reconstructed interventional distribution.

Theorem 5.3

(Structural error bound for interventional outcome distributions)
.

Under Conditions 1–3,

\displaystyle B\leq

\displaystyle 2L_{\psi}^{2}\,W_{1}^{2}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{ED}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

Thus, the overall error of DCMA admits a clean additive decomposition into two terms, controlled by conditional mediator and outcome distribution estimation errors. If both terms vanish, the estimated interventional outcome distribution converges to the oracle distribution in ED.

However, establishing nonasymptotic error bounds under the ED metric remains an
open problem. In contrast, the Wasserstein distance admits tractable
nonasymptotic error analysis under suitable regularity conditions. As shown in Appendix
B
, the Wasserstein distance between the estimated and true interventional outcome distributions,
W_{1}\big(\widehat{R},R\big)
, converges at a rate determined by the slower of the WGR estimation rates for the mediator and outcome generators, up to logarithmic factors.

7
Discussion and Conclusion

In summary, DCMA provides a principled generative framework for distributional causal mediation analysis, enabling the estimation of mediation effects beyond conventional mean contrasts. By characterizing interventional effects at the level of entire outcome distributions, DCMA offers additional insights into complex and heterogeneous mediation mechanisms that are not captured by mean-based analyses.

DCMA reconstructs interventional outcome distributions via Monte Carlo forward simulation by resampling exogenous noise and plugging it into the trained conditional generators, a mechanism that naturally aligns with structural causal models and do-interventions. By training with proper distribution discrepancies, DCMA ensures consistent estimation of the
underlying interventional outcome distributions. While alternative generative models such as normalizing flows or diffusion models could also be incorporated, our main contribution lies in establishing a causally aligned, theoretically grounded, and computationally stable framework for distributional causal mediation analysis.

Appendix B
Wasserstein-Based Training and Error Analysis

In this appendix, we adopt the Wasserstein-1 distance as the primary metric

\mathcal{D}
for measuring distributional errors and derive non-asymptotic
error bounds for interventional outcome distributions under this metric.
This provides a complementary perspective to the ES-based
analysis presented in the main text.

Wasserstein-1 distance.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denote the set of probability measures on

\mathbb{R}^{d}
with finite first moments. For any
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
the Wasserstein-1 distance is defined as

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int\|x-y\|\,\pi(\mathrm{d}x,\mathrm{d}y),

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
.
Equivalently, by the Kantorovich–Rubinstein duality,

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}\leq 1}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[f(X)]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f(X)]\Big\},

where the supremum is taken over all real-valued
1
-Lipschitz functions
f
.

Intuitively,
W_{1}(\mu,\nu)
measures the minimal transportation cost required
to transform one distribution into the other, where the cost of moving a unit
of mass from
x
to
y
is given by the Euclidean distance
\|x-y\|
.
The dual formulation further shows that
W_{1}
can be interpreted as the
largest difference in expectations over all
1
-Lipschitz test functions.

B.1
Wasserstein generative regression

Building on the Wasserstein-1 distance,
16
proposed the
Wasserstein generative regression (WGR) framework for learning
conditional generative models. WGR augments the Wasserstein loss with an
additional
\ell_{2}
regularization term, which primarily serves to improve
training stability and to reinforce the learning of structural
relationships between covariates and responses, rather than merely matching
their distributional shapes.

Specifically, the WGR objective is given by

\min_{g}\;\max_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{Lip}}}\lambda_{w}\,\big\{\mathbb{E}[f(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}[f(X,g(X,\varepsilon))]\big\}\;+\;\lambda_{l}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|Y-g(X,\varepsilon)\|^{2}\big],

where
f
denotes a
1
-Lipschitz critic,
\varepsilon
is an exogenous noise
vector,
0\leq\lambda_{\ell}<1
,
0<\lambda_{w}\leq 1
, and
\lambda_{\ell}+\lambda_{w}=1
balance the Wasserstein matching
objective and the
\ell_{2}
regression regularization, respectively.

B.2
Error Decomposition

Our ultimate goal is to derive a non-asymptotic upper bound for the
expected prediction error measured by the Wasserstein-1 distance,

\mathbb{E}_{S}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\,\Big[W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{R},\;R\right)\Big],

where the expectations are taken over the training sample
S
and the
exogenous noise
Z
, respectively.
To this end, we first establish an error decomposition analogous to that
under the ED metric in the main text.

By the triangle inequality, we obtain the following decomposition:

W_{1}(\widehat{R},R)\leq W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{R},\;R^{\mathrm{med}}\big)+W_{1}\!\big(R^{\mathrm{med}},\;R\big).

Unlike the ED, which corresponds to the squared norm of a
metric in an RKHS, the Wasserstein-1 distance is itself a metric on

\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and therefore directly satisfies the triangle
inequality.

To derive an error propagation result analogous to that in the main text
under the Wasserstein metric, we impose a slightly modified set of
regularity conditions.

Condition 1
′

For all relevant
(a,\bm{m},\bm{z})
, the conditional outcome distributions

\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}
and
P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}
belong to

\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{Y}})
.
Moreover, the mapping

m\mapsto W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},\,P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\big)

is measurable and integrable with respect to
P_{M\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
.

Condition 2
′

For all relevant
(a^{\prime},\bm{z})
, the conditional mediator distributions

\widehat{P}_{M\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
P_{M\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
belong to

\mathcal{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{M}})
.

Condition 3
′

There exists a constant
L_{M}<\infty
such that for all relevant
(a,\bm{z})

and all
m,m^{\prime}
,

W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},\,\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m^{\prime}},\bm{z}}\big)\leq L_{M}\,\|\bm{m}-\bm{m}^{\prime}\|.

Theorem B.1

(Wasserstein error decomposition)
.

Under Assumptions 1’–3’,

W_{1}(\widehat{R},R)\leq L_{M}\,W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{P}_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\big)+\int W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\big)\,P_{\bm{M}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(\mathrm{d}\bm{m}).

B.3
Nonasymptotic error bounds

After establishing the Wasserstein error decomposition, we next derive
non-asymptotic convergence bounds by leveraging the results of
16
.
For notational simplicity and theoretical convenience, we adopt a unified
notation throughout the paper. We use
g\in\mathcal{G}
to represent both
the mediator and outcome generators, mapping an input

X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and an exogenous noise vector

\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
to an output

Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
via

Y=g(X,\varepsilon).

The dimensions
d
,
q
, and
m
denote the input, output, and noise dimensions, respectively, and may vary across different components of the
model.
The following regularity conditions are needed.

Condition 4
′

For any
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the joint distributions of
(X,Y)
and

(X,g(X,\varepsilon))
are supported on a compact set

\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\subset[-B_{1},B_{1}]^{d+q}
for some constant

0<B_{1}<\infty
.

Condition 5
′

The latent noise variable
\varepsilon
has support

\Omega_{\varepsilon}\subset[-B_{1},B_{1}]^{m}
.

Condition 6
′

The true conditional generative mechanism

g^{*}=(g_{1}^{*},\ldots,g_{q}^{*})^{\top}
satisfies

g_{k}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(\mathcal{X}\times\Omega_{\varepsilon},B_{1}),\qquad k=1,\ldots,q,

for some
\beta>0
. Here,

\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(\mathcal{X}\times\Omega_{\varepsilon},B_{1})
denotes the
Hölder class of
\beta
-smooth functions on

\mathcal{X}\times\Omega_{\varepsilon}
with Hölder constant bounded by
B_{1}
.

Condition 7
′
.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a vector

\varepsilon_{x}\in\Omega_{\varepsilon}
such that

\bigl\|\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[g(x,\varepsilon)]-\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}[\tilde{g}(x,\varepsilon)]\bigr\|_{1}\leq\|g(x,\varepsilon_{x})-\tilde{g}(x,\varepsilon_{x})\|_{1},\qquad\forall\,g,\tilde{g}\in\mathcal{G}.

Here,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes the
\ell_{1}
-norm.

Under the above conditions and appropriate network parameter choices,
the Wasserstein-1 estimation error of the learned conditional
distributions admits the following non-asymptotic bound.

Lemma B.2

(Corollary 1 of
16
)
.

Under Conditions 4
′
–7
′
and appropriate network parameter choices, for

J\gtrsim n
, the WGR estimator
\widehat{g}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}_{S}\,\mathbb{E}_{X}\!\left[W_{1}\!\big(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid X},\,P_{Y\mid X}\big)\right]\leq C_{2}\,n^{-\alpha}\,(\log n)^{\frac{2\beta}{m+d}\,\vee\,1},

where

\alpha:=\frac{\beta}{\,2\beta+\{3(m+d)\}\vee\{2\beta(d+q+1)\}\,}.

Here,
n
denotes the sample size,
J
is the number of Monte Carlo samples
drawn from the noise distribution of
\varepsilon
, and
C_{2}
is a positive
constant independent of
n
and
J
.

Building on Lemma
B.2
, we now derive a non-asymptotic bound
for the estimation error of the interventional outcome distribution
measured by the Wasserstein-1 distance.

Theorem B.3

(Non-asymptotic bound for interventional outcome distributions)
.

Assume the conditions of Theorem B.1 hold, and Conditions 4
′
–7
′
in
Lemma
B.2
hold for both the mediator and outcome generators
under appropriate network parameter choices with
J\gtrsim n
.
Then for any treatment levels
a,a^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}
with

\mathbb{P}(A=a)>0
and
\mathbb{P}(A=a^{\prime})>0

\mathbb{E}_{S}\,\mathbb{E}_{Z}\!\left[W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{R},\;R\right)\right]\leq L_{M}\,\frac{C_{M}}{\mathbb{P}(A=a^{\prime})}\,n^{-\alpha_{M}}(\log n)^{\frac{2\beta_{M}}{m_{M}+d_{M}}\,\vee\,1}+\frac{C_{Y}}{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}\,n^{-\alpha_{Y}}(\log n)^{\frac{2\beta_{Y}}{m_{Y}+d_{Y}}\,\vee\,1},

where

are positive constants independent of
n
and

,
and

\alpha_{M}:=\frac{\beta_{M}}{\,2\beta_{M}+\{3(m_{M}+d_{M})\}\vee\{2\beta_{M}(d_{M}+q_{M}+1)\}\,},\quad\alpha_{Y}:=\frac{\beta_{Y}}{\,2\beta_{Y}+\{3(m_{Y}+d_{Y})\}\vee\{2\beta_{Y}(d_{Y}+q_{Y}+1)\}\,}.

Hence, the convergence rate of the interventional distribution estimator
is effectively controlled by the slower of the WGR rates for the
mediator and outcome generators.

(a,z)

\mathcal{D}(P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}},P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}})\leq\mathcal{D}(Q,P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}}),

with equality if and only if
Q=P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}}
.
Hence
P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}}
is the unique minimizer of the conditional expected score.

If
Q_{M}
differs from
P_{\bm{M}\mid A,\bm{Z}}
on a set of positive
P_{A\bm{Z}}
-measure,
the inequality is strict on that set.
Integrating over
(A,\bm{Z})\sim P_{A\bm{Z}}
yields

\mathcal{L}_{M}(Q_{M})-\mathcal{L}_{M}(P_{\bm{M}\mid A,\bm{Z}})>0,

and therefore
Q_{M}
cannot be a global minimizer of the population objective.
Thus any minimizer
f_{M}^{\star}
must induce

P_{\bm{M}\mid a,\bm{z}}
for
P_{A\bm{Z}}
-almost every
(a,\bm{z})
.

The same argument, conditioning on
(a,\bm{m},\bm{z})
and integrating over
P_{A\bm{M}\bm{Z}}
,
establishes the claim for the outcome stage.
∎

C.3
Proof of Lemma
5.1

Proof.

By the definitions of
\widehat{R}
and
R^{\mathrm{med}}
, we have

\widehat{R}(B)=\int\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}(B)\,\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}),\qquad\forall\,B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

and analogously for
R^{\mathrm{med}}
.
By Condition 1, the mapping

\bm{m}\mapsto\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}})

is strongly measurable and Bochner integrable with respect to

\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and

P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
.
Recall that the kernel mean embedding of a distribution
P
is given by

\mu_{k_{Y}}(P)=\int k_{Y}(\cdot,y)\,P(dy).

By the defination of
\widehat{R}
, we have

\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})=\int k_{Y}(\cdot,y)\,\widehat{R}(dy)=\int\left\{\int k_{Y}(\cdot,y)\,\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}(dy)\right\}\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

By the Bochner–Fubini theorem, the order of integration can be exchanged,
which yields

\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})=\int\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

An analogous argument gives

\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})=\int\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

Let

\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})=\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}.

Then

\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})-\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})=\int\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})\,\Big(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}-P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\Big)(d\bm{m}).

By the dual characterization of the Hilbert norm,

\Big\|\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})-\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}=\sup_{\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq 1}\left|\int\langle h,\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\,\Big(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}-P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\Big)(d\bm{m})\right|.

Fix any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}
with
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq 1
and define

f_{h}(\bm{m})=\langle h,\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}
.
By Condition 3, for any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}
,

|f_{h}(\bm{m})-f_{h}(\bm{m}^{\prime})|=\big|\langle h,\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})-\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m}^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\big|\leq\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\,\big\|\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})-\widehat{\psi}_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m}^{\prime})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq L_{\psi}\,\|\bm{m}-\bm{m}^{\prime}\|.

Hence
f_{h}/L_{\psi}
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the Euclidean metric on the
mediator space.

By Condition 2, the measures

\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}
and
P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}

have finite first moments, so the
1
-Wasserstein distance is well defined and
admits the Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual representation:

W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right)=\sup_{\mathrm{Lip}(g)\leq 1}\left|\int g(\bm{m})\,\Big(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}-P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\Big)(d\bm{m})\right|,

where
\mathrm{Lip}(g)\leq 1
denotes that
g
is
1
-Lipschitz with respect to
the Euclidean norm. Since
f_{h}/L_{\psi}
is
1
-Lipschitz, plugging

g=f_{h}/L_{\psi}
into the above supremum yields

\left|\int\frac{f_{h}(\bm{m})}{L_{\psi}}\,\Big(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}-P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\Big)(d\bm{m})\right|\leq W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right).

Multiplying both sides by
L_{\psi}
gives

\left|\int f_{h}(\bm{m})\,\Big(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}-P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\Big)(d\bm{m})\right|\leq L_{\psi}\,W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right).

Taking the supremum over all
\|h\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq 1
gives

\Big\|\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})-\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}\leq L_{\psi}\,W_{1}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}},P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}\right).

Finally, using the RKHS representation of the energy distance,

\mathrm{ED}\!\left(\widehat{R},R^{\mathrm{med}}\right)=2\Big\|\mu_{k_{Y}}(\widehat{R})-\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}^{2},

the desired bound follows.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.2

Proof.

By the same reasoning as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, and by
Condition 1, Fubini’s theorem for Bochner integrals yields

\displaystyle\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})

\displaystyle=\int\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}),

\displaystyle=\int\mu_{k_{Y}}\!\left(P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

Since the squared RKHS norm is a convex function, Jensen’s inequality for

\big\|\mu_{k_{Y}}(R^{\mathrm{med}})-\mu_{k_{Y}}(R)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}^{2}\leq\int\|\Delta_{a,\bm{z}}(\bm{m})\|_{\mathcal{H}_{k_{Y}}}^{2}\,P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}).

we obtain

\mathrm{ED}\!\left(R^{\mathrm{med}},R\right)\leq\int\mathrm{ED}\!\left(\widehat{P}_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}},P_{Y\mid a,\bm{m},\bm{z}}\right)\,P_{\tilde{\bm{M}}\mid a^{\prime},\bm{z}}(d\bm{m}),

which completes the proof.

Proof.

Appendix F
Ablation Study

To assess whether distributional recovery truly requires a flexible outcome generator, we conduct a targeted ablation within the DCMA framework under the bimodal outcome scenario S1. We compare the full DCMA model with a variant in which the outcome generator is replaced by a linear Gaussian model, while keeping the mediator generator unchanged.

A linear Gaussian outcome model is inherently centered on conditional mean recovery under a unimodal normality assumption. Even when used for Monte Carlo sampling, it can only produce Gaussian outcomes and is therefore incapable of representing bimodal or more complex interventional outcome distributions. This mismatch is clearly illustrated in Figure
7
, where the ablated model generates only unimodal Gaussian samples that fail to match the true bimodal structure. The consequence of this limitation is quantified in Figure
8
. Across 100 repetitions, the resulting EDs for the Linear–Gaussian ablation are consistently and substantially larger than those obtained by the full DCMA model for all interventional outcome distributions.
