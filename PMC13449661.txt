Isolated renal hydatic cyst in a 14-year-old girl: A case report
Authors: Amina Karray, Maryem Marzouki, Abir Jouini, Khalil Matoussi, Faouzi Nouira, Said Jlidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal hydatid cysts rarely occur in children. The kidney is the third most frequently affected organ, following the liver and lungs, and presents diverse clinical manifestations. This report describes a previously healthy 14-year-old girl whose mother underwent hepatic hydatid cyst surgery a year earlier. The girl reported three months of left-sided lumbar pain. Clinical examination revealed a palpable, non-tender left flank mass. Imaging revealed two cystic lesions in her left kidney. She received a partial pericystectomy and recovered uneventfully. The family was counseled on how to prevent Echinococcus. Subsequent screening detected a pulmonary hydatid cyst in her 9-year-old brother.

Introduction

Hydatid cyst (HC) is a zoonotic infection caused by the larval stage of Echinococcus granulosus and remains endemic in numerous countries worldwide. HC represents a significant public health concern due to high treatment costs and increased mortality rates in both humans and animals.1 In pediatric populations, renal HC is rare. Isolated renal HC is particularly uncommon, as renal involvement typically occurs alongside other disease localizations.2 Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific, with hydaturia being the only pathognomonic sign. The rarity of isolated renal hydatid cysts and their vague clinical presentation create significant diagnostic difficulties. This case highlights these challenges in diagnosing uncommon, isolated renal hydatidosis in pediatric patients from endemic regions.

Case report

A previously healthy 14-year-old girl, whose mother had undergone surgical treatment for hepatic HC one year earlier, presented with a three-month history of episodic left-sided lower back pain. There were no associated symptoms, including weight loss, night sweats, or urinary complaints such as dysuria or hematuria. Clinical examination indicated a stable general condition, with no fever or hypertension. Abdominal palpation revealed a painless, smooth-bordered mass on the left flank extending to the lumbar region, measuring 7 cm at its longest dimension. No additional palpable masses were identified. Abdominal ultrasound (US) demonstrated two cystic lesions in the left kidney, one of which was multilocular, without evidence of excretory cavity dilatation (Fig. 1). These findings were suggestive of a renal HC, with no other abdominal involvement, particularly no hepatic HC. Chest X-ray excluded pulmonary involvement, and hydatid serology was negative. Further evaluation with abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed two mid-renal cystic lesions on the anterior lobe of the left kidney (Fig. 2). The diagnosis of renal HC was retained. Surgical exploration through a left supraumbilical transverse incision revealed a large, bilobed, exophytic hydatid cyst measuring 8 cm and occupying the entire lower pole of the kidney. The procedure included cystoparietal adhesiolysis, field protection with hypertonic saline, needle aspiration of clear fluid, cyst opening with removal of the germinal layer and daughter vesicles (Fig. 3), sterilization of the residual cavity with hypertonic saline, partial pericystectomy, peritoneal lavage, and hemostasis. The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient and her family received counseling on preventing Echinococcus infection. Discussion

Hydatid disease is a benign parasitic condition that continues to pose a significant public health concern because of its endemic presence in regions of Latin America and the Mediterranean.3 This zoonotic infection is caused by the larval stage of the dog tapeworm, Echinococcus granulosus, which develops in humans. Canids serve as the definitive hosts, while herbivores function as intermediate hosts. Humans are considered accidental hosts.1 Transmission to humans occurs via the oral-fecal route, typically through the consumption of vegetables contaminated with dog feces containing parasite eggs. Following ingestion, parasite embryos penetrate the duodenal mucosa, enter the portal system, and subsequently reach the venous or lymphatic circulation.4 These embryos can disseminate to various organs. In pediatric populations, the lungs are most affected, followed by the liver. Renal involvement, resulting in kidney HC, is rare and occurs in approximately 2% of pediatric cases and 3% of adult cases.4 The lesion is usually single and unilateral, primarily located in the polar region, with a slight predominance in the left kidney.3 In our case the location was mediorenal. Upon reaching the kidney, parasitic embryos form fluid-filled sacs called HC. Each HC comprises two primary layers. The outer layer is derived from kidney tissue, while the inner layer consists of a thin covering and a specialized membrane. This membrane generates parasite heads, which subsequently develop into new small sacs within the cyst. The mother cyst typically contains multiple smaller daughter cysts. Renal HC remains asymptomatic for prolonged periods due to their slow growth and retroperitoneal location, particularly in pediatric patients. Clinical manifestations generally arise when the HC reaches a size sufficient to exert mass effect, rather than from inflammation. The main symptoms are flank pain, a palpable mass, and abdominal distension or pain.4 Hematuria and dysuria are rare and nonspecific findings. In contrast, hydatiduria, defined as the passage of hydatid sand or daughter cysts in urine, is the only pathognomonic clinical sign of renal HC.3 Clinical examination can reveal a palpable abdominal mass in the lumbar region. In cases involving pulmonary or hepatic hydatid cysts, the presence of respiratory signs and a right upper quadrant abdominal mass can aid diagnosis. The examination should also assess skin rash and fever. Laboratory findings, including blood hyper-eosinophilia and serological tests, offer only presumptive evidence. Immunological tests are positive in approximately 70% of cases but are not pathognomonic.3 US is the primary diagnostic modality for intra-abdominal hydatid cysts, using the Gharbi classification (Table 1).5 Although originally developed for hepatic hydatid cysts, the Gharbi classification is also applicable to renal HC. During US assessment, clinicians should systematically evaluate additional sites of hydatid dissemination and assess the contralateral kidney, which is involved in approximately 6% of cases.3 If diagnostic uncertainty remains after US, CT is recommended. Without intervention, renal HC often led to complications, while calcification remains rare. HC commonly expands and may rupture into the urinary tract, elevating infection risk. Rupture can trigger severe allergic responses due to parasite release and may cause life-threatening anaphylaxis in pediatric patients.1 Additionally, the cyst may compromise renal function by compressing the parenchyma or obstructing the urinary tract. The standard management of renal hydatid disease involves surgical intervention, most commonly partial pericystectomy is sufficient. Total pericystectomy is not recommended in pediatric patients because of the increased risk of bleeding.3 In exceptional cases, total nephrectomy depending on the extent of renal parenchymal involvement.4 During surgery, meticulous care is required to prevent cyst dissemination. The recurrence rate of the lesion ranges from 4% to 22%.1 Recurrence may occur in the original organ or in a different one. Laparoscopic procedures are a viable alternative in current management strategies. However, the conventional surgical approach is most frequently used. Conclusion

Renal HC in children are a rare form of this zoonotic disease, even in endemic regions. Diagnosis is usually straightforward when renal UC is associated with cysts in the lungs or liver. However, it is more challenging with isolated renal hydatid cysts. Surgical resection is the preferred standard treatment; it should be as conservative as possible to achieve favorable outcomes. Despite this, prevention is the most effective and cost-efficient approach in endemic areas. Preventive strategies disrupt human transmission through targeted health education for children and families.
