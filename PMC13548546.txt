Analysis of retina, choroid, and microvascular structures in amblyopic patients using OCT and OCTA
Authors: Oğuzhan Karakaş, Gülce Gökgöz Özışık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate structural and microvascular changes in amblyopic eyes using optical coherence tomography (OCT) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA), and to compare these findings with those of fellow eyes and healthy individuals.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional controlled study. Retinal and choroidal structures were examined using the Spectralis OCT device (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany), and vascular analyses were performed with the Topcon Triton SS-OCT (Topcon Triton, Tokyo, Japan). Structural parameters such as macular thickness, ganglion cell layer (GCL), inner plexiform layer (IPL), peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (PpRNFL), and choroidal thickness were measured. Additionally, luminal area (LA), total choroidal area (TCA), and choroidal vascular index (CVI) were calculated. Group comparisons were performed using linear mixed-effects models to account for the non-independence of amblyopic and fellow eyes.ResultsA total of 130 individuals were included in the study: 65 patients and 65 control subjects. The study included 65 amblyopic eyes (Group 1), 65 clinically healthy fellow eyes from the same patients (Group 2), and 65 healthy control eyes (Group 3). Amblyopic eyes were further divided into two groups: those with amblyopia due to anisometropia (Group 1 A) and those with amblyopia due to strabismus (Group 1B). Choroidal thickness was higher in Groups 1 and 2 compared to Group 3. OCTA values were not significantly different between the groups. Macular thickness, except for foveal macular thickness, was thicker in Groups 1 and 2 compared to Group 3. Foveal macular thickness was not significantly different between the groups. Several significant sectoral differences in GCL measurements were observed among the groups. The inner and outer sectors of the inner plexiform layer were significantly thicker in Groups 1 and 2 compared to Group 3, whereas foveal IPL thickness did not differ significantly among the main groups. In PpRNFL measurements, the temporal superior quadrant was thinner in both the amblyopic and fellow eye groups compared to the control group, whereas the nasal inferior quadrant was thicker in the amblyopic eye group than in the fellow eye and control groups. There were no differences between the groups in PpRNFL in the other quadrants. In CVI measurements, the control group had higher CVI values compared to both the amblyopic and healthy eyes.ConclusionsAmblyopia was associated with structural retinal and choroidal differences, whereas OCTA parameters did not differ significantly among the groups. Future larger-scale and longitudinal studies may better elucidate the contribution of retinal microvascular changes to the pathophysiology of amblyopia.

Background

Amblyopia is a decrease in visual acuity resulting from the inability of visual stimuli to be adequately transmitted to the cerebral cortex. This condition, which occurs during the critical developmental period of the visual system, usually happens without any structural defect in the eye. The basis of amblyopia lies in disruptions in the developmental process of neuronal connections between the retina and the cerebral cortex [1].

Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies in amblyopic eyes have shown that stimuli from amblyopic eyes reduce neural activity in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) [2, 3]. Structural changes at the LGN level have also been observed in amblyopic patients. These changes are characterized especially by a decrease in gray matter density [4].

Although amblyopia is considered a developmental disorder of the visual system, in recent years, changes in retinal structures have also been demonstrated using optical coherence tomography (OCT). The peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) is thicker in amblyopic eyes compared to the healthy peer eye [5]. Macular thickness is higher in amblyopic eyes than in the patient’s healthy eye [6]. A meta-analysis found that the fovea, macula, and peripapillary RNFL are thicker in amblyopic eyes than in the fellow eye [7].

There are also choroid studies related to this patient group [8]. Studies have shown increased choroidal thickness and decreased choroidal vascular index (CVI) in amblyopic eyes [9, 10].

OCT-Angiography (OCTA) provides non-invasive visualization of the retina’s microvascular structure. Studies in amblyopic patients have shown a decrease in retinal vascular density [11, 12].

Previous studies have generally focused on isolated retinal, choroidal, or microvascular parameters rather than evaluating these findings together, and most have included relatively small sample sizes.

In this study, we compared OCT, enhanced depth imaging-OCT (EDI-OCT), CVI, and OCTA findings between amblyopic eyes, fellow eyes, and healthy control eyes in children with anisometropic and/or strabismic amblyopia. We aimed to evaluate retinal and choroidal changes associated with amblyopia and to examine possible microvascular differences between the groups.

Methods

Our study is a controlled, cross-sectional study. It included amblyopia patients aged 5–14 years who presented to the Hitit University Ophthalmology Clinic between March and May 2025.

Our study was held in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of Ethics and received approval from the Hitit University Faculty of Medicine Research Ethics Committee on February 11, 2025, with decision number 2025-25.

Our study included 130 participants: 65 patients and 65 controls. Participants’ eyes were divided into three groups: the amblyopic eye of 65 amblyopic patients (Group 1), the non-amblyopic eye of the same 65 patients (Group 2), and the right eye of 65 age- and gender-matched healthy control patients (Group 3). Furthermore, amblyopic eyes were divided into two subgroups based on the cause of amblyopia: those with amblyopia due to anisometropia (Group 1 A) and those with amblyopia due to strabismus (Group 1B).

Patients with a difference in visual acuity between the two eyes, resulting from a refractive error difference of 2 lines on the scale, were included in the anisometropic amblyopia group.

Patients with manifest esotropia observed in the cover test, with a difference of 2 lines on the scale between the two eyes, were included in the strabismic amblyopia group.

Patients under 5 or over 14 years of age, those with a history of or current retinal disease (diabetic retinopathy, retinal vascular occlusion, degenerative myopia, retinopathy of prematurity, etc.), high myopia, high astigmatism, a history of ocular surgery, a diagnosis of glaucoma, an oncological diagnosis, uncooperative for examination or OCT imaging, systemic disease, lens, cornea, or vitreous opacity, optic nerve disease or anomaly, a history of ocular trauma, a history of or active uveitis, or nystagmus were excluded from the study. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were evaluated separately for each patient’s eye, and data from eyes meeting the inclusion criteria were included in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from the guardians of all participants. Refraction measurements of patients and the control group were determined with an autorefractometer (TOPCON KR-8900; Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) under cycloplegia. Refraction measurements were calculated as spherical equivalent (SE). Best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was determined in decimal form, and anterior segment examination was performed with slit lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus examination was performed with 1% cyclopentolate (Cycloplegin, Abdi İbrahim, Istanbul, Turkey). OCT (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) images were obtained from the dilated pupil. Axial length (AL) (mm) measurements were performed with Nidek AL-Scan (Nidek CO, Aichi, Japan) optical biometry using three consecutive measurements. For each eye, the most reliable of the three consecutive measurements was selected and included in the study as the AL measurement.

For EDI-OCT images, the Heidelberg Spectralis® OCT (with a central wavelength of approximately 840 nm and a scan rate of 40,000 A-scans per second) was used with its enhanced depth imaging (EDI) mode. A single experienced technician took measurements. Choroidal thickness (µm) was measured manually between the lower limit of the RPE and the choroid-sclera border using the caliper tool in the OCT software. Measurements were taken in three regions: the subfoveal region, 1000 μm nasal to the fovea, and 1000 μm temporal to the fovea. All choroidal measurements were taken between 9:00 AM and 12:00 PM to avoid circadian effects.

The free Fiji program (http://fiji.sc/Fiji) was used to calculate the choroidal vascular index (CVI). The total choroidal area (TCA), defined as the area between the choroid-scleral junction and the RPE, was manually marked using the polygonal tool and added to the region of interest (ROI) manager. After converting the image to 8-bit format, Niblack’s automatic thresholding tool was applied to delineate the luminal area (LA) and stromal area (SA). Then, the image was converted back to red, green, and blue (RGB) colors using the color thresholding tool. This image was added to the ROI manager. In the region-of-interest manager, TCA and SA were selected and combined using the “AND” operation. This third area was added to the ROI manager. The first and third areas in the ROI manager represented TCA and LA, respectively. The choroidal vascular index was calculated as the ratio of LA to TCA.

In each group, peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness (µm), macular thickness (µm), ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness (µm), and inner plexiform layer (IPL) thickness (µm) were determined.

Peripapillary RNFL thickness (µm) was obtained using the instrument’s automated circular scanning protocol. Scans were performed over a circular area with a 3.4 mm diameter. Image quality was evaluated, and only scans with a signal quality ≥ 20 were included in the analysis. Measurements were evaluated as the average of four quadrants (superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal) and global RNFL. All imaging was performed under constant light conditions, by the same experienced technician, and according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Total macular thickness was obtained from the thickness map section of the measured macular OCT. GCL and IPL measurements were obtained from the thickness map section after segmentation on the macular OCT image. Measurements were taken as 1- and 3-mm inner and outer circles, in accordance with ETDRS dials.

The macula, GCL (ganglion cell layer), and IPL (inner plexiform layer) were measured from a 1 mm diameter circle centered on the macula (foveal); the inner superior (IS), inner nasal (IN), inner temporal (IT), and inner inferior (II) quadrants were measured from a 3 mm diameter inner circle (parafoveal). The outer superior (OS), outer nasal (ON), outer temporal (OT), and outer inferior (OI) quadrants were measured from a 6 mm diameter outer circle (perifoveal). Measurements were taken as 1- and 3-mm inner and outer circles, in accordance with ETDRS dials.

For OCT angiography (OCTA) imaging, a multimodal Swept-Source Optical Coherence Tomography (SS-OCT) device with an OCTA module (Topcon Triton, Tokyo, Japan) was used. The device utilizes Swept-Source technology operating at 1050 nm, providing high penetration depth and high-resolution analysis of vascular structures. OCTA images were acquired from each eye using a 3 × 3 mm macular scanning protocol. Images were analyzed at the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and deep capillary plexus (DCP) levels. Vessel density (VD) data were obtained from SCP and DCP. The device’s automatic alignment and tracking features were used to minimize motion artifacts during imaging. All measurements were performed under constant light conditions, by the same technician, and according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Segmentation accuracy and image quality were reviewed before analysis, and scans with significant segmentation errors or poor image quality were excluded.

Statistical method

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as number and percentage for categorical variables. The normality of continuous variables was evaluated using visual methods and distributional characteristics, and the data were found to show approximately normal distribution. The Chi-square test was used to compare participants in the patient and control groups by sex, and the independent-samples t-test was used to compare them by age. Post hoc power analysis performed using G*Power 3.1.9.7 via the “F tests → ANOVA: Fixed effects, omnibus, one-way” module showed that the final sample size of 195 observations provided adequate statistical power exceeding 90% at α = 0.05. For the main analyses, Groups 1 A and 1B were combined into Group 1. Group comparisons among Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3 were performed using linear mixed-effects models. Participant ID was included in the model as a random intercept, and group was entered as a fixed effect. For CT, OCTA measurements, macular thickness, GCL thickness, IPL thickness, peripapillary RNFL thickness, LA, TCA, and CVI, AL, and SE were included as covariates. Pairwise comparisons were performed using a Bonferroni adjustment. In addition, Group 1 A and Group 1B were compared as an exploratory subgroup analysis using Welch’s t-test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

In this study, 65 children with amblyopia and 65 healthy control subjects were included. Among the amblyopic patients, 52 eyes were classified as anisometropic amblyopia (Group 1 A) and 13 eyes as esotropic/strabismic amblyopia (Group 1B). Among the 52 amblyopic patients with anisometropic vision, 45 (86.54%) were hypermetropic anisometropic, while 7 (13.46%) were myopic anisometropic. Notably, our study group did not include any patients with anisometropic astigmatism or high myopia. For the main analyses, Groups 1 A and 1B were combined into Group 1, representing all amblyopic eyes. Group 2 comprised the clinically healthy fellow eyes of the same amblyopic patients, whereas Group 3 comprised healthy control eyes. In the patient group, 49.2% of participants were male, and 50.8% were female, whereas in the control group, 47.7% were male and 52.3% were female. The mean age was 8.78 ± 2.49 years in the patient group and 9.45 ± 2.30 years in the control group. No significant differences were observed between the patient and control groups in terms of sex distribution or age (p > 0.05).

The mean visual acuity was 0.60 ± 0.18 decimal in Group 1 A, 0.67 ± 0.12 decimal in Group 1B, 0.61 ± 0.17 decimal in Group 1, 1.00 ± 0.00 decimal in Group 2, and 1.00 ± 0.00 decimal in Group 3. The mean AL was 22.03 ± 1.03 mm in Group 1 A, 21.69 ± 0.98 mm in Group 1B, 21.96 ± 1.02 mm in Group 1, 22.47 ± 1.01 mm in Group 2, and 23.04 ± 0.90 mm in Group 3. The mean SE was 2.61 ± 2.50 Diopter (D) in Group 1 A, 5.33 ± 2.66 D in Group 1B, 3.15 ± 2.65 D in Group 1, 2.08 ± 2.50 D in Group 2, and − 0.35 ± 1.42 D in Group 3. In the exploratory subgroup analysis, visual acuity and AL did not differ significantly between Group 1 A and Group 1B (p > 0.05), whereas SE was significantly higher in Group 1B than in Group 1 A (p < 0.05). In the main linear mixed-effects model, visual acuity, AL, and SE were significantly different among Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3. Visual acuity was higher in Groups 2 and 3 than in Group 1, AL was higher in Group 3 than in Groups 1 and 2, and SE was higher in Groups 1 and 2 than in Group 3 (Table 1).

Discussion

In this study, choroidal thickness was greater in both amblyopic and fellow eyes than in control eyes, whereas OCTA vessel density measurements did not differ significantly among the groups. Macular thickness was higher in both amblyopic and fellow eyes in most quadrants, although foveal macular thickness was similar among the groups. Sectoral differences were observed in GCL, IPL, and PpRNFL measurements. In addition, IPL thickness was greater in both the amblyopic and fellow eyes than in the control eyes, while CVI values were lower in the amblyopic and fellow eye groups.

In amblyopic eyes, RNFL thickness did not differ from that in fellow and control eyes [9, 13–15]. Our study also supports this, showing no difference between groups in most RNFL quadrants and in total values. RNFL was thin in the temporal superior quadrant in both amblyopic and fellow eyes, and thick in the nasal inferior quadrant in amblyopic eyes.

Studies have also found the RNFL to be thicker in amblyopic eyes than in control eyes. It has been suggested that amblyopia may affect postnatal retinal maturation. It has been emphasized that appropriate stimulation is required to reduce ganglion cell and axon numbers in the optic nerve during the normal postnatal process [5]. In our study, global RNFL thickness did not differ significantly between the groups, although some sectoral differences were observed.

Macular thickness is a critical parameter for understanding the pathophysiology of visual developmental disorders such as amblyopia, providing information about the functional state of the retina. In the meta-analysis by Li et al., macular thickness was higher in amblyopic eyes than in fellow or control eyes [7]. Some publications reported high macular thickness in amblyopic eyes [6, 9]. In our study, the macula of Groups 1 and 2 was thicker than that of Group 3. However, in our study, foveal macular thickness was not different between the groups. Some studies do not find that foveal thickness differs between amblyopic and fellow or control eyes [9, 16]. In a study showing significant foveal thickening in amblyopic eyes, it was argued that blurred vision in amblyopia impairs macular maturation and displaces Henle’s fibers from the fovea [17]. The macular thickness findings of our study were generally consistent with those reported in previous studies.

Ganglion cell complex (ganglion cell layer, nerve fiber layer, and inner plexiform layer) (GCC) measurement is important in the diagnosis and monitoring of neurodegenerative diseases [18]. Although some studies found the GCC to be no different in amblyopic eyes than in control or fellow eyes [9, 13, 18], others found it thicker in amblyopic eyes than in the control group [15]. In a study evaluating GCL + IPL together, the complex was thinner in amblyopic eyes than in the fellow eye [19]. Some studies do not find this complex difference in amblyopic eyes [20].

In our study, sectoral analysis showed lower GCL values in the IS, IT, and II quadrants of amblyopic eyes, whereas higher values were observed in the OS, OT, and ON quadrants of fellow eyes. GCL thinning in the inner quadrants of amblyopic eyes and thickening in the outer quadrants of fellow eyes may indicate regional retinal adaptation between eyes. The underlying mechanism is unclear and cannot be established. In addition, IPL thickness was greater in all quadrants of both amblyopic and fellow eyes compared with control eyes. In animal experiments, when visual deprivation was created by monoocular lid suturing, a decrease in the number of bipolar synapses in the IPL and an increase in amacrine connections were observed [21]. In another animal experiment, a decrease in ganglion cell volume and thinning of the IPL were shown in eyes exposed to darkness [22]. The differences between our findings and some previous reports may be related to the separate assessment of retinal inner layers rather than to the evaluation of the ganglion cell complex as a single unit. A notable finding of our study was the increased IPL thickness observed in both amblyopic and fellow eyes compared with control eyes. Tuğcu et al. also reported increased GCC thickness in amblyopic patients, and our findings may support their results [15]. Nishikawa et al. compared GCL and IPL as separate retinal layers in amblyopic and fellow eyes and reported increased foveal GCL thickness in amblyopic eyes, whereas parafoveal GCL, parafoveal IPL, and foveal IPL measurements did not differ significantly between the two eyes. The absence of marked overall GCL and IPL differences between amblyopic and fellow eyes in their study is partly consistent with our findings; however, their study did not include a healthy control group [23]. Although not definitive, increased IPL thickness in amblyopic eyes may suggest alterations in cellular connections within this retinal layer. Further tissue-level and prospective clinical studies are needed to better clarify this issue.

In the literature, CT is thicker in amblyopic eyes than in fellow or control eyes [9, 24, 25]. Nishi et al. discussed in their article whether the thick choroid in amblyopic eyes was due to refractive error-dependent defocus or originated from the amblyopic eye itself. Ultimately, they concluded that CT plays a role in normal eye development and that choroidal thickness in amblyopic eyes limits eye growth [24]. However, our findings were not fully consistent with this interpretation. In our study, there was no difference in CT between the amblyopic and fellow eyes. Huang et al. suggested that in anisometropic amblyopia, functional deficiency occurs not only in the amblyopic eye but also in the fellow eye of the same patient. While some studies found only subfoveal CT significantly higher in amblyopic eyes [26], others found no difference in CT [8]. Differences in AL and SE may cause the variability in results between studies. After adjustment for AL, Hansen et al. reported increased choroidal thickness in amblyopic eyes, whereas foveal retinal thickness did not differ significantly between the groups [27]. Hansen et al.‘s results are similar to ours [27]. Overall, our choroidal thickness findings were consistent with those reported in previous studies.

In addition, in our study, the macular and choroidal thicknesses were thicker in both the amblyopic and fellow groups compared to the control group. When we evaluated macular and choroidal thickness together in our study, choroidal thickening may have occurred in this region to support the thick macula in amblyopic eyes. Xu et al. also mentioned this interpretation in their study [25]. To reach this interpretation, it is also important to evaluate choroidal vascularization.

In the study by Baek et al., both CT and choroidal vascularity were higher in amblyopic eyes than in fellow or control eyes. Baek et al. interpreted the increase in choroidal vascularity as possibly related to the vascular needs of the thickened macula [28]. Another interpretation is the accommodation response of the nonvascular smooth muscle that forms the choroidal stroma. It is suggested that the impaired accommodation response in amblyopic eyes results in a thinned stromal layer and a relatively enlarged vascular layer [29–31]. However, these interpretations do not align with our study. In our study, although CT was high in both amblyopic and fellow eyes, CVI was low. Our study supports the finding that the choroidal stromal area is increased in both amblyopic and fellow eyes. Many studies in the literature have shown that choroidal vascularization is increased in amblyopic eyes [30–32]. There are also studies showing a decrease in CVI in adult hyperopic amblyopia [33] and in pediatric amblyopia [10, 34]. In the adult hyperopic amblyopia group, lower CVI values were attributed to completed emmetropization and a relative increase in choroidal stromal area compared with the luminal area [33]. Another study reported lower CVI values in pediatric patients with anisometropic amblyopia, findings similar to ours [34]. In our predominantly hyperopic anisometropic pediatric population, CVI values were lower in both amblyopic and fellow eyes. Although our findings differ from most previous studies investigating CVI in amblyopia, they are consistent with studies reporting reduced CVI in amblyopic eyes [33, 34]. Our study emphasizes that the increase in choroidal thickness is not parallel to the dilation of vascular structures, but rather that stromal components show a greater increase.

In their OCTA studies, Yılmaz Çınar et al. found that SCP VD and DCP VD were lower in the fovea of amblyopic eyes, but did not find a difference in the foveal avascular zone (FAZ) between groups. Outside the fovea, SCP VD was measured lower in amblyopic eyes [35]. Simsek et al. evaluated OCTA findings across different amblyopia subgroups and observed lower foveal and parafoveal vessel densities in both SCP and DCP measurements in anisometropic, strabismic, and deprivation amblyopia [36]. Chen et al. reported a decrease in SCP VD in amblyopic eyes in analyses performed after AL adjustment [37]. In a meta-analysis by Gao et al., they found that parafoveal VD of SCP and DCP were lower in amblyopic eyes than in control eyes. They did not find a significant change in FAZ [38]. Other studies have also emphasized that VD of SCP and DCP are lower in amblyopic eyes, but there is no significant difference in FAZ [39–41]. In studies, low VD in amblyopic eyes has been interpreted as a need for less nutritional support to receive the retinal arterial blood supply in this region [12, 40]. We did not evaluate FAZ in our study. When evaluating VD of SCP and DCP between groups, we did not find any significant difference. Compared with other studies, our OCTA device differs, and our patient number is higher [12, 35, 39–42]. Some studies performed analysis after refractive error and AL adjustment and found significantly lower VD of SCP and DCP in amblyopic eyes [11, 43]. Pujari et al., using the same OCTA device as in our study, did not find a significant difference in SCP vessel density between strabismic amblyopic eyes and fellow eyes [44]. Araki et al. and Demirayak et al. found no differences in VD of SCP and DCP in the amblyopia children group [45, 46]. Demirayak et al. reported a significant reduction in DCP vessel density in adults with amblyopia [47], whereas they did not observe a similar decrease in pediatric amblyopia [46]. They suggested that reduced vessel density in adult amblyopic eyes may be related to long-term underuse of vision [47]. In our study, no significant differences were detected in SCP or DCP vessel density measurements in pediatric amblyopia. Although this finding has been reported less frequently in the literature, our results were consistent with those of these previous studies [44–46]. Our findings suggest that retinal microvascular alterations may not be consistently present across all amblyopia subgroups.

The limitations of our study include its cross-sectional design and the inability to evaluate different treatment stages in amblyopia patients. Another limitation was the relatively small number of patients in the strabismic amblyopia subgroup; therefore, this subgroup was evaluated only exploratorily and was not included in the main comparative analyses. In addition, CVI measurements involve a manual component, and repeatability analyses were not performed in the present study. Although pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, caution is still warranted when interpreting sector-specific OCT and OCTA findings, as evaluating numerous parameters may increase the risk of false-positive results. Another limitation is that ocular magnification correction methods based on axial length were not applied to OCT and OCTA measurements. Although axial length was included as a covariate in the statistical analyses, residual magnification-related effects cannot be completely excluded.

The strengths of our study include the relatively large sample size, the use of age- and sex-matched groups, and the analysis with adjustment for refractive error and axial length.

Conclusions

In conclusion, pediatric amblyopia was associated with structural retinal and choroidal changes in both amblyopic and fellow eyes. Choroidal and macular thickness values were higher, whereas CVI values were lower, suggesting that increased choroidal thickness may be mainly related to stromal rather than vascular changes. OCTA vessel density measurements did not differ significantly between the groups. These findings indicate that structural changes may be more prominent than retinal microvascular alterations in pediatric amblyopia. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these findings.
