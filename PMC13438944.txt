Cardiac autonomic dysfunction is associated with advanced stage, fast progression and poor survival in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Zehui Li, Jingjing Fan, Zhenxiang Gong, Jiahui Tang, Yuan Yang, Fengfei Ding, Li Ba, Mao Liu, Min Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the association of cardiac autonomic dysfunction with clinical staging and disease progression rate upon diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and its impact on survival.Methods24-hour Holter was performed in 95 ALS patients at diagnosis and 39 controls. Patients were grouped by King’s Clinical Staging and progression rate (ΔFS, median cut-off 0.67). Heart rate variability parameters were compared across groups. A survival-based cut-point optimization approach was used to determine the cut-off value of standard deviation of all normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN). Kaplan-Meier estimator and multivariable Cox regression analysis were used to evaluate of the effect of SDNN on survival during a median follow-up of 21.5 months.ResultsΔFS in ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 upon diagnosis was faster (P=0.007) compared to that of those diagnosed in earlier stages, featuring a higher incidence of respiratory (P<0.001) and bulbar symptoms (P<0.001). In addition, ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 exhibited elevated HR-min (P=0.007) and decreased RR intervals (RRI) (P=0.032), SDNN (P=0.004), and standard deviation of the averages of NN intervals in all 5-min segments (SDANN) values (P=0.020) compared to controls. Patients in the ΔFS-fast group had a higher HR-min than those in the ΔFS-slow group (P=0.013) and controls (P=0.003) and lower SDNN (P=0.018) than controls. Both Kaplan-Meier estimator (P=0.03) and multivariable Cox regression analysis (P=0.019) showed that lower SDNN (cut-off value: 111 ms) was associated with poor survival in ALS patients.ConclusionUpon diagnosis, ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 and with faster disease progression demonstrated significantly diminished cardiac autonomic control. Lower SDNN was associated with poor survival in ALS.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, autonomic nervous system, heart rate variability, progression rate, survival

1. Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), traditionally recognized as a disease affecting the motor neurons, is increasingly found to have non-motor manifestations, including cognitive, behavioral, sensory and autonomic dysfunction.1–3 In particular, various studies have highlighted the involvement of different branches of the autonomic nervous system.

In term of the sympathetic involvement, one study found Lewy bodies in the intermediolateral column (IML) of the brainstem and thoracic spinal cord in ALS patients, suggesting the involvement of preganglionic sympathetic neurons. 4 Other studies found reduced muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA) activation during head-up tilt, lower body negative pressure (LBNP) and cold pressor test (CPT).5–9 One study found impaired sympathetic skin responses in ALS patients, attributing this to degeneration of postganglionic sympathetic fibers. 10 While one study using Metaiodobenzylguanidine-single photon emission computed tomography identified cardiac sympathetic denervation in early-stage ALS, 11 another study found that hyperactivity of cardiac sympathetic activity was linked to poor survival. 12 By measuring QTc interval and dispersion, one study also found reduced sympathetic activity in advanced ALS. 13

In terms of the parasympathetic and baroreflex dysfunction, one study showed ALS patients consistently had elevated resting HR compared to controls, 14 though resting BP remained unchanged. 14 While one study demonstrated impaired E/I ratio during deep breathing and Valsalva ratio in ALS patients, 15 others found no significant alterations. 16 Previous studies have shown impaired cardiovagal response during baroreflex activation in the early-stage of ALS with preserved sympathetic vasomotor control 17 and intact afferent chemoreflex function. 18 Additionally, questionnaire-based assessments have revealed frequent parasympathetic symptoms in ALS patients, such as urge urinary incontinence (73%) and constipation (46%).19–22

Importantly, heart rate variability (HRV), a non-invasive marker of autonomic modulation, 23 has provided additional insights into cardiac autonomic dysfunction in ALS. Short-term HRV analyses revealed reduced parasympathetic tone and sympathetic predominance at rest,11,15,24–26 corroborated by long-term Holter monitoring showing diminished global autonomic modulation. 27 Despite these findings, the relationship between autonomic dysfunction and clinical staging of ALS is unknown. Current disease severity or staging tools, such as the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) score, 28 may not provide adequate granularity for assessing associations with autonomic dysfunction, whereas the validated King’s Clinical Staging system, 29 a robust tool for tracking disease progression, has not been systematically evaluated in autonomic studies. 19

Prognostic factors in ALS include age, bulbar onset, significant weight loss, faster progression rate at the time of diagnosis, and comorbid frontotemporal dementia (FTD).30–32 Emerging evidence suggests that autonomic symptoms, such as urinary dysfunction, may independently predict survival. 19 Reduced HRV is known to be associated with increased mortality in other conditions.23,33–35 One study showed that reduced HRV parameters including the standard deviation of total normal RR intervals (SDNN) were significantly associated with all-cause mortality in an elderly cohort, 35 while another study reported that low SDNN indicated a poor prognosis in patients who had acute myocardial infarction. 36 In contrast, little evidence exists regarding the prognostic value of HRV in ALS patients.

Thus, the current study aims to evaluate the cardiac autonomic function via HRV in a Chinese ALS cohort grouped by King’s Clinical Staging and progression rate, and to explore the prognostic value of HRV in ALS patients.

2. Methods

2.1. Participants

A total of 95 patients with possible, probable, or definite amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) according to the revised EI Escorial criteria 37 admitted to the Department of Neurology, Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, and 39 age- and gender-matched healthy controls (HC) were recruited from November 2019 to September 2022. The datasets of 65 patients and 38 controls were used in a previous study. 38 Telephone and outpatient visits were scheduled for follow-up. Death was used as the study endpoint. Exclusion criteria for all participants include 1) history of diseases (Frontotemporal dementia, cardiac diseases including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, cerebrovascular diseases such as hypertension, stroke, transient ischemia attack, other diseases including diabetes, thyroid diseases, malignancies, anxiety and depression) or medication use (β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, anxiolytic or anti-depressive medications, antipsychotics) potentially affecting the autonomic nervous system; 2) pregnancy; 3) use of alcohol, coffee, tea in the 3 days preceding Holter monitoring; 4) use of recreational substances; 5) self-reported sleep symptoms. None of the patients had been started on long-term medical therapy, such as Riluzole or Edaravone until Holter monitoring was completed. Our study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJ-IRB20201222). The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All subjects or their legal representatives provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

2.2. Clinical data acquisition

Clinical data, including age, gender, disease duration (the time from symptom onset to diagnosis), Body Mass Index (BMI), and body regions affected by disease (brainstem/spinal cord), were collected from medical records. The severity of physical disability and bulbar involvement were evaluated by the ALS Functional Rating Scale-Revised (ALSFRS-R) score (total score 48), with lower scores indicating higher disability. 39 Disease progression rate (ΔFS) was calculated using the formula (48-ALSFRS-R)/disease duration with a result of a median value of 0.67, which is the same as the original study from Kimura et al. 32 Patients with ΔFS below 0.67 were categorized into the slow-progression group “ΔFS-slow”, while those with ΔFS equal to or higher than 0.67 were classified into the fast-progression group “ΔFS-fast”. 32

2.3. Clinical staging of disease

King’s Clinical Staging criteria 40 is defined as the following: stage 1, the onset of symptoms (with involvement of the first region); stage 2A, diagnosis; stage 2B, involvement of the second region; stage 3, involvement of the third region; stage 4A is reached when weight loss is 10% or more, and gastrostomy is recommended; stage 4B is reached when positive respiratory symptoms are present and FVC percentage is less than 80% or in case of FVC percentage <50%; stage 5, death or mechanical ventilation. 29 In this study, stages 2A and 2B were combined into stage 2, while stages 4A and 4B were combined into stage 4.

2.4. Holter monitoring

24-hour Holter (DMS300-4AL, USA) was installed for each participant with a sample rate of 4096 Hz. During the monitoring, participants could perform routine daily activities, including showering or bathing. The analysis of HRV parameters followed the international Task Force recommendations. 41 Beat-to-beat heart rate data were imported into the Holter Analysis Software. Ectopic beats that exceeded 5% of total heartbeats were removed manually. HRV parameters of time and frequency domains were then calculated from the full recording as well as separately from the daytime and nighttime recordings. Nighttime segments were analyzed with fixed window lengths to reduce behavioral confounding. Fast Fourier transformation was applied for frequency analysis. HRV parameters were compared between the groups of the 24-hour, daytime, and nighttime periods. None of the participants reported insomnia during recording at night. An overview of the description and implication of each HRV parameter is presented in Table S1. 38

2.5. Statistical methods

Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normal distribution of data. Normally distributed data were presented as “mean ± standard deviation” while non-normally distributed data were presented as median, minimum, and maximum, namely “median (min-max)”. Parameters with normal distribution were compared between health controls and ALS patients in the four stages as well as those categorized into the group “ΔFS-slow” and the group “ΔFS-fast” using one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA); for non-normally distributed data, Kruskal-Wallis test was used with Bonferroni test for post-hoc comparison. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square test, chi-square correction test, or Fisher’s test as statistically appropriate. A survival-based cut-point optimization approach was used to determine the optimal SDNN cut-off value for additional survival analysis. Kaplan-Meier estimator was used to evaluate the effect of SDNN grouping on the survival. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the effect of SDNN grouping on the survival by adjusting for other clinical parameters. The Shoenfeld residuals test was used to assess the proportional hazards assumption for each independent variable to ensure the adequacy of the Cox regression model. SPSS 25.0 software was used for statistical analysis with the significance set as P < 0.05. GraphPad 8.3.0 and Adobe Illustrator 2024 were used for graph production.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic data of ALS patients in four stages

ALS patients and HC had similar age and gender distribution. The median disease duration was 11 months, and the median ALSFRS-R score was 41. 17, 43, 22, and 13 ALS patients were in stage 1, 2, 3, and 4 according to King’s Clinical Staging. ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 at diagnosis had a faster progression rate compared to those in earlier stages (2.33 [0.33-5.40], P=0.002) (Table S2). No differences in disease duration and BMI existed among the four ALS groups. The ratio of bulbar involvement (P<0.001) and respiratory involvement (P<0.001) differed between the four groups, positive in 100% and 92.3% of patients in King’s stage 4, respectively (Table 1, Table S3, Table S4). 72 of the 95 ALS patients established outpatient follow-up, among which 32 were alive, 22 died, and 18 lost follow-ups by February 2024. The median duration of follow-up was 21.5 months.

3.2. Cardiac autonomic parameters of ALS patients in four stages

During the 24-hour recording, HR-min was higher in ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 (57 [46-81] bpm) than HC (49 [34-58] bpm), P=0.007; Table 2, Table S5, Figure 1(a)). RRI, the reciprocal of HR, was lower in those in King’s Stage 4 (762.4 [96-999.7] ms) compared to HC (852.5 [646.4-1032.9] ms, P=0.032; Table 2, Table S5, Figure 1(b)). Among time-domain parameters, SDNN (HC:130 [81-181] ms; King’s Stage 4: 96 [0-149] ms, P=0.004) and standard deviation of the averages of NN intervals in all 5-min segments (SDANN) (HC: 118 [71-177] ms; King’s Stage 4: 90 [31-146] ms, P=0.020) were decreased in ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 than HC (Table 2, Table S5, Figure 1(c)–(d)). There were no significant differences among the five groups in HRV parameters of the frequency domain. Detailed data are shown in Table 2 and Figure 1.

3.5. Effects of SDNN on survival in patients with ALS

A survival-based cut-point optimization approach identified 111ms as the optimal prognostic cut-off value of SDNN. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed that reduced SDNN (≤111 ms) was associated with poor survival in ALS patients (P=0.030, Figure 3). Multivariable Cox regression showed lower SDNN was associated with poor survival in ALS patients (HR=3.103, 95% CI [1.204, 7.996], P=0.019, Table 3) by adjusting for age, BMI, ALSFRS-R (P=0.041), and disease duration (P=0.039). In the best-case scenario of sensitivity analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) for SDNN remained significantly elevated (3.385 [1.304, 8.786], P=0.012) which was consistent with our primary analysis, while in the worst-case scenario, the HR was not significant (1.703 [0.770, 3.367], P=0.188) (Table S9).

4. Discussion

4.1. Altered cardiac autonomic control in King’s Stage 4

ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 at diagnosis exhibited elevated HR-min, reduced RRI, and decreased SDNN and SDANN compared to HC, while heart rate variability parameters in the earlier clinical stages remained similar to HC. Previous long-term HRV analysis found that time-domain parameters, including SDNN, SDANN, mean of the standard deviations of all the NN intervals for each 5 min segment of a 24 h HRV recording (SDNN Index), square root of the mean of the sum of squares of differences between adjacent NN intervals (RMSSD), and percentage difference between adjacent NN intervals that are greater than 50 ms (pNN50), and frequency-domain parameters, including high-frequency (HF) power, decreased in ALS patients. 42 In contrast, short-term HRV analysis showed increased LF/HF (ratio of low-frequency power to high-frequency power), 27 and reduced pNN50, total power, HF power, and HFnu (HF power in normalized units) in ALS patients.15,17,43 However, most of the prior studies did not provide information on the clinical staging or progression rate, which could potentially lead to an “all” 42 or “none” 44 conclusion. While increased HR or reduced RRI could be from sub-clinically diminished parasympathetic cardiac or increased sympathetic cardiac modulation, decreased SDNN and SDANN suggest reduced overall cardiac modulations. 23 Notably, our ALS patients in King’s Stage 4 had the fastest progression rate (mean value 2.33) compared to those from earlier stages, as the patients from the four stages had similar duration from symptom onset to diagnosis. In contrast, SDNN calculated from separate daytime and nighttime segments did not differ between the groups. Daytime VLF was significantly lower in patients in King’s stage 4 compared to HC, although its clinical significance is unclear. 23 It is possible that HRV alterations in our ALS cohort are subtle and thus more detectable over a full day period. While two previous studies showed that SDNN 19 and the coefficient of variation of HR during sleep 45 longitudinally decreased during the natural course of ALS, our findings suggest subclinical cardiac autonomic dysfunction is more likely to occur when ALS patient progress into King’s Stage 4 or have a higher progression rate.

4.2. King’s Stage 4 and faster progression rate: Possible mechanisms of cardiac autonomic dysfunction

As defined by the King’s clinical staging, our patients in stage 4 had prominent bulbar (100%) or respiratory symptoms (92.3%). 40 Thus, the significant reduction in HRV in King’s Stage 4 suggests a possible role of bulbar or respiratory involvement in HRV changes. ALS patients with bulbar involvement had higher LF power in normalized units (LFnu) and lower HFnu, which suggested a more marked autonomic imbalance mainly involving the parasympathetic function. 15 Previous studies showed Trans-activation response DNA protein 43 depositions in nucleus ambiguus, dorsal vagus nucleus, and brainstem reticular structure,4,46 which are the vital structures of the baroreflex pathway. 46 Thus, involvement of these structures could potentially alter cardiac autonomic processing in the brainstem.25,42,47 In addition, involvement of respiratory muscles 48 in ALS patients will lead to insufficient oxygen inhalation and carbon dioxide excretion,18,48 which could further result in an abnormal interaction between the baroreflex and chemoreflex, 18 increased sympathetic activity, 18 reduced vagal modulation, or a combination of these responses. 49 As the present study design did not incorporate baroreflex sensitivity, the proposed pathophysiological mechanisms linking the baroreflex to HRV remain speculative and warrant further validation in future studies.

Various factors have been linked with faster disease progression in ALS, including muscle/fat mass,50,51 respiratory status (such as hypoventilation and hypercapnia), 52 nutritional status, metabolic profile, anxiety and depression, immune profile (inflammatory state), and genetic variabilities.23,53–55 Of note, anxiety and depression,56,57 genetic, immune, and environmental factors58,59 could affect cardiac autonomic function. Thus, it is possible that various factors related to faster disease progression in ALS could also contribute to decreased SDNN.

4.3. Association between SDNN and survival in ALS

Previous survival studies showed that various factors including age, disease progression rate, bulbar onset, behavioral symptoms, and autonomic symptoms, could help predict survival in ALS patients.19,32,60–65 Our results showed that lower ALSFRS-R score and shorter disease duration was associated with worse prognosis, consistent with previous studies.64–67 We identified an optimal prognostic SDNN cut-off value of 111 ms, and ALS patients with lower SDNN (≤111 ms) had higher mortality than those with higher SDNN (>111 ms) after adjusting for clinical covariates. This is consistent with one previous study showing that in patients after acute myocardial infarction, 36 lower SDNN (less than 50 ms) was associated with increased mortality than higher SDNN (more than 100 ms). We also performed sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the survival results in the ALS cohort. Under the “worst-case” scenario, i.e., if dropouts predominantly represent individuals with rapid deterioration and early death, the observed association might be overestimated. Nevertheless, the worst-case scenario is an extreme and possibly overly pessimistic assumption, as it presumes that all 25% of dropouts experienced the endpoint immediately, a scenario unlikely in the clinical context. In contrast, the “best-case” scenario (assuming all dropouts survived to the end of the study), though also extreme, may be closer to how dropouts are handled as censored cases in routine practice, and under this assumption the main conclusion remained robust. Taken together, our primary multivariable Cox results provide important evidence that lower SDNN (≤111 ms) was a prognostic factor of mortality, although the precision of the effect size was to some extent affected by loss of follow-up. Further large-scale studies are warranted to validate these findings.

Our study also provides novel insights into the prognosis and management of ALS. HRV could be a simple, non-invasive tool for risk assessment and prognosis upon ALS diagnosis. We found that reduced HRV was most notable in patients in King’s Stage 4 and those with rapid progression, suggesting that HRV measurement could help clinicians identify the patients with a more aggressive clinical trajectory and thus those who need closer follow-up. This could potentially enable timely intensification of multidisciplinary care, including cardiopulmonary support and palliative planning, even before severe functional deterioration is clinically evident.

4.4. Limitations

Our study has several limitations. The sample size of this study is relatively small with patients recruited from a single Chinese tertiary referral center. Thus, selection bias cannot be eliminated, and generalization of the findings to other racial populations needs caution. Additionally, we could not measure HRV data longitudinally due to resource limitations and thus could not analyze the dynamic changes of HRV during the natural course of disease, which remains a challenging reality. One fourth of the patients lost follow-up, which could bias the results of survival analysis and interpretation. Additional confounding factors that can affect HRV, such as sleep, respiratory disorders, nutrition, and inflammation state, were not controlled in this study. Future studies should consider incorporating additional measures including polysomnography, FVC, nocturnal SpO2, capnography, mood evaluation scales, quantitative nutritional assessment, and inflammatory biomarkers for analysis. Finally, the optimal SDNN cut-off value identified in this study may not be generalizable to all ALS patients, and further validation through large-scale follow-up studies is warranted.

4.5. Conclusions

Reduced heart rate variability more often occurred in ALS patients who were in King’s Stage 4 and had a faster disease progression upon initial diagnosis. In ALS patients, baseline SDNN≤111 ms was associated with poor survival during a 21.5-month follow-up. This suggests that SDNN has the potential to serve as a prognostic tool in the care of ALS patients.
