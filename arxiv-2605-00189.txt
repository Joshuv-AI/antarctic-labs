Local Asymptotic Patterns for Viscous Approximations of Conservation Laws
Alberto Bressan, Laura Caravenna, Wen Shen

Abstract
Solutions to hyperbolic conservation laws can be approximated in many different ways: by vanishing viscosity, relaxations, discrete or semi-discrete numerical schemes, approximation with a nonlocal flux, etc … \ldots For some of these methods, general 𝐋 1 {\bf L}^{1} convergence results are available. Aim of this paper is to understand the local behavior of these approximations, in a neighborhood of point where the hyperbolic solution has a singularity. Specifically: a point along a shock, or where two shocks interact, or where a new shock is formed. Given a sequence of ϵ \epsilon -approximate solutions, a general expectation is that, by a suitable local rescaling of coordinates, as ϵ → 0 \epsilon\to 0 a well defined limit is obtained. This corresponds to a specific “eternal solution" (globally defined both in space and in time) to the approximating equation. Precise results this direction are here given, in the case of vanishing viscosity.



Key Words:
Hyperbolic conservation laws, generic singularity, local asymptotic approximation.

Introduction

The emergence of singularities in solutions to PDEs has been a topic of extensive study.
For various classes of nonlinear hyperbolic equations, solutions can be uniquely extended in time
beyond singularity formation. In such cases, it is of interest to identify “structurally stable singularities",
which arise from an open set of initial data (in a suitable topology), and are thus more likely to be
observed in a physical context. For some particular classes of PDEs, such as scalar conservation laws,
variational wave equations, or Camassa-Holm equations, a classification of generic
singularities can be found in
[
22
]
,
[
6
,
10
]
and
[
28
]
, respectively.
A similar approach is of interest also in connection with multidimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equations.

Typically, this classification corresponds to a limit of local rescalings.
Namely, if a solution
u(t,x)
has a singularity at a point
(\tau,\xi)
,
one can consider
the rescaled functions

u^{\delta}(t,x)~=~u\bigl(\tau+\delta t,\xi+\delta x\bigr),

(1.1)

or, depending on the type of singularity,

u^{\delta}(t,x)~=~\delta^{-\gamma}\Big[u\bigl(\tau+\delta^{\alpha}t,\,\xi+\delta^{\beta}x\bigr)-u(\tau,\xi)\Big],

(1.2)

for suitable exponents
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.
In a favorable case, taking the limit as
\delta\to 0
, one obtains a self-similar function, depending on a finite number of parameters, which describes the asymptotic behavior of the solution in a neighborhood of the singular point
(\tau,\xi)
.

Purpose of the present paper is to point out that, in many cases, this classification of generic singularities
can be carried out on a further level, involving various classes of approximate solutions.

As an example,
consider a scalar conservation law with smooth, convex flux

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}~=~0,\qquad\qquad t\in[0,T].

(1.3)

For a generic initial data

u(0,x)~=~\bar{u}(x),

(1.4)

with
\bar{u}
in the intersection of countably many open dense subsets of
{\cal C}^{3}({\mathbb{R}})
,
it is well known
[
22
,
31
]
that the unique entropy weak solution is piecewise smooth, with finitely many shock curves.
Structurally stable singular points can be of three types (see Fig.
1
).

(I)

points along a shock curve,

(II)

points where two shock curves merge,

(III)

points where a new shock is formed.

Various approximations of (
1.3
) have been extensively studied in the literature.
In particular:

(i)

Vanishing viscosity approximations
[
17
,
23
,
25
]
, such as

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}~=~\varepsilon\bigl(b(u)u_{x}\bigr)_{x}\,.

(1.5)

(ii)

Relaxation approximations
[

,

,

,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}v_{t}+v_{x}&=\,\varepsilon^{-1}\bigl(w-g(v)\bigr),\\[5.69054pt]
w_{t}&=-\varepsilon^{-1}\bigl(w-g(v)\bigr),\end{array}\right.

(1.6)

where

(iii)

Approximations with nonlocal flux. More specifically, motivated by models of traffic flow

u_{t}+F(u,v)_{x}~=~0,\qquad\qquad v(t,x)\doteq\,\int_{0}^{+\infty}\varepsilon^{-1}e^{-y/\varepsilon}\,u(t,x+y)\,dy,

(1.7)

where
F(u,u)=f(u)
.

(iv)

Various numerical approximations
[
23
,
27
]
.

Let now
u=u(t,x)
be a generic solution of (
1.3
) with
{\cal C}^{3}
initial data.
In addition, let
u_{\varepsilon}
be a family of approximations of one of the types (i)–(iv),
such that the initial data
u_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
converge to
u(0,\cdot)
in
{\cal C}^{3}
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

If
(\tau,\xi)
is a point where
u
has a structurally stable singularity, as already remarked
the rescaled functions in (

1.2

\delta\to 0
.
In this same setting we conjecture that, by suitably choosing
points
(\tau_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon})\to(\tau,\xi)
, for suitable exponents
\alpha,\beta,\gamma

(not necessarily the same as in (
1.2
)), the rescaled

U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)~\doteq~u_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\tau+\varepsilon^{\alpha}t,~\xi+\varepsilon^{\beta}x\bigr),

(1.8)

or, depending on the type of singularity,

U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)~\doteq~\varepsilon^{-\gamma}\Big[u_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\tau_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\alpha}t,\,\xi_{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon^{\beta}x\bigr)-u(\tau,\xi)\Big],

(1.9)

converge to a unique limit

, uniformly on compact sets. This limit is defined for all
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. It provides an “eternal

In the remainder of this paper we shall give a proof of the above conjecture in the basic case of
viscous approximations to a scalar conservation law with strictly convex flux:

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}~=~\varepsilon\,u_{xx}\,.

(1.10)

The following assumptions will be used.

(A1)

The flux function

0~<~c_{1}~\leq~f^{\prime\prime}(u)~\leq~c_{2}\qquad\qquad\hbox{for all}~u\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(1.11)

(A2)

The initial data satisfies
\bar{u}\in\mathbf{L}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
.

We recall that, for a generic initial data
\bar{u}\in{\cal U}\subset{\cal C}^{3}({\mathbb{R}})

1.4
)
is piecewise smooth with a locally finite number of shocks

Here
{\cal U}

{\cal G}_{\delta}

For such generic solutions, singularities can only be of the above types (I)–(III).
For each type, there is a natural asymptotic rescaling that
characterizes the singularity up to leading order.

CASE 1.

If
(\tau,\xi)
is a point along a shock, we consider the rescalings

u^{\delta}(t,x)~\doteq~u\bigl(\tau+\delta t,\,\xi+\delta x\bigr).

(1.12)

As
\delta\to 0+
, the solutions
u^{\delta}
converge in
\mathbf{L}^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
to a
piecewise constant solution
U=U(t,x)
of (
1.3
)
defined for all
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and containing a single shock:

U(t,x)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{-}&\hbox{if}~~x<\lambda t,\\[2.84526pt]
u^{+}&\hbox{if}~~x>\lambda t.\end{array}\right.

(1.13)

Here

u^{\pm}\doteq u(\tau,\xi\pm),\qquad\qquad\lambda={f(u^{+})-f(u^{-})\over u^{+}-u^{-}}\,.

CASE 2.

If
(\tau,\xi)
is a point where two shocks interact, the rescaled functions
u^{\delta}
defined at (
1.12
)
now converge to a piecewise constant solution with two interacting shocks

U(t,x)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}u^{-}&\hbox{if}~~x<\lambda_{1}t,\\[2.84526pt]
u^{*}&\hbox{if}~~\lambda_{1}t<x<\lambda_{2}t,\\[2.84526pt]
u^{+}&\hbox{if}~~\lambda_{2}t<x,\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad\hbox{for}~t<0,

(1.14)

U(t,x)~=~\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}u^{-}&\hbox{if}~~x<\lambda t,\\[2.84526pt]
u^{+}&\hbox{if}~~x>\lambda t,\end{array}\right.\qquad\qquad\hbox{for}~t\geq 0.

(1.15)

Here
u^{-},u^{*},u^{+}
are the limits of left, middle and right states before the interaction, while

\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda
are the Rankine-Hugoniot speeds of the shocks,
before and after the interaction.

CASE 3.
At the point
\overline{P}=(\tau,\xi)
a new shock is formed.
Assuming that the initial data
\bar{u}
is
{\cal C}^{3}
, call

x_{0}\,\doteq\,\xi-f^{\prime}\bigl(u(\tau,\xi)\bigr)\,\tau,

the initial point of the characteristic
through
\overline{P}
, so that

\bar{u}(x_{0})~=~u(\tau,\xi),\qquad\qquad\tau\,=\,{1\over f^{\prime\prime}\bigl(\bar{u}(x_{0})\bigr)\bar{u}_{x}(x_{0})}\,.

Following
[
22
]
, we say that this singularity is
structurally stable
if

{\partial\over\partial x}f^{\prime}\bigl(\bar{u}(x)\bigr)\bigg|_{x=x_{0}}=~0,\qquad\qquad{\partial^{2}\over\partial x^{2}}f^{\prime}\bigl(\bar{u}(x)\bigr)\bigg|_{x=x_{0}}<~0.

In this case, the rescaled functions

u^{\delta}(t,x)~=~\delta^{-1}\Big[u\bigl(\tau+\delta^{2}t,\,\xi+\delta^{3}x\bigr)-u(\tau,\xi)\Big],

(1.16)

as
\delta\to 0
converge to a global solution of Burgers’ equation

u_{t}+\left({au^{2}\over 2}+bu\right)_{x}~=~0,

(1.17)

with

u(0,x)~=~-cx^{1/3}.

(1.18)

Here
a=f^{\prime\prime}(u(\tau,\xi))
,
b=f^{\prime}(u(\tau,\xi))
, while
c
can be recovered in terms of the third derivative
x_{uuu}

of the inverse
function

u\mapsto x(\tau,u)
, at the point
u=u(\tau,\xi)
.
The proof is elementary, since for
t<\tau
in a neighborhood of
(\tau,\xi)
the solution of (
1.3
)
can be explicitly constructed by characteristics.

Remark 1.1

All solutions to (
1.17
)-(
1.18
) can be obtained from the single solution where

a=c=1
and
b=0
, after an affine change of variables. Indeed, setting

u(t,x)~=~\gamma\,w(\alpha t,\,x-\beta t),\qquad\qquad\alpha=ac,\quad\beta=b,\quad\gamma=c,

a direct computation shows that
w
provides a solution to

w_{t}+\left(w^{2}\over 2\right)_{x}\,=\,0,\qquad\qquad w(0,x)~=~-x^{1/3}.

Next, we consider smooth functions which are obtained as limits of suitable asymptotic rescalings
of the viscous solutions (
1.10
).
These can be of three types.

TYPE 1:
A traveling viscous shock for the viscous equation

u_{t}+f(u)_{x}~=~u_{xx}\,,

(1.19)

with asymptotic states
u^{-},u^{+}
as
x\to\pm\infty
.
As it is well known, this has the form

U(t,x)~=~S(x-\lambda t),

(1.20)

where
S
provides a solution to

-\lambda S+f(S)~=~S^{\prime}+C,

(1.21)

\lambda~=~{f(u^{-})-f(u^{+})\over u^{-}-u^{+}}\,,\qquad\qquad C\,=\,f(u^{+})-\lambda u^{+}\,=\,f(u^{-})-\lambda u^{-}.

(1.22)

Notice that
S
is determined up to a shift. To remove this ambiguity, we
impose the additional condition

S(0)~=~{u^{-}+u^{+}\over 2}\,.

(1.23)

TYPE 2:
Two traveling viscous shocks merging together.
Let
S_{1},S_{2}
be two viscous traveling profiles for
the equation (
1.19
), connecting the states
(u^{-},u^{*})
and the states
(u^{*},u^{+})
,
respectively (see Fig.
10
). These profiles are uniquely determined up to a shift.
To remove this ambiguity we assume

S_{1}(0)\,=\,{u^{-}+u^{*}\over 2}\,,\qquad\qquad S_{2}(0)\,=\,{u^{*}+u^{+}\over 2}\,.

The corresponding Rankine-Hugoniot speeds are

\lambda_{1}~=~{f(u^{-})-f(u^{*})\over u^{-}-u^{*}}~>~{f(u^{*})-f(u^{+})\over u^{*}-u^{+}}~=~\lambda_{2}\,.

As it will be shown in Section
8
,
there exists a unique solution
W=W(t,x)
of (
1.19
),
defined for all

(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, with the following property. Choosing any intermediate speed

\lambda_{2}<\lambda<\lambda_{1}
, as
\tau\to-\infty
the profile
W(\tau,\cdot)
satisfies

\lim_{\tau\to-\infty}\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\lambda\tau}\Big|W(\tau,x)-S_{1}(x-\lambda_{1}\tau)\Big|\,dx+\int^{+\infty}_{\lambda\tau}\Big|W(\tau,x)-S_{2}(x-\lambda_{2}\tau)\Big|\,dx\right\}~=~0.

(1.24)

TYPE 3:
To describe the appropriate rescaled limit of viscous approximations, in a neighborhood where a new shock is formed, we proceed as follows.
Let
z=z(t,x)
be the solution to the inviscid Burgers’ equation

z_{t}+zz_{x}~=~0\,,\qquad\qquad z(0,x)=-x^{1/3}.

(1.25)

This is well defined for all
(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and smooth for
t<0
. Then we
define
Z^{(n)}:[-n,+\infty[\,\times{\mathbb{R}}\mapsto{\mathbb{R}}
to be the solution of
the Cauchy problem for the viscous Burgers’ equation

Z_{t}+ZZ_{x}~=~Z_{xx},\qquad\qquad Z(-n,x)\,=\,z(-n,x).

(1.26)

Finally, we take the limit

Z(t,x)~=~\lim_{n\to\infty}Z^{(n)}(t,x).

(1.27)

Details about the existence, uniqueness, and some properties of these limit solutions will be given
in Section
8
.

In the literature on hyperbolic systems of conservation laws, a general theorem on the
\mathbf{L}^{1}
convergence of
vanishing viscosity approximations was proved in
[
4
]
. The local construction of viscous approximation
by matched asymptotic expansions was achieved in

[
20
]
in a neighborhood of an isolated shock, and more recently in
recently in
[
1
,
13
]
near a point where a new shock is formed, up to the time of singularity formation.
Our present point of view is somewhat different, since we assume that some
family of viscous approximations is already given, and try to understand their local behavior near a point of generic singularity. We believe this approach can be fruitful also in connection with different kinds of approximations.

2
Variable rescaling at shock formation

Let
u=u(t,x)
be a solution to (
1.3
)-(
1.4
), under the assumptions
(A1)-(A2)
.

As long as the solution remains Lipschitz continuous, it is computed by the method of characteristics.
For every initial point
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}
, along the characteristic
t\mapsto x(t,\xi)~=~\xi+tf^{\prime}(\bar{u}(\xi))

one has

u(t,x(t,\xi))~=~\bar{u}(\xi),\qquad u_{x}(t,x(t,\xi))~=~\left[{1\over\bar{u}_{x}(\xi)}+t\,f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{u}(\xi))\right]^{-1}

(2.1)

where the second identity is obtained by integrating

u_{xt}+f^{\prime}(u)u_{xx}~=~-f^{\prime\prime}(u)u_{x}^{2}\,,

(2.2)

along a characteristic.
If
\bar{u}_{x}(\xi)<0
, we consider the blow-up time

{\cal T}(\xi)~=~\Big[-f^{\prime\prime}(\bar{u}(\xi))\cdot\bar{u}_{x}(\xi)\Big]^{-1}.

(2.3)

A new shock is formed at time
{\cal T}(\xi^{*})
along the characteristic starting at
\xi^{*}
if the map

\xi\,\mapsto\,{\cal T}(\xi)

(2.4)

has a local minimum at
\xi=\xi^{*}
.
For generic initial data
\bar{u}\in{\cal C}^{3}({\mathbb{R}})
with
\bar{u}_{x}(x)\to 0
as
x\to\pm\infty
, there will be finitely many points
\xi_{1}<\xi_{2}<\cdots<\xi_{N}
where the
map (
2.4
) attains a local minimum, with
{\cal T}(\xi_{k})\leq T
. In this case, the solution to (
1.3
)-(
1.4
)
will contain finitely many shocks within the time interval
[0,T]
.

On regions where the solution is smooth and monotonically decreasing, we can consider the inverse function

x=x(t,u)
. This satisfies the evolution equation

x_{t}(t,u)~=~f^{\prime}(u).

(2.5)

New shocks originate at points where
x_{u}=0
. Since

x_{ut}~=~f^{\prime\prime}(u),\qquad\qquad x_{u}(0)~=~{1\over\bar{u}_{x}}\,,

(2.6)

the time where
x_{u}=0
along a characteristic is again given by (
2.3
).

2.1
Variable rescaling for an inviscid solution.

To simplify our notation, we now assume that the solution
u=u(t,x)
is defined for
(t,x)\in[-1,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}
,
and a new shock forms at
(t,x)=(0,0)
. In view of Remark
1.1
, w.l.o.g. we assume that

u(0,0)\,=\,0,\qquad f(0)\,=\,f^{\prime}(0)\,=\,0,\qquad f^{\prime\prime}(0)=1,

(2.7)

f^{\prime}(u)~=~u+\psi_{1}(u)u^{2},

(2.8)

for some bounded function
\psi_{1}
.
To describe the process of shock formation, up to leading order, we perform a
family of variable rescalings. This amounts to “zooming in" at the singular point, i.e.,

t\,=\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}\tau,\qquad x\,=\,\varepsilon^{\beta}y,\qquad u\,=\,\varepsilon^{\gamma}v.

(2.9)

Inserting (
2.9
) in (
1.3
), using (
2.8
) one obtains

\varepsilon^{\gamma-\alpha}v_{\tau}+f^{\prime}(\varepsilon^{\gamma}v)\varepsilon^{\gamma-\beta}v_{y}~=~\varepsilon^{\gamma-\alpha}v_{\tau}+\Big(\varepsilon^{\gamma}v+\psi_{1}(\varepsilon^{\gamma}v)\varepsilon^{2\gamma}v^{2}\Big)\varepsilon^{\gamma-\beta}v_{y}~=~0.

(2.10)

The limit
\varepsilon\to 0
will thus be a solution to
Burgers’ equation

v_{\tau}+vv_{y}~=~0

provided that

\gamma-\alpha~=~2\gamma-\beta,\qquad\qquad\gamma\,=\,\beta-\alpha\,>\,0.

In the limit, we wish to capture a solution where

x~=~-bu^{3}+tu.

That is, the above solution should be invariant under rescaling.
This requires

\varepsilon^{\beta}y~=~-b\,\varepsilon^{3\gamma}v^{3}+\varepsilon^{\alpha+\gamma}\tau v,

hence

\beta=3\gamma=\alpha+\gamma
. The three exponents should thus satisfy the proportionality relation

\alpha:\beta:\gamma~=~2:3:1.

2.2
Rescaling of viscous solutions.

For solutions to the viscous equation (
1.10
), the choice
of the constants
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
in the rescaling (
2.9
) is dictated by two requirements:

(i)

If
u(t,x)
is a solution to Burgers’ equation with
\varepsilon
-viscosity, then
v(\tau,y)
is a solution
of the same equation with unit viscosity:

u_{t}+uu_{x}~=~\varepsilon\,u_{xx}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad v_{\tau}+vv_{y}~=~v_{yy}\,.

(2.11)

(ii)

If the map
x\mapsto u(t,x)
is monotone decreasing, and has a smooth inverse
u\mapsto x(t,u)
, then
the same holds for
v
and the map
v\mapsto y(\tau,v)
satisfies

x_{uuu}~=~y_{vvv}\,.

(2.12)

Inserting (
2.9
) in (
2.11
) one obtains

\varepsilon^{\gamma-\alpha}v_{\tau}+\varepsilon^{2\gamma-\beta}vv_{y}~=~\varepsilon^{1+\gamma-2\beta}v_{yy}\,.

Moreover, inserting (
2.9
) in (
2.12
) we obtain

x_{uuu}~=~\varepsilon^{\beta-3\gamma}y_{vvv}~=~y_{vvv}\,.

The requirements (i)-(ii) thus imply the three identities

\gamma-\alpha~=~2\gamma-\beta~=~1+\gamma-2\beta,\qquad\qquad\beta-3\gamma\,=\,0.

This system has the unique solution

\alpha\,=\,{1\over 2},\qquad\beta\,=\,{3\over 4}\,,\qquad\gamma\,=\,{1\over 4}\,.

(2.13)

This indicates that the appropriate rescaling of viscous solutions, in a neighborhood of a point where a new shock is formed, is

u^{\varepsilon}(t,x)~=~\varepsilon^{-1/4}u(\varepsilon^{1/2}t,\,\varepsilon^{3/4}x).

(2.14)

Differentiating (

Using the identities

w_{t}\,=\,w_{\tau}+w_{u}u_{t},\qquad\quad w_{x}~=~w_{u}\,u_{x}\,,\quad\qquad w_{xx}\,=\,w_{uu}u_{x}^{2}+w_{u}\,u_{xx}\,,

\begin{array}[]{rl}w_{\tau}(\tau,u)&=w_{t}-w_{u}u_{t}\\[11.38109pt]

Recalling (
3.2
), we finally obtain

w_{\tau}~=~\bigl(w-f(u)\bigr)^{2}\,w_{uu}\,.

(3.5)

From (

w_{u}~=~{w_{x}\over u_{x}}~=~{f^{\prime}(u)u_{x}-u_{xx}\over u_{x}}~=~-{u_{t}\over u_{x}}\,.

t^{\varepsilon}=0
we have

\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(u^{\varepsilon})~=~\varepsilon^{-3/4}\bar{x}(\varepsilon^{1/4}u^{\varepsilon}),\qquad\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{u}(u^{\varepsilon})~=~\varepsilon^{-1/2}\bar{x}_{u}(\varepsilon^{1/4}u^{\varepsilon}),\qquad\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{uu}(u^{\varepsilon})~=~\varepsilon^{-1/4}\bar{x}_{uu}(\varepsilon^{1/4}u^{\varepsilon})

The above relations imply

\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(v)~=~-v^{3}+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}v^{4},\qquad\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{u}(v)~=~-3v^{2}+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}v^{3},

(10.10)

\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{uu}(v)~=~-6v+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}v^{2},\qquad\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{uuu}(v)~=\,-6+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}v.

(10.11)

We now have (writing
t,v
in place of
t^{\varepsilon},u^{\varepsilon}
to simplify notation)

x^{\varepsilon}(t,v)~=~tf_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(v)+\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(v)~=~tv-v^{3}+\varepsilon^{1/4}\,\bigl[{\cal O}(1)\cdot tv^{2}+{\cal O}(1)\cdot v^{4}\bigr],

x^{\varepsilon}_{u}(t,v)~=~tf_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}(v)+\bar{x}_{u}^{\varepsilon}(v)~=~t-3v^{2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}\,\bigl[{\cal O}(1)\cdot tv+{\cal O}(1)\cdot v^{3}\bigr].

Moreover, by (
9.12
)–(
9.16
), we can assume the implications

\varepsilon^{1/4}|v|~\geq~\epsilon_{0}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v)~=~v,\qquad\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}(v)~=~-v^{3}.

(10.12)

From (
10.9
) and (
10.10
), for
t<0
it thus follows

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle 0~>~U^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t,v)~\displaystyle=~{1\over x^{\varepsilon}_{u}(t,v)}~=~{1\over tf^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v)+\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{u}(v)}\\[11.38109pt]
\quad\displaystyle=~{1\over t-3v^{2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\bigl[{\cal O}(1)\cdot tv+{\cal O}(1)\cdot v^{3}\bigr]}~\displaystyle\geq~{2\over t-3v^{2}}\,.\end{array}

(10.13)

Indeed, by (
10.12
), if
|v|\geq\varepsilon^{-1/4}\epsilon_{0}
the last inequality is trivial.
On the other hand, if
|v|<\varepsilon^{-1/4}\epsilon_{0}
, then

\begin{array}[]{l}\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\Big|{\cal O}(1)\cdot tv+{\cal O}(1)\cdot v^{3}\Big|~=~\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\Big|{\cal O}(1)\cdot t\varepsilon^{-1/4}\epsilon_{0}-3v^{2}\cdot{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-1/4}\epsilon_{0}\Big|\\[8.53581pt]
\qquad\displaystyle=~|t-3v^{2}|\cdot{\cal O}(1)\cdot\epsilon_{0}~<~{1\over 2}|t-3v^{2}|,\end{array}

(10.14)

provided that
\epsilon_{0}>0
was chosen sufficiently small. This yields (
10.13
).

Next, from

U^{\varepsilon}_{xx}(t,v)\,=\,-{tf^{\prime\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v)+\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{uu}(v)\over\bigl(tf^{\prime\prime}_{\varepsilon}(v)+\bar{x}^{\varepsilon}_{u}(v)\bigr)^{3}}

(10.15)

by (
10.11
) and (
10.13
) we obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\bigl|U_{xx}^{\varepsilon}(t,v)\bigr|~\leq~8\cdot{\Big|-6v+\varepsilon^{1/4}\bigl[{\cal O}(1)\cdot t+{\cal O}(1)\cdot v^{2}\bigr]\Big|\over|t-3v^{2}|^{3}}\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\qquad\leq~{\Big|-48v+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\epsilon_{0}v\Big|\over|t-3v^{2}|^{3}}+{{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}\over|t-3v^{2}|^{2}}\,.\end{array}

(10.16)

For a suitable constant
C_{3}
, this yields

\bigl|U_{xx}^{\varepsilon}(t,v)\bigr|~\leq~C_{3}\Big(|t|^{-5/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-2}\Big).

(10.17)

For any
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small, we claim that

\bigl|U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-U^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)\bigr|~\leq~C_{4}\,\left(|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}\,{\bigl|\ln|t|\bigr|+|\ln\varepsilon|\over|t|}\right),

(10.18)

for some constant
C_{4}
independent of
\varepsilon
.
The bound (
10.18
) will be proved by constructing upper and lower solutions,
as in the proof of (
8.18
).
Observing that by (
10.13
)

\bigl|U^{\varepsilon}_{x}(t,x)\bigr|~\leq~2|t|^{-1},

(10.19)

an upper solution to the equation

U^{\varepsilon}_{t}+f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{\varepsilon})_{x}~=~U^{\varepsilon}_{xx}\,,\qquad\qquad U^{\varepsilon}(-\varepsilon^{-1/2},x)~=~U^{(\varepsilon)}(-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},x),

(10.20)

is obtained as follows. Recalling (
9.15
), define

\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)~\doteq~C_{f}\int_{-\varepsilon^{-1/2}\tau}^{t}C_{3}\left({2\over 3}|s|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|s|^{-1}\right)ds,\qquad\qquad C_{f}~\doteq~\max_{u}f^{\prime\prime}(u)~\leq~1+\epsilon_{0},

(10.21)

and
set

U^{+}(t,x)~\doteq~U^{(\varepsilon)}\bigl(t,x-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)+C_{3}\left({2\over 3}|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-1}\right).

To check that
U^{+}
is indeed an upper solution, using (
10.17
) and (
10.21
) and observing that

U^{+}_{x}(t,x)~=~U^{(\varepsilon)}_{x}\bigl(t,x-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)~<~0,

we compute

\begin{array}[]{rl}U^{+}_{t}(t,x)&=~U^{(\varepsilon)}_{t}\bigl(t,x-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)-\dot{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}(t)U^{(\varepsilon)}_{x}\bigl(t,x-\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)\bigr)+C_{3}\Big(|t|^{-5/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-2}\Big)\\[5.69054pt]
&\geq~-f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\left(U^{+}-C_{3}\Big({2\over 3}|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-1}\Big)\right)U^{+}_{x}-\dot{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}(t)U^{+}_{x}+U^{+}_{xx}\\[5.69054pt]
&\geq~-f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{+})U^{+}_{x}-C_{f}C_{3}\Big({2\over 3}|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-1}\Big)\ |U^{+}_{x}|+\dot{\sigma}_{\varepsilon}(t)|U^{+}_{x}|+U^{+}_{xx}\\[5.69054pt]
&=~-f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{+})U^{+}_{x}+U^{+}_{xx}\,.\par\end{array}

A lower solution is constructed in the same way.
Recalling (
10.19
) and the definition of
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
at (
10.21
), we have

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle U^{+}(t,x)-U^{(\varepsilon)}(t,x)~\leq~\sigma_{\varepsilon}(t)\bigl\|U^{(\varepsilon)}_{x}(t,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}}+C_{3}\left({2\over 3}|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}|t|^{-1}\right)\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\qquad\leq~C_{4}\left(|t|^{-3/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}{\bigl|\ln|t|\bigr|+|\ln\varepsilon|\over|t|}\right),\end{array}

and a similar estimate holds for the lower solution.
This yields (
10.18
).

3.

Putting together the three bounds (
10.4
), (
10.6
) and (
10.18
),
at time
t_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{-\alpha}
we obtain

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int_{|x|<\varepsilon^{-\beta}}\bigl|U^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)-Z(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|\,dx~\leq~\int_{|x|<\varepsilon^{-\beta}}\bigl|U^{(\varepsilon)}(t_{\varepsilon},x)-z(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|\,dx\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{|x|<\varepsilon^{-\beta}}\Big(\bigl|U^{(\varepsilon)}(t_{\varepsilon},x)-U^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|+\bigl|z(t_{\varepsilon},x)-Z(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|\Big)\,dx\\[11.38109pt]
~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}\varepsilon^{-5\beta/3}+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-\beta}\varepsilon^{3\alpha/2}+{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-\beta}\bigl(\varepsilon^{3\alpha/2}+\varepsilon^{1/4}\varepsilon^{\alpha}|\ln\varepsilon|\bigr).\end{array}

(10.22)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
all the integrals on the right hand side of (
10.22
) approach zero
provided we choose

\beta<{3\over 20}\,,\qquad\alpha>{2\beta\over 3}\,,\qquad\alpha>\beta-{1\over 4}\,.

(10.23)

To guarantee that the domains of dependence of the intervals
I_{\varepsilon}
in
(
9.6
) invade the entire
t
-
x
plane, we shall also need
\alpha<\beta
.
Throughout the following, we thus choose the constants
\alpha,\beta
so that

{2\beta\over 3}~<~\alpha~<~\beta~<~{3\over 25}\,.

(10.24)

4.
Next, we need to estimate the difference between the viscous solutions
U^{\varepsilon}
and
Z
on the domain of dependency of the interval
I_{\varepsilon}
at (
9.6
). For this purpose, we can modify both of these functions at time
t_{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon^{-\alpha}
, replacing them with smooth functions

\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},\cdot)
,
\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},\cdot)
, such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)=U^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x),\qquad\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},x)=Z(t_{\varepsilon},x)&\hbox{if}\quad|x|\leq-\varepsilon^{-\beta},\\[5.69054pt]
\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)~=~\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},x)~=~Z(t_{\varepsilon},2\varepsilon^{-\beta})&\hbox{if}~~x\geq 2\varepsilon^{-\beta},\\[5.69054pt]
\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)~=~\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},x)~=~Z(t_{\varepsilon},-2\varepsilon^{-\beta})&\hbox{if}~~x\leq-2\varepsilon^{-\beta},\end{array}\right.

(10.25)

In view of (
10.22
) we can arrange so that

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\bigl|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)-\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|\,dx~=~\int_{|x|<2\varepsilon^{-\beta}}\bigl|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},x)-\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},x)\bigr|\,dx~\to~0

(10.26)

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
For
t>t_{\varepsilon}
, we shall denote
\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
and
\widehat{Z}(t,\cdot)
the solutions to the
viscous conservation laws

\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}_{t}+f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon})\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}_{x}~=~\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}_{xx}\,,\qquad\qquad\widehat{Z}_{t}+\widehat{Z}\widehat{Z}_{x}~=~\widehat{Z}_{xx}\,,

with the above initial data assigned at time
t=t_{\varepsilon}
.

5.
To estimate the difference
\bigl\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)-\widehat{Z}(t,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}}
, we denote by
S

the semigroup generated by the viscous Burgers’ equation (
1.25
). This is well
defined for all bounded measurable initial data
and is contractive w.r.t. the
\mathbf{L}^{1}
distance. We use the error formula

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\bigl\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)-\widehat{Z}(t,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}}~\leq~\bigl\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t_{\varepsilon},\cdot)-\widehat{Z}(t_{\varepsilon},\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}}+\int_{t_{\varepsilon}}^{t}\lim_{h\to 0+}{1\over h}\Big\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau+h,\cdot)-S_{h}\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)\Big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{1}}\,d\tau\\[11.38109pt]
\qquad\doteq E_{1}+E_{2}(t)\,.\end{array}

(10.27)

For each
\tau\in[t_{\varepsilon},t]
, by (
10.9
), recalling that
\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)
is monotone decreasing and by (
10.25
)

\bigl\|\widehat{U}(t_{\varepsilon},\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}}~\leq~\Bigl|Z(t_{\varepsilon},2\varepsilon^{-\beta})\bigr|~=~(2\varepsilon)^{1/3},

the integrand function in (
10.27
) can be bounded as

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\int\Big|f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,x)\bigr)-\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,x)\Big|\,\bigl|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}_{x}(\tau,x)\bigr|\,dx~\leq~\Big\|f^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\bigl(\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)\bigr)-\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)\Big\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}}\cdot\int\bigl|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}_{x}(\tau,x)\bigr|\,dx\\[11.38109pt]
~\leq~\displaystyle{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}\bigl\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)^{2}\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}}\cdot\bigl\|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(\tau,\cdot)\bigr\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}}~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\beta}.\end{array}

(10.28)

The first term on the right hand side of (
10.27
) is bounded by (
10.22
).
In view of (
10.28
), for
t>t_{\varepsilon}
we have

E_{2}(t)~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot(t-t_{\varepsilon})\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\beta}~=~{\cal O}(1)\cdot\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\varepsilon^{1/4}\cdot\varepsilon^{-\beta},

(10.29)

which approaches zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

6.

Finally, consider the trapezoidal domain (see Fig.
19
)

{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}~\doteq~\Big\{(t,x)\,;~t\in[-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{-\gamma}]\,,\qquad|x|\leq q_{\varepsilon}(t)\Big\},

(10.30)

q_{\varepsilon}(t)~\doteq~\varepsilon^{-\beta}-{\varepsilon^{-\beta}-2\varepsilon^{-\delta}\over\varepsilon^{-\gamma}+\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}(t+\varepsilon^{-\alpha}),\qquad\qquad\bar{q}_{\varepsilon}\doteq q_{\varepsilon}(0).

(10.31)

Here we choose

0\,<\,\gamma\,<\,\delta\,<\,\beta-\alpha,\qquad\qquad\gamma\,<\,{\beta-\alpha\over 4}\,.

(10.32)

We need to check that the differences

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle e_{1}(t)~\doteq~\int_{|x|\leq q_{\varepsilon}(t)-\varepsilon^{-\delta}}|\widehat{U}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)\bigr|\,dx,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle e_{2}(t)~\doteq~\int_{|x|\leq q_{\varepsilon}(t)-\varepsilon^{-\delta}}|\widehat{Z}^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-Z(t,x)\bigr|\,dx\end{array}

(10.33)

both approach zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For this purpose, we shall use Lemma
6.2
,
showing that characteristics move strictly outward from the domain
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}
.

For Burgers’ equation (
8.1
), the characteristic speed is simply
z
. Moreover, we have

\bigl|\widehat{Z}(t,x)\bigr|~\leq~\bigl|Z(t,x)\bigr|~\leq~\bigl|z(t,x)\bigr|\qquad\hbox{ for all}~t,x.

(10.34)

We compare the the speed
\pm\lambda
of the boundary of
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}
with
speed of characteristics, in the strips

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{+}~\doteq~\left\{(t,x)\,;~t\in[-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{\gamma}]\,,\qquad x\in\Big[q_{\varepsilon}(t)-{\varepsilon^{-\delta}},~q_{\varepsilon}(t)\Big]\right\},\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{-}~\doteq~\left\{(t,x)\,;~t\in[-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{\gamma}]\,,\qquad x\in\Big[-q_{\varepsilon}(t),~-q_{\varepsilon}(t)+{\varepsilon^{-\delta}}\Big]\right\}.\end{array}

(10.35)

On
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{+}
, since
z(t,x)<Z(t,x)<\widehat{Z}(t,x)<0
, the speed of characteristics satisfies

\begin{array}[]{l}\widehat{Z}(t,x)~\geq~Z(t,x)~\geq~z(t,x)~\geq~z(0,\bar{q}_{\varepsilon})~=~-\bar{q}_{\varepsilon}^{1/3}~=~-\left(\varepsilon^{-\beta}-{\varepsilon^{-\beta}-2\varepsilon^{-\delta}\over\varepsilon^{-\gamma}+\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}\varepsilon^{-\alpha}\right)^{1/3}\\[11.38109pt]
\qquad\approx~-\Big(\varepsilon^{-\beta}-\varepsilon^{-\beta}(1-2\varepsilon^{\beta-\delta})(1-\varepsilon^{\alpha-\gamma})\Big)^{1/3}~\approx~-(2\varepsilon^{-\delta}+\varepsilon^{\alpha-\beta-\gamma})^{1/3}.\end{array}

(10.36)

On the other hand, the speed of the boundary of
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{+}
is

-\lambda~=~-{\varepsilon^{-\beta}-\varepsilon^{-\delta}\over\varepsilon^{-\gamma}+\varepsilon^{-\alpha}}~<~-{1\over 2}\varepsilon^{\alpha-\beta}

(10.37)

for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small.
By construction, the thickness of the boundary layer
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{+}
is
\varepsilon^{-\delta}
. By the choice of
\delta
at (
10.32
), in view of (
10.36
)-(
10.37
), for all

\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small we thus have

\begin{array}[]{l}{\hbox{[difference in speed]}\times\hbox{[thickness of the boundary layer]}}\\[11.38109pt]
\qquad>~\left(-2\bigl(2\varepsilon^{-\delta}+\varepsilon^{\alpha-\beta-\gamma}\bigr)^{1/3}+{1\over 2}\varepsilon^{\alpha-\beta}\right)\varepsilon^{-\delta}~\to~+\infty.\end{array}

(10.38)

The same estimate holds for
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}^{-}
.
We can thus use Lemma
6.2
with
U=Z
,
V=\widehat{Z}
,
q>1
, and conclude that

\sup\left\{\bigl|Z(t,x)-\widehat{Z}(t,x)\bigr|\,;~~t\in[-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{-\gamma}],~~|x|\leq q_{\varepsilon}(t)-{\varepsilon^{-\delta}\over 2}\right\}~\leq~\varepsilon

(10.39)

for all
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. This proves that the integral
e_{2}(t)
in (
10.33
)
approaches zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
,
for all
t\in[\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{-\gamma}]
.

A similar argument shows that the same holds for the first integral
e_{1}(t)
.

7.
We now argue as in the proof of Lemma
6.1
.
Since the domains
{\cal D}_{\varepsilon}
invade the entire plane
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and the functions
Z,U^{\varepsilon}
are uniformly Lipschitz continuous on bounded sets, the integral bounds

\sup_{t\in[-\varepsilon^{-\alpha},\varepsilon^{-\gamma}]}\int_{|x|\leq q_{\varepsilon}(t)-\varepsilon^{-\delta}}|U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-Z(t,x)\bigr|\,dx~\to~0\qquad\qquad\hbox{as}~~\varepsilon\to 0

(10.40)

yield the convergence
U^{\varepsilon}(t,x)-Z(t,x)\to 0
, uniformly for
t,x
in bounded sets.

This proves Theorem
1.2
in the case (
9.1
) where all solutions
u_{\varepsilon}

have the same initial data.

8.
To cover the general case, it suffices to observe that, if the high order convergence of the initial data (
1.31
) holds,
then in the corresponding inviscid solutions
u^{(\varepsilon)}

a new singularity is formed at points
(\tau_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon})\to(\tau,\xi)
. Calling

u\mapsto x^{(\varepsilon)}(\tau_{\varepsilon},u)

a local inverse to the map
x\mapsto u^{(\varepsilon)}(\tau_{\varepsilon},x)
, one has the convergence
relations

u^{(\varepsilon)}(\tau_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon})\,\to\,u(\tau,\xi),\quad\qquad{\partial^{k}\over\partial u^{k}}x^{(\varepsilon)}\Big(\tau_{\varepsilon},u^{(\varepsilon)}(\tau_{\varepsilon},\xi_{\varepsilon})\Big)~\to~{\partial^{k}\over\partial u^{k}}x\bigl(\tau,u(\tau,\xi)\bigr),\quad k=1,2,3.

(10.41)

In this case, the previous analysis provides an estimate on the difference
between the rescaled functions

U^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.32
) and the functions

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t,x)~=~c_{\varepsilon}\,Z(\sigma_{\varepsilon}t,\,x-\lambda_{\varepsilon}t),

for suitable
(c_{\varepsilon},\sigma_{\varepsilon},\lambda_{\varepsilon})\to(c,\sigma,\lambda)
.
This convergence is uniform on a family of compacts
D_{\varepsilon}
that invades
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Hence the conclusion.

Remark 10.1

In Theorem
1.2
the convergence of rescaled solutions was proved assuming that the initial data
converge in
{\cal C}^{4}
. This assumption simplifies some of the estimates, but is likely not needed.
Indeed, we expect that the same conclusion can be reached assuming that the convergence takes place locally
in
{\cal C}^{3}
.

On the other hand,
{\cal C}^{2}
convergence of the initial data
u_{\varepsilon}(0,x)\to\bar{u}(x)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
is not enough.
For example, let
u,v
be the inviscid solutions of Burgers’ equation where a shock forms at time
\tau=1
, such that

u(1,x)~=~-2x^{1/3},\qquad\qquad v(1,x)=-x^{1/3}\,.

Let
\bar{u}(x)=u(0,x)
and
\bar{v}(x)=v(0,x)
be the corresponding initial data.

One can now construct a sequence of smooth initial data
\bar{u}_{n}
such that

\bigl\|\bar{u}_{n}-\bar{u}\bigr\|_{{\cal C}^{2}}~\to~0,\qquad\qquad\bar{u}_{n}(x)=\bar{v}(x)\quad\hbox{for}~|x|\leq{1\over n}\,.

By the previous analysis, for each fixed
n\geq 1
, the rescaled viscous solutions
U^{\varepsilon}_{n}
converge to the function
Z
constructed in Lemma
8.2
uniformly on bounded sets.
Hence, taking a suitable sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
, we still have the convergence

U^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{n}(t,x)\to Z(t,x)
uniformly on bounded sets.
However, the limit of the rescaled solutions
U^{\varepsilon}
does not converge to
Z
.
