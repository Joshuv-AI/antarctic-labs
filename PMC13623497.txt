Vengeful Vines: Cell Death and Neoplasm Formation in Aristolochia (Aristolochiaceae, Piperales) in Response to Oviposition of Troidini (Papilionidae, Lepidoptera) Butterflies

Veerle van Beeck, Justinah Parantis, Berber E Zandstra, Tjomme Fernhout, Arthur Chung, Nazirah Mustaffa, Nina E Fatouros, Corné F H van der Linden

Abstract

The ecological interactions of the iconic Troidini swallowtail butterflies and their host plants remain largely unknown, despite the popularity of these butterflies in collections and their significance as conservation flagships. We sought to elucidate the ecological interactions of perhaps the least known life‐stage of the butterflies: that of the egg with its host plant. We recorded oviposition location and egg survival rates for Troides helena, Troides amphrysus and Pachliopta antiphus on their host plants Aristolochia acuminata and Aristolochia cf. foveolata through systematic monitoring of the Poring Butterfly Garden (Sabah, Malaysia). Here, we make the first observation of hypersensitive response (HR)‐like cell death and neoplasm formation in response to butterfly eggs deposited on Aristolochia leaves. This represents the first documented case of such egg‐killing traits in the highly diverse Magnoliids (> 10,000 spp.). HR‐like cell death under eggs showed a statistically significant negative effect on butterfly egg survival. Additionally, we provide a quantitative overview of the oviposition sites selected by Troidini, establishing that up to 37.6% of eggs are not oviposited on the host plant. Together, this suggests a fitness cost to butterflies and a possible behavioral avoidance mechanism. These findings on egg‐killing and oviposition behavior provide a significant contribution to the study of coevolutionary interactions between Troidini and Aristolochia. Furthermore, these observations provide valuable information for butterfly gardens and conservation projects, where egg‐killing by host plants may significantly impact butterfly survival. Overall, this research provides a unique egg‐based perspective on the ecological interactions between Troidini and their host plants.
This study investigates the oviposition ecology of the iconic Troidini butterflies on their host plants of the genus Aristolochia in Sabah, where we made the first observation of HR‐like cell death and neoplasm formation in response to oviposition of Troidini, signifying the first such record in the entire Magnoliid clade (> 10,000 spp.). We provide a quantitative overview of Troidini egg survival and show the negative effects of HR‐like cell death on egg survival rates. Additionally, a large proportion of eggs was recorded as oviposited on structures other than leaves, suggesting a behavioral avoidance mechanism used by butterflies to escape egg‐killing by their host plants. HR‐like cell death reaction on Aristolochia acuminata leaf in reaction to an egg of Pachliopta antiphus.

Introduction

Coevolution is one of the key driving mechanisms behind the formation of the Earth's biodiversity (Edger et al. ; Ehrlich and Raven ; Hembry et al. ; Speed et al. ). It occurs as a reciprocal process of adaptations and counteradaptations between interacting species. A classic example occurs between herbivorous insects and plants, where insect herbivory drives the diversification of plant anti‐herbivory chemicals, and these plant chemical defenses in turn drive the diversification of herbivorous insects (Becerra ; Beran and Petschenka ; Edger et al. ; Ehrlich and Raven ; Speed et al. ). A system that illustrates this is that of the Pieridae butterflies and Brassicales, where caterpillars evolved the ability to detoxify the plants' glucosinolates, with major innovations in this coevolutionary process being associated with gene and genome duplications in the butterflies and plants (Edger et al. ; Wheat et al. ). Examples of such interactions are widespread across the order Lepidoptera, including between Troidini caterpillars (Lepidoptera: Papilionidae) and their Aristolochiaceae host plants (Nishida et al. ; Nishida and Fukami ). Troidini larvae have adapted to detoxify toxic aristolochic acids (AAs) produced by host plant genera Aristolochia and Thottea, which allows them to feed on these plants (Nishida et al. ; Nishida and Fukami ). Additionally, several Troidini species can sequester these AAs in their larval stages and eggs, rendering them toxic to nonspecialized predators (Nishida et al. ; Nishida and Fukami ).

Plant anti‐herbivore defenses against insects have not only been found to affect actively feeding life‐stages but have also been shown to occur during the comparatively less studied egg stage (Griese et al. , ; Hilker and Fatouros ). Different types of defense strategies against insect eggs have been recorded in different plants (Hilker and Fatouros ; Fatouros et al. ). An example of a defense mechanism is wound tissue growth, by which plants are able to crush eggs (Aluja et al. ; Desurmont and Weston ; Mazanec ). Additionally, eggs can induce leaf cell death resembling a hypersensitive response (HR) typically initiated by plant pathogens (Little et al. ; Shapiro and Devay ). In this case, the plant tissue surrounding the egg‐laying site becomes necrotic, often resulting in the desiccation of eggs (Fatouros et al. ). This reaction was first found in Brassicales and is hypothesized to be a counteradaptation of some brassicaceous species to the glucosinolate detoxifying abilities of Pieridae caterpillars (Hilker and Fatouros ). Neoplasm formation is another defense trait against eggs, where rapid growth of cells surrounding the egg can cause an egg to fall off the plant (Fatouros et al. ; Hilker and Fatouros ; Petzold‐Maxwell et al. ). At the time of writing this article, the expression of egg‐killing traits has been found in over thirty five plant species, spread over sixteen families (Bittner et al. ; Costa et al. ; de Oliveira Pinto et al. ; Fatouros et al. ; Li, Pei, et al. ; Lortzing et al. ; Da Song et al. ; Subramanyam et al. ; Wei et al. ). No records of leaf‐related egg‐killing traits have so far been documented for the entire Magnoliid plant clade, despite the presence of adaptations in Troidini and host plants that may have arisen through coevolutionary processes (Nishida et al. ; Nishida and Fukami ).

Here, we aim to address this knowledge gap by investigating possible egg‐killing traits of Aristolochia against the eggs of Troidini occurring in Sabah, Malaysian Borneo. Five genera of Troidini are found in Borneo: Troides, Trogonoptera, Atrophaneura, Losaria and Pachliopta (Chung et al. ). It is well known that these genera are oligophagous on host plant genera Aristolochia and Thottea (Böhm , , ; Böhm et al. ; Matsuka ). Despite this, their exact host plant ranges are largely unknown due to the poorly studied taxonomy of the plant genera and the difficulty of studying host plant interactions in the field. Aristolochia acuminata Lam. is one of the well‐studied and well‐documented species serving as host plant for various Troidini (Böhm ; Böhm et al. ; Haugum and Low ; Matsuka ; Straatman ). Moreover, this species is often used for captive breeding of these butterflies, particularly in conservation projects for threatened birdwings, the troidine genera Troides & Ornithoptera spp. (Peggie et al. ; Sutton et al. ).

We conducted a survey of Troidini butterfly oviposition on Aristolochia host plants in the Poring Butterfly Garden (Malaysian Borneo) and aim to address the following questions: (1) Does Aristolochia exhibit defense mechanisms against the eggs of Troidini? (2) To what extent do these defense mechanisms affect egg survival? and (3) Do Troidini exhibit oviposition counteradaptations to egg‐killing traits? In‐depth knowledge of the oviposition ecology can improve captive and semi‐captive breeding efforts and enable more efficient conservation efforts. Furthermore, knowledge on host plant–egg interactions and oviposition ecology will afford a more complete view of coevolutionary interactions between Troidini and their Aristolochia host plants.

Methodology

Study Site

We conducted this research in the Poring Butterfly Garden in Kinabalu National Park (Sabah, Malaysian Borneo), where a seminatural butterfly rearing is located on the edge of the rainforest (Figure , “Butterfly rearing”). The butterfly rearing is surrounded by a butterfly garden and planted with a high density of local butterfly nectar‐ and host plants. This provides a suitable study site for making life‐history observations of free‐living butterflies which are attracted from the adjacent rainforest (Figure , “Butterfly garden”). The host plants of local Troidini butterflies, Aristolochia acuminata Lam. and Aristolochia cf. foveolata Merr., are cultivated in the gardens. Despite 
A. foveolata
 being mentioned frequently by botanical and lepidopterological literature on Borneo (Chung et al. ; Haugum and Low ; Hou ; Matsuka ; Sutton et al. ), there is increasing support that A. foveolata may in fact be absent from Borneo and may belong to one of several similar‐looking species in the “papillifolia group” (Frank et al. ). We refer readers to Frank et al. () for a detailed taxonomic perspective but continue to refer to the studied taxon as A. cf. foveolata in this manuscript following the identification key in (Hou ). The host plants grew on trellises that were approximately between 160 and 185 cm tall. A roughly estimated one third of the butterfly garden was occupied with Aristolochia plants, in a patch where 
A. acuminata
 contributed 75% and A. cf. foveolata 25% of vegetation cover. The most commonly occurring Troidini species are Troides helena (Linnaeus, 1758), Troides amphrysus (Cramer, 1782), Trogonoptera brookiana (Wallace, 1855) and Pachliopta antiphus (Fabricius, 1793).

Survey of Butterfly Oviposition Location and Resulting Plant Responses

From the 4th of March to the 22nd of April 2024, the butterfly garden was monitored for eggs of the Troidini clade. Initially, oviposition sites and plant responses were surveyed opportunistically. Eggs were collected to monitor egg survival rates, in total allowing 86 eggs to be surveyed. After making the initial observations of HR‐like cell death and neoplasm formation, a 10‐day survey of direct egg‐killing traits was performed from the 13th to the 22nd of April. During this daily survey, a directed search was carried out for newly deposited eggs. Both the adaxial and abaxial leaf sides were checked for newly deposited eggs, as well as the stem and support material (trellises and support sticks) of all 
A. acuminata
 and A. cf. foveolata individuals present in the garden. When an egg was encountered, the plant on which it was found was marked with a code, the location of oviposition was noted, and the egg was collected. If multiple eggs were present on the same leaf, all eggs were collected at the same time. Egg placement on and near host plants 
A. acuminata
 and A. cf. foveolata was divided into seven categories: dead stem, stem, adaxial and abaxial side of the leaf, other plant and “other” for oviposition on non‐plant material. When HR‐like cell death was expressed underneath an egg, a picture of the adaxial and abaxial side of the leaf was taken and the intensity of the response was later scored based on the picture. When HR‐like cell death was present, eggs were collected together with leaves. After collection, the eggs were kept in plastic containers of approximately 20 × 10 × 5 cm. Containers were kept at room temperature (approximately 23°C) and checked daily for the emergence of caterpillars. Each egg was identified to species level based on morphological characteristics such as size and color.

Scoring and Statistics

Pictures of eggs inducing HR‐like cell death were scored on a scale from zero to three in a modified version of the system used by Griese et al. (2017). Scores of zero were given to leaves that displayed no visible response (Figure ). The score of one was allocated to leaves where HR‐like cell death was only present in a small circle under the egg and not on the opposite side of the leaf. Leaves where HR‐like cell death was present under the eggs and where it was visible on both sides of the leaf were classified as score two. Leaves on which the HR‐like cell death had spread from under the eggs and could be seen clearly on both sides of the leaf were scored as three. Additionally, the presence of cell growth/neoplasm formation beneath the eggs was separately scored as one for presence and zero for absence.

After this, the effect of HR‐like cell death formation on egg survival was tested by using a binomial generalized linear model (Bates et al. ). Neoplasm formation was not included in this analysis due to the low sample size. Butterfly species and HR‐like cell death were tested as additive predictors. This was done using the nnet package version 7.3.20 (Venables and Ripley ) in R version 4.5.0 (R Core Team ). All observations of eggs made between the 4th of March and the 22nd of April 2024 were included for the egg survival rate analysis. Troides amphrysus could not be included in this model because of the limited sample size.

Results

Egg Placement

We were able to record egg placement for three species of butterflies, P. antiphus, T. amphrysus, and 
T. helena
, in the Poring butterfly garden on and near host plants 
A. acuminata
 and A. cf. foveolata (Figures , , ). The survey showed that eggs were not only laid on the adaxial and abaxial sides of leaves on the plants, but also on stems and petioles (Figures and ). Oviposition location varied between plant and butterfly species. Where T. amphrysus only oviposited on the abaxial side of leaves of both host plants, P. antiphus oviposited on the petiole of both host plants, while the other butterfly species did not, and 
T. helena
 was the only species to oviposit on a stem (Figure ). Oviposition patterns also differed for host plant species of both P. antiphus and 
T. helena
 (Figure ).

Next to this, we noted that up to 37.6% of eggs were not laid on the Aristolochia host plants, but on surrounding structures (other) and, in the case of 
T. helena
, even on surrounding non‐Aristolochia plants (Figures and ).

HR‐Like Cell Death and Neoplasm Formation

We recorded 23 observations of HR‐like cell death and 8 observations of neoplasm formation over the total duration of 1 month of research. For the cell death responses, the tissue surrounding the eggs initially turns yellow, progresses to brown, and eventually turns black upon dying. Neoplasm formation is when tissue growth occurs under the eggs, raising the egg up in relation to the leaf. Both HR‐like cell death and neoplasm formation were only detected on 
A. acuminata
 and on the abaxial side of the leaves (Figure ).

Out of all 23 responses, 22 scored a severity of three for cell death, where it is visible from both sides of the leaf and cell death spread from under the eggs. The only response that scored a one for cell death was recorded on a developing leaf (Figure ). Neoplasm formation was also recorded on this leaf. Unfortunately, the leaf dried out immediately after collection, preventing further monitoring of the egg‐induced response. Not all eggs deposited on a plant that expressed cell death induced a cell death response. On five plants that previously showed HR‐like cell death induced by P. antiphus or 
T. helena
 eggs, we also recorded eggs that did not trigger any HR‐like cell death in the plant.

During a 10‐day survey focusing on the placement of eggs, we labeled 26 
A. acuminata
 and six plants of A. cf. foveolata. In total, 12 plants of 
A. acuminata
 developed an HR‐like cell death (Figure ). Neoplasm formation, if encountered, was always found in combination with cell death (Figure ). We recorded a total of 39 eggs belonging to P. antiphus, T. amphrysus, and 
T. helena
 on 
A. acuminata
 (Figure ). Neoplasm formation was only found for eggs of P. antiphus (n = 7).

We found a significant effect on egg survival rates by butterfly species and cell death (Figure ). P. antiphus had a significantly higher probability to emerge as a caterpillar than the reference species, 
T. helena
 (Binomial model, log odds = 1.7656, p = 0.0334, Figure ). For the eggs where HR‐like cell death was present, the odds of a caterpillar emerging were lower than the reference without cell death formation (Binomial model, log odds = −1.3168, p = 0.0458, Figure ).

Egg survival rates were split per butterfly species and the absence and presence of cell death; rates ranged between 22% and 56% (Figure ). No eggs of the species 
T. helena
 hatched when cell death was present (Figure ). Out of a total of seven eggs with neoplasm reactions recorded during this study, two caterpillars emerged. Neoplasm formation was not included in the figure and model due to low sample size.

Discussion

We surveyed oviposition of Troidini butterflies in the Poring Butterfly Garden where we recorded egg placement and novel plant responses to butterfly eggs. Our observations provide the first record of HR‐like cell death and neoplasm formation in Aristolochia acuminata, and this reaction was induced by eggs of three species of Troidini: 
T. helena
, T. amphrysus, and P. antiphus. Next to this, we statistically confirm a negative effect of the HR‐like cell death on egg survival, highlighting that this reaction may be an effect of coevolutionary processes between the egg stage of Troidini and Aristolochia plants.

First Record of HR‐Like Cell Death and Neoplasm Formation in the Magnoliids

As of 2016, 22 plant species were shown to express egg‐induced direct defense traits (Fatouros et al. ), with an additional 9 species recorded since then (Bittner et al. ; Costa et al. ; Li, Pei, et al. ; Lortzing et al. ; Da Song et al. ; Subramanyam et al. ; Wei et al. ). To our knowledge, our observations provide the first record of leaf‐related defense strategies against the egg stage of insects in the Magnoliids (> 10,000 species). Until now, wound tissue growth in the avocado (
Persea americana
 Mill., Lauraceae) in response to flies of the genus Anastrepha in Mexico was the only known observation of plant defense against insect eggs within the Magnoliids (Aluja et al. ). In 
P. americana
, wound tissue growth was found on the fruit, which was mechanically damaged by the hole‐boring activity of ovipositing Anastrepha flies. We observed egg‐induced response only on leaves, which were not mechanically damaged by butterfly oviposition. The effects of the responses also differed; wound tissue growth mechanically crushes eggs, while HR‐like cell death likely causes egg desiccation and/or eggs falling off the leaf, and neoplasm formation is thought to push eggs off the plant (Aluja et al. ; Fatouros et al. ). These differences illustrate that the formation of HR‐like cell death and neoplasm are distinct defense mechanisms, making our discovery the first documented case of these responses in the Magnoliids.

The complexity of the mechanisms of direct defense traits is not only shown by interspecies relationships but is also underscored by intraspecific variation. Not all 
A. acuminata
 plants consistently expressed cell death, and this phenotypic variation may be due to underlying genetic variation as all plants were grown under common conditions. The provenance of the studied population of Aristolochia is unknown, as plants have been transplanted from a small number of “donor” plants growing in the wild, obscuring insights into intraspecies variation of the traits investigated. Comparably, different 
Mutarda nigra
 Bernh. populations were found to show phenotypic variation in HR‐like cell death (Bassetti et al. ). Next to intraspecific variation, variation in response severity was detected on the same plant, with some eggs from the same butterfly species eliciting a response while others did not. This could possibly be explained by differences in egg‐casing chemistry among ovipositing butterflies, which may trigger reactions and influence their severity or presence. Seeing as the AA content per butterfly has been shown to differ according to dietary intake, individual butterflies may also differ in their concentrations of other plant‐derived chemicals (Dimarco and Fordyce ). Chemistry of leaves also changes as they age and grow (Jeong et al. ), introducing another possible source of variation in responses within an individual plant. Next to this, consistently non‐reacting plants may potentially be explained by a time bias in our study. Plants were surveyed daily, so responses might not have developed completely at the time of making observations. However, the only egg found in the initial stage of cell death (score = one) was found on a very young leaf, suggesting that the responses likely start quickly but take some time to reach the full extent. However, the plants were surveyed once daily, so at least in some cases, the reaction is able to reach full severity (score = 3) within 24 h.

Our research is constrained by its observational design, single‐location, seminatural, and butterfly garden setting, which may influence butterfly oviposition behavior. The high Aristolochia densities at this location facilitated the observation of oviposition behavior but may not accurately reflect oviposition patterns in nature. Quantitatively studying responses to butterfly oviposition on multiple individuals of Aristolochia plants in their natural environment would allow for understanding this trait in the wild. Furthermore, combining such studies with genomic studies of plants from different populations would enable the study of intra‐specific phenotypic and genetic variation. Common garden experiments with Aristolochia from a known genetic background and experimentally exposing these to ovipositing butterflies would allow for the study of the variation in response between and within plants.

Possible Coevolutionary Interactions Between Aristolochia and Troidini Eggs

Aristolochia also has defense traits that act on the caterpillars of Troidini, including trichomes influencing feeding behavior of caterpillars and diterpenoid compounds causing differential gene expression in gut and fat body tissues (Fordyce and Agrawal ; Silva‐Brandão et al. ). Our findings broaden the defense traits in this context to the egg stage, with HR‐like cell death being associated with lower egg survival for the species 
T. helena
 and P. antiphus (Figure ). Overall, P. antiphus had higher odds of egg survival compared to 
T. helena
 (Figure ). The eggs of P. antiphus had an egg survival rate of 56% on non‐responding leaves, compared to 29% where HR‐like cell death was present (Figure ). Eggs of 
T. helena
 had a survival rate of 22% on non‐responding leaves, whilst no eggs hatched under the presence of HR‐like cell death (Figure ). In the Pieris Brassicaceae study system it is well‐established that HR‐like cell death negatively influences egg survival through the desiccation of eggs (Fatouros et al. , ; Griese et al. ; Peters et al. ). Caterpillars of Troidini are known to consume large amounts of leaf tissue during development (Alam et al. ; Barua and Slowik ), even defoliating entire plants (Straatman ), and reduced survival in the egg stage most likely confers a significant benefit for the plants.

Larger sample sizes and longer monitoring of eggs on the plant is necessary to fully understand the fitness effects of this interaction on both butterfly and host plant. It would also be interesting to characterize the chemical composition of these lesions to gain more understanding of chemical processes involved. Additionally, a comprehensive survey of egg‐killing traits across Aristolochia species and co‐occurring Troidini species, combined with phylogenetic information for both groups, could elucidate a broader coevolutionary pattern between host plants and herbivore eggs.

Egg Placement: Behavioral Adaptations as a Result of Coevolutionary Interactions?

During our studies, we observed a relatively high proportion of eggs not laid on host plant leaves. On 
A. acuminata
, 6.2% of the detected P. antiphus eggs were oviposited on petioles, while 
T. helena
 deposited 46.7% of eggs on stems (Figures and ). Additionally, we observed that eggs were also oviposited on structures other than host plants: T. amphrysus oviposited 14.3% of detected eggs on nearby non‐plant structures, and 4.3% of eggs contributed to this category for 
T. helena
. Moreover, 
T. helena
 oviposited 29.2% of its observed eggs on plants not belonging to the genus Aristolochia (Figures and ). The few sources documenting Troidini oviposition solely mention the abaxial and adaxial sides of leaves or stems of host plants as oviposition locations (Alam et al. ; Barua and Slowik ; Böhm et al. ; Haribal ; Straatman ; Straatman and Schmid ). Straatman () mentions that eggs of T. brookiana ovipositing on Aristolochia sp. can be found up to 20 cm from the host plant on dead twigs or other plants. A recent study on T. amphrysus in Java identified A. zollinggeriana
Miq. as a new host plant, noting that most eggs were deposited on other plants near this host plant (Shahroni et al. ). Not only do we also find a diverse array of oviposition sites used by 
T. helena
, T. amphrysus and P. antiphus, but our results also provide a rare quantitative perspective. Taken together, this suggests that oviposition on nonhost plant sites may be relatively common across the Troidini. Based on our observations on egg survival in response to defense traits in leaves, we cautiously attribute this behavior to be a potential “avoidance mechanism” used by the butterflies to escape HR‐cell death response and neoplasm formation in A. acuminata. Several species within the Pieridae seem to exhibit a similar oviposition preference when exposed to HR‐like cell death expressing Brassicaceae (Griese et al. ), suggesting counteradaptation by the butterflies (Peters et al. ).

Other drivers of the observed oviposition patterns may be the selection of more concealed oviposition locations by butterflies to avoid predation (Fatouros et al. ). Differing microclimate conditions around the hostplant may also determine the selection of oviposition sites (Eilers et al. ). Additionally, Satyrinae species with superabundant hosts often do not deposit their eggs on leaves their larvae will feed on as they can easily be found by the neonate caterpillars (Wiklund ). An alternative explanation is that females may oviposit on several nonhost species before they find their preferred host if these have similarly shaped leaves (Wiklund ). Aristolochia is not superabundant in the wild, and has visually distinct leaves, but the density of plants in the butterfly garden could influence oviposition behavior. Furthermore, another Troidini butterfly, Battus polydamas (Linnaeus, 1758), has been shown to locate its host through olfactory and tactile cues, suggesting that visual identification may be less important in Troidini oviposition (Pinto et al. ). Despite not being able to experimentally rule out other mechanisms, our observations serve as an indication of a behavioral counteradaptation to egg‐killing host plant traits. Understanding of the extent of fitness effects on Troidini butterflies and their host plant must be established to fully understand the complexity of this interactive relationship.

New Insights Into Egg Survival Rates of Troidini Butterflies

In this survey, we were able to record the egg survival rates of 
T. helena
 (22%) and P. antiphus (56%, Figure ), providing one of the few overviews of egg survival in Troidini to date. To accurately determine the extent of the effects of HR‐like cell death and neoplasm formation on egg survival, baseline knowledge on egg survival rates of Troidini in the wild would be advantageous. However, literature on the egg survival rates of Troidini is scarce since eggs are difficult to locate and monitor in the wild. The only available study on Troidini egg survival rates was performed on Troides aeacus (Felder, 1860), which was reported to peak at 40% survival for an egg clutch and went as low as 5.7%, showing a similar range to our rates for different butterfly species (22%–56%) (Hsieh et al. ). T. aeacus lays eggs in clusters, while the species in our study laid solitary eggs. This difference in oviposition strategy may influence egg survival, thus not making these rates directly comparable, but rather providing a general reference. Another example from within the Papilionidae but outside the Troidini is a study on Papilio godeffroyi (Semper, 1866), reporting an egg survival rate of 14.7%, slightly below the mean of our own measurements (Banko et al. ). Next to improving our knowledge on butterfly egg‐plant relationships, information on such is also valuable for breeding programs of threatened species within the Papilionidae.

Informing Conservation Efforts

Because of international trade, drastic habitat loss and habitat quality decline, the IUCN has classified several Troidini species as vulnerable (Benedick et al. ; Böhm et al. ; Gaveau et al. ; IUCN ). In response, conservation efforts have been implemented for several species (Swallowtail and Birdwing Butterfly Trust ). In Sabah, these efforts have been focused on the endemic Kinabalu birdwing (Troides andromache), recently proclaimed as Sabah's state butterfly (mysabah.com ; Sabah Biodiversity Centre ; Sutton et al. ; Swallowtail and Birdwing Butterfly Trust ). Conservation measures implemented by the Kinabalu Birdwing Butterfly project include the propagation of host plants to bolster naturally occurring populations, planting of nectar gardens and the development of education packages and ecotourism with T. andromache as flagship species (Sutton et al. ; Swallowtail and Birdwing Butterfly Trust ). To properly inform such conservation initiatives it is crucial to gather more information on their ecological interactions (Benedick et al. ; Böhm et al. ; Bonebrake et al. ; Gaveau et al. ). Knowledge on egg‐killing traits is key in breeding projects and can be used to ensure that only non‐expressing host plants are planted in butterfly gardens and to support wild butterfly populations. Additionally, our research provides information on egg survival rates, which can be used as a reference point for alerting to possible problems related to egg survival rates.

Complementary to aiding local conservation efforts in Sabah, the knowledge gained from our survey can be transferred to conservation projects for other Troidini. For example, the conservation project of the near‐threatened Wallace's birdwing, Ornithoptera croesus (Wallace, 1859), endemic to North Maluku (Indonesia) (Böhm ). Seminatural breeding efforts for this species rely on 
A. acuminata
 as a host plant, instead of the more difficult‐to‐cultivate wild host plants of the species, which belong to the Pararistolochia group of Aristolochia (Peggie et al. ). Here, potential egg‐killing by 
A. acuminata
 could impact egg survival and reduce the effectiveness of captive breeding. Our insights may also aid in the conservation of the enigmatic Queen Alexandra's Birdwing (Ornithoptera alexandrae (Rothschild, 1907), classified as threatened by the IUCN). Additionally, it has been reported that in experimental settings these butterflies mature well, and even faster on the more easily available 
A. acuminata
 (Straatman ). However, survival of eggs has not been quantified, and may be lower on 
A. acuminata
 opposed to their naturally occurring host plants, depending on the presence of if egg‐killing traits. It is unknown if HR‐like expressing plants are used in the breeding programs of these threatened butterfly species, but this warrants investigation as it may significantly reduce conservation breeding success. Understanding the interactions of these butterflies and their hosts, and in particular the oviposition behavior and egg‐killing traits of their hosts, is imperative for successful conservation strategies.

Conclusion

In this study, we observed the presence of defense mechanisms in Aristolochia acuminata in response to eggs of several Troidine butterfly species. To our knowledge, this observation marks the first record of such mechanisms on leaves within the Magnoliids (> 10,000 species). We provided one of the few quantitative overviews of Troidini egg survival and showed the negative effects of HR‐like cell death on egg survival rates. Additionally, we noted a large proportion of eggs oviposited in locations other than leaves, possibly suggesting a behavioral avoidance mechanism used by butterflies to escape egg‐killing by their host plants. Since our research was exploratory, sample sizes were dictated by the abundance and presence of butterflies. Future research would benefit from larger sample sizes and comparisons between 
A. acuminata
 plants with and without HR‐like responses in a comparative setting where multiple species are investigated in parallel. The addition of a temporal component, where the response is tracked over time, would provide valuable additional information. Overall, our results address a significant gap in the literature on the ecological interactions of Troidini eggs and Aristolochia and thereby provide essential ecological information with practical implications for conservation projects of these well‐known butterflies.
