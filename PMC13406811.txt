Conversion to Pathological Complete Response by Enfortumab Vedotin in Metastatic Sarcomatoid Urothelial Carcinoma With Nectin‐4 Expression: A Case Report
Authors: Sonin Chon, Shoichi Nagamoto, Takahisa Yamashita, Kenta Fujii, Kazuki Yokota, Kojiro Tachibana, Sachi Kitayama, Yohei Okada, Satoru Kawakami, Akihiro Yano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSarcomatoid urothelial carcinoma (UC) is an extremely rare and aggressive subtype with a poor prognosis. Introduction

Sarcomatoid urothelial carcinoma (UC) is an extremely rare subtype, accounting for only 0.1%–0.3% of all UCs [1]. It is characterized by biphasic morphology with both epithelial and mesenchymal features and is generally regarded as an aggressive tumor subtype, often showing limited responsiveness to platinum‐based combination chemotherapy such as gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GC) or methotrexate, vinblastine, doxorubicin, and cisplatin (MVAC). Enfortumab vedotin (EV), an antibody–drug conjugate targeting Nectin‐4, has demonstrated significant clinical efficacy in advanced UC. However, evidence specifically addressing the efficacy of EV in sarcomatoid UC remains extremely limited.

Here, we report a case of metastatic sarcomatoid UC with preserved Nectin‐4 expression that achieved pathological complete response (CR) after EV therapy and discuss the clinical implications of EV treatment in this aggressive subtype.

Case Presentation

A 74‐year‐old man presented with gross hematuria. His medical history included hypertension and cataract. He was taking two antihypertensive medications with no known drug allergies. He had a 50‐year history of smoking approximately 15 cigarettes per day.

Cystoscopy revealed a broad‐based papillary tumor extending from the right lateral to the posterior bladder wall. MRI demonstrated muscle‐invasive bladder tumor with perivesical fat invasion and lymph node metastases in the right obturator and presacral regions. The clinical stage was cT3N2M0. Transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) was performed. Histopathological examination revealed a biphasic tumor composed of an epithelial component consistent with conventional UC and a sarcomatoid component (Figure 1a,d). The sarcomatoid component was characterized by a proliferation of individually distributed spindle‐shaped tumor cells with marked nuclear pleomorphism and increased cellular atypia, accompanied by prominent mitotic features and accounted for approximately one‐sixth of the total tumor area in the TURBT specimen (Figure 1g). Immunohistochemically, both the epithelial and sarcomatoid components were positive for pan‐cytokeratin (AE1/AE3), although staining intensity was slightly attenuated in the sarcomatoid component, supporting the diagnosis of sarcomatoid UC (Figure 1b,e). In addition, diffuse Nectin‐4 positivity (clone: EPR15613‐68, Abcam) was observed in both components (Figure 1c,f).

First‐line chemotherapy with gemcitabine and carboplatin (GCb) therapy was initiated (Figure 2), as GC or GCb therapy was the standard treatment at that time. Although renal function was preserved (eGFR, 65.3 mL/min/1.73 m2) and cisplatin‐based chemotherapy was considered feasible, GCb therapy was selected considering the patient's preference and overall tolerability.

Contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) images demonstrating serial changes in the (a) primary bladder tumor, (b) right obturator lymph node metastasis, (c) para‐aortic lymph node metastasis, and (d) pulmonary metastasis throughout the treatment course. T0, baseline imaging before treatment initiation; T1, after one cycle of gemcitabine plus carboplatin (GCb); T2, after three cycles of pembrolizumab; T3, after two cycles of enfortumab vedotin (EV).

However, after 1 cycle, CT showed enlargement of the nodal metastases, consistent with progressive disease (PD) (Figure 2b). Second‐line therapy with pembrolizumab was administered for 3 cycles, but new pulmonary and para‐aortic lymph node metastases appeared on CT (Figure 2c,d), confirming PD. Although pseudo‐progression cannot be completely excluded, the emergence and persistence of new pulmonary and para‐aortic lymph node metastases over multiple treatment cycles were considered clinically more suggestive of true disease progression.

Third‐line therapy with EV was administered at a dose of 1.25 mg/kg on days 1, 8, and 15 of a 28‐day cycle for 4 cycles. Post‐treatment imaging demonstrated partial response (PR) of the primary bladder lesion, with a 73% reduction in tumor size and CR of the pulmonary and nodal metastases. After the first course of EV, grade 2 dysgeusia and grade 2 skin rash emerged, and grade 1 peripheral sensory neuropathy developed after the third course; however, these adverse events improved after discontinuation of EV.

Subsequently, the patient underwent robot‐assisted laparoscopic radical cystectomy with ileal conduit diversion after confirming CR of the distant metastases, 7 months after the initiation of GCb therapy. Pathological examination of the resected specimens, including an extended pelvic lymph node dissection comprising the obturator, external iliac, internal iliac, and common iliac lymph nodes bilaterally, as well as the presacral lymph nodes, revealed no residual carcinoma (ypT0 ypN0 [0/30]), confirming pathological CR. The patient has been followed without additional therapy and no evidence of recurrence or metastasis has been observed at 30 months postoperatively.

Discussion

This case represents a rare clinical course of sarcomatoid UC that was refractory to both platinum‐based chemotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibition but showed a marked response to EV, enabling subsequent radical cystectomy and resulting in pathological CR. This observation suggests that EV may induce substantial tumor regression even in this otherwise aggressive and treatment‐resistant subtype.

Sarcomatoid UC is generally characterized by rapid progression, early metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes, and several retrospective studies have reported low pathological response rates to platinum‐based chemotherapy [2, 3, 4]. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors are widely used in advanced urothelial carcinoma, their efficacy in variant histologies such as sarcomatoid carcinoma remains insufficiently characterized [5]. These features indicate that there is an unmet clinical need for effective systemic therapies in this subtype.

EV is an antibody–drug conjugate targeting Nectin‐4, an adhesion molecule widely expressed in UC. In the phase I EV‐101 study, only Nectin‐4–positive tumors were enrolled, whereas the phase III EV‐301 trial demonstrated the efficacy of EV regardless of Nectin‐4 expression status, likely reflecting the high prevalence of Nectin‐4 expression in conventional UC [6]. Although Nectin‐4 expression provides a biological rationale for EV efficacy, its predictive value remains uncertain. Importantly, sarcomatoid UC generally exhibits lower Nectin‐4 expression than pure UC. Several analyzes have shown that Nectin‐4 expression is generally low in sarcomatoid UC, with immunohistochemical positivity reported in approximately 10%–29% of cases across sarcomatoid and related undifferentiated variant cohorts [7, 8].

In our case, both the conventional and sarcomatoid components of the tumor showed diffuse Nectin‐4 expression and demonstrated a marked clinical response to EV. Although a definitive predictive role of Nectin‐4 expression cannot be established from a single case and its predictive value remains uncertain, the present findings suggest that Nectin‐4 expression may be associated with treatment response and may have potential utility as a biomarker in this setting. Further accumulation of similar cases and prospective evaluation will be required to clarify the predictive value of Nectin‐4 expression and to optimize treatment strategies for this rare and aggressive subtype.
