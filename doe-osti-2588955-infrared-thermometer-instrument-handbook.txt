1.0 Instrument Description
 The infrared thermometer (IRT) is a ground-based radiation pyrometer that provides measurements of the brightness
 temperature of the scene in its field of view. The ground IRT (sometimes referred to as the upwelling instrument) lens has
 a wide field of view for measuring the radiating temperature of the ground surface. It measures in a narrow range of the
 infrared band, details of which can be found in Table 2. It provides data to the user averaged to 1-minute resolution.

 The infrared thermometer for sea-surface temperature (IRTSST) is a ship-based radiation pyrometer that provides
 measurements of the temperature of the sea surface. The upwelling infrared emission is determined with two different
 IRTs for over-ocean field campaigns and the data is at 1-second temporal resolution.

 The IRTs are mounted at heights varying between 2 and 42 m above the ground, oriented so the mounting platform and
 other human made objects are not in the field of view, and also to ensure that the ground and vegetation cover are
 representative of the local area. The Heitronics IRT is generally mounted inside a small enclosure, and the Apogee IRT is
 shielded by a cylindrical enclosure that is part of the design from the manufacturer.

1.2 Instrument/Measurement Theory
Earth’s atmosphere and its surfaces emit radiation in the infrared portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. The IRT
sensors described in this document detect electromagnetic radiation within a certain spectral range (see spectral sensitivity
in Table 2) that is emitted, and to a lesser degree reflected, by these sources. These sensors are called radiation
thermometers, radiation pyrometers, or simply pyrometers. The Heitronics sensor is designed to measure temperature
using the pyroelectric effect and the Apogee sensor design uses a thermopile. Both instruments produce very similar
outputs when measuring ground surfaces (Krishnan et al. 2020).

The Heitronics IRT sensors use an optical chopper (mechanical blades driven by an electric motor) that periodically
interrupts the incident radiation from the measured target to the detector. During each interruption the detector is exposed
to an internal blackbody reference source having a known temperature. Infrared detectors of the pyroelectric type must be
operated with the “chopped radiation” method because they respond to radiation differences and not to absolute radiation
intensities. The detector intercepts infrared radiation emitted by the target and, in short succession, radiation emitted by
the blackbody reference. The blackbody reference will be at approximately the same temperature as the pyrometer’s
housing. During the short chopping cycles, in the millisecond range, the temperature of the pyrometer's housing does not
change. The bias is thus eliminated and substituted by the reference signal, which can be easily measured or controlled
within the specified reference accuracy over the operational ambient temperature range. Thus, the “chopped radiation”
method eliminates thermal drift and automatically provides a modulated signal with a precisely defined frequency.

Both Apogee and Heitronics sensors are factory-calibrated using blackbody calibration sources with emissivity values
very near 1.0. Brightness temperature is the temperature that a blackbody (emissivity = 1.0) would have at a given
radiance value. A full description of brightness temperature can be found in textbooks such as Grant Petty’s A First
Course In Atmospheric Radiation. Since the calibration sources function as near-perfect blackbodies, the IRT sensors are
calibrated to output brightness temperatures. This means the sensors assume all measured targets have an emissivity of
1.0. Users must determine the target emissivity and apply appropriate corrections to convert brightness temperature
readings to actual surface temperatures. Currently the emissivity setting (1.0) of each IRT unit are recorded in the global
attributes of the output NetCDF files.

 2.2 Data Quality and Uncertainty
 The ARM Data Quality Office produces plots, tables, and other tools that contain techniques used by data quality
 analysts, instrument mentors, and site scientists to monitor and diagnose data quality.

 On a regular basis, student analysts from the DQ office inspect plots of the data from the IRTs at the ARM sites.
 The instrument mentors correct problems as they arise and periodically inspect data. Time series plots are created
 to compare the surface temperature measured by the IRT and the temperature from the precision infrared
 radiometer (PIR) after it has been converted from irradiance to temperature. Data Quality Problem Reports
 (DQPRs) and Data Quality Reports (DQRs) are submitted when needed. Data Quality Reports are provided to
 the user when data is downloaded, and they provide the time spans and variables where data quality is known to
 have issues or may have issues.

 Most datastream fields contain a corresponding, sample-by-sample automated quality check field in the b1-level
 datastreams. These flags are named qc_<fieldname>. For example, the sfc_ir_temp field also has a companion
 qc_ sfc_ir_temp field. Descriptions of each quality check can be found in the metadata attributes of the
 companion qc variable, or in the global attributes in some cases. The minimum and maximum thresholds for the
 ground surface temperatures will vary by site and sensor manufacturer. Users should look at the NetCDF files for
 these details.

4.0 Maintenance Plan
 For both the Heitronics and Apogee sensors, fine dust is removed from the lens by means of isopropyl alcohol and a
 micro-fiber cloth or lens tissue. For Apogee only, a cotton swab and isopropyl alcohol can be used, and is the method

 6

 recommended by the vendor. For very dirty lenses, caution must be used when cleaning to avoid scratching the lens. For
 contamination that cannot be cleaned with the isopropyl alcohol method, acetone may be used as a last resort for both
 types of sensors. For the Heitronics sensor, removal of the lens is required for this step because acetone is not compatible
 with O-ring seals in the lens assembly. The lens removal needs to be done in a clean environment, and the mentor should
 be contacted prior to using this method.

5.0 Calibration Plan
 Calibration of the Apogee and Heitronics IRTs are done through two methods; a field calibration check at a single
 temperature done between all AMF deployments, and full calibration at the vendors done on a regular, but less frequent,
 basis. Based on ARM experience with the Heitronics IRT, the calibration has been found to be relatively stable over time.
 The Apogee instrument is somewhat new to ARM, and so its calibration drift will be monitored and the calibration plan
 adjusted accordingly.

 5.1 Field Calibration Checks
 Between each AMF deployment, the calibration of ground IRT sensors not being sent to the vendors are checked at
 ambient temperature with a Palmer-Wahl plate source blackbody. This is done in the field or at the Los Alamos Staging
 Facility. The Palmer-Wahl model (HSICBB-P) floats with ambient temperature and so the check is done at the
 temperature of the surrounding environment. The Palmer-Wahl has a stated accuracy of +/- 0.2 ℃. While its emissivity
 (0.95) is relatively low compared to higher-quality calibration sources, this method is only used to verify that no major
 changes have occurred in the calibration of the sensor.

 5.2 Vendor Calibration
 Vendor calibration of the Heitronics IRTs on a routine basis started in July 2025 and it is planned to send each unit back
 on approximately a three-year cycle. Users can reference the previous IRT handbook for calibration methods prior to
 2025. Heitronics sensors are sent to Wintronics, a U.S. representative for Heitronics. Wintronics uses a Heitronics ME-30
 or equivalent as the calibration source and target for both the transfer radiation thermometer (TRT) and the unit under test.
 By comparing the TRT reading against the unit being tested, the calibration can be checked and updated if needed. The
 calibration checks are currently performed at -10 ℃, 10 ℃, 30 ℃, 50 ℃, and 100 ℃ target temperatures.

 For the Apogee sensors, the plan is for them to be returned to Apogee for recalibration. We anticipate that they will follow
 a similar schedule as the Heitronics sensors, but it will depend on how much the units drift over time.

6.0 User Notes and Known Issues
 An apparent warm bias of the surface temperature is exhibited by the upwelling IRT, compared to the PIR, at sites where
 the instrument is mounted on a tower 10 m above the ground, especially in summer. The PIR responds strongly to air
 temperature while the IRT responds strongly to ground temperature. The bias goes away or becomes very small at night
 because the ground temperature approaches equilibrium with the air. The bias is also small when it is raining or very
 overcast (as indicated by low solar values). The bias becomes large during the day when the sun is out. For example, on
 sunny days at TWP/CF3, the ground temperature as measured by the IRT was about 51°C while the PIR measured a
 temperature about 10°C cooler. The actual air temperature at Darwin in the afternoon is about 32°C. So it appears that the
 PIR was reporting a temperature approximately mid-way between the ground temperature and the air temperature. This

 7

 effect was also experienced at Manus and Nauru, but it was more pronounced at Darwin because the radiometers were
 further from the ground.

 The internal reference temperature of the upwelling IRTs at AMF3 (Oliktok Point: OLI/M1) and North Slope of Alaska
 (NSA C1) in winter is frequently less than the minimum measurable value of -20°C.

7.0 Frequently Asked Questions
 What is the difference between the skin temperature, the brightness temperature measured by upwelling IRTs,
 and the radiative temperature determined with the upwelling PIRs?

 The skin temperature is the actual temperature of the ground surface, as measured with a thermometry device. The
 brightness temperature is the irradiance measured by the IRT and assumes the ground surface acts as a blackbody, which
 means an emissivity of 1. As such, conversion from brightness temperature to skin temperature needs to be done by the
 user. The upwelling PIRs measure longwave irradiance and this measurement can be converted to brightness temperature
 with the Stefan-Boltzmann law.

 In what datastream are the surface temperature measurements at the SGP Central Facility?

 The surface IR temperature data on the 10m tower are found in datastream ‘sgpirt10mC1.b1’ from 1996-04-16 to present.
 The surface IR temperature data on the 25m tower can be found in ‘sgpmfrirt25mC1.b1’ from 2001-04-01 to 2001-09-26,
 under the variable ‘sfc_ir_temp’. From 2001-10-09 until present, the 25m temperature data can be found in
 ‘sgpirt25mC1.b1’.
