DIFFRACT: Neuralized Utility Maximization for Wireless Networks by Differentiable Programming
Chee Wei Tan 1 , Siya Chen 2

Abstract
Next-generation wireless networks, including satellite-to-Open RAN systems, demand agile and intelligent resource management capable of handling dynamic multi-user interference under stochastic quality of service constraints. This paper introduces DIFFRACT, a neuralized utility maximization framework that leverages differentiable programming to integrate deep learning with optimization in wireless networks. Central to our approach is the exploitation of the mathematical structure of standard interference functions, which are foundational in wireless power control. By developing a duality theory for these functions, we map iterative interference management algorithms into differentiable neural network architectures via algorithm unrolling. This enables distributed, end-to-end gradient-based learning at the network edge, supporting real-time adaptation to interference in both terrestrial and non-terrestrial environments. DIFFRACT allows for scalable and robust utility maximization by modeling complex channel dynamics and leveraging the expressiveness of differentiable models. Experimental results confirm the framework’s theoretical soundness and practical effectiveness for next-generation wireless systems.

II
Interference Framework and System Model

II-A

Standard Interference Function

We revisit the standard interference function framework from
[
48
]
, revealing its underlying monotone operator structure and differentiability properties. This enables a new class of data-driven algorithms via differentiable programming.

Definition 1
(Standard Interference Function
[
48
]
)

\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is a standard interference function if, for all
\mathbf{p}\geq 0
, the following properties are satisfied:

1.

(Monotonicity) If
\mathbf{p}_{1}\geq\mathbf{p}_{2}
, then
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}_{1})\geq\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}_{2})
.

2.

(Scalability) For any
\alpha>1,\alpha\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})>\mathbf{I}(\alpha\mathbf{p})
.

Lemma 1

[
48
]

If
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{p}
is feasible,
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
has a unique fixed point
\mathbf{p}^{*}
, which can be computed by the iteration
\mathbf{p}(t+1)=\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}(t))
with geometric convergence rate.

Lemma 2

Positivity and concavity of
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
implies scalability of
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
.

To see this, note that for all
\alpha>1
, we have
\alpha\mathbf{I}(\bm{p})=\alpha\mathbf{I}(1/\alpha\cdot(\alpha\bm{p})+(1-1/\alpha)\cdot\bm{0})\geq\alpha\left((1/\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{I}(\alpha\bm{p})+(1-1/\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{I}(\bm{0})\right)>\alpha\cdot(1/\alpha)\cdot\mathbf{I}(\alpha\bm{p})+\bm{0}=\mathbf{I}(\alpha\bm{p})
where the last two inequalities are due to concavity and positivity respectively.
However, the converse does not hold; that is, positivity and scalability do not imply concavity. A counterexample is
\mathbf{I}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=p_{i}^{2}+3
if
0\leq p_{i}\leq 1
and
\mathbf{I}_{i}(\mathbf{p})=2p_{i}+2
if
p_{i}>1
.

We first state a new result for the standard interference function framework in
[
48
]
with applications to utility maximization in the next section. Suppose that
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is differentiable and concave in
\mathbf{p}
. Denote the derivative of
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
by:

\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})=\left(\frac{\partial I_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{\partial p_{j}}\right)_{L\times L}.

(1)

Observe that
\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is an irreducible nonnegative matrix whose entries are nonnegative and continuous in
\mathbf{p}
. The feasibility of
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{p}
can be established by the following necessary and sufficient condition.

Theorem 1

If
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is differentiable and concave, then
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{p}
is feasible if and only if
\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}))<1
for some
\mathbf{p}\geq\bm{0}
.

Definition 2

\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is a log-log convex standard interference function, if it fulfills the condition of standard interference function and, in addition, in the logarithmic domain, i.e.,
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\log\mathbf{p}
,
\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
is log-convex in
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
.

We study a broad class of concave and log-log convex standard interference functions that subsume all known cases and underpin wireless utility maximization
[
48
,
13
]
. Notably, as
\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
is convex and monotone,
\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
can be treated as a
monotone operator
established as follows.

Theorem 2
(Interference Function as Monotone Operator)

Let
I:\mathbb{R}^{L}_{++}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}_{++}
be a standard interference function that is continuously differentiable, positive, monotone and scalable. Define
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{L}
by

\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}-\log\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
. Then
\Phi
is monotone (e.g., see
[
5
]
), i.e.,
\left(\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1})-\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2})\right)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2}\right)\geq 0
for all
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
.

Let us sketch the proof outline. Define
\Psi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=\log\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
, so
\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}-\Psi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})
. The Jacobian is

\nabla\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=I_{L}-\nabla\Psi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}),

where
I_{L}
is identity matrix, and by the chain rule with
\mathbf{q}=e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}
,

\nabla\Psi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}})=\text{diag}(\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{q}))^{-1}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{q})\cdot\text{diag}(\mathbf{q}).

Now, the symmetric part of
\nabla\Phi
is
\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Phi+\nabla\Phi^{T})=I_{L}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Psi+\nabla\Psi^{T})
. Since
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{q})
is monotone and differentiable,
\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{q})
is positive semi-definite, and
\text{diag}(\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{q}))^{-1}
,
\text{diag}(\mathbf{q})
are positive definite. Thus,
\nabla\Psi
has a positive semi-definite symmetric part, so
I_{L}-\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\Psi+\nabla\Psi^{T})
is positive semi-definite, implying that
\Phi
is monotone, i.e.,
\left(\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1})-\Phi(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2})\right)^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1}-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2}\right)\geq 0
for all
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{1},\tilde{\mathbf{p}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}

[
5
]
.

Theorem
2
enables the use of operator splitting techniques such as proximal point or forward-backward methods in
[
6
,
5
]
to solve equations of the form
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}=\log\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}})
with guaranteed convergence. An example is Nesterov’s adaptive step-size rule update
[
6
]
:

\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k+1)=\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)+\alpha_{k}(\log\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)})-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)),

(2)

with
\alpha_{k}=\frac{2}{1+\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)})}
where the distance between one and the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of the Jacobian,
\delta
(i.e.,
1-\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}))
as the Jacobian is nonnegative) governs the convergence rate
[
6
,
5
]
. The error after
k
iterations of (
2
) satisfies the bound
[
6
,
5
]
:
\|\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}^{\star}\|_{2}\leq C(1-\delta)^{k}=C\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}(k)}))^{k},
for some positive constant
C
determined by the initialization. This is as competitive as the fixed-point iteration in Lemma
1
! This operator perspective enables differentiable programming
[
7
]
to address a broad class of wireless network optimization efficiently, as shown in Fig.

II-B

System Model

Consider the system model of a wireless network with
L
users, each comprising a transmitter-receiver pair, communicating simultaneously over a shared spectrum. The vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{L})^{\top}
denotes the allocation of transmit power, where
p_{l}
represents the transmit power of the
l
th user. Let
\mathbf{G}=[G_{lj}]_{l,j=1}^{L}>0_{L\times L}
represent the wireless channel gain, where
G_{lj}
is the channel gain from the
j
th transmitter to the
l
th receiver, and
n=(n_{1},\dots,n_{L})^{\top}>\mathbf{0}
, where
n_{l}
is the noise power at the
l
th user. We define the Signal-to-Interference-and-Noise Ratio (SINR) at the
l
th receiver as the ratio of the received signal power to the combined interference and noise power under frequency-flat fading, given by:

\textup{SINR}_{l}(\mathbf{p})=\displaystyle\frac{G_{ll}p_{l}}{\sum_{j=1,j\neq l}^{L}G_{lj}p_{j}+n_{l}}.

(3)

Next, let us define a nonnegative vector:

\mathbf{v}=\left(\frac{n_{1}}{G_{11}},\frac{n_{2}}{G_{22}},\dots,\frac{n_{L}}{G_{LL}}\right)^{T},

and a nonnegative matrix
\mathbf{F}
with entries:

F_{lj}=\left\{\begin{matrix}0,&\mbox{if}\;\;l=j\\
G_{lj}/G_{ll},&\mbox{if}\;\;l\neq j\end{matrix}\right..

(4)

Moreover, we assume that
\mathbf{F}
is irreducible, i.e., each link has at least one interferer. Then the SINR of the
l
th user can be rewritten as:

\textup{SINR}_{l}(\mathbf{p})=\displaystyle\frac{p_{l}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}F_{lj}p_{j}+v_{l}}.

(5)

Suppose each
\textup{SINR}l(\mathbf{p})
must exceed a reliability threshold
\gamma_{l}
, i.e.,
\textup{SINR}l(\mathbf{p})\geq\gamma_{l}
. This constraint induces the well-known standard interference function
[
48
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{l}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\gamma_{l}F_{lj}p_{j}+\gamma_{l}v_{l}\leq p_{l}\quad\forall,l,

(6)

which is feasible if and only if
\rho(\text{diag}(\bm{\gamma})\mathbf{F})<1
, as established in Theorem
1
. When the wireless channels experience fading (e.g., Rayleigh fading, Rician fading or Nakagami fading), the received power from the
j
th transmitter at the
l
th receiver is given by
G_{lj}h_{lj}p_{j}
where
h_{lj}
is a random variable reflecting the fading environment. The SINR of the
l
th user is a random variable in terms of the channel realization:

\textup{SINR}_{l}(\mathbf{p})=\displaystyle\frac{h_{ll}p_{l}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}F_{lj}h_{lj}p_{j}+v_{l}}.

(7)

An outage occurs when the received SINR of the
l
th user falls below
\gamma_{l}
, a minimum SINR threshold for reliable communication. This means that when
\operatorname{SINR}_{l}(\mathbf{p})\geq\gamma_{l}
, the transmission at the
l
th receiver is successful; otherwise, the transmission fails. Denote the outage probability of the
l
th receiver/transmitter pair for a power vector
\mathbf{p}
by
O_{l}(\mathbf{p})
and the outage constraint for the
l
th user by
\bar{O}_{l}
. The link reliability function of the
l
th user is given as the complement of the outage probability:

\displaystyle O_{l}(\mathbf{p})=\operatorname{Prob}(\textup{SINR}_{l}(\mathbf{p})<\gamma_{l})\leq\bar{O}_{l}.

(8)

While a closed-form standard interference function expression for (
8
) is available under Rayleigh fading (e.g., see
[
45
]
), such expressions are generally intractable for other fading conditions, particularly atmospheric fading in space-to-ground communication scenarios
[
31
,
14
,
10
]
. This motivates the development of a framework that can algorithmically handle implicit interference functions for utility maximization under these more complex channel conditions.

III
Utility Maximization with log-log convex Interference Function

In this section, we study a utility maximization problem with log-log convex differentiable interference function constraints. We adopt an operator perspective by exploiting the problem’s duality, which yields fast fixed-point algorithms. Differentiability of these operators then enables a differentiable programming framework to learning implicit models and paving the way for
neuralized utility maximization
.

III-A

Utility Maximization and Iterative Algorithm

Let
u(\mathbf{p})
denote the network utility, representing a metric of network quality-of-service. The power vector
\mathbf{p}=[p_{1},\ldots,p_{L}]^{\top}
is adjusted to maximize this utility, subject to interference constraints
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{p}
. Consider the wireless utility maximization problem:

maximize

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{p})

subject to

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})\leq\mathbf{p}.

(9)

Letting
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\log\mathbf{p}
, we assume that the utility function
u(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
is concave and monotonically increasing in terms of
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
. One example is the minimum of a series of competitive utility functions that satisfy the following assumptions
[
50
]
.

Definition 3
(Competitive Utility Functions
[
50
]
)

1.

Competitiveness: For all
i
, the utility
u_{i}(\mathbf{p})
is strictly increasing with respect to
p_{i}
and strictly decreasing with respect to
p_{j}
for all
j\neq i
, whenever
p_{i}>0
.

2.

Directional Monotonicity: For any
s>1
and
\mathbf{p}>\mathbf{0}
,
u_{i}(s\mathbf{p})>u_{i}(\mathbf{p})
for all
i
.

3.

u_{i}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})
is concave and monotonically increasing for all
i
.

Now, taking a logarithmic transformation of the variables, (
III-A
) can be reformulated as:

maximize

\displaystyle u(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})

subject to

\displaystyle\log(\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}))\leq\tilde{\mathbf{p}},

(10)

which reduces to a convex optimization problem, thus allowing (
III-A
) to be solved optimally. Suppose the constraints in (
III-A
) are feasible and there exists an
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}
that is strictly feasible, i.e., Slater’s condition
[
9
]
is satisfied. Let us introduce the dual variable
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{L}
and form the partial Lagrangian of (
III-A
) as:

\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\mathbf{p}},\bm{\lambda})=-u(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}(\log(\mathbf{I}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}}))-\tilde{\mathbf{p}}).

Applying the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) stationarity condition
[
9
]
and transforming back to the original variables, we obtain the following key result.

Theorem 3

The optimal solution
\mathbf{p}^{*}
of (
III-A
) and the optimal dual solution
\bm{\lambda}^{*}
of (
III-A
) satisfy:

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{l}^{*}}{p_{l}^{*}}=-\frac{\partial u(\mathbf{p}^{*})}{\partial p_{l}}+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\frac{\lambda_{j}^{*}}{p_{j}^{*}}\frac{\partial I_{j}(\mathbf{p}^{*})}{\partial p_{l}}\;\;\forall\;l.

(11)

Furthermore, the following iterations compute
\mathbf{p}^{*}
in (
III-A
) and
\bm{\lambda}^{*}
in (
III-A
) from any positive initial points
\mathbf{p}(0)
and
\mathbf{x}(0)
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}(t+1)=\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}(t)),

(12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}(t+1)=-\nabla u(\mathbf{p}(t))+\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}(t))\mathbf{x}(t),

(13)

and

\bm{\lambda}(t+1)=\mathbf{p}(t+1)\circ\mathbf{x}(t+1)

if and only if
\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}))<1
for some
\mathbf{p}\geq\bm{0}
.

Remark 1

The fact that the spectral radius of the Jacobian matrix
\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}(t))
is strictly less than one for any
\mathbf{p}(t)
(as shown in Theorem
1
), has been established in
[
1
]
from a control-theoretic perspective based on studying the existence of a fixed point and its convergence behaviour, which are established using the contractive interference function findings in
[
19
,
18
]
. Theorem
3
offers an alternative and practical interpretation of this result by linking Theorem
1
to the use of iterative algorithms for solving optimization problems.

III-B

Convex Relaxation to Utility Maximization

When the problem (
III-A
) is infeasible, we consider an approximate power solution that relaxes the original constraints. Specifically, we introduce the following relaxation of (
III-A
):

maximize

\displaystyle u(\mathbf{p})

subject to

\displaystyle\prod_{l=1}^{L}\left(\frac{I_{l}(\mathbf{p})}{{p}_{l}}\right)^{w_{l}}\leq 1,

(14)

where
\bm{w}=[w_{1},\cdots,w_{L}]^{\top}
is a positive vector that is a convex combination of the individual constraints in (
III-A
) and satisfying
\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_{l}=1
and
w_{l}\geq 0
. By letting
\tilde{\mathbf{p}}=\log\mathbf{p}
, (
III-B
) can be solved by the following convex optimization problem:

maximize

\displaystyle u(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{L}w_{l}\left(\log I_{l}(e^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})-\tilde{p}_{l}\right)\leq 0.

(15)

Remark 2

The term
\prod_{l=1}^{L}\bigl(I_{l}(\mathbf{p})\bigr)^{w_{l}}
in (
III-B
) is concave as it is the weighted geometric mean of concave nonnegative functions, which preserves concavity under vector composition
[
9
]
. If (
III-A
) is indeed feasible, then by Theorem
1
and Theorem
3
, setting
\mathbf{w}=\mathbf{p}^{\star}\circ\mathbf{x}^{\star}
in (
III-B
) recovers the solution
\mathbf{p}^{\star}
of (
III-A
), and the relaxation is tight.

III-C

Relationship to Nonlinear Perron-Frobenius Theory

We remark that an interesting duality arises between the algorithms for computing

\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{x}
, which can be understood via nonlinear Perron–Frobenius theory
[
46
,
50
,
49
,
27
,
28
]
:
the vectors
\mathbf{p}
and

can be viewed as Perron–Frobenius eigenvectors of suitably constructed nonlinear operators.

Theorem 4
(Perron–Frobenius Theorem Characterization)

Let
\mathbf{I}:\mathbb{R}^{L}_{+}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}^{L}_{+}
be a differentiable concave standard interference mapping and let
u:\mathbb{R}^{L}_{+}\!\to\!\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable utility function. Suppose
\mathbf{p}^{\star}
satisfies
\mathbf{p}^{\star}=\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})
and
\rho(\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star}))<1
. By nonlinear Perron–Frobenius theory, such a map is nonexpansive in Hilbert’s projective metric

d_{H}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}):=\log\frac{\max_{i}p_{i}/q_{i}}{\min_{i}p_{i}/q_{i}}
, and admits a unique fixed point
p^{\star}
up to scaling, which minimizes the projective diameter of the orbit of
\mathbf{I}(\cdot)
. The fixed point
\mathbf{p}^{\star}=\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})
plays the role of the nonlinear Perron–Frobenius eigenvector, characterized by a Collatz–Wielandt–type formula

\inf_{\mathbf{p}>\mathbf{0}}\max_{i}\frac{I_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{p_{i}}\;=\;\sup_{\mathbf{p}>0}\min_{i}\frac{I_{i}(\mathbf{p})}{p_{i}}\;=\;1.

Furthermore,
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
in Theorem
3
satisfies

\mathbf{x}^{\star}=\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})\,\mathbf{x}^{\star}-\nabla u(\mathbf{p}^{\star})
, or equivalently,

\mathbf{x}^{\star}=\bigl(\bm{I}_{L}-\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(-\nabla u(\mathbf{p}^{\star})\bigr).

Moreover, since
\mathbf{I}(\cdot)
is concave, the Jacobian mapping
\mathbf{p}\mapsto\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})
is order-preserving and concave, and the induced operator
\mathbf{x}\mapsto\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})\mathbf{x}
belongs to the class of concave Perron–Frobenius operators. Consequently,
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
lies in the Perron–Frobenius cone of
\nabla\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p}^{\star})
.

The special case where
\nabla u(\mathbf{p}^{\star})=-\mathbf{w}^{T}\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{I}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}
leads to a solution
\mathbf{p}^{\star}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\star}
that recovers the
uplink-downlink duality
and yields a PageRank-like structure, as shown in
[
44
]
. Also, the Collatz-Wielandt-type objective function is a restatement of scalability in Definition 1
[
36
]
.

Remark 3

\mathbf{I}(\bm{e}^{\tilde{\mathbf{p}}})

VI
Conclusion

This paper presents DIFFRACT, a general differentiable programming framework for utility maximization in wireless networks, particularly effective in managing resources under unpredictable interference, as commonly encountered in satellite-terrestrial systems. Our work reveals new duality results by establishing the differentiability and monotone operator characterization of standard interference functions. This provides a nonlinear Perron-Frobenius operator perspective that enables smooth integration into differentiable programming and modern deep learning frameworks with efficient automatic differentiation. DIFFRACT models complex interference patterns–such as outage in atmospheric fading, which is critical for space-to-ground communication–within an
end-to-end trainable, AI-native stack
by unrolling a utility maximization algorithm with implicit interference constraints into differentiable primal-dual updates. Experiments show DIFFRACT offers theoretically sound performance and scalability for AI-native resource control in wireless network optimization.
