Executive Summary
Northwest Colorado faces a potential economic gap from the loss of tax revenue, labor income,
and jobs within the region due to the planned closures of the Craig and Hayden Power Stations
and related mining operations at Trapper and Colowyo mines. Existing coal power plants
provided 43% of 2023 property tax revenue for Moffat County and accounted for 437 jobs.
Together, the Trapper and Colowyo mines provided another 9% of Moffat County tax revenue
(England 2024).

The U.S. Department of Energy Communities Local Energy Action Program connects coalitions
of community stakeholders with a technical assistance provider network that provides expertise
and support to take advantage of energy-related economic development pathways. The
Northwest Colorado Coalition (Coalition) sought technical assistance to inform planning for
future energy development and deepen their understanding of the economic implications of
various regional energy development scenarios. The Coalition was specifically interested in
existing coal plant conversion and siting of new energy facilities that can contribute to local job
creation, job retention, economic development, and reliable tax revenue, including carbon
capture and storage derived from coal plant conversion to natural gas, geothermal, hydrogen,
advanced nuclear, and wind and solar with storage.

Analysis of opportunities for advanced nuclear, and carbon capture, utilization, and storage were
conducted by the Gateway for Innovation in Advance Nuclear and Los Alamos National
Laboratory, respectively. NLR provided technical assistance to the Coalition to aid in their
exploration of three energy scenarios—enhanced geothermal systems (EGS), solar-powered
hydrogen, and land-based wind--and opportunities to expand the local economic base in
anticipation of retiring coal power plants and mines. This effort was conducted during March
2024 to August 2025 and does not include subsequent policy, technology or legal changes.

The scope of NLR’s technical assistance began with an assessment of community background
and energy-related goals for northwest Colorado. Researchers then conducted technoeconomic
and workforce analyses of regional energy generation options across Moffat, Routt, Rio Blanco,
and Garfield counties using NLR’s Energy Workforce Impact and Regional Economic
Development (WIRED), Jobs and Economic Development Impact (JEDI), Workforce Readiness
Index, and job transferability tools. Figure ES-1 illustrates the location of the four counties
studied, outlined in black.

NLR’s WIRED and JEDI tools were used to determine economic impacts of the three modeled
energy technologies. To benchmark the potential economic impacts, the analysis modeled the
economic and workforce impact of 1,700 Megawatts ([MW] or 1.7 Gigawatts [GW]) of
enhanced geothermal system, solar-powered hydrogen, and wind, a capacity equivalent to
current coal generation in the region. The impacts presented within the report analysis is not a
direct comparison to existing coal jobs or economic impacts but rather is illustrative of potential
impacts associated with three different energy scenarios—EGS, solar-powered hydrogen, and
land-based wind--at the scale of the existing Craig and Hayden coal power plants.
Each technology was modeled and evaluated independently. The northwest Colorado-specific
results show the economic impacts that could be generated with distinct profiles in terms of
intensity, sectoral distribution, and investment requirements for each technology scenario. Figure
ES-2 shows a summary of annual results including annual cost, jobs, value added, earnings, and
tax revenue by technology during the construction phase or operations phase to reach 1.7 GW.
To reach the 1.7 GW of generation capacity, the modeled technologies would be replicated,
either as a stand-alone deployment or in combination with the other technology scenarios.

The enhanced geothermal system scenario’s highest economic impact is during the construction
phase and the highest annual job creation during operations. It also provides the greatest overall
economic contribution in terms of output, employment, earnings, and tax revenues. However,
this technology also entails the largest upfront investment. The solar-powered hydrogen scenario
creates a moderate level of jobs compared to EGS - supporting a more varied occupational base
and spreads economic benefits across a wide range of sectors. The range of occupations involved
highlights the potential of hydrogen to drive demand for construction, professional, and technical
services alike. The land-based wind scenario provides the lowest job opportunities, either within
the construction or operation phase, when compared to EGS and solar-powered hydrogen.

The occupations in highest demand across all three technologies were construction,
transportation and warehousing, and professional and technical services, underlining the
importance of broad industry supply-chain linkages needed for development of these three
technologies.

Beyond economic impacts, the WIRED analysis compared workforce skills needed to support
these technologies. The study applied Input-Output analysis, a widely used framework for
measuring how a change in demand, such as the construction or operations of an energy facility,
ripples through a regional economy. The Input-Output analysis modeled the economy as an
interconnected network of sectors that trade goods and services with one another, with
households and governments, and with external markets.

Based on job demand insights from WIRED, the Workforce Readiness Index assessed the
likelihood that a county possesses the workforce development infrastructure necessary to support
the labor pool required for each of the three proposed technologies. Workforce readiness is

 vii

defined from four perspectives: workforce availability (i.e., ability to recruit from the general
labor market), transportation access (i.e., evaluated local transportation networks to determine
ease of workers to get to job and training sites), transferable skills (i.e., concentration of required
skill sets), and program availability (i.e., local training programs for the target industry)
(McDowell et al. 2025).

Overall, the Workforce Readiness Analysis found that the four-county region has sufficient labor
supply but faces infrastructure frictions—most notably below-average transportation access and
below-average workforce supports (education and training programs) that apply across all three
of the modeled technologies. These frictions limit job skill matching, workforce mobility within
the region, and training uptake in all scenarios. Location quotient analysis indicates Moffat and
Rio Blanco have stronger transferable capacity of workers, especially for geothermal, compared
to Routt and Garfield counties. The availability of education and training resources within
northwest Colorado counties is limited for the modeled energy technologies. Some educational
and training opportunities are available at the state level, though these are located further from
the counties in question.

The job transferability analysis further uses WIRED outputs to assess how easily the current coal
power plant and mining workforce could support the three energy technologies by comparing
worker skill sets. Across the three energy technologies, solar-powered hydrogen shows the
highest potential opportunity for transferring workers through readily available training for
reskilling, but it also exhibits the largest skill and knowledge gaps. Land-based wind creates
roles that could largely be staffed by a small subset of the existing workforce, yet its overall
potential transfer opportunity is low because only a few displaced workers map clearly into those
roles based on existing skills and knowledge. Geothermal aligns most closely with the
workforce’s average skills and knowledge, but occupational matching is modest, leaving several
critical roles underfilled.

Finally, this report’s appendices summarize Colorado coal facilities (Appendix A), data from the
WIRED analysis (Appendix B), state and regional workforce resources (Appendix C), and a
summary of findings from community-led public surveys of energy in northwest Colorado
(Appendix D).

Background
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Communities Local Energy Action Program
(Communities LEAP) provides customized technical assistance to 30 communities across the
United States as they develop strategies to facilitate long-term economic benefits through locally
driven energy planning and development. Communities LEAP connects community coalitions of
businesses, nonprofits, and state, local, and Tribal governments with an extensive technical
assistance provider network that provides expertise and support to take advantage of energy-
related economic development pathways.

Northwest Colorado faces a potential economic gap from the loss of tax revenue, labor income,
and jobs within the region due to the planned closures of the Craig and Hayden Power Stations
and related mining operations at Trapper and Colowyo mines. Existing coal power plants
provided 43% of 2023 property tax revenue for Moffat County and accounted for 437 jobs.
Together, the Trapper and Colowyo mines provided another 9% of Moffat County tax revenue
(England 2024).

The Northwest Colorado Coalition (Coalition)—comprised of the Northwest Colorado Energy
Initiative and the Joint Organizations Leading Transition (JOLT)—applied for technical
assistance under the DOE Communities LEAP. The Coalition sought technical assistance to
inform planning for future energy development and deepen their understanding of the economic
implications of various regional energy development scenarios. The Coalition’s objectives
include supporting local energy planning, identifying opportunities for new and diversified
energy industries, and developing strategies to retain a skilled regional workforce.

Northwest Colorado Energy Initiative serves as the lead technical assistance recipient and
operates under the purview of the Associated Governments of Northwest Colorado (AGNC),
which is a council of governments and a designated Economic Development District. AGNC
represents the northwestern Colorado counties of Garfield, Mesa, Moffat, and Rio Blanco and
associated municipalities therein (Battlement Mesa, Collbran, Craig, DeBeque, Dinosaur, Fruita,
Glenwood Springs, Grand Junction, Hayden, Meeker, New Castle, Palisade, Parachute, Rangely,
Rifle, Silt, and Yampa).

JOLT is an ad hoc committee formed in 2022 by current and former state and local elected
officials and a university president. JOLT’s mission is to advocate, communicate, and educate on
behalf of coal impacted communities, encourage the Colorado General Assembly to direct more
resources from the Colorado Office of Just Transitions to those communities, and promote an
“all-of-the-above” approach including nuclear, carbon capture, natural gas, geothermal, biomass,
wind, solar, and hydrogen technologies.

The Coalition’s technical assistance efforts focused on identifying new opportunities for local
job creation, job retention, economic diversification, and stable tax revenue, including carbon
capture and storage, geothermal, hydrogen, advanced nuclear, and wind and solar with storage.
To support these goals, NLR provided technical assistance through Communities LEAP in three
primary areas:
 1. Assessing community background, goals, and the state policy and regulatory context.

 1

 2. Conducting technoeconomic and workforce analyses of regional energy generation
 options across Moffat, Routt, Rio Blanco, and Garfield counties using NLR’s Workforce
 Impact and Regional Economic Development (WIRED), Jobs and Economic
 Development Impact (JEDI), Jobs Transferability Analysis, and Workforce Readiness
 Index tools.
 3. Developing case studies and resources highlighting other coal community strategies,
 including effective practices, funding mechanisms, and community engagement
 frameworks.
In parallel to NLR’s technical assistance, the Gateway for Accelerated Innovation in Nuclear
(GAIN) and Los Alamos National Laboratory conducted analysis of opportunities for advanced
nuclear and carbon capture, utilization, and storage derived from coal plant conversion to natural
gas, respectively. The advanced nuclear report by GAIN evaluated the Craig and Hayden power
plants and sites for co-locating coal plants, data centers, and advanced nuclear technology and
identified potential pros and cons associated with the locations of interest. The carbon capture,
utilization, and storage report by Los Alamos National Laboratory assessed regional natural gas
infrastructure, regional storage locations, carbon dioxide (CO2) capture technologies that could
be deployed at the Craig power plant, commercial options to use or convert CO2 into new
valued-added products, and an evaluation of economic, policy, and environmental
considerations.

Together these analyses and reports are intended to inform energy planning decisions for
Northwest Colorado.

Workforce and Economic Impact Analysis for Three
Energy Technologies
In light of pending Northwest Colorado coal facility closures, this analysis explores the
economic and workforce impacts of different future energy development technologies.

NLR modeled the impact of three energy technologies deployed to provide approximately 1,700
MW of generation capacity, equivalent to the generation capacity from the coal plants at Craig
and Hayden (Appendix A). The analysis provides economic estimates and considers three energy
technologies for Northwest Colorado selected in consultation with the Coalition: an enhanced
geothermal system (EGS) facility, 1 a solar-powered hydrogen plant, 2 and a land-based wind
farm.

The analysis focuses on a cluster of four Northwest Colorado counties most directly affected by
the coal-plant closures: Moffat, Routt, Rio Blanco, and Garfield. By developing economic
estimates of construction and operational impacts, this section provides insights into local
economy impacts and workforce skill alignment and transferability.

The workforce and economic impact analysis section has three distinct elements: first, the
WIRED analysis compared the economic impact of the three technologies. Second, the
workforce assessment used a Workforce Readiness Index to look at job roles and determine how
easily the current workforce could reskill or be ready for a new job. Finally, the job
transferability analysis identified the opportunities and gaps by comparing skill sets and
identifying pathways for implementation like available training programs, educational resources,
job availability or opportunities to upskill.

1
  Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) represent advanced geothermal technologies capable of extracting heat from
deep, dry rock formations in areas where natural water and permeability are insufficient.
2
  Solar-powered hydrogen production involves the use of solar electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen
through electrolysis. The resulting hydrogen can be used directly for electricity generation, industrial processes,
transportation (such as fuel cell vehicles), or stored for future use.

technology-specific cost and workforce estimates and the regional economic structure of
Northwest Colorado.

IMPLAN Data:

 1. 2023 input-output tables for Moffat, Routt, Rio Blanco, and Garfield counties
 2. Regional Purchase Coefficients (RPCs), which capture the share of purchases sourced
 within the county cluster, the rest of the state, and the rest of the United States. See

 3. Wage and Salary Employment Data, used to calculate location quotients and adjust for

Together, these data inputs enable the estimation of economic effects at the local level, ensuring
that results reflect both the technology-specific characteristics of each energy project and the
unique economic structure of the Northwest Colorado county cluster.

Methodology

The modeling estimates the total impact of new energy development on the regional economy.
The analysis relies on IMPLAN modeling software’s input-output county-level data to represent
sectoral linkages and quantify the flow of goods, services, and labor expenditures.

The WIRED model represents the updated and more advanced version of the JEDI model. Both
NLR tools are designed to assess local jobs, workforce, and economic impacts of power
generation facilities and infrastructure, covering both construction (short-term, temporary
effects) and operations (long-term effects). WIRED and JEDI models both include biofuels, coal,
concentrating solar power, geothermal, natural gas, or photovoltaic power plants. This study used
WIRED 3 estimated construction and operation costs for an EGS plant and JEDI to approximate
land-based wind values. For the solar-powered hydrogen facility, the Hydrogen Financial

To conduct this assessment, the study applied input-output analysis, a widely used framework for
measuring how a change in demand, such as the construction or operations of an energy facility,
ripples through a regional economy. The input-output analysis models the economy as an
interconnected network of sectors that trade goods and services with one another, with
households and governments, and with external markets (Figure 3).

 Figure 3. Input-Output Model Methodology

The analysis distinguishes three types of effects on employment and the economy:

 • Direct effects: reflecting the initial investment (e.g., expenditures on turbines and heat
 exchangers).
 • Indirect effects: capturing supply-chain activity (e.g., purchases from suppliers of
 drilling rigs, cement, and specialized engineering services).
 • Induced effects: arising from household spending of wages earned by workers in both
 the project and supplier industries (e.g., on housing, groceries, and healthcare).

Once the construction and operation costs were obtained, these costs were then assigned to their
corresponding economic sectors using 2022 North American Industry Classification System
codes, which were subsequently mapped to the IMPLAN 528 commodity classification. To
ensure comparability in monetary terms, all sector-assigned costs are adjusted to 2023 prices.

The purchasing values in constant-year prices are decomposed into producer costs, transportation
costs, and wholesale/retail margins following the Bureau of Economic Analysis’ 2017 margins
(before redefinitions). This step captures the full set of expenditures across the supply chain
rather than attributing the entire value to the producer. The result of this sequence is an impact
vector in 2023 prices, which provides the standardized input for the subsequent economic impact
analysis.

Methodology Limitations and Gaps

The results presented in this study should be interpreted within the context of the modeling
assumptions and the inherent constraints of the Input–Output analysis. Accordingly, the
estimates provided should be viewed as indicative rather than as precise forecasts.

Several key assumptions underpin the analysis. First, projects of the same technology are
assumed to be built sequentially rather than in parallel and a fixed number of construction years
is defined for each facility. Projects were modeled sequentially to remain consistent with the
WIRED framework and to ensure comparability across scenarios, states, and technologies.
Modeling parallel technology development would require detailed project-level scheduling
information that is not available and would introduce additional assumptions, reduce the
comparability of results and potentially distort the annualized impact estimates. Importantly,
because the economic impacts are reported on an annualized basis, sequential deployment does
not materially affect the cumulative economic impacts. It simply offers a consistent temporal
framework that avoids introducing speculative assumptions about project overlap while still
capturing the total magnitude of impacts.
RPCs from IMPLAN are applied to capture local sourcing of goods and services (Appendix B),
while Wage and Salary Employment data from IMPLAN are used to calculate Location

 6

Quotients. These assumptions, while consistent with standard practice in economic impact
modeling, inevitably influence the magnitude and distribution of the results, and should be kept
in mind when interpreting the findings. For example, local hire requirements would need to be
adopted to ensure jobs are maintained within a preferred geographic area.

The study relies on JEDI and WIRED models, which are project-specific tools that, by design, do
not capture potential economies of scale in large-scale deployment analyses. Moreover, the
analysis employs a static input-output model, reflecting the economy-wide linkages and spending
patterns at the time of analysis for the county cluster of Moffat, Routt, Rio Blanco, and Garfield.
As such, results do not incorporate dynamic changes in the regional economy, such as shifts in
the electricity grid mix, demographic changes, updated policies, or evolving business conditions.

It is also important to emphasize what this study does not assess. The analysis does not evaluate
potential increases in demand for public services (e.g., safety, roads, water treatment, housing,
schools), nor does it consider environmental or cultural impacts, potential spinoff business
development, or industry expansion. Additionally, it does not assess ownership and distribution
of benefits, cost recovery, or the impacts on ratepayers. This study represents a gross economic
impact assessment; it does not account for potential offsetting effects such as the loss of coal-
related employment or other industry contractions.

Taken together, these limitations highlight that the results should be interpreted as broad, order-
of-magnitude indicators of potential economic outcomes, rather than as precise forecasts of
project-level or long-term regional impacts.

Modeled Energy Technology Assumptions

To benchmark the potential economic impacts, the analysis assumes that three technologies will
be deployed, equivalent to approximately 1,700 MW (1.7 GW) of existing power plant capacity.
These three technologies—EGS, land-based wind, and solar-powered hydrogen—were modeled
under standardized deployment assumptions. All projects of the same technology are assumed to
be built sequentially to achieve 1.7 GW, rather than in parallel to provide a consistent framework
for modeling the timing, scale, and economic impacts of the three energy deployment pathways.
Each technology is assessed independently to evaluate the relative economic and workforce
impacts of the modeled technologies.

The EGS scenario models a 400 MW facility that would require three years to construct. The
land-based wind scenario models a 300 MW plant and assumes one year for construction. The
solar-powered hydrogen scenario includes colocation of solar photovoltaics with an electrolyzer
system and is modeled to produce 400 MW and would require five years for construction. Both
the land-based wind and solar-powered hydrogen facility are identified as baseline facilities,
which in this study function as modular plant units, that is, a representative module that can be
replicated to scale up capacity. The total capacity of the target 1.7 GW is obtained by replicating
this modular unit as many times as needed to reach the equivalent generation. Under the modeled
technologies, EGS will take 12 years, wind five years, and the solar-power hydrogen 20 years to
scale fully to 1.7 GW. As the technologies are modeled for economic impacts associated with
scaling of costs, exact sizing of technologies or land requirements estimates are not available as
part of this study.

Definitions of the economic metrics used in the analysis will be expressed in terms of jobs, 5
output, value added, earnings, and tax revenues.

 • Output, which reflects the total value of production, measured as the sales of goods and
 services generated by the project.
 • Value added: representing the wealth generated through wages and benefits, proprietor
 income (e.g., self-employment income), other property income (e.g., rent, interest,
 dividends), and taxes on production (e.g., sales taxes).
 • Jobs: Sum of FTE (2,080 hours/year) workers employed at the business location.
 Includes wage and salary employees as well as proprietors (e.g., self-employed
 individuals, business partners, or cooperative members). Jobs also count positions
 supported locally, even if some workers live outside the study area.
 • Earnings: the portion of value added corresponding to pre-tax employee compensation
 (wages and benefits) and proprietor income (e.g., income of sole proprietorships,
 partnerships, and tax-exempt cooperatives).
 • Tax revenue: which accounts for local, county, and federal taxes collected through the
 production process.

about 57 direct operation FTEs across the cluster of counties for the solar portion of the project,
the rest of the state, and the rest of the United States, equivalent to 0.03 FTE/MW.

To validate these results, we benchmarked them against other utility-scale solar facilities.
Comparable values are reported for other recent large solar-only projects: Agua Caliente (290
MW) in Yuma County, Arizonia; Athos III (224 MW) in Riverside County, California; and
Riverstart Solar Park (200 MW) in Randolph County, Indiana, with operations job intensities
ranging from 0.02 to 0.03 FTE/MW. These comparisons suggest that the operations-phase
employment intensity of the solar component of the solar-powered hydrogen facility analyzed
here is consistent with the range observed in other large-scale solar developments.

Workforce Impacts Assessment
The Workforce Readiness Index, developed by NLR and building on earlier related indexes, is a
structured framework designed to assess a county’s readiness by evaluating the likelihood that it
possesses the workforce development infrastructure necessary to support occupations required by
energy-related industries or sectors. Workforce readiness is defined as the ability to recruit from
the general labor market, transition workers with comparable skill sets, and train a workforce
using scalable or existing training programs near a specific location (McDowell et al. 2025). The
objective of this analysis is to provide data-driven insights to inform targeted local workforce
initiatives and to promote a community-based approach to infrastructure planning and site
selection through a workforce perspective (McDowell et al. 2025).

Within the context of this study, the Workforce Readiness Index provides local decision makers
with a conceptual framework that identifies each county’s relative strengths and weaknesses. For
the three technologies the goal of using the Workforce Readiness Index is to look at different
needed resources for each technology and assess the feasibility of the existing workforce to fill
these new roles.

For example, if a county were to develop a new power generation facility, the Workforce
Readiness Index provides insights into the existing workforce development resources and
potential gaps that may require attention to enhance workforce readiness.

The structure of the Workforce Readiness Index is illustrated in Figure 10 and is composed of
four sub-indices: workforce availability, transportation access, transferable skills, and program
availability.

The index methodology provides a standardized process to assess the workforce readiness level
of a county with respect to the nation or a particular state. A county-level granularity was chosen,

Transferrable Skills and Program Availability

For the transferrable skills sub-indexes, we used the location quotient—a measure that compares
the concentration of an occupation within a region to the national average—to assess the
availability of local capacity (occupations in need) and related occupations (transferrable skills).
Location quotient is frequently used in labor market analyses to identify areas of occupational
specialization or workforce gaps (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics 2022; Yu and Liu 2021).

LQ > 1: The occupation is more concentrated in the region than nationally.

LQ = 1: The occupation has the same concentration as the national average.

LQ < 1: The occupation is less concentrated than the national average.

The “Local Capacity” column in Table 17 represents the average location quotient for all
operation occupations listed on the occupation map associated with the proposed energy
technology—EGS, solar-powered hydrogen, and land-based wind—in each county. The
“Adjacent Occupation Strength” measures the weighted average Location quotient for all related
occupations on the occupation map for the proposed power generation technology in each
county. Related occupations, as defined by O*NET, 10 have skills and expertise that are
transferable to the targeted occupations. The specific methodology for calculating these values
can found in McDowell et al. (2025, section 2.1.3). The methodology takes the five most related
occupations from O*NET and averages their location quotients. A higher location quotient
indicates a greater local workforce possessing the skillsets needed for the proposed new power
generation technology.

Job Transferability Analysis
The goal of the job transferability analysis is to identify potential pathways for individuals in
coal mining and power generation jobs to enter O&M roles at the modeled power generation
facilities. This analysis characterizes the potential for current workers in the region to compete
for job opportunities created by the deployment of new technologies. It also assesses whether
and which knowledge or skill gaps exist across technologies. The job transferability analysis is
composed of three elements: transfer pathway analysis, skills, and knowledge difference. Among
which, transfer pathway analysis is a title-based transfer test provides a yes/no indicator of
whether workers can be placed directly into jobs that match their current occupation. For
workers who cannot be placed directly, the analysis also evaluates related placements by
allocating remaining workers to remaining demand, prioritizing occupations with the largest
shortages. The skills and knowledge different analyses are competency-based, using
Occupational Information Network (O*NET) importance and level ratings normalized from 0 to
1, shows alignment and gaps in underlying skills and knowledge.

Transfer pathways relate to whether current job roles in coal mining industry and power
generation industry could be transferred to direct operation jobs in the proposed new energy
technologies, and what level of training would be required.

The skill and knowledge gap analysis uses the O*NET database, a national catalog of U.S.
occupations that describes each occupation’s tasks, required skills and knowledge, and
proficiency levels. Using O*NET, NLR researchers computed employment-weighted average
scores for each skill and knowledge domain for two groups: (1) current local coal mining and
power generation workers and (2) the proposed facility operations workforce for each
technology. NLR researchers then compared these averages to assess skill and knowledge
alignment between the groups. The calculation of skill difference followed methodology
illustrated in Matching Geographies and Job Skills in the Energy Transition (Greenspon and
Raimi 2024).

Conclusion
The results for northwest Colorado show that the three technologies assessed (EGS, solar-
powered hydrogen, and wind) have the potential to generate significant economic impacts, with
differences in terms of intensity, sectoral distribution, and investment requirements. Although the
technologies were evaluated independently to assess economic and workforce impacts, they
could be pursued in tandem, as stand-alone facilities that could be replicated (as in the solar-
power hydrogen and land-based wind facility options), or interchanged with other technologies
under consideration by northwest Colorado.

The four-county region has sufficient labor supply to support new energy development but faces
infrastructure friction that limits matching, mobility, and training uptake, most notably below-
average transportation access and workforce education and training programs that are relevant to
the proposed technologies. Moffat and Rio Blanco have stronger job transferability, especially
for geothermal, whereas Routt and Garfield trail on transferability. Comparisons with recent
geothermal, wind, and solar projects confirm that the modeled job intensities (FTE/MW) for
northwest Colorado fall within published ranges.

The three technologies modeled include an analysis of job opportunities in comparison to coal
mining and power generation. While the number of operations and maintenance jobs identified
within the three individual technologies are less than the combined coal mining and power
generation job count of 630, the analysis shows some alignment between the occupation types
and needed skills to provide opportunities for the existing workforce within the three modeled
energy technologies. Across all three technologies, the most consistently relevant occupations
include construction, transportation and warehousing, and professional and technical services.
EGS has relatively higher impact during the construction phase and for leading annual job
creation during operations. It also provides the greatest overall economic impact in terms of
output, employment, earnings, and tax revenues. However, this technology also entails the
largest upfront investment, which may constrain near-term deployment. Geothermal aligns most
closely with the workforce’s average skills and knowledge and shows meaningful alignment with
current coal mining and power generation workforce, especially in supervisory, safety,
maintenance/electrical, and earth-drilling roles, but several high-volume and in-demand
occupations still lack clear transfer pathways, leaving significant gaps unmet.

Solar-powered hydrogen supports a more varied occupational base, spreading benefits across a
wide range of sectors, though at moderate intensity for jobs created. This variation highlights the
potential for solar-powered hydrogen to drive the need for construction, professional, and
technical services. Across the three pathways, solar-powered hydrogen shows the highest
potential opportunity for transferring workers through short bridge training, but it also exhibits
the largest skill and knowledge gaps.

Wind emerges as the leanest option for employment with around 2% of the current coal mining
and power generation workforce able to transition into land-based wind facility operations roles
with minimal or limited additional training. It also has the lowest total number of jobs of the
three technologies. Land-based wind can staff most operations roles with relatively small number
of opportunities for the current workforce, while ten of the largest coal mining and power
generation source occupations show no potential transfer, suggesting strong role misalignment.

 52

The skill gaps are modest and concentrated in equipment repair, troubleshooting, and
maintenance, while the knowledge gaps center on engineering and technology—notably
computers and electronics, mechanical systems, core engineering fundamentals, and
telecommunications.

Taken together, these high-level findings suggest that deploying these technologies in northwest
Colorado could generate substantial economic benefits, though each comes with different trade-
offs in terms of investment requirements, time requirements for upskilling, employment
potential, and long-term economic contributions that communities and policymakers should
carefully consider.

Sectoral Impacts on Output and Value Added for Each Scenario
NLR compared the cumulative impacts across 20 sectors (as identified by IMPLAN modeling
software) to determine impacts of gross output and value added in terms of personal income for
each of the three modeled technologies–EGS, solar-hydrogen, and wind--for both the
construction and operation phases. The sector categories represent market-based entities and
tracks activities associated with economic output and labor income such as producing goods or
services within a given sector.

For example, the sector category “government enterprises” includes state and local government
passenger transit, state and local government electric utilities, other state and local government
enterprises, and other federal government enterprises. The economic analysis does not include or
administrative burden--such as general staff labor hours--associated with public administration or
government regulatory activities, nor captures permitting or tax revenue resulting from energy
project development.

Building on the previous aggregate results found in Table 2, Figures B-1, B-3, and B-5 breaks
down the construction phase impacts across sectors for each of the three technologies analyzed.
For the geothermal facility, the largest contributions appear in the construction, mining, and real
estate and professional services sectors, reflecting the drilling- and labor-intensive nature of the
project. In the case of the solar-powered hydrogen facility, the effects are more evenly
distributed, with significant impacts in construction, transportation and professional services.
Meanwhile, the onshore wind project shows impacts at a comparatively smaller scale but still
registers important linkages with construction, transportation, and wholesale trade.

Following the construction phase results, the next set of figures focus on the operations phase
impacts across the three technologies (Figures B-2, B-4, and B-6). Figure B-2 shows that the
geothermal project generates the largest ongoing impacts, with mining emerging as the most
affected sector. The solar-hydrogen facility records notable contributions in other services
(Figure B-4), while the wind project shows a more modest but steady influence across wholesale
trade, and real estate services (Figure B-6).

Appendix C. Workforce Resources
Appendix C provides supporting resources related to state-wide and regional workforce
development programs. These resources are not intended to be an exhaustive list but rather serve
as examples of state and regional offerings.

State Workforce Resources
Colorado Workforce Centers

The Colorado Department of Labor and Employment hosts Workforce Centers in northwest
Colorado, notably in the towns of Craig in Moffat County, Meeker in Rio Blanco County, and
Glenwood Springs in Garfield County. Workforce Centers provide a variety of free services to
assist employers and job seekers across various sectors. For example, the business services staff
support local employers in their recruitment, retention and ongoing staffing needs through a wide
array of services, including hiring services, workforce intelligence and trends analysis,
community connections for training opportunities, incentives and resources within the
community, and upskilling through work-based learning opportunities. These training and
education services are also available to job seekers to provide greater access to employment
opportunities and resources (Colorado Department of Labor and Employment, 2025).

Colorado Community College System

The Colorado Community College System (CCCS) is Colorado’s largest higher education and
workforce training provider. With 13 colleges and more than 35 locations, these centers of
education serve over 124,000 students each year. With locations in Craig and Rangely,
northwestern coal workers have access to degree programs, employment training, and career
services to expand their workforce versatility. CCCS cultivates relationships with statewide non-
profit organizations, businesses, economic development councils, workforce development
boards, and state agencies to provide these services to workers in response to workforce
demands. CCCS offers new and existing businesses several workforce development initiatives
for employee upskilling, including customized workforce training, industry recognized
credentials, and apprenticeships (Colorado Community College n.d.).

In particular, the Skill Advance Colorado Grant Program can assist Colorado businesses to
strengthen competitiveness, foster talent, and improve employment opportunities. The state-
funded grants are offered through the First Job Training Program, which funds customized
training for new hires of new and expanding businesses, and the Existing Industry Job Training
Program, which funds customized incumbent worker training of established businesses. These
training grants support new hire and incumbent worker reskilling and upskilling, short term
worksite training aligned with organization needs, essential and technical skills development,
and employee retention support.

Additionally, the Workforce Resilience Grant Program, launched in July 2023, offers two-year
funding to rural organizations for developing training programs that support skill development
for current and new employees (Colorado Community College, n.d. -a). Aiming to enhance
workforce skills through business-led training and accessible education, this program may

 86

include reskilling and upskilling employees in industry skills, essential skills, or technology to
support new career pathways. Multiple northwestern counties have been recipients of the grant
since 2023, providing examples for how the region could further develop their involvement with
the Workforce Resilience Grant and at CCCS.

CCCS also offers Zero-Cost Training Programs, where training is free of tuition, fees, and course
material costs in given programs. Seventeen participating Colorado universities and technical
colleges offer these programs and training in high-demand occupations, such as construction,
firefighting, education, and other areas. These programs are a part of Career Advance Colorado,
which is funded through House Bill 23-1246, and dedicates $38.6 million to fully cover
enrollment costs for training programs in sectors with significant workforce shortages.

My Colorado Journey

An online clearinghouse sponsored by the Colorado Department of Labor and Employment,
Department of Higher Education, Workforce Development Council, and Department of Human
Services, My Colorado Journey is a platform that connects job seekers and students to careers,
education planning and support resources. This no-cost platform helps jobseekers with actionable
steps toward their professional development and career search.

Users can search for skills needed, salary data, and training providers for 16 career pathways. For
coal workers, job seekers can select relevant occupational training resources from either the
Electrical Energy Transmission and Distribution or the Renewable Energy Production Pathways.
From there, they can find educational program and training providers that can support reskilling,
with potential funding opportunities if available (Colorado Department of Labor and
Employment, et. al. 2025).

Colorado Workforce Development Council

The Colorado Workforce Development Council (CWDC) is a governor-appointed, public-private
partnership established to advise, oversee, and integrate the work of the Colorado talent
development network. The CWDC leverages various strategies in order to enhance and sustain a
skills-based talent development network that meets the needs of employers, workers, job seekers,
and learners.

The CWDC oversees the development of a strategic state and regional or local workforce
development plans. These plans are necessary to secure federal workforce assistance funds and
are a requirement of the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act which was signed into
federal law on July 22, 2014. The main purpose of Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act is
to connect job seekers with employment opportunities, education and training resources, and
support services while matching employers with skilled workers.

Regional Workforce Resources
Northwest Colorado Development Council

The Northwest Colorado Development Council (NWCDC) serves the three-county region of
Moffat, Rio Blanco, and Routt counties and their seven communities. NWCDC’s mission is to
diversify economies by promoting tourism, encouraging the stabilization and expansion of
existing businesses, and to stimulate the development of new, environmentally responsive
businesses, including reskilling the energy workforce as new energy industries come into the
region. As part of its strategic plan, the council works with federal, state, local organizations,
higher education and K-12 partners to create or support workforce development programs for
reskilling or upskilling (Northwest Colorado Development Council 2025).

The Northwest Colorado Resiliency and Recovery Roadmap

The Northwest Colorado Resiliency and Recovery Roadmap, prepared for NWCDC and the
Colorado Department of Local Affairs, is an extension of the Colorado COVID-19 Regional
Resiliency and Recovery Roadmaps Program funded by the federal EDA. The Roadmaps
Program entailed a two-year planning and implementation process to support 16 community-
based teams in diversifying and strengthening rural economies while also building regional
resiliency. The Northwest Colorado Regional Community Team (RCT) includes local
government and local economic development representatives including from Moffat, Rio Blanco,
and Routt counties.

The RCT selected energy generation projects as a focus area within the objective of economic
diversification that can supplement the loss of coal power plants in the region and leverage
existing infrastructure and workforce assets. Specific opportunities identified include reuse of
coal power plants for molten salt battery storage; expansion of biomass, solar, wind, and
geothermal energy generation; development of nuclear and hydropower generation; and land use
updates to support regional energy projects.
Another objective for the RCT is on-going workforce development with a focus within four core
areas – workforce transition, entrepreneur support education and skills training, and workforce
support resources. (Economic and Planning Systems, Inc., 2022).

Rural Innovation Center

Leveraging national economic development funds for coal communities, the NWCDC and Routt
County have plans to open a Rural Innovation Center to serve as a central location for economic
development and business services. The center will provide entrepreneur support (e.g.,
mentorship and angle investors), a small business revolving loan, and workforce assistance
programs in partnership with local community colleges (Just Transition Fund 2025).

Routt County Economic Development Partnership
The Routt County Economic Development Partnership (RCEDP) is a nonprofit fostering
economic development through business retention, relocation, expansion, and diversification.
RCEDP operates the Yampa Valley Entrepreneurship Center, which provided supportive
services such as business planning, mentoring, and counseling (Routt County Economic
Development Partnership 2024).

conducted two public surveys to gauge and compare regional, statewide, and national opinions

Capacity Building for Repurposing Energy Assets Opportunity grant. Communities LEAP
technical assistance was not involved in design of the survey instruments or implementation of
the surveys but did provide review of responses from both surveys to identify potential
community priorities. These priorities are discussed below.

The second survey expanded the type of technologies explored and introduced the concept of a
