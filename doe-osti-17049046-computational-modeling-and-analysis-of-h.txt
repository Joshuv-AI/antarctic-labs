1 Introduction
The state DOTs have been reviewing their catch basin inlet spacing design approaches to assess if
the hydraulic efficiency of the system meets the stormwater runoff requirements [1]-[4].
Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) has been used for many years for various applications and,
with advances in the computational power available to researchers, it has gained in popularity in
modeling highway hydraulics; as such, it is a useful computational tool that can successfully
supplement physical testing of existing and new designs of roadway drainage.

In a previous study [5], selected CFD models were validated for use in modeling hydraulic capacity
of roadway drainage in sag locations by comparing the computational results with measurements
obtained in physical testing as well as the approach presented in HEC-22. Three grates presented
in HEC-22 were selected for analysis. (1) A P-50 grate with straight bars, for which the projected
area is equal to the open area. The opening ratio was established in physical tests [6] as 0.9. (2) A
curved vane grate, for which the projected open area is equal to a fraction of the open area, and
the opening ratio was established in physical tests [6] as 0.35. (3) A 45°-tilt bar grate, for which
the projected open area is zero, and the opening ratio was not established in physical tests. The
computational results compared well with experimental measurements with most data points
within ±10 % difference range. The HEC-22 estimate of the grates’ capacity gives a conservative
estimate i.e., higher water levels for a given flow rate than the physical and computational models.

South Carolina DOT has expressed interest in modeling multiple inlets used for sump
applications. In this report, sump refers to a sag or depressed location where runoff collects at a
low point and must pass through the inlet system. These are: drop inlets (DI; DI 24×36, DI 112,
DI 125) and catch basins (CB; CB 18, CB 9). Table 1-1 presents the inlet types and their
applications.

2 Scope of the Study
The current study focuses on the hydraulic capacity of drop inlets and catch basins installed at
low points where all runoff must pass through the inlet. The hydraulic capacity of an inlet in sump
is defined as the relationship between ponding depth and flow rate through the inlet. Three flow
regimes can be distinguished as the flow conditions over the inlet change with water depth. When
there is a pool of water with a nearly level and unbroken surface above the grate, the flow is in the
orifice flow regime. In the weir flow regime, the grate is partially overtopped, and the water surface
is broken, dipping down toward the grate. These two flow conditions have been studied
extensively in the past, and equations relating flow rate to water depth are available in the
literature. The transition between these two regimes has been studied less extensively.

HEC-22 [7] provides weir-flow and orifice-flow equations to calculate the relationship between
flow rate and water depth. In addition, Chart 9—A for SI units and B for English units—was
developed for a set of standard grate inlets of various sizes. The chart presents line plots on a log-
log scale of the weir-flow equation as a function of inlet perimeter and the orifice-flow equation
as a function of “clear open area.” In HEC-22, clear open area is defined as the inlet area
multiplied by an opening ratio, which was derived for the standard set of grates based on the
physical tests of Burgi et al. [6]. The two sets of lines do not intersect on the chart, and the method
used to select their endpoints is not explicitly described. When Chart 9 is used, HEC-22
recommends drawing a curve between the weir-flow and orifice-flow lines to estimate the depth-
flow relationship in the transition region but does not specify the form of that curve. When the
equations are used directly, HEC-22 recommends computing the depth required by both the weir
and orifice equations and selecting the larger depth for a given flow rate.

The main goal of this study is to develop the relationship between ponding depth and flow rate
through each inlet for the weir and orifice flow regimes for the analyzed drop inlets and catch
basins. These relationships are intended to support evaluation of inlet performance for applicable
rainfall events ranging from 100% AEP to 0.2% AEP. The range of ponding depth varies by inlet
type according to its hydraulic capacity. For ditch applications, water depths can reach
approximately 3 to 4 ft, and the initial water depth in the CFD simulations was therefore taken as
5 ft or greater. For pavement applications, the water depth is lower than in ditch applications but
remains sufficient to cover both weir and orifice flow regimes.

In this study, the HEC-22 equations are used as a reference for comparison. The CFD results are
intended to supplement those equations by resolving inlet-specific geometry effects, vegetation-
related resistance, and transition-regime behavior that are not fully captured by simplified design
formulas.

Appendix A compiles the hydraulic-capacity results developed in this study in a format intended
to support practical design use. It includes tabulated relationships between ponding depth and
intercepted flow rate for each analyzed catch basin and drop inlet under the modeled installation,
slope, surface roughness, vegetation, and blockage conditions. The appendix also summarizes the
case identifiers and scenario definitions, presents the fitted breakpoint depths used to distinguish
weir, transition, and orifice flow regimes, and provides inputs and guidance for use in the
companion spreadsheet-based design aid. These materials are intended to help users interpolate
inlet performance within the simulated range of conditions, estimate the applicable flow regime,
and, where relevant, evaluate related design quantities such as pavement spread.

The hydraulic-capacity relationships developed in this study are specific to the inlet geometries,
installation configurations, slope combinations, surface roughness assumptions, vegetation
representations, and blockage conditions modeled herein. These results are intended for
interpolation within the simulated range of conditions and should not be extrapolated beyond
that range without additional analysis or validation.

For ditch applications, the modeled section was assumed to have no longitudinal slope and no
continuing inflow during the simulation. Instead, the computational domain was initialized with
a specified ponding depth and allowed to drain through the inlet. Soil infiltration was neglected.
These assumptions were adopted to isolate inlet hydraulic capacity under sump conditions and
do not represent the full hydrologic response of an actual ditch system.

Ponding depth reported in this study is the water depth measured at the monitoring location
defined for each CFD configuration. Because the monitoring location differs among ditch,
pavement, valley-gutter, and curb-opening cases, direct comparison of reported ponding depths
across inlet types should be made with care.

For interpretation of the CFD results, the simulated depth-discharge relationships were
partitioned into weir, transition, and orifice regimes using a continuous piecewise fit. The
breakpoint depths reported in Appendix A should be interpreted as approximate hydraulic
thresholds for the modeled cases rather than sharp physical discontinuities.

3 Modeling Approach
Three-dimensional CFD models are developed, and the simulations are run using the TRACC
High-Performance Computing cluster at Argonne. The Unsteady Reynolds Averaged Navier-
Stokes Solver with k-ω turbulence model combined with the Eulerian two-phase flow model with
air for the gas phase, and water for liquid phase, together with the Volume of Fluid physics model,
are selected to model the free surface water flow. Detailed information on the formulation and
usage can be found in the Simcenter STAR-CCM+ User’s Manual [9]. The modeling approach
follows the previous studies [1]-[5].

The drop inlets are installed in pavement sections and grass areas. These types of surfaces differ
in roughness, and that has an effect on the water flow in the vicinity of the drains. In hydraulic
engineering, the Manning roughness coefficient is often used to characterize roughness, and its
values are derived from experiments or assumed from empirical tables. In most CFD software,
STAR-CCM+ included, the user needs to specify the equivalent sand-grain roughness height on
the surface. Previous studies showed that this approach is reliable for relatively small roughness
height, usually up to a few millimeters. Another approach, used to model larger roughness
heights, is to model the rough pavement surface or grassy area as a porous medium with the
thickness of the roughness height.

The geometry of the computational domain includes a section of a roadway in the vicinity of an
inlet (for the analysis of catch basin on pavement) or a section of a ditch (for the analysis of the
catch basins used for this application), as well as the inlet and catch basin underneath. An example
geometry of the model used in simulations for CB-9 is shown in Figure 3-1.

between the cylinders). The formula for 𝐶 is valid for 𝜙 ≤ 0.4 and 𝑑 ≤ 0.025m.

According to HEC-15, section 4.1 on grass lining properties [11], the number of grass stems in a
given area for a good grass lining is about 2,000 to 4,000 stems/m2 (200 to 400 stems/ft2). For
agricultural ditches, grass heights can reach 0.3 m (~1.0 ft) to over 1.0 m (~3.3 ft). The grass
height is kept much lower near a roadway for safety reasons and is typically in the range of 0.075
m (0.25 ft) to 0.225 m (0.75 ft). Figure 3-2 shows a sketch of the model of the vegetation,
consisting of 4000 0.2 m-tall cylinders distributed uniformly over a 1 m by 1 m section of bed.

An example geometry of the model is presented in Figure 3-3, and a snapshot in time of the

needs to speed up to conserve the mass flow rate. The distribution of the shear stress in the fluid
and on the channel surface also differs. In the case of a smooth surface, the maximum shear occurs
on the bottom surface of the channel. Introduction of the drag from the bed of cylinders shifts the
maximum to the interface between the vegetation and the free flow region above the stems with
unit porosity.

 Figure 3-7 and Figure 3-8 show the velocity and shear stress vertical profiles on the centerline. In
 the smooth wall model, the velocity profile follows the log-law profile. When the porous media is
 introduced to represent a bed of cylinders, the velocity profile changes within the porous section
 as compared to the section above. The profiles change slope at the intersection of the two regions,
 and the change is more pronounced with increasing solid volume fraction in the porous medium;
 for a higher solid volume fraction within the porous medium (when the number of cylinders per
 unit area increases) the bulk flow velocity drops there but increases in volume above for the flow
 rate to remain the same. The same trend is observed when the height of the porous region

 10

increases; taller cylinders reduce the volume of the free flow, thus increasing velocity flow above
them. This is why the non-smooth surfaces have higher velocity in the top section.

For a smooth wall boundary, the shear distribution is close to linear with the maximum at the bed.
The addition of the vegetation momentum sink shifts the maximum shear to the intersection
between the two sections, and the maximum magnitude increases significantly because the flow
velocity above the vegetation is higher and the gradient of velocity along the vertical direction is
greater above the porous medium. With increasing number of cylinders per unit area and their
height, flow rate decreases in the porous region; in order to preserve the mass balance, the flow
rate increases above the porous region, the velocity difference in vertical direction increases,
resulting in higher shear.

This approach to modeling vegetation is used in the full ditch model with a drop inlet or catch

sudden drop in magnitude under the dashed orange line (marking the border between regions

The local velocity decreases within the vegetated region because the vegetation introduces
additional drag and reduces the unobstructed flow area. In the model, this is represented through
porous-media resistance and reduced porosity relative to the free-flow region.

The vegetation representation used in this study is intended to capture the bulk hydraulic
resistance associated with maintained roadside vegetation. It does not explicitly represent flexible
stem motion, lodging, seasonal variability, nonuniform density or height, or debris entanglement
within the vegetation. Accordingly, the vegetation model should be interpreted as an idealized
resistance model for comparative hydraulic-capacity evaluation.

4 Hydraulic Capacity of Catch Basins and Drop Inlets
4.1 Catch Basin CB 9

CB type 9 is a catch basin with a 5” by 3’ opening on each of its four sides. It is applied in ditch
sections outside of the clear zone, low areas behind curb and gutter sections, and yard drains
where debris is not an issue.

The geometry of the computational domain represents a section of a ditch as well as the drain
inlet and catch basin underneath. Due to the double symmetry of the CB-9 geometry, half of the
model was analyzed, which makes the simulations less computationally intensive but does not
introduce any errors to the solution. Simulations are initialized with 4.5 feet of water above the
bottom of the ditch. During the simulation, no water enters the ditch; it only drains out. The
surface roughness of the ditch was assumed to be 3 mm, which represents a uniform earth
channel. In the next steps, the flow resistance coming from the grass, weeds etc. was accounted
for in the model.

Figure 4-2 shows the screenshots of water surface at 80 seconds of simulated time for varying
ditch slopes and Table 4-1 presents the water surface in the ditch at various times during the
simulations for two ditch geometries. All simulations were started with the same initial water
depth. With the decreasing slope, the volume of water stored in the ditch increases, and therefore
it takes more time to drain it.

The change of the (a) flow rate and (b) water depth during the simulation for various ditch
geometries is shown in Figure 4-3. Despite the difference in the time needed to drain the ditch,
caused by variation of the volume of water stored in the ditch, the flow rate through the catch
basin as a function of ponding water depth is nearly independent of ditch boundary geometry.
With no vegetation resistance, the drainage flow is primarily gravity driven, and flow rate
variation due to varying total resistance from sloped and bottom channel surfaces is at most a
secondary effect. At water depths below ~0.8 ft, the flow is in weir regime, and above ~0.8 ft, it is
in orifice regime.

As an example, the flow rate variation in time was plotted in Figure 4-4 for the case of an earth
ditch with 2:1 front and 2:1, 4:1 and zero back slopes. The flow rate through the openings facing
the sloped ditch surfaces is lower than through the openings facing the flat surface of the ditch
bottom and it varies with the slope; at the same time, the two openings facing the flat bottom of
the ditch intercept the same flow rate independently of the considered scenario.

Figure 4-5 (a) shows the relationship between the water depth and flow rate for CB 9 located in
an earth ditch with varying front and back slopes. The curves obtained for different back slopes

are very similar which suggests that the impact of the ditch slope on the hydraulic capacity of the
catch basin is small. The close-up views in the figures (b) and (c) show that for the same flow rate,
the greater the slope, the higher the water depth and the ponding depth varies by an order of
magnitude of an inch at most.

4.3 Drop Inlet DI 24” × 36”

The grated catch basin DI 24” × 36” is for applications in low areas within clear zones, yard drains
where debris is not an issue, and low areas with pedestrian traffic. The grate type is a parallel bar
grate with 1 3/8" by 4 5/8” (4 9/16” on the sides) rectangular openings. In the base case, the
pavement around the grate is assumed to be horizontal, which is the most conservative
assumption. Based on the findings of this study, additional mild pavement slopes toward the drain
were not modeled because their effect on hydraulic capacity was found to be small.

The geometry of the grate has two planes of symmetry. Based on previous research [3], it can be
expected that the flow through the grate is close to symmetric. For this reason, a quarter of the
model of the drain and surrounding pavement without a slope is modeled and used to simulate
the weir and orifice flow regimes. The model is shown in Figure 4-15. In order to gain more
confidence in the results, a simulation of the flow through the full grate was performed for the
orifice flow regime and both computational results are compared with the orifice flow regime
equation from HEC 22. Figure 4-16 shows the comparison with power function trendlines for the
CFD solutions.

The impact of the slope of the ground surface surrounding the grate on the water depth vs. flow
rate relationship was analyzed through a comparison of two scenarios, when the surface is
horizontal and sloped with a 2:1 slope in both directions, like shown in Figure 4-17. The flow aver
the grate is more straightforward to analyze in the case of a flat surface. The computational

from the grate. Because of that, the curve in Figure 4-19 is smoother than for the case of a sloped

water surface, the impact of the surface waves on the depth vs. flow rate curve is visible.
Nevertheless, the two curves are close to each other and the flat surface scenario gives a more
conservative result in terms of the ponding depth.

4.4 Drop Inlet DI 112

The drop inlet DI type 112 is used in ditch sections, grassed medians, and low areas located on
controlled access and divided highways. The opening is 3’ by 3’ and covered with four Ø 3.5”
parallel bars. A minimum of 4 feet of pavement is placed around the basin at 20:1 grade. The
geometry of the model for drop inlet DI 112 installed in a ditch with 2:1 slopes is shown in Figure
4-21.

Firstly, it was assumed that the ditch is clear of any vegetation and the only surface resistance to
the flow comes from surface roughness equal to 3 mm. The hydraulic capacity of this drop inlet
was analyzed for varying front slope of the ditch changing from 2:1 to a horizontal surface. In this
case, the relationship between the water depth and flow rate through DI 112 does not depend on
the ditch geometry, as shown in Figure 4-22, where all curves overlap with good accuracy. It is

 29

worth noting that the water depth is measured from the flat bottom surface of the ditch. The
opening to the catch basin is lower than the ground surface due to the 20:1 slope of the apron.

The plot also includes the estimate of the relationship according to the HEC 22 weir and orifice
flow equations. The equation for the orifice flow was used with a calibrated loss coefficient; the
value of 𝐶 = 0.67, is too conservative for this inlet and was increased to 1.0. The transition
between the orifice and weir flow regimes is established at ~1 ft for the computations and HEC
22 formulas.

Figure 4-23 and Figure 4-24 show the relationship between the water depth and flow rate through
DI 112 for scenarios with and without vegetation in the ditch. In the case with vegetation, it was
assumed that it consists of 0.2-m-tall (10 in) cylinders with density of 4000 per square meter. The

5 Conclusions
South Carolina DOT has been reviewing their drainage inlet spacing design approach to assess if
the hydraulic efficiency of the system meets the stormwater runoff requirements. The previous
studies include grate inlet CB 25, curb opening inlets CB 16 and CB 17, and a combination inlet
CB 1. In the current phase of the study, the hydraulic capacity in a sag location of grated drop
inlets: DI 24×36, DI 112, DI 125, and curb opening inlets CB 18, and CB 9, was evaluated.

Three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics models were developed for various installation
configurations of the inlets, in pavement sections and in roadside grassy ditches. The difference
is that surface roughness has an effect on the water flow in the vicinity of the drains. For this
reason, an important part of this study was modeling the impact of the presence of vegetation on
the flow. Modeling included an earth ditch surface and a Type C grass lining. A porous media
approach to the modeling of momentum losses from the presence of grass stems in the flow was
implemented in the CFD models and evaluated by comparison with literature. When the porous
media is introduced, the velocity profile in an open channel changes from the log-law profile; its
magnitude is significantly lower in the porous section than above it. The shear stress profile
changes as well and maximum shifts from the bed to the intersection between the two sections
and the maximum magnitude increases significantly because the flow velocity above the
vegetation is higher.

The following are the most relevant findings on the hydraulic capacity of the inlets.

In a ditch location, CB 9 has four rectangular curb openings. Depending on the slope of the ditch,
the water depth vs. flow rate curves differ slightly (order of magnitude of an inch) with the depth
increasing with the steepness of the slope. If there is no vegetation resistance present, the drainage
flow is primarily gravity driven, and flow rate variation due to varying total resistance from sloped
and bottom channel surfaces is not significant. The main impact on the water depth vs. flow rate
relationship comes from the surface roughness; depending on whether the ditch is a uniform earth
channel, or it is covered with vegetation, the rate of flow into the catch basin differs. With
increasing vegetation resistance, the hydraulic capacity of the inlet significantly decreases.

In a gutter valley location, CB 9 has one opening on the roadway side and its performance is
similar to a curb opening inlet type.

CB 18 is a curb opening inlet with depression. It is similar to previously evaluated CB 16 and CB
17 which are used on a grade, but its opening is wider than the other two CBs. When used in sag
in a gutter location, its hydraulic capacity is not impacted by the cross-slope of the pavement, as
long as the water surface is unbroken, i.e., for orifice flow type. For shallower water conditions,
the impact of the cross-slope is noticeable (weir flow type).

The grate type used in DI 24”x36” drop inlet is a parallel bar grate. As in the case of the other grate
inlets, the relationship between the flow rate and water depth in orifice flow regime is not
significantly affected by the presence of vegetation. The weir type flow is affected when the
vegetation is present, with the water depth increasing as compared to the earth channel for the
same flow rate. The impact of the slope of the surrounding ground surface on the hydraulic
capacity is also not significant. The model with a horizontal surface around the grate gave a more
conservative result.

The drop inlet DI 112 is a square opening partially covered with four parallel bars and a concrete
apron extending at least 4’around the basin. The apron protects the catch basin from being
overgrown by vegetation. Nevertheless, the impact of the increased flow resistance from
vegetation is seen when the flow is of weir type. When the flow is of orifice type, the added

 39

resistance does not have any significant impact on the hydraulic capacity. The front and back
slopes have only a secondary impact on the flow depth vs. flow rate curve.

DI 125 is a grate inlet which is installed as single or double and does not have an apron around
the grate that would keep the vegetation from growing close to it. It is open from the top, and
therefore the water depth vs. flow rate relationship in orifice flow regime is not significantly
impacted by the slope nor the vegetation, and that the relationship for flow in the weir flow regime
differs with and without the presence of vegetation.

In conclusion, proper maintenance of grassy ditches, which includes mowing the vegetation and
removal of debris, may increase the hydraulic capacity of the catch basins and drop inlets.
Installation of an apron around an inlet may reduce the risk of the vegetation overgrowing the
inlet and reducing its interception.

A comparison of the water depth to flow rate relationships for an earth ditch shows that DI 112
has the highest hydraulic capacity, followed by DI 125, CB 9, and DI 24x36. For a gutter location,
CB 18 shows a higher capacity than CB 9. Figure 5-1 presents a column plot of the flow intercepted
by the inlets at various ponding water depths.

 Figure 5-1 Comparison of the ﬂow intercepted by the inlets at various ponding water depths.
The results of the study will help SCDOT engineers to calculate the spacing of the inlets considered
in new designs and assess if additional inlets are needed. In the case of the ditch applications the
spacing needs to be such that the ponding depth for design and other analyzed events do not
increase flooding or have any negative effects on the traveling public or adjacent property owners.
Gutter applications must meet the spread requirements and must not have a depth that could lead
to hydroplaning.

These results can also serve as design aids for evaluating both proposed and existing conditions
by helping engineers estimate inlet performance, compare alternatives, assess spacing adequacy,
and identify locations where additional interception capacity or maintenance may be needed.

This appendix compiles the hydraulic-capacity relationships developed in this study for the
analyzed catch basins and drop inlets. The appendix provides tabulated ponding-depth-versus-

spreadsheet-based evaluation of inlet performance within the range of modeled conditions.

The tabulated relationships are based on CFD simulations performed for the inlet geometries,
installation conditions, and surface characteristics described in the main body of the report. These
data may be used to interpolate inlet performance within the simulated range for each
configuration. Extrapolation beyond the listed ranges of ponding depth, slope geometry, surface
resistance, vegetation condition, or blockage condition is not recommended.

For configurations in which hydraulic behavior changes with increasing depth, the tabulated data
are also interpreted in terms of flow regime. Where applicable, the reported values distinguish
between weir behavior, transition behavior, and orifice behavior. The regime thresholds were
identified using a continuous piecewise discharge-depth fit applied to each CFD case. In this
approach, low-depth behavior is represented by a weir-form relationship, high-depth behavior is
represented by an orifice-form relationship, and the intermediate transition region is defined so
that the overall fitted curve remains continuous across the regime boundaries.

For each case, two characteristic breakpoint depths are identified: 𝑑 , corresponding to the
approximate end of weir-controlled behavior, and 𝑑 , corresponding to the approximate onset of
orifice-controlled behavior. The corresponding flow rates at these depths are also reported. These
breakpoint values should be interpreted as approximate hydraulic thresholds for the modeled
cases rather than exact discontinuities in physical behavior.

Ponding depth is the water depth measured at the monitoring location defined for the
corresponding CFD configuration. Flow rate is the total intercepted flow entering the inlet. Spread
is the lateral extent of water across the pavement surface, where applicable. The weir regime
corresponds to a broken water surface overtopping the inlet, the orifice regime corresponds to a
relatively unbroken water surface above the inlet, and the transition regime represents
intermediate behavior between these two limiting conditions.

Interpolation between tabulated values is appropriate only within the simulated range shown for
each case. The tabulated relationships are configuration-specific and should be applied only to
the inlet type, installation condition, and surrounding surface condition identified in the

To identify hydraulic flow regimes for each inlet case, a continuous piecewise discharge–depth
model was developed and applied to the CFD dataset. The objective was to separate each case into
three physically meaningful regions:

 1. Weir flow regime at lower depths,
 2. Transition regime at intermediate depths, and
 3. Orifice flow regime at higher depths.

The method assumes that discharge 𝑄 depends on water depth 𝑑 according to different functional
forms in different regimes.

For the weir regime, discharge is modeled as

 𝑄=𝑎 𝑑 /

where 𝑎 is a fitted coefficient.

For the orifice regime, discharge is modeled as

 𝑄=𝑎 𝑑 /

where 𝑎 is a fitted coefficient.

independently, it is defined as a straight line connecting the end of the weir curve to the beginning
of the orifice curve. This ensures that the overall discharge–depth relationship is continuous and
avoids artificial jumps between regimes.

 A.6 Guidance for Spreadsheet / Design-Aid Use

The spreadsheet-based design aid developed from these results should require the user to specify
only input conditions that fall within the scope of this study, including inlet type, installation
configuration, applicable slope case, and surface condition. The spreadsheet should use
interpolation between tabulated values to estimate ponding depth for a specified intercepted flow
rate, identify the applicable flow regime, and, for CB-18, estimate pavement spread. Use of the
spreadsheet outside the simulated range of depths or for inlet geometries or surface conditions
not analyzed in this report is not recommended.
