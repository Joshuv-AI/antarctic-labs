 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 This report details a comprehensive process improvement study designed to optimize
team efficiency and strategic focus within an Identity and Access Management (IAM) technical
unit at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). Faced with a high volume of operational
support and a fragmented project landscape, the team experienced "capacity fatigue" and
delivery times extending into years.

 Project Overview

 The study was initiated following a Blanchard team assessment that revealed a strong
desire among 16 IT professionals for an explicit workflow to prioritize simultaneous projects.
The team currently faces "process overload," attempting to manage 48+ active priorities
alongside approximately 2,500 annual support tickets. This fragmentation has resulted in project
delivery times extending into years and an imminent risk of employee burnout.

 Key Findings & Data Analysis

 A comprehensive 360-degree evaluation utilized six data sources, including historical
ticket data and Viva Insights digital communication metrics. Major findings include:

 • Deep Work Deficit: After accounting for meetings, emails, and distractions, holidays and
 PTO leave, team members average only 2.6 hours per day for uninterrupted, deep-focus
 work.
 • Operational Floor: Support tickets and “keeping the lights on” operational activities
 consume nearly half of the team's total bandwidth before any project work is considered.
 • Productivity Score: AI analysis of communication data assigned the team a productivity
 score of 63/100 for January 2026.
 • Feasibility Gap: The current workload of 48+ projects is mathematically impossible to
 deliver with the existing 15 FTEs.

 Proposed Solutions & Strategy

 Since hiring additional staff is not currently an option, the project focuses on resource
optimization rather than team expansion. The study recommends:

 • Strategic Prioritization: Leadership must implement "ruthless prioritization," reducing the
 active project list to the top 15–20 core initiatives.
 • Potentially hire additional FTEs to increase team capacity.
 • Implementation of Systems:
 o Value Ranking System (VRS): A mathematical hierarchy to score projects based
 on effort and organizational impact.

 2

 o  Resource Allocation System (RAS): A dynamic matrix to assign Subject Matter
 Experts (SMEs) based on skill and availability.
 • Tool Utilization: Leveraging the ServiceNow Strategic Portfolio Management (SPM)
 module, which is already deployed at LANL but currently underutilized, for capacity
 planning, resource allocation, project prioritization and road mapping.

 Expected Outcomes & ROI

 The implementation is projected to transform the team into a highly focused, high-
performing unit. Key performance goals include:

 • Delivery Speed: An estimated 3% increase in completed projects within years 0–2 and a
 projected 6% increase within years 3–5.
 • Financial Impact: An estimated $576,000 in recovered productivity value over five years
 through efficiency gains.
 • Intangible Benefits: Improved employee engagement, reduced burnout, and better
 alignment with the organization's strategic goals.

1.1 Project Proposal

 This research aims to investigate and potentially implement solutions to better prioritize
the daily tasks, projects, and requests managed by a team of 16 IT professionals. The necessity
for this study originated from a recent Blanchard Team Assessment conducted among all team
members.

 The assessment results indicated a collective desire for improved workflow definitions,
specifically regarding the prioritization of multiple simultaneous projects to ensure more tangible
results are delivered on schedule. Consequently, the purpose of this study is to analyze relevant
literature and industry best practices utilized by other organizations to achieve these objectives.

 The ultimate scope of the project is to implement a solution designed to foster positive
employee engagement, boost morale, and increase the timely delivery of organizational projects.

B. Problem

 In December 2023, the author transitioned from an individual contributor to the leader of
a newly formed IT technical team consisting of 13 individuals. Despite a lack of formal training
or prior management experience, the author accepted the challenge with the goal of becoming an
effective leader. While they successfully established a strong rapport with the staff, it soon
became evident that the volume of incoming requests and projects was overwhelming the team.
This realization highlighted the urgent need for a structured process to manage projects and daily
tasks efficiently, based on the specific availability and skill sets of each team member.

B. Literature review for organizational project prioritization and effectiveness

 The literature review for this study was conducted using the following methodology:
first, a comprehensive list of existing team projects, O&M tasks, and organizational initiatives
for which the team is responsible was created [1]. The list contains approximately 35 “keep the
lights on” operational tasks and approximately 48 strategic initiatives for various stakeholders.
This list establishes a baseline for the problem this study aims to solve. Another part of the
baseline creation was a recent team assessment study conducted as a result of a Blanchard Team
Leadership seminar [2]. The assessment creates a picture of the team's current performance state
based on members' responses to a 29-question survey. According to the evaluation, 73% of the
team considers the team’s stage to be in “Production” (high-performance) state, 20% in
“Integration” (norming), while 6% in “Orientation” (forming) state. In the assessment, in the
“Our team leader can help the team by” section, it was pointed out that the team leader should:
a.) prioritize and better distribute work, b.) educate leadership on how their decisions impact the
team, and get the team adequate time to complete projects, c.) fight for more resources and
staffing. In the “Our greatest challenges” section, it was pointed out that a) “too many number
one priorities” and b) “Overcoming the amount of tasks and projects that need to be done with
the limited resources that we have”, c.) “others continually expecting us to do more and jump at

 9

a moment's notice”, d.) “Inadequate staffing, extremely limited time/resources/skills for cross-
training & ever-expanding scope of responsibilities”. As highlighted above, the purpose of this
study is to find a solution to the team’s request from the team leader to “prioritize and better
distribute work”.

 Based on the workload and team assessments, the next phase of this study's literature review
was to identify how leaders in various organizations approach the challenges of efficiency and
prioritization. An excellent opportunity for this research presented itself during an unexpected
scenario: the author’s long commute to work during which he extensively listened to a particular
podcast: The No Bullsh!t Leadership Podcast by Martin G. Moore. Mr. Moore is a former
executive at CS Energy in Australia who also held various leadership positions in IT, marketing,
sales, and mining before his CEO role. Mr. Moore is also the author of the Wall Street Journal
best-selling book with the same title. Several episodes provided valuable insights on how to
deliver value, prioritize projects, and hold team members accountable. One of these episodes was
Episode 366, “Your 7-Step Playbook to Replace Busywork with Real Results” [3]. In this
episode, the host argues that "busy" is the enemy of productivity and that leaders must actively
replace low-value work with high-impact tasks. He explains that a culture of busyness is often a
result of ingrained habits and a reluctance to say "no" to new requests. He draws on concepts
from books like The 4 Disciplines of Execution and Slow Productivity to emphasize that the goal
is not to do more work, but to do less while delivering greater value. The core of the message is a
7-step playbook for leaders to shift their team's focus from "busy" to productive:

 1. Get buy-in from your boss to focus on high-value work.
 2. Know and communicate what's valuable to your team and your boss.
 3. Say "no" to low-value work and reinforce this message daily.
 4. Prevent "pet projects" from getting onto the work program.
 5. Focus on stopping low-value work already in progress.
 6. Hold people accountable for delivering on what they've promised.
 7. Prove the value by tracking and recovering the benefits of your work.

 Ultimately, the episode encourages leaders to shift their focus from being seen as busy to
being recognized for delivering tangible, valuable results. Another entertaining, yet useful insight
on productivity was outlined in the same podcast’s episode 149: “The Nike Boss: Just Do It” [4].
In this episode, Martin G. Moore defines a "Nike boss" as a leader who avoids challenging their
own superiors by simply telling their team to "just do it," even when faced with unrealistic
demands or known problems. This type of leader often exaggerates a project's status and
prioritizes maintaining a positive appearance to their own boss over delivering actual results.
Moore argues that working for or being a Nike boss is a lose-lose situation. He offers a four-step
strategy for managing a Nike boss:

 1. Gain Clarity on Objectives: Ensure a shared understanding of what's truly important to
 back up your pushback.
 2. Use Value Ranking: Know the exact value of your projects so you can have a factual
 conversation about what to prioritize and what to drop.
 3. Negotiate Everything: Formally negotiate project scope, resources, and deadlines, and
 confirm these agreements in writing to hold your boss accountable.

 10

 4. Give the Boss Occasional Wins: Take on minor, low-effort tasks that don't jeopardize
 your primary goals to maintain a positive relationship.

 To avoid becoming a Nike boss yourself, Moore advises developing the courage to push back
on your own superiors. By consistently delivering high-quality work, you can become a trusted
advisor rather than a "workhorse," which demonstrates your ability to lead at higher levels.
Ultimately, he stresses that authentic leadership means protecting your team from unnecessary
pressure rather than simply passing it down.
 Although current leadership does not align with the "Nike boss" description, the insights
provided in this episode are highly relevant to the feedback captured in the Blanchard
assessment. Specifically, the content addresses a concern voiced by a team member regarding
"the amount of projects leadership assigns without the knowledge or understanding of how their
decision affects our job." A key takeaway from Martin Moore that can be implemented is the
importance of protecting the team from unnecessary pressure rather than simply passing it down.
Additional relevant episodes from this podcast series are listed in the Additional References
section.
 During the literature review, common themes that consistently emerged were the harmful
effects of multitasking, distractions, a lack of simplicity, and a lack of focus. A great read on this
topic and relevant to this study was Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted
World by C. Newport [4]. Deep Work by Cal Newport argues that the ability to focus without
distraction on a demanding task is a rare and valuable skill. He defines this as deep work,
contrasting it with low-value shallow work (e.g., email, meetings). Newport provides a four-rule
framework to master deep work and achieve professional success:

 1. Work Deeply: Schedule and ritualize uninterrupted, intense work sessions.
 2. Embrace Boredom: Train your brain to resist constant distraction by allowing yourself to
 be bored.
 3. Quit Social Media: Mindfully evaluate if a tool's benefits outweigh its distractions.
 4. Drain the Shallows: Systematically minimize low-value work to free up time for deep
 work.

 The core idea of accomplishing more by focusing on fewer tasks directly applies to the
team’s workload of over 50 projects, tasks, and O&M operations. Frequent distractions from
daily requests, meetings, and emails represent forms of shallow work that generate a sense of
busyness while providing minimal value. These distractions prevent the team from focusing on
and completing individual projects. Consequently, Newport’s methodology should be applied to
the project prioritization framework within this study.
 To be able to set priorities properly, a project and task ranking model should be developed
within this study. During the literature review, there were several project ranking techniques
identified. One of them was the work of Antonio Nieto-Rodriguez, “How to Prioritize Your
Company’s Projects” [6]. In this article from Harvard Business Review, the author emphasizes
the crucial importance of effective project prioritization for an organization's success. The key
takeaways from the article are:

 • Project Overload: Many companies suffer from having too many projects, which slow
 down execution and obscures strategic goals.

 11

 • The "Hierarchy of Purpose" Framework: the author introduces a framework to help
 leaders prioritize projects by asking a series of questions:
 o Purpose: What is the organization's purpose and strategic vision?
 o Priorities: What matters most to the organization now and in the future?
 o Projects: Which projects align with the purpose and priorities?
 o People: Who are the best people to execute these projects?
 o Performance: Are we measuring real performance and value creation (outputs)
 instead of just inputs (cost, time)?
 • Saying "No": The article highlights that a well-defined prioritization process enables an
 organization to say "no" to low-value projects and to stop or kill projects that are no
 longer relevant, which in turn frees up resources and improves focus.
 • Cultural Shift: Prioritization is not just a process; it's a cultural shift that builds an
 "execution mindset" and aligns all levels of the organization with strategic goals.

 Another useful project ranking framework identified during the literature review was
Baba Oyewole’s work “Prioritization Techniques for Decision Makers and Leaders,” hosted on
Udemy Business training platform [7]. The instructor identifies several prioritization techniques,
such as:

 • The Eisenhower Matrix: Urgency vs. Importance
 • The ABC Method: Simple Task Ranking
 • The MoSCoW Method: Must have, Should have, Could have, Won’t have
 • The RICE scoring model: (Reach x Impact x Confidence) / Effort
 • Value vs. Effort Matrix
 • The Pareto Principle (80/20 Rule)

 To create a priority ranking system for this study, the above techniques will be analyzed in
more depth to determine which will be the most appropriate technique that can be applied for
maximum effect to this project.

C. Summary

The literature review reveals three key lessons about team efficiency:
 1. Busyness is not the same as productivity. Being constantly busy with many tasks doesn't
 mean the team is delivering the most valuable results.
 2. Focus is a rare and powerful skill. Distractions and "shallow work" (such as emails and
 meetings) hinder the team from engaging in "deep work" on high-impact projects.
 3. Prioritization requires a system. To focus on the right work, a team needs a
 straightforward way to rank projects, understand their value, and have the courage to say
 "no" to less important tasks.

Implications for the Project

 The implementation of a new task assignment tool alone is insufficient to resolve existing
operational challenges. The core issue lies in a localized culture characterized by excessive

 12

project volume and chronic overextension. Consequently, this study must address these systemic
cultural factors directly.
 As the team leader, the author must advocate for this strategic shift to organizational
leadership, demonstrating how a focus on fewer, high-value projects will yield superior results
for the entire organization. The proposed system must be innovative, incorporating a scoring
mechanism based on value versus effort to facilitate objective decision-making regarding project
selection and termination.
 Furthermore, the system must empower the lead to exercise a "strategic refusal" policy.
By providing a transparent framework for declining low-priority requests, the process will
protect the team from non-essential labor. Ultimately, the project represents a strategic shift in
mindset, transitioning the team from a "do everything" approach to a "do what matters most"
philosophy.

Recommendations and Discussion

 • Implementation of a Scoring Model: The study recommends adopting a standardized
 framework, such as the RICE model, to score all projects and tasks. This approach
 provides an objective, data-driven basis for prioritization.
 • Establishment of "Deep Work" Intervals: To combat fragmentation, the team should
 schedule dedicated "deep work" blocks. These periods allow for focused progress on
 high-impact projects without the disruption of meetings, emails, or administrative
 distractions.
 • Protective Leadership: A critical component of the leadership role involves shielding the
 team from unrealistic external demands. Proactively managing stakeholder expectations
 is essential for maintaining team efficiency and preventing professional burnout.

 2. METHODS

 This section outlines the mixed-methods research design used to develop and validate a
strategic prioritization and resource allocation system. The study follows a sequential exploratory
approach, beginning with qualitative analysis of current operational bottlenecks and concluding
with a quantitative evaluation of the new systems' impact on project delivery speed and
employee engagement.

2.1 Planned Methodology

 This Capstone project addresses the critical challenge faced by one of the Infrastructure
Services Division's IT technical teams: the disproportionate relationship between the
overwhelming number of simultaneous projects/requests and the available resource/time
allocation. Driven by a Blanchard team assessment that highlighted a strong desire for more
explicit workflow definition, the current system is characterized by "thrashing," "shallow work"
distractions, and a crippling inability to complete strategic initiatives in a timely manner,
resulting in delivery times extending over months or even years.

 14

 The overall goal of this study is to research, design, and implement two integrated process
improvements: a Value Ranking System for prioritization and a Resource Allocation System for
focused execution. The project aims to systematically achieve greater efficiency, defined by a
measurable reduction in project delivery time by at least 3% within two years and 6% within five
years, while simultaneously increasing employee engagement and maintaining or improving
business partner satisfaction. To systematically address this complex, high-difficulty problem, a
robust mixed-methods research design is required to ensure both process development
(qualitative) and outcome confirmation (quantitative).

B. Research Design

 The study will employ a Mixed-Methods Exploratory Sequential Design. This design is
strategically chosen because the qualitative phase is necessary to explore the root causes of
current inefficiencies and develop the core components of the new system, which will then be
rigorously tested in the quantitative phase.

 1. Phase I (Qualitative - Exploratory): Data will be collected and analyzed to gain a deep
 understanding of the current workflow friction, the types of work that lead to "thrashing"
 and "focus erosion," and the diverse definitions of "value" and "responsiveness" held by
 the team and its key business partners. This exploratory data is essential for designing the
 systematic Value Ranking System.
 2. Phase II (Intervention): The Value Ranking and Resource Allocation Systems will be
 developed, institutionalized, and adopted by the team and organizational leadership.
 3. Phase III (Quantitative - Confirmatory): Quantitative data will be collected over a five-
 year period to measure the impact of the new processes against the established success
 metrics (delivery time reduction, employee engagement, and customer satisfaction).

 This sequential approach ensures that the implemented solution is not based on assumptions
but is systematically grounded in evidence from both internal challenges and external
stakeholder requirements, mitigating the risk of simply making the team happy while failing to
meet business partner needs.

C. Participants

 The study's participants are divided into two distinct groups corresponding to the project's
phases:

Group 1: Qualitative (Exploratory) Participants

 • Internal Team Members: All 16 team members will be interviewed and their input
 requested for the following intent:
 o Role: Provide insights into internal workflow bottlenecks, perceived workload,
 personal experiences with context-switching ("thrashing"), and current capacity
 challenges.

 15

 • External Stakeholders / Key Business Partners: A sample of 5 to 7 individuals
 representing the main sources of requests (e.g., Leadership, Customers, Other Groups)
 will be selected.
 o Role: Articulate their expectations for priority, responsiveness, and their
 qualitative definition of "high value" work to ensure the new system aligns with
 organizational impact.

Group 2: Quantitative (Confirmatory) Participants

 • The entire technical team of 16 IT professionals will be the subject of the confirmatory

 6. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

6.1 Discussion

 The findings of this process improvement study confirm that the Identity and Access
Management (IAM) team is currently operating in a state of "capacity fatigue," driven by a
mathematical impossibility in their workload. The data analysis revealed a stark "Feasibility
Gap": the team is attempting to manage over 48 active requests alongside approximately 2,500
annual support tickets with only 15 Full-Time Equivalents (FTEs). This fragmentation has
directly led to delivery times extending into years, validating the team's initial assessment of
"process overload".

 A critical discovery in this research is the "Deep Work Deficit". After accounting for
operational tasks, meetings, emails, PTO and personal distraction time (derived from an external
study), team members average only 2.6 hours per day for uninterrupted, high-concentration
work. This result strongly aligns with the theories of Cal Newport, who argues that "deep work"
is essential for creating new value and improving skills, while "shallow work" (like emails and
meetings) provides a false sense of productivity. The current environment forces the team into
"thrashing", a state of unproductive activity where constant task-switching prevents meaningful
progress.

 To address these challenges, the proposed shift from a "do everything" to a "do what
matters most" philosophy is supported by Martin G. Moore’s leadership principles. Moore’s "7-
Step Playbook" and the concept of avoiding behaviors where leaders simply pass down
unrealistic demands, justify the study’s recommendation for potential new FTE hiring and
"ruthless prioritization". By reducing the active project list from 49 to the top 15–20 core
initiatives, leadership can protect the team from the "boundary pressure" that has historically
hindered delivery.

 The implementation of the Value Ranking System (VRS) and Resource Allocation
System (RAS) provides the objective framework necessary to sustain this cultural shift. The
projected Return on Investment (ROI) of $576,000 in recovered productivity over five years
demonstrates that efficiency gains through resource optimization are a viable and financially
superior alternative if team expansion is not possible.

6.2 Conclusion

 This Capstone project has established a data-driven roadmap to transform the IAM
technical unit into a high-performing, focused organization. The research successfully identified
that the root cause of the team’s "capacity fatigue" was not a lack of technical competence, but a
systemic culture of over-commitment and fragmented focus.

 By institutionalizing the VRS and RAS and leveraging existing tools like ServiceNow's
Strategic Portfolio Management (SPM) module, the team is positioned to achieve an estimated
3% increase in delivery speed within two years and a 6% increase within five years. However,

 APPENDIX A: PROJECT CHARTER

 I. Project Introduction

 This project is a Capstone study intended to research and implement solutions to better
prioritize the daily tasks, projects, and requests that the IT technical team, led by Zoltan Csibi, is
asked to perform.

 The project is driven by a recent Blanchard team assessment that highlighted a strong desire
among the 16 IT professionals for a more explicit workflow definition for prioritizing multiple
simultaneous projects to deliver more tangible results on time. The team's challenges include an
overwhelming number of requests, "too many number one priority projects," inadequate staffing,
and a disproportionate relationship between the number of initiatives and resource/time
allocation. As a result, certain initiatives take months, or even years, to complete, leading to an
overwhelmed and less productive team.

 J. Sponsor Information

 Andy Eggleston is the deputy division leader of the Infrastructure Services Division (ISD) at
Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL). The division is responsible for providing
comprehensive support and management of organizational infrastructure, including network
services, telecommunications, programmatic and institutional services, technical operations, and
specialized systems.

 The division is structured into multiple teams that oversee areas such as network engineering,
server management (Windows, Linux, and specialty systems), identity and access management,
public key infrastructure, edge security, data center operations, and project management. ISD
ensures the reliability, security, and efficiency of the organization's IT environment, supporting
both daily operations and strategic initiatives through a diverse staff of technical experts,
administrators, and support personnel, consisting of four groups, 24 teams, and close to 300
employees, who serve approximately 20,000 customers.

 K. Project Definition

 The primary goal of this study is to develop and implement a new work prioritization and
allocation method. This system must efficiently distribute work to team members based on their
skill level and availability, which will ultimately avoid burnout and ensure the timely delivery of
valuable results for the organization.
 The research for this study focuses on established methodologies such as principles from
"The 4 Disciplines of Execution," "Deep Work," and "No Bullsh!t Leadership" to emphasize
focus and the need to replace low-value "busywork" with high-impact tasks.
