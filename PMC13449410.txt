Superior Sensitivity of the Forgotten Joint Score in Total Knee Arthroplasty: A Meta-Analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials
Authors: Anand Dhaliwal, Alexander Baur, Brendan J Liakos, Muzammil Akhtar, Shaunton Stubbs, Jaspreet Sidhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) is a patient-reported outcome (PRO) measure assessing joint awareness after total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Unlike traditional PROs, it is particularly sensitive to the “forgotten” quality of the joint, correlating with patient satisfaction and long-term success. This systematic review evaluates the FJS compared to conventional PROs in assessing TKA outcomes.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library identified randomized controlled trials reporting FJS alongside other PROs in primary TKA. Studies included various alignment techniques, implants, and robotic assistance. Independent reviewers performed data extraction and risk of bias assessments. Pooled FJS scores were analyzed using random-effects models, with subgroup analyses by surgical technique and prosthesis type.ResultsFourteen randomized controlled trials (1244 knees) met inclusion criteria. FJS scores ranged from 19.00 to 98.00, with a mean of 73.61. Unlike Western Ontario and McMaster Universities, Oxford Knee Score, and Knee Society Score, the FJS showed no significant ceiling effect. Chi-square tests demonstrated that FJS detected significant postoperative improvements compared to Western Ontario and McMaster Universities and Knee Society Score (P = .011 and P = .037). Subgroup analyses found no significant differences in FJS or other PROs, though recent studies suggest a trend favoring kinematic alignment.ConclusionsThe FJS demonstrates superior sensitivity and avoids ceiling effects compared to traditional PROs, making it a valuable tool for evaluating subtle differences in TKA outcomes. Its ability to detect nuanced improvements highlights its clinical relevance in optimizing surgical techniques and enhancing patient-centered care. Future research should prioritize the FJS to refine alignment strategies and implant designs.Keywords: FJS, PROM, Arthroplasty, TKA

Introduction

With ongoing advancements in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) techniques, there has been a shift from traditional surgeon-based ratings to patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures. These tools provide a more patient-centered perspective on surgical outcomes. The first widely used PROs, such as the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities (WOMAC) osteoarthritis index (1986) and the Knee Society Score (KSS, 1989), were followed by the Oxford Knee Score (OKS) in 1998. However, many argue that these earlier tools have limitations, such as ceiling effects and an inability to distinguish between patients with good or excellent outcomes.

The Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) is a newer PRO measure designed to assess how unaware patients are of their replaced joint in daily life, reflecting optimal function and minimal discomfort. Unlike traditional PROs, the FJS focuses on the degree to which patients forget their joint arthroplasty, which correlates with higher patient satisfaction and long-term success in TKA. Its simplicity and ease of use make it a valuable tool for clinicians. Studies by Adriani et al. [1] confirmed the FJS’s validity, reliability, and utility in clinical practice. The minimal clinically important difference for the FJS, established by Clement et al. [2], is 13.7, with a range of 0-100, indicating its ability to detect significant changes in patient satisfaction.

The FJS was developed by Behrend et al. [3] to represent the ultimate goal of joint arthroplasty: minimal awareness of the joint. Its popularity has grown rapidly due to its focus on this patient-relevant outcome. Recent systematic reviews, such as those by Cortina et al. [4] and Fu et al.[5], have used the FJS to assess emerging surgical techniques like robotic-assisted surgery and alignment strategies, reinforcing its importance in TKA research.

Given the variety of surgical options available, it is essential to evaluate how these choices affect patient outcomes. While many studies have validated the FJS, there is limited research on its sensitivity to different implants or alignment approaches (kinematic alignment [KA] vs mechanical alignment [MA]). This systematic review aims to fill this gap by assessing the FJS’s ability to differentiate between these alignment techniques and examine its role in capturing the benefits of advanced implants and robotic systems.

The primary goal of this review is to determine if the FJS is a superior PRO for measuring overall success in TKA. Secondary objectives include identifying factors such as alignment technique, implant type, or robotic assistance that are most strongly linked to improved FJS outcomes, providing insights into elements that contribute to patient satisfaction and functional success.

This investigation aims to establish the FJS as a high-ceiling metric that can reveal subtle distinctions in PROs influenced by advanced surgical techniques. By examining the FJS in the context of TKA innovations, the review will contribute to a better understanding of how these advancements affect the patient experience and long-term joint functionality, guiding future TKA optimization and patient-centered care.

Material and methods

Protocol registration

Methods for analysis of this article and both inclusion and exclusion criteria were established and registered in advance in PROSPERO, a registry that creates a publically available record of the study, with the registration number: CRD42024586596.

Study design and population

This systematic review evaluates the reliability of the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) as a patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) for assessing the success of TKA and explores factors influencing FJS outcomes. The secondary aim is to identify patient and procedural factors associated with higher FJS scores. Included studies focused on adults (≥18 years) who underwent primary TKA and reported FJS along with additional PROMs. A search following guidelines established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta analyses was performed in 3 databases on November 9, 2024: PubMed, Embase, and Google Scholar. Two authors identified all articles included in the study. The following search strategy was used to perform the systematic review: “forgotten joint score” AND “knee arthroplasty” OR “TKA.” Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with extractable mean FJS values and standard deviations or confidence intervals were included. Exclusion criteria encompassed unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, preoperative procedures, rehabilitation protocols, and nonoperative measures.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was identifying predictors of higher FJS scores, such as prosthesis type and surgical technique. Secondary outcomes focusing on how FJS compares with other well-established PROMs, including the WOMAC, OKS, and KSS.

Study selection process

The study selection process is outlined in Figure 1. Initially, 5500 records were identified from a literature search. Following a title screen, 578 records were identified, including 445 from MEDLINE and PubMed and 133 from Google Scholar. Further abstract screening led to exclusions based on the following criteria: 88 studies focused on unicompartmental knee arthroplasty, 18 on other arthroplasty procedures, 6 on unrelated orthopaedic procedures, 27 on soft tissue procedures, 11 on preoperative scores, 10 on nonoperative treatments, 34 on rehabilitation protocols, 25 lacking PROMs, 38 without FJS data, 41 case series, 115 systematic reviews, 15 non-English studies, and 21 for other reasons. This resulted in 129 articles for full-text review. After reviewing these articles, additional exclusions were made: 17 lacking PRO data, 15 without clear FJS reporting, 13 focused on score validation, 16 case series, 23 cohort studies, 25 systematic reviews, and 4 for other reasons. Two additional studies were identified through citation searches. Ultimately, 14 studies were included in the final review.

PRISMA flow diagram demonstrating study identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion. Of 578 records identified through database searches, 445 underwent title and abstract screening, and 125 underwent full-text review with 14 studies meeting inclusion criteria.

Data extraction

Data on FJS outcomes, patient demographics, prosthesis type, surgical technique, follow-up duration, and other reported PROMs were extracted. Additional study-specific details such as sample size and risk of bias potential were also recorded. Two independent reviewers performed the data extraction, resolving disagreements by consensus. Authors were contacted when clarification on missing data was needed.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias for each study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for RCTs. Two independent reviewers evaluated the risk of bias for each study, with a third reviewer resolving conflicts.

Statistical analysis

Pooled mean FJS scores were calculated using random-effects model, as we only included RCTs in this study. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the influence of demographic factors, prosthesis type, surgical technique, and follow-up duration on FJS outcomes. Meta-regression was used to identify predictors of FJS outcomes, while sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings.

A chi-square test was conducted to determine whether a statistically significant proportion of studies found a significant relationship between FJS and other PROs, such as the WOMAC, OKS, or KSS. A P value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Additionally, a ceiling effect analysis was performed to assess the proportion of patients achieving the highest possible score in each PROM. This analysis helped determine whether a ceiling effect was present, which could limit the ability to detect meaningful differences in patient outcomes. A ceiling effect was defined as more than 30% of participants achieving the maximum score on a given PROM.

The certainty of the evidence for both primary and secondary outcomes was assessed usingGrading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, a method of rating the certainty of evidence and strength of recommendations. This system evaluates factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias across the included studies.

IRB exemption

IRB approval was not required as all data were obtained from publicly available sources.

Descriptive statistics for clinical outcome measures are shown in Table 2. The Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) had a broad range from 19.00 to 98.00, with a median of 78.60 and a mean of 73.61, indicating sensitivity to variations in outcomes. WOMAC scores ranged from 3.00 to 94.00, with a median of 34 and a mean of 49.0; lower scores represent better outcomes. The OKS ranged from 19.90 to 46.00, with a median of 39.85 and a mean of 36.89, indicating high functional outcomes. The KSS, reported in 2 studies, ranged from 85.70 to 95.50, with a median of 92.15 and a mean of 91.38, reflecting applicability to high-functioning individuals.

Ceiling effect analysis showed variability across scales. The FJS showed no ceiling effect (0%), while WOMAC exhibited a ceiling effect of 33.33%, indicating many participants scored at the upper end. OKS had a ceiling effect of 16.67%, and KSS had 12.50%, suggesting moderate limitations in capturing higher-end improvements.

Chi-square tests compared the FJS with other outcome scales. The FJS showed significant improvements compared to the WOMAC (chi-square = 16.55, P = .011) and KSS (chi-square = 6.58, P = .037). There was a trend for FJS to show greater improvements than OKS, but this did not reach statistical significance (P = .09).

Subgroup analysis of MA vs KA revealed no significant differences in functional outcomes across the FJS, WOMAC, and OKS scores. The MA group (n = 256) had a mean FJS score of 62.9 ± 20.3, WOMAC of 62.5 ± 33.8, and OKS of 42.8 ± 2.7. The KA group (n = 200) had an FJS of 62.8 ± 20.6, WOMAC of 61.1 ± 33.8, and OKS of 40.6 ± 2.9. Statistical analysis showed no significant differences (P > .05). However, recent studies [9,6] indicated a trend toward superior outcomes for KA.

A meta-analysis was conducted where applicable. Comparing FB vs MB components in 2 RCTs [11,14], pooled data showed no significant difference (P = .86) (Figure 2). Similarly, comparing KA vs MA revealed no significant difference (P = .41) (Figure 3). Including rKA in the KA group brought results closer to significance, but remained insignificant (P = .1) (Figure 4).

Discussion

Building on prior research validating the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) as a reliable primary outcome measure, our study investigates its ability to offer deeper insights compared to traditional outcome measures. We aim to determine whether the FJS provides greater clinical relevance, particularly in detecting subtle differences in patient outcomes and satisfaction, as TKA implants and techniques evolve. Small, yet significant differences in outcomes have become increasingly important in guiding optimal surgical practices.

Our study confirms that the FJS is a sensitive and reliable metric for evaluating functional outcomes and patient satisfaction in TKA. The FJS demonstrated superior sensitivity in detecting significant differences across surgical categories compared to traditional PROs such as WOMAC and OKS. Unlike these measures, which show ceiling effects, the FJS effectively captured nuanced variations in outcomes without any ceiling effect. This is crucial for identifying the most effective techniques and implants in TKA, enabling a better understanding of what enhances patient satisfaction and functional success. The wide range of FJS scores observed further highlights its utility in identifying optimal strategies for achieving the best outcomes.

Our findings support and expand upon prior research on KA in TKA. Although our subgroup analysis showed no statistically significant differences in FJS, WOMAC, or OKS scores between KA and MA, a trend favoring KA emerged, consistent with recent studies by Cortina et al. [4] and Van Essen et al. [20]. Van Essen’s study reported significantly better outcomes with KA, including higher OKS, FJS, KSS Objective Knee, and KSS Functional Activity scores. Subgroup analysis also found rKA had superior FJS scores compared to PSI. Cortina’s review confirmed the noninferiority of KA to MA in short- and mid-term clinical outcomes, with MA showing excellent survival results. These findings suggest that improvements in KA techniques, particularly with robotic assistance, and increased surgeon familiarity may further enhance its clinical advantages over time.

While meta-analyses of the studies did not show specific implants or techniques leading to superior FJS scores, they still provide valuable insights. FB vs MB components did not show a difference in FJS scores. Other studies, not included here, have highlighted factors significantly influencing FJS scores. Howell et al. [21] demonstrated that medial deviation of the prosthetic trochlear angle resulted in worse FJS and OKS scores compared to lateral deviation of the prosthetic trochlear angle, pointing to the potential impact of the quadriceps vector and Q angle on FJS and overall satisfaction following TKA.

rKA demonstrated promising results in Ettinger et al [9] study, suggesting its potential to optimize implant positioning and improve soft tissue balance. Fu et al. [5] also supported this, noting that robotic assistance enhances alignment accuracy and functional outcomes. Our findings reinforce this by showing the FJS’s ability to capture these subtle, clinically meaningful differences. When this article was included in our meta-analysis of KA vs MA, it strengthened the results, showing a trend favoring KA that nearly reached statistical significance (P = .1). This emphasizes the need for further research on KA vs MA, as well as the role of robotics in FJS and patient satisfaction following TKA.

A key strength of this systematic review is its high level of evidence, as only RCTs were included. RCTs minimize bias and provide reliable data for clinical decision-making. The inclusion of 14 RCTs ensures robust, evidence-based conclusions, strengthening the role of FJS in evaluating TKA outcomes across alignment techniques and technological advancements.

However, variability in surgical techniques and implant designs across studies is a limitation. Despite efforts to categorize procedures into groups like MA, KA, and robotic-assisted techniques, these categories encompass a wide range of methodologies. Within MA and KA groups, differences in soft tissue handling, alignment philosophies, and instrumentation were evident. Similarly, robotic-assisted techniques varied in system type, surgeon experience, and protocol adherence. Implant designs also differed across studies, with variations in fixation methods (cemented vs cementless), bearing surfaces (mobile vs fixed), and constraint types (cruciate-retaining vs posterior-stabilized), which may have influenced outcomes. While these categories provide a framework for analysis, the heterogeneity limits the precision and generalizability of the findings.

Future research should focus on refining categorizations and standardizing methodologies within alignment and implant groups to reduce variability in surgical techniques and implant designs. This would allow more accurate assessments of factors influencing outcomes as measured by the FJS. Additionally, the trend favoring KA observed in recent studies highlights the need for larger, long-term randomized trials to confirm its potential advantages over MA. The FJS’s ability to capture high-ceiling outcomes positions it as a valuable tool for evaluating emerging technologies and surgical techniques, ultimately guiding patient-centered care decisions and advancing clinical practice.

The evolution of TKA has introduced new implant designs and alignment techniques aimed at enhancing patient outcomes. Traditional MA aims for consistent implant positioning by aligning components along the knee’s mechanical axis. In contrast, KA seeks to restore the patient’s natural knee anatomy and movement patterns, with the goal of improving comfort and functionality postsurgery. KA is gaining traction, as studies such as Cortina et al. [4] and Van Essen et al.[20] suggest it offers outcomes comparable to or superior to those achieved by MA. Robotic-assisted systems have further enhanced these capabilities, providing surgeons with precision and control for individualized implant placement and soft tissue balance tailored to each patient's unique anatomy. Robotic assistance has shown promising results in reducing postoperative pain, improving alignment accuracy, and potentially contributing to better FJS scores and joint function.

Conclusions

This systematic review highlights the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS) as a sensitive and reliable metric for evaluating functional outcomes and patient satisfaction in TKA. Compared to traditional PROs like WOMAC, OKS, and KSS, the FJS showed superior sensitivity in detecting meaningful differences while avoiding ceiling effects. This makes it an effective tool for assessing variations in alignment techniques, implant designs, and surgical technologies, offering valuable insights for optimizing patient outcomes.

Although no statistically significant differences were found between MA and KA, emerging trends favoring KA, particularly with robotic assistance, suggest potential long-term benefits. The inclusion of 14 RCTs strengthens these conclusions, providing robust evidence for clinical decision-making.

However, variability in surgical techniques, implant designs, and study methodologies limits the precision and generalizability of the findings. Future research should focus on standardizing methods within alignment and implant categories and exploring the long-term impact of KA using larger, high-quality trials. The FJS, with its ability to capture nuanced outcomes, is poised to play a key role in evaluating advancements in TKA and shaping patient-centered surgical strategies.
