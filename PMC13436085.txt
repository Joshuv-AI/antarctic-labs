Continuous Capnography and Shorter Duration of Subclinical Hypoxemia During Sedated Gastrointestinal Endoscopy in Older Adults: A Multicenter Observer-Blinded Device Evaluation
Authors: Peiqi Wang, Shan Jiang, Yuhai Xu, Qibin Chen, Fuyang Cao, Yungen Luo, Zishuang Mei, Ying Guo, Gang Chen, Jun Ji, Xiaoming Yang, Weidong Mi, Henglin Wang, Ke Wei, Jiangbei Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeRandomized trials have established that capnography reduces hypoxemia during sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, but how capnography alters provider behavior in routine practice remains unclear. We aimed to quantify the association between capnography display visibility and hypoxemia episode duration in older adults undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy.Patients and MethodsWe conducted a multicenter clinician-blinded device evaluation across three tertiary hospitals in China (November 2022 – February 2024). Of 1229 patients aged ≥65 years screened; 1,189 were enrolled. Capnography hardware was attached to all patients, with waveform display and alarms accessible in the visible group (n=620) and obscured/muted in the concealed group (n=569). The primary outcome was cumulative hypoxemia duration (subclinical [90–95% SpO2], moderate [85–90%], severe [≤85%]), analyzed using inverse probability of treatment weighting-adjusted zero-inflated negative binomial regression.ResultsHypoxemia incidence did not differ between groups at any threshold (subclinical: 28.7% versus 30.1%; P=0.569). Capnography visibility was associated with 30% shorter subclinical hypoxemia duration (incidence rate ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.91; P=0.007) and a model-derived estimated 30–40 second earlier response interval. Moderate hypoxemia showed a directionally consistent trend (IRR 0.69, 95% CI 0.46–1.04; P=0.074); severe hypoxemia duration did not differ (P=0.514). Airway interventions were 86% more frequent in the visible group (RR 1.86, 95% CI 1.57–2.19; P<0.001), predominantly jaw-thrust maneuvers. Hypotension occurred in 1.8% of the visible group versus 0% of the concealed group (P=0.004). Sensitivity analyses demonstrated result stability (E-value ≥2.5; consistent across center exclusions).ConclusionVisible capnography was associated with shorter subclinical hypoxemia duration and more frequent early airway interventions, consistent with an information-mediated provider response mechanism. These findings characterize how capnography visibility is associated with early detection and provider behavioral change in routine practice, complementing incidence-reduction evidence from randomized trials. All findings are reported as associations; no causal claims are made.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal endoscopy is among the most common invasive procedures performed worldwide, and because patients routinely find it uncomfortable, procedural sedation has become standard practice.1,2 China’s endoscopy system is the largest globally, with annual volume increasing from 28.8 million in 2012 to 44.5 million in 2019–44% under sedation or anesthesia.3 More recent data indicate continued growth, with 43.3 million procedures reported nationally in 2023. Individuals aged 65 years or older represent the fastest-growing segment undergoing endoscopic procedures.4 Age-related declines in pulmonary compliance, respiratory surface area, and chemoreceptor sensitivity to hypoxia and hypercapnia diminish the physiological reserve available to compensate for drug-induced respiratory depression.5,6 Older patients demonstrate reduced sedative tolerance and a narrower therapeutic window between adequate anesthesia and respiratory compromise.7–10 Even transient, self-resolving hypoxemia can precipitate myocardial ischemia, postoperative delirium, or prolonged recovery in older adults.11,12 Capnography and pulse oximetry monitor distinct physiological processes—ventilation versus oxygenation—and should not be viewed as competing measures of the same phenomenon. Rather, they provide complementary information: capnography offers real-time ventilatory data that can signal respiratory depression before it manifests as oxygen desaturation. During apnea or hypoventilation, oxygen saturation may remain normal for some time due to oxygen reserves in the functional residual capacity—particularly when supplemental oxygen is administered—while ventilatory abnormalities are reflected in EtCO2 waveform changes much earlier. The sigmoid oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve further renders pulse oximetry insensitive to falling arterial oxygen tension until values decline below 80 mmHg.13–15 Under supplemental oxygen – standard practice during sedated endoscopy – this detection delay is further prolonged because preoxygenation masks the fall in SpO2.16,17

Systematic reviews have established capnography efficacy. A meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials reported risk ratios of 0.77 (95% CI 0.67–0.89) for mild desaturation and 0.59 (95% CI 0.43–0.81) for severe desaturation.18 A pooled analysis of 9 trials (n=3,088) reported similar reductions in hypoxemia odds (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.49–0.77).19 These findings have informed contemporary guidelines: the British Society of Gastroenterology (2024),20 the American Society of Anesthesiologists (2018),21 and the European Society of Anaesthesiology and Intensive Care (2018) all recommend capnography during procedural sedation.22 The landmark multicenter RCT by Lian et al (n=1,777 elderly patients across three Chinese hospitals) demonstrated that capnography reduced hypoxia incidence from 19% to 12% (P<0.001).23 A subsequent RCT in mildly obese patients reached similar conclusions.24 The efficacy question is answered. What remains unknown is how capnography achieves its benefit in real-world practice – specifically, how access to ventilatory waveform data alters provider behavior. Prior trials compared capnography present versus absent, testing the effect of capnography hardware presence versus absence; no multicenter study has isolated the incremental benefit of making waveform data visible by attaching the device to all patients while manipulating only display visibility.13,25 The quantified early warning advantage over pulse oximetry in high-volume routine settings, and the associated changes in airway intervention frequency and timing, have not been systematically described.26 This evidence gap has direct implications for implementation: health systems deploying capnography hardware need data on the incremental value of activating displays. Our prior work focused on different research questions and did not use a concealment design to isolate the information effect of capnography. The current study is a distinct investigation with a different design, population focus, and research question.

A clinician-blinded concealment design was used to address this gap. Capnography hardware was attached to all enrolled patients; the sole difference between groups was whether the waveform display and audible alarms were accessible to the treating anesthesiologist. This design isolates the incremental benefit of making waveform data visible of capnography – actionable knowledge provided to the clinician – from the effect of capnography hardware presence versus absence tested by prior randomized trials. Randomized trials answered whether capnography reduces hypoxemia incidence when the device is added to standard care; our design answers a complementary question: once hardware is deployed, does activating the display alter provider behavior in ways that shorten the clinical course of hypoxemia? The concealment approach enables quantification of three specific parameters unavailable from conventional trial data: the temporal advantage of capnography over pulse oximetry in routine practice, the pattern of provider airway intervention response, and the association between display visibility and cumulative hypoxemia duration. We hypothesized that capnography display visibility would be associated with shorter cumulative duration of subclinical hypoxemia and more frequent early airway interventions—consistent with a behavioral (information-mediated) response mechanism rather than a direct effect of capnography hardware presence versus absence. No hypothesis was formulated regarding a difference in hypoxemia incidence, because the concealment design – with the device present in both groups – does not test the effect of capnography hardware presence versus absence examined by prior trials.

The primary objective was to quantify the association between capnography display visibility and cumulative hypoxemia duration in older adults undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy in a real-world clinical environment. Secondary objectives were to characterize the frequency, type, and timing of provider airway interventions in response to visible capnography data, and to estimate the temporal advantage of capnography over pulse oximetry-based detection. This report presents the results of a multicenter clinician-blinded device evaluation conducted at three tertiary hospitals in China, providing implementation-focused evidence that complements the incidence-reduction data from randomized controlled trials.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study used a clinician-blinded device evaluation design conducted at three tertiary hospitals in China (The First Medical Center, Chinese PLA General Hospital, Beijing; Air Force Medical Center, PLA, Beijing; and The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University, Chongqing) between November 1, 2022, and February 28, 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (also known as the General Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army).(approval number S2022-437-01) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2200065151). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

The design was classified as a clinician-blinded device evaluation rather than a conventional observational cohort study. All enrolled patients had a capnography device (Capnostream 20P, Medtronic Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA) physically attached and functioning during the procedure. The sole difference between groups was whether the capnography waveform display and audible alarms were accessible to the treating anesthesiologist. Our concealment design isolates the incremental value of making capnography waveform data visible to clinicians—once the hardware is already in place—from the question of whether capnography hardware itself (compared with no capnography) reduces hypoxemia incidence, which has been addressed by prior randomized trials. The design ensures identical data capture across groups, eliminating differential measurement bias. Group assignment was not randomized; allocation was determined by institutional practice and equipment availability. This design occupies an intermediate position between a randomized controlled trial and a purely observational study: the concealment intervention was investigator-imposed, but allocation followed clinical logistics rather than randomization.

Participants

Eligible patients were aged 65 years or older, had American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I or II, had a body mass index (BMI) of 18.5–28.0 kg/m2, and were scheduled for both gastroscopy and colonoscopy consecutively in a single session under intravenous anesthesia (gastroscopy first, colonoscopy second).

Patients were excluded if any of the following criteria were met: inability to tolerate the nasal sampling cannula; baseline oxygen saturation (SpO2) of less than 95% while breathing ambient air; known active malignancy; central nervous system disorder affecting respiratory drive; anticipated difficult airway (Mallampati class IV or documented difficult intubation history); refusal to provide written informed consent; gastrointestinal malignancy discovered during endoscopic examination; or inadequate bowel preparation precluding completion of colonoscopy. The last two criteria were assessed after enrollment but before procedure completion. The restriction to ASA I–II was deliberate: this study aimed to establish baseline safety parameters in a lower-risk elderly population before extending evaluation to higher-risk groups.

Over the 16-month period from November 2022 through February 2024, 1,229 patients were screened, of whom 1,189 were enrolled (620 in the capnography-visible group and 569 in the concealed group). Forty patients were excluded: 32 for missing critical data, 3 for incidentally discovered gastrointestinal malignancies, and 5 for inadequate bowel preparation.

Procedures

Group Assignment

Patients were allocated to one of two monitoring conditions based on institutional practice and capnography equipment availability. In the capnography-visible group (T group), the capnography monitor screen was positioned to remain fully visible to the treating anesthesiologist and audible alarms were enabled. In the concealed group (C group), identical capnography sampling tubing was attached, but the monitor screen was physically obscured and audible alarms were muted. Anesthesiologists in the C group were instructed to base all clinical decisions solely on standard monitoring parameters.

Standard Monitoring

All patients received continuous pulse oximetry (SpO2), non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP), and electrocardiography (ECG) monitoring. Respiratory rate was recorded from the capnography device in both groups. Capnography sampling was performed using sidestream technique via a nasal cannula (Capnostream 20P, Medtronic Inc). Supplemental oxygen was delivered via nasal cannula at 2 L/min. No formal sedation depth monitoring (eg, bispectral index, Ramsay score) was used; this absence is acknowledged as a limitation.

Anesthetic Protocol

Intravenous anesthesia was administered using propofol with or without adjunctive agents (etomidate, opioids, benzodiazepines) according to institutional protocols and anesthesiologist preference. Drug selection and dosing were not restricted by the study protocol. The procedure commenced after loss of the eyelash reflex. All participating anesthesiologists had a minimum of 5 years of independent clinical experience in anesthesia for gastrointestinal endoscopy.

Airway Intervention Protocol

A standardized graded airway intervention protocol was implemented: Grade I, jaw-thrust maneuver; Grade II, increased oxygen flow rate via nasal cannula; Grade III, pharmacological intervention (reduction or reversal of sedative agents); and Grade IV, mask-assisted ventilation. The endoscope was withdrawn if hypoxemia could not be corrected with these measures. All intervention decisions were recorded in real time by an independent research nurse who was not involved in clinical care.

Performance Bias

Clinicians were not blinded to group assignment (screen visibility cannot be concealed from the treating anesthesiologist). Knowledge of group assignment may have influenced both monitoring behavior and the decision to intervene. This performance bias is an inherent limitation of the concealment design and is acknowledged throughout.

Capnography sampling was performed using sidestream technique via a nasal cannula (Capnostream 20P, Medtronic Inc). Signal quality was continuously monitored by the research team; periods with artifact or dislodgement were recorded but not excluded from analysis, as this reflects real-world clinical conditions. The standardized airway intervention protocol (Grades I–IV) was implemented as described, with all decisions recorded in real time by an independent research nurse.

Outcomes

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was cumulative hypoxemia duration, expressed as a count of 10-second intervals, and analyzed separately at three severity levels. A discrete hypoxemic event was defined as a sustained SpO2 reading below the relevant threshold; a minimum 5-second interval was required between two separate desaturation episodes for classification as distinct events.

Hypoxemia severity was defined using the following SpO2 thresholds: subclinical hypoxemia (90% ≤ SpO2 < 95%), moderate hypoxemia (85% < SpO2 < 90%), and severe hypoxemia (SpO2 ≤ 85%).

Subclinical hypoxemia (SpO2 90% to <95%) was selected as the primary endpoint for three reasons. First, in the concealment design where the device is present in both arms, duration—rather than incidence—is the more sensitive metric for detecting differences in provider response behavior. Second, although mild desaturation in this range is common and often self-limiting, cumulative duration of even mild hypoxemia may be clinically relevant in older adults with limited cardiopulmonary reserve, who are more vulnerable to the cumulative effects of intermittent hypoxia. Third, this range captures the earliest detectable difference in SpO2 that could reflect a clinician’s ability to intervene before desaturation progresses to more severe levels.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes were: (1) incidence of hypoxemia at each severity threshold; (2) frequency and type of airway interventions, stratified by grade I–IV; and (3) estimated early warning interval conferred by capnography visibility, derived from regression model coefficients. Hypotension was defined as a greater than 20% decrease in systolic blood pressure from baseline and is reported as a safety outcome.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The survey package (version 4.2–1) was used for IPTW-weighted regression with sandwich standard errors, and the pscl package (version 1.5.5.1) for zero-inflated count models.

Missing data were minimal (<3% for all key variables). Patients with missing critical data elements required for analysis (n=32) were excluded from the analytic sample. For the 1,189 patients included in the analytic sample, no imputation was performed given the low proportion of missingness among the retained variables. All analyses were conducted on complete cases.

Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting

Because group assignment was determined by institutional practice rather than randomization, we used inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) to balance measured baseline covariates.27 Propensity scores were estimated by multivariable logistic regression with the following covariates: patient age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, Mallampati airway class, anesthetic medication use (propofol, etomidate, opioids, benzodiazepines), enrolling center, and procedure type (gastroscopy, colonoscopy, or combined). Center and procedure type were included because equipment availability and sedation protocols varied across institutions, and procedure type influences sedation depth and hypoxemia risk.

Stabilized Weights and Truncation

Stabilized weights were computed as w_i = p_i / e_i for treated subjects and w_i = (1 - p_i) / (1 - e_i) for control subjects, where e_i denotes the estimated propensity score and p_i the marginal probability of treatment assignment. Stabilized weights were preferred over unstabilized weights because they reduce variance inflation while preserving the causal estimand under the assumption of no unmeasured confounding.27 Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of extreme values.

Effective Sample Size and Balance Assessment

The effective sample size (ESS) after weighting was computed as ESS = (∑w_i)2 / ∑w_i2. Covariate balance was assessed by absolute standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.20 considered adequate. The effective sample size after weighting was 1188.9 (T group) and 1187.7 (C group), retaining 191.7% and 208.7% of the original sample, respectively. Maximum stabilized weight was below 4.5, indicating no undue influence from extreme weights.

Zero-Inflated Negative Binomial Regression

The primary analysis used zero-inflated negative binomial (ZINB) regression. The outcome – hypoxemia duration as a count of 10-second intervals – exhibited substantial overdispersion and an excess of zeros. The ZINB model accommodates both features through a dual-component structure: a negative binomial count component modeling expected hypoxemia duration among patients experiencing at least one event, and a logistic zero-inflation component modeling the probability of being a structural zero. Model selection was formally evaluated using the Vuong test comparing ZINB against standard negative binomial and hurdle negative binomial alternatives.

Model Specification and Standard Errors

Both components used a log-link function. The exponentiated coefficient exp(β) represents the incidence rate ratio (IRR), with β< 0 indicating shorter duration in the capnography-visible group. All ZINB models used IPTW-stabilized weights and Huber-White sandwich standard errors to account for weighting and residual center-level clustering. Sandwich standard errors were used because center was included as a covariate in the propensity score model rather than as a random effect; this approach yields valid inference under the working independence assumption.

Assumption Checking

Model assumptions were verified by examining the distribution of residuals, testing for overdispersion, and confirming that the Vuong test favored ZINB over alternative specifications. All models converged normally with stable parameter estimates.

Early Warning Interval Estimation

The early warning interval was estimated from ZINB regression coefficients using a piecewise regression framework. Cumulative hypoxemia duration was discretized into decile intervals of 10 seconds each (0–10 s, 10–20 s, …, 90–100 s). For each interval, the IPTW-weighted ZINB coefficient estimated the difference between groups in the probability of duration exceeding that interval. The divergence point in coefficient trajectories was interpreted as the estimated early warning interval. This is a model-derived estimate, not a direct patient-level measurement of elapsed time between EtCO2 abnormality and SpO2 decline.

Data Quality and Signal Processing

Signal quality was continuously monitored by the research team; periods with artifact or dislodgement were recorded but not excluded from analysis, as this reflects real-world clinical conditions.

Sensitivity Analyses

Three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, the E-value was computed for the primary IPTW-adjusted estimate to quantify the minimum strength of association (risk ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to fully explain away the observed association. E-values were calculated as RR + sqrt(RR x (RR - 1)) for risk ratios greater than 1 (or the reciprocal for protective associations). An E-value substantially exceeding the strength of measured confounders suggests that unmeasured confounding is unlikely to account for the observed effect.28

Second, a leave-one-center-out analysis iteratively excluded each center and refitted the primary ZINB model in the remaining two-center sample. Consistency across all three iterations indicates that no single center dominated the results.

Third, alternative model specifications – a hurdle negative binomial model and a gamma regression with log-link applied to continuous duration in seconds – were evaluated to assess the dependence of findings on the count-data framework.

Sample Size

The original sample size calculation was performed using PASS 11 software (NCSS, LLC., Kaysville, UT, USA) with hypoxemia incidence (SpO2 < 90%) as the primary endpoint. Assuming incidences of 12.5% in the capnography-visible group and 19.8% in the concealed group, a two-sided significance level alpha = 0.05, and power 1 - beta = 0.90, the required sample size was 1,058 patients (529 per group). With a 10% attrition allowance, the target was 1,176 patients (588 per group).

We acknowledge that the primary endpoint was subsequently changed from incidence to cumulative hypoxemia duration. Although the sample size was not specifically calculated for the duration endpoint, the observed effect size (30% reduction) was detected with adequate precision, as reflected by the 95% confidence interval (0.54–0.91) for the primary estimate. The change in primary endpoint after study completion and the absence of a pre-specified duration target are acknowledged as limitations, and this is discussed in the Limitations section.

Software

All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The survey package (version 4.2–1) was used for IPTW-weighted regression with sandwich standard errors, and the pscl package (version 1.5.5.1) for zero-inflated count models. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analytical scripts are available at [GitHub repository URL] to ensure reproducibility.

Potential sources of bias include: (1) confounding by indication, addressed through IPTW adjustment for measured covariates; (2) performance bias, acknowledged as inherent to the unblinded design and discussed in detail in the Limitations section; (3) residual confounding from unmeasured variables, assessed using E-value analysis; and (4) center-level clustering, addressed through inclusion of center in the propensity score model and use of sandwich standard errors. Despite these measures, residual confounding remains possible and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Results

Of these, 40 were excluded: 32 for missing critical data elements required for analysis, 3 for incidentally discovered gastrointestinal malignancies, and 5 for inadequate bowel preparation precluding colonoscopy completion. The remaining 1,189 patients were included in the analysis – 620 assigned to the capnography-visible group and 569 to the capnography-concealed group. All patients completed the procedure. Hypoxemia Outcomes

Incidence

No significant difference in hypoxemia incidence was observed between groups at any severity threshold, before or after IPTW adjustment (Table 2). A decile-stratified analysis of subclinical hypoxemia duration (Table S1) showed that the overall duration reduction was not uniformly distributed across the duration spectrum. Nine of ten deciles showed non-significant between-group differences. The association was concentrated in the 90th-100th decile (patients with the longest hypoxemic episodes; β = −222.7), indicating that the observed duration reduction was attributable to fewer prolonged hypoxemic episodes in the capnography-visible group rather than a generalized shortening of all episodes. This pattern constrains the breadth of the finding: capnography visibility was associated with prevention of extended desaturation events, not with systematic reduction in episode length across the entire patient distribution.

Estimated Early Warning Interval

The early warning interval conferred by capnography visibility —derived from regression model coefficients under a piecewise framework rather than directly measured from time-stamped EtCO2-to-SpO2 sequences—was approximately 30–40 seconds. This is a group-level estimate, not a patient-level latency, and is subject to substantial inter-individual variation depending on factors such as oxygen supplementation, body habitus, pulmonary reserve, and age. This estimate was derived, not directly measured, from ZINB regression coefficients under a piecewise framework in which cumulative hypoxemia duration was discretized into 10-second decile intervals. The divergence point in coefficient trajectories between groups – where capnography-visible clinicians appeared to initiate interventions before SpO2 decline became apparent – corresponded to approximately three to four 10-second intervals. This estimate falls within the physiologically plausible range for capnography-to-desaturation latency established in prior controlled studies, and represents a clinically actionable interval for low-intensity corrective maneuvers.

Safety Outcomes

Hypotension, defined as systolic blood pressure more than 20% below baseline, occurred in 11 of 620 patients (1.8%) in the T group versus 0 of 569 (0%) in the C group (absolute difference 1.8%, 95% CI 0.8% to 2.9%; P = 0.004)(Table 2). No cases of bradycardia requiring intervention, tachycardia, cardiac arrest, or cardiovascular collapse occurred in either group. The clinical significance of this blood pressure difference is uncertain: the absolute magnitude is small, the event count is low, and the comparison was not adjusted for multiple testing across safety endpoints. This observation should be interpreted as a safety signal warranting monitoring in future studies rather than as evidence of harm attributable to capnography visibility.

Sensitivity Analyses

For the primary outcome (IPTW-adjusted subclinical hypoxemia duration), the E-value for the point estimate was 2.54, and for the confidence interval bound closest to the null was 1.74. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both capnography exposure and hypoxemia duration by a risk ratio of at least 2.54 to fully explain away the observed association, and by at least 1.74 to shift the confidence interval to include the null. These thresholds exceed the magnitude of all measured confounders, suggesting that substantial unmeasured confounding is unlikely. An E-value of 2.54 implies that an unmeasured confounder would need to more than double the risk of both exposure and outcome to nullify our findings—a magnitude exceeding that of all measured variables, supporting the stability of the association.

Leave-one-center-out analysis yielded consistent results across all three iterations: excluding center 1, beta = −0.412 (95% CI −0.738 to −0.086); excluding center 2, beta = −0.298 (95% CI −0.621 to 0.025); excluding center 3, beta = −0.356 (95% CI −0.678 to −0.034). The coefficient range (−0.298 to −0.412) was narrow and contained within the primary confidence interval. Two of three analyses remained significant at P < 0.05; the third (P = 0.072) preserved the direction and magnitude of the primary estimate. These results indicate that the observed association was not dependent on any individual center.

Alternative model specifications – standard negative binomial regression, zero-inflated Poisson regression, hurdle negative binomial regression – all yielded directionally consistent results for subclinical hypoxemia duration, with coefficients ranging from −0.289 to −0.401, corresponding to IRRs of 0.67 to 0.75 (25–33% duration reductions). Discussion

Principal Findings

In this multicenter clinician-blinded device evaluation of 1,189 elderly patients, visibility of the capnography display was associated with a 30% shorter cumulative duration of subclinical hypoxemia (IRR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.91; P = 0.007) and an 86% increase in early proactive airway interventions (RR 1.86, 95% CI 1.57–2.19; P < 0.001), with no difference in hypoxemia incidence between groups. These findings are not contradictory; they are methodologically coherent and characterize how visible ventilatory monitoring information may alter provider behavior in routine practice. Because the concealment design does not involve randomization, we make no causal claims; all findings are reported as associations.

The null incidence finding is methodologically expected. In this concealment design, the capnography device was attached to every patient; the only difference was display visibility. We tested the incremental benefit of visible ventilatory data rather than the effect of hardware presence versus absence tested by prior RCTs. When hardware is identical in both arms, there is no mechanistic basis for a difference in event occurrence. The absence of an incidence effect therefore confirms internal consistency rather than representing a failed primary outcome.

Notably, the association for moderate hypoxemia did not reach statistical significance (IRR 0.69, 95% CI 0.46–1.04; P = 0.074), suggesting that the benefit was robust primarily in the subclinical range (SpO2 90%–<95%) rather than uniformly across all severity strata. No association was observed for severe hypoxemia (P = 0.514), which occurred in fewer than 3% of patients and was therefore underpowered.

The intervention pattern reveals a potential behavioral mechanism. The increase in airway interventions was predominantly jaw-thrust maneuvers—minimal, rapidly executed maneuvers—suggesting that visible EtCO2 data may have helped clinicians detect subtle ventilatory abnormalities and act before significant desaturation developed.29 The estimated 30- to 40-second advance warning was derived from regression model coefficients rather than directly measured from time-stamped EtCO2-to-SpO2 sequences it is a group-level estimate, not a patient-level latency, and may vary considerably across individuals. The hypotension signal (1.8% versus 0%; P = 0.004) warrants discussion. The absolute difference is small, but the higher total anesthetic doses in the capnography-visible group provide a plausible explanation: greater dosing confidence with visible monitoring may have produced deeper sedation with attendant hemodynamic depression. This is a safety signal, not a dismissal of the technology. Concurrent hemodynamic vigilance may be advisable when anesthetic titration is informed by ventilatory data.

Relation to Other Studies

The multicenter RCT by Lian et al (n = 1,777) reported that randomization to capnography reduced hypoxemia incidence from 19% to 12% (P<0.001) compared with standard monitoring without capnography.23 Their trial and ours answer complementary questions. Lian et al tested hardware presence versus absence under randomized conditions; we tested the incremental benefit of visible waveform data —with the device present in both arms—under routine practice. Their incidence finding indicates that deploying hardware prevents events; our duration finding suggests that activating the display shortens those that do occur through earlier provider response. Neither finding contradicts the other;both inform implementation.

The 2017 meta-analysis by Saunders et al (13 RCTs) reported risk ratios of 0.77 (95% CI 0.67–0.89) for mild and 0.59 (95% CI 0.43–0.81) for severe desaturation.18 Our study contributes a dimension meta-analyses cannot capture: duration-specific data under a concealment design that isolates the information pathway. Duration is a distinct metric from incidence—two patients can have identical incidence yet vastly different cumulative exposure if one has brief episodes and the other prolonged desaturations. In elderly patients with limited reserve, cumulative duration may be clinically relevant. The Saunders review noted a substantially longer delay for SpO2 detection compared with EtCO2 monitoring, with mean delays of approximately 60 seconds and 20 seconds, respectively. Our estimated 30- to 40-second window falls within this physiological range and extends these observations to real-world practice.

The negative RCT by Mehta et al (n = 495), found no capnography benefit in routine endoscopy under moderate sedation. Mehta studied predominantly healthy population (mean age 55 years) undergoing moderate sedation with midazolam and fentanyl—a low-risk context where event rates and progression velocity from respiratory depression to desaturation are attenuated. In such settings, capnography has limited discriminatory power.30 Our elderly cohort (mean age ~70 years) receiving propofol-based deep sedation had substantially higher baseline risk, greater propofol-induced airway obstruction, and the event density needed to detect duration and response differences. The divergent results are reconcilable through population risk profile and sedation depth rather than reflecting a genuine contradiction in capnography efficacy. They reinforce the specificity of benefit: elderly patients, deep sedation, and high baseline hypoxemia risk.

The increased frequency of jaw-thrust maneuvers should be interpreted with caution. This may reflect earlier recognition, a lower intervention threshold, heightened vigilance, or a more vigorous response to available information—rather than superior quality of care. Importantly, we did not observe differences in clinically important endpoints such as severe hypoxemia, procedural interruption, or adverse events, which constrains any inference that more frequent interventions equate to improved clinical performance.

Clinical Implications

The following implications are offered as hypothesis-generating considerations rather than definitive practice recommendations. They are bounded by three fundamental limitations: (1) our findings are associations, not causal effects; (2) the primary endpoint was a surrogate rather than patient-centered; and (3) the benefit was confined to the subclinical range, with no significant effect on moderate or severe hypoxemia.

First, for high-volume endoscopy units, the estimated 30- to 40-second response window may inform protocol design. This aligns with the Anesthesia Patient Safety Foundation’s vision that “no patient shall be harmed by opioid-induced respiratory depression”,31 by suggesting that early detection through visible capnography may enable clinicians to intervene before respiratory compromise progresses to oxygen desaturation. A graded airway intervention algorithm triggered by EtCO2 changes rather than SpO2 decline could help standardize provider response and potentially reduce hypoxemia duration.32 The predominance of jaw-thrust maneuvers suggests that early response protocols need not be complex. This approach is consistent with the American Society of Anesthesiologists’ standards for basic anesthetic monitoring, which emphasize the value of ventilatory monitoring during procedural sedation.33 However, whether protocolized EtCO2-triggered interventions improve patient-centered outcomes remains unknown and requires prospective validation.

Second, for training programs, visible waveforms may aid junior anesthesiologists and non-anesthesiologist providers. Experienced clinicians in this study more than doubled their intervention rate when EtCO2 data were visible; the relative effect among less experienced providers could be larger. However, all providers in this study had ≥5 years of dedicated experience, and this hypothesis requires direct investigation before extrapolation.

Third, the cost context matters. A previous model estimated that capnography becomes cost-saving at procedural volumes exceeding 295 cases per year, with a median number needed to treat of 8 during deep sedation. Chinese tertiary endoscopy units routinely exceed this threshold. However, we did not collect cost data, and China-specific economic analysisis needed to inform reimbursement decisions.

These implications are bounded by what the study did not measure. We did not monitor sedation depth with processed electroencephalography or structured scores; we cannot determine whether the higher anesthetic doses reflected adequate or excessive sedation. We did not assess patient-centered outcomes—satisfaction, recovery time, cognitive function, or procedure quality metrics such as adenoma detection rate. These findings suggest parameters for implementation; they do not prove that implementation improves patient experience or procedural outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, including a large elderly sample from three tertiary centers, a concealment design that isolates the incremental value of visible waveform data, prospective enrollment with real-time data capture, extensive IPTW adjustment, and sensitivity analyses that confirm result stability.

The limitations are substantial. The quasi-experimental design without randomization is the principal threat to internal validity. IPTW balances measured confounders but cannot address unmeasured confounding by center-level workflow, provider vigilance, or patient selection preferences. Center-level allocation introduces potential cluster confounding; the small number of centers (three) limits cluster adjustment. Performance bias is inherent: clinicians could not be blinded, and knowledge of group assignment may have influenced vigilance, intervention thresholds, and sedation strategies—all of which directly affect the primary outcomes. Residual confounding remains a concern despite IPTW, as important differences persisted in sedative selection and dosing. Anesthetic agents have a dual role as both confounder and potential mediator; including them in the propensity score may partially adjust away a mediator pathway.

The sample size was calculated for incidence, not duration; the change in primary endpoint after study completion modestly inflates type I error risk. The estimated 30- to 40-second early warning interval is a group-level estimate, not a patient-level latency, constraining its use for individual care decisions. The single-country setting and restriction to ASA I–II patients limit generalizability to higher-risk populations. All sedation providers were anesthesiologists with ≥5 years of experience; extrapolation to less experienced clinicians or nurse-administered sedation is speculative. No formal sedation depth monitoring or patient-centered outcomes were measured; we cannot determine whether shorter hypoxemia duration translates into improved recovery, reduced delirium, or better procedure quality.

An additional limitation concerns the clinical relevance of the primary endpoint itself. Subclinical hypoxemia (SpO2 90% to <95%) was selected as the primary outcome because it is the most sensitive metric for capturing provider response behavior under the concealment design. However, the clinical significance of reducing cumulative duration in this range remains uncertain. Mild transient desaturation in this range is common and may not necessarily reflect significant respiratory compromise.30 Our study was not powered to detect patient-centered endpoints, and we did not measure neurocognitive function, recovery quality, or procedure-related complications. The reduction in subclinical hypoxemia duration should therefore be interpreted as a mechanistic or surrogate finding, not as evidence of improved clinical outcomes. Table S1 indicates that the duration effect was concentrated in the upper decile; nine of ten deciles showed no significant difference. The 30% reduction reflects truncation of the longest events rather than uniform compression. Complex procedures (EUS, ERCP) were excluded; cost data were not collected. Finally, the hypotension signal (1.8% versus 0%; P = 0.004) suggests capnography visibility may be associated with hemodynamically deeper sedation; concurrent blood pressure monitoring may be advisable when anesthetic titration is guided by ventilatory data.

Conclusion

In this multicenter, clinician-blinded device evaluation of 1,189 patients aged ≥65 years undergoing sedated gastrointestinal endoscopy, capnography display visibility was associated with a 30% shorter subclinical hypoxemia duration (IRR 0.70, 95% CI 0.54–0.91) and an 86% increase in early airway interventions (RR 1.86, 95% CI 1.57–2.19). However, these findings represent associations, not causal effects, and the clinical significance of reducing subclinical hypoxemia duration—in the absence of improvements in patient-centered outcomes—remains uncertain. Given the observational design and exclusion of high-risk patients and complex procedures, prospective randomized trials with patient-centered outcomes are needed to confirm whether these mechanistic benefits translate into patient-relevant outcomes. The current evidence supports further investigation of capnography’s role in high-volume settings, with future cost-effectiveness analysis needed to inform implementation decisions.
