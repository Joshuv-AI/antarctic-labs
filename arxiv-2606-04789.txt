Combinatorial and analytic aspects of independence polynomials of zero divisor graphs
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
The independence polynomial of a graph encapsulates all independent sets of differing sizes, a task classified as NP-hard in theoretical computer science. This article examines the independence polynomial of zero divisor graphs in commutative rings. We demonstrate that the independent sets, represented as a sequence of coefficients of the independence polynomial, exhibit unimodality and log-concavity. Therefore, for the independence polynomial of some zero divisor graphs, the unimodal conjecture is true. Additionally, the characteristics of the zeros of the independence polynomial are delineated, along with their corresponding annular regions on the plane.



Keywords:
Zero divisor graphs, Independence polynomial; NP-hard problem; Zeros; Unimodal; Log-concave

Independence polynomial of zero divisor graph of integral modulo ring

An
independent set
in a graph
\displaystyle G
is a set of vertices no two of which are adjacent. For a graph
\displaystyle G,
the cardinality of the maximum size of an independent set, is called the
independence number
of
\displaystyle G
, and is denoted by
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
. The associated
decision problem

\text{Independent set}=\{(G,k):\alpha(G)\geq k\},

is one of the classical NP-complete problems, and the optimization problem of computing
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
is therefore NP-hard, where
\displaystyle k
is a positive integer. Karp showed in his seminal list of NP-complete problems that the independent set problem is NP-complete, and a fundamental problem
[
23
,
15
]
. Since, the independent set problem is equivalent (under complementation) to clique set in
\displaystyle G
, known inapproximability bounds for clique number transfer immediately. It is NP-hard to approximate the maximum independent set within a factor of
\displaystyle n^{1-\varepsilon}
for any fixed
\displaystyle\varepsilon>0
, unless
\displaystyle\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}

[
13
]
. The NP-completeness of independent set implies that, unless
\displaystyle\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}
, there is no polynomial-time algorithm that computes
\displaystyle\alpha(G)
on general graphs. Moreover, the strong inapproximability results rule out polynomial-time algorithms with approximation ratio
\displaystyle n^{1-\varepsilon},\varepsilon>0
, placing the problem among the most intractable maximization problems from the perspective of worst-case approximation.

Let
\displaystyle s_{k}(G)
be the number of independent sets of size
\displaystyle k
in
\displaystyle G
. Then the
independence polynomial
of
\displaystyle G
is the generating function of possible independent sets, and is given as

I(G,x)\;=\;\sum_{k=0}^{\alpha(G)}s_{k}(G)\,x^{k}.

The polynomial
\displaystyle I(G,x)
was introduced and studied systematically in the 1980s and has since become a standard enumerative invariant with connections to statistical physics (the hard–core model), combinatorial optimization, and algebraic graph theory
[
17
,
24
]

\displaystyle I(G,x)
at special points recovers several natural quantities. In particular,
\displaystyle I(G,1)=\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}(G)
counts all independent sets of
\displaystyle G
, while
\displaystyle I(G,0)=1
and
\displaystyle I^{\prime}(G,0)=s_{1}(G)=|V(G)|
. For
\displaystyle x>0
,
\displaystyle I(G,x)
is (up to a trivial change of variable) the partition function of the hardcore lattice gas on
\displaystyle G

\displaystyle x

[
34
]

If
\displaystyle G
and
\displaystyle H
are vertex disjoint graphs, then

I(G\cup H,x)\;=\;I(G,x)\,I(H,x),

where
\displaystyle G\cup H
is the union of graphs. The above expression follows as an independent set in
\displaystyle G\cup H
is precisely the disjoint union of an independent set of
\displaystyle G
and one of
\displaystyle H

[

,

,

,

\displaystyle L(G)

\displaystyle m_{k}(G)

\sum_{k\geq 0}m_{k}(G)\,x^{k}\;=\;I\bigl(L(G),x\bigr).

Thus, many properties of independence polynomials transfer to matching generating functions, and vice versa

For

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}})

\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}

\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}})

I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}),x)=(1+x)^{p^{2}-p}+(1+(p-1)x)-1=(1+x)^{p^{2}-p}+(p-1)x.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle n=p^{3}
, the set of non-zero zero divisors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}
are the multiples of
\displaystyle p
in
\displaystyle\{1,\dots,p^{3}-1\}
. We partition the vertex set
\displaystyle V
into two disjoint subsets:
\displaystyle V_{1}=\{a\in V\mid\gcd(a,p^{3})=p\}=\{kp\mid 1\leq k<p^{2},p\nmid k\}
, and
\displaystyle V_{2}=\{a\in V\mid\gcd(a,p^{3})=p^{2}\}=\{kp^{2}\mid 1\leq k<p\}
, where
\displaystyle\nmid
represents does not divide. The sizes of these sets are:
\displaystyle|V_{1}|=(p^{2}-1)-(p-1)=p^{2}-p
and
\displaystyle|V_{2}|=p-1
. Let
\displaystyle a=k_{1}p
and
\displaystyle b=k_{2}p
be distinct vertices in
\displaystyle V_{1}
, where
\displaystyle p\nmid k_{1}
and
\displaystyle p\nmid k_{2}
. Then their product is
\displaystyle ab=k_{1}k_{2}p^{2}
. Since
\displaystyle p
is prime, it does not divide
\displaystyle k_{1}
or
\displaystyle k_{2}
, and
\displaystyle p\nmid k_{1}k_{2}
. Thus,
\displaystyle ab\not\equiv 0\pmod{p^{3}}
, and it follows that there are no edges between any two vertices in
\displaystyle V_{1}
. The subgraph induced by
\displaystyle V_{1}
is an edgeless graph
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{p^{2}-p}
. Let
\displaystyle a=k_{1}p^{2}
and
\displaystyle b=k_{2}p^{2}
be distinct vertices in
\displaystyle V_{2}
. Then their product is
\displaystyle ab=k_{1}k_{2}p^{4}=(k_{1}k_{2}p)p^{3}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{3}}
. Thus, every pair of distinct vertices in
\displaystyle V_{2}
is adjacent. So, the subgraph induced by
\displaystyle V_{2}
is a complete graph
\displaystyle K_{p-1}
. Let
\displaystyle a=k_{1}p\in V_{1}
and
\displaystyle b=k_{2}p^{2}\in V_{2}
. Their product is
\displaystyle ab=(k_{1}p)(k_{2}p^{2})=k_{1}k_{2}p^{3}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{3}}
. So, if follows that every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{1}
is adjacent to every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{2}
. Thus,
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}})\cong K_{|V_{2}|}\vee\overline{K}_{|V_{1}|}.
Keeping in mind the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{n}
and
\displaystyle K_{n}

2.2

I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}),x)=(1+x)^{p^{2}-p}+(1+(p-1)x)-1=(1+x)^{p^{2}-p}+(p-1)x.

The following result gives unimodal and log-concave property of
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}),x).

Theorem 2.2
.

The independence polynomial
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}),x)
of the zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}})
, with prime
\displaystyle p
is log-concave and unimodal.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle P(x)=I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{3}}),x)
, and with
\displaystyle n=p^{2}-p
, we have

P(x)=(1+x)^{n}+(p-1)x=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}x^{k}+(p-1)x.

Let the coefficient sequence of
\displaystyle P(x)
be
\displaystyle(\ell_{k})_{k=0}^{n}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\ell_{0}=\binom{n}{0}=1,\ell_{1}=\binom{n}{1}+p-1=(p^{2}-p)+p-1=p^{2}-1
, and
\displaystyle\ell_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
for
\displaystyle k\in\{2,3,\dots,n\}.

Clearly, the coefficients of
\displaystyle P(x)
are positive for
\displaystyle p\geq 2
. For
\displaystyle k=1
, we must have
\displaystyle\ell_{1}^{2}\geq\ell_{0}\ell_{2}
. Which translates to
\displaystyle(p^{2}-1)^{2}\geq 1\cdot\binom{p^{2}-p}{2}
, and gives
\displaystyle(p-1)^{2}(p+1)^{2}\geq\tfrac{p(p-1)(p^{2}-p-1)}{2}
. It simplifies to
\displaystyle 2(p-1)(p+1)^{2}\geq p(p^{2}-p-1)
, which is true for
\displaystyle p\geq 2.
For
\displaystyle k=2
,
\displaystyle\ell_{2}^{2}\geq\ell_{1}\ell_{3}
implies that
\displaystyle\binom{p^{2}-p}{2}^{2}\geq(p^{2}-1)\binom{p^{2}-p}{3}
.
For
\displaystyle p=2
, the inequality
\displaystyle\ell_{2}^{2}\geq 0
is trivially true. For
\displaystyle p\geq 3
, with
\displaystyle n=p^{2}-p\geq 6
, the inequality is

\displaystyle\left(\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\right)^{2}\geq(p^{2}-1)\tfrac{n(n-1)(n-2)}{6}
. And by dividing
\displaystyle\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}
, it yields
\displaystyle\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\geq(p^{2}-1)\tfrac{n-2}{3}
.

\displaystyle 3n(n-1)\geq 2(p^{2}-1)(n-2)
, which after simplification gives
\displaystyle(p-1)^{3}+5\geq 0
. Thus, the inequality holds. For
\displaystyle k\geq 3
, and consider
\displaystyle\ell_{j}=\binom{n}{j}
for
\displaystyle j\in\{k-1,k,k+1\}
. So, we must show
\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}^{2}\geq\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{n}{k+1}
, which is a standard property of binomial coefficients. It is equivalent to the ratio test
\displaystyle\tfrac{\binom{n}{k}}{\binom{n}{k-1}}\geq\tfrac{\binom{n}{k+1}}{\binom{n}{k}}
, which is
\displaystyle\tfrac{n-k+1}{k}\geq\tfrac{n-k}{k+1}
. This simplifies to
\displaystyle(n-k+1)(k+1)\geq k(n-k)
, which reduces to
\displaystyle n+1\geq 0
. That is true as
\displaystyle n=p^{2}-p\geq 2
. Thus, the coefficient sequence
\displaystyle(\ell_{k})_{k=0}^{n}
of
\displaystyle P(x)
is log-concave, and a log-concave positive real sequence is unimodal.

\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})
as given below

I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)=(1+x)^{q-1}+(1+x)^{p-1}-1.

(2.3)

The following result gives the details about the real zeros of
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle x
be a zero of the polynomial
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)
. The properties of the zeros depend on the primes
\displaystyle p<q
.

1.

If
\displaystyle p,q
are odd primes. Then there are exactly two real zeros, one in the interval
\displaystyle(-1,0)
and another in
\displaystyle(-2,-1)
.

2.

If
\displaystyle p=2
and
\displaystyle q
is an odd prime, there are exactly two real zeros, one in the interval
\displaystyle(-1,0)
and another in
\displaystyle(-3,-2)
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle P(x)=(1+x)^{q-1}+(1+x)^{p-1}-1
be an independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})
. With
\displaystyle y=1+x
, the problem reduces to finding the roots of the equation
\displaystyle y^{q-1}+y^{p-1}=1
. We analyze the roots based on the parity of the primes
\displaystyle p<q
. If
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle q
are odd primes, then
\displaystyle p-1=2k
and
\displaystyle q-1=2m
for some integers
\displaystyle k,m\geq 1
. Thus, the equation becomes
\displaystyle y^{2m}+y^{2k}=1
. For real
\displaystyle y
, let
\displaystyle z=y^{2}\geq 0
. Then the equation becomes
\displaystyle z^{m}+z^{k}=1
. If
\displaystyle f(z)=z^{m}+z^{k}-1
, then for
\displaystyle z>0
, we get
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(z)=mz^{m-1}+kz^{k-1}>0
. So, the function
\displaystyle f(z)
is strictly increasing. Clearly,
\displaystyle f(0)=-1
and
\displaystyle f(1)=1
, so by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there is a unique positive real root
\displaystyle z_{0}\in(0,1)
. This gives two real roots for
\displaystyle y
, namely
\displaystyle y=\pm\sqrt{z_{0}}
. Since
\displaystyle 0<z_{0}<1
, we have
\displaystyle 0<\sqrt{z_{0}}<1
. The corresponding zeros of
\displaystyle P(x)
are
\displaystyle x_{1}=\sqrt{z_{0}}-1\in(-1,0)
and
\displaystyle x_{2}=-\sqrt{z_{0}}-1\in(-2,-1)
. That proves (1).

If
\displaystyle p=2
,
\displaystyle q
is an odd prime, then the equation for
\displaystyle y=1+x
becomes
\displaystyle y^{q-1}+y=1
.
With
\displaystyle f(y)=y^{q-1}+y-1
, we have
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(y)=(q-1)y^{q-2}+1
. As
\displaystyle q
is an odd prime,
\displaystyle q-2
is odd, and
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(y)=0
has a unique real solution
\displaystyle y_{c}=-(1/(q-1))^{1/(q-2)}\in(-1,0)
, which is a local minimum. Also,

\displaystyle f(0)=-1
and
\displaystyle f(1)=1
, so by Intermediate Value Theorem, there is a root
\displaystyle y_{1}\in(0,1)
.
As
\displaystyle y\to\pm\infty
, then
\displaystyle f(y)\to+\infty
. The minimum value is
\displaystyle f(y_{c})=y_{c}(1-1/(q-1))-1<-1
. Thus, there must be another real root
\displaystyle y_{2}<y_{c}<0
. Since
\displaystyle f(-1)=-1
and
\displaystyle f(-2)=(-2)^{q-1}-3=2^{q-1}-3\geq 1
for
\displaystyle q\geq 3
, this second root lies in
\displaystyle(-2,-1)
.
The corresponding zeros of
\displaystyle P(x)
are
\displaystyle x_{1}=y_{1}-1\in(-1,0)
and
\displaystyle x_{2}=y_{2}-1\in(-3,-2)
. That proves part (2).

For the remaining zeros of
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x),
we have the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle x
be a zero of the polynomial
\displaystyle P(x)=I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)
. With
\displaystyle p<q
, the zeros
\displaystyle x
of
\displaystyle P(x)
lie in the open annular region
\displaystyle r_{1}<|x|<r_{2}
, where

r_{1}=\frac{R-1}{R^{q-1}+R^{p-1}-2}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{2}=R+1,\quad\text{with}\quad R=2^{1/(q-p)}.

Proof.

By Equation (
2.3
), the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})
is

P(x)=I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)=(1+x)^{q-1}+(1+x)^{p-1}-1.

Let
\displaystyle z
be a zero of the polynomial
\displaystyle P(x)
, such that

P(z)=(1+z)^{q-1}+(1+z)^{p-1}-1=0.

Let
\displaystyle w=1+z
. Then
\displaystyle z=w-1
, and
\displaystyle w
must be a zero of the new polynomial
\displaystyle Q(w)=w^{q-1}+w^{p-1}-1
. The degree of
\displaystyle P(x)
is
\displaystyle q-1
, so there are
\displaystyle q-1
such zeros. Note that
\displaystyle P(0)=1
, so
\displaystyle z=0
is not a zero, and consequently
\displaystyle w=1
is not a zero of
\displaystyle Q(w)
. We first establish bounds for the modulus of the zeros of
\displaystyle Q(w)
. We claim that any zero
\displaystyle w
of
\displaystyle Q(w)=w^{q-1}+w^{p-1}-1
satisfies
\displaystyle|w|<2^{1/(q-p)}
. The equation
\displaystyle Q(w)=0
can be written as
\displaystyle w^{q-1}=1-w^{p-1}
. If
\displaystyle|w|\leq 1
, the claim holds, since
\displaystyle p,q
are primes with
\displaystyle p<q
, so
\displaystyle q-p\geq 1
, which implies
\displaystyle 2^{1/(q-p)}>1
. Now, if
\displaystyle|w|>1
, then with the help of the triangle inequality, we have

|w|^{q-1}=|1-w^{p-1}|\leq 1+|w|^{p-1}.

If
\displaystyle x=|w|
, then with
\displaystyle x>1
and
\displaystyle p-1>0
, we can divide by
\displaystyle x^{p-1}
, and obtain

x^{q-p}\leq\frac{1}{x^{p-1}}+1<1+1=2.

The above inequality is strict as
\displaystyle x>1
. Thus,
\displaystyle|w|^{q-p}<2
, which implies that
\displaystyle|w|<2^{1/(q-p)}
. So, with
\displaystyle R=2^{1/(q-p)}
, it shows that all zeros
\displaystyle w
of
\displaystyle Q(w)
lie inside the circle
\displaystyle|w|=R
. Next, we find the annular region for the zeros
\displaystyle z=w-1
. The modulus of a zero
\displaystyle z
is given by
\displaystyle|z|=|w-1|
. By the triangle inequality and by
\displaystyle|w|^{q-p}<2
, we have

|z|=|w-1|\leq|w|+1<R+1=2^{1/(q-p)}+1.

Next, as
\displaystyle w
is a zero of
\displaystyle Q(w)
, so
\displaystyle Q(w)=0
. Also
\displaystyle w\neq 1
, and
\displaystyle Q(1)=1^{q-1}+1^{p-1}-1=1
. Now consider the difference
\displaystyle Q(w)-Q(1)=(w^{q-1}+w^{p-1}-1)-1=-1.

Also, we have

Q(w)-Q(1)=(w^{q-1}-1)+(w^{p-1}-1)

Comparing and factoring the differences of powers, we have

(w-1)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{q-2}w^{k}\right)+(w-1)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}w^{k}\right)=-1.

With
\displaystyle z=w-1
, and
\displaystyle S(w)=\sum_{k=0}^{q-2}w^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}w^{k}
, we get
\displaystyle z\cdot S(w)=-1
.
Since
\displaystyle z\neq 0
, so
\displaystyle S(w)\neq 0
, and we can write
\displaystyle|z|=\tfrac{1}{|S(w)|}
.
To find a lower bound for
\displaystyle|z|
, we need an upper bound for
\displaystyle|S(w)|
. So, by triangle inequality, we have

|S(w)|\leq\left|\sum_{k=0}^{q-2}w^{k}\right|+\left|\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}w^{k}\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{q-2}|w|^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}|w|^{k}.

Since
\displaystyle|w|<R
, the above geometric series sums are bounded as

|S(w)|<\sum_{k=0}^{q-2}R^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{p-2}R^{k}=\frac{R^{q-1}-1}{R-1}+\frac{R^{p-1}-1}{R-1}=\frac{R^{q-1}+R^{p-1}-2}{R-1}.

Therefore, we obtain

|z|=\frac{1}{|S(w)|}>\frac{R-1}{R^{q-1}+R^{p-1}-2}

Now, combining these results, the zeros
\displaystyle z
of
\displaystyle P(x)
are located in the annular region

\frac{R-1}{R^{q-1}+R^{p-1}-2}<|z|<R+1,

where
\displaystyle R=2^{1/(q-p)}
.

Next result show that the independence polynomial
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)
of graph
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})

is unimodal and log-concave.

Theorem 2.5
.

The polynomial
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)
is log-concave and unimodal.

Proof.

Since the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle Gamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq})
is

\displaystyle P(x)=I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)=(1+x)^{n}+(1+x)^{m}-1,

where
\displaystyle n=q-1
and
\displaystyle m=p-1
. As
\displaystyle p<q
are primes, so we have
\displaystyle 1\leq m<n
. Let

\displaystyle P(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\ell_{k}x^{k}.

Then

\displaystyle\ell_{0}=1,

and, for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\ell_{k}=\binom{n}{k}+\binom{m}{k},

where we use the convention
\displaystyle\binom{m}{k}=0
whenever
\displaystyle k>m
. Thus,

\displaystyle\ell_{k}=\binom{n}{k}+\binom{m}{k}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq m,

and

\displaystyle\ell_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
for
\displaystyle m+1\leq k\leq n.

We must show that

\displaystyle\ell_{k}^{2}\geq\ell_{k-1}\ell_{k+1}
for
\displaystyle 1\leq k\leq n-1.

For
\displaystyle k=1
, we have

\displaystyle\ell_{1}^{2}-\ell_{0}\ell_{2}=\tfrac{n^{2}+m^{2}+4mn+n+m}{2}>0,
and hence the log-concavity inequality holds for
\displaystyle k=1
.
Next, assume that
\displaystyle 2\leq k\leq m
. If

\displaystyle N_{j}=\binom{n}{j},
and
\displaystyle M_{j}=\binom{m}{j},

then

\displaystyle\ell_{j}=N_{j}+M_{j}

for
\displaystyle j=k-1,k
, while the same formula also holds for
\displaystyle j=k+1
, if we agree that
\displaystyle M_{m+1}=0
when
\displaystyle k=m
. We will prove that

(N_{k}+M_{k})^{2}\geq(N_{k-1}+M_{k-1})(N_{k+1}+M_{k+1}),

or equivalently, by expanding, it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle N_{k}^{2}\geq N_{k-1}N_{k+1},M_{k}^{2}\geq M_{k-1}M_{k+1},

and

\displaystyle 2N_{k}M_{k}\geq N_{k-1}M_{k+1}+M_{k-1}N_{k+1}.

The first two inequalities are the standard log-concavity inequalities for binomial coefficients. It remains to prove the mixed type third inequality. As

\displaystyle\binom{r}{k}=\frac{r-k+1}{k}\binom{r}{k-1}

and

\displaystyle\binom{r}{k+1}=\frac{r-k}{k+1}\binom{r}{k},

the mixed inequality becomes

2\binom{n}{k}\binom{m}{k}\geq\binom{n}{k-1}\binom{m}{k+1}+\binom{m}{k-1}\binom{n}{k+1}.

Thus, by substituting their values, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2\frac{n-k+1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}\frac{m-k+1}{k}\binom{m}{k-1}\geq

\displaystyle\displaystyle\binom{n}{k-1}\frac{m-k}{k+1}\frac{m-k+1}{k}\binom{m}{k-1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\binom{m}{k-1}\frac{n-k}{k+1}\frac{n-k+1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}.

Since
\displaystyle 2\leq k\leq m
, the factors involved are non-negative, and after simplifying,
we obtain

2(k+1)(n-k+1)(m-k+1)\geq k(m-k)(m-k+1)+k(n-k)(n-k+1).

With compact notations

\displaystyle A=n-k+1,
and
\displaystyle B=m-k+1.
, we have

2(k+1)AB\geq k(B-1)B+k(A-1)A

which is equivalent to

2(k+1)\geq k\left(1-\frac{1}{B}\right)+k\left(1-\frac{1}{A}\right).

The right-hand side of above inequality is at most
\displaystyle 2k
, while the left-hand side is
\displaystyle 2k+2
. Hence the inequality holds, and
\displaystyle\ell_{k}^{2}\geq\ell_{k-1}\ell_{k+1}

for every
\displaystyle 2\leq k\leq m
. For the boundary index
\displaystyle k=m+1
, which occurs only when
\displaystyle m+1\leq n-1
, that is, when
\displaystyle n\geq m+2
. So,
\displaystyle\ell_{m+1}^{2}\geq\ell_{m}\ell_{m+2}
gives

\binom{n}{m+1}^{2}\geq\left(\binom{n}{m}+1\right)\binom{n}{m+2}.

If
\displaystyle a=n-m,
then
\displaystyle a\geq 2
as
\displaystyle n\geq m+2
. Thus, with

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m+1}=\binom{n}{m}\frac{a}{m+1}

and

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m+2}=\binom{n}{m}\frac{a(a-1)}{(m+1)(m+2)},

the inequality is equivalent to

\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}(n+1)\geq(a-1)(m+1).

Now
\displaystyle 1\leq m\leq n-2
, so
\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}\geq n
,
\displaystyle(a-1)+(m+1)=n,

and hence

(a-1)(m+1)\leq\frac{n^{2}}{4}\leq n(n+1)\leq\binom{n}{m}(n+1).

Therefore, the log-concavity inequality also holds at
\displaystyle k=m+1
. Finally, suppose
\displaystyle k\geq m+2
. Then

\displaystyle\ell_{k-1}=\binom{n}{k-1},\ell_{k}=\binom{n}{k},
and
\displaystyle\ell_{k+1}=\binom{n}{k+1}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\ell_{k}^{2}\geq\ell_{k-1}\ell_{k+1}

follows directly from the log-concavity of the binomial coefficients in the expansion of
\displaystyle(1+x)^{n}
.
Combining all cases, the coefficient sequence

\displaystyle(\ell_{0},\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n})

is log-concave. Since all coefficients are positive, the sequence has no internal zeros. A positive log-concave sequence is unimodal
[
33
]
. Therefore,
\displaystyle P(x)=I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pq}),x)
is both log-concave and unimodal.

3
Independence polynomial of zero divisor graph of
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q}

The following result gives the independent polynomial of the zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For primes
\displaystyle p<q
, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})
is

I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q});x)=(1+x)^{(p-1)(p+q-1)}+(1+x)^{p(q-1)}-(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)}+(p-1)x(1+x)^{p(p-1)}.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle G\cong\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q})
be the zero divisor graph of ring
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q}.
The vertex set
\displaystyle V
can be partitioned into four disjoint sets based on the greatest common divisor (gcd) with
\displaystyle n=p^{2}q
, that is,

\displaystyle V_{d}=\{k\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}\mid\gcd(k,n)=d\}
. The non-zero zero divisors correspond to
\displaystyle d\in\{p,p^{2},q,pq\}
, and their cardinalities are:

\displaystyle\displaystyle|V_{p}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\varphi(n/p)=\varphi(pq)=(p-1)(q-1),|V_{p^{2}}|=\varphi(n/p^{2})=\varphi(q)=q-1,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|V_{q}|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\varphi(n/q)=\varphi(p^{2})=p(p-1),|V_{pq}|=\varphi(n/pq)=\varphi(p)=p-1.

For each
\displaystyle d\in\{p,q,p^{2}\},
the set
\displaystyle V_{d}
is an independent set as
\displaystyle d^{2}
is not multiple of
\displaystyle n
. Also, the
\displaystyle V_{pq}
is a clique, since
\displaystyle(pq)^{2}
is multiple of
\displaystyle n
. Furthermore, each vertex of
\displaystyle V_{pq}
is adjacent to every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{p}
and
\displaystyle V_{p^{2}}
, and each vertex of
\displaystyle V_{q}
is adjacent to every vertex of
\displaystyle V_{p^{2}}.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
be the set of all independent sets of
\displaystyle G
. We partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
into two disjoint collections:

\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{S\in\mathcal{I}\mid S\cap V_{pq}=\emptyset\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{I}_{2}=\{S\in\mathcal{I}\mid S\cap V_{pq}\neq\emptyset\}.

Based on the above decomposition, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle G
can be written as:

I(G,x)=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{1}}x^{|S|}+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{I}_{2}}x^{|S|}.

First consider the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}
. In this case, the independent sets of
\displaystyle G
are the independent sets of the subgraph
\displaystyle G^{\prime}=\varGamma[V_{p}\cup V_{p^{2}}\cup V_{q}]
. As there are no edges between
\displaystyle V_{p}
and
\displaystyle V_{p^{2}}\cup V_{q}

\displaystyle G^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of
\displaystyle\varGamma[V_{p}]
and
\displaystyle\varGamma[V_{p^{2}}\cup V_{q}]
. We recall that the independence polynomial of a disjoint union of graphs is the product of their independence polynomials.
So, the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma[V_{p}]\cong\overline{K}_{|V_{p}|}

\displaystyle I(\varGamma[V_{p}];x)=(1+x)^{|V_{p}|}
. By (
2.2
), the independence polynomial of

\displaystyle\varGamma[V_{p^{2}}\cup V_{q}]\cong K_{|V_{p^{2}}|,|V_{q}|}

\displaystyle I(\varGamma[V_{p^{2}}\cup V_{q}];x)=(1+x)^{|V_{p^{2}}|}+(1+x)^{|V_{q}|}-1
.
Thus, the total contribution from
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}
is

I(G^{\prime},x)=(1+x)^{|V_{p}|}\left((1+x)^{|V_{p^{2}}|}+(1+x)^{|V_{q}|}-1\right).

Next, consider
\displaystyle S\in\mathcal{I}_{2}
. As
\displaystyle V_{pq}
is a clique, and it follows that
\displaystyle S
can contain at most one vertex from
\displaystyle V_{pq}
. So,
\displaystyle S\cap V_{pq}\neq\emptyset
, and
\displaystyle|S\cap V_{pq}|=1
. Assume that
\displaystyle S\cap V_{pq}=\{v\}
for some
\displaystyle v\in V_{pq}
, and by structure of
\displaystyle G,
we note that

\displaystyle v
is adjacent to every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{p}
and
\displaystyle V_{p^{2}}
. Thus, we obtain
\displaystyle S\cap V_{p}=\emptyset
and
\displaystyle S\cap V_{p^{2}}=\emptyset
. As there are no edges between
\displaystyle V_{pq}
and
\displaystyle V_{q}
, so the set,
\displaystyle S\setminus\{v\}
may be any subset of
\displaystyle V_{q}
.
Thus, any set in
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}
is of the form
\displaystyle\{v\}\cup S_{q}
where
\displaystyle v\in V_{pq}
and
\displaystyle S_{q}\subseteq V_{q}
. With this information, the generating function for such sets is:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V_{pq}}\sum_{S_{q}\subseteq V_{q}}x^{|\{v\}\cup S_{q}|}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in V_{pq}}\sum_{S_{q}\subseteq V_{q}}x^{1+|S_{q}|}=|V_{pq}|\sum_{k=0}^{|V_{q}|}\binom{|V_{q}|}{k}x^{1+k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=|V_{pq}|x\sum_{k=0}^{|V_{q}|}\binom{|V_{q}|}{k}x^{k}=|V_{pq}|x(1+x)^{|V_{q}|}.

Summing the contributions from
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}
, and substituting the values
\displaystyle|V_{p}|=(p-1)(q-1),|V_{p^{2}}|=q-1,|V_{q}|=p(p-1)
, and
\displaystyle|V_{pq}|=p-1
, we obtain the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle G
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(G;x)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)}\left((1+x)^{q-1}+(1+x)^{p(p-1)}-1\right)+(p-1)x(1+x)^{p(p-1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)+q-1}+(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)+p(p-1)}-(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+(p-1)x(1+x)^{p(p-1)}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(1+x)^{p(q-1)}+(1+x)^{(p-1)(p+q-1)}-(1+x)^{(p-1)(q-1)}+(p-1)x(1+x)^{p(p-1)}.

Theorem 3.2
.

For primes
\displaystyle p<q

Proof.

For brevity, we let

\displaystyle a=(p-1)(q-p-1),b=q-1,c=p(p-1),
and
\displaystyle d=p-1.

Since
\displaystyle p<q
, we have
\displaystyle a\geq 0
,
\displaystyle b\geq 2
,
\displaystyle c\geq 2
, and
\displaystyle d\geq 1
. It is easy to see that

\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{p^{2}q});x)=(1+x)^{c}H_{p,q}(x),

where

H_{p,q}(x)=(1+x)^{a}\left((1+x)^{b}+(1+x)^{c}-1\right)+dx.

Clearly,

\displaystyle c+a+c=(p-1)(p+q-1),c+a+b=p(q-1),

and

\displaystyle c+a=(p-1)(q-1).

We shall use the following standard fact: if two polynomials have non-negative log-concave coefficient sequences with no internal zeros, then their product also has a log-concave coefficient sequence. This is the usual convolution property of log-concave sequences
[
33
]
. Further, we note that

\displaystyle(1+x)^{m}+(1+x)^{n}-1

has a log-concave coefficient sequence for all positive integers
\displaystyle m,n
. Its coefficients are

1,\quad\binom{m}{1}+\binom{n}{1},\quad\binom{m}{2}+\binom{n}{2},\quad\ldots,

where
\displaystyle\binom{r}{k}=0
for
\displaystyle k>r
, and the log-concavity follows by the usual binomial-ratio verification.
Let

\displaystyle J_{p,q}(x)=(1+x)^{a}\left((1+x)^{b}+(1+x)^{c}-1\right).

The polynomial
\displaystyle(1+x)^{a}
is log-concave, and by the preceding observation, the polynomial
\displaystyle(1+x)^{b}+(1+x)^{c}-1
is log-concave. Hence,
\displaystyle J_{p,q}(x)
is log-concave. Let

\displaystyle J_{p,q}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}u_{k}x^{k}

and

\displaystyle H_{p,q}(x)=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{k}x^{k}.

Since
\displaystyle H_{p,q}(x)=J_{p,q}(x)+dx

\displaystyle h_{0}=u_{0}=1,h_{1}=u_{1}+d,
and
\displaystyle h_{k}=u_{k}
for
\displaystyle k\geq 2.

Therefore, all log-concavity inequalities for
\displaystyle H_{p,q}(x)
are inherited from
\displaystyle J_{p,q}(x)
except possibly the inequalities centered at
\displaystyle h_{1}
and
\displaystyle h_{2}
. Thus, it remains to verify

\displaystyle h_{1}^{2}\geq h_{0}h_{2}

and

\displaystyle h_{2}^{2}\geq h_{1}h_{3}.

Since

we get

\displaystyle h_{0}=1,
and
\displaystyle h_{1}=a+b+c+d=pq-1.
For
\displaystyle k\geq 2

\displaystyle dx

\displaystyle h_{k}=\binom{a+c}{k}+\binom{a+b}{k}-\binom{a}{k}.

In particular,

Similarly,

It remains to prove

\displaystyle h_{2}

\displaystyle h_{3}

\displaystyle+35p^{3}s-61p^{3}-4p^{2}s^{2}-34p^{2}s+37p^{2}+6ps^{2}+17ps-3p-6s-2.

Since
\displaystyle p(p-1)>0

\displaystyle y=0

\displaystyle x=-1

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle y\neq 0

\displaystyle I(y-1)=y^{p(p-1)}Q(y),

\displaystyle A=(p-1)(q-1),B=p(q-p),
and
\displaystyle C=(p-1)(q-p-1).

Thus the zeros of
\displaystyle I(x)
different from
\displaystyle x=-1
correspond exactly to the non-zero zeros of
\displaystyle Q(y)
. It is therefore enough to prove that every zero
\displaystyle y
of
\displaystyle Q(y)
satisfies

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}<|y|<2.

We shall prove separately that
\displaystyle Q(y)\neq 0
for
\displaystyle|y|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle|y|\geq 2

First suppose that
\displaystyle|y|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle Q(y)\neq 0

(p-1)=y^{A}+y^{B}-y^{C}+(p-1)y.

Now, with absolute values, we have

p-1\leq r^{A}+r^{B}+r^{C}+(p-1)r.

Next, we check that the right-hand side of above inequality is always strictly smaller than
\displaystyle p-1
.
If
\displaystyle(p,q)=(2,3)

\displaystyle Q(y)=2y^{2}+y-2.

If
\displaystyle|y|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
and
\displaystyle Q(y)=0

which is impossible. Next, let
\displaystyle p=2

Since
\displaystyle q\geq 5

\displaystyle\displaystyle|V_{p}|

Next, we classify all independent sets in
\displaystyle G.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}
be the set of all independent sets of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr})

\displaystyle V_{pq},V_{pr},V_{qr}
are pairwise connected, any independent set can have a non-empty intersection with at most one of them. This allows us to partition
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\{I\in\mathcal{I}\mid I\subseteq V_{p}\cup V_{q}\cup V_{r}\},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{I\in\mathcal{I}\mid I\cap V_{pq}\neq\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle I(x)=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}}x^{|I|}
is the sum of the polynomials for each family. Let
\displaystyle I_{k}(x)
be the polynomial for family
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{k}
. For the family
\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{0}
, an independent set
\displaystyle I\in\mathcal{I}_{0}

\displaystyle V_{p}\cup V_{q}\cup V_{r}
. Since there are no edges within or between these partitions, any subset of
\displaystyle V_{p}\cup V_{q}\cup V_{r}
is an independent set. Their cardinal number is
\displaystyle|V_{p}|+|V_{q}|+|V_{r}|
, and the corresponding polynomial is

I_{0}(x)=(1+x)^{|V_{p}|+|V_{q}|+|V_{r}|}=(1+x)^{(q-1)(r-1)+(p-1)(r-1)+(p-1)(q-1)}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle I\in\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle I\cap V_{pq}\neq\emptyset

and
\displaystyle I\cap V_{qr}=\emptyset

\displaystyle V_{pq}

\displaystyle V_{r}

\displaystyle I\cap V_{r}=\emptyset

\displaystyle I
must be a subset of
\displaystyle V_{p}\cup V_{q}\cup V_{pq}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle V_{pq}

\displaystyle(1+x)^{|V_{p}|+|V_{q}|}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr}),x)
be the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr})

\displaystyle p<q<r
are primes. Then
\displaystyle x=-1
is a zero of
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr}),x)

\displaystyle(p-1)(q+r-2)

\displaystyle x
of
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr}),x)
satisfies

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}<|1+x|<2.

Proof.

We use compact notations with

\displaystyle a=p-1,b=q-1,
and
\displaystyle c=r-1.
So, it is clear that
\displaystyle 1\leq a<b<c
. In the independence polynomial of
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr})
, we let
\displaystyle y=1+x
, and obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle I(y-1)=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\,y^{ab+bc+ca}+y^{ab+ac+a}-y^{ab+ac}+y^{ab+bc+b}-y^{ab+bc}+y^{ac+bc+c}-y^{ac+bc}.

Since
\displaystyle a<b<c
, the smallest exponent appearing in this expression is
\displaystyle ab+ac=a(b+c)
, and its coefficient is
\displaystyle-1
. Hence,
\displaystyle y=0
is a zero of
\displaystyle I(y-1)
with multiplicity
\displaystyle a(b+c)
, and thereby
\displaystyle x=-1

\displaystyle I(x)
with multiplicity

\displaystyle a(b+c)=(p-1)(q+r-2).
We can put
\displaystyle I(y-1)

\displaystyle I(y-1)=y^{ab+ac}H(y),

We first prove that
\displaystyle H(y)
has no zeros in
\displaystyle|y|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
. On the circle
\displaystyle|y|=\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle H(y)=-1+S(y),

Since
\displaystyle a\geq 1

\displaystyle bc\geq 8

\displaystyle|S(y)|<|-1|=1.

\displaystyle H(y)

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle|y|<\tfrac{1}{2}

\displaystyle H(y)
has no zeros in
\displaystyle|y|<\tfrac{1}{2}
. The strict inequality also shows that
\displaystyle H(y)\neq 0
on
\displaystyle|y|=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Hence, every zero of
\displaystyle H(y)
satisfies

\displaystyle|y|>\tfrac{1}{2}.
Next, we prove that every zero of
\displaystyle H(y)
satisfies
\displaystyle|y|<2
. Let

\displaystyle H(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{d}c_{j}y^{j},

where
\displaystyle d=\deg H
and
\displaystyle c_{d}\neq 0

\displaystyle|c_{j}|\leq|c_{d}|
, for all
\displaystyle 0\leq j<d.

Clearly, the only possible coincidence between two positive exponents in

and
\displaystyle b(c-a)+c

\displaystyle bc=b(c-a)+c,

which is equivalent to
\displaystyle c=ab

\displaystyle c_{d}=2

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle c(b-a)

\displaystyle b(c-a)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle 1
or
\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle H(y)=c_{d}y^{d}+R(y),

where

\displaystyle R(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}c_{j}y^{j}.

On the circle
\displaystyle|y|=2

\displaystyle|R(y)|<|c_{d}|2^{d}=|c_{d}y^{d}|.

\displaystyle H(y)

\displaystyle c_{d}y^{d}

\displaystyle|y|<2

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle|y|<2

\displaystyle|y|=2

Combining the two facts, every zero
\displaystyle y

\displaystyle H(y)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{2}<|y|<2.

\displaystyle p<q<r
. Then prove that
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{pqr}),x)
is a unimodal and log-concave polynomial.

5
Conclusion

This article examines the independence polynomial
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n},x)
of the zero divisor graph
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
of commutative rings
\displaystyle\mathbb{Z}_{n}
for
\displaystyle n\in\{p,p^{2},p^{3},pq,p^{2}q,pqr\}
, where
\displaystyle p<q<r
are prime integers. The unimodal and log-concave properties of the previously stated graphs are analyzed. The positions of the zeros
\displaystyle I(\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n}),x)
in the plane are delineated, supplemented by pictures obtained from numerical computations. The independence polynomial’s application can be broadened to further values of
\displaystyle n
in the graph
\displaystyle\varGamma(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
, and its components may be explored in subsequent research.
