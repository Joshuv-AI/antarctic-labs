Purpose: This Core Model Proposal (CMP-411) introduces the GCAM Macro-Economic Module
KLEAM (Capital-Labor-Energy-Agriculture-Materials), an extension of the existing GCAM-
Macro (KLEM) framework that explicitly integrates primary agriculture, energy systems, and
materials production within a unified macroeconomic accounting and market-clearing structure.
Building on prior macroeconomic and socioeconomic updates in GCAM (CMP-332, CMP-399,
CMP-410), this proposal advances the representation of labor, capital, savings, and investment
across agriculture, energy, and materials sectors, enabling more consistent feedback between
sectoral production decisions and aggregate economic outcomes (e.g., GDP, capital rental prices,
and wage rates). In addition essential data updates in agriculture (crops, livestock, and forestry)
and socioeconomic modules, key advancements include: (i) explicit representation of labor and
capital inputs in primary agricultural sectors with technology- and region-level value tracing; (ii)
updated capital cost and investment tracking for primary agriculture, energy supply and energy
service sectors; (iii) extensions to the materials nested CES production function to incorporate
agricultural nonfood services and revised energy service value assumptions; (iv) improved
treatment of final consumption (including agricultural food services), trade balance, and national
accounting consistency; and (v) new mechanisms for labor mobility, savings–investment market
clearing, capital price feedbacks, and aggregate GDP responses. These advances establish a more
internally consistent macroeconomic structure for GCAM and lay the foundation for deeper
integration of human-Earth systems.

1. Introduction

Global economic multisector dynamic models increasingly rely on internally consistent macroeconomic
representations to capture feedbacks between economic growth, sectoral production, and resource supply
(Bauer et al., 2008; Fernández-Villaverde et al., 2025; Harmsen et al., 2021). Capturing interactions
between macroeconomic responses and sectoral drivers (e.g., biophysical, technological, or policy shocks
is essential for evaluating economy-wide impacts of energy transitions, agricultural change, and land-use
change, as sector-specific dynamics can propagate through factor markets, prices, and investment
decisions (Edmonds et al., 2025; Lima et al., 2021; McFarland et al., 2018).

Historically, GCAM relied on exogenous trajectories of GDP to drive final demand and sectoral activity,
which provided a transparent linkage between socioeconomic pathways and sectoral activity but limited
the model’s ability to represent feedbacks between sectoral dynamics and aggregate economic outcomes.
As the representation of energy systems, agriculture, and land use in GCAM became more detailed (and
increasingly policy-relevant in many applications), the absence of endogenous macroeconomic
adjustment increasingly constrained the model’s analytical scope.

To address this limitation, the GCAM-Macro KLEM framework was introduced as an optional model
feature (CMP-332) (Patel et al., 2023), establishing a parsimonious yet flexible macroeconomic structure
that links GCAM energy systems to an aggregated materials sector representing the rest of the economy
through materials production and capital investment constraints. This framework enabled endogenous
GDP responses through energy–materials interactions and factor market clearing for labor and capital.

At the same time, agriculture plays a central role in economic development, labor market evolution, and
land and water use, while interacting closely with energy and food systems (Edmonds et al., 2017; Hertel,
2011). Structural transformation associated with agricultural productivity growth has long been
recognized as a key driver of labor reallocation and income growth, particularly in developing economies,
while agricultural production and consumption remain closely linked to energy use and materials demand
across the broader economy (Fuglie, 2018; Sheng et al., 2025).

The gap between primary agriculture and the macroeconomic framework is therefore particularly notable
given that GCAM already includes a sophisticated and data-rich representation of primary agriculture,
including land-use allocation, crop and livestock production, forestry, and associated biophysical
processes (Zhao et al., 2024a; Zhao and Wise, 2023). Bridging this detailed agricultural system with the
macroeconomic framework is therefore important to ensure internal consistency between sectoral
production decisions, factor markets, final demand, and aggregate economic outcomes.

Building on the KLEM foundation (CMP-332) (Patel et al., 2023) and recent socioeconomic and macro
data updates (CMP-399 and CMP-410) (Zhao et al., 2025, 2024b), the KLEAM extension further expands
the macroeconomic representation by explicitly integrating primary agriculture into the materials
production, final consumption, investment, and factor market structure. In doing so, KLEAM strengthens
the endogenous determination of capital rental prices (gross capital rates of return) and wage rates,
allowing changes in economy-wide factors, such as savings, labor force supply, and productivity growth,
to feed back consistently into agriculture and energy system decision-making.

 3

This Core Model Proposal (CMP-411) documents the conceptual design, data foundations, and technical
implementation of the KLEAM framework, along with initial diagnostic results and model validation
runs. In addition, the CMP documents several updates to GCAM agricultural sector parameterization and
assumptions that were required for compatibility and generating useful scenarios with the KLEAM
framework.

2. Conceptual and Technical Advancements in GCAM-Macro KLEAM

The GCAM-Macro KLEAM framework introduces several structural extensions to the GCAM modeling
system, expanding the macroeconomic representation and strengthening integration between
macroeconomic dynamics and sectoral modules. Key advances include the explicit representation of labor
and capital in primary agriculture, the introduction of a unified regional capital market with endogenous
capital rental price feedback, revised materials production structure, and updated representations of
productivity and biomass supply calibration. An overview of the accounting framework is presented in
Fig 1.

Because several components of the KLEAM framework involve substantial structural modifications and
extensive data development, the full methodological descriptions are documented in the accompanying
GCAM-Macro KLEAM Research Memoranda, which are included as appendices to this CMP. These
memorandums provide detailed descriptions of the model architecture, data construction, and
implementation procedures that underpin the changes summarized in this proposal. Accordingly, the main
CMP document focuses on the overarching conceptual advances and their implications for model
behavior, while the technical memorandums provide more comprehensive documentation of individual
model components. In many cases, figures presented in the main CMP are extracted from the appendices,
where they are accompanied by additional technical explanations and supporting details. Each
memorandum is designed to function as a standalone reference document; however, all appendices are
attached to and released together with this CMP. The following memorandums/appendices are included:

• Appendix A: Architecture of the GCAM-Macro KLEAM Framework, Accounting Structure,
 Materials Production Function, and Agricultural and Energy Service Inputs
 o See Fig. 1 and additional details in Appendix A.
• Appendix B: Value-Added (Land, Labor, Capital) Representation in Primary Agriculture, Labor
 Supply, and Labor Market Clearing
 o In the KLEAM implementation, we updated the source datasets to more recent versions,
 improved several underlying data assumptions (e.g., land rental profit, feed prices, forest
 production costs) and associated data processing procedures, and conducted additional cross-
 verification using an agricultural value-added dataset from FAO.
 o Labor supply is now represented at the regional level using a logit-based sharing function that
 allocates total regional employment (derived from working-age population estimates and
 corresponding labor participation and employment assumptions) between the agriculture and
 materials sectors. A common wage rate is assumed across primary agricultural sectors,
 reflecting the assumption that labor is perfectly mobile within agriculture. Thus, the Materials
 and Agriculture sectors are connected through regional labor supply while their sectoral labor
 markets clear separately.

• Appendix C: Capital Costs, Capital Accumulation, Investment Allocation, Savings–Investment
 Closure, and Capital Rental Price Feedback
 o Introduces a unified regional capital market with an endogenous capital rental price that links
 macroeconomic conditions with investment costs in the Materials, Energy, and Agriculture
 sectors.
 o Documents the treatment of sectoral investment demand, capital accumulation, and savings–
 investment market clearing within GCAM-Macro’s recursive dynamic framework, including
 capital price feedbacks through fixed charge rate (FCR) calculations.
• Appendix D: Agricultural Factor Productivity Growth Representation
 o Generalizes agricultural productivity representation by explicitly modeling factor-augmenting
 productivity growth for land, labor, capital, water, and fertilizer inputs within the KLEAM
 production structure.
 o Develops agricultural labor productivity trajectories anchored to land productivity growth
 while maintaining consistency with structural transformation dynamics and economy-wide
 productivity trends.
• Appendix E: Purpose-grown Energy Crop Introduction: Ghost Share Recalibration and Assumption
 Updates
 o Delays the introduction year for purpose-grown energy crops from 2025 to 2030 and
 recalibrates biomass share-weight parameters to reflect updated agricultural and forest land
 rental profit assumptions.
 o Introduces regional differentiation in biomass share-weight assumptions.
 o Change is required because the "ghost-share" is based on the profit of biomass, which in early
 years at least will be set by its price in the energy sector - so the profit of biomass will not
 automatically change proportionally with other crops when they are re-baselined. For
 example, a decrease in the computed profit rates of food crops will result in a higher share of
 biomass, all else equal. Therefore, the change in profits from KLEAM lowered food crops,
 resulting in a need for reduced ghost shares for biomass.

3. Overview of key changes in GCAM and gcamdata

The implementation of the KLEAM framework required coordinated updates across the GCAM core model
and the associated gcamdata preprocessing pipeline to ensure internal consistency between macroeconomic
accounting, sectoral production structures, and socioeconomic inputs. Table 1 provides a structured
summary of the major code and data changes introduced in the present CMP, focusing on updates that
directly support the KLEAM architecture. Relevant minor bug fixes and housekeeping changes are also
included for completeness.

Key updates include:

• Updated macroeconomic data inputs: Transition from Penn World Table (PWT) v10 to v11
 (Feenstra et al., 2015), extending historical coverage from 2019 to 2023.
• Expanded agricultural and labor data sources: Integration of new USDA and ILO datasets, with
 harmonized preprocessing of agricultural labor and capital inputs across FAO, USDA, ILO, and
 GTAP-based sources.
• Improved agricultural labor and capital downscaling and cost tracing: Downscaling of labor and
 capital inputs to agricultural technologies and enhanced tracing of labor costs, wages, and capital
 returns (e.g., zaglu_L2082.ag_an_for_laborcapital.R).
• Introduced parameters to differentiate labor- and capital-intensive technologies: Leverage the
 existing technology structure to assign capital-intensive assumptions to high-input technologies and
 labor-intensive assumptions to low-input technologies using a cost-consistent technology
 downscaling approach.
• Restructured agricultural productivity processing: Consolidation of water, fertilizer, labor, capital,
 and land productivity inputs into a coherent and extensible framework.
• Enhanced capital and investment transparency with factor price feedbacks: Improved tracking
 of sectoral capital stocks and investment flows, enabling endogenous capital rental prices (gross rates
 of return) and consistent propagation of capital cost signals across agriculture, energy, and materials
 sectors.
• Refined use of GTAP-derived data: Removal of legacy GTAP-based land value processing and
 using land cost shares to improve agricultural land valuation and improve transparency.
• Updated assumption of biomass introduction: Purpose-grown energy crop introduction is pushed
 to 2030 (from 2025) with recalibrated (regionally) differentiated parameters.
• National account consistency improvements: Updates to national accounting variables to better
 align production, final consumption, trade balance, and factor income across sectors.
• Agricultural nonfood demand parameter: Updated price and income elasticity for nonfood
 agriculture demand of crops and livestock, and increase the income elasticity for forestry products.
• Harmonization of near-term drivers and parameters: Adoption of a unified scenario alignment
 year (MODEL_SCENARIO_ALIGN_YEAR = 2025) for consistent calibration across scenarios.
• Greater regional parameter flexibility: Expanded support for region-specific labor allocation
 parameters and materials-sector CES coefficients.
• Targeted bug fixes and data consistency improvements: Fixes to base-year forest yield processing
 and improved handling of woodpulp demand.

5. Summary and future work

This CMP introduces the GCAM-Macro KLEAM framework, extending the existing GCAM-
Macro KLEM structure by explicitly integrating primary agriculture into the macroeconomic
accounting, production, investment, and factor-market system. Building on the KLEM
foundation established in CMP-332 and the socioeconomic and macroeconomic data updates in
CMP-399 and CMP-410, this CMP advances GCAM’s ability to represent two-way feedbacks
among sectoral production decisions, factor markets, capital accumulation, and aggregate
economic outcomes. It also builds on recent AgLU, forestry, and bioenergy updates ii, by 1F

improving agricultural value tracing, land-rental profit assumptions, biomass introduction
assumptions, and macro-consistent accounting of agricultural production and final demand.

The main contribution of KLEAM is to strengthen two-way feedbacks between GCAM’s
detailed sectoral systems and the broader macroeconomy. Labor and capital are now represented
explicitly in primary agricultural sectors, with value-added traced by region, sector, and
technology. Agricultural labor is linked to regional employment and allocated between
agriculture and materials, while agricultural capital is incorporated into the broader savings–
investment closure. At the same time, the Materials production function is extended to include
agricultural nonfood services, agricultural food services are represented in final demand, and
investment demand from Agriculture, Energy, and Materials clears against regional savings
through an endogenous capital rental price.

Initial validation results indicate that the updated framework preserves broad GCAM behavior
while adding new macro-agricultural responses. The model now tracks agricultural labor, capital,
investment, food and nonfood services, and expanded national-account variables in a more
internally consistent way. The validation also highlights important areas for continued checking,
including agricultural production and price responses, land-use change emissions, water
withdrawals, capital rental prices, investment allocation, and GDP feedbacks under open-GDP
scenarios. The validation comparison presented in this CMP is based on GCAMv8.6 master
compared with the KLEAM addition. The KLEAM development will be rebased to the
recent GCAM core before final integration, which includes additional developments after
GCAMv8.6. These newer core updates may have moderate effects on the validation results
and are expected to further improve model behavior in several areas, for example through
updated technoeconomic assumptions and other recent sectoral data improvements.

Future work should focus on several areas. First, demand elasticity assumptions should be
reviewed more broadly, especially for agricultural nonfood demand and other sectors affected by
GDP feedbacks. Second, energy-service value calibration and capital allocation assumptions
should be further tested, since the current treatment relies on a calibration assumption rather than
directly observed national-account data. Third, sectoral investment calibration and historical
value/price data should be improved as better data become available. Fourth, the agricultural
productivity framework should be used for additional sensitivity analysis, including alternative
assumptions for labor, land, water, fertilizer, capital, livestock, and forestry productivity. Fifth,
remaining agricultural “other costs” should be further disaggregated, especially farm energy and
non-nitrogen fertilizer inputs. Future development should also add fisheries, aquaculture, food
processing, and potentially energy-sector labor once the corresponding sectoral supply and
value-added representations are available.

Bioenergy assumptions should also continue to be evaluated. This CMP delays the introduction
of purpose-grown energy crops to 2030 and introduces more regionally differentiated ghost-
share assumptions, reflecting the lack of current large-scale deployment and updated land-rental
profit assumptions. Future work should further improve the implementation and examine the
parameter sensitivity.

Finally, additional work is needed to integrate KLEAM consistently with GCAM-USA. The
global KLEAM framework closes labor and capital markets through regional production
functions and endogenous factor-price feedbacks. In GCAM-USA, however, the U.S. energy
system (in global GCAM) is replaced by detailed state-level energy systems, while the United
States and individual states do not currently have corresponding KLEAM production functions
(global GCAM and GCAM-USA share the same AgLU representation). Therefore, they do not
generate dynamic demands for Materials, labor, and capital that can be used to close U.S. labor
and capital markets. For the current implementation, U.S. agricultural labor and capital are
treated as unlimited resources with fixed prices, allowing the U.S. agricultural system to operate
without imposing an inconsistent macro closure. The rest of the world continues to use the full
global KLEAM structure with endogenous labor, capital, savings–investment, and GDP
feedbacks. Future work is underway to develop a more complete GCAM-USA macro treatment,
potentially including national or state-level production functions and a consistent mechanism
linking detailed state energy systems with labor and capital market dynamics.

1. Motivation: balanced accounts and micro–macro consistency
Macroeconomic consistency in multisector economic models requires that production, income,
and expenditure accounts are internally balanced. In standard national accounting frameworks,
GDP can be equivalently measured from three perspectives: (i) the sum of factor compensation
(income approach) 1, (ii) the value of ﬁnal demand (expenditure approach), and (iii) the value of
production (output approach). Ensuring consistency across these identities is essential for
balanced data construction and for maintaining coherent economy-wide responses to sectoral
changes.
At the same time, detailed sectoral models—such as those representing energy systems and
agriculture in GCAM—operate in physical units and incorporate technology-speciﬁc processes,
constraints, and behavioral responses. Linking these sectoral dynamics to macroeconomic
outcomes therefore requires a framework that reconciles physical production systems with value-
based economic accounting.
The GCAM-Macro framework, particularly with the KLEAM extension, addresses this challenge by
embedding sectoral modules within a recursive-dynamic macroeconomic structure in which factor
markets and national accounts are consistently balanced. In this structure, macroeconomic
variables such as GDP, investment, and factor prices are determined endogenously and feed back
to sectoral demand and production decisions.
All macroeconomic consistency is enforced in value terms, while physical quantities are
determined through sectoral production systems. This separation allows GCAM to preserve
detailed sectoral representations while ensuring internally consistent economy-wide accounting.
Building on this foundation, the KLEAM extension expands the macroeconomic framework to more
fully integrate agriculture alongside energy and materials production, strengthening the
consistency and scope of micro–macro linkages.

2. Evolution of the accounting structure: from KLM to KLEM and KLEAM
The development of the GCAM-Macro framework can be understood as a sequence of extensions
to the underlying accounting structure, progressively strengthening the integration between
sectoral systems and the macroeconomy. Here, we also describe the KLM framework, which is the
origin of the KLEM & KLEAM extensions. An overview of the accounting framework of KLEAM is
presented in Fig. A1, and the corresponding KLEM framework is illustrated in Fig. A2 2.

2

2.1. KLM: single-sector macroeconomic structure
In a single-sector KLM (Capital-Labor-Materials) formulation, the economy is represented by a
single aggregated production sector (Materials), which combines capital and labor to produce
goods for ﬁnal consumption, investment, and trade. There are no explicit intermediate inputs from
other sectors. Within this structure, standard national accounting identities hold:

 • Income approach: GDP equals the sum of compensation to labor and capital.
 • Expenditure approach: GDP equals the sum of household and government consumption,
 investment, and net exports.
 • Production approach: GDP equals the gross output of the Materials sector.
Because Materials represents the entire economy, its gross output simultaneously corresponds to
value added and ﬁnal demand. This structure provides a simple and internally consistent
macroeconomic representation but does not explicitly capture interactions with sectoral systems
such as energy or agriculture.
2.2. KLEM: introducing energy system linkages
The KLEM framework (Patel et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2025) extends the KLM structure by
disaggregating the energy system and establishing linkages between sectoral outcomes (GCAM)
and macroeconomic accounts. Three key accounting linkages (blue cells in Figs A1 & A2) connect
the energy system to the macroeconomy:
1) Energy investment: capital investment associated with energy and energy service technologies
 contributes to aggregate investment demand. Consumer durable sectors relevant to energy
 systems (i.e., residential heating, cooling, and other uses, as well as passenger road
 transportation) are also included and tracked.
2) Energy service input: Energy services produced by the GCAM energy system enter the
 Materials production function as an intermediate input. These services are represented as a
 Laspeyres quantity index that aggregates detailed technology-level energy service outputs
 using base-period prices.
3) Energy net exports: Net exports from the energy system are aggregated and incorporated into
 the macroeconomic trade balance.
With these linkages, the Materials sector no longer represents the entire economy but instead
corresponds to the non-energy portion of economic activity 3. Its production is represented by a
nested constant elasticity of substitution (CES) function that combines the value-added aggregate
(capital and labor) and energy services (intermediate). Macroeconomic accounting identities are
maintained through adjustments to the input–output structure. In particular:

 • GDP remains consistent across income and expenditure deﬁnitions.
 • Energy-sector activities are integrated through value ﬂows rather than physical aggregation.
 • Trade balance assumptions are imposed at the regional level, with net exports phased out
 over time to ensure long-run consistency.
A key feature of the KLEM framework is the introduction of endogenous GDP, which feeds back into
GCAM sectoral demand through sector-speciﬁc income elasticities. GDP feedback extends beyond
demand responses, as technological progress in certain domains is also tied to GDP, e.g., through

dynamics and macroeconomic outcomes so that changes in the energy system affect GDP, which
in turn inﬂuences demand for energy and other goods.
2.3. KLEAM: integrating agriculture into the macroeconomic framework
The KLEAM framework further extends the accounting structure by explicitly incorporating primary
agriculture into the KLEM macroeconomic system, alongside energy and materials. This extension
involves several key modiﬁcations.
1) Expanded factor representation: In sectoral modules (GCAM), labor and capital are
 introduced as explicit factors of agricultural production in addition to land. Factor
 compensation from land, labor, and capital is represented at the sectoral and technology levels
 within the agricultural system and is used to partition value added across economy-wide
 sectors during calibration. As a result, value added previously embedded within the aggregated
 Materials sector is partially reallocated to agriculture, improving the consistency between
 sectoral and macroeconomic accounts.
2) Sectoral integration with GCAM agriculture: The agricultural system retains its detailed
 GCAM representation, including land-use allocation, crop and livestock production, and
 interactions with energy systems (e.g., bioenergy and feed). These interactions are resolved
 endogenously within the sectoral modules. The linkage to the macroeconomy is established
 through value-based accounting ﬂows rather than through the direct aggregation of physical
 outputs while preserving traceability to physical quantities.
3) Agricultural service ﬂows: Agricultural production is linked to the macroeconomy through two
 distinct service channels, including agricultural nonfood service and agricultural food service.
 This separation allows the model to maintain consistency between macroeconomic accounting
 and the detailed representation of food systems in GCAM.
 a. Agricultural nonfood service: A Laspeyres quantity index representing agricultural outputs
 used in nonfood (also nonfeed & nonbioenergy) applications (e.g., primarily ﬁber crops and
 forestry products, but broadly from all agricultural sectors) 4. This index is aggregated across
 GCAM sectors using base-period prices and enters the Materials CES production function
 as an intermediate input, analogous to energy services.
 b. Agricultural food service: The value of food consumption derived from GCAM’s food
 demand system. This component enters ﬁnal demand directly (household and government
 consumption) and does not pass through the Materials production function, preserving the
 structure and behavioral detail of the food demand module5 in GCAM.
4) Updated macroeconomic identity: With the inclusion of agriculture, GDP is deﬁned as the
 sum of materials gross output, agricultural food services, and GCAM sectoral net export
 (energy & agriculture). This formulation reﬂects the separation of food-related value ﬂows from
 the Materials sector and ensures consistency between production, income, and expenditure
 accounts.

Together, these extensions transform the GCAM-Macro framework from a system linking energy and

2.4. Materials production function and agricultural services input in KLEAM
The Materials sector in GCAM-Macro KLEAM is represented using a nested CES production function
that combines value-added inputs (capital and labor) with intermediate inputs, including energy
services and agricultural nonfood services (Fig. A3). This structure extends the original KLEM
formulation by explicitly incorporating agricultural services as an intermediate input, thereby
strengthening the linkage between the agricultural system and the broader economy.

Fig. A3 Structure of the nest CES Materials production function with agricultural services added.

The nested CES structure is parameterized through substitution elasticities speciﬁed in
socioeconomics/gcam_macro_nested_CES_function.csv. In the current implementation, the
elasticity of substitution is set to 0.5 in the top nest, governing substitution between value-added
and intermediate inputs (including energy and agricultural services), and 1.2 in the value-added
nest, governing substitution between capital and labor. These values are applied uniformly across
regions. Note that the CES function is implemented using its dual parameterization (both form
speciﬁed in the input csv above), where the parameters (ρ and γ) are mapped from the elasticity of
substitution (CES Elasticity = 1 / (1 – CES Parameter).
The parameterization is consistent with the empirical literature, e.g., including those used in
computable general equilibrium (CGE) models, which generally ﬁnds lower substitution between
value-added and intermediate inputs than among primary factors. Empirical estimates for the
former typically range from 0.5 to 1.0, while substitution between capital and labor commonly falls
between 0.8 and 1.5 (Gohin and Koutchade, 2024; Ivanic et al., 2023). The selected values therefore
provide a conservative but empirically grounded representation of production behavior.
Agricultural nonfood services are constructed by aggregating outputs from GCAM agricultural
sectors using base-year prices as weights. As a Laspeyres quantity index is used, macro-level
demand drivers and price feedbacks are not explicitly represented in this aggregation. To ensure
consistency between sectoral demand and the macroeconomic representation, the income
elasticities governing agricultural nonfood demand in GCAM are updated. In the standard
conﬁguration, these demands (e.g., ﬁber crops, meat byproducts, and forestry products) often
exhibit low or zero income elasticity. In KLEAM, however, these nonfood uses are aggregated into
agricultural nonfood services that enter the Materials production function as intermediate inputs
representing demand from the rest of the economy.

3. Energy service value calibration: uncertainty and assumptions
3.1.  Conceptual role of energy services and limitations
In the GCAM-Macro framework, energy services represent the economic value of energy system
outputs that enter the broader economy as intermediate inputs. Within the Materials production
function, energy services are treated as a distinct input alongside value-added components
(capital and labor) and, in KLEAM, agricultural nonfood services.
Energy services are constructed as a price-weighted quantity index, aggregating detailed energy
system outputs across sectors (e.g., industry, buildings, and transport) using base-period prices.
This formulation enables the integration of detailed physical energy-system dynamics into a value-
based macroeconomic framework, in which “energy service value” is calibrated in the base year.
A key challenge in implementing this structure is the absence of directly observable data on the
value of energy services in standard national accounts. Several factors contribute to this limitation:
1) The deﬁnition of energy services in GCAM does not correspond directly to sectoral
 classiﬁcations in national accounts (e.g., industries or ﬁnal demand categories).
2) Energy system outputs in GCAM are represented at the technology level and are not explicitly
 mapped to value-added components such as capital and labor in national statistics.
3) Available datasets (e.g., GTAP) provide sectoral value-added and capital compensation shares,
 but do not offer a clear mapping to the energy service constructs used in GCAM.
As a result, the value of energy services cannot be directly observed or constructed from primary
data sources.
To maintain consistency in the input–output accounting structure, the value of energy services is
derived as a residual component within the Materials production account. Speciﬁcally, in the base-
year calibration of KLEAM Materials production:

• Energy service value = Materials gross output − labor compensation − capital
 compensation − agricultural nonfood service value
This approach ensures that the Materials production function is fully balanced, with all inputs
summing to total output. At the same time, it implies that the energy service value depends
critically on how other components—particularly capital and labor compensation—are partitioned
across sectors.
A major source of uncertainty arises from the allocation of capital and capital compensation
between the Materials sector and energy-related activities. In previous implementations (e.g., CMP-
410), sectoral capital shares derived from GTAP were used to partition value-added across sectors 6.
However, this approach did not fully account for capital associated with energy service provision,
primarily due to the lack of a clear mapping between GCAM energy service outputs and standard
sectoral classiﬁcations. As a result, a portion of capital services associated with energy systems
was effectively attributed to the Materials sector. This led to an overestimation of Materials capital
compensation and a corresponding underestimation of the residual energy service value.

3.2. Calibration adjustment in KLEAM
To address this issue, KLEAM introduces an explicit calibration parameter that allocates a portion of
non-agricultural, non-energy capital to energy service provision. In the current implementation, a
ﬁxed share (40%) of relevant capital is allocated to energy service activities across regions in
the ﬁnal base-year calibration7. The assumed 40% share is informed by values used in the original
KLEM implementation and supported by initial sensitivity tests; it may be further reﬁned at the
regional level as additional data and evidence become available.
This adjustment increases the implied capital intensity of energy services and correspondingly
raises the estimated value of energy service inputs in the Materials production function. This
parameter should be interpreted as a calibration assumption that reﬂects structural uncertainty in
the allocation of capital services across sectors, rather than as a directly observed empirical
quantity. The calibration of energy service value has important implications for model behavior (per
our initial sensitivity tests):

• A higher energy service value increases the contribution of energy to Materials production,
 strengthening the linkage between the energy system and the broader economy.
• Changes in energy system costs or policies (e.g., mitigation scenarios) have a larger impact on
 GDP through their effect on energy service inputs.
• The sensitivity of macroeconomic outcomes to energy system dynamics is therefore directly
 inﬂuenced by this calibration choice.
More broadly, this highlights the importance of consistent value partitioning in linking sectoral
models to macroeconomic accounting, particularly in the absence of directly observed data.

1. Background and motivation
In GCAM, representative proﬁt-maximizing producers in crop, livestock, and forestry sectors make
production and management decisions by choosing input use under constant-returns-to-scale
technologies, given a vector of input and output prices. Historically, agricultural production in
GCAM has mainly focused on land competition. Land allocation is governed by a nested logit
structure that relates land shares to relative rental proﬁts, enabling endogenous price-induced
yield responses through technology transitions and competition between managed and natural
land.
In addition to land, irrigation water and nitrogen fertilizer are explicitly represented as inputs in
agricultural production (crops). However, labor and capital—arguably more economically
important in production decisions and long-run agricultural transformation—have not been
explicitly represented. Instead, these inputs were embedded within “other costs” (and in some
cases partially reﬂected in land costs), limiting the model’s ability to capture interactions between
agricultural production, factor markets, and macroeconomic dynamics.
Recent work, including Sheng et al. (2025), highlights the importance of labor market evolution in
shaping agricultural systems. Two key forces drive this evolution: (i) labor push, arising from
productivity growth that releases labor from agriculture, and (ii) labor pull, driven by increasing
labor demand in non-agricultural sectors and associated wage differentials (Alvarez-Cuadrado and
Poschke, 2011). These dynamics are highly heterogeneous across regions, reﬂecting differences in
demographic trends, structural transformation, and economic development. In parallel, capital
deepening—through mechanization and increased use of capital-intensive technologies—has
played a central role in agricultural development, reinforcing labor–capital substitution.
At the same time, agriculture is intrinsically linked to the broader economy through value-added
accounting. Without explicit representation of labor and capital, these linkages remain incomplete.
To address this gap, we introduce explicit labor and capital inputs into agricultural production at the
technology level in GCAM and integrate them within a macro-consistent framework.
In KLEAM, labor and capital are explicitly represented in agricultural technologies, and markets for
labor and investment–savings are closed (Fig. B1). This enables agriculture to transition from a
cost-based representation to a factor-driven production system, establishing the foundation for
linking agricultural production to endogenous wages and capital rental prices and enabling
consistent interactions between agriculture and the rest of the economy.

2. Labor and capital input demand in agricultural production

Fig. B1 illustrates the updated representation of primary agricultural production in KLEAM.
Agricultural output by technology is modeled using a Leontief structure (multiple technologies on
the production frontier) that combines land, water, fertilizer, and other intermediate inputs. A key
advancement is the decomposition of “other inputs” into explicit labor and capital components.
This allows labor and capital to be represented as physical inputs while maintaining consistency
with value-based accounting in GCAM-Macro.

Before partitioning labor and capital, input cost shares and prices are updated where necessary to
ensure consistency between value ﬂows and physical production. In particular, land rental cost
shares and prices are revised in selected regions, and cost and price data for feed crops and forest
products were substantially improved. These updates are important for ensuring that the residual
“other cost” category can be consistently decomposed into labor and capital components.

Labor and capital costs are ﬁrst identiﬁed at the sectoral level and then allocated to production
technologies. This ensures that factor inputs are consistent with regional value-added accounting
while preserving the structure of technology-level production in GCAM. To capture heterogeneity in
production systems, we introduce parameters that differentiate labor and capital intensities across

3. Data construction and calibration

Introducing explicit labor and capital inputs requires maintaining consistency across physical
quantities, value-added accounting, and prices. Agricultural labor and capital data are derived from
multiple sources—including ILO, USDA, FAOSTAT, and GTAP—which differ in deﬁnitions, coverage,
and aggregation. To address this, we construct a harmonized dataset of agricultural labor and
capital inputs at the regional level and downscale these inputs to sectoral and technology levels.
This process ensures that:

 • physical input quantities are consistent with observed production,
 • value ﬂows align with regional value-added accounts, and
 • implied factor prices are economically consistent.

Cost shares in agricultural production are updated using GTAP and FAO data, improving
consistency with observed value-added structures. These updates generally reduce land cost
shares and reﬁne the distribution of costs across labor, capital, and intermediate inputs, with
important implications for rental proﬁtability and technology competitiveness.

At the global level (Fig. B2), agricultural employment increased through the late 20th century and
peaked in the early 2000s, followed by a gradual decline associated with structural transformation
and productivity growth. A recent increase in agricultural employment around 2020–2021 is
observed, likely reﬂecting COVID-related disruptions, with India contributing signiﬁcantly to this
surge. In contrast to labor, global agricultural capital has exhibited sustained growth over time (Fig.
B2), reﬂecting capital deepening through mechanization and increased use of capital-intensive
technologies.

4. Labor supply and market clearing

The representation of labor in agriculture is fully integrated with the macroeconomic framework in
KLEAM. Total labor supply is derived from SSP-based projections of working-age population and
employment. Labor is allocated across agriculture and materials sectors using a logit-sharing
function that captures sectoral labor mobility. Regional differences in structural transformation are
reﬂected through the logit exponent. Regions undergoing rapid transformation are characterized by
higher labor mobility (logit exponent = 2), while regions with largely completed transformation
exhibit more limited reallocation (logit exponent = 0.5). Transitional regions fall in between (logit
exponent = 1). The regional parameterization is speciﬁed in gcamdata
(“socioeconomics/A_labor_supply_sector.csv”).

Within agriculture, labor is assumed to be perfectly mobile across sectors, resulting in a single
regional agricultural wage rate. The regional labor market clears by equating total labor supply with

inﬂuence technology-level production decisions. This establishes a two-way linkage between

CMP-411 Appendix C:
Capital Costs, Capital Accumulation, Investment Allocation, Savings–Investment Closure,
and Capital Rental Price Feedback
1. Motivation and Scope
The representation of capital accumulation and investment allocation is a core structural
component of GCAM-Macro (Hogan and Manne, 2015). In the earlier KLEM implementation (CMP-
332) (Patel et al., 2023), capital dynamics were only partially integrated across sectors. While the
Materials sector maintained an explicit capital stock, energy-sector capital costs were embedded
in exogenous levelized cost assumptions and did not respond to economy-wide capital conditions.
In particular, there was no endogenous capital rental price linking macroeconomic conditions to
sectoral investment costs. As a result, capital-intensive technologies in the energy system were not
directly affected by changes in the regional rate of return on capital, and cross-sector competition
for capital operated only indirectly.
Beyond integrating the agricultural sectors, the KLEAM extension (CMP-411) introduces a uniﬁed
regional capital market and an endogenous capital rental price. Investment demand from the
macro module (Materials) and sector modules (Energy 1 and Agriculture) is aggregated and cleared
against savings-determined capital supply. The resulting regional capital rental price directly
affects Materials-sector production decisions and, through scaling mechanisms in ﬁxed charge
rate (FCR) calculations, inﬂuences capital-intensive technology costs in the Energy and Agriculture
sectors. This structure enables capital price feedback across sectors and strengthens macro–
sectoral consistency while preserving GCAM’s recursive dynamic framework.
GCAM-Macro KLEAM follows the recursive-dynamic tradition of macro–energy modeling while
incorporating an internally integrated capital market. Like EPPA and IMACLIM (CGE models), the
model solves sequential equilibria with savings-driven capital supply and period-by-period market
clearing (Chen et al., 2022; Luderer et al., 2012). At the same time, by introducing an endogenous
regional capital rental price that feeds back into sectoral technology costs, KLEAM strengthens
macro–sector consistency beyond earlier GCAM implementations. In contrast to intertemporal
frameworks such as MESSAGE–MACRO, REMIND, or DICE (Luderer et al., 2012; Messner and
Schrattenholzer, 2000) 2, KLEAM does not optimize investment over time but instead emphasizes
transparent capital allocation and cross-sector competition within a recursive dynamic structure.
These advancements move GCAM-Macro closer to the recursive-dynamic IAM/CGE tradition while
maintaining GCAM’s detailed bottom-up sectoral structure. In addition to documenting this
architectural advancement, this memo/appendix provides a detailed description of how sectoral
investment demand is derived, how capital stocks evolve over multi-year model periods, and how
the savings–investment balance is enforced within the recursive dynamic framework.
2. Overview of the regional capital architecture in KLEAM

GCAM-Macro KLEAM operates in a recursive dynamic framework in which each model period
solves a static equilibrium given inherited capital stocks, exogenous depreciation rates, and a
speciﬁed savings rule. Capital accumulation 3 evolves sequentially across periods; there is no
intertemporal optimization of savings or investment. Instead, savings behavior determines the
supply of new capital in each period, and the regional capital rental price adjusts to clear the
capital market.
Each GCAM region has a single capital market. In each period, total investment demand—derived
from the Materials sector and the Energy and Agriculture modules—is aggregated and balanced
against capital supply determined by regional savings (net of capital transfers during the transition
period). The regional capital rental price equates aggregate investment demand with available
savings.
The regional capital rental price represents the gross return on capital services in the Materials
sector. It is not a ﬁnancial market interest rate nor a social discount rate, but the endogenous price
of capital services that clears the regional capital market. This rental price directly determines
capital demand in the Materials production function and indirectly affects investment decisions in
the Energy and Agriculture sectors through its role in scaling the interest rate used in ﬁxed charge
rate (FCR) calculations.
Capital stocks are explicitly partitioned across sectors in both historical calibration and
projections. The Materials capital stock includes only non-energy and non-agricultural capital.
Agricultural capital is tracked separately by technology and appears explicitly in model outputs and
accounting identities. In the Energy sectors, while capital stock is not explicitly represented at the
macro level, capital cost is tracked at the technology level for investment demand derivation.
This uniﬁed regional capital architecture enables cross-sector competition for capital while
preserving sector-speciﬁc representations of technology and production structure.

3.3 Agriculture: hybrid capital representation
The Agriculture sector combines elements of the Materials and Energy representations. Agricultural
production functions include capital as an explicit input, and agricultural capital stock is tracked
and reported. However, new investment demand is derived using a ﬁxed charge rate–based tracking
approach similar to that used in the Energy sector.
For each agricultural technology, capital requirements are speciﬁed along with depreciation
assumptions. Capital cost is annualized using an FCR formulation in which the interest rate is
scaled proportionally to the regional capital rental price. This structure ensures that changes in the
regional capital rental price inﬂuence agricultural investment costs.
Investment demand in Agriculture is derived from technology-speciﬁc capital requirements and
output levels, and is aggregated to contribute to total regional capital demand. Agricultural capital
stock is then updated using a law-of-motion structure consistent with multi-year depreciation
compounding.
This hybrid representation allows agricultural capital to respond to economy-wide capital price
signals while maintaining explicit stock accounting and sectoral detail.

CMP-411 Appendix D:
Agricultural Factor Productivity Growth Representation
1. Motivation and scope
Productivity growth is a central driver of long-run projections in global economic and multi-sector
dynamic models. Productivity trajectories shape projections of agricultural production, land use,
labor allocation, capital accumulation, and structural transformation, with cascading implications
for integrated human–Earth system dynamics (Charlton et al., 2021; Fuglie, 2018). At the same
time, long-run projections are highly sensitive to underlying productivity assumptions, while the
derivation and implementation of those assumptions are often complex and insufficiently
documented. Differences in productivity speciﬁcations have also been identiﬁed as a major source
of divergence across global economic models (Foure et al., 2020; Robinson et al., 2014; Schmitz et
al., 2014).
In GCAM, agricultural sectors are integrated with the broader economy through land and water
competition, food, ﬁber, timber, and bioenergy supply, and price feedbacks. GCAM has historically
incorporated productivity growth in agriculture asymmetrically across factors and sectors, i.e.,
primarily land-augmenting and concentrated in crop production. While this structure provided a
tractable baseline, it limited the model’s ability to transparently represent factor-biased technical
change and to systematically explore alternative productivity scenarios. More importantly, how
productivity growth is represented in agriculture is critical for macroeconomic consistency and
structural transformation dynamics.
GCAM-Macro (KLEAM) generalizes the agricultural production structure by explicitly representing
labor and capital, in addition to land, water, nitrogen fertilizer, and feed, as distinct factor inputs.
With these inputs modeled explicitly, productivity is speciﬁed as factor-augmenting parameters
embedded directly in the production function. This structure enables transparent speciﬁcation of
factor-biased technical change through factor-augmenting parameters and allows Hicks-neutral
total factor productivity (TFP) to emerge as a special case when productivity growth is uniform
across inputs 1.
This research memorandum documents the baseline agricultural productivity representation in
GCAM, describes the generalized factor-speciﬁc framework introduced in KLEAM, and details the
derivation of agricultural labor productivity growth relative to land productivity growth. The
objective is to provide a transparent and internally consistent foundation for future scenario design
and sensitivity analysis.
2. Baseline agricultural productivity representation in GCAM

In summary, baseline GCAM before KLEAM can be characterized as:
• Land-driven in exogenous productivity growth (primarily for crops),
• Technology-driven but not exogenously augmented for water and fertilizer,
• Without explicit labor or capital productivity growth in agriculture,
• Without systematic factor productivity growth in livestock and forestry sectors.
This asymmetric structure, strongly land-focused for crops and largely neutral for other factors and
sectors, provides the motivation for the generalized, factor-speciﬁc productivity framework
introduced in GCAM-Macro (KLEAM) and documented in the following sections.
3. Conceptual framework of factor productivity in agriculture
The introduction of GCAM-Macro (KLEAM) generalizes the agricultural production structure by
explicitly representing land, labor, capital, irrigation water, and nitrogen fertilizer as distinct factor
inputs 3. As a result, the cost share previously attributed to residual “other inputs” is substantially
reduced 4. This extension enables the systematic and transparent speciﬁcation of factor-
augmenting productivity growth and avoids implicitly attributing productivity improvements solely
to land.
Agricultural productivity growth arises from multiple underlying mechanisms. Land productivity
increases are typically associated with improvements in crop genetics, seed technology, agronomic
practices, irrigation management, and improved input applications. Labor productivity growth
reﬂects mechanization, human capital accumulation, learning-by-doing, and structural
transformation. These processes often interact, e.g., improved seed varieties, digital technologies,
and better management practices may simultaneously enhance both land and labor productivity.
Input-speciﬁc productivity growth may also occur for irrigation water and nitrogen fertilizer,

Agricultural development frequently involves capital deepening, whereby increased mechanization

increasingly embodied in capital equipment and interacts with capital deepening to drive labor

importance of distinguishing between capital accumulation effects and productivity frontier shifts.
At the same time, separating embodied technological change, input substitution, and factor-
augmenting productivity shifts is inherently difficult in global models.
Rather than attempting to parameterize each of these channels separately, GCAM-Macro (despite
its currently parsimonious baseline assumptions) adopts a ﬂexible and internally consistent
structure that allows factor-speciﬁc productivity assumptions to be speciﬁed explicitly and
evaluated systematically through sensitivity analysis. Within this structure, agricultural capital

under the updated KLEAM macro closure, in which the saving–investment market clears
endogenously, and the gross rate of return on capital is determined endogenously within the
integrated macroeconomic system. Under this closure, capital deepening emerges endogenously
through investment allocation and accumulation decisions. An additional exogenous capital-
augmenting productivity path would represent an independent frontier shift and could conﬂate
capital accumulation effects with technological change. Maintaining constant capital productivity,
therefore, preserves the distinction between capital accumulation and shifts in the productivity
frontier and avoids potential double-counting of embodied technological change. This approach is
consistent with the global economic equilibrium modeling practice (Chateau et al., 2020).
Similarly, exogenous productivity growth for irrigation water and nitrogen fertilizer is held constant
in the baseline conﬁguration. Improvements in their effective productivity occur only through
endogenous technology transitions. This parsimonious treatment provides a transparent starting
point while preserving ﬂexibility for future exploration of input-speciﬁc productivity shocks.
Given these considerations, the present extension focuses on the development of agricultural labor
productivity trajectories while maintaining the established FAO-based land productivity path as the
structural anchor.
4. Agricultural labor productivity derivation
In most regions undergoing structural transformation, output per worker has historically grown
faster than output per hectare, as labor productivity reﬂects both yield improvements and capital
deepening (Fuglie, 2018; Gollin et al., 2014). Land productivity improvements primarily reﬂect
biological and agronomic advances that raise output per hectare, including crop genetics,
improved seed varieties, and enhanced management practices (Alexandratos and Bruinsma, 2012;
Evenson and Gollin, 2003). In contrast, agricultural labor productivity incorporates not only these
land productivity gains but also mechanization, human capital accumulation, scale economies,
and labor reallocation within and across sectors. As agricultural economies modernize, output per
worker increases not only because yields improve, but also because fewer workers operate larger
and more capital-intensive production systems.

Agricultural labor markets are increasingly integrated with the broader economy (Sheng et al.,
2025). As non-agricultural sectors experience productivity growth (captured in GCAM-Macro
through the macro-calibrated materials TFP used to reproduce SSP per-capita GDP trajectories),
wage convergence pressures emerge across sectors. To maintain labor market equilibrium and
avoid implausible wage divergence, agricultural labor productivity growth must remain broadly
comparable to economy-wide productivity growth over the long run 5.
The adopted scaling approach therefore reﬂects two structural principles:
 (i) agricultural labor productivity growth should exceed land productivity growth due to
 mechanization and capital deepening, and
 (ii) it should remain broadly aligned with macro-calibrated economy-wide productivity
 growth, particularly in transforming economies.
Rather than specifying an independent productivity path for each region or directly linking labor
productivity mechanically to materials TFP growth, agricultural labor productivity growth is deﬁned
as a multiple of the long-run average land productivity growth rate. Regions are grouped according
to their stage of structural transformation in the baseline conﬁguration:
• Low (2x multiplier): Mature agricultural economies, where labor transition is largely
 complete and agricultural productivity growth converges toward economy-wide dynamics.
• Medium (3x multiplier): Transitional economies, where modernization continues and
 agricultural labor productivity growth remains above land productivity growth.
• High (4x multiplier): Structurally transforming regions, where substantial labor reallocation
 potential and mechanization lead to accelerated agricultural labor productivity growth.
The multiplier is applied to the average annual land productivity growth rate over the modeling
horizon, thereby anchoring labor productivity growth to biophysical yield trends while avoiding
ampliﬁcation of short-term volatility or mid-horizon kinks (e.g., around 2050 when FAO projections
end).

5. Summary and key takeaways
The generalized factor-speciﬁc productivity structure introduced in GCAM-Macro (KLEAM)
transforms agricultural productivity representation from a predominantly land-driven mechanism
into a transparent and internally consistent framework. By explicitly distinguishing land, labor,
capital, water, and fertilizer inputs and their productivity growth, the model enables systematic
exploration of biased technical change while preserving macroeconomic consistency under
endogenous saving–investment closure.
Anchoring labor productivity growth to land productivity while maintaining constant capital and
other input factor-augmenting productivity parameters in the baseline provides a parsimonious yet
ﬂexible starting point. The structure allows alternative productivity scenarios to be implemented
transparently and evaluated through sensitivity analysis, thereby improving both interpretability and
robustness of long-run projections.
More broadly, this framework provides a structured input-augmenting technical change
architecture that enables systematic evaluation of how agricultural factor productivity assumptions
inﬂuence land use, water demand, fertilizer application, labor allocation, capital accumulation,
and macroeconomic outcomes. It introduces an explicit and traceable degree of freedom in
productivity speciﬁcation, laying the groundwork for ensemble-based sensitivity analysis and
alternative biophysical shock scenarios.

CMP-411 Appendix E:
Purpose-grown Energy Crop Introduction: Ghost Share Recalibration and Assumption Updates
1. Background and problem
In GCAM, modern biomass supply originates from three sources: municipal solid waste (MSW),
agricultural and forestry residues, and purpose-grown energy crops (Zhao et al., 2024). In the
current base year (2021), however, there is no production of purpose-grown energy crops. This
remains effectively true as of 20261. In GCAM, all modern biomass supply in the calibrated base
year is derived from MSW and agricultural/forestry residues. MSW supply is represented using
simple supply curves linked to socioeconomic variables, while agricultural and forestry residues
are tied to GCAM crop and forestry production2. In aggregate, base-year modern biomass supply
from these sources is calibrated to match IEA bioenergy supply estimates.
Purpose-grown energy crops are introduced in future periods through the GCAM land allocation
system by assigning a ghost-unnormalized-share to new land uses—specifically BiomassGrass in
the crop nest and BiomassTree in the forest nest (Wise et al., 2014). This mechanism introduces a
share weight for technologies with zero base-year land allocation, allowing them to enter the
nested land competition structure.
The assumption that dedicated energy crops will become viable in the future is widely used in
global economic multisector dynamic models, although it has also been critically discussed in the
literature (Ai et al., 2021; Hanssen et al., 2022; Heck et al., 2018). Potential constraints—including
additional labor, capital, irrigation requirements, biodiversity impacts, and other environmental
tradeoffs—may limit large-scale expansion beyond what is implied by conventional land
competition frameworks. Moreover, the anticipated large-scale deployment of dedicated bioenergy
crops has not yet materialized.
Reflecting this uncertainty, previous GCAM updates have delayed the assumed introduction of
energy crops. When the base year was 2015, CMP-351 moved the introduction year from 2020 to
2025. However, this assumption was not revisited in the recent base-year update to 2021.
Assuming linear interpolation of production between model periods, specifying a 2025 introduction
effectively implies that biomass crop production began around 2022, which is inconsistent with
current observations.
This GCAM-Macro KLEAM CMP therefore further delays the introduction year for purpose-grown
energy crops to 2030. At the same time, additional regional differentiation in ghost share values
is implemented to allow a slower introduction in regions that currently lack strong bioenergy policy
support. Note that this regional differentiation feature was already implemented and tested in CMP-
391, although it was retained only as an optional feature (uniform values are still the default
option).

2. Introduction of purpose-grown energy crops using ghost-unnormalized-share
Within the GCAM land allocation system, land uses compete through a nested logit formulation
with land share (𝑠𝑖 ) for land use 𝑖 derived as
𝑠𝑖 = ,

where 𝑆𝑊𝑖 is the share weight, 𝜋𝑖 the land profit rate, and 𝜆 the logit exponent.
For technologies with no base-year land allocation, GCAM introduces a ghost-unnormalized-
share, which directly specifies the initial competitiveness term 𝑈𝑖 used to calibrate a share weight
for the new land use.

These unnormalized shares are calculated for all competing land uses within a nest, and land
shares are then determined by normalizing these competitiveness terms across the alternatives.
Introducing a ghost-unnormalized-share in a future model period allows purpose-grown biomass
crops to enter the land competition structure despite having zero observed land area in the base
year. However, because the unnormalized competitiveness term depends on land profit rates (with
share weights defined relative to other land uses), updates to the rental profit rates of cropland and
forest land affect the relative competitiveness of existing land uses.
For example, if updated data reduces the average cropland rental profit by two-thirds, and the logit
exponent is 𝜆 = 1.75, the corresponding unnormalized competitiveness term for cropland would
decline to ( 1/3)1.75 ≈ 0.146 times of the original value. To maintain similar implied share weights
and entry competitiveness for purpose-grown biomass crops under the revised profit-rate
environment, the biomass ghost-unnormalized-share values must therefore be recalibrated
downward accordingly.
In this CMP, the cost shares in agricultural production, including land cost shares, were updated
based on data from GTAP and FAO. As a result, land shares generally became considerably smaller,
particularly for managed forest, as illustrated in Fig. E1. It is therefore essential to update the ghost
share parameters for biomass introduction, as failing to do so would substantially increase the
implied competitiveness of biomass and lead to much faster land expansion than previously
assumed.

3. Regional ghost share assumption updates
It is also notable that the updates to rental profit rates exhibit regional variation. More broadly, this
regional heterogeneity should also be reflected in the ghost share specification, as many
developing regions are unlikely to experience large-scale near-term expansion of purpose-grown
bioenergy crops. Accordingly, this CMP further refines the regional differentiation of biomass ghost
share parameters.

Table E2 summarizes the regional differentiation applied to the purpose-grown energy crop

support and deployment readiness, only a limited set of key regions with relatively strong existing
bioenergy support are assigned the “High” assumption, while all other regions are assigned the
“Low” assumption. The “High” designation is therefore used selectively for regions such as the
USA, Brazil, China, EU-12, Europe Non-EU, Australia_NZ, and Argentina, where stronger policy
support, market development, or production potential make earlier or more sustained deployment
more plausible.
This approach allows the model to retain substantial long-term production in a few key regions
while avoiding unrealistically rapid expansion in regions that currently lack strong bioenergy policy
support. E.g., the USA, the “High” assumption preserves a long-term production pathway that
remains broadly comparable to that in previous CMPs, while the assumptions for many other
regions are reduced relative to earlier specifications. These regional assumptions can also be
further differentiated by scenario (e.g., SSPs) when needed.
