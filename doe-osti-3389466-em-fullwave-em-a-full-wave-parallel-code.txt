 Abstract

 FullWave is a parallel full-wave RF simulation code for hot magnetized tokamak plasmas developed by
FAR-TECH, Inc. The theoretical foundation and initial implementation of the hybrid iterative algorithm were
established under prior DOE SBIR funding (2016–2017), with results published in [1, 2]. The present project
(DOE-OFES Award DE-SC0018959, 2018–2026) substantially extended the code to 2D, expanded the hot
plasma model to wider RF frequency ranges, and demonstrated its capabilities across multiple RF scenarios
relevant to present and future tokamaks. This report describes the code as delivered at the conclusion of the
project: its physical formulation, numerical algorithm, implementation, and demonstrated capabilities.
 FullWave solves the linearized Vlasov–Maxwell system in the frequency domain, discretized in con-
figuration (real) space on a staggered rectangular grid in the (R, Z) poloidal plane. The hot plasma current
is computed via direct numerical integration of the linearized Vlasov equation along unperturbed electron
trajectories, capturing the full non-local finite-Larmor-radius (FLR) dielectric response without spectral
approximation. The core algorithmic innovation is the hybrid iterative solver, which combines explicit time-
domain evolution of Maxwell’s equations with periodic frequency-domain relaxation via steepest descent.
This approach achieves spatial resolution exceeding 1011 degrees of freedom in the cold plasma model and
over 108 in the full hot plasma model—more than three orders of magnitude beyond leading direct-solver
codes (TORIC, AORSA)—with near-ideal parallel scaling on distributed-memory supercomputers.
 The code capabilities demonstrated under this project span several RF heating and current drive scenarios
relevant to present and future tokamaks: ECRH beam modeling in DIII-D (1D and 2D, cold and hot
plasma); full-scale 2D helicon and lower hybrid wave simulations at experimental DIII-D parameters on grids
exceeding 400 million points; and electron Bernstein wave simulations as a stringent validation benchmark
for the 2D non-local hot plasma model. A direct solver version was also developed as an independent
benchmarking and validation reference.
 The code is written in Fortran 90 and uses MPI for distributed-memory parallelism. Results are published
in five peer-reviewed journal articles, one under review, and presented at ten APS Division of Plasma Physics
annual meetings.

1 Introduction

1.1 Background and Motivation

Radio-frequency (RF) waves are a primary means of heating plasma and driving non-inductive current in
tokamak fusion devices. Several RF heating and current drive schemes are used in present experiments and
planned for future devices such as ITER: Electron Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ECRH), Lower Hybrid
(LH) current drive and heating, helicon wave current drive, and Ion Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ICRH).
Each of these schemes operates in a distinct frequency range and exploits different wave–plasma interaction
mechanisms, but all share a common requirement: accurate physics-based simulation of wave propagation,
mode conversion, and power deposition in the complex geometry of a magnetized toroidal plasma.
 Predictive modeling of RF wave fields is essential for the design and optimization of heating scenarios,
for interpretation of experimental measurements, and for the development of operational strategies for
next-generation fusion reactors. The accuracy of such modeling depends critically on the ability to resolve
fine-scale wave structures and to correctly capture kinetic effects—particularly the non-local response of
electrons where the electron Larmor radius ρe is not negligible compared to the local wavelength (finite
Larmor radius, or FLR, effects).

1.2 Overview of RF Waves in Tokamaks

The primary RF wave types relevant to this project span a wide range of frequencies and plasma interaction
physics.
 Electron Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ECRH) operates at frequencies near the second harmonic
of the electron cyclotron frequency ωce (typically 110–170 GHz for present devices). Electromagnetic
waves launched from outside the plasma propagate as O-mode or X-mode and are absorbed at the electron
cyclotron resonance layer or its harmonics, depositing power in a narrow, well-localized region. ECRH
is used for plasma heating, current drive (ECCD), and stabilization of magnetohydrodynamic instabilities.
Full-wave modeling of ECRH beams requires resolution of fine beam structures and correct treatment of
mode conversion between X- and O-modes.
 Lower Hybrid (LH) waves propagate at frequencies between the ion and electron cyclotron frequencies
(Ωi ≪ ω ≪ ωce , typically 1–8 GHz) and are primarily used for off-axis current drive, important for sustaining
steady-state tokamak operation. LH waves are launched from the plasma edge and propagate into the plasma
via wave accessibility. The simultaneous presence of fast and slow waves, combined with steep density
gradients, makes accurate full-wave simulation of LH waves computationally demanding.
 Helicon waves are whistler-mode waves in the lower hybrid frequency range (typically 0.5–1.0 GHz)
that propagate at oblique angles to the magnetic field and can penetrate deeply into high-density plasma.
Helicon current drive is of increasing interest as a candidate for off-axis current profile control. DIII-D has
been developing a helicon current drive launcher, and simulation of helicon wave propagation in realistic
DIII-D geometry is a key application of this project.
 Electron Bernstein Waves (EBW) are electrostatic waves propagating nearly perpendicular to the
magnetic field at frequencies near ωce and its harmonics. Unlike electromagnetic ECRH waves, EBWs can

1.3 Limitations of Existing Full-Wave Codes

Full-wave codes solve Maxwell’s equations self-consistently with the plasma dielectric response, without the
geometric-optics approximation used in ray tracing. The leading full-wave codes for tokamak RF modeling—
TORIC [3, 4] and AORSA [5, 6]—use spectral (Fourier) representations of the RF fields and direct linear
solvers (LU decomposition via ScaLAPACK) to invert the resulting dense matrices. STELEC for stellarator
ECH modeling. Several codes using direct solvers have been developed for LH and helicon waves in hot
plasma [7, 8, 9, 10].
 This spectral approach has enabled significant advances in physics fidelity but imposes a fundamental
resolution limit through memory constraints. Because all Fourier modes are coupled through the hot plasma
conductivity kernel, the resulting matrix is dense. Its storage and inversion require memory scaling as the
square of the number of grid points, limiting practical resolution to approximately 107 grid points for a
cold plasma model and approximately 106 grid points for a fully non-local hot plasma model on modern
supercomputers. The demonstrated resolution of leading direct-solver codes reaches ∼1 million grid points
represents the current state of the art for hot plasma full-wave codes.
 This resolution ceiling has direct physical consequences: fine-scale ECRH beam structures, mode
conversion layers, short-scale LH and EBW wave structures, and the detailed spatial variation of kinetic
damping all require finer resolution. Extending simulations to 3D geometry or next-generation device scales
makes the problem worse.
 Standard iterative solvers require far less memory and could in principle overcome this limitation, but
their application to wave equations has historically been unsuccessful. The fundamental difficulty is that the
matrix from the discretized wave equation is indefinite—its eigenvalue spectrum spans both positive and
negative real parts—making Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, and Krylov subspace methods (GMRES, LSQR) either
divergent or impractically slow without highly effective preconditioning. No practical iterative solver for
large-scale full-wave plasma simulations existed prior to our project described in this report.

configuration-space discretization of Maxwell’s equations and a hybrid iterative algorithm that combines
time-domain evolution with frequency-domain relaxation. The theoretical foundation was established under
prior SBIR funding [1, 2].
 The present project (DE-SC0018959) was carried out in three phases. As the overall goals were ambitious
relative to the available funding, a non-trivial amount of work carried over to the following renewal grants.

2.4 Conductivity Kernel: Formulation and Properties

The conductivity kernel is computed by numerical particle tracking in three-dimensional velocity space. For
each test point in the (R, Z) configuration space, electron orbits are integrated over approximately 20–200
gyro-periods for ECRF, and approximately 100 times longer periods for LH, in the equilibrium magnetic
field, with contributions accumulated over a spatial region extending approximately 10–100 Larmor radii
around the test point.
 An important property of the 2D kernel is that it is finite at the test point, in contrast to the strictly 1D case
— 1D both in space and in velocity space — where it diverges logarithmically. Note that the 1D helicon/LH
simulations presented in Section 4 use a kernel projected from the 2D calculation and are therefore also finite.
In the local coordinate frame (e1 , e2 , e3 ) with e3 ∥ B0 , the kernel is strongly localized in the perpendicular
directions (within ∼10 ρe ) but extends much further along e3 (up to 100–200 ρe ). This anisotropy reflects the
physics of wave–particle interaction along unperturbed gyro-orbits.

2.5 Particle Trajectory Calculation

Particle trajectories are computed using the Boris mover, which advances the exact equations of motion in the
equilibrium field:

                                            = v′ × B0 ,        = −v′ .                                   (13)
 dτ dτ
The Boris time-stepping scheme conserves the particle’s perpendicular speed exactly in a uniform field. Time
steps of ∼ 1/100 of the gyro-period resolve the cyclotron motion accurately.
 For the LH and helicon frequency range (ω ≪ ωce ), where the Boris pusher becomes CPU intensive due
to the need to resolve many hundreds of cyclotron periods, a guiding center (drift) approximation combined
with an analytic time integration technique is employed. The guiding center equations of motion are integrated
using a 2nd-order Runge–Kutta method with time steps 10–20 times larger than the Boris time step. Within
each cyclotron period, contributions to the orbit integral from the rapidly oscillating part of the integrand
are computed analytically, while the guiding center evolves on the slower drift timescale. Together these
techniques appear to reduce the kernel computation cost at LH frequencies by approximately two orders
of magnitude relative to full orbit tracking. In practice, however, when non-local values matter, the phase
information of the orbit is needed, resulting in overall acceleration that was not as significant as expected.
Considering the complexity of the drift orbit calculations while retaining phase information, this approach
was not pursued as a routine production calculation.

2.6 Coarse-Grid Interpolation Strategy

Computing the conductivity kernel at every grid point in the simulation domain is computationally prohibitive.
The kernel is therefore pre-computed on a coarse grid of test points (e.g. 11 × 23 or 11 × 11 points spanning
the simulation domain) and bilinearly interpolated onto the fine computational grid.

 An important difference between the ECR and LH regimes is the spatial extent of the kernel. At LH
frequencies, the relevant wave–particle interaction involves electrons traveling long distances along B0 over
many gyro-periods, extending the kernel to ∼100–200 ρe along the field line, compared to only 10–20 ρe at

 Despite this, the kernel remains well-localized perpendicular to B0 , and the coarse-grid interpolation
strategy remains effective. Non-local effects at LH frequencies are less immediately pronounced than at
ECR or EBW frequencies because LH wavelengths are typically much larger than ρe . This is precisely why
EBW simulations were used as the primary validation benchmark for the hot model: EBW wavelengths are
comparable to ρe , making non-local effects large and unambiguous.

2.8 Conductivity Kernel Convergence

The non-local conductivity kernel at each test point is computed by integrating electron trajectories over a 3D
velocity-space grid. Convergence was verified by systematically varying four numerical parameters: (a) the
integration time step; (b) the total trajectory tracking length; (c) the number of trajectories representing the
velocity distribution; and (d) the test point spacing.
 Figure 1 shows an example kernel calculation taking a DIII-D equilibrium in the (R, Z) poloidal plane.
The ZZ component of the conductivity kernel σZZ at a sample test point (R, Z) = (0, −14 cm) for an inboard
LH case (4.6 GHz, nϕ = 265, Te = 3 keV), computed on a velocity grid of 4001 × 2001 × 21 (parallel ×
perpendicular × azimuthal) with time step of 1/10 of the gyro-period, total tracking time 100 gyro-periods,
and spatial grid size ∼ ρe /2. The kernel is highly localized in the perpendicular direction but extends
100–200 ρe along the magnetic field, as expected for LH frequencies. Results are shown in both field-aligned
and laboratory coordinates.
 Figure 2 shows convergence of Im(σ33 ) with respect to (a) number of trajectories in velocity space and
(b) tracking length. Results are well converged for trajectory counts of 2001 × 1001 × 21, 4001 × 2001 × 21,
and 6001 × 2001 × 21. Doubling the tracking time from tmax to 2tmax shows no significant change, confirming
convergence. Similar convergence was verified for azimuthal trajectory counts (11 to 61) and time step
variations by a factor of 2.

3.1 Overall FullWave Code Architecture

FullWave is written in Fortran and parallelized using MPI for distributed-memory computing platforms. The
code has three main components: (1) the equilibrium and plasma profile input module, which reads EFIT
equilibrium files and constructs density, temperature, and magnetic field profiles on the simulation grid; (2)
the conductivity kernel calculation module, which computes the hot plasma response on a coarse test-point
grid using particle tracking; and (3) the wave equation solver module, which implements the hybrid iterative
algorithm (and, as a separate option, the direct solver) to find the RF field solution.
 The simulation domain is a rectangular region in the (R, Z) poloidal plane. Fields are defined on a uniform
staggered square grid using 2nd-order finite differences. Electric and magnetic field components are defined
on integer and half-integer grid points respectively (a Yee-type staggered grid), which ensures numerical
stability of the time advance and provides a natural, divergence-free field representation.

3.2 Configuration-Space Discretization

The wave equations (3)–(4) from Section 2 are discretized on the uniform staggered grid in (R, Z) using
2nd-order centered finite differences for spatial derivatives. Toroidal dependence is handled analytically
through the einϕ ϕ factor, reducing the problem to a 2D computation in the poloidal plane.
 The use of configuration-space (real-space) discretization, rather than the spectral decomposition used in
leading full-wave codes, is a key architectural choice enabling high spatial resolution with modest memory.
Because the cold plasma conductivity tensor is diagonal (or near-diagonal) in local coordinates, the resulting
cold-model matrix is sparse and its memory requirement scales linearly with the number of grid points. For
the hot plasma model, the non-local conductivity kernel couples each grid point to its neighbors within the
kernel extent, but the matrix remains far sparser than the fully dense spectral matrices of direct-solver codes.

3.3 Hybrid Iterative Algorithm

The central innovation of FullWave is the hybrid iterative algorithm for solving the full-wave boundary value
problem. Each iteration cycle contains two steps:
 Step 1—Time evolution: Maxwell’s equations (3)–(4) are evolved in time over a specified number of RF
periods, starting from the current field state. The antenna source term oscillates at frequency ω, driving the
field into the simulation domain. This step rapidly populates the domain with an RF field propagating at the
group velocity, and its dissipation drives the solution toward steady state.
 Step 2—Iterative relaxation: The intermediate solution from Step 1 is used as the starting point for
iterative relaxation of the frequency-domain equations (5)–(6) using a steepest-descent (gradient descent)
minimization of the residual. A small number of relaxation steps (typically 2–4) are applied.
 The two steps are complementary: time evolution efficiently brings the field to an approximate solution
over the entire domain, while relaxation corrects local errors and accelerates convergence. Together, they
achieve convergence where neither step alone succeeds. The substitution E(t, r) = Ẽ(t, r)eiωt removes the
fast carrier oscillation, so source terms become time-independent after the initial transient.

3.7 Direct Solver

A direct solver version of FullWave was developed to serve as a benchmarking reference and as a practical
simulation tool for cases within its resolution limits. The direct solver assembles the full matrix of the
frequency-domain wave equations (5)–(6) and solves the resulting linear system using standard parallel direct
methods.
 For the cold plasma model, the matrix is sparse, and the direct solver can handle grids up to approximately
5 × 107 grid points (3 × 108 DOF) before memory becomes limiting. For the hot plasma model, the non-local
conductivity kernel densifies the matrix significantly (each grid point coupled to 10–1000 neighbors), making
the direct solver impractical for 2D full-scale simulations.
 The direct solver has been validated against the hybrid iterative solver, providing machine-precision
agreement for 1D problems and good agreement for 2D cold-model problems. It serves as the ground-truth
reference for validating the hybrid iterative algorithm.

3.8 Aligned Coordinates

For accurate simulation of LH and helicon waves, the parallel component of the RF electric field E∥ must
be resolved to high precision. In laboratory (R, ϕ, Z) coordinates, numerical errors from finite-difference
discretization can introduce spurious parallel fields.
 A local coordinate system aligned with the equilibrium magnetic field direction was implemented in
FullWave. At each grid point, a rotation matrix transforms between the aligned frame (one parallel, two
perpendicular components relative to B0 ) and the laboratory frame. The wave equations and boundary
conditions are formulated in the aligned frame, then transformed back for spatial differencing. This option is
implemented; however, most of the work was performed in laboratory coordinates.

3.9 Parallel Implementation and Scalability

FullWave uses MPI for distributed-memory parallelism with 1D domain decomposition in the Z direction.
The wave field time evolution and relaxation steps are local (near-neighbor communication) and scale nearly
ideally for cold model.
 The hot plasma conductivity kernel calculation is parallelized by dividing the initial velocity space
among processors. A key optimization is that each processor communicates with at most 8 others—a fixed

A post-processing module computes RF beam characteristics from the FullWave field solutions: the power

4.4 2D Hot-Model Proof of Concept

Extending the non-local kernel to 2D poloidal geometry is computationally demanding once a poloidal field
component Bθ is present: electron orbits become helical and field-aligned, elongating the kernel to ∼100
electron Larmor radii along the field line (versus ∼10 in the purely-toroidal 1D case), which increases the
coupling-point count—and CPU cost—by roughly two orders of magnitude. As a tractable proof of concept,
we therefore used a simplified Bθ = 0 DIII-D-geometry equilibrium with frequency, field, and density scaled

our knowledge, the first 2D full-wave simulation of LH waves using a non-local hot-plasma conductivity
kernel with no small-Larmor-radius approximation: the slow wave damps near the resonance layer while

resonance layer (R/a ≈ 0.65–0.7 vs. 0.6), consistent with the kinetic FLR shift already established in the 1D
results. Extending this proof of concept to realistic Bθ ̸= 0 geometry is identified as the next step and will
require improved parallel kernel communication (SHMPI).

5 ECRH Beam Modeling in DIII-D

5.1 Overview

Full-wave simulation of ECRH beam propagation and absorption in DIII-D was a central application of
FullWave during the first project period. These simulations were the first to compute complete ECRH RF
beams in a tokamak cross-section using realistic plasma parameters, without the paraxial approximation. The
results and numerical details are fully described in Svidzinski, Zhao, Kim, and Barov [14], which should be
referred to for the complete account. This section provides a concise summary.

5.2 Physics and Motivation

ECRH beams launched into a tokamak propagate as a mixture of X-mode and O-mode waves, undergoing
diffraction, mode conversion, and, for the 2nd harmonic scenario, absorption near the second electron
cyclotron resonance layer. Standard paraxial codes (TORAY, TRAVIS) treat the beam as a ray bundle
and cannot capture diffraction, interference between modes, or the detailed beam structure near cutoff and
resonance layers. Full-wave treatment is required for these effects.
 The ECR frequency range is computationally favorable for FullWave: the explicit time-stepping scheme
is stable, and the cold plasma model is accurate outside the narrow 2nd harmonic absorption region where the
hot plasma response is needed.

5.3 Code Adaptations and Post-Processing

A Gaussian beam launcher was implemented at the simulation domain boundary with configurable beam
width, launch angle, and polarization. A beam analysis post-processing module computes beam width vs.
propagation distance, X-mode and O-mode power fractions along the beam, and optimal launcher polarization.
More than 20 production runs were carried out at NERSC for a range of DIII-D discharge conditions in
collaboration with the DIII-D RF group.

6 Electron Bernstein Wave Simulations as Hot Model Benchmark

Electron Bernstein Wave (EBW) simulations were performed as a bonus validation benchmark for the
FullWave non-local hot plasma model. EBW is an electrostatic mode relevant to over-dense spherical
tokamaks and compact devices — not a standard wave in conventional tokamaks such as DIII-D. However,
since EBW operates near ωce and its wavelength (λ ∼ ρe ) makes non-local FLR kinetic effects exceptionally

inaccessible to cold or local models. In 2D (R0 = 55 cm, a = 15 cm, 11 × 21 kernel test-point grid), the

7 Computational Performance

7.1 Parallel Scalability — Wave Equation Solver

The hybrid iterative algorithm in FullWave exhibits near-ideal parallel scaling for the wave equation solver
(time evolution and relaxation steps) in cold model. This is a direct consequence of the algorithm’s structure:
the time evolution is a local operation involving only near-neighbor grid communications, and the relaxation
step is also local. The MPI domain decomposition assigns slabs of the (R, Z) grid to each processor, and the
communication at slab boundaries involves only a fixed, small number of neighboring processors regardless
of the total processor count.
 An optimized communication algorithm was developed in which each processor communicates with at
most 8 other processors — a fixed number independent of the total processor count. For hot model, this MPI
scheme is ineffective because data communications are well beyond a fixed small number of processors. As
the total number of processors increases, the amount of data each processor must communicate decreases
proportionally, ensuring that communication overhead does not grow with problem size. This near-ideal
scaling for cold model was demonstrated in production runs at NERSC [12], where FullWave has been run
on grids exceeding 400 million grid points across hundreds of compute nodes.
 For the hot plasma model, however, the current MPI scheme becomes ineffective beyond a fixed small
number of processors. The non-local conductivity kernel requires data from a spatial region extending
100–200 ρe along the magnetic field, which spans multiple MPI slabs. The resulting inter-slab communication
grows with the kernel extent and is not bounded by a fixed number of neighbors, limiting parallel efficiency
for the hot model.
 This scalability stands in sharp contrast to direct solvers, where the parallel efficiency degrades with
increasing processor count due to the global communication requirements of distributed matrix factorization
(ScaLAPACK). The iterative solver’s near-ideal scaling means that FullWave’s resolution advantage over
direct solvers grows with each generation of supercomputer.

7.3 CPU Time — The Main Current Limitation

Despite the resolution advantage, demanding CPU time is the primary practical limitation of FullWave in its
current state, particularly for the 2D hot plasma model. The computational cost arises from two sources: the
conductivity kernel calculation, and the iterative solver convergence.

Conductivity Kernel Calculation

The conductivity kernel calculation is the most computationally expensive component of the hot plasma
model. For each test point in the coarse kernel grid, millions of electron trajectories must be tracked and
integrated to obtain a converged kernel. Representative timings on NERSC Perlmutter:

 • ECR range (Boris pusher): kernel at one test point requires ∼4 minutes on 8 nodes (2,000 CPUs) on
 Perlmutter.

 • LH range (guiding center + analytic time integration): tracking time per particle is ∼200 gyro-periods;
 kernel computation is significantly more expensive than ECR range due to longer orbit integration
 times.

 • Computing kernels at low temperatures is CPU intensive due to slower convergence with lack of phase
 mixing. However, for most tokamaks, kernel elements at temperatures below 0.5 keV may be replaced
 with a local or cold analytical model.

For a 2D LH hot model simulation requiring kernels on an 11 × 11 test-point grid (121 points), total kernel
computation time on 8 NERSC nodes is on the order of hours. Scaling to a denser kernel grid or including
poloidal field effects (which extend the kernel to ∼100 Larmor radii along the field line) would increase this
cost substantially.

Solver Convergence

For cold model 2D simulations, the local implicit solver converges efficiently:

 • 1D LH, experimental DIII-D parameters: machine accuracy after ∼3,000 RF cycles.

      • Full-scale 2D DIII-D helicon (16,384 × 24,576 grid, ∼400M points): error ∼ 10−4 after 72 wave
 periods. Each wave period requires 40,000 time steps. Total wall time: order of days on hundreds of
 NERSC nodes.

For hot model 2D simulations, convergence is slower due to the dense non-local coupling:

 • 2D EBW hot model (900 × 1,440 grid, 11 × 21 kernel grid): ∼48 CPU-hours on 8 NERSC nodes to
 reach 10−4 relative error.

 • 2D LH hot model (proof-of-concept, 11 × 11 kernel grid): comparable cost.

These timings make single-case validation feasible but parameter scans or experimental scenario modeling

7.4 GPU Acceleration — Exploratory Work

GPU acceleration of the conductivity kernel calculation was explored as a potential path to large speedups,
since the trajectory tracking is an embarrassingly parallel loop over millions of independent particles. Initial
GPU porting was performed in Fortran using NVIDIA compiler directives (pgf90) and later OpenMP target
offloading on NERSC Perlmutter A100 GPUs. Results were mixed:

 • The particle trajectory tracking loop achieves a 7× speedup over a single CPU core in isolation.

 • The kernel deposition step — accumulating trajectory contributions onto the spatial grid via random-
 access writes — causes severe memory contention on GPU, largely offsetting the tracking speedup.

 • Best result: 4.8 seconds per test on GPU vs. 5.6 seconds on a single Perlmutter CPU core — no
 significant net speedup.

 • The fundamental bottleneck is the irregular memory access pattern of the deposition step. Approaches
 under consideration include restructuring deposition as a histogram operation with per-thread buffers,
 and AI/ML surrogate models to bypass direct kernel calculation entirely.

7.5 Path Forward on Performance

Addressing the CPU time limitation will require architectural improvements — in particular transitioning to
Shared Memory MPI (SHMPI) to handle the extended kernel communication demands of the 2D non-local
hot model — along with algorithmic acceleration of the conductivity kernel calculation. The coarse-grid
kernel approach has already demonstrated that the essential non-local physics can be retained with greatly
reduced memory cost; further reduction through AI/ML surrogate models trained on pre-computed kernel
libraries is a natural extension. GPU porting of the time evolution and relaxation steps in the hybrid iterative
solver represents an additional opportunity for large speedups, given the regular memory access structure
of those components. These directions build directly on the algorithmic and computational foundation
established under this grant.

9 Conclusions

This report has described FullWave, a full-wave RF simulation code for hot tokamak plasmas. The theoretical
foundation and initial implementation were established under prior DOE SBIR funding (2016–2017) [1, 2];
the present project (DOE-OFES Award DE-SC0018959, 2018–2026) extended the code to 2D, expanded
the hot plasma model to lower RF frequency ranges, and demonstrated its capabilities across multiple RF
scenarios. The code delivered at the conclusion of this project has the following demonstrated capabilities
and properties:

 • Algorithm. The hybrid iterative solver combines Maxwell evolution with frequency-domain steepest-
 descent relaxation. It is the only available practical iterative solver for large-scale full-wave plasma
 simulations, achieving spatial resolution exceeding 1011 degrees of freedom in the cold plasma model
 and over 108 in the full FLR hot plasma model—more than three orders of magnitude beyond leading
 direct-solver codes—with near-ideal MPI parallel scaling on NERSC supercomputers.

 • 1D and 2D implementations. FullWave is implemented in both 1D and 2D (poloidal plane). The 1D
 version serves as the primary algorithm development and validation platform; the 2D version handles
 realistic tokamak geometry in the (R, Z) poloidal plane with MPI parallelization across distributed-
 memory supercomputers.

 • Hot plasma model. The non-local hot plasma dielectric response is computed by direct numerical
 integration of the linearized Vlasov equation along unperturbed electron trajectories, without spectral
 approximation. A coarse test-point grid with interpolation to the simulation grid makes the approach
 memory-practical at 2D scale. The model is validated across the ECRH, LH/helicon, and EBW
 frequency ranges.

 • Local implicit time-stepping. A local implicit scheme replaces the explicit Courant-limited time
 advance at lower RF frequencies, providing orders-of-magnitude speedup and enabling extension of
 FullWave’s capabilities from the ECR range to cover the LH and helicon frequency ranges.

 • ECRH beam modeling. FullWave computed complete ECRH RF beams in DIII-D geometry for the
 first time without the paraxial approximation, capturing beam diffraction, X-O mode interference,
 mode conversion, beam splitting, and 2nd-harmonic absorption in both cold and hot plasma models. A
 beam analysis post-processing module was developed. Results are published in Physics of Plasmas 31,
      042504 (2024) [14].

 • Helicon and LH wave simulations. Full-scale 2D simulations of helicon and LH waves at experimental
 DIII-D parameters (shot 196153, 476 MHz, nϕ = −71) were completed on NERSC Perlmutter on
 grids exceeding 400 million points (2.4 billion degrees of freedom, 2.7 TB memory), simultaneously
 resolving fast and slow wave branches. Hot non-local 1D simulations achieved machine-precision
 convergence. Results are described in the manuscript submitted to Physics of Plasmas [13].

 • EBW simulations (bonus). As additional validation beyond the original project scope, the 2D non-
 local hot plasma model was applied to electron Bernstein wave (EBW) physics. EBW is an electrostatic
 mode relevant primarily to over-dense spherical tokamaks and compact devices rather than conventional
 tokamaks, but its wavelength (λ ∼ ρe ) makes non-local FLR effects exceptionally strong and directly

Full Wave Parallel Code for Modeling RF Fields in Hot Tokamak Plasma 28

FAR-TECH, Inc. | DOE Award DE-SC0018959                                         FullWave RF Code Development

 verifiable against analytical dispersion theory—making it an ideal and stringent benchmark for the hot
 plasma model. The simulation correctly reproduced X-mode to EBW mode conversion at the upper
 hybrid resonance (UHR), strong cyclotron damping near the ECR, and quantitative agreement with the
 hot-plasma dispersion relation (λEBW ≈ 0.8 cm, ∼ 10 ρe ). Results are published in AIP Advances 16,
      065306 (2026) [11].

 • Direct solver. A direct solver version of FullWave was developed as an independent benchmarking
 reference and a practical tool for scenarios within its resolution limits, confirming the hybrid iterative
 solver results across all tested configurations.

 • Current limitation. The primary practical limitation of FullWave in its current state is CPU time,
 particularly for 2D hot plasma simulations. Conductivity kernel computation requires tracking of
 billions of electron trajectories per test point; representative 2D hot model runs require tens to hundreds

 acceleration of the kernel calculation, and AI/ML surrogate models for the conductivity kernel.

 FullWave is written in Fortran 90 and uses MPI for distributed-memory parallelism. The code has been
run on NERSC Cori and Perlmutter supercomputers, with the largest runs utilizing up to 0.5 × 106 CPU-hours.
The code is available to qualified fusion research institutions; distribution is subject to applicable export

B Conductivity Kernel Code

B.1 Overview

The physical formulation of the non-local hot plasma conductivity kernel ← →
 σ (r, r′ ) and its computation by
electron trajectory integration are described in Section 2. In the FullWave code architecture, the kernel
calculation is implemented as a standalone parallel Fortran program (ck), separate from the wave solver.
This separation allows the kernel to be precomputed once for a given equilibrium and plasma profile, stored
to disk, and then reused across multiple wave solver runs without recomputation — an important practical
advantage given the high cost of kernel evaluation (Section 3).
 The ck program takes a set of user-specified test point locations (Rt , Zt ) in the tokamak poloidal plane,
computes the 3 × 3 complex conductivity tensor σi j as a function of displacement from each test point, and
writes the results in sparse HDF5 format to ck_data.h5. The FullWave wave solver reads this file and
interpolates the kernel onto the fine simulation grid using bilinear interpolation, as described in Section 2.5.

B.2 Kernel Computation Variants

The ck program provides multiple kernel computation subroutines, selectable via the model flag in the
input namelist. The choice of variant depends on the RF frequency range and the desired accuracy:

 • CK_plasma.f90 — main module for trajectory integration using laboratory coordinates. Two
 options are available:

 1. Without magnetic boundary checking — trajectory tracking continues until the specified integra-
 tion time is reached.
 2. With LCFS boundary checking — trajectory integration stops when a particle exits the last closed
 flux surface (LCFS); used near the plasma edge.

 MPI parallelization distributes test points across MPI ranks, with each rank computing the kernel for its
assigned subset independently. The innermost kernel evaluation tracks 27 electron trajectories per velocity-
space point on a 3 × 3 × 3 stencil for second-order accuracy. Additional variants using guiding-center
coordinates and uniform plasma approximation are available for benchmarking; see the source code for
details.

B.3 Connection to the Wave Solver

The workflow connecting the ck program to the FullWave wave solver is as follows:

 1. Select test point locations (Rt , Zt ) on a coarse grid spanning the simulation domain (typically 11 × 11
       to 11 × 23 points).

 2. Run ck on NERSC using mpirun (see Makefile in the code distribution). For the ECR range, each
 test point requires ∼4 minutes on 8 nodes (2,000 CPUs); LH range costs are higher due to longer orbit
 integration times.

 3. Inspect kernel output using the Python plotting routine plot/plot_ck.py (make plt) to verify
 kernel localization and convergence before running the wave solver.

 4. Provide the kernel output file (ck_data.h5) to the FullWave wave solver input. The solver reads the
 sparse kernel data and interpolates to the fine simulation grid of the hybrid iterative solver.

 The restart capability of the ck program (checkpoint save/restore via ck_data.h5 and ck_data.h5.bak)
is particularly useful for long kernel runs on NERSC where wall-clock limits may interrupt a single job

D FullWave 1D Direct & Hybrid Solver (fullwave1d)

D.1 Overview

The 1D solver (fullwave1d) solves the full-wave Maxwell equations along the major radial direction R,
assuming no variation in Z or φ . It is the primary platform for algorithm development, hot plasma model
validation, and benchmarking — the code in which machine-precision convergence of the hybrid iterative
algorithm was first demonstrated (Section 3).
 The solver supports four methods via the solver flag:

 • solver=1 — Hybrid with Crank-Nicolson (CN). Both E and B are advanced using the CN fully
 implicit scheme, requiring solution of a global linear system at each time step. Cold plasma only; not
 tested in recent years.

 • solver=2 — Direct. Assembles and solves the full 1D BVP as a sparse linear system via PETSc +
 SuperLU_DIST. Runs on one processor only. Cold and hot models.

 • solver=3 — Explicit. Fully explicit time advance of both E and B. Cold plasma only; not tested in
 recent years.

 • solver=4 — Hybrid with local-implicit. B updated explicitly; E updated via a local 3 × 3 system
 at each grid point, followed by steepest-descent frequency-domain relaxation. Cold and hot models.
 Production solver for all 1D results in this project.

The solution procedure is:

 1. Initialize EFIT equilibrium and simulation grid (bnr×bnz); precompute coordinate alignment tensors

E.4 Input File

The 2D Direct input file uses two namelists only — &model_control (grid size bnr, bnz) and
&plasma_input (all physics parameters) — with no &output_options namelist. This is simpler than
the 2D Hybrid input since there are no time-stepping or convergence control parameters to specify.
 The LH input files for both solvers use identical physical parameters, enabling direct cross-validation

F 2D Hybrid Solver (fullwave2d)

F.1 Overview

The 2D Hybrid solver (fullwave2d) is the primary production code of the FullWave suite. It solves the
2D full-wave Maxwell equations in the poloidal (R, Z) plane using the hybrid iterative algorithm described in
Section 3, alternating between two phases:

 1. Time evolution. Maxwell’s equations are advanced in time. B is updated explicitly. E is updated via a
 local-implicit scheme: at each grid point a small 3 × 3 linear system coupling E, the plasma current J,
 and the conductivity tensor ←→
 σ is solved locally.

 2. Relaxation. After each time evolution sub-cycle, a steepest-descent frequency-domain relaxation step
 accelerates convergence to the monochromatic steady-state solution. The number of relaxation loops
 per cycle is set by nrelax in the input file.

 This combination enables steady-state solutions on grids far exceeding the resolution limits of direct
solvers (Section 3, Appendix E).

F.2 Unique Source Modules

 • fullwave2d.f90 — Main program. Contains the driver, time evolution routines timeev and
 timeev1 (the local-E scheme), boundary condition routine boundc, RF beam launch function erb,
 and the local 3 × 3 linear solver solve.

 • global16sl.f90 — Global variables, namelist parameter definitions, domain initialization (parameters_adjust
 and RF beam parameter setup (rf_beam_parameters).

 • halo_exchange.f90 — MPI halo (ghost cell) exchange. The (R, Z) grid is decomposed into
 horizontal slabs along Z, one per MPI rank. Before each time step, one layer of ghost cells at each slab
 boundary is communicated between neighboring ranks.

 • farphdf5.f90 — Parallel HDF5 I/O wrapper (MPI-IO). All MPI ranks write field output simulta-
 neously to a single HDF5 file, avoiding I/O bottlenecks at large processor counts.

 • header.f90 — Global physical constants.

F.3 Input File

The input file uses the same three-namelist structure as the 1D solver (&model_control, &plasma_input,
&output_options). Key differences from the 1D input:

 • Grid is 2D: nrgrid and nzgrid specify the number of points in R and Z

 • solver=2 selects local-implicit (the only production option); solver=3 selects the direct solver

 • Beam launch geometry specified by rf_location, theta_launch, phi_launch, eyp, ezp

 • Output frequency in cycles via nskip_write, nskip_print; CPU time limit via tw_stop

 A fully annotated 2D LH example input file is in the annotated LH input file in the code distribution.
