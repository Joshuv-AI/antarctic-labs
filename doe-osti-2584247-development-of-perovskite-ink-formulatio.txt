Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

PowerRoll Ltd and NREL will work on development of perovskite ink formulations and
corresponding coating techniques to best coat PowerRoll’s micro-structure substrates with high
quality, high-performance perovskite materials.

CRADA benefit to DOE, Participant, and US Taxpayer:

Enhances the laboratory’s core competencies and enhances U.S. competitiveness by utilizing
DOE developed intellectual property and/or capabilities.

Summary of Research Results:

The work was broken up into six tasks. The CRADA was later expanded to focus on four tasks
inspired by initial work and needs identified by PowerRoll. The data and analysis from each task
was presented biweekly to PowerRoll in a virtual meeting with PowerRoll employees from both
their headquarters and their local offices in United States.

Task 1: Review of Ink formulations

NREL was tasked with developing a summary report on 3-4 promising ink formulations,
processing conditions, device architectures, surface treatments, etc. A thorough investigation of
the literature was followed by a report that included an evaluation of key characteristics of ink
formulations that were of concern to PowerRoll. A summary of the report was captured in a
table, which is reproduced here for reference (Table 1), where different ink formulations were
broken down into categories and characterized by fundamental physical properties and other
concerns that PowerRoll wanted to have taken into account, such as toxicity, chemical
compatibility, and examples in the literature.

The report was also presented in the form of a virtual presentation to PowerRoll to discuss
findings and discuss directions for experimental work. We determined logical places to focus
research on inks and printing on PowerRoll substrates.

Task 2: Start of initial blade coating trials to identify optimal candidates for R2R trials

Task 2 makes up most of the time and resources applied in this agreement. For this work,
PowerRoll supplies their micro-structured substrates for coating using NREL-developed inks on
equipment at NREL. A decision was made between NREL and PowerRoll to start with slot die
coating rather than blade coating to develop inks with processes closer to what will be eventually
commercialized. The ink trials were based on the ink formulations developed in Task 1. Those
formulations would serve as a baseline, but there were many unknowns that were investigated
during Task 2. Process parameters such as increased temperature, deposition rate, or the use of
an air knife, for example. Furthermore, additional cosolvents, additives, and surfactants were
included in experiments parameters.

Figure 1: a) Photograph of halide perovskite printed on a PowerRoll microstructured substrate. b,
c) Optical microscopy image of a halide perovskite coating on a substrate that is untreated (b) and
 treated (c) with a chemical process.

Initial evaluation of films was made with visual investigation of the films for defects. Optical
images were reported to PowerRoll (Figure 1a). Defects are initially screened with optical
microscopy. An example of the result of printing a halide perovskite on an untreated substrate
(Figure 1b) versus a chemically treated substrate (Figure 1c) is shown as an example in Figure 1.

Task 3: Stability assessment of coated microgrooves R2R coatings trials with micro
gravure and/or slot-die.

The goal of this task was to undertake standard testing of intrinsic, operational, and accelerated
aging stability of halide perovskite modules produced with PowerRoll substrates. The tests
should utilize the lessons learned in the above step to push toward roll-to-roll coating processes.

Though stability testing of modules was not undertaken, NREL put research effort into
addressing a fundamental stability issue with PowerRoll stability by performing laser scribing
studies on the delineation feature of the PowerRoll microstructured substrate. The delineation
feature is responsible for electrically isolating sets of series-connected cells. Typical experiments
involved measuring the open-circuit voltage of samples before and after ablating material with a
laser. Laser conditions such as power, pulse frequency, and number of passes were studied. To
optimize conditions for adequate material ablation to electrically isolate sets of cells.

Task 4: Optimization of the processing conditions through blade coating and R2R

Processing conditions of the slit-die coated halide perovskite films were optimized by
downselecting promising films and undertaking further characterization. On a biweekly basis,
inks were formulated and printed onto PowerRoll substrates to do a parametric study on printing
process conditions or other experimental parameters (additives, ink chemistry, etc.). Samples
were downselected based on initial visual and optical microscopy screening for further
characterization to understand coating coverage and structure.

 Figure 2: Optical profilometry images of a halide perovskite printed on a PowerRoll
 microstructured substrate at various temperatures.

Example of characterization performed after downselection is included in Figures 2 and 3. Figure
2 shows optical profilometry images of halide perovskite inks deposited on the PowerRoll
microstructures substate at different temperatures. Optical profilometry reveals defects that are
not visible to the naked eye, which increase as a function of temperature.

Figure 3: a) Ultraviolet-visible-near infrared spectroscopy of halide perovskite films deposited on
PowerRoll microstructured substrates under different deposition conditions. b) X-ray diffraction
patterns of halide pro perovskite films deposited on PowerRoll microstructured substrates under
 different temperatures.

Figure 3 shows two methods used to further characterize downselected samples. Ultraviolet-
visible-near infrared spectroscopy yields information about thickness and morphology of films
(Figure 3b). X-ray diffraction gives information about the crystallinity, phase, and presence of
impurities in halide perovskite samples. It also yields information about strain and
crystallographic texturing of the halide perovskite film.

Performing roll-to-roll desposition activities were deemed beyond reasonable scope for the
budget and deprioritized in this work. All work was done batch-style on substrates supplied by
PowerRoll.

Modification 4

Task 1: Perovskite Coating Studies:

Small panel sections will be coated using a blade-coating procedure. Halide perovskite precursor
inks developed by the contractor and coating conditions will be optimized. Film quality will be
determined using optical profilometry and possibly other imaging techniques.

The work in Task 1 of the modified agreement clearly builds on results obtained in Tasks 1, 2
and 4 of the original agreement. After agreement modification, a new process was also
undertaken to increase productivity. The project worked with a graduate intern to drive
experimental results, which resulted in higher throughput on samples. The decision was also
made to use blade-coating as a faster screening method of inks and deposition conditions rather
than slot-die coating.

Figure 4: Example of a correlational heatmap to evaluate halide perovskite solar cell performance
 for a particular deposition condition.
 8

Halide perovskite precursor inks were blade-coated onto microstructured substrates supplied by
PowerRoll to complete photovoltaic devices for testing. Devices were tested using current-voltage
characterization, which is used to extract several parameters of performance that are correlated to
the deposition conditions (Figure 4). Correlational heatmaps were generated to determine
significant parameters and guide new ink formulation development.

Task 2: PowerRoll Panel Testing:

The Participant will supply the instrumentation (Keithley Testing unit) to allow for the
Contractor to conduct performance testing of full panels up to 400V.

The original plan was for the contractor to purchase an instrument that is capable of testing high-
voltage panels. Instead, an instrument was leant to NREL from PowerRoll, which allowed panels
to be tested up to 400 V, which is unique to the PowerRoll technology. Solar cell power
conversion efficiency (PCE) was extracted from current-voltage scans of modules (Figure 5).

Figure 5: Example current-voltage scan of a completed PowerRoll modules. Forward and reverse
 scans are highlighted.

The goal was to obtain power conversion efficiency greater than 10% on a PowerRoll substrate
with an active area of 80 cm2. Significant progress toward this goal was achieved over the course
of only a few months, and we ultimately got close to achieving that goal.

Task 3: Encapsulation:

The Participant will supply encapsulation materials and processes to the Contractor, Modules
will be encapsulated with pressure-sensitive adhesive for barrier films applied to the module.

The goal of this task was to encapsulate module for durability testing in Task 4. This work was
not undertaken due to limited time and resources. Other tasks were prioritized.

 9

Task 4: Module Stability:

The Contractor will test Encapsulated modules with accelerated testing to evaluate stability.

The goal of this task was to test encapsulated modules with accelerated testing to evaluate
stability of PowerRoll module performance. Samples were aged and reevaluated using current-
voltage scans to evaluate changes in performance. The data is presented as an example in Figure
4. More advanced accelerated aging measurements were planned but not undertaken due to
limited time and resources.

Task 5: Ink-jet Printer for D-Lin Printing:

The Contractor will optimize ink formulation and process for robust cell isolation by printing in
the delineation feature. The Contractor will explore the effects of ink chemistry and printing
conditions such as UV-curing during the printing process. Ultimately, once the Contractor has
gotten traction on the ink performance, the Contractor can print several units prior to blade
coating and encapsulation.

The goal of Task 5 was to utilize inkjet printing for robust cell isolation by printing in the
delineation feature. NREL studied the effects of ink chemistry and printing conditions such as
UV-curing during the printing process. Both Parties will utilize units with printed delineation
features to coat perovskite onto. Inkjet printing studies focused on ink chemistry and print
parameters such as drop spacing, voltage, and frequency.

 Figure 6: Optical microscopy and profilometry images of the delineation feature of a
 PowerRoll microstructured substrate. In this example, inkjet printing was used to fill the
 second of four trench features with a UV-cured polymer.

Inkjet printing runs were evaluated using optical microscopy and profilometry methods. The
methods allowed for evaluation of selecting filling of delineation features. A highlight of the
work was the modification of NREL’s printer to accommodate UV-curing during deposition,
which yielded critical successes to the task.
