Genomic evolution and minority variant dynamics of RSV during the first season of nirsevimab implementation in Italy (2024–5)
Authors: Greta Romano, Roberta Campagna, Guglielmo Ferrari, Antonino Maria Guglielmo Pitrolo, Ombretta Turriziani, Matteo Fracella, Eleonora Coratti, Lilia Cinti, Piergiorgio Roberto, Laura Petrarca, Raffaella Nenna, Fabio Midulla, Alice Bonetti, Amelia Licari, Antonio Piralla, Guido Antonelli, Fausto Baldanti, Alessandra Pierangeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) remains a leading cause of hospitalization despite the recent implementation of infant immunoprophylaxis programs. The inter- and intrahost levels of genetic variation across the genome have not been extensively investigated. Hence, the aim of this study was to characterize the whole genome of RSV strains circulating within two Italian areas during the 2024–5 RSV season, corresponding to the first season after implementation of the national nirsevimab prophylaxis program. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 88 respiratory samples that tested positive for RSV. Clinical records showed that none of the sequenced patients had received nirsevimab prophylaxis. The WGS strains obtained in the present study were correlated with the RSV strains circulating in Europe using phylogenetic inference. A newly designed bioinformatic pipeline was developed to evaluate the extent of genetic variation within hosts and reveal the distribution of minority mutations among RSV lineages. RSV-A (n = 54) showed significantly higher levels of nucleotide diversity than RSV-B (n = 34). Among the RSV-A strains, lineage A.D.3 was the most prevalent (35/54, 64%), followed by A.D.1 (18/54, 33%). The A.D.5 lineage was identified in only one strain, while all 34 RSV-B strains were found to be lineage B.D.E. Analysis of variants across the major lineages (A.D.1, A.D.3, and B.D.E) revealed heterogeneous mutation patterns. The G, L, and F genes showed the highest variability. Minority viral variants (0.02–0.05 frequency) followed the same gene-specific dynamics as the broader quasispecies population. A > G transitions consistent with ADAR activity were detected in all samples, but their frequency was similar to the other nucleotide substitutions across the genomes. The analysis of minor variants also targeted mutations known to reduce nirsevimab efficacy, and none were detected. Overall, RSV-A showed substantially greater intragenotypic and intrahost diversity than RSV-B. The elevated diversity in RSV-A resulted from a wider distribution of mutations across the viral genome, including both high-frequency substitutions that shaped the consensus sequence and low-frequency variants contributing to the underlying quasispecies complexity. Although no nirsevimab-treated patients were included in this study, the observed intrahost diversity highlights the importance of continued genomic surveillance to detect potential escape variants as prophylactic programs expand.

Introduction

Infection caused by Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) represents the major cause of infants and young children hospitalization for bronchiolitis and pneumonia (Li et al. 2022) and is also associated with severe illness in older age (Savic et al. 2023). RSV is an enveloped, negative-stranded RNA virus that belongs to the Pneumoviridae, genus Orthopneumovirus. RSV is further classified into two antigenic subtypes, A and B, and in lineages (Goya et al. 2024). Subtypes A and B can co-circulate, or one may predominate during a specific epidemic season (Cantú-Flores et al. 2022). Moreover, multiple strains of both subtypes often circulate simultaneously during a single season, and their local distribution can change from year to year (Goya et al. 2023a). RSV genomic variability is higher than previously thought, particularly in the gene that codes for the external glycoprotein G. Amino acid substitutions in G may be involved in subtype alternance and in lineage replacement driven by immune selection (Pangesti et al. 2018). However, the extent to which host immune pressure shapes viral evolution over time remains unclear, as sequence variations of the G glycoprotein do not change much the main epitopes recognized by monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) (Trento et al. 2015). Accordingly, the relative contribution of other evolutionary forces to strain diversification and selection remain unclear (Trento et al. 2015). Recent RSV evolutionary patterns have been significantly influenced by the pandemic restrictions, which caused an unprecedented interruption in viral circulation for over a few years (Chow et al. 2022). Following this genetic bottleneck, the disappearance of prepandemic RSV strains, and the diffusion of emerging lineages within the RSV-A and -B subtypes have been reported (Redlberger-Fritz et al. 2023, Goya et al. 2023b, Pierangeli et al. 2024, Rios-Guzman et al. 2024, Rice et al. 2025). To facilitate comparison among studies and reflect variations in the whole genome, the classification of RSV lineages and strains has recently been unified (Goya et al. 2024), following the long-used classification based on the gene that codes for the external glycoprotein G (Pangesti et al. 2018). The other surface glycoprotein is named F for its fusogenic activity is more conserved as compared to G protein and contains the major antigenic sites for viral neutralization (McLellan et al. 2013b, McLellan 2015). According to these characteristics, the F glycoprotein emerged as the primary target for vaccine and mAb development (McLellan et al. 2013a). Recent advances in stabilizing the prefusion conformation of the F protein have enabled the development and approval of F-based vaccines for use in older adults and during pregnancy, as well as novel long-acting monoclonal antibodies (Terstappen et al. 2024). Among those recent RSV prophylactic options, nirsevimab which targets site Ø of the RSV prefusion conformation of the F protein (Hammitt et al. 2022), proved very effective in reducing RSV hospitalizations, since winter 2023/2024 in several countries (Moline et al. 2025, Sumsuzzman et al. 2025). In addition, clesrovimab, a long-acting mAb directed against antigenic site IV of the F glycoprotein, has been recently licensed in the USA for use in the same target population as nirsevimab (Zar et al. 2025).

The F sequence and protein conformation are relatively conserved. Between 2015 and 2021, a few variants containing substitutions in the nirsevimab binding site were found at a low prevalence (<1%) in RSV strains circulating worldwide (Wilkins et al. 2023). Among these substitutions, mutants with an amino acid substitution at position 68 of the F gene in both RSV subtypes exhibited reduced nirsevimab neutralization activity in vitro (Wilkins et al. 2023). During the first 2 years of nirsevimab administration, no major resistance-associated mutations were detected in the F gene of RSV-A clinical samples. However, the K68N substitution (Piñana et al. 2024) and other mutations in the nirsevimab binding sites were identified in RSV-B infections despite the infants having received nirsevimab prophylaxis (Fourati et al. 2025). Although RSV breakthrough infections were generally low during the initial years of prophylactic measures (Furgier et al. 2025, Mallah and Martinón-Torres 2025), ongoing molecular surveillance with a large, representative sample of hospitalized RSV cases is necessary to monitor the emergence of mutations associated with reduced efficacy of mAbs or vaccines. While most sequencing efforts have focused on studying RSV evolutionary dynamics and possible variations in the F gene at the population level, the analysis of overall RSV genome variations is far more limited compared to other respiratory viruses, such as influenza and SARS-CoV-2. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) approach with high base coverage enables the analysis of the extent of intrahost genetic variation. This analysis may reveal minority mutations that could be selected under the immune pressure posed by novel mAbs and F-based vaccines. A prospective study using WGS to analyse within-host RSV diversity in an immunodeficient infant before and after a bone marrow transplant showed a significantly higher rate of diversity in viral minority variants in the presence of restored immunity (Grad et al. 2014). Another study analysed deep-sequencing data from clinical samples collected from 2017 to 2020 and revealed a higher within-host diversity in RSV-B strains as compared to RSV-A (Lin et al. 2021). Furthermore, two RSV-B strains and one minor RSV-B variant carried possible resistance mutations to mAbs (Lin et al. 2021). However, no deep sequencing analysis examined minority variants in clinical samples from patients infected with RSV during a period of nirsevimab utilization. In Italy, the Ministry of Health issued a statement on 27 March 2024 regarding ‘Prevention and immunization measures against respiratory syncytial virus’, highlighting the authorization for the use of nirsevimab in newborns and young children during their first season of RSV (Misure di prevenzione e immunizzazione contro il virus respiratorio sinciziale (VRS), https://www.seremi.it/sites/default/files/Circolare%20RSV%2027.03.2024_signed.pdf). During October 2024–March 2025, a large-scale immunization campaign using nirsevimab was launched in Lombardy, Italy, a region of northern Italy with nearly 10 million inhabitants, for infants <12 months of age (Programma di immunizzazione contro il virus Respiratorio Sinciziale (VRS) in età pediatrica. Intesa Stato-Regioni 2024, https://www.statoregioni.it/it/conferenza-stato-regioni/sedute-2024/seduta-del-17-ottobre-2024/atti-17-ottobre-2024/repertorio-atto-n-188csr/) (Villa et al. 2026). An Italian study conducted during the 2024–5 season analysed the results of immunization campaigns with nirsevimab in the Lombardy region showing a decrease of 49.3% and 55.0% of emergency department (ED) visits and hospitalizations for RSV among infants, respectively (Villa et al. 2026). Similarly, an observational study conducted at a large hospital in Rome, revealed a 52.0% and 59% reduction in the number of RSV bronchiolitis ED visits and hospitalizations, respectively (La Regina et al. 2026). Previous studies have reported on the molecular epidemiology of circulating strains in Italy after the pandemic, with a focus on identifying mAb resistance mutations prior to the introduction of nirsevimab (Lai et al. 2025, Piralla et al. 2025). Thus, the present study aimed to extend surveillance of contemporary RSV isolates to the 2024–5 winter season, as well as evaluate minority variants in RSV-A and -B in clinical samples. Although this study was conducted during the first RSV season following the introduction of nirsevimab in northern and central Italy, none of the cases received prophylaxis. We then compared and discussed the extent of within-host variability in specific viral genes in light of viral evolution under the immune pressure exerted by immune prophylactic measures.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Paediatric patients presenting with acute respiratory infection (ARI) symptoms to the Paediatric Emergency Department (ED) of Policlinico Umberto I, Sapienza University Hospital, were tested for 23 respiratory pathogens using a syndromic assay (QIAstat-Dx® Respiratory SARS-CoV-2 Panel, Qiagen) at the Laboratory of Virology. Residual diagnostic samples from RSV positive cases (September 2024–February 2025) were subjected to typing by a real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) specific for RSV-A and RSV-B, as described (Pierangeli et al. 2024). Samples positive for RSV were collected in the same study period at Microbiology and Virology Department, Fondazione IRCCS Policlinico San Matteo, Pavia. Viral genome extraction was performed on the QIAsymphony platform using the ‘Virus Pathogens DSP Midi Kit’ (Qiagen, Heidelberg, Germany), starting from a volume of 400 μl with a final elution of 60 μl. Real-time RT-PCRs were performed to detect and quantify the viral load as previously described (Giardina et al. 2022, Pierangeli et al. 2024). Typing of RSV-positive samples was performed by real-time RT-PCR specific for RSV-A and RSV-B using AgPath-ID One-Step RT-PCR enzyme (Applied Biosystems) as previously reported (Pierangeli et al. 2024). The institutional review board and the Ethics Committee of Sapienza University Hospital approved the study (Prot. 107/12).

Whole genome sequencing

Residual diagnostic specimens with Cq values < 32 were randomly selected for WGS using our in-house amplicon-based protocol previously published (Ferrari et al. 2025). The SuperScript IV One-Step RT-PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was employed to amplify the entire RSV genome using three distinct RT-PCR reactions, starting from nucleic acid extracted with the EZ1&2™ Virus Mini Kit v2.0 (Qiagen). The library preparation was performed using Illumina DNA Prep (i.e. tagmentation protocol) with MiSeq Reagent Kit v2 (2 × 150) and run on MiSeq Platform. Read quality was assessed and filtered using fastp (v1.0.1) with a minimum base quality of Q20, removing low-quality bases and adapter sequences while retaining sufficient sequencing depth for downstream analyses (Chen et al. 2018). Cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genomes (MN163125.1 for RSV-A and KU950570.1 for RSV-B) using BWA (v0.7.19-r1273) using default parameters and minimum mapping quality ≥30 to retain uniquely mapped reads (Li and Durbin 2009). Specifically, 51 out of 88 samples (i.e. samples from Rome) were mapped to reference genomes containing the characteristic duplications in the G gene (72-nt duplication in RSV-A and 60-nt duplication in RSV-B; MN163125.1 and KU950570.1), while the remaining samples were aligned to earlier reference genomes lacking these duplications. The generated SAM files were converted to BAM and processed with samtools (v1.22) to filter unmapped reads, sort alignments, fix mate information, remove duplicates, and index the final BAM files (Li and Durbin 2009). Coverage depth was calculated using samtools depth, and per-region coverage was obtained with samtools coverage (Li et al. 2009). The consensus sequences were derived using samtools consensus (Li et al. 2009). A base was included in the consensus sequence when supported by ≥100 reads. At positions where 50%–100% of reads supported a single variant, that base was called; below 50%, International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) ambiguity codes (N, R, Y, W, S, K, etc.) were assigned.

Phylogenetic analysis

A dataset comprising RSV-A and RSV-B sequences, along with associated metadata, was assembled using strains available on the Nextstrain platform and deposited in the GISAID database (Elbe and Buckland-Merrett 2017, Hadfield et al. 2018). In detail, 200 sequences for RSV-A and B were selected from 2023 onwards, with at least 95% coverage of the consensus. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.525) (Katoh et al. 2002) and phylogenetic reconstruction was performed with IQ-TREE (v2.3.3) using the best-fit model selected by ModelFinder, with branch support assessed through ultrafast bootstrap (10 000 replicates) and Shimodaira–Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio tests (10 000 replicates) (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017, Hoang et al. 2018, Minh et al. 2020, Nayeem et al. 2023). The phylogenetic tree was visualized using the ggtree package (Xu et al. 2022) and in-house R script available upon request (R core team 2021).

Intrahost viral diversity

Nucleotide diversity (π) was defined as the probability that two randomly chosen reads differ at a given genomic position, averaged across the whole genome. To calculate π following the method of Zhao and Illingworth (Zhao and Illingworth 2019), we used the frequency and total read depth of each minority variant at every position within each RSV gene and sample. Only positions with sufficient coverage (≥100 reads) were included. Genome-wide π was calculated as the mean of πi across positions, and mean values were also computed per gene region. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were performed to assess statistically significant differences in genome-wide and per region π.

Variant calling

Variant calling was performed selecting only those samples in which breadth of coverage resulted ≥90% of the whole genome. The >90% genome coverage cutoff was selected to retain complete representation of the 15.2 kb RSV genome for strain characterization (Le et al. 2025). Sequences below this threshold frequently contain unsequenced regions in conserved loci due to secondary structure and homopolymer-prone regions. A dedicated step with samtools for the removal of technical PCR duplicates was included in the bioinformatic pipeline prior to variant calling (Li et al. 2009). The first step was to generate pileup files with samtools using a specific reference genome (Li et al. 2009). Variant detection was performed using iVar, applying a more stringent minimum base quality threshold of Q30 to minimize false-positive variant calls, particularly for low-frequency variants. Variants were required to have a minimum allele frequency of 2% and were restricted to the coding regions (CDS) of RSV genes (NS1, NS2, N, P, M, SH, G, F, M2, and L) (Grubaugh et al. 2019). A minimum variant allele frequency threshold of 2% was applied to reduce the inclusion of low-frequency sequencing artefacts, such as stochastic base-calling errors and homopolymer-associated errors reported for the Illumina MiSeq platform. The 1%–2% minimum allele frequency aligns with recommendations from deep viral sequencing literature (Lin et al. 2021, Roder et al. 2023).

Untranslated region (UTR) and intergenic regions were excluded from the analyses. The variants were annotated using snpEff tool and the gene annotation file (GFF3) downloaded from NCBI (ref NC_001803.1 for RSV-A and NC_001781.1 for RSV-B) (Cingolani et al. 2012). Variants were selected using the iVar P-value of Fisher’s exact test (≤.05) (i.e. Fisher’s exact test of variant frequency compared to mean error rate, P-value > .05), passing filter (TRUE), and the total depth at each position (≥100) (Lythgoe et al. 2021). The presence of ADAR-induced mutations was also evaluated using the final table of variants of iVar and filtering for A > G transition.

Results

In the season 2024–5, a total of 5652 respiratory samples (Rome: 455, Pavia: 5197) were processed for ARI. Of these, 295 (5.2% of the total) resulted positive for RSV (Rome: 86, Pavia: 209). The proportion of samples with Cq values < 32 was 215 (Rome: 75, Pavia: 140). A total of 88/215 samples (41.0%) underwent WGS (Rome: 51, Pavia: 37). All RSV-A (n = 54) and B (n = 34) samples were collected from paediatric patients (median age 1.4 range 1.05–3.57), and none of these children received prophylaxis with nirsevimab. Of the 88 RSV-positive samples, 79 (89.8%) achieved genome coverage of ≥90%. The remaining nine samples (10.2%) had lower coverage, ranging from 32% to 68%. Despite Ct values < 32, incomplete genome recovery in 10% of samples may be attributable to RNA degradation, suboptimal sample preservation, as reported by World Health Organization (World Health Organization (WHO). End-to-End Integration of SARS-CoV-2 and Influenza Sentinel Surveillance: Revised Interim Guidance, https://apps.who.int/iris/handle/10665/351409). The mean depth was 4 729× for RSV-A and 7 022× for RSV-B, the mean coverage 98.52% RSV-A and 99.65% RSV-B (Supplementary Fig. S1). Other information regarding sequencing quality metrics is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic trees of RSV strains circulating in Europe over the 2023–5 period are presented in Fig. 1. Among RSV-A strains, the lineages A.D.1 and A.D.3 were predominant among the study strains: A.D.3 accounted for 64.8% of the dataset (35/54), A.D.1 for 33.3% (18/54), and A.D.5 for one strain (Fig. 1A). This distribution was similar to those observed the previous season (2023–4) in Italy and previously described (Lai et al. 2025, Villa et al. 2026). In detail, lineages A.D.1 and A.D.5 circulate mainly in 2023, whereas A.D.3 appeared in 2024 and continued the spread in 2025. In addition, some A.D.1 strains are still present in 2025 (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were observed in terms of lineages between Pavia and Milan strains. Conversely, all 34 RSV-B strains were represented by lineage B.D.E, as well as all the other strains of the dataset (Fig. 1B). This phylogeny reflects the actual evolution of RSV-B where B.D.E lineage and all its sub-lineages now circulate predominantly compared to their progenitor (designed as B.D.4 lineage), which began circulating in the early 2000s and then gave way to B.D.E between 2013 and 2015 (Elbe and Buckland-Merrett 2017, Hadfield et al. 2018).

Intrahost viral diversity

To evaluate changes in diversity in RSV-A and -B populations, we applied the formula to estimate the nucleotide diversity (π) from Zhao and Illingworth (Zhao and Illingworth 2019). The choice of this metric is due to the fact that it facilitates a fair assessment and comparison of sequence diversity, especially when contrasting samples with distinct read depth profiles (Zhao and Illingworth 2019). At the genomic level (i.e. nucleotide diversity calculated per site), RSV-A lineage A.D.3 showed higher nucleotide diversity (π ≈ 0.0201) compared to A.D.1 (π ≈ 0.0089), while RSV-B lineage B.D.E displayed intermediate diversity (π ≈ 0.0098). Overall differences among groups were statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 2.2 × 10−16). Post hoc Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction indicated that B.D.E differed significantly from both A.D.1 and A.D.3 (P < .001), whereas the difference between A.D.1 and A.D.3 was not statistically significant (P = .057).

To assess lineage-specific differences in nucleotide diversity at the gene level, pairwise comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for each gene. Prior to analysis, extreme outliers were removed based on the interquartile range criterion [values exceeding 1.5 × interquartile range (IQR) from the first or third quartile]. Significant differences in nucleotide diversity (π) were observed for a subset of genes. In particular, the N gene showed significant differences between A.D.1 and A.D.3 (P = .000372) and between A.D.3 and B.D.E (P = 7.2 × 10−7), while no significant difference was detected between A.D.1 and B.D.E (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). For the G gene, a significant difference was observed between A.D.3 and B.D.E (P = .001). Additionally, significant differences between A.D.1 and A.D.3 were found in the SH gene (P = .026), whereas the M2 gene showed a significant difference between A.D.1 and B.D.E (P = .026) (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). No statistically significant differences were detected for the remaining genes (NS1, NS2, P, M, F, and L). Overall, these results indicate that differences in intrahost nucleotide diversity among RSV lineages are gene-specific rather than uniformly distributed across the genome.

Whole genome variant discovery

Of the 88 WGS obtained, 79 sequences had a breadth of coverage over 90% and were optimal candidates for variant discovery. The analysis was performed using iVar tool (Grubaugh et al. 2019) using the criteria described in the Materials and Methods section: 78 sequences had variants that met these criteria, 47 RSV-A, and 31 RSV-B. The variants were grouped based on the lineage of the strains (Fig. 3). For RSV-A, 17 strains belonged to A.D.1, 29 to A.D.3, and 1 to A.D.5. For RSV-B, 31 strains belonged to the B.D.E lineage.

The distribution and type of mutations detected across the RSV lineages more frequently detected, A.D.1, A.D.3, and B.D.E., are summarized in Fig. 3. The analysed variants were point mutations classified according to three categories: missense, nonsense, and silent. Overall, the highest total number of nucleotide substitutions was detected in the lineages A.D.3 and B.D.E. (7667 and 7293 mutations, respectively), followed by A.D.1 (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table S3). Silent mutations represented the majority of substitutions, followed by missense mutations, whereas nonsense mutations were relatively rare in all groups. The difference in the total number of substitutions reflects the uneven number of sequences available for each lineage (29 for A.D.3, 17 for A.D.1, 31 for B.D.E.), rather than intrinsic genetic variability. Thus, we normalized the count for the number of strains of each lineage to compare the average mutation density per sample between lineages. Looking at the normalized count (i.e. mutation counts normalized by the number of strains within each lineage) The A.D.1 lineage showed the highest normalized number of mutations and also the highest relative number of missense mutations (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Table S4). When mutations were mapped along the viral coding sequences, their distribution was not uniform among lineages (Fig. 3C). When considering the median number of variants per gene and the corresponding IQR, the L, G, and F genes consistently showed the highest variability across all lineages (Fig. 3C). Despite the presence of a certain number of missense substitutions in the F gene, no mutations associated with amino acids linked with nirsevimab were identified. In contrast, SH, NS1, and NS2 genes exhibited limited variability. Overall, the IQRs observed for lineages A.D.1 and B.D.E indicate substantial intragenotypic variability, whereas A.D.3 displayed a more homogeneous distribution of variants across genes (Fig. 3C). The high number of mutational events in the L, G, and F genes as compared to the SH, NS1, and NS2 genes is biased by the increased length of those genes and the disproportionate number of strains for each lineage (Fig. 3D). Therefore, a normalization has been performed considering mutation counts normalized by gene length (i.e. number of mutations per gene length) as illustrated in Fig. 3D. As concerns the G gene, in all strains, it appears to have a higher median number of mutational events relative to its length. Furthermore, missense mutations outnumbered silent mutations in the G gene of all three RSV lineages, while the opposite trend was observed in all other genes (Fig. 3D).

In order to evaluate the variants trend among the strains, we analysed the number of per-sample variants (Fig. 3E and F). The lineages showed a heterogeneous pattern, with the number of variants per genome ranging widely among samples. Lineages A.D.1 and A.D.3 exhibited a higher median number of variants (median = 210) compared to lineage B.D.E (median = 118), supporting the greater intragenotypic diversity within the RSV-A lineages (Fig. 3E and F). We also investigated whether the observed outlier samples with elevated mutation counts could be explained by differences in sequencing depth, intrahost diversity, or technical factors. In type A samples, outliers exhibited lower mean sequencing coverage compared to nonoutliers (2 917× versus 6 096×), suggesting that increased mutation counts were not driven by higher read depth. Consistently, nucleotide diversity (π) was comparable between groups (0.000583 versus 0.000607), indicating no evidence of increased intrahost variability. In contrast, type B outliers showed slightly higher coverage (8508 versus 6342) and increased nucleotide diversity (0.000864 versus 0.000524), suggesting a potential contribution of intrahost heterogeneity. Notably, all outlier samples from both groups were sequenced at a single center (Pavia), whereas nonoutlier samples originated from multiple sequencing sites. This pattern is consistent with a potential batch effect linked to library preparation and/or amplicon sequencing protocols. Overall, these observations suggest that elevated mutation counts in type A outliers are most likely attributable to technical artefacts, while type B outliers may reflect a combination of increased biological diversity and site-specific technical effects.

Minority variant assessment

To explore low-frequency substitutions, the number of mutations with frequency between 2% and 5% were further investigated (Fig. 4). Minority variant stratification into frequency bins (i.e. 2%–5%) was guided by published literature on RSV population genetics and quasispecies structure (Lin et al. 2021, Roder et al. 2023). Lower-frequency variants (2%–5%) may indicate early fixation events or linked mutations under positive selection. Variants at higher frequencies (≥5%) suggest either fixation in progress or evidence of co-infecting viral subpopulations, warranting closer epidemiological investigation. Although silent mutations were also detected in these regions, the minority variant landscape showed a predominance of missense mutations in B.D.E and A.D.3 lineages. Minority variants analyses revealed a gene-specific distribution pattern similar to the other nucleotide variants, with L and G as the most variable genes across all lineages. Of note, nonsense mutations were sporadic and gene-restricted (Fig. 4).

Minority variants classification divided per lineage across RSV genes. A minority variant is defined with a frequency between 0.02 and 0.05.

Comprehensive analysis of missense and nonsense mutations across the complete genomes of RSV-A and RSV-B lineages revealed marked differences in the distribution and frequency of amino acid substitutions among viral genes (Fig. 5). Overall, minor variants with frequency below 5% (Fig. 5, red dots) were more widely represented throughout most coding regions, whereas major variants (Fig. 5, green dots) tended to cluster at specific genomic loci. Notably, some of these clusters represent mutational hotspots that are consistently observed across multiple lineages, particularly within the G protein, suggesting recurrent or convergent evolutionary patterns (Fig. 5, green dots). In contrast, other regions, such as specific positions within the F protein, showed a more heterogeneous distribution of variants, with limited overlap across lineages, indicating lineage-specific evolutionary trajectories. To assess whether mutations observed at ~50% frequency co-occur within the same viral genomes, we reconstructed mutation profiles at the individual patient level. This analysis showed that these variants do not consistently occur in tandem but rather appear in distinct and nonrecurrent combinations across samples, with no shared mutation pattern among multiple sequences.

Detection and frequency analysis of transitions and transversions

The family of human ADAR proteins has been shown to induce A > G mutations in a series of viruses, including RSV (Rueda et al. 1994, Martínez et al. 1997, Samuel 2012). Thus, we investigated whether signs of hyper-editing could be identified in different lineages by analysing all possible transition and transversion events. First, we detected the distribution of the different types (transition and transversion) of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) among the different lineages in terms of the number of patients presenting them (Supplementary Table S5). Of the 78 RSV samples analysed, all exhibited evidence of ADAR-mediated mutations (A > G) across the whole genome of the different lineages, 1039 for A.D.1, 1108 for B.D.E, and 1263 for A.D.3 (Supplementary Fig. S2A). To assess that the A > G substitutions were not a consequence of sequencing errors, the mean quality of the alternate bases was checked and resulted in the range 36.8–37.6, which corresponds to an expected error rate of ~1 in 4000 or 1 in 5000 bases (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

We also looked for the frequency of the alternate variant A > G in the G gene compared with the other coding regions (Supplementary Fig. S2B). Regarding RSV-A, A.D.1 and A.D.3 exhibited the presence of A > G among the major variants with frequencies of 0.96 and 0.95, respectively. B.D.E had a lower frequency (0.93) of A > G in the G gene than the other lineages. No significant differences were observed in the presence of G-related ADAR-mediated mutations with respect to other RSV genes.

Discussion

It is notable that certain RNA viruses exhibit remarkably elevated mutation rates, while others demonstrate a remarkable degree of stability, despite the absence of proofreading activity in their replicative enzymes. It is evident that persistent viral infections permit the accumulation of genomic substitutions within the infected hosts. This phenomenon has been extensively studied in the context of the most prevalent examples of hepatitis C virus and human immunodeficiency virus (Domingo et al. 2019, Gelbart et al. 2020). The application of WGS has now been extended to the field of virology, enabling researchers to gain valuable insights into the evolution of virus populations, even in clinical samples from acute infections. This approach has been successfully implemented to define the dynamics of influenza virus in infected patients, contributing to our understanding of viral pathogenesis and the development of effective therapeutic interventions (McCrone et al. 2018). However, the mechanisms by which RSV genomes mutate and differentiate during individual infections remain to be elucidated (Grad et al. 2014, Lin et al. 2021).

The present study aims to provide proof of concept for the investigation of the RSV viral diversity through WGS, at both the population level and within the individual infected hosts. While the majority of studies have hitherto concentrated on the variability of the F and G genes, this study has explored the entire viral genome, including genes that are not typically subject to strong selective pressure. An amplicon-based approach was applied with the objective of deriving the whole genome of circulating RSV in Italy during the 2024–5 season (Ferrari et al. 2025). The limitations of the amplicon-based approach are that it can introduce coverage bias across genomic regions and preferential amplification of abundant templates in mixed populations. Lineages A.D.1 and A.D.3 were predominant among RSV-A strains, and the B.D.E. lineage was the only RSV-B strain identified in the study. Phylogenetic analysis revealed that lineages A.D.1 and A.D.5 were present across Europe in 2023 but were no longer observed during 2024. Meanwhile, lineage A.D.3 emerged in 2024 and persisted in 2025, becoming the predominant lineage in the study group. Some Italian strains belonging to A.D.1 are still present in 2025, suggesting the simultaneous circulation of two RSV-A lineages during the 2024–5 RSV epidemic season. In contrast, the phylogeny of RSV-B illustrates the present circulation of a primary lineage, namely, the B.D.E. lineage, which continues to be dominated by its sub-lineages in 2025 (see Fig. 1B). These data extend findings from Italy (Pierangeli et al. 2024, Lai et al. 2025, Piralla et al. 2025, Villa et al. 2026) and from other European countries (Redlberger-Fritz et al. 2023, relative to the 2023–4 season, which also described a similar prevalence of these RSV lineages. Studies available for the 2024–5 season reported the dominance of locally evolved clades of lineage A.D.1 in Germany (Le et al. 2025) and in the USA (Zhuang et al. 2025), which co-circulated with lineage A.D.3. WGS of RSV-B strains collected during the postpandemic period, revealed that B.D.E. was the only circulating RSV-B lineage (Redlberger-Fritz et al. 2023, Goya et al. 2023b, Piñana et al. 2024, Le et al. 2025, Zhuang et al. 2025). In contrast, multiple RSV-B genetic lineages circulated in the prepandemic period (Elbe and Buckland-Merrett 2017, Hadfield et al. 2018).

Comparison of the intrahost viral diversity at genome level in the study samples, revealed that RSV-A lineage A.D.3 and A.D.1 have greater diversity than RSV-B lineage B.D.E (Kruskal–Wallis test, P < .001), particularly as expected in the G and N genomic regions (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S2). Taken together, these findings indicate that the A.D.3 lineage tends to exhibit higher intrahost nucleotide diversity and may represent a major source of variability within the dataset. These findings suggest that differences in intrahost nucleotide diversity among RSV lineages are gene-specific rather than uniformly distributed across the genome.

These findings are consistent with data from the prepandemic period and may reflect differences in evolution, selection, or viral dynamics between the two subgroups (Lin et al. 2021). It is hypothesized that greater intrahost diversity may lead to viral population shifts within the infected host. This phenomenon may provide a rationale for the greater variety of RSV-A strains in circulation compared with RSV-B, as is commonly reported in the literature (Langedijk et al. 2024, Franco et al. 2025, Rice et al. 2025, Zhuang et al. 2025). The mutational landscape varied considerably among RSV-A lineages. Indeed, the A.D.1 lineage displayed the highest average number of mutations, while the A.D.3 lineage had a mutational burden similar to the RSV-B lineage B.D.E (Fig. 3A). However, the normalized mutation count showed that A.D.1 and A.D.3 are actually more similar to B.D.E, confirming intrahost viral diversity (Fig. 3B). Across lineages, the G gene consistently harboured the highest density of missense mutations, confirming its role as a mutational hotspot. This observation aligns with the structural flexibility of the G protein and its relevance in antigenic diversification, and immune evasion (Trento et al. 2015, Pangesti et al. 2018, Goya et al. 2023a. A relatively high number of substitutions was also found in the L gene, consistent with recent studies that identified L mutations as the second most frequent after those in the G gene (Goya et al. 2023a, Le et al. 2025, Zhuang et al. 2025). Nevertheless, mutations in specific domains may impact viral mRNA transcription and genome replication (Franco et al. 2025). Conversely, SH, NS1, and NS2 genes were relatively conserved, reflecting their essential roles and structural constraints. Interestingly, the missense mutations were relatively more represented among minority variants. In particular, the percentage of missense mutations with respect to the total substitution is even higher in the L gene. As the viral polymerase may have more functional constraints than the external glycoproteins, the minority variants with missense mutations in L could be less fit than other strains. When looking at the complete genome mutational landscape, recurrent high-frequency mutations were observed within the G and L genes, suggesting that these proteins are subject to stronger selective pressures, possibly related to immune evasion mechanisms and functional constraints associated with viral replication. In contrast, internal and structural proteins such as N, P, and M appeared to be more conserved, displaying a limited number of amino acid changes, most of which remained at low frequency. This heterogeneous distribution of missense and nonsense mutations could reflect the distinct evolutionary pressures acting on different viral proteins, with surface-exposed glycoproteins undergoing more intense adaptive evolution, while internal components remain constrained by structural and functional requirements.

Signs of ADAR editing (i.e. an increased frequency of A-to-G transitions) were previously described on RSV genomes (Rueda et al. 1994, Martínez et al. 1997, Samuel 2012). This resulted in the diversification of epitopes mainly into the G glycoprotein leading to antigenic escape or, on the other hand, to loss of function in viral proteins (Rueda et al. 1994, Martínez et al. 1997). Analysing major and minor nucleotide variants, we could not find evidence of enhanced levels of the ADAR mediated A-to-G transitions among the RSV study strains. Further studies are needed to clarify whether ADAR-mediated hypermutation in specific RSV genes may have a role as an RSV evolutionary force.

In conclusion, our data identified the RSV strains circulating during 2024–5, immediately before and after the nirsevimab introduction in Italy. Collectively, these findings highlight the dynamic nature of RSV genomic evolution, which appears to be different among lineages. Indeed, the RSV genetic diversity is shaped by both lineage- and gene-specific dynamics, suggesting differential selective pressures acting along the viral genome. In this regard, the ongoing and future preventive measures may exert selective pressure that favours the emergence of variants capable of impacting the efficacy of monoclonal antibodies and vaccines. Thus, this study emphasizes the importance of monitoring RSV variability across major and minor variants to better understand the mechanisms of viral adaptation and prevent their effect on treatment efficacy. A key limitation of this study is the absence of pre-nirsevimab genomic data and the lack of prophylaxis history among sequenced cases. As a result, it is not possible to assess whether the introduction of nirsevimab has influenced the genetic diversity of circulating RSV strains, nor to identify putative resistance-associated substitutions in the context of prophylaxis failure. The study should therefore be interpreted as a descriptive genomic survey conducted in the early post-introduction period, providing baseline data that could inform future longitudinal studies comparing RSV diversity before and after widespread nirsevimab use.
