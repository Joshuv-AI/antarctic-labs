Mechanism of enhanced crystallization of stannous sulfate by ultrasound-negative pressure synergy
Authors: Dongbin Wang, Congrong Gu, Tian Wang, Thiquynhxuan Le, Libo Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtmosphere evaporation crystallization of stannous sulfate (SnSO4) is constrained by low efficiency. Although negative-pressure evaporation accelerates solvent removal, the resulting abrupt increase in supersaturation often triggers uncontrolled explosive nucleation, forming irregular agglomerates with broad particle size distributions. To address this issue, this study proposes a novel synergistic ultrasonic-negative pressure crystallization strategy. Under optimized conditions (80 °C, 40 kHz, 107 W, 0.03 MPa, 150 rpm), the synergistic process achieved an exceptional 88.6 %±1.6 % efficiency and produced rod-shaped crystals with a uniform unimodal particle size distribution (D50 = 38.42 ± 0.27 μm). In-situ high-speed visualization revealed a unique transformation in bubble behavior under the synergistic field, negative pressure reduced the cavitation threshold, generating two distinct bubble populations—transient bubbles enhanced by negative pressure (boosting volumetric mass transfer) and stable oscillating bubbles (serving as heterogeneous nucleation sites). This study elucidates the effects of negative pressure coupled with ultrasonic on solution cavitation behavior and crystal growth, providing a theoretical foundation for controlled crystallization under negative-pressure and ultrasonic synergy.Keywords: Ultrasound crystallization, Nucleation, Negative-pressure crystallization, Ultrasound-negative pressure synergy

Introduction

Stannous sulfate (SnSO4) is an industrially important inorganic salt with applications spanning electroplating, organic synthesis, and the new energy sector [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. Although evaporative crystallization is a core unit operation in chemical separation, the crystallization of SnSO4 presents significant challenges. Conventional atmospheric evaporation crystallization without ultrasound (CA) suffers from low efficiency and prolonged exposure to heat, leading to SnSO4 oxidation and high energy consumption [6]. Although negative-pressure evaporation crystallization without ultrasound (CN) effectively accelerates solvent removal, the rapid removal of solvent causes a sudden surge in supersaturation, triggering explosive nucleation. This process generates fine grain and severe agglomeration, resulting in poor crystal habit and broad particle size distributions that fail to meet high-quality standards [5], [7], [8], [9].

To overcome these limitations, power ultrasound has emerged as a promising intensification technique. The mechanism is primarily attributed to acoustic cavitation, which involves the formation and subsequent implosive collapse of microbubbles. This process generates localized, transient extremes of temperature and pressure, accompanied by intense micro-jets and shockwaves. These phenomena collectively contribute to a marked reduction in the nucleation energy barrier, thereby promoting nucleation [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Previous studies have corroborated these benefits; for instance, Xu et al. [15] observed that under ultrasound, the crystallization yield of sodium sulfate increased from 62 % to 95 %, representing a significant improvement. Similarly, In the crystallization of L‑glutamic acid, ultrasonic treatment reduced the induction time from 30 min to 8 min and narrowed the metastable zone width (MSZW) from 5.2 K to 2.1 K [16]. However, excessive ultrasonication can induce severe crystal fragmentation. The violent cavitation effect generates intense shock waves, leading to crystal fracture and the formation of fine crystal debris [17]. Recently, the influence of ambient pressure on acoustic cavitation dynamics has emerged as an important frontier in sonochemistry [18]. Under ultrasound–negative pressure coupling, external pressure can influence acoustic cavitation by regulating the cavitation threshold, bubble dynamics, and sonochemical activity. Previous studies have demonstrated that ultrasound–negative pressure coupling can enhance mass transfer and improve recovery efficiency in natural product extraction, and can reduce precipitate particle size during fractional precipitation [19], [20]. These findings indicate that the coupled field can modify transport and phase-separation behavior in liquid systems. Nevertheless, its application to inorganic evaporative crystallization, particularly SnSO4 crystallization, remains much less explored. Since external pressure is a key parameter controlling cavitation threshold and bubble dynamics, it may strongly affect ultrasound-assisted crystallization. Merouani et al. reviewed the effects of external pressure on sonochemistry and emphasized that external pressure can regulate cavitation threshold, bubble oscillation, collapse intensity, and sonochemical activity [18], [21]. Smirnov et al. further showed that reduced ambient pressure promotes larger bubble expansion before collapse while weakening shock-wave intensity and microjet velocity [22], [23]. These findings suggest that negative pressure may tune ultrasonic cavitation from violent collapse toward a more moderate bubble-mediated nucleation environment.

According to cavitation dynamics theory, the ambient static pressure (P∞) is a fundamental controlling variable for bubble oscillation and collapse. Reducing the static pressure not only lowers the cavitation threshold and promotes the formation of an expanded cavitation zone with larger and more numerous bubbles, but also reduces the driving pressure difference during bubble collapse. This suggests that appropriately adjusting the negative pressure could potentially mitigate the adverse mechanical effects of ultrasound, such as crystal fragmentation caused by violent shock waves, while still preserving its beneficial effects on mass transfer and nucleation control. This uniquely modulated ultrasound–negative pressure (UN) synergy thus holds promise for enhancing mass transfer, avoiding explosive nucleation, and providing a stable environment to prevent crystal fracture, ultimately improving the quality of the crystalline product.

However, the application of coupled ultrasound–negative pressure crystallization to SnSO4 remains insufficiently understood, particularly with respect to how pressure-regulated cavitation behavior affects crystallization performance. In this study, we established an UN crystallization system for SnSO4, and systematically investigated the effects of parameters on crystallization yield, including ultrasonic power, crystallization temperature, and negative pressure level. Besides of process optimization, this study investigated the influence of combined ultrasound and negative pressure on the metastable zone width (MSZW) and elucidated its specific effects on nucleation kinetics. Furthermore, high-speed photography was employed to directly visualize the evolution of cavitation bubbles under UN. This work elucidates the mechanism of how expanded cavitation dynamics under negative pressure cooperatively enhance crystallization, providing a theoretical foundation for high-quality crystallization control.

Materials and methods

Materials

Stannous sulfate (SnSO4, analytical grade) was purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The ultrasonic device was custom-developed by the Key Laboratory of Unconventional Metallurgy, Ministry of Education, Kunming University of Science and Technology. The equipment featured a bottom-mounted ultrasonic emitter, to ensure data accuracy, all ultrasonic power values reported in this study represent the acoustic power measured calorimetrically, rather than the nominal electrical power. The measurement method is detailed in the SI text 1.

Experimental procedure

Solution configuration and crystallization process

A saturated SnSO4 solution was prepared at 20 °C for the experimental study on the optimization of the UN crystallization process. The solution was then transferred to a 200 mL three-necked flask and subjected to evaporative crystallization in an ultrasonic reactor, the total volume of the crystallization solution was 100 ml. A stirring paddle was inserted flask while a negative-pressure condenser was connected to maintain the vacuum degree within the system. The ultrasound was activated to initiate the ultrasonic negative-pressure evaporation crystallization. The process was terminated once the crystallization time reached the expected reaction duration. Subsequently, solid–liquid separation was performed. The obtained crystals were washed with anhydrous ethanol and dried at 80 °C for 4 h to yield the final product. The calculated SnSO4 yield and crystallization efficiency were determined by weighing. Single-factor experiments were conducted to investigate the effects of crystallization temperature, stirring speed during crystallization, ultrasonic power, crystallization time, and negative pressure level on SnSO4 yield and crystallization efficiency. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and corresponding error bars are included in figures.

MSZW testing and nucleation kinetics experiments

To investigate the effects of CA process, CN, Ultrasonic at atmospheric (UA) process and UN process on the metastable zone width (MSZW), the saturated solubility of SnSO4 was first determined at 50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C. Suspensions containing excess SnSO4 were stirred and heated for 4 h at the predetermined temperatures to ensure dissolution equilibrium. The solutions were maintained under sealed conditions to prevent the oxidation of stannous ions. The supernatant was then filtered, evaporated to dryness, and the mass of solid SnSO4 was measured. Based on these data, the solubility of SnSO4 at each temperature was calculated, and solubility-temperature curves were fitted. Subsequently, isothermal evaporation crystallization experiments were conducted using saturated SnSO4 solutions under CA, CN, UA and UN conditions. The concentration of SnSO4 measured at the onset of crystal precipitation was defined as the supersolubility limit at that temperature. Based on experimental data, supersolubility curves were fitted for the four experimental to evaluate the effects of the intensification fields on the MSZW of SnSO4 crystallization and its critical supersaturation. Detailed descriptions of the experimental setup, isothermal control strategy, and the method for maintaining a stable evaporation rate are provided in the Supporting Information. The schematic diagram of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. S1.

To investigate the effects of synergistic ultrasound-negative pressure, high-speed photography was employed to capture the evolution of ultrasonic cavitation bubbles under both atmospheric pressure and negative-pressure conditions, thereby examining the influence of negative pressure on cavitation bubbles. Under negative pressure of 0.04 MPa and atmospheric pressure, an external ultrasonic field was applied to the solution, and the dynamics of the cavitation bubbles were observed and their effects were analyzed.

Characterization analysis

The surface morphology of the SnSO4 crystals was analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS Sigma 300, Germany). An inverted fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Axio Vert. A1, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) was employed to analyze the growth process of the SnSO4 crystals. The crystal phase and crystallinity were characterized via X-ray diffractometer (Agilent, Dlmax2200, USA), the XRD data were analyzed using Jade software. Particle size distribution under varying crystallization conditions was determined using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer (Malvern Mastersizer 2000,UK). A high-speed camera (nac ACS-1 M60, Japan) was used to visualize the evolution of bubble behavior in the solution. High-speed video recordings were first converted into sequential images using the proprietary software of the high-speed camera. All image processing and particle analysis were performed with ImageJ (Fiji distribution, National Institutes of Health, USA).

Results and discussion

Effect of ultrasound on the evaporating crystallization of SnSO4 under negative pressure

Synergy of ultrasound and negative pressure

To determine the efficacy of external field intensification on SnSO4 crystallization, a comparative investigation was conducted under four distinct process environments: Conventional Atmospheric without ultrasonic (CA), Conventional Negative pressure without ultrasonic (CN), Ultrasonic at Atmospheric (UA), and Synergistic Ultrasonic-Negative Pressure (UN).

As shown in Fig. 1(a), the crystallization yield and crystallization efficiency across the four experimental regimes. The CA process exhibited the lowest performance, with a yield of only 2.21 ± 0.12 g. This inefficiency is attributed to the slow natural evaporation efficiency and the broad metastable zone of SnSO4. Implementing the CN process increased the yield to 4.43 ± 0.13 g, primarily by accelerating solvent evaporation. The adoption of the UA process resulted in an increase in yield to 5.03 ± 0.16, primarily due to the mass transfer enhancement induced by ultrasonic cavitation. Notably, the UN process significantly improved production efficiency, achieving a yield of 9.08 ± 0.18 g. This represents a 4.1-fold increase compared to the CA process. Crucially, this value exceeds the simple sum of the individual enhancements, demonstrating a robust positive synergistic effect.

(a) Comparative evaluation of SnSO4 crystallization yield under different intensification strategies; Effects of process parameters on yield and crystallization efficiency: (b) crystallization temperature, (c) stirring speed, (d) actual ultrasonic power, (e) crystallization time, (f) negative pressure and (g) ultrasonic frequency.

Effect of crystallization temperature

Since solubility of SnSO4 decreases with increasing temperature, the crystallization temperature exerts a substantial influence on the yield of crystals. The effect of temperature (50, 60, 70, 80, and 90 °C) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 was investigated under conditions of a 30 min crystallization time, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, 107 W actual ultrasonic power, 150 r/min stirring speed, and a negative pressure of 0.03 MPa.

As shown in Fig. 1(b), in the range of 50–80 °C, increasing the temperature significantly promoted crystal production. Under CN conditions, the yield surged from 0.91 ± 0.09 to 11.97 ± 0.52 g. Under UN conditions, the yield further increased from 3.97 ± 0.28 to 13.39 ± 0.38 g. This substantial enhancement is attributed to the fact that SnSO4 exhibits retrograde solubility, the solubility of SnSO4 decreases as temperature rises. According to classical nucleation theory, increasing the temperature directly increases the supersaturation ratio (S= C/C0, where the equilibrium concentration C0 decreases), thereby acting as the primary thermodynamic driving force for nucleation and growth. Furthermore, elevated temperatures exponentially increase the vapor pressure of the solvent. Under CN conditions, this leads to rapid solvent removal, maintaining a high level of supersaturation to continuous crystallization of SnSO4.

However, when the temperatures was elevated from 80 to 90 °C, the increase in crystallization efficiency slowed significantly. The crystallization efficiency improved by only 4.1 %, rising from 44.6 %±1.3 % to 48.7 %±1.5 % [6]. Comparative studies reveal that under identical temperatures, the crystallization efficiency under UN process consistently outperforms the CN process. This enhancement stems from the intensification of mass transfer. The collapse of microbubbles generates shockwaves that disrupt the stagnant hydrodynamic layer around crystal surfaces, reducing mass transfer resistance and accelerating solute diffusion. Meanwhile, the intense vibration and micro-turbulence homogenize the supersaturation field [24], [25].

Effect of stirring speed

Stirring speed significantly influences the crystallization process of SnSO4 by governing the interplay between solute mass transfer and crystal integrity. The effects of stirring speed (120, 150, 180, 210, and 240 r/min) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 were investigated under the conditions of 80 °C temperature, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, 107 W actual ultrasonic power, 0.03 MPa negative pressure, and a 60 min crystallization time. The results are shown in Fig. 1(c).

Regardless of the presence of ultrasound, both crystal yield and crystallization efficiency initially increased and then decreased with rising stirring speed. When the stirring speed increased from 120 to 150 r/min, the crystallization yield under UN conditions rose from 22.96 ± 0.24 g to 26.58 ± 0.47 g, while the crystallization efficiency increased from 76.5 %±0.8 % to 88.6 %±1.6 %. Under CN conditions, crystallization yield increased from 21.14 ± 0.21 g to 25.83 ± 0.19 g, and the crystallization efficiency rose from 70.5 %±0.7 % to 86.1 %±0.6 %. Moderate stirring speeds enhance crystallization efficiency [26]. This enhancement is attributed to the reduction of the hydrodynamic boundary layer thickness, a thinner boundary layer increases the mass transfer coefficient(k), thereby accelerating the diffusion of Sn2+ and SO42- ions from the bulk solution to the crystal lattice. In addition, an appropriate stirring speed can ensure the uniform distribution of crystal nuclei, prevent crystal sedimentation, and facilitate the uniform growth of crystals.

However, when the stirring speed exceeded 150 r/min, both yield and crystal quality exhibited a decreasing trend. When the stirring speed reached 240 r/min, the yield under synergistic conditions decreased to 21.28 ± 0.22 g, and the crystallization efficiency decreased to 72.83 %±0.7 %. The decline in crystallization yield at mechanical stirring speeds exceeding 150 rpm can be attributed to the interference of mechanical agitation with the ultrasonic cavitation field. Liu et al. demonstrated that at elevated stirring speeds, intensified macroscopic turbulence alters cavitation bubble dynamics, and that too high a stirring speed can reduce the acoustic cavitation intensity [27]. Furthermore, Aghelmaleki et al. [28] showed that stirring-induced rapid bulk liquid flow alters bubble population distributions through enhanced gas diffusion and bubble separation or de-clustering effects, particularly affecting populations of smaller and larger bubbles. The resulting excessive bubble coalescence under turbulent conditions decreases the number density of active cavitation nuclei and increases the bubble size, thereby governing the acoustic bubble population. The larger but fewer bubbles scatter and attenuate the incident ultrasound, weakening the beneficial effects of cavitation on nucleation and mass transfer and leading to the observed yield reduction. Therefore, a stirring speed of 150 rpm was identified as the optimum, balancing the enhancement of mass transfer against the detriment of crystal breakage.

Effect of ultrasound power

The ultrasonic power exerted influences the processes of crystal nucleation and growth to a significant degree. The effects of actual ultrasonic power (48, 68, 88, 107, and 121 W) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 were investigated under the conditions of 80 °C temperature, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, 150 r/min stirring speed, 0.03 MPa negative pressure, and 60 min crystallization time. The results are shown in Fig. 1(d).

As actual ultrasonic power increased from 48 to 107 W, the crystallization yield of SnSO4 rose from 19.11 ± 0.46 g to 26.58 ± 0.47 g. At lower ultrasonic power levels, both crystallization yield and efficiency increased with higher power. However, when ultrasonic power was further increased to 180 W, the crystallization yield decreased to 22.12 ± 0.44 g, and the efficiency dropped to 73.7 %±1.4 %. The initial increase in yield is attributed to the enhancement of ultrasonic cavitation. Higher power input generates a greater number of denser cavitation bubbles. The collapse of a large number of cavitation bubbles produces stronger microjets and shock waves, thereby enhancing mass transfer. The acoustic streaming effectively thins the diffusion boundary layer around the crystal surface, accelerating the transport of solute ions to the crystal interface. Additionally, it promotes the secondary nucleation process, thus improving the overall crystal quality [29], [30].

However, excessively increasing the power to 121 W resulted in a decline in yield to 22.12 ± 0.44 g. Excessive ultrasonic power produces stronger impact effects, the changes in crystal particle size under different powers are shown in Fig.S2. Although breaking crystals is beneficial for promoting secondary nucleation, excessive crystal breakage can produce a large number of small grains with higher solubility (Gibbs-Thomson effect), causing the already crystallized product to undergo re-dissolution, ultimately resulting in yield loss [17], [31], [32], [33]. Therefore, 107 W was identified as the optimized ultrasonic power setting.

Effect of crystallization time

The effects of crystallization time (20, 30, 40, 50, 60 min) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 were investigated under the conditions of 80 °C temperature, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, 107 W ultrasonic power, 150 r/min stirring speed, and 0.03 MPa negative pressure. The results are shown in Fig. 1(e).

Both the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 continuously increased with extended crystallization time under both CN and UN conditions. However, the UN strategy demonstrated more pronounced crystallization advantages, particularly in the initial stage. At 20 min, the crystallization yield under synergistic conditions reached 10.58 ± 0.51 g, markedly higher than the 8.64 ± 0.39 g obtained under CN. This indicates that ultrasonic cavitation effectively shortens the crystallization induction time, facilitating the overcoming of the nucleation energy barrier and achieving a higher crystal yield in the same duration. At 60 min, the UN process achieved a peak yield of 26.58 ± 0.47 g, maintaining its superiority over the CN process with yield of 25.83 ± 0.52 g. Extended experiments (70 min) revealed that the yield reached a plateau with no significant further increase, Consequently, 60 min was identified as the optimal duration.

Effect of crystallization negative pressure vacuum degree

The negative pressure level could regulate both the solvent evaporation rate and the ultrasonic cavitation threshold. The effects of negative pressure of (0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.03, 0.04 MPa) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 were investigated under the conditions of 80 °C temperature, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, 107 W actual ultrasonic power, and 150 r/min stirring speed, with a 30 min crystallization time. The results are shown in Fig. 1(f).

As the negative pressure increased from 0 to 0.03 MPa, the crystallization yield of SnSO4 rose rapidly from 10.11 ± 0.44 to 26.58 ± 0.47 g, with the crystallization efficiency increasing from 33.7 %±1.5 % to 88.6 %±1.6 %. This enhancement is governed by the thermodynamics of evaporation, the static pressure decreases reduce the boiling point of the solvent, significantly accelerating the evaporation rate. This rapid solvent removal maintains a high level of supersaturation, which provides the persistent thermodynamic driving force required for continuous crystal precipitation. Furthermore, the cavitation threshold decreases with the static pressure decreases, allowing ultrasound to generate a larger number of active bubbles, thereby enhancing mass transfer. Despite the weakened intensity of microjets and shock waves, a higher crystallization yield is achieved under negative pressure. This is because the reduced ambient pressure shifts the bubble population toward stable, non-inertial oscillation. Although the peak collapse intensity is moderated, the extended bubble lifetimes and enlarged sizes provide a larger gas–liquid interfacial area for heterogeneous nucleation [34], while the milder mechanical action reduces the over-fragmentation of primary crystals. Consequently, the synergistic field amplifies the stable cavitation effects and mitigates the yield-limiting destructive effects of violent microjets.

However, further increasing negative pressure to 0.04 MPa reduced the crystal yield to 23.42 ± 0.71 g. Excessively low ambient pressure drastically lowers the cavitation threshold, leading to the generation of dense, long-lived bubbles within the solution. These bubbles hinder the propagation of ultrasonic waves and cause severe acoustic attenuation, which diminishes the intensification efficacy of ultrasound [18], [22], [35], [36]. Furthermore, although reduced static pressure theoretically facilitates bubble expansion to a larger maximum radius (Rmax), an excessive reduction in ambient pressure decreases the pressure differential (ΔP) driving the bubble collapse. Consequently, the intensity of the resulting micro-jets and shock waves is significantly weakened, rendering them insufficient to effectively promote crystal nucleation.

Effect of ultrasonic frequency

The ultrasonic frequency could significantly influence the cavitation bubble dynamics and the subsequent nucleation process. The effects of ultrasonic frequencies of (20, 40, 60, 80, 100 kHz) on the yield and crystallization efficiency of SnSO4 were investigated under the conditions of 80 °C temperature, 107 W actual ultrasonic power, 0.03 MPa negative pressure, and 150 r/min stirring speed, with a 30 min crystallization time. The results are shown in Fig. 1(g).

At frequencies of 20 to 40 kHz, the crystallization performance was highly comparable, reaching an optimal value at 40 kHz with a crystal yield of 26.58 ± 0.47 g and a crystallization efficiency of 88.6 %±1.6 %. In this low-frequency regime, the extended acoustic period allows cavitation bubbles to expand to a larger maximum radius. This generates intense microjets and shock waves, effectively enhancing mass transfer and inducing secondary nucleation. Although the yield at 20 kHz is comparable to that at 40 kHz, its longer rarefaction half-cycle under negative pressure conditions promotes excessive bubble coalescence. This phenomenon can trigger severe acoustic shielding, thereby attenuating the effective cavitational volume [37], while the violent individual bubble collapses at 20 kHz may increase the risk of primary crystal over-fragmentation.

However, as the ultrasonic frequency was further increased from 40 kHz to 100 kHz, the crystal yield progressively declined from 26.58 ± 0.47 g to 8.2 ± 0.34 g, accompanied by a drop in crystallization efficiency from 88.6 ± 1.6 % to 27.3 ± 1.1 %. This significant decline occurs because at high frequencies (≥ 60 kHz), the acoustic period becomes too short for cavitation bubbles to undergo substantial growth. Consequently, the system produces extremely small bubbles whose collapse predominantly yields chemical effects (e.g., radical generation) rather than the macroscopic physical mechanical forces required for crystallization enhancement [38]. The diminished intensity of microjets and shock waves directly compromises the mass transfer rate and the physical driving force essential for nucleation. Therefore, 40 kHz effectively balances the intense physical nucleation enhancement with minimized acoustic shielding effects.

Comparison of the effects of negative pressure crystallization

(1) X‑ray diffraction analysis.

X‑ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on the products obtained from CN crystallization and UN crystallization, and the results are shown in Fig. 2. A comparison with the standard SnSO4 reference card (PDF#97‑000‑2748) confirms that all samples are pure-phase SnSO4. XPS analysis is used to verify the valence state distribution of tin element as shown in the Fig.S3. The results confirmed that the crystals prepared under the ultrasound-negative pressure (UN) condition retained high purity, with no significant oxidation to tetravalent tin species (Sn(IV)). This can be attributed to the continuous degassing of the solution under the applied negative pressure of 0.03 MPa, which markedly depleted dissolved oxygen and thereby effectively suppressed the oxidative chemical effect associated with ultrasonic cavitation.

XRD diagrams (a) negative pressure without ultrasonic crystallization; (b) negative pressure with ultrasonic crystallization.

Under ultrasonic irradiation, despite unchanged peak positions, significant variations in relative peak intensities reveal a fundamental shift in crystal growth behavior. Compared to samples treated with static CN, ultrasonically assisted samples exhibit disproportionate enhancement of specific diffraction peaks. This phenomenon indicates the formation of a preferred orientation (texture effect), rather than a mere increase in overall crystallinity. This crystallographic texture is directly correlated with the morphological transition observed in the SEM analysis (Section 3.2). This transformation is caused by the ultrasonic field regulating growth kinetics across different crystal planes. Ultrasound promotes anisotropic crystal growth: According to Periodic Bond Chain (PBC) theory and the modified Gibbs-Curie-Wulff theorem, crystal growth rates vary across different crystal planes due to differences in surface energy and nucleation kinetics. Intense shear forces inhibit lateral growth while accelerating solute alignment and distribution along the longitudinal axis. This differential growth rate drives the formation uniform crystals. Consequently, the specific enhancement of XRD peaks indicates that the synergistic action of ultrasonic vibration and negative pressure effectively modulates crystal habit, promotes oriented growth, and yields highly uniform SnSO4 crystals [39].

To quantitatively confirm the evolution of this preferred orientation, the texture coefficients (TCs) of the key diffraction planes were calculated, as shown in Table S1. The ultrasonic field promotes anisotropic growth along the (101) and (303) facets, increasing the TC value of the (101) crystal plane from 1.92 to 2.41 and the TC value of the (303) crystal plane from 1.68 to 2.57. Conversely, it suppresses growth along the (002) and (104) planes, decreasing the TC value of the (002) crystal plane from 1.68 to 0.67 and that of the (104) plane from 1.17 to 0.61. The transformation under ultrasound demonstrates that ultrasound can modulate anisotropic growth kinetics, suppressing the growth of specific crystal facets while promoting the growth of others, thereby yielding a more uniform crystal morphology.

(2) Particle size distribution (PSD).

Under CA conditions (Fig. 3a), the product exhibits a broad bimodal distribution (D50 = 14.07 ± 0.12 μm), which is attributed to uncontrolled secondary nucleation. When negative pressure is introduced without ultrasound (Fig. 3b), the particle size distribution is uneven and retains a bimodal distribution. A large number of coarse-grained crystals were formed (D90 = 225.19 ± 0.97 μm), confirming that negative pressure accelerates the growth of early-nucleated crystals due to high supersaturation. However, the presence of a significant number of fine grains (D10 = 0.83 ± 0.07 μm) indicates that these grains resulted from both continuous nucleation and agglomeration. Under UA condition (Fig. 3c) still produces a pronounced bimodal particle size distribution (D50 = 36.49 ± 0.25 μm), but with a significant number of fine particles (D10 = 0.33 ± 0.21 μm). This is attributed to the mechanical fragmentation effect generated by ultrasonication, which produces a large quantity of fine particles. By contrast, under the UN condition (Fig. 3d), the crystal particle size exhibits a uniform unimodal distribution (D50 = 38.42 ± 0.27 μm). Effective growth of stannous sulfate crystals was achieved under synergistic conditions. The distribution span narrowed significantly (D10 = 17.48 ± 0.13 μm, D90 = 84.20 ± 0.47 μm). This indicates that crystal nucleation and growth were effectively controlled, thereby avoiding both explosive nucleation and excessive fragmentation.

(3) The crystal morphologies of scanning electron microscopy.

The crystal morphologies under different crystallization conditions are shown in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4(a), the crystal morphology obtained from CA crystallization, where the crystals exhibits uneven sizes and irregular shapes, indicating that the nucleation and growth processes were not well-controlled under conventional conditions.

SEM images of crystal morphology: (a) conventional crystallization; (b) negative pressure crystallization; (c) ultrasonic crystallization; (d) combined negative pressure with ultrasonic crystallization.

As shown in Fig. 4(b), the under CN crystallization, the crystal size is reduced, accompanied by the presence of numerous fine-particle agglomerates and crystals with irregular morphologies. The sample contains crystals of varying shapes and sizes, along with some agglomerates. This is attributed to negative pressure accelerating the evaporation process, which accelerates crystal growth. However, the mixing effect within the solution is limited, resulting in an uneven supersaturation distribution. This causes significant variations in crystal growth rates, leading to an uneven product with inconsistent sizes. Additionally, during negative-pressure crystallization, prolonged supersaturation continuously generates new nucleation sites. This results in markedly different crystal growth times, leading to significant variations in crystal morphology.

The morphology of crystals obtained from UA crystallization at atmospheric pressure is shown in Fig. 4(c). After ultrasonic treatment, the crystal morphology exhibits superior dispersion compared to the conventional group, yet significant defects remain. The intense acoustic streaming effect effectively reduced the boundary layer thickness and promoted isotropic growth. However, under atmospheric pressure conditions, the cavitation collapse process was extremely violent. These strong shock waves effectively fragmented the agglomerates, promoting crystal homogenization, but also subjected the primary crystals to severe mechanical fragmentation, generating a large number of irregular fine particles.

The crystal morphology under UN was shown in Fig. 4(d), SnSO4 crystals exhibit highly uniform rod-like morphology with excellent monodispersity. This optimal outcome arises from the synergistic regulation mechanism of negative pressure and ultrasonication. Negative pressure facilitates rapid solvent evaporation while maintaining a highly supersaturated environment, providing a sustained growth driving force for crystals. Concurrently, the external ultrasonic field reduces the crystal nucleation energy barrier and optimizes crystal morphology through its regulation of crystal plane growth. Furthermore, the reduced cavitation threshold under negative-pressure expands the cavitation zone, generating extensive volumetric micro-mixing. This significantly enhances mass transfer coefficients, ensuring that solute ion replenishment keeps pace with the evaporation-driven crystallization efficiency. This kinetic equilibrium effectively suppresses the formation of diffusion-limited dendrites and other defect structures. Simultaneously, the negative pressure creates larger cavitation bubbles with weaker collapse strength. This mitigates the ultrasonic fragmentation of crystals, preventing the generation of fine particles. The resulting cavitation environment thoroughly disperses agglomerates while gently preserving the structural integrity of growing rod-shaped crystals, ultimately yielding superior crystalline products.

(4) The SnSO4 crystal morphology.

The effects of crystallization time on crystal morphology under CN and UN crystallization methods were investigated, as shown in Fig. 5. The results indicated that ultrasound significantly altered crystal growth behavior and morphological characteristics. In the CN crystallization process (Fig. 5(d)-5(f)), the crystals exhibited irregular morphology with a broad size distribution, and aggregation occurred during the nucleation stage. As crystallization time increased, the crystals grew larger with increasingly complex and non-uniform morphologies. In contrast, under UN crystallization (Fig. 5(a)-5(c)), crystal growth demonstrated notable preferential crystal face growth, leading to the formation of uniform rod-like structures with a markedly narrower size distribution. Further analysis revealed that ultrasonic cavitation and its associated mechanical effects not only promoted homogeneous nucleation but also suppressed particle aggregation and excessive random growth. As a result, the crystals maintained their rod-like morphology while undergoing continuous refinement.

In conclusion, the ultrasonic cavitation effect and its mechanical action can effectively regulate crystallization kinetics, achieving grain refinement and morphological homogenization. Compared with CN crystallization, the UN crystallization technique yields crystal products with smaller size, more concentrated distribution, and more consistent morphology, which contributes to improving the physicochemical properties and overall performance of the product.

Mechanism of synergistic ultrasonic-negative pressure crystallization

Ultrasound-negative pressure coupled reduces the width of the MSZW

Under identical thermodynamic conditions, as illustrated by the solubility-temperature relationship curve (Fig. 6), the width of the MSZW during crystallization under CN is significantly reduced compared to CA crystallization. This confirms that negative pressure crystallization effectively promotes crystal nucleation and precipitation. Simultaneously, the MSZW in NA crystallization is significantly narrower than in CA crystallization, further indicating that ultrasound effectively destabilizes supersaturated solutions. This weakens the ability of the solution to maintain a metastable state and accelerates crystal precipitation. Crucially, under the UN condition, the MSZW is further minimized, promoting crystal precipitation and crystallization. The synergistic cavitation effect induced by negative pressure in the liquid phase promotes nucleation, while the accelerated solvent removal under negative pressure enables the system to reach critical supersaturation and overcome the nucleation energy barrier more rapidly, thereby triggering crystal precipitation.

Impact of ultrasonic and negative pressure fields on the solubility boundary and Metastable Zone Width (MSZW) of SnSO4.

It should be noted that partial overlap of the fitting uncertainty bands was observed in some regions, especially when the MSZW became narrow. This overlap reflects the amplification of relative measurement uncertainty in narrow metastable zones. However, the local overlap did not change the overall trend. The UN nucleation boundary was generally closer to the SnSO4 solubility curve than the CN/CA boundaries, indicating a narrower metastable zone. In addition, the comparisons between CN and UN, as well as between CA and UA, consistently showed that ultrasound reduced the MSZW. The UN condition also exhibited a narrower MSZW than the conventional atmospheric-pressure condition, confirming that the ultrasound-negative pressure coupled field effectively promoted nucleation and narrowed the metastable zone. Therefore, although local uncertainty exists, the repeated measurements and the overall separation of the nucleation boundaries support the reliability of the MSZW reduction trend.

Under the UN conditions, SnSO4 crystals (Fig. 4(d)) exhibit highly uniform rod-like morphology with excellent monodispersity. This ideal outcome stems from a robust synergistic kinetic regulation mechanism: rapid solvent evaporation under negative pressure maintains a sustained supersaturated environment, providing a continuous thermodynamic driving force for crystallization; while ultrasonication optimizes crystal structure to promote uniform crystal growth. In stark contrast to the severe mechanical attrition observed under atmospheric pressure ultrasonication, the synergistic negative pressure-ultrasonic interaction effectively disperses agglomerates while gently preserving the structural integrity of the growing rod-shaped crystals. This approach ultimately yields products with superior crystal perfection [40], [41].

Crystallization kinetics analysis

During ultrasonic treatment with negative pressure, complex cavitation dynamics occur in the solution, encompassing both the pronounced mechanical micro-jets from transient collapses and the oscillating gas–liquid interfaces provided by stable bubbles. These synergistic effects directly influence the interactions among solute molecules, effectively lowering the activation energy barrier for the crystallization process and thereby enhancing crystallization efficiency [42]. The heterogeneous nucleation behavior was evaluated using an induction-time-based heterogeneous nucleation analysis [43], [44], [45], [46]. The detailed theoretical derivation and fitting procedure are provided in Text S4 of the SI. The induction time decreased continuously with increasing supersaturation under both CN and UN conditions, indicating that higher supersaturation promoted nucleation. At the same supersaturation, the induction time under UN was much shorter than that under CN, confirming that the coupled ultrasound-negative pressure field significantly accelerated nucleation, as shown in Fig. 7.(a).

Induction time analysis of SnSO4 nucleation (a)induction time as a function of supersaturation ratio; (b) the plots of ln tind versus (ln S)−2 under CN and UN conditions.

To distinguish the nucleation behavior in different supersaturation regions, the relationship between ln tind and (ln S)-2 was fitted separately in the low- supersaturation and high-supersaturation regions, as shown in Fig. 7.(b). The low-supersaturation region was defined as S = 1.04–1.10, while the high-supersaturation region was defined as S = 1.13–1.23. In the high-supersaturation region, the fitted slopes for UN was 0.0045 and fitted slopes for CN was 0.0046. This indicates that the high-supersaturation nucleation behavior under the two conditions was comparable.

In contrast, the low-supersaturation slopes were significantly different. The slope decreased from 0.0011 under CN to 0.0006 under UN, suggesting that the ultrasound-negative pressure field reduced the nucleation resistance in the low-supersaturation region. Based on theinduction-time-based heterogeneous nucleation analysis, the apparent effective heterogeneous correction factor (feff(θ)) was calculated from the ratio of the low-supersaturation slope to the high-supersaturation slope. The feff(θ) value decreased from 0.243 under CN to 0.136 under UN. Correspondingly, the apparent effective contact angle (θeff) decreased from 69.1° to 57.5°.

These results indicate that UN provides a better heterogeneous nucleation environment than CN. As a result, SnSO4 nuclei can form more readily on effective heterogeneous interfaces. Compared with CN, the heterogeneous nucleation barrier under UN was reduced by approximately 44.2 % under the same homogeneous reference state. Therefore, the shortened induction time under UN is not only caused by enhanced mass transfer, but also by the reduction of the heterogeneous nucleation barrier.

Enhanced cavitation effect under combined ultrasonic and negative pressure action

(1) Visualization of bubble evolution

To elucidate the mechanism of the UN effect, high-speed photography was employed for the in-situ visualization of cavitation bubble behavior. This technique allows for a direct comparison of the cavitation field characteristics under atmospheric pressure and negative pressure conditions. Changes in bubbles in solution (a-d) Atmospheric pressure with ultrasonic; (e-h) Negative pressure without ultrasonic; (i-l) Negative pressure with ultrasonic.

Observations reveal noticeable changes in bubble behavior within the solution system.

As shown in Fig. 8(a-d), under UA conditions, bubbles appear microscopic with cavitation strictly confined to localized areas near the transducer. These bubbles are transient and sparsely distributed, restricted the scope of ultrasound action. Under CN conditions (Fig. 8(e–h)), only a few distinct bubbles were observed slowly diffusing toward the liquid surface, representing a simple evaporation process with negligible fluid mixing.

However, under UN conditions (Fig. 8(i–l)), the bubble population undergoes a pronounced transformation, giving rise to two distinct morphologies: transient cavitation bubbles and stable (non-inertial) cavitation bubbles (Fig. 8(j–k)). Compared to CA, these cavitation bubbles exhibit a significantly larger maximum radius prior to collapse. Under identical ultrasonic power, the cavitation regions are more extensive, generating a higher population density of cavitation bubbles. They undergo rapid expansion followed by violent collapse, inducing intense micro-turbulence throughout the reactor volume. To further characterize the bubble populations, detailed observations of the high-speed images were performed. As shown in Fig. 9, some bubbles underwent violent expansion followed by collapse within a few ultrasonic cycles, exhibiting typical transient cavitation behavior (marked with red circles, Fig. 9a–b, e–g). In contrast, another population of bubbles persisted over extended periods without collapse, displaying only subtle vibrations and slight volume fluctuations at their interfaces (marked with yellow circles). These long-lived bubbles were identified as stable oscillating bubbles undergoing non-inertial cavitation. Notably, the smaller stable oscillating bubbles (marked with blue and green circles) exhibited more pronounced volume oscillations.

The long-lived bubbles observed under negative pressure actively promoted the nucleation process rather than merely attenuating the acoustic energy. The persistent gas–liquid interfaces of these bubbles, which intrinsically possess a lower interfacial free energy. Wohlgemuth et al. [34] demonstrated that the mere introduction of gas bubbles could reduce the metastable zone width and alter the crystal size distribution, confirming that bubble surfaces alone can promote nucleation without any violent collapse. Furthermore, the continuous radial oscillations of these bubbles generated intense acoustic microstreaming, which significantly accelerated the mass transport of solute ions toward the nucleation sites [47].

However, further reduction in ambient pressure led to excessive increases in the number density and size of cavitation bubbles, triggering extensive coalescence. The resulting severe acoustic shielding and attenuation overwhelmed the interfacial enhancement effects, causing a decline in crystallization yield. As reported by Sutka [37], dense cavitation clouds attenuate incident ultrasound and restrict the active cavitation zone. This is consistent with our experimental results: further reduction in pressure generates excessive cavitation bubbles, triggering large-scale coalescence and causing acoustic shielding to dominate, which in turn weakens the ultrasonic enhancement of crystallization.

Conclusions

This study investigated a synergistic ultrasonic-negative pressure crystallization strategy for SnSO4, resolving the contradiction between evaporation rate and crystal quality in conventional processes. The key findings are summarized as follows:

(1)Under optimized conditions (crystallization temperature of 80 °C, an ultrasonic frequency of 40 kHz, actual ultrasonic power of 107 W, stirring speed of 150 rpm, 0.03 MPa negative pressure, 60 min), this strategy achieved a high crystal yield of 26.58 ± 0.47 g (crystallization efficiency of 88.6 %±1.6 %) and produced crystals with a uniform, monomodal size distribution (D50 = 38.42 ± 0.27 μm) with excellent rod-like morphology. The moderated cavitation intensity and continuous degassing under negative pressure also suppressed unwanted sonochemical oxidation, helping maintain the high purity of the SnSO4 crystals.

(2)High-speed visualization revealed a unique coupling mechanism: negative pressure amplifies cavitation bubbles to generate extensive micro-jets for enhanced mass transfer, while stable quasi-static bubbles serve as heterogeneous nucleation sites. This dual mechanism prevents the mechanical attrition typically caused by atmospheric ultrasound, ensuring the integrity of the growing crystals. Collectively, this work provides a theoretical foundation for the high-efficiency, high-quality crystallization of inorganic salts.
