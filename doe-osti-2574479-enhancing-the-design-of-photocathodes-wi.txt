Enhancing the Design of Photocathodes with 90% polarization and QE > 1% for DOE NP
Principal Investigator and Co-PIs for collaborative work:
PI: Dr. Sylvain Marsillac and Dr. Matt Grau, ODU
Co-PI; Dr. Matthew Poelker and Dr. Joseph Grames, JLab
Co-PI: Dr. Erdong Wang, BNL

This project’s 3 major goals were the following:
 1. Fabricate state-of-the-art spin-polarized photocathodes using MOCVD and develop
 knowledge of fabrication parameters.
 2. Enhance the design of 2 structures of spin-polarized photocathodes (strained-superlattice
 and strained-superlattice with distributed Bragg reflector) to assess paths to the next
 generation of spin-polarized photocathodes.
 3. Provide a supply of state-of-the-art spin-polarized photocathodes to both Brookhaven
 National Laboratory and the Thomas Jefferson National Accelerator Facility.

All devices discussed in this report were grown in a MOCVD system on Zn-doped (100) GaAs
substrates. An initial layer of GaAs was grown directly on top of the substrate that serves to verify
proper systematics. This layer is followed by several metamorphic graded (MMG) layers that
reduce the lattice constant of the material from that of GaAs (5.653 Å) to GaAs0.625P0.375 (5.577
Å). These layers are sufficiently larger than the critical thickness, which allows relaxation and
forces the layer to take on the lattice constant of the new composition. A final fallback layer with
composition GaAs0.65P0.35 serves as the virtual substrate for the superlattices grown on top.
Distributed Bragg Reflectors (DBR) were grown on some of the layers, with alternating layers of
high refractive index materials and low refractive index materials. A spacer layer with composition
GaAs0.65P0.35 was then grown to finalize the optical cavity. The structure of the superlattices
remained constant in layer thicknesses (2.8 nm GaAsP barriers, 3.8 nm GaAs wells) with various
number of pairs varied. A 5 nm carbon-doped GaAs layer was placed on top of the superlattice to
facilitate band-bending for the Negative Electron Affinity surface layer.

A summary of the main results for this project are reported in the following 4 different sections,
describing in detail the enhancement to either the main layers that constitute the spin-polarized
photocathode or the device modeling, namely:
 1. Device Modeling
 2. Modification of the Metamorphic Grade Layer
 3. Fabrication of the enhanced Distributed Bragg Reflector
 4. Doping Study

This project resulted in devices with quantum efficiency as high as 2.3% with polarization of
92% at 780 nm. These devices also achieved 11 nC with the free limit range and 9 nC with an
8 mm diameter laser.

1. Device Modeling

1.1. DBR Modeling
In parallel to the fabrication process, and in order to enhance the quality of the DBR, we started
modeling the optical properties of the DBR and its impact on the overall photocathode
performance. The first step to do that was to look into the influence of the number of pairs of
GaAs0.65P0.35 and In0.30Al0.70P. We used COMSOL Multiphysics and MATLAB as our simulation
software. We fixed GaAs0.65P0.35 and In0.30Al0.70P nominal thicknesses at 54 nm and 64 nm,
respectively. We then modified the number of pairs from 4 all the way to 20. The reflectivity of
the DBR as a function of wavelength is illustrated Figure 1. As one can see, there is a dramatic
change from 4 to 8 pairs, and still a significant change from 8 to 12, but a reduced benefit
afterwards, with nearly no change for 16 to 20 pairs.

Figure 1: DBR reflectivity as a function of wavelength for various number of pairs of GaAs0.65P0.35 and In0.30Al0.70P

After that, we modeled the expected absorption, reflectivity and transmittance as well as the
quantum efficiency of the devices, for 8 pairs to 20 pairs. Note that the position of peak reflectivity
does not change with number of DBR pairs, as this is set by the respective thickness of the
GaAs0.65P0.35 and In0.30Al0.70P pairs, as well as by the thickness of the GaAs0.65P0.35 spacer layer
deposited in between the DBR and the superlattice. We are most interested in the quantum
efficiency at wavelength of around 770-780 nm, corresponding to the wavelength of the laser we
use for testing the devices. At that wavelength, we observed that the modeled quantum efficiency
we can get from the devices changes from 4.5% in a device with 8 DBR pairs, to 7.5% in a device
with 16 DBR pairs. An additional increase from 16 pairs to 20 pairs does not seem to have much
effect for the peak of interest but does increase slightly the peak at 725 nm.
These results were used to optimize the DBR (see Section 3)

1.2. Superlattice Modeling
A model has been developed to predict the impact of quantum confinement in semiconductor
superlattices, particularly relevant for high polarization photocathodes. Quantum confinement
arises from the discrete energy levels of electrons and holes in quantum well structures. By solving
the Schrodinger Equation in 1 dimension, accounting for the band structure of the semiconductor
materials, the model calculates energy level shifts due to this quantum confinement. The valence
band, composed of heavy hole (HH), light hole (LH), and split-off bands, undergoes splitting due
to different effective masses, modifying individual excitation of the specific spin states. The model
determines new band gaps and splitting as functions of well and barrier thicknesses as well as the

phosphorous composition. Linear extrapolations of band gap properties are used to compute the
conduction band and valence band offsets, defining the potential wells. Effective masses are
defined for each band, allowing the Schrodinger Equation to be solved in each region. Results of
these computations yield the band gap dependence on well width and splitting between sub-bands
(Figure 2-4).
Optimal lattice ratios yielding the best band splitting offered insights for photocathode design
and were used for the fabrication of our photocathodes.

2. Modification of the Metamorphic Grade Layer

Based on our previous findings that there was a degradation of the metamorphic grade (MMG)
layers during growth, parameters were adjusted to provide refinement to this process. Final
composition of the MMG was originally found to be GaAs0.71P0.29. Post refinement reciprocal
space mapping shows the composition to be GaAs0.63P0.37. This provides better lattice-matching
to our current superlattice design. Reciprocal space mapping (RSM) was taken using Bruker D8
X-ray diffractometer. The units on the images in Figure 5 are in nm-1 meaning that a larger value
indicates smaller lattice constant of the associated layers. The peak in the lower left corner is from
the GaAs wafer with a lattice constant of 0.565 nm. The lattice constant of the GaAs0.63P0.37 buffer
layer is 0.558nm and is seen as the bright red peak in the top right corner. The white triangle
overlay is used to determine strain of the grown layers. At full strain, the RSM peaks will occur
along the vertical line. Peaks associated with fully relaxed growth will occur along the dotted line.
Comparing the image on the right to the image on the left, we can see the value of qx is larger with
our updated growth recipe. This is what we would expect to see as we increase phosphorous
composition from 29% to 37% because the lattice constant would decrease.
This optimized Metamorphic growth process was used to fabricate subsequent devices.

 Figure 5: (Left) RSM of MMG growth using old parameters. (Right) RSM of new MMG growth with final
 composition optimized for our current superlattice design.

3. Fabrication of enhanced distributed Bragg reflector

3.1. Initial layer: AlAs0.61P0.39
The distributed Bragg reflector (DBR) is a type of superlattice structure, where materials of
alternating refractive indices are deposited to create a highly reflective mirror. The wavelength of
peak reflectivity and the width of the resonance can be controlled through the thickness of each
layer and the number of periods. To maintain lattice matching to GaAs0.65P0.35, we considered
AlAs0.61P0.39. The AlAs0.61P0.39 compound has been used in the past to construct a similar DBR
when the overall photocathode was deposited by molecular beam epitaxy.
Calibration runs were done to deposit AlAs0.61P0.39 layers, to control composition and thickness.
However, we encountered several difficulties due to chemical instability and some safety risks.

3.2. Choice of new DBR layer
We therefore needed to change the material. The requirements for the new material were:
 - Similar refractive index at wavelength of interest
 - Lattice matching to GaAs0.65P0.35
 - Eg(Material) > Eg(strained GaAs)

As one can see in Figure 6 left, In0.30Al0.70P is a potential match in terms of lattice constant and
band gap. Calculations of refractive index indicate that it is also a good match to GaAs0.65P0.35,

3.3. Fabrication with new DBR layer

Calibration runs were therefore performed with In0.30Al0.70P. Composition and thickness
calibration were confirmed via high resolution X-ray diffraction, in-situ reflectivity measurements
and EDS analysis. Thickness of the layers was confirmed by TEM (Figure 7)

 Figure 7: Illustration of the composition and thickness of the individual layers in a 12 pairs DBR

Using COMSOL and the modeling of the refractive indices, simulations were performed to show
potential improvements to the device by increasing the number of DBR pairs. The modeling shows
that for wavelength of 780 nm (Figure 8), we should expect the following reflectivity:
 - 12 pairs = 87% reflectivity
 - 16 pairs = 95% reflectivity
 - 20 pairs = 98% reflectivity
Simulations were done assuming smooth surface so actual measured values will not match
simulated values due to surface scattering. The lack of change in reflectance in the measured data
was still unexpected (Figure 8).

 Figure 8: (Left) simulated reflectance for DBR with various pairs; (Right) Measured reflectance for 12, 16 and 20
 pairs

To elucidate this issue, the reflectance was measured across a wafer (Figure 9 left), and variations
in peak position were observed. This could result from either variation in composition or thickness.
Simulations as a function of thickness were then performed and a good match with experimental
results for variation in thickness of 4% was obtained (Figure 9 right).

Figure 9 (Left) measured reflectance on a single wafer as a function of location; (Right) simulated reflectance for 12
 pairs DBR with small change of thickness from the ideal value (0-4%)

Assuming this was an issue with thickness, we reviewed the in-situ calibration run from in-situ
data, using reflectance and curvature data. For the same position on the wafer, we observed little
variation from one wafer to another wafer within the same run (inter-run variation) or from one
run to another (run-to-run variation). We also recalibrated the temperature of the wafer to ensure
that there would not be a variation across the wafers and verified that the temperature was the same
within 10oC across the wafer. The last verification we did was to look at the variation of thickness
throughout individual wafers for various materials. We observed a variation of around 1% for most
materials, but not for the newest material used in the DBR. The process parameters were therefore
modified in order to enhance the uniformity across the wafer. This led to devices with quantum
efficiency above 2% with polarization above 90% at 780 nm (Figure 10).

4. Modification of the dopant

Colleagues from BNL first tested our devices in a high voltage d.c. photogun and reported that our
devices exhibited a surface charge limit at high bunch charge. We recognized that it was possible
the doping level in our devices was not as high as our calibration runs made us believe, so we
decided to fabricate in a series of runs with several dopant level as well as a new dopant. Indeed,
we used carbon instead of zinc as the dopant for the top layer (5 nm of GaAs), as carbon is known
to diffuse less than zinc in GaAs. Devices were therefore fabricated with targeted doping
concentrations of 2×1019 cm-3, 5×1019 cm-3, 1020 cm-3 and 2×1020 cm-3.
Devices were tested for 9 hours at 20µA operation (approximately 20 weeks-worth of EIC
operation) with only 10% decay. Samples with high doping 1020 cm-3 and 2.2 1020 cm-3 had lower
performance than expected due to defects forming in the metamorphic grade layer.

Test results from BNL’s HVDC gun showed sample with doping of 5 1019 cm-3 managed to
achieve 11 nC with the free limit range and 9 nC with an 8 mm diameter laser (Figure 11).
This is above the EIC requirement of 7 nC.
