Tight Lower Bounds and Optimal Constructions of Locally Repairable Convertible Codes in the Split Regime
Haoming Shi, Weijun Fang

Abstract
Erasure codes are a key technique for achieving fault-tolerant storage in modern distributed storage systems. As storage systems evolve, their code parameters often need to be adjusted to accommodate changes in storage scale, reliability requirements, and disk failure rates. Such adaptation is performed through code conversion, where data encoded under an initial code are transformed into data encoded under a final code. Convertible codes are designed to carry out this transformation efficiently while preserving desirable properties of the underlying codes. In this work, we study conversions between systematic optimal-distance locally repairable codes (LRCs), focusing on the read-bandwidth cost in the global split regime. We concentrate on the parameter range g I , g F ≤ r g^{I},g^{F}\leq r , where the numbers of initial and final global parity nodes do not exceed the local dimension. For this entire range, we derive read-bandwidth lower bounds for stable optimal-distance locally repairable convertible codes (LRCCs) via an information-theoretic approach, without imposing any linearity assumption on the initial and final codes or on the conversion procedure. We then develop constructions based on MDS array codes with prescribed repair properties. According to the relative sizes of g I g^{I} and g F g^{F} , the construction is divided into the three cases g F = g I g^{F}=g^{I} , g F > g I g^{F}>g^{I} , and g F < g I g^{F}<g^{I} ; in each case, it attains the corresponding lower bound. Hence we characterize the optimal read-bandwidth cost for stable optimal-distance LRCCs throughout the parameter range g I , g F ≤ r g^{I},g^{F}\leq r .



I
Introduction

In modern distributed storage systems, large-scale data are stored across many storage nodes, where node failures and disk errors are unavoidable. Erasure codes are therefore widely used to provide reliability with lower storage overhead than replication
[
7
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
24
]
. Among them, maximum distance separable codes, or MDS codes, achieve the optimal tradeoff between storage efficiency and fault tolerance: an
(n,k)
MDS code can recover the original data from any
k
out of
n
encoded symbols, and hence can tolerate any
n-k
erasures
[
28
]
. However, repairing a failed
node in an MDS-coded system often requires accessing many surviving nodes,
resulting in high repair bandwidth
[
23
]
,
[
22
]
. Locally repairable codes
[
8
]
, or LRCs, address
this issue by introducing local parity symbols within small local groups, so
that a failed symbol can be repaired by contacting only a limited number of
symbols in the same group. Thus, while MDS codes optimize erasure tolerance,
LRCs provide a more practical balance between reliability, storage overhead, and repair efficiency in distributed storage systems
[
1
]
,
[
9
]
.

In practical storage systems, the reliability requirement of coded data may
change over time
[
11
]
. For instance, the failure rate of storage devices is not constant over their lifetime, and is often modeled by a bathtub-shaped curve with an early-failure period, a relatively stable useful-life period, and a wear-out period. As the underlying failure rates and access patterns evolve, a fixed set of coding parameters may no longer be optimal for balancing storage overhead, fault tolerance, and repair efficiency. To exploit such time-varying reliability characteristics, prior work
[
13
]
showed that dynamically adapting the code rate can lead to substantial savings in both storage and operational costs. Adjusting the code rate requires changing the parameters of the underlying code. This procedure was formalized as code conversion
[
17
]
, where data encoded under an initial code are transformed into data encoded under a final
code. A naive approach is to fully decode the existing data and then re-encode it using the final code, but this can require reading and rewriting a large amount of data. Convertible codes are designed to perform this transformation more efficiently while preserving desired decodability properties of the initial
and final codes.

In this paper, we study code conversion for optimal-distance
(k,g,r,\ell)
locally repairable codes
[
19
]
in the
global split regime. Specifically, we consider stable conversions in the global split regime from an optimal-distance initial
(k^{I},g^{I},r,\ell)
-LRC to an optimal-distance final
(k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRC, where one initial codeword is converted into
\lambda^{F}
final codewords, with
k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F}
and the locality parameters
r
and
\ell
preserved. Since stable conversions maximize the number of unchanged nodes, the write cost is fixed in this setting, and the central question becomes how much data must be read from the initial codeword in order to generate the final codewords. Our goal is to characterize the fundamental read-bandwidth cost of such conversions and to determine whether this cost can be achieved by matching constructions.

I-A

Related Work

The problem of code conversion was first introduced by Maturana and
Rashmi
[
17
]
as a framework for efficiently updating the parameters of already encoded data in distributed storage systems. Their initial line of work focused on conversions between MDS codes, especially in the merge regime, where multiple initial codewords are combined into a single final codeword. Under the access cost metric, they established fundamental lower bounds and provided optimal constructions attaining these bounds.

Subsequent works refined the study of convertible codes in several directions.
First, the cost of conversion has been evaluated under different metrics, including access cost, which counts the number of accessed nodes during conversion, and bandwidth cost, which measures the total amount of data read from and written to storage nodes.
Second, the conversion process itself has been categorized into several regimes, including the merge regime, the split regime, and general conversions between arbitrary code parameters.
For MDS convertible codes, a series of works
[
16
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
29
]
have established lower bounds and optimal constructions under these different regimes and cost metrics.
In particular, information-theoretic approaches have recently been used to
derive bandwidth lower bounds for MDS convertible codes in the split regime
without imposing linearity or uniformity assumptions on the downloaded data
[
27
]
.

Before the formal notion of convertible codes was introduced, related ideas had already appeared in the context of LRC-based storage systems. Xia et
al.
[
30
]
proposed a storage architecture using two different LRCs to encode frequently accessed data and less frequently accessed data separately. Their scheme includes a conversion mechanism between the two LRCs, whose purpose is to reduce the number of accessed nodes when data change their access frequency.
Later, Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
formally initiated the study of efficient code conversion for LRCs within the framework of convertible codes. Under the bandwidth cost metric, they proposed constructions for LRC conversion in both the global merge regime and the global split regime, and computed the bandwidth costs achieved by their constructions.

Since then, several works have further investigated the conversion problem for LRCs. Kong
[
14
]
studied locally repairable convertible codes and gave lower bounds and matching constructions under the access cost metric. Ge et al.
[
6
]
improved the access cost lower bound and proposed more general access-optimal constructions. Shi et al.
[
26
]
further studied
generalized merge-convertible codes involving LRCs and developed new bounds and optimal constructions. Recently, Chopra et al.
[
4
]
derived lower bounds on the bandwidth cost of LRC conversion in the global merge regime and showed that the global merge constructions of Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
are
bandwidth-optimal in a broad range of parameters.

Prior to this work, however, the global split regime was much less understood. No bandwidth lower bound was known for LRC conversion in this regime, and the available bandwidth cost constructions were essentially limited to those of Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
. Chopra et al.
[
4
]
explicitly posed the problem of deriving bandwidth lower bounds for the global split regime of LRCs and of determining whether the constructions in
[
19
]
achieve the optimal bandwidth cost.

I-B

Main Contributions

In this paper, we focus on stable optimal-distance LRCCs in the global split regime under the parameter condition
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
. Our main contributions are summarized as follows.

•

On the converse side, by extending the information-theoretic approaches of
[
4
]
and
[
27
]
to this setting, we derive two fundamental constraints on the data downloaded during conversion, and use them to obtain lower bounds on the read bandwidth of stable optimal-distance LRCCs. These results do not impose any linearity assumption on the initial and final LRCs or on the conversion procedure. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to establish read-bandwidth lower bounds for stable optimal-distance LRCCs in the global split regime.

•

On the achievability side, we provide constructions whose read bandwidth attains the lower bounds derived above throughout the parameter range
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
. According to the relative sizes of
g^{I}
and
g^{F}
, we divide the construction into the three cases
g^{F}=g^{I}
,
g^{F}>g^{I}
, and

g^{F}<g^{I}
. For each case, we design the underlying MDS array code via piggybacking ideas so that it has the repair or alignment property required by the corresponding conversion procedure. We then show that, over sufficiently large finite fields, these MDS array blocks can be combined to form stable optimal-distance LRCCs whose read bandwidth exactly matches the corresponding lower bound. Consequently, our converse and achievability results together characterize the optimal read-bandwidth cost for stable optimal-distance LRCCs in the global split regime whenever
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
.

Therefore, we answer the open problem raised in
[
4
]
for a broad parameter range of the global split regime. In particular, we characterize the optimal read-bandwidth cost for stable optimal-distance LRCCs when
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
. Moreover, by comparing our optimal bandwidth with the bandwidth achieved by the global split constructions of Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
, we show that their constructions are not bandwidth-optimal in general in this parameter regime.

background and notation

In this section, we introduce the notation used throughout the paper and
review some background material needed for our results.

We use the following notation, largely following
[
4
]
and
[
27
]
. For positive integers
a
and
i
, let

[a]^{i}\coloneqq\{a(i-1)+1,a(i-1)+2,\ldots,ai\}
, and let
[a]\coloneqq[a]^{1}
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
denote the finite field of size
q
, where
q
is a prime power. For an indexed collection
Z=\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
and a subset
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, we write
Z_{\mathcal{A}}\coloneqq\{Z_{i}:i\in\mathcal{A}\}
. For a vector
x=(x_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, the notation
x_{\mathcal{A}}
denotes its projection onto the coordinates indexed by
\mathcal{A}
. Similarly, for a family of deterministic functions
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, we write
f_{\mathcal{A}}(X_{\mathcal{A}})\coloneqq\{f_{i}(X_{i}):i\in\mathcal{A}\}
. For random variables
X
and
Y
, we denote the entropy of
X
by
H(X)
, the conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
by
H(X\mid Y)
, and the mutual information between
X
and
Y
by
I(X;Y)
. All entropies are measured in units of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-symbols.

An
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
array code
\mathcal{C}
is an injective map

\mathcal{C}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha k}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha n}.

We identify
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha k}
with
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha})^{k}
and regard a message vector as
m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})
, where each
m_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
is a message block. For a message
m
, let
c=\mathcal{C}(m)
be the corresponding codeword and write

c=(c_{1}^{\top},\ldots,c_{n}^{\top})^{\top},\qquad c_{i}=(c_{i,1},\ldots,c_{i,\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha},\quad i\in[n].

Each block
c_{i}
is called a code symbol, or equivalently a node, and its
\alpha
entries are called subsymbols. More generally, for any node
Z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
, we write
Z[s]:=z_{s}
for its
s
-th subsymbol, where
s\in[\alpha]
. The parameter
\alpha
is referred to as the sub-packetization level. When
\alpha=1
, this definition reduces to the usual scalar code model.

Consistent with the above notation, for a subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
, let
c_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the projection of the codeword
c
onto the nodes indexed by
\mathcal{S}
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}\coloneqq\{c_{\mathcal{S}}:c\in\mathcal{C}\}
denote the punctured code on
\mathcal{S}
. The Hamming distance of array codewords is measured at the node level, i.e., it counts the number of node positions at which the corresponding
\alpha
-symbol blocks differ. The minimum distance of an array code is defined with respect to this node-wise Hamming distance.

In the storage interpretation, each node
c_{i}
is stored on one storage node; hence we will use the terms code symbol and storage node interchangeably when discussing repair and conversion procedures. The code
\mathcal{C}
is systematic if, after a possible relabeling of nodes, the first
k
nodes store the message blocks uncoded, i.e.,

c_{i}=m_{i},\qquad i\in[k].

These
k
nodes are called information nodes, and the remaining
n-k
nodes are called parity nodes.

II-A

Locally Repairable Codes

We recall the standard definition of
(r,\delta)
-locality, stated for codes without imposing any linearity assumption.

Definition II.1

(
(r,\delta)
-locality,
[
12
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
array code, and let

1\leq r\leq k
and
\delta\geq 2
. A subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
is called an
(r,\delta)
-locality set of
\mathcal{C}
if

1.

|\mathcal{S}|\leq r+\delta-1
;

2.

the minimum distance of the punctured code
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
satisfies
d(\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}})\geq\delta
.

The code
\mathcal{C}
has
(r,\delta)
all-symbol locality if every node
t\in[n]
belongs to at least one
(r,\delta)
-locality set. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{C}
is systematic and, without loss of generality, the information nodes are indexed by
[k]
, then
\mathcal{C}
has
(r,\delta)
information locality if every information node
t\in[k]
belongs to at least one
(r,\delta)
-locality set.

Next, we introduce the class of locally repairable array codes considered in this paper.

Definition II.2

(
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC,
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
systematic array code. Suppose that
r\mid k
, and let
\mu=\frac{k}{r},n=k+\mu\ell+g.
The code
\mathcal{C}
is called a
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

\mathcal{C}
has
(r,\ell+1)
information locality.

2.

The
k
information nodes are partitioned into
\mu
disjoint sets
\mathcal{I}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{I}_{\mu}
, each of size
r
. For each
s\in[\mu]
, there is an associated set
\mathcal{L}_{s}
of
\ell
local parity nodes. These local parity nodes are deterministic functions only of the information nodes in
\mathcal{I}_{s}
, and the sets
\mathcal{I}_{s}\cup\mathcal{L}_{s}
are pairwise disjoint. Moreover,
\mathcal{I}_{s}\cup\mathcal{L}_{s}
is an
(r,\ell+1)
locality set of
\mathcal{C}
. We refer to
\mathcal{I}_{s}\cup\mathcal{L}_{s}
as the
s
-th local group.

3.

In addition to the information nodes and local parity nodes, each codeword contains
g
global parity nodes. Each global parity node is a deterministic function of the
k
information nodes.

When
\alpha
is clear from the context, we simply call
\mathcal{C}
a
(k,g,r,\ell)
-LRC.

Let
d
denote the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
. By the Singleton-type bound
[
12
]
for codes with
(r,\ell+1)
information locality,

d\leq n-k-\left(\left\lceil\frac{k}{r}\right\rceil-1\right)\ell+1.

Since
r\mid k
and
n=k+\frac{k}{r}\ell+g
, this bound reduces to

d\leq g+\ell+1.

A
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC is called optimal-distance if it meets this bound with equality.

A standard way to construct optimal-distance LRCs is the basic pyramid code construction
[
9
]
. In this construction, one starts from an MDS code and splits suitable parity nodes into local parity nodes, while preserving the optimal-distance property.

II-B

MDS Array Codes and Repair Bandwidth

We next recall the notion of MDS array codes and the repair bandwidth bounds that will be used in our constructions.

Definition II.3

(MDS array code)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
array code. We say that
\mathcal{C}
is an MDS array code if any
k
out of the
n
nodes determine the original message. Equivalently, for every subset
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[n]
with
|\mathcal{S}|=k
, the projection
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
is injective. With respect to the Hamming distance, an MDS array code satisfies

d(\mathcal{C})=n-k+1.

Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]
be two disjoint subsets, where
\mathcal{E}
is the set of erased nodes and
\mathcal{R}
is the set of helper nodes. We denote by
N(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})
the minimum number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-symbols that need to be downloaded from the helper nodes in
\mathcal{R}
in order to recover the erased nodes in
\mathcal{E}
. The downloaded symbols may be arbitrary functions of the contents stored in the helper nodes.

Theorem II.1

(Centralized repair cut-set bound
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
MDS array code. Let
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]
be two disjoint subsets with

|\mathcal{E}|=h,\qquad|\mathcal{R}|=d.

If
1\leq h\leq n-k
and
k\leq d\leq n-h
, then

N(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})\geq\frac{hd}{h+d-k}\alpha.

If equality holds, we say that the nodes in
\mathcal{E}
can be optimally repaired from the helper nodes in
\mathcal{R}
.

The previous theorem defines optimal repair for a prescribed erased set
\mathcal{E}
and a prescribed helper set
\mathcal{R}
. The following stronger property requires optimal repair for every choice of erased and helper sets of the same sizes.

Definition II.4

(
(h,d)
-optimal repair property)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an
[n,k,\alpha]_{q}
MDS array code. For integers
h
and
d
satisfying
1\leq h\leq n-k,k\leq d\leq n-h,

we say that
\mathcal{C}
has the
(h,d)
-optimal repair property if, for every erased set
\mathcal{E}\subseteq[n]
with
|\mathcal{E}|=h
and every helper set
\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]\setminus\mathcal{E}
with
|\mathcal{R}|=d
, the nodes in
\mathcal{E}
can be optimally repaired from
\mathcal{R}
, i.e.,

N(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{R})=\frac{hd}{h+d-k}\alpha.

Theorem II.2

(Existence of MDS array codes with optimal repair

[
31
]
)
.

Let
1\leq k<n
, set
\rho:=n-k
, and define
s:=\operatorname{lcm}(1,2,\ldots,\rho).
For every finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q\geq n+1
, there exists an
[n,k,s^{n}]_{q}
MDS array code having the
(h,d)
-optimal repair property simultaneously for all

1\leq h\leq\rho,\qquad k\leq d\leq n-h.

II-C

Convertible Codes

We next recall the formal model of code conversion.

Definition II.5

(Convertible code)
.

A convertible code with parameters
(n^{I},k^{I};n^{F},k^{F},\alpha)
consists of the following objects.

1.

An initial code
\mathcal{C}^{I}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha k^{I}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha n^{I}}
and a final code
\mathcal{C}^{F}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha k^{F}}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha n^{F}}
.

2.

Two partitions
\mathcal{P}^{I}
and
\mathcal{P}^{F}
of the common message index set
[M]
, where
M=\operatorname{lcm}(k^{I},k^{F})
. Each block in
\mathcal{P}^{I}
has size
k^{I}
, and each block in
\mathcal{P}^{F}
has size
k^{F}
.

3.

A conversion procedure which, for every message
m\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha M}
, takes the initial codewords
\{\mathcal{C}^{I}(m_{P}):P\in\mathcal{P}^{I}\}
as input and produces the final codewords
\{\mathcal{C}^{F}(m_{P}):P\in\mathcal{P}^{F}\}
as output, where
m_{P}
denotes the message blocks indexed by
P
.

During conversion, nodes are divided into three categories. An
unchanged node
is an initial node whose stored vector is kept unchanged and appears as a node in the final codewords. A
retired node
is an initial node that does not appear in the final codewords after conversion. A
new node
is a node of the final codewords that is generated during conversion and was not present among the initial nodes.

The conversion is carried out by a
conversion coordinator
, which downloads data from the initial nodes and computes the new nodes. A convertible code is called
stable
if the number of unchanged nodes is maximized among all conversion procedures with the same initial and final code parameters. The
read bandwidth cost
, denoted by
\gamma_{R}
, is the total number of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-symbols downloaded by the conversion coordinator during the conversion procedure. Since each node stores
\alpha
symbols over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we also use the normalized read bandwidth
\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}\coloneqq\gamma_{R}/\alpha
, which measures the read cost in units of full-node contents and allows us to compare constructions with different subpacketization levels. For stable conversions, once the set of new nodes is fixed, the write bandwidth cost is fixed as well. Therefore, under the stability assumption, the conversion cost can be studied through the read bandwidth cost.

In this paper, we study code conversion between
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRCs in the global split regime.

Definition II.6

(LRCC)
.

Suppose the initial code
\mathcal{C}^{I}
is a
(k^{I},g^{I},r^{I},\ell^{I},\alpha)
-LRC and the final code
\mathcal{C}^{F}
is a
(k^{F},g^{F},r^{F},\ell^{F},\alpha)
-LRC. Then the corresponding convertible code is called a locally repairable convertible code, or LRCC, with parameters
(k^{I},g^{I},r^{I},\ell^{I};k^{F},g^{F},r^{F},\ell^{F},\alpha).
If both
\mathcal{C}^{I}
and
\mathcal{C}^{F}
are optimal-distance LRCs, then the LRCC is called an optimal-distance LRCC.

For an LRCC, let
\mu^{I}=\frac{k^{I}}{r^{I}},\mu^{F}=\frac{k^{F}}{r^{F}}
denote the numbers of local groups in the initial and final codewords, respectively.

Definition II.7

(Global Split regime,
[
19
]
)
.

The split regime refers to the class of LRCCs in which one initial codeword is converted into multiple final codewords while the locality parameters are preserved. More precisely, for an integer
\lambda^{F}\geq 2
, we have
k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},r^{I}=r^{F}=r,\ell^{I}=\ell^{F}=\ell
. Moreover,
\mu^{I}=\lambda^{F}\mu^{F}
.

For LRCCs in the global split regime, we use the shorthand
(k^{I},g^{I},r,\ell;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell,\alpha)\text{-LRCC}.

Remark II.1
.

Throughout this paper, we use the canonical stable split conversion in which the systematic information nodes are identified with the same message blocks before and after conversion. Hence these nodes can be preserved without rewriting.

For later comparison, we recall the global split construction of Maturana and Rashmi. Using the piggybacking framework, they constructed optimal-distance LRCCs in which all information nodes and local parity nodes are preserved during conversion. In particular, for the global split regime with
k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F}
, their construction gives an optimal-distance

(k^{I},g^{I},r,\ell;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRCC whose read bandwidth cost is

\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{MR}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(k^{F}+\mu^{F}\ell)+g^{I}}{(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}+g^{I}+\ell(\lambda^{F}-1)}\alpha,&\text{if }g^{F}\leq g^{I},\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(k^{F}+\mu^{F}\ell)(\lambda^{F}g^{F}-g^{I})+g^{I}g^{F}}{\lambda^{F}g^{F}(g^{F}+\ell)-g^{I}\ell}\alpha,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Recent work on the global merge regime raised the corresponding converse problem for the global split regime, namely, to derive lower bounds on the bandwidth cost of LRCCs in this regime and to determine whether the split regime construction of Maturana and Rashmi is bandwidth-optimal.

III
Information-Theoretic Characterization of LRC Storage

In this section, we formulate several information-theoretic properties of a single systematic optimal-distance
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC. No conversion-specific assumption is made in this section. These results will be instantiated in Section
IV
after the structure of stable LRCCs in the global split regime has been established.

Consider a DSS encoded using a systematic optimal-distance
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC and let
u=\frac{k}{r}
. A codeword is stored across
n=k+u\ell+g
nodes, consisting of
k
information nodes,
u\ell
local parity nodes, and
g
global parity nodes. The
k
information nodes are partitioned into
u
disjoint local groups, each containing
r
information nodes and
\ell
associated local parity nodes.

[\ell]^{\tau}
denote the indices of the
\ell

H\!\left(L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}\mid X_{[r]^{\tau}}\right)=0.

Similarly, since each global parity node is a deterministic function of the
k

H\!\left(G_{[g]}\mid X_{[k]}\right)=0.

Moreover, the local code induced by each local group has minimum distance

\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\ell]^{\tau}
and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq[r]^{\tau}

\tau

L_{\mathcal{A}}
and
X_{\mathcal{D}}

H\!\left(X_{[r]^{\tau}}\mid L_{\mathcal{A}},X_{\mathcal{D}}\right)=0.

Since the LRC has optimal distance

\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]

\mathcal{R}\subseteq[u\ell]

H\!\left(G_{\mathcal{Q}},L_{\mathcal{R}},X_{\mathcal{S}}\mid G_{[g]\setminus\mathcal{Q}},L_{[u\ell]\setminus\mathcal{R}},X_{[k]\setminus\mathcal{S}}\right)=0.

We assume that the information nodes store independent and uniformly distributed message blocks. Thus,

H(X_{j})=\alpha,\qquad j\in[k].

(1)

All nodes have storage capacity
\alpha
symbols over

H(L_{a})\leq\alpha,\qquad a\in[u\ell],\qquad H(G_{i})\leq\alpha,\qquad i\in[g].

(2)

We next recall several previously established propositions that will be used in our proofs. The first is the total-entropy identity, which states that the parity nodes do not introduce any additional entropy beyond that of the

Proposition III.1

(

For any systematic optimal-distance

H\!\left(X_{[k]},L_{[u\ell]},G_{[g]}\right)=k\alpha.

The next proposition isolates the MDS-like structure hidden inside an optimal-distance LRC. After conditioning on all information nodes outside a fixed local group, the symbols in that local group together with the global parity nodes behave as an
(r+\ell+g,r)
-MDS array code.

Proposition III.2

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
\tau\in[u]
be an arbitrary local group. For any
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[g]
,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq[\ell]^{\tau}
, and
\mathcal{D}\subseteq[r]^{\tau}
satisfying

we have

H\!\left(G_{\mathcal{A}},L_{\mathcal{B}},X_{\mathcal{D}}\mid X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)=\bigl(|\mathcal{A}|+|\mathcal{B}|+|\mathcal{D}|\bigr)\alpha.

From Proposition

Corollary III.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
satisfy
|\mathcal{Q}|\leq r

\tau\in[u]

H(G_{\mathcal{Q}})=|\mathcal{Q}|\alpha

and

I\!\left(G_{\mathcal{Q}};X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)=0.

Proof.

By Proposition

, we have
H\!\left(G_{\mathcal{Q}}\mid X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)=|\mathcal{Q}|\alpha.

On the other hand, by Equation (
2

H(G_{\mathcal{Q}})\leq|\mathcal{Q}|\alpha.

Since conditioning cannot increase entropy, we must have
H(G_{\mathcal{Q}})=H\!\left(G_{\mathcal{Q}}\mid X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)=|\mathcal{Q}|\alpha.

Thus
I\!\left(G_{\mathcal{Q}};X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)=0.

∎

Next, we recall another structural property of optimal-distance LRCs: every global parity node must be a function of all information nodes.

Proposition III.3

For any optimal-distance

The preceding propositions summarize the structural properties of optimal-distance LRCs that will be used in the sequel. We now record a general entropy inequality on the conditional mutual information between functions of random variables.

Lemma III.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Let
Z_{1},Z_{2},\ldots,Z_{n}
be a collection of random variables, and let
\{f_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
be a collection of deterministic functions. For any pairwise disjoint subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{D}\subseteq[n]
and any subsets
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
, if the random variables
f_{i}(Z_{i}),i\in(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B})\setminus(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{B}^{\prime})

are conditionally independent given
f_{\mathcal{D}}(Z_{\mathcal{D}})
,
then

I\!\left(f_{\mathcal{A}}(Z_{\mathcal{A}});f_{\mathcal{B}}(Z_{\mathcal{B}})\middle|f_{\mathcal{D}}(Z_{\mathcal{D}})\right)\leq H\!\left(f_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}}(Z_{\mathcal{A}^{\prime}})\middle|f_{\mathcal{D}}(Z_{\mathcal{D}})\right)+H\!\left(f_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}(Z_{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})\middle|f_{\mathcal{D}}(Z_{\mathcal{D}})\right).

The next lemma translates Proposition
III.2
and Lemma
III.1
into averaged mutual-information bounds for deterministic functions of stored nodes. In Section
IV
, these deterministic functions will be instantiated as the data downloaded during conversion.

Lemma III.2
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be an optimal-distance
(k,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC with
g\leq r
. For each
j\in[k]
, let
f_{j}
be a deterministic function of
X_{j}
; for each
a\in[u\ell]
, let
\varphi_{a}
be a deterministic function of
L_{a}
; and for each
i\in[g]
, let
\psi_{i}
be a deterministic function of
G_{i}
.

First, for any fixed local group
\tau\in[u]
,

\displaystyle I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{[r]^{\tau}}(X_{[r]^{\tau}}),\varphi_{[\ell]^{\tau}}(L_{[\ell]^{\tau}})\middle|X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{g+\ell}{r+\ell}\left(\sum_{j\in[r]^{\tau}}H(f_{j}(X_{j}))+\sum_{a\in[\ell]^{\tau}}H(\varphi_{a}(L_{a}))\right).

Second,

\displaystyle I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{[k]}(X_{[k]}),\varphi_{[u\ell]}(L_{[u\ell]})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{g+\ell}{k+u\ell}\left(\sum_{j\in[k]}H(f_{j}(X_{j}))+\sum_{a\in[u\ell]}H(\varphi_{a}(L_{a}))\right).

Proof.

Fix a local group
\tau\in[u]
, and choose subsets
\mathcal{D}\subseteq[r]^{\tau},\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\ell]^{\tau}
such that
|\mathcal{D}|+|\mathcal{A}|=g+\ell
. By Proposition
III.2
, the random variables
G_{[g]},X_{[r]^{\tau}\setminus\mathcal{D}},L_{[\ell]^{\tau}\setminus\mathcal{A}}
are conditionally independent given
X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}
. Therefore, their deterministic functions are also conditionally independent given
X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}
. Applying
Lemma
III.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{[r]^{\tau}}(X_{[r]^{\tau}}),\varphi_{[\ell]^{\tau}}(L_{[\ell]^{\tau}})\middle|X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq H\!\left(f_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{\mathcal{D}}),\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}(L_{\mathcal{A}})\middle|X_{[k]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{D}}H(f_{j}(X_{j}))+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}H(\varphi_{a}(L_{a})).

Averaging this inequality over all choices of
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{D}|+|\mathcal{A}|=g+\ell
, each local node in the
\tau
-th local group appears in
\binom{r+\ell-1}{g+\ell-1}
choices, while the total number of choices is
\binom{r+\ell}{g+\ell}
. Hence the averaging factor is
\frac{\binom{r+\ell-1}{g+\ell-1}}{\binom{r+\ell}{g+\ell}}=\frac{g+\ell}{r+\ell}
. This proves the first inequality.

For the second inequality, we again fix
\mathcal{D}\subseteq[r]^{\tau}
and
\mathcal{A}\subseteq[\ell]^{\tau}
with
|\mathcal{D}|+|\mathcal{A}|=g+\ell
. Let
\overline{\mathcal{D}}=[k]\setminus\mathcal{D},\overline{\mathcal{A}}=[u\ell]\setminus\mathcal{A}
. Since
X_{\mathcal{D}}
and
L_{\mathcal{A}}
form an erasure pattern of size
g+\ell
, the optimal-distance property implies that
X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}},G_{[g]}
determine the whole codeword. Hence

H\!\left(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}},G_{[g]}\right)=k\alpha.

Moreover,

H\!\left(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\right)=(k-g)\alpha.

Indeed, all local groups except the
\tau
-th one contribute
(u-1)r\alpha
entropy, and the
r-g
remaining local nodes in the
\tau
-th local group contribute
(r-g)\alpha
entropy by Proposition
III.2
. Since
g\leq r
, Corollary
III.1
gives
H(G_{[g]})=g\alpha.

Therefore,

\displaystyle H\!\left(G_{[g]}\middle|X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\right)

\displaystyle=H\!\left(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}},G_{[g]}\right)-H\!\left(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\right)

\displaystyle=k\alpha-(k-g)\alpha=g\alpha=H(G_{[g]}).

Thus

I\!\left(G_{[g]};X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}},L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}\right)=0.

By the data-processing inequality,

I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}),\varphi_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}(L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}})\right)=0.

Hence

\displaystyle I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{[k]}(X_{[k]}),\varphi_{[u\ell]}(L_{[u\ell]})\right)

\displaystyle=I\!\left(\psi_{[g]}(G_{[g]});f_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{\mathcal{D}}),\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}(L_{\mathcal{A}})\middle|f_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}(X_{\overline{\mathcal{D}}}),\varphi_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}}(L_{\overline{\mathcal{A}}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq H\!\left(f_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{\mathcal{D}}),\varphi_{\mathcal{A}}(L_{\mathcal{A}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{D}}H(f_{j}(X_{j}))+\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}H(\varphi_{a}(L_{a})).

Finally, average the above inequality over all local groups
\tau\in[u]
and all choices of
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{A}
with
|\mathcal{D}|+|\mathcal{A}|=g+\ell
. The total number of choices is
u\binom{r+\ell}{g+\ell}
, and each local node appears in
\binom{r+\ell-1}{g+\ell-1}
choices. Therefore, the averaging factor is
\frac{\binom{r+\ell-1}{g+\ell-1}}{u\binom{r+\ell}{g+\ell}}=\frac{g+\ell}{u(r+\ell)}=\frac{g+\ell}{k+u\ell}
.
This proves the second inequality.
∎

IV
Lower Bound on the Bandwidth Cost

In this section, we derive lower bounds on the read bandwidth cost of stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;

\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)\text{-LRCC}
in the global split regime. We first identify the node structure of stable conversions in this regime and introduce the random variables used to model the downloaded data. We then establish two entropy constraints on the downloaded data, which lead to the desired read-bandwidth lower bounds.

We first record the following structural property of LRCCs in the global split regime.

Lemma IV.1
.

For any optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRCC, we have:

1.

all initial global parity nodes are retired nodes;

2.

all final global parity nodes are new nodes;

3.

every unchanged initial local parity node must remain a local parity node associated with the same local group in the final codewords.

Proof.

We first prove that all initial global parity nodes are retired in the split regime. Assume to the contrary that there exists an initial global parity node that is not retired. Then this node must be unchanged and hence must appear in one of the final codewords, say the
t
-th final codeword for some
t\in[\lambda^{F}]
. Therefore, it must serve as either a local parity node or a global parity node of this final codeword, and thus it is a function of at most the
k^{F}
information symbols indexed by
[k^{F}]^{t}
. On the other hand, by Proposition
III.3
, every global parity node of the initial codeword must be a function of all
k^{I}
information symbols. Since
k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F}
with
\lambda^{F}\geq 2
, this gives a contradiction. Thus, all initial global parity nodes are retired nodes.

We now show that the global parity nodes of the final codewords can be regarded as newly generated nodes. We first deal with the borderline case
k^{F}=r
. In this case, each final codeword is an
(r+\ell+g^{F},r)
MDS code, and hence the distinction between local parity nodes and global parity nodes is only a matter of labeling. Fix a final codeword, say the
t
-th one. We claim that at most
\ell
parity nodes in this final codeword can be inherited from the initial codeword. Indeed, an inherited parity node cannot come from an initial global parity node by the first part of this proof. Moreover, an initial local parity node associated with a different local group depends only on the information symbols in that different group, and hence cannot be a symbol of the
t
-th final codeword. Thus, the only possible inherited parity nodes are the initial local parity nodes associated with the local group indexed by
[k^{F}]^{t}
, and there are exactly
\ell
such nodes. Therefore, we may label all inherited parity nodes in the
t
-th final codeword as local parity nodes, and label the remaining parity nodes as global parity nodes. Under this labeling, all final global parity nodes are newly generated.

It remains to consider the case
k^{F}>r
. Suppose, for contradiction, that some final global parity node is unchanged, say a global parity node in the
t
-th final codeword for some
t\in[\lambda^{F}]
. Since all information nodes are preserved as information nodes by Remark
II.1
, this unchanged node must have been a parity node in the initial codeword. If it was an initial local parity node, then it is a function of at most
r
information symbols, which contradicts Proposition
III.3
that every global parity node of the final codeword must depend on all
k^{F}
information symbols. If it was an initial global parity node, then it should be retired by the first part of the proof. Consequently, no final global parity node can be unchanged when
k^{F}>r
.

Combining the two cases, we conclude that all final global parity nodes are newly generated nodes.

Finally, we characterize the possible unchanged local parity nodes. By the preceding argument, a local parity node that remains unchanged cannot become a global parity node in any final codeword; hence it must still be used as a local parity node in some final codeword. Furthermore, every local parity node depends on the information nodes in its own local group. Therefore, if a local parity node is unchanged during the conversion, then the set of information nodes on which it depends cannot change. In other words, it must remain a local parity node associated with the same local group in the final codeword as in the initial codeword.
∎

By Remark
II.1
, the
k^{I}
information nodes are reused as the corresponding information nodes in the final codewords. Lemma
IV.1
further shows that no global parity node can be an unchanged node, and that any unchanged local parity node must remain a local parity node associated with the same set of
r
information nodes. Hence, for any optimal-distance LRCC conversion in the global split regime, the number of unchanged nodes is at most
k^{I}+\mu^{I}\ell
. Moreover, equality holds if and only if all local parity nodes are also preserved, or equivalently, if and only if every local group is preserved.

For the global split parameters considered in this paper, this upper bound is attainable; for instance, the construction of Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
preserves all information nodes and all local parity nodes. Therefore, the stability requirement is equivalent to requiring that every local group remain unchanged. In the sequel, we focus on stable optimal-distance LRCCs in this sense: all local groups are preserved, all initial global parity nodes are retired, and all final global parity nodes are new.

Proposition IV.1
.

For each information node
j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]

f_{j}
be a deterministic function of

a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]

be a deterministic function of
L_{a}

For
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[\lambda^{F}]

[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}:=\bigcup_{t\in\mathcal{J}}[k^{F}]^{t},\qquad[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}:=\bigcup_{t\in\mathcal{J}}[u\ell]^{t}.

For
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[\lambda^{F}]
, define

[g^{F}]^{\mathcal{S}}:=\bigcup_{t\in\mathcal{S}}[g^{F}]^{t}.

Then, for any
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{J}\subseteq[\lambda^{F}]

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{\mathcal{S}}}\middle|f_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}(L_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}\middle|f_{[k^{F}]^{t}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{t}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{t}}(L_{[u\ell]^{t}})\right).

Proof.

Since distinct final codewords are defined over disjoint information-node sets, the random vectors

\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}},f_{[k^{F}]^{t}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{t}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{t}}(L_{[u\ell]^{t}})\right),\qquad t\in[\lambda^{F}]

are mutually independent. Enumerate

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{\mathcal{S}}}\middle|f_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}(L_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{m}H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{b}}}\middle|f_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}(L_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}),G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{1}}},\ldots,G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{b-1}}}\right).

For each

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{b}}}\middle|f_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}(X_{[k^{F}]^{\mathcal{J}}}),\varphi_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}(L_{[u\ell]^{\mathcal{J}}}),G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{1}}},\ldots,G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t_{b-1}}}\right)

Summing over

We then establish an entropy constraint on the downloaded data, which will serve as the key ingredient for the following read bandwidth lower bound.

Lemma IV.2
.

If
g^{F}\leq r
, then for any stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRCC, it holds that

H(U_{[g^{I}]})+\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}\left(\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})\right)\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha.

Proof.

By the conversion coordinator property (
3
), all final global parity nodes are determined by the downloaded data
U_{[g^{I}]},V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]},W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}
. Hence

\displaystyle H(U_{[g^{I}]})

\displaystyle\geq H\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]}\mid V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]},W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\right)

\displaystyle\geq I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};G^{F}_{[\lambda^{F}g^{F}]}\mid V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]},W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\right)

\displaystyle=H\!\left(G^{F}_{[\lambda^{F}g^{F}]}\mid V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]},W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\right).

By Proposition
IV.1
, applied with the local-node functions
f_{j}(X_{j})=V_{j}
and
\varphi_{a}(L_{a})=W_{a}
, we have

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{[\lambda^{F}g^{F}]}\mid V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]},W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\right)=\sum_{t\in[\lambda^{F}]}H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}\mid V_{[k^{F}]^{t}},W_{[u\ell]^{t}}\right).

For each final codeword
t
, since
g^{F}\leq r
, Corollary
III.1
gives

H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}\right)=g^{F}\alpha.

Therefore,

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}\mid V_{[k^{F}]^{t}},W_{[u\ell]^{t}}\right)=g^{F}\alpha-I\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}};V_{[k^{F}]^{t}},W_{[u\ell]^{t}}\right).

Applying Lemma
III.2
to the
t
-th final LRC, with
f_{j}(X_{j})=V_{j}
and
\varphi_{a}(L_{a})=W_{a}
, yields

\displaystyle I\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}};V_{[k^{F}]^{t}},W_{[u\ell]^{t}}\right)\leq\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}\left(\sum_{j\in[k^{F}]^{t}}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[u\ell]^{t}}H(W_{a})\right).

Combining the above inequalities and summing over
t\in[\lambda^{F}]
, we get

\displaystyle H(U_{[g^{I}]})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha-\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}\sum_{t\in[\lambda^{F}]}\left(\sum_{j\in[k^{F}]^{t}}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[u\ell]^{t}}H(W_{a})\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha-\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}\left(\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})\right),

where the last equality follows because
\{[k^{F}]^{t}:t\in[\lambda^{F}]\}
partitions
[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]
and
\{[u\ell]^{t}:t\in[\lambda^{F}]\}
partitions
[\lambda^{F}u\ell]
. Rearranging gives the desired inequality.
∎

From this lemma, we can derive the first lower bound on the read bandwidth cost.

Theorem IV.1
.

Let
g^{F}\leq r
. For any stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRCC, the read bandwidth satisfies

\gamma_{R}\geq\lambda^{F}\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha-g^{I}\left(\frac{k^{F}+u\ell}{g^{F}+\ell}-1\right)\alpha.

Proof.

Let
S:=\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a}),T:=H(U_{[g^{I}]}),
and set
c:=\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}.
By the definition of read bandwidth and the entropy bounds on the downloaded data, we have

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[g^{I}]}\sigma_{i}+\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}\beta_{j}+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\delta_{a}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in[g^{I}]}H(U_{i})+\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})

\displaystyle\geq T+S.

By Lemma
IV.2
,

T+cS\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha.

Moreover, since
g^{F}\leq r
and
k^{F}=ur
, we have

c=\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}=\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{u(r+\ell)}\leq 1.

Therefore,

\displaystyle T+S

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}(T+cS)-\left(\frac{1}{c}-1\right)T

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha}{c}-\left(\frac{1}{c}-1\right)T.

Since each initial global parity node has storage capacity
\alpha
(Equation (
2
)), the downloaded data from such a node has entropy at most
\alpha
. Hence

T=H(U_{[g^{I}]})\leq\sum_{i\in[g^{I}]}H(U_{i})\leq g^{I}\alpha.

Combining the above inequalities yields

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle\geq T+S\geq\frac{\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha}{c}-\left(\frac{1}{c}-1\right)g^{I}\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda^{F}\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha-g^{I}\left(\frac{k^{F}+u\ell}{g^{F}+\ell}-1\right)\alpha.

This completes the proof.
∎

Next, we derive a lower bound on

\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a}),

which, together with Lemma
IV.2
, will yield another lower bound on the read bandwidth cost.

Lemma IV.3
.

Suppose that
g^{F}\leq r
,
g^{I}\leq r
. Then, for any stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)\text{-LRCC}
, we have

\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})\geq\frac{\lambda^{F}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}(k^{F}+u\ell)}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha.

Proof.

For each local group
\tau\in[\lambda^{F}u]
, define

R_{\tau}:=\big(V_{[r]^{\tau}},W_{[\ell]^{\tau}}\big),\qquad\Delta_{\tau}:=\sum_{j\in[r]^{\tau}}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\ell]^{\tau}}H(W_{a}).

For
s\in[\lambda^{F}]
and
\tau\in[u]^{s}
, write

X_{s,\bar{\tau}}:=X_{[k^{F}]^{s}\setminus[r]^{\tau}},\qquad X_{\bar{\tau}}:=X_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]\setminus[r]^{\tau}}.

We first record two local mutual-information bounds. Applying Lemma
III.2
to the
s
-th final LRC gives, for every
\tau\in[u]^{s}
, we have

I\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{s}};R_{\tau}\mid X_{s,\bar{\tau}}\right)\leq\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau}.

(4)

Applying Lemma
III.2
to the initial LRC, with
\psi_{i}(G_{i}^{I})=U_{i}
, gives

I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};R_{\tau}\mid X_{\bar{\tau}}\right)\leq\frac{g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau}.

(5)

Now fix a tuple

\mathcal{T}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{\lambda^{F}}),\qquad\tau_{w}\in[u]^{w},

and define

B_{\mathcal{T}}:=\big\{X_{w,\bar{\tau}_{w}},R_{\tau_{w}}:w\in[\lambda^{F}]\big\}.

Since each local parity node is determined by the information nodes in its own local group,
B_{\mathcal{T}}
determines
V_{[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}
and
W_{[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}
.
Fix
s\in[\lambda^{F}]
, and denote by

G^{F}_{\bar{s}}:=G^{F}_{\bigcup_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}[g^{F}]^{w}}

the collection of final global parities outside the
s
-th final codeword. By the conversion coordinator property (
3
),

H\!\left(G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid U_{[g^{I}]},B_{\mathcal{T}}\right)=0.

Therefore,

H\!\left(G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid B_{\mathcal{T}}\right)=I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid B_{\mathcal{T}}\right).

(6)

We lower bound the left-hand side of (
6
). By Proposition
IV.1
,

\displaystyle H\!\left(G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid B_{\mathcal{T}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{w}}\mid X_{w,\bar{\tau}_{w}},R_{\tau_{w}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}\Big[H\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{w}}\mid X_{w,\bar{\tau}_{w}}\right)-I\!\left(G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{w}};R_{\tau_{w}}\mid X_{w,\bar{\tau}_{w}}\right)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}\left(g^{F}\alpha-\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{w}}\right),

where the last step follows from Corollary
III.1
applied to the
w
-th final LRC and from (
4
).

We next upper bound the right-hand side of (
6
). Since
(G^{F}_{\bar{s}},B_{\mathcal{T}})
is a deterministic function of
(X_{\bar{\tau}_{s}},R_{\tau_{s}})
, the data-processing inequality gives

\displaystyle I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid B_{\mathcal{T}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};G^{F}_{\bar{s}},B_{\mathcal{T}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};X_{\bar{\tau}_{s}},R_{\tau_{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};X_{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\right)+I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};R_{\tau_{s}}\mid X_{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\right).

By Corollary
III.1
applied to the initial LRC and by data processing inequality,

I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};X_{\bar{\tau}_{s}}\right)=0.

Combining this with (
5
), we obtain

I\!\left(U_{[g^{I}]};G^{F}_{\bar{s}}\mid B_{T}\right)\leq\frac{g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{s}}.

Putting the preceding lower and upper bounds into (
6
), for every
s\in[\lambda^{F}]
and every tuple
\mathcal{T}
, we have

\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}\left(g^{F}\alpha-\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{w}}\right)\leq\frac{g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{s}}.

Equivalently,

\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]\setminus\{s\}}\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{w}}+\frac{g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}\Delta_{\tau_{s}}\geq(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}\alpha.

(7)

Finally, sum (
7
) over all
s\in[\lambda^{F}]
and all tuples
\mathcal{T}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{\lambda^{F}})
, where
\tau_{w}\in[u]^{w}
. For a fixed final codeword
w
and a fixed local group
\tau\in[u]^{w}
, the term
\Delta_{\tau}
appears in
u^{\lambda^{F}-1}
tuples. Its total coefficient over all choices of
s
is
\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}
. Thus,

u^{\lambda^{F}-1}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{r+\ell}\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]}\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{w}}\Delta_{\tau}\geq\lambda^{F}u^{\lambda^{F}}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}\alpha.

Since

\sum_{w\in[\lambda^{F}]}\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{w}}\Delta_{\tau}=\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a}),

we get

\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})\geq\frac{\lambda^{F}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}u(r+\ell)}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha.

Using
u(r+\ell)=k^{F}+u\ell
, the desired bound follows.
∎

Now we use Lemma
IV.2
and Lemma
IV.3
to derive a lower bound on the read bandwidth cost of stable optimal-distance LRCCs in the split regime in the following theorem.

Theorem IV.2
.

Let
g^{F}\leq r
and
g^{I}\leq r
. For any stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRCC, the read bandwidth satisfies

\gamma_{R}\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(k^{F}+u\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha.

Proof.

Let

S:=\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a}),\qquad T:=H(U_{[g^{I}]}).

By the definition of read bandwidth and the entropy bounds on the downloaded data,

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[g^{I}]}\sigma_{i}+\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}\beta_{j}+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}\delta_{a}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in[g^{I}]}H(U_{i})+\sum_{j\in[\lambda^{F}k^{F}]}H(V_{j})+\sum_{a\in[\lambda^{F}u\ell]}H(W_{a})

\displaystyle\geq T+S.

Set
c=(g^{F}+\ell)/(k^{F}+u\ell)
. Since
g^{F}\leq r
and
k^{F}=ur
, we have
c\leq 1
. Hence

\gamma_{R}\geq T+S=T+cS+(1-c)S.

By Lemma
IV.2
,

T+cS\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha.

Moreover, by Lemma
IV.3
,

S\geq\frac{\lambda^{F}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}(k^{F}+u\ell)}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha.

Combining the above inequalities gives

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle\geq\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha+\left(1-\frac{g^{F}+\ell}{k^{F}+u\ell}\right)\frac{\lambda^{F}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}(k^{F}+u\ell)}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda^{F}g^{F}\alpha+\frac{\lambda^{F}(\lambda^{F}-1)g^{F}(k^{F}+u\ell-g^{F}-\ell)}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(k^{F}+u\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha.

This completes the proof.
∎

Combining Theorems
IV.1
and
IV.2
, we finally get our lower bound on the read bandwidth cost.

Theorem IV.3
.

Let
g^{F}\leq r
and
g^{I}\leq r
. For any stable optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda^{F}k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell;\;k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell)
-LRCC, the read bandwidth satisfies

\gamma_{R}\geq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(k^{F}+u\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha,&\text{if }g^{F}\leq g^{I},\\[9.47217pt]
\displaystyle\lambda^{F}\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha-g^{I}\left(\frac{k^{F}+u\ell}{g^{F}+\ell}-1\right)\alpha,&\text{if }g^{F}>g^{I}.\end{cases}

Proof.

By the preceding two theorems, we have

\gamma_{R}\geq A\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{R}\geq B,

where

A:=\lambda^{F}g^{F}\frac{(\lambda^{F}-1)(k^{F}+u\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}{(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell}\alpha

and

B:=\lambda^{F}\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha-g^{I}\left(\frac{k^{F}+u\ell}{g^{F}+\ell}-1\right)\alpha.

Thus
\gamma_{R}\geq\max\{A,B\}
. It remains only to compare
A
and
B
. A direct calculation gives

B-A=\frac{(g^{F}-g^{I})(\lambda^{F}\ell+g^{I})(k^{F}+u\ell-g^{F}-\ell)}{(g^{F}+\ell)\bigl((\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}+\ell\bigr)}\alpha.

Since
k^{F}+u\ell=u(r+\ell)
and
g^{F}\leq r
, we have
k^{F}+u\ell-g^{F}-\ell\geq 0
. The remaining factors in the denominator are positive, and
\lambda^{F}\ell+g^{I}\geq 0
. Therefore, the sign of
B-A
is determined by
g^{F}-g^{I}
. Hence
A\geq B
when
g^{F}\leq g^{I}
, while
B\geq A
when
g^{F}\geq g^{I}
. Consequently, when
g^{F}\leq g^{I}
, the stronger lower bound is
A
, and when
g^{F}>g^{I}
, the stronger lower bound is
B
. This gives the desired piecewise bound. ∎

Remark IV.1
.

Theorem
IV.3
is stated for the unnormalized read bandwidth
\gamma_{R}
. Dividing both sides by
\alpha
gives the corresponding lower bound on the normalized read bandwidth
\widetilde{\gamma}_{R}=\gamma_{R}/\alpha
. In the next section, we show that this normalized lower bound is attainable over sufficiently large finite fields.

Remark IV.2
.

Let
\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{MR}}
denote the read bandwidth achieved by the global split constructions of Maturana and Rashmi
[
19
]
, and let
\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{LB}}
denote the right-hand side of the preceding theorem. Set

S:=k^{F}+u\ell,\qquad D:=(\lambda^{F}-1)(g^{F}+\ell)+g^{I}.

A direct subtraction gives

\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{MR}}-\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{LB}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\lambda^{F}g^{F}\ell(\lambda^{F}-1)(S-g^{F}-\ell)}{D(D+\ell)}\alpha,&g^{F}\leq g^{I},\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\frac{g^{I}\ell(\lambda^{F}g^{F}-g^{I})(S-g^{F}-\ell)}{(g^{F}+\ell)\bigl(\lambda^{F}g^{F}(g^{F}+\ell)-g^{I}\ell\bigr)}\alpha,&g^{F}>g^{I}.\end{cases}

Since
k^{F}=ur
and
g^{F}\leq r
,

S-g^{F}-\ell=(u-1)(r+\ell)+(r-g^{F})\geq 0.

Therefore,
\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{MR}}\geq\gamma_{R}^{\mathrm{LB}}
. Under the standing assumptions
\lambda^{F}\geq 2
and
g^{I},\ell>0
, equality holds if and only if
u=1
and
g^{F}=r
; otherwise, the inequality is strict. Since, in the next section, we will present constructions that attain the lower bound in the preceding theorem, the global split constructions of Maturana and Rashmi are not bandwidth-optimal in general in this parameter regime.

Set

. Throughout this subsection, we assume

Lemma V.1
.

Let
r,\ell,g
be positive integers and set
\alpha=g+\ell
. Over any finite field admitting a
(g+\ell)\times r
superregular matrix, there exists a systematic
[r+\ell+g,r,\alpha]
MDS array code with nodes

X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},\quad L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell},\quad P_{1},\ldots,P_{g}

such that
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g}
can be optimally repaired from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
by downloading the first
g
subsymbols from each helper node. In particular, it is sufficient to take
q\geq r+g+\ell
, since in this case an
(g+\ell)\times r
Cauchy matrix exists and is superregular.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=(\gamma_{\rho,i})_{\rho\in[g+\ell],\,i\in[r]}
be a
(g+\ell)\times r
superregular matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
i\in[r]
, let
X_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,g+\ell})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g+\ell}
. For each
\rho\in[g+\ell]
, define
Q_{\rho}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{\rho,i}X_{i}=(q_{\rho,1},\ldots,q_{\rho,g+\ell})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g+\ell}
. Thus
q_{\rho,c}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{\rho,i}x_{i,c}
for
c\in[g+\ell]
. Fix
\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Define the local parity nodes by
L_{b}:=Q_{b}+\eta\sum_{h=1}^{g}q_{\ell+h,g+b}e_{h}
,
b\in[\ell]
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{g+\ell}
are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g+\ell}
, and define the global parity nodes by
P_{h}:=Q_{\ell+h}
,
h\in[g]
.

We first prove that the resulting array code is MDS. For every coordinate
c\in[g+\ell]
, before adding the piggyback terms, the systematic scalar code with information symbols
x_{1,c},\ldots,x_{r,c}
and parity symbols
q_{1,c},\ldots,q_{g+\ell,c}
is an
[r+g+\ell,r]
MDS code, because
\Gamma
is superregular.

Fix any set of
r
surviving nodes. We first decode the tail coordinates
g+1,\ldots,g+\ell
. These coordinates contain no piggyback terms: the
c
-th coordinate of each surviving information node is
x_{i,c}
, the
c
-th coordinate of each surviving local parity node
L_{b}
is
q_{b,c}
, and the
c
-th coordinate of each surviving global parity node
P_{s}
is
q_{\ell+s,c}
. Hence, for each
c\in\{g+1,\ldots,g+\ell\}
, the
r
surviving subsymbols are exactly
r
symbols of the
c
-th scalar MDS instance. Thus all information subsymbols
x_{i,c}
,
i\in[r]
, are recovered for
c=g+1,\ldots,g+\ell
. Consequently, all values
q_{\ell+h,g+b}
,
h\in[g]
,
b\in[\ell]
, are known.

We then decode the front coordinates
1,\ldots,g
. Fix
h\in[g]
. In coordinate
h
, the only piggybacked symbols are the local parity subsymbols
q_{b,h}+\eta q_{\ell+h,g+b}
,
b\in[\ell]
. Since
q_{\ell+h,g+b}
is already known from the decoded tail coordinates, the decoder subtracts
\eta q_{\ell+h,g+b}
from every surviving local parity subsymbol in coordinate
h
. After this subtraction, the
r
surviving subsymbols in coordinate
h
are exactly
r
symbols of the
h
-th scalar MDS instance. Hence all information subsymbols
x_{i,h}
,
i\in[r]
, are recovered. Repeating this for every
h\in[g]
, we recover all front coordinates as well. Therefore any
r
surviving nodes determine the whole message, and the array code is MDS.

It remains to verify the repair property. Let
S:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g+\ell}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g}
be the projection onto the first
g
coordinates. The repair downloads
SX_{i}
from each
X_{i}
and
SL_{b}
from each
L_{b}
. From the systematic downloads, one can compute
SQ_{\rho}=(q_{\rho,1},\ldots,q_{\rho,g})^{\top}
for all
\rho\in[g+\ell]
. Hence the first
g
coordinates of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g}
are known. Moreover, for each
b\in[\ell]
,

SL_{b}-SQ_{b}=\eta\bigl(q_{\ell+1,g+b},\ldots,q_{\ell+g,g+b}\bigr)^{\top}.

Since
\eta\neq 0
, this recovers the
(g+b)
-th coordinates of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g}
. As
b
ranges over
[\ell]
, all remaining coordinates of
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g}
are recovered. The total download is
g(r+\ell)
, which equals the centralized repair cut-set bound

\frac{g(r+\ell)}{g+(r+\ell)-r}\alpha=g(r+\ell),

where
\alpha=g+\ell
. Thus the repair is optimal.
∎

We now construct the LRCC. For each
t\in[\lambda]
, the local groups of the
t
-th final codeword are indexed by
[u]^{t}
. For
\tau\in[u]^{t}
, let
\iota(\tau):=\tau-(t-1)u\in[u]
, which denotes the local index of the group
\tau
inside the
t
-th final codeword.

Throughout this construction, each node is viewed as a column vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. For each global local group
\tau\in[\lambda u]
, take an independent copy of the MDS array code in Lemma
V.1
. The
r
information nodes of this copy are
X_{[r]^{\tau}}
, and the
\ell
local parity nodes are
L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}
. The same copy also produces
g
virtual global parity nodes
P_{\tau,1},\ldots,P_{\tau,g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
, which are stacked as

\mathbf{P}_{\tau}:=(P_{\tau,1}^{\top},\ldots,P_{\tau,g}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

Now we choose some mixing matrices from the block-scalar diagonal subfamily. More precisely, for
c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, let
D(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}):=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1}I_{\alpha},\ldots,c_{g}I_{\alpha})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.
We choose
A_{i}=D(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,g}),i\in[u],B_{t}=D(b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,g}),t\in[\lambda],
where all
a_{i,h}
and
b_{t,h}
are nonzero. These matrices will be used below to make both the initial code and the final code optimal-distance LRCs. The existence of such choices over sufficiently large finite fields follows from the generic determinant argument in Appendix
A
.

For the
t
-th final codeword, define the stacked vector of its
g
global parity nodes by

\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{t}}:=\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{t}}A_{\iota(\tau)}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

Writing

\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{t}}=\big((G^{F}_{(t-1)g+1})^{\top},\ldots,(G^{F}_{tg})^{\top}\big)^{\top},\qquad G^{F}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha},

the
g
consecutive
\alpha
-dimensional blocks
G^{F}_{i}
,
i\in[g]^{t}
, are stored as the
g
final global parity nodes of the
t
-th final codeword. Thus the
t
-th final codeword consists of the information nodes
X_{[k^{F}]^{t}}
, the local parity nodes
L_{[u\ell]^{t}}
, and the global parity nodes
G^{F}_{[g]^{t}}
. All the final codewords are codewords of the same final LRC and this final code is an optimal-distance
(k^{F},g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC by the choice of the matrices
A_{i}
’s and
B_{t}
’s.

The initial codeword contains all
\lambda u
local groups and we write the stacked vector of its
g
initial global parity nodes as

\mathbf{G}^{I}_{[g]}:=\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}B_{t}\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{t}}=\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{t}}B_{t}A_{\iota(\tau)}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

Writing

\mathbf{G}^{I}_{[g]}=\big((G^{I}_{1})^{\top},\ldots,(G^{I}_{g})^{\top}\big)^{\top},\qquad G^{I}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha},

the
g
consecutive
\alpha
-dimensional blocks
G^{I}_{1},\ldots,G^{I}_{g}
are stored as the
g
initial global parity nodes. By the choice of the matrices
A_{i}
’s and
B_{t}
’s, the initial code is an optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda k^{F},g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC.

We next describe the conversion procedure. All information nodes and local parity nodes are preserved. The coordinator first downloads all
g
initial global parity nodes, equivalently the whole stacked vector
\mathbf{G}^{I}_{[g]}
, which costs
g\alpha
symbols. Then, for each
t\in[\lambda-1]
and
\tau\in[u]^{t}

V.1
to recover
\mathbf{P}_{\tau}
from the preserved local nodes
X_{[r]^{\tau}}
and
L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}

\frac{g(r+\ell)}{g+\ell}\alpha
symbols for each local group. After obtaining all
\mathbf{P}_{\tau}
with
\tau\in[u]^{t}
, the coordinator computes

\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{t}}=\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{t}}A_{\iota(\tau)}\mathbf{P}_{\tau},\qquad t\in[\lambda-1].

Finally, since
B_{\lambda}
is invertible, the stacked vector of the last final global parity nodes is obtained as

\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{\lambda}}=B_{\lambda}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{G}^{I}_{[g]}-\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda-1}B_{t}\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{t}}\right).

The vector
\mathbf{G}^{F}_{[g]^{\lambda}}
is then split into its
g

\alpha
-dimensional blocks, which are stored as the global parity nodes
G^{F}_{[g]^{\lambda}}
of the last final codeword.

Therefore, the read bandwidth is

\gamma_{R}=g\alpha+(\lambda-1)u\frac{g(r+\ell)}{g+\ell}\alpha=g\alpha+(\lambda-1)\frac{g}{g+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha.

When
g^{F}=g^{I}=g
, the lower bound in Theorem
IV.3
reduces to this quantity. Hence the construction attains the optimal read bandwidth in the case
g^{F}=g^{I}
.

General Construction

Set
\lambda:=\lambda^{F}
and
\alpha:=g^{F}+\ell
. Throughout this subsection, we assume
g^{I}<g^{F}\leq r
and
u=k^{F}/r
. Although one may use MDS array codes with universal optimal-repair properties, such as those guaranteed by Theorem
II.2
, the present case only requires two fixed repair patterns. In the first, all
g^{F}
global parity nodes are optimally repaired from the
r+\ell
local nodes. In the second, the last
g^{F}-g^{I}
global parity nodes are optimally repaired from the same local nodes together with the first
g^{I}
global parity nodes. We therefore first give a small-subpacketization construction tailored to these two repair patterns, using one layer of local-parity piggybacks and one layer of head-global piggybacks.

Lemma V.2
.

Let
r,\ell,g^{I},g^{F}
be positive integers with
g^{I}<g^{F}\leq r
, and set
\alpha=g^{F}+\ell
. Over any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
admitting a
(g^{F}+\ell)\times r
superregular matrix, there exists a systematic
[r+\ell+g^{F},r,\alpha]
MDS array code with nodes

X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},\quad L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell},\quad P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}

such that
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}
can be optimally repaired from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
by downloading the first
g^{F}
subsymbols from each helper node. Moreover,
P_{g^{I}+1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}
can be optimally repaired from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell},P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{I}}
by downloading from each helper node the
g^{F}-g^{I}
subsymbols in coordinate positions
g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
. In particular, if
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{I}}
are already known as side information, then
P_{g^{I}+1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}
can be recovered by downloading only these
g^{F}-g^{I}
subsymbols from each of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
. It is sufficient to take
q\geq r+g^{F}+\ell
, since in this case a
(g^{F}+\ell)\times r
Cauchy matrix exists and is superregular.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma=(\gamma_{\rho,i})_{\rho\in[g^{F}+\ell],\,i\in[r]}
be a
(g^{F}+\ell)\times r
superregular matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For
i\in[r]
, let
X_{i}=(x_{i,1},\ldots,x_{i,\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. For each
\rho\in[g^{F}+\ell]
, define
Q_{\rho}:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{\rho,i}X_{i}=(q_{\rho,1},\ldots,q_{\rho,\alpha})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. Thus
q_{\rho,c}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{\rho,i}x_{i,c}
for
c\in[\alpha]
.

Fix
\eta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. Define the local parity nodes by
L_{b}:=Q_{b}+\eta\sum_{h=1}^{g^{F}}q_{\ell+h,g^{F}+b}e_{h}
,
b\in[\ell]
, where
e_{1},\ldots,e_{\alpha}
are the standard basis vectors of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. For
j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
, define
P_{j}:=Q_{\ell+j}
. For
h\in[g^{I}]
, define
P_{h}:=Q_{\ell+h}+\sum_{j=g^{I}+1}^{g^{F}}q_{\ell+j,h}e_{j}
. Thus
P_{h}[j]=q_{\ell+h,j}+q_{\ell+j,h}
for
j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
, while
P_{h}[c]=q_{\ell+h,c}
for all other coordinates
c
.

We first prove that the resulting array code is MDS. For every coordinate
c\in[\alpha]
, before adding the piggyback terms, the systematic scalar code with information symbols
x_{1,c},\ldots,x_{r,c}
and parity symbols
q_{1,c},\ldots,q_{g^{F}+\ell,c}
is an
[r+g^{F}+\ell,r]
MDS code, because
\Gamma
is superregular.

Fix any set of
r
surviving nodes. We first decode the last
\ell
coordinates
g^{F}+1,\ldots,g^{F}+\ell
. These coordinates contain no piggyback terms: the
c
-th coordinate of each surviving information node is
x_{i,c}
, that of
L_{b}
is
q_{b,c}
, and that of
P_{h}
is
q_{\ell+h,c}
. Hence, for each
c\in\{g^{F}+1,\ldots,g^{F}+\ell\}
, the
r
surviving subsymbols are exactly
r
symbols of the
c
-th scalar MDS instance. Thus all information subsymbols
x_{i,c}
,
i\in[r]
, are recovered in these coordinates. Consequently, all values
q_{\ell+h,g^{F}+b}
,
h\in[g^{F}]
,
b\in[\ell]
, are known.

We next decode coordinates
1,\ldots,g^{I}
. Fix
h\in[g^{I}]
. In coordinate
h
, the only piggybacked symbols are the local parity subsymbols
q_{b,h}+\eta q_{\ell+h,g^{F}+b}
,
b\in[\ell]
. Since
q_{\ell+h,g^{F}+b}
is already known from the decoded last
\ell
coordinates, the decoder subtracts
\eta q_{\ell+h,g^{F}+b}
from every surviving local parity subsymbol in coordinate
h
. After this subtraction, the
r
surviving subsymbols are exactly
r
symbols of the
h
-th scalar MDS instance. Hence all information subsymbols
x_{i,h}
,
i\in[r]
, are recovered. Repeating this for every
h\in[g^{I}]
recovers coordinates
1,\ldots,g^{I}
.

Finally, consider a coordinate
j\in\{g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}\}
. The piggybacked local parity subsymbols in this coordinate are
q_{b,j}+\eta q_{\ell+j,g^{F}+b}
,
b\in[\ell]
, where the added terms are known from the last
\ell
coordinates. Moreover, for each
h\in[g^{I}]
, the
j
-th subsymbol of the head global parity node is
q_{\ell+h,j}+q_{\ell+j,h}
, where
q_{\ell+j,h}
is known from the previously decoded coordinate
h
. Hence all piggyback terms in coordinate
j
can be subtracted. The remaining
r
surviving subsymbols are exactly
r
symbols of the
j
-th scalar MDS instance and therefore determine all information subsymbols in this coordinate. Repeating this for
j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
recovers the whole message. Therefore any
r
surviving nodes determine all information symbols, and the array code is MDS.

It remains to verify the two repair properties. Let
S:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g^{F}}
be the projection onto the first
g^{F}
coordinates. For the first repair, download
SX_{i}
from each
X_{i}
and
SL_{b}
from each
L_{b}
. From the systematic downloads, one can compute
SQ_{\rho}=(q_{\rho,1},\ldots,q_{\rho,g^{F}})^{\top}
for every
\rho\in[g^{F}+\ell]
. Moreover, for each
b\in[\ell]
,

SL_{b}-SQ_{b}=\eta\bigl(q_{\ell+1,g^{F}+b},\ldots,q_{\ell+g^{F},g^{F}+b}\bigr)^{\top}.

Since
\eta\neq 0
, this recovers the
(g^{F}+b)
-th coordinates of
Q_{\ell+1},\ldots,Q_{\ell+g^{F}}
. As
b
ranges over
[\ell]
, all remaining coordinates of these vectors are recovered. Therefore
Q_{\ell+1},\ldots,Q_{\ell+g^{F}}
, and hence
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}
, are completely determined. The total download is
g^{F}(r+\ell)
, which equals the centralized repair cut-set bound

\frac{g^{F}(r+\ell)}{g^{F}+(r+\ell)-r}\alpha=g^{F}(r+\ell),

where
\alpha=g^{F}+\ell
. Thus the first repair is optimal.

For the second repair, suppose that
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{I}}
are available as helper nodes. From every helper node, download the
g^{F}-g^{I}
subsymbols in coordinate positions
g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
. Fix
j\in\{g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}\}
. From the systematic downloads, one can compute
q_{\rho,c}
for every
\rho\in[g^{F}+\ell]
and every
c\in\{g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}\}
. In particular, the coordinates
q_{\ell+j,c}
,
c=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
, of
P_{j}=Q_{\ell+j}
are known.

For each
b\in[\ell]
, the downloaded local parity subsymbol is
L_{b}[j]=q_{b,j}+\eta q_{\ell+j,g^{F}+b}
. Since
q_{b,j}
is computable from the systematic downloads, this determines
q_{\ell+j,g^{F}+b}
. Hence the last
\ell
coordinates of
Q_{\ell+j}
are known. Finally, for every
h\in[g^{I}]
, the downloaded subsymbol of
P_{h}
is
P_{h}[j]=q_{\ell+h,j}+q_{\ell+j,h}
. Since
q_{\ell+h,j}
is computable from the systematic downloads, this determines
q_{\ell+j,h}
. Thus the first
g^{I}
coordinates of
Q_{\ell+j}
are also known. It follows that
P_{j}=Q_{\ell+j}
is completely recovered. Repeating this argument for every
j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
recovers all the desired parity nodes.

The second repair downloads
g^{F}-g^{I}
subsymbols from each of
r+\ell+g^{I}
helper nodes, for a total of
(g^{F}-g^{I})(r+\ell+g^{I})
subsymbols. This equals the centralized repair cut-set bound

\frac{(g^{F}-g^{I})(r+\ell+g^{I})}{(g^{F}-g^{I})+(r+\ell+g^{I})-r}\alpha=(g^{F}-g^{I})(r+\ell+g^{I}).

Thus the second repair is also optimal.
∎

We now construct the LRCC. Set
\lambda:=\lambda^{F}
and
u:=k^{F}/r
. Recall that
\alpha=g^{F}+\ell
. For each
t\in[\lambda]
, the local groups of the
t
-th final codeword are indexed by
[u]^{t}
. For
\tau\in[u]^{t}
, let
\iota(\tau):=\tau-(t-1)u\in[u]
, which denotes the local index of the group
\tau
inside the
t
-th final codeword.

Throughout the construction, each node is viewed as a column vector in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. For every local group
\tau\in[\lambda u]
, take an independent copy of the construction in Lemma
V.2
. Its information and local parity nodes are
X_{[r]^{\tau}}
and
L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}
, respectively. For
h\in[g^{F}]
, let
P_{\tau,h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
denote the body virtual global parity vector corresponding to
Q_{\ell+h}
in the proof of Lemma
V.2
. Thus the local parity piggybacks are already included in
L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}
, whereas the head-global piggybacks will be added after mixing the local groups. Write

\mathbf{P}_{\tau}:=\bigl(P_{\tau,1}^{\top},\ldots,P_{\tau,g^{F}}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g^{F}\alpha}.

We next choose the mixing matrices. Let
\mathcal{C}_{0}
denote the unpiggybacked MDS array code underlying Lemma
V.2
; namely, its local parity nodes are
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{\ell}
, and its virtual global parity nodes are
Q_{\ell+1},\ldots,Q_{\ell+g^{F}}
. For
c_{1},\ldots,c_{g^{F}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, define

D(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g^{F}}):=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(c_{1}I_{\alpha},\ldots,c_{g^{F}}I_{\alpha}\bigr).

Applying Lemma
A.1
to
\mathcal{C}_{0}
with the parameter
g
in Appendix
A
instantiated as
g^{F}
, over a sufficiently large finite field we can choose

A_{i}=D(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,g^{F}}),\quad i\in[u],\qquad B_{t}=D(b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,g^{F}}),\quad t\in[\lambda],

where all
a_{i,h}
and
b_{t,h}
are nonzero, such that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}\bigl(B_{t}A_{i}:t\in[\lambda],\,i\in[u]\bigr)
are optimal-distance LRCs. Here and below, these two body codes are formed from the unpiggybacked MDS array code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

We first define the final code. For each
t\in[\lambda]
, let

\mathbf{Y}_{t}:=\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{t}}A_{\iota(\tau)}\mathbf{P}_{\tau}=\bigl(Y_{t,1}^{\top},\ldots,Y_{t,g^{F}}^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g^{F}\alpha},

where
Y_{t,h}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
. For

h\in[g^{F}]
, define the corresponding final global parity node by

G^{F}_{(t-1)g^{F}+h}:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle Y_{t,h}+\sum_{j=g^{I}+1}^{g^{F}}Y_{t,j}[h]e_{j},&h\in[g^{I}],\\[5.69054pt]
Y_{t,h},&h=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F},\end{cases}

where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}
.

For
h\in[g^{I}]
, the preceding definition can equivalently be written as

G^{F}_{(t-1)g^{F}+h}=\sum_{\tau\in[u]^{t}}a_{\iota(\tau),h}\left(P_{\tau,h}+\sum_{j=g^{I}+1}^{g^{F}}\frac{a_{\iota(\tau),j}}{a_{\iota(\tau),h}}P_{\tau,j}[h]e_{j}\right).

Thus, before the
h
-th virtual global parity vector of a local group is multiplied by
a_{\iota(\tau),h}
, its piggyback from
P_{\tau,j}[h]
is assigned the coefficient
a_{\iota(\tau),j}/a_{\iota(\tau),h}
. After multiplication, this term has coefficient
a_{\iota(\tau),j}
, which is exactly the coefficient of
P_{\tau,j}
in
Y_{t,j}
. Consequently,

G^{F}_{(t-1)g^{F}+h}[j]=Y_{t,h}[j]+Y_{t,j}[h],\qquad h\in[g^{I}],\quad j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}.

This is the coefficient-compatible head-global piggyback required by the conversion.

The
t
-th final codeword consists of the information nodes
X_{[k^{F}]^{t}}
, the local parity nodes
L_{[u\ell]^{t}}
, and the global parity nodes
G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}
. Since the same matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{u}
and the same coefficient-compatible piggyback rule are used for every
t
, all final codewords are codewords of the same final LRC.

We next define the initial code. It contains all
\lambda u
local groups and
g^{I}
global parity nodes. For
h\in[g^{I}]
, define

G^{I}_{h}:=\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}b_{t,h}G^{F}_{(t-1)g^{F}+h}.

After removing the head-global piggybacks, the body part of
G^{I}_{h}
is
\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}b_{t,h}Y_{t,h}
, whose coefficient on
P_{\tau,h}
, for
\tau\in[u]^{t}
, is
b_{t,h}a_{\iota(\tau),h}
. Hence the
g^{I}
initial body global parity nodes are precisely the first
g^{I}
global parity nodes obtained by puncturing
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(B_{t}A_{i}:t\in[\lambda],\,i\in[u])
. By construction, all information nodes and all local parity nodes are preserved, and therefore the conversion is stable.

We now verify that the initial and final codes are optimal-distance LRCs. Before adding either piggyback layer, the final body code is
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
, and the full
g^{F}
-global initial body code is
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}(B_{t}A_{i}:t\in[\lambda],\,i\in[u])
. Both are optimal-distance by Lemma
A.1
. Puncturing the last
g^{F}-g^{I}
global parity nodes from the latter gives a body initial code whose minimum distance is at least
g^{F}+\ell+1-(g^{F}-g^{I})=g^{I}+\ell+1.
The Singleton-type bound for a
(\lambda k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC gives the reverse inequality, so the punctured initial body code is also optimal-distance.

It remains to restore the two piggyback layers. The local parity piggybacks add symbols from coordinate
g^{F}+b
to coordinate
h
, for
b\in[\ell]
and
h\in[g^{F}]
. The head-global piggybacks add symbols depending on coordinate
h\in[g^{I}]
to coordinate
j\in\{g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}\}
. Hence all dependencies are acyclic under the coordinate order
g^{F}+1,\ldots,g^{F}+\ell,\quad 1,\ldots,g^{I},\quad g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}.
The unpiggybacked code
\mathcal{C}_{0}
has no cross-coordinate coupling, and the matrices
A_{i}
and
B_{t}
are block-scalar. Therefore, for any correctable node-erasure pattern, one first decodes coordinates
g^{F}+1,\ldots,g^{F}+\ell
, then subtracts the known local parity piggybacks and decodes coordinates
1,\ldots,g^{I}
, and finally subtracts both the local parity and head-global piggybacks before decoding coordinates
g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
. Thus neither piggyback layer changes the erasure-correcting capability of the corresponding body code. Consequently, the final code is an optimal-distance
(k^{F},g^{F},r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC, and the initial code is an optimal-distance
(k^{I}=\lambda k^{F},g^{I},r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC.

We now describe the conversion procedure. The coordinator first downloads all initial global parity nodes
G^{I}_{1},\ldots,G^{I}_{g^{I}}
, which costs
g^{I}\alpha
symbols.

Next, for each
t\in[\lambda-1]
and every
\tau\in[u]^{t}
, the coordinator downloads the first
g^{F}
subsymbols from every node in
X_{[r]^{\tau}},L_{[\ell]^{\tau}}
. As shown in the proof of Lemma
V.2
, these downloads recover all the body virtual global parity vectors
P_{\tau,1},\ldots,P_{\tau,g^{F}}
. The coordinator can therefore compute
\mathbf{Y}_{t}
and then form all global parity nodes
G^{F}_{[g^{F}]^{t}}
. The read cost of this step is

(\lambda-1)ug^{F}(r+\ell)=(\lambda-1)\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha.

It remains to generate the last final codeword. For every
h\in[g^{I}]
, since
b_{\lambda,h}\neq 0
, the coordinator first obtains

G^{F}_{(\lambda-1)g^{F}+h}=b_{\lambda,h}^{-1}\left(G^{I}_{h}-\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda-1}b_{t,h}G^{F}_{(t-1)g^{F}+h}\right).

Thus the first
g^{I}
global parity nodes of the last final codeword are known.

To recover the remaining global parity nodes, the coordinator downloads from each node in
X_{[k^{F}]^{\lambda}},L_{[u\ell]^{\lambda}}
the
g^{F}-g^{I}
subsymbols in coordinate positions
g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
. Fix
j\in\{g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}\}
. The downloaded information-node subsymbols determine
Y_{\lambda,j}[c]
for
c=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
, and also determine
Y_{\lambda,h}[j]
for every
h\in[g^{I}]
. The downloaded local parity subsymbols, by the local-piggyback relation in Lemma
V.2
, determine
Y_{\lambda,j}[g^{F}+1],\ldots,Y_{\lambda,j}[g^{F}+\ell]
. Finally, for every
h\in[g^{I}]
, the already recovered head global parity node satisfies

G^{F}_{(\lambda-1)g^{F}+h}[j]=Y_{\lambda,h}[j]+Y_{\lambda,j}[h],

and hence determines
Y_{\lambda,j}[h]
. Therefore the whole vector
Y_{\lambda,j}
, and thus
G^{F}_{(\lambda-1)g^{F}+j}=Y_{\lambda,j}
, is recovered. Repeating this for every
j=g^{I}+1,\ldots,g^{F}
generates all remaining final global parity nodes. The read cost of this step is

(g^{F}-g^{I})(k^{F}+u\ell)=\frac{g^{F}-g^{I}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha.

Hence the total read bandwidth is

\displaystyle\gamma_{R}

\displaystyle=g^{I}\alpha+(\lambda-1)\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha+\frac{g^{F}-g^{I}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{g^{F}}{g^{F}+\ell}(k^{F}+u\ell)\alpha-g^{I}\left(\frac{k^{F}+u\ell}{g^{F}+\ell}-1\right)\alpha.

Since
\lambda=\lambda^{F}
, this is exactly the
g^{F}>g^{I}
lower bound in Theorem
IV.3
. Therefore the construction attains the optimal read bandwidth.

We set

Lemma V.3
.

Let
r,\ell,g^{F},g^{I}
be positive integers with
g^{F}<g^{I}
, and set
\alpha_{0}:=g^{F}+\ell
. Over any finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
admitting a
(g^{I}+\ell)\times r
superregular matrix, there exists a systematic
[r+\ell+g^{I},r,\alpha_{0}]
MDS array code with nodes

X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},\quad L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell},\quad P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{I}}

such that
P_{1},\ldots,P_{g^{F}}
can be optimally repaired from
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}
by downloading the first
g^{F}
subsymbols from each helper node. Moreover, the same downloads determine the first
g^{F}

When

. Hence the construction attains the optimal read bandwidth.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we studied the fundamental limits on the bandwidth cost of stable optimal-distance LRCCs in the global split regime, under the parameter condition
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
. To the best of our knowledge, by using an information-theoretic approach, we obtained the first nontrivial normalized bandwidth lower bounds for this setting. Moreover, we constructed MDS array codes with special repair properties by using piggybacking ideas, and then used these array codes as building blocks to construct LRCCs that attain the derived lower bounds. Therefore, our results show that the normalized bandwidth lower bounds are tight throughout the parameter range
g^{I},g^{F}\leq r
.

A natural problem is to impose stronger repair or recovery properties on the underlying codes, for example by considering maximally recoverable codes, and to study whether lower bounds and matching constructions can be obtained in this framework when
g^{I}
or
g^{F}
is larger than
r
. Another direction is to consider conversions between different types of codes, such as conversions between LRCs with different locality parameters, MDS-to-LRC conversions, and LRC-to-MDS conversions, and to investigate whether information-theoretic methods can be used to derive lower bounds together with corresponding optimal constructions. It would also be interesting to study conversion problems for broader classes of codes, including Reed–Muller codes, regenerating codes, algebraic geometry codes, and other related code families.

g
nodes as

\mathbf{P}:=(P_{1}^{\top},\ldots,P_{g}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

For
c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
, define the block-scalar diagonal matrix

D(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}):=\operatorname{diag}(c_{1}I_{\alpha},\ldots,c_{g}I_{\alpha})\in\mathrm{GL}_{g\alpha}(\mathbb{F}_{q}).

Let

\mathcal{D}_{g}:=\{D(c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}):c_{1},\ldots,c_{g}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\}.

For
N\geq 1
and
M_{1},\ldots,M_{N}\in\mathcal{D}_{g}
, define the code
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
as follows. Take
N
independent copies of
\mathcal{C}
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal{C}^{(N)}
. For the
i
-th copy, write its nodes as

X^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{r},\quad L^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,L^{(i)}_{\ell},\quad P^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,P^{(i)}_{g}.

The nodes
X^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(i)}_{r},L^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,L^{(i)}_{\ell}
form the
i
-th local group of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
. The last
g
nodes of this copy are only used to define the global parity nodes.
Write

\mathbf{P}^{(i)}:=\bigl((P^{(i)}_{1})^{\top},\ldots,(P^{(i)}_{g})^{\top}\bigr)^{\top}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

Let

\mathbf{G}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}M_{i}\mathbf{P}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha},

and write

\mathbf{G}=(G_{1}^{\top},\ldots,G_{g}^{\top})^{\top},\qquad G_{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha}.

The nodes
G_{1},\ldots,G_{g}
are stored as the global parity nodes of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
. Thus
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
consists of the
N
local groups above together with these
g
global parity nodes, and is a systematic
(Nr,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
u
and
\lambda
be positive integers. If
q
is sufficiently large, then there exist matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{u},B_{1},\ldots,B_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{D}_{g}
such that both
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(B_{t}A_{i}:\ t\in[\lambda],\ i\in[u])
are optimal-distance LRCs.

Proof.

For
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
, let
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{|\mathcal{Q}|\alpha}
be the projection onto the blocks indexed by
\mathcal{Q}
. Write
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{r+\ell}:=X_{1},\ldots,X_{r},L_{1},\ldots,L_{\ell}.
For
\mathcal{E}\subseteq[r+\ell]
, define
h(\mathcal{E}):=\max\{|\mathcal{E}|-\ell,0\}.
Let

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}}:=\{x\in(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\alpha})^{r}:Z_{j}(x)=0\ \text{for all }j\in[r+\ell]\setminus\mathcal{E}\}

and

\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}:=\{\mathbf{P}(x):x\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{g\alpha}.

We first record the following consequence of the MDS property. If
h=h(\mathcal{E})\leq g
, then
\dim\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}=h\alpha
, and, for every
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=h
, the map
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}:\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{h\alpha}
is an isomorphism. Indeed, if
|\mathcal{E}|\leq\ell
, then the number of surviving local nodes is at least
r
, and hence
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}}=\{0\}
. Now assume
|\mathcal{E}|=\ell+h
with
h>0
. The number of surviving local nodes is
r-h
. Since
\mathcal{C}
is MDS, the projection onto these
r-h
nodes has rank
(r-h)\alpha
. Hence
\dim\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}}=r\alpha-(r-h)\alpha=h\alpha.
For
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=h
, the surviving local nodes together with the global parity nodes
P_{a}
,
a\in\mathcal{Q}
, form
r
nodes of the MDS array code
\mathcal{C}
. Thus the map
x\mapsto\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathbf{P}(x)
is injective on
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}}
. Consequently,
\dim\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}=h\alpha
, and
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}
is an isomorphism on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}}
.

We next give a criterion for
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
to be optimal-distance. Fix
M_{1},\ldots,M_{N}\in\mathcal{D}_{g}
. Suppose that for every
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N}\subseteq[r+\ell]
, with
h_{i}:=h(\mathcal{E}_{i}),H:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}\leq g,
and every
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=H
, the map
(u_{i})_{i:h_{i}>0}\longmapsto\sum_{i:h_{i}>0}\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i}u_{i}
is injective on
\bigoplus_{i:h_{i}>0}\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}.
Then
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N})
is an optimal-distance
(Nr,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC.

To prove this criterion, note first that each local group is an
[r+\ell,r,\alpha]
MDS array code, and hence has local distance
\ell+1
. Consider any erasure pattern of size at most
g+\ell
. Let
s
be the number of erased global parity nodes, and let
\mathcal{E}_{i}\subseteq[r+\ell]
be the set of erased nodes in the
i
-th local group. Put
e_{i}:=|\mathcal{E}_{i}|
,
h_{i}:=h(\mathcal{E}_{i})
, and
H:=\sum_{i}h_{i}
. If
H=0
, then all local erasures are recovered locally, and the erased global parities are then recomputed. Assume
H>0
. Let
R:=\sum_{i}e_{i}
. Since
H>0
, at least one local group contributes an excess erasure, and hence
R=\sum_{i}e_{i}\geq\ell+\sum_{i}h_{i}=\ell+H
. Together with
R+s\leq g+\ell
, this gives
H\leq g-s
. Thus at least
H
global parity nodes survive. Choose a surviving set
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=H
. If two codewords agree on all surviving nodes, then their difference gives vectors
u_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
, and the surviving global parity nodes indexed by
\mathcal{Q}
give
\sum_{i:h_{i}>0}\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i}u_{i}=0.
By the injectivity assumption,
u_{i}=0
for all
i
with
h_{i}>0
. For the groups with
h_{i}=0
, the preceding MDS consequence gives
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}=\{0\}
. Since the map from
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
is injective for
h_{i}>0
, all local message differences are zero. Hence the two codewords are equal. Thus every erasure pattern of size at most
g+\ell
is correctable, so
d\geq g+\ell+1
. The Singleton-type bound for an
(Nr,g,r,\ell,\alpha)
-LRC gives
d\leq g+\ell+1
. Hence the code is optimal-distance.

We now prove that the required injectivity conditions can be enforced by block-scalar diagonal matrices. Fix
N\geq 1
, local erasure sets
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N}\subseteq[r+\ell]
, and put
h_{i}:=h(\mathcal{E}_{i}),H:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{i}.
Assume
1\leq H\leq g
, and fix
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=H
. For each
i
with
h_{i}>0
, let
U_{i}
be a matrix whose columns form a basis of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}
. Let
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}
be the indices satisfying
h_{i_{j}}>0
. For
M_{i}=D(m_{i,1},\ldots,m_{i,g})
, define

\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N},\mathcal{Q}}(M_{1},\ldots,M_{N}):=\det\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i_{1}}U_{i_{1}}&\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i_{2}}U_{i_{2}}&\cdots&\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i_{s}}U_{i_{s}}\end{bmatrix}.

Here the matrices inside the determinant are concatenated horizontally. Indeed,
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}M_{i}U_{i}
has
H\alpha
rows and
h_{i}\alpha
columns, and
\sum_{j=1}^{s}h_{i_{j}}=H
, so the concatenated matrix is square. Thus
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N},\mathcal{Q}}
is a polynomial in the variables
m_{i,j}
. We show that it is not the zero polynomial.

Choose a partition
\mathcal{Q}=\bigsqcup_{i:h_{i}>0}\mathcal{Q}_{i},|\mathcal{Q}_{i}|=h_{i}.
By the preceding MDS consequence,
\det(\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}U_{i})\neq 0
for every
i
with
h_{i}>0
. Specialize the variables by setting
m_{i,j}=0
whenever
j\notin\mathcal{Q}_{i}
. Under this specialization, after permuting row blocks according to the partition
\mathcal{Q}=\bigsqcup_{i}\mathcal{Q}_{i}
, the concatenated matrix in the definition of
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N},\mathcal{Q}}
becomes block diagonal with diagonal blocks
\operatorname{diag}(m_{i,j}I_{\alpha}:j\in\mathcal{Q}_{i})\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}U_{i},i:h_{i}>0.
Hence the specialized determinant equals

\pm\prod_{i:h_{i}>0}\left[\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{Q}_{i}}m_{i,j}^{\alpha}\right)\det(\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}U_{i})\right],

which is a nonzero polynomial. Thus
\Delta_{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{N},\mathcal{Q}}
is not the zero polynomial.

We apply this to the final code. For
N=u
, let
\mathcal{P}_{F}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
be the product of all nonzero polynomials

\Delta_{E_{1},\ldots,E_{u},Q}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
over all
\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{u}\subseteq[r+\ell]
and all
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
satisfying
1\leq\sum_{i=1}^{u}h(\mathcal{E}_{i})\leq g,

|\mathcal{Q}|=\sum_{i=1}^{u}h(\mathcal{E}_{i}).
Since this is a finite product of nonzero polynomials over an integral domain,
\mathcal{P}_{F}
is nonzero.

It remains to handle the product form
B_{t}A_{i}
for the initial code. Write

A_{i}=D(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,g}),\qquad B_{t}=D(b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,g}).

Fix local erasure sets
\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\subseteq[r+\ell],t\in[\lambda],\ i\in[u],
put
h_{t,i}:=h(\mathcal{E}_{t,i})
, and assume
1\leq H:=\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{u}h_{t,i}\leq g.
Fix
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
with
|\mathcal{Q}|=H
. For each
(t,i)
with
h_{t,i}>0
, let
U_{t,i}
be a basis matrix of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}}
. Let
(t_{1},i_{1}),\ldots,(t_{s},i_{s})
be all pairs satisfying
h_{t_{j},i_{j}}>0
, listed in lexicographic order. Define

\Delta^{I}_{\{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\},\mathcal{Q}}:=\det\begin{bmatrix}\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}B_{t_{1}}A_{i_{1}}U_{t_{1},i_{1}}&\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}B_{t_{2}}A_{i_{2}}U_{t_{2},i_{2}}&\cdots&\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}B_{t_{s}}A_{i_{s}}U_{t_{s},i_{s}}\end{bmatrix}.

Again, the matrices inside the determinant are concatenated horizontally. The order of the pairs only affects the determinant by a sign, and hence does not affect whether the determinant polynomial is identically zero. The concatenated matrix is square because
\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}}B_{t}A_{i}U_{t,i}
has
H\alpha
rows and
h_{t,i}\alpha
columns, while
\sum_{(t,i):h_{t,i}>0}h_{t,i}=H
. We prove that
\Delta^{I}_{\{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\},\mathcal{Q}}
is not the zero polynomial in the variables
a_{i,j},b_{t,j}
.

Choose a partition
\mathcal{Q}=\bigsqcup_{(t,i):h_{t,i}>0}\mathcal{Q}_{t,i},|\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}|=h_{t,i}.
Then
\det(\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}}U_{t,i})\neq 0
for all
(t,i)
with
h_{t,i}>0
. For
j\in\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}
, specialize
b_{t,j}=1,a_{i,j}=z_{t,i,j},
and set
b_{t^{\prime},j}=0\quad(t^{\prime}\neq t),a_{i^{\prime},j}=0\quad(i^{\prime}\neq i).
The variables with
j\notin\mathcal{Q}
may be assigned arbitrarily. Under this specialization, after permuting row blocks according to the partition of
\mathcal{Q}
, the concatenated matrix in the definition of
\Delta^{I}_{\{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\},\mathcal{Q}}
becomes block diagonal with diagonal blocks
\operatorname{diag}(z_{t,i,j}I_{\alpha}:j\in\mathcal{Q}_{t,i})\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}}U_{t,i},(t,i):h_{t,i}>0.
Thus the specialized determinant equals

\pm\prod_{(t,i):h_{t,i}>0}\left[\left(\prod_{j\in\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}}z_{t,i,j}^{\alpha}\right)\det(\Pi_{\mathcal{Q}_{t,i}}U_{t,i})\right],

which is nonzero. Hence
\Delta^{I}_{\{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\},\mathcal{Q}}
is not the zero polynomial.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{I}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u},B_{1},\ldots,B_{\lambda})
be the product of all such nonzero polynomials
\Delta^{I}_{\{\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\},\mathcal{Q}}
over all choices of
\mathcal{E}_{t,i}\subseteq[r+\ell]
and
\mathcal{Q}\subseteq[g]
satisfying

1\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{u}h(\mathcal{E}_{t,i})\leq g,\qquad|\mathcal{Q}|=\sum_{t=1}^{\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{u}h(\mathcal{E}_{t,i}).

Since this is again a finite product of nonzero polynomials over an integral domain,
\mathcal{P}_{I}
is nonzero.

Finally, define

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{inv}}:=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{u}\prod_{j=1}^{g}a_{i,j}\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{\lambda}\prod_{j=1}^{g}b_{t,j}\right),

and

\mathcal{P}:=\mathcal{P}_{F}\mathcal{P}_{I}\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{inv}}.

The polynomial
\mathcal{P}
is nonzero. By the Schwartz–Zippel lemma
[
25
,
32
]
, if
q
is sufficiently large, there exists a choice of
a_{i,j},b_{t,j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
such that
\mathcal{P}\neq 0
. Since
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{inv}}
is a factor of
\mathcal{P}
, all
a_{i,j}
and
b_{t,j}
are nonzero for this choice. Therefore

A_{i}=D(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,g}),\qquad B_{t}=D(b_{t,1},\ldots,b_{t,g})

belong to
\mathcal{D}_{g}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{P}_{F}\neq 0
implies, by the criterion above, that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{u})
is optimal-distance, and
\mathcal{P}_{I}\neq 0
implies that
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{C}}(B_{t}A_{i}:\ t\in[\lambda],\ i\in[u])
is optimal-distance. This proves the lemma.
∎
