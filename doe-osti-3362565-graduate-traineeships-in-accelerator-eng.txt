Abstract / Executive Summary

This final report summarizes the Norfolk State University portion of the project Graduate
Traineeships in Accelerator Engineering and Physics. In the funded proposal, Norfolk State
University participated as a collaborating institution in a broader multi-institution
traineeship effort designed to prepare graduate students for work in accelerator
engineering and physics, including students from Old Dominion University, Norfolk State
University, and Hampton University.

For the NSU portion of the project, the NSU PI was able to support one graduate student,
Bryan A. Belcher, who completed the Master of Science in Computer Science on time in
July 2024. His work resulted in a thesis titled Sentimental Awareness Influence on
Paraphrase Detection in Transformer Models, specialized training through USPAS
attendance in June 2023, and a conference paper presented virtually in December 2024 at
the 2024 International Conference on Computer and Applications (ICCA).

The NSU portion therefore achieved a meaningful student-centered outcome through one
completed thesis-based master’s degree, related research dissemination, and specialized
training activity. However, the NSU PI was unable to identify additional U.S.-citizen
students at NSU interested in this area of work at the level anticipated, and the remaining
traineeship positions associated with the NSU portion were not filled.

1. Original NSU Scope Within the Proposal

The funded proposal described a multi-institution graduate traineeship program intended
to strengthen the pipeline of students entering accelerator engineering and physics. It
stated that graduate students at ODU, NSU, and HU would be given opportunities to enter
the fields of particle accelerator engineering or physics, with training and stipend support
tied to research performed at Jefferson Lab or ODU in one of four FOA priority areas. The
proposal identified me, Dr. Rasha Morsi, as the Norfolk State University co-investigator
representing Computer Science.

The proposal also stated that funding would allow a full two years of support for thesis-
based master’s students and that students from all university partners were expected to
make use of relevant training opportunities such as USPAS coursework.

2. Intended Objectives of the NSU Component

Consistent with the funded proposal, the NSU portion of the project was intended to:

 1. Contribute to the broader graduate traineeship pipeline by supporting eligible NSU
 graduate students in relevant technical areas.

 2. Provide two years of thesis-based graduate support where appropriate.

 3. Enable participation in specialized technical training opportunities relevant to the
 traineeship model, including USPAS coursework.

 4. Help prepare students for advanced technical research and related workforce
 pathways connected to national laboratory environments and accelerator-related
 work.

3. Project Activities and Work Performed at NSU

During the project period, the NSU portion of the award supported one graduate student
through a thesis-based master’s pathway in Computer Science. This support included
academic progress toward the M.S. degree, thesis completion, coordination with faculty
advising, and specialized training exposure aligned with the broader traineeship concept.

As mentioned earlier, the supported student, Bryan A. Belcher, completed the thesis
Sentimental Awareness Influence on Paraphrase Detection in Transformer Models and
graduated with the Master of Science in Computer Science in July 2024. The thesis
committee included Dr. Musbah Abdulgader as chair, Dr. Rasha Morsi, and Dr. Isaac
Osunmakinde. The acknowledgements also note advisor support connected to Jefferson
Lab.

The project also supported Bryan Belcher’s attendance at the USPAS course Unifying
Physics of Accelerators, Lasers and Plasma – Synergy and Bridges held June 12–16, 2023, in
Melville, New York. In addition, the student’s research was disseminated through a
conference paper presented virtually in December 2024 at the 2024 International
Conference on Computer and Applications (ICCA).

4. Scientific and Technical Outcomes

The main scientific and technical outcome of the NSU-supported work was the successful
completion of a thesis-based research project in natural language processing and machine
learning. The thesis investigated the effect of integrating sentiment analysis into
paraphrase detection using transformer-based language models including BERT, RoBERTa,
DeBERTa, and ELECTRA. The research found that incorporating sentiment information had
a positive effect on the tested paraphrase detection models.

This outcome demonstrates that the NSU portion of the traineeship produced a completed
graduate research project, a defended thesis, and a trained graduate who successfully
completed the master’s degree within the support period.

5. Student Support and Training Outcomes

For the NSU portion, the project achieved the following student-focused outcomes:

 • support of one NSU graduate student in a thesis-based M.S. pathway consistent
 with the proposal’s two-year support model for master’s students

 • successful on-time completion of the M.S. in Computer Science in July 2024

 • completion of a thesis with documented technical findings

 • specialized training through attendance at a USPAS course in June 2023

 • dissemination of the student’s research through a refereed conference paper
 presented virtually in December 2024

Tuition and stipend support were provided for two years for the supported student, which
aligns with the proposal structure for thesis-option master’s students.

7. Activities Not Fully Achieved

Although the NSU portion produced one successful graduate outcome, the broader trainee
recruitment target anticipated for this program was not met at NSU. The NSU PI was unable
to identify additional U.S.-citizen students interested in this work in sufficient numbers,
and the remaining traineeship positions associated with the NSU portion were therefore
not filled.

8. Challenges and Limiting Factors

The primary challenge for the NSU portion was recruitment. The project depended on
identifying graduate students who both met the required eligibility conditions and were
interested in pursuing the technical direction of the traineeship. While one student
successfully progressed through the full thesis-based pathway, additional recruitment did
not occur at the scale anticipated.

9. Overall Assessment of the NSU Portion

The NSU portion of the project is best characterized as a partially completed but
substantively successful implementation. It did not meet the full recruitment scale
originally envisioned, but it did produce a meaningful graduate research and training
outcome through two-year support of a thesis-option master’s student, successful degree
completion, a technical thesis, specialized training, and a conference paper.

10. Lessons Learned

Several lessons emerged from the NSU portion of the project.

First, thesis-based support can be highly effective for individual student success when a
well-matched student is identified early and guided consistently through the program.

Second, recruitment for specialized federally funded traineeship work can be difficult when
eligibility conditions narrow the available student pool.

Third, even when broader cohort goals are not achieved, focused support of one qualified
student can still generate strong outcomes in degree completion, thesis production,
training, and scholarly dissemination.

11. Conclusion

The Norfolk State University component of Graduate Traineeships in Accelerator
Engineering and Physics contributed to the broader multi-institution DOE-supported effort
by successfully supporting one graduate student through a thesis-based M.S. program in
Computer Science. The supported student completed the degree on time, produced a
thesis with positive findings related to sentiment-aware paraphrase detection in
transformer models, participated in USPAS training, and disseminated the work through a
conference paper.

At the same time, the NSU PI was unable to recruit additional qualified U.S.-citizen
students at NSU at the level originally anticipated. Accordingly, the NSU portion is best
described as having achieved strong individual student outcomes but only partial
realization of the broader intended scale of participation.
