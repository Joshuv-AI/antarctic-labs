 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 Project 1 (Mods 0-2):

 Combined Power’s Hyperlight Energy and NLR will collaborate on the DOE Concentrating
 Optics for Lower Levelized Energy CosTS (COLLECTS) project, DE-FOA-0001268.
 COLLECTS funding opportunity announcement (FOA) seeks to further concentrated solar
 power (CSP) system technologies by soliciting disruptive, transformative projects for the
 concentrating solar collectors in the CSP plant. These innovative projects will seek to surpass the
 targets set out in the SunShot Vision Study, enabling CSP to be cost-competitive with
 conventional forms of electric power generation. Projects will target the design and
 manufacturing of novel solar collectors with the ability to significantly reduce the solar field
 contribution to the overall levelized cost of energy (LCOE) of the CSP plant, and which have not
 been previously specifically targeted by CSP FOAs.

 Project 2 (Mod 3):

 Hyperlight will perform measurement and verification (M&V) of Green House Gases and NLR
 will evaluate energy consumption reduction.

   P1.Ph1.Task 5:

 NLR will develop a CSP Thermal Energy Generation Model. The model will be able to use a
 weather file (including direct normal irradiance (DNI) and ambient conditions) to predict thermal
 energy generation on a daily, monthly, and annual basis for a linear Fresnel collector using
 synthetic thermal oils.

 P1.Ph1.Task 5 Results:

 The combined collector/receiver system model was used to predict optical efficiency for each
 hour of the year. Since approximately half of the generated points were zero, due to the time
 being before sunrise or after sunset, the non-zero points were interpolated to generate values for
 all 8760 hours of the year. This hourly efficiency data was combined with DNI data from NLR’s
 theoretical meteorological year three (TMY3) database to calculate annual optical energy
 generation. The thermal energy generated for every hour and day of a year by a single acre in
 Brawley, CA, is shown below in Figure 5.

 Installation of a Linear Fresnel Collector to Generate Heat for a Cheese Production

 Measurement and Verification Plan

 Josh McTigue (JoshuaDominic.McTigue@nlr.gov), Guangdong Zhu (Guangdong.Zhu@nlr.gov)

 National Laboratory of the Rockies, 15013 Denver West Parkway, Golden, CO, 80401

                                               October 30, 2020

 Introduction

 The heat load at the Saputo cheese production facility in Tulare, CA, is currently met with
 natural gas. The aim of this project is to quantify the reduction in GHG emissions that can be
 achieved by providing heat with CSP. A Linear Fresnel Reflector (LFR) system known as
 ‘Hylux’ and developed by Hyperlight Energy will be installed at the dairy factory.

 This measurement and verification plan will describe protocols to quantify the current GHG
 emissions, the performance of the LFR, and calculate the GHG emission reduction. The
 protocols will outline the required equipment, instrumentation, input parameters, output metrics,
 and calculation methods.

 Measurements and Verification for the Cheese Production Facility

 The current fossil fuel consumption at the Saputo cheese production facility should first be
 established, and then the current GHG emissions may be estimated. Data on the natural gas
 consumption at the facility may be obtained directly from Saputo. For instance, recent
 communications suggest that the natural gas consumption in fiscal year 2020 was 1.3 MMBTU.

 It will be possible to validate these numbers using data from the newly installed LFR. For
 instance, the measurement system will evaluate the total heat delivered by the LFR, and this
 value can be converted to a natural gas equivalent.

 Monitoring Equipment and Instrumentation

 If the data provided by Saputo is not satisfactory, it is possible to directly measure the natural gas
 consumption. A flow meter to measure the mass flow rate of natural gas is required. Thermal
 properties for the natural gas should also be obtained. The heat provided by natural gas can then
 be simply calculated.

 Calculation of GHG Emissions

 The natural gas consumption will be converted into equivalent GHG emissions using the
 procedure specified in Attachment 8 of the grant solicitation. Sufficient data will be obtained to

 13

 facilitate an estimation of the annual fossil fuel consumption and GHG emissions in a one-year
 period.

 tubes. One basin on each side of the receiver assembly forms a collector module.

 In this section, a protocol for calculating the heat delivered by this LFR is outlined, and follows

 Monitoring Equipment and Instrumentation

 This section will describe the parameters that should be measured and the necessary equipment
 to evaluate the heat output and efficiency of the LFR.

 Firstly, clear definitions are required of the LFR system layout and where data will be collected.
 The LFR system currently comprises three fluid loops:

 1. A thermal oil loop that passes through the receiver tubes
 2. The heat is then transferred from the thermal oil to a water loop
 3. Heat is transferred again from the water loop to steam which is then delivered to the
 cheese production facility.

 These three fluid loops are illustrated on the process flow diagram in Figure 2. The purpose of
 the water loop is to isolate the cheese factory from the thermal oil in the event of a leak.
 Different instrumentation may be required depending on where the heat addition is measured. (It
 is assumed that heat addition to the thermal oil and water/steam are equal – i.e. negligible heat
 losses in the heat exchanger).

 14

 The following steps should be followed to obtain the necessary data:

 1. Obtain thermal properties of the heat transfer fluid – e.g. density, heat capacity
 2. Obtain LFR geometry from Hyperlight technical specification sheets
 3. Clearly define the locations before and after the heat addition (LFR) where data are
 measured

 Verification

 The System Advisor Model (SAM) developed at NLR will be used to verify the performance of
 the LFR and estimate annual energy generation [5]. SAM requires numerous input parameters to
 model LFRs, including:

 • LFR geometry
 • Solar field location
 • Layout of the collectors
 • LFR performance parameters as determined in Section 0 and 0, such as the optical
 efficiency at normal incidence, the incidence angle modifiers, and the heat loss
 parameters

 19

 First, it should be established that there is a good agreement between the measured data and the
 SAM calculations. SAM will be executed using input data from a selection of the measured test
 periods. The output of SAM will then be compared against the measured power output, and the
 error between the two should be satisfactorily low (e.g., less than 5%). If the error is larger than
 this, then further optimization of the performance parameters (IAMs, etc.) is required, and an
 iterative process can be adopted to tune these parameters until the agreement between SAM and
 the measured data is acceptable. To maximize the accuracy of the model, it is important to verify
 SAM against a range of test data conducted at a wide variety of incidence angles, DNIs, ambient
 conditions, and mirror cleanliness.

 SAM may then be used to predict the annual performance of the LFR, as well as explore the
 impact of a variety of factors that may be difficult to control in the field. SAM typically
 completes annual calculations in several seconds, meaning that a range of scenarios and
 uncertainties can be considered in a short time frame.

 Greenhouse Gas Emission Reductions

 The heat generated by the LFR is used to offset natural gas consumption. The annual heat
 generation from the LFR will either be measured directly over the course of a year or estimated
 using SAM as described in Section 0.

 Once the reduction in natural gas consumption is known, this energy value will be converted into
 greenhouse gas emissions using a factor provided in Attachment 8 of the original grant
 solicitation.
