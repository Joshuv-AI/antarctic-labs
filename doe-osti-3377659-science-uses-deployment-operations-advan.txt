Preface
As the world moves toward more digitally connected and software-defined energy solutions,
advanced technologies such as the Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) are key to improving
energy systems. O-RAN promotes openness, flexibility, and interoperability, offering new
opportunities to enhance microgrids, distribution systems, and smart grids, particularly with the
increasing integration of distributed energy technologies at the grid edge.

This report focuses on O-RAN’s application to energy systems, drawing on the collaborative
efforts of the National Laboratory of the Rockies, Argonne National Laboratory, Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory, and the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Sciences Network
(ESnet). Under the Science Uses Deployment Operations—Advanced Wireless (SUDO-AW)
project, these organizations explore how O-RAN can drive innovation in scientific applications
and energy infrastructure. ESnet, operated by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, is
conducting due diligence on advanced wireless technologies and performing testing and
evaluation of various components to expand the science network’s capabilities to include
advanced wireless capabilities, ensuring that it can better support scientific use cases across DOE
facilities and field sites.

By examining the technical components and use cases, this report highlights how O-RAN can
help expand ESnet to enable scientific use cases that are focused on the energy sector and aid
utilities and stakeholders in accelerating advanced energy system technologies.

Executive Summary
The Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) is emerging as a solution to the increasing demand
for more flexible, cost-effective, and advanced mobile network infrastructures. This evolution is
driven by advancements in wireless technologies and the increasing complexities of deploying
and managing these networks. O-RAN represents a significant shift in wireless technology,
building on the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP) framework to foster openness,
flexibility, and interoperability. By decoupling hardware and software components, O-RAN
enables a multi-vendor ecosystem that encourages innovation and diverse solutions.

O-RAN’s potential extends beyond traditional wireless applications, with increasing interest in
its role in advancing energy systems, particularly within the context of smart grids, microgrids,
and the integration of renewable energy sources. Although the role of open wireless technologies
in driving the energy transformation is increasingly recognized, further exploration is needed.
Vendors and utilities are investigating how O-RAN technologies can optimize energy use cases
and improve the performance of fifth generation (5G) and beyond applications.

This report outlines efforts under the Science Uses Deployment Operations—Advanced Wireless
(SUDO-AW) project, a collaboration among the National Laboratory of the Rockies’
Cybersecurity Research Center, Argonne National Laboratory, Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory, and the U.S. Department of Energy’s Energy Sciences Network (ESnet) research
and operations staff. This project focuses on performing due diligence, through testing and
evaluation, in preparation for the deployment of advanced wireless infrastructure for scientific
use cases, with an emphasis on O-RAN technology, its components, integrations, and its ability
to support vertical stack applications across the energy sector. Additionally, the report highlights
the value cases for utilities, underscoring how adopting open wireless standards can accelerate
advanced energy system technologies, foster innovation, and improve the integration of critical
energy technologies.

Introduction
The Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) is rooted in the increasing demand for more flexible,
cost-efficient, and advanced wireless network infrastructures. This demand stems from
advancements in wireless technologies and the increasing complexities of deploying and
managing networks. O-RAN represents a paradigm shift in fourth generation (4G) and fifth
generation (5G) wireless technology, aligning with and building on the 3rd Generation
Partnership Project (3GPP) framework, promoting openness, flexibility, and interoperability by
decoupling hardware and software components (O-RAN Alliance 2025a). This approach fosters
a more diverse, multi-vendor ecosystem, allowing a broader range of solutions to emerge across
different vendors.

The significance of open wireless technologies in driving the energy transformation of
microgrids and distribution systems, especially within the context of smart grids and critical
energy resource integration, is growing, but it still needs further exploration. Some organizations
are making major investments in O-RAN with a focus on understanding how O-RAN
technologies can enhance energy sector applications (NTIA 2024), while some utilities and
organizations are investigating existing energy use cases and the role 5G and beyond
applications can play in improving these scenarios (Hill 2023).

The purpose of this report is to highlight the application of O-RAN technologies in critical
energy resources and distribution systems, inform on relevant scientific research and
development (R&D) capabilities within this context, and underscore the value proposition for
utilities and other energy sector stakeholders. This will help others better understand how
adopting open wireless standards can accelerate advanced energy system technologies and their
critical energy resources.

1 O-RAN System Overview
Before examining the application of O-RAN within the context of critical energy resources and
distribution systems, science uses, and its value propositions for utilities and other stakeholders,
it is crucial to first establish a clear understanding of the O-RAN technology stack, its
components, and how it aligns with and enhances the 3GPP framework. In simple terms, 3GPP
defines the core technical requirements for radio access networks (RANs), while O-RAN enables
the physical and software components of the network to be sourced from a diverse set of
vendors, fostering a more open, flexible, and adaptable infrastructure (Morand 2022).

When referring to O-RAN, it is important to understand the context of “open,” which is based on
open interoperability standards—but is not synonymous with open-source software. O-RAN
focuses on defining open interfaces and architectural standards, but the actual software and
hardware implementations can be proprietary or open-source.

O-RAN technology is built on disaggregated components that move away from traditional,
centralized, and virtualized RAN architectures, which typically integrate the base band unit and
the radio unit (RU). By separating the base band unit into a virtualized central unit (CU) and a
distribution unit (DU), O-RAN introduces greater flexibility in system design while also
enabling the development of data-driven, intelligent control applications. The key components of
O-RAN are listed in Table 1 (O-RAN Alliance 2025b).

 Table 1. Key O-RAN Components

 O-RAN Component Description
 Radio unit (RU) The hardware that connects to the RAN and communicates wirelessly with
 user devices
 Distribution unit (DU) Handles lower layers of the RAN, such as signal processing, resource
 management, and scheduling for wireless communications
 Central unit (CU) Manages higher layers of the RAN and coordinates the overall system,
 including tasks such as mobility management and radio resource control
 RAN Intelligent A software platform that provides network intelligence, allowing for real-time
 Controller (RIC) optimization and management of the RAN using artificial intelligence (AI) and
 machine learning (ML)

This disaggregation into a customizable and flexible architecture enables the system and its
components, enabling easy integration with virtualized, cloud-based, and software-defined
networking infrastructures and management systems. Further, the O-RAN architecture, in
alignment with the 3GPP framework, supports seamless integration with other virtualized and
software-defined components, such as the 5G core (5GC) and multi-access edge computing
(MEC), to create a comprehensive end-to-end wireless system that supports user applications.
The system components integrated with O-RAN are listed in Table 2 (ETSI 2023).

The open mid-haul and front-haul are critical components in the paradigm shift enabled by O-
RAN, which contrasts with traditional, centralized proprietary RAN architectures. O-RAN’s
system architecture empowers consumers to make end-to-end solution decisions based on user
and business requirements, rather than being locked into specific telecommunication vendor
solutions across the mid-haul and front-haul.

2 The Evolution of Radio Access Networks
The evolution of RAN is driven by technological advancements and user needs in wireless
communications, transitioning from traditional hardware-based architectures to more flexible,
software-defined, and open solutions. The U.S. Department of Defense (DOD) has established a
private 5G deployment strategy (DOD CIO 2024) and has mandated the transition of wireless
infrastructure toward O-RAN-specific 5G components and system architectures, and major
investments are being made to enable this transition across roughly 800 military bases (Dano
2024). This shift from closed hardware systems to O-RAN is crucial not only for
telecommunications and DOD but also for additional industries. For sectors like energy, it is
important to understand how this transformation has unfolded over the years to better grasp the

2.1 O-RAN, 5GC, and MEC Capabilities and Use Cases
The integration of O-RAN, 5GC, and MEC enables next-generation 5G and beyond networks,
offering flexible, low-latency, and cost-effective solutions that enhance the efficiency of
microgrids and distribution systems. These technologies have the potential to optimize grid
operations and support dynamic energy management (Hitchens 2024). This section explores the
capabilities and use cases of these components, with a focus on their applications in microgrids
and distribution systems.

 • O-RAN capabilities and use cases:
 o Disaggregated architecture: O-RAN’s separation of CU, DU, and RU for cross-
 vendor interoperability and enhanced scalability in microgrids
 o AI/ML optimization with RIC: Real-time traffic management and network slice
 allocation for efficient energy flow
 o Virtualized network slicing: Tailored slices for load balancing, fault detection,
 and demand response in microgrids
 o Smart cities and industrial automation: Supports smart city infrastructure and real-
 time industrial automation, improving energy use and manufacturing
 o Rural connectivity: Cost-effective multi-vendor solutions for expanding 5G to
 underserved areas, aiding remote microgrid deployments.
 • Core network capabilities and use cases:
 o Service-based architecture: Microservices for scalable, flexible grid management
 in microgrids
 o Network slicing: Custom slices for energy-specific needs, such as load balancing,
 fault detection, and demand response
 o Traffic prioritization: The 5G quality-of-service identifier enables critical traffic
 to be prioritized for grid communications.
 o Control and user plane separation: Enhances scalability, security, and
 communications within distribution systems
 o Private 5G networks: Secure, low-latency connectivity for remote microgrids and
 distributed devices
 o Internet of Things (IoT) and edge analytics: Real-time monitoring and automation
 for smarter grid operations.
 • MEC capabilities and use cases:
 o Reduced latency: MEC lowers latency by processing data closer to the grid,
 optimizing load balancing and fault detection.
 o Application availability: Edge-level computing allows local data storage and
 processing, reducing the need to transfer large datasets to central data centers.
 Redundancies can be implemented across local, regional, and central data centers,
 ensuring application availability through distributed edge devices and redundant
 RANs.
 o Real-time decision-making: Local data processing enables faster decision-making
 in distribution systems without relying on centralized data centers.
 o Edge AI for grid optimization: Edge AI can analyze data and trends in real time to
 optimize energy flow, storage, and the integration of renewable resources.

 6

O-RAN, 5GC, and MEC all provide essential capabilities to enable smarter and more cost-
effective energy management and extend coverage to underserved areas. To better conceptualize
the impact of MEC, 5GC, O-RAN, and UE, the reader can consider the capabilities within the
context of a highway and traffic metaphor:

 • The 5GC acts as the foundation of a highway, providing secure and resilient
 infrastructure for data to travel efficiently. And just like a well-built highway withstands
 heavy traffic and harsh conditions, 5GC ensures that the network remains strong, secure,
 and capable of handling increasing demand. The O-RAN RIC is the real-time traffic
 controller, dynamically managing the data flow to prevent congestion and network
 failures, ensuring smooth, secure communications.
 • The MEC is like a local traffic management center at the network’s edge, processing data
 closer to the user to reduce delays, optimize performance, and maintain security. The UE
 is the vehicle on the highway, the device sending and receiving data. Data analytics
 within 5GC act as security inspectors, monitoring unusual behavior or potential threats,
 ensuring the data follows security protocols, and identifying emerging risks. With real-
 time oversight, the RIC also inspects individual data packets, ensuring that each is secure,
 well-maintained, and following the most resilient route through the network.

3 O-RAN Science Applications for Microgrids and
  Distribution Systems
As part of the SUDO-AW project, researchers have performed due diligence through the
research, testing, and evaluation of RAN technologies to understand and enable scientific
research scenario applications across the U.S. Department of Energy’s (DOE’s) Energy Sciences
Network (ESnet) and national laboratories. This alignment of advanced wireless scientific use
cases and infrastructure is driven by DOE’s Office of the Chief Information Officer Advanced
Wireless Strategy strategic pillars (DOE 2022). At the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR), the Cybersecurity Research Center has been focused on the following R&D scenarios
within the context of RAN applications:

 • Enhanced communications for distributed energy resources:
 o Defining the role of RAN in managing secure communications between cloud
 environments, distributed energy resources, and the grid.
 o Real-time data transmission for advanced metering infrastructure, monitoring,
 commands, and status of assets.
 • Automation and controls in smart grids:
 o Supporting advanced grid control systems (e.g., power optimization [voltage and
 current regulation], fault detection).
 o RAN as a facilitator of autonomous decision-making and proactive maintenance.
 • Data-driven decision-making and analytics:
 o Supporting edge computing and IoT integration for enhanced grid intelligence and
 massive scalability.
 o Facilitating the data-driven optimization of energy production, consumption, and
 prioritization of critical infrastructure.

3.1 Research and Development Efforts
At NLR, researchers and developers have made strides toward integrating advanced wireless
systems and capabilities into its Next Generation Cyber Range (NGCR) (O-RAN Alliance 2023).
These integrated capabilities enable researchers to mix components and assets across NLR’s
Energy Systems Integration Facility and the Flatirons Campus to model and run a range of
scenarios using integrated hardware, emulation, and co-simulation capabilities.

3.2 Cyber Ranges and Emulation Capabilities
The development and application of cyber range test beds and integrated capabilities used for
emulating and/or simulating real-world network, security, and operational scenarios is essential
for scientific research, testing, and analysis. NLR’s NGCR is a controlled emulation and
simulation environment designed to enable research staff (individuals, teams, and organizations)
to perform research and development studies with a focus on cybersecurity and resilience within
the context of the power grid and broader energy systems (Rivera et al. 2024). It allows
researchers to replicate real-world networks, systems, and scenarios to provide hands-on
experience for systems analysis and integration with an emphasis on identifying, preventing, and
mitigating cyber threats focused on energy systems.

 11

Large investments from organizations such as the U.S. Department of Commerce’s National
Telecommunications and Information Administration, which funded the Open RAN Center for
Integration and Development, demonstrate the significance of cyber range ecosystems focused
on advanced wireless capabilities (NTIA 2025). Cyber ranges and emulation capabilities can
provide validation and verification of advanced wireless technology deployments and
integrations within microgrid and distribution systems. This critical research serves NLR’s
mission focus on grid transformation and the DOE’s focus on advancing resilient energy (NLR
2025). Figure 5 demonstrates the 5G emulation experiment and workflow environment within
NLR’s Cyber Range.

Open 5G emulation facilitates R&D of additional core functions and custom tools by providing a
flexible platform where new features, protocols, and services can be tested and refined. This
open environment encourages innovation and accelerates the development of advanced
functionalities tailored to specific network needs.

It supports the exploration of defense strategies and architectural designs by enabling researchers
to simulate real-world attack vectors, allowing for the testing of various defense mechanisms and
the fine-tuning of network architectures. This helps identify vulnerabilities and enhance the
overall security posture of 5G networks.

The ability to scale and integrate with other co-simulation scenarios within the 5G system
context further broadens the scope of R&D. Researchers can simulate complex, multilayered
network interactions across different system components, including edge networks, IoT devices,
and cloud infrastructures. This scalability aids in understanding system-wide behaviors and
performance under varying conditions.

Open 5G emulation also enables R&D of threat scenarios against 5G systems using threat
emulation tools, such as MITRE ATT&CK (GitHub 2025a). This capability allows for the
comprehensive testing of potential cyberattacks and the validation of response strategies in a
controlled, risk-free environment, ensuring that 5G networks are resilient to emerging threats.

time, driving innovation in intelligent network monitoring and management tools.

4.1 Microgrid System and Network Innovation Case
R&D efforts within the Cybersecurity Research Center at NLR push the application of 5G
technologies toward a state of innovation, with a beyond 5G perspective. Considering the 3GPP
framework in relation to MEC, 5GC, and O-RAN components, these technologies have paved
various pathways for industry innovation. In sectors aiming to leverage advanced wireless
capabilities, they enable thoughtful approaches to enhancing network flexibility, scalability, and
efficiency. Researchers at NLR worked toward taking the application of MEC, 5GC, and O-RAN
to the next level with deep consideration of future energy systems (microgrids and distribution
systems) and what the advanced wireless transformation has enabled.

Figure 10 illustrates MEC, 5GC, and O-RAN within the context of a microgrid policy control
scenario, providing a system that enforces grid policy from the MEC “Hive-Node” to the UE
local grid controller “Worker-Node.” This hive-node and worker-node distributed control system
architecture was introduced in NLR’s 5G-SER: Final Report (Rivera et al. 2024). But deeper
considerations have been taken with a focus on how to advance the distributed control system
architecture to enable an intelligent, data-driven, hybrid policy decision system and method that
leverages multi-domain datasets.

leverages multi-domain state datasets (weather and environment, wireless network, and
microgrid) to determine and decide the most optimal configuration state (baseline/change) policy
(safety, security, resilience) to wholistically apply to coupled wireless communication and
microgrid systems.

Figure 11 also highlights the linkages between the MEC hive-node services that are used to read
data from (weather and environment services) and/or write data to (microgrid and wireless) the

 • Read weather and environmental state data (hurricanes, extreme snow, flooding, fires,

 • Read/write data (e.g., voltage and current state, change logical policy) from or to the

4.3 Technical Considerations and Challenges
The integration of O-RAN technology with the existing grid infrastructure can present
compatibility challenges, particularly when interfacing with legacy systems and existing
communication protocols or grid management systems. Additionally, cybersecurity concerns

secure communication networks and address vulnerabilities. Data privacy and management also
become critical, as sensitive data are generated through microgrid operations and user
interactions. Utilities must ensure compliance with regulatory standards, such as the North

5 Conclusions
Though there are some challenges, O-RAN technology stands to significantly enhance
microgrids and distribution systems by driving improvements in operational efficiency,
scalability, and grid resilience. As advancements in O-RAN, such as network slicing, continue to
evolve, they will provide optimized communication capabilities, allowing microgrids to adapt
and perform more efficiently. The integration of AI and machine learning will further
revolutionize grid management by enabling predictive analytics and autonomous control
systems, improving overall reliability and responsiveness. Additionally, the collaboration
between O-RAN and 5G networks will unlock new levels of performance, offering the low-
latency, high-speed communications that are essential for real-time decision-making and grid
management. For utilities looking to adopt O-RAN, it is crucial to assess the current
infrastructure, align operational strategies, and take deliberate steps toward implementation. By
addressing these factors, utilities can effectively harness the full potential of O-RAN, driving
long-term value and positioning themselves for future growth in an increasingly complex energy
landscape.

Further, the potential for modern 5G technologies such as O-RAN to scale and integrate with
previously untapped campuses, facilities, and field sites for scientific research—particularly in
data collection, analysis, and intelligent control applications—is becoming increasingly apparent.
DOE’s ESnet as well as the broader energy industry stands to significantly benefit from the
adoption of 5G and O-RAN technologies. As Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory works to
shape the future of ESnet with advanced wireless technologies, it is essential that 5G, and
specifically O-RAN, play a central role in ensuring that the network is scalable, future-proof, and
capable of supporting emerging needs in energy science.
