Background

In the United States (U.S.), childhood asthma is a leading cause of childhood morbidity, including missed school days, emergency room visits, and hospitalizations [1,2]. Asthma morbidity is also disproportionately experienced by Black and African American children. Black children with asthma have four times the rates of emergency room visits and three times the rate of hospitalization [2] and death from asthma compared to White peers [3]. Gaps in implementation of guideline-based management strategies represent modifiable drivers of morbidity and health disparities. Previous national guideline management strategies for treating persistent asthma have been inconsistently and inequitably adopted by health care providers [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]] and poorly adhered to by patients [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Further, traditional step-based asthma medication management is complex with separate inhalers for maintenance and acute symptom relief that are often confused for one another by children and families [13].

In 2020, the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) codified a new paradigm of asthma management, single maintenance and reliever therapy (SMART), as the preferred approach for children age 5 and above with moderate to severe asthma given its efficacy and real-world effectiveness evidence [14,15]. A systematic review with meta-analysis demonstrated that compared with conventional two inhaler therapy, SMART reduces severe exacerbation risk by 32% in adults and children ages 12 years and older, and by 62% in children ages 4-11, though the latter finding is from a single trial [16]. SMART has also demonstrated real-world effectiveness in international settings [17], yet these studies are not demographically representative of the U.S. childhood asthma population.

Several mechanisms likely drive SMART's comparative effectiveness and offer promise in narrowing asthma inequities, including improved lung function and better maintenance medication adherence, likely secondary to simplicity of use [[18], [19], [20]]. Superior effectiveness and greater simplicity are foundations of interventions to improve health disparities [21,22]. Additionally, similar combined approaches have previously demonstrated superior effectiveness in Black adults [23] and children [24] relative to standard step-based approaches in the U.S. Pilot data in this southeastern Pennsylvania and southern New Jersey primary care network study setting – where asthma is common and severe, particularly among non-Hispanic Black children [[25], [26], [27]] – demonstrate that while SMART is part of clinical care and clinical asthma pathways [28], less than 20% of potentially eligible children were prescribed a medication compatible with SMART [internal, unpublished data]. There are many practical considerations that explain lower adoption, including insurance coverage barriers and that SMART-compatible inhalers are not approved for reliever use by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Several recent survey studies have documented frequency of and barriers to provider SMART prescribing [29,30], however, studies assessing population-level adoption and real-world effectiveness of SMART among eligible children are needed in the US.

To address these needs, we will conduct a type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial [31] funded by the NHLBI to evaluate implementation strategies that may promote adoption of pediatric asthma guideline-concordant care. This study will implement a suite of interventions, beginning with electronic health record (EHR) clinical decision support plus provider education and then adding population health management support strategies, while assessing guideline-concordant SMART prescribing (implementation outcome) and time to severe asthma exacerbation (effectiveness outcome).

Methods

This study follows Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) cluster-randomized trial extension (supplementary file 1) [32], Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines (supplementary file 2) [33], and Standards for Reporting Implementation studies (StaRI) (supplementary file 3) [34].

Trial management and protection of human subjects

Principal Investigator (Kenyon) will lead this trial. The Principal Investigator and the study team will oversee trial conduct and management. Trial co-Investigators will guide executive decisions employing clinical, primary care, asthma, informatics, biostatistics, implementation science, and data expertise. The Principal Investigator (Kenyon), study team, and members of the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Data Science and Biostatistics Unit will be responsible for the protection and security of data collected and analyzed for the purposes of the trial. This study was approved by the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Institutional Review Board (23-021702) and any modifications to study design or engagement with human subjects will undergo further institutional review board evaluation. The study team will be responsible for conducting informed consent procedures for research participants of applicable study activities.

Trial overview

The SMART & SIMPLE Trial is a type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial [31] deploying a cluster-randomized design within the CHOP primary care network. Clinics (clusters) will be randomized to one of two study arms: usual care condition or two sequential, cumulative implementation strategies (Fig. 1). Prior to randomization, we will measure baseline SMART adoption and seasonal variation – as a categorical variable applied to date of SMART-eligible encounters – in recruited clinics (Interval 0). For detailed descriptions of how we will assess SMART adoption and SMART eligibility, see sections 2.7 Outcomes and 2.6. Eligibility Criteria and Recruitment, respectively. In the first year following randomization (Interval 1), active strategy clinics will receive the first implementation strategy, clinical decision support plus education (CDS+). We will then add population health management support to CDS + for a subsequent year (Interval 2). Implementation strategies are discussed in detail in section 2.4. Lastly, there will be a six-month follow-up period to assess ongoing real-world sustainment after withdrawal of population health management support and education in active implementation strategy arm clinics (Interval 3). There will be a four-week ramp up period at the start of each study interval (ramp up period data will not be included in analyses).Fig. 1SMART & SIMPLE study flow diagramRandomization is at the level of the clinic (cluster).

Aims and hypotheses

Our primary objective (Aim 1) is to compare the effect of successive implementation strategies to usual care on implementation outcomes. We hypothesize each implementation strategy, first CDS + alone and then CDS + augmented with population health management, will be associated with increases in visit-level SMART adoption (primary implementation outcome, defined below), visit-level SMART sustainment, and clinic-level penetration compared to usual care (secondary implementation outcomes, defined below).

The second objective (Aim 2) is to assess the real-world effectiveness of SMART on time to severe asthma exacerbation in a repeated cross-sectional sample of children ages 5 to 18 years with poorly controlled asthma using target trial emulation. We hypothesize SMART adoption during a SMART eligible visit will be associated with a longer duration until severe asthma exacerbation (primary effectiveness outcome) compared to SMART-eligible visits where SMART was not adopted.

Our third objective (Aim 3) is to assess mechanisms of successful SMART implementation and effectiveness using mixed methods. We hypothesize sustained adoption of SMART will be mediated by order set use, templated SMART asthma action plans, and provider situational self-efficacy, and effectiveness will be mediated by patient prescription fills, use of templated SMART asthma action plans, medication adherence, and caregiver and patient self-efficacy.

Implementation strategies

This study will feature two successively introduced multicomponent implementation strategies, which we will administer to all active implementation strategy arm clinics at the cluster level: CDS plus education (CDS+) and then CDS+ with population health management. A preliminary implementation needs assessment found several key provider barriers, including insufficient provider guidance on SMART prescribing at the point of care [35], lack of ongoing caregiver and patient support once prescribed, and systems-level issues, such as negative reinforcement by providers unfamiliar with SMART and SMART medication insurance denials [36]. We mapped SMART implementation determinants, driven by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), to tractable and evidence-based strategies [[37], [38], [39]] (Fig. 2) and identified performance objectives, determinants, and change objectives, leveraging behavior change frameworks [40]. We also identified opportunities for health equity-oriented human-centered design [41] and equitable implementation by developing tailored, adaptable, and family- and patient advocate-informed strategies that respond to the needs of families on SMART [42]. Clinical decision support plus education

The clinical decision support plus education implementation strategy targets adoption, sustainment, and penetration outcomes. Our implementation evaluation of the clinical decision support (CDS) strategy will focus on addressing “inner setting” implementation determinants. Specifically, the CDS will provide pediatricians with guidance on SMART eligibility and promote easy access to patient education materials (e.g., SMART handout, educational animated video) at the point of care (action target) [38]. The CDS will be developed with a human-centered design approach using the SEIPS framework [44]. This approach will be led by a human-centered design expert in partnership with a software engineer, and prioritize input from clinicians, caregivers, and patient stakeholders.

This iterative development process will begin with user research interviews. In the formative stage, we will use clinical scenarios to define provider workflow use cases, gather feedback on high-level specifications, and design mockups. In the validation stage, we will evaluate usability with clinician participants and conduct a beta testing phase in one urban and one suburban clinic to collect objective and subjective data in the context of real-world clinical workflows. We will assess CDS usability, usefulness, and functionality with attention to managing the expected and unexpected differences in provider preferences, clinical workflows, and patient characteristics. Throughout this process we will regularly review interview findings and system designs with experts in asthma care, informatics, and implementation science. Participation of these stakeholders will be critical in further informing interview analysis, refining system design, and enhancing feasibility.

The CDS will launch for providers when accessing EHR charts of SMART-eligible patients (see section 2.6 for Eligibility Criteria of patients for whom the CDS will fire) who are not already on SMART during well and asthma-related sick visits in primary care. The software engineer will map the cohort definitions to the CDS firing logic, which will deliver the CDS strategy. CDS will support providers in identifying SMART-eligible patients in multiple points within the provider workflow in the EHR (patient summary panel, alert, and note-based decision support). The support system will provide links to existing provider tools, such as a medication dispense report and asthma history summary application [45], as well as new tools supporting appropriate SMART prescribing, including a targeted order set, provider and patient education materials, and materials to facilitate medication fulfillment. CDS aims to facilitate medication ordering via a specialized SMART order set, including age-based SMART prescription templates with pre-populated combined (scheduled and as needed) signetur (sig) to facilitate pharmacy recognition of SMART and filling. The CDS will feature SMART resources including a provider tip sheet and patient-facing education materials in English, Spanish, and Arabic. To alleviate costs for families, the order set will also include provider formulary guidance for commercial insurances and Medicaid, linking to preferred drug lists by insurer and plan, a SMART generic prior authorization form, and SMART-compatible medication coupons.

The education implementation strategy will target primary care providers, nurses, and families during Intervals 1 and 2 (Fig. 2). The study team will host two synchronous provider education sessions per intervention interval with active implementation strategy arm clinics. During these 1-h sessions, the study team will educate providers on SMART prescribing best practices, including using the previously described prescription templates and knowledge of insurance coverage policies and practices to attain multiple inhalers. The CDS will provide additional provider educational resources, as described above. Implementation determinants related to education focus on individual characteristics of understanding of and self-efficacy in prescribing or taking SMART [38]. All materials will be reviewed by institutional pediatric asthma experts. In addition to circulating SMART CDS job aids, the study team will host two case-based clinician education sessions, using a model for enhancing clinical guidelines adoption [24] tailored to SMART. Sessions will integrate CDS into several use cases to model clinical workflows.

Nurse and ancillary staff education will include triage nurses, the first contact for families reaching out for medical advice and assistance, often during asthma exacerbations. The study team will conduct yearly educational outreach with this group focusing on nurse triage relevant domains of SMART, including awareness of and familiarity with SMART, identification of SMART in the child's EHR, and asthma exacerbation management with SMART based on a child's asthma action plan instruction and nurse-triage guidelines. The study team will host at least one synchronous session with triage nurses from the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia triage pool and individual sessions with active implementation strategy arm clinics who have their own nurse triage systems. The study team will also coordinate with nurse triage leadership to provide asynchronous educational content on triaging family needs for those on SMART.

The family education strategy will include patient-facing overviews of SMART included in CDS order sets that will populate after-visit summaries and through the patient portal, which providers can order through the SMART order-set. All materials will be reviewed by pediatric asthma experts, primary care pediatricians, and local specialists. They will also undergo standard hospital system family education approval processes. We will provide patient and family education in web, print, and animated video formats, and education will be available in English, Spanish, and Arabic. The study team will encourage primary care providers to share SMART-specific education with families during and after visits. Ultimately, clinicians will be the actors in delivering SMART-focused patient and family education and determine for whom and under what circumstances to use these resources.

Population health management strategies

In Interval 2 (Fig. 1), the population health management implementation strategy will target the implementation sustainment outcome. This strategy includes two actors, an asthma navigator and an asthma nurse care coordinator, who will support SMART-eligible patients in clinics randomized to receive active implementation strategies. The asthma navigator and nurse care coordinator will provide navigational support with other providers and address insurance barriers, aligning with “outer setting” implementation determinants related to health care system navigation (Fig. 2) [38]. The action targets of this implementation strategy are situational self-efficacy of families in taking SMART, appropriate use of SMART medications, and medication fill data.

An asthma navigator – a community health worker trained to deliver and coordinate asthma education and care – will conduct a series of virtual or home visits focused on asthma treatment, prevention, and SMART-specific education following a visit where a provider initiated a patient on SMART. They will reinforce appropriate SMART inhaler use with families, including daily preventative and timely, symptom-based ICS-formoterol inhaler use to prevent symptom progression. They will use principles of motivational interviewing when they encounter challenges such as poor adherence to daily preventative medication use or difficulties de-implementing separate reliever inhaler use. Navigators will ensure home and school inhaler access and partner with providers and the nurse care coordinator where necessary. Navigators will also help facilitate connection with programs that address unmet social needs, when appropriate, including issues with utilities, food insecurity, housing conditions, transportation, and medication access.

The asthma nurse care coordinator will serve as a liaison between providers from primary care and specialty when there may be discordant asthma management recommendations with respect to SMART. The nurse care coordinator will also address prescribing and refill issues with primary care providers and insurance companies (action targets). This includes navigating medication prior authorization and quantity limits, which vary by insurer and can be particularly challenging for patients who need inhalers in multiple sites, such as secondary homes, school, or aftercare. The nurse care coordinator efforts to facilitate SMART medication fulfillment may contribute to the implementation sustainment outcome.

Study setting and sample

The setting of this study is within the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) Pediatric Research Consortium, a 31-clinic practice-based research network with a unified EHR. All sites are eligible to participate.

Eligibility Criteria and Recruitment

The study team will recruit approximately 18 clinics through presentations to clinic medical directors and provider teams on planned study implementation strategies and procedures. We will then randomize clinics (clusters) to either usual care or active implementation strategy arms in a 1:1 allocation. While the unit of randomization is the clinic (cluster), this study includes three populations of individuals: SMART-eligible patients for whom we will extract EHR data (population 1); providers who participate in questionnaires and interviews (population 2); and caregivers of SMART-eligible patients and SMART-eligible patients who are ≥7 years old who may participate in questionnaires and interviews (population 3). The assessment of patients, providers, and caregivers will follow a repeated cross-sectional design over the 3.5-year study period.

SMART-eligible patient criteria include: (1) age 5-18 years, (2) in-person visit at a participating CHOP primary care clinic during the study interval; (3) at least one prescription for an inhaled asthma controller medication in the last year, and (4) evidence of poor control either by impairment (reported “poorly controlled” or “uncontrolled” by symptom report on the Asthma Control Tool (ACT) [46] in the past 6 months) or risk criteria (two or more systemic steroids prescribed for an asthma exacerbation in the past 12 months, one occurring in the most recent 6 months). All patient encounters in clinics (clusters) meeting SMART-eligible criteria and their corresponding primary care well and sick visits will be included for the purposes of this study.

Outcomes

The primary implementation outcome, visit-level SMART adoption, will be an indicator of whether SMART is prescribed – either through use of a “combined sig” prescription template (scheduled and as needed use) for ICS-formoterol or ICS formoterol being newly present in both the green and yellow zones of the AAP – during a primary care visit (sick, well, or follow-up visit) when a child is eligible to start SMART. Secondary implementation outcomes include (1) visit-level sustainment, defined as an indicator of whether a patient is prescribed (initiated) on SMART at an eligible visit and subsequently continues on SMART for at least 6 months, and (2) clinic-level (cluster-level) penetration, defined as the proportion of SMART-eligible patients who are prescribed (initiated) and not subsequently discontinued from this regimen by the end of the interval in a clinic.

The primary effectiveness outcome will be patient-level time to severe asthma exacerbations, defined as the time from SMART-eligible visit to first asthma exacerbation [47]. An encounter when a systemic corticosteroid was prescribed will constitute an asthma exacerbation. A secondary outcome will be change in patient-level asthma control, defined as the categorical change in asthma control assessment (improved, same, worse) from baseline and after a SMART-eligible encounter, as measured by the Asthma Control Tool [46].

Randomization

This cluster-randomized trial uses covariate constrained randomization [48] to assign clinics (clusters) to usual care or active implementation strategy arms [49,50]. To do this, we will identify a set of balancing factors, driven by a conceptual framework of priority covariates related to SMART prescribing, asthma morbidity, and representativeness considerations, to minimize confounding in the cluster design. We will record the value of balancing factors for each practice, and the co-investigator biostatisticians will integrate into the covariate constrained randomization algorithm. Using a tailored SAS program, we will generate all possible allocations of 18 clinics to active implementation strategy or usual care and rank them for balance, where balance is defined by a distance metric based on differences in the balancing factors between active implementation strategy or usual care clinics for a given allocation. Clinics will be balanced with respect to locality (urban vs. suburban); number of SMART-eligible children in the past year; and proportion of SMART-eligible patients who are SMART-initiated; non-Hispanic Black; publicly insured; and currently 5-11 years old. All criteria will utilize data from the past year. One allocation will be randomly selected from the top 20 balanced possible allocations. The allocation file will be password-protected and will not be opened by the co-investigator biostatistician before it is sent to the program manager, who will then inform the relevant team of the assignments. The statistician and analytic team will be blinded to cluster (clinic) study arm assignments until analysis is complete.

For the patient-level effectiveness outcome of mean number of asthma exacerbations (original Aim 2 primary effectiveness outcome), we conducted a simulation study leveraging available practice network data. Based on prevalence estimates from asthma registry reports, we assume a sample size of at minimum 1000 children (over Intervals 1 and 2). We sampled from exacerbation count distribution across all clinics to inform SMART non-initiator exacerbation rates and determined over 90% power to detect a difference in exacerbation rate between initiators and non-initiators assuming a true rate ratio of 0.8, a conservative estimate based on existing literature [16,17,51]. Considering subgroup analysis by age, we confirmed over 80% to detect a difference between initiators and non-initiators separately for 5-11 and 12+ year olds if the true rate ratio in each group were 0.75. Our analysis assumed quasi-Poisson models with fixed effects for site.

For implementation and effectiveness mechanistic analyses using mixed methods (Aim 3), we expect to reach thematic saturation after performing approximately 30 semi-structured interviews with providers and approximately 30 caregiver and patient interviews.

Analytic approaches

This study primarily relies on large data extractions from the EHR. The extraction team will pull all EHR data, and the analytic team will clean, organize, and restructure visit- and patient-level data into analytic files to execute study analyses. Mechanistic analyses will rely on EHR, questionnaire, and interview data.

Implementation analyses

To evaluate the effect of implementation strategies on visit-level adoption of SMART, we will estimate the difference between the two arms in the difference in the rate of SMART adoption across consecutive intervals. We will use population-averaged logistic models estimated via generalized estimating equations (GEE) and Fay-Graubard [52] adjustment to correct for potential small-sample bias. For additional robustness, we will use the bootstrap percentile method with resampling by cluster. Each model will include terms for interval, study arm, balancing factors, and their interaction. The coefficient (or exponentiated coefficient) of the interaction term will estimate the difference-in-differences (DID) effect.

We will report results on the risk difference scale by marginalizing over the model estimates, and we will calculate 95% confidence intervals. To assess visit-level sustained adoption, we will estimate the DID in SMART sustained adoption at the visit level. The modeling approach and estimated contrasts will mirror those used for the adoption outcome described above.

To evaluate clinic-level (cluster-level) SMART penetration, we will estimate the DID in clinic-level penetration. We will use population-averaged logistic models assuming a binomial distribution for the proportions (i.e., n successes out of m trials). The model structure and estimated contrasts will follow the same approach as for the visit-level outcomes.

Additionally, we will evaluate adoption by key sociodemographic characteristics (race, ethnicity, primary language spoken) to measure equitable adoption and implementation, given objectives to measure how strategies may contribute to closing pediatric asthma disparity gaps by increasing guideline-concordant care.

Effectiveness analyses

Severe asthma exacerbation (effectiveness outcome) will be defined as the time to first severe asthma exacerbation [47]. Since implementation strategies are at the clinic (cluster) level and providers and patients determine when it is appropriate to switch to SMART, initiation is not randomized in this trial. Given this, methods for estimating causal effects in observational studies need to be employed. We will follow the target trial emulation framework to define components including eligibility criteria, treatment assignment, and follow up period to ensure the only source of bias relative to the ideal randomized trial is the confounding of the treatment and outcome [53].

Implementation and effectiveness mediation analyses using mixed methods

Implementation mechanistic analyses (see Fig. 3, Panel A) will assess potential relationships between SMART implementation strategies and sustained SMART adoption by retrospectively assessing visit-level SMART order set use (binary) and other CDS tools developed as part of the study. Since providers can prescribe SMART several ways but only active implementation strategy arm clinics will receive the CDS, visit-level order set use and activation of other CDS tools may mediate sustained adoption. Specifically, an order set written for successful medication fulfillment, and other CDS tools, such as templated SMART asthma action plan home instructions, may facilitate sustained SMART adoption. We will quantitatively evaluate implementation mediation analyses using EMR data. Additionally, we will conduct exploratory qualitative evaluations of potential mediation effects of provider situational self-efficacy in prescribing SMART on the relationship between implementation strategies and sustained SMART adoption. Effectiveness mechanistic analyses (see Fig. 3, Panel B) will evaluate relationships between SMART-initiation and severe asthma exacerbations by investigating hypothesized mediation effects of (1) prescription fills using a binary indicator from quantitative EHR data and (2) use of templated SMART asthma action plans, which provide clear dose escalation instructions for caregivers and triage nurses for increasing asthma symptoms. Additionally, we will conduct exploratory mediation analyses using mixed methods. We will assess caregiver and patient medication adherence and situational self-efficacy effects utilizing caregiver-reported information from a questionnaire to measure patient medication adherence [54] and SMART situational self-efficacy [55] and conduct interviews with caregivers and age-eligible patients.

To further understand implementation and effectiveness mechanistic relationships, we will extract and analyze themes from qualitative interviews with providers, caregivers, and SMART-eligible patients. We will double code and consistently search for negative cases within data to refine our analysis as we discover themes and patterns [56]. Then, our team will apply the CFIR framework [38], allowing new concept emergence and integration into the coding scheme using an iterative, inductive coding approach to generate explanations for emerging patterns [57].

Discussion

This type II hybrid effectiveness-implementation trial will test a suite of strategies to facilitate adoption of SMART, while assessing real-world clinical effectiveness of SMART in a pediatric population with high asthma prevalence and morbidity. Implementation strategies will feature clinical decision support, educational outreach, and two population health management strategies, including an asthma navigator and nurse care coordinator.

This study and its pragmatic, cluster-randomized design will enable us to clearly evaluate provider and patient adoption of SMART in the context of different implementation strategies. Additionally, this research will contribute to the literature on the real-world clinical effectiveness of SMART and will be the one of the first performed in a U.S. pediatric population. To accomplish this, we will employ a target trial emulation causal inference framework to evaluate effectiveness outcomes, offering robust analytic approaches to assess SMART effectiveness. We will utilize mediation analyses to describe mechanistic relationships of implementation outcomes and effectiveness outcomes using mixed methods. To orient this work towards addressing prevalent pediatric asthma disparities and representative prospective data collection (Aim 3), we will evaluate differences in SMART adoption (Aim 1) by demographic characteristics to measure representative research participation and equitable adoption. We will assess who is exposed to implementation strategies and evaluate impact in a way that reflects our intent to address modifiable drivers of morbidity and health disparities [42,[58], [59], [60], [61]].

Meaningful engagement and input from various clinical team members that interact with patients on SMART and multidisciplinary stakeholders position these implementation strategies to adequately support patients and families on SMART [58]. Patient- and family-centered strategies will focus on well-articulated barriers to adherence [12], while provider strategies will employ user-centered design methods. We will focus on numerous challenges to caring for patients on SMART, including de-implementation of additional rescue medications to promote guideline-concordant care, care co-management with within network and out-of-network specialty providers, and insurance barriers. Implementation strategies will strategically address insurance challenges, such as formulary and inhaler limits, to promote equitable access. We will pair our efforts with complementary policy advocacy work when disseminating findings to mitigate insurance barriers, particularly for state Medicaid formularies [62].

This study has many strengths including engaging many individual primary care practices caring for a varied population that includes children at high-risk of poor asthma outcomes, objective outcome assessment by EHR, quasi-experimental methods to minimize confounding and bias, and a highly pragmatic design to contemporaneously assess implementation and effectiveness. This cluster-randomized design prevents provider-level contamination and enables the population-level implementation strategy bundle to address multiple levels utilizing a health care systems approach.

The study has several limitations. All involved practices are part of a pediatric primary care network with study setting-specific facilitators, such as a common EHR, research supporting infrastructure, and a multidisciplinary asthma population health team committed to improving asthma care and outcomes [63]. When reporting findings, we will acknowledge limitations of findings measured in this uniquely positioned environment, and, as applicable, we will articulate the transportability of elements of these implementation strategies elsewhere. For findings to be reproducible, researchers and clinician leaders will need to account for health system-specific infrastructure, including ability to extract EHR data elements to identify SMART-eligible patients and distinguish patients initiated on SMART. While we will rigorously calibrate EHR logic to determine patient eligibility and initiation on SMART, our current estimate of 20% SMART adoption is an unpublished estimate; nuanced clinical characteristics may limit our ability to identify all patients who are eligible and initiated in certain circumstances, and the data is limited to what is available in the EHR, obscuring information between visits. To minimize these concerns, we have completed a formal, manual clinician review of identified patients and a structured comparison across data sources to thoroughly validate and articulate CDS and research definitions. Thirdly, while we have attempted to address coverage barriers for SMART through both provider education and direct assistance with prior authorization, there are circumstances where ICS-formoterol cannot be dispensed because (a) it is not on the insurance company's formulary or (2) pharmacists will not dispense medications for a non-FDA approved indication. Lastly, the observational nature of the effectiveness study (Aim 2) inherently limits our ability to make causal conclusions around effectiveness of SMART. We have, however, elected to use a causal inference methodology – target trial emulation – to attempt to mitigate selection bias and confounding.

This study represents one of the first effectiveness-implementation studies of SMART, an evidence-based practice that, if implemented broadly, has the potential to improve childhood asthma outcomes and disparities. Behavior change around SMART will require shifts in provider asthma management decisions, patient and family behaviors and routines, and proactive health care system support and reinforcement, intervening at multiple levels. This trial is suited to identify effective implementation strategies by virtue of foundational implementation mapping using determinants frameworks grounded in behavioral theory and equity-oriented, community-centered implementation approaches. Evidence gained from this trial will help overcome barriers to uptake of SMART in healthcare settings that treat diverse populations of children whose socioeconomic and other factors, such as insurance, may hinder access to equitable asthma care and subsequent asthma outcomes.
