Association of musculoskeletal impairments and balance with quality of life in adults with severe hemophilia A: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Necati Muhammed Tat, Ayşe Merve Tat, Ali Doğan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal complications remain common in people with severe hemophilia A (PwsHA) despite advances in medical treatment. Joint damage, pain, and functional limitations may affect health-related quality of life (QoL). This study aimed to evaluate QoL in PwsHA and examine associations of joint health, pain severity, dynamic balance, and fall history with QoL.MethodsThirty-six PwsHA participated in this cross-sectional study. QoL was assessed using the adult version of the Haemophilia-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire. Joint health was evaluated using the Hemophilia Joint Health Score, pain severity using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale, and dynamic balance using the Limits of Stability test on a computerized posturography platform. Fall history was recorded as the self-reported number of falls during the previous 12 months. Correlations examined relationships between variables, and multiple linear regression identified factors independently associated with QoL.ResultsQoL analyses were completed for 35 participants (mean age 29.9 ± 9.4 years). Joint health, pain and dynamic balance showed a moderate correlation with QoL (r = 0.378, p = 0.30; r = 0.438, p = 0.011; r = − 0.35, p = 0.046, respectively). Fall history was not significantly correlated with QoL. However multiple regression analysis including joint health, pain, and dynamic balance did not identify a statistically significant model for explaining QoL.ConclusionsJoint health impairment, pain severity, and dynamic balance performance were associated with QoL in PwsHA. These findings support comprehensive musculoskeletal assessment including balance evaluation to help identify potential targets for rehabilitation interventions in this population.Trial registrationThe study protocol was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07523399) on 04/04/2026.Keywords: Hemophilia A, Arthropathy, Musculoskeletal pain, Postural balance, Quality of life, Accidental falls

Introduction

Hemophilia is a congenital bleeding disorder caused by deficiency of coagulation factor VIII (hemophilia A) or factor IX (hemophilia B). It is characterized by recurrent bleeding episodes, most commonly into joints and muscles, which may lead to chronic musculoskeletal complications over time [1]. Hemophilia A is the most common clinical type, accounting for approximately 80–85% of all hemophilia cases, and nearly half of affected individuals have severe disease, defined by clotting factor activity levels below 1% [1]. Individuals with severe hemophilia A may experience spontaneous bleeding episodes, particularly in the absence of adequate medical treatment; these episodes, especially hemarthrosis may lead to progressive joint damage and functional impairment [1, 2]. Advances in treatment, prophylactic factor replacement therapies and especially newer treatment approaches, have considerably increased life expectancy in people with hemophilia (PwH) [2]. As survival has improved, clinical attention has gradually shifted from acute bleeding management to long-term musculoskeletal outcomes, physical functioning, and participation in daily life [3]. Recurrent hemarthrosis may result in hemophilic arthropathy, which is associated with joint damage, chronic pain, reduced mobility, and limitations in daily activities [4]. These musculoskeletal complications are considered important contributors to impaired health-related quality of life (QoL) in PwH [5].

Hemophilic arthropathy develops as a consequence of repeated hemarthrosis and may lead to progressive cartilage degeneration, synovial inflammation, and structural joint damage [6]. These pathological changes are commonly associated with chronic pain, joint stiffness, reduced range of motion, and muscle weakness, which may negatively affect functional capacity [4, 5]. Pain is one of the most frequently reported symptoms among adults with hemophilia and has been shown to influence daily activities, work participation, and general well-being [5, 7]. Even in individuals receiving prophylactic treatment, joint damage and persistent pain may lead to activity limitations and long-term disability [2, 4]. Previous studies have shown that deterioration in joint health is associated with reduced functional independence and poorer QoL in PwH [5, 7]. Therefore, joint health and pain are considered key musculoskeletal factors that may influence functional outcomes and QoL in this population.

In addition to structural joint impairments, neuromuscular factors may also contribute to functional limitations in PwH. Hemophilic arthropathy may affect proprioception, muscle strength, and postural control, which are essential for maintaining balance. Previous studies have reported decreased muscle strength, impaired postural stability, and altered sensorimotor function in PwH compared with healthy individuals [8–11]. Impaired balance may limit mobility and participation in daily activities and may increase the risk of falls [8, 12]. Falls are clinically relevant in PwH because trauma related to falls may trigger bleeding episodes and may contribute to further joint damage [12]. A recent study conducted in children with hemophilia reported that impaired dynamic balance and increased fall risk were not associated with QoL [13]. However, evidence regarding the relationship between dynamic balance, falls, and QoL in adults with hemophilia remains limited.

Although previous studies have investigated joint health, pain, and functional limitations in PwH, the combined contribution of joint health, pain, dynamic balance, and falls to QoL has not been fully clarified. Understanding how musculoskeletal impairments and functional performance relate to patient-reported outcomes may help identify clinically relevant and potentially modifiable targets for rehabilitation interventions. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate QoL in people with severe hemophilia A (PwsHA) and to investigate the associations of joint health, pain severity, dynamic balance performance, and falls with QoL. PwsHA were specifically evaluated because this subgroup represents the most common clinical presentation and is frequently associated with recurrent hemarthrosis and musculoskeletal complications. It was hypothesized that poorer joint health, higher pain levels, impaired dynamic balance, and a history of falls would be associated with lower QoL.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted with PwsHA who were recruited from the Adult Hematology Unit of the Faculty of Medicine at Van Yüzüncü Yıl University.

Of the 102 hemophilia (A or B) patients registered at our center, 46 had severe hemophilia A. Of the 46 patients assessed for eligibility, 10 were excluded according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, and 36 patients were finally included in the study and completed all assessments. Inclusion criteria were: diagnosis of severe hemophilia A, age ≥ 18 years, and evidence of joint involvement defined as a minimum single-joint score of 3 points for the knee or ankle according to the Hemophilia Joint Health Score (HJHS). As no validated HJHS cutoff has been established and previous studies have used different approaches, an HJHS score of ≥ 3 was selected pragmatically to identify participants with mild joint involvement. Exclusion criteria included the use of walking aids (e.g., crutches), the presence of neurological or psychiatric disorders, chronic inflammatory disease, regular use of medications that could affect balance, a history of lower extremity surgical procedures (e.g., arthroscopic synovectomy or total knee replacement) within the previous six months. Participants who had experienced an acute ankle or knee hemarthrosis within the previous two weeks were not assessed until the acute hemarthrosis episode had resolved. Eligible patients were initially identified and contacted by a physiotherapist specialized in hemophilia care and hematolog. Detailed study information was provided to patients by the same physiotherapist. Written informed consent was subsequently obtained and all assessments were completed in a single session to minimize inter-session variability. All evaluations were performed by two physiotherapists experienced in hemophilia, using a standardized protocol to reduce inter-rater variability. All participant recruitment and assessments were conducted between February and April 2026.

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Since the primary aim of the study was to investigate the associations of joint health and dynamic balance with QoL in PwsHA, an a priori F test for multiple linear regression (fixed model, R² deviation from zero) was performed. Assuming a medium-to-large effect size (f² = 0.25), a significance level of α = 0.05, and statistical power of 0.70, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 34 participants for two independent variables (joint health and dynamic balance). Considering the rarity of hemophilia, the statistical power was set at 0.70. A total of 36 participants meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the study.

Participant characteristics

Participants’ age, height, and body weight were recorded, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. Information regarding prophylactic treatment type was obtained from medical records.

Joint health assessment

Joint health was assessed using the HJHS version 2.1, a clinician-administered measure commonly used to evaluate joint status in PwH. The HJHS evaluates joint impairment in the elbows, knees and ankles bilaterally and includes domains such as swelling, muscle atrophy, crepitus, pain, strength, and range of motion. Since this study focused on lower-extremity function, elbow joints were not included in the evaluation. Therefore, the total HJHS score was calculated based solely on knee and ankle joints together with the global gait score. Thus, the maximum possible HJHS score was 84 instead of 124. Higher scores indicate poorer joint health status. The HJHS version 2.1 is a valid and reliable measure for evaluating joint health in PwH [14].

Pain assessment

Pain severity was assessed using the Numeric Pain Rating Scale. Participants were asked to rate their average pain intensity experienced during daily activities, such as walking, over the previous month using an 11-point numeric rating scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). This scale is a widely used, valid, and reliable measure for assessing pain intensity in clinical populations [15].

Dynamic balance assessment

Dynamic balance was evaluated using the ProKin Tecnobody Stabilometric Analysis System (ProKin 212, TecnoBody S.R.L., Dalmine, Italy). Detailed technical information about the ProKin Tecnobody device has been previously described by Ferrazoli et al. [16].

Dynamic balance was assessed using the Limits of Stability (LoS) test. For this test, the posturography platform was set to a movable condition. Participants were instructed to perform the test barefoot and to shift their center of pressure toward the visual targets on the screen while maintaining balance without using the safety rails. If the participant took a step or grasped the support rails, the trial was considered invalid and subsequently repeated. The LoS test assessment consisted of weight-shifting tasks toward eight visual targets displayed sequentially, each presented for a standardized duration according to the ProKin device protocol. After one familiarization trial, the second trial was recorded for analysis. A detailed description of the LoS test has also been provided in a previous publication by the authors [17]. LoS scores range between 0% and 100%, with higher scores indicating better dynamic balance performance [18].

Fall history assessment

Falls were assessed based on the self-reported number of falls experienced during the previous 12 months. A fall was defined as an event in which a person unintentionally comes to rest on the ground or a lower level. The number of falls was recorded for each participant [12, 19].

QoL assessment

QoL was assessed using the Turkish version of the Haemophilia-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire for adults (Haem-A-QoL), a disease-specific patient-reported outcome measure developed for adults with hemophilia. The Haem-A-QoL consists of 46 items covering ten domains: Physical Health, Feelings, View of Yourself, Sports and Leisure, Work and School, Dealing with Hemophilia, Treatment, Future, Family Planning, and Partnership and Sexuality. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, and domain scores are transformed to a 0–100 scale. A total QoL score was calculated based on domain scores. Higher scores indicate poorer QoL. The Turkish version of the Haem-A-QoL is valid and reliable [20].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables, median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables.

Bivariate associations between joint health, dynamic balance, pain severity, fall history, age, BMI, and QoL were evaluated using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients, as appropriate. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as low (0.10–0.29), moderate (0.30–0.49), and strong (≥ 0.50) [21].

Multiple linear regression analysis (enter method) was performed to identify factors independently associated with QoL. Total QoL score was included as the dependent variable. Joint health, pain and dynamic balance were entered as independent variables because they showed the significant correlations with QoL and were considered clinically relevant musculoskeletal parameters. Regression assumptions were assessed prior to analysis. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factor (VIF < 5). Model fit was assessed using the adjusted R² value. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 36 PwsHA were included in the study. One participant did not complete the QoL questionnaire; therefore, analyses including QoL were conducted with 35 participants. Participant characteristics are presented in Table 1. The median joint health was 10 (IQR 4–24), indicating varying levels of joint involvement. Overall, 30 knee joints and 31 ankle joints had a modified HJHS score of ≥ 3, indicating that joint involvement was observed in both the knee and ankle joints within the study cohort. The median pain score during activity was 3 (IQR 0–6). The mean dynamic balance score was 86.2 ± 9.8. Eight participants (23.5%) reported experiencing at least one fall during the previous 12 months. The mean number of falls of the all participants in the previous year was 0.28 ± 0.52. All participants were receiving prophylactic treatment with extended half-life factor VIII replacement therapy.

QoL scores are summarized in Table 1. The mean total QoL score was 36.9 ± 13.8. Among QoL domains, higher (worse) scores were observed in the Work, Treatment, Physical Health, and Sports and Leisure domains, whereas lower scores were observed in the Sexuality, Family Planning, and Dealing with Hemophilia domains.

Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between QoL and clinical variables (Table 2, Fig. 1). Joint health and pain showed a moderate positive correlation with QoL, indicating that poorer joint health and high pain level was associated with worse QoL. Dynamic balance showed a moderate negative correlation with QoL, indicating that lower dynamic balance performance was associated with worse QoL. Falls, age, and BMI were not significantly correlated with QoL (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The present study investigated the associations of joint health, pain severity, dynamic balance, and falls with QoL in PwsHA. The correlation findings showed that poorer joint health, higher pain severity and lower dynamic balance were associated with worse QoL outcomes. Fall history were not significantly associated with QoL. The multiple regression model including joint health, pain, dynamic balance and QoL was not statistically significant, suggesting that their combined contribution to QoL could not be confirmed in this sample. Overall, our findings partially supported the study hypothesis, as all hypothesized associations were confirmed except for the relationship between fall history and QoL (Fig. 1).

Joint health is an important indicator of long-term musculoskeletal status in PwH. The median modified HJHS score observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports demonstrating persistent joint impairment in PwsHA despite prophylactic treatment [7, 22]. In the present study, poorer joint health was associated with worse QoL scores, supporting previous findings showing that joint deterioration is an important determinant of QoL in PwsHA [5]. The modified HJHS used in this study primarily reflected lower-extremity joint health rather than overall joint status, as the elbow joints were excluded. This approach was considered appropriate because lower-extremity joint function is more directly related to balance performance. Nevertheless, upper-extremity joint impairment may also contribute to quality of life through its impact on activities of daily living. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted primarily in the context of lower-extremity joint health.

Pain is also frequently reported in PwH and may negatively influence physical activity levels and participation in daily life [7]. The median pain score during activity in the present study was generally consistent with pain levels reported in previous studies involving adults with severe hemophilia [23]. Despite prophylactic treatment, musculoskeletal pain remained evident and was associated with poorer quality of life, in agreement with previous reports. Previous studies investigating QoL in hemophilia have generally included mixed samples of individuals with hemophilia A and B [5, 7]. In contrast, the present study included only PwsHA, which represents the most common clinical subtype. Previous studies have suggested that PwsHA may experience a higher frequency of hemarthrosis and greater long-term joint morbidity than those with severe hemophilia B although this remains a matter of debate [24, 25]. All participants in the present study were receiving prophylactic treatment with extended half-life factor VIII replacement therapy, which has been shown to reduce annual bleeding rates and frequency of hemarthrosis compared with conventional factor replacement therapies [26, 27]. Despite effective prophylactic treatment, joint-related impairments were still associated with QoL outcomes in this sample.

Dynamic balance was also associated with QoL in this study. Although direct numerical comparison is limited because different balance assessment systems and protocols have been used, the mean dynamic balance score in the present study supports previous evidence showing balance-related impairments in PwsHA compared to PwH and healthy controls [8, 11]. This finding is also consistent with our previous study using the Technobody ProKin system in adults with hemophilia [17]. Impaired balance performance may lead to limitations in mobility and may restrict participation in daily activities, which may negatively influence QoL. Falls were not significantly associated with QoL in the present study. However, falls are still clinically important in patients with hemophilia because fall-related injuries may cause bleeding episodes and may contribute to further joint damage [12]. Previous studies have reported that falls are common in PwH, with approximately 25–50% of patients reporting at least one fall within the past year [28–30]. In the present study, approximately 23.5% of participants reported experiencing at least one fall within the previous year which is consistent with rates reported in the literature. The observed proportion was similar to fall rates reported for young and middle-aged adults in the general population, which are typically estimated to range between approximately 15–25% [31, 32]. Considering that the mean age of our sample was relatively low, this finding is not unexpected. PwH and their families are often highly aware of injury risk from early childhood and this awareness may reduce trauma risk.

The findings of this study may have clinical implications. Joint health, pain severity, and dynamic balance were associated with QoL in present study. These findings suggest that joint-related impairments and balance performance may influence patient-reported outcomes in this population. However, the multiple regression model including these variables was not statistically significant, indicating that their combined contribution to QoL could not be confirmed in the present sample. This finding may indicate that QoL in PwsHA is influenced by additional factors beyond joint health, pain, and dynamic balance, including other physical, psychological, and social domains assessed by the Haem-A-QoL questionnaire. Nevertheless, the observed associations suggest that comprehensive musculoskeletal assessment may contribute to a better understanding of musculoskeletal health in PwsHA. Regular assessment of joint health, pain severity, and dynamic balance may help identify individuals at risk of reduced QoL. In addition to medical treatment, rehabilitation approaches targeting joint function, muscle strength, proprioception, and balance may improve functional outcomes and support participation in daily activities in PwH [4, 33, 34]. A multidisciplinary approach including hematologists and physiotherapists may be beneficial for optimizing long-term musculoskeletal outcomes in PwsHA [1].

This study has some limitations. The sample size was relatively small, and the a priori power analysis was conducted using a statistical power level of 0.70, which is below the conventional threshold of 0.80 and may limit the detection of smaller effect sizes. In addition, only PwsHA were included; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to individuals with hemophilia B or milder disease severity. Falls were evaluated based on self-report and may be affected by recall bias. Furthermore, fall risk was not assessed using a standardized instrument, and only the number of falls within the previous 12 months was recorded. Although the modified HJHS primarily reflected lower-extremity joint health rather than overall joint status, this approach was considered appropriate because lower-extremity joint function is more directly related to balance performance. However, upper-extremity joint impairment may also affect quality of life through its impact on activities of daily living. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted primarily in the context of lower-extremity joint health. Additionally, because annual bleeding rate and annual joint bleeding rate data were unavailable, the potential influence of bleeding burden, including acute hemarthrosis episodes at other joint sites, on QoL could not be evaluated.

Conclusion

Joint health, pain severity, and dynamic balance were associated with QoL in PwsHA. These findings highlight the relevance of musculoskeletal health and functional performance for patient-reported outcomes in this population. Regular assessment of joint health, pain, and balance may help identify individuals at risk of reduced QoL and may support targeted rehabilitation strategies.
