Purpose: Malaria treatment in children requires treatment adherence, but caregiver-related factors affecting adherence are
under-researched. The purpose of the study is to establish the extent of antimalarial adherence among children with malaria
and to assess the quality of caregiver-reported measures of symptom improvement, side effects, and patient satisfaction.
Methods: A cross-sectional survey of 386 caregivers of children aged 5-17 years was conducted among patients with malaria.
The Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), a caregiver-reported outcome measure, and demographic instruments
were used to collect data. Descriptive statistics, Spearman correlation, Kruskal-Wallis tests and multiple regression were examined.
Results: Caregiver-reported treatment outcomes were positively correlated with medication adherence (r = 0.312, p < 0.001).
There were also significant differences in adherence and perceived outcomes across age groups, with higher adherence and
more positive perceived outcomes among children aged 11-13 years. The regression analysis showed that adherence (0.164,
p = 0.001), child age (0.149, p = 0.001), caregiver age (0.110, p = 0.008), and medication source (0.126, p = 0.001) were likely
predictors of treatment outcomes. The gender of children was negatively associated with the outcome (β = -0.092, p = 0.034).
Conclusion: Increased medication adherence is strongly associated with more favourable caregiver-reported treatment
outcomes in paediatric malaria. Child and caregiver characteristics, as well as access to formal medication sources, also deter­
mine these outcomes. Malaria management can be strengthened by supporting adherence and improving caregiver education
in the endemic areas.

Introduction
Malaria, caused by Plasmodium parasites, is a
significant health issue worldwide and a primary
cause of childhood morbidity and mortality. It is
similar to other infections in that it has overlapping
symptoms, and early diagnosis and timely treat­
ment are essential1, 2). The prevalence among chil­
dren, in general, is approximately 9%, with higher
prevalence in symptomatic cases, which can result in
serious complications like anaemia, cerebral malaria,
or respiratory distress3).
Risk factors include household construction, elec­
tricity, and caregiver education; interventions such
as indoor residual spraying can reduce malaria
risk4). Combination artemisinin-based therapy
(ACTs) proves to be very effective and well toler­
ated in the treatment of uncomplicated malaria
caused by P. falciparum in children, where success
rates are greater than 93% at 42 days of treatment5).
However, emerging therapies are required due to
the emergence of drug-resistant parasites6).
Drug efficacy depends on patient compliance
with antimalarial therapy, although reported compli­
ance may range from less than 50% to nearly 100%.
Patient characteristics, quality of consultation, and
methodology are among the factors that influence
compliance7). Poor compliance with antimalarial
therapy is a major contributor to poor outcomes.
Interventions that may improve adherence include
community education, visual media, verbal informa­
tion, and medication adherence, with no single
technique clearly superior to the others8).
Caregivers are likely to wait to receive proper
care, but instead apply home remedies or visit drug
stores, which have low service quality9). A majority
of the caregivers (50%) have correct knowledge
and attitudes regarding malaria prevention, yet
there is a practice gap, and malaria remains highly
prevalent in children under five (54%). Community
engagement and targeted education should be
increased to improve prevention and treatment
outcomes10).
Assessment of treatment adherence and care­
giver-reported outcomes will provide an opportu­
nity to identify gaps in managing malaria cases and
to implement interventions to enhance treatment
adherence and outcomes. Therefore, the research
aims to evaluate adherence to antimalarial treat­

ment among children with malaria and to explore
caregiver-reported treatment response, side effects,
and satisfaction. These areas should be clarified to
optimise therapeutic action, improve health educa­
tion and strengthen malaria control interventions
among the children.
Aims
The primary objective of the proposed research
is to assess children's compliance with antimalarial
treatment. In addition, the study evaluates care­
giver-reported outcomes, perceptions of symptom
improvement, experiences of side effects, and satis­
faction with the overall treatment process. By
assessing caregivers' adherence and opinions, the
study will help identify factors associated with
successful therapy completion and shed light on
potential obstacles to effective malaria manage­
ment among child populations. Lastly, the findings
inform policymakers who can enhance treatment
adherence, optimise therapeutic outcomes, and
promote health education for caregivers in malariaendemic regions.
Materials and Methods
Study design
This study employed a cross-sectional research
design to assess compliance with antimalarial therapy
and reported measures among caregivers of pedi­
atric patients with malaria.
Study setting and population
The study was conducted in sampled health
institutions among children with malaria. Caregivers
of children (5-17 years) who participated in the
study were required to administer the prescribed
antimalarial therapy and provide informed consent.
Caregivers were recruited directly and adminis­
tered the medication to the child to obtain an accu­
rate report on adherence and the experience with
the treatment process.
Sampling technique
A non-probability convenience sampling tech­
nique was used to select qualified pairs of care­
givers and children for data collection. It was
assumed that the population was unlimited because
the number of pediatric malaria cases in the study
area was unknown. Thus, the standard formula for

estimating proportions was used to calculate the
sample size:
\[n = \frac{Z^2 \cdot p (1 - p)}{d^2}\]
In the formula, Z refers to the z-score for a 95%
confidence interval (1.96); p is the estimated prev­
alence, set to 0.5 since no reliable local data are
available to determine the maximum sample size;
and d denotes the acceptable margin of error, set
to 0.0511). This yielded a minimum required sample
of 384, and the actual sample obtained in this study
was 386, which was sufficient to represent and
account for the small non-response.
Inclusion and exclusion criteria
Caregivers of children aged 5-18 years with a
diagnosis of malaria and prescribed antimalarial
therapy were included in the study. The caregivers
who participated were those directly involved in
administering medication to the child and who
agreed to take part. Caregivers were omitted when
the child had severe comorbidities that might affect
adherence to the treatment or reporting of symp­
toms, when the caregiver did not participate in the
administration of the medication, or when the child
was undergoing a course of treatment for recurrent
or complicated malaria that would require longterm hospitalisation. Further, caregivers who could
not communicate effectively to complete the inter­
view, even with help, were excluded from the study.
Data collection tool
This study employed structured tools, including
standardised and researcher-created scales, to collect
data on treatment adherence, caregiver-reported
outcomes, and relevant demographic and clinical
data.
Demographic information
Structured data collection tools were used, and
the first part collected basic demographic and clin­
ical data for the child and caregiver, including age,
gender, education, and type of antimalarial therapy
administered, which were used to interpret adher­
ence and outcome data. All data were collected
using an interviewer-based method to ensure clarity,
accuracy, and complete responses.

Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8)
The Morisky Medication Adherence Scale
(MMAS-8) was used to measure treatment adher­
ence. MMAS-8 was initially designed as a stan­
dardised measure of medication-taking behaviour
by Donald E. Morisky and colleagues in 2008. It has
eight items, and scoring is based on a structured
system: the first seven are dichotomous (Yes/No),
and the final item is rated on a five-point Likert
scale. The total scores range from 0 to 8, and adher­
ence is classified as high (8), medium (6-7), or low
(less than 6). The internal consistency of the
MMAS-8 is high, typically reported as a Cron­
bach's alpha of about 0.83; the adapted caregiver
version also shows similar reliability but measures
caregiver-mediated adherence behaviours12).
Caregiver-reported treatment outcome form
The caregiver-reported treatment outcome form
was used to measure caregiver-reported treatment
outcomes and was explicitly designed by the research
team for this study. This form was intended to
assess the child's recovery at the end of antima­
larial treatment, as observed by the caregiver. The
form consists of 6 items on a 5-point Likert scale
(1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree). This
will involve the following statements: My child no
longer has a fever after taking the medicine, my
child has a better appetite since taking the drug,
and I am satisfied with the overall improvement in
my child's health after taking the medicine. Each
item scores 1 to 5, and the total score ranges from
6 to 30; the higher the score, the better the results
on the caregiver-reported outcomes. No formal
validation or reliability testing of the tool was condu­
cted in this study; it was used solely as a screening
and assessment tool. It allowed the research team
to gain insight into the caregiver's perceptions of
symptom improvement, side effects, and overall
satisfaction with the antimalarial therapy.
Procedure
After the concerned institutional review board
had obtained ethical clearance, the caregivers of
children aged between 5 and 17 years diagnosed
with malaria in the sampled healthcare facilities
were approached. The purpose of the research was
explained, and oral informed consent was obtained
from the caregivers who agreed to participate. The

researchers used caregivers who were directly
involved in administering the prescribed antima­
larial therapy. Data collection tools, including the
MMAS-8 (caregiver-adapted version) and the care­
giver-reported treatment outcome form, were
administered in English; they were not translated
into Urdu or any other local language. The research

Ethical considerations and data analysis
This study was pre-approved by the Universiti
Sains Malaysia institutional review board IRBUSM/JEPeM/ETHICS/2025-087. Participation was
voluntary, and caregivers provided informed consent.
Participants' privacy was fully ensured through the
keeping of confidentiality, and no personal identi­
fiers were logged to ensure privacy.
The data were analysed using descriptive and
inferential statistics. Descriptive statistics were used
to describe the participants' demographics, medica­
tion adherence (MMAS-8), and caregiver-reported
treatment outcomes. To analyse relationships among
medication adherence, child and caregiver charac­
teristics, and treatment outcomes, inferential statis­
tics were used, including correlations, age-group
comparisons, and multiple regression analyses. This
method allowed for obtaining a complete picture of
factors affecting treatment compliance with anti­
malarial therapy and caregivers' perceptions of the
treatment's usefulness.
Results
Participant demographics and baseline
characteristics
Table 1 shows the demographic attributes of 386
children and their caregivers. The majority of the
children were aged 14-17 years (221, 57.3%) and
male (234, 60.6%). The distribution was relatively
equal, with the highest percentage (113, 29.3)
weighing over 30 kg (113, 29.3). The most preva­
lent types of malaria were Plasmodium falciparum
(158, 40.9%) and mixed infections (94, 24.4%).
Mothers (170, 44.0%) and fathers (150, 38.9%) were
the most dominant caregivers, and fewer grand­
parents (66, 17.1%). The majority of caregivers
were 35-44 years old (120, 31.1%) or 45 years and

older (116, 30.1%), and many had no formal educa­
tion (131, 33.9%). There was a wide range of employ­
ment, with the most significant proportion being
unemployed/housewives (120, 31.1%). A small
number of participants lived in rural regions (210,
54.4%). Concerning healthcare access, 146 (37.8%)
were within 1-5 km of the closest health facility,
and 95 (24.6%) were further than 10km. Most chil­
dren hospitalised with antimalarial drugs were
acquired at government facilities (127, 32.9%) or in
private clinics (110, 28.5%) and were treated by a
healthcare provider (242, 62.7%).
Normality assessment of MMAS-8 and caregiverreported treatment outcome scores
Table 2 presents the results of the normality test
for the MMAS-8 and caregiver-reported treatment
outcome scores in 386 participants. In the MMAS-8,
the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test (p = 0.200) and the
Shapiro-Wilk test (p = 0.312) both had p-values >
0.05, indicating a non-significant difference from
the distribution. In the same manner, the caregiverreported treatment outcome score also showed
non-significant scores on both Kolmogorov-Smirnov
(p = 0.200) and Shapiro-Wilk (p = 0.485) tests. In
general, these results demonstrate that both vari­
ables pass the normality test and can be used in
parametric tests.
Correlation between medication adherence and
caregiver-reported treatment outcomes
Table 3 presents the Spearman correlation between
medication adherence (MMAS-8) and caregiverreported treatment outcomes in 386 participants.
The two variables showed a strong positive rela­
tionship (r = 0.312, t(384) = 6.44, p = 0.001), indicating
that higher medication adherence was associated
with more positive caregiver-reported treatment
responses. This implies that the caregivers who
had reported a better treatment outcome were
more likely to have children with increased adher­
ence to antimalarial medication. The correlation,
although moderate, is statistically robust and demon­
strates the significant association between adher­
ence behaviours and perceived treatment success.

Predictors of caregiver-reported treatment
outcomes
Table 5 shows the results of a multiple regres­
sion analysis of predictors of caregiver-reported
treatment outcomes in 386 participants. Medication
adherence was a significant predictor of positive
treatment outcomes (B = 0.254, p < 0.001), indicating
that higher MMAS-8 scores were associated with
better caregiver-reported outcomes. The child's age
(B = 0.412, p = 0.001) and other significant positive
predictors were found, with older children and
those with a particular malaria classification showing
better outcomes. The caregiver's age was posi­
tively correlated with treatment outcomes (B =
0.112, p = 0.008), whereas the child's gender was
negatively correlated, albeit insignificantly (B =
-0.098, p = 0.034). Also, receiving antimalarial medi­
cation from other sources was associated with
treatment outcomes (B = 0.198, p = 0.001). Overall,
this model suggests that children's and caregivers'
characteristics, as well as medication adherence,
play significant roles in caregivers' perceived treat­
ment effectiveness.
Discussion
This study has examined children's adherence to
antimalarial treatment and discussed caregiverreported outcomes of treatment. In our research,
increased medication adherence was positively
correlated with improved caregiver-reported treat­
ment outcomes. A past study supports this finding
and indicates that higher MMAS-8 scores are asso­
ciated with greater treatment satisfaction among
patients. Combined, these results suggest a reliable

association between adherence and better treat­
ment perceptions13).
There was a difference in medication adherence
according to age, with children aged 11-13 years
having the highest levels of adherence. The trend
is reinforced by past research showing that age is
a significant predictor of adherence behaviours,
with changes in medication-taking behaviours across
an individual's developmental stage14). Likewise,
this age group had the best caregiver-reported
treatment outcomes, as confirmed by studies indi­
cating that caregivers provide more applicable
ratings of child health and are more accurate in
anticipating outcomes than those documented in
medical records15).
In our research, increased medication adherence
and older children correlated with improved treat­
ment outcomes, as reported by the caregiver. This
is consistent with evidence from previous studies
that adherence positively correlates with treat­
ment satisfaction13) and that child age affects care­
givers' perceptions of health outcomes15). Child
gender in our study had a small adverse predictive
effect on caregiver-reported outcomes. This is in
line with earlier studies, which revealed that parentproxy reports of children's health-related quality of
life showed no significant differences between
daughters and sons, suggesting that gender did not
play an essential role in shaping caregiver percep­
tions16).

better reported treatment outcomes, consistent with
the finding that caregiver factors, such as age, can
also affect perceptions of treatment burden and
treatment evaluation17). Finally, the source of anti­

Altogether, these results indicate that medica­

The research has several limitations, including a
cross-sectional design that precludes causal infer­
ence and convenience sampling, which limits gener­
alizability. Adherence and treatment outcomes
reported by caregivers may be affected by recall
or social desirability bias, and the researcherdeveloped outcome form was not formally tested.
Also, the questionnaires were given in English,
which could have affected caregivers' understanding,
particularly those who were less literate. Longitu­
dinal designs addressing adherence and recovery
over time should be implemented in future studies;
standardised outcome measures should be vali­
dated, translated, and culturally adapted scales
should be applied, and clinical measures should be
used rather than relying solely on self-report.
Additional studies are also needed to assess specific
adherence-support interventions, investigate regi­
men-specific adherence variations, and investigate
caregiver and child variables, including age and
gender, to gain deeper insights into their effects on
malaria treatment outcomes.
Conclusion
The research shows that medication adherence
is a major predictor of caregiver reports of treat­
ment effectiveness for malaria in children. Children
who scored higher on adherence had significantly
better recovery as perceived by their caregivers.
Age disparity was also a key factor, with young
adolescents showing higher compliance and better
results than younger children. Besides, the care­
giver's age, the child's gender, and the medication
source also contributed to differences in treatment
outcomes.
The findings indicate the need to improve care­

giver education, expand access to formal health
care providers, and introduce interventions to
support adherence, particularly in young children.
All these factors suggest that malaria treatment
programs may substantially improve treatment
effectiveness and reduce disease burden in endemic
regions.
