An analytic approach to the finite R R -transform
Octavio Arizmendi, Katsunori Fujie

Abstract
We revisit Marcus’ finite free analogue of Voiculescu R R -transform from an analytic viewpoint. By relating the finite free Fourier transform to the Laplace transform, we study the finite R R -transform through logarithmic potentials and Legendre transforms. Under suitable assumptions, we prove that the finite R R -transform of a polynomial differs from the Voiculescu R R -transform of its empirical root distribution by O ⁡ ( N − 1 ) O(N^{-1}) . As an application, we obtain an analytic proof of the convergence of finite free additive convolution to free additive convolution.



1
Introduction

Finite free probability is a relatively recent branch of probability theory, initiated by the work of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava on the resolution of the Kadison–Singer problem and the construction of Ramanujan graphs
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
.
Its central theme is to develop polynomial analogues of the results in free probability.
By now, many such analogies have been established; see
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
6
,
7
,
11
]
for example.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
denote the set of monic polynomials over
\mathbb{C}
of degree
N
.
For
p\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, we write

p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x-\lambda_{i}(p))=\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{N-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{N}{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p),

where
\{\lambda_{i}(p)\}_{i=1}^{N}
are the roots of
p
, and
\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)
denotes the
k
-th normalized elementary symmetric polynomial of the roots, namely

\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)=\binom{N}{k}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}\lambda_{i_{1}}(p)\cdots\lambda_{i_{k}}(p),

with the convention
\widetilde{e}_{0}^{(N)}(p)=1
.
Although this normalization may look unusual at first glance, it is particularly convenient in finite free probability.
Throughout the paper, we always identify a monic polynomial
p
, its roots
\{\lambda_{i}(p)\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and its coefficients
\{\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)\}_{k=0}^{N}
.
We also associate to
p
its empirical root distribution
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{i}(p)}.

When the degree
N
is clear from the context, we often omit the superscript
(N)
and simply write
\widetilde{e}_{k}(p)
.
For a subset
S\subset\mathbb{C}
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{N}(S)
for the subset of
\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
consisting of polynomials whose roots lie in
S
.
In particular, if
p\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
, then
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, the finite free additive convolution
p\boxplus_{N}q\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
is defined by

\widetilde{e}_{k}(p\boxplus_{N}q)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\binom{k}{i}\widetilde{e}_{i}(p)\widetilde{e}_{k-i}(q),\qquad k=0,\dots,N.

This binary operation already appeared in classical work of Szegő and Walsh
[
16
,
19
]
; see also
[
12
]
.
A fundamental contribution of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava was the discovery of its random matrix expression:
if

p(x)=\det(xI-A),\qquad q(x)=\det(xI-B),

where
A
and
B
are
N\times N
normal matrices, then

p\boxplus_{N}q(x)=\mathbb{E}_{U}\!\left[\det(xI-A-UBU^{*})\right],

where
U
is an
N\times N
Haar unitary matrix.
This formula reveals a close connection between finite free probability and free probability, since the random matrices
A
and
UBU^{*}
are asymptotically free in the large
N
limit.

Motivated by this connection, one is naturally led to ask whether finite free convolution approximates free additive convolution in the large-degree limit.
More precisely, if
p_{N},q_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
\mu\llbracket q_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu
, it is natural to expect that
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\boxplus_{N}q_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxplus\nu
, where
\mu\boxplus\nu
denotes the free additive convolution of probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
.
This convergence is by now well established, see
[
3
,
6
,
8
]
.
We provide an alternative proof from a direct analytic study of the finite
R
-transform, see Corollary
3.6
.

A basic tool for studying
\mu\boxplus\nu
is Voiculescu
R
-transform, which linearizes free additive convolution.
It plays the role in free probability analogous to that of the logarithm of the characteristic function (Fourier transform) in classical probability.
Recall that, for a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the Cauchy transform is defined by

G_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-t}\,d\mu(t),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp(\mu).

It is known that
G_{\mu}
is univalent in a suitable region near infinity, and the
R
-transform is then defined by

R_{\mu}(z)=G_{\mu}^{-1}(z)-\frac{1}{z}

for
z
near
0
.
Its key property is the linearization formula:
R_{\mu\boxplus\nu}=R_{\mu}+R_{\nu}
.
The
R
-transform and the associated subordination theory provide a powerful framework for analyzing free convolution; see
[
4
,
5
,
14
,
15
,
17
,
18
]
.

The convergence of finite free convolution to free convolution was first suggested by Marcus through a finite analogue of the
R
-transform
[
8
]
.
Roughly speaking, Marcus observed that this finite transform should converge to Voiculescu
R
-transform as the degree tends to infinity.
However, the analytic content of this observation was not fully developed there.
Later, Arizmendi and Perales
[
3
]
gave a rigorous proof of the convergence of finite free convolution by introducing finite free cumulants, namely the coefficients of Marcus’ finite
R
-transform, and showing that these cumulants converge to the free cumulants of the limiting measure.
Their argument is purely combinatorial and works under compact support assumptions.
See also
[
13
, Section 3]
for related remarks on the convergence of the finite
R
-transform.

The purpose of the present paper is to revisit Marcus’ finite
R
-transform from an analytic point of view and to make its asymptotic relation with Voiculescu
R
-transform precise.
Our starting point is the finite free Fourier (FFF) transform; for
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{N-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{N}{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, define

\widehat{p}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)}{k!}s^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)}{k!}(-s)^{k}.

For formal power series
f,g\in\mathbb{C}[[s]]
, we write
f\overset{N}{=}g
if
f\equiv g\pmod{s^{N+1}}
.
Then one can check that
\widehat{p}(\partial_{x})x^{N}=p(x),
and
r=p\boxplus_{N}q
if and only if
\widehat{r}(s)\overset{N}{=}\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s)
for
p,q,r\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
.
This motivates the definition of the finite
R
-transform by Marcus:

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{d}{ds}\log\widehat{p}(Ns),

which will be discussed more precisely in Section 2.
Thus, the finite
R
-transform linearizes finite free convolution
R_{p\boxplus_{N}q}^{(N)}\overset{N-1}{=}R_{p}^{(N)}+R_{q}^{(N)}
in the sense of equality modulo
s^{N}
.

A key idea is that the FFF transform
\widehat{p}
can be expressed in terms of the Laplace transform of
p
.
This allows us to study
R_{p}^{(N)}
through logarithmic potentials, Legendre transforms, and a quantitative Laplace principle.
In this way, the finite
R
-transform acquires an analytic interpretation and can be compared directly with the Voiculescu
R
-transform of the empirical root distribution.

Our main result is as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
.
Assume that
p_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}((-\infty,-\varepsilon])
is a sequence of polynomials whose empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket
converge weakly to a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,-\varepsilon])
, and set
\alpha:=G_{\mu}(0)
.
Then, for each
s\in(0,\alpha)
, we have

R_{p_{N}}^{(N)}(s)-R_{\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket}(s)=O(N^{-1}).

As an application, we recover the convergence of finite free additive convolution to free additive convolution; see Corollary
3.6
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2, we introduce the necessary notation and present the analytic framework underlying the finite
R
-transform.
In Section 3, we prove the quantitative convergence theorem and discuss several applications.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we review the necessary background and develop the analytic framework underlying the finite
R
-transform.

2.1
Voiculescu
R
-transform

For a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R})
, the Cauchy transform is defined by
G_{\mu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-t}\,d\mu(t),
for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp(\mu)
,
and the
R
-transform is given by
R_{\mu}(z)=G_{\mu}^{-1}(z)-\frac{1}{z}
for
z
near
0
.
A basic difficulty in working with the
R
-transform is that it is essentially defined as the inverse of the Cauchy transform.
For our purposes, it is more convenient to reformulate this in terms of logarithmic potentials and Legendre duality.

Throughout this paper, unless otherwise stated, we assume that
\mu
is supported on the non-positive real line.

Definition 2.1

(Logarithmic potential)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,0])
.
Assume that
\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left|\log(z-t)\right|\,d\mu(t)<\infty
for all
z>0
.
The logarithmic potential of
\mu
is defined by

H_{\mu}(z)=\int_{-\infty}^{0}\log(z-t)\,d\mu(t),\qquad z>0.

We next recall a simple variant of the Legendre transform adapted to concave functions.
Although the classical Legendre transform is usually formulated for convex functions,
the following concave version is sufficient for our purpose.

Definition 2.2

(Concave Legendre transform)
.

Let
h
be a differentiable strictly concave function on
(0,\infty)
.
Its (concave) Legendre transform is defined by

h^{*}(s):=\inf_{x>0}\{sx-h(x)\}.

The following property is the analogue of the usual Legendre duality.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
h
be differentiable and strictly concave on
(0,\infty)
.
Then

h^{*}(h^{\prime}(x))=xh^{\prime}(x)-h(x),

and

(h^{*})^{\prime}(h^{\prime}(x))=x.

In other words, the derivative of
h^{*}
is the inverse function of
h^{\prime}
.

We now apply this to the logarithmic potential.
Since

\frac{d}{dz}H_{\mu}(z)=G_{\mu}(z)

and
G_{\mu}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,\infty)
, the function
H_{\mu}
is strictly concave on
(0,\infty)
.
Hence we may consider its Legendre transform

H_{\mu}^{*}(s)=\inf_{x>0}\{sx-H_{\mu}(x)\}.

Let
\alpha:=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}G_{\mu}(x)\in(0,\infty].

Since
G_{\mu}(+\infty)=0
, the function
H_{\mu}^{*}(s)
is naturally defined for
s\in(0,\alpha)
.

Corollary 2.4
.

For
s\in(0,\alpha)
, we have

\frac{d}{ds}H_{\mu}^{*}(s)=G_{\mu}^{(-1)}(s)=R_{\mu}(s)+\frac{1}{s}.

2.2
Finite
R
-transform and Laplace transform

For
p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{N-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{N}{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
, the finite free Fourier (FFF) transform is

\widehat{p}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}^{(N)}(p)}{k!}s^{k}.

Then,
\widehat{p\boxplus_{N}q}(s)\overset{N}{=}\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s)
for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
.
This motivates the logarithmic FFF transform

C_{p}^{(N)}(s):=\log\widehat{p}(s),

which is well defined near the origin since
\widehat{p}(0)=1
.
Then

C_{p\boxplus_{N}q}^{(N)}(s)\overset{N}{=}C_{p}^{(N)}(s)+C_{q}^{(N)}(s).

(2.1)

Expanding

C_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}c_{n}^{(N)}(p)}{n!}s^{n},

we have the following moment-cumulant formula

\widetilde{e}_{n}(p)=\sum_{\pi\in P(n)}c_{\pi}^{(N)}(p),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}

and equivalently

c_{n}^{(N)}(p)=\sum_{\pi\in P(n)}\widetilde{e}_{\pi}^{(N)}(p)\mu_{n}(\pi,1_{n}),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}

(2.2)

where
P(n)
denotes the set partitions of
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\mu_{n}
the Möbius function of
P(n)
; see
[
3
,
15
]
.
In particular, Eq. (
2.1
) implies

c_{n}^{(N)}(p\boxplus_{N}q)=c_{n}^{(N)}(p)+c_{n}^{(N)}(q)

(2.3)

for
n=1,\dots,N
.

However, the function
C_{p}^{(N)}(s)
does not converge to
R_{\mu}
even when
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
; the reason for this will become clear later.
We therefore introduce the following finite
R
-transform instead.

Definition 2.5

(Finite
R
-transform and finite free cumulants)
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{C})
.
The finite
R
-transform of
p
is defined by

R_{p}^{(N)}(s):=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{d}{ds}C_{p}^{(N)}(Ns)=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{d}{ds}\log\widehat{p}(Ns)=-\frac{\widehat{p}^{\prime}(Ns)}{\widehat{p}(Ns)}

We also define the finite free cumulants of
p
as the coefficients of the expansion

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{n+1}^{(N)}(p)s^{n}.

From the definition and Eq. (
2.2
), the finite free cumulants satisfy

\kappa_{n}^{(N)}(p)=\frac{(-N)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}c_{n}^{(N)}(p)=\frac{(-N)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\sum_{\pi\in P(n)}\widetilde{e}_{\pi}^{(N)}(p)\mu_{n}(\pi,1_{n}),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

By Eq. (
2.1
), the finite
R
-transform linearizes finite free convolution, at least up to order
N-1
:

R_{p\boxplus_{N}q}^{(N)}(s)\overset{N-1}{=}R_{p}^{(N)}(s)+R_{q}^{(N)}(s);

(2.4)

in other words, one has the linearization property of finite free cumulants

\kappa_{n}^{(N)}(p\boxplus_{N}q)=\kappa_{n}^{(N)}(p)+\kappa_{n}^{(N)}(q)

for
n=1,\dots,N
by Eq. (
2.3
).

We do not pursue the combinatorial theory of finite free cumulants any further and refer the interested reader to
[
3
]
for details.
Instead, we turn to the main analytic question: whether the finite
R
-transform converges to the usual
R
-transform in the large-degree limit.
This problem forms the central theme of the present paper.
Our approach is to give an analytic interpretation of
R_{p}^{(N)}
.
More precisely, we show that the FFF transform is closely related to the Laplace transform of the polynomial
p
, which leads to a natural integral representation of
R_{p}^{(N)}
.
This representation will serve as the starting point of our analysis.

From now on, we assume that

p(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x+\lambda_{i})\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{R}_{<0}),\qquad\lambda_{i}>0.

Definition 2.6

(Laplace transform)
.

Let
f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function.
For
s>0
, the Laplace transform of
f
is defined by

\mathcal{L}[f](s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-sx}\,dx,

whenever the integral converges.

The key observation is that the FFF transform coincides with the Laplace transform of
p
, up to a multiplicative factor.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}x^{N-k}(-1)^{k}\binom{N}{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}(p).

Then

\widehat{p}(s)=\frac{s^{N+1}}{N!}\mathcal{L}[p](s).

Consequently,

\log\widehat{p}(s)=\log\mathcal{L}[p](s)+(N+1)\log s-\log N!,

and therefore

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=-\frac{1}{N}\frac{d}{ds}\log\mathcal{L}[p](Ns)-\frac{N+1}{Ns}.

Proof.

Since

\mathcal{L}[x^{k}](s)=\frac{k!}{s^{k+1}},

we have

\mathcal{L}[p](s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}(-1)^{k}\binom{N}{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}(p)\frac{(N-k)!}{s^{N-k+1}}.

Multiplying both sides by
s^{N+1}/N!
yields

\frac{s^{N+1}}{N!}\mathcal{L}[p](s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{k}\widetilde{e}_{k}(p)}{k!}s^{k}=\widehat{p}(s),

as claimed. The rest follows readily.
∎

2.3
Integral representation and the Laplace principle

Let
H_{p}
and
G_{p}
denote the logarithmic potential and the Cauchy transform of
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket
:

H_{p}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\log p(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\log(x+\lambda_{i}),

and

G_{p}(x)=H_{p}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{x+\lambda_{i}}=\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{Np(x)}.

Let

\alpha:=G_{p}(0)\in(0,\infty).

For
s\in(0,\alpha)
, define

H_{p}^{*}(s):=\inf_{x>0}\{sx-H_{p}(x)\},\qquad L_{p}^{(\infty)}(s):=-H_{p}^{*}(s)=\sup_{x>0}\{-sx+H_{p}(x)\}.

Then

\frac{d}{ds}H_{p}^{*}(s)=G_{p}^{-1}(s)=R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)+\frac{1}{s},

where
R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s):=R_{\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket}(s)
denotes the Voiculescu
R
-transform of the empirical root distribution of
p
.

On the other hand, motivated by Proposition
2.7
, define

L_{p}^{(N)}(s):=\frac{1}{N}\log\mathcal{L}[p](Ns).

To compare
L_{p}^{(N)}
with
L_{p}^{(\infty)}
, fix
s\in(0,\alpha)
and let
x_{s}>0
be the unique point such that

G_{p}(x_{s})=s.

Set

\phi_{s}(x):=-sx+H_{p}(x),\qquad\psi_{s}(x):=\phi_{s}(x)-\phi_{s}(x_{s}).

Since

\phi_{s}^{\prime}(x)=-s+G_{p}(x),\qquad\phi_{s}^{\prime\prime}(x)=G_{p}^{\prime}(x)<0,

the function
\phi_{s}
is strictly concave and attains its maximum at
x_{s}
.
Hence

L_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)=\phi_{s}(x_{s})

and
\psi_{s}(x)\leq 0
.

Proposition 2.8
.

For every
s\in(0,\alpha)
,

L_{p}^{(N)}(s)=L_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)+\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Proof.

Since

p(x)e^{-Nsx}=\exp\bigl(N\phi_{s}(x)\bigr),

we have

\displaystyle L_{p}^{(N)}(s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\phi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\log\left(e^{N\phi_{s}(x_{s})}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N(\phi_{s}(x)-\phi_{s}(x_{s}))}\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{s}(x_{s})+\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

∎

Thus the comparison between
L_{p}^{(N)}
and
L_{p}^{(\infty)}
reduces to estimating the integral

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

This observation reflects the Laplace principle underlying our approach.
Roughly speaking,

\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx\longrightarrow 0

as
N\to\infty
.
A related statement also appears in
[
13
, Eq. (72)]
.
The main issue here is to obtain a quantitative bound.

Definition 2.9
.

For
s\in(0,\alpha)
, define a probability measure on
[0,\infty)
by

\nu_{p,s}^{(N)}(dx):=\frac{p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}=\frac{e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx}.

Lemma 2.10
.

For
s\in(0,\alpha)
,

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}xG_{p}(x)\,\nu_{p,s}^{(N)}(dx)-\frac{1}{s}.

In particular,

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)-R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)=\frac{1}{s}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}xG_{p}(x)\,\nu_{p,s}^{(N)}(dx)-sx_{s}\right).

Proof.

By Proposition
2.7
,

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=-\frac{d}{ds}L_{p}^{(N)}(s)-\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)\frac{1}{s}.

Since
p(x)
is a polynomial and
e^{-Nsx}
decays exponentially for
s>0
, both

\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}xp(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx

are finite.
Hence we may differentiate under the integral and obtain

\frac{d}{ds}L_{p}^{(N)}(s)=-\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}xp(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}.

Thus,

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}xp(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}{\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}-\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\right)\frac{1}{s}.

Integration by parts yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}xp(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx=\frac{1}{Ns}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}xp^{\prime}(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx\right),

so

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}xp^{\prime}(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}{Ns\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}-\frac{1}{s}.

Since
p^{\prime}(x)/(Np(x))=G_{p}(x)
, this becomes

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}xG_{p}(x)p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}{s\int_{0}^{\infty}p(x)e^{-Nsx}\,dx}-\frac{1}{s},

which is the first identity.
The second follows from the definition:

R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)=G_{p}^{-1}(s)-\frac{1}{s}=x_{s}-\frac{1}{s}.

∎

Heuristically, the probability measure
\nu_{p,s}^{(N)}
concentrates near
x_{s}
as
N\to\infty
, and hence one expects
\int_{0}^{\infty}xG_{p}(x)\,\nu_{p,s}^{(N)}(dx)\sim x_{s}G_{p}(x_{s})=sx_{s}
.
In the next section, we justify this quantitatively by elementary estimates.

Example 2.11
.

We now consider the simplest case corresponding to the delta measure.
Let

p(x)=(x+\lambda)^{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\lambda^{k}x^{N-k},\qquad\lambda>0.

Then
\mu\llbracket p\rrbracket=\delta_{-\lambda}
.
In this case

\widehat{p}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}s^{k}=\frac{s^{N+1}}{N!}\mathcal{L}[p](s).

Moreover,

G_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(x)=\frac{1}{x+\lambda},\qquad H_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(x)=\log(x+\lambda),

and therefore

R_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(s)=G_{\delta_{-\lambda}}^{(-1)}(s)-\frac{1}{s}=-\lambda,\qquad 0<s<\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Since
x_{s}=-\lambda+1/s
, we have

L_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(s)=-H_{\delta_{-\lambda}}^{*}(s)=\phi_{s}(x_{s})=s\lambda-\log s-1,

where
\phi_{s}(x)=-sx+\log(x+\lambda)
.

On the other hand,

L_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\frac{1}{N}\log\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+\lambda)^{N}e^{-Nsx}\,dx.

By the change of variables
y=x+\lambda
and
z=sy
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+\lambda)^{N}e^{-Nsx}\,dx=e^{Ns\lambda}\int_{\lambda}^{\infty}y^{N}e^{-Nsy}\,dy=\frac{e^{Ns\lambda}}{s^{N+1}}\int_{s\lambda}^{\infty}z^{N}e^{-Nz}\,dz.

Since
0<s\lambda<1
, putting
w=z-1
yields

\int_{s\lambda}^{\infty}z^{N}e^{-Nz}\,dz=\frac{1}{e^{N}}\int_{s\lambda-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw.

Hence

L_{p}^{(N)}(s)-L_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(s)=\frac{1}{N}\log\!\left(\frac{1}{s}\int_{s\lambda-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw\right).

Since
-1<s\lambda-1<0
, we have

\int_{s\lambda-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw\leq\int_{-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw=\frac{N!e^{N}}{N^{N+1}}\leq e^{\frac{1}{12N}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}},

where the last inequality follows from Stirling’s formula.
Moreover,

\int_{s\lambda-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw=\frac{N!e^{N}}{N^{N+1}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N}N^{k}/k!}{e^{N}}.

Consequently,

L_{p}^{(N)}(s)-L_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(s)=-\frac{1}{N}\log s+\frac{1}{N}\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)-\frac{1}{2}\log N\right)+o(N^{-1}),

and also

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)-R_{\delta_{-\lambda}}(s)=\frac{(s\lambda)^{N+1}e^{-N(s\lambda-1)}}{Ns\int_{s\lambda-1}^{\infty}(w+1)^{N}e^{-Nw}\,dw}.

Remark 2.12
.

More generally, if
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
p(x)=(x-\lambda)^{N}
, then the definition gives

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)=\lambda\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}(\lambda Ns)^{k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}(\lambda Ns)^{k}}.

Although this formula follows directly from the definition, we include the above calculation because it provides a useful preview of the estimates that will appear later and because the resulting bound will be used again in the sequel.

R
-transform

After that, we state some of the applications.

3.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\sigma_{s}^{2}:=-G_{p}^{\prime}(x_{s})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})^{-2}.

Then

\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N\sigma_{s}^{2}}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx\leq p(x_{s})^{1/N}e^{1/(12N)}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}.

Proof.

We first prove the lower bound.
Since

\displaystyle\psi_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=-s(x-x_{s})+H_{p}(x)-H_{p}(x_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\log\left(1+\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)-\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right),

let
v(t):=\log(1+t)-t+\frac{t^{2}}{2}
for
t>-1
,
and define
V(x-x_{s}):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}v\!\left(\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)
for
x>0
.
Then

\psi_{s}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})^{2}+V(x-x_{s}).

Since
v(t)\geq 0
for
t\geq 0
, we obtain
\psi_{s}(x)\geq-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})^{2}
for
x\geq x_{s}
.
Hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x_{s}}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x_{s}}^{\infty}e^{-N\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})^{2}/2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-N\sigma_{s}^{2}x^{2}/2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N\sigma_{s}^{2}}}.

For the upper bound,

\displaystyle e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\log\left(1+\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)-\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p(x_{s})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}(x+\lambda_{i})\exp\left(-\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right).

Therefore, by Hölder’s inequality,

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{p(x_{s})}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}(x+\lambda_{i})^{N}\exp\left(-N\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)\,dx\right)^{1/N}.

By the calculation in Example
2.11
, for
\lambda>0
and
0<s<\lambda^{-1}
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{(x+\lambda)^{N}}{e^{Nsx}}dx=\frac{e^{Ns\lambda}}{s^{N+1}}\int_{s\lambda}^{\infty}\frac{z^{N}}{e^{Nz}}dz\leq\frac{e^{Ns\lambda}}{s^{N+1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{N}}{e^{Nz}}dz\leq\frac{e^{N(s\lambda-1)}e^{\frac{1}{12N}}}{s^{N+1}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}.

Applying this with
\lambda=\lambda_{i}
and
s=(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})^{-1}
for each
i=1,\dots,N
, we have

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}dx\leq p(x_{s})^{\frac{1}{N}}e^{\frac{1}{12N}}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{N}}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Assume that
p_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}((-\infty,-\varepsilon])
is a sequence of polynomials whose empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket
converge weakly to a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}((-\infty,-\varepsilon])
.
Then, for each
s\in(0,\alpha)
, where
\alpha:=G_{\mu}(0)
, we have

L_{p_{N}}^{(N)}(s)-L_{p_{N}}^{(\infty)}(s)=O\!\left(\frac{\log N}{N}\right).

Proof.

This follows immediately from Proposition
2.8

and Lemma
3.1
.
∎

We include Proposition
3.2
to make explicit the sense in which
L_{p}^{(N)}
approximates
L_{p}^{(\infty)}
. Although it is not used directly in the proof of the main theorem, it may be of independent interest.

Lemma 3.3
.

1.

Define

T_{p}(x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})}.

Then
T_{p}
is monotonically decreasing,
0\leq T_{p}(x)\leq s
, and

xG_{p}(x)-x_{s}G_{p}(x_{s})=T_{p}(x)(x-x_{s}).

2.

For
x\geq 0
, we have

-\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)\leq\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})\leq-\frac{x}{x_{s}}\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle xG_{p}(x)-x_{s}G_{p}(x_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{x}{x+\lambda_{i}}-\frac{x_{s}}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}(x-x_{s})}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})}

\displaystyle=T_{p}(x)(x-x_{s}),

which gives the first claim.
Since each summand is nonnegative and

\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})}\leq\frac{1}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}},

we obtain

0\leq T_{p}(x)\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{x_{s}+\lambda_{i}}=G_{p}(x_{s})=s.

Next,

\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)=-s+G_{p}(x)=G_{p}(x)-G_{p}(x_{s}),

and

\displaystyle G_{p}(x)-G_{p}(x_{s})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{x-x_{s}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})^{2}}.

Since the correction term is nonnegative, we obtain

-\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)\leq\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s}).

Moreover,

\frac{(x-x_{s})^{2}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})^{2}}\leq\frac{x-x_{s}}{x_{s}}\cdot\frac{x-x_{s}}{(x+\lambda_{i})(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})},

and summing over
i
yields

\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})\leq-\frac{x}{x_{s}}\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x).

∎

Proposition 3.4
.

For
s\in(0,\alpha)
, we have

-\frac{\alpha}{sNx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}\leq R_{p}^{(N)}(s)-R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)\leq\frac{1}{Nx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.10
, it suffices to estimate

A:=\int_{0}^{\infty}\bigl(xG_{p}(x)-x_{s}G_{p}(x_{s})\bigr)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

By Lemma
3.3
,

A=\int_{0}^{\infty}T_{p}(x)(x-x_{s})e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Using

-\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)\leq\sigma_{s}^{2}(x-x_{s})\leq-\frac{x}{x_{s}}\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x),

we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}-T_{p}(x)\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx\leq\sigma_{s}^{2}A\leq\frac{1}{x_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}-xT_{p}(x)\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Multiplying by
N
and applying integration by parts to the left-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle N\sigma_{s}^{2}A

\displaystyle\geq-\bigl[T_{p}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\bigr]_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}T_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle=T_{p}(0)e^{N\psi_{s}(0)}+\int_{0}^{\infty}T_{p}^{\prime}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Since
T_{p}(0)=s
and

-T_{p}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{(x+\lambda_{i})^{2}(x_{s}+\lambda_{i})}\leq\frac{1}{x_{s}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}\leq\frac{\alpha}{x_{s}},

we get

N\sigma_{s}^{2}A\geq-\frac{\alpha}{x_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Similarly, on the right-hand side,

\displaystyle x_{s}N\sigma_{s}^{2}A

\displaystyle\leq-\bigl[xT_{p}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\bigr]_{0}^{\infty}+\int_{0}^{\infty}(T_{p}(x)+xT_{p}^{\prime}(x))e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}T_{p}(x)e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq s\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx.

Therefore

-\frac{\alpha}{sNx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}\leq\frac{A}{s\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{N\psi_{s}(x)}\,dx}\leq\frac{1}{Nx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}.

By Lemma
2.10
, this is exactly

-\frac{\alpha}{sNx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}\leq R_{p}^{(N)}(s)-R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)\leq\frac{1}{Nx_{s}\sigma_{s}^{2}}.

This is what we wanted.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Put
\alpha_{N}:=G_{\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket}(0)
.
Since
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
, we have
\alpha_{N}\to\alpha
.
Hence, for each fixed
s\in(0,\alpha)
, we have
s\in(0,\alpha_{N})
for all sufficiently large
N
.

Applying Proposition
3.4
to
p_{N}
, and using again the weak convergence
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
, the quantities appearing in the bound converge to their counterparts for
\mu
.
Thus

R_{p_{N}}^{(N)}(s)-R_{\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket}(s)=O(N^{-1}),

as desired.
∎

3.2
Applications

We conclude the paper with several applications of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
, and let
(p_{N})_{N=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of polynomials with
p_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}((-\infty,-\varepsilon])
.

1.

If the empirical root distributions
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket
converge weakly to a probability measure
\mu
, then
R_{p_{N}}^{(N)}(s)
converges to
R_{\mu}(s)
for
s\in(0,G_{\mu}(0))
.

2.

Suppose, in addition, that there exists
M>\varepsilon
such that
p_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}([-M,-\varepsilon])
for all
N
.
If the finite
R
-transforms
R_{p_{N}}^{(N)}(s)
converge to a function
R(s)
defined on
(0,1/M)
, then there exists a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{M}([-M,-\varepsilon])
such that
\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu
and
R_{\mu}(s)=R(s)
.

Proof.

These assertions follow from Theorem
1.1
, together with the standard fact that, for compactly supported measures, weak convergence is equivalent to convergence of the Voiculescu
R
-transforms near the origin.
∎

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
0<a<b
, and let
p_{N},q_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}([-b,-a])
satisfy

\mu\llbracket p_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu,\qquad\mu\llbracket q_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\nu

for probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0})
.
Then

\mu\llbracket p_{N}\boxplus_{N}q_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxplus\nu.

For the proof of Corollary
3.6
, we use the following inequality.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
p,q\in\mathcal{P}_{N}(\mathbb{R}_{<0})
.
Then

R^{(N)}_{p\boxplus_{N}q}(s)\geq R^{(N)}_{p}(s)+R^{(N)}_{q}(s)

for all
s>0
.

This should be contrasted with
[
11
, Theorem 1.12]
, which gives an inequality in the opposite direction:

R_{p\boxplus_{N}q}^{(\infty)}(s)\leq R_{p}^{(\infty)}(s)+R_{q}^{(\infty)}(s).

(3.1)

Proof.

Put
r=p\boxplus_{N}q
and
f=\widehat{p}\widehat{q}-\widehat{r}.

Since
\widehat{r}(s)\overset{N}{=}\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s)
, the polynomial
f
has the form

f(s)=\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N}\frac{d_{k}}{k!}s^{k},

where the coefficients
d_{k}=(-1)^{k}\sum_{i+j=k}\binom{k}{i}\widetilde{e}_{i}(p)\widetilde{e}_{j}(q)
are positive.
We also write
\widehat{r}
in a similar form:

\widehat{r}(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{c_{k}}{k!}s^{k},

with
c_{k}>0
.

Taking logarithms, we obtain

\log\widehat{p}(s)+\log\widehat{q}(s)=\log\widehat{r}(s)-\log(1-g(s)),

where
g(s):={f(s)}/{\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s)}.

Note that
0<1-g(s)={\widehat{r}(s)}/{\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s)}<1
for
s>0
.
Differentiating, we obtain

\frac{\widehat{p}^{\prime}(s)}{\widehat{p}(s)}+\frac{\widehat{q}^{\prime}(s)}{\widehat{q}(s)}=\frac{\widehat{r}^{\prime}(s)}{\widehat{r}(s)}+\frac{g^{\prime}(s)}{1-g(s)}.

By the definition of the finite
R
-transform, this yields

R_{p}^{(N)}(s)+R_{q}^{(N)}(s)=R_{r}^{(N)}(s)-\frac{g^{\prime}(Ns)}{1-g(Ns)}.

(3.2)

Thus it remains to show that
g^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s>0
.
Indeed,

g^{\prime}(s)=\frac{f^{\prime}(s)(\widehat{p}\widehat{q})(s)-f(s)(\widehat{p}\widehat{q})^{\prime}(s)}{(\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s))^{2}}=\frac{f^{\prime}(s)\widehat{r}(s)-f(s)\widehat{r}^{\prime}(s)}{(\widehat{p}(s)\widehat{q}(s))^{2}}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(s)\widehat{r}(s)-f(s)\widehat{r}^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N}\frac{d_{k}}{k!}ks^{k-1}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{c_{l}}{l!}s^{l}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N}\frac{d_{k}}{k!}s^{k}\right)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{c_{l}}{l!}ls^{l-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=N+1}^{2N}\sum_{l=0}^{N}\frac{d_{k}c_{l}}{k!l!}(k-l)s^{k+l-1}.

(3.3)

Since
k-l>0
in the last sum, Eq. (
3.3
) implies
g^{\prime}(s)>0
for
s>0
.
Hence the correction term
g^{\prime}(Ns)/(1-g(Ns))
in Eq. (
3.2
) is positive, and the desired inequality follows.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.6
.

Set
r_{N}:=p_{N}\boxplus_{N}q_{N}
. By Walsh’s theorem, or by
[
11
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have

r_{N}\in\mathcal{P}_{N}([-2b,-2a]).

Hence the sequence
(\mu\llbracket r_{N}\rrbracket)
is tight, and every subsequence has a weakly convergent subsequence.

Let
(\mu\llbracket r_{N_{k}}\rrbracket)
be such a subsequence and let
\mu\llbracket r_{N_{k_{l}}}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\rho
. By Corollary
3.5
and Proposition
3.7
, we obtain

R_{\rho}(s)\geq R_{\mu}(s)+R_{\nu}(s)

for
s\in(0,1/2b)
. On the other hand,
[
11
, Theorem 1.12]
, in the form (
3.1
), gives the reverse inequality

R_{\rho}(s)\leq R_{\mu}(s)+R_{\nu}(s).

Therefore

R_{\rho}(s)=R_{\mu}(s)+R_{\nu}(s)=R_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(s)

for
s\in(0,1/2b)
, and hence
\rho=\mu\boxplus\nu
.
Since every subsequence of
(\mu\llbracket r_{N}\rrbracket)
has a further subsequence converging to
\mu\boxplus\nu
, we conclude that

\mu\llbracket p_{N}\boxplus_{N}q_{N}\rrbracket\xrightarrow{w}\mu\boxplus\nu.

∎
