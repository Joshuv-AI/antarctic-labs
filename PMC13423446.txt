Preoperative Radiotherapy as a Major Risk Factor for Pelvic Abscess after Abdominoperineal Resection and Total Pelvic Exenteration: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Tomoaki Kaneko, Shuichiro Matoba, Mitsunori Ushigome, Satoru Kagami, Yasuo Nagashima, Kimihiko Yoshida, Yasuyuki Miura, Takayuki Suzuki, Kentaro Watanabe, Kimihiko Funahashi, Yuichiro Otsuka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives: Pelvic abscess, alongside perineal wound infection, is a major postoperative complication after abdominoperineal resection (APR) and total pelvic exenteration (TPE). Because the underlying mechanisms of these two complications differ, separate evaluation is warranted. This study aimed to identify risk factors specifically associated with pelvic abscess. Methods: We retrospectively analyzed patients who underwent APR or TPE at our institution between December 2004 and August 2025. Patients were divided into abscess and non-abscess groups, and patient characteristics and surgical variables were compared using univariate and multivariate analyses. Results: Among 215 patients, 187 (87%) underwent APR and 28 (13.0%) underwent TPE. The incidence of pelvic abscess was 18.1% (39/215). Univariate analysis identified significantly higher abscess rates in patients receiving preoperative radiotherapy (28.2% vs. 8.5%, p = 0.002) and in those with greater blood loss (894.0 vs. 390.0 mL, p = 0.015). Multivariate analysis confirmed preoperative radiotherapy as an independent predictor (p = 0.002). No difference in pelvic abscess incidence was found among patients who underwent APR with or without combined resection of adjacent pelvic organs. Conclusion: Preoperative radiotherapy is a significant risk factor for pelvic abscess after APR and TPE. Future studies should refine preventive strategies and develop targeted approaches for high-risk patients. Keywords: abdominoperineal resection, pelvic abscess, surgical site infection, total pelvic exenteration, preoperative radiotherapy

Introduction

Perineal wound infections and pelvic abscesses are well-known complications of abdominoperineal resection (APR) and total pelvic exenteration (TPE). The incidence of perineal wound infections is particularly high, reaching 30.2% in patients who have undergone preoperative radiotherapy[1]. Similarly, the reported incidence of pelvic abscess ranges from 15.3% to 25%[2,3]. These postoperative infections can lead to prolonged hospitalization[3,4] and increased medical costs[4], and may even adversely affect oncological outcomes[5]. Previous studies identified several risk factors, including preoperative radiotherapy, prolonged operative time, and excessive intraoperative blood loss. However, most of these studies have not distinguished between perineal wound infection and pelvic abscess in their analyses[6-10].

Because infectious fluid accumulated in the pelvic cavity can occasionally drain through a perineal wound, pelvic abscesses and perineal wound infections are often interrelated.

Nevertheless, perineal wound infection is mainly attributed to poor elasticity and limited blood supply to the perineal skin, as well as excessive tension on the wound during closure after skin excision[11]. In contrast, the development of pelvic abscesses is believed to result from different mechanisms such as the accumulation of bacteria-contaminated blood or exudate within the pelvic dead space after rectal resection[11-13]. Therefore, clearly distinguishing between these two types of infections is essential for establishing effective preventive strategies.

We have previously reported that prophylactic negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT) for perineal wound management significantly reduced the rate of wound infection[14]. However, no significant reduction in hospital stay was observed, suggesting that pelvic abscesses may contribute to a prolonged recovery.

Based on these findings, the present study aimed to identify risk factors specifically associated with pelvic abscesses formation after APR and TPE.

Methods

Ethical approval

The retrospective study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Toho University School of Medicine (T2025-108).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Toho University School of Medicine (T2025-108). Owing to its retrospective design, informed consent was obtained through an opt-out process on the institutional website, as approved by the ethics committee.

Patient recruitment and data collection

This retrospective study included consecutive patients who underwent APR or TPE at our institution between December 2004 and August 2025.

Associations between patient- and surgery-related variables and postoperative pelvic abscess (organ/space surgical site infection [SSI]) were evaluated by comparing these variables among patients with and without abscess. Variables that were considered clinically relevant or significant from univariate analysis were fitted into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors.

To account for procedural heterogeneity between APR and TPE and further assess the impact of preoperative radiotherapy (RT), two additional subgroup analyses were performed. First, a subgroup analysis was performed among patients who underwent TPE alone, in which the incidence of pelvic abscess was described and exploratory univariate analysis was performed. Second, the patients who received preoperative RT were allocated into abscess and non-abscess groups, wherein potential risk factors for pelvic abscess were assessed.

The patient-related variables analyzed included sex, age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification (≥3 vs. <3), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus (glycated hemoglobin > 6.5%), hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin < 3.0 g/dL), smoking status, obesity (body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2), preoperative RT and neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The surgery-related variables assessed included operation type (APR vs. TPE), surgical approach (laparoscopic vs. open), myocutaneous flap reconstruction (presence vs. absence), drain insertion route (perineal vs. abdominal), operative time, blood loss, and the use of postoperative negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT).

The duration of antibiotic administration was defined as the number of days the initially administered antibiotic was continued. In some cases, continuation of the same agent for suspected postoperative infection could not be distinguished from that for purely prophylactic use.

Surgical procedure

Laparoscopic surgery was gradually introduced during the study period. The surgical approach was determined at a preoperative surgical team conference based on the overall clinical assessment.

The procedure was performed with the patient in the lithotomy position. Total mesorectal excision with high ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery is routinely performed in patients with advanced malignant tumors. In patients with benign or early stage malignant tumors, high or low ligation was performed based on oncological safety. When tumor invasion of the hypogastric nerves or pelvic plexus was not suspected, these structures are preserved.

Depending on the extent of tumor invasion, TPE, including bladder resection, was performed when necessary. The perineal approach involved making a spindle-shaped incision around the anus and dividing the levator ani muscles attached to the rectum were divided. When vaginal wall invasion was present, partial vaginectomy was performed.

A single-lumen stoma was constructed in the left lower quadrant via either the extraperitoneal or intraperitoneal route. Additionally, in patients undergoing TPE, an ileal conduit was created in the right lower quadrant. From December 2004 to October 2008, a single closed-suction pelvic drain was placed via the transperineal route. From November 2008 onward, a single closed-suction drain was placed via the transabdominal route. In principle, the drain was removed on or after postoperative day 4 when the daily output was ≤100 mL without clinical signs of infection in the drainage fluid.

For perineal wound closure, the subcutaneous layer was closed with 1-0 absorbable sutures and the skin was closed using 2-0 non-absorbable monofilament vertical mattress sutures. Extralevator APR, pelvic floor reconstruction, omental filling, or mesh repair were not performed.

Indication for myocutaneous flap reconstruction

Myocutaneous flap reconstruction was performed in cases in which primary perineal closure was considered difficult due to extensive perineal skin resection or large vaginal wall defects following tumor invasion. Reconstruction was also considered when wide perineal soft tissue excision was required for achieving oncologically adequate margins.

Antibiotic prophylaxis

No oral antibiotics were administered preoperatively. Second-generation cephalosporins were administered postoperatively for a median duration of three days (range, 1–6 days). Levofloxacin was administered for two days to one patient who was allergic to cefmetazole.

Pelvic abscess diagnosis

A pelvic abscess was defined according to the CDC criteria for organ/space surgical site infection (SSI)[15]. Cases were considered positive when all of the following criteria (A–C) were met:

A) Infection occurred within 30 days of surgery and appeared to be associated with the operative procedure.

B) The infection involved any part of the body that was opened or manipulated during surgery (excluding the incision, fascia, or muscle layers).

C) At least one of the following was present:

a. Purulent drainage from a drain placed through a stab wound into an organ or space.

b. Organisms isolated from fluid or tissue were obtained aseptically from the organ or space.

c. An abscess or other evidence of infection involving the organ or space was found by direct examination, reoperation, or histopathological or radiological examination.

d. Diagnosis of organ or space SSI made by the attending surgeon or physician.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables, including age, operative time, blood loss, and length of hospital stay were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate, for univariate analysis. Variables with a p-value < 0.1 in the univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for SSI. All analyses were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R software (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria)[16].

Results

Patient characteristics

Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A total of 215 patients (136 men and 79 women) with a median age of 69 years (range, 33–91 years) were included in this study. The surgical indications included: rectal cancer (161 [74.8%]), anal cancer (24 [11.2%]), local recurrence (19 [8.8%]), fecal incontinence after intersphincteric resection (3 [1.4%]), malignant melanoma (2 [0.9%]), gynecological cancer (2 [0.9%]), inflammatory bowel disease (1 [0.5%]), retroperitoneal primary cancer (1 [0.5%]), peritoneal dissemination of gastric cancer (1 [0.5%]), and Paget's disease (1 [0.5%]).

Operative procedures and reconstruction

APR and TPE were performed in 87.0% (187/215) and 13.0% (28/215) of the patients, respectively. Among patients who underwent APR, 25 (13.4%) underwent additional procedures, including lateral lymph node dissection in 9 patients and combined resection of adjacent pelvic organs in 16. Adjacent organ resections comprised hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy in 11 patients and partial vaginal resection in 5.

Myocutaneous flap reconstruction was performed in 5.1% (11/215) of the patients: anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap in four, rectus abdominis myocutaneous (RAM) flap in two, gluteus maximus myocutaneous (GMM) flap in two, combined ALT and RAM flaps in two, and gracilis myocutaneous flap in one patient. Among these 11 patients, the indications for myocutaneous flap reconstruction were wide perineal soft tissue excision including previous perianal abscess sites (n = 5), extensive vaginal wall resection due to tumor invasion (n = 4), and large tumor size requiring broad perineal skin resection (n = 2).

Drain management

Regarding postoperative drain management, after excluding patients with postoperative pelvic abscess, reoperation, or death before drain removal, the median duration of drain placement was 11 days (range, 4–23); moreover, the median drainage volume at removal was 35 mL (range, 0–405).

Pelvic abscess

The incidence of pelvic abscess was 18.1% (39/215). Among the 39 patients diagnosed with pelvic abscesses, bacterial cultures were performed with samples from 33 (84.6%) patients, and positive cultures were obtained in 32 (82.0%) patients.

Based on the CDC criteria, 32, 5, and 2 patients met criterion C-b (organisms isolated from fluid or tissue obtained aseptically), C-a (purulent drainage from a pelvic drain), and C-c (radiological evidence of abscess), respectively. No cases were diagnosed solely based on the attending physician's clinical judgment (criterion C-d).

Preoperative radiotherapy

Preoperative RT was administered to 26 patients. Among them, 24 received concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) and 2 received RT alone. RT was delivered in 25–33 fractions with a total dose of 45–61.2 Gy. Most patients received 45–50 Gy to the whole pelvis. Concurrent chemotherapy consisted mainly of oral S-1 (tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil potassium) or capecitabine.

Analysis

In the univariate analysis, the incidence of pelvic abscesses was significantly higher in patients who received preoperative radiotherapy (28.2% vs. 8.5%, p = 0.002). Further, the incidence of pelvic abscesses tended to be lower in the laparoscopic group than in the open group (17.9% vs. 33.5%, p = 0.083). Patients with greater intraoperative blood loss experienced a significantly higher incidence of pelvic abscesses (874.0 mL vs. 390.0 mL, p = 0.015). The route of pelvic drain placement (transperineal vs. transabdominal) was not associated with pelvic abscess development (30.8% vs. 21.0%, p = 0.208). The hospital stay was significantly longer in the pelvic abscess group than in the non-abscess group (33.0 days vs. 24.0 days, p < 0.001; Table 2). Multivariate analysis identified preoperative radiotherapy (p = 0.009) as an independent risk factor for pelvic abscesses (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, preoperative radiotherapy was identified as a significant risk factor for pelvic abscesses. Previous studies have reported several potential risk factors. Reported patient-related risk factors include advanced age[3], diabetes mellitus[3], African American race, ASA physical status ≥4, body mass index ≥35 kg/m2, and preoperative weight loss[13]. Surgery-related factors include, an enlarged pelvic cavity after extended APR or multiorgan resection[2], excessive intraoperative blood loss[3], and perineal closure using myocutaneous flaps[13]. Finally, tumor-related factors, including a good response to chemoradiotherapy (low tumor regression grade)[3], may also influence the risk of pelvic abscess formation.

Although few studies have identified radiotherapy as a direct risk factor for pelvic abscess, multiple reports have linked it to perineal wound complications, including infection and abscess formation[1,7]. Furthermore, the incidence of deep surgical site infections (SSI) in patients receiving preoperative radiotherapy has been reported to be as high as 25%[3], suggesting a plausible association between radiotherapy and deeper infectious sequelae such as pelvic abscess.

Multiple mechanisms may explain the increased risk of pelvic abscess after radiotherapy. Radiation exposure impairs wound healing by affecting several processes, for example, reduced wound strength, collagen deposition, and angiogenesis[17,18]. In addition, the pelvic dead space created after rectal resection tends to accumulate exudative or blood-contaminated fluids, which may serve as a reservoir of infection[11]. Moreover, radiation decreases neutrophil function and bactericidal activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli[19], thereby diminishing local resistance to infection.

Within the RT subgroup, no patient- or surgery-related variables were associated with pelvic abscess. This finding suggests that, among patients who had already received RT, no additional factors further stratified the risk of pelvic abscess. However, this result should be cautiously interpreted due to the limited sample size of the subgroup analysis.

TPE is generally associated with longer operative time and greater blood loss compared with APR. In addition, TPE results in a substantially larger pelvic dead space; furthermore, division of branches of the internal iliac artery during the procedure may potentially compromise pelvic tissue perfusion.

However, in the present study, the incidence of pelvic abscess did not differ between APR and TPE. This finding suggests that the extent of surgical invasiveness alone might not be the sole determinant of pelvic abscess formation. Rather, the impact of preoperative RT might play a more influential role compared with the magnitude of surgical resection itself.

Furthermore, the lack of difference in abscess incidence between APR alone and APR with combined resection of adjacent pelvic organs suggests that the absolute volume of pelvic dead space might not independently determine abscess formation. Although arterial division during TPE could theoretically reduce pelvic blood flow, collateral circulation may compensate for this reduction. However, radiation-induced microvascular damage, tissue fibrosis, and impaired wound healing might exert a more profound effect on local tissue integrity than macroscopic reductions in blood flow.

In this context, several techniques have been proposed to prevent pelvic abscesses by reducing pelvic dead space, including omental filling[12,20], myocutaneous flap reconstruction[21,22], and the use of absorbable gel materials to fill cavities[23].

Omental filling reduces dead space and promotes perineal wound healing through the intrinsic wound-healing properties of the omentum[24]. Although earlier reviews have suggested that omental filling may improve perineal wound healing[20], more recent analyses have failed to demonstrate its efficacy in preventing perineal wound infections or intra-abdominal abscesses[12].

Myocutaneous flap reconstruction has been reported to promote wound healing and enhance infection control by filling the pelvic dead space with healthy, well-vascularized tissue[10,13]. Even in irradiated fields, transferring non-irradiated vascularized tissue into the wound bed has been shown to be effective[21]. Many previous studies have reported that flap reconstruction reduces the incidence of pelvic abscesses[21,22], making it one of the most promising techniques for infection prevention. However, some reports have shown a higher incidence of deep infections following flap reconstruction[13,25], leaving its overall benefit controversial. Furthermore, flap reconstruction is associated with longer operative time and greater intraoperative blood loss, reflecting its increased surgical invasiveness[13].

In addition, several adjunctive methods have been explored. Continuous pelvic irrigation with negative-pressure wound therapy has been attempted in some reports, with occasional findings of benefit[26]. A recent report has suggested that piperacillin/tazobactam may reduce the incidence of SSI after APR compared with cefmetazole sodium[27]. Although this study primarily evaluated incisional SSI rather than organ/space infection, the findings raise the possibility that antimicrobial selection might also be associated with pelvic abscess development. Although these findings remain exploratory, they might provide additional options for infection prevention. However, careful consideration is required when using piperacillin/tazobactam due to the potential for antimicrobial resistance.

Limitations

This study has a few limitations. First, it was a retrospective analysis conducted at a single institution; therefore, selection bias could not be completely eliminated. However, the sample size was adequate, and a multivariate analysis was performed to ensure the reliability of the results. Second, the diagnosis of pelvic abscesses was based on the CDC guidelines for surgical site infections. Because routine drain cultures and computed tomography (CT) were not performed for all patients after APR or TPE, mild cases that improved with antibiotic therapy alone might not have been diagnosed as pelvic abscesses.

Third, the number of TPE cases and abscess events was limited, which might have reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Similarly, the sample size within the RT subgroup was relatively small, potentially limiting the ability to detect additional associated factors.

Conclusion

This study identified preoperative radiotherapy as a major risk factor for pelvic abscesses after abdominoperineal resection and TPE. Although omental filling and myocutaneous flap reconstruction have been reported to support infection control and wound healing by providing well-vascularized tissue, their efficacy remains unclear. Future studies should aim to optimize the existing preventive strategies and develop novel approaches, particularly for high-risk patients who have undergone preoperative radiotherapy.
