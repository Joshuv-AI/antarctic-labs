Introduction

1.1
Background and previous work

The seminal work of Viola
[
25
]
initiated the systematic study of a topic referred to as
the complexity of distributions
. In this framework, one has a target distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
that one wishes to sample from using uniformly random bits. Sampling is then the task of designing an algorithm
A
that, given a uniformly random input bit string
X\sim\{0,1\}^{m}
, outputs a string
A(X)\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that the resulting output distribution is close to
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
in statistical distance. The class of algorithms
A
under consideration, i.e., the model of computation, and the target distribution determine the nature of the problem in this context. One is then led to both upper- and lower-bound questions: to either show the existence of an algorithm that (approximately) samples the distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
, or to show that every such algorithm fails to generate a distribution close to
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
.

Over the past fifteen years this area has received considerable attention
[

,

started by showing the first impossibility result that local functions

, where every output bit

depends on only a few input bits, are unable to sample a uniformly random string from a Hamming slice

. The work also featured an application of such sampling lower bounds to lower bounds for succinct data structures which were extended in
[

. Subsequent works

explored applications of a different kind, namely separations between the sampling power of quantum and classical circuits. The hardness of sampling was also used to construct explicit codes

The following typical example illuminates why sampling is easier than computing in general, and therefore, proving lower bounds is a more difficult task. For uniformly random
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
the distribution
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n},X_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus X_{n})

Examples of this kind brought attention to the study of the sampling power of local maps or related models such as shallow decision forests. On this front, Filmus, Leigh, Riazanov, and Sokolov
[
7

show a lower bound against shallow decision forests for sampling from a Hamming slice

r

Analogous results have been shown for

While it is natural to expect that for any

An essential (and non-trivial) step in our results is to show that a single polynomial

A related problem to this result is the weight distribution of binary Reed-Muller codes

As mentioned above, the parameter

At a high level, our results form a hierarchy where we show lower bounds against degree

One of the main technical tools in establishing our results is a
Chebyshev lemma
stating the following. Suppose we are given a distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
, where each
P_{i}
can be written as
P_{i}=\Gamma_{i}(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})
for some degree
d

Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir}

almost pairwise independent
. That is, for each
i\neq j

2r

{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}
from the distribution
\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}

\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-o_{r}(1)-o_{n}(1)

One issue is that, generally, polynomial distributions are not
almost pairwise independent
. However, we can hope for a relaxed scenario of finding a
subset
of the
P_{i}

(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})

for

P_{i}

(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})
are almost pairwise independent
up to a small collection of

that appear in all of them

In order to quantify

for a collection of polynomials, we use the notion of

, which has been studied in the context of structure versus randomness of polynomials
[
10
,
21
]

Using similar machinery, we show that any collection of low degree polynomials contains a large subset that form a sunflower, whose petals are pairwise regular polynomials in the sense described above. This is of independent interest to us, and, to the best of our knowledge, has not appeared previously in the literature.
While sunflowers have appeared before in related contexts (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
30

2.2
Lower bounds against linear distributions

We start with the following simple claim, showing a lower bound for degree-
1
distributions. We include this result for completeness rather than for novelty.

Theorem 2

(Degree-
1
distributions; formal version in
Section

7
)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}=(L_{1},\dots,L_{n})
be a degree-
1
distribution.
Then
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\exp(-cn)
for some absolute constant
c>0
.

The (simple) proof of this theorem illustrates a strategy that is used frequently in proving lower bounds for sampling: reducing to a win-win scenario. The argument for this theorem goes as follows. Let
D
be the maximum number of linearly independent
L_{i}
’s. The key is to analyze what happens when
D
is large and small respectively. If
D
is large, say, more than
n/2
, then the
D
linearly independent coordinates form a uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{D}
, thus giving
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
distance from the target distribution
\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}
. On the other hand, if
D
is small, then conditioning on the values of the
D
linearly independent
L_{i}
’s fixes the value of all other coordinates. In other words,
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
is expressed as a convex combination of
2^{D}
many distributions, each with singleton support. This case also gives a
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
lower bound, using a certain union bound claim for convex combinations of distributions.

Our first interesting result is the following theorem, showing a sampling lower bound for bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{1}
distributions. These are distributions
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
where each
P_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
can be described by a bounded number of linear/degree-
1
polynomials. Already at this point, we are considering a more general class of distributions than local maps. For
r
-local maps the works of
[
28
,
15
]
essentially prove a

lower bound for sampling
\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}

Theorem 3

(Bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{1}
distributions; formal version in
Theorem

8.1
)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be a distribution, where each
P_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
can be written in the form
P_{i}=\Gamma_{i}(L_{i1},\dots,L_{ir})
, where
\Gamma_{i}\colon\{0,1\}^{r}\to\{0,1\}
is some function and
L_{i1},\dots,L_{ir}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
are degree-
1

Then
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\exp(-2^{-Cr^{2}}n)
for some absolute constant
C>0
.

The proof of the theorem is an induction on
r

r
-local functions in

15

r

3

P_{i}
is adjacent to all
L\in\operatorname{span}(L_{i1},\dots,L_{ir})
. Then the argument considers a maximal subspace

W
is adjacent to many polynomials, then by conditioning/fixing the values of the linear functions in
W
we reduce the rank of its neighbors, which allows for applying induction. Otherwise, the neighborhood of
W
is rather small, and its maximality ensures that we can find a large subset of polynomials that depend on linearly independent subspaces, which reduces to the scenario where a large subset of
P_{i}
’s are
independent
, and hence the subset corresponds to a product distribution with each coordinate having
\Omega(2^{-r})
distance from
\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)
.

For the second case for each

for some function
\Gamma_{j}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{r}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}

. That almost reduces it to the setting of

By choosing the appropriate values of

Theorem 4

(Quadratic distributions; formal version in
Theorem

)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})
be a distribution, where each

is a quadratic polynomial.
Then
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-n^{-c/\log\log(n)}
for some absolute constant

.

The next natural step is to show a lower bound for bounded

Theorem 5

(Bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{2}
distributions; formal version in

)

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be a distribution, where each

Below we describe the ideas that go into the proof of the theorem. The main idea is to use the technique known as
regularization
in higher order Fourier analysis
[
10
,
13
]
. We describe the special case of it for

\operatorname{rank}_{2}
. Later, we will generalize it to arbitrary degree

Suppose we have a collection of quadratic polynomials
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
.
We define the structural notion of
regularity
as follows. A collection or

of polynomials
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is said to be

-regular if all nonzero linear combinations
\tilde{Q}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
have rank greater than

By the the structure of quadratic polynomials (Dickson’s Lemma

),
if the factor
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is
k

then all nonzero linear combinations
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
are almost balanced in the sense that
\Pr_{X}[\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}(X)=1]=(1\pm 2^{-k/2})/2
.
A standard Fourier-analytic argument shows that if
k\gg r
, then all nonzero linear combinations
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
are almost balanced, and the distribution of
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
must be close to uniform.

Suppose now that

has
\operatorname{rank}_{2}(P)\leqslant r
, and hence can be written as
P=\Gamma(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
for some quadratic polynomials
Q_{i}
. Observe that if the factor of quadratics
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is not

-regular, then there exists some linear combination

whose rank is at most

, and hence we can remove one of the

’s, and replace it with at most
k
linear functions, and hence

can be written as
P=\Gamma(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r-1},L_{1},\dots,L_{k})
, where all

’s are linearly independent. Continuing this process, we get a

(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r^{\prime}},L_{1},\dots,L_{s})
consisting of

quadratic polynomials and

linear function such that

and
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r^{\prime}},L_{1},\dots,L_{s^{\prime}})
is
k
-regular.

We can now return to the proof overview of the lower bound for distributions of bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{2}
. The proof uses induction. Let
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be such a distribution, where each

can be written as a function of

as a shorthand for

.

We may assume that all
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}

k

{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}
. Now, we construct a graph, similar to

{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}

and

if the pair

is not

Once again, the win-win scenario of

If a large number

Otherwise, there exists some

We show that in this case, we can change the basis of

Lower bounds against polynomial distributions of degree greater than 2

In the next step we lift

Theorem 6

Let

Theorem 7

(Degree-

)

Let

We now sketch the main idea behind the proof of this

2.5
Chebyshev lemma

Before proceeding to this lemma, we recall the definition of degree-
d
rank. The
\operatorname{rank}_{d}(P)
of a function
P\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is the smallest positive integer
k
for which
k
polynomials
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}
of degree
d
exist so that
P
can be written as
P=\Gamma(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k})
for some
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. When we speak of the rank of a polynomial of degree exactly
d+1
, we mean its
\operatorname{rank}_{d}
.

Lemma 1

(Chebyshev lemma; formal version in
Lemma

4.1
)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be such that each
P_{i}
has bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{d}
; i.e.,
P_{i}=\Gamma_{i}(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})
for some
\Gamma_{i}
and degree-
d
polynomials
Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir}
. Suppose further that for all
i\neq j
the distribution defined by the tuple

{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{ij}=(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir};Q_{j1},\dots,Q_{jr})\qquad\text{ is $2^{-r}$-close to uniform.}

(Pairwise Uniform)

Then,
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-2^{-r}-o(1)
.

The requirement of being close to uniform can be replaced by the structural requirement of
regularity
. A collection or
factor
of degree-
d
polynomials
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is
k
-regular if all nonzero linear combinations
\tilde{Q}=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q_{i}
have rank more than
k
. The celebrated rank-bias theorem of
[
10
,
21
]
gives a tradeoff between
k
and the bias of
\tilde{Q}
. More precisely, if
k
is large enough as a function of
\delta
and
d
, then
\Pr_{X}[\tilde{Q}(X)=1]=(1\pm\delta)/2
.
Hence, if the factor of polynomials
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is
k
-regular, then all nonzero linear combinations of are balanced and the joint distribution
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
is close to uniform by a standard Fourier-analytic argument.
It follows that for some
k=k(d,\delta,r)
the following implies
Eq.

Pairwise Uniform
.

{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{ij}=(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir};Q_{j1},\dots,Q_{jr})\qquad\text{ is }k\text{-regular}.

(Pairwise Regularity)

Loosely speaking, our Chebyshev lemma enables us to prove a sampling lower bound by applying induction on the
\operatorname{rank}_{d}
. We explained this idea to some extent in our lower bound for bounded quadratic rank. If we have a distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
such that each
P_{i}
has bounded
\operatorname{rank}_{d}
, then either

1.

there exists a large subset of coordinates satisfying
Eq.

Pairwise Regularity
, which puts us in the setting of
1
; or

2.

there exists a low-rank structure, which allows us to reduce a certain parameter (roughly corresponding to
r
), and hence apply the induction hypothesis.

The main tool that makes the Chebyshev lemma useful is
regularization
. We have already discussed regularization in part when considering distributions of bounded quadratic rank. In full generality, regularizing a factor
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
of degree-
d
polynomials
Q_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
, is the process of producing another factor of degree-
d
polynomials
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime}_{1},\dots,Q^{\prime}_{r^{\prime}})
that enjoys the following properties.

•

(Regularity)
Each linear combination
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}Q^{\prime}_{i}
has high rank.

•

(Refinement)
Everything that was computable by
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}
, is also computable by
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\prime}
. Formally, if
P=\Gamma(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r})
for some
\Gamma
, then
P=\Gamma^{\prime}(Q^{\prime}_{1},\dots,Q^{\prime}_{r^{\prime}})
for some
\Gamma^{\prime}
.

•

(Small size blow-up)
The number of polynomials in
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{\prime}
is not much larger than
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}
. More precisely,
r^{\prime}
depends only on
r,d,
and the regularity parameter
k
, and it is independent of the number of variables
m
.

We now sketch the main idea behind the proof of
7
. The proof will be similar to
5
for distributions with bounded quadratic rank. Roughly speaking, the proof of
7
works by formulating a generalized version of the idea depicted in
Fig.

1
as a separate structural theorem, which we call

Theorem 8

(Sunflower pairwise regularization; formal version in
Theorem

5.2
)
.

Let
(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be a collection of degree-
d
polynomials
P_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Then there exists a subset
S\subseteq[n]
of polynomials of size
w=n^{\Omega(1)}
such that for each
i\in S
the following holds.

•

Every
P_{i}
can be written in the form
P_{i}=\Gamma_{i}(Q_{1}^{*},\dots,Q^{*}_{c};Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})
where
Q_{1}^{*},\dots,Q^{*}_{c}
appear in all of the

and
Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir}
is unique to

. That is, the sets
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}_{i}=\{Q_{1}^{*},\dots,Q^{*}_{c},Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir}\}
form a sunflower with the core
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{*}=(Q^{*}_{1},\dots,Q^{*}_{c})
and petals
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}=(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir})
.

•

The number
r+c
is small; in particular, it is independent of
m
and
n
.

•

For all
i\neq j
the tuple
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{ij}=(Q_{i1},\dots,Q_{ir};Q_{j1},\dots,Q_{jr})
satisfies
Eq.

Pairwise Regularity
.

With
8
in hand, the only remaining issue is handling the common polynomials
Q^{*}_{1},\dots,Q^{*}_{c}
without which we can apply our Chebyshev lemma (
1
) to the pairwise regular set
S
. In a separate lemma we prove that because
c\ll w
, the effect of
Q^{*}_{i}
’s is not significant (see
Lemma

3.6
), which concludes the proof of our lower bound for degree-
d
distributions.

We close our discussion of the results by turning to the proof of
8
. The goal in this theorem is to write
P_{i}
in the form
P_{i}=\Gamma_{i}({\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{*};{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i})
satisfying the three conditions. At the beginning, such a representation exists trivially by letting
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{*}
to be empty and
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}
to be simply
(P_{i})
; however,
Eq.

Pairwise Regularity
is not necessarily satisfied. At this point, we construct a graph similar to
Fig.

1
connecting
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}
to
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{j}
whenever the pair
({\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i};{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{j})
is not regular enough. Then if we find a large independent set in this graph (
Fig.

1(a)
) we simply stop and announce the corresponding independent set as our final subset of polynomials. Otherwise, a vertex of large degree exists as in
Fig.

1(b)
, which allows for a change of basis, that introduces a new common polynomial into the core
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}^{*}
at the expense of introducing a few polynomials into the petals
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}
. We repeat this step until we get a subset of polynomials that are pairwise regular. To ensure that
{\bm{\mathcal{Q}}}_{i}
are individually regular, we need to interweave such steps with usual refinement/regularization steps. The main concern is why this process must terminate. The reason is that at each step, a polynomial is replaced by a few polynomials of strictly smaller degree, so the process can continue only for finitely many steps.

Open problems

Below are some problems that arise naturally from this work.

•

Improve the lower bounds on the statistical distance. In particular, is it true that

•

What about samplers defined by polynomials of degree

, where

grows as a function of

. The results of this work seem to be useful only when

is a

.

•

Characterize all achievable values of

, where
P\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is a polynomial of degree
d
and
m
is allowed to be any positive integer. A related problem is the weight distribution of binary Reed-Muller codes; see
[
20
,
18
,
19
]
.

•

For degree
d=3
how close can
\Pr_{X}[P(X)=1]
be to
1/3
? Below we provide several examples. First consider the following polynomials with
\Pr_{X}[P(X)=1]<1/3
.

–

The simplest example is
P(x)=x_{1}x_{2}
with
\Pr[P(x)=1]=1/4
. The gap is
1/3-1/4=1/12
.

–

A more interesting example is the polynomial
P(x)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}
. The first term gives

, and the second term gives
\Pr[x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}=1]=1/8
. Hence
\Pr[P(x)=1]=1/4+1/8-2\cdot(1/4)\cdot(1/8)=5/16
. The gap is

.

Next, we describe several examples with
\Pr_{X}[P(X)=1]>1/3
.

–

The polynomial
P(x)=x_{1}\cdot(1-x_{2}x_{3})
satisfies
\Pr_{X}[P(X)=1]=1/2\cdot 3/4=3/8
. The gap is
3/8-1/3=1/24\approx 0.0417
.

–

The polynomial
P(x)=x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}(x_{4}x_{5}+x_{6}x_{7})
. The first term
\Pr[x_{1}x_{2}=1]=1/4
, and the second term gives
\Pr[x_{3}(x_{4}x_{5}+x_{6}x_{7})=1]=3/16
. Hence
\Pr[P(x)=1]=1/4+3/16-2\cdot(1/4)\cdot(3/16)=11/326
. The gap is

.

–

Our last example is

.
In this example, we have
\Pr[P(x)=1]=43/128
, and the gap is

.

It would be interesting to better understand such examples, and compare the gaps we obtain here to the lower bound
\delta_{3}
from

.

3
Preliminaries

3.1
Basic definitions and notation

Polynomials.

We work with multilinear polynomials
P\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{m}]
of degree
d
, viewed as functions
P\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
. It is understood that a degree-
d
polynomial means a polynomial of degree
at most

d
unless otherwise stated.

The definition of polynomials can be extended to functions of the form
P\colon V\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
where
V
is an
m
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. We use the fact that
V
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
, so after fixing some linear isomorphism
\phi\colon\mathbb{F}^{m}_{2}\to V
we say
P
is a polynomial of degree
d
if the function
P^{\prime}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
given by
P^{\prime}(x)=P(\phi(x))
is a polynomial of degree
d
. This can be further extended in the natural way to functions
Q\colon W+h\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
where
W+h
is an affine subspace of
V
. In this case,
Q
is defined to be a polynomial of degree
d
if
Q^{\prime}(x)=Q(\alpha(x)+h)
is a polynomial of degree
d
, where
\alpha\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{\dim(W)}\to W
is a linear isomorphism. This definition is independent of the particular choice of isomorphisms
\phi
and
\alpha
. The class of polynomials is closed under affine restrictions.

We now define our main object of study, polynomial distributions, sometimes also referred to as polynomial sources in the context of randomness extractors.

Definition 3.1

(Degree-
d
polynomial distribution)
.

A distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}
over
\{0,1\}^{n}
is a
degree-
d
polynomial distribution
if for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
n
polynomials
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
each of degree
d
such that the tuple sampled according to
(P_{1}(X),\dots,P_{n}(X))
where
X
is drawn uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
has the same distribution as
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}
. We assert this by writing
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
usually suppressing the common random input
X
.

Often, we will identify
P
with the distribution
P(X)
it defines on a random input
X
, and so we treat
P
both as a polynomial and a random variable.

The
bias
of a polynomial
P
is the quantity defined as
\operatorname{bias}(P)\coloneq\lvert\E_{X}[(-1)^{P(X)}]\rvert
. This quantity without the absolute values is referred to as the
signed bias

\operatorname{bias}_{\pm}(P)
.

Distributions.

Bernoulli distribution
\mathrm{Ber}(\rho)
with parameter
\rho
will be more compactly written as
{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}_{\rho}
. The uniform distribution on
\{0,1\}
is denoted by
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}
. The total variation distance between two distributions
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1},{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}
defined over the same countable domain
\Omega
is
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}-{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\coloneq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Omega}\lvert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}(x)-{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}(x)\rvert
. Equivalently,
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}-{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}\rVert=\max_{A\subseteq\Omega}\big({\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}(A)-{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{2}(A)\big)
, where
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}(A)
is the probability that the output of
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}
belongs to
A
. If
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
is a distribution over a set
S
, we denote by
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}^{\otimes n}
the distribution over
S^{n}
obtained by
n
independent copies of
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
. Given a collection of distributions
\{{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}:i\in I\}
over a common domain, and a distribution
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}
over the index set
I
, their convex combination (weighted by
{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}
) is denoted by
\E_{i\sim{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}}[{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}]
; a sample
X
from
\E_{i\sim{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}}[{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}]
is obtained by first sampling an index
i\sim{\bm{\mathcal{C}}}
and the sampling
X\sim{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}
.

Other conventions.

We use the standard notation
[n]
to refer to
\{1,\dots,n\}
. We include zero in the set of natural numbers, so
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
. Throughout,
\log
denotes the base-
2
logarithm, and
\exp(x)
denotes
2^{x}
. For disjoint sets
A
and
B
, the union is sometimes written as
A\sqcup B
to highlight disjointness. For real numbers
a,b,c
, whenever we write
a=b\pm c
, that means there exists some
d\in[-c,c]
that satisfies the equality
a=b+d
. For a function
f\colon A\to B
its
restriction
to
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
is the function
f{{\mkern-3.0mu\upharpoonright\mkern-2.0mu}_{A^{\prime}}}\colon A^{\prime}\to B
given by
f{{\mkern-3.0mu\upharpoonright\mkern-2.0mu}_{A^{\prime}}}(x)=f(x)
. A
dyadic number
is a rational number
q
that can be written in the form
a/2^{b}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b\in\mathbb{N}
. We call
b
the granularity of this dyadic representation.

3.2
Some properties of total variation distance

A lemma we use extensively throughout the paper is the following XOR lemma, commonly attributed to Vazirani, which states that an
\varepsilon
-biased distribution is close to uniform whenever
\varepsilon
is exponentially small in the output length.

Lemma 3.2

(Vazirani’s XOR lemma)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a distribution over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. If for all nonzero linear functions
L\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
it holds that

\Pr[L(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=0]=\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

then,
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leqslant 2^{n/2}\varepsilon
. (Proof in
Appendix

B
.)

The following lemma may be regarded as union bound for the distances between distributions.

Lemma 3.3

(Distance from convex combination)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}},{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}
be two distributions over a finite domain
X
, and let
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}=\sum_{i}c_{i}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}
be the convex combination of distributions
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{k}
according to the weights
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
where
c_{i}>0
and
\sum_{i}c_{i}=1
. Suppose that
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}\rVert_{\text{TV}}=1-\varepsilon_{i}
for some
\varepsilon_{i}\in[0,1/2]
. Then,

\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+c_{i})\varepsilon_{i}.

Proof.

Let
A_{i}=\{x\in X:{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(x)>{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}(x)\}
. Then we have
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}\rVert_{\text{TV}}={\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(A_{i})-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}(A_{i})
.
In particular
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(A_{i})\geqslant 1-\varepsilon_{i}
and
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}(A_{i})\leqslant\varepsilon_{i}
.
Consider the set
A=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
, and note that

{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(A)\geqslant 1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(X\setminus A_{i})\geqslant 1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}.

On the other hand,

{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}(A)\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}(A_{i})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\varepsilon_{i}.

Therefore,

{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}(A)-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}(A)\geqslant\Bigl(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{i}\Bigr)-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\varepsilon_{i}=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(1+c_{i})\varepsilon_{i},

as required.
∎

Lemma 3.4

(Distance between product distributions
[
14
]
)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}={\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{n}
and
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}={\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{n}
be product distributions over
\{0,1\}^{n}
where all the marginals
i\in[n]
satisfy
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}_{i}-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{i}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant\delta
. Then
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{D}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-2e^{-\delta^{2}n/12}
.

Lemma 3.5

(Conditioning)
.

Fix a mapping
f\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Let
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m}
be the uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{m}
and let
{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
\lVert f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
.
Let
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S}
be the uniform distribution over
S\subseteq\{0,1\}^{m}
of size
\lvert S\rvert=\delta\cdot 2^{m}
.
Then
\lVert f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S})-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon(1+1/\delta)
.

Proof.

Let
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
be an event such that
\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})\in A]-\Pr[{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\in A]=\lVert f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
. In particular,
\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})\in A]\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
and
\Pr[{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\in A]\leqslant\varepsilon
.
By the assumption on
S
, we have
\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S})\notin A]\leqslant\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})\notin A]/\delta
.

\displaystyle\lVert f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S})-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S})\in A]-\Pr[{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\in A]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}_{S})\notin A]-\Pr[{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\in A]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1-\Pr[f({\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes m})\notin A]/\delta-\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon/\delta-\varepsilon

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\varepsilon\cdot(1+1/\delta),

as required.
∎

Lemma 3.6

(Fixing coordinates)
.

Let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to\{0,1\}
and
Q^{*}\colon\{0,1\}^{m}\to S
be
k+1
functions, and fix
\Gamma\colon\{0,1\}^{k}\times S\to\{0,1\}^{n}
.
For
\sigma\in S
define
\Gamma_{\sigma}\colon\{0,1\}^{k}\to\{0,1\}^{n}
to be the restriction of
\Gamma
by setting the last variable to be
\sigma
,
that is
\Gamma_{\sigma}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\Gamma(x_{1},\dots,x_{k},\sigma)
.

Let
X
be the uniform distribution over
\{0,1\}^{m}
, and let
{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}
be a distribution over
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for all
\sigma\in S
we have
\lVert\Gamma_{\sigma}(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X))-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon
.
Suppose that
\Pr[Q^{*}(X)=\sigma]\geqslant\tau
for all
\sigma\in S
.
Then

\lVert\Gamma(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X),Q^{*}(X))-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\frac{4\varepsilon\lvert S\rvert}{\tau}.

Proof.

Note that

\Gamma(Q_{1}(x),\dots,Q_{k}(x),Q^{*}(x))=\sum_{\sigma\in S}\mathbb{1}_{\langle Q^{*}(x)=\sigma\rangle}\cdot\Gamma_{\sigma}(Q_{1}(x),\dots,Q_{k}(x)).

By
Lemma

3.5
if we condition on
Q^{*}(X)=\sigma
, then

\lVert\Gamma_{\sigma}(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X)){{\mkern-3.0mu\upharpoonright\mkern-2.0mu}_{Q^{*}(X)=\sigma}}-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant 1-\varepsilon(1+1/\tau).

Since
\Gamma(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X),Q^{*}(X))
is the weighted average of
\Gamma_{\sigma}(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X))
, it follows by
Lemma

3.3
that

\displaystyle\lVert\Gamma(Q_{1}(X),\dots,Q_{k}(X),Q^{*}(X))-{\bm{\mathcal{Y}}}\rVert_{\text{TV}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1-\sum_{\sigma\in S}(1+\Pr[Q^{*}(X)=\sigma])\cdot\varepsilon(1+1/\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-(\lvert S\rvert+1)\varepsilon(1+1/\tau)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle 1-4\varepsilon\lvert S\rvert/\tau,

as required.
∎

3.3
Rank, regularity, and factors of polynomials

Next, we introduce the standard notions relating polynomials which are also found in previous work
[
10
,
21
,
3
]
.

Definition 3.7

(Rank
[
10
,
21
]
)
.

Let
P\colon\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}
be a function, not identically zero. The degree
d
rank of
P
, denoted by
\operatorname{rank}_{d}(P)
, is the smallest positive integer
k
for which there exist
k
degree
d
polynomials
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{k}
and a function
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}
such that

P(x)=\Gamma(Q_{1}(x),\dots,Q_{k}(x))\qquad\text{ for all }x.

We may use the terms
linear rank
,
quadratic rank
, and
cubic rank
to refer to
\operatorname{rank}_{1}(P),\operatorname{rank}_{2}(P)
and
\operatorname{rank}_{3}(P)
respectively. The most important notion of rank for a degree
d
polynomial
P
is
\operatorname{rank}_{d-1}(P)
.

As with degree, our convention is that a polynomial of rank
r
means a polynomial of rank
at most

r
. We will state explicitly whenever an exact degree or rank is intended.

Fact 3.8
.

Let
P\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
be a polynomial of degree
d
and rank
r
. Then the restriction of
P
to any affine subspace
V+h
is again a polynomial of degree
d
and rank
r
.
In other words, upper bounds on degree and rank are preserved under affine restrictions.

Definition 3.9

(Factors)
.

A
factor

{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
of degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
with
dimension vector

\vec{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
is a tuple of polynomials
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})
where
K=M_{1}+\dots+M_{d}
and for exactly
M_{\ell}
many
P_{i}
’s we have that
\deg(P_{i})=\ell
. The number
K
is called the
dimension
or
locality
of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
and is denoted by
\dim({\bm{\mathcal{F}}})
or
\lvert{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}\rvert
.

We equip
\mathbb{N}^{d}
with the
inverse lexicographic ordering
: for
\vec{N}=(N_{1},\dots,N_{d})
and
\vec{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
, we declare
\vec{N}<\vec{M}
if, at the largest index
j
for which
N_{j}\neq M_{j}
, one has
N_{j}<M_{j}
.

Definition 3.10

(Refinement)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})
be a factor. We say a factor
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}=(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{K^{\prime}})
is a
refinement
of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
(or that
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}

refines

{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
) if there exists some
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K}
such that

(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})=\Gamma(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{K^{\prime}}).

Definition 3.11

(Rank & regularity of a factor)
.

A factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{K})
of degree
d
is said to be
r
-regular
if for all nonzero linear combinations of
P_{i}
’s it holds that

\operatorname{rank}_{\ell-1}(\lambda_{1}P_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{K}P_{K})>r,

where
\ell=\max_{i}\deg(\lambda_{i}P_{i})
is the
degree of the linear combination
. Given a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, the factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
is
f
-regular
if it is
f(K)
-regular.

The
rank of the factor

{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
, denoted by
\operatorname{rank}({\bm{\mathcal{F}}})
, is the maximal number
r
for which
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
is
r
-regular.

Given a factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
that is free of linear dependencies, for
P\in\operatorname{span}({\bm{\mathcal{F}}})
we denote by
\deg_{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}(P)
the degree of the unique linear combination of polynomials in
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
that defines
P
. Note that
\deg(P)\leqslant\deg_{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}(P)
.

Sometimes, especially in
Section

5
, we will view a factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
as a set
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{K}\}
. Accordingly, all the relevant definitions (e.g., dimension vector, refinement) then transfer naturally.

A
growth function
is a function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
that is non-decreasing and satisfies
f(r)\geqslant r
for all
r
.

Definition 3.12

(A rapidly growing function)
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a growth function and let
d
be a positive integer. Now define the function
\psi_{d,f}\colon\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
recursively as follows. For the base case we have,

\psi_{d,f}(M_{1},0,\dots,0)=M_{1}.

Otherwise, if
M_{i}>0
for some
i\geqslant 2
, then let
\ell\geqslant 2
be the smallest of such indices. Then,

\psi_{d,f}(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})=\psi_{d,f}(M_{1},\dots,M_{\ell-1}+f(\textstyle\sum_{i}M_{i}),M_{\ell}-1,\dots,M_{d}).

Now set
\psi_{d,f}^{*}(K)=\psi_{d,f}(0,\dots,0,K)
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\psi=\psi_{d,f}
be defined as in
Definition

3.12
. Then,
\psi(N_{1},\dots,N_{d})\leqslant\psi(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
whenever
\sum_{i}N_{i}\leqslant\sum_{i}M_{i}
and
(N_{1},\dots,N_{d})\leqslant(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
.

The proof of this proposition is deferred to the
Appendix

B
as it is a rather tedious induction and is not a primary focus of ours. This function serves as an upper bound on some parameters in regularization processes that appear here.

Theorem 3.14

(Regularization
[
10
]
)
.

Every factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
of degree
d
admits an
f
-regular refinement
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}
with
\dim({\bm{\mathcal{G}}})\leqslant\psi_{d,f}^{*}(\dim({\bm{\mathcal{F}}}))
, where
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
is a growth function.

Proof.

The proof is by strong induction on the dimension vector
\vec{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
, showing that for every
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
there exists an
f
-regular refinement
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}
with
\dim({\bm{\mathcal{G}}})\leqslant\psi_{d,f}(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
. The bound
\dim({\bm{\mathcal{G}}})\leqslant\psi^{*}_{d,f}(\dim({\bm{\mathcal{F}}}))
follows immediately from
Proposition

3.13
.

As the base case, the empty factor is vacuously
f
-regular and there is nothing to do. Now suppose that
M_{i}>0
for some
i
, and that the claim holds for all smaller dimensions.

We shall further assume that
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
is free from linear dependencies, because otherwise we can discard some polynomials from
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
and the resulting factor is smaller and still refines
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
. If
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
is already
f
-regular, then we are done. Otherwise, identify some linear combination
R=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}P_{i}
such that
\operatorname{rank}_{\ell-1}(R)=r\leqslant f(K)
, where
\ell=\deg_{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}(R)
.

Then we replace a maximal degree
P_{i^{*}}
with
\lambda_{i^{*}}\neq 0
by
r
many polynomials of degree at most
\ell-1
to obtain a new factor
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{\prime}
. Doing so would change the dimension vector from
\vec{M}=(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
to
\vec{M}^{\prime}=(M^{\prime}_{1},\dots,M^{\prime}_{d})
such that

(M^{\prime}_{1},\dots,M^{\prime}_{d})<(M_{1},\dots,M_{\ell-1}+f(K),M_{\ell}-1,\dots,M_{d}).

We may now invoke the induction hypothesis on
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{\prime}
to obtain an
f
-regular refinement
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}
of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{\prime}
. The factor
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}
is also a refinement of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}
. Its dimension satisfies
\dim({\bm{\mathcal{G}}})\leqslant\psi_{d,f}(\vec{M}^{\prime})
by induction. By
Proposition

3.13
it follows that
\psi_{d,f}(\vec{M}^{\prime})\leqslant\psi(\vec{M})
.
∎

3.4
The structure theorems and the bias-rank tradeoffs

Consider the beautiful theorem of bias implies low rank
[
10
,
21
]
.

Theorem 3.15

(Structure of Biased Polynomials
[
21
]
)
.

There exists a function
c_{\text{KL}}(d,\delta)
such that for all polynomials
P
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
of degree at most
d
and
\operatorname{bias}(P)\geqslant\delta>0
it holds that
\operatorname{rank}_{d-1}(P)\leqslant c_{\text{KL}}(d,\delta)
.

Contrapositively, this theorem says that if
\operatorname{rank}_{d-1}(P)>c_{\text{KL}}(d,\delta)
, then
\operatorname{bias}(P)<\delta
. We will use this fact extensively. This theorem is commonly known as
bias implies low rank
and gives a tradeoff between bias and rank. It was first proved by Green and Tao in the case of
d\leqslant\lvert\mathbb{F}_{p}\rvert
, but was later extended to all small prime fields by Kaufman and Lovett
[
21
]
.

For polynomials of degree
1,2,3
, explicit and efficient tradeoffs are known.

Fact 3.16

(Structure of Degree
1
)
.

Given a degree-
1
polynomial
L
, if
\operatorname{bias}(L)>0
then
L
is constant. In other words,
c_{\text{KL}}(1,\delta)=0
for any positive
\delta
.

Theorem 3.17

(Dickson’s lemma: Structure of quadratic polynomials
[
22
, Theorem 6.30]
)
.

Given a quadratic polynomial
Q
there exists an even number
r
and
L_{1},\dots,L_{r+1}
linear
forms
where
Q
can be written as

Q=L_{1}L_{2}+\dots+L_{r-1}L_{r}+L_{r+1}+c,

where
c
is a field constant, and
L_{1},\dots,L_{r}
are linearly independent.

We have
\operatorname{rank}_{1}(Q)=r+1
if
L_{r+1}
is linearly independent from
L_{1},\dots,L_{r}
, and
\operatorname{rank}_{1}(Q)=r
otherwise. In the former case
\operatorname{bias}(Q)=0
and in the latter case
\operatorname{bias}(Q)=2^{-r/2}
. This implies that
c_{\text{KL}}(2,\delta)=2\log(1/\delta)
.

Theorem 3.18

(Structure of biased cubic polynomials (Haramaty–Shpilka
[
11
]
))
.

Let
C
be a cubic polynomial with
\operatorname{bias}(C)\geqslant\delta>0
. Then there exist linear functions

L_{1},\dots,L_{r+r^{\prime}}
, quadratic polynomials
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{r}
, and a cubic polynomial
\Gamma

(in
r^{\prime}
variables) such that

C=\sum_{i=1}^{r}L_{i}Q_{i}\;+\;\Gamma(L_{r+1},\dots,L_{r+r^{\prime}}),

where
r=O(\log(1/\delta))
and
r^{\prime}=O(\log^{4}(1/\delta))
. This implies that
c_{\text{KL}}(3,\delta)=O(\log^{4}(1/\delta))
.

For
d\geqslant 4
the dependence on
\delta
is very weak, so we leave it unspecified. We summarize this in the following table.

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&d=1&d=2&d=3&d\geqslant 4\\
\hline\cr c_{\text{KL}}(d,\delta)&0&2\log(1/\delta)&O(\log^{4}(1/\delta))&O_{d,\delta}(1)\\
\end{array}

3.5
Sunflowers for subspaces

Definition 3.19
.

A collection
S=\{V_{1},\dots,V_{s}\}
of (possibly repeated) subspaces in
\mathbb{F}^{m}
is said to be a sunflower if for
C=\bigcap_{j=1}^{s}V_{j}
it holds that
V_{i}\cap V_{i^{\prime}}=C
for all
i\neq i^{\prime}
.

The common intersection
C=\bigcap_{j=1}^{s}V_{j}
is called the
core
of the sunflower.

Lemma 3.20

(Sunflower Lemma for subspaces)
.

Fix a finite field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
and
m>k\geqslant 1
.
Let
S
be a collection of linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{m}
of dimension
k
(the collection might have repetitions).
If
\lvert S\rvert\geqslant s^{k+1}p^{\frac{k^{2}+k-2}{2}}
,
then
S
contains a sunflower of size at least
s
.

Remark 3.21
.

The proof is a straightforward adaptation of the standard proof of the lemma for set systems without a linear structure.

Note however that the bound on the size of
S
is better than a naive application of the sunflower lemma, as our sets are of size
p^{k}
, and so the naive bound would be double exponential in
k
.

Proof of
Lemma

3.20
.

The proof is by induction on
k
. For the base case of
k=1
we either have at least
s
repetitions of the same subspace or we have at least
\lvert S\rvert/s\geqslant s
different one dimensional subspaces, which all intersect only at the origin.

For the induction step, let
k\geqslant 2
.
Let
V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
be a maximal subset of
S
whose pairwise intersection is only
\{0\}
.
If
t\geqslant s
, we are done, as the sets form a sunflowers with the trivial core
C=\{0\}
.

Otherwise, consider the set
X=\cup_{i=1}^{t}V_{i}\setminus\{0\}
, and note that
\lvert X\rvert=t\cdot(p^{k}-1)<s\cdot p^{k}
.
By the maximality of the subspaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{t}
, it follows that
V\cap X\neq\emptyset
for all
V\in S
.
Therefore, there exists
x^{*}\in X\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lvert\{V\in S:x^{*}\in V\}\rvert\geqslant\frac{\lvert S\rvert}{\lvert X\rvert}
.

For all
V\in S
such that
x^{*}\in V
define
V_{-x^{*}}\subseteq V
to be any
k-1
dimensional subspace of
V
such that
x^{*}\notin V_{-x^{*}}
. There might be several such subspaces, and we define
V_{-x^{*}}
to be one of them arbitrarily.

Given such
x^{*}
, consider now the collection
S^{\prime}
of subspaces of dimension
k-1
:

S^{\prime}=\{V_{-x^{*}}:x^{*}\in V\text{ and }V\in S\}.

(Note that since
S
might contain the same subspace more than once, the same applies also to
S^{\prime}
.)

The collection
S^{\prime}
contains at least
\frac{\lvert S\rvert}{\lvert X\rvert}\geqslant\frac{s^{k+1}\cdot p^{\frac{k^{2}+k-2}{2}}}{s\cdot p^{k}}=s^{(k-1)+1}\cdot p^{\frac{(k-1)^{2}+(k-1)-2}{2}}
subspaces of dimension
k-1
. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis
S^{\prime}
contains a sunflower of size
s
, which induces a sunflower of size
s
in
S
.
∎

4
Chebyshev lemma

Lemma

3.4
talks about the distance between two product distributions. Indeed, our target
{\bm{\mathcal{B}}}_{1/3}^{\otimes n}
is a product distribution, but in general hoping for a product structure in polynomial distributions is too much. Therefore, we prove an analog of
Lemma

3.4
assuming that our polynomials are
pairwise almost independent
, where our notion of independence is captured by regularity.

Lemma 4.1

(Chebyshev lemma)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{P}}}=(P_{1},\dots,P_{n})
be an
n
-tuple of functions
P_{i}\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
\operatorname{rank}_{d}(P_{i})\leqslant r
, and suppose
\lVert P_{i}-\mathrm{Ber}(1/3)\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant\delta>0
for all

\displaystyle\E_{x}[(-1)^{P(x)}]

We start the technical part with the following easy proof of

Appendix A

In this section we prove

We will be using the standard concepts defined in

n
. Note that the dimension vector of
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}^{(n)}
is at most
(M_{1},\dots,M_{\ell-1}+r,M_{\ell}-1,M_{\ell+1},\dots,M_{d})
which is smaller than
(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
in our ordering of
\mathbb{N}^{d}
. By the induction hypothesis there exists some fully regular
{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}
that refines
{\bm{\mathcal{G}}}^{(n)}
. Finally, by transitivity of refinement,
{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}
also refines
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}=(P^{(n)}_{1},\dots,P^{(n)}_{K})
be a fully regular sequence of factors of degree
d
. Then the joint distribution of polynomials
(P^{(n)}_{1},\dots,P^{(n)}_{K})
converges to the uniform distribution
U_{K}
.

Proof.

We show that for every positive
\varepsilon
there exists some
N_{0}
such that for all
n\geqslant N_{0}
we have
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}-U_{K}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leqslant\varepsilon
, which implies our claim.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Then define
r=c_{\text{KL}}(d,\varepsilon 2^{-K/2})
, where
c_{\text{KL}}
comes from
Theorem

3.15
. Since
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}
is fully regular there exists some
N_{0}
such that
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}
is
r
-regular for all
n\geqslant N_{0}
. Then by
Theorem

3.15

\operatorname{bias}(\lambda_{1}P^{(n)}_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{K}P^{(n)}_{K})\leqslant\varepsilon 2^{-K/2},\qquad\text{for all }n\geqslant N_{0}\text{ and }\lambda_{i}\text{ not all zeros.}

Thus, by Vazirani’s XOR lemma, we have that
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}-U_{K}\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leqslant\varepsilon
for all
n\geqslant N_{0}
.
∎

Definition A.5

(Computable distribution)
.

We say a distribution
D
over
\{0,1\}^{s}
is
computable
if there exists some
K\in\mathbb{N}
and function
\Gamma\colon\{0,1\}^{K}\to\{0,1\}^{s}
such that
D=\Gamma(U_{K})
.

It is an easy observation that a distribution
D
is computable if and only if the probability of every outcome is a dyadic number.

Lemma A.6
.

Let
P^{(n)}
be a sequence of polynomials of degree
d
that converges to the distribution
D
in
TV
-distance. Then, there exists a positive integer
K
and a function
\Gamma\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{K}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
such that
D=\Gamma(U_{K})
. In other words, the limit distribution
D
is computable.

Proof.

Let us describe
P^{(n)}
by the trivial sequence of factors that computes it; that is to say
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}=(P^{(n)})
with the dimension vector
(0,\dots,0,1)\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

By
Lemma

A.3
there exists
K
and a fully regular degree
d
sequence
{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}=(Q_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Q_{K}^{(n)})
of dimension
K
that is a refinement of
{\bm{\mathcal{F}}}^{(n)}
. That is, for a subsequence
P^{\prime(n)}
of
P^{(n)}
there exists some
\Gamma
such that for all
n
,
P^{\prime(n)}=\Gamma(Q_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Q_{K}^{(n)})
.

Since
{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}^{(n)}
is fully regular, by
Lemma

A.4
the joint distribution
(Q_{1}^{(n)},\dots,Q_{K}^{(n)})
converges to the uniform distribution, and therefore,
P^{\prime(n)}=\Gamma(Q^{(n)}_{1},\dots,Q_{K}^{(n)})
converges to
\Gamma(U_{K})
.
∎

Remark A.7
.

In fact,
Lemma

A.6
extends with essentially no additional effort to the setting of joint distributions of low-degree polynomials. We have presented it in the present form purely for the sake of slightly cleaner notation.

Theorem A.8
.

Let
0<\rho<1
be a
non-dyadic
number, and let
d
be a positive integer. Then for any degree
d
polynomial
P\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
it holds that
\lVert P-\mathrm{Ber}(\rho)\rVert_{\text{TV}}\geqslant\Omega_{d,\rho}(1)
.

Proof.

Toward contradiction, suppose this were not the case. That is, suppose there exists a sequence
P^{(n)}
of polynomials of degree
d
such that

\lVert P^{(n)}-\mathrm{Ber}(\rho)\rVert_{\text{TV}}\leqslant o_{d,\rho}(1)\qquad\text{ for }n\to\infty.

Then this sequence converges to
\mathrm{Ber}(\rho)
in total variation distance. On the other hand, by
Lemma

A.6
there exist
K
and
\Gamma
such that the limit of
P^{(n)}
is the distribution
\Gamma(U_{K})
. This is a contradiction as
\rho
is non-dyadic.
∎

Appendix B
Deferred proofs

Proof of Vazirani’s XOR lemma (
Lemma

3.2
).

Let
\mu,u\colon\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to[0,1]
be the probability mass functions of
{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}
and
{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}
respectively. We will bound
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}\rVert_{2}
from above. We have

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}(\mu(x)-u(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=2^{n}\E_{x}\big[(\mu(x)-u(x))^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=2^{n}\E\big[\mu(x)^{2}\big]+2^{n}\E\big[u(x)^{2}\big]-2^{n+1}\E\big[\mu(x)u(x)\big]

\displaystyle=2^{n}\sum_{\gamma}\widehat{\mu}(\gamma)^{2}+2^{-n}-2\widehat{\mu}(0)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\sum_{\gamma\neq 0}\widehat{\mu}(\gamma)^{2}.

(because
\widehat{\mu}(0)=2^{-n}
)

Consider some non-trivial character
\chi_{\gamma}=(-1)^{L}
where
L
is some nonzero linear form. Then,
\lvert\widehat{\mu}(\gamma)\rvert=\lvert\E_{x}[\chi_{\gamma}(x)\mu(x)]\rvert=\lvert 2^{-n}\operatorname{bias}(L(X))\rvert\leqslant\varepsilon\cdot 2^{-n}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{2n-1}(2^{-2n}\varepsilon^{2})\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}.

Then
\lVert{\bm{\mathcal{X}}}-{\bm{\mathcal{U}}}^{\otimes n}\rVert_{1}\leqslant 2^{n/2}\varepsilon
is immediate by Cauchy-Schwarz.
∎

Proof of
Proposition

3.13
.

We prove this claim by induction on
\vec{M}\coloneq(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
, where the tuples in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
are ordered by inverse lexicographic ordering. Our base case covers all cases where
N_{2}=\dots=N_{d}=0
. We have,

\psi(N_{1},0\dots,0)=N_{1}\leqslant\sum_{i}M_{i}\leqslant\psi(M_{1},\dots,M_{d}).

From now on we assume that
N_{i}>0
for some
i\geqslant 2
. If
\vec{N}=\vec{M}
, then the statement clearly holds. Thus we further assume that
\vec{N}<\vec{M}
.

Let
j
be the right-most index such that
M_{j}>N_{j}
(i.e.
j
is the index that shows
\vec{M}
is greater than
\vec{N}
in inverse lexicographic order). In particular, the entry
M_{j}
is nonzero.

Define
\ell\geqslant 2
(resp.
k\geqslant 2
) to be the first index of
\vec{M}
(resp.
\vec{N}
) that is nonzero. Note that
\ell\leqslant j
.

Now by definition of
\psi
, we have
\psi(\vec{M})=\psi(\vec{M}^{\prime})
and
\psi(\vec{N})=\psi(\vec{N}^{\prime})
where,

\displaystyle\vec{M}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(M_{1},\dots,M_{\ell-1}+f(\textstyle\sum_{i}M_{i}),M_{\ell}-1,\dots,M_{d})

\displaystyle\vec{N}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(N_{1},\dots,N_{k-1}+f(\textstyle\sum_{i}N_{i}),N_{k}-1,\dots,N_{d})

Clearly,
\sum_{i}\vec{N}_{i}^{\prime}\leqslant\sum_{i}\vec{M}_{i}^{\prime}
still holds because of monotonicity of
f
. To show that
\vec{N}^{\prime}<\vec{M}^{\prime}
, we consider three cases.

•

(
k\geqslant j
) If
k\geqslant j
then
N^{\prime}_{k}<M^{\prime}_{k}
and therefore
\vec{N}^{\prime}<\vec{M}^{\prime}
.

•

(
k<j
and
\ell<j
) Then
N^{\prime}_{j}=N_{j}<M_{j}=M^{\prime}_{j}
and
\vec{N}^{\prime}<\vec{M}^{\prime}
.

•

(
k<j
and
\ell=j
) Then
M^{\prime}_{j}\geqslant N^{\prime}_{j}
, and if this inequality is strict, then we are done. Otherwise,
M^{\prime}_{j-1}\geqslant f(\sum_{i}M_{i})\geqslant\sum_{i}M_{i}>N^{\prime}_{j-1}
.

The three cases show that
\vec{M}^{\prime}>\vec{N}^{\prime}
and the claim follows by the induction hypothesis.
∎
