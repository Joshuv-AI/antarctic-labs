Site-specific oral microbiome profiles in healthy young Korean women
Authors: Mu-Yeol Cho, Je-Hyun Eom, Ji-Won Kim, Yun-Woo Kim, Jiyoung Hwang, Dahye Lee, Young Youn Kim, Hye-Sung Kim, Inseong Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We characterized site-specific organization of the oral microbiome in 59 healthy, non-smoking women in their twenties using 16S rRNA (V3–V4) sequencing of dental plaque (DQ), mouthwash (MW), and tongue coating (TC) samples (177 libraries; rarefied to 29,853 reads). The order of alpha diversity was DQ > MW > TC (p < 0.001), with mean Observed Features of 227.6, 208.7, and 142.4, respectively, consistent with differences in local niche structure within the oral cavity. Beta diversity (Bray–Curtis; weighted/unweighted UniFrac) showed clear separation by site (PERMANOVA overall p < 0.001), with DQ most distinct from MW and TC. At the phylum level, DQ was enriched for Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria, whereas Firmicutes predominated in MW and TC, indicating compositional shifts across oral microenvironments. Dominant genera included Streptococcus (20.6%), Neisseria (11.7%), Haemophilus (10.1%), Prevotella (7.1%), Veillonella (6.0%), Rothia (5.1%), and Lactobacillus (4.4%). Site-associated taxa included Cardiobacterium, Corynebacterium, and Campylobacter in DQ; Actinobacillus in MW; and Absconditabacteriales (SR1) and Lactobacillus in TC. TC exhibited the lowest alpha diversity but the highest genus richness (n = 340), indicating an uneven community with many low-abundance taxa. LEfSe identified discriminant taxa, including Rothia, Actinomyces, Fusobacterium (DQ), Streptococcus, and Gemella (MW); and Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Veillonella, and Haemophilus (TC). These results demonstrate pronounced site specificity within the oral cavity of a well-defined healthy cohort and indicate that multi-site resolution is essential for advancing mechanistic and translational oral microbiome research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available athttps://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60927-7.

Introduction

The human oral cavity harbors a highly diverse and complex microbial ecosystem, comprising bacteria, archaea, fungi, and viruses that collectively influence both oral and systemic health1,2. Over 700 prokaryotic species have been identified in this niche, forming structured communities across diverse microenvironments, including dental surfaces, buccal mucosa, saliva, and the tongue dorsum3,4. These microbiomes exhibit unique compositional and functional profiles shaped by local physicochemical conditions, host immune interactions, and microbial metabolic exchanges2,5. Recent advances in high-throughput DNA sequencing have enabled detailed characterization of these microbial communities, revealing their critical roles in maintaining homeostasis and driving pathologies such as dental caries, periodontitis, and even systemic diseases2,6–9.

The oral microbiome’s biogeography is a cornerstone of its ecological complexity. Dental plaque (DQ) forms architecturally complex biofilms on tooth surfaces, characterized by stratified microbial communities embedded within an extracellular matrix composed of polysaccharides and proteins10. Plaque microbiome composition is influenced by the anatomy and physiology of the tooth surfaces and the perigingival area11 as well as dentition states12. Early cultivation studies identified Streptococcus and Actinomyces as the initial, or “pioneer”, colonizers. Modern sequencing techniques have revealed that mature subgingival plaques are dominated by anaerobic genera such as Fusobacterium, Tannerella, and Porphyromonas9,11. Within these communities, a sophisticated structural organization enhances antimicrobial resistance, with Gram-negative pathogens forming synergistic consortia13. For instance, P. gingivalis relies on Fusobacterium nucleatum for initial attachment, while Treponema denticola co-aggregates with both to form pathogenic triads10. In subgingival plaque associated with periodontitis, there is a shift toward proteolytic metabolism, where virulent strains of P. gingivalis degrade host proteins while suppressing local immune responses14–16. Conversely, in caries-prone supragingival regions, acidogenic Streptococcus mutans metabolizes dietary sugars to produce lactic acid, leading to enamel demineralization17.

In contrast, the tongue dorsum presents a distinct microbial habitat compared to DQ18. The tongue’s papillary and cryptic topography, along with its constant exposure to saliva and air, creates a unique environment that supports a structurally complex and diverse community, including both aerobic and anaerobic bacteria19. Dominant genera in tongue coating (TC) samples often include Prevotella, Neisseria, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Rothia, and Fusobacterium with a higher proportion of facultative anaerobes compared to plaque20–22. The tongue microbiome plays a significant role in halitosis23 and nitric oxide metabolism24,25 and may be linked to various systemic conditions—including gastrointestinal disorders, liver cirrhosis, and type 2 diabetes26.

Mouthwash (MW) or oral rinse samples offer a readily accessible, minimally invasive method for assessing the overall microbial composition of the oral cavity, although they do not directly reflect the microbial composition of a specific oral surface18,27,28. The microbial profile of MW samples typically reflects a mixture of communities from saliva, buccal mucosa, and loosely attached bacteria from other oral surfaces, biased towards planktonic bacteria and those easily detached from oral surfaces27,29,30. Numerous commercial mouth-rinsing solutions designed for microbiota analysis have yielded results that are either comparable27 or even superior29 to those obtained from saliva samples, depending on the study design. Notably, simple saline MW collection has been shown to be equally effective as other commercial methods in preserving microbial diversity and taxonomic abundances29,31. Studies using MW samples have been valuable in large-scale epidemiological studies and for monitoring changes in the oral microbiome over time or in response to interventions, owing to the higher compliance and accessibility of the sampling procedure27.

While numerous studies have compared oral microbiomes between health and disease states, detailed site-specific microbial profiles in demographically homogeneous healthy young adults remain less defined. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare the microbiomes of DQ, MW, and TC samples obtained from healthy, non-smoking women in their twenties. Using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, the microbial composition across these sample types was compared. By focusing on this homogeneous demographic, we aimed to minimize confounding variables (e.g., age, smoking, periodontal status, and systemic health) and establish a refined reference profile for future comparative studies. Furthermore, identifying stable “core” taxa in this healthy cohort may inform the development of probiotics or microbiome-based diagnostics.

Results

A total of 100 sets of oral samples (DQ, MW, and TC) collected from 100 subjects were provided by the Korea Oral Biobank Network (KOBN), operated by the National Institute of Health (NIH) of the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KCDC)32. After screening, 41 subjects were excluded: 32 were over 30 years old, 5 were male, 1 was a female smoker, and 3 had poor DNA quality. The final dataset included oral samples from 59 non-smoking females in their twenties (Fig. 1).

After processing, 10,133,361 sequences were clustered into 5,885 ASVs. Each sample averaged 57,251 sequences (median: 53,076; range: 30,045–106,262). The feature table was rarefied to a depth of 29,853, retaining all 177 samples with 5646 features and 5,283,981 sequences. Observed feature counts at this depth were 227.6 for DQ, 208.7 for MW, and 142.4 for TC.

Microbial diversity across sites

Alpha diversity was assessed using multiple metrics, including Shannon, Chao1, Simpson, Observed Features, Faith’s Phylogenetic Diversity (Faith PD), and Pielou’s Evenness. Statistical significance was evaluated with the Friedman test for repeated measures and the Kruskal–Wallis test for method comparison. DQ samples had the highest diversity according to Shannon and Simpson indices, while TC samples had the lowest diversity across several indices (p < 0.001; Fig. 2a). In general, alpha diversity followed the order: DQ > MW > TC, regardless of the statistical method used.

Beta diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, Weighted UniFrac, and Unweighted UniFrac. PCoA plots revealed divergent microbial compositions at DQ, MW, and TC sites (PERMANOVA: overall, p < 0.001; between groups, p < 0.001 except MW vs. TC for Weighted UniFrac at p = 0.0069) (Fig. 2b). DQ samples were most distinct with p < 0.001 when compared to MW and TC.

Taxonomic abundance and community profiling across sites

We analyzed the relative and actual abundances after taxonomic assignment. Actinobacteriota were predominant in DQ samples compared to MW and TC, both in relative (Fig. 3a and b) and actual (Fig. S1) abundances. Statistically significant genera were noted in the legend (Fig. 3b). The seven most abundant phyla included Firmicutes (36.3%), Proteobacteria (27.8%), Bacteriodota (15.9%), Actinobacteriota (11.5%), Fusobacteriota (5.6%), Patescibacteria (1.7%), and Campilobacterota (0.9%), comprising 99.8% of total phyla (Fig. 3c, Table S1).

In DQ samples, Proteobacteria (28.0%), Actinobacteriota (22.3%), Firmicutes (17.4%), Bacteriodota (15.5%), Fusobacteriota (11.6%), Patescibacteria (2.5%), and Campilobacterota (2.1%) accounted for 99.5% of total phyla (Fig. 3d). In MW and TC samples, the order of the seven most abundant phyla comprising 99.9% of total phyla was identical: Firmicutes (47.2%), Proteobacteria (28.2%), Bacteriodota (13.5%), Actinobacteriota (7.7%), Fusobacteriota (2.2%), Patescibacteria (0.8%), and Campilobacterota (0.3%) for MW samples (Fig. 3e), and Firmicutes (44.2%), Proteobacteria (27.2%), Bacteriodota (18.9%), Actinobacteriota (4.6%), Fusobacteriota (2.9%), Patescibacteria (1.8%), and Campilobacterota (0.3%) for TC samples (Fig. 3f).

Core microbiome analysis showed site-specific distribution of genera, both across all groups (Fig. 3g) and within each group (Fig. 3h). The top seven genera of total samples were Streptococcus (20.6%), Neisseria (11.7%), Haemophilus (10.1%), Prevotella (7.1%), Veillonella (6.0%), Rothia (5.1%), and Lactobacillus (4.4%) (Fig. 3g). Some genera appeared exclusively in specific samples: Cardiobacterium, Corynebacterium, Campylobacter, and others in DQ samples; Actinobacillus only in MW samples; Absconditabacteriales (SR1) and Lactobacillus exclusively in TC samples. Additionally, Aggregatibacter was detected only in DQ and MW, whereas TM7x was listed only in MW.

The taxonomic data and the shared genera among the sample groups were analyzed using an upset plot (Fig. 3i). The total number of genera observed was 174, 229, and 340 for DQ, MW, and TC, respectively, with site-specific genera numbering 25, 57, and 166 for DQ, MW, and TC, respectively. The TC samples had the lowest α-diversity and the highest number of taxa, suggesting low evenness and the dominance of specific genera, as shown in Fig. 2.

Figure 4 depicts a heatmap illustrating the log-transformed relative abundance profiles of microbial genera across individual samples. Hierarchical clustering of the samples reveals distinct microbial signatures. Genera such as Rothia, Fusobacterium, Actinomyces, Streptococcus, Neisseria, and Haemophilus demonstrate relatively high abundance across various sample sites. Notably, DQ samples display a distinctive distribution pattern compared to MW and TC samples. The highly clustered Lactobacillus in TC samples is notable for its clear abundance contrast. Consistent with this site-specific structure, unsupervised bi-clustering resolved the samples into two major clades: an upper clade composed almost exclusively of DQ samples, and a lower clade in which MW and TC samples were interleaved rather than separated, reflecting a shared saliva- and mucosa-mediated community. Reciprocally, the genus dendrogram partitioned taxa into two anti-correlated co-occurrence blocks—a mucosa- and saliva-associated block (Streptococcus, Neisseria, Haemophilus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Porphyromonas, Alloprevotella, Granulicatella, and Gemella) enriched in the MW + TC clade and depleted in DQ, and a plaque-associated block (Actinobacillus, TM7x/Saccharibacteria, Saccharimonadales, Aggregatibacter, Lautropia, Corynebacterium, Capnocytophaga, Leptotrichia, Campylobacter, Kingella, and Selenomonas) showing the reciprocal pattern. Overall, the distinct clustering of microbial communities underscores the unique ecological niches within the oral cavity.

Differential analyses

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) plot illustrates microbial genera that are significantly associated with distinct oral niches (Fig. 5a, Fig. S2). Specifically, genera such as Capnocytophaga, Corynebacterium, Lautropia, Leptotrichia, Rothia, Actinomyces, Fusobacterium, Cardiobacterium, Selenomonas, Campylobacter, Aggregatibacter, Kingella, Saccharimonadaceae, Bergeyella, Comnamonas, Bacillus, Eikenella, and Pseudopropionibacterium were found to be enriched in DQ samples. In contrast, Streptococcus, Gemella, and Actinobacillus exhibited the highest enrichment in MW samples, while Lactobacillus, Neisseria, Prevotella, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, Alloprevotella, Granuicatella, and TM7x were notably abundant in TC samples. The LDA score signifies the effect size and statistical significance of each genus in distinguishing microbiomes associated with various oral sites. Cladogram analysis also showed a similar site-specific phylogenetic distribution in order and family levels (Fig. 5b).

The phylogenetic trees were produced using MEGA12 and QIIME2 View (Fig. S3). The overall microbiome samples exhibited the highest number of genera (Fig. S3a). In contrast, DQ samples had the lowest genus count and displayed a distinct phylum distribution pattern in circular cladograms (Fig. S3b), with prominent Actinobacteriota and Proteobacteria representation, compared to the Firmicutes-dominant patterns observed in MW and TC samples (Fig. S3f, h).

Discussion

Baseline oral microbiome profiles in healthy young women provide an essential reference against which the effects of hormonal cycling, lifestyle change and incipient disease can be assessed. Here we characterize DQ, MW, and TC samples from 59 non-smoking women in their twenties and resolve pronounced site specificity across alpha diversity, beta diversity and taxonomic composition. These distinctions emerge despite tight demographic homogeneity of the cohort, reinforcing the view of the oral cavity not as a single microbial habitat but as a mosaic of anatomically and physicochemically discrete niches2,3,33.

Prior niche-resolved surveys of the healthy oral microbiome have been limited to cohorts of ten or fewer subjects18,20,34. Ren et al. profiled supragingival DQ, stimulated saliva, and TC in ten preschool children and reported the highest alpha diversity, richness, and evenness in plaque and the lowest in tongue coating34—a rank order that our adult data recapitulate despite the generational gap. Jo et al. likewise identified TC as the least diverse of saliva, MW, and TC18. Hall et al., by contrast, sequencing the same V3–V4 region in ten healthy young adults (six men, four women), placed DQ at the bottom of the Faith PD ranking20. That our 56-subject, sex-matched Asian female cohort inverts the Hall et al. ordering while reproducing the pattern seen in children indicates that the rank of DQ diversity is sensitive to cohort composition rather than to sequencing strategy alone (Table S2). At the same time, our DQ results refine rather than merely reproduce the prior literature. Hall et al. found clear site separation, but their longitudinal data also highlighted strong similarity between tongue and salivary communities and did not always place plaque in exactly the same position across all diversity measures20. Our findings, therefore, suggest that differences among healthy studies in the rank order of diversity metrics should not be interpreted as a contradiction at the ecological level. Rather, they likely reflect differences in age, oral health definition, plaque maturity, sampling granularity, sequencing strategy, and site definition.

The taxonomic architecture of our dataset aligns closely with the canonical healthy oral core. Streptococcus, Neisseria, Haemophilus, and Rothia dominated every site, echoing foundational surveys3,18,20,33–35, and the Human Oral Microbiome Database (HOMD) oligotyping has established that these shared genera typically partition into site-preferential oligotypes or species when examined at higher resolution35. Two departures from the HOMD reference set are worth noting: we recovered Lautropia and Gemella, neither of which features prominently in HOMD core lists, and failed to detect Schaalia and Segatella. A more striking observation was the enrichment of Lactobacillus on the TC. Because sampling was confined to a pre-lunch window but dietary intake was not standardized, this signal is not attributable to a controlled exposure; nevertheless, Lactobacillus appeared almost exclusively on TC. The Expanded HOMD-linked healthy HMP V1–V3 dataset (https://www.homd.org/taxa/ecology?rank=genus&name=Lactobacillus) shows a similar TC-over-DQ bias, although the pattern is not reproduced in the V3–V5 release, and no sex-stratified data are available. Within the constraints of our cohort, these observations support a tongue-preferential niche for Lactobacillus among the healthy oral sites sampled.

MW is where our study makes one of its most translationally relevant contributions. Classical oral microbiome mapping studies have predominantly focused on plaque, saliva, tongue, mucosa, and gingival sites3,12,20,34,35, whereas our design positions mouthwash-derived material within a niche-comparison framework. In our data, MW was intermediate between DQ and TC in diversity, clustered more closely with non-surface-associated oral communities than with DQ, and was characterized by Firmicutes-rich communities with enrichment for Streptococcus, Actinobacillus, and Gemella. This interpretation is strongly supported by the literature on rinse-based and saliva-like sampling. Jo et al. found that MW closely resembled stimulated and unstimulated saliva and had significantly higher diversity than TC18. Fan et al. similarly showed that MW and whole saliva samples had highly comparable alpha diversity and taxonomic composition27. Long et al. reported that buccal-cell MW samples resembled saliva but were clearly distinct from DQ and TC36. Yano et al. and Bang et al. further demonstrated that the collection method can materially influence microbiome profiles, but that mouthwash-based methods remain valid and practical when used consistently29,30. Our findings extend these studies by showing that MW is not only a technical saliva surrogate, but also an ecologically interpretable composite sample that captures circulating oral microbial structure while blunting the sharper habitat specificity seen in DQ and TC.

Enrichment of Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Neisseria in MW highlights a methodological advantage of MW sampling: it mobilizes and concentrates the populations that saliva has already ecologically selected. Saliva’s slightly basic, weakly buffered, continuously flowing matrix—rich in mucin- and proline-rich glycoproteins, amino acids, urea, lactate, and dietary nitrate—selectively sustains specific oral commensals37. A rinse exploits this by (i) mechanically detaching loosely adherent streptococci and Prevotella that whole saliva captures less efficiently38; (ii) preferentially recovering the Neisseria aerobic-to-microaerophilic, nitrate-dependent guild39; and (iii) bypassing the expectoration-rate, viscosity, and circadian-flow biases of saliva collection, standardizing volume and contact time for large-cohort use. MW is therefore not a saliva surrogate but a sampling strategy that harnesses saliva’s selectivity as an in-mouth enrichment medium.

TC produced the most conceptually informative signal in our dataset. It harboured the greatest total genus count and the largest set of site-specific genera, yet registered the lowest alpha diversity—an apparent paradox that dissolves once evenness is considered alongside richness. The TC community is taxonomically broad but steeply uneven, dominated by a restricted set of high-abundance taxa. Enrichment of Lactobacillus, Prevotella, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Porphyromonas, Alloprevotella, and TM7x in TC, together with the tight lactobacilli cluster in the heatmap, supports this broad-but-skewed architecture. The pattern coheres with Wilbert et al., who resolved the tongue dorsum as a spatially organized epithelial biofilm with micron-scale taxonomic segregation and strong local ecological structure19, and with Hall et al., who found tongue and salivary communities more similar to each other than either is to dental communities, yet ecologically distinct20. TC is therefore not a saliva-adjacent specimen but a specialized papillary reservoir with its own dominance regime—a distinction with direct consequences for studies of halitosis, epithelial biofilms, nitrate metabolism and local host–microbe interaction2,19.

Our study has several limitations that bound its interpretation. First, 16S V3–V4 amplicon sequencing almost certainly understates the true extent of site specialization: shared genera such as Streptococcus, Haemophilus, Prevotella, Veillonella, and Rothia likely encompass species- or strain-level complexes with narrower habitat preferences than our resolution permits35,40. Our dataset therefore constitutes a robust genus-level ecological map but remains conservative relative to what species-resolved metagenomics, oligotyping, metatranscriptomics or spatial imaging would reveal. Second, although participants were systemically healthy young non-smokers, their periodontal status corresponded to Stage I periodontitis, so the cohort deviates from a strictly pristine periodontal-health baseline. Third, the narrow demographic window sharpens internal validity but constrains extrapolation to men, smokers, older adults, adolescents and ethnically or geographically diverse populations. Fourth, the cross-sectional design forgoes the temporal dimension that Hall et al. identified as a defining feature of the healthy oral microbiome20. Finally, we did not collect conventional saliva; our framing of MW as a saliva-adjacent composite niche therefore rests partly on triangulation with rinse-based studies in the literature rather than on direct within-study comparison18,27.

Overall, this study defines site-specific baseline profiles of the oral microbiome in a demographically homogeneous cohort of healthy young non-smoking women and establishes the local microenvironment as a primary determinant of community structure. Our data confirm that healthy oral communities are strongly site-structured, with a shared taxonomic core layered over pronounced habitat specialization, and resolve each niche with distinct ecological character: MW functions as an intermediate, saliva-like integrative sample; DQ represents the most architecturally specialized biofilm compartment; and TC emerges as a taxonomically broad but low-evenness papillary reservoir. Together, these profiles provide a refined reference framework for investigations of oral dysbiosis, biomarker discovery, longitudinal dynamics, and site-aware diagnostic design.

Materials and methods

Participant demographics and sample collection

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Review Board of Apple Tree Dental Hospital (approval number: ATDH-2021-0001) and the Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy (approval number: P01-202111-31-002). This study was registered in https://trialsearch.who.int (KCT0011333). The DQ, MW, and TC biospecimens, collected from 100 healthy adults (15 males, 85 females; mean age 28.23 years; range 21–51 years), were distributed by the Biobank of Apple Tree Dental Hospital, a part of the KOBN operated by the NIH-KCDC. All samples were obtained with informed consent from donors who had agreed to the collection and use of their biospecimens for research purposes at the time of donation to the biobank, and all biospecimens were collected within a single one-month window to minimize temporal variability. After eligibility screening, 177 samples remained (59 samples each of DQ, MW, and TC) with 41 subjects excluded due to age > 29 years (n = 32), male gender (n = 5), smoking status (n = 1), and quality control failure (n = 3). Demographic information contained periodontal conditions assessed at six sites (mesiobuccal, midbuccal, distobuccal, mesiolingual, midlingual, and distolingual) on Ramfjord teeth (16, 21, 24, 36, 41, and 44)41. The average periodontal pocket depth (PD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) were 1.58 ± 0.22 and 1.60 ± 0.23, respectively, rendering Stage I periodontitis42. Three subjects reported chronic disease, and one subject had metabolic syndrome (Table 1).

For DQ collection, target teeth were isolated with cotton rolls and air-dried using a three-way syringe. DQ was scraped out using sterilized 11/12 and 13/14 Gracey currettes (Hu-Friedy Mfg. Co., LLC, Chicago, IL) from all six sites per Ramfjord teeth, dissolved in 5 mL of PBS contained in a 50 mL polycarbonate tube chilled on ice, mixed by vortexing, aliquoted into cryovials containing glycerol (23% final), labeled, and stored at -80 °C. Next, TC was collected by gently scraping the mid-dorsum of the tongue ten times using sterilized wooden depressors, followed by the same storage procedure as the plaque samples. Lastly, MW samples were collected from participants who were provided with 10 mL of saline solution in a 50 mL tube. Participants vigorously swished the solution for 30 s while maintaining occlusion without tilting their heads backward. They then expectorated the solution into the same tube. The samples were centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 1 min using a swing-bucket rotor (Labogene 1248R) at room temperature. The supernatant was transferred to a 15 mL tube and centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature using the rotor. The supernatant was resuspended in 3 mL PBS by gentle pipetting, aliquoted into 1 mL cryovials containing 0.4 mL 80% glycerol (23% final), labeled, and stored at -80 °C. Throughout the procedure, the samples were stored at 4 °C immediately after the collection and processed within 24 h. Quality control of the biospecimens was ensured through standard operating procedures established by the KOBN32.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using LaboPass™ Bacteria Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (COSMOgenetech, South Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and quantity of purified whole gDNA were assessed using fluorometry (Qubit, Invitrogen). Genomic DNA (5 ng) was used as input for 16S rRNA gene library preparation, following the Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library protocols to amplify the V3–V4 region. The first PCR (25 µL) was performed using 5× reaction buffer, 1 mM dNTP mix, 500 nM of each universal forward and reverse primer, and Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The thermal cycling program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 25 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The universal primer pair used in the first PCR contained Illumina Nextera XT adapter overhangs (Forward: 5′-TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGCCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG-3′, Reverse: 5′-GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). After the first PCR, the amplicons were purified using AMPure beads (Agencourt Bioscience, Beverly, MA, USA). For final library construction, 10 µL of the purified first PCR product was used as template in a second PCR to add sample-specific indices (Nextera XT Index Primers). The second PCR used the same thermal cycling parameters as the first, but was run for 10 cycles instead of 25. The resulting indexed libraries were again purified with AMPure beads. Final purified libraries were quantified by qPCR using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina Sequencing Platforms (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library size distribution and quality were verified using the TapeStation D1000 ScreenTape system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbronn, Germany). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq™ platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted using QIIME 2 (amplicon 2024.10)43. After primer trimming with the Cutadapt plugin, the DADA2 plugin was used for denoising, paired-end merging, and chimera removal, resulting in an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table and representative sequences. To mitigate artificial bias, the ASVs table was rarefied prior to analysis. Taxonomic classification was performed using a customized naïve Bayes classifier trained with scikit-learn. This classifier was based on the SILVA (v138.2) full-length sequences and taxonomy classifier44, trimmed specifically using V3-V4 primer sequences.

Alpha and beta diversities were obtained using the QIIME diversity plugin. Alpha diversity indices from repeated samples of the same subject were analyzed using a non-parametric Friedman test followed by a pair-wise Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Beta diversity indices were visualized using PCoA and the statistical significance was determined by PERMANOVA (adonis2, 9999 permutations, Euclidean distance, strata = group) with subsequent Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

Microbial composition analysis and visualization were performed using phyloseq (1.50.0), vegan (2.6.10), compositions (2.0.8), ggplot2 (3.5.1), and StackbarExtended45 packages in R (4.4.2; Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Relative abundance data at the genus level were imported and processed into a phyloseq object, integrating taxonomy and sample metadata. For statistical comparison at the genus level, the non-parametric Friedman test was employed to account for the repeated-measures design across three experimental groups (DQ, MW, TC), followed by Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction.

For the hierarchical clustering heatmap of bacterial genera of oral samples, the feature table was filtered by abundance at least 1% in at least 1% of the samples before collapsing at the genus level with taxonomy data. The collapsed table was transformed by centered log-ratio by adding a pseudo-count of 0.5 before heatmap production using pandas (2.2.2), NumPy (1.26.4), seaborn (0.13.2), and matplotlib (3.8.4) libraries in Python (3.10.14). The plot contains the top 30 genera with at least 1% abundance.

For LEfSe analysis, the input table was generated using the QIIME 2 dokdo plugin. Publicly available Python codes were used for LEfSe and cladogram plot46 with minor modifications.

Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the QIIME 2 empress plugin and MEGA1247. To enhance the visualization, the feature table was filtered by abundance of at least 0.5% in at least 0.5% of the samples. Representative sequences and taxonomy data were exported from QIIME 2 artifacts into FASTA format. To annotate bacterial taxonomy on the phylogenetic tree, FASTA headers were modified by replacing the original sequence identifiers with corresponding taxonomy assignments. Taxonomy labels were obtained from the taxonomy artifact file and integrated into FASTA headers using a custom Python script. The final output was a taxonomy-labeled FASTA file used in subsequent phylogenetic analyses in MEGA12. Unless otherwise noted, the statistical significance was annotated as p < 0.05, *; p < 0.01, **; p < 0.001, ***.
