ABSTRACT
Assessing structural integrity of large underground tunnel facilities is often a time consuming and
human-labor intensive task. Thus, research using various modes of sensing and automated detection
of key structural components in mines is posed to aid in safety assessments and establishing overall
structural health. We propose an approach utilizing off-the-shelf camera and lidar technology fixed
to a custom sensing platform, image stitching techniques, fine-tuned object detection models, and
specialized model-inference methods to automatically detect, count, and map roof bolts for
assessment of structural safety in man-made underground tunnels. Results show a novel workflow
for effective object counting in orthomosaic tunnel ceiling images generated from collections in
GPS-denied mining environments. Additionally, we demonstrate effective fine-tuning of
EfficientDet object detectors utilizing state-of-the-art image augmentation techniques known as the
mosaic and mixup transformations. Our work is demonstrated on sensed data and imagery collected
from the Department of Energy (DOE) Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) where miles of tunnel
ceiling must be assessed for structural integrity.

1. INTRODUCTION
 For any underground facility, it is necessary to reinforce the structure from collapse and monitor
the health of those engineered structures over time. As a primary member for stabilizing a variety of
geo-engineered structures, monitoring of the commonly referred to rock bolt is a crucial task for
maintaining structural safety. Consideration for any given bolt’s load induced stress, anchoring, and
integrity plus the area density of their installation can be indicative of the overall health of a structure
such as an underground mine or tunnel [1]. Generally, much work has been done regarding
monitoring of individual bolts through a variety of non-destructive methods. Most of these
approaches involve instrumenting individual bolts and monitoring their health based on some
physical principle relating to their levels of strain or corrosion [2]. However, for a large facility in a
more fluid geological structure such as the Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP), installing,
instrumenting, and managing the resulting data stream of the requisite number of bolts and is likely
too cumbersome a task to implement on a reasonable timescale and cost basis. Thus, we turn to
simpler monitoring using optical collection methods combined with computer vision techniques to
count and assess bolt types, health, and distribution throughout the facility.
 A few examples of 3D point cloud monitoring exist that aim to perform similar tasks for rock
bolt monitoring but most of these focus on treating the point cloud data to classify clusters of points
as “bolt or not-bolt” in a binary sense [3, 4]. Others have used machine learning approaches to
estimate stress or anchorage quality of specific rock bolt types [5, 6]. In our implementation, we
widen the scope of what must be identified to include a variety of objects of interest for assessing
mine safety. For instance, we not only focus on resin anchoring rock bolts (Figure 1) but also other
objects such as mechanical bolts which are often seen securing chain-link retainment fencing, fire
extinguishers mounted to the ceiling (mine ceilings are often referred to as “back” or “roof” and
mine corridors as “drifts”), and failed bolts.
 Thus, by detecting and counting multiple objects relevant to the safety & health of the facility
using an optical imaging platform coupled with a robust image processing pipeline, we demonstrate
a practical automation solution to monitor the ceiling structural integrity of WIPP.
 WIPP is the United States’ underground site for transuranic waste disposal, located outside of
Carlsbad, NM. The facility is built into a 2,000 foot thick salt bed originating from evaporation
cycles of the Permian sea 250 million years ago [7]. This salt bed is ideal for permanent disposal of
radioactive waste due to its relative geologic stability, absence of water, ease to mine, and tendency
to self-heal or fill fractures slowly over time. However, these disposal advantages increase the
requirement for engineered reinforcement such as the rock bolt and contribute to their subsequent
failure modes (Figure 1).
 Traditionally, orthomosaic generation is accomplished using structure-from-motion (SfM),
simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM), or 2D image mosaic framework methods [8]. Of
these, SfM is thought to provide the most accurate spatial localization by first generating the 3D
structure of the scene captured by the multi view images and subsequently extracting the 2D
orthomosaic. Pix4D (Pix4D S.A., Prilly, Switzerland) is a commercial software tool that employs this
method. However, examples of this can be observed with several open-source software suites
available such as OpenMVG [9] or Bundler [10]. Additionally, when camera pose measurement is
insufficient, SfM can be assisted with GPS data in unmanned arial vehicle (UAV) applications to
improve performance & accuracy. Without need for real-time execution as seen with some SLAM
methods [11], SfM is an appropriate and accurate choice for stitching a collection of 2D images.

 9

 A cornerstone task in computer vision has long been object detection and identification. With
the realization of deep neural networks (DNNs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), this
task can now be accomplished in a more generalized setting for many different object types across
many types of imagery. Briefly, model components such as You-Only-Look-Once (YOLO) and
Single-shot detector (SSD) allow for both the localization and classification of objects in an image
with a single forward pass through the network architecture [12, 13]. Most often, the localization is
accomplished by predicting a bounding box whereas the classification is usually inferred from a
probability distribution of all possible classes on which the model was trained. There are many
nuances and intricate model architecture specifications in this field beyond the scope of this paper
but can be found in references such as Amjoud et al. [14].

2. METHODS AND MATERIALS

2.1. Imaging System
 One of the main challenges of this work was the collection of data suitable for analysis. WIPP is
a unique environment in its purpose, longevity, and geometry. The facility has been operationally
emplacing waste since 1999 [15] and our goal is to create a self-contained, operationally feasible
automation system for rock bolt monitoring. The designed geometry of underground drifts was
known a priori with the caveat that all dimensions of the WIPP underground change over time due
to salt creep. One of the main challenges was how to collect imagery data in such a way to avoid
double-counting of rock bolts. Three possible solutions to this problem are:
 • Collection of non-overlapping, adjacent imagery. This is akin to collecting the pieces of
 a puzzle that need to be assembled to recreate an overall image. To solve this problem, one
 would need to assume a range of static underground geometries that could be adjusted for
 either a priori or in real-time. The dynamic nature of salt creep at WIPP makes this method
 impractical.
 • Localization of rock bolts in imagery. This solution requires accurate localization of the
 imaging system and associated images. WIPP is a hard GPS-denied environment that
 requires an alternative solution for localization. Self-contained SLAM systems exist that
 operate as part of a moving platform to render an estimation of pose. Relying solely on
 localization would require a system with a high degree of precision to discern image objects
 only inches apart.
 •  Orthomosaic creation from overlapping images. This solution entails collecting a large
 amount of overlapping imagery, feeding that data into a SfM program, and generating an
 orthomosaic image resolved of any overlap. As was found through experimentation,
 orthomosaic creation needs a priori pose estimates for each image to ensure robust
 reconstruction.
 An imaging platform, shown in Figure 2, was conceived through trial-and-error experimentation
at WIPP to generate orthomosaics from overlapping localized imagery. WIPP has both a relatively
large footprint and many rock bolts. Mapping rock bolts in a large area requires moving through
space while capturing images. Five distinct systems were integrated to facilitate collection of imagery
sufficient for orthomosaic generation.

2.1.1.     Cameras
 Both full frame mirrorless and point-and-shoot camera options were tested to determine
feasibility for image capture. The tradeoffs between these options are image resolution, ease of use,
and capture speed. During development, we used a Sony Alpha mirrorless full-frame camera
connected to a triggering device to enable timelapse photography. The trigger was only able to
operate reliably at a period of one second due to limited lighting intensity, focusing speed, and data
storage write speed. This resulted in a maximum linear capture velocity of around 0.5 m/s (~1.1
mi/h), the speed of a very slow walk. Such a slow velocity was not desirable for the data collectors in
this study. Thus, these higher resolution cameras were set aside for more readily operable options.

 Figure 3. An array of GoPro HERO11 Black cameras mounted for image collection. They have internal
 batteries and are also outfitted with pass-thru battery doors for optional USB power while operating.

 GoPro cameras were found to have sufficient resolution, along with several other desirable
features. As GoPro cameras are designed for action sports photography, they prioritize ease of use
and high image capture rates at the cost of image resolution. Resulting orthomosaic resolutions from
these cameras are sufficient to read the writing on individual rock bolts, a desirable trait for human
inspection of the image. GoPro HERO11 Black cameras (Figure 3) are used as the primary imaging
system during data collection. The minimum timelapse photography interval for these cameras is
0.5s. This allows a data collection speed of 1.0 m/s (~2.2 mi/h) based on the necessary overlap of
adjacent images. GoPro cameras come standard with a fisheye lens and a software setting to linearize
the image output. Linearization of images at the GoPro or in post (e.g. via Adobe Lightroom) is
required for the Pix4D software to successfully create orthomosaics as the fisheye lens models did
not converge.

2.1.2.     Lighting
 Given that WIPP is an underground facility, manmade lighting is necessary for illumination. The
lighting that does exist in the underground is installed on the walls and ceiling, with the primary goal
being pathway illumination. As these lights either shine downwards or sideways, a separate source of
light was required to illuminate the ceiling. Any source of lighting used must be constant in output
or have a switching frequency so rapid that it has no effect on image capture. We experimented with
several lighting systems at increasing cost points to arrive at a lighting system that met requirements.
 The first lights used were two 600W tungsten halogen flood lamps commonly available from
hardware stores. These are relatively inexpensive and due to the analog nature of the incandescent
bulb, are ideally suited for imaging applications. Around 86% of the energy output from tungsten
halogen bulbs is in the form of infrared radiation and around 7% radiated as visible light [16]. This
large proportion of infrared radiation was initially desirable for proposed use of infrared imagers to
avoid exposing the underground to bright white light but has since been discarded in favor of
optimizing visible light operations. Tungsten halogen lamps get very hot to the touch and quickly
consumed battery energy using 1200W of power making them non-ideal for our application.
 For a more power efficient lighting system, a switch was made to light emitting diode (LED)
lights with the same or greater visible light output as tungsten halogen lamps. An ARRI SkyPanel
S60-RP with a 5600K remote phosphor panel was identified for this purpose. This lighting system
provides a lighting output of 230 lumens m-2 at 7 m, roughly equal to the tallest points of the WIPP
underground ceiling. Designed for the film industry, the remote phosphor panel natively emits a
uniform beam of white light with a half peak angle of 105 degrees, and can be dimmed from full
output as necessary [17]. Given these characteristics, this lighting system is proven capable of
illuminating rock bolts for image collection in the WIPP underground.

2.1.3. Lighting Power
 Lighting is the only system component that does not come from the manufacturer with an
integrated energy supply. Large lithium-ion batteries are not allowed in the WIPP underground and
commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) lead-acid battery/inverter combinations are limited. We utilize a
12V 150Ah sealed lead-acid (SLA) absorbed glass mat (AGM) battery acquired from a local
hardware store. This is paired with a Tripp-Lite 12V inverter/charger that can output 120VAC
power to an Edison plug as well as recharge the battery when not in use. AC power was used for the
lighting due to an ARRI product specification allowing only 50% of maximum output when
operating on battery power. The lighting power system could be more efficient running directly on
DC battery power without this constraint. Still, the 150Ah battery can power the 400W light for 2.0h
before remaining charge falls to the 20-40% range. This duration is more than sufficient to collect
imagery data in about ten distinct WIPP underground drifts without recharge.

2.1.4. Wheeled Platform
 Collection of imagery in stretches up to 1,000 feet requires consistent movement of the imager
as well as the other components in the system. The lighting and power supply are particularly heavy
and the floor of WIPP is salt varying in consistency from densely compact to as loose as sand. For
traction and a carrying capacity of 750lb, a Granite Industries Overland Easy Wagon powered cart
was chosen with large width pneumatic turf tires. The internal 24V, 24Ah SLA AGM battery pack
meets the requirements for battery power in the WIPP underground while providing an operating
time sufficient for a working day of image collection [18]. We add a potentiometer in series with the
resistive throttle control to enable adjustment of the maximum throttle velocity. By adjusting
maximum velocity, the operator can select a suitable operation speed and hold the throttle fully

 15

open, which is more ergonomic than throttle modulation during data collection runs lasting up to 15
minutes in duration.

2.1.5. Localization
 The WIPP underground is GPS-denied and has no alternative absolute localization system
installed in the environment. Through experimentation, we discovered that relative localization of
images was vital for successful orthomosaic construction. Each image is seeded with pose metadata
that indicates the position and orientation of the camera relative to the starting pose of the system.
To collect this localization data, we utilize an Exyn ExynPak handheld mobile lidar scanning system
that runs a SLAM algorithm onboard [19]. Collected lidar data is post-processed to increase accuracy
via loop closure algorithms and then the output pose trajectory is aligned with image collections by
coinciding their start and end points. The start and end of motion in trajectory data can be found by
plotting velocity and the start and end of motion in image data is observed in the timelapse
photography. Aligning these two data streams and linearly interpolating pose data allows one-to-one
association of image and pose estimate with the knowledge that images are collected at a constant
frequency. The SfM algorithm takes this information as a local seed value and further optimizes it
during the orthomosaic creation process.

2.2. Orthomosaic Generation
 Pix4Dmapper Pro version 3.3.29 is used to create orthomosaics from sets of relatively localized
image data. Standard processing options are used for the template “3D Maps”, with the addition of
outputting undistorted images as artifacts and using geometrically verified matching. According to
Pix4Dmapper documentation, selecting geometrically verified matching results in a more robust
reconstruction “when many similar features are present throughout the project” [20]. Given the
WIPP underground ceiling is an environment with repetitive features, this setting is helpful for
orthomosaic reconstruction. Guidance is also taken from Pix4Dmapper documentation on data
acquisition planning and imaging system design and applied to data collection in WIPP’s unique
environment. Recommendations implemented include utilization of at least two flight lines for
corridor mapping, greater than 85% image overlap for interior reconstruction in the flight direction,
at least 60% side-to-side image overlap, use of a fisheye lens for high image overlap, and use of
artificial lighting for tunnel reconstruction [21].

 Figure 6. Resulting orthomosaic example with image collection points in green.

 Pix4Dmapper recreates the scene in 3D using SfM and then uses this reconstruction to extract a
downward, or upward in our case, facing orthomosaic of the scene. We find that long thin drifts do
not work well for Pix4D’s automated orthomosaic extraction. Repeated processing of slender scenes
results in varying orientation of the vertical vector and requires manual interventions to correct. This
situation is rectified by collecting “L”-shaped segments of data: two slender collections orthogonal
to each other, thus leading to large dimensions in each planar direction where Pix4D automatically
infers the vector to create the orthomosaic view. The “L” data collection pattern may only be
necessary when individual images are not localized, but more testing is necessary. Considering image
pose data as high accuracy may short-circuit further optimization during SfM for more consistent
results. This will be dependent on how accurate the image pose data collected is.

2.3. Data Labeling and Augmentation

 Figure 7. Examples of objects labeled in imagery of tunnel ceilings.

 To fine-tune our object detection models, we first sample and label images from collections
performed underground using the open source Labelme1 package. Nine objects with examples
shown in Figure 7 are annotated and localized in each image. Notable classes include the Dywidag (a
brand name of a common resin anchoring rock bolt), chain-link, initial bolt, and failed bolt objects.
 We generally take all large (often labeled with date-of-install) bolts to be Dywidags and all small,
unobstructed bolts to be chain-links. Initial bolts are labeled for any bolt that is observed to be
below the retainment netting/chain-link indicating they were installed beforehand. Failed bolts are
bolts which have broken, mainly according to the mechanisms in Figure 1. These bolt counts are
critical to assessing the safety of the mine ceiling. As a prominent and additional object, we include
fire extinguishers which are fixed to the ceiling to aid in the model’s ability to differentiate multiple
objects. Though we have labeled images containing all nine of the classes presented, we eliminate
cast angle brackets and straps (Figure 7) from the set of desired classes due to their limited
appearance on the ceiling or being deemed out of scope for the task studied, respectively.

1 https://github.com/wkentaro/labelme

 18

 For image augmentation, we rely on strategies implemented on-the-fly during training which
include the “mixup” and “mosaic” strategies. Mixup primarily overlays two images sampled from the
training dataset with some scaling factor to adjust the visibility of the overlayed image. All objects
and annotations are retained, adding variability for each instance. Mosaic augmentation samples up
to three images from the training set and stiches them together in a random assortment. The intent
of this augmentation includes generalizing the model over varying backgrounds in a single image and
producing small objects annotations for enhanced sensitivity. Both augmentations are applied with
some sampling probability (e.g. frequency) for each sampled instance during training.

2.4. EfficientDet Architectures, Scoring, and Fine-tuning
 We choose a specific class of models, referred to as EfficientDets, to fine-tune for our rock bolt
detection problem. EfficientDet models, first reported by Tan et al., were designed to operate on
relatively large image inputs at lower floating point operations per second (FLOPs) than comparable
models at the time [22]. We focus on the model variants shown in Table 1 with their respective
trainable parameter counts and image input sizes.

 When first demonstrated, these models superseded state-of-the-art performance on the

 To study fine-tuning of our models, we rely on a dataset consisting of 1309 training images and

4872 pixels) collected GoPro images then taking 1536 x 1536 pixel chunks of those images
containing the labeled objects of interest. This is done to ensure the EfficientDet networks receive
images with objects that exhibit a large enough relative appearance for effective detection and
identification.

2.5. Labeling Application for Human-in-the-loop Validation and Labeling
 A key goal of this workflow is to build a system that, through human expert intervention,
becomes more accurate and less cumbersome over lifecycle iterations. Figure 8 displays a schematic
of the intended lifecycle of the rock bolt monitoring workflow.

 Figure 8: Schematic of the rock bolt detection workflow.

 To meet this goal, we developed an application we call ARBITER (AI-assisted Rock Bolt
Identification Tool for Expert Review) to adjudicate all machine learning derived detects in
orthomosaic imagery allowing a user to both visualize objects in all original images used to form the
orthomosaic and export annotations in the original image space post-adjudication. Thus, the user

2.6. Orthomosaic Inference Methods
 A typical problem found in remote sensing applications is counting objects once they have been
detected. Complexity increases as area, number of objects, and image size increases. If the collection
is done with a staring wide-area imager using sufficient resolution, the object counting process can
be accomplished with lightweight, deterministic algorithms but that doesn’t necessarily make the
task trivial [26, 27]. However, this is not feasible in our case and objects must be counted across
several associated collections after being stitched into an orthomosaic. Counting in similar instances
can be complicated by adjudicating multiple counts of the same objects, moving objects appearing in
multiple frames, and non-uniform frame collection complicating stitching [28]. In our case, we don’t
have moving objects but still suffer from non-uniform frame collection and a need to remove
multiple counts. We solve this by running inference on the collected imagery comparing two
schemes to generate our ML-based detections ingested downstream by the labeling and validation
application.

2.6.1. Simple Image Chunking Method
 The first of the methods is the simple image chunking method previously presented [29]. Put
simply, we directly load the large orthomosaic as a standard RGB image and “chunk” it into fixed
size square segments while repeating the process with some offset to ensure overlapping chunks. We
then feed the collection of chunks to the EfficientDet model where the resulting detects are mapped
back to the orthomosaic reference frame and consolidated using a quadtree filter.

2.6.2. Multi-angle Method
 The multi-angle inference method is motivated by the observation that, when inferencing on
orthorectified imagery it can be difficult if not impossible to discern a failed bolt from a dywidag.
Thus, by running inference on the collected, original images one can base detections on multiple
angle views of a single object. A schematic of the multiangle workflow can be seen in Figure 10.

 22

 Figure 10. Schematic detailing the workflow for the multi-angle inference pipeline.

 To realize this workflow, we devise a coordinate transform system based on equations 1-4 and
generate a database partitioned by original image which maps the image pixel coordinates into the
orthomosaic frame of reference. Based on the image processed by the object detector, we can take
arbitrary detection box coordinates and transform these to the orthomosaic frame of reference
where we then coalesce coincident detects as before with the quadtree filter. These detections can
then be fed to the ARBITER application for a human-expert to validate.

model, the smaller variants still produced relatively high scores, despite being outliers from their
general training dynamics. Best models scores from each class are shown in Table 3.

 It’s worth noting that these are average validation scores based on a validation set consisting of a
simple split of the labeled images reserved for finetuning. While this score is a good indicator of
performance, it does not directly represent expected performance on the desired goal of
orthomosaic inference which is discussed in more detail in the remaining sections.
 To analyze hyperparameter dependence more closely, we look at the dependence plot produced
in Figure 13 across 32 runs sampled for the D4 variant. For both the mixup and mosaic image
augmentation methods, it appears the model favors low-zero probability of implementation for
either case. This is surprising, especially given the wide prevalence of image augmentation in object
detection [30]. This augmentation trend generally held on a per object validation mAP basis
however, some runs exhibited some higher per object scores at higher probabilities for the mixup
augmentation indicating this method produces less of a negative impact. Learning rates trended to
higher magnitudes across the range sampled with the best model preferring a starting learning rate of
0.005. However, there appears to be no discernible trend relating to validation scores.

4.2. Inference method comparisons
 Based on mAP score alone, the results shown in section 3.3 are unexpected. However, the
resulting counts determined from the two methods tell a more nuanced story. Count comparisons in
Figure 16 are unfavorable to the multiangle method given the significant overcount of dywidags.
This is occurring even after confidence threshold tuning. However, the multiangle method appears
to excel at correctly attributing boreholes and failed bolts. Both objects are difficult to discern from
a single, orthorectified viewpoint. The multiangle inference method which shows the model multiple
views of these objects provides greater information. The resulting decision made after coalescing the
detects from multiple angles to the orthorectified space is thus better informed.

 29

 This, however, does not address dywidag overcounting with either inference case. There is likely
no single culprit to the overcounting problem but, at least for the multiangle case, the large disparity
could be driven by propagating dywidag detects which appear in the collected imagery and not in the
orthomosaic imagery due to warping effects observed to occur in the orthomosaic. This would cause
the multiangle method to over count and the human-labeler to effectively undercount. The warping
effects could also confuse the model in the chunking method, producing false positives.
 Orthomosaic rendering effects also make a notable impact on typically easily detected objects
like the fire extinguishers. Though few in number, both methods undercount here likely due to low
confidences driven by observable warping effects shown in Figure 18 below.

 Figure 18. Example of poorly orthorectified pixel mappings for a fire extinguisher object in the
 maintenance hall ceiling.

 These effects can likely confuse the model to produce a false negative in the chunking case. For
the multiangle case, the projection of the fire extinguisher pixels to the disparate locations in the
orthomosaic can produce seemingly erroneous box placement resulting in an inconclusive detection
decision when the detects are coalesced by the quadtree step.

 For chain-links, these likely get mistaken for dywidags or missed due to poor resolution and
their small size contributing to their undercount for both methods. Initial bolts boast reasonably
close counts for both methods but could potentially be improved. For either case, multiple boxes

 30

and split boxes for the same object are some other minor contributors to miscounts. While the
quadtree generally works for clean mine ceilings, the coalescing behavior tends to break down when
the ceiling becomes more cluttered as is the case with the maintenance hall ceiling. Broadly speaking,
the chunking method appears to be more conservative and produces better detection boxes than the
multiangle method. However, the multiangle method can excel at detecting difficult to discern
objects using multiple views. An inference workflow that combines both could be a valid option
when engineering a production detection pipeline.

4.3. Human-in-the-loop labeling and dataset expansion
 When analyzing the projected detects in Figure 17 there are poorly projected bounding boxes
most likely due to greater error associated with the geometric transform’s fit at the extents of the
orthomosaic image. This is especially true for the wall bolt detects. Additionally, this image was
captured shortly after the camera cart turned down a narrow pathway which has been observed to
create anomalies in the resulting orthomosaic. Thus, some boxes are slightly shifted from where one
would expect them. While this is an exceptionally bad example, it does illustrate the ways in which a
data curator will need to adjust these detections to be suitable for training. Of the 369 images used
to form the orthomosaic for this test study, we vetted and labeled 307 to re-ingest back into the
training loop, greatly expanding our training dataset by 263% after following the same training image
chunking procedure described above. A quick experiment using the same hyperparameters for the
best D4 model did not yield any significant improvement in validation or test scoring. This might
indicate that these model’s learning capacities have been reached. Newer architectures and object
detection techniques have been released since the EfficientDet which may be more promising for
future platform improvements [31-33].

5. FUTURE WORK

5.1. Data Collection System
 The data collection system has several components that were state-of-the-art at the time of their
acquisition for this project that have since been deprecated by new versions. The latest GoPro
camera version is the GoPro HERO13 Black. Two generations beyond those used for this work,
these cameras tend to gain improved hardware over time for higher resolution and faster data
collection. Better still would be a facility-specific camera optimized for high resolution image capture
with infrared light to avoid distracting visible light emissions during rock bolt illumination.
 The priority of this work was to create a manually movable data collection system that could be
quickly realized to facilitate data analysis. Revisiting the system requirements, a semi to fully
autonomous robotic data collection system should be considered. A robot does not need to move
fast to collect imagery and will have a localization system integral to its design, perhaps with the
feature of absolute localization in the WIPP underground. Both features can serve to improve
orthomosaic reconstruction quality. Also, new cameras could be integrated to capture images of the
ribs and floor, and other sensors could capture air quality/quantity readings, as additional
monitoring options.

5.2. Data Analysis Pipeline
 Future ML components should consider more modern object detection architectures to improve
scores and detection counts. While it works for this demonstration, the quadtree coalescing method
described will require better tuning or replacement with a custom filtering algorithm which could
reduce some of the erroneous detection box behavior observed. Regarding the inference workflow,
optimizing the combination of chunking and multiangle methods could be a solution to detect
difficult objects and conservatively detect common objects. For end-user processing, the ARBITER
tool should be deployed in an environment which can utilize semi-frequent data uploads and data
pipeline scheduling tools to effectively automate the human-in-the-loop pipeline described earlier.
With these additions, this system will be in a production ready state and can potentially scale to
other underground facilities and industries.

5.3. Predictive Maintenance
 The system could be expanded to include features which enable physical measurements of
objects of interest, such as bolts, crack propagation, and gross drift movement. Furthermore, the
system could mature from reactive failure identification to proactive monitoring by forecasting the
remaining life expectancy of underground objects. By integrating additional sensing modalities and
analytical capabilities, the tool could provide accurate spatial dimensional data and track structural
changes over time. This would enable predictive maintenance and long-term profiling on a per
instance basis and overall structural health assessments. This capability is especially relevant given
the continued excavation of the WIPP underground for emplacement of waste [34]. Such a system
in operation at the present time could capture the entire history of additional excavations and
provide a comprehensive timeline of rock bolt installations and failures for salt creep convergence
modeling.

 32

6. CONCLUSION
 In this work, we have demonstrated an end-to-end data collection and analysis system to
monitor the roof of the WIPP underground. This monitoring system can produce spatial, temporal,
and safety factor data products while reducing strain on the employees responsible for manually
monitoring rock bolts and other objects. By detecting the rock bolts and other roof objects pertinent
to mine health in a semi-automated fashion, the authors believe this system can be a useful tool in
maintaining a safe and effective facility. Results from WIPP demonstrate the system’s ability to
detect, count, and map safety-critical objects in GPS-denied environments, offering a pragmatic
solution for large-scale structural monitoring. The technology has the potential for additional
improvements to enable more sophisticated monitoring functionality, like predicative maintenance,
and to be executed robotically.
