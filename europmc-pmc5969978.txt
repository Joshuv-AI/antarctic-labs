CYSTIC PNEUMATOSIS OF THE BOWEL *
By DANIEL LAMONT, M.B., Ch.B., F.R.C.S.Edin.
The specimen which I am exhibiting is one of a rare condition
of the bowel, most suitably designated " cystic pneumatosis."
This term implies the presence in the bowel wall of gas-
containing cysts. The recorded cases of the condition are
very few. In the last two centuries there have been published
in this country and in America, as far as I have been able
to ascertain, only some ten cases. In Continental literature
there are something like another eighty cases. An early
reference to the condition is made in the year 1737 in the
catalogue of the Anatomical Museum in Amsterdam?" A
portion of the jejunum of a man showing tumour which arose
from wind." Various other post-mortem specimens are
described, but not until 1899, 'when abdominal surgery had
become a regular undertaking, was the first case described
occurring in the living. The writer, in this case?Halm by
name?found a large number of sessile and pedunculated gas-
containing cysts on the small and large intestines. I should
like to emphasize also that the stomach and ascending colon
are described as having been greatly dilated.
If you will, for a moment, permit a reference to comparative
pathology, it is of interest to note that gas cysts are frequently
found in the intestine and rectum of swine killed in summer.
Furthermore, John Hunter drew attention to this, and sug-
gested that the air escaped from the blood through diseased
blood-vessels. Two of his specimens are in the Royal College
of Surgeons, and one of them is reputed to have been sent
to him by Jenner.
In modern surgical text-books, Babcock and Da Costa devote
small paragraphs to the condition. Cyril Nitch, of London,
supplies a comprehensive bibliography, to which I record my
indebtedness for many references.

the peritoneum. This is not so, however. The cysts are

microscope, subserously, intramuscularly, and submucously.
As in most other specimens, the little grape-like cysts are
disposed mainly 011 the anti-mesenteric border. The general
appearance, as seen at operation, was of masses of glistening
cysts draped on the bowel in festoon-like fashion. These
cysts were filled with gas, and this gas was under considerable
tension, so much so that, pressed between the forefinger and
thumb, one of them would go off with a distinct " pop,"
leaving the collapsed cyst wall.
By the injection of colloidal and other solutions I have been
unable to demonstrate any communication between the cysts
and the bowel lumen or between individual cysts.
The next point concerns the situation of the cysts. Most
often the ileum?as in this case?then the jejunum, are
affected. The duodenum, appendix, and rectum enjoy freedom.
The large bowel, as far only as the splenic flexure, is reputed
to have suffered. The stomach has been known to be affected
very rarely?in my case there was present a single, large,
albuminous fluid-containing cyst on the greater curvature.
Mesenteric glands, distended with air bubbles, have been
described.
It should be stated that cases of diffuse emphysema of the
csccum are known?analogous to true surgical emphysema?
but these should not be included in the term cystic pneuma-
tosis. The most remarkable?and I think significant?fact is
that in all cases described in detail there has been a co-existing
lesion of the alimentary tract. Fifty per cent have been
definitely associated with an ulcer of the stomach or duodenum.
Other lesions have been pyloric cancer, tuberculosis, ulceration,
and intestinal obstruction. In my specimen there was a great
mass of adhesions around the first part of the duodenum?
an ulcer was present, and, most conspicuous of all, there was
an enormously dilated stomach, which measured 20 inches
by 8 inches, and easily accommodated 8 pints of fluid.

As regards the histology of the cysts, time does not permit
a full description, but I should like to mention that the cyst
wall is of connective tissue with a complete endothelial lining,
and, as has been constantly noted by others, there are
numerous giant cells present. Further, the descriptions of
other cases state that no inflammatory cells have been present,
but , as Mr. Burton, who kindly examined my specimen, says,
there are numerous areas of lymphocyte infiltration present
in this case.
Now, if you will grant me your indulgence further, I should
like to say a word on the remarkable clinical picture presented
by the patient, whom I saw in Mr. Patrick's wards, in the
Royal Infirmary. The man?the condition is commoner in men
by 3 to 1?was 43 years of age, and had complained of pain
in the abdomen the day prior to admission. He had vomited
large quantities of foul-smelling material. He had observed
that his abdomen had become swollen.
When I saw him next day he expressed himself as having
slight pain in the abdomen. He looked well; the face was
fresh-looking and placid. There was some tenderness in the
epigastrium, but no marked rigidity anywhere. The most
striking feature, however, was the distended abdomen, which
everywhere gave a definite resonant note to percussion. The
liver dulness was quite inappreciable. The peritoneal cavity
in a word was obviously tightly distended by gas. The tem-
perature was 100? F., the pulse 100, and respiration 30 per
minute. Blood films showed 85 per cent polymorphonuclears,
7 per cent lymphocytes, and 8 per cent mononuclears. (The
blood was cultured, but no growth was obtained.)
On a somewhat guarded diagnosis of '' pneumo-peritoneum
the abdomen was opened, and, on incision of the peritoneum,
gas escaped very audibly, and with greater force and in greater
abundance than one associates with the escape of gas in
ruptured ulcer. In addition to the condition described there
was evidence of peritonitis with exudate. This was sterile.
On the second day after operation the abdomen was again
becoming distended, and under local anesthesia a tube was
inserted and the gas collected in vacuum tubes. The patient
died on the fifth day from diffuse peritonitis.

Dr. Cuthbertson kindly examined the gas obtained from the
peritoneal cavity for me, and he considered it to be free from
oxygen and C02 and to consist mainly of methane. This
constitution is entirely different from that of the gas contained
in the intact cysts, and would be explained by the infection
of the peritoneum by gas-forming organisms.
This patient's previous history is interesting. For eighteen
years he had suffered from bouts of vomiting and eructation
of foul-smelling gas. His appetite was good, he suffered no
pain after food. He was not losing weight. He was con-
stipated, and took liquid paraffin.
In 1923 he had been operated on in St. Bartholomew's
Hospital in the wards of Sir Holburt Waring, to whom I am
indebted for the information that bacillus coli peritonitis of
unknown origin was found. Evidently there were at that
time no cysts present on the bowel.
In conclusion, as regards the etiology of this condition,
various theories are advanced, but none are entirely convinc-
ing ; but I shall refer briefly to the different suggestions :?
1. Neoplasm.?Finney, in America, inclines to the view that
this is a distinct variety of tumour whose cells have gas-forming
properties. No such tumour, however, is known elsewhere in
the body.
2. The giant cells have been accredited with a pathogenic
role, inasmuch as a process of vacuolation is suggested with
subsequent gas formation. In this connection it is interesting
to note that the gas gland in the swim bladder of fish possesses
no giant cells. Probably the giant cells are there in their
usual capacity of attendants in a chronic irritative process.
3. Bacterial.?It is suggested by some that bacteria gain
access to the bowel wall through abrasions in the mucosa and
there produce gas. However, oxygen is present in the gas
of the cysts, and the property of forming this gas is not
possessed by any of the well-known gas-forming organisms.
Further, in living specimens, no culture is obtained as a rule.
4. Mechanical.?This view is that intestinal gas is forced
into the bowel wall under high pressure through a break in

the mucosa. A certain amount of justification for this view
is found in the fact that where the condition has occurred there
has been some condition which would cause increased pressure
within the bowel. However, intestinal gas contains a relatively
large proportion of C02 (40 per cent) and a small proportion
of 02 (05 per cent), which is not in accordance with the
analysis of the gas in these cysts. (This was found to be
14 per cent oxygen and 4 per cent C02. It was Dr. Wishart,
in Professor Cathcart's department, who solved the difficulty
for me of analysing the minute quantities of gas available.)
In my own view, the gas is neither forced into nor formed
in tissue spaces, but is formed in actual lymph channels. The
anatomical arrangement of the lymphatics, which form a
complicated series of plexuses, would be quite compatible with
the tier-like arrangement of the cysts. Further, the histo-
logical appearances support this view. As to how the gas
is produced I am not ready at this stage to suggest more than
that I believe the gas formation is purely a chemical reaction,
related to the absorption of the contents of a dilated stomach
and bowel. Secondarily to the gas production there is, I
believe, a pathological process of perilymphangitis which
results in the loculation leading to cyst formation.
