 Euro-Deterrent: Analysis of Proposals for a European Nuclear Arsenal (CNSIS Paper)
 Harry Green
 Analyst, A-2
                                         9 October 2025

Intro
The idea of a “Euro-deterrent”—a nuclear arsenal under European control and mandated to protect
multiple European countries—has been proposed many times as a means for European self-defense
independent of United States security guarantees under the North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO).
This paper examines not only the concept’s multiple guises, but the reasons for their failure. It will
address the idea of a Franco-British umbrella, a French umbrella, a European “pool”, and the nuclear
arsenal of a hypothetical “united Europe”. Reasons for rejection largely fall into three categories:
sovereignty concerns, credibility gaps, and a desire to preserve existing norms and structures. This paper
concludes that the Euro-deterrent will likely remain only hypothetical unless the US unequivocally signals
its intent to withdraw its nuclear umbrella from Europe, thereby forcing European countries to build a
new defense arrangement.

Proposals:
 Franco-British Umbrella

 • A French nuclear umbrella supplemented by British weapons arguably already exists, with the
 United Kingdom stating (as early as 2015) “We would consider using our nuclear weapons only
 in extreme circumstances of self-defense, including the defense of our NATO allies” 1 – and with
 France treating its vital interests as concomitant with those of the UK since the 1995 Chequers
 Declaration. 2 In fact, Britain’s nuclear forces have been “assigned to NATO” since 1962. 3 In 1974,
 the Ottawa Declaration also recognized that the French and British nuclear arsenals contributed
 to the security of the alliance as a whole. 4 However, France is not part of NATO’s Nuclear
 Planning Group (NPG), and its nuclear forces, unlike those of the UK, do not have a speciﬁc
 mandate to defend NATO. 5

 • The Lisbon Treaty, which entered into force in 2009 after being ratiﬁed by all member countries
 of the European Union at the time, 6 can be interpreted as providing a nuclear umbrella of sorts. 7
 It does not provide speciﬁcs, although an umbrella from the Lisbon Treaty would have been a de
 facto Franco-British umbrella prior to Britain’s departure from the European Union. 8 Article 42.7
 reads:

 If a Member State is the victim of armed aggression on its territory, the other Member States
 shall have towards it an obligation of aid and assistance by all the means in their power, in
 accordance with Article 51 of the United Nations Charter. This shall not prejudice the speciﬁc
 character of the security and defense policy of certain Member States. Commitments and
 cooperation in this area shall be consistent with commitments under the North Atlantic Treaty

 Organisation, which, for those States which are members of it, remains the foundation of their
 collective defense and the forum for its implementation.
 One academic article notes that “This interpretation—that ‘all the means in their power’ may
 include nuclear weapons—is shared by the Bundestag Research Service.” 9

• France and the UK signed multiple agreements in July 2025, expanding the scope of a notional
 Franco-British nuclear deterrent and deepening nuclear cooperation between the two countries.
 The Northwood Declaration states that there is “no extreme threat to Europe that would not
 prompt a response by our two nations.” 10 The UK-France Leaders’ Declaration creates a Nuclear
 Steering Group to “coordinate across nuclear policy, capabilities and operations”. 11

• The idea of a Franco-British nuclear umbrella may have existed as early as 1954, when French
 Marshal Alphonse Juin, Commander-in-Chief of NATO’s Central Area—supported by France’s
 chiefs of staﬀ—proposed a fully European, nuclear-equipped defense organization within NATO
 to avoid full European dependence on the US. The French chiefs of staﬀ believed France could
 not aﬀord to build this force by itself, and therefore hoped for British participation in an “Atlantic
 nuclear pool”. European nations would provide raw materials, scientists and ﬁnancing for the
 construction of these weapons, to be deployed jointly. 12 However, neither the US nor Britain
 were enthusiastic, with Britain speciﬁcally disliking the idea of pooling its nuclear weapons with
    others’. 13

• There has been recent German interest in a Franco-British umbrella. A legal review
 commissioned by the German Parliament found in 2017 that Germany could legally ﬁnance
 either the British or French nuclear weapons programs. 14 Then German Finance Minister
 Christian Lindner “hinted at” Germany co-ﬁnancing French nuclear weapons in a 2024 op-ed. 15
 Germany’s current Chancellor, Friedrich Merz, has expressed interest in a Franco-British
 umbrella as a supplement to US guarantees. 16 17

 French Umbrella

• A solely French nuclear umbrella has been ﬂoated many times, occasionally leading to
 diplomatic agreements. One such agreement signed in 1958 was known as the “FIG” for the
 three countries involved: France, Italy, and West Germany. Under it, West Germany and Italy
 would have provided money and expertise for the production of French nuclear weapons to
 which they would eventually have had access. 18 Later that year, newly elected French president
 Charles de Gaulle immediately withdrew France from the agreement, wanting a strictly national
 nuclear program. 19 20 The FIG was proposed again in 1963 by several French cabinet ministers, as
 a response to the Multilateral Force (addressed later), but the idea lacked the full support of the
 French government and was not pursued. 21 More recently, the 2019 Aachen treaty between
 France and Germany reads that both parties “shall aﬀord one another any means of assistance
 or aid within their power, including military force, in the event of an armed attack on their
 territories”. 22 Current French President Emmanuel Macron has clariﬁed that “any means” should
 be seen as including nuclear weapons. 23

• Besides more concrete discussions, French heads of state and cabinet ministers have made
 speeches about and proposals for a French nuclear umbrella over Europe at several points in the
 1960s, 1990s 24, 2008, 25 and 2020s. 26 Macron has vocally promoted the idea that France’s vital
 interests include (and have always included) the protection of Europe. 27 Regardless, multiple
 authors indicate a lack of interest—if not outright hostility—from other European countries
 regarding a French nuclear umbrella; in 1995 Swedish foreign minister Lena Hjelmwallen
 rejected a European nuclear umbrella, being promoted by France at the time, as “an awful
 idea”. 28 29 30 Several authors argue that these French proposals have been purely political, either
 to prevent German proliferation, to make France’s Paciﬁc nuclear tests more palatable, or to
 justify the French nuclear deterrent in the absence of the Soviet threat. 31 32 33

• However, there has been some interest in a French nuclear umbrella beyond that expressed by
 Germany. In 2022 and 2024, the Polish president and prime minister expressed interest in a
 European nuclear umbrella, potentially through NATO, using either American or French
 weapons.34 35 Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk described the signing of a treaty to deepen
 Franco-Polish security ties in 2025 as progress toward potentially extending a French nuclear
 umbrella over Poland. 36

 “European Pool” Proposals: Multilateral Force, Atlantic Nuclear Force

• The Multilateral Force (MLF), ﬂoated by the United States in 1960, was a proposal to put
 American nuclear weapons in European hands, but with an American veto over their use. 37 The
 MLF would have been “a multinational ﬂeet of ships and submarines armed with American
 nuclear weapons but operated, owned, and controlled entirely by European NATO allies.” 38 De
 Gaulle opposed this idea. 39 Later, Britain instead proposed the “Atlantic Nuclear Force” (ANF) in
 1964. This strongly resembled the MLF, with the addition of British submarines and bombers to
 the force. Under the ANF proposal, British submarines would sail with these multinational forces
 and be assigned to NATO, 40 and every member of the force including Britain would have a veto
 over the use of its nuclear weapons. 41

• Some researchers have described the MLF and the ANF as disingenuous proposals that served
 largely political purposes. One author claims the MLF was only ever a ploy to mollify European
 concerns of US abandonment, and that Kennedy, who inherited the plan from Eisenhower, 42 had
 no plans to implement it. 43 The ANF is described by another author as a stalling tactic by Britain,
 to avoid openly disagreeing with the United States on the MLF, about which it was not
 enthusiastic. 44 British Defense Minister Peter Thorneycroft said at the time that the only purpose
 of the ANF was to “sink the MLF”. 45

 “European Federation”

• One option for a Euro-deterrent would require the creation of a new state. In their ratiﬁcations
 of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), West Germany and Italy introduced “European

 clauses”, with the West German statement saying “no provision of the treaty shall be interpreted
 as to hamper the development of European uniﬁcation”, while the Italian statement added that
 the NPT would not ban the production of “peaceful nuclear devices” that were “diﬀerent from
 nuclear armaments”. 46 Several authors claim this would allow West Germany and Italy to “inherit
 the rights to build nuclear weapons” once a European Federation is created: 47 i.e., it would allow
 some form of “united Europe” to have its own nuclear weapons. 48 In 1967, German Vice
 Chancellor Brandt said he believed the American interpretation of a “united Europe” referred to
 what US records call “a political organization which has common control over defense and
 political policy”. 49 In 1997, one member of the nuclear disarmament organization Pugwash
 Conference on Sciences and World Aﬀairs claimed this arrangement would not be a violation of
 the NPT. 50

Reasons for Rejection:
Sovereignty and credibility concerns are the most-cited reasons that proposals for a European nuclear
deterrent have ﬂoundered, speciﬁcally within high-level discussions between governments. Even if sole
control of an umbrella rests in the hands of one country, the term umbrella still constrains that nation’s
autonomy. Almost any hypothetical Euro-deterrent also struggles with credibility, not only in terms of
coherent command and control but also weapons capability. The leaders of European countries have
also wanted to avoid disrupting existing structures and norms, including nonproliferation, US
involvement in Europe, and NATO’s Nuclear Planning Group.

 Sovereignty Concerns

 • Some of the earliest nuclear proposals were rejected over sovereignty concerns. One author
 argues that Britain was uninterested in Juin’s 1954 proposal due to its proposal that it pool its
 nuclear weapons with those of other European nations. 51 De Gaulle, when elected, immediately
 withdrew France from the FIG 52 over concerns about sole control over its nuclear deterrent. 53
 Britain disliked the MLF because it wanted to maintain control over its own weapons, and
 speciﬁcally did not want West Germany having any control over them. 54

 • France, in particular, insists on a sovereign deterrent. 55 Despite proposals for a French nuclear
 umbrella, France has not joined the NPG. 56 Even at a time when France’s presidents were
 promoting the idea of a French nuclear umbrella, a French 1994 defense white paper said there
 would be no French nuclear umbrella without a concrete sense of common European defense
 interests. 57

 Credible capability

 • Credibility discussions focus on two issues: command and control, and military capabilities.
 Apart from posing sovereignty issues, proposals for a Euro-deterrent operated by a multilateral
 force also are criticized as non-credible due to lack of clear control. 58 59 A Franco-British nuclear
 umbrella might oﬀer less buy-in for other European countries wanting involvement in nuclear

 planning, but the decision-making would be much quicker. Weapons are also an issue, as Russia’s
 nuclear arsenal is not only vastly larger than France’s and Britain’s put together, but much more
 diverse in its capabilities. 60 61 62 For example, one author claims that Russia’s overwhelming
 numerical advantage at the sub-strategic level would make it diﬃcult for a European nuclear
 arsenal to manage escalation by responding in kind. 63

• Expanding and diversifying an arsenal carries its own ramiﬁcations. One Foreign Aﬀairs article
 depicts a nuclear trilemma for a Euro-deterrent among credibly deterring Russia, strategic
 stability (i.e. discouraging ﬁrst use), and nonproliferation, averring that by choosing any two
 goals, Europe must reject a third. 64 The authors conclude that abandoning strategic stability to
 preserve credible deterrence against Russia while upholding the nonproliferation norm is the
 best option, and that Germany speciﬁcally avoids discussing a Euro-deterrent to avoid having to
 make a choice among these options.

• Regardless, some Russian defense literature (recently reviewed by the RAND Corporation) 65
 considers both the British and French nuclear arsenals capable; Russian President Vladimir Putin
 described both arsenals as a threat in 2023. 66 This literature assesses France’s deterrent as being
 comparatively independent of NATO and US interests, 67 68 and French public opinion as strongly
 in favor of nuclear weapons. 69 70 Several Russian authors have argued that France is willing to
 protect its vital interests by extending its deterrent to cover other countries. 71 72 By contrast,
 some Russian defense authors have dismissed the UK’s arsenal as a prestige project 73 74 and
 have suggested the UK lacks the will to become a full-ﬂedged nuclear power 75—perhaps owing
 to anti-nuclear public opinion. 76 However, more recent Russian defense literature has argued
 that the UK arsenal needs to be taken seriously; 77 one paper further argues that the UK can
 guarantee “unacceptable damage to any aggressor”. 78 One author suggested the British arsenal
 functions as an extension of the American nuclear deterrent. 79

 Preservation of Existing Structures/Arrangements

• Proliferation has been a concern since Euro-deterrent proposals were ﬁrst discussed, even
 before the creation of the NPT. When the FIG was proposed, there was concern that West
 Germany could wind up acquiring nuclear weapons, 80 potentially as a result of collaboration
 with France. 81 The MLF was, according to some diplomatic communication, intended mainly as a
 proposal to keep West Germany within NATO structures and away from nuclear weapons. 82 Even
 so, the Soviet Union argued strongly against the MLF, claiming it would be a violation of the
 nascent non-proliferation treaty. 83

• European leaders are also concerned that the creation of a Euro-deterrent could bring about the
 very US disengagement that they wish to avoid. The desire for a Euro-deterrent has often been
 rooted in the fear that US security guarantees for Europe cannot be relied upon. 84 85 86 87 88 89
 British and German leaders in particular have worried that the creation of a Euro-deterrent could

 convince the United States that it was no longer needed in Europe, 90 91 92 which may have been
 the impetus for Merz’ carefully-worded interest in a Euro-deterrent as a supplement to, not a
 replacement for, the US nuclear umbrella. 93

 • One article also claims that European countries are worried that nuclear proliferation would
 undermine Atlantic integration. 94 While Germany prefers to work within NATO structures, France
 refuses to join the NPG. 95 Accepting any umbrella provided by French forces acting outside the
 NPG would undermine the legitimacy of that body.

Conclusion
At time of writing, public opinion in Europe may be more sympathetic to a Euro-deterrent than in the
past. Several European publics, especially in Germany, 96 have been staunchly opposed to nuclear
weapons; EU members Austria and Ireland are parties to the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear
Weapons. 97 However, a 2019 poll by the Körber Foundation noted that 45% of German survey
respondents would prefer nuclear protection from France and the UK, over continued German reliance
on the US nuclear umbrella, which only 22% supported. 98 Foreign Aﬀairs noted in July 2025: “…in a
March poll of nine European countries, 61 percent of respondents said they would welcome a French
nuclear umbrella covering the entire continent.” 99

None of the Euro-deterrent proposals discussed here ever reached the implementation stage. On top of
more speciﬁc issues such as sovereignty and credibility concerns, multiple authors argue that they have
failed due to the absence of concrete, shared priorities of the sort referenced in the 1994 French defense
white paper. 100 101 102 Closer nuclear coordination and planning was a more appealing alternative to the
MLF for European countries in the 60’s, and is still pursued and discussed as an alternative and/or
necessary precursor to the Euro-deterrent today. 103 104 105 106 The Euro-deterrent will likely remain only a
hypothetical barring an exogenous factor forcing European countries to build a new defense
arrangement, such as the US unequivocally signaling its intent to withdraw its nuclear umbrella or a
signiﬁcantly increased threat from Russia.
