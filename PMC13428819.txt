Carrying Strength and Silence: A Patient Perspective on Postpartum Mood in Black Motherhood
Authors: Amber Nelson-Fuller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractI first learned about the baby blues in medical school as a short definition: mood swings, tearfulness, and irritability resolving within two weeks. When I experienced them after giving birth during my second year of training, they felt far more complex than what I had read. I remember lying in the hospital that first night, overcome with tears and questioning if I was already falling short as a mother. Intellectually, I knew shifting hormones were at play, yet the sadness and guilt felt undeniably real. As a Black woman, I also carried the weight of cultural expectations. There is a persistent narrative that we must be strong, resilient, and unshaken no matter the circumstance. This expectation, often described as the Superwoman Schema, can make vulnerability feel unacceptable and discourage seeking support. For me, it added another layer to an already difficult transition. This experience deepened my understanding of how cultural identity shapes postpartum emotions and underscored the importance of making intentional space for emotional health, particularly for those whose cultural narratives may silence their struggles.Keywords: equity; inclusion; patient perspectives/narratives, social determinants of health; women’s health

Introduction

The postpartum period is a time of rapid biological, psychological, and social transition. The “baby blues,” marked by mood swings, irritability, and tearfulness, are the most common postpartum mood disturbance. Many mothers experience the baby blues in the first two weeks after delivery, though estimates vary widely, ranging from about 13% to as high as 76%. 1 This wide range reflects how hard it is to neatly define these emotional changes, and it reminds us that even feelings labeled “temporary” can feel overwhelming when a mother is living through them.

Recognition of the blues matter because they may increase vulnerability to postpartum depression if they go unacknowledged. 2 When they are treated as something brief or expected it can mean missing a moment to simply acknowledge what a mother is feeling and reassure her that it is real.

For Black women, the stakes are even higher. Disparities in postpartum care access mean that fewer Black mothers receive timely follow-up or treatment for mood symptoms, despite equal or higher need.3,4 These disparities are not just statistics. They influence whether a mother’s distress is recognized, believed, and supported in the days after delivery.

Cultural expectations can also influence how Black women experience and talk about emotional struggles. Many Black women grow up with the message that they should stay strong and put the needs of others first. This idea has been described in the literature as the Superwoman Schema.4,5 While this identity can foster pride and resilience, it can create hesitation around sharing the reality of postpartum emotions. This feeling of pressure to maintain this image can limit opportunities to acknowledge vulnerability or seek care. 5 I have felt this tension in my own life: the pride of being seen as capable, and the quiet hesitation about revealing when I felt overwhelmed. These expectations can affect more than how a woman feels emotionally, and they have been connected to higher stress during pregnancy and increased risks for mothers and their babies. 6

Perspective

The first night after my baby was born, I remember sitting in the hospital and suddenly feeling waves of emotion I could not hold back. Tears came easily, and with them a sense of unease I had not anticipated. I knew from my medical training that postpartum mood changes were common, but the knowledge did little to blunt the intensity in those moments. What struck me was not simply that I felt sad, but how quickly emotions could swell and take over, even when I could logically connect them to hormonal changes.

In those early days, I often thought about the gap between what I had studied in the classroom and what I was now experiencing firsthand. The textbooks described “mood lability” and “tearfulness,” but those words felt pale compared to the reality of being in it. The challenge was not only feeling emotional, but also deciding how much of that internal storm to share.

As a Black woman, I was also aware of the cultural expectation to appear strong and composed, even when life feels overwhelming. That narrative lingered in the background and shaped the way I processed my emotions. In my own experience, I was not only questioning whether to admit feelings of sadness but also weighing how to explain the sheer intensity of what I was going through in a way that would be understood and taken seriously. Experiencing this period as both a patient and a medical trainee shaped how I interpreted these moments, allowing me to understand the clinical explanations while also living the emotional reality in a way that medical knowledge alone cannot fully convey. My journey as both a patient and a medical trainee illustrates how these structural and cultural forces intersect with individual vulnerability, and why humanistic care must actively make room for emotional well-being.

What steadied me most was the support of my husband. He offered patience and reassurance when I struggled to explain what I was going through. He did not experience the same hormonal surges, but his willingness to be present helped anchor me in those moments. Reflecting back, I see how important it is to prepare support persons with education about postpartum emotional changes, because they can play a crucial role in recognizing distress and offering comfort when mothers themselves feel unsure how to express it.

Key Considerations

My experience reminded me that postpartum care is not only about physical recovery but also about emotional adjustment. While pain and physical healing are regularly assessed, emotions can be harder to notice unless they are directly discussed. Even brief, open-ended questions about how a new mother is feeling can provide reassurance and create an opening for meaningful conversation. For patients, that invitation matters and signals that emotions are part of the visit rather than an afterthought.

For Black mothers, these conversations carry particular importance. Systemic disparities in maternal health outcomes are well documented, with women of color often describing both perinatal and postpartum care where their concerns are dismissed or their voices minimized. 7 When that pattern is present, feeling heard and taken seriously can determine whether a mother feels safe speaking up again.

Cultural expectations around strength and composure may make it harder for some Black women to share the depth of their emotions.5,6,8 When clinicians bring gentle curiosity and normalize these feelings, it creates space for patients to open up without fear of judgment.

Support from the people around a new mother can make a profound difference in those early weeks after delivery. Mothers who lack support from partners, family, or friends experience higher rates of perinatal depression and anxiety, while even support offered from a distance can help reduce these symptoms. 9 In my own experience, my husband’s steady presence mattered most, and I believe that giving support people a little structure can help more families feel anchored in those first weeks.

In this way, humanistic care is not purely about adding new protocols, but about weaving awareness and compassion into interactions that already occur. Checking in about emotions, listening without rushing and responding with affirmations can turn routine encounters into powerful opportunities for connection and care.

Practical Recommendations

Based on my experience and the broader literature, I propose three strategies to strengthen humanistic support for new mothers:

1. Incorporate emotional well-being into routine check-ins, while also asking about daily functioning.

1. Incorporate emotional well-being into routine check-ins, while also asking about daily functioning.

Just as pain and physical recovery are regularly assessed, brief, open-ended questions about emotions can be a natural part of postpartum conversations. This routine acknowledgment affirms that the emotional side of recovery is just as important as the physical and provides mothers with permission to share what they are feeling. Building on this practice, recent research on Black women shows that maternal well-being is also shaped by how mothers manage daily tasks such as infant care, self-care, and balancing responsibilities. 10 By pairing questions about mood with gentle inquiries into functioning, providers can offer a fuller picture of postpartum health and strengthen the support already being provided.

2. Equip support persons with education.

2. Equip support persons with education.

Partners, family members, and other caregivers should receive practical information about postpartum emotional changes. Unlike mothers, they do not experience hormonal shifts firsthand, but they are often the first to notice signs of distress. Including them in education empowers them to provide timely reassurance and to encourage care-seeking when needed.

3. Model humanism in every patient interaction.

Humanistic care involves not only clinical excellence but also compassion, presence, and respect. By creating space for mothers to share their emotions without fear of judgment, clinicians can embody the values of safety and dignity that improve both patient experience and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Experiencing the baby blues showed me how different it is to live through postpartum emotions than to study them. As a Black mother and medical trainee, I felt how cultural expectations of strength shaped the way I processed and shared my feelings. Knowledge of physiology gave me context, but it could not capture the weight of those moments. What mattered most was compassion and understanding from those around me. Living these experiences while simultaneously training to care for patients deepened my awareness of how easily emotional realities can be overlooked in clinical discussions. This reminded me that humanistic care must recognize both cultural narratives and individual experiences, combining clinical expertise with empathy to truly support mothers. Institutions that prioritize these values can better align care with the lived realities of patients and create environments where all mothers feel seen and supported.
