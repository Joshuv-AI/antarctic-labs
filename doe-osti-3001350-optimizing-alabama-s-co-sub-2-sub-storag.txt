Introduction
The OASIS CarbonSAFE Phase II Project OASIS (Optimizing Alabama’s CO₂ Storage in Shelby
County) is a geologic and reservoir characterization study designed to evaluate deep saline
formations for potential long-term carbon dioxide (CO₂) storage in central Alabama near the
National Carbon Capture Center (NCCC) and Alabama Power’s Plant Gaston. The project centers
on understanding the potential of the Cambro-Ordovician Knox Group and underlying strata such
as the Conasauga and Rome Formations for geologic storage of CO2. These formations were
investigated as part of SECARB-USA (DE-FE0031830) and Project OASIS through the drilling
of two stratigraphic test wells to obtain electronic well logs, core, and sidewall core plugs. These
data provide direct measurements of porosity, permeability, and lithologic variability critical for
reservoir characterization. Complementing the well program, a limited 2D seismic survey was
conducted to help select the site for Westover #2 as part of SECARB-USA (DE-FE0031830), and
a more regional Seismic Exchange (SEI) seismic survey was licensed and interpreted to define
structural and stratigraphic frameworks in a new Static Earth Model (SEM), map reservoir
continuity, and to identify potential sealing intervals. Integrated with geologic and reservoir
modeling, these datasets form the basis for evaluating storage capacity, injectivity, and
containment. While this document endeavors to provide readers with a high-level overview of
Project OASIS activities and its suitability for subsequent CarbonSAFE Phases, such as a Phase
III effort. Other project deliverables and milestones will provide more details on individual
subjects.

Operating costs ($/ton CO₂ stored). For the development of revenue models, operating
expenses are held constant at 20% of total capital expenses for the two base case scenarios. To
calculate the cost per ton of CO2, the total debt payment to finance the project (assuming 100%
debt financing) is added to the anticipated operating expenses and divided by the total volume of
CO2 injected over a 12-year period (the timeframe over which 45Q can be captured). For the first
scenario (lower capture island CAPEX), the time integrated cost to transport and store CO2 for 12
years is $88.5/ton and $124.1/ton for the second base case scenario (higher capture island
CAPEX). When extrapolating out to 30 years of operation, the cost per ton for each scenario
decreases to $38.4/ton and $53.8/ton for scenarios 1 and 2, respectively, reflecting the importance
of the time integrated volume and revenue streams outside of the 45Q capture window such as
enhanced hydrocarbon recovery.

Internal rate of return analysis. A cashflow model was developed for the two scenarios

Technical Subsurface Data Evaluation

Geologic Framework
Depositional system and stratigraphic framework. The Alabama Valley and Ridge Province
extends across much of the northeastern portion of the State. It is a distinct geological region
characterized by a series of parallel valleys and ridges running southwest to northeast and
featuring rolling hills and elongated ridges formed by sedimentary strata. The thrust belt is
situated above an intracontinental rift complex locally called the Birmingham Graben and
referred to at Project OASIS as local basement.

The Coosa Deformed Belt is approximately 13,000 feet thick and ranges in age from Cambrian to
lower Pennsylvanian. It follows the orientation of the Valley and Ridge with dips to the southeast.
It is bounded to the northwest by the Coosa Synclinorium which ends at the Yellowleaf thrust
fault. Additionally, it is bounded to the southeast by the Pell City thrust fault which also strikes
northeast until it shifts to the southeast at Harpersville. There are several other bounding faults
and features that create a complex triangle zone in the field area (Figure 8). The triangle zone is
composed of heavily deformed Mississippian-Pennsylvanian sedimentary formations and thick
brittle Cambro-Ordovician carbonates.

Resource estimates (Prospective/Contingent Storage Resources). The lower members of the

3,000 to 5,000 ft thick. In the thrust fault structure, they are duplexed and may repeat two or three
times in sequence adding additional thickness to the storage complex. However, the reservoir
quality is highly variable with aggregate low permeability and porosity. Observed permeability
measurements in the Westover #2 are typically less than 1 mD and porosity averages between 0-
5%. Zones defined as “sweet spots” for reservoir simulation only had moderately better
measurements and were controlled by secondary porosity from fractures and faults which are
poorly characterized and defined due to limited well penetrations. This structural complexity
introduces significant uncertainty in estimating connected pore volumes and injectivity. This
makes it challenging to confidently extrapolate P10, P50, and P90 volumetrics across the broader
field area. As such, more characterization from additional wells and/or additional seismic would
be needed in a hypothetical Phase III to reduce the range of uncertainty.

Storage Reservoir
Characterization of target reservoir(s) including depth, thickness, porosity, permeability,
extent. The Alabama Valley and Ridge Province is underlain by a series of graben faults in the
Precambrian crystalline basement associated with the Appalachian Orogeny and locally referred
to as the Birmingham Graben or Basement. This rock is predominately granite. The
accommodation space created by these faults is filled by the Lower Cambrian Rome and
Conasauga Formations. The Rome Formation consists of shales, mudrocks, sandstone, anhydrite,
and carbonates. In the Arco-Anschutz #1 well there was a higher-than-average amount of
evaporites observed. The Upper Cambrian Conasauga Formation gradationally overlies the Rome
with gray shale, limestone, and dolostone. Near the Westover #2 well site a basal fault
decollement near the top of the Conasauga separates the lower basement and Cambrian strata
from the Cambro-Ordovician Knox Group. In this intermediate zone is the Ketona Dolomite,
which is diagenetic, and sometimes included as a basal member of the overlying Knox Group.

The Knox Group is a greater than 5,000 feet thick carbonate sequence composed of dolomite,
limestone, chert, and thin sandstones made up of several members that are difficult to
differentiate based on seismic reflectors and are thus often treated as an undifferentiated
stratigraphic sequence (Figure 9).

Confining System
Characterization of confining zone(s) including formation type, thickness, facies analysis.
The Floyd-Parkwood Formation shales represent a MUSHWAD (malleable unctuous shales
within a ductile duplex) system that developed during late Paleozoic Alleghanian thrust faulting
in the southern Appalachian foreland. These thick, regionally extensive shale units consist of
highly deformed, low-permeability mudstones and siltstones that act as an effective primary

23

confining system for underlying carbonate and sandstone reservoirs of the Cambro-Ordovician.
Their ductile nature allowed them to accommodate significant shortening through folding,
faulting, and detachment, resulting in a complex but laterally continuous seal. Total thicknesses
range from 800 feet at the crest of the fault ramp and fault bend fold to more than 6,000 feet off
structure in the flat lying structural panels between thrust faults.

Regional Considerations & Site Selection
Summary of site selection process. After the completion of the Westover #1 stratigraphic test in
early 2022, the project team determined that seismic data was necessary to delineate the
subsurface structures at Project OASIS before selecting a follow-up location for the Westover #2
well. The initial stratigraphic test wellbore (Westover #1) was unable to reach the target storage
formations due to adverse drilling conditions encountered in a tectonically thickened section of
geomechanically weak shales of referred to as MUSHWAD. Figure 1 shows the location of
Project OASIS in proximity to regional lime and cement plants, paper pulp plants, and Alabama
Power’s Plant Gaston. Following this first attempt, seventeen line-miles of 2D seismic data were
acquired by ExoDuas to improve structural interpretation and to identify a more favorable
location with reduced drilling risk.

The Westover #2 Strat Test was originally planned to be drilled on the same pad as the Westover
#1, which would have eliminated the impact of constructing a new well pad. However, the
thickness of the MUSHWAD section was unknown, and there was uncertainty regarding the
depth to the target formations. As a result, the decision was made to conduct a 2D seismic survey
across the Westervelt property to identify a new well site.

In November 2022, a multi-2D seismic reflection survey was acquired by ExoDuas across the
areas of interest. Three lines of receivers, totaling 17 line-miles, were laid out along roadways
with 55-foot receiver spacing and 110-foot source spacing. Four vibroseis trucks, sweeping 2–100
Hz and leaving all receivers active for all shots, produced a trace length of 5,000 ms. The post-
stack depth migrated lines from the survey imaged to the crystalline basement at depths greater
than 15,000 feet. Interpretation of the newly acquired 2D seismic data provided an improved
understanding of the geologic structures present within the field area, which led to the selection of
a new, optimized drilling location with reduced geologic risk (Figure 6 and Figure 9).

Land use and ROW impacts. The study area is comprised of a mixture of populated
municipalities as well as timber and farmland. There are existing power line rights-of-way

28

easements between Plant Gaston and the proposed storage field. Utilizing the exiting power line
easements could reduce pipeline construction impacts.

Conflicts with protected/sensitive areas and pore space ownership, access, and

for future operations. Modeling to date has shown that significant acreage (approximately 32,000
acres) will be needed for a CarbonSAFE eligible project. As of September 2025, no work has

Proximity to population centers. As shown in Figure 2, the proposed study area is located near
the town of Wilsonville, AL and is comprised of populated municipalities as well as surrounding
timber and farmlands. Any future work to develop a CO2 storage field would have the ability to
locate the proposed storage field in unpopulated areas and remain in the study area.

Regulatory approval and construction timeframe risks. Class VI permitting is currently
handled by EPA Region 4, however the State of Alabama (through its Oil and Gas Board) is
coordinating with the U.S. EPA and advancing toward receiving primacy over UIC Class VI
activities in the state. Permitting and construction timeframe risks are difficult to gauge at this

storage facility is contemplated in the future.

Use of consent-based siting principles and community input and conflicts with resource
development (surface/subsurface). Conflicts with resource development are not expected but
the study area does have some resource mining in support of the local cement industry. These

CO₂ Technical Analysis
Aggregate source analysis demonstrating ≥ 50 Mt CO₂ over 30 yrs. As a regional storage
facility, any future Phase III CarbonSAFE project will need to be designed to receive CO2 from a
wide range of emitters. The industries most likely to deliver CO2 to a future storage site in the
study area includes power generation, cement manufacturing and the pulp and paper industry. It
will be important for a future project to develop a CO2 source report to understand the anticipated
capture technology to be deployed at each site, the characteristics of the flue gas and captured

CO2, the timing and volume of CO2 to be delivered to the complex, as well as an overarching

Phase III Project Financing Plan
Currently, the project team is not considering the pursuit of a Phase III CarbonSAFE project at
this location. The limited storage potential of the Knox Formation and resulting capital costs for
injection and monitoring wells result in challenging economics. Several factors could influence
increased interest in a future Phase III CarbonSAFE project including, but not limited to:

 • Greater confidence in the ability to store CO2 in the Rome Formation resulting in a
 significant decrease in storage field development costs;
 • Nth-of-a-kind cost reductions in capture costs;
 • Policy changes to 45Q such as an increase in value and/or extension of term to receive
 the credit (12 years at the time of writing); and
 • Development of EOR or EGR facilities in the Black Warrior Basin.

Conclusions
Project OASIS responded to growing demand for CO2 storage opportunities in the Alabama
Valley and Ridge Province. This demand was driven by regional emitters (electricity generation,
lime and cement facilities) and their interest in evaluating proximal storage opportunities against
the infrastructure demands associated with transporting CO2 to known storage resources (e.g., the
Alabama Coastal Plain). The Project OASIS team completed a successful field program that
resulted in the materials necessary to evaluate subsurface potential for storing CO2 in the
Cambro-Ordovician section of the Alabama Valley and Ridge Province. These data were used to
generate both static earth models and dynamic simulations. Simulations show limited
opportunity, in the absence of development strategies that are currently atypical for CCUS
projects, such as perforating the entire Knox Group. Further, cashflow models illustrate
significant challenges associated with (1) the number of injection wells required to achieve
CarbonSAFE metrics (27 injectors to achieve 1.6 million metric tons of CO2) and (2) the cost to
capture CO2 from a natural gas combined cycle facility. Taken together, the two result in a project
that is currently uneconomic. However, there are opportunities for further exploration that may

30

yield better porosity and permeability in the deep Rome Formation. If proven, this, along with
Nth-of-a-kind improvement to capture technologies (cost reductions) would likely dramatically
improve the viability of CO2 storage in the region. Importantly, Project OASIS provided
invaluable lessons learned, data, and best practices for project developers evaluating CO2 storage
opportunities in similar terrain.

A. Overview of Testing and Monitoring Plan and Strategy
This initial Monitoring, Verification, and Accounting (MVA) Plan is designed to ensure the safe
and effective injection and storage of CO₂ in the Project OASIS Storage Complex, Shelby
County, Alabama. The monitoring plan is structured to comply with U.S. EPA UIC Class VI
requirements (40 CFR 146.90 and 146.91) and is based on the information gathered from the
geologic characterization resulting from project Oasis. In the event a commercial storage site is
developed near project Oasis, the following plan will need to be reviewed and updated as
additional geologic data becomes available.

Monitoring objectives are:
- Verify injection performance and plume behavior
- Confirm protection of Underground Sources of Drinking Water (USDWs)
- Track pressure development in the injection interval and confining units
- Provide data for Area of Review (AoR) reevaluation
- Ensure early detection of any migration or leakage

B. Carbon Dioxide Stream Analysis
The injected CO₂ will be captured from nearby sources including Alabama Power’s Plant Gaston,
cement plants, and pulp/paper facilities. The stream will be required to meet pipeline-quality
specifications with approximately 95% CO₂. Sampling will occur at the custody transfer point
before injection. Continuous on-site gas chromatography will measure CO₂ purity, N₂, O₂, H₂O,
and other impurities. Laboratory confirmation samples will test for sulfur compounds,
hydrocarbons, and other trace constituents.

C. Corrosion Monitoring
Corrosion coupons and monitoring loops will be installed in representative flowlines to monitor
the effects of supercritical CO₂ on casing, tubing, and completion materials. Coupons will be

32

removed and analyzed quarterly per NACE RP0775 standards. Additional subsurface monitoring
tools may also be utilized and could include ultrasonic logs, cement bond logs, pulsed neutron
capture logs (PNC), and annular pressure monitoring.

D. Injection Well Monitoring
OASIS injection wells are planned to target the Knox carbonate units at depths ranging from
approximately 4,500 to 8,500 feet. These thick, regionally extensive carbonates provide modest
reservoir characteristics for fluid injection and long-term containment. The primary confining
interval consists of the low-permeability, tectonically thickened Floyd-Parkwood section, which
acts as a tectonically continuous seal above the reservoir. Additional containment is provided by
secondary confining units within the upper portions of the Knox Group, which include dense,
low-porosity carbonate intervals. This includes the Lenoir, Newala, and Chepultepec units.

Well-based monitoring will employ a multi-tiered approach to ensure safe operations and to
verify containment performance. Surface and downhole instrumentation will record continuous
pressure, injection rate, and volume data to track reservoir response in real time. Fiber optic
cables installed in the wellbores will enable advanced monitoring through Distributed Acoustic
Sensing (DAS) and Distributed Temperature Sensing (DTS), providing high-resolution
measurements of fluid movement, thermal changes, and potential fracture activity along the
wellbores. To complement these continuous measurements, annual mechanical integrity tests
(MITs) will be conducted to verify casing, tubing, and packer integrity, ensuring that the wells
maintain isolation between injection zones and any overlying freshwater resources.

Oasis Well Design

Overview
The injection well design process relies on a number of analytical tools. First, numerical flow
modeling is used to understand the well outflow in terms of injection rates and pressures. These
results are then fed into a robust nodal analysis program that accounts for pressure-volume-
temperature (PVT) properties of CO2, friction pressures within the tubulars, hydrostatic effects,
and fluid velocity to optimize the required injection tubing size. This allows the remainder of the
telescoping well design to be constructed, ensuring sufficient space for downhole monitoring
technologies and completion equipment. Then, the stress exerted on the wellbore tubulars during
cementing, injection, shut in, workover, and abandonment should be calculated and analyzed to
confirm the weight and material yield strength tubulars selected are also capable of handling these
stresses over the life of the well

Nodal Analysis
The nodal analysis software Pipesim calculates the wells maximum injection rate, surface
injection pressure, bottom hole pressure, and the erosional velocity limits of the injection tubing.
By inputting these parameters for both the Knox & Deep Rome injection scenarios, multiple
tubing sizes were then evaluated, and the most suitable size was selected that meets the criteria
for the desired injection performance and maintains an erosional velocity ratio (EVR) within safe
limits, which can vary based on grade of material, typically ≤ 1.0 per API RP 14E. This ensures
the selected tubular size minimizes the risk of erosion, material degradation, and operational
failure while maintaining injectivity and pressure containment.

Tubing Design and Analysis Overview

Casing Strength Calculations
Casing stresses and loadings were modeled using SLB’s Tubing Design and Analysis Software
(TDAS). To ensure sufficient structural strength and mechanical integrity throughout the life of
the project, stresses were analyzed and calculated according to worst-case scenarios and tubular
specifications were selected accordingly. Minimum design factors are presented in Table and are
based on API TR 5C3 [2]. Design Factors are defined as the Strength Rating of the Casing /
Maximum Expected Load. As an example, if the calculated maximum pressure difference
between the annulus and inside the casing is 3,000 psi, the casing must be rated to withstand at
least 1.1 times that, or 3,300 psi. The maximum expected burst pressure, collapse pressure, and
tensile stress of each tubular was calculated according to the scenarios defined below, based on
fracture gradients, mud weight, depths, and minimum safety factors. Modeling was completed at
a static temperature gradient of 1.4 °F per 100 ft.

The casing and tubing materials were selected to be compatible with the CO2 stream and
formation fluids and the stresses induced throughout the sequestration project. SLB Integrated
Drilling Systems design standards were incorporated to develop the casing design load scenarios,
and SLB Completions group standards were incorporated to develop the tubing design load
scenarios.

The following additional assumptions were made during the design process for the injection well:

 1. Wall tolerance of 87.5% is assumed as per API standard TR 5C3.
 2. Temperature deration is considered on the design of the production casing string.
 3. The production casing is being proposed and engineered to comply with a casing
 designed to pass a two-thirds evacuation loading on collapse.

If the casing as designed is not available, final casing selection should be based on available
technical options that are in stock at the time of construction provided they satisfy or exceed the
design criteria discussed here.
