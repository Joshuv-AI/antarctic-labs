Mathematical Models of Evolution and Replicator Systems Dynamics Chapter 2: Geometry of the Fitness Surface and Trajectory Dynamics of Replicator Systems A. S. Bratus 1 , S. Drozhzhin 1 , T. Yakushkina 2


Abstract
This chapter studies the geometry of the fitness surface Σ \Sigma of replicator systems and its relationship to trajectory dynamics. Starting from the symmetric–antisymmetric decomposition 𝐀 = 𝐁 + 𝐂 {\bf A}={\bf B}+{\bf C} of the fitness landscape matrix, we derive a formula for the rate of change of mean fitness along trajectories and establish a necessary condition for its monotonicity. We show that in general, trajectories do not reach the maximum of Σ \Sigma even when a unique asymptotically stable equilibrium exists, and identify the precise algebraic conditions — expressed in terms of 𝐁 {\bf B} and 𝐂 {\bf C} — under which an equilibrium coincides with a local extremum of the fitness surface. A nontrivial class of matrices satisfying these conditions is provided by circulant matrices. We then establish the connection between local maxima of the fitness surface and evolutionarily stable states: an evolutionarily stable equilibrium always furnishes a local maximum, and under the identified conditions the converse holds as well. If the unique asymptotically stable equilibrium coincides with a local maximum, it is evolutionarily stable and realizes the global maximum of Σ \Sigma ; instability of the equilibrium forces the global maximum to the boundary of the simplex. The same geometric framework is extended to the general Lotka–Volterra system, where an analogue of mean fitness is identified and shown to share the same extremal properties. The results are illustrated through six examples including autocatalytic and hypercyclic replication, a parametric system exhibiting Andronov–Hopf bifurcation and heteroclinic cycles, and the Eigen quasispecies model.

1
Trajectory Dynamics on the Fitness Surface

Each trajectory
\gamma_{t}
of system (
1
) corresponds to a curve
\Gamma_{t}
on the surface
\Sigma
. The relationship between the geometry of the fitness surface
\Sigma
and the nature of the trajectory dynamics
\gamma_{t}
of replicator systems is interesting in its own right, and also in connection with Fisher’s fundamental theorem of natural selection
[
2
]
, which asserts that the mean fitness of a species does not decrease over the course of evolution.

The term “theorem” should not mislead the reader: Fisher’s assertion is not tied to any mathematical proof for a specific biological model but rather has the character of an additional postulate supplementing Darwin’s evolutionary triad.

Write the fitness landscape matrix
{\bf A}
of the replicator system (
1
) in the form

{\bf A}={\bf B}+{\bf C},\quad{\bf B}=\frac{{\bf A}+{\bf A}^{T}}{2},\quad{\bf C}=\frac{{\bf A}-{\bf A}^{T}}{2}.

(2)

If
{\bf A}
is symmetric, then
{\bf C}=0
. On the other hand, since
\Big({\bf Cu,u}\Big)=0
, the mean fitness of the system is completely determined by the matrix
{\bf B}
:

f({\bf u}(t))=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n}a_{ij}u_{i}u_{j}=\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)=\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big).

The following formula holds:

\dot{f}({\bf u}(t))=2\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}^{2}u_{i}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}\Big({\bf Cu}\Big)_{i}u_{i}-f^{2}({\bf u})\right),

(3)

\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}u_{j},\,\,\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}b_{ij}u_{j},\,\,\Big({\bf Cu}\Big)_{i}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Cu}\Big)_{i}u_{i},

representing the rate of change of mean fitness along the system’s trajectories.

When
{\bf A}
is symmetric,

\dot{f}({\bf u}(t))=2\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}^{2}u_{i}-\Bigg(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}u_{i}\Bigg)^{2}\right)\geqslant 0,

since in this case the expression gives the variance of the quantity
\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}
, where each value is realized with probability
u_{i}

[
4
]
.

Rewrite (
3
) in the form

\dot{f}({\bf u}(t))=f({\bf u})\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}v_{i}-f({\bf u})\right),

where

\displaystyle v_{i}=\dfrac{\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}u_{i}}{f({\bf u})},\quad i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n,

\displaystyle f({\bf u})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}u_{i}=\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)=\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big).

If
{\bf A}
is non-negative, then
0\leqslant v_{i}\leqslant 1
for
i=1,\,2,\,\ldots,\,n
, and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}=1
.

This allows the quantities
v_{i}
to be interpreted as a new tilted probability measure for the values
\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}
. The expression

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}v_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf v}(t)}^{\bf B}

then represents the expectation under that tilted measure. We denote by
\mathbb{E}_{{\bf u}(t)}^{\bf A}
the expectation of
\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}
under the original measure,
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
.

A necessary condition for the mean fitness to be non-decreasing along all trajectories of the system is

\mathbb{E}_{{\bf v}(t)}^{\bf B}\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{{\bf u}(t)}^{\bf A}.

When this condition holds, it follows by LaSalle’s theorem
[
5
,
6
]
that the trajectories of the replicator system converge to the largest invariant subset of

K=\left\{\dot{f}({\bf u})=0,\quad{\bf u}\in S_{n}\right\}.

One checks directly that every rest point of the replicator system lies in this set.

Suppose that the replicator system has a limit cycle
\gamma_{l}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
with period
T
, and the above inequality holds. Then at every point of the limit cycle

\mathbb{E}_{{\bf v}(t)}^{\bf B}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bf u}(t)}^{\bf A}.

This equality allows the equation of the limit cycle
\gamma_{l}
to be obtained in explicit form.

For a hypercycle, the cubic form in the components of
{\bf u}\in\gamma_{l}
is indefinite in sign. This means that along the cycle, mean fitness may both increase and decrease, while its time-averaged value remains constant.

We consider the following three examples.

Example 1. Autocatalytic replication.

Consider autocatalytic replication, described by the system

\dot{u}_{i}=u_{i}(u_{i}-f(t)),\quad i=\overline{1,n},\quad f(t)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2},\quad{\bf u}\in S_{n}.

(4)

The maximum fitness value (
f=1
) is attained at the vertices of the simplex
S_{n}
,

e^{k}=(0,\,\ldots,\,0,\,1,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0),\quad k=\overline{1,n},

where the unit occupies the
k
-th position, and the minimum is attained at the equilibrium
u_{i}=\dfrac{1}{n}
,
i=\overline{1,n}
.

The phase trajectories of the system (
4
), depending on the initial conditions, converge to one of the vertices, which acts as an attractor. Thus, the maximum fitness ultimately attained depends on which initial data are realized. This phenomenon is known as the
adaptive fitness landscape
and was first studied by S. Wright
[
7
,
8
]
.

\square

Example 2. Hypercyclic replication.

Let us now focus on replication defined by the matrix

{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&k_{1}\\
k_{2}&0&0\\
0&k_{3}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!.

The coordinates of the interior equilibrium are given by

\bar{u}_{i}=\frac{\bar{m}}{k_{i+1}},\quad i=\overline{1,3},\quad k_{4}=k_{1},\quad\bar{m}=\Bigg(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{3}\frac{1}{k_{i}}\Bigg)^{-1}\!\!.

The matrices
{\bf B}
and
{\bf C}
are computed directly from:

{\bf B}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&k_{2}&k_{1}\\
k_{2}&0&k_{3}\\
k_{1}&k_{3}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,\quad{\bf C}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-k_{2}&k_{1}\\
k_{2}&0&-k_{3}\\
-k_{1}&k_{3}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!.

The maximum of the fitness surface is attained at the point
{\bf u}_{m}
with

\displaystyle u_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{3}(k_{1}+k_{2})-k_{3}^{2}}{\sigma},\quad u_{2}=\frac{k_{1}(k_{2}+k_{3})-k_{1}^{2}}{\sigma},

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{2}(k_{1}+k_{3})-k_{2}^{2}}{\sigma},

where
\sigma=2(k_{1}k_{2}+k_{2}k_{3}+k_{1}k_{3})-(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}+k_{3}^{2})
.
For
k_{1}=0.25
,
k_{2}=0.3
,
k_{3}=0.35
, the unique stable equilibrium is

{\bf\bar{u}}=(0.3272,\,0.2803,\,0.3925),

while the maximum of the fitness surface is attained at

{\bf u}_{m}=(0.2692,\,0.3846,\,0.3462),

with

f({\bf\bar{u}})<f({\bf u}_{m}).

This inequality shows that the unique stable equilibrium does not provide the maximum of the surface
\Sigma
. Consequently, the curve
\Gamma_{t}
does not reach the peak of
\Sigma
.

\square

Example 3. A fitness landscape with multiple local extrema.

Consider the replicator system defined by the matrix

{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-\mu&1\\
1&0&0&-\mu\\
-\mu&1&0&0\\
0&-\mu&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!.

For
|\mu|<1
, the system is permanent, meaning its trajectories remain bounded away from the boundary of the simplex. At
\mu=0
, a Poincaré–Andronov–Hopf bifurcation occurs, giving rise to a stable limit cycle for
\mu>0
. For
|\mu|>1
the system ceases to be permanent
[
9
,
11
]
.

The matrix
{\bf B}
in this case is

{\bf B}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&-2\mu&1\\
1&0&1&-2\mu\\
-2\mu&1&0&1\\
1&-2\mu&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,

with eigenvalues

\lambda_{1}=1-\mu,\quad\lambda_{2,3}=\mu,\quad\lambda_{4}=-(1+\mu).

The orthogonal transformation reducing
{\bf B}
to diagonal form is given by

{\bf U}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&-\sqrt{2}/2&0&1/2\\
1/2&0&-\sqrt{2}/2&-1/2\\
1/2&\sqrt{2}/2&0&1/2\\
1/2&0&\sqrt{2}/2&-1/2\\
\end{pmatrix}\!.

In the new variables, the mean fitness is

f({\bf w})=(1-\mu)w_{1}^{2}+\mu(w_{2}^{2}+w_{3}^{2})-(1+\mu)w_{4}^{2}.

The constraint
\Big({\bf w,U^{T}I}\Big)=1
gives
w_{1}=\dfrac{1}{2}
. The simplex
S_{4}
maps to the convex set

W_{4}=\left\{{\bf w}:\begin{array}[]{l}-\dfrac{1}{4}-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}w_{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}w_{4}\geqslant 0,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{4}-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}w_{3}-\dfrac{1}{2}w_{4}\geqslant 0,\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}w_{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}w_{4}\geqslant 0\end{array}\right\}\!.

For
|\mu|<1
, the maximum of the fitness surface is attained when
w_{4}=0
. The set
W_{4}
is then a square in the
(w_{2},w_{3})
-plane with vertices at
p_{i}=\{\pm\sqrt{2}/4,\,\pm\sqrt{2}/4\}
, and at each of these four vertices the fitness landscape surface attains the same maximum value:

f(p_{i})=\frac{1}{4}(1-\mu)+\frac{1}{4}\mu=0.25.

In the new coordinates, the interior equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}=(1/4,1/4,1/4,1/4)
corresponds to
{\bf\bar{w}}=(1/2,0,0,0)
, and the fitness surface at this point equals

f({\bf\bar{w}})=\frac{1}{4}-\frac{\mu}{4}.

For
-1<\mu<0
this equilibrium is asymptotically stable, and the fitness surface attains its global maximum there since
f({\bf\bar{w}})>f(p_{i})
. In general, the fitness surface has five peaks; the central one corresponds to the equilibrium. Orbits
\gamma_{t}
converge to
{\bf\bar{w}}
, while the curve
\Gamma_{t}
on
\Sigma
approaches the central peak.

For
0<\mu<1
, an Andronov–Hopf bifurcation produces a limit cycle, and
\gamma_{t}
converges to a closed orbit surrounding
{\bf\bar{w}}
. In this regime, mean fitness at the vertices
p_{i}
exceeds mean fitness at the central peak. On the simplex
S_{4}
, the points
p_{i}
correspond to
P_{1}=(0,\,0,\,1/2,\,1/2)
,
P_{2}=(1/2,\,0,\,0,\,1/2)
,
P_{3}=(1/2,\,1/2,\,0,\,0)
,
P_{4}=(0,\,1/2,\,1/2,\,0)
.

For
\mu>1
, the points
P_{i}
still furnish the maximum of
f({\bf u})
, but at the central point
f({\bf\bar{w}})<0
, so the global minimum of the fitness landscape is attained there. This case corresponds to a heteroclinic cycle.

Finally, for
\mu<-1
, the equilibrium
{\bf\bar{w}}
is unstable and the maximum mean fitness
f({\bf w})
is achieved at
w_{2}=w_{3}=0
, corresponding to the points
Q_{1,2}=(1/2,\,0,\,0,\,\pm 1/2)
, where
f(Q_{1,2})=-\dfrac{\mu}{2}
and consequently
f(Q_{1,2})>f({\bf\bar{w}})
. A heteroclinic cycle again arises in this case.

.

2
Necessary Conditions for a Local Extremum of the Fitness Surface

Mathematically, the mean fitness function is a quadratic form defined by the symmetric matrix
{\bf B}
:

f({\bf u}(t))=\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)=\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big).

Therefore, there exists an orthogonal transformation
{\bf U}
such that

{\bf U^{T}BU}={\bf\Lambda}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\,\lambda_{2},\,\ldots,\,\lambda_{n}),

where
\lambda_{i}
are the real eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
.

The change of variables
{\bf u=Uv}
brings the quadratic form to its canonical (diagonal) form

f({\bf v})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}^{+}w_{i}^{2}+\sum\limits_{j=k+1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{-}w_{j}^{2},

where
\lambda_{i}^{+}
and
\lambda_{j}^{-}
denote the positive and negative eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
, under the assumption that
\det{\bf B}\neq 0
.

Following standard geometric terminology, we call the fitness surface
elliptic
if all
\lambda_{i}>0
or all
\lambda_{i}<0
(
{i=\overline{1,n}}
), and
hyperbolic
if there exist
i\neq j
such that
\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}<0
.

In Example 1 (autocatalytic replication), the fitness surface is elliptic, whereas in Example 3 it may be either elliptic or hyperbolic depending on the parameter
\mu
.

The transformation
{\bf U}
maps the simplex
S_{n}
to the convex set
W_{n}
:

\displaystyle W_{n}=\left\{{\bf v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\Big({\bf v,U^{T}I}\Big)=1,\,\,{\bf Uv}\geqslant 0,\right.

\displaystyle\left.{\bf I}=(1,\,1,\,\ldots,\,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

Finding the extrema of the fitness surface, therefore, reduces to the mathematical programming problem

f({\bf v})\to{\rm extr},\quad{\bf v}\in W_{n}.

A solution always exists, but it does not need to be unique
[
10
]
.

An alternative approach to locating extrema of the fitness surface uses classical Lagrange multipliers. Consider the Lagrangian:

\pounds({\bf u},\mu)=\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big)-\mu_{0}\Bigg(\Big({\bf u,I}\Big)-1\Bigg)+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}u_{j},

where the vector
\mu=(\mu_{0},\,\mu_{1},\,\ldots,\,\mu_{n})
,
\mu_{j}\geqslant 0
,
j=\overline{1,n}
, represents the Lagrange multipliers arising from the constraint
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
.

If
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
is a local maximum of the quadratic form
\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big)
, there exists a nonzero vector of Lagrange multipliers
\mu
such that

\frac{\partial\pounds}{\partial u_{j}}({\bf u},\mu)\leqslant 0,\quad u_{j}\frac{\partial\pounds}{\partial u_{j}}({\bf u},\mu)=0,\quad j=\overline{1,n}.

When
{\bf u}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
, the necessary conditions for a local extremum take the simpler form

\frac{\partial\pounds}{\partial u_{j}}({\bf u},\mu_{0})=0,\quad j=\overline{1,n},

which amounts to

{\bf Bu}=\mu_{0}{\bf I},\quad{\bf I}=(1,\,1,\,\ldots,\,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(5)

If
{\bf B}
is non-singular, the constraint
\Big({\bf u,I}\Big)=1
gives
\mu_{0}=\Big({\bf B^{-1}I,I}\Big)
. If
{\bf B}
is singular, any local extremum lies on the boundary of the simplex rather than in its interior.

Suppose
{\bf u}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a strict local maximum of the fitness surface. Then for sufficiently small
\varepsilon\neq 0
,

\Big({\bf B}({\bf u}+\varepsilon{\bf w}),{\bf u}+\varepsilon{\bf w}\Big)-\Big({\bf Bu,u}\Big)<0,

where
{\bf w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
{\bf u}+\varepsilon{\bf w}\in S_{n}
, i.e.,
\Big(({\bf u}+\varepsilon{\bf w}),{\bf I}\Big)=1
. The latter condition gives
\Big({\bf w,I}\Big)=0
. For any
\varepsilon\neq 0
we then have

2\varepsilon\Big({\bf Bu,w}\Big)+\varepsilon^{2}\Big({\bf Bw,w}\Big)<0,

which is possible only if

\Big({\bf Bu,w}\Big)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Big({\bf Bw,w}\Big)<0.

(6)

Consider a basis for the
(n-1)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
orthogonal to
{\bf I}=(1,\,1,\,\ldots,\,1)
:

\displaystyle r_{1}=(1,\,-1,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0),\quad r_{2}=(1,\,0,\,-1,\,\ldots,\,0),\quad\ldots,

\displaystyle r_{n-1}=(1,\,0,\,0,\,\ldots,\,-1).

Any
{\bf w}
may be written as

{\bf w}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n-1}\xi_{k}r_{k},\quad\xi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}.

To verify conditions (
6
) it therefore suffices to analyze the sign of the quadratic form

\Big({\bf Bw,w}\Big)=\sum\limits_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\beta_{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j},\quad\beta_{ij}=\Big({\bf Br_{i},r_{j}}\Big)

on the linear manifold

\Big({\bf Bu,w}\Big)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n-1}\alpha_{i}\xi_{i}=0,\quad\alpha_{i}=\Big({\bf Bu,r_{i}}\Big).

3
Coincidence of a Local Extremum of the Fitness Surface with an Equilibrium

A natural question arises: under what conditions does a stationary equilibrium coincide with an extremum of the fitness surface?

The preceding examples make clear that such a coincidence is not generic. In hypercyclic replication (Example 2), the maximum of the fitness surface does not coincide with the unique equilibrium, whereas in Example 3, coincidence occurs under certain conditions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
{\bf B}
and
{\bf C}
be the matrices in decomposition (
2
). An isolated equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in S_{n}
of the system (
1
) coincides with an extremal point of the fitness surface if and only if there exists a non-singular matrix
{\bf M}=\Big(\mu_{i,k}\Big)_{i,k=\overline{1,n}}
such that

{\bf C=MB},

(7)

together with

{\bf MI}=0,\quad{\bf I}=(1,\,1,\,\ldots,\,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(8)

Proof.

From decomposition (
2
), at any stationary equilibrium of the replicator system, the following equality must hold:

{\bf A\bar{u}=B\bar{u}+C\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I},\quad{\bf I}=(1,\,1,\,\ldots,\,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(9)

where
f({\bf\bar{u}})
is the stationary fitness value.

The solution of (
9
) satisfies (
5
) if and only if
{\bf C\bar{u}}=0
and
\mu_{0}=f({\bf\bar{u}})
.

We prove the necessity of (
7
) and (
8
). Taking the inner product of (
9
) with
{\bf\bar{u}}
and using
\Big({\bf C\bar{u},\bar{u}}\Big)=0
gives
\Big({\bf B\bar{u},\bar{u}}\Big)=f({\bf\bar{u}})=\mu_{0}
.

We now show that for a solution of

{\bf B\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I}

(10)

to satisfy
{\bf C\bar{u}}=0
, conditions (
7
) and (
8
) are both necessary and sufficient.

If these conditions hold, then

{\bf C\bar{u}=MB\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf MI}=0.

Conversely, by the linear independence of the columns of
{\bf B}
, a representation (
7
) exists for some matrix
{\bf M}
. If
{\bf\bar{u}}
solves (
10
), then
{\bf C\bar{u}}=0
and consequently
{\bf MI}=0
.
∎

Example 4. Hypercycle replication and extremum conditions.

Returning to Example 2, we show that conditions (
7
) and (
8
) imply
{\bf\bar{u}_{max}}={\bf\bar{u}}
.

We have

c_{1}=\frac{k_{1}}{k_{3}}b_{1}-\frac{k_{2}}{k_{3}}b_{3},\quad c_{2}=-\frac{k_{3}}{k_{1}}b_{1}+\frac{k_{2}}{k_{1}}b_{3},\quad c_{3}=\frac{k_{3}}{k_{2}}b_{1}-\frac{k_{1}}{k_{2}}b_{2}.

Conditions (
7
) and (
8
) are satisfied if and only if
k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3}=k
. Taking
k=1
, the eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
are
\lambda_{1}=1
,
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=1/2
. The orthogonal change of the variables
{\bf u=Uw}
that brings the mean fitness to the canonical form
w_{1}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2}(w_{2}^{2}+w_{3}^{2})
is given by

{\bf U}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{3}/3&0&2/\sqrt{6}\\
\sqrt{3}/3&-\sqrt{2}/2&-1/\sqrt{6}\\
\sqrt{3}/3&\sqrt{2}/2&-1/\sqrt{6}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

From
\Big({\bf u,I}\Big)=\Big({\bf w,U^{T}I}\Big)=1
it follows that
w_{1}=\sqrt{3}/3
, and the simplex
S_{3}
maps to the convex set

W_{3}=\left\{{\bf w}:\begin{array}[]{l}w_{1}=\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3},\\[6.0pt]
\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}w_{2}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}w_{3}+\dfrac{1}{3}\geqslant 0,\\[6.0pt]
-\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}w_{2}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}w_{3}+\dfrac{1}{3}\geqslant 0,\\[6.0pt]
w_{3}\geqslant-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\end{array}\right\}\!.

In the new coordinates, the fitness surface is

f({\bf w})=\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{2}(w_{2}^{2}+w_{3}^{2}),

a paraboloid with apex at
w_{2}=w_{3}=0
. In this special case, the equilibrium coincides with the maximum of the fitness surface.

A nontrivial class of matrices satisfying all conditions of Theorem
3.1
is provided by the so-called circulant matrices.

{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\ldots&a_{n-1}&a_{n}\\
a_{n}&a_{1}&\ldots&a_{n-2}&a_{n-1}\\
\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots&\ldots\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&\ldots&a_{n}&a_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}\!.

Indeed, equation
{\bf B\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I}
then has the solution
{\bf\bar{u}}_{1}={\bf\bar{u}}_{2}=\ldots={\bf\bar{u}}_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n}
,
f({\bf\bar{u}})=\dfrac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
, and one directly verifies that this solution lies in the kernel of
{\bf C}
. Thus, the interior rest point of a replicator system with a circulant matrix is always a local extremum of the fitness surface.

\square

\square

It is of interest to find sufficient conditions for the mean fitness to be monotone along trajectories of a general replicator system.

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose
{\bf B}
is non-singular and the matrix
{\bf Q=CB^{-1}}
is positive semi-definite, i.e.
\Big({\bf Q\xi,\xi}\Big)\geqslant 0
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the mean fitness of the replicator system (
1
) is a monotone function, and the system has no closed trajectories in
\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
.

Proof.

Consider formula (
3
) for the rate of change of mean fitness along trajectories. By the stated hypothesis,

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}\Big({\bf Cu}\Big)_{i}u_{i}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n}q_{ij}\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t)u_{i}(t),

(11)

where
q_{ij}
are the entries of
{\bf Q}
and
\xi_{i}(t)=\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}
,
i=\overline{1,n}
.

Since
{\bf u}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
, this expression is non-negative, so
\dot{f}({\bf u}(t))>0
.

If the system had a closed trajectory, we would need

\int\limits_{t}^{T+t}\dot{f}(\tau)\,d\tau=0,

which is impossible when the form (
11
) is positive semi-definite.
∎

4
Evolutionarily Stable State as a Maximum of the Fitness Surface

Another notable property of local maxima of the fitness surface
\Sigma
is their connection to the concept of an evolutionarily stable state (ESS), introduced in
[
7
]
in the context of analyzing replicator dynamics from a game-theoretic perspective. The payoff matrix is taken to be the matrix
{\bf A}
of the replicator system (
1
). Each entry
a_{ij}
represents the payoff to species
i
when interacting with species
j
, and this payoff is assumed to affect the species’ replication rate. Only pure strategies are used: each species employs a fixed strategy (the row
a_{i1},\,a_{i2},\,\ldots,\,a_{in}
) and offspring inherit the parental strategy. The entries
a_{ij}
are thus the payoffs when strategy
i
is played against strategy
j
. The fitness of species
i
against the entire population is

f_{i}({\bf u})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}u_{j}=\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i},\quad{\bf u}\in S_{n},

and the mean population payoff is

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}u_{i}=\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)=f({\bf u}),

which is the mean fitness.

The advantage of strategy
i
is

\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}-\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)=\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}-f.

The growth rate of species
i
is assumed proportional to this advantage:

\dot{u}_{i}=u_{i}\Bigg(\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}-f\Bigg),\quad i=\overline{1,n},\quad{\bf u}\in S_{n},

which is precisely the general replicator system (
1
).

This game-theoretic framework allows the methods of
[
7
]
to be applied to replicator systems, in particular the concepts of Nash equilibrium and evolutionarily stable state.

We recall the following definitions
[
9
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

A state
{\bf\bar{u}}\in S_{n}
is a
symmetric Nash equilibrium
of the replicator system (
1
) if

\Big({\bf u,A\bar{u}}\Big)\leqslant\Big({\bf\bar{u},A\bar{u}}\Big)

(12)

for all
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
.

Definition 4.2
.

A state
{\bf\bar{u}}\in S_{n}
is
evolutionarily stable
if

\Big({\bf\bar{u},Au}\Big)>\Big({\bf u,Au}\Big)

(13)

for all
{\bf u}\neq{\bf\bar{u}}
in some neighborhood of
{\bf\bar{u}}
in
S_{n}
.

We are interested in the relationship between states
{\bf\bar{u}}\in S_{n}
satisfying (
12
) or (
13
) and the extremal properties of the fitness surface
\Sigma
.

Theorem 4.3
.

If an equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in S_{n}
is evolutionarily stable, then it is a strict local maximum of the fitness surface
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Let
{\bf\bar{u}}
be evolutionarily stable and consider
{\bf u}={\bf\bar{u}}+\varepsilon{\bf w}\in S_{n}
. Since
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
, we have
\Big({\bf w,I}\Big)=0
. Condition (
13
) yields

\varepsilon\Big({\bf w,A\bar{u}}\Big)+\varepsilon^{2}\Big({\bf w,Aw}\Big)<0.

Since
{\bf\bar{u}}
is an equilibrium,
{\bf A\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I}
, so the first term vanishes and

\Big({\bf w,Aw}\Big)<0,\quad{\bf w}\neq 0.\quad

∎

The converse holds only under the additional conditions (
7
) and (
8
).

Theorem 4.4
.

Suppose conditions (
7
) and (
8
) of Theorem
3.1
hold and
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a strict local maximum of the fitness surface
\Sigma
. Then
{\bf\bar{u}}
is evolutionarily stable.

Proof.

For any
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
,

\Big({\bf\bar{u},Au}\Big)=\Big({\bf\bar{u},Bu}\Big)+\Big({\bf\bar{u},Cu}\Big)=\Big({\bf\bar{u},Bu}\Big)-\Big({\bf C\bar{u},u}\Big)=\Big({\bf B\bar{u},u}\Big).

On the other hand,

f({\bf\bar{u}})=\Big({\bf B\bar{u},u}\Big)=\Big({\bf A\bar{u},\bar{u}}\Big).

Since
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a strict local maximum,

\Big({\bf A\bar{u},\bar{u}}\Big)>\Big({\bf u,Au}\Big)\quad\forall\,{\bf u}\in S_{n}.

Therefore
\Big({\bf\bar{u},Au}\Big)>\Big({\bf u,Au}\Big)
.
∎

Regarding Nash equilibria: if conditions of Theorem
3.1
hold and the equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a local extremum of the fitness surface, then it is a Nash equilibrium. Indeed, condition (
5
) holds and
{\bf A\bar{u}=B\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I}
, so (
12
) is satisfied with equality for all
{\bf u}\in S_{n}
.

Corollary 4.5
.

If the equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a strict local maximum of the fitness surface for a replicator system with a circulant matrix, then
{\bf\bar{u}}
is a globally stable equilibrium.

Proof.

As shown above, conditions (
7
) and (
8
) are always satisfied for circulant systems. It is known
[
9
]
that an evolutionarily stable state
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is a globally stable equilibrium.
∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Suppose the unique equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
of system (
1
) coincides with a local maximum of the fitness surface
\Sigma
. If this equilibrium is asymptotically stable, it is evolutionarily stable and furnishes the global maximum of
\Sigma
on
S_{n}
. If
{\bf\bar{u}}
is unstable, the global maximum of the fitness surface is attained on the boundary
\partial S_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose the asymptotically stable equilibrium coincides with a local maximum. Consider the Lyapunov function

V({\bf u})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-\bar{u}_{i}\ln{\frac{u_{i}}{\bar{u}_{i}}}\Big]>0,\quad{\bf u}\neq{\bf\bar{u}},\quad V({\bf\bar{u}})=0.

Then

\displaystyle\dot{V}({\bf u})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\dot{u}_{i}\Big(1-\frac{\bar{u}_{i}}{u_{i}}\Big)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}-\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)\Big](u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})=

\displaystyle=\Big({\bf Au,u-\bar{u}}\Big)<0,\quad{\bf u}\neq{\bf\bar{u}}.

This inequality implies that
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is evolutionarily stable and hence globally stable in
\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
by Corollary
4.5
.

Set
{\bf u}={\bf\bar{u}+\xi}
. Since
{\bf\bar{u},u}\in S_{n}
,

\Big({\bf\xi,I}\Big)=\Big({\bf u,I}\Big)-\Big({\bf\bar{u},I}\Big)=0,\quad{\bf I}=(1,1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Using
{\bf A\bar{u}}={\bf I}\bar{f}
, we obtain

\Big({\bf Au,u-\bar{u}}\Big)=\Bigg({\bf A}\Big({\bf u-\bar{u}}\Big),{\bf u-\bar{u}}\Bigg)=\Big({\bf A\xi,\xi}\Big).

Global stability therefore gives

\Big({\bf A\xi,\xi}\Big)=\Big({\bf B\xi,\xi}\Big)<0

for all
{\bf\xi}={\bf u-\bar{u}}
with
{\bf\bar{u},u}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
. This shows that the fitness surface attains its global maximum in
\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
, and by continuity and boundedness of
f({\bf u})
this property extends to all of
S_{n}
.

If the unique equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
is unstable, then

\Big({\bf\bar{u},Au}\Big)<\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big)

in some neighborhood
U_{{\bf\bar{u}}}
. Since

\Big({\bf Au,\bar{u}}\Big)\leqslant\max\limits_{{\bf u}\in S_{n}}\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big),\quad{\bf u}\in U_{{\bf\bar{u}}},

and by the conditions of the theorem

{\bf C}={\bf MB},\quad{\bf MI}=0,\quad{\bf B\bar{u}}=f({\bf\bar{u}}){\bf I},

we have

\displaystyle\Big({\bf\bar{u},Au}\Big)=\Big({\bf\bar{u},(B+C)u}\Big)=\Big({\bf\bar{u},Bu}\Big)+\Big({\bf\bar{u},Cu}\Big)=

\displaystyle=\Big({\bf B\bar{u},u}\Big)-\Big({\bf C\bar{u},u}\Big)=f({\bf\bar{u}})\Big({\bf I,u}\Big)-\Big({\bf MB\bar{u},u}\Big)=f({\bf\bar{u}}).

Therefore,

f({\bf\bar{u}})<\max\limits_{{\bf u}\in S_{n}}\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big).

The maximum of the fitness surface is thus not attained at
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{n}
. By Theorem
4.3
, it cannot be attained at any interior local maximum, so the global maximum of
\Sigma
is attained on
\partial S_{n}
.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

[
13
]

If the equilibrium of a replicator system with a circulant matrix is asymptotically stable, it is evolutionarily stable and furnishes the global maximum of the fitness surface
\Sigma
on
S_{n}
. If it is unstable, the global maximum is attained on the boundary of the simplex.

Example 5. A circulant replicator system.

Consider the matrices
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
:

{\bf A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
a_{3}&0&a_{1}&a_{2}\\
a_{2}&a_{3}&0&a_{1}\\
a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,\quad{\bf B}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\alpha&\beta&\alpha\\
\alpha&0&\alpha&\beta\\
\beta&\alpha&0&\alpha\\
\alpha&\beta&\alpha&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\!,

\alpha=\frac{a_{1}+a_{3}}{2},\quad\beta=a_{2}.

The eigenvalues of
{\bf B}
are

\lambda_{1}=2\alpha+\beta,\quad\lambda_{2,3}=-\beta,\quad\lambda_{4}=\beta-2\alpha.

The first eigenvalue corresponds to the eigenvector
{\bf u}^{1}=(1,\,1,\,1,\,1)
, which is orthogonal to the simplex
S_{4}
and is thus excluded from consideration.

For
\beta>0
and
\beta<2\alpha
, the equilibrium
{\bf\bar{u}}=(1/4,\,1/4,\,1/4,\,1/4)
is asymptotically stable, and the maximum of the fitness surface is

f({\bf\bar{u}})=\frac{2\alpha+\beta}{4}.

For
\beta<0
, the maximum of
\Sigma
is attained at the boundary points of the simplex
S_{4}
:
p_{1}=(1/2,\,1/2,\,0,\,0)
,
p_{2}=(0,\,1/2,\,1/2,\,0)
,
p_{3}=(1/2,\,0,\,0,\,1/2)
,
p_{4}=(0,\,0,\,1/2,\,1/2)
:

f(p_{i})=\frac{\alpha}{2}>\frac{2\alpha+\beta}{4},\quad\beta<0.

\square

We now turn to Eigen’s quasispecies model. In this case, the fitness surface is a linear function defined on the convex set
S_{l}
:

{\bf\bar{w}}({\bf p}(t))=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}p_{i}(t),\quad{\bf p}(t)\in S_{l}.

Consequently, both the maximum and minimum of
{\bf\bar{w}}(t)
are attained at the vertices of
S_{l}
.

Assuming
w_{1}>w_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant w_{l}
,

\max_{{\bf p}\in S_{l}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}w_{i}p_{i}=w_{1},\quad\min_{{\bf p}\in S_{l}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}w_{i}p_{i}=w_{l}.

Here,
{\bf p}^{1}=(1,\,0,\,\ldots,\,0)
and
{\bf p}^{l}=(0,\,\ldots,\,0,\,1)
are the vertices of the simplex at which the maximum and minimum of the fitness surface are respectively attained.

From the Eigen model
[
14
]
, one derives the following expression for the rate of change of the fitness surface along system trajectories:

{\bf\dot{\bar{w}}}({\bf p}(t))=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{l}w_{i}\dot{p}_{i}(t)=\Big({\bf QWp}(t),{\bf w}\Big)-{\bf\bar{w}}^{2}(t).

(14)

From this expression, we can determine the sign of
\dot{\bar{w}}
along trajectories emanating from the vertices
{\bf p}^{1}
and
{\bf p}^{l}
of
S_{l}
:

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{\bar{w}}}({\bf p}^{1})

\displaystyle=w_{1}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}q_{1j}w_{j}-w_{1}^{2}<w_{1}^{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}q_{1j}-w_{1}^{2}=0,

\displaystyle{\bf\dot{\bar{w}}}({\bf p}^{l})

\displaystyle=w_{l}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}q_{lj}w_{j}-w_{l}^{2}>w_{l}^{2}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{l}q_{lj}-w_{l}^{2}=0.

By continuity, these inequalities remain in force for trajectories starting at points
{\bf p}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{l}
in a neighborhood of
{\bf p}^{1}
or
{\bf p}^{l}
. Consequently, along trajectories
\gamma_{t}
of the quasispecies system, mean fitness may either increase or decrease. This does not, however, mean that the equilibrium
{\bf\bar{p}}\in\mathrm{int}\,S_{l}
fails to be extremal for trajectories
\gamma_{t}
as

. In particular, from formula (

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}{\bf\dot{\bar{w}}}({\bf p}(t))=\Big({\bf QW\bar{p},w}\Big)-{\bf\bar{w}}^{2}=0,

\displaystyle{\bf\bar{w}}=\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}{\bf\bar{w}(p}(t)),\quad{\bf\bar{p}}=\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}{\bf p}(t).

Direct computation shows that

\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\frac{d^{s}}{dt^{s}}{\bf\bar{w}(p}(t))=0,\quad s=2,\,3,\,\ldots,

so
{\bf\bar{w}(p}(t))\to{\bf\bar{w}}
exponentially as
t\to+\infty
.

The Lotka–Volterra System

Consider the general Lotka–Volterra system in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
:

\displaystyle\frac{du_{i}}{dt}=u_{i}\Big(r_{i}-\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}\Big),\quad i=\overline{1,n},

(15)

\displaystyle\Big({\bf Au}\Big)_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}u_{j},\quad{\bf r}=(r_{1},\,r_{2},\,\ldots,\,r_{n}).

(16)

The natural question is what function plays the role of mean fitness for this system. This function was proposed in
[
15
]
:

F({\bf u})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}u_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\Big({\bf Au,u}\Big).

A direct calculation gives

\dot{F}({\bf u}(t))=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\Big(r_{i}-\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}\Big)^{2}+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big({\bf Cu}\Big)_{i}\Big(\Big({\bf Bu}\Big)_{i}-r_{i}\Big)u_{i},

(17)

using decomposition (
2
) of
{\bf A}
.

Formula (
17
) is the analogue of (
3
). In particular, when
{\bf A}
is symmetric,
\dot{F}({\bf u}(t))\geqslant 0
, just as for replicator systems.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
be the unique stable equilibrium of system (
16
). This equilibrium furnishes the global maximum of the surface
z=F({\bf u})
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
if and only if the matrix
{\bf C}
in decomposition (
2
) admits the representation

{\bf C=MB},\quad|{\bf M}|\neq 0,\quad{\bf Mr=0}.

(18)

Proof.

Assuming (
18
) and
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, we have

{\bf A\bar{u}}={\bf r}\text{ with }|{\bf A}|=0.

Since
{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is a stable equilibrium, the matrix
{\bf B}
in (
2
) is positive definite
[
15
]
.

Condition (
18
) is sufficient to ensure

{\bf B\bar{u}}={\bf r}.

(19)

The necessary condition for
{\bf\bar{u}}
to be a critical point of

is satisfied:

\frac{\partial F({\bf u})}{\partial u_{k}}\Bigg|_{{\bf u}={\bf\bar{u}}}=r_{k}-\Big({\bf B\bar{u}}\Big)_{k}=0,\quad k=\overline{1,n}.

The sufficient condition for a maximum is the negative definiteness of the Hessian

z_{kj}=\frac{\partial^{2}F({\bf\bar{u}})}{\partial u_{k}\partial u_{j}}=-b_{kj},\quad k,j=\overline{1,n}.

This holds since

is therefore strictly concave on

{\bf\bar{u}}\in\mathrm{int}\,\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}

The necessity of (

for replicator systems.
∎

Corollary 5.2

Suppose condition (

In this case,

It is known

The inequality

6
Conclusion

In general, trajectories of replicator systems do not reach the maximum of the fitness surface, even when a unique asymptotically stable equilibrium exists (Example 2). Coincidence is possible only in special cases where the dynamics of the replicator system are aligned in a specific way with the geometry of the fitness surface. Additional conditions are required for this to occur. A nontrivial class of matrices satisfying these conditions is furnished by circulant and symmetric matrices.

There is a clear connection between evolutionarily stable equilibria and states furnishing local and global maxima of the fitness surface. An evolutionarily stable equilibrium furnishes a local maximum of the fitness surface. If the unique asymptotically stable equilibrium is a local maximum of the fitness surface, then it is evolutionarily stable and realizes the global maximum. If instead the unique equilibrium is unstable, the global maximum is attained on the boundary. The fitness surface of the general Lotka–Volterra system shares these properties. When the fitness surface has multiple local maxima, closed and heteroclinic trajectories may appear, arising when the unique equilibrium of the system is unstable (Example 3).
