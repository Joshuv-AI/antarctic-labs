ABSTRACT
Collecting radiation measurements can be a hazardous task, especially in the presence of highly
active sources. Various scenarios necessitate source search, classification, and quantification,
often with limited or no a priori information. Activities such as disaster mitigation, emergency
response, environmental monitoring, and site remediation may involve dangerous radioactive
sources. In these situations, there is a pressing need for remote monitoring capabilities that
protect human operators from potential harm and enhance adherence to the principle of “As
Low as Reasonably Achievable” (ALARA) for radiation doses.
The first step toward achieving remote radiation measurement capabilities is the remote
operation of a radiation sensor. Once this milestone is reached, the next challenge is to
integrate this remote sensing capability into suitable actuation agents, collectively referred to as
uncrewed systems (UxS). These systems include familiar platforms such as robotic
quadrupeds, aerial multirotor vehicles, and ground vehicles, any of which may be teleoperated,
act autonomously, or utilize a combination of both.
A critical factor in achieving remote radiation sensing is the availability of data from the
appropriate sensor. Many commercially available radiation sensors have closed-source
documentation for their communication protocols. Typical end-user products are often self-
contained, handheld devices designed for manual measurement scenarios. While there are
commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) integrations of radiation sensors with UxS available for
purchase, these solutions are typically tailored for specific use cases and may not meet the
requirements of different applications.
This paper discusses efforts to remotely acquire radiation measurements from a small form-
factor CZT gamma spectrometer. Sandia National Laboratories has successfully demonstrated
the initial capability to integrate low size, weight, and power (SWaP) gamma spectroscopy into
various UxS, alongside co-located GPS data logging and sensor calibration and qualification.
With remote gamma spectroscopy achieved, the stage is set for UxS integration of this
capability.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project has laid the foundation required to mount the Mirion-supplied Kromek GR1+
Cadmium Zinc Telluride (CZT) gamma spectrometer onto an uncrewed system (UxS). This detector
is low size, weight and power (SWaP) at a package size of 1”x1”x2.5”, a weight of 60g, and power
consumption of 250mW [1]. The sensor has the physical interface of a single USB port for power
and data, an available software development kit (SDK), and can provide a gamma energy spectrum
of the target. This detector is a room-temperature semiconductor with improved energy resolution
over typical scintillation-based spectrometers. The gamma spectrum can be used for qualitative
(presence/absence, radionuclide identification) as well as quantitative (radioactivity) measurements
when processed through spectrometric analysis. However, the small detector size makes the overall
radiation detection efficiency much lower than larger detectors such as Sodium Iodide (NaI(Tl)).
Because of its lower efficiency, this detector is useful for performing measurements in higher
radiation fields than the more sensitive detectors. This lends itself to be a natural fit for embedding
to UxS systems because these will typically be used in more hazardous environments. Having the
ability to mount this detector to UxS and process the spectroscopic data provides the foundation for
Sandia National Laboratories to demonstrate our capabilities to future sponsors.

This project had the following tasks and goals:
1. Build knowledge of the Kromek-SDK by creating custom code to control the acquisition of
 spectra from the detector.
2. Perform benchmark tests of the detector with certified radionuclide sources using both vendor
 software and custom code.
3. Conduct a full Gamma Detector Response and Analysis Software (GADRAS) characterization
 of the detector.
4. Develop custom code that allows users to select detector acquisition time, extract GPS
 coordinates, and format data for compatibility with the Advanced Visualization and Integration
 of Data (AVID) software.
5. Measure radiation exposure in a NIST-traceable rate field to establish a calibration curve for
 converting detector gross gamma count rate to gamma exposure rate.
6. Perform prototype system tests with a simple uncrewed ground vehicle (UGV) to demonstrate
 outdoor mapping and AVID processing.

1. CUSTOM KROMEK-SDK DRIVER CODE
A custom code was developed to allow for the control of the GR1+ detector by specifying spectrum
collection time, start and stop triggers, and clear Multi-Channel Analyzer commands. Spectra are
then exported in the standard N42 file format that is compatible with most spectrum analysis
software tools such as PeakEasy, GADRAS, and AVID. The code is stored on Sandia’s GitLab
instance and has been documented for transition to future programming teams.

1.1. Comparison of Custom Code with Standard Software
A test of the code was conducted using a Cs-137 source. The exported spectra were visualized in
PeakEasy, demonstrating the code was functional as designed. Figure 1-1 below shows the resultant
spectrum collected with the Sandia code and the vendor’s K-Spect software. Only statistical spectral
fluctuations were observed in the spectra and all spectrum metadata were confirmed to be correct.

  Figure 1-1: Comparison of Cs-137 spectra captured using custom code and vendor software.
300 second spectrum of Cs-137 source collected by Sandia custom Kromek-SDK driver (top), 300
second spectrum of Cs-137 source collected through the Kspect Software (bottom). Both spectra
  were exported in the standard N42 file format and visualized in PeakEasy. This demonstrates
 successful integration of the KROMEK-SDK to control the GR1+ detector.

2. BENCHMARK RADIATION SOURCE TESTS
The GR1+ system and Sandia code were brought to the Radiological Assistance Program Regional
Operations Center (RAPROC) facility on Kirtland Airforce Base (KAFB) for radiation source
testing. The RAP program owns a set of NIST-traceable radioactive sources of various radionuclides
and activities. These benchmark measurements can be used to assess detector efficiency when
compared to other detector systems. Peak height corresponds to detection efficiency and peak
resolution (full width at half maximum) and centroid energy relate to the detector’s ability to identify
radionuclides in a complex mixed gamma field.

2.1. GR1+ Energy Calibration
The GR1+ Detector was used to measure a mix of radionuclides (Am-241, Cs-137, and Co-60) to
obtain an energy calibration curve. This curve converts the channel number to energy. Using
Interspec, a gamma spectrum analysis tool, the following linear calibration was performed.

 Figure 2-1: Screenshot of the Interspec gamma spectrum analysis tool showing linear energy
 calibration curve parameters.
In summary the energy calibration for this detector is:
                             E[keV] = 0.754[kev/ch]*Ch – 2.43 [keV]
This energy calibration can be used for any measurement on this detector system and minor
adjustments can be made to account for normal gain shift during operations. All gamma
spectroscopy analysis software packages allow for manual entry of energy coefficients.

3. GADRAS CHARACTERIZATION
The GR1+ detector was characterized for use in GADRAS by a qualified Sandia engineer. This
detector response function can be imported into the tool and used to model any source-detector
geometry, perform quantitative full spectrum analysis for radioactivity measurements, be used in
other tools with GADRAS embeds (such as AVID), and finally be used to generate simulated
spectrum to perform scoping of mission applications and detector sensitivity evaluations for source
scenarios and shielding configurations. The detector response function is currently stored locally but
a request will be made to release this DRF to the community during the next GADRAS production
release.

4. DEVELOP GEO-REFERENCED SPECTRAL ACQUISITION CODE
A custom C++ code was developed to leverage the GR1+ control code on a single-board computer
(SBC) with GPS location and time correlation. The purpose of this is to have the foundational
capability to build a telemetered radiation detection system aboard a UxS. This prototype code runs
on the command line and asks the user to specify the duration of the telemetry event as well as the
amount of time of each spectrum collection. The default is one spectra per second. Once executed,
the code acquires a spectrum for the duration of the count, stores it to a .CSV file, clears the
spectrum and starts a new count until the telemetry event time has concluded. The CSV output is in
the following format:

performed.

5. EXPOSURE RATE RESPONSE CURVE MEASUREMENT
Raw detector measurements include the number of counts as a function of energy from 0 – 3000
keV. This spectrum can be summed across the energy spectrum to yield a total gamma counts per
second value which is a measure of the general amount of radioactivity the detector is sensing. While
this value is useful in qualitatively measuring general radiation levels it does not provide any
actionable value such as gamma exposure rate or dose rate that end-users will need from the system.
Thus, a calibration curve is needed to convert gross gamma count rate to exposure rate. Sandia has a
NIST-certified radiation calibration range on site which is primarily used to calibrate radiation
detectors used by the Sandia Radiation Protection program. As part of this project, the GR1+ was
brought to the calibration range to determine:
1. a calibration curve that can be used to convert detector gross gamma count rate (in counts per
 second) to gamma exposure rate in (µR/hr).
2. the upper limit of exposure rate that the detector can measure before saturation.
3. how spectral response degrades with increased pulse pileup and analog-to-digital converter
 saturation.
A test plan matrix was built specifying the steps in exposure rate that would be needed to develop an
adequate curve. Due to the limitations of the range itself, a few points in the lower range were not
possible. When the exposure rate is plotted against the measured total counts per second, a linear
relationship is observed from background to about 10 mR/hr. Beyond 10 mR/hr, results become

6. UGS DEMONSTRATOR TEST AT RAPROC
The GR1+ detector, SBC + GPS were tested aboard an uncrewed ground vehicle to demonstrate
the Sandia code’s ability to provide geo-referenced spectral data. The code was run during the drive
from the Sandia Buena Vista facility near the Albuquerque Sunport along Gibson boulevard to the
RAPROC facility on base. This quick test demonstrated that the system was working in a moving
vehicle (a government van). Next, four ~100 µCi point sources were “hidden” outside in the
RAPROC courtyard. The ground vehicle was manually driven through the courtyard to attempt to
locate the sources.
The system performed well and was able to deliver a CSV file with all the data. This was imported to
AVID and an import template was saved that maps the fields to the AVID database fields for
advanced visualization and analysis.

7. SUMMARY AND FUTURE WORK
This project successfully demonstrated the integration of the Kromek GR1+ CZT gamma
spectrometer into an uncrewed ground vehicle (UGV) for remote radiation sensing applications.
The prototype system showcased the ability to collect geo-referenced spectral data and process it
using AVID software. These achievements lay the groundwork for expanding the system’s
capabilities to other uncrewed systems (UxS) platforms, enabling radiation sensing in diverse
operational environments.

7.1. Future Work: Integration with Additional UxS Platforms
Building on the success of the UGV prototype, future efforts will focus on integrating the GR1+
detector with a broader range of UxS platforms to address unique challenges posed by different
environments and mission requirements. These platforms include:
1. Aerial Drones
 Integration with aerial multirotor or fixed-wing drones will enable radiation sensing over large
 areas, such as during environmental surveys, disaster response, or nuclear facility monitoring.
 Aerial platforms offer the advantage of rapid deployment and the ability to access hard-to-reach
 or hazardous locations.
2. Robotic Quadrupeds
 Robotic quadrupeds provide mobility in confined spaces, uneven terrain, or areas with obstacles
 that ground vehicles cannot navigate. These platforms are ideal for applications such as site
 remediation, building inspections, or search-and-rescue operations in radiological environments.
3. Underwater Systems
 Incorporating the GR1+ detector into underwater uncrewed systems will enable radiation
 sensing in aquatic environments, such as monitoring contaminated water sources, inspecting
 underwater nuclear facilities, or conducting environmental assessments in oceans, lakes, and
 rivers.
4. Surface Vehicles
 Uncrewed surface vehicles (USVs) can be used for radiation sensing in maritime environments,
 such as monitoring radioactive materials transported by waterways or surveying coastal areas for
 contamination.
By expanding the integration of the GR1+ detector to additional UxS platforms, Sandia aims to
enhance the versatility and applicability of remote gamma spectroscopy systems. These
advancements will enable safer and more effective radiation sensing across a wide range of
environments, positioning Sandia as a leader in the development of innovative solutions for
radiological monitoring and management.
