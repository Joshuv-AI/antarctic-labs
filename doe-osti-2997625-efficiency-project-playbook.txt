Introduction
This eﬃciency project playbook is intended to assist those developing eﬃciency improvement projects with
tools and templates to simply prepare plans that can be communicated to and approved by leadership. Not all
templates are needed for all projects. The following four steps will walk through each phase.

 1. Scope the Eﬀort
 The scoping phase is crucial for deﬁning the boundaries and objectives of the eﬃciency improvement
 project. This involves clearly identifying the speciﬁc areas, processes, or departments that will be the
 focus of the improvement eﬀorts. The scoping phase helps ensure that the project remains focused and
 aligned with Los Alamos National Laboratory’s (LANL) overall goals and priorities.

 Key Steps and Templates:
 • Fill out the Project Plan Overview: The Project Plan Overview will help you outline the steps to
 get to a decision on whether to proceed with the project and establish a timeline for getting
 there.
 • Draft the Project Charter: The Project Charter contains the key items that must be determined
 before proposing a project for approval. While it may not be possible at this phase to ﬁll out all
 the details, starting will make you aware of the types of information you will need to collect as
 you diagnose the problem. Start with a working draft and then update as your plans are
 informed by your research.
 • Identify the team members
 – Analyst Team: Working level collecting the information and developing the plan
 – Delegate Team: Key stakeholders needed to get to the best decision
 – Leadership Team: Management level that can approve resources for the project and to
 whom the working team is responsible for completing the project when it is approved.

 2. Diagnose Challenges and Opportunities
 In this phase, you will diagnose the challenges and opportunities. This phase involves a deep dive into
 the current state of the organization, identifying the key pain points, bottlenecks, and areas for
 potential improvement. This may include analyzing data, gathering feedback from employees, and
 conducting process mapping or other diagnostic tools to uncover the root causes of ineﬃciencies. The
 goal is to gain a comprehensive understanding of the challenges and opportunities that exist.
 Remember to maintain curiosity rather than judgment as you investigate. Existing processes often
 arose for a reason and understanding that reason may be the key to unlocking a better way that will be
 adopted rather than resisted.

Key Steps and Templates:
 • Assess the current state (where are we now and why?) and deﬁne the ideal state (where do
 we want to be and why?). Ideas to consider:
 – Map current system or process
 – Interview process owners, participants, and customers
 – Identify bottlenecks, waste, and risk points
 – Identify best practices or deﬁne state of the art
  What does industry do?
  What do other sites do?
 – Collect baseline metrics
 – Document constraints
 • Brainstorm and prioritize improvement ideas
 – Ideation Workshop
 – 5 Whys
 – Fishbone Diagram
 • Design target-state process (or select solution prototypes and process)
 – Create implementation roadmap
 – Assign roles and responsibilities

 3. Develop a Recommendation
 With a clear understanding of the challenges and opportunities, the next step is to develop and reﬁne
 potential solutions and approaches into a recommendation. The exploration phase should consider a
 range of options, weighing the pros and cons of each, to identify the most promising strategies for
 improving eﬃciency. This involves synthesizing the insights gathered during the previous phases,
 evaluating the feasibility and potential impact of the various options, and ultimately selecting the most
 appropriate strategies to implement.

Key Steps and Templates:
 • Deﬁne options and the beneﬁts
 – [Options/Tradeoﬀ Matrix]
 – [Eﬀort/Impact Matrix]
 • Evaluate feasibility and impact
 – Score ideas using impact-eﬀort matrix
 – Run risk and feasibility assessments
 – Perform a Return on Investment using one of the ﬁnancial viability tools
 – Identify who can make the decision to approve the project
 • Select the option to recommend and update the project charter

 4. Inform and Make Decisions
 This phase is focused on making a decision about the correct course of action and getting approval
 from the right management level to use resources to complete the project. The decision-making
 process should be data-driven and aligned with LANL’s overall objectives and constraints.

Key Steps and Templates:
 • Write a decision paper or prepare a decision brief
 • Update the project charter with information gained from the previous phases and submit it to
 the decision maker
 • Deﬁne the change management strategy (see Leading Organizational Change by
 Ryan L. Raﬀaelli for ideas on how best to implement change depending on your project)
 • Develop metrics scorecard for tracking the project execution

 5. Execute
 When the decisions have been made, the next step is to execute and monitor the project. This phase
 involves creating detailed action plans, allocating resources, and establishing clear roles and
 responsibilities. Importantly, it also includes ongoing monitoring and evaluation to track the progress
 and eﬀectiveness of the eﬃciency improvement eﬀorts. Regular feedback loops and adjustments may
 be necessary to ensure the project remains on track and delivers the desired outcomes. Project
 management methods are beyond the scope of this playbook.

 6. Templates
 See Playbook Templates for the templates for the following templates.

Financial Viability Metrics

Financial Viability
 Metrics.docx

Because LANL is not a for-proﬁt company, it is not always simple to ﬁgure out a way to assess the viability of a project. Here are a few methods that
can be used depending on your project. You do not need to use all of them, just those that make sense for your particular project. You should have
some way of conveying to those who review the project what the expected returns are. The document above gives more detailed descriptions of
each and links to articles showing how to do the calculations in Excel.

“There are both simple and sophisticated ways to assess the ﬁnancial viability of your project. Without a thorough and appropriate ﬁnancial
analysis, there is a great chance the project will end up losing money. To ensure accuracy, have any estimates validated by your ﬁnance department.
The most common metrics are these:

 • ROI: measures the project’s gross ﬁnancial return on the investment, expressed as a percentage of the total estimated cost.

 • Net present value (NPV): helps you assess what that future money is worth in today’s dollars. If the NPV is positive, it means that the money
 you are investing today will generate future cash ﬂows that are earning the required amount of return (above the discount rate). A negative
 NPV means that project may not be worth doing.

 • Internal rate of return (IRR): the annualized and compounded interest rate that the project will return as a percentage. IRR is perhaps the
 most diﬃcult to calculate but arguably gives the most accurate ﬁnancial assessment of a project. A good IRR will be at a minimum more than
 what you can get by putting your money in the bank.

 • Payback period: how long it takes to earn back the investment made in the project. You decide on an appropriate period (e.g., two years),
 and if the project earns back the investment within that period, it’s good. If it takes longer, then it’s not a good idea.

 • Opportunity costs: the other initiatives that will be missed out on if you decide to carry out this project. If the value of other projects is
 higher, then you should reconsider which projects you invest in.”

Pg 93-94 of HBR Project Management Handbook by Antonio Nieto-Rodriquez
