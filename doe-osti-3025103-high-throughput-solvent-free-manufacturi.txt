1. Project Goals/Objectives:
 The project goal is to develop and demonstrate an advanced solvent-free lithium-ion
 battery electrode process through proposed Advanced Dry Electrode Process (ADEP)
 equipment, which is expected to exhibit a better binder fibrillization and high throughput
 and suitable for high performance electrode manufacturing, and commonize the anode and
 cathode dry processing equipment, supply chain and operation for lithium-ion battery
 OEMs for replacing the solvent-based slurry casting. Our proposed approach will facilitate
 low-cost battery production by addressing the following gaps in present dry electrode
 processing:
 • Extend the dry electrode fabrication process to lithium-ion battery anode
 manufacturing
 • Increase the active material content for anodes and cathodes
 • Intensify the process through improved mixing, powder rheology and surface
 modifications
 • Enable processing of next-generation electrode materials that are not stable to solvent
 or ambient air exposure.
 The project objectives include the development of anode-compatible binder and binder
 fibrillization promoter for low irreversible capacity loss, low electrode binder content yet
 higher film mechanical strength, and the optimization of solvent-free anode and cathode
 process for low cost (>60% electrode cost reduction), high performance (10% increase in
 energy density without sacrificing cycle life) and high throughput to enable next generation
 lithium-ion battery electrode production. Solvent-free electrode manufacturing will also
 enable next-generation cell designs based on prelithiated anodes or solid-state electrolytes.

2. Background:
 Solvent-free electrode manufacturing is a broadly applicable platform technology that can
 enable next-generation cell designs. However, the current solvent-free electrode processing
 has many limitations, such as low active material and high binder content, binder is not
 compatible with anode, etc.
 Navitas is a rapidly growing US Manufacturer of lithium-ion batteries for defense and
 industrial market and has expertise in electrode fabrication and automated cell
 manufacturing, especially has many experiences on solvent-free lithium-ion battery
 electrode fabrication with some completed and on-going DOE or DOD dry process
 projects. Navitas will collaborate with partners with strong industry leadership on either
 polymer binders (Arkema), or carbon additives (Cabot), or polymer nanocomposite and
 advanced analytical capabilities (UTK and ORNL) to develop the novel solvent-free
 electrode processing technology.
 Solvent-free or ‘dry’ electrode process (DP) innovations will include both new process
 technology and new materials, driven by the adoption of new advanced dry electrode
 continuous blending technology. Combining this with development of multi-functional
 carbon materials (Cabot) and novel fibrillizable binder formulations (Arkema, UTK) to
 improve both process efficiency and cell energy density will address key gaps in prior
 solvent-free electrode processes by providing a continuous two-side roll to roll (R2R)
 electrode fabrication process that is anode-compatible and employ common equipment and
 methods for both electrodes.

 3.1. The team has demonstrated the capability of producing 20m continuous dry-process
 anode and cathode free-standing film on its vertical, heated calender.

 3.2. The team has developed multiple variations of multifunctional carbons that act
 simultaneously a the conductive carbon and fibrillizing agent in dry-process electrode
 manufacturing.

 3.3. The team has fabricated and tested representative 0.5Ah cells containing dry-process

 NMC/Si-Gr cell with 300 Wh/kg specific cell capacity and capacity retention of 80%
 through ~120 cycles.

5. Commercial Viability and Competitiveness of Dry-Process Electrodes
 As mentioned previously, dry-process manufacturing enables the facile production of thick
 cathodes while simultaneously eliminating solvent, drying, and solvent recovery systems
 in a pilot-scale or production coater. The elimination of the entire coating system thereby
 further decarbonizes the electrode process, all the while reducing manufacturing costs.
 Several cost analyses have been previously conducted that demonstrate the commercial
 interest in replacing slurry-coated electrodes with dry-process electrodes. Oak Ridge
 National Lab has shown that removing the solvent, solvent drying, and solvent recovery in
 full cell production modeling can net an absolute cost reduction of approximately
 $36/kWh, or 15% of the total cell cost. Even in 2021, coating, drying, and solvent recovery
 combine for almost 50% of the energy consumption of large-scale lithium-ion battery
 manufacturing. For a 100,000-battery pack/year plant that makes a 67-Ah cell, the
 elimination of the solvent-based systems would result in a net energy savings of 6.40
 kWh/cell.
 Due to this cost-reduction potential, as well as the ability to jump-start solid-state battery
 technology and production, many start-up and well-established battery manufacturers are
 developing their own in-house techniques and dry-process variations. The original creators
 of the dry-process technology for supercapacitors, Maxwell, was bought by Tesla in 2019,
 to further drive Tesla’s own internal research for higher energy automotive battery packs
 thus demonstrating potential for dry-process electrodes in the EV market. LiCap
 Technologies has demonstrated upwards of roll-to-roll production of 200m, 96% graphite
 anode and 20m, 94% NMC811 cathode. Other competitors are expected to emerge in the
 coming 1-3 years as individual companies’ technologies and proficiencies mature in dry-
 process electrode manufacturing. Market penetration for dry-process electrodes will
 largely be dependent on the differentiation of individual competitor’s electrodes and their
 ability to maintain good electrochemical performance at high mass loadings.
 However, to fully make dry-process electrodes and solvent-free manufacturing truly
 competitive with the current slurry-coating technology employed by the largest scale
 battery manufacturers, the areas of interest in Section 4 must be addressed and
 implemented. Of paramount importance is the implementation of the first area of interest,
 the developing of equipment to increase the production the line speed and web width of
 generating fully formed (i.e. double-sided laminated) electrodes from raw or pre-fibrillated
 powders. Currently, state-of-the-art slurry-coating pilot coaters for companies such as
 Panasonic, LG Chem, and CATL operate at a line speed of at least 50 m/min with some
 configurations and chemistries able to be coated at 80-100 m/min and web widths between
 1-3m. Though current “state-of-the-art” throughput metrics for solvent-free manufacturing
 is unclear at this time, the slurry-coating throughput that can be achieved vastly outpaces
 the capability of solvent-free manufacturing. The team is aware of developmental dry-
 process calendering systems, such as those made by the Chinese company Naknor, that can
 run at 30 m/min at web widths of ~1m. Such equipment is invariably a step in the right
                                       Page 7 of 115

 direction, but a further 2-5 years of development and evolution in these equipment systems
 is necessary to fully compete against the current generation of slurry-coaters.
 In addition to coating throughput, slurry-coating still has the advantage of solvent-free
 manufacturing as a more ubiquitous, facile manufacturing technology that can produce all
 the lithium-ion coatings necessary for both large-scale, pilot-scale, and small-scale
 production and development. As this program has shown, very specific formulations and
 materials are required to make dry-process electrodes, and the team was largely limited to
 nickel manganese cobalt (NMC) cathodes as well as natural/artificial graphite anodes. This
 is not to say that other chemistries cannot be developed and fully understood, but there is
 a barrier to entry for many chemistries until researchers and engineers take the time to
 develop optimal formulations with appropriate processing techniques. Some chemistries,
 such as lithium-sulfur cathodes, may require highly processed base materials and
 equipment, which could further limit the flexibility of solvent-free manufacturing if a
 battery company wishes to offer or already has a wide product catalog.
 On top of the throughput and formulation challenges, companies such as Navitas are still
 trying to determine whether now is the correct time to jump into dry-process electrodes
 and solvent-free manufacturing. While solvent recovery and the coating process accounts
 for 50% of the energy consumption in producing a cell, material costs are still upwards of
 50% for a cell and solvent-free processing has no down-stream benefit to the cell making
 process (i.e. notching, pouching, formation, etc. are all unaffected). As such, maturity in
 the manufacturing process is vital to create more accurate technoeconomic analysis for
 both small and large-scale companies as they decide whether or not to invest into solvent-
 free manufacturing.

 Considering the manufacturing of the twin-screw extruder (TSE) equipment takes more
 than 8 months, Navitas scheduled trials in the manufacturer’s lab to test these operating
 conditions using their TSE equipment for the team’s ADEP. The first 1-week trial was
 completed in March 2021 and with the results summarized in Table 1-5.

During the trial, Navitas tested baseline cathode, cathode with higher active material, and
modified baseline anode with carbon black (CB) to replace the original conductive carbon
(CC) additive in anode due to safety concern to use the nanomaterial in their lab. The
electrode formulations used are described below:
(1) Cathode: NMC622: Conductive carbon (CC) : Activated carbon (AC) : PTFE polymer binder
= 92 : 1 : 2 : 5, wt%.
(2) Anode: Graphite: Conductive carbon (CB or Carbon Black) : Activated carbon (AC) : PTFE
polymer binder = 92 : 1 : 2 : 5, wt%.

The cathode and anode composite powder were pre-mixed before adding to ADEP
blending equipment. The manufacturer mixed all components except PTFE in a low-shear
                                   Page 9 of 115

mixer, then added PTFE and continued to mix in low-shear mixer to fully disperse the
components. This secondary addition of the PTFE was included to help mitigate PTFE
fibrillization before it was added to the TSE.
The premixed cathode or anode composite powder was then added into the TSE in a certain
feed rate from 2 kg/h up to 10 kg/h to test the effect of feed rate to PTFE fibrillization. The
PTFE fibrillization degree may be detected by SEM/EDAX or other post characterizations,
but a quick and real-time metric to assess the PTFE fibrillization degree could be the
percentage of torque of the blending unit/motor, because higher PTFE fibrillization degree
will cause higher rotating resistance to the driving motor and thus requires higher torque.
Therefore, in the whole series test, we use torque% as a metric of PTFE fibrillization degree.
 As we can see from Table 1, while the cathode powder feed rate increased from 2, 4, 6, 8,
and finally to 10 kg/h, the torque increased from 13%, 17%, 23%, 26% to 32% while
blending at 150 °C. This indicated that increasing the feed rate of starting materials could
increase the torque for better PTFE fibrillization, which might because higher filling degree
improved the contact of PTFE with other particles and promote the binder fibrillization.
Navitas also tested various rotating speed of the blending units for both cathode and anode
processing at 150 °C and low feed rate of 2 kg/h (Table 2). The torque for cathode was
16%, 13%, 13%, 11%, 10% at 200, 300, 400, 500 and 600 rpm. The torque for anode was
11%, 11%, 9.5% at 200, 400, and 600 rpm. While the blending speed increased, the torque
decreased. It might indicate that the fibrillization process needs a relatively longer response
time, or the fibrillated PTFE fiber might break at higher speed, which needs further study.
PTFE is easier to fibrillate under high shear force, but according to the PTFE phase diagram,
the most favorable fibrillating zone is under a certain range of pressure and temperature.
Therefore, controlling the shear force and temperature is important for better binder
fibrillization. For this ADEP equipment, one advantage is the shear force can be finely
tuned by using different blending units and combination, and the temperature can be tuned
in 5 independent zones up to 300 °C. In Table 3, we summarized the results of cathode and
anode processing at a low temperature (45 °C) and a higher temperature (150 °C) under 3
levels of shear force setup (Low, Medium, and High). We clearly saw the trend that high
shear force or high temperature resulted in high torque (or high fibrillization degree) in
almost all cases, except for anode at both high temperature and high shear force, where 18%
torque was lower than that of 23% at high shear force and low temperature. It seemed
consistent with the conclusion from the phase diagram that the pressure and temperature
should be in a certain range. The cathode at both high temperature and high shear force
may have the same exception, but due to the time limit, we could not test all the
combinations. We will test it in later trial or on Navitas site after the ADEP equipment is
installed.
As mentioned above, the blending temperature is an important condition for PTFE binder
fibrillization. Navitas tested solvent-free cathode and anode processing from 30 to 200 °C
range. Based on the results in Table 4, the torque for cathode processing increased from 8%
at 30 °C, increased to maximum value of 13% at 100 °C. the high torque remained same
until 150 °C, but after that it started to decrease with 11% at 200 °C. It indicated that 100-
                                    Page 10 of 115

150 °C was the best range for high PTFE fibrillization for cathode at this setup. Navitas
did not test anode on all temperature, but it showed the same trend of fast torque increasing
between 30-100 °C and slow increasing in 100-150 °C range.
All above were solvent-free electrode processing. Navitas also tested processing with a
certain amount of solvent (e.g., IPA) as lubricant (Table 5). As expected, the torque
decreased while solvent content increased from 0 to 30% by weight. Adding small amount
of solvent may change the fibrillization degree, blending resistance, microstructure, and
electrochemical performance of the electrodes.

Navitas employed a general strategy where large conveying elements in the first 15-20%
of the total screw length quickly move raw powder material into the main body of the
screw. The main body of the screw, approximately 60-65% of the total screw length, has
all the forward conveying kneading elements that will both initiate and complete the
fibrillization process of the PTFE. The material is then removed in the last 20% of the total
                                   Page 16 of 115

screw length where is then collected and prepared for post-processing. A general depiction
of this process can be seen in Figure 6.

Figure 6: General Strategy for Creating and Optimizing ADEP Powder Production

Back conveying elements may be added as necessary for the anode powder procedure to
further initiate PTFE fibrillization by partially reversing the flow of the powder back into

  Figure 41. Study of the time required to produce a high fibrillization degree in PTFE-

As is seen in Figure 41, approximately 15 minutes is the optimal time required to reach a
high fibrillization degree using this particular set of equipment, such that the generated
fiber network can encompass many of the graphite granules with multiple connecting
fibers. Times that are below 15 minutes show no or fewer fibers while there is a significant
decrease in fiber density when going above 15 minutes, likely due to the ball-milling and
inter-particle shearing degrading the integrity of the initially produced fibers. This study
corroborates what Navitas has seen previously, where ranges of 5-15 minutes is the optimal
shearing time for both pure PTFE as well as PTFE/PVDF hybrid binder systems. Further
optimization in the ball-milling can be conducted and Navitas can translate this optimal
shearing time to its ADEP system to better approximate an ideal residence time inside the
twin-screw extruder.

capability testing, a noticeable decrease in charge transfer resistance was observed. This

compacted top layer of the cathode after it has been calendared. This compacted top layer
provides poor wettability, and it is not uncommon for the team’s dry-process electrodes to
increase in specific capacity after 2-4 initial formation cycles are conducted at low C-rates.
Therefore, the lower resistance after cycling may incorporate the improved wettability after
the compacted top layer is disrupted by the formation process and more electrolyte can
readily access the bulk of the cathode. The impedance data can be seen in Figure 77.

As was seen with the various porosity cathode study previously, the first cycle of the dry-
process cathode showed extremely high charge transfer resistance, which was significantly
reduced on subsequent cycles. The slurry-based cathode did not exhibit this first cycle
behavior but did show higher charge transfer resistances during their charge cycles, which
was like the 2nd and 3rd cycles for the dry-process cathodes.

Previously, it was thought that the high internal resistance of the dry-process cathode was
caused by the poor wettability of the electrode. The first charge cycle therefore disturbed
the compacted top layer of the electrode to provide more access to the electrode bulk, thus
decreasing the internal resistance of the cell and leading to higher material capacities on
formation cycles 2-4. To test this hypothesis, both the dry-process and slurry-based cells
were soaked for up to 60 hours before their initial formation charges, with the cell
impedance measured in 10-hour intervals. As seen in Figure 78, both types of cathodes
showed increasing resistance the more the cells were allowed to soak, with comparable
charge transfer resistances. As such, it may not be the electrolyte penetration that
determines the large initial resistance but another cell component, likely the crushed active
material at the electrode surface.

ORNL characterized NMC622 dry-process cathodes with some interesting results. In one
of the internal team meetings, Navitas brought up a question of whether the PTFE on the
cathode side was under-going any degradation during the initial cathode electrolyte
interphase (CEI) formation. The team had largely considered the cathode inert to PTFE
degradation and instead had focused solely on the anode for this phenomenon. As such,
ORNL devised an experiment where NMC622 dry-process cathodes were subjected to the
typical Gen 2 electrolyte containing 1.2M LiPF6 and another set was subjected to
electrolyte containing LiClO4. The main goal of this experiment was to determine any
changes in the CEI of the electrodes and what possible mechanisms are responsible for
good CEI formation. TEM and XPS data were taken of pristine, LiPF6, and LiClO4
cathodes and the results can be seen in Figure 86.

Figure 86. TEM and XPS of pristine, LiPF6, and LiClO4 dry-process NMC622 cathodes.

As can be seen in Figure 86, the cathodes cycled with LiPF6-containing electrolyte
exhibited approximately twice CEI thickness of the cathodes cycled with LiClO4
                                   Page 74 of 115

containing electrolyte. The thinner CEI layer for the LiClO4 cathodes is confirmed with
the XPS as the LiClO4 cathodes show much sharper Ni, Mn, and Co peaks than the LiPF6
cathodes. Since XPS is a surface characterization technique, with only several nanometer
penetration depth generally, thinner surface layers would allow more of the characteristic
peaks of the underlying bulk material. Therefore, the team can conclude the F content
present in the Gen 2 electrolyte does play a significant role in the CEI formation, not the F
content present in the PTFE. While there may be slight degradation of the PTFE to form
the CEI on the immediate cathode particle surface, the electrolyte and solvent are the main
participants in the general CEI formation.

Navitas and the rest of the team have largely been mixing all the formulation components

 1. Mixing all materials together

Navitas made a final determination that an “optimal” dry-process NMC83 cathode should
be made via the fewest number of calendering passes with the lowest possible calendering
pressure. To reach this conclusion, Navitas again took its ADEP prepared cathode, in the
traditional 92/3/5 formulation, and post-blended the material for either an increasing
number of calendering passes (1-10 passes) at 300 psi or conducting a singular pass at
increasing calender pressure (500-2000 psi). After the post-blending was complete, initial
free-standing sheets were formed and then evaluated under SEM to determine if the initial
film formation was the source of the particle degradation. The results of studies can be
seen respectively in Figures 94 and 95.

Figure 94. (From left to right) Cross-sectional SEM of 300 psi samples taken after 1, 2,
and 5 calender passes.

Figure 95. (From left to right) Cross-sectional SEM of NMC 92/3/5 samples taken after

As can be seen in the both figures, 1-2 calendering passes and the lowest pressure is optimal
for the thickness reduction of the dry-process NMC83 cathodes. Logically this makes
sense as the fewer the number of passes and the lower the pressure means that the individual
polycrystalline NMC83 particles undergo the least amount of stress. Therefore, this overall
decrease in stress in turn decreases the probability of particle fracturing. This conclusion
also agrees with previous electrode manufacturing done for coin cells and pouch cells,
which found that higher electrochemical performance was generally tied to cathodes that
could be formed in 1-3 passes (versus cells that too upwards up 10-15 passes due to
environmental and machine difficulties). As such, Navitas will be striving to further
refining the formulations, ADEP processing parameters/conditions, and the post-blending
process such that cathode production can be created in a facile, 1-step calendering process
at the lowest calendering pressure possible.

Subtask 6.2 Optimization of 5 mg/cm2 sulfur cathodes (Month 39)

Navitas for the first time disclosed its work into a dry-process sulfur-infused cathode as a
means for fulfilling the program’s desire for a next generation electrode. Work on the
sulfur-infused cathode began as far back as December 2021/January 2022, when work on
another program using a sulfur-infused cathode manufactured by conventional slurry
techniques was proving quite problematic. As such, the Navitas team developed an initial
dry-process sulfur-infused cathode, which was then refined by an internal Navitas team led
by Dr. Taylor Xu. The results forthwith were collected by Dr. Xu’s team.

Currently, Navitas has a vested interest in achieving a high-loading sulfur-infused cathode
(5+ mg/cm2 sulfur) for various space and high-energy applications. As such, initial work
was completed at a 3 mg/cm2 sulfur loading and then scaled-up to the 5 mg/cm2 sulfur
loading. Initial attempts at the cathodes largely revolved around conventional slurry-
casting formulations, where 80%+ of the formulation contained a sulfur-infused active
material and the remaining 20% of the formulation involved the conductive carbon and
binder. Using these formulations were challenging, as they resulted in low porosity free-
standing cathodes that only showed 20-50 mAh/g instead of the typical 1000+ mAh/g
initial discharge that is seen in literature. A literature review of sulfur-infused cathodes
gave the team indication that porosities above 50% are critical to achieving good capacity
from sulfur-infused cathodes and that a low E/S ratio is necessary for the 500 Wh/kg target
most researchers and commercial manufacturers want to achieve.

As such, the Navitas team decreased the active material and began using a formulation of
40% sulfur-infused active material, 57% conductive carbon, and 3% PTFE. Here, the
active material was KJ-Black infused with sulfur (made in-house at Navitas), the
conductive carbon was Denka Black HS-100, and the PTFE was the same Chemours 601X
previously used in this program’s anodes and cathodes. This formulation was able to make
free-standing cathodes utilizing a hand-blend and rolling process, as seen in Figure 104.
                                  Page 86 of 115

 Figure 104. Process flow diagram of making sulfur-infused cathodes.

In this process, the formulation components are ground with a mortar and pestle in a dry
room after which the material is rolled on a 70°C hot plate into a free-standing cathode.
Afterwards, the cathode is laminated and then punched out to be tested in 2032-coin cells
using a 1M LiTFSI electrolyte employing DOL/DME as the solvent.

The mortar and pestle method was preferred over the high-shear culinary mixer due to the
fact that the KJ-Black itself acted similar to the developmental carbons provided by Cabot.
KJ-Black is a high-surface carbon and as such provides a high degree of fibrillization, too
much for the 3% PTFE that was included in the formulation. Ergo, the team wanted to
produce cathodes for electrochemical testing using a more bench-scale method to first
prove the feasibility of such cathodes before moving on to a more scalable method. Results
of the experimentation can be seen in Figure 105.

Figure 105. Electrochemical testing of dry-process (DP) and slurry-coated (slurry) sulfur-
infused cathodes in 2032-coin cells

Both the 3 and 5 mg/cm2 sulfur-infused dry-process cathodes exhibited initial discharge
capacities above 1200 mAh/g, which is extremely encouraging. The 3 mg/cm2 dry-process
version performed very similarly to the low-loading slurry comparison cell while the 5
mg/cm2 dry-process version significantly outperformed the high-loading slurry
comparison. Additionally, the sulfur-infused dry-process cathodes seem to provide
relatively good capacity retention of approximately 100 cycles. For the capacity retention
curve, the specific capacity at cycle 10 set as the 100% point to exclude the quick drop-off
in capacity due to the polysulfide shuttling effect. The team is hopeful to reduce the
polysulfide shuttling effect throughout the course of this program either through improved
microstructure in the dry-process cathodes or via electrolyte/separator mitigation.

cycles at C/10 for conditioning, and then proceeded into C/5 charge and C/3 discharge
cycling. It was found that cell SE-AMO-002-2 lost a cathode plate during the formation
process, likely due to an internal tab tear inside the cell. As such, cell SE-AMO-002-1
reached ~0.52Ah as expected, while the 002-2 cell only reached ~0.38Ah. Additionally,
both cells showed much higher ICLs than anticipated for 1% PTFE hybrid anodes, coming
in at ~27-28%. This could be due to the anode material being a bit older or improper pre-
mixing/kneading of the formulation in the TSE. The Navitas team will have to take care

agglomerations are present that would cause and undue increase in the cell ICL.

8. Changes/Problems:

 a) Scope issues, Risks and mitigation strategies:
 Tasks and milestones delayed in Q4 due to late starting date of the project (3 months)
 and the delay on the completion of the subcontract and NDAs (12 months). To mitigate
 the task delay, we have tried to speed up the discussion and collaboration with Cabot
 and Arkema while we were waiting for document processing. A 6-month NCE has been
 confirmed by the DOE AMO to compensate for the delay in the subcontract and NDAs.

 One minor SOPO change request has been noted in the Q8 quarterly report, as well as
 the BP1 to BP2 continuation report, of changing the wording “tensile strength” to
 “Young’s Modulus” to better clarify the mechanical strength portion of Milestone 4.1.
 This SOPO change was approved before Phase II work began.

 Navitas put forth a request for scope change in Q10 to change Task 6 from the focus in
 pre-lithiated anode and solid-state electrolyte to the newly developed sulfur-infused
 cathode. It is believed that Navitas, as well as the internal partners, can more readily
 achieve a scalable, roll-to-roll, solution to a sulfur-infused dry-process cathode, instead
 of starting from scratch to focus on a tangent methodology with the two originally
 proposed technologies. While the ADEP could help enable both prelithiation and SSE,
 it is believed that developing dry-process sulfur-infused cathodes, with the aid of the
 ADP and the already developed processing technology, is better aligned with the spirit
 of the funded program. This request for scope change was approved and the tasks and
 milestones have been updated accordingly.

 Navitas has submitted a continuation request for moving from BP2 to BP3, which was
 approved and executed. No significant changes to the SOPO have been recommended
 and Navitas has already agreed to a 6-month NCE for BP3 to minimize the need for
 subsequent contract mods. As such, the final contract date will now be June 30, 2024.
