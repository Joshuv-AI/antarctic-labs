 ABSTRACT

This report describes the calculations and simulations to determine the optimal guide layout, dimensions
and coatings for the NB4 (MARS) beam line post beryllium reflector changeout. The reported guide
performance meets the goals described in the MARS scientific requirements document (1).

 1. Instrument description

1.1 OPERATIONAL MODE

As part of HBRR, the MARS imaging instrument will move from the CG-1D beamline to the new NB-4
beamline. The instrument’s main operational mode will be that of a pinhole camera: The beam will be
shaped by a circular aperture of diameter D at the guide exit, and the detector can be placed at a varying
distance L of between 3 and 11 m downstream of that. The resolution of such an instrument is described
by the ratio of L/D, with the usual trade off between resolution and intensity: L/D = 75 is a coarse-
resolution, high-intensity setting, and L/D = 2000 is the finest resolution, lowest intensity setting.
Common intermediate settings are L/D = 275 and L/D = 400. Two main detectors are available: One
optimized for high count rates (size 5x5 cm2), and one for fine resolution measurements (10x10 cm2).

1.2 POST-HBRR PHYSICAL LAYOUT

The guide layout is dictated by 4 fixed points: The HFIR cold source, the HB4 Shutter, the Velocity
Selector (VS), and the instrument location. All these are tightly controlled by building constraints and
practically immovable, with the sole exception of the height coordinate of the VS and instrument. The VS
marks the end of the common shielding, and the guide between VS and the instrument must be straight to
avoid radiation and background issues in e.g. neighboring instruments associated with a curved guide.
This means the guide layout can be described by the two straight trajectories Source ↔ Shutter and VS ↔
Instrument, which get connected via a circular curved guide of the maximally possible radius of curvature
(Fig. 1).
An attempt was made to connect the trajectories directly from the Main Shutter to the VS using a Bezier
spline curve, but no performance difference was observed, and the spline function would have resulted in
considerably more complex manufacturing specifications (e.g. individual length and angle for each guide
piece).
The NB4 Beamline utilizes the top part of the center channel (“S2”) of the cold guide main shutter as
described in [2]. Its initial direction is 0.022° off the center line of the cold source beam tube in the
horizontal direction, and points 0.53° upwards. Starting after the main shutter, the remaining beam line
uses a guide. The guide width and height aspects are described in chapter 1.3 “Dimensions”.
Horizontally, the beam is diverted by 6.2° by means of a curved guide (“Guide1”) with a radius of
curvature of 264 m (arc length: 28.64 m), followed by a straight guide (“Guide2”) of 31.66 m length.
Vertically, the guide rises straight for the first 11.19 m of Guide1, before leveling off at 0.23 m above the
Cold Source center line using a curvature of 1850 m. Guide2 is parallel to the floor for the entirety of its
length (distance to nominal floor level: 1.29 m).
Guide2 is interrupted after 0.69 m by a velocity selector cave. The current design assumes this to be 1.0 m
long, with the options of either having the velocity selector or a bridging a guide piece in place. It is likely
that the precise dimensions of the cave will change as the design progresses.

1.3 DIMENSIONS

The width and height of the guides throughout the guide system are optimized to retain a high phase space
density (n/s/cm2/sr, “Brilliance”). Since Liouville’s Theorem means that phase space density can at best
be constant, and all losses are irreversible, this sometimes requires discarding low density phase space to
avoid it diluting the beam arriving at the sample.
The Main Shutter guide insert is 4 cm wide and has no segmentation along its height, meaning the beam
could theoretically be up to 8 cm high. However, the top end of this channel is severely under illuminated,
and attempting to catch the entirety of this beam would only result in diluting the phase space further
down the beamline. The guide system curves in both the horizontal and vertical plane, which usually
results in clustering of neutrons on the outer side of the curvature at the end of the guide, which leads to
the inner side of the curvature being starved of neutron density. When these two parts of the phase space
get then mixed in a straight guide, two undesirable things happen: The maximal phase space density is
reduced, and the phase space at the end of the guide is often heavily structured with gaps and holes. To

 6

avoid that, one could either pick up only the high brilliance aspect of the beam coming out of the curved
guide, or attempt to create a uniform high brilliance beam by tapering down the curved guide’s diameter.
Either way, the start of the straight Guide2 will be narrower than the start of the curved Guide1. For NB4
MARS, the approach of tapering down the guide dimensions was chosen (“squeezer” type guide).
To reduce background effects on neighboring beam lines, the guide outside the common shielding (the
majority of Guide2) is not allowed to taper down, since this would increase the neutron’s reflection angles
and therefore guide losses, which is accompanied by increased radiation around the guide. Tapering out
reduces the reflection angles and is therefore allowed.
The guide geometry resulting from these considerations (explored and optimized using McStas, see
below) is the following (expressed as “width[mm] x height[mm]” tuples): Guide1 starts with 40x50, and
linearly tapers down to 24x40 at its end. Guide2 picks up the beam at 24x40, and linearly tapers out to
40x40 at the instrument entrance. These values were determined using McStas simulations (see below).

1.4 MCSTAS MODEL

See also Appendix A. The instrument was modeled in McStas using the Cold Source, Beam Tube and
Main Shutter description originally developed by Lee Robertson (maintained and updated by M. Frost, G.
Granroth, T. Huegle, (2)). All values in the model are arbitrarily rounded to six digits, and realistic
thresholds still need to be decided. The guide start is above the guide start of NB-5 GP-SANS, assuming a
glass thickness of 3.5 mm.

Guide1 curves in both the horizontal and vertical direction, and the curvatures are approximated by
individual straight sections. Since the radii of curvature and the angles covered by the curve are different
in horizontal and vertical directions, the corresponding arc lengths are different. Therefore, the length of
the individual straight sections needs to be carefully chosen to avoid a mismatch between possible and
desired arc lengths, while at the same time keeping the sections lengths at a reasonable length (~25-100
cm). This analysis needs to be redone for each guide starting height investigated: the center line of the
guide aims at the center of the source, which combined with the fixed lower edge of the guide means that
a change in guide entrance height results in a change of initial guide trajectory.

A circular “master aperture” is placed at the end of the guide in the McStas model (it will not be part of
the actual instrument), its radius being the smaller of the guide’s width or height. This is done to avoid
accidentally creating a partially illuminated circular aperture when the guide is for example too narrow
for the L/D chosen. Immediately following this is the instrument aperture, whose size is controlled by the
L/D input parameter, where L = 11 m. The instrument operational modes are modeled by two different
sized sets of detectors at 3 m (5x5 cm2) and 11 m (10x10 cm2) from the guide exit.

 2. MCSTAS CALCULATIONS

2.1 OPTIMIZATIONS

McStas simulations were performed to optimize the following parameters:

 Guide dimensions (width and height variations along the guide)

 Guide coatings (m-Values of each surface (bottom, top, left, right) of each guide segment)

The following settings were investigated:

 “Workhorse” setting, L/D = 400: A 10x10 cm2 detector is set 11m downstream of the guide exit.

 “High Intensity” setting: A 5x5 cm2 detector is placed 3m downstream of the guide exit. Assigning
 this a particular L/D value is avoided because the exact resolution does not matter in this case.

 The “high resolution” setting of L/D = 2000 was not taken into account during the optimization
 process, since the parameters resulting from this will always be considerably off from the other two
 options: the ideal guide dimensions will be tiny by comparison.

2.2 RESULTS

2.2.1 Guide dimensions

To optimize the guide geometry, guide width and height were defined at three positions: Guide entrance
(w1 x h1), End of Guide1/ Start of Guide2 (w2 x h2), and End of Guide2 (w3 x h3). Since we consider
rectangular guide systems, horizontal and vertical dimensions and their effects can be considered
separately. While optimizing the dimensions in one direction, the dimensions in the other direction were
kept at 40 mm throughout the guide system. A large number of permutations of reasonable values for
these dimensions were simulated.
To obtain the values most likely to satisfy both the “High Intensity” and the “Workhorse” settings, the
integrated flux numbers were set in relation to the maximal value achieved for each respective setting,
and only the permutations reaching above 90% (horizontal) and 95% (vertical) of the optimum of both
instrument settings were selected for further investigation (Figure 2). It is worth noting that in the
horizontal plane, three dominant width datasets emerged:

          40 mm → 22-28 mm → 38 mm
          40 mm → 20-28 mm → 40 mm
          40 mm → 24-26 mm → 45 mm

Likewise, in the vertical plane, three height datasets emerged:

          45 mm → 35-40 mm → 40 mm

2.2.2 m-Value optimization

To obtain reasonable m-Values along the guide, McStas tally components were used: For each neutron
trace, its entire journey through the guide system was recorded, including all reflection angles for each
interaction with a guide surface. Based on these angles and the wavelength of the neutron, one can
calculate the m-Value each surface would need to be to transport this particular neutron trajectory.
Combined with the knowledge of the guide surface areas as well as a cost estimate curve for the m-Values
($/cm2), one can now calculate how much a theoretical guide system would cost that would transport
exactly this neutron trajectory. Normalizing this cost to the statistical weight of the neutron trace at the
detector position results in a cost value ($/n) for each recorded neutron history. One can now eliminate the
costliest neutrons by introducing upper thresholds and filtering the dataset against these. The cost of the
resulting guide system can be inferred by using the maximal m-Value for each surface of the remaining
dataset.
The cost estimates used here follow a quadratic equation, are based on historic values and have not been
adjusted for factors like inflation or market fluctuations. They therefore can only provide a very rough
estimate of actual costs and should be only used for the comparison between the guide options described
in this document.

Figure 6 shows the resulting m-Values of 9 different cost/neutron thresholds for the four sides of the
guide (“inner” and “outer” referring to the inner and outer side of the horizontal curve, which translates to
“right” and “left” when looking from the source downstream). It is worth noting that the last set of values
(all zeroes) was not obtained by setting the cost threshold to zero, but rather by reducing the previous one
slightly, which means that there exists a minimal cost threshold below which no neutrons can be
transported.

Figure 7. Intensity as a function of cost estimates.

When looking at the Intensity at the detector position resulting from these guide system for each option as
both the “wavelength spectrum” and the “integrated intensity as a function of guide system cost” (Figure

 15

7), one can see that at ~$300k, a plateau of ~7E9 n/s is reached, and further cost increases quickly run
into an area of diminishing returns. Below this threshold, however, the intensity very quickly falls off,
and any cost savings come with significant intensity losses.
On a side note, these spectra were produced by histogramming the aforementioned data sets. When
comparing to McStas simulation results using the same m-Values, small but noticeable differences in the
spectral shape are observed. These differences can be explained by two mechanisms: On the one hand, the
tally analysis can assume arbitrary m-Values below one, whereas the McStas simulations have m = 1 (the
reflectivity of pure Nickel) as the lowest value. On the other hand, the tally analysis assumes a sharp
cutoff in reflectivity at a selected m-Value, whereas the McStas simulation has a sloped cutoff. The
differences between tally analysis and McStas simulation are not significant here and do not change the
result of the analysis.
When analyzing the effect of the various guide systems on the “High Intensity” measurement mode
(Figure 7 bottom), similar trends to the “Workhorse Mode” results can be observed: a steep intensity
increase at low costs, and diminishing returns at high costs. It is however worth noting that the intensity
gains do not stall the same way they do for the workhorse mode, and the curve never becomes truly flat.
When choosing the most effective guide version, several arguments can be made:

 Most options exceed the scientific requirements (Figure 7 top, dotted line)

 Beyond ~$500k (red dot and above), no intensity is gained for L/D = 400, and gains for HI mode
 become marginal

 Below ~$300k (pink dot and below), the intensity losses can not be justified by the modest monetary
 savings

 Going from ~$300k to ~$400k (brown and purple dot, respectively) is the last increment on which HI
 mode benefits substantially (+8% intensity)

 While no detailed analysis has been carried out to investigate effects of misalignment and fabrication
 errors, the position of the $300k option (brown dot) right at the onset of dramatic intensity losses
 could mean that this system is especially vulnerable to these effects, whereas the purple dot option
 ($430k) should be more resilient to this.

Taking all these factors into account, the decision was reached to proceed with the m-Values represented
by the purple dot in Figure 7, a rough cost estimate of which came to ~$430k.

2.2.3 Description of the final model

The guide design can be described by a curved Guide1 and a straight Guide2. Guide1’s segment length is
0.329174 m. To account for glue gaps etc., each segment of Guide1 is shortened by 0.5 mm, while the
distance between segment starts is kept at exactly 0.329174 m. Guide1 consist of 87 of these straight
segments, each one being rotated along the horizontal direction by -0.070452°. Starting at segment 35
(“NB4_Guide1_36”), each one is also rotated by 0.010005° along the vertical direction. The guide tapers
linearly from a cross section (width x height) of 40x50 mm2 to 24x40 mm2.

 16

Guide2 is straight and 28.64 m long. It is interrupted after 0.69 m by a velocity selector cave. The current
design assumes this to be 1.0 m long, with the options of either running the velocity selector or bridging
the gap using a guide piece. Guide2 tapers out from 24x40 mm2 to 40x40 mm2.

 3. SUMMARY

A design for the NB4 MARS guide was developed by consecutive optimizations of layout, guide
dimensions and guide coating m-Values. The result satisfies the values laid out in the scientific
requirements document.
