 ABSTRACT
Modern heavy-duty vehicles rely on complex networks of Electronic Control Units (ECUs) that commu-
nicate using the J1939 protocol. While this system makes it easier to update and configure vehicle compo-
nents, it also opens the door to serious cybersecurity risks if not properly secured. This work investigates
the potential for firmware tampering through the J1939 communication protocol, which enables ECU con-
figuration and reprogramming over the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus. By monitoring CAN traffic
during legitimate maintenance operations and reverse-engineering OEM diagnostic software, we identified
common and proprietary J1939 message identifiers, authentication patterns, and vulnerabilities within Uni-
fied Diagnostic Services (UDS). These findings demonstrate that inadequate authentication mechanisms
can allow malicious actors to alter ECU firmware or disable safety functions, posing severe operational
and safety risks. Our analysis contributes to the development of vehicle intrusion detection systems capa-
ble of recognizing abnormal reprogramming activity and future firmware fingerprinting methods to verify
software integrity across ECUs. This work highlights the importance of standardizing secure firmware au-
thentication across manufacturers to strengthen cyber resilience in heavy-duty vehicle systems.

 1. INTRODUCTION
As heavy-duty vehicles evolve toward increasingly interconnected and software-driven systems, the line
between mechanical control and digital management continues to blur. Electronic Control Units (ECUs)
now govern nearly every critical vehicle subsystem—engine performance, transmission, braking, and
emissions control—communicating through the Controller Area Network (CAN) using the SAE J1939
standard. This standard simplifies maintenance and fleet operations by enabling remote configuration,
diagnostics, and firmware updates over the in-vehicle network. However, the same capability introduces
substantial cybersecurity risks when authentication and access control mechanisms are insufficiently pro-
tected.
In modern trucks and buses, Many ECUs (including the tested ECM and brake controller) can be repro-
grammed directly through J1939 diagnostic messages using Unified Diagnostic Services (UDS). Each
manufacturer implements its own proprietary authentication algorithms and seed-key exchanges to au-
thorize these operations. Unfortunately, this lack of standardization and transparency has resulted in un-
even levels of security across Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs). Through reverse-engineering
of OEM diagnostic software such as Cummins INSITE and Navistar service tools, we found that encryp-
tion keys, seed-key generation functions, and even plaintext authentication routines can often be recovered
with moderate effort using tools like Ghidra, IDA, and Binwalk. In some cases, the algorithms protecting
firmware flashing are implemented in easily decompiled .DLL libraries, exposing predictable logic that can
be replicated to bypass ECU access restrictions.
Additionally, during this work, we were able to capture J1939/UDS CAN traffic while OEM diagnostic
tools performed legitimate ECU reimaging and maintenance operations. By analyzing these communi-
cation sequences, we were able to reconstruct the firmware image directly from the captured CAN logs,
confirming that firmware data is transmitted in a recoverable format during reprogramming. This demon-
strated that, with access to diagnostic traffic, an attacker could potentially extract or replicate ECU firmware
without physically tampering with the hardware.
The potential consequences of such vulnerabilities are significant. A malicious actor with access to the
CAN network could exploit these weaknesses to flash unauthorized firmware, disable safety systems, or
insert persistent malware into an ECU. During our research, we monitored real CAN traffic on J1939 net-
works during legitimate maintenance operations and identified message patterns—including Parameter

 1

Group Numbers (PGNs) and Source Addresses—that could signal firmware modification attempts. These
findings reveal how observable network behavior, combined with knowledge of UDS authentication flows,
can serve as an early indicator of tampering attempts.
This work situates itself within the broader goal of improving cyber resilience in heavy-duty transporta-
tion systems. By combining CAN traffic analysis with reverse-engineered insights from OEM diagnostic
software, we aim to enable vehicle intrusion detection systems (IDS) capable of recognizing unauthorized
reprogramming activity. The next phase of this research involves extending these methods to firmware fin-
gerprinting, where extracted ECU firmware can be compared to verified OEM baselines to detect hidden or
malicious changes. Ultimately, standardizing secure firmware authentication and verification across man-
ufacturers is critical to ensuring that the safety and reliability of heavy-duty vehicles keep pace with their
growing digital complexity.

 2. BACKGROUND
2.1 ELECTRONIC CONTROL UNITS IN HEAVY-DUTY VEHICLES
Heavy-duty trucks and buses, much like modern passenger cars, rely on numerous Electronic Control
Units (ECUs) to manage vehicle functions. A typical heavy vehicle can contain dozens of distributed
ECUs (on the order of 50 or more) that each oversee specific subsystems such as the engine, transmis-
sion, braking (ABS/ESC), and body controls. These ECUs are networked together and share data over
in-vehicle communication buses (primarily CAN bus in heavy vehicles). Each ECU runs firmware (em-
bedded software) tailored to its role – for example, the engine control module’s firmware regulates fuel
injection timing, turbo boost, and emission controls, while other ECUs handle functions like gear shifting
or air brake management. All ECUs coordinate via the vehicle’s networks to ensure the heavy-duty vehicle
operates as an integrated system. Connectivity to the outside is provided through standard interfaces like
the diagnostic connector and increasingly through wireless links (telematics units with Wi-Fi or cellular)
for remote monitoring. This complex ECU architecture forms the foundation on which diagnostic and up-
date mechanisms operate, and thus it is central to understanding firmware tampering risks (Harmon and
Struthers 1982), (Johnson, Daily, and Kongs 2014).

2.2 IN-VEHICLE NETWORKS: CAN AND THE SAE J1939 PROTOCOL
Heavy-duty vehicles predominantly use the Controller Area Network (CAN) bus as the backbone for ECU
communication, with the SAE J1939 protocol as the common language on top of CAN (CSS Electronics
2023). SAE J1939 is a set of standards that define how ECUs communicate via CAN in trucks, buses, and
other heavy machinery, ensuring interoperability across different manufacturers.In simple terms, J1939
provides a unified, standardized way for heavy-duty vehicle ECUs to exchange messages – a “common
language” – whereas light-duty automobiles often rely on manufacturer-specific protocols. Technically,
J1939 is a higher-layer protocol built on CAN 2.0B: it utilizes 29-bit extended CAN identifiers and typ-
ically a 250 kbit/s bus speed (with newer provisions for 500 kbit/s in updated standards). Communica-
tion on J1939 is largely broadcast-oriented; ECUs transmit parameter data (e.g. engine RPM, oil temper-
ature, wheel speed) using standardized Parameter Group Numbers (PGNs), and any listening ECU can
use the data. Some J1939 messages are only sent on-request (for instance, diagnostic trouble code reports)
by using a special request PGN. The use of J1939 in all US heavy vehicles means that a common set of
messages and behaviors is present across engine and vehicle makers. This standardization has benefits
for maintenance and integration, but from a security perspective it also means an exploit developed for
one J1939-using vehicle could apply to many others. Notably, unlike modern passenger car networks, the
J1939/CAN protocol itself has no built-in authentication or encryption – any device with physical or net-
work access to the bus can send messages impersonating ECUs. This lack of security, combined with the
ease of physically accessing the J1939 port (a standardized 9-pin diagnostic connector), has been demon-
strated to enable safety-critical attacks if an attacker gains in-network access. Researchers have shown that
crafting malicious J1939 messages can induce unintended behaviors such as uncontrolled acceleration,
disabling of a driver’s throttle input, or deactivation of engine brakes on a heavy truck, illustrating the im-
portance of securing these in-vehicle networks (Jichici et al. 2022).

2.3 DIAGNOSTIC PROTOCOLS AND UDS FOR FIRMWARE UPDATES
Beyond normal run-time communication, vehicles also implement diagnostic protocols that allow exter-
nal tools (e.g. service laptops or scan tools) to query and modify ECUs. A key standard in this realm is
Unified Diagnostic Services (UDS), defined by ISO-14229. UDS is a communication protocol used by

 3

automotive ECUs (across both light and heavy-duty) to facilitate diagnostics, configuration changes, and
firmware flashing. In practice, UDS operates on a client-server model: a diagnostic tester tool (client)
connects to the vehicle network (often via the OBD or diagnostic port) and communicates with an ECU
(server) by sending UDS service requests. If the ECU supports the requested service, it will respond ac-
cordingly. Through UDS, a maintenance tool can read or clear diagnostic trouble codes, retrieve sensor
values or identification data, initiate self-test routines, and crucially, trigger reprogramming routines to
update the ECU’s firmware or calibration data. For example, UDS defines services for requesting down-
load or upload of data to an ECU’s memory, enabling a technician to flash a new software version into an
ECU’s flash memory. To protect sensitive operations, UDS includes a security mechanism (commonly the
“Security Access” service) which requires an unlocking step (challenge-response authentication) before al-
lowing critical actions like firmware erasure or writing. In theory, only authorized diagnostic tools with the
correct keys or algorithms can perform firmware updates or change protected settings. In heavy-duty ve-
hicles, UDS is often used in combination with the J1939 network (sometimes referred to as UDS-on-CAN
or ISO-15765 transport in this context) to perform advanced diagnostics and flashing over the J1939-CAN
data bus. It coexists with industry-specific diagnostic messages (for example, J1939 also defines some di-
agnostic message formats for emissions and fault codes under the J1939-73 standard), but UDS provides
a more general and powerful superset for ECU servicing. In summary, UDS and similar protocols make
it possible to reconfigure ECUs and load new firmware in the field – functionality that is indispensable
for vehicle maintenance and recalls, but which must be tightly controlled to prevent abuse (Bielawski et
al. 2020). In our experimentation we used UDS (ISO 14229) over J1939 to (1) transition ECUs into differ-
ent diagnostic sessions, (2) exercise SecurityAccess, (3) trigger ECU resets, and (4) systematically extract
Diagnostic Identifiers (DIDs) for system fingerprinting and configuration analysis across two trucks.

2.4 OEM DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS (CUMMINS INSITE, NAVISTAR SERVICE SOFTWARE)
Vehicle and engine manufacturers supply proprietary software tools to interface with their ECUs via the
aforementioned networks and protocols. These OEM diagnostic tools are crucial for authorized service
centers to troubleshoot issues, adjust parameters, and apply firmware updates (often termed reflashing or
calibration updates). For example, Cummins INSITE is a Windows-based electronic service tool used for
Cummins engines (commonly found in heavy trucks and equipment). INSITE allows technicians to con-
nect to the engine’s ECM via a CAN/J1939 adapter and perform tasks such as reading fault codes, moni-
toring engine live data, changing operational parameters, and updating the ECM’s calibration (firmware) to
the latest version. Cummins INSITE provides different access levels (e.g. basic, R/W, engineering) and can
download official calibration files into the ECU’s flash memory, essentially performing a firmware flash
when needed (for instance, to apply an update or a factory software patch). Similarly, Navistar (manu-
facturer of International trucks and engines) offers service software such as Navistar Engine Diagnostics
(NED) and formerly ServiceMaxx for older engines. These tools serve a similar purpose for Navistar’s
MaxxForce engines and other controllers. Navistar ServiceMaxx, for instance, is described as a diagnostic
and programming service tool for Navistar engines. With it, a technician can connect to an International
truck’s 9-pin diagnostic connector and run diagnostic tests or reprogram engine control modules with ap-
proved calibrations. Both INSITE and Navistar’s software adhere to the industry standards (J1939/UDS
where applicable) under the hood, but incorporate vendor-specific knowledge (like how to enter program-
ming mode or the format of calibration files). They typically require authentication or licenses to use, re-
flecting that firmware flashing is a sensitive operation reserved for authorized users. These OEM tools are
examples of legitimate, manufacturer-approved ways that firmware updates are delivered to heavy-duty ve-
hicle ECUs in the field. They highlight the intended method for modifying ECU software – a method that
firmware tampering attacks may seek to subvert or mimic illicitly (Diesel Laptops 2023). As mentioned
the CUMMINS INSITE tool has a calibration feature that uses an ECM code and calibration file to com-

pletely calibrate and reset the CUMMINS ECM to a factory setup. We also used the BigBoss Tuner PDI
device to perform ECM flashing and engine profile modes (Economy, Stock, Power).

2.5 FIRMWARE FLASHING AND CYBERSECURITY IMPLICATIONS
Allowing ECUs to be reprogrammed in the field (whether via a wired CAN bus connection or remotely
“over-the-air”) raises significant cybersecurity considerations. If the process is not secured with strong au-
thentication and integrity checks, attackers could potentially manipulate it to install malicious or unautho-
rized firmware. In many vehicles, the diagnostic programming routines are protected only by a challenge-
response key or password. Unfortunately, if this authentication mechanism is weak or has been reverse-
engineered, an adversary or unauthorized party can defeat it. For instance, heavy-duty ECUs often rely on
manufacturer-specific “seed/key” algorithms for UDS security access – and it has been documented that
aftermarket tuning communities frequently crack or bypass these protections in order to reflash ECUs with
custom software. In fact, tampering by reprogramming the vehicle’s powertrain ECU (re-flashing it with
non-factory software) is a known issue, commonly done to disable emissions controls or increase perfor-
mance. Aftermarket tools or “tuners” that plug into the diagnostic port can upload custom firmware (often
called installing a new “tune”) which alters how the engine operates (Giechaskiel et al. 2022). This exem-
plifies how the firmware update feature can be abused when access control is insufficient – in these cases,
the attackers are often truck owners or repair shops seeking to bypass regulations, but the same methods
could be used with more malicious intent. From a cybersecurity standpoint, once an attacker can flash their
own code to an ECU, they have near full control of that vehicle system, potentially enabling persistent ma-
licious functions (like disabling safety features or causing erratic behavior at critical moments).
The risk is amplified by the trend toward connectivity in heavy vehicles. Modern fleets often include Re-
mote diagnostics module and connectivity for remote diagnostics and even over-the-air (OTA) software
updates. If an attacker can remotely compromise that unit or the update server, they might inject arbi-
trary firmware into many vehicles at once. One real-world indicator of this threat was a 2016 finding that
over 1,500 fleet management devices (aftermarket Remote diagnostics module units connected to truck
CAN/J1939 networks) were accessible on the public internet with an open Telnet port (no password) (Bu-
rakova et al. 2016). This exposed an avenue for a remote attacker to potentially bridge into a truck’s in-
ternal CAN bus. Once on the bus, that attacker could impersonate a diagnostic tool and initiate firmware
flashing or other destructive commands. Past cybersecurity research on heavy trucks has shown that phys-
ical access to the diagnostic port is not the only vector – vulnerabilities in wireless interfaces or connected
services can provide a foothold. The famous 2015 Jeep Cherokee hack (while on a passenger vehicle)
demonstrated the nightmare scenario: researchers remotely infiltrated the vehicle’s cellular-connected in-
fotainment system and then sent CAN messages that ultimately led to a recall (King and Klinedinst 2016).
Heavy-duty vehicles, which now often have Wi-Fi/cellular gateways for remote diagnostics, face a similar
challenge. A successful breach of the update mechanism (absent strong cryptographic safeguards) could
allow an attacker to distribute malware or corrupt firmware to engine or brake controllers across an entire
fleet. The consequences of such firmware tampering range from costly downtime (if ECUs are bricked) to
catastrophic safety failures if critical functions are sabotaged.
In summary, the ability to flash firmware over CAN or OTA is a double-edged sword: it is essential for
maintenance and feature upgrades, but it opens an attack surface that must be secured. Strong authen-
tication (e.g. unforgeable challenge-response), message encryption, and digital signature verification of
firmware packages are recommended measures. Without these, as case studies and researcher demonstra-
tions have shown, attackers – whether motivated by illegal “tuning”, sabotage, or other goals – can under-
mine the integrity of heavy vehicle ECUs. Ensuring that only valid, authorized updates are applied, and
that diagnostic interfaces cannot be easily spoofed or accessed, is a critical aspect of heavy-duty vehicle
cybersecurity going forward.

 3. RESEARCH OBJECTIVES
The primary objective of this project is to examine how firmware tampering can occur in heavy-duty vehi-
cles through the J1939 and UDS diagnostic pathways used during legitimate maintenance. By understand-
ing how firmware is transferred, authenticated, and written to ECUs, we aim to identify where attackers
could exploit these processes.
First, we seek to analyze how J1939/UDS services involved in firmware flashing could be misused to load
unauthorized software onto ECUs. This includes evaluating diagnostic routines such as Security Access,
ECU/Firmware Resets, Request Download, and firmware transfer sequences.
Second, we aim to collect and study CAN traffic generated during real maintenance and reprogramming
operations. This allows us to characterize message patterns and determine whether firmware content or
authentication sequences are observable and reconstructable from the network.
Third, we aim to reverse-engineer OEM diagnostic tools—such as Cummins INSITE and Navistar service
software—to understand how they implement authentication, seed-key algorithms, and firmware transfer
routines, and where weaknesses may exist.
Finally, the project supports ongoing efforts to develop intrusion detection and firmware-fingerprinting
systems by providing insights into normal versus abnormal reprogramming behavior and by showing how
firmware images can be reconstructed from diagnostic traffic.

 4. METHODOLOGY AND KEY FINDINGS
This section summarizes the end-to-end methodology used to study Navistar ECU reprogramming behav-
ior and the key findings derived from that work. We first built a repeatable bench-top J1939 environment
using production Navistar modules and captured UDS-over-J1939 traffic during reflashing in both the lab
and an operational truck. We then reconstructed firmware payloads directly from TP.CM/TP.DT traffic to
validate that the update data can be recovered from CAN logs. In parallel, we reverse-engineered OEM
diagnostic software components to understand how authentication, message formatting, and transport
behaviors are implemented at the tool level, and to map where implementation details expose attack sur-
face. These artifacts combined connect observable bus traffic to the software logic generating it, enabling
grounded conclusions about security controls and practical tampering pathways.

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
A bench-top J1939 network was built using production Navistar components removed from a heavy-duty
truck shown in Figure 1: the Engine Control Module (ECM), Body Control Module (BCM), and Bendix
EC-80 brake controller. Each controller was connected through its OEM diagnostic harness to preserve
authentic CAN signaling and message routing. The modules were functional off-vehicle, enabling us to
run the complete UDS-over-J1939 reprogramming workflow used during service operations In our ex-
periments, UDS-over-J1939 services were confirmed on the ECM and Bendix EC-80 brake controller.
The BCM diagnostic and programming behavior was vendor-specific and did not consistently follow ISO
14229 UDS semantics; BCM activity is treated as proprietary diagnostics unless explicitly noted.
The controlled setup allowed us to isolate and capture only the diagnostic and firmware-update traffic of
interest, including:
 • Diagnostic session control (UDS 0x10, 0x11)
 • SecurityAccess authentication (UDS 0x27 seed-key exchange)
 • RoutineControl commands to enter programming mode (UDS 0x31)
 • Memory transfer operations (UDS 0x34, 0x36)
 • Firmware block transport using J1939 TP.CM / TP.DT
Independent control over module power and ignition states allowed us to trigger bootloader mode, repeat
programming attempts, and capture full sequences from session initialization to final ECU reset. These
traces aligned with what we observed on an operational Navistar vehicle, confirming that the testbed ac-
curately represents real-fleet behavior while offering the repeatability and visibility needed for firmware
tampering research.

4.2 CAPTURING J1939/UDS TRAFFIC
To analyze the ECU reprogramming process, we captured J1939/UDS diagnostic traffic in two environ-
ments: a controlled testbed and a real Navistar heavy-duty truck.
The Testbed: To support detailed observation of the reflashing process, ECU firmware update traffic was
also captured in a controlled laboratory environment. In this setup, the OEM diagnostic software estab-
lished a UDS reprogramming session over J1939, and all CAN traffic was passively recorded for later pars-
ing and reconstruction. This allowed us to isolate critical sequences such as SecurityAccess (UDS 0x27),
routine control (0x31), and transfer data operations carried by J1939 Transport Protocol (TP.CM/TP.DT).

By restricting the network topology to only the diagnostic tool and the target ECU, the captured CAN logs
were free of unrelated broadcast PGNs typically present in a full vehicle environment. This reduced noise
made it easier to identify message ordering, timing constraints, and transitions between diagnostic sub-
functions. Additionally, complete control over power and session state allowed us to bracket each capture
precisely from session initialization through the final ECU reset, ensuring full coverage of the firmware
delivery workflow.
In-Vehicle Capture: To validate the real-world behavior, we also recorded a firmware update session on
an actual Navistar truck. The OEM diagnostic service tool was connected to the truck’s J1939 diagnostic
port (9-pin connector), and a Kvaser adapter was used in parallel to sniff the CAN traffic. The heavy-duty
truck’s engine was off (ignition on) during reprogramming, ensuring that the flash procedure could pro-
ceed and that the bus carried primarily diagnostic messages. The capture from the vehicle confirmed that
the sequence of UDS messages observed in the lab also occurs in practice, while revealing any additional
network interactions present in a production environment (such as background CAN messages from other
ECUs or gateway-mediated behaviors).
In both settings, the goal was to log the entire ECU reflashing dialogue — from session initiation to the
final reboot. This includes the security handshake, memory operations, and the bulk data transfer of the
new firmware. Concretely, the diagnostic tool first negotiates a special programming session and performs
a Security Access challenge-response (service 0x27). In this step, the ECU’s bootloader issues a seed and
the tool computes/transmits the correct key, unlocking programming function. Once security is passed,
the tool commands the ECU to prepare for writing: for example, it may invoke a memory erase routine
via UDS RoutineControl (service 0x31) to clear the flash memory region designated for new code. With
the ECU ready to receive data, the firmware download is initiated by a Request Download (service 0x34),
which specifies the memory address and size of the incoming firmware image. The ECU responds with a
positive acknowledgment and often a suggested block size. Thereafter, the diagnostic tool sends a series of
Transfer Data packets (service 0x36) carrying the actual firmware, segmented into blocks for transmission.
Each 0x36 message contains a sequence counter and a chunk of the binary. After all firmware blocks are

 8

sent, a Transfer Exit (service 0x37) concludes the upload, possibly accompanied by a checksum or signa-
ture of the received data. The ECU then verifies the integrity of the new code (often using another Rou-
tineControl service for post-programming validation) and, if all checks pass, the process ends with an ECU
reset command (service 0x11) to reboot the controller into the updated software. All of these UDS-level
steps were reflected in the captured CAN traffic, allowing us to reconstruct the flashing sequence in detail.
The use of the OEM tool ensured that the captured data represents the authentic, intended update proce-
dure executed in the field.
Since heavy-duty vehicles use the SAE J1939 standard for CAN communications, the diagnostic messages
above were encapsulated in 29-bit extended CAN frames following J1939 addressing conventions. In our
captures, the external diagnostic tool operated as a node on the J1939 network (using a high-numbered
source address reserved for service tools) and addressed the target ECU by its assigned address. One im-
portant aspect of J1939 is how it handles messages larger than 8 bytes (the CAN frame payload limit).
ECU firmware files are on the order of hundreds of kilobytes or more, so multi-packet transport was em-
ployed. J1939 defines a Transport Protocol to fragment and reassemble large messages such as software
downloads. In our logs, the actual firmware transfer occurred via J1939’s segmented transfer: the diag-
nostic tool first sent a Connection Management frame (known as TP.CM, using Parameter Group Number
60416) to initiate the upload session, followed by a series of Data Transfer frames (TP.DT, PGN 60160)
carrying the firmware data. Each TP.DT frame contains a 1-byte sequence number and 7 bytes of data,
so up to 1785 bytes (255 frames * 7 bytes each) can be sent per sequence. The ECU reassembles these
packets into the original binary. Our capture thus contains every piece of the transmitted firmware, in or-
der. For example, we recorded the initial TP.CM (which specifies the size of the firmware and the number
of upcoming packets) and then hundreds of TP.DT frames that sequentially bore the firmware contents.
J1939’s flow control was handled automatically: in RTS/CTS mode the ECU could pace the transfer, or in
BAM (Broadcast Announce Message) mode the tool could stream packets without CTS, depending on the
implementation. In practice, OEM reprogramming typically uses a peer-to-peer (RTS/CTS) approach to
ensure reliability, but either way the entire set of TP.CM/TP.DT frames were captured for analysis. These
transport-layer details are crucial, as they allowed us to later reassemble the exact firmware image from the
log files, confirming that the update data was captured losslessly.
All CAN frames were recorded with high-resolution timestamps and identifiers preserved. Using a SocketCAN-
based logger (e.g., Linux candump or a custom capture tool), each frame was time-stamped and stored
in raw format. This not only facilitates replay or chronological analysis of the sequence, but also enables
extraction of the payload data. In post-processing, we leveraged the timestamps to understand the tim-
ing of each step (for instance, the delay between the seed and key in the 0x27 security challenge, or the
inter-frame gaps during data transfer). The 29-bit CAN IDs in the log indicate the source and destination
of each message, so we could distinguish requests vs. responses and identify the ECU’s address and the
tester’s address in the traffic. By parsing the TP.CM and TP.DT frames, we could concatenate the 7-byte
data segments in order, reproducing the full firmware binary that was sent.
The logs also captured any negative responses (UDS NACK messages 0x7F) or error conditions if they
occurred, though in our case the reflash sessions completed normally. The ability to power-cycle the ECU
on the bench meant we could also capture the ECU’s behavior immediately after programming (e.g., the
ECU’s own reboot broadcast or any diagnostic trouble code resets following reflashing). All of this pro-
vides a rich dataset for offline analysis. In summary, by capturing the J1939/UDS traffic from both a real
truck and a laboratory setup, we obtained a comprehensive trace of the ECU firmware update procedure
– including the security unlocking phase, memory erase and programming commands, and the bulk data
transfer using J1939’s transport protocol – under conditions that balance realism with experimental con-
trol. This logged data forms the foundation for subsequent security assessments of the diagnostic reflash
process in an automotive cybersecurity context.

4.3 RECONSTRUCTING FIRMWARE FROM CAN LOGS
To recover the ECU firmware from an over-the-bus reprogramming event, we treat the CAN reflash ses-
sion as a file-transfer protocol running on top of J1939 and decode it layer by layer. The raw capture is a
candump-style log where each line consists of a timestamp, interface name (e.g., can0), a 29-bit extended
CAN identifier, and an 8-byte payload. During the reflash, the traffic is dominated by J1939 Transport Pro-
tocol (TP) messages using PGN 0xEC00 for connection management and PGN 0xEB00 for data transfer.
In the logs we collected, these appear as CAN IDs 0x18EC00F9 / 0x18ECF900 (TP.CM) and 0x18EB00F9
(TP.DT), corresponding to the standard J1939 TP.CM and TP.DT frames between the diagnostic tool and
the ECM. The first step is to identify the firmware-carrying transfers within the log. Each large PDU trans-
fer starts with a TP.CM “Request To Send” (RTS) frame on PGN 0xEC00. In the logs, these RTS frames
appear with ID 0x18EC00F9 and a payload whose first byte is the TP control code (0x10 for RTS), fol-
lowed by the total payload size in bytes, the number of 7-byte data packets, and the target PGN. For exam-
ple, a frame of the following form:
18EC00F9#10 F6 00 24 FF 00 EF 00
encodes the start of a segmented transfer of a specific length from the ECU at source address 0xF9. The di-
agnostic tool responds with a TP.CM “Clear To Send” (CTS) or other flow-control frames on 0x18ECF900.
The sequence of RTS/CTS/EndOfMsgAck frames allows us to identify each logical J1939 PDU that is be-
ing transported in segments.
Once the TP.CM handshake is observed, the firmware bytes themselves are carried in the corresponding
TP.DT packets on PGN 0xEB00. In the logs, these frames show up as lines like shown in figure 2:

In figure 2 the first byte of the 8-byte payload (0x10, 0x11, 0x12, . . . ) is the TP.DT sequence number (start-
ing at 1 and incrementing by 1 for each subsequent packet), while the remaining 7 bytes are the actual
data payload. For each RTS session, we reconstruct the original J1939 PDU by ordering all associated
TP.DT frames by their sequence number and concatenating bytes 2–8 from each frame until the expected

packet count is reached. This produces a contiguous byte array that constitutes one “logical” message at
the J1939-TP layer.

The next step is to interpret these reassembled PDUs as application-layer reflash messages. The firmware
update tool typically uses a diagnostics protocol (UDS-like or proprietary) encapsulated in these J1939
PDUs. In this protocol, some PDUs carry control information (e.g., “enter programming session,” “erase
memory,” “request download”), while others carry raw firmware payload. Differentiation is straightfor-
ward once the PDUs are reassembled: control messages are short and have recognizable service or opcode
patterns, whereas firmware messages are maximally sized, binary-looking payloads with little structure or
ASCII content. For the firmware-carrying PDUs, a small header is usually present at the start of the PDU
(e.g., a block index, memory address, or region identifier), followed by a large contiguous data field. In
practice, we strip the protocol-specific header bytes (whose format can be inferred from recurring patterns
across blocks) and treat the remaining bytes as raw firmware data.
Reconstructing the full firmware image then becomes a matter of concatenating these data fields in the cor-
rect order. Each download “block” corresponds to a portion of the ECU flash address space. The order can

 11

be inferred either from explicit address/offset fields in the PDU headers or from monotonically increas-
ing block indices embedded in the data. For example, if each PDU’s payload begins with a block counter
and the blocks are sent in ascending order, we simply sort the extracted blocks by that counter and append
their data sections. Where multiple regions are reflashed (e.g., a bootloader segment and an application
segment), we maintain separate buffers keyed by the region identifier and merge them according to the ad-
dresses declared in the download/erase commands earlier in the session.
Finally, the reconstructed binary images can be validated using transport-layer completion signals and
post-transfer control traffic visible in the CAN logs. As shown in Figure 3, the conclusion of each seg-
mented transfer is indicated by the final TP.DT data packets (PGN 0xEB00) followed by a TP.CM End-
OfMsgACK frame (e.g., 18ECF900#13A80018FF00EF00). The screenshot highlights this transition, where
the last contiguous sequence of TP.DT frames is immediately followed by the EndOfMsgACK confirming
successful reception of the segmented payload. This acknowledgment mirrors the parameters announced in
the corresponding RTS message, explicitly confirming both the total payload size (0x00A8 bytes) and the
packet count (0x18), thereby signaling transport-layer completion for PGN 0x00EF00. Immediately after
this point—also visible in the screenshot—the traffic shifts to proprietary diagnostic messages (18EFF900
and 18EF00F9), consistent with ECU post-download handling such as internal verification, memory com-
mit, or diagnostic session transitions. Although no standalone CRC or checksum field is explicitly ob-
servable in the captured frames, integrity validation is achieved by verifying that (i) all expected TP.DT
sequence numbers are present, (ii) packet ordering is contiguous and monotonic, and (iii) the EndOfMs-
gACK parameters match the reconstructed byte count. Once these conditions are met, the assembled bi-
nary accurately represents the firmware image delivered to the ECU during the reflash and can be used for
subsequent static analysis, including disassembly, binary comparison, or cross-session diffing.

4.4 REVERSE-ENGINEERING OEM DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS
To understand how firmware flashing is implemented—and where attackers might exploit weak points—we
reverse-engineered key components of two widely used OEM diagnostic platforms: Cummins INSITE and
Navistar’s service software. The goal was not to modify these tools but to analyze how they authenticate
with ECUs, package firmware, and manage UDS service routines during a reflash. This analysis was per-
formed strictly within the bounds of static program inspection and controlled test-bench execution.

4.4.1 Cummins INSITE
INSITE is a Windows-based diagnostic and calibration platform used to service Cummins engines. Inter-
nally, the software relies on a set of DLLs that implement the diagnostic protocol stack, including J1939
transport, UDS services, calibration handling, and Security Access logic.
We used tools such as Ghidra, IDA, Binwalk, DotPeek, and dnSpy to inspect these binaries and supporting
files. By analyzing INSITE’s libraries, we identified:
 • Seed-key algorithms used to unlock ECUs before a reflash INSITE stores the Security Access rou-
 tines inside a dedicated DLL, where seed values are parsed from ECU responses and processed
 through a reversible transformation (often XOR or simple arithmetic operations) to generate the
 unlock key. Unlike more secure implementations that rely on cryptographic key exchange, these
 algorithms were short and deterministic, making them susceptible to replication by an adversary.
 • Firmware encryption and packaging logic INSITE’s calibration files (e.g., .cal, .ecfg) include sec-
 tions that are AES-encrypted or XOR-masked. Decompilation of the related libraries revealed the
 AES key material and table lookups used during decryption. This means that anyone with read ac-
 cess to the installation directory could theoretically decrypt the firmware segments, undermining
 confidentiality.

 • UDS routine handlers for Request Download, Transfer Data, and Transfer Exit These routines de-

 • Session control and timing parameters INSITE statically defines the timing of periodic “TesterPre-

4.4.3 Navistar Software
To understand how Navistar implements UDS SecurityAccess and firmware authentication, we performed
a full reverse-engineering workflow that included decompressing the OEM Java application, decompiling
its classes, locating the native DLLs responsible for cryptographic operations, and reconstructing the secu-
rity algorithm.

4.4.3.1 Extracting and Decompiling Application Components
To understand how Navistar’s diagnostic software performs its security functions, we began by examin-
ing the structure of the application itself. The tool is packaged as a compressed Java-based application,
but many of its critical operations—especially those related to authentication and ECU unlocking—are
handled by embedded native library files rather than the Java code that users interact with. By unpacking
the application and decompiling the Java classes, we were able to trace how the software loads these hid-
den components. This process revealed that the program relies on a specific native library (a DLL file) to
carry out the SecurityAccess routine required before an ECU can be reprogrammed. Identifying this DLL
was the key first step, because it showed where the actual unlocking logic resides and confirmed that the
authentication mechanism is included directly in the diagnostic software rather than stored on protected
hardware.

4.4.3.2 Exploring and Identifying UDS Functions
Unified Diagnostic Services (UDS) provide a standardized interface for interacting with vehicle electronic
control units (ECUs) to perform diagnostic, configuration, and programming-related operations. In this

 14

work, a subset of UDS services was exercised to characterize diagnostic session behavior, security mecha-
nisms, and the availability of internal ECU data on heavy-duty vehicle platforms.
The following UDS services were tested during experimentation:
 • Read Data By Identifier (0x22)
 • TesterPresent (0x3E 00)
 • DiagnosticSessionControl – Default Session (0x10 01)
 • DiagnosticSessionControl – Extended Session (0x10 03)
 • DiagnosticSessionControl – Programming Session (0x10 02)
 • SecurityAccess – Request Seed (0x27 01)
 • ECU Reset – Hard Reset (0x11 02)
Successful responses to these services confirmed UDS communication support and allowed further diag-
nostic interaction. Read Data By Identifier (DID) requests were then used to extract internal ECU meta-
data, including software revision information, serial identifiers, ECU descriptors, and vehicle identification
data.
When tested on a Bendix EC80ESP brake controller, DID extraction revealed representative information
such as software calibration timestamps (e.g., 20170928), composite software revision strings (e.g., Rev
1.287.1.19), ECU serial identifiers (e.g., K139962, 1030031885), and software or calibration pack-
age identifiers (e.g., A82J170202A_bdx82_215#NHSA500K_001). Similar DID extraction was also suc-
cessful tested on the engine control module (ECM), which yielded similarly detailed identification and
configuration data. Retrieved identifiers included CMMNS), engine and calibration family identifiers (e.g.,
FC0PA54), vehicle manufacturer information (INTERNATIONAL), and platform-specific model identifiers
(e.g., LT62F00). Additional DIDs exposed vehicle-level attributes such as model year (2013) and the full
vehicle identification number (VIN), confirming that the ECM stores persistent bindings between engine
software, vehicle platform, and chassis identity. Collectively, these results demonstrate that UDS-based
DID access provides a comprehensive view of both ECU-internal state and vehicle-level metadata across
multiple controller domains.
SecurityAccess behavior was evaluated to assess access-controlled diagnostic functionality. Repeated Di-
agnosticSessionControl requests consistently transitioned ECUs into the extended diagnostic session (0x10
03), with updated timing parameters returned by the ECU. Subsequent SecurityAccess Level 1 seed re-
quests (0x27 01) reliably produced valid 4-byte seed values, demonstrating stable and repeatable seed gen-
eration.
Attempts to escalate security access using sub-function 0x02 consistently resulted in negative responses
(NRC 0x13: IncorrectMessageLengthOrInvalidFormat). This behavior indicates that key submission re-
quires OEM-specific formatting or derivation logic beyond the scope of standard UDS service definitions,
and that successful programming authorization depends on proprietary implementations.
To further investigate this mechanism, the diagnostic software’s native security library was analyzed. Re-
verse analysis identified a function that derives the response key from the ECU-provided seed and the re-
quested security level. Further testing of this function produced keys that matched those observed during
legitimate firmware update procedures, confirming correct identification of the SecurityAccess logic used
by the diagnostic tool.

4.4.3.3 Reconstructing the Navistar Encryption Algorithm
Once we identified the function used for SecurityAccess, we analyzed how it transforms the ECU’s seed
into the unlock key. By examining the native code extracted from the native library, we fully recovered
Navistar’s SecurityAccess key-generation algorithm:
 • PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 used to derive a key from the ECU’s serial number.
 • Salt value selected from a static lookup table.
 • Iteration count encoded in table bytes.
 • Output key used to initialize AES-ECB.
 • Final UDS unlock key = AES-ECB(seed)
Navistar implements a cryptographically stronger approach than simpler XOR-based or arithmetic rou-
tines found in other OEM tools. However, because the entire function resides in a DLL that ships with the
diagnostic software, an attacker could replicate the algorithm once the DLL is reverse-engineered.

 16

4.4.3.4 Modules Using Hardcoded Secret Keys
During our analysis, we found that not all Navistar modules rely on the stronger cryptographic process
used by the main engine controllers. Some smaller subsystems, such as certain cruise control or accessory
modules, use a much simpler method for SecurityAccess. Instead of deriving a key from the ECU’s serial
number and salt values, these modules rely on a fixed, hardcoded key that is embedded directly in the diag-
nostic software’s code. This means the unlock key is the same for every unit of that module type and never
changes. Once this static key is discovered—either through decompiling the Java classes or inspecting the
library files—anyone could unlock those modules without needing ECU-specific information. This incon-
sistency creates a weaker point in the overall security design, because these modules can be accessed and
reprogrammed with far less effort compared to the main engine controllers.

4.5 MAPPING ATTACK SURFACES
With a clear picture of how Navistar’s diagnostic tool authenticates and performs firmware updates, we
mapped the points in this workflow where an attacker could interfere or take advantage of weak protec-
tions. The flashing process follows a fixed sequence: the diagnostic tool requests access, the ECU re-
sponds with a seed, the software generates an unlock key, and then the new firmware is transferred over
the CAN/J1939 network in sequential data blocks. Because the entire authentication process and firmware-
transfer logic are contained within the diagnostic software itself, a determined attacker who reverse-engineers
the tool could potentially replicate every step of the process. This creates several attack surfaces directly
tied to firmware tampering, including the ability to generate unauthorized unlock keys, replay or spoof se-
curity messages, or upload modified firmware that changes how the ECU behaves. Our CAN-bus captures
confirmed that these messages follow predictable patterns, meaning an attacker with network access could
also attempt to inject forged firmware blocks during a legitimate update or trigger a reflash without autho-
rization. By aligning the reverse-engineered behaviors with real diagnostic traffic, we identified exactly
where tampering could occur and how an attacker could manipulate the flashing sequence. This mapping
makes clear that the firmware update path is one of the most sensitive and exposed components in the ECU
security chain.

 5. DISCUSSION
This work demonstrates that heavy-duty ECU reflashing is both highly observable and largely reproducible
from the J1939 CAN bus when an attacker can gain diagnostic-network access. Across both the bench-
top environment and an in-vehicle capture, we consistently observed the expected UDS-over-J1939 work-
flow: session establishment, SecurityAccess, programming preparation routines, and bulk firmware deliv-
ery via J1939-21 Transport Protocol. The controlled testbed was especially valuable because it reduced
background PGN noise and enabled repeated captures with clean bracketing from session start to ECU re-
set, allowing direct comparison to real-fleet behavior. The close alignment between the two environments
suggests that laboratory captures can be used as a reliable stand-in for studying real-world reflashing se-
quences and for building security detectors without requiring continuous vehicle access.
A key finding is that firmware content can be reconstructed directly from TP.CM/TP.DT frames in pas-
sively collected logs. In our traces, the firmware payload appears in standard segmented transfers initi-
ated by TP.CM control traffic and carried by consecutive TP.DT sequence numbers, allowing straightfor-
ward reassembly of the transmitted byte stream. Importantly, we found that completion of a transfer is
best inferred from transport-layer signaling rather than assumptions about the frequency of certain back-
ground identifiers. For example, 18ECF900 appears repeatedly throughout the log and is not, by itself, a
reliable marker of transfer termination. Instead, transport completion is indicated by the end of the TP.DT
sequence for a given RTS session followed immediately by an EndOfMsgACK that mirrors the size and
packet count parameters announced at the start of the transfer. This behavior is visible in Figure 3, where
the final TP.DT frames are followed by an EndOfMsgACK (e.g., 18ECF900#13A80018FF00EF00), after
which traffic transitions into proprietary diagnostic/control exchanges consistent with post-download fi-
nalization. Practically, this implies that integrity checks for reconstruction can be implemented without a
standalone CRC field by validating monotonic TP.DT sequencing and consistency between RTS parame-
ters and the corresponding EndOfMsgACK.
The reverse-engineering results contextualize why these network-level observations matter: the barrier to
producing a valid reflashing session is often the diagnostic tool’s embedded authentication and packaging
logic rather than any protections on the bus itself. Analysis of Cummins INSITE indicates that key rou-
tines for SecurityAccess and calibration handling are implemented in software components that can be
inspected and replicated, and that portions of packaging/decryption logic may expose key material or pre-
dictable transformations. In parallel, Navistar’s implementation shows stronger cryptographic structure
(e.g., PBKDF2-derived key material and AES operations) than simple arithmetic/XOR schemes; however,
the critical security property is not solely the algorithm choice but where it resides. Because the Secu-
rityAccess logic and supporting material live inside deployable software (e.g., native DLLs), an attacker
who can obtain and reverse-engineer the tool can potentially reproduce the unlock process and generate
authorized-looking programming sessions. This highlights a common systemic weakness: even when
cryptography is present, the lack of hardware-backed secrets and attestation can reduce the effective se-
curity boundary to “obscurity of the tool binary.”
Taken together, the CAN capture analysis and tool reverse-engineering reinforce that firmware update
pathways are a high-impact attack surface for heavy-duty vehicles. Once an adversary can emulate the
diagnostic client, the remaining steps—entering a session, pacing TP transfers, and delivering payload
blocks—follow stable, repeatable patterns that are observable on the wire. This has two immediate impli-
cations for defense. First, intrusion detection systems can focus on detecting anomalous reflashing work-
flows by monitoring UDS service sequences, session transitions, and sustained TP.CM/TP.DT bursts that
match programming behavior, even in the absence of payload decryption. Second, firmware fingerprint-
ing becomes feasible when defenders can reconstruct the transmitted image from logs and compare it to

 18

known-good baselines, enabling post-event verification and fleet-scale auditing after suspected compro-
mise. However, these defensive techniques must be paired with stronger OEM-side controls—such as
hardware-rooted authentication, signed firmware enforcement, and stricter authorization for programming
sessions—because network monitoring alone cannot prevent an attacker from completing a reflash if the
ECU accepts the session.
Finally, several limitations shape how these results should be interpreted. Our reconstruction confirms
what was delivered over the bus during captured sessions, but it does not prove that the ECU committed
the image to flash or that post-programming checks (if present) were cryptographically strong; those prop-
erties depend on ECU-internal verification routines that may be proprietary. Additionally, while the bench-
top setup preserved OEM harnessing and signaling, it cannot capture every interaction present in a fully
populated vehicle network (e.g., gateway behavior, concurrent ECU chatter under different ignition/engine
states, or telematics mediation). Despite these constraints, the combined evidence from real-truck captures,
repeatable bench traces, and tool-level reverse engineering provides a cohesive and actionable picture of
how reflashing occurs, how firmware can be recovered from traffic, and why OEM diagnostic implementa-
tions materially influence the tampering threat model.

 6. FUTURE WORK
This study establishes a foundation for understanding firmware tampering risks in heavy-duty vehicles,
but several important directions remain for future investigation. A primary extension of this work is the
reverse-engineering of additional OEM and diagnostic tools used by Navistar and not analyzed here. Par-
ticular attention will be given to identifying where key material, seed–key logic, and session-control be-
havior reside within software binaries, and how these elements differ between engine, brake, and body
controllers.
Building on this software analysis, a second objective is to fully replicate SecurityAccess behavior in a
standalone script. Using information recovered from reverse-engineered diagnostic tools, we plan to im-
plement custom client software capable of negotiating diagnostic sessions, requesting seeds, computing
valid keys, and transitioning ECUs into programming mode without relying on OEM tooling. This func-
tionality will first be validated on the bench-top testbed and then evaluated on an operational truck un-
der controlled conditions. Successfully reproducing the authentication workflow would demonstrate how
closely OEM security mechanisms are tied to software secrecy rather than hardware-backed trust, and it
would provide a practical framework for testing intrusion-detection strategies against unauthorized but
protocol-compliant reflashing attempts.
In parallel, we intend to investigate fault-injection approaches as an alternative pathway to firmware ac-
cess. Rather than deriving or replicating the SecurityAccess algorithm, this line of work will explore whether
timing faults, voltage glitches, bus-level manipulation, or malformed diagnostic sequences can cause ECUs
to bypass authentication checks or enter unintended memory-read or programming states. Such experi-
ments would assess whether firmware memory can be extracted or modified without completing the nomi-
nal security handshake, and whether these behaviors differ across ECU types or firmware versions. Under-
standing the feasibility of such bypasses is critical for evaluating the robustness of current ECU bootload-
ers against active attacks.
Finally, future work will leverage insights from reverse-engineered diagnostic software to better interpret
proprietary J1939 traffic. By correlating observed CAN frames with internal software routines, we aim
to identify and decode manufacturer-specific PGNs and SPNs that are currently undocumented. Mapping
these proprietary identifiers to their semantic meaning would improve situational awareness during repro-
gramming and enable more precise intrusion-detection rules that distinguish benign background traffic
from security-relevant operations. Collectively, these efforts will deepen understanding of OEM repro-
gramming ecosystems and support the development of comprehensive defenses against firmware tamper-
ing in heavy-duty vehicles.

 7. CONCLUSION
This work examined firmware tampering risk in heavy-duty vehicles by combining (i) J1939/UDS CAN
traffic captures from both a repeatable bench-top testbed and an operational Navistar truck and (ii) reverse-
engineering of OEM diagnostic software used to authenticate and deliver firmware. Across both environ-
ments, we observed a consistent reflashing workflow and confirmed that bulk firmware delivery occurs
through standard J1939-21 Transport Protocol segmentation (TP.CM/TP.DT), making reflashing activity
highly observable on the bus.
A central result is that firmware payloads can be reconstructed directly from CAN logs by reassembling
TP.DT sequences under the associated TP.CM sessions. We also established that transport-layer comple-
tion is best determined using the RTS/EndOfMsgACK relationship rather than the presence or repetition
of individual identifiers. As shown in Figure 3, the end of a transfer is characterized by the final contiguous
TP.DT sequence followed immediately by a TP.CM EndOfMsgACK (e.g., 18ECF900#13A80018FF00EF00),
after which traffic transitions to post-download proprietary diagnostics consistent with finalization. This
provides a practical integrity check for reconstruction: verifying monotonic TP.DT sequencing and match-
ing EndOfMsgACK parameters to the reconstructed byte count, even when no explicit CRC field is visible
in the captured frames.
In parallel, reverse-engineering of OEM diagnostic platforms demonstrated that the effective security
boundary often depends on how authentication and packaging logic are implemented and where secrets
reside. While some tools employ stronger cryptographic primitives than others, embedding key-derivation
and unlock routines in distributable software artifacts can enable replication by an adversary with access
to the tool binaries. Taken together, these findings show that the reflashing pathway is both a high-value
attack surface and a high-signal target for monitoring, and they motivate defenses that combine stronger
ECU-side enforcement (e.g., signed firmware and hardware-rooted authorization) with network-level de-
tection and firmware fingerprinting. Ultimately, improving heavy-duty vehicle cyber resilience requires
both protocol-aware monitoring of reprogramming behavior and standardized, verifiable protections that
prevent unauthorized firmware from being accepted in the first place.
