Executive Summary
The Sunrise County Economic Council (SCEC), an economic and community development
nonprofit in—and Regional Planning Organization for—Washington County, Maine, sought
assistance through the Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP), supported by
the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), to address community energy challenges. Focused on
community and economic impact, SCEC envisioned a strategic energy plan (SEP) that would
strengthen collaboration with its local utility, Versant Power. The organization also sought to
empower residents and town leaders to better understand energy challenges and participate in
actions that reduce outage risk, address rising electricity costs, and improve public safety and
disaster preparedness.

This SEP development followed the multistage approach established by ETIPP, which included
organizing a team of local leaders and partners, facilitating regular project action meetings and
an on-site community workshop, data collection and baselining, and identifying and analyzing
the feasibility of project options. The local partners included SCEC and the local utility, Versant
Power. The regional partner was the Island Institute, and the national laboratory partner was the
National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR). The project kicked off at the end of November 2024,
with one on-site workshop held March 24–27, 2025. The team met once or twice per month
virtually from January to December 2025 to develop and draft the SEP which concluded early
2026.

Data analysis included electricity reliability at the grid feeder level from 5-year consecutive data
provided by Versant Power (2025): CAIDI (Customer Average Interruption Duration Index),
SAIDI (System Average Interruption Duration Index), and SAIFI (System Average Interruption
Frequency Index), outage causes were also provided. Versant also provided data and details of
rate history, its vegetation management program, and its low-income assistance program. Open
data sources included utility rates, the U.S. Department of Agriculture Forest Inventory and
Analysis (U.S. Department of Agriculture 2025), Maine Public Utilities Commission electricity
source disclosures, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services emPOWER Program
data for electricity-dependent durable medical equipment dependence, and hospital encounter
data from Main Health Data Organization.

This plan introduces a “relational infrastructure” approach, recognizing improved energy
outcomes depend as much on coordination and trust as on physical grid investments. Examples
of practice used in this relational infrastructure approach include beginning with and sustaining
two-way communication and engagement between the public and project planners, and
outcomes-based strategies (Ansell & Gash, 2008; Arnstein, 1969; Bryson et al., 2013; Fung,
2015).

 The projects prioritized by SCEC (Sec. 3.2) focus on the reduction of outage risk and the
increase of public safety. Examples include a public awareness and safety campaign, “Power to
the People (P2P),” which includes community outreach at public events, town meetings, and a
webpage providing a public channel for current and future information on safety and vegetation
management and amplifying important communication links from Versant Power. SCEC also
plans to develop resilience tools and guidance for residents, town leadership, and public works

 iv

staff. Another potential project includes risk analysis of Washington County residents reliant on
electricity-dependent medical equipment, including variables of outage history, access to
generators and fuel, and remoteness of residences.

This SEP summarizes the data, actions, vision and goals developed through the planning process,
and potential funding streams. It is intended as a public document that SCEC can share with
existing and potential partners—and Washington County, Maine residents—to adopt and
implement. It provides a starting structure to build on as SCEC scales its economic and
community development in terms of energy reliability, resilience, safety, and affordability. With
new information and initiatives, this document should be revisited and updated.

Introduction
1.1 Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
This Strategic Energy Planning Project was developed as a part of the Energy Technology
Innovation Partnership Project (ETIPP), supported by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE).
ETIPP is a community-led technical support program to help coastal, remote, and island
communities build resilient and sustainable energy systems. These communities have unique
physical and geographic features that influence energy security and resilience and limited access
to the available solutions that can support community energy goals.

The Sunrise County Economic Council (SCEC) applied to ETIPP seeking to increase
understanding of electrical outages, electricity rates, and grid instability for themselves and for
Washington County residents, business owners, and town leadership. SCEC envisioned a county
that would increase its agency to improve its electricity reliability and rates, and reduce health
and safety risks from power outages and downed lines.

1.2 Community Overview
The Indigenous Peoples of Maine are collectively known as the Wabanaki “People of the
Dawnland,” of which today there are four federally recognized tribal nations: the Houlton Band
of Maliseet, the Mi’kmaq Nation, the Penobscot Nation, and the Passamaquoddy Tribe (About
the Wabanaki Nations 2025). For thousands of years, the Wabanaki people have been sustained
by the vast resources of forest and sea. Today, Maine relies on these same environments to
support its primary industries of lumber, shipbuilding, and fishing.

Maine is proportionally the most rural and forested state in the nation (Woodall et al. 2022) with
88% forest land (USDA 2025). Forests are dense, with fast-growing trees such as the eastern
white pine, that can scale up to 3 feet per year (Wendel and Smith, 1990). The state endures
extreme weather exposure, including along the approximately 3,500 miles of coastline when
counting all coves, bays, and islands (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration 2025).
Washington County, in eastern Maine, is situated along nearly 20% of Maine’s coastland and
shares its northeastern border with New Brunswick, Canada. Most of the county’s population
live along the coast and 100% of the population is classified as rural by the U.S. Census (U.S.
Census Bureau 2020). Weather events—especially high wind and ice storms that take place
across dense forest coverage—are significant contributors to Maine having the highest outage
risk in the United States (U.S. Energy Information Administration [EIA] 2024). With 90% forest
land, higher than the Maine average (USDA 2025), Washington County residents experience an
average of 33 hours of outages per year. Two out of three power outages are caused by falling
trees (Versant Power 2025).

The electricity received by Washington County residents is provided through two utilities: 1) the
nonprofit, Eastern Maine Electric Cooperative, serving approximately 13,000 accounts in
Washington County and two other counties, and 2) Versant Power, an investor-owned utility,
serving 165,000 accounts.

1.3 Energy Accomplishments
Recent statewide efforts demonstrate strong alignment with a community-driven strategy
centered on hazard mitigation, safety and emergency readiness, and energy assistance. This plan
complements—but does not duplicate—utility capital planning and state energy policy by
focusing on the community-level actions that influence reliability, safety, and affordability
outcomes. Future coordination with county planning efforts, commissioners, and managers
would strengthen the implementation of this plan.

The Maine Won’t Wait report (Maine Climate Council 2024) advances energy efficiency,
electrification, and grid modernization to improve reliability, safety, and affordability. Through
the Maine Community Resilience Partnership, it directly supports local emergency preparedness
and infrastructure resilience—providing a foundation for collaborative hazard mitigation and
community-informed outreach.

The Maine Energy Plan (Maine Governor’s Energy Office 2025) reinforces these priorities by
emphasizing efficiency upgrades, diversified energy resources, and grid modernization. Its focus
on infrastructure resilience and emergency preparedness strengthens utility-community
coordination, particularly in reducing outage risks and improving system safety under extreme
conditions.

The Maine Infrastructure Resilience Plan (Maine Infrastructure Rebuilding and Resilience
Commission 2025) further operationalizes these goals through investments in risk reduction,
resilient rebuilding, and coordinated response systems. Its emphasis on data, communication, and
local capacity-building directly supports enhanced outage preparedness, including proactive
communication strategies and community-level emergency planning.

Finally, legislation H.P. 1311/L.D. 1967 establishes a community-based energy coaching pilot
that improves access to efficiency programs, reduces energy burden (a household’s residential
energy costs, divided by household income), and promotes safer energy use. This model is
highly relevant to advancing energy assistance and thermal safety by helping residents prioritize
weatherization, navigate incentives, and adopt technologies in a safe and cost-effective sequence.

 3

Together, these efforts demonstrate a clear pathway for collaboration between utilities and
community organizations. Initiatives such as vegetation management outreach, expanded
emergency communication, and targeted energy coaching can amplify existing utility actions
while ensuring communities are informed, prepared, and able to access critical resources—
ultimately strengthening system resilience, public safety, and energy security.

1.4 Energy Vision, Goals, and Focus Areas
Challenges
Foundational challenges for Washington County in terms of energy strategy include limited
resources for governance at both county and town levels. Of the 34 municipalities in Versant’s
Washington County service area, 1/4 are unincorporated townships governed by the state’s Land
Use Planning Commission. In addition, Washington County is a rural environment with a
majority remote population, with many residents falling below the poverty line (17.1% in 2022
[U.S. Census 2024]) and a large population of citizens aged 65 and older—many of whom are
aging in place in rural areas.

Another challenge is the high occurrence of natural disasters, especially during the winter
months with wind and ice storms. Nine such disasters from March 2022 and May 2024 merited
presidential disaster or emergency declarations (Maine Infrastructure Rebuilding and Resilience
Commission 2025).

Other potential rate-impacting challenges that cascade down from the state level include energy
market policies that determine responsibilities for transmission costs and structures for
compensation such as net energy meeting for distributed energy generation. Customers in Maine
are also billed for programs to promote energy efficiency and conservation (via Efficiency Maine
Trust), to support state policies (e.g., the low-income assistance programs), and to recover costs
associated with the restructuring of the electricity industry. Other rate-impacting drivers include
market fluctuations, especially for the primary energy source for New England: natural gas.

Opportunities
The opportunities that emerged from extended workshop conversations included encouraging
more actions with small local governments noted as effective stakeholders, there are a total of 44
local government entities in Washington County. In addition, explaining the economics of
electricity was suggested as an opportunity to get people’s attention and trust because, according
to SCEC, mistrust of the utilities in Washington County has grown as renewable energy
generation has increased. The concern that outsiders are profiting by siting renewable generation
projects in Maine and exporting power to other states—while electricity rates are increasing for
the residents of Maine—is voiced often by the County’s residents.

Opportunities to partner with Versant Power included collaborating on vegetation maintenance
education and town ordinances, coordinating messaging and public outreach on electric and
outage safety, amplifying messaging through coordinated campaigns including web resources,
and gathering and assessing data on outage risk in the county.

 4

Coordinating with the utilities and emergency responders presents an opportunity to plan for the
needs of energy vulnerable residents, those with dependence on electricity to power medical
equipment, lack of backup power, economic hardship, and remoteness.

An opportunity to partner with Washington County Community College for workforce
development in energy trades was suggested as well as coordination with Washington County on
emergency operations. It was noted that more skilled tradespeople were needed to install,
maintain, and fix heat pumps and heat pump water heaters because of the increase in adoption
prompted by Maine’s energy goals and incentives (Maine Governor’s Energy Office 2025).

Finally, there was urging for a partnership with, or funding from, the Maine Office of
Community Affairs’ Community Resilience Partnership and possible Efficiency Maine Trust.

Focus Areas that were discussed but relegated to future planning phases and projects include
energy efficiency because it has already received much focus from state and county strategy. On-
site generation assets and resilience hubs were also discussed and held for the future in
anticipation that initial SEP projects will increase participation and improve understanding,
perception, and adoption of energy issues among members of Washington County. Impacts of
energy interruption, outages, and cost on the fishing industry was another topic held for future
consideration. The economic well-being of Washington County depends significantly on the
fishing industry. Fishing and related industry services such as ice and cold storage, fuel and bait,
and all logistics for processing and distribution make up a waterfront that faces numerous risks.

Responding to themes that emerged in the March 2025 community workshop in Washington
County, the energy vision statement was finalized by SCEC from which to root its strategic
energy priorities, projects, and goals. In most ETIPP projects, it is common for energy visioning
to happen after a baseline assessment of potential energy technology is provided. However, with
SCEC’s unique interest in a relationship-based energy strategy, the visioning preceded most of
the data baselining.

 Energy Vision Statement
 The people of Washington County, Maine, have the information and resources
 they need to actively influence decisions that increase safety, affordability, and
 reliability of the electrical grid now and in the future.

2 Baseline Assessment
Determining an energy baseline is a key step in the ETIPP Strategic Energy Plan development.
Baselining helps quantify risk, prioritize projects, understand feasibility, set goals, and measure
results.

Based on the energy priorities of SCEC, the analysis effort focused on information that could
improve public knowledge on the energy landscape in Washington County and present
educational, empowering, influential, and impactful solutions

For Washington County, the energy baseline focused on electrical system reliability and
resilience, energy affordability, and electric and outage safety.

Data that could inform or improve institutional planning such as outage readiness, emergency
response, and vegetation management were determined to be of the highest value and historically
unavailable to SCEC at a level more granular than ZIP Code.

2.1 Baselining Electrical System Reliability and Resilience
Baselining of reliability and resilience for this plan is high level, not a full assessment.
Reliability focuses on higher-probability and lower-consequence everyday disruptions, and in the
electricity sector is defined as “the ability to meet the electricity needs of end-use customers,
even when events reduce the amount of available electricity” (National Infrastructure Advisory
Council 2010). Resilience focuses on disruptions that are lower probability but higher
consequence and cause outages lasting days or months, and the power system’s ability to
“anticipate, prepare for, and adapt to changing conditions and to withstand, respond to, and
recover rapidly from disruptions” (Hotchkiss and Dane 2019).

To guide infrastructure investments and improve both reliability and resilience, utilities consider
risks of threat, potential damage that could be caused, and the consequences of the damage.
Resilience practices vary because of service area circumstances. In Washington County, below-
ground mitigation—i.e. burying lines—is limited by environmental and geologic factors such as
hard bedrock inland and erosion at the coastline present. Another barrier making undergrounding
cost-prohibitive is sparse, rural population. Versant has 6,400 miles of primary distribution lines
and 1,275 miles of transmission lines in its service territory with 165,000 customers, which is
about 22 customers per mile of line (Versant Power 2026). This means high infrastructure costs
relative to the base rate collected (Ross et al. 2018). This cost dynamic is further covered in
Section 2.3, Energy Analysis.

From Versant’s 2026 Integrated Grid Plan (Versant Power 2026), a primary reliability and
resilience initiative is a comprehensive cycle- and risk-based vegetation management program,
critical for Washington County, which is 90% forest land (USDA 2025). Guided by arborists, in
2025, 650 risk trees were removed by Versant in Washington County, and vegetation was
trimmed along 250 miles of primary line and 250 miles of secondary line (Versant Power 2025).
Actions such as these are shown to reduce outages and quicken restoration times (Lawrence
Berkeley National Laboratory 2024; Taylor et al. 2022) but are limited by right of way, which in
Maine is a significant barrier because forest land ownership is 91.74% private (USDA 2025). In

 6

Versant’s territory, two-thirds of outages are caused by falling trees, and 75% of outages are
from trees outside of Versant’s right of way (Versant Power 2025).

Other above-ground mitigation measures recently implemented by Versant include installing
compact modular reclosers that connect to power lines and monitor and manage faults and, in
some cases, restore power automatically. Versant has also been insulating power lines to prevent
short circuiting. Further details on Versant’s actual capital investments for 2021–2025 and
projected for 2026–2030 are provided in its 2026 Integrated Grid Plan (Versant Power 2026).

(System Average Interruption Frequency Index); see Table 3 and Figure 1. The data included

by a standardized industry statistic (IEEE 2012, 2022).

Baselining Electrical and Outage Safety
A main dimension of electrical and outage safety is power system reliability. Table 3 provides
the standard power interruption indices—CAIDI (Customer Average Interruption Duration
Index), SAIDI (System Average Interruption Duration Index), and SAIFI (System Average
Interruption Frequency Index)—using data from Versant Power (2025). Table 4 provides
averages at four interval levels for how often and how long power outages last historically for
their Washington County service area. Based on these data, the overall averages indicate 1)
residents could expect four outages a year that last longer than 5 minutes, 2) the total duration of
outages one experiences in a given year could average 25 hours, and 3) restoration can take up to
6 hours. Reliability estimates differ by grid feeder line performance.

A utility’s power outage restoration protocol—Versant Power included—prioritizes critical
facilities such as hospitals and first responders and hazardous downed power lines first, then
main transmission line equipment and infrastructure repairs followed by distribution lines that
bring power back to the highest concentration of customers first (Versant n.d.b). Typically, the
last to have power restored are the remote, difficult-to-reach areas—like much of Washington
County, which is sparsely populated at just 12 people per square mile (U.S. Census Bureau
2020). In anticipation of outages, Versant Power provides practical preparation tips to customers,
for example, its Winter Safety Flyer, 2025–2026 for sustained electrical outages (see Appendix
A).

For standard operation, Versant’s LifeLight Program aims to prevent the unnecessary or
accidental disconnection of electric service for people who use electricity and battery-dependent
assistive technology and medical devices. Customers reliant on medical equipment can confirm
their needs, verified by a health-care provider, with Versant (Versant 2026). This does not
prioritize a customer during nonstandard events such as weather-caused outages.

For nonstandard power outage events, this section baselines Washington County’s most
vulnerable customers to sustained service interruptions. According to the U.S. Department of
Health and Human Services (HHS) emPOWER program (HHS emPOWER 2025), an estimated
480 residents rely on electricity-dependent durable medical equipment such as ventilators,
oxygen concentrators, or wheelchairs to live independently. Beyond the immediate failure of
life-sustaining equipment, prolonged outages increase the risk of temperature-related mortality—
a danger that is heightened by age, disability, and poverty (Anderson and Bell 2009; Klinger et
al. 2014). These risk factors are significantly more prevalent in Washington County than the
national average. According to the 2024 American Community Survey 5-year data, the percent
of persons aged 65 and older is 26% of residents (1.5x the national average), the percent of
persons with disabilities is 22% of residents (1.7x the national average), and residents living in
poverty was 17% in 2022 (U.S. Census 2024), 1.4x higher than the national average.

Cooling and warming centers are a common resource for people during extended outages.
Daytime capacity is generally higher than overnight. Maine Emergency Management Agency’s

 9

(MEMA) Response and Recovery oversight provides a list of warming centers by county posted
on its Mass Care webpage (MEMA 2020). As of April 2026, there were only two for
Washington County. Due to the number being so low, it is unlikely the most current or complete
source of information. Leaving the home for safe shelter during extended outages is hampered by
limited shelter space (real or perceived); winter storm conditions such as high wind, snow, and
ice that make travel dangerous especially across long rural distances; the cost of gasoline
(Aguilar & Fuentos-Albero 2025); and a lack of transportation. Many households in Washington
County do not have a vehicle; that number is estimated between 7.4% (Grow Smart Maine 2024)
and 15% (Maine Council on Aging 2024).

Evaluating electrical outage impact is advancing to characterize the consequences to customers
from interruption of service. Recent research on electrical resilience in rural Minnesota
introduces a new metric that includes the impact on community critical services including
residential access to safe temperatures and refrigeration for food and medication. This metric—
critical service access—and the results could be used to inform the prioritization of utility
investments and increase outage safety (Matsuda-Dunn et al. 2025).

2.2 Existing Energy Infrastructure
The electricity received by Washington County residents is provided through two utilities: 1) the
nonprofit, Eastern Maine Electric Cooperative, serving approximately 13,000 accounts in
Washington county and two other counties, and 2) Versant Power, an investor-owned utility,
serving 165,000 accounts. Future engagement with Eastern Maine Electric Cooperative is ideal;
however, with most Washington County residents serviced by Versant Power, this planning
process began with them.

This plan does not provide a detailed engineering assessment of power system infrastructure.
Instead, it focuses on equipping communities and utilities with shared technical understanding to
strengthen coordination—recognizing improved outcomes depend not only on infrastructure but
also on the effectiveness of these relationships.

 10

Electric Infrastructure Description
The Versant Power Integrated Grid Plan (2026) and supporting studies describe a system where
Versant operates transmission and distribution infrastructure—more than 1,200 miles of
transmission and 6,000 miles of distribution lines serving more than 160,000 customers—
whereas electricity supply is sourced externally, with a mix of gas, hydro, nuclear, wind, solar,
biomass, and limited oil and coal. The system is largely a simple radial design, like a hub and
spoke, where the power flows one way from the main source. It is geographically dispersed, and
overhead, creating exposure to weather-related disruptions. In their Integrated Grid Plan, Versant
outlines power quality challenges including voltage variability, equipment stress, and load
sensitivity, compounded by increasing electricity demand and energy variability from an increase
in power sources . Reliability is highly influenced by winter weather and high winds, with
documented vulnerabilities across distribution lines, substations, and operational processes—
including vegetation management and emergency response.

Heating and Gas Description
Maine is unique nationally with more than half of homes still relying on heating oil, with
propane, electricity (rapidly growing via heat pumps), and wood as secondary fuels. There is
limited natural gas, and homes often use multiple fuels for backup in extreme cold. Commercial
and government buildings are more diversified (oil, gas, electricity), but fuel price volatility and
rural delivery logistics are defining constraints across the state.

Additional Infrastructure Description
Fuel supply logistics and risks are largely external to Versant Power’s operations as they are a
transmission and distribution utility. Critical infrastructure includes transmission and distribution
lines, substations, transformers, poles, and system control functions, all of which face increasing
risk from extreme weather hazards such as ice, wind, and heavy precipitation (Department of
Energy 2021). Anticipated changes include grid modernization, distribution hardening,
automation, and enhanced vegetation management to improve resilience, safety, and system
reliability (Versant Power 2026).

2.3 Energy Analysis
Electricity Consumption
Based on the Versant Power Integrated Grid Plan (2026), system peak demand is around 364
MW, and electricity consumption in Washington County reflects a low-growth, weather-driven
rural load profile. Historical trends show relatively flat demand with modest growth, with usage
peaking in winter months because of electric heating and declining in shoulder seasons. Peak
demand is largely noncoincident and winter-driven, varying by circuit and substation.

Load is dominated by residential customers, with smaller commercial and limited industrial
presence. Seasonal variation is noticeable; with higher winter loads and lower summer demand
compared to more urban regions.

Versant Precent load forecasts indicate gradual growth across numerous scenarios (Versant
Power 2026) driven by electrification (heat pumps, electric vehicles) and distributed energy
resources. Versant incorporates substation- and circuit-level hourly data to capture noncoincident

 11

peaks and variability, supplementing regional forecasts with more granular system measurements
for planning accuracy.

Energy Costs
Methods for characterizing the cost of household energy typically start with an absolute
economic ratio: percentage of household income spent on energy bills (Boardman 1991). This
method should be considered a minimum for baselining home energy costs because it is limited
in expressing the relative ability a household has to maintain a safe and comfortable home
environment.

When data are available, assessments of energy affordability should consider both physical and
behavioral dimensions of energy burden (Hernández, 2016). Physical dimensions include
housing deficiencies, such as inadequate insulation, that increase energy costs. Behavioral
dimensions include actions households take to cope with those costs, such as reducing electricity
or heating use, limiting appliance use, or making difficult trade-offs between utility bills and
other necessities, including food and medication. These considerations are particularly relevant
in Maine, where much of the housing stock lacks adequate weatherization, making homes more
difficult and expensive to heat and cool. Research also indicates that, compared to the national
average, Maine households are more likely to struggle with electricity costs and, at times, must
make trade-offs among basic needs because of financial constraints (VEIC & Beech Hill
Research, 2024).The cost of energy has been rising nationwide. U.S. Energy Information
Administration electricity data show the average retail price of electricity (cents per kilowatt
hour) in the United States was 13.66¢ in 2021, and 16.48¢ in 2024—a 20% increase in 3 years—
whereas Maine residents experienced a 43% increase (17.02¢ to 24.29¢; EIA 2026). Versant’s
10-year rate history, 2014–2023 (Versant Power 2023) is provided later in Figure 3.

On average, Washington County residents spend about $254 per month on electricity, which is
15% higher than the national average electric bill. These rates are difficult for the energy
burdened, those whose residential energy costs are a high percentage of their household income,
which for Washington County is estimated at 19% (VEIC and Beech Hill Research 2024). The
U.S. Census Household Pulse Survey 2024 data indicate on average 25% of Maine residents
were unable to pay their utility bill at least once in the previous 12 months (U.S. Census 2024).

Assistance to pay for heating costs is sought by many in Washington County. As of July 2025,
more than 1,100 households in Washington County—8% of Versant’s residential customers
(Versant Power 2025)—were participating in Versant Power’s Low Income Assistance Program.
In addition, Maine’s Energy Crisis Intervention Program provides up to $500 for fuel delivery or
utility reconnection fees as well as the Home Energy Assistance Program, which can be used for
heating costs or weatherization. According to Maine State Housing Authority, Home Energy
Assistance Program benefits in 2025 were $780,570, and Energy Crisis Intervention Program
were $88,861 (see Figure 2).

 Energy Efficiency
 Washington County faces persistent and interrelated challenges around residential energy
 efficiency and weatherization, driven by climate, housing characteristics, and fuel dependence. A
 significant impact on energy costs in Maine are the physical deficiencies in housing stock. Much
 of the state’s housing stock predates modern standards for insulation, air sealing, and energy-
 efficient construction. Many homes—particularly in coastal and rural areas—were originally
 built as seasonal or second homes and were never designed or retrofitted for year-round
 occupancy, leaving them poorly weatherized and costly to heat. According to the U.S. Census
 Bureau’s 2018–2022 American Community Survey data, nearly one-fourth of Maine’s 741,803
 housing units—23.4%—were built before 1940.

 Energy upgrades or retrofitting are financially burdensome and, in mobile homes, which
 represent 17.9% of housing stock in Washington County (U.S. Census Bureau 2020), additional
 restrictions related to structure, ownership, and allowable measures further limit weatherization
 or efficiency investments.

 14

The cold, prolonged winters create exceptionally high heating demand, and about two-thirds of
Maine households rely on delivered heating fuels such as oil and propane (EIA 2025). Maine has
no significant reserves of coal, natural gas, or crude oil and therefore relies on imported fuels to
meet its overall energy needs. (EIA 2025). These fuels are expensive and represent a critical
point of failure for household energy security during winter storms or supply disruptions.
Survival on delivered fuels remains common, especially in rural areas.

Key Findings
The baseline highlights that reliability and resilience challenges are primarily vegetation- and
weather-driven, with two-thirds of outages caused by falling trees and 75% originating outside
utility rights of way (Versant Power 2025). Washington County experiences high outage
duration and frequency far exceeding national benchmarks. Characteristics such as low customer
density and rocky terrain make undergrounding cost-prohibitive and restoration slower. Given
the structural constraints on traditional infrastructure solutions such as undergrounding, SCEC’s
strategy emphasizes distributed, community-based risk reduction and preparedness.

There is also a clear concentration of vulnerability among specific populations, including
residents dependent on electricity-powered medical equipment and higher-than-average elderly,
disabled, and low-income populations. The cost of energy is a major issue, with high energy
burden and rising electricity costs. Housing inefficiency and reliance on delivered fuels further
compounds risk.

The baseline assessment identifies several important gaps, uncertainties, and data needs that limit
more targeted planning. Historically, data have not been available below the ZIP Code level, and
although feeder-level outage data could be available through collaboration with Versant, further
granularity is still needed to support localized decision-making. Some infrastructure details are
lacking, and data from Eastern Maine Electric Cooperative have not yet been incorporated—
leaving an incomplete picture of the full system. There are also gaps in outage preparedness
information, including an incomplete or potentially outdated inventory of warming centers and
limited real-time data on medically vulnerable populations who rely on electricity-dependent
equipment.

Uncertainty exists in key socioeconomic indicators, such as the percentage of households
without vehicles, which affects emergency response planning. In addition, the baseline does not
include detailed analysis of fuel supply logistics, heat recovery systems, or comprehensive
outage impact metrics such as “critical service access.” Overall, improved data integration,
validation, and accessibility across utilities, health systems, and community services would
improve the execution of the community’s priority actions.

3 Strategy Analysis
3.1 Options and Feasibility Analysis
This strategic energy plan outlines a potential collaborative roadmap for Washington County,
Maine, to embark on high-impact projects that address priorities of power reliability, resilience,
safety, and affordability. Execution depends on funding, staffing, and collaboration with other
organizations. Collaborative action between the community and utility has expected outcomes of
reaching more people with education and an increase in appropriate and safe vegetation
mitigation and power safety actions among residents, business owners, and town leadership. In
addition to Versant Power, other organizations are named in Section 5 of this report as potential
collaborators.

3.2 Project Priorities
Resulting from the workshop, data baseline, and stakeholder discussions, this plan is grounded in
a central premise: relational infrastructure. This approach recognizes improved energy outcomes
in Washington County depend not only on electrical system investment but also on strengthening
trust, transparency, and coordination between the utility and the communities it serves. Three
priority areas were identified to operationalize this approach.

Priority Areas 1 and 2 focus on reducing outage risk where it is not fully within utility control. In
Washington County, approximately two-thirds of outages are caused by vegetation, and 75% of
that vegetation originates outside utility rights of way. Accordingly, these priorities emphasize
increasing private landowner participation and municipal coordination in hazard mitigation
(Priority 1; see Table 5) and outage safety and preparedness (Priority 2), directly targeting a
primary driver of outages. Although these actions will not eliminate outages, they are expected to
reduce the frequency and severity of vegetation-related disruptions at the local level.

Given that high energy burden and inefficient housing increase vulnerability during outages—
particularly during extreme temperatures—Priority Area 3 addresses the underlying conditions
that amplify outage impacts, such as inefficient housing stock, and a disproportionately large
population of elderly and disabled residents. This priority focuses on reducing vulnerability by
improving access to energy assistance, weatherization, and informed decision-making around
home energy use.

To increase public safety and electricity reliability, SCEC has focused energy planning priorities
where they can empower members of Washington County to work with Versant Power, the
primary utility, through collaborative infrastructure hazard mitigation, safety and emergency
readiness, and energy assistance. SCEC’s approach is repeatable, scalable, and necessary for a
state that has been experiencing a high occurrence of natural disasters. SCEC’s “Power to the
People” approach aligns with a key strategy of Maine’s Infrastructure Rebuilding and Resilience
Commission (subsequently “The Commission”), which is to become “more active, capable, and
self-resourced in hazard mitigation and disaster recovery” (The Commission 2025).

SCEC determined collaborative actions for communities and utilities across three priority areas:
hazard mitigation for energy infrastructure, safety and emergency readiness, and energy
assistance. Examples of the collaboration for each area include the following:

During the ETIPP planning process, SCEC made gains with Priority Area 1, including kicking
off the Power to the People outreach campaign and establishing a relationship with Versant
Power through virtual meetings during 2025 and an on-site workshop in March 2025.

During summer 2025, SCEC got an early start on its SEP community outreach goal of reaching
15,000 people—half of the number of people in all Washington County towns combined—with
energy literacy by tabling at the Machias Wild Blueberry Festival (Aug. 15–17, 2025), where it
reached 200–300 people, followed by the Eastport Pirate Festival (Sept. 5–7, 2025), where it
reached more than 100 people. At these events (see Figures 4, 5, and 6), SCEC introduced
community members to the Power to the People mascot—Buzz the Beaver—and provided
Buzz’s power outage safety tips through refrigerator magnets, brochures, and games. The Power
to the People table included a model of a house connected to the grid; youth attendees were
encouraged to “be a big storm” and knock a model tree onto a power line. Sometimes the tree
broke the power line completely; sometimes it stayed caught on the line. These scenarios
provided an opportunity to discuss safety issues, tree health, and safe planting distances. Buzz
the Beaver, educational activities, trivia questions, and a photo station to pose with Buzz while
wearing a hard hat and wielding a (toy) chainsaw attracted youth and adults to the table and, in
just 6 days, SCEC achieved 3% of its outreach goal.

5 Conclusion
The Washington County strategic energy plan represents a foundational shift toward a more
informed, engaged, and resilient energy future—one that is rooted in the lived realities of a rural,
forested, and weather-exposed region. Through the ETIPP process, the Sunrise County
Economic Council has not only developed a data-informed understanding of reliability,
affordability, and safety challenges but has also established the critical relationships and
community-centered approach necessary to address them.

This plan underscores that improving energy outcomes in Washington County is not solely a
function of infrastructure investment but of coordination, trust-building, and shared
responsibility among utilities, local leadership, and residents. By prioritizing hazard mitigation,
outage safety and emergency readiness, and energy assistance, SCEC has identified actionable
pathways that empower individuals and institutions alike to reduce risk and improve
preparedness. The Power to the People initiative exemplifies this approach—translating technical
challenges into accessible, community-driven solutions that can scale over time.

Importantly, this SEP acknowledges Washington County’s energy challenges are deeply
interconnected with broader socioeconomic conditions, including aging populations, housing
quality, and economic vulnerability. As such, the strategies outlined herein are designed not only
to improve grid performance but to enhance public health, economic stability, and quality of life.
The emphasis on education, outreach, and energy coaching reflects a long-term investment in
local capacity—ensuring residents are equipped to make informed decisions and advocate for
their needs.

Although this plan provides a clear roadmap, its success will depend on sustained collaboration,
funding, and adaptive implementation. The relationships established with Versant Power and
regional partners—along with identified funding pathways—position SCEC to move from
planning to action. As emphasized by SCEC’s Tora Johnson, “I’ve learned a colossal amount
from Versant and I appreciate that immensely. Now SCEC understands at a more technical level,
and that will be very helpful as we do our town meetings to discuss electrical systems.” Equally
important, this report is intended to evolve—serving as a living framework that can be revisited
as new data, technologies, and opportunities emerge.

Ultimately, this SEP affirms a central premise: Resilient energy systems are built not only
through engineering but also through empowered communities. By advancing a model of
partnership, transparency, and shared accountability, Washington County is positioned to
strengthen its energy future in a way that is equitable, practical, and enduring.

Appendix A. Additional Supporting Context
Community Energy Planning Process Through ETIPP
Strategic energy plans are designed to meet the needs and goals of the community. Multiple
discussions with Sunrise County Economic Council (SCEC), Versant Power, and other members
of the community allowed time to discuss Washington County, Maine’s, unique energy
circumstances, challenges, opportunities, and priorities. The themes that arose from these
discussions provided the starting point for the vision statement. Much of the visioning process
took place at the on-site workshop in March 2025.

Workshop
The SCEC and the Island Institute hosted a community workshop March 24–27, 2025, to discuss
the energy needs and resources in Washington County. Focus areas included energy
affordability, efficiency, reliability, and resilience. The objective was for residents to learn about
energy planning and systems and to develop focus areas that are the highest priority to the people
of Washington County, Maine.

There were 22 participants who took part, in addition to members from the National Laboratory
of the Rockies (NLR), Island Institute, and Versant Power.

The workshop began with an introduction of Energy Technology Innovation Partnership Project
(ETIPP) by Bryan Mentlik, ETIPP Northeast Regional Lead at NLR, followed by a presentation
by NLR researcher, Neah Downs Dybas, on the power grid, sources of electricity in Maine,
power reliability, and resilience. Judy Long, Senior Manager, Communications & Brand at
Versant Power, then gave a presentation on Versant’s role in transmission and distribution, grid
planning and resilience measures, and the unique regional challenges to delivering electricity
including rural/forested geography, island communities, and aging infrastructure.

Key takeaways included the reliability challenges with electrical service, including frequent and
long outages—especially in remote areas of Washington County. In addition, municipal capacity
challenges are significant because of a lack of planning staff and limited resilience planning
expertise (Eastern Research Group 2020).

Community priorities focused on electricity affordability, reliability, and resilience—especially
in remote areas—and economic development opportunities related to energy, including jobs and
attracting business. There appeared to be sustained interest in energy efficiency and
electrification (heat pumps, electric vehicles) but concerns about grid capacity. It was also
expressed that education was needed on energy options, the economics of electricity, and how
energy decisions are made in Maine and in Washington County.

Challenges and Opportunities
After the morning presentations, participants were engaged in an energy vision activity to
articulate local challenges, opportunities, and energy priorities that were later used to guide the
development of the vision statement. The activity aligned with the objectives of Maine’s Energy
Plan (Maine Governor’s Energy Office 2025), which emphasizes affordable, reliable, and

Current Electricity Rates, Incentives, and Legislation
Understanding current electricity rates, available incentives, and relevant legislation is critical to
evaluating the total cost of energy for Washington County residents and informing community
and utility decision-making. These factors are variable and subject to change based on market
conditions, regulatory decisions, and policy updates and should be reviewed regularly.

Electricity Rates
Electricity rates in Washington County are determined by multiple components, including supply
(standard offer), transmission, distribution, and policy-related charges, which are regulated or
administered by the Maine Public Utilities Commission. Distribution service is provided
by Versant Power, whereas supply rates are set through standard offer procurements
administered by the Commission (Maine PUC 2025).

According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, the average residential retail
electricity price in Maine was 24.29¢/kWh in 2024, compared to the national average of
16.48¢/kWh, representing a 43% increase from 2021 levels (U.S. Energy Information
Administration n.d.). Historical data from Versant Power indicate between 2014 and 2023,
standard offer (supply) rates increased from $0.0743/kWh to $0.1597/kWh, transmission rates
increased from $0.0248/kWh to $0.0433/kWh, and distribution and other charges increased from
$0.0756/kWh to $0.0890/kWh (Versant Power 2023).

These trends reflect underlying cost drivers. In Maine, recent electricity cost increases reflect a
combination of region-specific and broader market factors. State and regional sources indicate
transmission investment, storm recovery costs, and supply price volatility—driven in part by
New England’s reliance on natural gas—have contributed to rising rates (Maine Public Utilities
Commission 2025; U.S. Energy Information Administration 2025). National analyses of
electricity pricing trends similarly identify load growth and expansion of distributed and utility-
scale generation as contributing factors in some regions, though these effects vary by state and
are not uniformly applied (Wiser et al. 2025). These cost drivers are not strictly additive and
reflect broader rate-setting dynamics.

Incentives and Assistance Programs
Several programs are available to reduce energy costs and improve efficiency. The Efficiency
Maine Trust administers incentives for heat pumps, insulation, heat pump water heaters, and
other efficiency measures as well as financing options for residential and commercial customers
(Efficiency Maine Trust 2026.).

For income-qualified households, the Maine State Housing Authority administers the Home
Energy Assistance Program and the Energy Crisis Intervention Program, which provide financial
support for heating costs, weatherization, and emergency energy needs (Maine Housing 2025).
In addition, Versant Power offers a low-income assistance program and billing options such as
budget billing and arrearage management to support customers facing financial hardship
(Versant Power 2025).

Legislation and Policy Context
State and regional policies influence electricity costs, program funding, and infrastructure
investment. Many programs supporting energy efficiency, distributed generation, and grid
modernization, administered through the Maine Public Utilities Commission and Efficiency
Maine Trust, are funded in part through ratepayer-supported mechanisms (Maine PUC 2025).

Recent legislation, including H.P. 1311/L.D. 1967, directs the design of a Maine Home Energy
Navigator and Coaching Pilot Program to improve access to energy efficiency resources and
reduce energy burden through community-based support (Maine Legislature 2025). Regional
market dynamics, including New England’s reliance on natural gas for electricity generation,
also contribute to seasonal price variability and overall electricity costs (U.S. Energy Information
Administration 2025).

Together, these rate structures, incentives, and policies shape the energy landscape for
Washington County. Although they contribute to cost pressures, particularly in a rural and
energy-burdened region, they also provide pathways for reducing energy use, improving
efficiency, and increasing resilience when effectively leveraged by communities and utilities.
