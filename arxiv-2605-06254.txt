Geodesic simplices of pseudo-hyperbolic space
Timothé Lemistre

Abstract
. We give a cohomological interpretation of the geodesic simplices of the pseudo-hyperbolic space of signature ( p , q ) (p,q) and formulate a necessary and sufficient condition for such a simplex to have finite volume. As a corollary, we obtain that every ideal geodesic polytope in the pseudo-hyperbolic space of signature ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) has finite volume.



Introduction

A central fact in hyperbolic geometry, whose importance was shown by the work of Gromov and Thurston, is that the geodesic simplices of the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{p}
have volume bounded above by a constant
\mathbf{v}(\mathbb{H}^{p})
depending only on
p
. Moreover, the maximal volume is attained only for the regular ideal geodesic simplex in
\mathbb{H}^{p}
(result of Haagerup-Munkholm
[
HM81
]
for
p\geqslant 4
). In his foundational article on simplicial volume and bounded cohomology
[
Gro82
]
, Gromov proves that for every closed hyperbolic
p
-manifold
M
, denoting by
\Vol(M)
its volume and by
\left\|M\right\|
its simplicial volume, one has

\frac{\Vol(M)}{\left\|M\right\|}=\mathbf{v}(\mathbb{H}^{p}).

He deduces from this an elegant proof of Mostow’s rigidity theorem for hyperbolic
p
-manifolds, using rigidity results of Thurston
[
Thu80
]
and the characterisation of the geodesic simplices of maximal volume.

Pseudo-hyperbolic space.

In this article, we study geodesic simplices in the pseudo-hyperbolic space of signature
(p,q)
, denoted by
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. The latter is a pseudo-Riemannian manifold of constant curvature that generalises the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{p}
. The recent interest in this space comes from its central role in higher-rank Teichmüller theory (see in particular
[
Mes
]
,
[
Bar13
]
,
[
BM12
]
,
[
DGK18
]
,
[
BK25
]
,
[
BS10
]
et
[
BS20
]
).

Let us call geodesic simplex, or more simply
simplex
of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
a set of
p+q+1
points in
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
, spanning
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
and whose interior of the convex hull, in some well-chosen affine chart, is contained in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q+1}
. Such a convex hull is determined up to isometry by the simplex. A simplex is said to be
ideal
if its points are in the projective boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. We deduce from our main theorem the following corollary (Proposition
3.7
and Corollary
3.8
).

Main corollary
.
There exist simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
but none is ideal. For all integers
p\geqslant 3
and
q\geqslant 1
, there exist simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
, both ideal and non-ideal.

Our main theorem is a finiteness criterion characterising simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. Given a simplex
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
, we define the undirected graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
having
\Lambda
as set of vertices and whose edges are the
\{\xi,\eta\}
such that the lines
\xi
and
\eta
are not orthogonal. We can then read properties of
\Lambda
on the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
: let us illustrate this. Given a subset
I
of the set of vertices of a graph, denote by
\partial I
the set of vertices that are not in
I
but adjacent to a vertex of
I
.
I
is said to be
stable
if there is no edge between vertices of
I
.

Main theorem
.
Let
\Lambda
be a simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
.
\Lambda
has infinite volume if, and only if, the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
contains a non-empty, stable set of vertices
I
with cardinality at least
\Card\partial I
.

We introduce a second tool : an interpretation of the isometry class of a simplex
\Lambda
as an element of a (twisted) cohomology group associated to the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
. This allows more generally to encode isometry classes of subsets of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
, which leaves hope for further applications.

Organisation.
Section
1
contains preliminaries of pseudo-hyperbolic geometry. Section
2
defines the cohomological interpretation and derives some consequences used in Section
3
. In Section
3
, we prove the
main theorem
and derive consequences from it. We show for example that every graph corresponding to an ideal simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
contains a
5
-cycle and deduce from this the
main corollary
.

1.
Preliminaries

From now on, let
p>0
and
q\geqslant 0
be integers. We denote by
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
the oriented vector space
\mathbb{R}^{p+q+1}
endowed with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
\mathbf{b}
of signature
(p,q+1)
, and by
\mathrm{O}(p,q+1)
its orthogonal group. The group
\mathrm{PO}(p,q+1)=\mathrm{O}(p,q+1)/\{\pm\id\}
acts on the projective space
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
preserving an open set, the
pseudo-hyperbolic space
, defined by

\mathbb{H}^{p,q}=\{[x]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})\mid\mathbf{b}(x,x)<0\}.

The stabiliser of a point of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
under the action of
\mathrm{PO}(p,q+1)
is conjugate to
\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
. Define a double covering of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
by

\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}\mid\mathbf{b}(x,x)=-1\}.

We have
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}/{\pm\id}=\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. A tangent space
\Tan_{x}\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
is identified with
\mathrm{Vect}(x)^{\perp}
, hence inherits the metric
\mathbf{b}
from
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
. This endows
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
with a metric of signature
(p,q)
which descends to a metric on
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. The metrics on
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
and
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
have constant curvature
-1
and respective isometry groups
\mathrm{O}(p,q+1)
and
\mathrm{PO}(p,q+1)
. Their respective Levi-Civita connections are geodesically complete, the geodesics of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
being its inter"s with projective lines.

Subsets of the projective space and graphs.

By a
graph
(with vertex set
\cal{S}
) we mean a pair
\cal{G}=(\cal{S},\cal{A})
where
\cal{S}
is a set (whose elements are called vertices) and
\cal{A}
is a set of subsets of
\cal{S}
of the form
\{\xi,\eta\}
with
\xi
and
\eta
two vertices (possibly equal). The elements of
\cal{A}
are called edges, and edges of the form
\{\xi,\xi\}
are called loops. Two vertices
\xi,\eta
(possibly equal) are said to be adjacent if
\{\xi,\eta\}
is an edge. A morphism from a graph
(\cal{S}_{0},\cal{A}_{0})
to a graph
(\cal{S}_{1},\cal{A}_{1})
is a map
\phi:\cal{S}_{0}\to\cal{S}_{1}
such that for every edge
{\xi,\eta}\in\cal{A}_{0}
, one has
{\xi,\eta}\in\cal{A}_{1}
.

In what follows,
\Lambda
will be a subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
, possibly infinite. To such a subset, we associate the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)=(\Lambda,\cal{A}(\Lambda))
where
\cal{A}(\Lambda)
is the set of edges
\{\xi,\eta\}\subseteq\Lambda
such that the lines
\xi
and
y
are not orthogonal. We give examples of such graphs in Section
3.3
. The set
\Lambda
is said to be
ideal
if
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
has no loops, that is if
\Lambda
is contained in the projective boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
, denoted

\partial\mathbb{H}^{p,q}=\{[x]\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})\mid\mathbf{b}(x,x)=0\}.

Definition
.

Let
\Lambda
be a subset of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
. We call
lift
of
\Lambda
the data, for every line
\xi\in\Lambda
, of a non-zero vector of
\xi
. In other words, a lift of
\Lambda
is a section of the projectivisation
\mathbb{P}:\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
over
\Lambda
. A lift
s
is said
semi-positive
​ if the convex hull
\Conv(s(\Lambda))
does not contain
0
and we have
\mathbf{b}(s(\xi),s(\eta))\leqslant 0
for all
\xi,\eta
in
\Lambda
. If
s
is semi-positive ​, then the projectivisation of
\Conv(s(\Lambda))
is well-defined and its interior in an open subset of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
which we denote
\Conv(s)
.

Lemma 1.1
.

If
\Lambda
admits a lift
s
such that
\mathbf{b}(s(\xi),s(\eta))\leqslant 0
for all
\xi,\eta\in\Lambda
, then
\Lambda
admits a semi-positive lift.

Proof.

It suffices to modify
s
so that
\Conv(s(\Lambda))
does not contain
0
. Endow
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
with a strict order
\prec
making it into an ordered vector space. For each isolated vertex
\xi
of
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
, up to multiplying
s(\xi)
by
-1
, we may assume that
0\prec s(\xi)
. Suppose now that there exists a relation
0=\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}s(\xi_{i})
with
t_{i}>0
,
\xi_{i}\in\Lambda
, and
I
a non-empty finite subset of
\Lambda
. Given an element
\eta
of
\Lambda
, we have

0=\mathbf{b}(s(\eta),\sum_{i\in I}t_{i}s(\xi_{i}))=\sum_{i}t_{i}\mathbf{b}(s(\eta),s(\xi_{i})).

Since
t_{i}>0
and
\mathbf{b}(s(\eta),s(\xi_{i}))\leqslant 0
, it follows that
\mathbf{b}(s(\eta),s(\xi_{i}))=0
for every
i
: thus
\eta
is not adjacent to any of the
\xi_{i}
. Hence the
\xi_{i}
are isolated vertices of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
, so
0\prec s(\xi_{i})
, and therefore
0\prec\sum_{i}t_{i}s(\xi_{i})=0
, which is absurd. Thus
0
does not belong to
\Conv(s(\Lambda))
and
s
indeed defines a semi-positive lift.
∎

Convex hulls.

A
convex hull
of a subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
is any open subset of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
of the form
\Conv(s)
, where
s
is a semi-positive lift of
\Lambda
.

Lemma 1.2

(uniqueness of the convex hull)
.

Assume that
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)
is non-degenerate. Two convex hulls of
\Lambda
are identified by an element of
\mathrm{PO}(p,q+1)
.

Proof.

Let
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
be two semi-positive lifts of
\Lambda
. There exists
g:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda}
such that
s^{\prime}=gs
, of constant sign
\varepsilon_{i}\in{\pm 1}
on each of the connected components of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
, denoted by
\Lambda_{i}
(
i\in I
). Let us show by contradiction that none of the
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})
can be degenerate : otherwise, we would have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})+\left(\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\subseteq\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})+\left(\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})^{\perp}\cap\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)\right)

since
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})
is degenerate,

\displaystyle\subsetneq\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)

which is a contradiction. Hence
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
is the orthogonal direct sum of
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)^{\perp}
and the
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})
. We define an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
equal to
\varepsilon_{i}\id
on each term
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda_{i})
, and preserving
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)^{\perp}
. It acts on
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
by sending
\Conv(s_{0})
to
\Conv(s)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Polytopes.

A
polytope
(in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
) is a finite subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1})
admitting a semi-positive lift and such that each of its points is extremal in a convex hull of
\Lambda
(ie. it cannot be written as a nontrivial convex combination of points
\Lambda
; it is then true in every convex hull by Lemma
1.2
). The points of
\Lambda
are called the vertices of the polytope. A
marked polytope
is a bijection
m
from
\cal{S}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
onto (the vertices of) a polytope. We say that
\phi\in\mathrm{PO}(p,q+1)
defines an isometry between the marked polytopes
m
and
m^{\prime}
if
m^{\prime}=\phi\circ m
. A polytope is called a
simplex
if it has
p+q+1
vertices spanning the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
. Lemma
1.2
shows that given a simplex
\Lambda
, its
convex hull
is unique up to isometry : its volume, called volume of
\Lambda
, is denoted by
\Vol(\Lambda)
.

2.
Cohomological viewpoint

In this section, we define a correspondence between isometry classes of subsets of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
and (twisted) cohomology classes of their graph. The isometry classes of simplices of graph
\cal{G}
then correspond to an open subset of a real vector space of finite dimension, whose dimension can be read on
\cal{G}
(Lemma
2.9
). We deduce from this a correspondence between geometric properties of simplices and properties of their graph, which will be used in Section
3.2
.

2.1.
Definitions

The idea is the following : a lift
s
of a subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
defines a function associating to an edge
\{\xi,\eta\}
of
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
the real number
\mathbf{b}(s(\xi),s(\eta))
, which corresponds to the classical notion of Gram matrix. The changes of lift (which we will see as
0
-cochains) modify these Gram matrices (which we will see as
1
-cochains). The set of Gram matrices modulo change of lift is then naturally defined as the cohomology of a very simple chain complex.

Definition 2.1

(cohomology of a graph)
.

Let a graph
\cal{G}=(\cal{S},\cal{A})
and an abelian group
(G,\star)
(which we take among
\mathbb{R}^{*}
,
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathbb{R}
). Let us define a cochain complex by
\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},G)=G{S}
,
\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},G)=G{A}
,
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\cal{G},G)=0
otherwise, the differential
d:\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},G)\to\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},G)
being given, for a function
f\in G^{\cal{S}}
and an edge
a
with endpoints
\xi
and
\eta
, by
\mathrm{d}f(a)=f(\xi)+f(\eta)
. Let us denote
\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},G)
its cohomology.

Any group morphism
\phi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
induces a morphism of complexes
\mathrm{C}^{*}(\cal{G},G_{1})\to\mathrm{C}^{*}(\cal{G},G_{2})
and hence a morphism in cohomology, both denoted
\phi_{*}
. Likewise, a graph morphism
\psi
determines morphisms
\psi^{*}
between the associated cochain complexes and the cohomologies of the graphs.

Let now
\Lambda
be a subset of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
. Given a lift
s
of
\Lambda
, we define a cocycle
\mathrm{Gram}(s)\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
by associating to an edge
\{\xi,\eta\}
of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
the real number
-\mathbf{b}(s(\xi),s(\eta))\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
. The following lemma states that the cohomology class of
\mathrm{Gram}(s)
depends only on
\Lambda
: we shall denote it
c(\Lambda)\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The set of
\mathrm{Gram}(s)
, for
s
a lift of
\Lambda
, forms a cohomology class in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

Proof.

Choosing another lift
s^{\prime}
of
\Lambda
is equivalent to choosing the function
g:\Lambda\to\mathbb{R}^{*}
such that
s^{\prime}=gs
, which is a cocycle
g\in\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
. We then have, given some edge
a
with endpoints
\xi
and
\eta
:

\mathrm{Gram}(s^{\prime})(a)=-\mathbf{b}(s^{\prime}(\xi),s^{\prime}(\eta))=-\mathbf{b}(s(\xi),s(\eta))g(\xi)g(\eta).

With the notations of Definition
2.1
, this can be written as
\mathrm{Gram}(s^{\prime})(a)=\mathrm{Gram}(s)(a)\star\mathrm{d}g
, which concludes.
∎

The following lemma states that a pair
(\Lambda,s)
with
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
such that
\mathrm{Vect}(\Lambda)
is non-degenerate and
s
a lift of
\Lambda
, considered modulo the action of
\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
, is determined by
\mathrm{Gram(s)}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\iota
be a map from a subset
E
of
\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
into
\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
, such that
\mathrm{Vect}(E)
and
\mathrm{Vect}(\iota(E))
are non-degenerate. The map
\iota
extends to an element of
\mathrm{O}(p,q)
if and only if one has
\mathbf{b}(x,y)=\mathbf{b}(\iota(x),\iota(y))
for all
x,y\in E
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathbf{b}(x,y)=\mathbf{b}(\iota(x),\iota(y))
for all
x,y\in E
. Let
(x_{i})
be a basis of
\mathrm{Vect}(E)
extracted from
E
. The family
(\iota(x_{i}))
has the same Gram matrix as
(x_{i})
, therefore is linearly independent and forms a basis of
\mathrm{Vect}(\iota(E))
. The linear map
g:\mathrm{Vect}(E)\to\mathrm{Vect}(\iota(E))
defined by
g(x_{i})=\iota(x_{i})
is then an isometry and satisfies
g(x)=\iota(x)
for all
x\in E
. As
\mathrm{Vect}(\iota(E))
and
\mathrm{Vect}(\iota(E))
are non-degenerate, each admits an orthogonal complement : this allows extending
g
to an element of
\mathrm{O}(p,q)
. The converse is immediate.
∎

We now show that a subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
, considered modulo the action of
\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
, is determined by the cohomology class
c(\Lambda)\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
be two subsets of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
spanning non-degenerate subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{p,q}
. Let
\iota:\Lambda_{1}\to\Lambda_{2}
be a map. The following conditions are equivalent :

(1)

\iota
extends to an element of
\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
;

(2)

\iota
defines isomorphism of graphs
\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1})\to\cal{G}(\Lambda_{2})
and we have
c(\Lambda_{1})=\iota^{*}c(\Lambda_{2})
in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1}),\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

Proof.

Assume that the first condition holds. Then
\iota
induces an isomorphism
\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1})\to\cal{G}(\Lambda_{2})
. Moreover, given a lift
s_{1}
of
\Lambda_{1}
and an isometry
\phi\in\mathrm{O}(p,q)
such that
[\phi]\in\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
extends
\iota
,
s_{2}=\phi\circ s_{1}
defines a lift of
\Lambda_{2}
. We have
\mathrm{Gram}(s_{1})=\iota^{*}\mathrm{Gram}(s_{2})\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1}),\mathbb{R}^{*})
, hence
c(\Lambda_{1})=\iota^{*}c(\Lambda_{2})
.

Conversely, assume that the second condition holds. Given two lifts
s_{1},s_{2}
of
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}
, we have in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1}),\mathbb{R}^{*})
the equality
[\mathrm{Gram}(s_{1})]=c_{\Lambda_{1}}=\iota^{*}c_{\Lambda_{2}}=[\iota^{*}\mathrm{Gram}(s_{2})]
, hence there exists
g\in\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G}(\Lambda_{1}),\mathbb{R}^{*})
such that
\mathrm{Gram}(s_{1})+\mathrm{d}g=\iota^{*}\mathrm{Gram}(s_{2})
. We define a second lift of
\Lambda_{1}
by
s_{1}^{\prime}(\xi)=g(\xi)s_{1}(\xi)
, which satisfies
\mathrm{Gram}(s_{1}^{\prime})=\iota^{*}\mathrm{Gram}(s_{2})=\mathrm{Gram}(s_{2}\circ\iota)
. By Lemma
2.3
, there exists an isometry
\phi\in\mathrm{O}(p,q)
such that
\phi\circ s_{1}^{\prime}=s_{2}\circ\iota
. Thus
[\phi]\in\mathrm{PO}(p,q)
extends
\iota
.
∎

Decomposition in cohomology and semi-positive lifts.

Define the quotient maps
\mathrm{sgn}:\mathbb{R}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{*}/\mathbb{R}_{>0}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
and
\mathrm{abs}:\mathbb{R}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{*}/{\pm 1}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The canonical decomposition
\mathbb{R}^{*}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
determines an isomorphism
\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})=\cohom^{*}(\cal{G},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\times\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}_{>0})
for every
k
, given by
\mathrm{sgn}_{*}\times\mathrm{abs}_{*}
. A class
c\in\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
satisfies
\mathrm{sgn}_{*}c=0
if and only if it lies in the image of
\exp_{*}:\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})\to\cohom^{k}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
. For any subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
, we denote by
c_{\pm}(\Lambda)=\mathrm{sgn}_{*}c(\Lambda)
and
c_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathrm{abs}_{*}c(\Lambda)
. In the above decomposition, one therefore has
c(\Lambda)=(c_{\pm}(\Lambda),c_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda))
.

Proposition 2.5
.

A subset
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
admits a semi-positive lift if and only if the cohomology class
c_{\pm}(\Lambda)\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
vanishes.

Proof.

The set of the
\mathrm{Gram}(s)
, with
s
a lift of
\Lambda
, forms the cohomology class
c(\Lambda)
. Moreover, a lift
s
is semi-positive if and only if
\mathrm{Gram}(s)
takes values in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, that is, if
\mathrm{sgn}_{*}\mathrm{Gram}(s)\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is trivial. Thus, there exists a semi-positive lift if and only if the cohomology class
\mathrm{sgn}_{*}c(\Lambda)
contains the trivial cocycle, that is, if
c_{\pm}(\Lambda)=\mathrm{sgn}_{*}c(\Lambda)=0
.
∎

2.2.
Topology and signature

We now define a topology on the cohomology groups : we wish to define a notion of convergence corresponding to the convergence between simplices. For this, given a set
\cal{S}
, we define as follows a space
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}^{*})
containing all the
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
for
\cal{G}
a graph whose set of vertices is
\cal{S}
.

First, define
\cal{A}
to be the set of subsets of
\cal{S}
with
1
or
2
elements and endow
\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})=\mathbb{R}{A}
and
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
with the product topology. Given a graph
\cal{G}
with vertices
\cal{S}
and a cochain
f\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
, define
\Mat(f)\in\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})
by extending
f
by
0
. We endow
\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})
with an action of
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
by
[g\cdot f](s,s^{\prime})=g(s)g(s^{\prime})f({s,s^{\prime}})
. The set of
0
-cochains
\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})=(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
is independent from
\cal{G}
. Moreover, the map
f\mapsto\Mat(f)
defines a bijection

\bigcup_{\cal{G}}\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Mat}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}),

where the union is taken over all graphs
\cal{G}
with vertex set
\cal{S}
. For such a graph
\cal{G}
, the action of
\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
on
\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
is thus identified with the action of
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
on a subset of
\Sym_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

Definition
.

We define a topological space (with the quotient topology) by

\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}^{*})=\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})/(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}.

For any graph
\cal{G}=(\cal{S},\cal{A})
, we identify
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
with its image under the embedding
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})\to\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

The induced topology on
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
is indeed the quotient topology on
\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})/\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
, these spaces being identified with
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
and
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){A}
endowed with the product topology. We shall for instance say, given graphs
\cal{G},\cal{G}^{\prime}
with vertex set
\cal{S}
, that a sequence
(c_{k})
of points in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
converges to
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}^{\prime},\mathbb{R}^{*})
if this is the case for their images in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}^{*})
. However, the orbits of
\Sym(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})
under the action of
(\mathbb{R}^{*}){S}
are not closed : thus
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R}^{*})
is not Hausdorff.

Signature.

Given a simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
, we wish to recover the signature
(p,q+1)
only from its cohomology class. This leads to the following definition. Let
\cal{G}
be a finite graph with vertex set
\cal{S}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. Given a cochain
f\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
,
\Mat(f)
is naturally identified with a real symmetric matrix with coefficients
-f(\{i,j\})
if
\{i,j\}
is an edge,
0
otherwise. We shall say that
f
has signature
(p,q)
(denoted
\mathrm{sgn}(f)=(p,q)
), rank
r=p+q
or is invertible if this holds for
\Mat(f)
.

Lemma 2.6
.

The signature, thus the rank are constant on each cohomology class.

Proof.

Given
g\in\mathrm{C}^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
and
f\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
, denoting
D
the diagonal matrix
\diag(g(1),\dots,g(n))
, we have
\Mat(f+\mathrm{d}g)=D\,\Mat(f)\,D^{\mathsf{T}}
. Sylvester’s law of inertia states that the signature of a symmetric matrix is invariant under congruence : we deduce that
f
and
f+\mathrm{d}g
have the same signature.
∎

Definition 2.7
.

We define the signature of a cohomology class in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
as that of its representatives. We define similarly its rank and its invertibility. The signature defines a partition of
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
, thus of the subspace
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\exp_{*}}}{{\hookrightarrow}}\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
.

2.3.
Study of simplices via graphs

We have classified the isometry classes of subsets
\Lambda
of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
by cohomology classes
c(\Lambda)\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(\Lambda),\mathbb{R}^{*})
(Proposition
2.4
), the subsets admitting a semi-positive lift being classified by real cohomology classes (Proposition
2.5
). From this, one deduces a classification of simplices in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
(Proposition
2.8
). We then seek to do the converse : given a graph, we try to find a subset of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
that defines it. This yields constraints on the possible signatures
(p,q)
(Lemma
2.11
and Corollary
2.14
). This allows reducing questions about simplices to graph-theoretic questions (Proposition
2.13
and Corollary
3.8
), in the spirit of the
main theorem
.

Classification of marked simplices.

Recall that a
marked simplex
is a bijection
m
from
\cal{S}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
onto a simplex
\Lambda
. This bijection defines an isomorphism
m
from a graph
\cal{G}(m)
with vertex set
\cal{S}_{n}
to
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
. We then define
c_{\mathbb{R}}(m)=m^{*}c_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda)
, which lies in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(m),\mathbb{R})
.
Recall
that
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}(m),\mathbb{R})
embeds into
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S}_{n},\mathbb{R})
. We thus deduce the following classification of marked simplices in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\cal{S}=\{1,\dots,p+q+1\}
. The map
c_{\mathbb{R}}
defines a bijection from the set of isometry classes of marked simplices in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
to the set of cohomology classes
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})
of signature
\mathrm{sgn}(c)=(p,q+1)
.

Proof.

Injectivity is a consequence of Propositions
2.4
and
2.5
. To prove surjectivity, consider
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{S},\mathbb{R})
, which therefore lies in
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
for some graph
\cal{G}
with vertex set
\cal{S}
. Choose a representative
f\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
of
c
. The matrix
\Mat(\exp_{*}f)
defines a non-degenerate bilinear form
\mathbf{b}
on
\mathbb{R}^{p+q+1}
, identifying it with
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
. The canonical basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{p+q+1})
defines a map
m:k\in\cal{S}\mapsto\mathbb{P}\{e_{k}\}
, whose image we denote by
\Lambda=\mathbb{P}\{e_{1},\dots,e_{p+q+1}\}
. Since the entries of
\Mat(\exp_{*}f)
lie in
\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}
, we have
\mathbf{b}(x,x)\leqslant 0
for
x\in\Conv(e_{1},\dots,e_{p+q+1})
, so the map
\mathbb{P}\{e_{k}\}\in\Lambda\mapsto e_{k}
defines a semi-positive lift of
\Lambda
: hence
m
is a marked simplex. By definition, it satisfies
\cal{G}(m)=\cal{G}
and
m^{*}c_{\mathbb{R}}(\Lambda)=c
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Dimension of cohomologies.

Let
\cal{S}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
. By
definition
,
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S}_{n},\mathbb{R})
minus the point corresponding to
0\in\Sym(n,\mathbb{R})
is the image of
\mathbb{P}\Sym(n,\mathbb{R})
by a continuous map : therefore,
\cohom^{1}(\cal{S}_{n},\mathbb{R})
is compact but not Hausdorff. It is partitioned into a finite number of cells: the
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
with
\cal{G}
of vertices
\cal{S}_{n}
, identified with finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\cal{G}=(\cal{S},\cal{A})
be a finite graph. We have
\dim\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})=\Card\cal{A}-\Card\cal{S}+\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
and
\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
is the number of bipartite connected components of
\cal{G}
. Similarly, we have
\dim\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})=\Card\cal{A}-\Card\cal{S}+\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
and
\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is the number of connected components of
\cal{G}
.

Proof.

The chain complex defining
\cohom^{*}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
is given by
\mathrm{C}^{0}=\mathbb{R}^{\cal{S}}
,
\mathrm{C}^{1}=\mathbb{R}^{\cal{A}}
, and
\mathrm{C}^{*}=0
otherwise. From this, we deduce
\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})-\dim\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})=\Card\cal{S}-\Card\cal{A}
, yielding the desired equality. To prove the assertion on
\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
, one may assume that
\cal{G}
is connected and then sum over the connected components. Under this assumption, if
g:\cal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is non-zero and satisfies
\mathrm{d}g=0
, then the sets
\cal{S}_{+}=\{s\in\cal{S}\mid g(s)>0\}
and
\cal{S}_{-}=\{s\in\cal{S}\mid g(s)<0\}
define a partition of
\cal{S}
. Every
0
-cocycle is constant on each of these sets, hence a multiple of
g
: this proves
\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})=1
. Conversely, if
\cal{S}=\cal{S}_{+}\sqcup\cal{S}_{-}
is a partition into stable sets, the function
g:\cal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
taking values
\pm 1
on
\cal{S}_{\pm}
is a cocycle, and every cocycle is a multiple of
g
, which concludes the proof the the cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
. The case of cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is similar.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

If a finite subset
\Lambda
of
P(\mathbb{R}^{p,q})
admits a semi-positive lift, then the proof of Lemma
1.2
shows it has exactly
2^{\dim\cohom^{0}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})-1}
distinct convex hulls, where the
-1
comes from projectivisation.

Possible signatures on a graph.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
\cal{G}
be a graph with vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
. Suppose there exists an invertible cohomology class
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R}^{*})
. Then there exists a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that for every
k
, the vertices
k
and
\sigma(k)
are adjacent in
\cal{G}
. Moreover, if there exists
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
invertible of signature
(p,q)
, then one can choose
\sigma
of signature
(-1)^{p}
.

Proof.

Let
(m_{i,j})=\Mat(f)
and
\varepsilon(\sigma)
denote the signature of a permutation
\sigma
. Let
f
a representative of
c
. The determinant
\mathrm{det}\Mat(f)
is non-zero and can be written as

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}m_{k,\sigma(k)}.

In particular, there exists a permutation
\sigma
for which the corresponding term
\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}m_{k,\sigma(k)}
is non-zero, meaning that
k
and
\sigma(k)
are adjacent for all
k
. Moreover, if
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
is invertible of signature
(p,q)
, then
\Mat(f)
is a symmetric matrix of signature
(p,q)
, so
\mathrm{det}\Mat(f)
has the sign of
(-1)^{q}
. Therefore, there exists
\sigma
such that
\varepsilon(\sigma)\prod_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}m_{k,\sigma(k)}
has the sign of
(-1)^{q}
. Since the
m_{k,\sigma(k)}
lie in
\mathbb{R}_{\leqslant 0}
, this product has the same sign as
\varepsilon(\sigma)(-1)^{n}
, hence
\varepsilon(\sigma)=(-1)^{n+q}=(-1)^{p}
.
∎

To prove a converse (Corollary
2.14
), we shall use the following fact. Given an integer
n>0
and real numbers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}<0
, the following matrix is symmetric, hence defines a quadratic form :

\mathrm{circ}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\begin{pmatrix}0&a_{1}&0&\dots&a_{n}\\
a_{1}&0&a_{2}&\dots&0\\
0&a_{2}&0&\dots&0\\
\dots&\dots&\dots&\dots&a_{n-1}\\
a_{n}&0&0&a_{n-1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Fact 2.12
.

If
n\equiv 0[4]
and
\prod_{k\,\text{even}}a_{k}=\prod_{k\,\text{odd}}a_{k}
, then
\mathrm{circ}(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is degenerate, with signature
(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}-1)
. Otherwise, it is non-degenerate with signature
(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2})
if
n
is even,
(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2})
if
n\equiv 1[4]
, and
(\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2})
if
n\equiv 3[4]
.

For any integer
n\geqslant 2
, let
C_{n}
denote the cycle of length
n
: it is the graph whose set of vertices is
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
, the edges being the
\{k,k+1\}
for
k
in
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}
. Define
C_{1}
as the graph with one vertex and a loop.

Proposition 2.13
.

The cohomology of
C_{n}
is as follows.

(1)

If
n\equiv 0[4]
, then
\cohom^{1}(C_{n},\mathbb{R})
is one-dimensional and each non-zero class has signature
(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2})
and the zero class has signature
(\frac{n}{2}-1,\frac{n}{2}-1)
.

(2)

If
n\equiv 1[4]
, then
\cohom^{1}(C_{n},\mathbb{R})
consists of a single point, of signature
(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{n+1}{2})
.

(3)

If
n\equiv 2[4]
and
n\neq 2
, then
\cohom^{1}(C_{n},\mathbb{R})
is one-dimensional and each class has signature
(\frac{n}{2},\frac{n}{2})
.
\cohom^{1}(C_{2},\mathbb{R}*)
consists of a single point, of signature
(1,1)
.

(4)

If
n\equiv 3[4]
, then
\cohom^{1}(C_{n},\mathbb{R})
consists of a single point, of signature
(\frac{n+1}{2},\frac{n-1}{2})
.

For example, if
n
is odd, there exists a unique graph simplex of
C_{n}
: it is realised with a single signature and is unique up to isometry.

Proof.

This is a consequence of the classification of simplices (Proposition
2.8
), the computation of the dimensions of the
\cohom^{1}
(Lemma
2.9
), and their partition according to signature (obvious for
n=1
and otherwise given by fact
2.12
).
∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
\cal{G}
be a finite graph with vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be a permutation such that
s
and
\sigma(s)
are adjacent for all
s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
l_{k}(\sigma)
denote the number of cycles of length
k
in the cycle decomposition of
\sigma
, and define integers
p,q
by

2p=\Card{S}-l_{0}-\sum_{k\equiv 1[4]}l_{k}+\sum_{k\equiv 3[4]}l_{k},\ q=\Card{S}-p.

There exists
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
invertible of signature
(p,q)
.

Proof.

Define a graph
\cal{G}_{\sigma}
with vertex set
\{1,\dots,n\}
and edges
\{k,\sigma(k)\}
for
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Let
\cal{G}_{i}
(
i\in I
) denote the connected components of
\cal{G}_{\sigma}
: these are cycles. We have

\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}_{\sigma},\mathbb{R}^{*})=\prod_{i}\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}_{i},\mathbb{R}^{*})

and Proposition
2.13
gives, for each
i
, a cohomology class
c_{i}\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}_{i},\mathbb{R}^{*})
invertible with explicit signature. The product of these classes is thus a cohomology class
c\in\cohom^{1}(\cal{G}_{\sigma},\mathbb{R}^{*})
, whose signature is the sum of the signatures of the
c_{i}
(given by Proposition
2.13
), equal to
(p,q)
. Since
\cal{G}_{\sigma}
is a subgraph of
\cal{G}
, there exists (by
definition
) a sequence
(c_{k})
of elements of
\cohom^{1}(\cal{G},\mathbb{R})
converging to
c
: intuitively, the
c_{k}
correspond to simplices whose graph is
\cal{G}
, on which we let degenerate the edges not corresponding to some
(k,\sigma(k))
. For sufficiently large
k
, the signature of
c_{k}
satisfies
\mathrm{sgn}(c_{k})=(p_{k},q_{k})\geqslant(p,q)
. Moreover, since
n=p+q\geqslant p_{k}+q_{k}
, it follows that
\mathrm{sgn}(c_{k})=(p,q)
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.
Finiteness criterion on the volume

This section is devoted to the proof of the
main theorem
. Recall that, given a graph and a set
I
of vertices of this graph, we denote by
\partial I
the set of vertices which are not in
I
but adjacent to a vertex of
I
.
I
is said to be stable if there is no edge between vertices of
I
.

Main theorem.

Let
\Lambda
be a simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
.
\Lambda
has infinite volume if, and only if, the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
contains a non-empty, stable set of vertices
I
with cardinality at least
\Card\partial I
.

3.1.
Proof

This section is devoted to the proof of the theorem. Let
n=p+q+1
, choose a semi-positive lift
s
of
\Lambda
whose image we denote by
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, and define a linear map
\alpha:\mathbb{R}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
by
\alpha(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=\sum t_{k}x_{k}
. In
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
, identify each tangent space with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
respectively. The volume forms
\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}
are related by
\alpha^{*}\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}=\mathrm{det}(\alpha)\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
for some non-zero real
\mathrm{det}(\alpha)=\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Define a quadratic form
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

f(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=-\mathbf{b}(\alpha(t),\alpha(t))=\sum-t_{i}t_{j}\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j}).

Lemma 3.1
.

Endow
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
with the measures coming from their respective (pseudo-Riemannian) metrics. We have

\Vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}}(\Conv(s))=n\left|\mathrm{det}(\alpha)\right|\Vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}}\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}.

Proof.

Notice that
f>0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
. We define an embedding of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}=\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\cap\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
into
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
, whose image is a lift of
\Conv(s)
:

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}\psi:&\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}&\longrightarrow&\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}\\
&t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})&\longmapsto&f(t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\alpha(t).\end{array}

Let
s\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}
. Let
\mathsf{n}
denote the unit normal vector at
s
to
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}
(viewed as a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) :
\mathsf{n}
is naturally identified with
s
. Then
f(t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}\alpha(\mathsf{n})
is a normal vector to
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
, naturally identified with
\psi(s)
. By the definition of
\psi
, we have

(3.1)

\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathsf{v})=f(t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha(\mathsf{v})+\mathrm{d}(f^{-\frac{1}{2}})(\mathsf{v})\ \alpha(s).

The volume forms of
\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
are given by
\vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}}_{x}=\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}(\cdot,\dots,\cdot,x)
(see Section
1
) and
\vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}_{s}=\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\cdot,\dots,\cdot,s)
. Let
\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n-1}\in\Tan_{s}\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}
. We have

\displaystyle(\psi^{*}\vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}})(\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n-1})

\displaystyle=\vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}}(\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathsf{v}_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathsf{v}_{n-1}))

so by (
3.1
),

\displaystyle=\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}(\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathsf{v}_{1}),\dots,\mathrm{d}\psi(\mathsf{v}_{n-1}),\psi(s))

\displaystyle=\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}}\left(f(s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha(\mathsf{v}_{1}),\dots,f(s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha(\mathsf{v}_{n-1}),f(s)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\alpha(\mathsf{n})\right)

\displaystyle=f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\ (\alpha^{*}\vol^{\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}})(\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n-1},\mathsf{n})

\displaystyle=f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\ \mathrm{det}(\alpha)\ \vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n-1},\mathsf{n})

\displaystyle=f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\ \mathrm{det}(\alpha)\ \vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(\mathsf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{v}_{n-1}).

This shows that

\psi^{*}\vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}}=f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\ \mathrm{det}(\alpha)\ \vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}.

We are reduced to studying the integrability of
\psi^{*}\vol^{\widehat{\mathbb{H}}^{p,q}}
over
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}
. This is equivalent to the convergence of

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}}f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\ \mathrm{d}\!\vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}(s)

by Fubini

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\left\{(s,h)\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mid f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\geqslant h\right\}

by Fubini

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{s\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\mid f(s)^{-\frac{n}{2}}\geqslant h\right\}\mathrm{d}h

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{s\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\mid f(s)\leqslant h^{-\frac{2}{n}}\right\}\mathrm{d}h

by the change of variable
r=h^{\frac{1}{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}n\ r^{n-1}\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{s\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\mid f(s)\leqslant r^{-2}\right\}\mathrm{d}r

since
f
is homogeneous

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}r^{n-1}\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{s\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\mid f(rs)\leqslant 1\right\}\mathrm{d}r

so by Fubini,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\int_{\mathbb{R}_{>0}}r^{n-1}\Vol^{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left\{s\in r\mathbb{S}^{n-1}_{>0}\mid f(s)\leqslant 1\right\}\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\Vol^{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Recall that the set
\cal{A}
of edges of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
is given by
\cal{A}=\{\{i,j\}\mid\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j})<0\}
. Let
\Delta=\left\{(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid\forall{i,j}\in\cal{A}\quad t_{i}t_{j}\leqslant 1\right\}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The volume of
\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}
is finite if and only if the volume of
\Delta
is finite.

Proof.

Recall that
f(t)=2\sum_{\{i,j\}\in\cal{A}}-t_{i}t_{j}\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j})
. Define

\begin{array}[]{rrcl}g:&\mathbb{R}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})&\longmapsto&\sup_{\{i,j\}\in\cal{A}}t_{i}t_{j}\end{array}

and choose constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that for all
(i,j)\in\cal{A}
, we have
0<C_{1}\leqslant-\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j})\leqslant C_{2}
. We hence have, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}
:

\frac{2}{\Card(\cal{A})}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\ g(t)\leqslant f(t)\leqslant 2\Card(\cal{A})\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}g(t).

Since
\Delta=\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid g(t)\leqslant 1\right\}
, the previous estimate shows that

\left(\frac{2}{\Card(\cal{A})}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\right)\cdot\Delta\subseteq\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}\subseteq\left(2\Card(\cal{A})\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}\right)\cdot\Delta.

Thus, the finiteness of the volume of
\Delta
is equivalent to the finiteness of the volume of
\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}
(with respect to the volume form on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). This proves Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Define
\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
\sigma(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})=\sum p_{k}
, as well as a convex cone
P=\{(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\forall\{i,j\}\in\cal{A}\quad p_{i}+p_{j}\leqslant 0\}
.
P
is diffeomorphic to
\Delta
via the map

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbb{R}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0},\\
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})&\longmapsto&(e^{p_{1}},\dots,e^{p_{n}}),\end{array}

whose Jacobian at
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
e^{\sigma(p)}
. We thus have

(3.2)

\vol\Delta=\int_{P}e^{\sigma(p)}\mathrm{d}p.

Lemma 3.3
.

The integral (
3.2
) diverges if and only if there exists a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Convergent case.
Suppose that there does not exist a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
. For each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, define an affine hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
H_{t}=\sigma^{-1}(t)
. We can rewrite (
3.2
) as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{P}e^{\sigma(p)}\ \mathrm{d}p=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{<0}}e^{t}\vol^{H_{t}}(P\cap H_{t})\ \mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{<0}}e^{t}t^{n-1}\vol^{H_{-1}}(P\cap H_{-1})\ \mathrm{d}t

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n-1}\Gamma(n)\vol^{H_{-1}}(P\cap H_{-1}).

This integral converges if and only if the volume of
P\cap H_{-1}
is finite. The assumption that there does not exist a non-zero
p\in P
with
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
implies that the cone
P
is a union of half-lines intersecting the affine hyperplane
H_{-1}
. Since
P
is moreover closed, the intersection
P\cap H_{-1}
is compact, and therefore has finite volume : hence the integral (
3.3
) converges.

Divergent case.
Suppose there exists a non-zero
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
. Let
U
be a bounded open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
contained in
P
. Since
P
is a convex cone, the set
\{u+tp\mid u\in U,\ t\geqslant 0\}
is a subset of
P
with infinite measure on which
\sigma
is bounded below. The integral of
e^{\sigma}
over this set diverges, and therefore the integral (
3.2
) also diverges.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that there exists a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
. There exists
(p^{*}_{1},\dots,p^{*}_{n})=p^{*}\in P
non-zero such that
\sigma(p^{*})\geqslant 0
and
p^{*}_{k}\in\{-1,0,1\}
for all
k
.

Proof.

Given
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in P
whose coefficients satisfy
-1\leqslant p_{k}\leqslant 1
, denote
I_{-}(p)=\{k\mid-1<p_{k}<0\}
and
I_{+}(p)=\{k\mid 0<p_{k}<1\}
. If
I_{-}(p)\cup I_{+}(p)
is not empty, for
t>0
sufficiently small, we modify
p
as follows.

•

If
\Card I_{+}(p)\geqslant\Card I_{-}(p)
, replace the
p_{k}
by
p_{k}+t
for
k\in I_{+}(p)
(ensuring
t
is small enough so that
p_{k}+t\leqslant 1
) and by
p_{k}-t
for
k\in I_{-}(p)
(ensuring
p_{k}-t\geqslant-1
).

•

If
\Card I_{+}(p)\leqslant\Card I_{-}(p)
, replace the
p_{k}
by
p_{k}-t
for
k\in I_{+}(p)
(ensuring
p_{k}-t\geqslant 0
) and by
p_{k}+t
for
k\in I_{-}(p)
(ensuring
p_{k}+t\leqslant 0
).

Moreover, by taking
t
maximal under these constraints, one obtains a new
p^{\prime}\in P
satisfying the strict inclusion
I_{-}(p^{\prime})\cup I_{+}(p^{\prime})\subsetneq I_{-}(p)\cup I_{+}(p)
. Furthermore,
\sigma(p^{\prime})\geqslant\sigma(p)
.

Suppose then that
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in P
is non-zero such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
. Up to acting by
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we may assume
-1\leqslant p_{k}\leqslant 1
for all
k
. Applying the previous modification to
p
as many times as possible, we obtain
p^{*}\in P
such that
I_{+}(p^{*})=I_{-}(p^{*})=\varnothing
(that is
p^{*}_{k}\in\{-1,0,1\}
) and
\sigma(p^{*})\geqslant 0
, as desired.
∎

Combining the previous lemmas, we obtain the following condition :
\Lambda
has finite volume if and only if there exists a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
, which can then be taken with coordinates in
\{-1,0,1\}
. Let us interpret this condition in terms of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
if and only if there exists a non-empty set
I
of the vertex set
\Lambda
of the graph
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
, which is stable and such that
\Card I\geqslant\Card\partial I
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in P
is non-zero such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
. By Lemma
3.4
, we may further assume that
p_{i}\in\{-1,0,1\}
. We define a non-empty stable set by
I=\{i\in I\mid p_{i}=1\}
, such that
p_{i}=-1
for any
i\in\partial I
. We thus have

0\leqslant\sigma(p)\leqslant\Card I-\Card\partial I.

Conversely, if a vertex set
I
satisfies the condition in the statement, define
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
by setting
p_{i}=1
for
i\in I
,
p_{i}=-1
for
i\in\partial I
, and
p_{i}=0
otherwise. Then
p\in P
by construction, and since
\Card I\geqslant\Card\partial I
, we also have
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0
.
∎

Proof of the
main theorem
.

We have proved:

\Conv(s)
has finite volume

(Lemma
3.1
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}\mid f(t)\leqslant 1\right\}
has finite volume

(Lemma
3.2
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

\Delta
has finite volume

(Lemma
3.3
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

there exists a non-zero
p\in P
such that
\sigma(p)\geqslant 0

(Lemma
3.5
)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\

there exists
\exists I\subseteq\Lambda
non-empty, stable, such that
\Card I\geqslant\Card\partial I
.

This concludes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The
main theorem
provides a criterion independent from the signature
(p,q)
. One would similarly obtain a criterion for the finiteness of the volume of simplices in
\mathbb{S}^{p-1,q}
, by considering the negative lifts, obtained by replacing the condition
\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j})\leqslant 0
with
\mathbf{b}(x_{i},x_{j})\geqslant 0
.

3.2.
Existence of simplices of infinite volume

Given a signature
(p,q)
, we seek to determine whether in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
there exist simplices of infinite volume or ideal simplices of infinite volume.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
p,q\geqslant 1
integers. There exists a non-ideal simplex of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
. If
p\geqslant 3
, then there exists an ideal simplex of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\Lambda_{0}
the unique ideal simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{1,0}=\mathbb{R}
: its vertices are the two isotropic lines of
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
. Let
\Lambda_{1}
be a simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{p-1,q-1}
. The simplices
\Lambda_{0}
and
\Lambda_{1}
are sets of lines of
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p-1,q}
respectively, which we consider as subspaces of the orthogonal sum
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{p-1,q}=\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
. Define a set of lines of
\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
by
\Lambda=\Lambda_{0}\cup\Lambda_{1}
. Positive lifts
s_{0}:\Lambda_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
and
s_{1}:\Lambda_{1}\to\mathbb{R}^{p,q+1}
define a lift
s=s_{0}\cup s_{1}
of
\Lambda
. We conclude by observing that
\mathbb{R}
acts freely by isometries on the factor
\mathbb{R}^{1,1}
while preserving
\Conv(s)
. Suppose now
p\geqslant 3
: we would like
\Lambda
to be an ideal simplex. For this, we only need that
\Lambda_{1}
be an ideal simplex. Let us choose a set of
p+q-1
distinct points of the boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{p-1}
, that is to say isotropic lines in
\mathbb{R}^{p-1,1}
. We can approximate them by a set of
p+q-1
isotropic lines of
\mathbb{R}^{p-1,q}
generating this space : this indeed defines an ideal simplex
\Lambda_{1}
of
\mathbb{H}^{p-1,q-1}
.
∎

In the spaces
\mathbb{H}^{0,q}=\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{q}
,
\mathbb{H}^{p,0}=\mathbb{H}^{p}
and
\mathbb{H}^{1,q}
, the question is trivial or classical : there exist ideal simplices only in
\mathbb{H}^{p,0}=\mathbb{H}^{p}
(
p\geqslant 1
) and the volume of simplices is bounded in each of the spaces
\mathbb{H}^{p,0}=\mathbb{H}^{p}
(
p\geqslant 2
) and
\mathbb{H}^{0,q}=\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^{q}
(
q\geqslant 1
).
\mathbb{H}^{2,1}=\mathbf{AdS}^{3}
contains an ideal simplex of infinite volume (example
3.3
) and the case of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
is surprising (see Corollary
3.8
). To my knowledge, it is not known whether there exist ideal simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{2,q}
with
q\geqslant 3
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Every ideal simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
has finite volume.

Proof.

Let
\Lambda
be an ideal simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
: it therefore has
5
vertices by
definition
. By Lemma
2.11
, there exists
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(\Lambda)
, of signature
1
such that
\xi
and
\sigma(\xi)
are adjacent in
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
. Since
\cal{G}(\Lambda)
has no loop (because
\Lambda
is ideal),
\sigma
has no fixed point. If
\sigma
decomposed as a
3
-cycle and a transposition, it would have signature
-1
:
\sigma
is therefore a
5
-cycle. Let
I\subseteq\Lambda
be a non-empty stable subset. The fact that
\xi
and
\sigma(\xi)
are adjacent for all
\xi
and that
\sigma
is a
5
-cycle implies
\Card\partial I>\Card I
.
\Lambda
therefore has finite volume by the
main theorem
.
∎

We summarise this in table
1
, which treats arbitrary simplices (left) and ideal ones (right). An entry
(p,q)
contains
f
if every simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
has finite volume,
\infty
if there exist simplices of infinite volume.

\begin{array}[]{c}\begin{array}[]{c|cc}&p=0&p\geqslant 1\\
\hline\cr q=0&\infty&f\\
q\geqslant 1&f&\infty\\
\end{array}\qquad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&p=2&p\geqslant 3\\
\hline\cr q=0&f&f\\
q=1&\infty&\infty\\
q=2&f&\infty\\
q\geqslant 3&?&\infty\end{array}\end{array}

Table 1.
existence of simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
(left) and existence of ideal simplices of infinite volume in
\mathbb{H}^{p,q}
(right)

Several questions remain. We do not know whether there exists an upper bound on the volume of ideal simplices of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
. A fortiori, we do not know whether there exists a maximum, nor where it would be attained : a good candidate would be the lightlike pentagon in
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
(example
3.3
). Finally, given an integer
n\geqslant 3
, we do not know whether every ideal simplex of
\mathbb{H}^{2,n}
has finite volume. Finally, let us state a differential-geometric corollary which is the original reason for this article.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
S
a space-like surface of
\mathbb{H}^{2,2}
, complete, maximal and of finite total curvature. The volume of
\Conv(S)
is finite.

Proof.

By a theorem of Moriani (voir
[
Mor24
]
), there exists a lightlike polygon
\Lambda_{0}
and a semi-positive lift
s
with convex hull
\Conv(s)=\Conv(S)
. Carathéodory’s theorem asserts that
\Conv(s)
is the (finite) union of the
\Conv s_{|\Lambda}
, for
\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda_{0}
of cardinal at most
5
, which have finite volume by Corollary
3.8
.
∎
