Abstract
The timing performance of bismuth germanate (BGO) scintillators in time-of-flight positron
emission tomography (TOF-PET) is limited by their slow scintillation decay, despite their high
density and stopping power. In this study, BGO-based detector configuration connected to silicon
photomultipliers (SiPMs) is used to improve energy resolution and coincidence time resolution
(CTR) by using dichroic optical filters. Shortpass and longpass filters with cutoff/on wavelengths
between 400 and 550 nm were examined. Double Gaussian fitting and waveform integration were
used to evaluate timing distributions and energy spectra. The optimal CTR with Full Width at Half
Maximum (FWHM) of 404 ± 171 ps and an energy resolution of 21.13 ± 0.48% was achieved
using the unfiltered setup, likely due to the added optical complexity induced by the presence of
the filters. Shortpass 500 nm dichroic filters outperformed the others in terms of time (FWHM:
420 ± 178 ps) while preserving a respectable level of energy resolution (26.68 ± 0.44). Longpass
filters severely reduced timing and pulse amplitude, especially at 550 nm. Filters transmitting
lower wavelengths retained the fast component with greater signal amplitudes and steeper rising
edges, according to average waveform analysis. This study suggests that while dichroic optical
filters can influence the fast light component in BGO, the best performance in terms of timing and
energy resolution was actually achieved with the unfiltered setup. This indicates a need to
reevaluate the role of dichroic filters in optimizing TOF-PET detector performance.
Keywords: Dichroic optical filters, bismuth germanate, coincidence time resolution, energy
resolution
Introduction
Time-Of-Flight Positron Emission Tomography (TOF-PET) is an extremely effective medical
imaging technology and the standard of care for imaging most malignancies. Over the past years,
significant advancements have been made in the instrumentation used in TOF-PET technology
[1,2, 3, 4, 5]. For instance, the image quality and accuracy of quantification are impacted by the
notable advancements in scintillators, photodetectors, and electronics, which improved
coincidence time resolution (CTR), directly enhancing image signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and
spatial localization of positron annihilation events [6, 7]. Fast scintillators with high light yields
are required to achieve high CTR and energy resolution, such as LYSO, which is commonly used
in TOF-PET. Among these scintillators, Bismuth Germanate (BGO) remains attractive because of
its high effective atomic number (Zeff =74), high density (ρ = 7.13 g/cm3), and greater stopping
power for 511 keV photons [8, 9]. Additionally, BGO crystals are relatively simple to cultivate,
which lowers production costs, particularly in comparison to lutetium-based fast scintillators [10].
BGO’s timing performance in TOF-PET applications has traditionally been limited due to
relatively slow scintillation decay around 300 ns and modest light yield (8.5 photons/keV) [1, 11].
A potential approach to boost the CTR of BGO for TOF-PET may be to exploit the small
population of promptly emitted Cherenkov photons that are generated much faster than the
scintillation, which are produced by 511 keV photon interactions. The high detection efficiency of
BGO allows it to be considered a good candidate for applying detection methods that use
Cherenkov radiation in TOF-PET systems. The BGO’s temporal signal has two components: a fast
decay component (40-60 ns) that represents only 8-10% of the emission and corresponds to the

 2

emitted Cherenkov photons, and a slow decay component (300-365 ns) that represents 92-90% of
the emission. One promising strategy to enhance BGO’s timing response is to use dichroic filters
for spectrum filtering. It should be mentioned that dichroic filters have lately been used in several
potential photon detection devices for large-scale neutrino detectors. These filters are starting to
be investigated in more detail for potential applications such as a photon-trap device for Hyper-
Kamiokande [12] and for the ARAPUCA and X-ARAPUCA light trap designs for ProtoDUNE
and DUNE [13]. A Wiston-style cone concentrator built from dichroic filters, which allow large-
scale neutrino detectors to detect photons by wavelength with small photon loss overall. Dichroic
filters function as real hybrid detectors due to their effective discrimination between Cherenkov
and scintillation light [14].
Interest in BGO for TOF-PET has increased recently due to developments in detector design and
readout methods, such as waveform-based timing extraction and rapid Cerenkov light detection.
Using dichroic filters for spectrum filtering is a promising strategy to enhance BGO’s timing
response [15]. These filters can selectively transmit fast, short-wavelength scintillation or
Cherenkov components while attenuating slower, long-wavelength emissions, thereby enhancing
the prompt fraction of the signal. Dichroic filters are ideal for maximizing the detected light
spectrum in SiPM based systems, where photon detection efficiency is also wavelength-dependent,
because they provide great spectral selectivity with low optical photon losses. By tailoring the
spectral response, dichroic filters can improve both the coincidence time resolution by
emphasizing the early-arriving photons and energy resolution by reducing noise contributions from
slow components and baseline fluctuations.
This study examines the impact of various dichroic filters (shortpass or longpass) on the energy
resolution and timing of BGO-based detectors. By analyzing their energy spectra, coincidence time
distributions, and waveform characteristics, we aim to determine optimal filtering conditions for
improving the applicability of BGO in TOF-PET systems.

Dichroic Filters: In this study, dichroic filters were employed to selectively transmit or reflect
specific wavelength ranges of light, facilitating precise spectral separation in the optical setup.
Shortpass dichroic filters with cut-off wavelengths of 400 nm, 450 nm, and 500 nm were used to
transmit 90% of light below their respective thresholds while reflecting 100% of longer
wavelengths. Conversely, longpass dichroic filters with cut-on wavelengths of 450 nm and 550 nm
were utilized to transmit 90% of light above these thresholds while reflecting 100% of shorter
wavelengths. The spectral characteristics of each filter were characterized using their transmission
and reflection spectra, as provided by the manufacturer (Figure 2), and these plots were
incorporated into the analysis to ensure accurate modeling of optical pathways in the system. To
enhance optical coupling between the filters and the SiPMs, all filters were sent to Edmund Optics
for dicing to dimensions of 3 × 3 mm², corresponding to the active area of the SiPMs (Table 2).
This ensured minimal photon loss and improved alignment, leading to more efficient light
collection and enhanced discrimination between the fast Cherenkov and slower scintillation
components.

Coincidence acquisition setup: a coincidence setup including a time reference crystal (LFS) and
the crystals under study (BGO) was constructed to analyze the time spectra of detected photons in
the presence of five dichroic filters. Meltmount (refractive index (n) of 1.58) was utilized to
optically couple each scintillator crystal to the 3 × 3 mm2 active area to a single SiPM (S14160-
3050HS, Hamamatsu, Japan). The SiPMs were biased by the DT5485P voltage power supply
(CAEN, Italy) to 42 V (+4 V overvoltage) for both LFS and BGO crystals. The reference crystal
(LFS) and crystal under study (BGO) were wrapped with more than 5 layers of Teflon to maximize
the light collection. The SiPM timing signal was amplified with a low-noise preamplifier (ZFL-
1000LN+) from Mini Circuits (USA). DRS4 evaluation boards V5 (designed at the Paul Scherrer
Institute, Switzerland) were used. Two signals were digitized, and the master trigger DRS4 was
programmed to trigger on the coincidence between the timing channels of the reference detector
LFS and the BGO crystal. Measurements were conducted with 22Na radioactive source (activity
10 µCi) placed 3 mm away from the crystals. This allows back-to-back 511 keV gamma ray
emission to be detected in coincidence (Figure 3a). For each dichroic filter, 10,000 coincidence
triggers were obtained. A block schematic of the readout electronics for timing measurements is
shown in Figure 3b. Full waveforms were digitized for offline signal processing to drive energy
through integration and time using the leading-edge threshold.

Waveform Analysis

Digitization: the waveforms were digitized using the DRS4 evaluation boards at a sample rate of
5 GS/s and 1024 sampling points, enabling the collection of 200 ns waveforms. We configured the
trigger logic in the master DRS to activate based on the leading edge of the coincident signal from
the SiPM connected to the LFS and the one connected to the BGO crystal. The trigger threshold is
configured of 0.4 V for the LFS reference detector and 0.05 V for BGO. Figure 4 shows the

 7

averaged voltage waveforms measured with different dichroic optical filters, including shortpass
(SP) and longpass (LP) configurations, compared to the unfiltered case. The waveforms
demonstrate clear variations in both rise time and amplitude depending on the filter used. The
unfiltered waveform (solid blue line) exhibits the highest amplitude and fastest rise, reaching a
peak voltage of approximately 0.43 V by around 100 ns. Shortpass filters (SP400, SP450, SP500)
retain much of the initial rising edge, with SP400 and SP500 reaching similar maximum
amplitudes (~0.42 V), whereas SP450 shows a reduced signal amplitude (~0.34 V). Longpass
filters (LP450, LP550), particularly LP550, exhibit significantly reduced signal amplitudes
(~0.2 V) and slower rise times, indicating stronger suppression of early scintillation components.
All waveforms plateau after ~100 ns, but with different final amplitudes depending on the filter
type.

Signal Rise Time: the accuracy of the signal arrival timing pickoff is greatly influenced by the
signal rise time. To determine the rise time of the BGO scintillation detector signal and the effect
of dichroic filters, the waveforms are first normalized to a scale of 0 to 1, based on their minimum
and maximum amplitude. The rise time is defined as the time interval between the points at which
the signal reaches 10% and 90% of its maximum amplitude. This method, known as the 10 – 90%
rise time calculation (rise time = 𝑡90% − 𝑡10% ), provides a consistent way to measure the sharpness
of the signal's leading edge. Linear interpolation is used between the discrete time samples to
accurately estimate the exact times corresponding to the 10% and 90% thresholds. This approach
allows for comparison of the detector's timing response under different experimental
configurations or filtering conditions. While the intrinsic rise time of BGO is quite fast (intrinsic

scintillator), it would be possible to get sub-nanosecond and good timing (typically around 0.5- 2
ns [19, 20]), the observed rise time may be influenced by the photodetector response, electronics,
and data acquisition system.

Experimental results
Energy spectra: To evaluate the impact of optical filtering on both energy and timing
performance, measurements were performed with and without the use of dichroic filters. Energy
spectra were obtained through waveform integration with event selection in the 511 keV photopeak
region [15]. A double Gaussian fit was applied to each spectrum to extract the energy resolution
(equation (1)) and the photopeak position. Table 3 summarizes the parameters derived from these
fits. The unfiltered configuration yielded an energy resolution of 21.13 ± 0.48% and a peak position
of 5.37 (arbitrary units). The uncalibrated energy spectra shown in Figure 6 and energy resolutions
and positions are summarized in Table 3.

Discussion
From Figure 1, we see that the spectra of BGO scintillation light lie in the wavelength band
between 400-660 nm, while Cherenkov light emission extends beyond this band. The scintillation
light provides a high light yield that is critical for good energy resolution and position
reconstruction. The energy resolution and corresponding photopeak positions varied across
different optical filter configurations (Table 3). The unfiltered setup yielded an energy resolution
of 21.13 ± 0.48% with a photopeak position at 5.37. Among the shortpass filters, the 400 nm filter
provided the best resolution (20.56 ± 0.36%) and a peak position of 4.18. This is likely due to the
transmission of light at higher wavelengths, as seen in Figure 2. While the 450 nm and 500 nm
filters exhibited degraded resolutions of 31.57 ± 0.39% and 26.68 ± 0.44%, with peak positions at
2.53 and 3.93, respectively. For the longpass filters, the 550 nm filter showed improved resolution
(22.52 ± 0.45%) compared to the 450 nm variant (27.77 ± 0.58%), with peak positions of 4.73 and
4.98, respectively. These results indicate a clear dependence of energy resolution and peak position
on the spectral transmission characteristics of the optical filters used.
The timing characteristics of the system, assessed using a double Gaussian fit to the coincidence
time data, demonstrate a clear dependence on the spectral properties of the applied optical filters.
The unfiltered setup achieved the narrowest overall FWHM of 404 ± 171 ps, with the first Gaussian
component centered at 1320 ps (FWHM: 385 ± 163 ps) and the second at 1640 ps (FWHM:
650 ± 276 ps), serving as a performance benchmark. The application of shortpass filters generally
broadened the timing response. The shortpass 400 nm and 450 nm filters exhibited the widest total
FWHMs of 484 ± 205 ps and 544 ± 231 ps, respectively, with corresponding shifts in peak timing
positions and increased spread in both Gaussian components, indicating greater temporal
dispersion likely due to the inclusion of shorter-wavelength scintillation components with longer
decay times. In contrast, the shortpass 500 nm filter produced a narrower FWHM of 420 ± 178 ps,

 14

closer to the nonfiltered performance, suggesting partial suppression of slower components while
retaining key fast scintillation light.
Longpass filters showed intermediate performance. The 450 nm longpass filter resulted in a total
FWHM of 476  202 ps, with first and second Gaussian centers at 1460 ps and 1810 ps,
respectively, while the 550 nm longpass yielded a slightly broader FWHM (500  212ps) but
maintained similar peak structure. These results suggest that both longpass filters modestly
suppress early fast components while allowing more delayed light, thereby increasing the overall
FWHM slightly compared to the nonfilter condition. Notably, the first Gaussian FWHM remained
relatively stable across all filter configurations (~340–440 ps), whereas the second Gaussian was
more variable, highlighting that timing degradation is primarily influenced by broader, delayed
signal contributions.
Overall, the filter choice significantly impacts timing resolution, with trade-offs between capturing
fast scintillation light and filtering out longer-wavelength delayed components. While no filter
surpassed the timing performance of the unfiltered condition, the shortpass 500 nm filter provided
a reasonable compromise, offering narrower FWHM while likely suppressing noise from slower
signal components. The reason for this is likely the added optical complexity due to the extra
interface surfaces (crystal-meltmount-filter-meltmount-SiPM). This could be mitigated with a
dedicated detector with an integrated scintillator-filter-SiPM structure via direct filter deposition
in BGO and SiPM. This potential sensor would require further study and optimization.
Conclusion
This study systematically evaluated the impact of various dichroic optical filters on the energy and
timing performance of a scintillation-based detection system, with a focus on the 511 keV
photopeak. Using waveform integration and a double Gaussian fitting method, we quantified
changes in energy resolution, peak position, and timing characteristics for both shortpass and
longpass filters. The results demonstrate that optical filtering significantly affects both detector
response and signal fidelity. Among all configurations, the unfiltered setup consistently provided
the best overall performance, yielding the highest signal amplitude, narrowest timing distribution
(FWHM: 404 ± 171 ps), and relatively good energy resolution (21.13 ± 0.48%). The shortpass
400 nm and 500 nm filters emerged as effective compromises, preserving much of the fast
scintillation light necessary for good timing resolution while maintaining moderate energy
performance. In contrast, the shortpass 450 nm and both longpass filters, especially the 550 nm
variant, introduced notable signal degradation, with broader timing distributions and lower pulse
amplitudes, likely due to suppression of fast, short-wavelength emission components. The average
waveforms further confirmed these trends: filters that transmitted fast, shorter-wavelength light
(e.g., SP400, SP500) retained steeper rise times and higher amplitudes, whereas longpass filters
allowed slower components to dominate the response. These findings highlight the importance of
spectral selection in optimizing scintillator-based detector systems, particularly for applications
requiring precise timing, such as time-of-flight PET or fast coincidence measurements. In
summary, careful choice of optical filtering can tune detector performance for specific application

 15

needs, with shortpass filters around 400–500 nm offering a favorable balance between energy
resolution, timing precision, and signal integrity.
