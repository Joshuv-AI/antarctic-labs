 2024 Second Half Semi Annual Report:
 Modeling plasticity-mediated flow in metals with pressurized cavities
 By Karl Garbrecht and Laurent Capolungo

                                                    July 15, 2025

1. Objective
 The objective is to better predict the bulk-scale mechanical behavior of porous metals that have over-
pressurized cavities (e.g., irradiated metals with helium bubbles) by quantifying the complex coupling among
cavity aspects (e.g., size distribution, inhomogeneous overpressure values, spatial arrangement) and metal prop-
erties (e.g., rate-dependency, crystallographic lattice). This requires up-scaling local mechanical fields from the
single crystal scale and will be accomplished using a homogenization approach that combines full-field numer-
ical simulations, analytical formalisms, and physics-informed machine learning to produce symbolically-defined
constitutive equations (e.g., gauge functions). These equations will satisfy the objective because they enable
computationally efficient predictions that approach the accuracy of computationally expensive full-field numeri-
cal simulations, abide by theoretical requirements (e.g., conservation of energy, work conjugacy), and retain the
transparency of analytical models.

2. Summary
 A spectral-based code for solving full-field crystal plasticity solid mechanics problems (LApx) has been
expanded to simulate a gas phase embedded in the solid matrix. The code leverages a conformal mapping
approach described in Zecevic et al. (2022) that produces a geometrically accurate boundary at the cavity/matrix
interfaces, even for microstructures with many (spherical) cavities with different diameters. The combination of
conformal mapping and gas-phase capabilities has been used to conduct preliminary simulations of polydispersed
microstructures in which individual cavities have a size-dependent overpressure values.
 To our knowledge, these are the first simulations of an anisotropic elasto-viscoplastic single crystal that
can have, for instance, a polydispersed cavity size distribution with inhomogeneous cavity overpressure values in
a (semi-)random spatial arrangement. This is a key requirement before development of homogenized constitutive
equations can occur using the proposed approach. Also, note that existing constitutive equations are often
based on considerations and variables that are averaged (e.g., cavity size distribution and overpressure values),
implicitly implied (e.g., hollow-sphere type cavity arrangement), or simply omitted (e.g., coupled effects of lattice
orientation and cavity arrangement). Furthermore, much of the existing work, including numerical simulations,
focus on modeling a rate-independent materials but a rate-dependent mechanical behavior is observed for many
metals in high temperature environments. Therefore, these capabilities enable us to quantify considerations that
are typically averaged, implied, or omitted and to determine under which cases that they need to be accounted
for to accurately predict the response of materials deployed in fusion reactors.

3. Progress and Status
 Two principle tasks were completed; (1) implementing the capability to simulate spherical gas phase
cavities and (2) developing and simulating as realistic as practical microstructures. The gas phase behavior was
implemented by strongly enforcing the stress state in gas-phase voxels in the LApx algorithm. However, there is
limited data on which to base the value of the stress state in the gas. Material aspects (e.g., rate-dependency) and
microstructural aspects (e.g., cavity size distribution and shape) were based on the best available information.
However, it is anticipated that different metals will be used for different components in fusion power plants and
that there will be significant variability in the loading (e.g., stress state) and environmental conditions (e.g.,
temperature, irradiation rate) to which they are subjected. Both of these considerations influence material and
microstructural aspects. This uncertainty must be approached strategically because the plastic deformation rate
is dependent on the coupled effects of material properties and cavity aspects and, in turn, the plastic deformation
influences their evolution. The following discusses details regarding completion of each task, including a strategy
to help ensure the current work is relevant to a variety of potential future applications.
 Regarding task 1, the stress state is enforced in the iterative procedure of the LApx algorithim,
which follows from the work of Lebensohn et al. (2012). It is restricted to a purely hydrostatic state (i.e., a
pressure) and its magnitude is given by a user specified value or computed based on the radius of the cav-
ity. In effect, the overpressure value of each cavity is simulated. That is to say, this is the stress expe-
rienced just inside the solid matrix at a cavity/matrix interface, which is the internal cavity pressure mi-
nus surface tension effects. This circumvents the need to determine material/gas specific surface tension en-
ergy or determine molar gas quantities in each cavity at this time. However, mathematic relationships for
these quantities (and their dependencies) can be directly integrated into the code in lieu of a fixed stress

in potential future applications. Another key capability of the current code

realistic interface geometries when using discretized microstructures that are
free of aliasing effects (i.e., solution errors arising from discretization limita-

grid in phase-space for spectral computations that is mapped to an irregular
grid that conforms to the smooth interfaces required for the problem in real

irregular grid configuration and induces a known stress that can be accounted
for in the regular grid configuration.

location, overlapping or coalescing cavities notwithstanding.
 We adopt the theoretically derived dislocation loop punchout overpressure value from the relationship
proposed by Donnelly (1985). This value is anticipated by Donnelly (1985) to be an overestimate. Accordingly,
it is taken to be an upper bound instead of a typical value that is expected in deployed materials. Since the
overpressure value depends on the internal cavity pressure which in turn depends on temperature, among other
considerations, we will scale (i.e., vary) the magnitude of this relationship such that the nature of the size
dependency is retained (i.e., smaller cavities can support high overpressure values than larger cavities). Altogether,
we are able to produce accurate interface geometries in discretized microstructures with realistic as practical cavity
shape, size distribution, and spatial arrangement with overpressure values that can range from zero overpressure
to a theoretical maximum. Figure 2 shows a microstructure that applies the conformal mapping procedure to
a polydispersed microstructure with the cavity size distribution given by a log-normal mixture with cavity size
dependent overpressure values computed by the theoretical maximum.

 The matrix is taken to be an anisotropic elasto-viscoplastic single crystal. Cubic linear elasticity is
assumed and the resolved shear stress on each slip system is used to predict glide-mediated deformation for a
given slip family. The matrix response is that of a power-law type material. It is acknowledged that there are other
rate-dependent constitutive relationships that could be better suited to one material or another. An advantage
motivating the use of power-law plasticity is that, while it can be challenging to acquire mechanical testing data
to calibrate other viscoplastic models, power-law plasticity is a well developed modeling formalism linking theory

 2

and a relatively simple mechanical test for calibrating a single parameter - the strain-rate sensitivity exponent.
Accordingly, the degree to which material testing results are consistent with power-law plasticity can indicate the
degree of accuracy that could be anticipated from the (matrix) predictions.
 The loading conditions of interest need to be determined to evaluate the mechanical behavior of the
microstructures. The difficulties of predicting the mechanical response of porous materials is predominantly asso-
ciated with the hydrostatic loading component and analytically derived equations have been shown to accurately
predict the deviatoric response (Rice and Tracey 1969; Leblond et al. 1994; Vincent et al. 2009; Han et al. 2013;
Das et al. 2021; Garbrecht et al. 2024). Furthermore, analytical analysis following well established approaches
(e.g., Gurson based methods and those using a variational approach analyzing a linear comparison composite) all
result in equations that linearly combine deviatoric and hydrostatic components. Thus, we plan to use analyti-
cally derived equations for the deviatoric component and the homogenization methods of Garbrecht et al. (2024)
that combine numerical simulations, analytical analysis, and physics-informed machine learning for the hydro-
static component. So, while purely hydrostatic loading is rarely encountered in practice, it is still the influence
of the hydrotstatic component that must be better resolved to make better predictions at typically encountered
triaxiality values (e.g., ≈0.5-6). All the above discussion taken together, an example of the simulated full-field

 Existing analysis using a variational approach has found that, for isotropic and rate-independent mate-
rials, the volumetric average cavity overpressure can be linearly combined with the externally applied hydrostatic
loading as a work-conjugate generalized variable (Danas and Ponte Castañeda 2009). However, this results is still
expected to be applicable to isolated cavities in an anisotropic elasto-viscoplastic matrix because they will have
concentric, radially decaying plastic fields that are symmetric, but not spherical. Figure 4 shows the full-field
simulation results of the same microstructures with an isolated cavity. The microstructure on the top has an
internal cavity pressure of 55 MPa but zero macroscopic loading and the bottom has zero cavity pressure but
55 MPa macroscopic hydrostatic loading. The first three columns from left to right show equal but opposite
mechanical stress fields for these two case and the last column on the left shows both case have the same von
Mises equivalent stress field. The point being, that the local von Mises stress drives plastic deformation and,
therefore, the same work-rate or dissipation is occurring in each case even though the Cauchy stress fields (the
first three columns) are inverted.

4. Results

 The capability to conduct full-field mechanical simulations of polydispersed porous microstructures
with inhomogeneous cavity pressures has been implemented in the LApx code. Preliminary simulations of rate-
dependent anisotropic materials indicate the superposition of external loading and the volumetric average cavity
overpressure is the work-conjugate generalized stress variable associated with the hydrostatic component, which is
consistent with analytical analysis for rate-independent isotropic materials. Therefore, analytical methods can be
used to incorporate the effects of overpressure in subsequent model development activities and the physics-informed
machine learning is only required for homogenizing the complex (analytically intractable) local mechanical fields
resulting from cavity interaction effects observed in Figure 3. Following the work of Garbrecht et al. (2024), this
means the data-driven methods can be restricted to two variables, the strain-rate sensitivity exponent and overall
volumetric cavity content, at least for polydispersed randomly arranged cavities.
