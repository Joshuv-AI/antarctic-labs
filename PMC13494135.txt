“Everyone Has a Different Body Constitution”: An Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis of the Experiences of Social Interaction and Related Supports in Chinese Autistic Postsecondary Students
Authors: Annabel Shin Ling Tong, Gary Yu Hin Lam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLittle research has investigated how autistic individuals make sense of their experiences with socializing and interacting with others in postsecondary education. Autistic students are also dissatisfied with insufficient social interventions and the lack of autistic input in the design of such support. This study investigated the lived experiences of social interaction and related support in autistic postsecondary students. We conducted interviews with eight Chinese autistic students currently enrolled in a university or college in Hong Kong. Analysis using an interpretative phenomenological analysis approach identified four themes, including (1) their attempts and motivation to connect and socialize, which was often felt ineffective and exhausting; (2) the internal negotiation and delicate balance between masking and unmasking; (3) the crucial role of both formal and informal supports in coping with social and emotional challenges; and (4) social support and interventions needed to include diverse strategies ranging from skills building to neurodiversity-informed practices that respond to individual preferences and contexts. Introduction

Social interaction is arguably one of the central focuses in the study of human beings, autistic and nonautistic alike. The American Psychological Association (2018) defines social interaction as “any process that involves reciprocal stimulation or response between two or more individuals,” which is fundamental to the development of social relationships and other social roles and functions. Similarly, the DSM (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) defined autism spectrum disorder (ASD) as having challenges in social communication and interaction, which includes a range of social behaviors such as social exchange, conversation, and nonverbal communication, as well as the development and maintenance of relationships. In studying social interaction (De Jaegher et al., 2010), especially in relation to autism (Bottema-Beutel, 2017; De Jaegher, 2013), it is crucial to view social behavior not just as an outcome of social cognition. Instead, the agency of autistic individuals and their active meaning-making process should be acknowledged, which can influence the dynamics and interaction between autistic individuals and their social partners. Conducting research from this perspective aligns with the view of the neurodiversity paradigm by recognizing autistic ways of social expression as valid and meaningful rather than deficient (Dwyer, 2022). It also resonates with the double-empathy problem (Milton, 2012), which emphasizes that misunderstandings arise from a mutual mismatch in worldviews and communication styles between autistic and nonautistic individuals, so it is important to listen to the autistic perspectives, which are often less heard, to understand how they contribute to the social dynamics.

Postsecondary education constitutes a unique context that influences how autistic students develop as emerging adults and interact with various types of people around them (Flegenheimer & Scherf, 2022). However, transitioning into higher education and adulthood in general can be challenging for many autistic students in not only academic but also social, daily living, and mental health aspects. For example, autistic postsecondary students were found to exhibit lower academic achievements, lower quality of social relationships, higher levels of victimization, lower psychological well-being, and higher rates of mental health challenges than nonautistic students (Ames et al., 2025; Davis et al., 2021; Wolpe, 2024). Concerning their social experience, autistic students tended to perceive socialization as more stressful (Lei et al., 2020b) and had a smaller social circle (Lei et al., 2020a) compared with their neurotypical peers. Autistic students reported feeling lonely, isolated, disengaged with others, and less able or confident to socialize (Gurbuz et al., 2019; Madriaga, 2010; Ward & Webster, 2017). During peer interaction, they expressed difficulties with having conversations to make friends, understanding their peers’ nonverbal communication, and experiencing social aversion related to the stigma associated with autism (Nelson et al., 2023). Autistic students also reported that participation in socially oriented academic activities, such as group projects and discussion, was often faced with peer conflicts and communication problems that they were left to resolve themselves (Wolpe, 2024). Inaccurate understanding and stigma associated with autism make autistic students hide their autistic characteristics to pass as nonautistic (i.e. camouflaging; Hull et al., 2019) and limit their disclosure in university to avoid judgment and discrimination (Underhill et al., 2024).

Despite the social challenges, past studies have found that autistic university students enjoyed positive social interaction with supportive and understanding people such as peers, professors, and professionals, as well as engagement in social groups and online communities that they could relate to and have interest in (Casement et al., 2017; Cullen, 2015; Goddard & Cook, 2022; Lam, 2025; Scott & Sedgewick, 2021). The university context is also filled with opportunities for autistic students to apply and develop social and prevocational skills, although the physical and social environments (e.g., sensory-overwhelming crowds) are not always friendly to their needs and preferences (Casement et al., 2017; Goddard & Cook, 2022; Madriaga, 2010). Autistic students appear to show a diversity of social motivation and concerns in postsecondary education contexts. For example, even though autistic students expressed similar levels of social desire as neurotypical students, many of them did not develop meaningful friendships or have any friends at all, which reflected a complex picture behind their social motivation and outcomes (Gurbuz et al., 2019). Past qualitative studies documented their diverse social experiences as a part of their overall university experience (e.g., Goddard & Cook, 2022; Gurbuz et al., 2019; Madriaga, 2010).

This study focuses on what autistic university students find helpful that supports or improves their social exchanges and communication experiences. Types of formal supports commonly available in postsecondary institutions that target autistic students’ social interaction include social skills training, peer-mediated or mentoring programs, support groups, counseling, and other disability services or university programs (Davis et al., 2021; Widman & Lopez-Reyna, 2020). Despite being helpful for some students, these supports are not always accessible to or welcomed by autistic students because of concerns such as nondisclosure of autistic identity, stigmatization of help-seeking, and limited awareness of the available support (Anderson et al., 2018; Davis et al., 2021; Gelbar et al., 2014). Many autistic students were also dissatisfied with the infrastructure and service systems in postsecondary institutions that provided disintegrated and inconsistent services that did not match their individual social needs (Tan et al., 2025). Instead of relying on formal supports, some autistic students resorted to informal means of support, such as gathering with other neurodivergent students and reading self-help books, to improve their social experiences and relationships with others (Goddard & Cook, 2022; Van Hees et al., 2015; Ward & Webster, 2017).

Although there is burgeoning literature worldwide that delves into autistic students’ experiences, the majority investigated their university lives across multiple domains (e.g., Kim et al., 2021; Lam, 2025; Lim et al., 2023; McPeake et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2025). Studies also expressed concerns over the relatively little attention and support on social aspects in postsecondary education compared to academics (e.g., Ames et al., 2025; Cai & Richdale, 2016; Flegenheimer & Scherf, 2022; Leake & Stodden, 2014; Van Hees et al., 2015). It is crucial to directly ask autistic students about their experiences and opinions on things that can support their social experiences. There were some qualitative studies focusing on the experience of social life and its impact on academic development and mental well-being of autistic students in university (e.g., Goddard & Cook, 2022; Gurbuz et al., 2019; Scott & Sedgewick, 2021). However, more importantly, research about the experiences of autistic individuals in postsecondary education is almost exclusively conducted in Western countries, leaving a huge gap as to how or whether the extant literature applies to populations outside of the WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrial, Rich, and Democratic) context (Muthukrishna et al., 2020). In Hong Kong, support for university students with neurodivergent or disability conditions, including autism, generally covers areas of academic, housing, transportation, financial, and campus accessibility, although services vary tremendously across institutions and are deemed inadequate, inconsistent, or, at best, underresearched (Chan & Yeung, 2020). Only one qualitative study has explored specifically the experiences of autistic university students in Hong Kong, which highlighted that caring and supportive people, including friends, peers, staff, and instructors, in specific learning or social contexts are conducive to autistic students’ positive experiences (Lam, 2025). Due to the lack of research in this region, specifically about autistic students’ lived social experiences, it is both empirically and practically uncertain what they find helpful to improve their social life in university and how educators, professionals, and policy-makers can improve the existing support. Therefore, we employ interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) to explore the following research question: How do autistic postsecondary students in Hong Kong make sense of their social lives and the supports that shape their social interaction experiences?

Method

Methodology

We employed a constructivist epistemological stance in this study, acknowledging that knowledge about human beings can be accessed through participants’ meaning-making and researchers’ interpretation of their experiences. Aligning with this epistemology, this study is focused on gaining an in-depth understanding of the subjective experiences and complex meanings of autistic individuals participating in social interaction in postsecondary education (Howard et al., 2019; MacLeod, 2019). The intentional emphasis on their lived experiences can offer radical insights that transform social interventions in ways that align better with autistic individuals’ subjective meanings and feelings that are often ignored by clinicians and researchers (Hartman & Squires, 2024).

We used the qualitative research methodology of IPA to explore how individuals understand and make sense of their lived experiences (Smith, 2011). Guided by the theoretical principles of phenomenology, hermeneutics, and idiography, IPA emphasizes a close examination of the meaning and interpretation of participants’ lived experiences that are unique to them (Smith, 2011). It employs the double hermeneutic approach in which the researcher is required to engage with and interpret the participants’ experiences according to how the participants make sense of their happenings (Ginev, 1998; Smith, 2011). This requires the researcher to reflect the participants’ own narratives and meaning and interpret their experiences while being aware of their presumptions and biases. This also demands researchers to reflect the participants’ insider perspective while being aware of their potential presumptions and biases when interpreting their meanings as an outsider (Smith et al., 2021). The IPA is also committed to being idiographic by encompassing both a detailed analysis of each individual’s experience and revealing patterns and shared themes among individuals (Smith, 2011).

Participatory Methods and Positionality

While IPA can allow nonautistic researchers to engage autistic participants in different research stages of sense-making and reflective interpretation of their narratives (Howard et al., 2019), our current study is not considered participatory, as autistic participants’ input is limited to seeking feedback from a pilot autistic adult and involving autistic participants in the research interviews and member checking. We acknowledged that this study was conducted by Annabel Shin Ling Tong (A.S.L.T.) and supported by Gary Yu Hin Lam (G.Y.H.L.), who are a nonautistic educational/school psychologist trainee and trainer, respectively. While we are skilled in providing educational and psychological services for children and youth, we recognized the scarcity of research on the social experiences of autistic students in university, especially outside Western contexts. A.S.L.T. has a background in social advocacy and diversity and is particularly concerned about the social stigma faced by autistic individuals, realizing that their social interactions are often misunderstood by educators and community members who may not understand autistic perspectives. We brought this interpretative lens to the present study to explore how autistic individuals make sense of their experiences. While acknowledging our role in interpreting these accounts through our non-autistic perspective, and thus engaging in the double hermeneutic central to IPA, we sought to center autistic participants’ perspectives and amplify their voices, which “may also go some way towards alleviating the ‘double empathy problem’” (Howard et al., 2019, p. 1871).

Participants

This study was approved by the Survey and Behavioural Research Ethics Committee at the Chinese University of Hong Kong (EDU2024-184). Recruitment posters were distributed through different channels (e.g., university email system, student affairs offices, and social media of community organizations serving the neurodivergent community). After interested participants completed an online application form, A.S.L.T. contacted the individuals for eligibility screening and to make the interview arrangements. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) formally diagnosed or self-identified as autistic, (2) currently enrolled in a full-time program in a university or college in Hong Kong, (3) aged 18 or above, and (4) able to participate in the interview in Cantonese Chinese.

Eight autistic postsecondary students were recruited using convenience sampling. This sample size is considered appropriate for a rigorous IPA study to detail individuals’ experiences in a relatively homogeneous sample (Smith et al., 2021). Participants’ background information was collected and summarized in Table 1. Participants were asked to select pseudonyms to represent themselves: While some preferred pseudonyms, others preferred initials, none of which were identifiable as their actual names. They were also asked to report relevant social experiences across various postsecondary contexts, such as residential halls, orientation, and support services, which were used to support the interview. Participants also completed the Chinese version (Chan & Liu, 2008) of the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ-10) (Baron-Cohen et al., 2001) as an estimate of their autistic traits.

Interview

All participants gave informed consent. Based on individual participants’ preferences, interviews were conducted face-to-face at the university campus (n = 5) and online via Zoom (n = 3). A.S.L.T. conducted all the interviews, while G.Y.H.L. provided on-site and off-site support and feedback. The interviews were conducted using Cantonese Chinese, with an average duration of 65 min and a range of 54 to 87 min. The interviews were audio recorded throughout the process and transcribed verbatim by G.Y.H.L. afterwards. A remuneration of a US$50 (approximately US$7) supermarket coupon was provided for each participant, a common rate for 1 hour of research participation in Hong Kong.

The team designed an interview protocol that outlined the major questions and prompts to facilitate participants in elaborating their experiences, which covered two major aspects, including the participants’ lived experiences with social interaction in postsecondary education contexts and the supports they received for social interaction (Table 2). A.S.L.T. conducted a pilot interview with a former autistic research participant who had graduated with a master’s degree. Based on the pilot participant’s feedback, A.S.L.T. amended the interview protocol (e.g., reducing general questions about postsecondary life) and logistics (e.g., allowing more flexibility for the interview time) to suit the needs of the participants. In the actual semistructured interviews conducted with the participants, A.S.L.T. was flexible with making adjustments to the sequence and wording of the interview questions, using various prompting questions, and focusing on different depths of probing based on the participants’ interests and concerns (Smith & Osborn, 2004).

Data Analysis

Data analysis was led by A.S.L.T. and supported by G.Y.H.L., which involved an iterative and reflexive process that moved between examining individual participant interviews and expanding to analyze commonalities and differences among participants’ experiences (Smith et al., 2021). The analysis began with A.S.L.T. reading interview transcripts while listening to the recordings, making reflective notes to create a chronological timeline of participant responses and record her emerging impressions. Next, A.S.L.T. used NVivo 15 to annotate transcripts with exploratory notes, including both descriptive and interpretative information that captured major concerns, potential meanings, and notable verbal and nonverbal expressions (Smith et al., 2021). This step was followed by a coding phase, where A.S.L.T. reevaluated and refined her notes to formulate experiential statements that sought to understand and interpret the essence of participants’ experiences (Smith et al., 2021). A.S.L.T. then clustered each participant’s experiential statements based on their qualities and meanings, resulting in the formation of personal experiential themes that delineated individual participant experiences. The above four steps were repeated for each participant, after which cross-case analysis was performed to derive group experiential themes (i.e. group themes), which encapsulated both the shared and distinct characteristics of participants’ experiences (Smith et al., 2021). The finalized group themes presented an organized reflection of prevalent themes while ensuring the individuality and richness of each participant’s narratives were preserved (Smith & Osborn, 2015). Participants’ quotes were translated to English by A.S.L.T. and reviewed by G.Y.H.L. in the report writing stage.

Research Quality

To ensure quality within the IPA research framework, we sought to adhere to its phenomenological, hermeneutic, and idiographic principles (Smith et al., 2021). A.S.L.T. kept reflective notes throughout the process to document her personal observations and reflections about the participants’ experiences, which helped her think through how her personal biases and identity might shape her interpretations (Pietkiewicz & Smith, 2014). In addition, A.S.L.T. used journaling to capture reflections and questions about different possibilities of interpretations during data analysis, attempting to yield a richer understanding of participant experiences (Nizza et al., 2021). This practice also provided an audit trail that documented the decision-making from transcripts, researchers’ notes, and experiential statements to themes. The overall research process was supervised by G.Y.H.L., who provided guidance during several interviews, offered insights on interpretations through independently annotating several transcripts, and gave feedback on thematic development. A.S.L.T. also conducted member checking by sharing resultant themes with participants for feedback. Two participants provided clarifications and feedback on wording, while others confirmed the accuracy of the themes.

Analysis

The analysis of the participants’ narratives revealed the nuanced realities of autistic postsecondary students’ experiences regarding social interactions and related supports in Hong Kong. We organized the data around four interrelated group themes that capture our interpretations of their lived experiences. Autistic students showed different attempts and motivation to connect and socialize but often felt ineffective and exhausted (Theme A). They also struggled between masking and unmasking, attempting to find a balance between maintaining authenticity and integrating into society (Theme B). While both formal and informal social networks served as a crucial role in helping autistic students cope with social and emotional challenges (Theme C), they expressed concerns over the lack of interventions that can meet their individual needs and preferences, and advocated for more neurodiversity-informed and naturalistic approaches to supporting their social development (Theme D). The following section will explore each of these themes and their respective subthemes in detail, which are summarized in Table 3. We describe the themes with representative quotes below.

Theme A: Trying to Connect and Socialize, But It Was Ineffective and Tiring

Subtheme A1: The Joy and Vulnerability of Socializing

Postsecondary education settings provided opportunities for more social interaction through less structured schedules and more social activities. While autistic students were motivated to initiate and sustain connections with others, they also showed concerns and often encountered barriers. Companionship was found to be an important anchor in some participants’ postsecondary school lives, which brought them happiness and support. For example, Y and TSW clearly valued friendships. Y felt happy about her friends’ company (e.g., as simply as having lunch together) and emotional support when she was down, which also translated to her academic success:

At least I don’t have to fight the battle alone. Someone is with me to defer the studies, take the same courses, and meet up to study together, making me feel not lonely. (Y)

TSW and her friends supported each other by companionship in activities outside of their personal interests. For example, a friend accompanied her for a train ride despite her having sensory challenges on transportation.

However, socializing had not always been desirable for participants who experienced vulnerability and insecurity when either being excluded or alone. Sometimes, their desire to connect was not well received by other peers. TSW and Sapphire felt tired and insecure when their friends were not around and felt disappointed and embarrassed when being excluded or ignored by peers in social situations. Likewise, Sapphire, Q, and A wanted social relationships but worried that their peers would discriminate or view them as “annoyance” (Sapphire) and “disturbance” (A). Although Yuki and Sasa appeared to show lower social motivation, they would feel “bored . . . dissatisfied [with social life] and sometimes empty” (Sasa) for being missed out from social networks.

Subtheme A2: “Our Values Just Click”: Common Interests and Values Foster Connection

Participants encountered difficulties in connecting with their peers due to having different or limited interests compared to others. Y “had nothing much to talk with them,” while TSW felt like “being from a different world” at the orientation camp because she had no common interests with others, so she felt uncomfortable and eventually left. Similarly, due to a lack of exposure and experience of peers’ interests, Sasa had limited engagement in conversations and interaction, thus feeling “lonely” and “empty.” These experiences led to a sense of resignation and social exclusion: “Even though I forced myself to stick with them, I could not blend in . . . or truly become friends with them” (Yuki).

In contrast, social connections and relationships could develop naturally and spontaneously when common values and interests were found: “Perhaps our values just clicked, even though we didn’t make it happen” (Yuki). TSW elaborated, “It’s not I cannot be social, but it depends on who,” as she narrated her experiences with socializing and befriending with fandoms of trains and public housing who shared similar intense interests as her. Q sought out people, including autistic peers, with shared interests and similar styles in clubs and online communities outside of the university.

Subtheme A3: Communication in Group Projects and Discussion Was Frustrating and Stressful

Many participants found communication, especially in group projects, ineffective, exhausting, and stressful. They struggled to explain tasks, align expectations, and express themselves in ways that their peers could understand and follow, especially when they had different perspectives or interpretations:

The problem was we had already discussed our ideas and written them in the document . . . But they didn’t really understand the main point . . . What they ended up writing wasn’t satisfactory . . . Even though there were both written and verbal explanations, how can I make them truly understand and agree with the direction we had already discussed? (Sasa)

Sasa and Yuki were concerned about efficiency but struggled to motivate their groupmates and negotiate task deadlines (e.g., “When is a reasonable time to start urging them?” [Sasa]; “How to tell [their poor quality of work] without hurting their self-esteem?” [Yuki]). These misunderstandings and ineffective communication left participants feeling “frustrated” (Ming), “too troublesome” (A), and “stressful” (A and Ming), leading to helplessness, self-doubt, and even cynicism about working with others: “The painful part is that I have to communicate and collaborate with these difficult people” (Yuki). In the case of A, even though he felt that “time is less tight” in group projects and so he could take the time to think and contribute, he generally struggled to engage in daily conversations and group discussions because of his difficulties with organizing his words and coming up with useful ideas:

It’s still tiring talking or communicating with others. It’s like taking an exam. Keep using my brain to think what I should say to look normal. (A)

Theme B: Relative Weight Between Masking and Unmasking

Subtheme B1: “Not Comfortable to Disclose My True Self”: Masking Helps Avoid Stigmatization but Comes with Physical and Emotional Costs

Many participants did not disclose their autism or used masking as a strategy to avoid social stigma, judgment, and the risk of appearing less socially competent: “I worry that others become aware of my weaknesses in this [social] aspect, lowering their expectations of me, like talking to me less” (Sasa). Q emphasized the need to control his stimming and “imitate the tone of a normal person” in lectures and meetings to “protect” himself, as he reiterated, “You wouldn’t know,” to express his vigilant view toward potential stigma: “How much percent of that group, out of a few dozens of students will have [stigma].”

Despite the perceived usefulness, performing like a normal person was “very tiring” (TSW), “exhausting . . . and effortful” (Sapphire) to many participants. For instance, TSW found prolonged masking at work unsustainable and “irritating,” sometimes failing to suppress her verbal stimming. Q considered masking may have worsened his mental health and partly contributed to the relapse of his depression. To deal with this, he adopted “adaptive masking” and limited masking to 15 hr per week to “survive and deal with the environment, while feeling okay and comfortable with it” and balance his well-being with adapting to society. Q also described washing his face frequently in crowded and noisy environments like Chinese restaurants as a “self-soothing strategy, a kind of stim . . . that I found adaptive, . . . helping my sensory and emotion regulation.”

Subtheme B2: Moments of Authenticity Without Masking

Not every participant learned coping strategies to deal with masking and its burdens, but Q, Sapphire, and TSW found social situations where they could feel being in their own skin and accepted for who they are, which made them energized by being their authentic selves without masking. For example, Q could unmask when engaging in “info-dumping” by passionately discussing his interests in research with like-minded colleagues and supervisors, as well as some autistic individuals in the online community who “have similar communication style . . . and share common interests.” His tone was particularly excited when sharing about “info-dumping,” indicating that such experience was exceptionally enjoyable and comfortable for him:

To a certain extent, when chatting with my colleagues or supervisor, I talk about topics that are of our common interests . . . We enjoy info-dumping with each other. Some of them are autistic. So autistic people communicate better with autistic people, on average, in general . . . We find each other more comfortable, with a similar communication style. (Q)

Sapphire valued authenticity in relationships and was touched when their university friends said, “Autism is a part of you, and I like the real you.” Relationships were especially meaningful when they were accepted unconditionally and embraced for being themselves without pretense.

Subtheme B3: Compromising by Using Masking to “Integrate into Society.”

To mask or to unmask had various beneficial or adverse effects, as some participants elaborated on their mindset of reconciling the need to balance both in the long run, with an ultimate goal to help them “integrate into society” (TSW). Participants were aware of their needs while also willing to understand what society demands, so they chose to adapt to societal norms and expectations as a means to respect others while finding a relatively comfortable position for being themselves. Sapphire reasoned that they were genuinely “concerned about social norms,” and thus they “chose” to mask instead of “forcing” themselves so others would not see their stimming and feel that they were being disrespectful. TSW also echoed the importance of adhering to what was considered “normal” and “appropriate” to avoid being “a nuisance to others”:

I know what I should do in what situation. I understand it all, as I am not a kid. But whether I do it depends on my mood and if there is a need to perform that well or not . . . At the end of the day, I need to integrate into society, get a job, and finish my studies. If I am not willing to conform, then my mom has to support me forever. That is not going to happen. So I have to make myself act normal. (TSW)

This narrative showed participants’ realistic and practical approach to masking to meet societal expectations by considering one’s roles, responsibilities, and social obligations at school, at work, and in public spaces. Such behaviors were something they found adaptive and beneficial to themselves and social others. This is exemplified by Q’s renewed insights after he came across the contemporary perspectives on neurodiversity, which made him understand how masking had affected his early life and the need to strike a balance:

I reflected on how I could adapt to society while staying true to myself. I think that’s really important. It was because of those [neurodiversity-related] articles that I started making a series of adjustments . . . I learnt how to practice self-compassion, how to care for and accept both my challenges and strengths. (Q)

Theme C: Formal and Informal Support Helped Cope With Emotional and Social Challenges

Subtheme C1: A Safe and Inclusive University Environment With the Support of Educators and Professionals

Participants received comfort and practical help from educators and professionals to cope with social challenges, such as forming project groups, communicating with groupmates, and addressing misunderstandings with supervisors. While Sapphire enjoyed “exploring my inner feelings” with a university social worker and a hospital psychologist, TSW similarly appreciated her counselor’s nonjudgmental listening ear, which allowed her to clear her head without giving advice:

Honestly, I felt that the counselor really enjoyed listening to me. After I finished talking, he/she knew that I needed to analyze things by myself. I tended to think through what I should do about the situation. Then I would ask, “Do you think this is right?” and they would assure me that it is okay, but there could be a few minor problems here and there. (TSW)

An inclusive climate where participants were accepted by professors and supervisors helped them learn and cope. Q felt more comfortable and less judged in his research team, where his colleagues were open about mental health issues and his supervisor adopted a direct communication style to suit his preferences. TSW’s professors respected her interests and motivated her to learn by using trains as an analogy to explain the concept of a logic chain in writing while also validating her creative efforts in an assignment about trains:

Sometimes when I talked about things related to the MTR (Mass Transit Railway), some classmates wouldn’t really listen or would just laugh at me. But the professors, or anyone really, even the Dean of our Faculty would talk to me seriously and say, “TSW, you spoke really well.” I truly think the school does a great job with diversity. When I was working at the student office, they really took good care of me. It’s even okay for me to put my train models in the office. (TSW)

Participants’ experiences in university seemed to be a stark contrast with the relatively lesser accepting attitudes they experienced in grade school. Some of them recalled in secondary schools, “whatever I did was wrong except studying” (TSW), and “some people really discriminated against me” (Q). Reflecting on their sensory issues that made them stand out from their primary school peers, in retrospect, Sapphire “could have realized I was neurodivergent much earlier” if people did not make them “[think] being my true self isn’t something that others would like” and “doubt myself.”

Subtheme C2: Mutual Support Among Peers: “We Are Saving Each Other.”

Peer support offered a space for being understood, emotional safety, and guidance through challenges navigating university life and studies. For example, the companionship of TSW’s friends provided her security when navigating unfamiliar environments, as she felt anxious being alone in lectures and student activities. Y mentioned several times the incident of losing her wallet, where she thanked her university friends’ company that soothed her:

[My friends] stayed with me and comforted me for a while. One of my friends even offered to treat me a meal. They really treated me well. I truly feel that they had tried their best to support me emotionally. (Y)

Some participants specifically acknowledged the role of their autistic peers who show similar challenges in giving them mutual support, which felt like “saving each other” (TSW). They felt safe and enjoyed mingling with those “who are like me” (A and TSW), where they “don’t need to care too much [about others’ perception]” (A). Q proactively sought out neurodivergent people on social media and in academia, who often shared similar struggles and exchanged coping strategies:

Since many of them are autistic and or have ADHD, they often face very similar challenges . . . So they also share about how they cope with masking. For example, in what kind of situations they mask, any stimming that doesn’t affect others and helpful to themselves, without causing self-harm. (Q)

TSW further mentioned her participation in a local autistic-led community group, where other autistic people with similar challenges made her reflect on how to improve her interpersonal skills and understanding:

I feel like I learn more [in this peer group], because we are all the same. You see someone across from you has a certain trait that I don’t like, but then I realize I have it too. So should I also change it? Or maybe they don’t seem confident about something, but I personally feel it’s totally okay. Sometimes I’m not confident either, so maybe others don’t really mind as well? I think this group is really great. I really think it should be promoted and expanded. (TSW)

Interacting with peers in the autistic group who shared similar struggles as TSW normalized her feelings of loneliness and misfortune, which gradually developed into her self-compassion and empathy toward others:

I used to think I was really lonely. I would wonder why I was so unlucky to be in this situation. But then I saw the seniors, or others in the group. Some of them are already in their twenties or thirties and some even have a daughter. Yet, they are still going through the same thing. And I realized that actually a lot of people are in this situation . . . In those moments, I find myself becoming more understanding. Actually, I’ve been learning to accept myself all along—because I hope that by doing so, society will do the same. (TSW)

Theme D: Social Development Is Important, but the Methods and Delivery Are Not One-Size-Fits-All

Subtheme D1: Reaping Practical Benefits Through “Learning the Neurotypical World’s Social Norms.”

Many participants recalled their experiences of learning about social norms and practicing social skills through different formal interventions or informal milieus (e.g., working with clinicians, participating in intervention programs, and interacting with peers and colleagues), through which they appreciated the practical benefits. Q and Ming believed that learning about the neurotypical ways of socializing could help them develop the skills and confidence to integrate into society:

There should be mutual understanding. An autistic individual gets to understand more the neurotypicals’ social norms and cues . . . Learning [these] can adapt to society, because the majority don’t understand autism, so it is useful for us autistic individuals to learn those things. (Q)

This pragmatic, “utilitarian” (Y) need of learning to adapt to the neurotypical way of socializing resonated with participants’ goals to gain positive evaluation from peers and lecturers through making “a good impression” (Y) and developing “good guan-xi (relationship)” (Y). Similarly, TSW treated learning to socialize as “playing a good impression game,” where she felt both enjoyable and meaningful:

It’s quite meaningful to treat it like a game. I don’t think it is pleasing others. I won’t. In fact, I think if you are happy I would be happy too. (TSW)

Even though socializing was sometimes not what they preferred personally, they reconciled with the practical need to socialize “for my future job and life” (Yuki) to fulfill academic and career responsibilities. For example, TSW saw her student office job as “training” and went from avoiding phone calls to calling multiple people: “The university has given me a chance to train [such skills], so I utilize this opportunity to improve myself.” Similarly, receiving training or advice from clinicians about practical interpersonal and communication skills, such as group project discussion and making a presentation speech, made participants feel “more relaxed” (Ming and Y), “helpful” (Ming), “useful” (Q), and “easier to adapt [to the environment]” (Y).

Subtheme D2: Social Interventions Misalign With Individuals With Diverse Preferences, Needs, and “Different Body Constitutions.”

Participants expressed the need for diverse types of social interventions to meet with autistic individuals’ different preferences, goals, and responses to interventions. Sapphire likened social interventions to medication, so “the same medicine has different effects on different people” with “different body constitutions,” making reference to the foundational concept in Traditional Chinese Medicine that emphasizes matching one’s natural body type to suitable lifestyle and medication or support. Likewise, Q asserted “the biomedical model can co-exist with neurodiversity, or even combine” by explaining that:

I felt like we need to respect different preferences. Someone thinks autism is a disability . . . Someone thinks it’s a disorder, a condition, a difference, or a combination of these terms. (Q)

Some participants expressed dissatisfaction with the therapeutic approaches or modalities of social interventions that they found not meeting their needs. Yuki and Sapphire perceived the common methods of social skills training not useful at their adult age, as they already developed “fixed ways of dealing with things” (Yuki) and “already learned how to mask” (Sapphire). Sapphire further reflected that their social and emotional challenges stemmed from their self-esteem and self-acceptance and perceived that the intervention recommendations they received so far (e.g., cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT], mindfulness, social skills workshops) were ineffective or at best irrelevant to addressing the “root” cause of their problems. Although Q agreed “some of the [social] skills were actually useful,” he still showed reservations over the potential “side effects” of prolonged masking reinforced by some social interventions. For instance, he was asked to “fake it until I make it” and “copy others,” which he reasoned might have caused him stress and compromised his long-term mental health. Similarly, Sasa reflected on her struggle between staying true to herself and “not force myself,” as she still wanted to improve her sociality and develop new interests. This speaks to the dilemma of how social interventions should serve to facilitate positive changes desired by autistic individuals while respecting their inner struggles and preferences.

Subtheme D3: Practical Supports Need to Be Closer to Real Life and Less Intimidating

Many participants recognized learning social skills was insufficient due to gaps between the design and implementation of social interventions and their actual needs in the real world. Sasa struggled to apply the skills to daily life due to “a lack of motivation . . . and real-life practices,” and a lack of integration across different interventions:

I was taught how to develop and think of topics to talk about. However, I did not really practice that intentionally. Therefore, I did not have a lot of improvements . . . Because there are so many therapies and different groups offering various strategies, when all of them are combined together, after attending so many similar groups or therapies, having so many different kinds of methods becomes a bit confusing. The new strategy I learnt may also replace the old ones. (Sasa)

Sasa, Q, and Sapphire stressed the importance of providing practical guidance to address their concerns in specific social situations by “focusing on the [social] circles I am facing” (Sasa), such as interacting with others in group projects, church activities, and the workplace, as well as coping with their autistic sensory needs.

Participants expressed the need for interventions to not only address the actual needs of autistic students but also be implemented in a relaxed manner and in a casual setting, which would help them become more comfortable with applying social skills and learning to socialize with others. Ming criticized the social skills programs he attended after school, which he said was a lesson that made him nervous as he felt “self-conscious” and stressed about conversing and answering others’ questions in a contrived setting. Instead, Ming and A advocated providing support for social interaction in informal social activities that are “more diverse, unique, and relaxing, instead of being too formal” and offer “more varieties and options for us to choose from.” For example, A suggested putting board games in a university common area: “Who knows if I have a chance getting to know people? That gives me an opportunity for social exchange, . . . which is not too reserved.”

Discussion

Postsecondary autistic students in this study had different relationship preferences, but their social motivation and attempts were notable, which should not be ignored or misconstrued (Jaswal & Akhtar, 2019), as autistic individuals in Hong Kong, including children (e.g., Ng & Ng, 2022) and adults (e.g., Lam & Wong, 2026), still face tremendous stigma and misunderstanding that negatively impact their interaction with others in the community. Some participants actively self-selected into interest-based communities outside classes or universities that matched their intense interests, showing how shared interests can drive social participation and meaningful relationships (Aiston et al., 2025). In contrast, they often had no choice but to participate in group work and navigate unfamiliar social groups and topics, where communication breakdown and social disconnection with nonautistic peers and instructors occurred. This pattern of social challenges has been reported in previous studies (e.g., Lim et al., 2023; Van Hees et al., 2015), but relatively little attention is paid to what nonautistic peers and instructors can do to mitigate or prevent such challenges. Future research should examine these interactions from the perspective of the double-empathy problem to devise ways to improve cross-neurotype communication and relationships in postsecondary education, especially in the Chinese context, where neurodiversity-informed practices and research are extremely scant.

This study revealed the nuanced reasons and costs of masking in Chinese autistic postsecondary students. Similar to existing research (e.g., Han et al., 2021; Hull et al., 2017; Pearson & Rose, 2021), some participants masked their autistic characteristics to navigate social contexts and protect themselves from stigma and negative judgment, despite feeling uncomfortable and exhausted. While some emerging research also showed the relatively positive aspects of camouflaging, such as gaining social opportunities and developing resilience (e.g., Bradley et al., 2021; Cook et al., 2021; Loo et al., 2024), participants in our study seemed to narrate their motivations for conforming to social norms with more prosocial values than they desired. For example, some decided to compensate by learning to behave and perform for better social integration. Some preferred altering their social presentation as a considerate act to avoid being an annoyance or a burden to others. This also served as a self-reliant attempt to impress others and gain social capital that they desire. Their motivation to integrate into mainstream or neurotypical society reflects a culturally specific concept that highlights a facet of Chinese culture that values conformity and group harmony over individuality (Hamamura et al., 2021). In other words, while fitting in is recognized as a core component of camouflaging (i.e. assimilation; Hull et al., 2019), it appears to be a culturally expected, and perhaps even sanctioned, way of life. Consequently, whether this process feels acceptable or forced for Chinese autistic individuals and how it may lead to potentially varying mental health outcomes, require further investigation.

One cross-cutting theme among our participants is the struggle between desiring to be authentic while using masking to integrate into society. Specifically, some participants reconciled that they “chose” to camouflage to achieve their goals while managing to be “self-compassionate,” which appears to be a volitional decision balancing practicality, adaptation, and authenticity for one’s long-term development, like playing a “good impression game.” While the camouflaging literature has documented forceful suppression of autistic characteristics and its negative consequences in mental health, these observations are drawn from exclusively Western samples (e.g., Alaghband-Rad et al., 2023; Bradley et al., 2021; Hull et al., 2019). One exception is a qualitative study conducted in Singapore with all but one participant of Chinese ethnicity (Loo et al., 2024). Autistic adults in their study reported developing more flexible camouflaging strategies across time that increased a sense of success and ease of camouflaging. Camouflaging also eventually became integrated with their self-identity, moving away from a forceful and tiring effort to a more natural way to manage and maintain their true self in social environments. Similar observations of impression management have also been documented in Israeli autistic adults (Schneid & Raz, 2020).

The idea of impression management describes a universal human experience not only unique to autistic individuals but also found in neurotypical individuals, where people try to strategically adjust their presentations to adapt to social expectations and influence how others perceive them (Leary & Kowalski, 1990). Understanding camouflaging as an adaptive strategy among a broader array of impression management strategies enables us to develop a nuanced conceptualization of autistic camouflaging that goes beyond its harmful impact to seeing its values and potential benefits to an autistic person. The extant literature on autistic camouflaging indeed echoes this perspective: While accumulated observations from qualitative studies reported overwhelmingly negative consequences of camouflaging, quantitative research failed to show a clear linkage between camouflaging and negative mental health (Khudiakova et al., 2025a). One of the key determinants of such a relationship may lie in whether camouflaging is voluntary or forced and whether it feels authentic or inauthentic in an autistic person (Ai et al., 2022; Khudiakova et al., 2025a). Indeed, some of our participants share this perspective, as they showed a more balanced way of life between camouflaging and authenticity, which directly contrasts a common assumption that camouflaging is an inauthentic expression of oneself (Ai et al., 2022).

Our participants described their experiences of negotiating the tasks of camouflaging while preserving their authentic selves, which we postulate were influenced by various intrapersonal and sociocultural factors. Social psychology has demonstrated that inner authenticity is a universal experience and essential across cultures, but it can be expressed differently (Sedikides & Schlegel, 2024). Similar to how the transactional impression management model conceptualizes assimilation within the construct of camouflaging as cultural assimilation in the immigration context (Khudiakova et al., 2025a), if autism can be considered a cultural identity (Davies et al., 2024; Farahar, 2022), autistic people may mask (and unmask) like bicultural people do when entering a new culture. Social psychology experiments have shown that biculturals’ authenticity depends on the psychological integration of their two cultural identities when they follow the dominant cultural norms (Mok, 2022). In situations emphasizing conformity to social norms, people with more compatible identification with the two cultures are more likely to feel authentic (i.e. true to themselves), while those with more conflictual bicultural integration are more likely to feel inauthentic because they fear losing or excluding one of their identities. In our study, some autistic participants (ref. Subtheme B3) expressed a genuine desire to learn about and integrate into the neurotypical culture. These autistic individuals can be considered to have high bicultural integration (i.e. reconciling their autistic identity and the neurotypical culture) and are more likely to feel authentic even if they need to conform to the dominant norms. In such a context, camouflaging can be an authentic way for them to adapt to the neurotypical world without losing their autistic identity. Such an adaptive response to the immediate cultural context is termed “frame switching,” which helps people navigate relationships among different cultural groups (Hong & Khei, 2014). Conceptualizing autistic camouflaging as frame switching suggests its linkage with one’s unique self-identities, which may explain the differential outcomes in autistic people’s immediate and long-term well-being. This hypothesis needs to be examined in future research.

We also observed participants’ use of “adaptive masking” to temporarily unmask and adjust the amount of masking to their daily needs. Unmasking seemed to be an example of coping capacity against masking’s consequences, while also helping autistic people develop authentic communication and relationships with others. Despite its potential advantages, this phenomenon remains understudied, which could be due to cultural factors. Cross-cultural studies have shown that individuals in collectivistic cultures (e.g., Chinese) tend to adopt a more flexible personality, enabling them to adapt to different roles and situations, while individuals in individualistic cultures (e.g., American) value authenticity as a stable, consistent personality regardless of context (English & Chen, 2011; Kashima et al., 2004). While the collectivistic culture in Hong Kong may make people more adept at shifting their self-expression to different contexts (Mok, 2022), such frame switching may be considered undermining one’s authenticity in Western cultures (West et al., 2018). One hypothesis is that autistic individuals from Western cultures may perceive masking as threatening their authenticity and feel truly authentic only during unmasking (e.g., Bernardin et al., 2021; Bradley et al., 2021; Cook et al., 2021; Evans et al., 2024; Khudiakova et al., 2025b; Lawson, 2020; Sutherland et al., 2025); in contrast, those from Eastern cultures may feel more at ease and authentic with switching the frames between masking and unmasking.

To investigate the abovementioned culturally specific and cross-cultural hypotheses on camouflaging, more research needs to be conducted. Future research can continue to use in-depth qualitative methodologies to explore the experiences of masking and unmasking across different social contexts in autistic people in Chinese or other collectivistic cultures. It is likely that the norm-conforming culture plays an integral role in shaping autistic camouflaging experiences in collectivist societies. Autistic individuals’ views are integral to understanding whether camouflaging is perceived as an adaptive outcome felt authentic to themselves, a compromise that offers survival values, or a reflection of internalized stigma or ableism (or a combination of the above). Experimental studies with cross-cultural samples can also directly compare camouflaging, bicultural identification, authenticity, and mental health outcomes between Chinese and Western autistic individuals, for example, to reveal any cross-cultural differences. Such research can contribute to the cross-cultural and transactional conceptualization of camouflaging in autism, which can broaden our understanding of this phenomenon currently dominated by Western literature (Pearson & Rose, 2021).

Although autistic university students reported that formal engagement with professionals (e.g., individual counseling or coaching, group-based skills-building interventions, and informational talks) is crucial in providing consistent and readily available support, they expressed the need for going beyond a one-size-fits-all solution to interventions and advocated for more diverse, personalized supports to meet their heterogeneous needs and preferences. On one hand, some participants shared the perspectives of the broader autistic community who disdain interventions that emphasize normalizing or passing of autistic traits (e.g., traditional manualized social skills training; Anderson, 2023; Bottema-Beutel et al., 2018; Hull et al., 2025; Leaf et al., 2021; Ne’eman et al., 2023). However, the current university support systems in Hong Kong often rely on piecemeal programs or reactive services that target more individual-student level instead of addressing systemic barriers and providing ecologically relevant services (Chan & Yeung, 2020; Lam & Wong, 2026), which echoes the critiques toward university support systems in the global literature (Nelson et al., 2023; Widman & Lopez-Reyna, 2020). For example, autistic university students in our study valued learning practical skills to navigate the social world and solve real-world problems, but they also preferred more naturalistic social development that builds on autistic students’ existing networks and social needs to motivate them to learn. They also desired social support that should be seamlessly integrated into their everyday contexts (e.g., university social activities with both autistic and nonautistic peers), so they can participate without feeling singled out for “fixing.” Peer programming, which can help autistic students and their peers support, learn, and connect with each other, is also popular in university, but how different mentors (e.g., neurotypical vs neurodivergent) should be matched with or can differentially benefit autistic students (Duerksen et al., 2021; Kim & Crowley, 2021), and how the mentoring relationships and dynamics should be fostered (Coyle et al., 2025; Lam et al., 2023) seem to vary across studies, which should be researched further in Hong Kong or other Chinese contexts. Similarly, autistic individuals were also found to show higher satisfaction and reduced masking when socializing with autistic peers (Crompton et al., 2020; Gillespie-Smith et al., 2024), so how that could support the social interaction experiences in university should be explored.

Based on what we learned about autistic students’ social experiences and critiques toward existing support, services and interventions should be designed and delivered in a more participatory and person-centered manner (Hull et al., 2025), while neurodiversity-informed approaches must be part of and complement a comprehensive support system in university and in the community. These recommendations advocate institution-wide efforts to shape an inclusive and neurodiversity-affirming environment to improve autistic students’ social and academic lives, which necessitate the active participation of autistic students and the leadership of university faculty and staff as well as their enhanced acceptance of autism and neurodiversity. Although Hong Kong and Chinese societies in general are not well informed of the neurodiversity paradigm (Kwok & Kwok, 2020; Lam & Wong, 2024), it is encouraging to hear emerging voices from our participants who recognized and advocated neurodiversity-informed practices, which suggests that neurodiversity-affirming principles can be applied across cultures. Interestingly, the metaphor of Chinese medicine is an innovative example that speaks to the conceptual parallel between traditional Chinese values and practices and the contemporary neurodiversity framework. Specifically, according to the Traditional Chinese Medicine model, every individual has a distinct type of “body constitution” (Sapphire), a classification that distinguishes a constellation of human differences such as body composition, functioning characteristics, psychological and emotional traits, and homeostasis of yin and yang, which have implications for the person’s interaction with the external environment, including one’s susceptibility or resistance to disease development and responsiveness to health management strategies (Sun et al., 2018). As such, matching one’s body constitution to nuanced considerations of lifestyle, care, and medicine, in our case the specific nature of supports by social others and tailored intervention services, is integral to supporting autistic individuals’ holistic well-being. Such thinking seems to resonate with the latest development of neurodiversity-affirming care that seeks to support autistic individuals to live their best autistic life that acknowledges variations in human functioning and well-being, emphasizes individualized care, prioritize coregulation with the environment to maximize person-environment fit (e.g., Bolourian et al., 2025; Flower et al., 2025; Wagland et al., 2025). Whether the Chinese Medicine model can benefit the development and reception of neurodiversity in Chinese societies, and how to balance or integrate different means of neurodiversity-informed support for Chinese autistic individuals, warrant further research.

We noted several limitations of this study. As IPA focuses on idiographic interpretation of participants’ lived experiences, our findings may not be transferable to other autistic postsecondary students. For example, participants self-selected into this study might be more outspoken about their lived experiences and reflective on their social interaction. Individuals with different intersectional experiences and identities (e.g., early-diagnosed vs. late-diagnosed autistics) may have different expectations of social interaction and help-seeking. Participants with cooccurring mental health conditions may also present more indicated social and support needs beyond their autism. Future research could consider a larger and more representative sample, which can help identify the common elements or factors contributing to autistic students’ social experiences. Moreover, although we had included autistic participants in various stages of research, no autistic coresearchers were formally involved in analysis or writing. Future studies should enhance meaningful partnership and participatory collaboration with the autistic community to incorporate their expertise throughout the research processes. Data triangulation using quantitative measurements and other media of expression can also yield richer findings.

Conclusion

This study was the first in Hong Kong to adopt an IPA approach to explore the lived experiences of the social interaction and related support of postsecondary autistic students. Through semistructured interviews with eight participants, we generated four themes related to their struggles with social connection and communication, relative concerns between masking and unmasking, the importance of formal and informal support from others, and their diverse needs and preferences for different types of social support and interventions. Moving forward, these findings call for further research into camouflaging and the double-empathy problem in autistic individuals in Chinese or other Asian societies. More diverse modalities of supports and interventions should be developed and evaluated that fit with the homogenous needs and preferences of local postsecondary autistic students.
