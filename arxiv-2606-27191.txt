On the parabolic Hausdorff dimension of orthogonal projections
Terence L. J. Harris, Pertti Mattila

Abstract
. For Borel sets A ⊂ ℝ n × ℝ A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R} we prove lower bounds for the parabolic Hausdorff dimension of the orthogonal projections of A A on generic m m -dimensional linear subspaces of ℝ n × ℝ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R} .



2000 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 28A75

1.
Introduction

Marstrand’s projection theorem
[
Mar54
]
tells us the following, see, for example,
[
Mat95
]
or
[
Mat15
]
:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1) If
\dim_{E}A\leq m
, then
\dim_{E}P_{V}(A)=\dim_{E}A
for almost all
V\in G(n,m)
.

(2) If
\dim_{E}A>m
, then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(n,m)
.

Here
G(n,m)
is the Grassmannian of linear
m
-dimensional subspaces of the Euclidean
n
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
P_{V}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to V
is the orthogonal projection,
\mathcal{L}^{m}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and on the
m
-planes
V\in G(n,m)
, and
\dim_{E}
denotes the Hausdorff dimension with respect to the Euclidean metric.

In this paper we shall investigate analogous results for the Hausdorff dimension
\dim
with respect to the parabolic metric:
let
\|\cdot\|
be the parabolic ‘norm’,

\|(x,t)\|=\sqrt{|x|^{2}+|t|},

in
\mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
and
d
the corresponding metric
d(p,q)=\|p-q\|
. Here, and later,
|x|
is the Euclidean norm of
x
. We have

\dim\mathbb{P}^{n}=n+2
and
\dim V=m+1
for every
V\in G(n+1,m)
, which is not contained in the horizontal plane
\{(x,0):x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
.

For
n=2
we shall prove the complete result:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
m=1
or
2
and let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a Borel set.

(1)

If
\dim A\leq m+1
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(3,m)
.

(2)

If
\dim A>m+1
, then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(3,m)
.

In general dimensions we have

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
1\leq m\leq n
and let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1)

If
\dim A\leq 2
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

(2)

If
\dim_{E}A\leq 1
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)=2\dim_{E}P_{V}(A)=2\dim_{E}A\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

(3)

If
\dim A>m+1
, then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

The cases
\dim A>m+1
or
m=1
in both theorems are quite easy, based on Theorem
1.1
and the comparison of the Euclidean and parabolic Hausdorff dimensions as given in Section
3
. The proof of (1) for
m=2,2<\dim A\leq 3,
in Theorem
1.2
uses the Fourier transform. The other cases are done by geometric arguments.

The condition
\dim A>m+1
is clearly sharp. We believe
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
should hold always when
\dim A\leq m+1
.

Parabolic Hausdorff measures have been used for a long time in connection of parabolic PDE’s. Recently parabolic analogs of the quantitative David-Semmes rectifiability,
[
DS93
]
, and its connections to harmonic analysis, PDE’s and other topics has been under active investigation, see
[
BHHLN25
]
and
[
HJ25
]
and the references given there. Analogs of the classical qualitative Besicovitch-Federer rectifiability,
[
Fe69
]
, were developed in
[
Mat22
]
and
[
MMP22
]
.

Much work has been done for projections and Hausdorff and other fractal dimensions, see Falconer’s recent survey
[
Fa26
]
. Closest to our parabolic case are projection theorems in Heisenberg groups. They were initiated in
[
BDFMT13
]
and
[
BFMT12
]
and continued in
[
FH16
]
,
[
FO23
]
,
[
Ha23
]
,
[
Ha25
]
and
[
Ha24
]
.

2.
Preliminaries

The metric
d
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}

is defined as in the Introduction.
For
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
,
d(A)
stands for the parabolic diameter of
A
.

We shall denote by
f_{\sharp}\mu
the push-forward of a measure
\mu
under a map
f:f_{\sharp}\mu(A)=\mu(f^{-1}(A))
.
For
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
let
\mathcal{M}(A)
be the set of Borel measures
\mu
with compact
\spt\mu\subset A
and with
0<\mu(A)<\infty
.

By the notation
a\lesssim b
we mean that
a\leq Cb
for some constant
C
, by
a\gtrsim b
the converse inequality, and by
a\sim b
that both
a\lesssim b
and
a\gtrsim b
hold. The dependence of
C
should be clear from the context, but sometimes we may specify it writing, for example,
\lesssim_{c}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{n}
the Lebesgue measure in the Euclidean
n
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n},n\geq 1,
and also in
n
-dimensional linear subspaces of higher dimensional Euclidean spaces.
\sigma^{n}
will stand for the surface measure on the unit sphere
S^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
normalized to
\sigma^{n}(S^{n})=1
.

Let
\dim
stand for the parabolic Hausdorff dimension:

\dim A=\inf\{s>0:\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists B_{i}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}\ \text{with}\ A\subset\cup_{i=1}^{\infty}B_{i}\ \text{and}\ \sum_{i=1}^{\infty}d(B_{i})^{s}<\varepsilon\}.

As usual, this can also be defined in terms of Hausdorff measures, but we don’t need them in this paper.
Then,
\dim\mathbb{P}^{n}=n+2
. The Euclidean diameter and Hausdorff dimension will be denoted by
d_{E}
and
\dim_{E}
.

We shall use the Frostman and energy characterizations of Hausdorff dimension.
For
0<s<n+2
let
k_{s}
be the kernel

k_{s}(x,t)=\|(x,t)\|^{-s}=(|x|^{2}+|t|)^{-s/2},\ (x,t)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}.

Define the growth exponent and the
s
-energy of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
by

c_{s}(\mu)=\sup_{p\in\mathbb{P}^{n},r>0}\frac{\mu(B(p,r))}{r^{s}},

and

I_{s}(\mu)=\iint k_{s}(p-q)\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q)

Then for Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
,

(2.1)

\displaystyle\dim A=\sup\{s>0:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\ \text{such that}\ c_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\sup\{s>0:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\ \text{such that}\ I_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\sup\{s>0:\exists\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)\ \text{such that}\ c_{s}(\mu)<\infty\ \text{and}\ I_{s}(\mu)<\infty\}.

For the Euclidean metric (
2.1
) follows from, for example,
[
Mat15
, Theorem 2.7]
. The proof also works for the parabolic metric. Or one could use the general Howroyd’s Frostman lemma, see
[
Ho95
]
or
[
Mat95
, Theorem 8.17]
. Then (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) follow from
[
Mat15
, Theorem 8.12]
and its proof.

The Euclidean
s
-energy of
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
is

I_{E,s}(\mu)=\iint|x-y|^{-s}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

Let
G(n+1,m)
denote the Grassmannian manifold of the
m
-dimensional (meaning linear dimension) linear subspaces of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
equipped with the unique rotationally invariant Borel probability measure
\gamma_{n+1,m}
. Let

H_{n}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}:t=0\}

be the horizontal
n
-plane and

G(H_{n},m)=\{V\in G(n+1,m):V\subset H_{n}\}

be the set of horizontal
m
-planes. Then, as is easily checked, for
V\in G(n+1,m)
,
\dim V=m
, if
V\in G(H_{n},m)
, and
\dim V=m+1
otherwise. Identifying
H_{n}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we set
\gamma_{H_{n},m}=\gamma_{n,m}
. The uniqueness of
\gamma_{n,m}
implies

(2.4)

\gamma_{n,m}(G)=\gamma_{n,n-m}(\{V\in G(n,n-m):V^{\perp}\in G\}),\ G\subset G(n,m).

The orthogonal projection onto
V\in G(n+1,m)
is denoted by
P_{V}
. Note that the projections onto horizontal and vertical (those containing the
t
-axis) planes are Lipschitz and they do not increase the Hausdorff dimension. But this is false for all other planes
V\in G(n+1,m)
;
P_{V}
is only Hölder continuous with exponent
1/2
.

We have for
\delta>0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
see Lemma 3.11 and Corollary 3.12 in
[
Mat95
]
,

(2.5)

\gamma_{n,m}(\{V\in G(n,m):|P_{V}(x)|<\delta\})\lesssim\delta^{m}|x|^{-m},

and for
0<s<m
,

(2.6)

\int|P_{V}(x)|^{-s}\,d\gamma_{n,m}V\lesssim|x|^{-s}.

For
m=1
, (
2.5
) is the same as

(2.7)

\sigma^{n-1}(\{e\in S^{n-1}:|\langle e,x\rangle|<\delta\})\lesssim\delta|x|^{-1}.

The following two simple lemmas will be used in Section
5
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n-1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(1)

If
0<t<|x|/2
, then

\int(|P_{V}(x)|+t)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)\lesssim|x|^{-1}\log(|x|/t).

(2)

If
k\geq 2
, then

\int|P_{V}(x)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)\lesssim|x|^{-1}.

Proof.

To prove (1), set
v=|x|^{-1}x
and
a=t/|x|<1/2
. Then

\displaystyle\int(|P_{V}(x)|+t)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)=|x|^{-1}\int(|P_{V}(v)|+a)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V).

We estimate this by dividing the integration into dyadic pieces. Let
N
be the smallest integer
j
for which
2^{j}a>1
, so
N\sim|\log a|=\log(|x|/t)
. Then, by (
2.5
),

\displaystyle\int(|P_{V}(v)|+a)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)

\displaystyle\sim a^{-1}\gamma_{n,k}(\{V:|P_{V}(v)|<a\})+\sum_{j=1}^{N}(2^{j}a)^{-1}\gamma_{n,k}(\{V:2^{j-1}a\leq|P_{V}(v)|<2^{j}a\})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{N}(2^{j}a)^{-1}(2^{j}a)^{k}\lesssim N\sim\log(|x|/t).

For (2), we get in the same manner

\int|P_{V}(x)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)=|x|^{-1}\int|P_{V}(v)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V),

and

\displaystyle\int|P_{V}(v)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,k}(V)\sim\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{j}\gamma_{n,k}(\{V:2^{-j}<|P_{V}(v)|\leq 2^{1-j}\})

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{j}2^{-kj}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{(1-k)j}\sim 1.

∎

The next lemma holds, and is easy to prove, for all Borel sets
B\subset S^{k}
. But since we don’t need it, we only state the following trivial form.

Write
e=(e_{1},e_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}
when
e\in S^{k}
. For
0<c<1
let

S^{k}_{c}=\{e\in S^{k}:|e_{1}|>c,|e_{2}|>c\},

C_{v}=\{e\in S^{k}_{c}:e_{1}/|e_{1}|=v\},\ v\in S^{k-1}.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any Borel set
B\subset S^{k}_{c}
,

\sigma^{k}(B)\sim\int\sigma^{1}(B\cap C_{v})\,d\sigma^{k-1}(v).

Proof.

The projection
\pi,\pi(e)=e_{1}
, is bilipschitz on the upper and lower halves of
S_{c}^{k}
and
C_{v}
for every
v\in S^{k-1}
. Hence
\sigma^{k}(B)\sim\mathcal{L}^{k}(\pi(B))
and

\int\sigma^{1}(B\cap C_{v})\,d\sigma^{k-1}(v)\sim\int\mathcal{L}^{1}(\pi(B\cap C_{v}))\,d\sigma^{k-1}(v).

Here
\int\mathcal{L}^{1}(\pi(B\cap C_{v}))\,d\sigma^{k-1}(v)\sim\mathcal{L}^{k}(\pi(B))
by integration in polar coordinates, because
\pi(B)\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}:|x|>c\}
. The lemma follows.
∎

3.
Comparison of Euclidean and parabolic Hausdorff dimensions

Immediately from the definition of the parabolic metric we get for any
p\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
|p|<1
and any
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
d(A)<1
,

(3.1)

|p|\leq\|p\|\leq 2\sqrt{|p|},\ d_{E}(A)\leq d(A)\ \leq 2\sqrt{d_{E}(A)}.

We have the following rather easy comparison inequalities:

Let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
d(A)<1
. Then,

(3.2)

\dim_{E}A\leq\dim A\leq 2\dim_{E}A,

(3.3)

\dim_{E}A\leq\dim A\leq\dim_{E}A+1,

(3.4)

2\dim_{E}A-n\leq\dim A\leq\dim_{E}A+1.

(
3.2
) is the best of these in the range
\dim_{E}A\leq 1
, (
3.3
) in the range
1\leq\dim_{E}A\leq n
, and (
3.4
) in the range
n\leq\dim_{E}A\leq n+1
.

Proof.

The first item and the left inequality of the second item follow immediately from (
3.1
). The right hand inequalities in (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) follow since any Euclidean ball with radius
r<1
can be covered with roughly
1/r
parabolic balls with radius
r
. Finally, the left hand inequality in (
3.4
) follows since any parabolic ball with radius
r
can be covered with roughly
r^{-n}
Euclidean balls with radius
r^{2}
.
∎

It is easy to find examples showing that these bounds are sharp.

If
V\in G(n+1,m)\setminus G(H_{n},m)
, then the orthogonal projection from
V
onto the vertical plane
\{(x,t):x\in V\cap H_{n},t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is both Euclidean and parabolic bi-Lipschitz. Hence the inequalities (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), with
n
replaced by
m-1
, are valid for subsets of
V
.

Similar, but much more difficult, comparison results were proved by Balogh, Rickly and Serra Cassano in the first Heisenberg group,
[
BRS03
]
, and by Balogh, Tyson and Warhurst in general Carnot groups,
[
BTW09
]
.

4.
Projections onto homogeneous linear subspaces

In this section we discuss the easy case of projections onto homogeneous subspaces. They are the
m
-planes
V\in G(n+1,m)
which are invariant under the dilations
(x,t)\mapsto(rx,r^{2}t),(x,t)\in\mathbb{P}^{n},r>0
. These are exactly the horizontal planes
V\subset H_{n}
and their orthogonal complements. The latter are the vertical planes; those containing the
t
-axis
\{(0,t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

The projections onto homogeneous linear subspaces are Lipschitz maps and do not increase the parabolic Hausdorff dimension. The vertical
m
-planes planes are given by
V_{W}=W+T,W\in G(H_{n},m-1),T=\{(0,t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
\dim V_{W}=m+1
. Let
e^{v}=(0,1)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}.
For
p=(x,t)
the projection is given by

P_{V_{W}}:\mathbb{P}^{n}\to V_{W},P_{V_{W}}(p)=P_{W}(p)+\langle e^{v},p\rangle e^{v}=(P_{W}(x),t).

Let

\pi:\mathbb{P}^{n}\to H_{n},\pi(x,t)=x,

be the projection onto the horizontal plane.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. For any homogeneous
U\in G(n+1,k)
,
A\subset P_{U}(A)+U^{\perp}
. Using this, it follows easily from the definition of the parabolic Hausdorff dimension that
\dim P_{U}(A)\geq\dim A-\dim U^{\perp}.
This gives for all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
,

\dim P_{W}(A)\geq\dim A+m-n-2,

\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)\geq\dim A+m-n,

\dim\pi(A)\geq\dim A-2.

All these inequalities are sharp, but we can improve them for almost all
W
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1) If
\dim A\leq m+2
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A-2
for almost all
V\in G(H_{n},m)
.

(2) If
\dim A>m+2
, then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(H_{n},m)
.

Proof.

Clearly,
P_{V}(A)=P_{V}(\pi(A))
for
V\in G(H_{n},m)
. Since
\dim\pi(A)\geq\dim A-2,
the result follows applying the Euclidean projection theorem
1.1
to
\pi(A)
.
∎

Examples of the form
A=B\times\mathbb{R}
show that
\dim A-2
is sharp.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1)

If
\dim A\leq m
, then
\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
.

(2)

If
m\leq\dim A\leq n
, then
\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)\geq m
for almost all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
.

(3)

If
\dim A\geq n
, then
\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)\geq\dim A+m-n
for all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
.

Proof.

To prove (1), let
0<s<\dim A\leq m
. By (
2.2
) there is
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)
with
I_{s}(\mu)<\infty
.
For any
(x,t)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\int\|P_{V_{W}}(x,t)\|^{-s}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W)=\int(|P_{W}(x)|^{2}+|t|)^{-s/2}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W)

\displaystyle\leq\int\min\{(|P_{W}(x)|^{-s},|t|^{-s/2}\}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W)\leq\min\{\int|P_{W}(x)|^{-s}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W),|t|^{-s/2}\}

\displaystyle\lesssim\min\{|x|^{-s},|t|^{-s/2}\}\sim\|(x,t)\|^{-s},

where we used (
2.6
) to get the second to last estimate. This implies

\displaystyle\int I_{s}(P_{{V_{W}}\sharp}\mu)\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W)=\iiint\|P_{V_{W}}(q-p)\|^{-s}\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q)\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m}(W)

\displaystyle\lesssim\iint\|q-p\|^{-s}\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q)\ =I_{s}(\mu)<\infty,

from which (1) follows by (
2.2
).

Item (2) is trivial by (1) and item (3) is the uniform estimate already mentioned above.
∎

The above estimates are sharp:

(1) and (2): For any
A\subset H_{n}
,
\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)\leq\min\{\dim A,m\}
for all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
, so (1) and (2) are sharp.

(3): Take
A=H_{n}\times B,B\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then
\dim A=n+\dim B,P_{V_{W}}(A)=W\times B
and
\dim P_{V_{W}}(A)=m+\dim B=\dim A+m-n
, whence (3) is sharp. Taking
B
with
\dim B=2
and
\mathcal{L}^{1}(B)=0
, we get
A
with
\dim A=n+2
, but
\mathcal{L}^{1}(P_{V_{W}}(A))=0
for all
W\in G(H_{n},m)
.

In Heisenberg groups, and more general homogeneous groups, projections defined via the group structure onto homogeneous subgroups are more natural than the orthogonal projections onto general linear subspaces. Consequently, they have recently been studied by several authors, see
[
BDFMT13
]
,
[
BFMT12
]
,
[
FH16
]
,
[
FO23
]
,
[
Ha23
]
,
[
Ha25
]
and
[
Ha24
]
. In Heisenberg groups these projections are much more complicated than in the above very easy parabolic case. So the main interest of the questions studied in this paper is not about homogeneous groups but about geometric measure theory in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with a non-Euclidean metric, in particular, behavior of parabolic Hausdorff dimension under general orthogonal projections.

5.
Projections onto general linear subspaces

Next we consider projections onto general lines and planes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
Recall that for
V\in G(n+1,m)
,
\dim=m
, if
V\subset H_{n}
, and
\dim V=m+1
, otherwise. For non-horizontal lines
L
we also have
\dim B=2\dim_{E}B
for any
B\subset L
.
Here is first the easy case
m=1
of Theorem
1.3
:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1)

If
\dim A\leq 2
, then
\dim P_{L}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
L\in G(n+1,1)
.

(2)

If
\dim A>2
, then
\mathcal{L}^{1}(P_{L}(A))>0
for almost all
L\in G(n+1,1)
.

Proof.

By the Euclidean projection theorem
1.1
and the dimension comparison (
3.2
),
we have for almost all lines
L
,

\dim P_{L}(A)=2\dim_{E}P_{L}(A)=\min\{2\dim_{E}A,2\}\geq\dim A,

which gives (1).

If
\dim A>2
, then
\dim_{E}A\geq\dim A/2>1
and again (2) follows by the Euclidean projection theorem.
∎

The bounds in Theorem
5.1
are sharp; consider subsets
A
of lines.

Let
m>1
. Almost every
V\in G(n+1,m)
intersects the horizontal plane
H_{n}
in an
(m-1)
-plane
W\in G(H_{n},m-1)
. So, it is uniquely (up to identifying
e
and
-e
) given as the plane spanned by
W
and
L_{e}=\{ue:u\in\mathbb{R}\},e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}
. Here and later the orthogonal complement is taken in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, whence
W^{\perp}
is an
(n-m+2)
-dimensional vertical plane. We then denote
V=(W,e)
and
P_{V}=P_{W,e}
. Let

G=\{(W,e):W\in G(H_{n},m-1),e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}\}\subset G(n+1,m),

and let
\lambda_{n+1,m}
be the Borel probability measure on
G
, defined by

(5.1)

\lambda_{n+1,m}(E)=\int\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:(W,e)\in E\})\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

for Borel sets
E\subset G
.

For
0<c<1
, writing
e=(e_{1},e_{2})\in(H_{n}\times\mathbb{R})\cap S^{n}
, let

G_{c}=\{(W,e)\in G:|e_{1}|>c,|e_{2}|>c\}.

Then

(5.2)

\gamma_{n+1,m}(G(n+1,m)\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}G_{1/i})=0.

The measures
\lambda_{n+1,m}
and
\gamma_{n+1,m}
are mutually absolutely continuous:

Lemma 5.2
.

For Borel sets
E\subset G
,
\lambda_{n+1,m}(E)=0
if and only is
\gamma_{n+1,m}(E)=0.

Proof.

Let
d_{n+1,m}
be the standard metric on
G(n+1,m)
given by

d_{n+1,m}(V,V^{\prime})=\sup\{d_{E}(x,V^{\prime}):x\in V\cap S^{n}\}.

Then
d_{n+1,m}
is easily seen to be locally equivalent with the metric

d^{\prime}((W,e),(W^{\prime},e^{\prime}))=d_{n.m-1}(W,W^{\prime})+d_{n+1,1}(L_{e},L_{e^{\prime}})

on the open sets
G_{c},0<c<1
. The dimension of
G(n+1,m)
is
m(n+1-m)
and
\gamma_{n+1,m}(B(V,r))\sim r^{m(n+1-m)}
for
V\in G(n+1,m),0<r<1
, see, for example,
[
Fe69
, 3.2.28(2)]
. Here balls are defined with the metric
d_{n+1,m}
. For the corresponding
d^{\prime}
balls the definition of
\lambda_{n+1,m}
gives
\lambda_{n+1,m}(B^{\prime}((W,e),r))\sim r^{m(n+1-m)}
for
(W,e)\in G_{c}
and for small
r>0
. This yields the lemma.
∎

Here is Theorem
1.3
for
m>1
:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
2\leq m\leq n
and let
A\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a Borel set.

(1)

If
\dim A\leq 2
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

(2)

If
\dim_{E}A\leq 1
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)=2\dim_{E}P_{V}(A)=2\dim_{E}A\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

(3)

If
\dim A>m+1
, then
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.

Proof.

We may assume that
d(A)<1
.
To prove (1) and (2), we use the parametrization of the planes with
(W,e)\in G
, that is,
W\in G(H_{n},m-1),e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}
, as above. The projection
P_{W,e}
onto the plane spanned by
(W,e)
is given by

P_{W,e}(p)=P_{W}(p)+\langle e,p\rangle e,\ p\in\mathbb{P}^{n}.

By (
5.2
), given
0<c<1
, it suffices to consider
P_{W,e}
with
(W,e)\in G_{c}
.
Writing again
e=(e_{1},e_{2}),e_{1}\in H_{n},e_{2}\in\mathbb{R},
we have

P_{W,e}(x,t)=(P_{W}(x)+\langle e,(x,t)\rangle e_{1},\langle e,(x,t)\rangle e_{2})\in H_{n}\times\mathbb{R}.

Therefore,

\|P_{W,e}(x,t)\|^{2}=|P_{W}(x)+\langle e,(x,t)\rangle e_{1}|^{2}+|\langle e,(x,t)\rangle e_{2}|.

Let
0<\delta<c/2
. Then, if
\|P_{W,e}(x,t)\|<\delta
, we get
|P_{W}(x)+\langle e,(x,t)\rangle e_{1}|<\delta
and, since
(W,e)\in G_{c}
,
|\langle e,(x,t)\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c<\delta/2
, which implies
|P_{W}(p)|=|P_{W}(x)|<2\delta
. Thus

(5.3)

\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\}\subset\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:|P_{W}(p)|<2\delta,|\langle e,p\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c\}.

Let
p=(x,t)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
with
|p|<1
and
1<\alpha<2
. We shall show that

(5.4)

\lambda_{n+1,m}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}|p|^{-1}.

The implicit constants are allowed to depend on
n,m,c
and
\alpha
. By (
3.1
) this gives

(5.5)

\lambda_{n+1,m}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}\|p\|^{-2}.

Suppose first that
|p|<\delta^{2}.
Then
\delta^{\alpha}|p|^{-1}>1
and (
5.4
) holds.

Next suppose that
|p|>2\delta.
Then
|P_{W}(p)|^{2}+|P_{W^{\perp}}(p)|^{2}=|p|^{2}
and
|P_{W}(p)|<\delta
imply
|P_{W^{\perp}}(p)|>|p|/2
and we obtain by (
2.7
) and (
3.1
),

\displaystyle\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:|\langle e,p\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c\})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:|\langle e,P_{W^{\perp}}(p)\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c\})

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}|P_{W^{\perp}}(p)|^{-1}\sim\delta^{2}|p|^{-1},

which gives (
5.4
) by (
5.3
) and (
5.1
).

Next, assume that
\delta^{2}\leq|p|\leq 2\delta,
and suppose first that, in addition,
|t|\geq\delta^{3}.
We have
P_{W^{\perp}}(x,t)=(P_{W^{\perp}}(x),t)\in H_{n}\times\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,m}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\{W:|P_{W}(p)|<2\delta\}}\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:|\langle e,p\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c\})\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

\displaystyle=\int_{\{W:|P_{W}(p)|<2\delta\}}\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{2}:|\langle e,P_{W^{\perp}}(p)\rangle|<\delta^{2}/c\})\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\{W:|P_{W}(p)|<2\delta\}}\delta^{2}|P_{W^{\perp}}(p)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}\int(|P_{W^{\perp}}(x)|+|t|)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

\displaystyle=\delta^{2}\int_{G(n,n-m+1)}(|P_{V}(x)|+|t|)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,n-m+1}(V),

where
x\in H_{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n},t\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
|x|\leq 2|t|
,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\int_{G(n,n-m+1)}(|P_{V}(x)|+|t|)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,n-m+1}(V)\sim\delta^{2}|t|^{-1}\sim\delta^{2}|p|^{-1},

and (
5.4
) holds. Suppose
2|t|<|x|
.
Then by Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\int_{G(n,n-m+1)}(|P_{V}(x)|+|t|)^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,n-m+1}(V)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}|x|^{-1}\log(|x|/|t|)\lesssim\delta^{2}|\log\delta||x|^{-1}\lesssim\delta^{\alpha}|p|^{-1}.

Therefore, (
5.4
) follows also in this case.

Finally, assume that
\delta^{2}\leq|p|\leq 2\delta
and
|t|<\delta^{3}.
We first consider the case
m=n
. We have again that if
(W,e)\in G_{c}
and
\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta
, then
|(\langle e_{1},x\rangle+e_{2}t)e_{2}|<\delta^{2}
,
so
|\langle e_{1},x\rangle|\leq 2\delta^{2}/c
. For the unit vector
v=e_{1}/|e_{1}|\in H_{n}
orthogonal to
W
we have
|\langle v,x\rangle|=|\langle e_{1},x\rangle|/|e_{1}|\leq 2\delta^{2}/c^{2}
.

For any
W\in G(H_{n},n-1)
let
v_{W}\in H_{n}\cap S^{n}
be the (two-valued) unit vector orthogonal to
W
. By the uniqueness of
\gamma_{H_{n},n-1}
,

\gamma_{H_{n},n-1}(F)=\sigma^{n-1}(\{v_{W}:W\in F\}),\ F\subset G(H_{n},n-1).

Thus

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,n}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})

\displaystyle=\int\sigma^{1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})\,d\gamma_{H_{n},n-1}(W)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{H_{n},n-1}(\{W\in G(H_{n},n-1):|\langle v_{W},x\rangle|<2\delta^{2}/c^{2}\})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{n-1}(\{v\in H_{n}\cap S^{n}:|\langle v,x\rangle|<2\delta^{2}/c^{2}\})

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}|x|^{-1}\sim\delta^{2}|p|^{-1},

and we again have (
5.4
).

We shall now finish the proof of (
5.4
) in the remaining case
2\leq m\leq n-1
and
\delta^{2}\leq|p|\leq 2\delta,|t|<\delta^{3}
. For
W\in G(H_{n},m-1)
we apply Lemma
2.2
in
W^{\perp}\in G(n+1,n-m+2)
with
k=n-m+1
and
B=B_{W}=\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:(W,e)\in G_{c},\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\}
. As above, if
v\in W^{\perp}\cap H_{n}\cap S^{n}
,
C_{v}=\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:e_{1}/|e_{1}|=v\}
and
C_{v}\cap B_{W}\neq\emptyset
, so that
(W,e)\in G_{c}
and
\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta
for some
e\in C_{v}\cap B_{W}
, then
|\langle v,x\rangle|\leq 2\delta^{2}/c^{2}
. Hence, by Lemma
2.2
and (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:(W,e)\in G_{c},\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{n-m+1}(B_{W})\sim\int_{W^{\perp}\cap H_{n}\cap S^{n}}\sigma^{1}(C_{v}\cap B_{W})\,d\sigma^{n-m}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{n-m}(\{v\in W^{\perp}\cap H_{n}\cap S^{n}:|\langle v,x\rangle|\leq 2\delta^{2}/c^{2}\})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{n-m}(\{v\in W^{\perp}\cap H_{n}\cap S^{n}:|\langle v,P_{W^{\perp}(x)}\rangle|\leq 2\delta^{2}/c^{2}\})\lesssim\delta^{2}|P_{W^{\perp}(x)}|^{-1}.

Consequently, by (
2.4
) and Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n+1,n}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})

\displaystyle=\int\sigma^{n-m+1}(\{e\in W^{\perp}\cap S^{n}:(W,e)\in G_{c},\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<\delta\})\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}\int|P_{W^{\perp}(x)}|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{H_{n},m-1}(W)=\delta^{2}\int|P_{V}(x)|^{-1}\,d\gamma_{n,n-m+1}(V)

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{2}|x|^{-1}\sim\delta^{2}|p|^{-1},

and (
5.4
) is verified in all cases.

Let
0<s<\dim A\leq 2
and
s<\alpha<2
with
2s/\alpha<\dim A
. By (
2.2
) there is
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)
with
I_{2s/\alpha}(\mu)<\infty
. We now apply (
5.5
) with
\delta=r^{-1/s}<c/2
for
r>(c/2)^{-2}
:

\displaystyle\int_{G_{c}}\|P_{W,e}(p)\|^{-s}\,d\lambda_{n+1,m}(W,e)

\displaystyle\leq(c/2)^{-2}+\int_{(c/2)^{-2}}^{\infty}\lambda_{n+1,m}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|^{-s}>r\})\,dr

\displaystyle=(c/2)^{-2}+\int_{(c/2)^{-2}}^{\infty}\lambda_{n+1,m}(\{(W,e)\in G_{c}:\|P_{W,e}(p)\|<r^{-1/s}\})\,dr

\displaystyle\lesssim 1+\|p\|^{-2s/\alpha}+\int_{\|p\|^{-2s/\alpha}}^{\infty}r^{-\alpha/s}\|p\|^{-2}\,dr

\displaystyle\sim 1+\|p\|^{-2s/\alpha}+\|p\|^{-2s/\alpha}=1+2\|p\|^{-2s/\alpha}.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{G_{c}}I_{s}(P_{W,e\sharp}(\mu))\,d\lambda_{n+1,m}(W,e)

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{c}}\iint\|P_{W,e}(p-q)\|^{-s}\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q)\,d\lambda_{n+1,m}(W,e)

\displaystyle\lesssim\mu(A)^{2}+\iint\|p-q\|^{-2s/\alpha}\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q)=\mu(A)^{2}+I_{2s/\alpha}(\mu)<\infty.

It follows that for all
0<s<\dim A
,
\dim P_{W,e}(A)\geq s
for almost all
(W,e)\in G_{c}
, whence
\dim P_{W,e}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
(W,e)\in G_{c}
. Due to (
5.2
), this proves (1).

To prove (2), let
0<s<\dim_{E}A\leq 1
and
2s<\alpha<2
with
2s/\alpha<\dim_{E}A
. Choose
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(A)
with
I_{E,2s/\alpha}(\mu)<\infty
. Use then (
5.4
) and the same argument as above to obtain

\int_{G_{c}}I_{2s}(P_{W,e\sharp}(\mu))\,d\lambda_{n+1,m}(W,e)\lesssim\mu(A)^{2}+I_{E,2s/\alpha}(\mu)<\infty.

This yields
\dim P_{W,e}(A)\geq 2\dim_{E}A
for almost all
(W,e)\in G_{c}
. By the dimension comparison and the Euclidean projection theorem, we have
\dim P_{W,e}(A)\leq 2\dim_{E}P_{W,e}(A)=2\dim_{E}A
for almost all
(W,e)
. (2) follows from this.

(3) follows immediately from the corresponding Euclidean result Theorem
1.1
and (
3.3
): if
\dim A>m+1
, then
\dim_{E}A>m
, whence
\mathcal{L}^{m}(P_{V}(A))>0
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
.
∎

Remarks 5.4
.

Under the restrictions
\dim A\leq 2
and
\dim_{E}A\leq 1
, (1) and (2) of Theorem
1.3
are sharp. What would be the sharp inequalities in general? Probably for (1),
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n,m+1)
could hold always when
\dim A\leq m+1
. This is open when
n\geq 3
and
m\geq 2
, and would be sharp because if
A\subset W
for some non-horizontal
W\in G(n+1,m)
, then
\dim P_{V}(A)=\dim A
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
. For (2), when
\dim_{E}A\leq m
, there should be an inequality
\dim P_{V}(A)\geq C\dim_{E}P_{V}(A)
for almost all
V\in G(n+1,m)
with some
C>1
. If so, what would be the best value of
C
and would it depend only on
m
and
n
or also on the Euclidean and/or parabolic Hausdorff dimensions of
A
? When
\dim_{E}A\leq 1
, it is
2
by Theorem
1.3
.

6.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

.

In this section,
d_{E}
will refer to Euclidean distance.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The case
\dim A\leq 2
already follows from case (1) of Theorem
1.3
, so it may be assumed that
m=2
and
2<\dim A\leq 3
. Using Frostman’s lemma (
2.1
), let
\mu
be a compactly supported nonzero Borel measure on
A
with
c_{\alpha}(\mu)\leq 1
, where
2<\alpha<\dim A
. By (
2.3
), it suffices to show that for any
2<s<\alpha
, for any
\epsilon>0
,

(6.1)

\int_{G_{\epsilon}(3,2)}I_{s}(P_{V\sharp}\mu)\,d\gamma_{3,2}(V)<\infty,

where
G_{\epsilon}(3,2)
is the subset of
V\in G(3,2)
with unit normal
v\in S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
, where

S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}=\left\{v\in S^{2}_{+}:d_{E}(v,(0,0,1))>\epsilon,d_{E}(v,\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\})>\epsilon\right\},

and
S^{2}_{+}
is the upper hemisphere of
S^{2}
. By scaling it may be assumed that
\mu
is supported in the unit ball.

Each
V\in G_{\epsilon}(3,2)
can be written as
V=\spn\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
, where
v_{1}=v_{1}(V)
is a horizontal unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}
, and
v_{2}=v_{2}(V)
is a unit vector orthogonal to
v_{1}
which makes an angle
\gtrsim\epsilon
with the horizontal plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\{0\}
. More precisely, if
V=v^{\perp}
where
v=\left(v^{(1)},v^{(2)},v^{(3)}\right)\in S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
, then
v_{1}
is the unit vector obtained by scaling
\left(-v^{(2)},v^{(1)},0\right)
, and
v_{2}
is the unit vector obtained by scaling
\left(-v^{(1)}v^{(3)},-v^{(2)}v^{(3)},\left(v^{(1)}\right)^{2}+\left(v^{(2)}\right)^{2}\right)
.

For
V\in G_{\epsilon}(3,2)
, write
v_{2}=v_{2}^{\prime}+v_{2}^{\prime\prime}
, where
v_{2}^{\prime}
is in the horizontal plane and
v_{2}^{\prime\prime}
is parallel to
(0,0,1)
with
|v_{2}^{\prime\prime}|\gtrsim\epsilon
(note that
v_{2}^{\prime}
and
v_{2}^{\prime\prime}
may not be in
V
). Then
v_{2}^{\prime}
and
v_{2}^{\prime\prime}
are orthogonal to
v_{1}
. Thus, if
x=x_{1}v_{1}+x_{2}v_{2}\in V
, and
y=y_{1}v_{1}+y_{2}v_{2}\in V
, then

d(x,y)=\sqrt{(x_{1}-y_{1})^{2}+(x_{2}-y_{2})^{2}|v_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}+\left\lvert(x_{2}-y_{2})v_{2}^{\prime\prime}\right\rvert}.

But
|v_{2}^{\prime}|\leq 1
and
|v_{2}^{\prime\prime}|\gtrsim\epsilon
, so this simplifies to

\displaystyle d(x,y)

\displaystyle\sim|x_{1}-y_{1}|+|x_{2}-y_{2}|^{1/2}

\displaystyle\sim\left(|x_{1}-y_{1}|^{4}+|x_{2}-y_{2}|^{2}\right)^{1/4},

in any bounded set around the origin, where the implicit constant depends only on
\epsilon
and the distance from the origin. Therefore, (
6.1
) is equivalent to

(6.2)

\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int\int\frac{1}{\left(\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{1}\rangle\right\rvert^{4}+\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{2}\rangle\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{s/4}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\,d\sigma(v)<\infty,

where
\sigma
is the surface measure on
S^{2}
. For
v\in S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
, let
\pi_{v}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be
\pi_{v}(x)=\left(\langle x,v_{1}\rangle,\langle x,v_{2}\rangle\right)
. Then (
6.2
) can be written as

(6.3)

\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int f_{s}(x)\,d\left(\iota_{\sharp}\pi_{v\sharp}\mu\ast\pi_{v\sharp}\mu\right)(x)\,d\sigma(v)<\infty,

where
\iota(x)=-x
, and

f_{s}(x,t)=\left(|x|^{4}+t^{2}\right)^{s/4},\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

By Plancherel’s theorem (more precisely
[
Ha23
, Lemma 3.3]
), to prove (
6.3
) it is enough to show that

(6.4)

\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\widehat{f_{s}}(\xi)\left\lvert\widehat{\pi_{v\sharp}\mu}(\xi)\right\rvert^{2}\,d\xi\,d\sigma(v)<\infty,

where the left-hand side is well-defined since
\widehat{f_{s}}>0
when
1<s<3
(see
[
Ha23
, Lemma 3.2]
). By
[
Ha23
, Lemma 3.2]
,
\widehat{f_{s}}\lesssim f_{3-s}
when
1<s<3
, so by dyadically decomposing the frequency space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
into regions where
|\xi_{1}|+|\xi_{2}|^{1/2}\sim 2^{j}
, to prove (
6.4
) it suffices to show that

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}2^{-j(3-s)}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}\left\lvert\widehat{\pi_{v\sharp}\mu}(\xi)\right\rvert^{2}\,d\xi\,d\sigma(v)<\infty,

where boundedness of the
j=0
term follows from the
L^{\infty}
bound on
\widehat{\pi_{v\sharp}\mu}
and the local integrability of
f_{3-s}
. It is enough to show that for
2<s<\alpha
, for any
j\geq 1
,

(6.5)

\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}\left\lvert\widehat{\pi_{v\sharp}\mu}(\xi)\right\rvert^{2}\,d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\ll 2^{j(3-s)},

where
A_{j}\ll B_{j}
in (
6.5
) is an abbreviation for
A_{j}\lesssim j^{-2}B_{j}
(the use of
j^{-2}
is not important; any summable series in
j
with slower than exponential decay would work too). By using
|z|^{2}=z\overline{z}
and writing out the Fourier transform, (
6.5
) is equivalent to

\int\int\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\,d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)},

for any
j\geq 1
. To avoid complicating the notation in what follows,
z\ll r
will mean
|z|\ll r
in case
z
is complex and
r
is real. Given
j
, dyadically decompose the domain
(x,y)
according to the parabolic distance
d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}
from
x
to
y
, where
1\leq k\leq j
. Since
s<\alpha
, the bound for the contribution from
d(x,y)<2^{-j}
follows easily from the fractal condition
c_{\alpha}(\mu)\leq 1
on
\mu
and the trivial upper bound of
2^{3j}
for the oscillatory integral. Therefore, it suffices to prove that

(6.6)

\sum_{k=1}^{j}\iint_{d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

The proof of (
6.6
) will be broken into two cases. It will be shown that

(6.7)

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\iint_{d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)},

and

(6.8)

\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\iint_{d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

For the sum over
j/2\leq k\leq j
, partition the domain further according to the Euclidean distance
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}2^{-\ell}
where
1\leq\ell\leq k
, where
d_{E}
is the Euclidean distance. Then to prove (
6.8
) it is enough to show that

\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

This will be further subdivided into the two sub-cases

(6.9)

\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)},

and

(6.10)

\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=j-k}^{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

The assumption
k\geq j/2
ensures that
j-k\leq k
, so the subdivision makes sense. For the first sub-case (
6.9
), by writing the 2-dimensional
\xi
integral as a product of two 1-dimensional integrals and calculating each explicitly,

(6.11)

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\\
\frac{1}{\max\left\{2^{-j},\left\lvert\langle v_{1},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}\max\left\{2^{-2j},\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}}\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

The integral over
S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
can be partitioned into dyadic regions where
\left\lvert\langle v_{1},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{1}}
and
\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}
, where
k+\ell\leq m_{1}\leq j
and
k+\ell\leq m_{2}\leq 2j
, with
\sim
replaced by
\lesssim
when either
m_{1}=j
or
m_{2}=2j
. It will be shown that each such dyadic region has surface area
\lesssim 2^{-m_{1}-m_{2}+2(\ell+k)}
.

If
\ell<k-C
for a large constant
C
, and
u=(x-y)/|x-y|
, then
u
makes an angle
<\epsilon^{100}
with the horizontal plane (since otherwise it would hold

2^{-k-\ell}\sim d_{E}(x,y)\sim|x_{3}-y_{3}|\sim d(x,y)^{2}\sim 2^{-2k},

which is a contradiction if
C
is sufficiently large depending on
\epsilon
). Therefore, the function
f_{u}:B_{2}\left(0,1-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{100}\right)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

f_{u}(w_{1},w_{2})=\left(-w_{2}u_{1}+w_{1}u_{2},-w_{1}u_{1}-w_{2}u_{2}+\frac{u_{3}(w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2})}{\sqrt{1-w_{1}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}}}\right)

has

\displaystyle\det(Df_{u}(w_{1},w_{2}))

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}u_{2}&-u_{1}\\
-u_{1}+O\left(u_{3}\epsilon^{-10}\right)&-u_{2}+O\left(u_{3}\epsilon^{-10}\right)\end{pmatrix}

(6.12)

\displaystyle=-(u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2})+O\left(u_{3}\epsilon^{-10}\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}}\right).

The reason for introducing this function is that
f_{u}(w_{1},w_{2})
is (up to a harmless scaling) the same as
\left(\langle v_{1},u\rangle,\langle v_{2},u\rangle\right)
when
v=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\sqrt{1-w_{1}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}}\right)
. By the inverse function theorem and (
6.12
), it follows that for any unit vector
u
with
|u_{3}|\lesssim\epsilon^{100}\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}+u_{2}^{2}}
, for any open set
O\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\mathcal{H}^{2}_{E}\left(f_{u}^{-1}(O)\right)\lesssim\mathcal{H}^{2}_{E}(O),

where
\mathcal{H}^{2}_{E}
is the 2-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Applying the above with
O
a rectangle of dimensions
\sim 2^{-m_{1}+\ell+k}\times 2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
verifies the bound of
2^{-m_{1}-m_{2}+2(\ell+k)}
for the surface area, in the case where
\ell<k-C
for a large constant
C
.

The other case for the surface area bound is
k-C\leq\ell\leq k
(and still
\ell\leq j-k\leq j/2
). In this case
2k-C\leq m_{1}\leq 2k
, and hence
2^{-m_{1}-m_{2}+2(\ell+k)}\sim 2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
, so it is enough to show that the region of
v
where
\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}
(with
\sim
replaced by
\lesssim
when
m_{2}=2j
) has surface area
\lesssim 2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
. If
F:S^{2}_{+,0}\to S^{2}_{+,0}
is

\displaystyle F(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left\lvert\left(-w_{1}w_{3},-w_{2}w_{3},w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}\right)\right\rvert}\left(-w_{1}w_{3},-w_{2}w_{3},w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{-w_{1}w_{3}}{\sqrt{w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}}},\frac{-w_{2}w_{3}}{\sqrt{w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}}},\sqrt{w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}}\right),

then writing
(x,y,z)=F(w)
gives
w_{3}=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}
,
w_{1}=\frac{-xz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}
, and
w_{2}=\frac{-yz}{\sqrt{1-z^{2}}}
. Therefore,
F
is a self-inverse diffeomorphism of
S^{2}_{+,0}
onto itself, and in particular
F
is bi-Lipschitz on
S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
. The region
\{v\in S^{2}:\left\lvert\langle v,x-y\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}\}
is the intersection of the
2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
-neighbourhood of a plane through the origin with
S^{2}
, which has area
\sim 2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
, so the inverse image of this set under
F:S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}\to S^{2}_{+}
also has surface area
\lesssim 2^{-m_{2}+\ell+k}
.

This verifies the claim that the dyadic regions described above have surface area
\lesssim 2^{-m_{1}-m_{2}+2(\ell+k)}
, for each pair
(m_{1},m_{2})
. Since there are
\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}
many such pairs
(m_{1},m_{2})
, summing over
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
in (
6.11
) yields the bound

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}2^{2(\ell+k)}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

For fixed
y
, the region of integration in
x
is contained in a Euclidean cylinder of dimensions
2^{-(k+\ell)}\times 2^{-2k}
, which can be covered by
\lesssim 2^{2\ell}
many parabolic balls of radius
2^{-(k+\ell)}
. Therefore, each such Euclidean cylinder contributes
\lesssim 2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}
in
\mu
-measure to the above. Hence, the above becomes

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}2^{2(\ell+k)}2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{j-k}2^{\ell(4-\alpha)-k(\alpha-2)}.

Since
\alpha<3<4
, summing the geometric series in
\ell
gives

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}2^{(j-k)(4-\alpha)-k(\alpha-2)}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}2^{-2k+j(4-\alpha)}.

Summing the geometric series in
k
gives

\eqref{highkboundell1}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}2^{j(3-\alpha)}.

This implies (
6.9
) in this sub-case, since
s<\alpha
. This verifies the first sub-case (
6.9
) (high
k
and low
\ell
).

For the second sub-case (
6.10
) (high
k
and high
\ell
), by calculating the integrals explicitly as before,

(6.13)

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=j-k}^{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\\
\frac{1}{2^{-j}\max\left\{2^{-2j},\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}}\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

The integral over
S^{2}_{+}
can be subdivided into dyadic regions where
\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m}
, where
k+\ell\leq m\leq 2j
, with
\sim
replaced by
\lesssim
when
m=2j
. Each such dyadic region has surface area
\sim 2^{-m+\ell+k}
by the same reasons as above; it is the inverse image (under a bi-Lipschitz function from
S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}\to S^{2}
) of the
2^{-m+\ell+k}
-neigbourhood of a plane through the origin intersected with
S^{2}
.

Since there are
\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)
many values of
m
, summing (
6.13
) over
m
using the above surface area bound yields

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=j-k}^{k}2^{\ell+k+j}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

As before, the Euclidean cylinder contributes
\mu
-measure
\lesssim 2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}
, so the above becomes

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=j-k}^{k}2^{\ell+k+j}2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}\sum_{\ell=j-k}^{k}2^{\ell(3-\alpha)-k(\alpha-1)+j}.

Since
\alpha<3
, summing the geometric series over
\ell
gives

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}2^{k(3-\alpha)-k(\alpha-1)+j}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=j/2}^{j}2^{-2k(\alpha-2)+j}.

Since
\alpha>2
, summing the geometric series over
k
gives

\eqref{highkboundell2}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)2^{-j(\alpha-2)+j}=\log\left(2^{j}\right)2^{j(3-\alpha)}.

This implies (
6.10
) in this sub-case, since
s<\alpha
. This is the remaining sub-case for high
k
, so this verifies the case
j/2\leq k\leq j
in (
6.8
).

It remains to prove (
6.7
) (low
k
). As before, partition the domain according to
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}2^{-\ell}
where
1\leq\ell\leq k
, where
d_{E}
is the Euclidean distance. To prove (
6.7
) it suffices to show that

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

As before, let
C
be a constant chosen sufficiently large depending on
\epsilon
. The above will be broken into the two sub-cases

(6.14)

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-C}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

and

(6.15)

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=k-C}^{k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

For the first sub-case where
\eqref{lowkbound}\lesssim\eqref{subcaseeasy}
, similarly to before, by calculating the innermost integral,

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-C}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\\
\frac{1}{\max\left\{2^{-j},\left\lvert\langle v_{1},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}\max\left\{2^{-2j},\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}}\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

Similarly to the low
\ell
sub-case of the high
k
case with
\ell\leq k-C
, this becomes

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-C}2^{2(k+\ell)}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

By using the same bound of
2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}
on the
\mu
-measure of the Euclidean cylinder as before, this becomes

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-C}2^{2(k+\ell)}2^{2\ell-\alpha(k+\ell)}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k-C}2^{\ell(4-\alpha)-k(\alpha-2)}.

Since
\alpha<3<4
, summing the geometric series in
\ell
gives

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}2^{2k(3-\alpha)}.

Since
\alpha<3
, summing the geometric series in
k
gives

\eqref{subcaseeasy}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}2^{j(3-\alpha)}.

This implies (
6.14
) in this sub-case, since
s<\alpha
. This verifies the case where
\eqref{lowkbound}\lesssim\eqref{subcaseeasy}
.

It remains to prove (
6.15
). By allowing the (slightly ambiguous) notation
\sim
to include dependence on
C
, this case can be abbreviated to

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
d_{E}(x,y)\sim 2^{-2k}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

Write
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, and dyadically partition the domain further into sets where either
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\lesssim 2^{-j}
, or
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-2k-\ell}
where
0\leq\ell\leq j-2k
. It suffices to show that

(6.16)

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-j}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)},

and

(6.17)

\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-2k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-2k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\int_{[-2^{j},2^{j}]\times[-2^{2j},2^{2j}]}e^{2\pi i\langle\pi_{v}(x-y),\xi\rangle}\\
d\xi\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y)\ll 2^{j(3-s)}.

The edge case (
6.16
) satisfies

\eqref{edgecase}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-j}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\frac{1}{2^{-j}\max\left\{2^{-2j},\left\lvert\langle v_{2},x-y\rangle\right\rvert\right\}}\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

Similarly to before, this becomes

\eqref{edgecase}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}2^{2k+j}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\leq 2^{-j}\end{subarray}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

By covering the
2^{-j}\times 2^{-2k}
Euclidean cylinder with
\lesssim 2^{2j-2k}
many parabolic balls of radius
2^{-j}
similarly to before, this becomes

\eqref{edgecase}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}2^{2k+j}2^{2j-2k}2^{-\alpha j}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{edgecase}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}2^{j(3-\alpha)},

which verifies (
6.16
) since
\alpha>s
.

It remains to consider the non-edge case (
6.17
). Similarly to previous cases,

(6.18)

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-2k}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-2k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\int_{S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}}\\
\frac{1}{\max\left\{2^{-j},\left\lvert\left\langle x-y,v_{1}\right\rangle\right\rvert\right\}\max\left\{2^{-2j},\left\lvert\left\langle x-y,v_{2}\right\rangle\right\rvert\right\}}\,d\sigma(v)\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

Dyadically partition
S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
according to
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{1}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{1}}
and
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{2}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}
, where
2k+\ell\leq m_{1}\leq j
and
2k\leq m_{2}\leq 2j
, with
\sim
replaced by
\lesssim
when either
m_{1}=j
or
m_{2}=2j
. It will be shown that each such region has surface area
\lesssim 2^{-m_{1}-m_{2}+2k+\ell+2k}
. If
m_{1}\leq 2k+\ell+C
then this follows easily from previous arguments, so it may be assumed that
m_{1}\gg 2k+\ell
. Similarly, it may be assumed that
m_{2}\gg 2k
. If
v
is written in (one version of) spherical coordinates

v=\left(\cos\phi\cos\theta,\cos\phi\sin\theta,\sin\phi\right),\qquad 0\leq\theta<2\pi,\quad c\epsilon\leq\phi\leq\frac{\pi}{2}-c\epsilon,

which includes
S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
if
c>0
is sufficiently small, then

v_{1}=\left(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0\right),

and

v_{2}=\left(-\sin\phi\cos\theta,-\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\phi\right).

Since
\partial_{\phi}v_{2}=-v
, it must hold that
\left\lvert\langle u,\partial_{\phi}v_{2}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 1
whenever
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{1}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{1}}
and
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{2}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}
, where
u=(x-y)/|x-y|
(this is where
m_{1}\gg 2k+\ell\geq 2k
and
m_{2}\gg 2k
get used). Therefore, the region of
v\in S^{2}_{+,\epsilon}
where
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{1}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{1}}
and
\left\lvert\langle x-y,v_{2}\rangle\right\rvert\sim 2^{-m_{2}}
has
\theta
contained in a set of length
\lesssim 2^{2k+\ell-m_{1}}
, and for each
\theta
,
\phi
is contained in a set of length
\lesssim 2^{2k-m_{2}}
. This verifies the claimed surface area bound of
2^{2k+\ell-m_{1}+2k-m_{2}}
.

Substituting this surface area bound into (
6.18
) gives

(6.19)

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-2k}2^{4k+\ell}\iint_{\begin{subarray}{c}d(x,y)\sim 2^{-k}\\
|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\sim 2^{-2k-\ell}\end{subarray}}\,d\mu(x)\,d\mu(y).

For each
y
, the region of integration in
x
is contained in a Euclidean cylinder of dimensions
2^{-2k-\ell}\times 2^{-2k}
, which can be covered by
\lesssim 2^{2k+2\ell}
many parabolic balls of radius
2^{-2k-\ell}
, and therefore contributes
\mu
-measure at most
2^{2k+2\ell}2^{-\alpha(2k+\ell)}
. Substituting into (
6.19
) gives

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-2k}2^{4k+\ell}2^{2k+2\ell}2^{-\alpha(2k+\ell)}.

This simplifies to

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{j-2k}2^{2k(3-\alpha)+\ell(3-\alpha)}.

Since
\alpha<3
, summing the geometric series in
\ell
gives

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{j/2}2^{2k(3-\alpha)+(j-2k)(3-\alpha)}.

The summand is independent of
k
, so this simplifies to

\eqref{nonedge}\lesssim\log\left(2^{j}\right)^{3}2^{j(3-\alpha)}.

Since
s<\alpha
, this finishes the remaining sub-case for the remaining case of low
k
in (
6.7
), and finishes the proof. ∎

Remark 6.1
.

If
f_{n,s}=\left(|x|^{4}+t^{2}\right)^{-s/4}
where
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
and
n\geq 1
, then
\widehat{f_{n,s}}
is given by a function which is smooth away from the origin and which satisfies

(6.20)

\left\lvert\widehat{f_{n,s}}\right\rvert\lesssim f_{n,n+2-s},\qquad 0<s<n+2.

This follows from an observation in the introduction to
[
GS06
]
, that the Fourier transform of a regular homogeneous distribution is a regular homogeneous distribution. This observation was overlooked in
[
Ha23
, Lemma 2.2]
, where it was shown using modified Bessel functions that

0<\widehat{f_{1,s}}\lesssim f_{1,3-s},\qquad 1<s<3.

Due to the smoothness and homogeneity mentioned above, the positivity conclusion automatically upgrades to

\widehat{f_{1,s}}\sim f_{1,3-s},\qquad 1<s<3.

Although a proof of positivity of
\widehat{f_{n,s}}
may require modified Bessel functions, the positivity and lower bound
\widehat{f_{1,s}}\gtrsim f_{1,3-s}
was not crucial to argument of this section, so (
6.20
) may be a first step towards generalising the argument of this section to higher dimensions.
