IL-35 promotes synovial fibroblast senescence via activation of cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Ziliang Yu, Zeyu Liu, Jianbo Fan, Yixuan Li, Jiafeng He, Jiawei Zhang, Pengfei Fu, Dagong Gao, Fei Xia, Youhan Mei, Rui Xu, Jie Hao, Wei Liu, Haiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterleukin-35 (IL-35) exerts immunosuppressive effects in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), but its direct impact on synovial fibroblasts (SFs) remains unclear. This study aimed to redefine the role of IL-35 in RA by uncovering its therapeutic mechanism through the induction of STING-dependent synovial fibroblast senescence. We first observed dysregulated IL-35 signaling in RA patients. In a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model, recombinant IL-35 treatment effectively alleviated disease severity, reducing clinical scores, joint swelling, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. The pivotal discovery emerged from in vitro experiments: IL-35, but not TNF-α, directly promoted cellular senescence in RA synovial fibroblasts (RASFs). Mechanistically, IL-35 signaled through the glycoprotein 130 (GP130) receptor to activate the cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway, leading to upregulated senescence markers (p16, p21, p53). This STING activation was essential, as its inhibition abolished the pro-senescent effect. Crucially, in vivo knockdown of endogenous IL-35 exacerbated arthritis, and this aggravation was rescued by co-treatment with an IRF3 agonist. Our findings establish a novel protective axis in which IL-35 activates the STING pathway to drive RASFs into a senescent state, thereby inhibiting their pathogenic activity and ameliorating RA progression. Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by erosive synovitis driven by dysregulated immune cells and inflammatory mediators [1, 2]. Clinically, RA manifests as progressive symmetrical inflammation of small joints, often leading to irreversible deformity and functional disability [3]. With a global disability rate of 60%-80%, RA imposes a severe socioeconomic burden [4]. Current therapies—nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, and biologics—alleviate symptoms but fail to halt disease progression. Immune and endothelial cell activation drives immune cell transendothelial migration and synovial fibroblast proliferation, ultimately causing joint damage [5, 6]. Therefore, investigating the regulatory mechanisms between immune cells and synovial fibroblasts (SFs) in RA is crucial for identifying new therapeutic targets and improving patient outcomes.

IL-35 has been identified as a diagnostic marker in various autoimmune diseases, and reduced serum levels of IL-35 have been observed in RA patients. Moreover, limited evidence suggests that IL-35 may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for RA7. However, although iTr35 cells, an inducible T-cell subset generated by IL-35, have demonstrated immunoregulatory effects in several inflammatory diseases, such as systemic sclerosis [8, 9] and asthma [10], their specific immunosuppressive mechanisms in RA remain unclear. Critically, its direct effects on the pathogenic synovial fibroblasts, the key effector cells in joint destruction, are poorly understood. Therefore, exploring the nTreg-to-iTr35 conversion process and the regulatory mechanisms of the IL-35/iTr35 axis may provide novel therapeutic strategies for RA.

In the human synovium, fibroblasts are key effector cells in RA tissue destruction. The primary roles of fibroblasts include providing structural support, lubrication to enable low-friction joint movement, and supplying nutrients to the avascular cartilage. In rheumatoid arthritis, synovial fibroblasts transform into a tumor-like phenotype, becoming overactivated and abnormally proliferative [11]. Activated synovial fibroblasts promote inflammation, angiogenesis, and matrix degradation by producing inflammatory cytokines, pro-angiogenic factors, and matrix metalloproteinases, thereby mediating cartilage damage [12]. Due to the dominant role of synovial fibroblasts in the pathophysiology of rheumatoid arthritis, novel therapies targeting activated synovial fibroblasts have become a central focus [13].

Cellular senescence is a state of permanent cell cycle arrest induced by multiple types of stresses, including over-replication, DNA damage stress such as radiation, oxidative stress, and oncogene activation [14–16]. Expression analysis reveals that senescent fibroblasts are locked in an activated state, mimicking the early remodeling phase of wound healing [17]. Later studies reported that in a mouse model of liver cirrhosis, senescent fibroblasts accelerate wound closure through PDGF-AA secretion and promote fibrosis reversal [18]. Research has found that in rheumatoid arthritis, activation of the melanocortin-1 receptor (MC1) induces a senescent phenotype in synovial fibroblasts, characterized by cessation of proliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and significant genetic alterations, resembling the remodeling stage of wound healing. In vivo activation of MC1 leads to anti-arthritic effects and cartilage protection associated with synovial tissue senescence [19]. These findings indicate that inducing fibroblast senescence is a feasible strategy for treating arthritis. Given this feasibility, we hypothesized that IL-35 might ameliorate RA by modulating the senescent state of SFs.

Despite its known immunoregulatory roles, whether and how IL-35 directly regulates the activation and senescence of synovial fibroblasts in RA remains unknown. Here, we combined clinical analysis, a collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model, and in vitro mechanistic studies to investigate the therapeutic effect of IL-35. We specifically focused on its potential to induce a senescent state in RASFs and explored the involvement of the cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway, a key mediator of cellular senescence and inflammation.

Methods

In silico analysis

Synovial CD4⁺ T cells were analyzed using single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE216245 (GEO). The SDY998 dataset (NIH AMP portal) compared 20 RA and 10 OA samples. Data were processed using Seurat (v4.0), excluding cells with > 20% mitochondrial UMIs, < 200 or > 4000 genes. After quality control, integrated datasets underwent PCA, followed by clustering (k-nearest neighbor graph + Louvain algorithm). Cell identities were annotated using CellMarker databases. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed with Monocle2 (v1.0.0). Senescence-related genes were functionally enriched via Metascape (GO/KEGG). IL-35 activity scores were calculated based on EBI3, IL-12 A, IL-12RB2, and IL-6ST expression (GSE89408), with high/low subgroups defined by median expression. Differential expression was assessed using Wilcoxon rank-sum test (P < 0.05).

Sample collection and patient grouping

In this study, peripheral blood samples were collected from a healthy control group (HC, n = 18) and a rheumatoid arthritis patient group (RA, n = 39). All participants signed informed consent forms, and the study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines approved by the Ethics Committee of Nantong First People’s Hospital. Participants in the RA group met clinical diagnostic criteria for RA, while healthy controls showed no RA-related symptoms.

iTr35 analysis

The specific procedures used were previously described [20]. Peripheral blood samples from RA patients and healthy controls were collected and processed for flow cytometry analysis. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via density gradient centrifugation via Ficoll-Lymphocyte Separation Medium (Solarbio, P8900, China). Viability was assessed using FVS780 (BD Horizon™, 565388, USA). After isolation, the cells were stained for surface markers via the following fluorescently labeled antibodies: anti-CD4 (APC-Cy7, clone OKT4) (Proteintech, APC-FcA65603, China) and anti-CD45RO (PE, clone UCHL1) (Proteintech, PE-65613, China). The cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. For detection of IL-35 expression on Treg cells (Tr35), the cells were stained with an anti-IL-12p35 antibody (PerCP, clone 27537) (Thermo Fisher, MA5-23622, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Flow cytometric analysis was performed via a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and the data were analyzed via FlowJo software (version 10.6.2). The percentage of IL-35+ Tr35 cells among CD4+ CD45RO+ memory T cells was calculated for both RA patients and healthy controls.

ELISA

Commercial kits for IL-6 (Enzyme-linked, ml038115 and ml063159, China), IL-1β (Enzyme-linked, ml058059, China), IL-35 (Invitrogen, EEL067, USA), TNF-α (Enzyme-linked, mIC50536, China), IL-27 (Cloud-Clone, SEA385Mu, China), MMP1 (Cloud-Clone, SEA097Mu) and CRP (Cloud-Clone, SEA821Mu) were utilized for detection, with strict instructions. Supernatants were aliquoted into a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by five washes. After adding enzyme-conjugated reagents and incubating (37 °C, 30 min), chromogenic substrates A and B were added and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C. Reactions were terminated and optical density was measured to quantify inflammatory factors [21].

Establishment of the RA model

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Center of Nantong University (Approval No. 2023KT044) and performed in accordance with the institutional guidelines.

The Collagen-Induced Arthritis (CIA) model was established as previously described [22]. Briefly, CIA was induced by intradermal injection of bovine type II collagen (2 mg/mL; Sigma‑Aldrich) emulsified with complete Freund’s adjuvant at the tail base (Day 0), followed by booster immunization on Day 21. Mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6): healthy controls (HC), CIA (RA), and CIA treated with recombinant IL-35 (RA + rIL-35; Cloud-Clone, China, 50 µg/mL in PBS). Mice received intra‑articular injections of rIL‑35 (1 μg/joint) on Days 25 and 30, and were sacrificed on Day 45 for histological analysis.

Adeno-associated virus serotype 6 (AAV6) vectors (Heyuan Technology Corp., Ltd.) were used to deliver IL-35-interfering fragments (targeting EBI3, sequence: ACCCATTGAAGCCACGACTTT) for gene silencing in mice. The AAV6 solution was administered via intra‑articular injection (10 µL, 1 × 10¹¹ vg/mL) to achieve local knockdown of IL-35 in the joint cavity. To activate IRF3, each mouse received daily intraperitoneal injections of 1 mg/kg KIN1148 (Selleck, S0289, USA) from day 23 until day 43.At the experimental endpoint (Day 45), mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation. Knee joints and serum samples were collected for subsequent analyses.

Histological analysis

Both normal and RA knee joints were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Tissue Sect. (6 μm thick) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Beyotime, C0105M, China) to evaluate the severity of the joint, and the immunohistochemistry marker p-TBK1 (MedChemExpress, HY-P87729, USA, 1:500) was detected. The specific staining method was performed according to the product manuals. For immunofluorescence detection of p16 and p21, sections were processed accordingly using primary antibodies against p16 (Proteintech, 10883‑1‑AP, 1:200) and p21 (Proteintech, 10355‑1‑AP, 1:100), followed by fluorophore‑conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI counterstaining.

Culture and treatment of human RASFs

Primary human rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs) were isolated from de-identified synovial tissue samples as previously described [23]. These cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Epizyme, CB014, China) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, A5670801, USA). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All experiments were conducted with RASFs at passages 4 through 10. Prior to stimulation, RASFs were allowed to grow to 80% confluence and were serum-starved overnight.

Gene knockdown in RASFs

For receptor subunit knockdown, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting human GP130 and IL12RB2 were designed and synthesized (Heyuan, Shanghai, China). The sense strand sequences were as follows: siRNA-GP130, 5′-GGC AUG CCU AAA AGU UAC YTT-3′; siRNA-IL12RB2, 5′-CAG CAG AUC AGU ACU GUU CAA-3′. A non-targeting scrambled siRNA was used as a negative control. RASFs were transfected with 50 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668027, USA) according to the manufacturer‘s instructions. Cells were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection for subsequent functional assays and western blot analysis to confirm knockdown efficiency.

Pharmacological inhibition of STING

To arrest STING activation, RASFs were treated with 100 nM C-176 (MCE, HY-112906, USA) for 48 h, after which the cells were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Cell proliferation assay

RASF proliferation was evaluated using a CCK-8 assay (Solarbio, CA1210, China). Cells at 80% confluence were treated for 48 h with PBS (control), TNF-α (10 ng/mL; Cloud-clone, RPA133Bo01), IL-35 (10 ng/mL; Cloud‑clone, RPC008Mu01), or TNF-α + IL-35 in a 37 °C/5% CO₂ incubator. After adding CCK-8 reagent and incubating for 4 h, absorbance was measured at 450 nm (Tecan, Infinite F50, Switzerland).

RASF cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 6‑well plates and subjected to the corresponding treatments. Five hours before the end of treatment, BrdU solution (Yeasen, 40204ES60, China) was added to the cells and incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂ at a final concentration of 0.03 µg/mL. Finally, the cells were fixed, and BrdU incorporation was evaluated according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Apoptosis analysis

Cell apoptosis was assessed by annexin V/PI double staining. Briefly, harvested cells were washed twice with cold PBS and subsequently stained using a commercial apoptosis detection kit (Yeasen, 40302ES60, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubation in annexin V binding buffer containing annexin V and PI for 15 min at room temperature, samples were analyzed immediately on a FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD, USA). Data were collected and processed using FlowJo 7.6 software (TreeStar, USA).

The TUNEL assay was performed using a commercial kit (Yeasen, 40306ES60, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h at 37 °C in the dark. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Stained samples were mounted and imaged under a fluorescence microscope. Apoptotic cells were identified by bright green nuclear fluorescence and quantified as a percentage of total DAPI-positive nuclei.

Cell migration and invasion assay

For the wound healing assay, a scratch was created in a confluent monolayer using a sterile pipette tip. After washing, cells were incubated in serum-free medium. Wound closure was monitored and quantified at 48 h. For the Transwell assay, serum-starved cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a Matrigel-coated insert (Corning, 8-µm pore). Complete medium was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h, cells that traversed the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

ROS detection

To measure the level of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), a reactive oxygen species assay kit (Beyotime, S0033, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In this assay, nonfluorescent DCFH is oxidized by ROS to form fluorescent DCF, and the fluorescence intensity of DCF was quantified by flow cytometry to determine the intracellular ROS levels.

SA-β-gal staining assay

RASFs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The cells were divided into four groups: PBS control, TNF-α (10 ng/mL), IL-35 (10 ng/mL), and TNF-α + IL-35. After 48 h of treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with an SA-β-Gal staining kit (Sigma-Aldrich, CS0030, USA). After washing with PBS, the stained cells were observed under a light microscope, and representative images were captured. The percentage of SA-β-Gal-positive cells was quantified by counting positive cells in five random fields per group.

RNA-seq assay and downstream analysis

Total RNA was extracted from synovial fibroblasts. Paired-end libraries were prepared via an ABclonal mRNA-seq Lib Prep Kit (ABclonal, RK20349, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR products were purified (AMPure XP system; Beckman, wsr-452506, USA), and library quality was assessed on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 4150 system (Agilent Technologies, USA). Finally, sequencing was performed with a 6000/MGISEQ-T7 instrument. The data generated from the Illumina platform were used for bioinformatics analysis. Then, the clean reads were separately aligned to the reference genome in orientation mode via HISAT2 software to obtain mapped reads. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted via the DESeq2 R package, with a significance threshold of Q < 0.05 and a fold change > 1.5. GO functional enrichment, and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were carried out via the clusterProfiler R software package.

DsDNA and cGAMP detection

Cells were lysed under non-denaturing conditions and dsDNA content was measured using the dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Yeasen, 12640ES60, China). Briefly, the cell lysate was mixed with the fluorescent dye-based working reagent, and fluorescence was quantified using a Qubit fluorometer, with high selectivity for dsDNA in the 0.2–100 ng range.

Simultaneously, the concentration of 2’,3’-cGAMP was determined using the species-independent, competitive 2’,3’-Cyclic GAMP ELISA Kit (Thermo Fisher, EIAGAMP) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was read at 450 nm, and sample concentrations were interpolated from a standard curve (range: 0.08–20 pmol/mL).

Western blot

Total protein was extracted from synovial fibroblasts and quantified using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, P0011, China). Proteins (40 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad, 1620177, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk for 2 h, then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies against Bio GP130 (ABclonal, A3365, 1:1000, China), p53 (Proteintech, 10442-1-AP, 1:5000, China), p21 (Proteintech, 10355-1-AP, 1:2000), p16 (Proteintech, 10883-1-AP, 1:2000), p-STING (ABclonal, AP1369, 1:1000), STING (ABclonal, A21051, 1:5000), p-TBK1 (ABclonal, AP1026, 1:1000), TBK1 (ABclonal, A3458, 1:2000), p-IRF3 (Abcam, ab320082, 1:1000, China), IRF3 (Abcam, ab68481, 1:1000), STAT1 (Abcam, ab92506, 1:2000), p-STAT1 (Abcam, ab109461, 1:2000), STAT4 (Proteintech, 67568-2-Ig, 1:5000), p-STAT4(Abcam, ab28815, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Beyotime, AF5009, 1:2000) were used. After incubation with the secondary antibodies (ABclonal, AS115 and AS139, 1:2000) the electrochemiluminescence plus reagent (Invitrogen) was used for visualization. Thereafter, the intensities of the blots were quantified via Image Lab 3.0 software (Bio-Rad).

Mitochondrial morphology TEM analysis

Mitochondrial morphology was examined using Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) (JEM-1400). Cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated with graded ethanol and acetone, and embedded in epoxy resin. Sections (60–80 nm) were collected onto copper grids and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The grids were mounted on the JEM-1400 specimen holder, vacuumed, and observed at 2,000× to 30,000× magnification. At least five cells and three fields per cell were analyzed for each sample. Mitochondrial parameters were quantified using ImageJ and analyzed with GraphPad Prism. Fixatives and staining solutions were freshly prepared, and proper precautions were taken during dehydration, embedding, and staining. The microscope was operated at ≤ 120 kV, and copper grids were not exposed to air for extended periods during staining.

Mitochondrial membrane potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using the JC-1 assay kit (Beyotime, China). Log-phase adherent cells were treated according to experimental groups, washed with 1× PBS, and incubated with freshly prepared JC-1 working solution at 37 °C in the dark for 20–30 min. After washing with 1× JC-1 staining buffer, fluorescence was measured using fluorescence microscopy (red/green channels) or flow cytometry (FL2/FL1 channels). The red/green fluorescence intensity ratio was calculated using ImageJ or flow cytometry software (a decrease in ratio indicates reduced membrane potential). Results were analyzed with GraphPad Prism and presented as 'mean±SD' with representative images. JC-1 should be used in the dark, washing should be gentle, and detection parameters should be consistent across groups.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism 9.0.0. An unpaired t-test was used to assess significant differences between the two treatment groups, whereas one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All the results are presented as the means ± SD, with n representing the number of biologically independent samples or experiments.

Results

IL-35 signaling is dysregulated in rheumatoid arthritis patients

We initiated our investigation by characterizing the status of the interleukin-35 (IL-35) pathway in patients with RA. The canonical IL-35 signaling pathway involves a receptor complex composed of the EBI3/p35 heterodimer, which associates with IL-12RB2 and GP130 subunits to initiate downstream JAK/STAT signaling cascades, prominently featuring STAT1 and STAT4 (Fig. 1A). Analysis of a publicly available synovial tissue transcriptomic dataset (GSE89408) revealed a significantly reduced mRNA expression of key components of this pathway—EBI3, IL12A (encoding p35), IL12RB2, and IL6ST (encoding GP130)—in RA patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1B).

Paradoxically, flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from a separate cohort showed that the frequency of CD4⁺CD45RO⁺ memory T helper cells capable of producing IL-35 (termed Tr35 cells) was approximately 1.6-fold lower in RA patients (Fig. 1C-D). This may indicate either an impaired generation, enhanced apoptosis, or altered trafficking of this ELISA measurements confirmed that circulating levels of IL-35 protein were significantly reduced in the serum of RA patients (Fig. 1E). Concurrently, the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β were markedly elevated (Fig. 1F-G). This concordance—reduced local pathway-component expression together with deficient circulating ligand—establishes a complex dysregulation of IL-35 homeostasis in RA.

Single-cell profiling associates aberrant IL-35 activity with senescent status in RA

To dissect the heterogeneity of synovial immune cells in RA at single-cell resolution, we performed an integrated analysis of two independent scRNA-seq datasets of synovial tissue-derived CD4⁺ T cells. First, we analyzed a published dataset (GSE216245) comprising 2,913 CD4⁺ T cells isolated from the synovial tissue of Japanese RA patients. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization of this cohort revealed distinct transcriptional clusters, annotating the cellular diversity within the RA synovial T helper cell compartment (Fig. 2A). To validate and extend these findings across a broader population, we incorporated a second, larger dataset from the Accelerating Medicines Partnership (AMP) consortium (ImmPort: SDY998). This dataset contained 10,099 synovial CD4⁺ T cells from a cohort that included both RA and osteoarthritis (OA) patients, allowing for comparative assessment. UMAP projection of these cells also resolved clear, transcriptionally defined subsets (Fig. 2B).To obtain a comprehensive view and identify conserved cell states across different cohorts, we performed a meta-analysis by integrating the cells from both datasets. The resulting combined UMAP visualization successfully aligned cells from the two independent studies, revealing both shared, core CD4⁺ T cell clusters and potential cohort-specific subpopulations (Fig. 2C). Protein interaction networks highlighting the coexpression of CD99 and MIF complexes (CD74+CD44/CXCR4) (Fig. 2D). This integrated map provides a robust reference framework for interrogating CD4⁺ T cell heterogeneity in the RA synovium and for anchoring subsequent functional analyses.

To directly link these findings to IL-35, we calculated an IL-35 pathway activity score based on the expression of EBI3, IL12A, IL12RB2, and IL6ST, which stratified RA patients into IL-35-High and IL-35-Low subgroups (Fig. 2E). Pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that the IL-35-Low subgroup was significantly associated with the upregulation of gene sets involved in mononuclear cell differentiation, B cell and T cell activation, and chemokine signaling (Fig. 2F). Broader immune-related pathways, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction and Th17 cell differentiation, were also enriched (Fig. 2G). Hallmark gene set analysis confirmed the dominance of inflammatory signatures, such as interferon-gamma and interferon-alpha response, in the IL-35-Low group (Fig. 2H). Collectively, these data implicate IL-35 as a central orchestrator of immune cell heterogeneity and inflammatory signaling in RA, providing mechanistic insights into its complex role in the inflammatory synovial milieu and prompting further investigation into its direct effects on stromal cells.

Recombinant IL-35 treatment ameliorates disease in a collagen-induced arthritis mouse model

We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of exogenous IL-35 in vivo using the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model. Mice were immunized on day 0 and 21, with recombinant IL‑35 (rIL‑35) administered on days 25 and 30 before sacrifice on day 45 (Fig. 3A). Treatment with rIL-35 resulted in a significant reduction in clinical arthritis scores over time compared to the untreated CIA group (Fig. 3B). This therapeutic effect was visually corroborated by a marked decrease in ankle joint swelling in the rIL-35 treated mice (Fig. 3C). At the molecular level, rIL-35 treatment significantly suppressed the elevated serum concentrations of the key pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Fig. 3D). Histopathological assessment of knee joints provided direct morphological evidence: rIL-35 treatment attenuated inflammatory synovial hyperplasia (H&E staining), better preserved cartilage proteoglycan content (Safranin O staining), and reduced the number of apoptotic cells (TUNEL staining) compared to the severely affected CIA control joints (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis of p16 and p21 in knee joint sections revealed that rIL‑35 treatment significantly increased the expression of both senescence markers in the arthritic joints of CIA mice (Fig. 3F-H), indicating that rIL‑35 induces a senescent phenotype in vivo. Together, these data unequivocally demonstrate the disease‑modifying efficacy of exogenous IL‑35 in a preclinical model of RA.

IL-35 induces cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in synovial fibroblasts

To define the direct cellular effects of IL-35 on RA pathogenesis, we focused on RASFs, the key effector cells in joint destruction. We first assessed the impact of IL-35 (10 ng/mL) on the aggressive phenotype of RASFs stimulated by TNF-α (10 ng/mL). Functional assays revealed that while TNF-α significantly enhanced RASF proliferation (CCK-8 assay, Fig. 4A) and BrdU incorporation (Fig. 4B), co-treatment with IL-35 effectively suppressed this pro-proliferative effect. Quantification of apoptosis by flow cytometry using Annexin V/PI staining revealed that treatment with IL‑35 significantly increased the percentage of apoptotic cells compared to treatment with TNF‑α alone (Fig. 4C). This pro‑apoptotic effect of IL‑35 was independently confirmed by TUNEL assay, which visually demonstrated a higher frequency of DNA fragmentation‑positive cells in the IL‑35 co‑treatment group (Fig. 4D). Extending this analysis, we found that IL-35 also potently inhibited the TNF-α-augmented migratory and invasive capacities of RASFs, as measured by wound healing and Transwell assays, respectively (Fig. 4E, F). Collectively, these data indicated that IL-35 broadly counteracts the TNF-α-driven, destructive activation program of RASFs, prompting us to investigate the underlying cellular state transition.

To investigate the cellular state underlying this functional suppression, we assessed mitochondrial health, a key determinant of cell fate. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that IL-35 treatment, particularly in concert with TNF-α, led to a significant accumulation of intracellular ROS (Fig. 5A). TEM provided ultrastructural confirmation, revealing pronounced mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption in cells exposed to IL-35, in contrast to the intact mitochondria in control cells (Fig. 5B). Concurrently, assessment of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) using the JC-1 dye showed a severe loss of ΔΨm in IL-35-treated groups, evidenced by a shift from JC-1 aggregates (red) to monomers (green) in fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 5C) and quantified by flow cytometry (Fig. 5D).

Based on the established link between proliferation arrest and cellular senescence, we next asked whether IL-35 induces a senescent phenotype. Indeed, SA-β-galactosidase staining demonstrated a marked increase in senescent cells following IL-35 treatment, either alone or in combination with TNF-α, compared to PBS or TNF-α-only controls (Fig. 5E). Consistent with this functional readout, western blot analysis confirmed a robust upregulation of the core senescence-associated proteins p16, p21, and p53 in response to IL-35 (Fig. 5F). Moreover, we found that IL‑35 treatment also promoted the phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT4 (Fig. 5G). Given that phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT4 has been reported to facilitate their mitochondrial translocation, this observation suggests an intrinsic pathway by which IL‑35 reduces mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to mtDNA leakage and cellular senescence. However, we did not detect any significant effect of IL‑35 on the secretion of IL‑1β, IL‑6, or MMP1 by RASFs (Fig. S1A), indicating that IL‑35 does not substantially modulate the intrinsic inflammatory signaling of RASFs. Together, these findings establish IL‑35 as a direct inducer of senescence in RASFs, providing a plausible mechanistic basis for its observed anti‑proliferative and anti‑migratory effects.

IL-35 activates the cGAS-STING pathway via GP130 to drive a pro-senescent state

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which IL-35 induces cellular senescence in RASFs, we first employed a transcriptomic approach to identify candidate pathways. RNA sequencing revealed that IL-35 treatment induced widespread differential gene expression compared to control-treated cells, as visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. 6A) and a clustered heatmap (Fig. 6B). Subsequent pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG and GO) of these differentially expressed genes pinpointed the cGAS-STING signaling pathway as one of the most significantly enriched pathways in response to IL-35 (Fig. 6C). To biochemically validate this finding, we performed western blot analysis on RASFs treated with IL-35. The results confirmed a robust activation of the STING pathway after IL-35, demonstrated by a significant increase in the phosphorylation levels of STING, its downstream kinase TBK1, and the transcription factor IRF3, with total protein levels remaining largely unchanged (Fig. 6D).

Having established IL‑35 as an activator of the STING pathway, we next sought to identify the upstream receptor responsible for initiating this signal and to functionally link pathway activation to the senescent phenotype. We used siRNA to specifically silence the expression of the two known IL‑35 receptor subunits, IL12RB2 and GP130, in TNF‑α‑primed RASFs prior to IL‑35 treatment. Silencing IL12RB2 had no significant effect on the cellular response to IL‑35. In stark contrast, silencing GP130 completely abrogated the anti‑proliferative effect of IL‑35, as measured by CCK‑8 assay (Fig. 7A). Quantification of cytosolic dsDNA and its immunostimulatory metabolite cGAMP revealed that IL‑35 treatment led to a significant accumulation of both molecules (Fig. 7B, C), indicating that IL‑35 triggers either the release or the impaired clearance of cytosolic nucleic acids, which are the natural ligands for the cGAS sensor that synthesizes cGAMP. Meanwhile, knockdown of GP130, but not IL12RB2, prevented this accumulation of dsDNA and cGAMP induced by IL‑35 (Fig. 7B, C). However, neither silencing of GP130 nor IL12RB2, nor IL‑35 treatment itself, affected the secretion of IL‑1β, IL‑6, or MMP1 (Fig. S1A), suggesting that the IL‑35‑GP130 and IL‑35‑IL12R axes do not modulate the inflammatory secretory phenotype of RASFs. Moreover, siGP130 attenuated the induction of cellular senescence by IL‑35, as shown by SA‑β‑gal staining (Fig. 7D). Western blot analysis from the same experiment confirmed that GP130 knockdown, but not IL12RB2 knockdown, effectively blocked IL‑35‑induced phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, and concurrently suppressed the upregulation of senescence markers p16, p21, and p53 (Fig. 7E). To further explore the mechanism, we found that IL‑35 markedly promoted GP130 dimerization to a significantly greater extent than IL‑6 (Fig. S1B), suggesting that IL‑35 may exert distinct functions from IL‑6 through GP130 homodimerization. This series of experiments definitively identifies GP130 as the critical and non‑redundant receptor subunit through which IL‑35 transmits its signal to activate the STING pathway and execute the senescent program.

To conclusively prove that STING activation is necessary for the observed senescence, we employed the specific STING inhibitor C‑176. C‑176 treatment completely rescued cells from IL‑35‑induced proliferation arrest, as measured by CCK‑8 assay (Fig. 7F). However, C‑176 co‑treatment did not reduce the IL‑35‑induced accumulation of cytosolic dsDNA and cGAMP (Fig. 7G, H). Moreover, C‑176 failed to reverse the IL‑35‑induced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. S1C), indicating that STING inhibition does not prevent IL‑35‑triggered mtDNA leakage; nevertheless, it effectively rescued the cells from senescence, as shown by SA‑β‑gal staining (Fig. 7I). At the molecular level, C‑176 potently inhibited the phosphorylation of STING, TBK1, and IRF3, and suppressed the expression of p16, p21, and p53 induced by IL‑35 (Fig. 7J). Notably, neither C‑176 nor IL‑35 treatment significantly affected the secretion of IL‑1β, IL‑6, or MMP1 by RASFs (Fig. S1A). This critical pharmacological intervention uncouples the upstream sensing of cytosolic nucleic acids from the downstream signaling output, demonstrating that STING kinase activity is the essential, non‑redundant node converting IL‑35 exposure into a senescent cell fate.

Endogenous IL-35 exerts a protective role in CIA, and its loss is rescued by IRF3 activation

The in vitro data suggested that the pro‑senescent effect of IL‑35 might be protective by limiting fibroblast hyperactivation. To test this in vivo, we used an AAV6 vector to deliver shRNA targeting EBI3 (shIL‑35) in CIA mice, aiming to knock down endogenous IL‑35 locally in the joint, with treatment initiated from day 0 (Fig. 8A). Contrary to a simple pro‑inflammatory prediction, knockdown of endogenous IL‑35 exacerbated disease, leading to significantly higher clinical arthritis scores (Fig. 8B), greater ankle swelling (Fig. 8C), and elevated serum levels of IL‑6 and TNF‑α (Fig. 8D) compared to control (shCtrl) mice. Histological analysis of knee joints from shIL‑35 mice revealed more severe synovitis, cartilage erosion, and reduced phosphorylation of TBK1 (p‑TBK1) (Fig. 8E). Remarkably, treatment with the IRF3 agonist KIN1148 fully rescued the aggravated disease phenotype in shIL‑35 mice, normalizing all disease parameters and thus pinpointing the activation of IRF3 as the essential downstream event mediating the protective effect of IL‑35 (Fig. 8B–E). Furthermore, KIN1148 treatment or AAV‑mediated shIL‑35 knockdown did not significantly affect body weight, spleen weight, or serum C‑reactive protein (CRP) levels in RA mice, indicating the safety of the KIN1148 dose used (Fig. S1D–F). Notably, although the expression of IL‑27 (which shares the EBI3 subunit) was elevated in the joint cavity of CIA mice, neither shIL‑35 nor KIN1148 treatment significantly altered IL‑27 levels (Fig. S1G). This critical rescue experiment demonstrates that the absence of IL‑35 creates a permissive environment for uncontrolled inflammation, which can be rectified by directly activating the downstream transcription factor IRF3.

Discussion

RA is a chronic autoimmune disease marked by persistent synovial inflammation and progressive joint damage. In this study, we explored the regulatory role of IL-35 in RA, focusing on its effects on SF senescence and the cGAS-STING-TBK1-IRF3 pathway (Fig S3). Using clinical samples, TNF-α-stimulated SF models, and CIA mice, we demonstrated that IL-35 signals through the gp130 receptor to activate the cGAS–STING–TBK1–IRF3 pathway, thereby promoting cellular senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction in SFs. This process inhibits their pathogenic activity and consequently attenuates RA progression. Our findings identify IL-35 as a unique cytokine that confers therapeutic benefit through senescence induction, highlighting the STING–senescence axis as a promising therapeutic target. This study proposes a novel treatment strategy for RA that concurrently targets inflammatory signaling and stromal fibroblast dysregulation.

IL-35, a member of the IL-12 cytokine family, is an immunosuppressive cytokine composed of IL-12p35 and EBI3 subunits. It is primarily secreted by CD4⁺Foxp3⁺ Tregs, especially inducible IL-35-producing Tregs, and can also be expressed by activated B cells, endothelial cells, and monocytes. IL-35 signals mainly through the heterodimeric receptors IL-12RB2 and gp130, activating STAT1 and STAT4 while inhibiting STAT3, thereby mediating immunosuppressive effects [24, 25]. IL-35 has demonstrated notable immunoregulatory effects in various autoimmune diseases [7, 8, 25–30]. Current research on IL-35 in RA primarily focuses on its regulatory effects on immune cells, particularly T cells [7] and macrophages [20, 31–33]. Recent findings also suggest that IL-35 can inhibit synovial fibroblast proliferation, induce apoptosis, and reduce their support for B cell activation, indicating its potential role in modulating stromal cell–driven inflammation [31–34]. Our analysis of the publicly available synovial tissue transcriptomic dataset (GSE89408) revealed that inflammatory pathways (e.g., interferon response, TNF signaling) were enriched in the IL-35-Low subgroup, but not in the IL-35-High subgroup. This indicates that reduced IL-35 pathway activity is associated with heightened inflammatory signatures, a finding that is fully consistent with the anti-arthritic and pro-senescent effects of IL-35 demonstrated in our CIA model. While recombinant IL-35 effectively mitigated disease severity in the CIA model, a more critical finding is that IL-35 directly promoted senescence in SFs. Our bioinformatic findings reinforce the protective role of IL-35 in RA, suggesting that the IL-35 pathway is endogenously engaged to counterbalance inflammation but may be insufficient to fully suppress established disease, thereby supporting the therapeutic rationale for exogenous IL-35 administration.

RNA-seq analysis revealed a significant enrichment of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway following IL-35 treatment. The cGAS–STING–TBK1–IRF3 signaling axis is a critical immunological defense mechanism against pathogens [35]. cGAS is an evolutionarily conserved cytosolic DNA sensor. Upon binding to DNA, cGAS synthesises cGAMP, which diffuses intracellularly to bind and activate the endoplasmic reticulum-localised receptor STING [35–37]. Activation of STING leads to the recruitment and activation of downstream signaling molecules, including TBK1 and IRF3, ultimately resulting in the production of type I interferons and other pro-inflammatory cytokines [38]. The cGAS-STING signalling pathway has emerged as a pivotal player in the innate immune response, responsible for detecting and responding to cytoplasmic DNA [39]. Beyond its role in innate immunity, the cGAS-STING pathway plays a crucial role in diverse pathophysiological processes due to its unique function in detecting cytoplasmic DNA, including cellular senescence and carcinogenesis[40]. In inflammatory arthritis, TNF induces mitochondrial DNA release and cGAS-STING-dependent interferon responses, thereby promoting disease progression [41]. Furthermore, a biomimetic nanomedicine (HA-RM-Cel-BR) based on the STING inhibitor celastrol (Cel) and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (RONS) scavenger bilirubin (BR), modified with hyaluronic acid (HA) for arthritis targeting, has demonstrated excellent therapeutic efficacy in RA rat models by inhibiting STING and scavenging RONS to remodel the joint immune microenvironment [42]. These findings may appear contradictory to our study’s conclusion that IL-35 activates the STING pathway to induce senescence in RASFs and ameliorate arthritis. This seeming contradiction underscores the complexity of RA pathogenesis. Previous research has primarily focused on the abnormal activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in immune cells, which drives the production of pro-inflammatory factors and exacerbates inflammation and immune responses. In contrast, our study reveals a previously underappreciated protective axis of this pathway in SFs—the STING-senescence axis, offering a novel perspective and therapeutic target for RA treatment.

Cellular senescence is a stable and irreversible state of cell cycle arrest, induced by factors such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, replicative stress, oncogenic signaling, and mitochondrial dysfunction [43–45]. Emerging evidence indicates that the cGAS-STING signaling pathway plays an essential role in the induction and regulation of cellular senescence [46]. By sensing oxidative DNA damage, the cGAS-STING pathway induces the release of SASP and promotes the expression of p16 and SA-β-Gal, thereby driving senescence [47, 48]. Our in vitro experiments confirmed that IL-35, via the gp130–STAT1/4 axis, directly triggers mitochondrial dysfunction characterized by loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, which leads to the accumulation of cytosolic dsDNA and cGAMP. Critically, the STING inhibitor C-176 failed to prevent IL-35-induced mitochondrial membrane potential loss, placing mitochondrial damage upstream of STING activation. The leaked dsDNA then activates the cGAS-STING pathway to promote cellular senescence in SFs. Mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by diminished respiratory capacity, loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and typically increased production of reactive oxygen species, serves as both a cause and a consequence of cellular senescence [49]. In RA, a model of aggressive SF proliferation exists, which may render these cells susceptible to senescence-inducing therapies. Previous studies have demonstrated that activation of the MC1 promotes SF senescence. This process halts SF proliferation and induces a pro-resolving phenotype highly similar to that observed in senescence-driven wound healing, ultimately contributing to arthritis remission. We further provide in vivo evidence that IL-35 treatment significantly increases p16- and p21-positive cells in the synovium of CIA mice, directly confirming that IL-35 promotes synovial fibroblast senescence in the arthritic joint.

In the present study, we observed that IL‑35 treatment simultaneously induced both apoptosis and cellular senescence in RASFs. Although these are generally considered distinct cell fates, accumulating evidence suggests that they are not mutually exclusive and can co‑exist depending on the cellular context and stimulus intensity [50]. Senescent cells may undergo secondary apoptosis, particularly when the senescence‑associated secretory phenotype (SASP) creates a pro‑apoptotic milieu or when the senescent state is unsustainable under sustained stress [51, 52]. In our experimental setting (48 h of IL‑35 stimulation), we propose that senescence is the primary response, as evidenced by the robust upregulation of p16, p21, and p53, along with functional growth arrest and SA‑β‑Gal positivity. The observed apoptosis likely represents a fraction of cells that, after entering senescence, activate apoptotic pathways—possibly as a failsafe mechanism to eliminate overly damaged cells. We acknowledge that the temporal relationship between these two outcomes was not systematically dissected in the current study. A detailed time‑course analysis (e.g., from 24 h to 72 h) and dose‑response experiments would be required to determine whether a switch from senescence to apoptosis occurs at different IL‑35 concentrations or time points. Nonetheless, the dominant functional consequence of IL‑35 exposure in RASFs is senescence‑mediated suppression of proliferation, migration, and invasion, which underpins its protective effects in RA.

IL-35 is composed of two subunits, p35 and EBI3, which interact with the receptor subunits IL-12Rβ2 and gp130 to form various receptor complexes. Diverse receptor combinations may exist in different diseases, providing a structural basis for the functional diversity of IL-35. Notably, we found that IL-35 predominantly promotes the formation of gp130 homodimers in RASFs, whereas IL-6, which shares the gp130 receptor, induces canonical IL-6Rα/gp130 heterodimers. This divergence in receptor complex architecture explains how the same gp130 subunit can mediate opposing biological outcomes—IL-35-driven senescence versus IL-6-driven survival and inflammation. In cardiac transplantation, studies have shown that IL-35 activates the gp130 receptor to stabilize the Treg phenotype, thereby reducing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration in allografts [53]. Conversely, in experimental autoimmune uveitis, IL-35 activates the IL-12Rβ2 receptor to promote B-cell proliferation and enhance immune regulation [54]. In cigarette smoke-exposed asthma, IL-35 modulates ferroptosis-related neutrophil extracellular traps via the neutrophil gp130 receptor through interaction with STAT3 [55]. In our study, knockdown of gp130 but not IL-12Rβ2 abolished IL-35-induced dsDNA and cGAMP accumulation, and prevented both cellular senescence and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway. This identifies gp130 as the critical and non-redundant receptor subunit through which IL-35 transmits signals to activate the STING pathway and execute the senescence program.

Building upon our findings, the ability of IL-35 to concurrently regulate inflammatory signaling and senescence-related pathways in synovial fibroblasts suggests its potential as a dual-function therapeutic agent. IL-35 may interrupt the feedforward loop between chronic inflammation and fibroblast dysfunction—a key contributor to persistent RA pathology. This highlights the promise of IL-35 as an adjunct or alternative to existing RA therapies. Meanwhile, the dynamics of Tr35 in the RA synovial microenvironment deserve further attention. This study suggests that reduced Tr35 abundance may contribute to disease progression. Whether elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α interfere with the differentiation of nTregs into Tr35 remains to be determined and could represent a feedback loop that exacerbates local inflammation. Beyond their effects on synovial fibroblasts, the potential of Tr35 to modulate other key synovial cell types, including M1/M2 macrophages and endothelial cells, remains unexplored. Elucidating their broader immunoregulatory roles within synovial tissue may expand our understanding of local immune homeostasis. Regarding preclinical models, the commonly used CIA model recapitulates T cell–driven inflammation but lacks the multicellular complexity of human synovium. Future studies could incorporate human synovial tissue xenografts or AAV-based IL-35 delivery systems to better simulate human RA pathology. Additionally, genetically modified mouse models targeting IL-12RB2, gp130, or IRF3 would help delineate critical nodes within the IL-35 signaling axis. The AAV6 serotype used can transduce multiple cell types in the joint, including synovial fibroblasts and, to some extent, infiltrating T/B lymphocytes; therefore, the observed exacerbation of arthritis upon EBI3 silencing reflects the collective loss of IL-35 from various cellular sources rather than a specific cell population. This does not undermine the conclusion that endogenous IL-35 is protective, but it precludes assigning the phenotype exclusively to RASFs. Conditional knockout mice targeting specific cell types will be required for precise lineage assignment. More critically, this study did not characterize the SASP profile of IL-35-induced senescent SFs, which is essential for understanding its long-term impact. Furthermore, although we have provided in vivo evidence of p16/p21 expression and have shown that an IRF3 agonist rescues the phenotype, we have not formally proven that IRF3 is the essential downstream transcription factor for STING-induced senescence in RASFs. STING can activate multiple effector branches (e.g., NF-κB, autophagy). Thus, a definitive demonstration would require genetic ablation of IRF3 (or IRF3/IRF7) in RASFs. We acknowledge this as a limitation and have added it to the revised Discussion. Future investigations should define the secretome features of IL-35-induced senescent SFs and employ animal models capable of conditionally knocking out or overexpressing IL-35 receptor components to validate its stage-specific roles in vivo.

In conclusion, our study reveals a previously underappreciated role of IL-35 in regulating both inflammation and cellular senescence in RA, highlighting the STING–senescence axis as a potential therapeutic target. These findings not only highlight IL-35 as a promising dual-target therapeutic candidate but also lay the foundation for deeper mechanistic studies and future translational exploration. Further elucidating the interplay between IL-35, stromal cells, and innate immune pathways may offer novel strategies to disrupt chronic inflammation and tissue degeneration in autoimmune diseases.
