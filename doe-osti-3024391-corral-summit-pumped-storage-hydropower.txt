ABSTRACT, SUMMARY, FOREWORD, AND ACKNOWLEDGMENTS
 In 2024, Idaho National Laboratory (INL) and Pacific Northwest National
 Laboratory (PNNL) initiated a technical-assistance project to support Cat Creek
 Energy, LLC, (CCE) in evaluating site suitability for the proposed Corral
 Summit Pumped Storage Hydropower (PSH) project in south-central Idaho near
 Mackay Reservoir. The Corral Summit facility incorporates battery storage and
 photovoltaic (PV) solar arrays in a Trybrid configuration to deliver large-volume
 long-duration (LVLD) storage solutions for rural electric cooperatives in eastern
 Idaho. The evaluation process focused on determining the most-suitable location
 for the upper reservoir of the PSH system, guided by a comprehensive
 assessment framework spanning multiple categories, including physical
 characteristics, environmental constraints, building infrastructure, regulatory
 constraints, cultural resources and sensitivity, social factors, and power market
 and grid integration.
 Each site was analyzed based on a ranking scale (0–1), which scores ranging
 from “severely disfavored” to “highly favored,” allowing detailed comparisons of
 site-specific conditions. Categories such as hydraulic head, utilities corridor, land
 ownership, and transmission-grid limitations emerged as key contributors to the
 overall assessment. Site 2 (Idaho Trust) demonstrated a slight advantage over
 Site 1 (Bureau of Land Management) primarily due to favorable outcomes in
 regulatory constraints, building infrastructure, and power-market integration.
 However, Site 1 outperformed Site 2 in factors related to physical characteristics
 and social factors.
 The report emphasizes the need for further evaluation of both sites before
 clear determination of which site is preferred, due to the limited information
 available on either site at the time of this report. Key areas of evaluation to
 clearly define the preferred site are ecological impacts, cultural-resource surveys,
 and economic-feasibility assessments. For successful project execution,
 recommended follow-up actions include seismic and geotechnical surveys,
 groundwater and habitat monitoring, regulatory reviews of water rights and right-
 of-way agreements, cultural engagement with local tribal governments, and
 enhanced stakeholder strategies. These efforts will ensure the Corral Summit
 Trybrid facility meets local energy needs while balancing environmental, social,
 and regulatory responsibilities.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 In 2024, a collaborative team of researchers, economists, engineers, and scientists from Idaho
National Laboratory (INL) and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) launched a technical
assistance project to support Cat Creek Energy, LLC, (CCE) in evaluating key aspects of a proposed
pumped storage hydropower (PSH) project in south-central Idaho near Mackay Reservoir. Known as the
Corral Summit Pumped Storage Hydropower project, this initiative contains battery storage and
photovoltaic (PV) solar arrays to create a TrybridTM project.
 As illustrated in Figure 1, CCE provided two potential upper reservoir locations for the PSH. To
determine the optimal site, the project team conducted an extensive review of site-specific characteristics
to assess preliminary feasibility of each location. This report documents the relative merits of the two
sites with the goal of supporting the selection of the preferred option. Using an exemplary case system
and scenarios, site-specific criteria (e.g., environmental, regulatory, topographical, social constraints) are
assessed with a scoring system. Based on these scores, this report examines the initial feasibility of each
upper reservoir site and recommends a preferred location for the TrybridTM PSH project.

 2. BACKGROUND
Modern power systems increasingly face challenges in maintaining reliable and stable energy delivery
due to growing demands and fluctuating energy supply. Integrating varied energy sources is essential to
enhance grid reliability, stabilize energy output, and ensure responsiveness to energy disruptions. PSH is
a critical technology to support this integration by providing long-duration energy storage and facilitating
the incorporation for variable renewable energy (VRE), such as wind and solar generation (Cohen and
Mowers 2022). Long-duration energy storage is crucial to enable continuous energy dispatch and reduced
dependence on a single category of energy sources to reduce energy pricing based on global market
fluctuations, such as that experienced with traditional coal or gas-fired power plants. A hydro-hybrid, or
TrybridTM setup, which combines PSH with battery storage and solar power, presents a promising
solution. This integrated design reduces VRE curtailment, enhances grid stability, and enables rapid
response to fluctuations and blackouts, playing a vital role in maintaining reliable energy.

 2

 CCE has received a preliminary permit from the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) for
the Corral Summit PSH facility by upgrading the Mackay Reservoir in Custer County, Idaho. CCE plans
to incorporate the TrybridTM setup at Corral Summit by co-locating PV solar arrays and battery storage
near the PSH facility. Large-volume, long-duration (LVLD) PSH projects, such as Corral Summit, offer a
long-term solution for local and reliable generation for rural electric cooperatives in Eastern Idaho.
 Mackay Reservoir is a significant man-made lake, offering both ecological and recreational value.
Created by the Mackay Dam on the Big Lost River, the reservoir is situated against the backdrop of the
Lost River Range, with the town of Mackay lying downstream to its southeast (Figure 2). The dam and
reservoir were originally constructed to support irrigation, storing and regulating water to assist regional
agriculture activities. The reservoir spans approximately 486 hectares and reaches depths up to 36.6 m.
Figure 3 and Figure 4 shows the reservoir storage capacity during the 2024 calendar year and for the
2000–2024 period, respectively.

 The reservoir is an important habitat for various species that are stocked by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife
Service, including rainbow trout and kokanee salmon, making it a popular destination for fishing
enthusiasts. Additional recreational activities include boating, camping, and birdwatching. Surrounded by
a rugged landscape that includes towering peaks of the Lost River Range and the White Knob Mountains,
Mackay Reservoir also offers scenic views (Figure 5). The geological landscape includes varied rock

formations, evidence of past seismic activity, and a history of mining, all of which contribute to its unique
character. Overall, Mackay Reservoir is a vital resource for both the local community and visitors,
blending practical utility with natural and recreational appeal.
 Built in 1918 on the Big Lost River, Mackay Dam is an earthen structure vulnerable to geological
hazards due to its proximity to the Lost River Fault. As a century-old dam, its original construction
materials and design may not align with contemporary engineering standards, necessitating ongoing
maintenance and periodic assessments to ensure its continued stability. The 1983 Borah Peak earthquake
exemplifies the potential threat of seismic activity to the dam’s structural integrity. Additionally, such
hydrological factors as seasonal water-level fluctuations and extreme-weather events further challenge the
dam’s stability by exerting variable pressures, adding to the risks associated with its age and geological
context. The dam’s stability relies heavily on the geotechnical properties of the surrounding area,
including the composition and stability of the earthen materials. Regular geotechnical evaluations are
essential to detect potential issues such as erosion, seepage, or other structural weaknesses. Consequently,
diligent maintenance and monitoring by relevant authorities are vital to address any weaknesses or
deterioration, ensuring the safety and functionality of Mackay Dam in the face of these multifaceted
challenges.

 3.1. Physical Characteristics
 The physical-characteristics category assesses the suitability of a location based on its natural and
geophysical attributes. This category includes key factors such as topography, distance between
reservoirs, hydraulic head, reservoir size and depth, non-power water constraints, and local geology.
These characteristics aid in determining the feasibility of constructing and operating the proposed
infrastructure while accounting for physical considerations and engineering requirements.
3.1.1.        Geology
 The geology subcategory reports on the examination of minerals, rocks, and fossils, as well as the
understanding of natural phenomena like earthquakes, volcanic activity, and plate tectonics. The ranking
criteria would be based on the favorability of the site’s geologic condition. Favorable criteria would
include no nearby volcanoes, fault lines, history of earthquakes, or no known Karst environments.
Unfavorable criteria would include active fault lines and underlying soils that would require
reinforcement.
 The geology surrounding Mackay Reservoir in Idaho is a complex and diverse tapestry that reflects
the dynamic geological history of the Basin and Range Province. The area is dominated by Paleozoic
sedimentary rocks, including extensive formations of limestone and dolostone, which have undergone
significant deformation and uplift over millions of years. The prominent Lost River Fault, a major normal
fault, has played a crucial role in shaping the landscape, contributing to seismic activity such as the
notable 1983 Borah Peak earthquake. The rugged terrain of the Lost River Range, which includes Borah
Peak—the highest point in Idaho—illustrates the significant tectonic-uplift characteristic of the region.
Additionally, the area exhibits evidence of past glaciation, with distinctive glacial valleys and moraines
marking the terrain. While recent volcanic activity is not prevalent in the immediate vicinity of Mackay
Reservoir, the broader region’s volcanic history has contributed to its geological complexity.
Furthermore, the region is historically significant for its rich mineral deposits, particularly around the
town of Mackay, where mining operations have extracted copper, lead, silver, and zinc. These geological
features collectively underscore the intricate interplay of tectonic, volcanic, and glacial processes that
have shaped the Mackay Reservoir area.
3.1.1.1. Site 1 Geology—Challis National Forest Region
 Site 1 is situated within the Challis National Forest, an area geologically defined by the Challis
Volcanic Group, a widespread Eocene-aged formation that blankets much of south-central Idaho. This
volcanic assemblage is composed primarily of dacite, andesite, and rhyolite tuffs and lava flows, with
lesser occurrences of basalt and latite. These volcanic rocks also extend into eastern and northern Idaho,
where they are locally associated with the Absaroka Volcanic Group near Henrys Lake, as documented by
the Idaho Geological Survey.
 Just north of the site lies the rugged terrain of the White Knob Mountains. These mountains are
predominantly made up of deformed Paleozoic sedimentary rocks—limestone, dolostone, and shale—that
have been uplifted through significant tectonic activity. The region is intersected by several fault systems,
most notably the Lost River fault, which has played a major role in shaping the local topography and
remains a source of seismic activity, as evidenced by the 1983 Borah Peak earthquake.
 The White Knob Mountains also have a rich mining legacy. Since the late 1800s, the area has been
mined for valuable metals including copper, lead, silver, and zinc. This long history of extraction has left
behind a landscape marked by historic mining sites and altered geomorphology. Additionally, glacial

during the Pleistocene. This site is located under the “m” in the (Bello et al. 2021)
3.1.1.2. Site 2 Geology—Lost River Mountain Range
 Site 2 is located near the southwestern flank of Idaho’s Lost River Range, a prominent and actively
uplifting fault-block mountain range at the northern edge of the Basin and Range Province. The site is
underlain by Pleistocene and Pliocene-aged sediments—primarily older gravels, sands, and silts—
deposited by ancient fans, streams, and lakes. These deposits include terrace gravels and the Tuana Gravel
formation, which is also found northwest of Twin Falls, as noted by the Idaho Geological Survey.
 The Lost River Range itself rises dramatically due to extensional faulting, which has uplifted the
range relative to the adjacent Big Lost River basin to the west. This tectonic activity has produced steep,
rugged slopes with minimal foothill development. The interior and eastern portions of the range are
deeply dissected by the Pahsimeroi and Little Lost Rivers, carving through thick sequences of Paleozoic
limestone and dolomite that dominate the central and southern parts of the range. Multiple episodes of
tectonic deformation have shaped the region’s geology, resulting in striking open to isoclinal folds that
range in scale from meters to kilometers. Glacial processes have also left their mark, with numerous
cirques and alpine lake basins scattered throughout the central and eastern highlands. These glacial
features, combined with the region’s tectonic complexity, have created a dramatic landscape of high-
angle faces and steep ridgelines with western, northern, and eastern exposures.

 The site lies just 2.4 km south of the Lost River fault system, which ruptured during the 1983 Borah
Peak earthquake—one of the most-significant seismic events in Idaho’s recent history (Figure 6). This
proximity to an active fault zone, along with the area’s diverse sedimentary and structural features, makes
Site 2 a compelling location at which to study the interplay of tectonics, sedimentation, and glacial
processes in shaping the northern Basin and Range. Site is located to the NE of the “m” on the (Bello et
al. 2021). Note the fault scarp is denoted as the black and red lines along the mountain front.
 Local geology plays a significant role in the decision-making process. Sites 1 and 2 exhibit markedly
different lithologies (Skipp et al. 2009) (Figure 7). To the southwest, the composition of Site 1 (Figure 8)
consists entirely of the Tertiary-age Challis Volcanic Group, specifically including dacite flows and
domes (Tcdu), rhyolitic intrusions and dikes (Trpi) volcaniclastic sediments (Tscu), and latite lavas and
intrusions (Tcll). Conversely, Site 2, to the northeast, is composed of Quaternary- and Tertiary-age
alluvial deposits. The more consolidated and erosion-resistant igneous rock present at Site 1 is likely to be
more suitable for reservoir construction. Furthermore, it should be noted that normal faults are present

 8

beneath both the Corral Summit 1 and 2 sites, although the seismic risk posed by these structures remains
undetermined.

 For this analysis, areal and volumetric analyses of the potential reservoir configurations were carried

 Five dam scenarios were created for Site 2 to determine the most-feasible design, based on site

transmission lines. Scenario 5 (Figure 12) addresses the challenges in Scenario 4 by relocating the second
dam upstream, with a length of 0.19 km and max height of 20 m, to prevent such flooding as in
Scenario 4 while still maintaining a high average water column height (28 m) and a sizeable storage
capacity of 10,811,600 m3.
 Based on the analysis of all five dam scenarios (Table 8), Scenario 5 stands out as the optimal
configuration for Site 2. It strategically prevents flooding of critical infrastructure while offering a
significantly high storage capacity and a high average water-column height (28 m) suitable for efficient
hydropower generation. Though Scenario 4 maximizes storage, it poses logistical challenges due to

 15

infrastructure submergence. Scenario 3 also provides a structurally sound alternative, but a reduced water
storage capacity.
 Site 2 is underlain by alluvium fill deposits, and it is also placed close to the Lost River Fault, which
would present some geotechnical challenges.
• Alluvial deposits consist of poorly consolidated materials that will make dams inherently less stable
 than bedrock foundations.
• If the alluvium is particularly fine-grained or loosely compacted, it can lead to excessive settlement of
 any dam structure constructed, especially over time and under the weight of impounded water.
 Differential settlement is also possible across the dam base and can cause cracking of the dam body,
 resulting in seepage.
• Alluvial deposits contain permeable zones that can serve as seepage pathways beneath or around the
 dam. Uncontrolled seepage can lead to piping failures and undermine the stability of the dam.
• Being just 1.9 km from the Lost River fault places the site well within the zone of seismic hazard in
 the event of any fault activity. Alluvial fills are prone to seismic settlement, which could compromise
 any dam body constructed by causing cracks in the dam body or foundation.

3.1.6. Non-Power Water Constraints
 The non-power water-constraints subcategory defines the limitations or restrictions on the use of
water resources for purposes other than generating power (e.g., agricultural irrigation, industrial
processes, municipal water supply, environmental conservation, recreation). Non-power water constraints
for Mackay Reservoir primarily involve irrigation demands, water-rights disputes, aging infrastructure,
and aquifer interaction, all of which are exacerbated by drought conditions. The reservoir serves the Big
Lost River Irrigation District and irrigates 15,176 hectares of farmland downstream. The main purpose of
Mackay Dam is to store water for irrigation of farmland in the Big Lost River Valley. The Big Lost River
Irrigation District manages water releases for its patrons, and unused water allocations are typically lost at
the end of the irrigation season. The reservoir frequently does not fill completely during low-runoff years,
impacting irrigation supplies. In these years, the system is less resilient to drought. It is also important to
note that significant portions of the Big Lost River lose water to the underlying aquifer, further straining
surface water supplies. This subsurface loss is especially pronounced below the Mackay Dam.
 Note that, Site 1, BLM land, is multi-use purpose, so Site 1 might require more integration of non-
power requirements. Site 2, Idaho Trust Land, is for maximum revenue, so it might not have as many
restrictions. The equivalence of a reservoir capacity of 1 million m2 square meters (1 Hm3), or 1 MW of
hydropower installed capacity as the threshold for inclusion of existing reservoirs under Topologies 1 and
2, was considered inappropriate. In effect, many reservoirs with less than 1 Hm3 of water capacity yield
much higher hydropower capacities than 1 MW, as shown in Figure 13.

3.2.1.1. Surface-Water Baseline Characteristics
 The current conditions of Mackay Reservoir and Big Lost River were defined using recent reports
from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), Idaho Department of Water Resources (IDWR),
and the USGS. Mackay Reservoir can only store about 20% of the average annual flow of the Big Lost

prioritize areas for shade tree plantings along the Big Lost River to address temperature impairment (EPA
2011).

River Basin. The surface-water samples had a range from 8.9 to 20.3°C, with an average of 15.08°C
(Womeldorph and Steimke 2022). There were no EPA maximum contaminant level (MCL) exceedances
for major ions or metals in any samples tested, and nitrate concentrations were below the EPA MCL of
10 mg/L for all samples.

current impairments within the watershed. In addition, the surface-water samples had no EPA MCL
exceedances. The temperature of the water did not impact ranking criteria. Therefore, both sites’ baseline
for surface water was ranked as neutral for this report.

3.2.1.2. Groundwater-Quality Baseline Characteristics
 The current conditions of the underlying aquifer of the Big Lost River Basin are defined using recent
reports from IDWR and USGS. A distinctive feature of the Big Lost River Basin is the large interchange
of water from surface streams to groundwater as a large portion of the Big Lost River disappears
underground near Chilly, Darlington, and other sinks and reappears around Mackay Narrows and in other
reaches (Crosthwaite et al. 1970). Another characteristic of the basin is that quick snowmelt rarely
reaches the river by overland flow, but flows underground (Crosthwaite et al. 1970). Another
characteristic of the basin is that quick snowmelt rarely reaches the river by overland flow. In the IDWR
2020 study, which characterized the current groundwater conditions from 50 wells across the basin, the
groundwater temperature ranged from 7 to 18.2°C in the 42 groundwater wells, averaging 9.2°C in the
September collection (Womeldorph and Steimke 2022).
 IDWR characterization from 50 wells across the basin in 2020 found that the dominant water type for
the samples was calcium bicarbonate, which implies water that has undergone minimal geochemical
change from its source (Womeldorph and Steimke 2022). Due to a substantive interchange of the surface
water and groundwater, the chemical composition of the waters is expected to be similar. While no EPA
MCLs for major ions or metals were exceeded in any samples, the IDWR 2020 study identified two
groundwater wells with manganese concentrations near EPA secondary maximum contaminant levels
(SMCLs) of 0.05 mg/L and two groundwater wells exceeding the EPA iron SMCL of 0.3 mg/L. Although
no EPA MCL for groundwater were exceeded, the two wells exceeding the SMCLs resulted in the overall
groundwater score to be neutral.

3.2.1.3. Groundwater Volume
 Groundwater in the Big Lost River Valley region is primarily recharged by precipitation and seepage
from streams and irrigation canals. Additional recharge occurs from infiltration caused by excess
irrigation application. Groundwater discharge supports wetlands, streams, and the Eastern Snake River
Plain Aquifer, while withdrawals are made through wells (Sukow 2024). Surface water infiltrates the
underlying aquifer in recharge pits and targeted reaches of the Big Lost River (Dudunake and Zinsser
2021). Aquifer storage in the Big Lost River Basin may fluctuate by more than 1.23 x 108 m3 in extremely
wet or dry years (Sukow 2024). Since the mid-1980s, water levels have declined more severely during
droughts, and the aquifer has not recovered during wet years, leading to a steady decline of approximately
0.08 m per year. A majority of the groundwater diversions in the Big Lost River Basin occur in the valley
south of Mackay Dam. This is observed in the wells across the basin, as shown in Figure 14.
 While changes in stress may result in seasonal changes in groundwater gradients and flow direction,
the hydrographs around Mackay Reservoir demonstrate that water-level fluctuations within that area are
moderated by the dam. The water supply below Mackay Dam is highly dependent on available upstream

variability in local groundwater conditions (Clark 2022). Due to the current trend of the water level’s

agriculture, both sites are ranked with disfavor.

3.2.2.1. Functionality

 Of the species listed, brook trout and yellow perch have specific habitat requirements. Brook trout

 A distinctive feature of the Big Lost River basin is the large interchange of water from surface
streams to groundwater as a large portion of the Big Lost River disappears underground near Chilly,
Darlington, and other sinks and reappears near Mackay Narrows and in other reaches (Crosthwaite et al.
1970). Gages on the four principal channels discharging into Mackay Reservoir monitored surface inflow
for more than 40 years. The data indicate that the inflow from ground water comes principally from the
west side of the valley although much water undoubtedly flows underground from mountains to the east
(Crosthwaite et al. 1970). Leakage at the toe of the dam tends to return to surface flow below the dam
while seepage below and around Mackay Dam tends to return to the river above the Narrows, where
limestone constricts both sides of the valley (Clark 2022).

3.2.2.2. Hydrological Processes
 The current water balance in the system was evaluated using the groundwater budgets for the Big
Lost River Basin, south central Idaho (Clark 2022). Within the Big Lost River Basin, groundwater is
extremely interconnected with surface water and has an amplified response to weather-pattern variation
and extreme weather events.
 The groundwater budget measures the inflow (recharge) and outflow (discharge) from groundwater.
The predominant sources of recharge to the Big Lost River Basin aquifer include the surface-water
underground reaches (33%), precipitation (subtracting the evapotranspiration and surface runoff-
comprising about 23%), underflow from snowmelt (20%), canal seepage (13%), recharge from irrigation
infiltration and runoff (11%), and Mackay Reservoir seepage (less than 1%). The primary source of
discharge is groundwater pumping to meet irrigation demand, domestic supply, and municipal supply
(76%) and river reaches where the groundwater becomes surface water (24%) (Clark 2022).
 The Big Lost River basin above Mackay Dam typically uses the groundwater inputs from snowmelt
and precipitation to meet irrigation demand above Mackay Dam and to provide for water supply below
the dam (Clark 2022). The water supply below Mackay Dam is highly dependent on available upstream
surface-water flows, the magnitude of the groundwater residual from above the dam, and annual
variability in local groundwater conditions (Clark 2022).
 In order to fill the constructed upper reservoir, surface water would be diverted from any release
above 17 m3/s over a period of 2–4 years (see Appendix A). This will affect the surface water and
subsequent groundwater downstream because the reaches below the dam tend to disappear underground
unless there is a significant storm event. After 3–4 years following the first diversion, the upper reservoir
would reach total capacity, and only a small input may be needed to cover the evaporation and potential
seepage losses.
 Overall, there will be a significant impact on the surface and groundwater downstream of the
reservoir as the upper reservoir is filled. The area will be eliminated as an opportunity for groundwater
infiltration due to the liner, and further study will be required to understand the difference between
potential seepage and the current infiltration. For both sites this would be neutral in the long term and
disfavored in the short term (Table 19 and Table 20).

3.3.2. Grid Interconnection
 In addition to power-plant infrastructure, transmission systems are necessary to export power to
desired customers. Building the power plant near existing power lines reduces initial investment and
decreases losses caused by power exchange in both pumping and generating modes, thereby increasing
overall efficiency and positively impacting the plant’s economics. Two transmission lines are located near
the site: a 110 kV line owned by Salmon River Electric Cooperative and a 230 kV transmission line
owned by Bonneville Power Administration (BPA). Both lines serve downstream load centers managed
by BPA and the Salmon River Electric Cooperative. However, these transmission lines currently lack the
capacity to export the power produced by the hybrid plant. The nearest substation available for connection
is the Lost River Substation, approximately 40 km from the project location (Figure 21). A 31.3-mile 230-
kV BPA line, rated at 195 kVA, connects the Lost River Substation to the Antelope Substation. The
Antelope Substation provides direct access to three large utilities—Idaho Power Corporation, PacifiCorp,
and Northwestern Energy—where the Trybrid plant can sell its power.

 Figure 21. Transmission lines and the project site.
 It is clear that a new transmission line must be constructed between the project site and the Lost River
Substation. Given that the power generated by the TrybridTM plant could be employed for the downstream

3.3.3. Intake Structure
 An upper-reservoir intake structure is a critical component of the PSH plant, designed to facilitate the
bidirectional flow of water between the upper reservoir and the powerhouse. During periods of low
electricity demand, the structure operates as an outlet for water pumped from the lower reservoir into the
upper reservoir. Conversely, during high demand, it functions as an intake, allowing water to flow back
down from the upper reservoir to generate power. The design of the intake structure is influenced by
hydraulic, environmental, and structural factors to ensure efficient and reliable operation.
 Favorable criteria would involve sites with stable geology and terrain to support the structural
stability, minimal environmental constraints, such as low likelihood of fish population or debris clogging
risks, and locations with optimal flow conditions that minimize hydraulic losses without extensive
suppression measures.
 Unfavorable criteria would involve locations that have high environmental stability, such as a fish-
populated reservoir, ice formation, or excessive wind that would cause wave formation; unstable geology
that would require more-extensive geotechnical work; or complex hydraulic conditions to require
extensive vortex suppression methods.
 The intake structure must handle bidirectional flow, which introduces design complexities unique to
PSH plants. Key hydraulic considerations include preventing vortex formation and maximizing hydraulic
efficiency. Air entrainment caused by vortex formation can result in vibration, uneven loading on the
pump-turbine, and cavitation, leading to machinery damage and pitting. The design must suppress
vortices using features such as minimum submergence, which depends on flow rate and intake geometry.
The intake should also minimize hydraulic losses from geometric design of the entrance and exit, friction,
bends, gates, and trash racks. At this stage, neither site has hydraulic studies; therefore, Site 1 and Site 2
are not evaluated for this category (Table 29).
 Environmental impacts must also be addressed with intake-structure design. This includes fish
protection, debris management, and water quality. If the upper reservoir is intentionally populated with
fish, the design would need to incorporate measures to prevent harm to aquatic life. Fish can unintendedly
be populated through endozoochory (Nuwer 2020). It is unknown if either site would be intentionally
populated with fish. Site 1 and Site 2 are located in a scrub/shrub environment, which are prolific with
tumbleweeds. The prevalence of tumbleweeds in aquatic environments can result in the blockage of
water, impacting hydropower operations (Osterloff 2021). The National Weather Service indicates that
the record-low temperatures in Mackay Lost River, Idaho is –-2.8°C, with a normal low temperature of -
16.7°C (National Weather Service 2024). Such low temperatures indicate a potential for ice formation,
which the intake structure and associated components must be designed against.
 Structurally, the intake must be built to withstand fluctuating water levels and pressures over its
operational lifetime. Trash racks are essential to prevent large debris from entering the water passages and
damaging the pump-turbines. Additionally, the intake opening must maintain low water velocity to avoid
screen vibration, air entrainment, and excessive head loss. Structural considerations for the sites are
pulled from Section 3.1.1. Site 1 has hard, consolidated rocks located at a distance from faults. Site 2 has
loose, weathered, unconsolidated material close to Lost River fault.

3.4.2. Licensing and Regulation
 The land ownership and permitting requirements for the proposed reservoir sites were evaluated using
information from the BLM, the Idaho Department of Lands, and Custer County zoning regulations.
Favorable rankings reflect positive steps or incentives facilitating project development while unfavorable
rankings highlight challenges or delays in permitting processes. Neutral rankings indicate minimal

 Site 1, located southwest of Mackay Reservoir, lies on BLM-administered land within the Challis
Field Office of the BLM Idaho Falls District. Under the FLPMA of 1976, BLM-administered land
supports multiple-use principles, including recreation, grazing, and energy development (43 USC 35).
Developing energy projects on BLM land requires obtaining a right-of-way (ROW) permit, which
authorizes specific uses of public lands for a defined term if there are any appreciable disturbance or
alteration of public lands. ROW permits undergo rigorous review, including evaluating whether the
application conforms to the resource management plant (RMP) for the area and whether there are no other
conflicts with authorization. As stated in Sections 3.2.3.2 and 3.2.3.4, BLM land is subject to Sage-
Grouse PHMA and BLM sensitive-species list restrictions. For hydropower projects, stringent exemption
criteria apply in areas identified as PHMA. In addition, ROWs on BLM lands are under additional
scrutiny by the Secretary of the Interior for solar projects (a component of the Corral Summit) (H.R. 1 and
EO 14315).
 Site 2, east of Mackay Reservoir, is located on Idaho State Endowment Trust Land, which is
governed under Article IX, Section 8 of the Idaho Constitution to maximize long-term revenue.
Developing energy projects on state endowment lands requires securing an energy lease through the Idaho
Department of Lands. The lease typically includes ROW permissions for utilities such as pipelines, power
lines, and roads required for project development. Energy leases, including solar and hydropower, are
approved under the Energy Leasing Policy with a typical lease term of 49 years (Idaho Department of
Lands 2023). The specific location of Site 2 would require an application to be submitted and a
subsequent proposal that ensures the project is in the best interest of the endowment beneficiaries, which
include Idaho public schools. This site is also under the PHMA.

 The cultural resources and sensitivity category refers to the presence or absence of Native American
and other historic resources connected to a specific population or cultural group of the past or present and

 The ranking criteria are based on specific factors influencing involvement with and evaluation of
cultural resources and tribal presence at the two sites. Favorable criteria include past assessments that
determine the lack of cultural and historic resources and tribal concerns revolving around the areas of
consideration. Conversely, disfavored ranking would include the lack of past evaluations, known presence

3.5.1. Federally Recognized Tribes
 The federally recognized tribes category for the proposed reservoir sites used information from FERC
regulations, federal policies, the U.S. Federal Government, the U.S. Department of Justice, and the
respective tribes’ websites. Favorable rankings reflect minimal tribal consultation requirements while
disfavored rankings indicate additional compliance measures tied to Tribal resources or interests. Neutral
rankings signify no significant impact on project execution due to Tribal engagement obligations.
 Since the development of the Corral Summit PSH Project and construction of the upper reservoir
would require a FERC license, this section discusses FERC’s stance on federally recognized tribes. FERC
prioritizes tribal input in hydropower licensing and other proceedings that may affect tribal interests. The
agency engages federally recognized tribes through its Office of External Affairs, Office of Energy
Projects, and Office of the General Council, as well as its Office of Public Participation for individual
tribal members. Under trust responsibility, FERC ensures compliance with the Federal Power Act,
National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA) Section 106, and other federal statutes in its environmental
and decision-making documents. According to FERC (2025) Tribal Relations, tribal consultation is
required if projects affect cultural resources, sacred sites, or tribal lands.
 Federal engagement with tribes is required under Section 106 when affecting tribal properties or
cultural sites. Federal agencies must notify tribal historic preservation officers (THPOs) or designated
tribal representatives with actions that occur on tribal lands or impact cultural resources. Section 110(f) of
the NHPA requires agencies to minimize harm to national historic landmarks (NHLs) that could be
impacted during the review processes.
 EO 14156, “Declaring a National Energy Emergency,” was signed on January 20, 2025, which
instructs the heads of federal agencies to use any lawful emergency authorities to facilitate domestic
energy production on federal lands, including expediting the processes. The NHPA established the
Advisory Council on Historic Preservation (ACHP) as a federal agency to address historic-preservation
issues (ACHP 1966). The ACHP provides advice on the availability of existing emergency provisions in
Section 106 regulations for EO 14156. During EO 14156, agencies such as BLM can follow an expedited
consultation process to address impacts on historic properties, which reduces a typical 30-day comment
period to 7-days (NHPA Section 800.12(b)(2)). In addition, Section 110(f), 54 U.S.C. §306107 of the
NHPA requires agencies to undertake the necessary planning and actions to minimize adverse effects to
NHLs, even in an energy emergency. Since the project occurs off tribal lands, if agencies choose to

 44

execute an emergency action, they must identify and notify tribes that may have properties of religious
and cultural significance in the geographical region (36 CFR §800.16[x]).
 Knowledge of the religious and cultural significance of the geographical region—including traditional
cultural properties, sacred sites, named places, and subsistence locations (e.g., hunting, fishing, root
collection, etc.)—are usually not shared outside of the tribal cultural group unless necessary. The areas
that contain these sites are part of a group’s cultural heritage and are often identified in treaties as “usual
and accustomed” areas or “ceded lands.” The specific location of these types of resources are often
confirmed through consultation between agencies and tribal governments and are protected in some
degree both by EO 13007, which directs federal land-managing agencies to accommodate access to, and
ceremonial use of, sacred sites, and by the American Indian Religious Freedom Act, which aims to
protect the rights of Native Americans to practice their traditional religions on sacred sites.
 There are five federally recognized tribes in Idaho (Figure 23): the Shoshone-Bannock, Shoshone-
Paiute, Coeur d’Alene, Kootenai, and Nez Perce Tribes. Most of Idaho’s Native American population
resides on four reservations, the Fort Hall Reservation (Shoshone-Bannock), Coeur d’Alene Reservation,
Kootenai Reservation, and the Nez Perce Reservation. The Duck Valley Reservation, on which the
Shoshone-Paiute Tribes reside, is shared with Nevada (U.S. Department of Justice 2021). These tribes
operate sovereign governments and manage cultural, economic, and social activities that contribute to
Idaho’s economy and community well-being (Shoshone-Bannock Tribes 2025).
 Though none of the federally recognized tribes listed have reservation lands bordering on or within
the proposed project site, the significance of the geographical region may still be relevant because
ancestral lands of the tribes occupied vast regions of land. The tribes of Shoshone-Bannock encompassed
Idaho, Oregon, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, Montana, and into Canada (Shoshone-Bannock Tribes 2025).
The tribes of the Shoshone-Paiute ranged from Idaho, Nevada, and Oregon (Sho-Pai Tribes 2025). The
Coeur d’Alene (Schitsu’umsh) Tribe’s original homeland spanned 2 million hectares, from Montana to
the Spokane River Valley, the Canadian border to the confluence of the Snake and Clearwater Rivers in
northern Idaho (Coeur d'Alene Tribe 2025). The Kootenai territories extended from Western Montana
into parts of Idaho, British Columbia, and Wyoming (Confederated Salish and Kootenai Tribes 2025).
The Nez Perce Tribes (Nimiipuu Oeople) previously occupied an area that included parts of Idaho,
Oregon, and Washington although they traveled into parts of Montana and Wyoming to fish, hunt, and
trade (Nez Perce Tribe 2025).

3.5.2.2. Executive Orders on Energy Emergency and Impact on Permitting

environmental-permitting timelines for energy-development projects. The ACHP, which oversees cultural
resources compliance, has opted to apply 36 CFR 800.12, an implementing regulation of the NHPA. This

likelihood for implementation on federally managed BLM land. Given the potential advantages and
drawbacks, this factor is considered equally favorable and disfavorable at both locations.

approaches to ensure equitable and optimal siting outcomes.

3.6.4. Social Acceptance
 Social acceptance is shaped by factors such as perceived environmental impacts, economic benefits to
surrounding communities, and alignment with local values. Favorable rating would identify sites that
have known positive opinions by citizens toward the components of the Corral Summit PSH Project, such
as energy generation or surface-water reservoir. Disfavorable ratings would indicate that citizens are
generally against the development of such a project.
 Hydropower can have a negative connotation, with perceptions that hydropower-dam operations are
responsible for the extinction and near-extinction of species and have a significant loss of aquatic
biodiversity, leave rivers dry or ranging within drought and flood conditions, diminish recreation
opportunities, and decimate local rural economies. Some believe dams do not live up to environmental
standards; further, some fear visual considerations and viewsheds will be altered (Idaho Rivers United
n.d.).
 However, the local region and Idaho as a whole seem to have a favorable opinion of hydropower. At
the local level, initial meetings with municipal leaders from the City of Mackay were positive towards
establishment of this project. These favorable views were likely motivated by the understanding that a
benefit of this project would entail rebuilding Mackay Dam, which many interested parties view as
something the region needs.
 At the state level, Idaho offers tax incentives for data centers. For example, Meta is currently
constructing an $800 million, 89,187 m2 data center on the east side of Kuna, and the rise in data centers
will require vast amounts of power. Recent roundtable discussions indicate that there is a lack of
information regarding energy systems, opportunities for energy conservation, the politicization of energy,
and fear around the privatization of nuclear energy with the increase in data centers (Boise State
University College of Business and Economics 2024).
 Broadly, support for hydropower is high in general. A survey showed that less than 30% of
respondents favor removing dams, 1200 Northwest residents (400 in Idaho, Oregon, and Washington)
support the use of hydroelectric dams on the lower Snake River to produce electricity (Weaver 2021).
Users believed removing dams would increase electricity costs, which would impact agriculture.
 With limited information, both sites are ranked the same due to their proximity. Both are ranked as

 3.7. Power Market and the Grid
 The power market and grid category evaluates the ability of the proposed project sites to efficiently
integrate generated energy into the electric grid and access viable power-market opportunities. This
category considers factors such as grid limitations and available power market options for distributing and
selling electricity to downstream users. Ensuring compatibility with the grid and accessing favorable
market conditions is vital to maximize project efficiency and economic returns.
 Ranking criteria are based on the extent of grid constraints and market accessibility. Sites with
inadequate transmission infrastructure or requiring extensive upgrades to export power would receive
lower scores due to added costs and delays. Favorable criteria include proximity to high-capacity
transmission lines, minimal infrastructure retrofits, and accessibility to competitive power markets. These

3.7.1. Limitations in a Power Grid
 As discussed in Section 3.3.2, the current transmission lines available near the project sites lack
capacity to export the generated power. New transmission lines, tower structures, and substations must be
built and existing infrastructures, retrofitted to connect the TrybridTM project to the Antelope substation,
from which utilities could purchase the bulk power generated by the TrybridTM plant.
 The increasing difficulty in deploying new electricity transmission suggests that the criterion of
distance to the electricity grid is as much as an environmental and social criterion as it is an economic
one. Whenever feasible, upgrading the transmission capacity of existing lines is faster and more efficient
than building new lines because it leverages existing infrastructure, avoids lengthy permitting and land-
acquisition processes, involves less construction, and faces fewer environmental and community
challenges.
 The current plan is to build transmission capacity to the Lost River Substation, in which case there
will be no significant difference between the two sites. If existing 230 kV BPA line is to be used, it will
have to be reinforced significantly to increase the transmission capacity up to Lost River substation. There
is no significant difference between the two sites regarding power-grid limitations. However, if existing
right-of-way BPA lines are to be used, Site 2 is located closer to the BPA lines than Site 1, except for
their proximity to existing transmission lines that can be utilized. Given the limited capacity of the current
lines, it is likely that upgrades will be necessary in either case. The recommended maximum distance to a
suitable grid connection is 10 km, based on the SETIS expert workshop on the assessment of the potential
of pumped hydropower storage, as per Arántegui et al. (2012). However, if existing right-of-way BPA
lines will be used, Site 2 would be closer to the BPA lines and ranked more favorable.

 4.1. Physical Characteristics
 Although the seismic risk posed by these structures remains undetermined, faults are present beneath
both the Corral Summit 1 and 2 sites. However, more recent movement has been experienced on the Lost
River Fault, as recent as 1983 when the Borah Peak earthquake produced a 6.9 magnitude earthquake
roughly 19.3 km northwest of the project site. It is therefore recommended to conduct a full seismic
survey to understand historic faulting and fault-recurrence intervals. It is recommended that more-detailed
site-specific investigations be conducted as well to understand geologic suitability including drilling of
core holes to sufficient depth to characterize the subsurface and its suitability to host a new reservoir on
either site.
 It is also recommended to carry further assessment regarding specific penstock locations and
competing energy constraints. Furthermore, areal and volumetric analysis of the potential reservoir
configurations is also recommended to have a clear understanding of multiple factors: number of dams
required (if appropriate), excavation to create a bowl-shaped reservoir (if appropriate), required cut-fill,
storage volume, water depth, etc. Further assessment is recommended to consider the horizontal distance
between the two reservoirs, and potential head offered from each.

 57

 Note that, the main purpose of Mackay Reservoir is to store water for irrigation of farmland in the Big
Lost River Valley. Also, significant non-power water constraints exist for Mackay Reservoir. These
primarily involve irrigation demands, water-rights disputes, aging infrastructure, and aquifer interaction,
all of which are exacerbated by drought conditions. The Big Lost River Irrigation District manages water
releases for its patrons, and unused water allocations are typically lost at the end of the irrigation season.
It is important to understand what requirements Big Lost River Irrigation District holds to divert water to
an upper reservoir. Note that when the new reservoir is the upper reservoir, its usable volume is limited by
the usable water in the lower reservoir. If the latter is an existing reservoir, water currently used for other
purposes should be taken into account as should the environmental impact of a changing shore level.
 4.2. Environmental Constraints
 Due to the distance between the two proposed sites and the current data, the sites share similar
characteristics. Therefore, scoring for environmental constraints closely match. The differentiator is the
BLM species list that only applies to Site 1. Next steps would include additional groundwater-well
installation and monitoring at both sites and habitat assessments. Additional groundwater wells could
provide data that will differentiate the sites based on groundwater quality and quantity and could affect
the functionality and hydrological conclusions. Habitat assessments would narrow the list of species at the
site. Currently, the federal- and state-listed species are identical for the sites, and further analysis would
narrow the list, which would support the NEPA analysis and U.S. Fish and Wildlife consultation.
 4.3. Building Infrastructure
 A new transmission line must be constructed to connect the project site with the Lost River
Substation. Because the power generated by the TrybridTM plant can serve downstream loads of both the
Salmon River Electric Cooperative and the Lost River Electric Cooperative, the full 195-MVA
transmission capacity between the Lost River and Antelope Substations can be allocated to the TrybridTM
project. However, the high installed capacity of solar PV may lead to transmission constraints during
peak-generation periods. While retrofitting the existing transmission line may be necessary, both PSH and
battery energy-storage systems will play a critical role in smoothing the generation profile. This will help
reduce the overall investment required for upgrades to transmission infrastructure.
 Constructing new transmission lines would incur additional costs and face regulatory challenges,
particularly related to land-acquisition and right-of-way issues. New transmission towers would also be
required to support the lines. In contrast, retrofitting existing infrastructure is more cost-effective because
it can leverage existing rights of way and involve reconductoring to increase transmission capacity.
Additionally, a new transmission substation will be required to deliver power generated from the Mackay
Reservoir powerhouse as well as from both land-based and floating solar installations. The existing Lost
River Substation would also need to be upgraded to expand the capacity of key components, including
transformers, circuit breakers, disconnect switches, and bus-bar configurations. Furthermore, additional
reactive power compensation—such as static capacitors and inductors—may be necessary to support the
import of power from the TrybridTM plant.
 Additional assessments critical to project development include:
• Geotechnical assessment to evaluate structural stability for the intake structure and utilities corridor
• Hydraulic assessment to provide input for the intake-structure and utilities-corridor hydraulic designs
• Fish population decision of the upper reservoir to provide additional services to the community.
This last decision should be made in conjunction with social factors.

 4.4. Regulatory Constraints
 Presently, Site 2, located on Idaho state land, offers a slightly more favorable ownership and licensing
position, due to the current administration’s view of solar development, the mandate to maximize profits
on Idaho State Trust lands, and habitat restrictions on BLM administered lands, where Site 1 resides.
However, preferences for particular types of energy generation may shift with the political winds in the
intervening years before this project moves to the construction phase. The recommended path forward
would be to investigate the feasibility of obtaining a right of way to develop the project on BLM and
Idaho State Trust lands, along with conditional use permits needed from Custer County for the utility and
road connections to the two locations. Either concurrently or subsequently, a FERC license application
could be initiated. Based on the findings of the NEPA process required for the FERC license application,
a preferred site may emerge. If both sites are amenable to permitting, other selection characteristics will
drive site preference. If only one site has a reasonable chance of securing a right of way and FERC
license, that will strongly drive site selection.
 Environmental regulations and energy policies appear to affect both sites equally. Similarly, because
no existing water rights exist for the installation, and Idaho has a permitting system, each site is equally
available to request water rights to fill the reservoir. There are two recommended paths of investigation
that are not site-dependent:
• Pursue water right with IDWR to fill the reservoir. This route involves working with the IDWR
 application process. The rights would be junior to all other rights in the basin but could be applied at
 diversion points upstream of Mackay, at Mackay, or groundwater withdrawal. The next step for this
 option involves working with a lawyer who specializes in water rights in conjunction with a
 hydrologist to begin talks with IDWR to craft a new water-rights application for either site.
• Pursuing a partnership with Big Lost River Irrigation District to fill the reservoir for 34-00012 and
 34-10873 over multiple years during the non-irrigation period. This would involve diverting water
 from the Big Lost River at the Mackay Reservoir diversion point while ensuring that the gauge at 2-B
 does not drop below 1.4 m3/s. The next step for this option involves working with a lawyer who
 specializes in water rights in conjunction with a hydrologist to begin talks with Big Lost River
 Irrigation District to determine if a partnership could be established to fill and maintain the new
 reservoir (regardless of site location) under 34-00012 and 34-10873.

• FERC will lead consultation with the five tribes by initiating NHPA Section 106, which has a 30-day

First Fill Analysis for New Reservoir

 66

 First Fill Analysis for New Reservoir
 Integration of a new reservoir in the overall pumped storage hydropower (PSH) project is dependent
on the construction of a new off-stream reservoir. An analysis to evaluate the timeframe for filling the
new reservoir (first fill) was performed using historical Mackay Reservoir information. The analysis used
reservoir-modeling software (HEC ResSim) that is publicly available from the U.S. Army Corps of
Engineers Hydrologic Engineering Center. Mackay Reservoir data was obtained from BLID, including
storage-elevation table, outlet, and spillway-rating curves. This information was input into HEC ResSim
to build the reservoir network. Operational rules were also developed to include in the model. There were
several major assumptions included with the operational rules. These assumptions include:
• The period of record provided by Big Lost Irrigation District (Jan 2007–Sep 2024) sufficiently
 represents the variability in hydrology for the Big Lost River
• Any historical release from Mackay above 17 m3/s is available for diversion to the new reservoir
• Pumping from Mackay Reservoir to the new off-stream storage reservoir had a fixed pumping rate of
 8.5 m3/s
• The new reservoir has a total storage capacity of 30,837,000 m3 of water.
 The main input for the HEC ResSim model was historical inflow for Mackay Reservoir. The top-
priority reservoir-model operational rule was set to match the historical reservoir outflow for Mackay
Reservoir (Figure 28). This ensures the inflow, outflow, and storage volumes for the reservoir are
consistent with historical observations.

Figure 28. HEC-ResSim output for Mackay Reservoir outflow. The operational rules forced the modeled
outflow to match historical observations.

 In evaluating historical years, it was determined that in low-water years, there is still approximately
17 m3/s maximum release required during the summer irrigation months. Therefore, any release above 17
m3/s, regardless of the season, could be considered available for diversion into the new off-stream
reservoir. The mean water available for storage between 2007 and 2024 was approximately 22,819,380
m3 (Figure 29). This suggests that a 30,837,000 off-stream reservoir would be able to be filled within
2 years. The Big Lost River has substantial hydrologic variability, and filling of the off-stream reservoir

 67

should be based on probabilities. Assuming the period of record used in this analysis (Jan 2007–Sep
2024) represents the long-term hydrologic variability in the watershed, then filling a 30,837,000 m3
reservoir in 1 year has less than a 40% chance (Figure 30); however, filling the reservoir over 2-year
period has approximately a 65% chance (Figure 31). These results indicate the new off-stream storage
reservoir would likely have enough storage to be functional within 2 years but would not reach total
storage capacity until 3–4 years following the first diversions.
