1 Executive Summary

 The objective of this project was to develop an electrochemical nutrient recovery technology –
electroN-P – for recovering a fertilizer product from wastewater centrate streams high in nitrogen and
phosphorous content. The original performance target was to have the electroN-P advance from a
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 4 to a pilot-ready TRL-6, by demonstrating continuous processing of
≥1 gallon per minute of wastewater for at least 1 month, while generating a fertilizer product with reduced
embedded energy and carbon emissions. This recovered product was expected to possess reduced
embedded energy and associated carbon emissions, while performing at a level similar to conventional
fertilizers. This associated reduced embedded energy and carbon emissions would then translate to a
reduced energy consumption and emissions in both the agricultural and residential sectors.
 The project scope entailed bench-scale reactor operation and optimization, centrate stream sampling
and characterization, conversion of the reactor from batch to continuous mode, soil and plant
characterization and techno-economic (TEA) and life-cycle (LCA) analyses across 9 tasks.
 The team converted a beaker reactor treating 250 mL of wastewater for a maximum time of 110
minutes into a continuous 4-tube (1-ft each in parallel) reactor treating 55 L of wastewater for a maximum
of 90 minutes, while recovering >80% of phosphorus during this treatment process. This results in a 220×
scale-up from the project start. The energy consumed during this recovery process was significantly less
than embedded energy in state-of-the-art fertilizers when operated at a constant voltage (at <10 mA/cm2),
while it was typically equal to or greater than the embedded energy in these fertilizers when operated at a
constant current (at tens of mA/cm2). However, operating under a constant current yielded a product that
was cost-competitive with state-of-the-art fertilizers and other recovered products from other centrate
stream treatment technologies. This is because this constant current operation would yield a smaller reactor
for the base case wastewater facility of a 700 gallons per minute (GPM) plant i.e., a 1 million gallon per
day (MGD) plant. These recovery costs could be further reduced by using recycled magnesium materials
or integrating with a larger plant, if running under constant current and using a magnesium rod as the
magnesium source. An alternate configuration using magnesium salt projects reduced overall costs and
embedded energy demand, but typically required much greater operation times at the bench-scale.
 The team also demonstrated the performance of the recovered product in soil and on plants, with the
recovered resource performing like or better than state-of-the-art fertilizers: diammonium phosphate
(DAP), triple super phosphate (TSP) and rock phosphate (RP). After two weeks in soil, readily soluble P
from the recovered product was similar to that available in DAP and TSP treated soil and 280% higher than
RP-treated soil. Similarly, 45 days after planting, the recovered product showed a similar plant height and
biomass content to TSP-treated soil and slightly better height and biomass than DAP-treated soil.
Integrating the constant-potential operating cell into a whole plant model showed a reduction in aeration
energy by nearly 50%, showing the impact of nutrient recovery on the most energy intensive portion of
wastewater treatment. This reduced energy consumption resulted in a slight improvement in the levelized
cost of water treatment from $0.668/m3 to $0.648/m3, which could be further decreased if electricity is more
expensive than $0.09/kWh.
 The original hypothesis for this project was that sidestream treatment at wastewater treatment facilities
with anaerobic digesters were crucial because of the increased nutrient output into the recycle stream.
Specifically, using an electrochemical technology would decrease these recycled nutrients and thus lower
energy consumption in the most energy-intensive area of the plant. The results from the project were in-
line with these findings with an open-source optimized plant-wide model showing a reduced aeration
energy when the plant is combined with side stream treatment.
 The results from this work provide the basis to pursue funding where higher throughput reactors can
be tested and demonstrates the need for assessing which reactor operation mode (constant potential or
constant current) would be of benefit to a plant based on the plant throughput. Initial commercialization
should target smaller plants (≤1 MGD) or specific facilities with high-strength anaerobic digesters where
the energy-saving benefits of nutrient removal are most pronounced. Further study on the re-usability of
sacrificial anodes is needed to better understand how transport and reaction kinetics evenly wear and
 5

corrode this anode during reactor operation. Finally, longer-term testing of the 4-channel cell at the
proposed throughput or similar would provide the opportunity prior to seeking wastewater facilities willing
to integrate the electroN-P.

2 Background

 Municipal wastewater treatment facilities in the United States process 30 billion gallons of water per
day, while consuming 0.1 Quads of energy annually. Approximately 60% of this energy is consumed by
secondary treatment due to aeration in the nitrification process. In addition, the use of sludge digesters
increases nutrient load recycled to the primary and secondary processes, therefore increasing the amount of
coagulant associated with phosphorus reduction and energy consumption by aeration associated with
nitrogen reduction. The electroN-P, upon meeting the pre-defined targets, proposes to reduce the energy,
emissions and costs associated with treating this side stream through nutrient recovery as an agricultural
fertilizer resource prior to recycling into the main treatment stream.
 The electroN-P uses electrochemical reactions to adjust solution pH using electricity1. Water reduction
(Equation 1) or oxygen reduction (Equation 2) at the cathode coupled with magnesium oxidation (Equation
3) at the anode can be used to drive the pH2, or couple with water oxidation at the anode and dissolved
magnesium salts in a reactor known as an electrolyzer.

3.2 Task 2 – Recovered Resource Soil Assessment from Batch
 This task was focused on assessing the behavior of the recovered resource in the soil through a series
of incubation tests. In these tests, the soil was treated with the material recovered from the real wastewater
and was called RecoP. An eight-week incubation experiment was established to determine how RecoP
changed soil fertility in comparison to conventional fertilizers – diammonium phosphate (DAP),
monoammonium phosphate (MAP), triple super phosphate (TSP) and rock phosphate (RockP).
 Results showed change in soil readily soluble P treated with RecoP closely resembled the pattern
exhibited by conventional fertilizers (Figure 5) i.e., the phosphate in RecoP appears just as soluble as the
material in DAP or TSP. On the other hand, the relatively insoluble RockP had a similar response to the
control (Figure 5). Over the 8 weeks, the change in readily soluble P declined in a similar matter for DAP
and TSP, which is a typical response.
In terms of the fertilizer content, TSP and RockP show higher contents of calcium, while DAP shows a
higher content of iron, while the recovered resource shows a higher content of magnesium (Table 2).

3.3 Task 3 – Models for Batch Operation
 This task was focused on developing process and transport models for describing the batch process,
albeit with the understanding that this system would be limited in comparison to the continuous system.
 Embedded/embodied energy analyses using process models provided an evaluation of the effect of Mg
type on the process energetics for P recovery by examining the operational, embodied and total energy for
P recovery from wastewater (Figure 6). The embodied energy for magnesium were from literature values
of 8.47 kWh/kg of MgCl2 and 49.07 kWh/kg Mg.4 These preliminary calculations show that even though
the magnesium anode offers the lower operational energy consumption (Figure 6 (a) and as illustrated in
 10

Figure 3), the embodied energy of the magnesium anode becomes equivalent to the magnesium salt around
75% P recovery (Figure 6 (b)). Therefore around 80% recovery, the magnesium salt becomes a better option
for overall energy consumption compared to the magnesium anode (Figure 6 (c)).

Figure 6. Energetics associated with phosphorus recovery with respect to (a) operational or electrical energy (due
to cell voltage), (b) embodied energy (due to Mg used) and (c) total energy (due to combination of operational and
embodied energy). These values suggest there is a limit to the advantage offered by the low operational energy from
the magnesium alloy after a certain threshold where the low embodied energy of the salt becomes more
advantageous. Note that these analyses do not consider the kinetics associated with P recovery, which would further
decrease the associated embodied energies in either case (less Mg dissolved in solution)
 The transport model was developed using COMSOL software and was used to estimate the current
generated in the presence of phosphate ions without precipitating ions (Ca2+ and Mg2+). This model
combined fluid transport (associated with agitation), mass transport (associated with forced convection of
hydroxide ions) and charge transport (current generation). The comparison between the experimental and
model results (Figure 7) showed similar values of the current generated, with the instabilities in the model
due to the moving boundary conditions associated with the stirrer.

Figure 7. Comparison between (a) experimental and (b) computational current in a system without precipitation
(i.e., no Ca2+ or Mg2+ to decouple effect of surface coverage/precipitation from mass transport limitation). Both
results report a current of ~44mA over the duration of the measurement in (a) and calculation in (b).
 As part of the model development, the team began collaborating with the WaterTAP team based within
the National Energy Technology Laboratory. The initial collaboration focused on converting the
experimental results generated from the optimization analyses into a surrogate model as input into the whole
plant model that the team was building. The expertise of this team was also to facilitate a transition from
linear regression based surrogate models to more robust models especially considering the specific energy
consumption can cut across different orders of magnitude. This collaboration was initiated in Budget Period
1, but strengthened in Budget Period 2 as will be described in Task 7.
3.4 Task 4 – Regional wastewater composition
 This task was focused on characterizing wastewater at two Ohio wastewater plants – 100 million
gallons per day (MGD) Southerly WWTP in Columbus and 4 MGD WWTP in Athens. The rationale for
 11

these analyses was the lack of data on centrate streams at both a granular and time-scale level. The data was
collected over the space of one year with samples collected twice a month at 2 locations (thickening and
dewatering centrifuge) in Southerly and 1 location (settling tank post digester) in Athens. The data collected
has been stored in a repository online for others to access,5 but the major results that are most likely to
impact the recovery process are summarized in Figure 8.
 The hardness (Mg and Ca concentrations) of the centrate stream generally reflect the local water
hardness,6,7 while the nutrient content (N and P) for the anaerobic digester demonstrate the dewatering
stream as the most promising candidate for resource recovery at wastewater treatment facilities. These
values showed that the P content of 126 – 346 mg/L and N content of 800 to 1300 mg/L provided an impetus
to focus on this stream.

3.5 Task 5 – Continuous Flow Operation
 This task was focused on converting the 1L batch reactor (Figure 1) demonstrated in Task 1 to a
continuous process capable of a similar (or better) performance in phosphorus recovered (equation (1)) and
specific energy consumed in the phosphorus recovery (equation (2)). As such aside from developing the
reactor, the goal was also to characterize the long-term performance of the cell as well as the important
factors affecting the maximum phosphorus recovery and minimum energy consumption.
 The cell configuration for this task is shown below (Figure 9) with the inlet into the base of the reactor
and the outlet at the top of the reactor, which was annular in configuration with an inner rod which served
as the anode (magnesium or graphite) and an outer tube which serves as the cathode (stainless steel).

Figure 9. Configuration of the continuous reactor with the inlet at the bottom and the outlet at the top. The tube
shown will be sealed at both ends and will have a rod placed concentrically. The rod serves as the anode and the
 tube serves as the cathode.
 The longest continuous testing performed was a 127-hour continuous test conducted by applying a
constant cell voltage of -0.8V (Figure 10). This test involved switching between reactors (as might be
needed at scale) and running counter current streams to remove particle deposits within the reactor. In some
cases, the switch also involved re-using the magnesium anode after particle deposits were removed as
indicated in the labels shown in Figure 10; the rationale for this was to better understand the differences
between re-using the sacrificial anode and inserting a new anode.

Figure 10. Continuous 127 hour operation of the cell configuration shown in Figure 9, while polarized at a cell
voltage of -0.8V. The discontinuities in the current measurement are stoppages in the experiment due to flushing
of the reactor or changing of electrodes. The longest re-used anode (after rinsing and polishing off deposits) lasted
17 hours, while the longest new anode lasted 32 hours.

 The data suggests that using a new electrode could last between 18 – 32 hours (considering the 2 hour
duration between the 28th and 30th hour as an outlier), while the re-used electrodes lasted between 2 – 17
hours, although it is more likely that the longer lasting reactors were for the short-lived anode that only
lasted 2 hours originally. Overall, there were 4 magnesium anodes and 3 stainless steel cathodes used over
the ~5¼ day continuous test.
 The phosphorus recovered over the duration of this 127-hour test was equivalent to 80% by measuring
the concentration at the outlet container in comparison to the inlet condition (Figure 11). In addition, the
residual magnesium in the outlet was practically zero (Figure 12), indicating no excess magnesium in the
outlet stream, which had been a prior concern in the batch reactor setup.

 In addition, a design of experiments for optimum continuous reactor performance was conducted based
on the previously identified critical variables listed in Figure 2. A central composite design (CCD) was
employed, with axial points set at the maximum and minimum values of the variables to ensure all
experimental conditions remain within defined limits. A total of 62 experiments (2 hours each) were
performed, including 14 center-point replicates and two replicates for each corner and axial point. The

 15

variables investigated were flow rate, current density, nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) concentrations as

 The estimated baseline energy and CO2 emission factors for both electrochemical struvite recovery
processes (MgSA and MgMC) are presented in Figure 32. MgSA has higher cumulative energy and CO2
emission factors (213 GJ/mtP,15.4 mtCO2/mtP) than MgMC (124 GJ/mtP, 9.6 mtCO2/mtP). This is due to the
high energy intensity of magnesium anode production, which is manufactured using energy-intensive
separations and electrowinning processes. In both electrochemical processes, the magnesium source
accounts for the majority of the processes’ energy demand and CO2 emissions, approximately 70% of
energy demand and 54% of CO2 emissions in MgMC, and 85% and 77% in MgSA, respectively. The
electrolysis reaction contributes about 9% of the total energy demand and 14% of the CO 2 emissions in
MgSA, while contributing about 20% and 30%, respectively, in Mg MC. The higher energy demand during
 25

electrolysis in MgMC is due to higher voltage requirement. The relatively lower energy demand of the
electrolysis reaction compared to the magnesium source in both MgMC and MgSA systems indicates the lower
energy intensity of the electrochemical process itself.

 Hence, the magnesium source, in both cases, is the primary contributor to embodied energy and carbon
emissions. Downstream processes such as drying, centrifugation, and pumping are less substantive. This
emphasizes the need to target improving the energy efficiency of the magnesium production process
especially that of magnesium anode to reduce the overall energy and emissions footprint of the
electrochemical struvite recovery process.

4 Key Achievements
 A key achievement of this project was the translation from a 250 ml for 110-minute batch reactor1 to
a 55 L for 90-minute continuous reactor with roughly equivalent phosphorus recovery (80%); a 220× scale-
up over the duration of the project. This is equivalent to translating from a TRL 3/4 to a TRL 5/6 and
resulted in 1 patent pending application for the design of the continuous annular reactor.
 In addition to the technology transfer and reported results, key research dissemination included 3
published manuscripts, 16 conference presentations, 5 invited seminars, 3 master’s student theses, 6 total
trainees (4 graduate students and 2 post-doctoral researchers). Additional details will be published in
forthcoming peer-reviewed manuscripts which are currently under preparation or in review.
 Some of these yet-to-be-published findings include the significant reduction in embedded energy of
the recovered fertilizer when comparing magnesium anode and magnesium salt as the magnesium salt (Task
7 – Models for Continuous Operation and Figure 33), the significant reduction in aeration energy in a
wastewater treatment plant due to nutrient recovery (Task 9 – Models for Scaled Operation and Table 7),
the competitive cost of the recovered fertilizer in comparison to state-of-the-art fertilizers and other
recovered products from side stream treatment (Task 7 – Models for Continuous Operation and Figure 23),
the impact of the recovered product on potted plants (Task 6 - Recovered Resource Soil Assessment from
Continuous Operation and Figure 19) and an integrated model that includes transport phenomena and
reaction kinetics (Task 7 – Models for Continuous Operation and Figure 27).

5 Benefits Assessment

 Overall, the findings indicate that selecting low embodied energy magnesium sources and optimizing
influent conditions and operating parameters are critical for improving the environmental performance of
the reactor. Specifically, these changes will make the recovered product have a more competitive embodied
energy and emissions with respect to state-of-the-art fertilizers based on the life-cycle analyses conducted.
On the other hand, from a whole wastewater plant perspective, this will reduce the energy consumed at the
wastewater facility.

6 Lessons Learned
 A key lesson learned is the need to conduct both a constant potential and a constant current operation
in the 2-electrode continuous reactor. This facilitates a balance between the size of the reactor (capital cost),
energy consumed by the reactor (operational cost) and the embedded energy in the recovered product
(overall process sustainability). From a process perspective, the operation of the reactor was more stable
under constant current conditions with the voltage decreasing much more slowly than the current did over
the same time duration. This stability in the constant current configuration would likely yield a better
opportunity for estimate cell performance deterioration. On the other hand, unlike other electrolyzer
systems (water electrolysis or CO2 electrolysis), it may not be relevant to operate at significantly high
current densities >100 mA/cm2 especially if a smaller plant (≤1 MGD plant) is the target deployment space.
This becomes critical for future research in this resource recovery via electrochemistry space.

7 Conclusions

 The original hypothesis for this project was that sidestream treatment at wastewater treatment facilities
with anaerobic digesters were crucial because of the increased nutrient output into the recycle stream.
Specifically, using an electrochemical technology would decrease these recycled nutrients and thus lower
energy consumption in the most energy-intensive area of the plant. The results from the project were in-
line with these findings with an open-source optimized plant-wide model showing a reduced aeration
energy when the plant is combined with side stream treatment.

 33

8 Future Work/Recommendations

 The results from this work provide the basis to pursue funding where higher throughput reactors can
be tested and demonstrates the need for assessing which reactor operation mode (constant potential or
constant current) would be of benefit to a plant based on the plant throughput. Initial commercialization
should target smaller plants (≤1 MGD) or specific facilities with high-strength anaerobic digesters where
the energy-saving benefits of nutrient removal are most pronounced. Further study on the re-usability of
sacrificial anodes is needed to better understand how transport and reaction kinetics evenly wear and
corrode this anode during reactor operation. Finally, longer-term testing of the 4-channel cell at the
proposed throughput or similar would provide the opportunity prior to seeking wastewater facilities willing
to integrate the electroN-P.
