Sorafenib analogues exhibit dual Colistin-potentiating and anti-MRSA activity
Authors: Amy Sorge, Andrew Stiles, Ansley M. Nemeth, Ian O'Keefe, Roberta J. Melander, Yohei Doi, Robert K. Ernst, Christian Melander
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In response to the antimicrobial resistance (AMR) global health crisis, physicians have been forced to employ antibiotics of last resort such as colistin, which had previously been removed from clinics due to toxicity concerns. The increase in colistin usage has in turn resulted in the increased incidence of colistin-resistant isolates, making novel therapeutic options for infections caused by these pathogens a priority. We previously reported that sorafenib, a eukaryotic kinase inhibitor, is a colistin adjuvant, augmenting colistin activity against colistin-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii. Herein, we describe the generation of a library of analogues based upon the sorafenib scaffold, lead compounds from which sensitize colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa to colistin, and exhibit reduced cytotoxicity compared to sorafenib. We also describe the standalone antimicrobial activity of these sorafenib analogues against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). Lead compound NDM-773 lowers the colistin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) against K. pneumoniae B9 from 512 μg mL−1 to 2 μg mL−1 at 1.5 μM, against A. baumannii 4106 from 1024 μg mL−1 to 1 μg mL−1 at 4 μM, and against P. aeruginosa TRPA162 from 8192 μg mL−1 to ≤0.125 μg mL−1 at 7.5 μM. NDM-773 acts as a standalone antibiotic against MRSA with an MIC of 0.781 μM (0.398 μg mL−1) and a frequency of mutation rate of <10−11 against MRSA BAA-1556. NDM-773 exhibits over three-fold reduction in HepG2 toxicity compared to sorafenib, and has therapeutic indices (TIs) up to 35.6 as a colistin adjuvant, and 68.4 as an MRSA antibiotic.

Introduction

The emergence of multi-drug resistant (MDR) and extensively-drug resistant (XDR) bacterial stains has led to a global increase in mortality and morbidity.1 Polymyxin E (colistin), an antibiotic discovered in the 1940s but scarcely used due to nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity concerns, has emerged as a last resort therapy due to its efficacy against MDR gram-negative bacteria.2–4 Colistin is utilized against Gram-negative pathogens such as Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which are included in a group of pathogens denoted the ESKAPE pathogens (E̲nterococcus faecium, S̲taphylococcus aureus, K̲. pneumoniae, A̲. baumannii, P̲. aeruginosa, and E̲nterobacter species).4,5 In 2024, each ESKAPE pathogen was reported in the Bacterial Priority Pathogens List by the World Health Organization, highlighting the importance of addressing MDR bacterial pathogens, and the critical need for new or alternative antimicrobial therapies.6

K. pneumoniae is prevalent in both the hospital and community setting and accounts for approximately a third of all Gram-negative infections.7,8 Treatment of K. pneumoniae infections has become exceedingly difficult as carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae steadily increases.8 There are limited effective antibiotic classes against carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), leading to an increase in the use of the polymyxins.9 Unsurprisingly, this has led to an increase in polymyxin resistance. In 2023, approximately 10% of tested CRKP strains in China were polymyxin-resistant.10A. baumannii, another major hospital associated pathogen, has a low infection rate in comparison to the other ESKAPE pathogens,11,12 however, its ability to develop antibiotic resistance is concerning. In the United States, over 60% of A. baumannii isolates are MDR, which is four times higher than MDR levels in K. pneumoniae or P. aeruginosa.12P. aeruginosa is reported to be responsible for 10% of all nosocomial infections.12,13 This pathogen is a major causative agent in urinary tract infections, pneumonia, and wound infections, and is often found in the airways of patients who suffer from cystic fibrosis (CF).14,15 As seen with K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii, increased antibiotic resistance has led to an increased use of colistin.14 The increase in colistin resistance in P. aeruginosa from 2% (seen during 2006–2010) to 5% (seen during 2020–2023) emphasizes the dangers of increased usage, and strains isolated from patients with CF exhibited an even higher colistin resistant rate of 7%.14 Finally, methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infections pose a challenge to treat clinically and are responsible for significant morbidity, mortality, and economic costs.16,17

Polymyxin resistance is typically conferred through the reduction of the net negative charge of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) component of the outer membrane via modification of lipid A.18–20 Modification is achieved through the addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-l-arabinose (l-Ara4N) and/or phosphoethanolamine (pEtN) to lipid A.18 Altering the charge of the outer membrane decreases the overall binding affinity for the positively charged side chains of colistin, in turn decreasing its efficacy.18,20

Rising resistance levels amongst multiple antibiotic classes, and the lack of novel effective antibiotics in development, necessitates innovative approaches to treat bacterial infections.21 One such approach is the use of antibiotic adjuvants, which are small molecules that do not possess standalone antimicrobial activity, but improve the efficacy of antibiotics.22 A notable example of the use of antibiotic adjuvants in the clinic is the combination of amoxicillin (a semisynthetic β-lactam antibiotic) and clavulanate (a β-lactamase inhibitor derived from Streptomyces clavuligerus) marketed as Augmentin.23 Augmentin possesses broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and is primarily used to treat community-acquired respiratory tract infections.23 The drug combination was introduced to the clinic in the early 1980s, and has set a precedent for the use of antibiotic adjuvants.

To date, the only antibiotic adjuvants approved for clinical use in the United States are β-lactamase inhibitors; however, antibiotic adjuvants that target other mechanisms of resistance are being explored.24 Previously, we disclosed that meridianin D (Fig. 1) modulates colistin activity in K. pneumoniae and A. baumannii.25 Meridianin D belongs to a family of compounds that inhibit various kinases, including cyclin-dependent kinases, glycogen synthase kinase-3, cyclic nucleotide-dependent kinases, and casein kinase 1.26

Previously identified colistin adjuvants: meridianin D, IMD-0354, NDM-240, and sorafenib.25,27,28

Based upon the known kinase inhibitory activity of meridianin D, we conducted a comprehensive screening of several commercially available kinase inhibitor libraries for potential colistin adjuvants.27 Amongst the compounds identified from this screening were IMD-0354 and sorafenib (Fig. 1).27 IMD-0354 is a well-studied inhibitor of NF-Kβ inhibitor kinase, and lowers the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of colistin 1024-fold at 5 μM against K. pneumoniae B9 (KPB9).27 Modification of the IMD-0354 scaffold led to NDM-240, which exhibits increased adjuvant activity, lowering the KPB9 colistin MIC 512-fold at 0.25 μM (Fig. 1).28 Against this same strain, sorafenib, an inhibitor of Raf serine/threonine kinases and tyrosine kinases, lowers the colistin MIC 256-fold to 2 μg mL−1 at 5 μM.27,29 Sorafenib was first approved for treatment of renal cell carcinoma in 2005 and then for unresectable hepatocellular carcinoma in 2007.30 It was also approved for the treatment of locally recurrent or metastatic, progressive and iodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer in 2013.31 Additionally, sorafenib and related compounds have been reported to exhibit stand-alone antimicrobial activity against MRSA.32–34

Herein, we report a structure activity relationship (SAR) study on sorafenib where over 30 derivatives were designed, synthesized, and evaluated. From this library, we identified a new lead compound, NDM-773, with improved colistin adjuvant activity against highly-colistin resistant isolates of K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, and P. aeruginosa, and greater than a ten-fold increase in anti-MRSA activity. Additionally, NDM-773 exhibits lower toxicity to HepG2 cells in comparison to sorafenib.

Results and discussion

We initiated our studies by synthesizing a pilot library of analogues to probe the SAR of the 3-trifluormethyl-4-chloroaniline moiety of sorafenib. Synthesis of each compound was initiated from the commercially available 4-(4-aminophenoxy)-N-methylpicolinamide and a substituted phenyl isocyanate. Isocyanates were either sourced from commercial vendors or formed in situ from the corresponding aniline with 1,1′-carbonyldiimidazole (CDI) or triphosgene (Scheme 1).

Synthesis of sorafenib and analogues. Reagents and conditions: a) ArNCO, DCM, rt, 16 h (20–97%); b) ArNH2, triphosgene, Na2CO3, H2O, DCM, rt, 3 h (10%); c) ArNH2, CDI, Et3N, DCM, rt, 3 h (10–53%).

All derivatives were initially evaluated against two representative colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae strains, KPB9 and KPC3, by measuring standalone microbicidal activity as well as colistin potentiation. For reference, the MIC of sorafenib against both strains is >200 μM (Table S1), while the standalone colistin MICs are 512 μg mL−1 (KPB9) and 128 μg mL−1 (KPC3). The breakpoint set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute for colistin against K. pneumoniae strains is ≤2 μg mL−1.35 In the presence of sorafenib at 60 μM (<30% its standalone MIC), the colistin MIC is lowered to 0.25 μg mL−1 against KPB9 and 2 μg mL−1 against KPC3 (Table 1).

All synthesized compounds were first screened for their stand-alone activity against both KPB9 and KPC3, and all MICs are >200 μM. Each compound was then initially screened for colistin potentiation at 60 μM (Table 1). Removal of the chloro- and trifluoromethyl substituents (NDM-446) was probed and led to abrogation of colistin activity against KPB9; however, against KPC3 the colistin MIC is lowered moderately to 16 μg mL−1. When an aromatic carbon atom is replaced with a nitrogen atom to afford the corresponding pyridine (NDM-450, NDM-447, NDM-800), all activity is lost. Removal of the 4-chloro (NDM-242) from the parent compound results in equipotent activity to sorafenib; while removal of the 3-triflouromethyl group (NDM-251) results in a loss of activity, indicating that the trifluoromethyl group could be crucial for adjuvant activity. When the 4-chloro of sorafenib is replaced with a methyl group (NDM-250), a moderate loss in activity occurs against KPB9, while the colistin MIC in KPC3 is still lowered 64-fold to 2 μg mL−1. When the 3-triflouromethyl group, however, is replaced with a methyl (NDM-249), activity is lost. When these same substituents are separately replaced with a fluoro substituent (NDM-326, NDM-684), activity is equipotent with the analogue that retains trifluoromethyl (NDM-326) and lost with the analogue that retains chlorine (NDM-684). Reversal of these substituents in compounds NDM-417 (3-fluoro, 4-triflouromethyl) and NDM-685 (4-fluoro, 3-chloro) results in complete loss of activity in both strains. Further, when a chloro group is singly substituted in the 2-position (NDM-434), ortho to the urea, activity is lost. In contrast, when a chloro group is singly substituted in the 3-position (NDM-433), meta to the urea, activity is equipotent to that of sorafenib against KPB9 and increased against KPC3, lowering the colistin MIC to 0.25 μg mL−1 from 128 μg mL−1. Additional singly substituted analogues investigated included compounds harboring 2-trifluoromethyl (NDM-413), 4-trifluoromethyl (NDM-327), 3-methyl (NDM-445), 3-fluoro (NDM-570), and 3-bromo (NDM-568). Of these compounds, NDM-570 and NDM-568 exhibit potent colistin adjuvant activity, where NDM-570 lowers the colistin MIC to 0.5 μg mL−1 and 1 μg mL−1, and NDM-568 lowers the colistin MIC to 1 μg mL−1 and 2 μg mL−1 against KPB9 and KPC3, respectively. NDM-327 lowers the colistin MIC to the breakpoint of 2 μg mL−1 against KPB9 but only lowers the colistin MIC to 8 μg mL−1 against KPC3. Compounds NDM-413 and NDM-445 are devoid of activity. Overall, this highlights the importance of an electron withdrawing substituent meta to the urea for colistin adjuvant activity, however a strongly electron withdrawing group at the 4-position (NDM-417) appears to offset this activity.

Given the activity of NDM-433, we evaluated a series of dichloro analogues (NDM-256, NDM-416, NDM-442, NDM-625, NDM-626, NDM-412) following the previously described synthetic route (Scheme 1). These compounds do not exhibit stand-alone toxicity to either K. pneumoniae strain (MIC >200 μM, Table S1). The 3,4-dicholoro compound (NDM-256) shares a substitution pattern with sorafenib, however it has only moderate colistin adjuvant activity against both strains. The 2,4-dichloro analogue (NDM-416) moderately reduces the MIC of colistin to 8 μg mL−1 and 16 μg mL−1 against KPB9 and KPC3 respectively. Interestingly, the analogue that harbors a 2,3-dichloro (NDM-442) is inactive despite the presence of the chloro substituent meta to the urea. Both the 2,5-dichloro (NDM-625) and the 2,6-dichloro (NDM-626) analogues are inactive, further demonstrating that ortho substitution is not tolerated for colistin adjuvant activity. Finally, the 3,5-dichloro analogue (NDM-412) exhibits comparable activity to the 3-chloro analogue (NDM-433), reducing the MIC of colistin to 0.125 μg mL−1 in KPB9 and 0.25 μg mL−1 in KPC3.

The necessity of an electron withdrawing group at the meta position was further explored through the synthesis of a series of 3,5-disubstituted compounds (NDM-328, NDM-571, NDM-572, NDM-772, NDM-449, NDM-437, NDM-415, NDM-773) containing either electron withdrawing (NDM-328 – bis(trifluoromethyl), NDM-572 – difluoro, NDM-772 – dibromo, NDM-415 – 3-fluoro-5-trifluoromethyl, NDM-437 – 3-chloro-5-trifluoromethyl, NDM-773 – 3-bromo-5-trifluoromethyl) or electron donating (NDM-571 – dimethyl, NDM-449 – dimethoxy) substituents. Each of these compounds have stand-alone MICs against KPB9 and KPC3 >200 μM (Table S1). Similar to the 3-methyl derivative (NDM-445), the 3,5-dimethyl derivative (NDM-571) and the 3,5-dimethoxy derivative (NDM-449) are devoid of activity. Additionally, the 3,5-difluoro analogue (NDM-572) is also inactive, while the 3-fluoro-5-trifluoromethyl analogue (NDM-415) lowers the colistin MIC 64-fold against KPB9 and KPC3 to 8 μg mL−1 and 2 μg mL−1, respectively. Both 3,5-bis(trifluoromethyl) (NDM-328) and 3-chloro-5-trifluoromethyl (NDM-437) derivatives display potent colistin adjuvant activity, lowering the colistin MIC to 0.5 μg mL−1 against KPC3 and to 0.25 μg mL−1 and 0.125 μg mL−1, respectively against KPB9. The two compounds that harbor a 3,5-dibromo substitution (NDM-772) or a 3-bromo-5-trifluoromethyl substitution (NDM-773) exhibit the highest activity, lowering the colistin MIC to ≤0.125 μg mL−1 in both KPB9 and KPC3.

Dose–response studies of sorafenib and derivatives that potentiate colistin in at least one strain were then performed (Table 2) against both KPB9 and KPC3 to determine the lowest concentration of compound needed to lower the colistin MIC to the breakpoint (2 μg mL−1).35 Compounds tested included sorafenib, NDM-252, NDM-250, NDM-326, NDM-433, NDM-327, NDM-570, NDM-568, NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-772, NMD-437, NDM-415, and NMD-773. Sorafenib maintains breakpoint activity at 10 μM and 15 μM against KPB9 and KPC3, respectively. Compounds NDM-250, NDM-327, NDM-570, and NDM-415 lose activity when the concentration is lowered to 30 μM. Compounds NDM-252, NDM-326, NDM-433, and NDM-568 lower the colistin MIC to ≤2 μg mL−1 in at least one of the two tested KP strains at concentrations ≤30 μM, however the activity of these four compounds does not exceed that of sorafenib. Compounds NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-772, NDM-437, and NDM-773, however, exhibit improved activity in comparison to sorafenib. Compounds NDM-412 and NDM-772 lower the colistin MIC to 2 μg mL−1 at 4 μM against KPB9 and to 2 μg mL−1 at 5 μM against KPC3. Compound NDM-328 lowers the colistin MIC to or below the breakpoint at 5 μM and 7.5 μM against KPB9 and KPC3, respectively. Compound NDM-437 lowers the colistin MIC to 2 μg mL−1 at 3 μM in KPB9, and at 4 μM in KPC3. The most active compound, NDM-773, lowers the colistin MIC to the breakpoint of 2 μg mL−1 at 1.5 μM against KPB9 and 2 μM against KPC3. The five compounds with the highest activity all harbor a 3,5-disubstitution pattern with chloro, bromo, or trifluoromethyl substituents.

Next, we investigated the activity of the lead compounds against other Gram-negative species, A. baumannii (AB4106) and P. aeruginosa (TRPA162) (Table 4). Similar to K. pneumoniae the clinical breakpoint of colistin for both A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa is 2 μg mL−1.35 Each compound was initially determined to have standalone MICs of >200 μM against the two strains (Table S2). NDM-437 (60 μM) only lowers the colistin MIC to 8 μg mL−1; however, at this same concentration NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-772, and NDM-773 lower the MIC to the breakpoint. Three of these four compounds, (NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-773) maintain breakpoint activity at lower concentrations; 20 μM, 15 μM, and 4 μM, respectively. TRPA162 is highly colistin-resistant with a standalone colistin MIC of 8192 μg mL−1. Only two compounds (NDM-772, NDM-773) lower the colistin MIC to the breakpoint at 60 μM. It has been previously reported that sorafenib and its analogues exhibit antimicrobial activity against MRSA.32,33 Therefore, we investigated the potential antibacterial activity of NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-772, NDM-437, and NDM-773 against S. aureus. First, MICs were determined against MRSA ATCC BAA-1556, with sorafenib having the lowest activity (MIC 12.5 μM (5.81 μg mL−1)) (Table 6). Compounds NMD-328 and NDM-437 exhibit moderate activity with MICs of 6.25 μM (3.11 μg mL−1) and 6.25 μM (2.91 μg mL−1), respectively, against MRSA BAA-1556. Compounds NDM-412, NDM-772, and NDM-773 exhibit potent antibacterial activity with MICs ≤1.5625 μM (≤0.674 μg mL−1) against the same MRSA strain. These three compounds were screened against three additional MRSA strains and one methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) strain, against all of which antibacterial activity is retained (Table 6).

HepG2 toxicity of sorafenib and the five lead compounds (Fig. S1) was next examined. We determined the IC50 of sorafenib to be 16.9 ± 0.8 μM, comparable to previously reported values.36 Compounds NDM-412, NDM-328, and NDM-437 exhibit increased cytotoxicity, with IC50s <16 μM. However, NDM-772 and NDM-773 are less cytotoxic than sorafenib, with IC50s of 67.8 ± 1.3 μM and 58.3 ± 8.9 μM, respectively (Table 7). In typical antibiotic development, the therapeutic index (TI) is defined as the mammalian cell IC50 divided by the antibiotic MIC;37,38 however, for the development of antibiotic adjuvants, we define the TI as the IC50 divided by the lowest concentration of adjuvant needed to lower the antibiotic MIC to the breakpoint.38 Compound NDM-773, the most active compounds from this study, has the highest TI as a colistin adjuvant, with an upper value of 38.9. Additionally, both NDM-772 and NDM-773 stand out as a viable stand-alone antibacterial against MRSA, with TIs of 86.8 and 74.6 respectively (Table 7).

To confirm that NDM-773 is acting as an adjuvant, as opposed to having its antibacterial activity potentiated by colistin, we examined the effect that polymyxin B nonapeptide (PMBN), a polymyxin that has no antibiotic activity but contains membrane-permeabilizing activity has on the MIC of NDM-773. The MIC of NDM-773 against AB4106 in the presence of 8 μg mL−1 PMBN is >200 μM, which is unchanged from that without PMBN (Table S3). We also investigated the impact of PMBN on AB5075, a colistin susceptible strain (MIC 1 μg mL−1). NDM-773 has no standalone antibacterial activity against AB5075 (MIC >200 μM), but in the presence of 8 μg mL−1 PMBN the MIC is decreased to 50 μM (Table S3). A similar effect is seen in the presence of sub-MIC levels of colistin (0.25 μg mL−1), where NDM-773 again exhibits an MIC of 50 μM. A lipooligosaccharide (LOS) deficient mutant of AB5075 was generated as previously reported,39 against which NDM-773 has an unchanged MIC of 200 μM. Given the large difference between the concentration at which NDM-773 achieves breakpoint levels and its MIC in the presence of the LOS-deficient mutant, this indicates that NDM-773 acts as an adjuvant to colistin in A. baumannii, and not as an antimicrobial.

Previously, our group has demonstrated that some classes of colistin adjuvants reverse modification of lipid A.27 The modification of lipid A can be detected using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS).40 KPB9, AB4106 and TRPA162 were cultured in the presence of or absence of 20 μM NDM-773 for 8 h. Lipid A was then extracted and analyzed by MALDI-TOF. Multiple known structures of lipid A have been described via this method and all m/z associated with pEtN and Ara4N modifications are known.40NDM-773 does not have an observable effect on lipid A modification (Fig. S2).

Iron chelation has also been proposed as a mechanism for colistin potentiation, with EDTA and deferoxamine reported to lower colistine MICs, though only at mg mL−1 concentrations.41–43 To test whether iron binding underlies the adjuvant activity of NDM-773, the affinity of NDM-773 for Fe(ii) and Fe(iii) was measured using isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). NDM-773 had no affinity for either Fe(ii) or Fe(iii) suggesting that iron chelation is likely not the mechanism of action for this compound (Fig. S3 and S4).

Reports indicate that induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in colistin-susceptible bacterial strains has been linked to increased colistin activity.44,45 Additionally, we have reported colistin adjuvants can induce ROS generation in colistin resistant strains.46,47 We examined the effect of NDM-773 on ROS generation using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) in KPB9, AB4106, and TRPA162 as previously reported.46,48 Using 0.075% t-BuOOH as a positive control, we examined the effect of colistin, NDM-773, or a combination of the two on ROS production in KPB9, AB4106, and TRPA162 (Fig. 2). In KPB9, addition of 2 μg mL−1 colistin or 2 μg mL−1 colistin with 1.5 μM NDM-773 leads to a marginal increase in ROS production of 69% and 56%, respectively, compared to an untreated control. Additionally, 1.5 μM NDM-773 has little effect on ROS in KPB9. In TRPA162 cells, all treatments with the exception of 8192 μg mL−1 colistin, show little to no change in ROS production compared to untreated cells. The negligible changes in ROS generation compared to the changes in the presence of the active concentration of colistin likely indicate that the production of ROS is not the primary cause of cell death in KPB9 and TRPA161. In contrast, in AB4106, 1 μg mL−1 colistin or 5 μM NDM-773 has minimal impact on ROS generation; however, the combination results in a significant increase in ROS levels of 378%. This is 56% the levels in cells treated with 1024 μg mL−1 colistin. Further investigation is required to determine if the increase in ROS induced by the combination of NDM-773 and colistin plays a primary role in cell death.

The Sieber lab has synthesized several sorafenib analogues that act as antimicrobial agents against S. aureus.32 With lead compounds from this study, they completed quantitative, gel-free and label-free AfBPP analyses, which indicate demethylmenaquinone methyltransferase (MenG) could be a target for sorafenib.32 MenG is an enzyme responsible for methylation of the polar ring of demethylmenaquinone (DMK) to form menaquninone (MK), a vitamin essential for electron transport during bacterial respiration and energy metabolism.32,49,50 Reports state that chemical inhibition of MenG in Mycobacterium tuberculosis is determinantal to the pathogen, leading to reduction in oxygen consumption and ATP synthesis.49 This precedent indicates that MenG could be a potential antimicrobial target of the novel analogues synthesized in this study.

To evaluate if NDM-773 interferes with the MK biosynthesis, exogenous MK was supplemented to the media when determining MICs. The shift in MIC observed is dependent on the concentration of MK, and renders MRSA BAA-1556 less susceptible to NDM-773 (Table S4). In the presence of 100 μg mL−1 menaquinone-4 (MK-4), the MIC of NDM-773 is 6.25 μM, eight-fold higher than if MK-4 is absent (0.78125 μM). While this may indicate the compound has an effect on the biosynthesis of MK, it must be acknowledged that other effects could be impacting the results, such as aggregation of MK with NDM-773.32 The MIC of NDM-773 with 100 μg mL−1 MK-4 was reevaluated in the presence of 0.01% (v/v) Triton X-100 to mitigate the potential formation of aggregates that could be impacting the efficacy of NDM-773.51 In the presence of Triton X-100 the MIC of NDM-773, supplemented with 100 μg mL−1 MK-4, remains 6.25 μM, indicating that the mechanism underpinning the antibacterial activity of NDM-773 is impacted by MK biosynthesis.

The impact of NDM-773 on the frequency of mutation, which results in resistance to NDM-773 and colistin combinations or NDM-773 alone, were quantified using a modified fluctuation assay based on the Luria–Delbrück model at high inoculum (∼5 × 107 CFU mL−1).52,53 Gram-negative cultures were exposed to a combination of 2× or 4× the MIC of colistin with NDM-773, while MRSA was subjected to 2× or 4× the MIC of NDM-773 (Table 10). KPB9 exhibits the highest mutation rate (∼3 × 10−8) when dosed with NDM-773 and colistin in comparison to AB4106 and TRPA162, which exhibit mutation rates of ∼10−10 (Table 10). The rates of mutation for colistin alone against susceptible K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, or P. aeruginosa are ∼10−6,53 demonstrating that the addition of NDM-773 reduces the occurrence of spontaneous resistant mutations in comparison to colistin alone. Resistant mutants were not detected (frequency <10−11) in MRSA in the presence of 2× and 4× the MIC of NDM-773 (Table 10). The spontaneous mutation frequency of linezolid, an antibiotic that has been instrumental in the treatment of MRSA, has been reported to be ∼10−9 in MRSA.54,55 The reduction in resistant mutants of NDM-773 when compared to linezolid presents NDM-773 as a promising option.

Conclusion

Sorafenib was previously identified as a colistin adjuvant against K. pneumoniae and an antimicrobial against MRSA.27,32 Herein, we have reported an SAR study on the 3-trifluoromethyl-4-chlorophenyl moiety of sorafenib from which five compounds containing electron-withdrawing, 3,5-disubstitution patterns were identified (NDM-412, NDM-328, NDM-772, NDM-437, NDM-773) as more potent colistin adjuvants. NDM-412 and NDM-328 demonstrate moderate adjuvant activity with colistin against AB4106; however, NDM-773 sensitizes colistin to the breakpoint of ≤2 μg mL−1 at 5 μM against colistin resistant K. pneumoniae, A. baumannii, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli strains. Additionally, the five lead compounds have antimicrobial activity against MRSA, with NDM-412, NDM-772, and NDM-773 being the most potent. NDM-772 and NDM-773 exhibit significantly reduced HepG2 toxicity compared to sorafenib, with IC50’s of 56.7 ± 4.8 μM and 53.4 ± 5.3 μM, respectively. NDM-773 does not appear to sensitize colistin via modification of lipid A, binding to iron, or through the induction of ROS production. However, the antimicrobial activity against could be partially driven through the inhibition of the biosynthetic pathway of MK, which is auxiliary in Gram-negative pathogens and vital for Gram-positive pathogens for bacterial respiration and metabolism. This is supported by partial loss in antimicrobial activity of NDM-773 against MRSA BAA-1556 in the presence of exogenous MK-4. Further work to modify the structure to augment both adjuvant and antimicrobial activities, reduce overall cytotoxicity, determine the full scope of the effect of NDM-773 on MK biosynthesis, and determine in vivo activity are currently ongoing.
