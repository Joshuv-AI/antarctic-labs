Clinical Outcomes of Lenvatinib–Pembrolizumab in a High-Risk, Dual ICI-Refractory Melanoma Cohort
Authors: Aslı Geçgel, Zeynep Sıla Gökdere, Talha Özüdoğru, Mustafa Murat Mıdık, Burçak Karaca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) remains a major therapeutic challenge in advanced melanoma. The phase II LEAP-004 trial demonstrated activity of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab after ICI failure; however, real-world data are limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective single-center case series of nine consecutive patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma refractory to prior immunotherapy who received lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab. Tumor responses were assessed according to RECIST v1.1 criteria. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.ResultsThe median age was 53 years; 44% of patients had baseline brain metastases, and all tumors were BRAF wild type. The objective response rate was 22.2%, and the disease control rate was 33.3%. Median progression-free survival was 4.0 months, and median overall survival was 9.6 months. Treatment-related adverse events were generally manageable, with one patient experiencing grade 3 colitis.ConclusionIn this high-risk, dual ICI-refractory cohort, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab showed manageable toxicity and clinical outcomes broadly consistent with previous reports. Although limited by the small sample size and retrospective design, these findings support the feasibility of this regimen in routine clinical practice and warrant further prospective evaluation.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved survival rates in cutaneous malignant melanoma. However, many patients develop primary or secondary resistance. 1 Resistance can result from tumor-intrinsic factors, such as defects in antigen presentation or impaired IFN-γ signaling, as well as from an immunosuppressive, angiogenic tumor microenvironment. Together, these factors reduce treatment efficacy and durability. 2 Following disease progression on ICIs, therapeutic strategies may include re-challenging with immunotherapy, employing alternative combinations, administering BRAF/MEK inhibitors in BRAF-mutant tumors, or pursuing advanced interventions such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy at specialized centers. Additionally, combining anti-angiogenic or multikinase inhibitors with PD-1 blockade represents another avenue of investigation.3-5 For example, lenvatinib targets the VEGFR/FGFR pathway and may remodel tumor vasculature and the immune environment, thereby potentially synergizing with PD-1 inhibition.6,7

In this single-center case series, we aimed to summarize the real-world clinical activity and tolerability of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in nine consecutive patients with ICI-refractory, unresectable/metastatic melanoma, to inform decision-making after ICI resistance.

Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

This retrospective, single-center case series included 9 consecutive patients with unresectable or metastatic melanoma who were treated with lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab at the Ege University Medical Oncology Clinic between January 2024 and July 2025. Eligible patients had confirmed disease progression following prior immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. All patients had previously received both anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4–based treatment and subsequently developed either primary or acquired resistance to dual immune checkpoint blockade. All patients had measurable disease according to RECIST version 1.1 and had received at least two prior lines of systemic therapy. 8 Exclusion criteria included uveal melanoma, prior treatment with lenvatinib–pembrolizumab, non-measurable disease, insufficient clinical or radiographic follow-up for response assessment, and incomplete medical records.

Ethics Approval

This retrospective case series was approved by the Ege University Faculty of Medicine Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 26-2T/15; Date: 05 February 2026). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, as revised in 2024). Written informed consent for participation and publication was obtained from all patients. All patient data were de-identified before analysis.

Treatment and Assessments

Lenvatinib was initiated at 20 mg orally once daily, with dose modifications based on clinical tolerability. Pembrolizumab was administered at 200 mg intravenously every three weeks. Radiographic tumor assessments were performed using cross-sectional imaging every 8–12 weeks, or earlier if clinically indicated. Tumor response was evaluated according to the RECIST version 1.1 criteria. Disease control (partial response or stable disease) required radiographic confirmation on repeat imaging performed at least six weeks after the initial assessment.

Adverse events were retrospectively extracted from medical records and graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. 9

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was objective response rate (ORR). Secondary endpoints included disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and safety. PFS and OS were calculated from treatment initiation to radiographic progression or to death, respectively. Patients without events were censored at the date of last follow-up. Early progression was defined as radiographic disease progression occurring within 12 weeks of treatment initiation. Time on treatment was used as a descriptive surrogate and should not be interpreted as a direct substitute for PFS.

Statistical Analysis

Given the limited sample size, analyses were primarily descriptive. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Survival outcomes were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. All subgroup analyses were considered descriptive and hypothesis-generating, and no formal inferential comparisons were performed. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27).

Results

Patient Characteristics

Nine consecutive patients with dual ICI-refractory unresectable or metastatic melanoma were included. The median age was 53 years, and the median follow-up was 5.0 months. Most patients (89%) had stage IV–M1c/d disease, and 44% had baseline brain metastases. All four patients with baseline brain metastases had received CNS-directed radiotherapy before lenvatinib–pembrolizumab initiation. At treatment start, three patients were clinically stable or asymptomatic after local CNS treatment, whereas one had symptomatic intracranial progression despite prior cranial radiotherapy. Separate intracranial RECIST-based responses were unavailable due to the retrospective design and limited sample size.

All patients had an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1. Elevated LDH was observed in 67% of patients, and all tumors were BRAF wild type. Melanoma subtypes included cutaneous melanoma in five patients (55.6%), acral melanoma in three patients (33.3%), and melanoma of unknown primary in one patient (11.1%).

All patients had prior exposure to both anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 therapy before initiation of lenvatinib–pembrolizumab. Lenvatinib–pembrolizumab was administered as second-line therapy in five patients (55.6%), third-line therapy in two patients (22.2%), and fourth-line therapy in two patients (22.2%). Acquired resistance to ICIs was documented in 67%, primary resistance in 22%, and an indeterminate pattern of resistance in 11%. Early disease progression (≤12 weeks) occurred in 66.7% of patients.

Treatment Response and Survival Outcomes

The ORR was 22.2% (2/9), and the DCR was 33.3% (3/9). All disease-control cases were radiographically confirmed at least six weeks after initial response assessment. Among responding patients, the median duration of response was 9.6 months. Individual response patterns are shown in Figure 1, and detailed patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In a descriptive analysis by melanoma subtype, ORR and DCR were 20.0% and 40.0% among patients with cutaneous melanoma, and 33.3% and 33.3% among patients with acral melanoma, respectively. Exploratory Subgroup Analyses

Early progression (≤12 weeks) was observed in 25.0% of patients with baseline brain metastases and 80.0% of those without baseline brain metastases. Given the very limited sample size, these findings were interpreted as descriptive observations, and no formal statistical comparison was performed. No clear descriptive pattern was observed between LDH status and early progression.

Patients with brain metastases had an ORR of 50.0% and a DCR of 50.0%, whereas patients without brain involvement had an ORR of 0% and a DCR of 20.0%. By LDH status, ORR was 16.7% in the elevated group and 33.3% in the normal group; DCR was 33.3% in both groups. Median time on treatment was 7.3 months in the elevated LDH group and 4.0 months in the normal LDH group.

Treatment Exposure and Safety

Lenvatinib was initiated at 20 mg daily, and pembrolizumab was administered at 200 mg every three weeks. During treatment, three patients developed grade ≥2 immune-related adverse events requiring temporary treatment interruption and corticosteroid initiation. Lenvatinib dose reduction was required in two patients. Treatment-related adverse events were generally manageable. One patient experienced grade 3 colitis. The most common toxicities were hypothyroidism (22%), colitis (22%), and mucositis (11%).

Discussion

Both primary and acquired resistance to PD-1/CTLA-4-based immunotherapy continue to pose significant challenges for patients with malignant melanoma. In everyday clinical practice, effective treatment options remain scarce once the disease progresses. The phase II LEAP-004 trial (NCT03776136) 10 demonstrated encouraging clinical activity of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in patients with advanced melanoma who had progressed after PD-1/PD-L1–based therapy. In this study, the ORR was 21.4% (complete response: 2.9%; partial response: 18.4%), and the median duration of response was 8.3 months. Median progression-free survival and overall survival were 4.2 and 14.0 months, respectively. Notably, the ORR rose to 33.3% among patients who had not responded to both anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 therapies.5,11 An extended follow-up of more than 4 years demonstrated stable efficacy outcomes (ORR 24.3%), with durable responses in approximately one-third of patients and no new safety concerns. 12 The frequent need for dose modifications and temporary treatment interruptions reflects pragmatic toxicity management in routine practice and is consistent with previously reported lenvatinib-related adverse events.

Although real-world data remain limited, available retrospective series suggest that this combination is feasible outside clinical trial settings. 13 In our cohort, outcomes were broadly aligned with those reported in LEAP-004. Differences in disease control may reflect the higher proportion of patients with central nervous system (CNS) involvement, mucosal or acral subtypes, elevated LDH levels, and the need for practical dose adjustments in routine care. A recent retrospective comparative study including 84 patients, of whom 39 received pembrolizumab–lenvatinib, reported an ORR of 23.1%, median PFS of 4.8 months, and median OS of 14.2 months in the pembrolizumab–lenvatinib cohort after dual ICI failure. 13 Our response rates were broadly comparable. This difference may be partly attributable to the higher proportion of patients with baseline CNS involvement in our cohort (44.4% vs 12.8%) and the relatively short follow-up period. Radiotherapy has also emerged as a potential strategy to delay treatment escalation in selected patients with immunotherapy-resistant melanoma, particularly in the setting of oligoprogressive disease. Recent real-world evidence suggests that local ablative approaches, including stereotactic radiotherapy, may prolong disease control and allow continuation of immune checkpoint inhibitors beyond progression in carefully selected patients. For example, Zagardo et al reported encouraging outcomes with the integration of radiotherapy and ongoing immunotherapy in immunorefractory, oligoprogressive solid tumors, including prolonged time to next systemic treatment and durable local control with limited additional toxicity. 15 Although the patient population was heterogeneous and not directly comparable to our cohort, these findings highlight the potential role of radiotherapy-based approaches as an alternative or complementary strategy after ICI resistance. In addition, emerging techniques such as lattice radiotherapy may further enhance antitumor immune responses through spatially fractionated dose delivery, although clinical evidence in melanoma remains limited and prospective validation is needed. 16

The numerically shorter OS may also reflect early censoring and limited follow-up. As such, the current data are not mature, and a longer follow-up is needed to provide a reliable estimate of median OS. More than half of our cohort experienced early disease progression within 12 weeks (6/9, 66.7%), which highlights the aggressive nature of the disease in these patients and likely contributes to the shorter median OS. Interestingly, patients with baseline brain metastases demonstrated numerically higher response rates than those without brain involvement. Although exploratory and limited by a small sample size, this counterintuitive finding may reflect sample variability or selection bias and should not be overinterpreted. Accordingly, survival outcomes are presented as descriptive endpoints rather than definitive measures of efficacy.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy has emerged as an important treatment option for patients with advanced melanoma after progression on immune checkpoint inhibitors. Lifileucel received FDA approval in 2024 based on the phase II C-144-01 study. In the final 5-year analysis, lifileucel achieved an ORR of 31.4%, a median response duration of 36.5 months, and a median OS of 13.9 months in heavily pretreated patients. 17 Although cross-study comparisons should be interpreted cautiously, these outcomes provide important context for evaluating salvage treatment strategies after dual immune checkpoint inhibitor failure. Access to TIL therapy, however, remains limited in many regions, and alternative approaches, such as lenvatinib–pembrolizumab, continue to be relevant treatment options in routine clinical practice.

Several baseline disease characteristics in our cohort likely influenced treatment outcomes. Most patients had elevated LDH, a well-known adverse prognostic factor in advanced melanoma that has been linked to reduced benefit from later immunotherapy approaches. 18 Similarly, CNS involvement can limit systemic disease control and is associated with shorter PFS and OS, particularly in patients who have received multiple prior treatments. 19 Additionally, emerging evidence indicates that tumor mutational burden, the integrity of interferon-γ signaling, and the degree of immune exhaustion shape the response to ongoing PD-1 blockade after acquired resistance. 2 Although we did not systematically assess these biomarkers in our retrospective case series, they are important considerations for future prospective studies. They could help guide patient selection for lenvatinib-based combination therapies.

According to the most recent NCCN (v.2025) and ESMO 2024/2025 melanoma guidelines, lenvatinib with pembrolizumab is now a treatment option for select patients with advanced melanoma. This applies to patients with disease progression after PD-(L)1–based immunotherapy, particularly in cases of acquired resistance.4,20 This recommendation is based on a strong biological rationale. Combining VEGFR/FGFR inhibition with PD-1 blockade may overcome angiogenesis-driven immune suppression and reinvigorate antitumor immune responses. 6 The clinical benefits of this combination have been observed in melanoma and other cancers, including endometrial cancer (KEYNOTE-775; LEAP-005) and renal cell carcinoma (CLEAR/KEYNOTE-581). These findings underscore the broader relevance of targeting angiogenesis with immunotherapy and support this strategy for melanoma patients who are resistant to prior immunotherapy.21-23

Because our study was a small, single-arm case series without a comparator group, the outcomes we observed should be considered exploratory and useful primarily for generating hypotheses rather than drawing definitive conclusions. These results cannot be generalized and should not be interpreted as evidence that this regimen is superior to other post–ICI treatments, such as PD-1 re-challenge, ipilimumab-based combinations, targeted therapies for actionable mutations, or participation in clinical trials. Notably, the observed activity in patients with baseline brain metastases warrants further prospective evaluation in CNS-enriched populations. Additionally, due to the limited follow-up period and the ongoing survival of several patients at the time of analysis, the overall survival data are still immature. They are presented here for descriptive purposes only. To confirm these findings and clarify the role of lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab in this setting, larger, more robust prospective studies are needed.

Conclusion

In this real-world cohort of heavily pretreated patients with dual ICI-refractory melanoma, lenvatinib plus pembrolizumab demonstrated clinically meaningful antitumor activity with manageable toxicity. Despite the limited treatment options available after failure of anti–PD-1 and anti–CTLA-4 therapies, this combination may represent a feasible therapeutic option in selected patients. Further prospective studies are warranted to better define its role in the treatment landscape of advanced melanoma.
