 ABSTRACT
This report presents the application of SAMMY (Larson 2008), an R-matrix Bayesian fitting system, for
analyzing neutron transmission data to enable non-destructive isotopic identification. Transmission mea-
surements were conducted at the VENUS (Bilheux et al. 2023) beamline of the Spallation Neutron Source
(SNS) using samples with unknown or partially characterized compositions. Through the application of
SAMMY’s R-matrix Bayesian fitting capabilities to the measured transmission spectra and comparison
against evaluated nuclear data libraries, isotopic constituents can be quantitatively determined based on
their characteristic neutron resonance signatures. This methodology demonstrates significant potential for
applications in nuclear safeguards, forensics, and post-irradiation examination.

 1. INTRODUCTION
Non-destructive assay (NDA) techniques play a critical role in nuclear science and engineering by en-
abling material characterization without sample alteration or destruction. These methods are applied ex-
tensively in nuclear safeguards, post-irradiation examination, nuclear forensics, and fuel cycle characteri-
zation. Among the various NDA approaches, neutron-based techniques offer unique advantages for pene-
trating dense materials and interrogating isotopic compositions through nuclear resonance phenomena.
Neutron resonance transmission analysis (NRTA) is a class of NDA techniques that exploit the energy-
dependent attenuation of neutrons passing through a sample. Individual isotopes exhibit distinctive reso-
nance signatures in the epithermal and fast neutron energy ranges, enabling element- and isotope-specific
identification. These characteristic signatures can be observed through time-of-flight (ToF) measurements
utilizing short-pulsed neutron sources such as those available at the Spallation Neutron Source (SNS).
Through analysis of transmission spectra, NRTA provides quantitative information regarding the areal den-
sity of individual isotopes within the sample. This technique is particularly valuable for complex or un-
known samples for which the use of conventional analytical methods (e.g., gamma spectroscopy or mass
spectrometry) may be limited by shielding effects, matrix interferences, or sample integrity constraints.
NRTA offers additional advantages in scenarios involving special nuclear materials (SNMs) or long-lived
fission products that require specialized handling procedures.
The SAMMY code—originally developed for neutron cross-section measurement analysis—has been
adapted for NDA applications through inverse application of its R-matrix formalism. When combined with
high-resolution neutron data from facilities such as the VENUS beamline at SNS, SAMMY enables pre-
cise isotopic identification and quantification. These capabilities make it a robust tool for non-destructive
material characterization.
This work demonstrates the application of high-resolution transmission measurements in the low–neutron
energy range, below 1 keV, on the VENUS beamline coupled with advanced R-matrix analysis using SAMMY
for extracting quantitative material composition data.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
The Versatile Neutron Imaging Instrument (VENUS) beamline is situated on beamline 10 (BL-10) at Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)’s SNS. This high-resolution ToF facility accommodates diverse ex-
perimental campaigns, including neutron imaging, scattering, and transmission measurements. For nuclear
data and non-destructive testing applications, VENUS provides broad energy coverage of a white neutron
beam, high neutron flux, and flexible sample environments.
The beamline utilizes pulsed neutron production from a liquid mercury target driven by the SNS accelera-
tor complex. Neutrons are moderated in a hydrogen moderators and transported along a flight path of ap-
proximately 25 m to the sample position. Neutron energy determination is accomplished via ToF methods,
with resolution sufficient for resolving narrow resonance structures in the epithermal energy regime.
For transmission measurements, a well-collimated neutron beam traverses the sample, and transmitted neu-
trons are detected using a detector system positioned downstream. The VENUS beamline employs multi-
ple detectors for different experimental purposes, with the Microchannel Plate (MCP) Timepix1 (TPX1)
detector currently used for resonance imaging applications. The TPX1 detector features a quad-chip con-
figuration operating in frame mode, which provides excellent spatial and temporal resolution for ToF mea-
surements. The experimental configuration accommodates varying sample thicknesses and can handle ma-
terials ranging from light elements to high-atomic-number isotopes.
The neutron flux at VENUS spans a broad energy spectrum—from thermal energies (0.0253 eV) to tens of
keV and beyond—and is thus well-suited for resonance spectroscopy applications. The pulsed beam char-
acteristics, combined with short pulse widths and fast detectors, yield good energy resolution for NRTA.
The beamline incorporates appropriate shielding and remote handling infrastructure to safely conduct ex-
periments involving irradiated or radioactive materials.
In this investigation, VENUS was employed to measure neutron transmission through samples of unknown
or partially characterized composition as part of a comprehensive non-destructive analysis campaign. The
resulting ToF spectra were used as input data for R-matrix analysis using SAMMY, enabling isotopic iden-
tification through resonance pattern matching with evaluated nuclear data libraries.

2.1 VENUS RESOLUTION FUNCTION
A critical component of high-resolution neutron transmission analysis is the accurate characterization of
the experimental resolution function, which describes the energy spread of neutrons at the detector due to
the finite pulse width, moderator effects, flight path uncertainties, and detector timing response.
For the VENUS beamline at SNS, a dedicated resolution function was generated using a high-fidelity
Monte Carlo N-Particle (MCNP) model developed for the detailed moderator performance characterization
(Iverson et al. 2002), Iverson et al. 2003, (Lu 2013). With this model, the resolution function was calcu-
lated as a collection of neutron time-emission spectra for monoenergetic (±3%) neutron pulses across the
energy range from 0.5 meV to 1 keV. Following the methodology described in (Zavorka et al. 2018), the
spectra were convoluted with the proton beam of a triangular pulse shape of 350 ns width. The collection
of spectra was then processed and formatted for use with SAMMY.
In this way, the MCNP simulations provide energy-dependent characterization of the instrumental re-
sponse function, capturing the non-Gaussian features and energy asymmetries characteristic of pulsed
spallation neutron sources. The resulting resolution function has been validated against benchmark trans-
mission measurements at VENUS and demonstrates high accuracy in reproducing experimentally observed
resonance line shapes.

 3. ANALYSIS METHODOLOGY
3.1 SAMMY CODE SYSTEM
The SAMMY code is a multilevel R-matrix analysis tool developed at Oak Ridge National Laboratory for
fitting neutron-induced cross section data using a generalized least squares (Bayesian) formalism. Origi-
nally designed for evaluating resonance parameters in thermal and intermediate energy regions, SAMMY
combines physical rigor with statistical inference to extract optimal parameter sets from experimental data.
The code supports the analysis of transmission, capture, and scattering measurements, and incorporates ex-
perimental effects such as Doppler broadening, resolution functions, multiple scattering, and self-shielding.
In this work, SAMMY is used in an inverse analysis mode to perform material characterization: rather
than refining known resonance parameters, the code is used to compare measured transmission spectra
against known evaluations from libraries such as ENDF/B-VIII.0.(Brown. et al. 2018) For each candi-
date isotope, SAMMY simulates the expected transmission curve and applies its Bayesian framework to
fit the experimental data, yielding estimates of isotopic areal density (atoms/barn). This approach leverages
SAMMY’s ability to account for resonance interference, background corrections, and uncertainty propaga-
tion, making it an effective tool for non-destructive assay and nuclear forensics.

3.2 SEARCH PROCEDURE
Candidate isotopes were selected from standard Evaluated Nuclear Data File (ENDF) libraries. For each
candidate, SAMMY was employed to simulate the expected transmission spectrum, and a Bayesian fit was
performed to estimate the areal density (atoms/barn). The quality of each fit was evaluated using residu-
als and chi-squared statistics. To evaluate the effectiveness of the SAMMY-based non-destructive assay
methodology, a comprehensive set of test measurements was performed using three naturally occurring el-
ements: tantalum, gold, and hafnium. These materials were selected for their distinctive nuclear resonance
structures, low-lying neutron resonances, and relevance to nuclear applications including neutron shield-
ing, activation monitoring, and structural materials in reactor environments.
Each selected element exhibits well-characterized neutron resonances in the epithermal energy range, mak-
ing them ideal candidates for validating resonance-based transmission analysis. Hafnium was chosen to
demonstrate SAMMY’s capability to resolve multi-isotopic compositions because it has a particularly
complex spectrum with contributions from multiple stable isotopes. Gold and tantalum, with relatively
simpler single-isotope compositions, were selected as baselines for testing fitting precision and model fi-
delity.
Transmission measurements were conducted at the VENUS beamline utilizing its high-resolution ToF ca-
pabilities. Samples of natural tantalum, gold, and hafnium were individually mounted and exposed to the
collimated pulsed neutron beam with a sample-to-detector distance of 20 cm.1 The transmitted neutron
spectra were recorded using time-sensitive detectors, enabling energy-dependent transmission curves over
the range of approximately 1 eV to 1000 eV. These datasets form the basis for the subsequent SAMMY
analysis described in the following sections.
A significant challenge in applying SAMMY-based analysis to non-destructive transmission data lies in
the isotopic composition of target materials. While elements such as gold and tantalum are effectively
monoisotopic and quasi monoisotopic—composed predominantly of 197 Au (100 % stable) and 181 Ta (99.988

When analyzing neutron transmission data for natural hafnium, SAMMY must be applied in a composite
model that includes all contributing isotopes, each with its own evaluated resonance parameters and areal
density to be determined. The resulting fit requires simultaneous adjustment of multiple isotope concentra-
tions while accounting for overlap, interference, and resolution effects.
This multi-isotope treatment increases both the computational complexity and the sensitivity of the fit to
initial conditions and model assumptions. Moreover, the presence of closely spaced or partially overlap-
ping resonances makes it more difficult to isolate the contributions of individual isotopes. Nonetheless, this
complexity also serves as a robust test of the methodology: the ability of SAMMY to extract isotopic areal
densities from overlapping structures demonstrates the power and flexibility of its Bayesian fitting frame-
work when applied to realistic, multi-component materials.
In contrast, the analysis of gold and tantalum provides a useful control case, where the measured reso-
nance structure can be attributed to a single isotope. This simplifies the fitting process and helps validate
the broader methodology under conditions of reduced isotopic ambiguity. Together, these three materials
represent a spectrum of analytical challenges, offering a comprehensive test of the resonance-based non-
destructive analysis approach developed in this work.

 4. HAFNIUM ISOTOPE CASE STUDY
To test the methodology, neutron transmission measurements were analyzed for samples containing hafnium
isotopes. Two approaches were used:
 1. Using the standard, unmodified version of SAMMY.
 2. Using a modified version of SAMMY with custom enhancements to streamline isotopic scanning
 and improve convergence handling.
Both approaches yielded consistent results, but the modified version demonstrated faster processing time
and greater flexibility for batch identification over multiple isotopic hypotheses. The modifications in-
cluded internal scripting hooks to automate the selection and fitting loop.

4.1 STANDARD SAMMY PROCEDURE
The standard implementation of SAMMY was first applied to the neutron transmission data obtained from
the VENUS beamline. In this mode, SAMMY was used in its conventional configuration, where the user
manually defines the candidate isotope(s), prepares the input file with ENDF-formatted resonance param-
eters, and executes the fitting of transmission data using R-matrix theory with Bayesian inference. Details
of the procedure used are shown in Appendix A.
Every isotope identified as a candidate in the unknown sample was treated independently. SAMMY per-
formed a full calculation of Doppler broadening, resolution convolution (using the VENUS resolution
function obtained from simulation), and self-shielding corrections. For each case, the number of atoms
per barn was estimated by adjusting the areal density parameter until convergence was achieved with the
measured data.
The standard workflow, while robust and well-tested, involved a considerable amount of manual effort in
iterating over candidate isotopes and interpreting the fits. It was effective for single- or dual-isotope sam-
ples such as gold and tantalum, but increasingly cumbersome for complex multi-isotopic materials like
hafnium; however, the gold and tantalum results are not included in this work.

4.2 MODIFIED SAMMY IMPLEMENTATION
To improve the efficiency and scalability of the analysis, a modified version of SAMMY was developed.
This implementation introduces internal scripting logic and control hooks that enable semi-automated
scanning of multiple isotopic candidates against a given transmission dataset. The modifications preserve
SAMMY’s core R-matrix formalism and Bayesian fitting approach but streamline the process of hypothe-
sis testing across a library of potential isotopes.
In the modified version, isotopic hypotheses can be queued, and SAMMY automatically adjusts the areal
density for each case, fits the resulting transmission spectrum, and returns statistical figures of merit such
as reduced chi-squared and posterior covariance matrices. This framework dramatically reduces manual
handling and supports batch processing of complex sample scenarios.
The modified SAMMY version was particularly valuable when analyzing hafnium, which consists of six
stable isotopes, each contributing distinct but overlapping resonance signatures. With the modified work-
flow, all isotopes could be evaluated and adjusted in a single loop, increasing both the reliability and repro-
ducibility of the analysis. For a full description of the procedure, refer to Appendix B.

 6

4.3 COMPARISON OF STANDARD AND MODIFIED APPROACHES
Both the standard and modified SAMMY procedures yielded consistent and physically meaningful results
for all tested materials. For monoisotopic elements such as gold and tantalum, the difference in workflow
efficiency was minor, but the numerical outputs matched closely in terms of areal density estimates and
fit quality. However, for multi-isotopic systems like hafnium, the modified version demonstrated a clear
advantage.
The modified workflow provided more rapid convergence, reduced human error in input management, and
enabled more systematic uncertainty quantification across multiple isotopes. It also facilitated the imple-
mentation of decision criteria based on statistical metrics, such as ranking isotopic fits by minimized chi-
squared or evaluating posterior uncertainties in fitted densities. Overall, the modified SAMMY implemen-
tation supports a more scalable and automation-ready path forward for applying neutron resonance analy-
sis in non-destructive assay campaigns.

 5. SAMMY ANALYSIS OF EXPERIMENTAL DATA
The analysis presented in this section employs the modified version of SAMMY to fit neutron transmission
data obtained from the VENUS beamline at the Spallation Neutron Source. Unlike traditional applications
of SAMMY, where resonance parameters such as energies, widths, and spin assignments are refined, this
implementation maintains all nuclear resonance parameters fixed at their evaluated values from ENDF
libraries. Instead, the fitting procedure focuses exclusively on adjusting experimental and sample-specific
parameters—including areal density (atoms/barn), temperature (for Doppler broadening), normalization
factors, and background corrections.
To establish a baseline reference for the analysis, SAMMY was initially employed to calculate neutron
transmission through the test samples using resonance parameters directly from the ENDF/B-VIII.0 eval-
uation, without any parameter fitting or adjustment. In this baseline configuration, sample areal density,
temperature, normalization factors, and resolution function effects were omitted entirely. The purpose of
this step is to demonstrate the extent to which evaluated nuclear data alone are insufficient to reproduce
experimental transmission measurements without proper modeling of experimental conditions.
As illustrated in Figure 2, the resulting theoretical transmission curve exhibits notable discrepancies when
compared with the experimental data. To illustrate this more clearly, Figure 3 presents the deviations ob-
served in the energy interval between 60 and 80 eV. These discrepancies are observed in both the depth
and width of resonance dips, as well as in the overall transmission normalization. Such mismatches are
expected because the evaluated resonance parameters are not optimized for specific experimental condi-
tions—such as actual sample thickness, temperature (affecting Doppler broadening), detector efficiency,
or the detailed time-energy resolution characteristics of the VENUS beamline. Hence, using the resonance
parameters from ENDF directly does not provide sufficient information to identify the constituents of a
sample.
This comparison emphasizes the critical importance of incorporating realistic experimental parameters
when applying SAMMY for non-destructive analysis. In the subsequent analysis, results from the modified
SAMMY approach—where density, temperature, normalization, resonance alignments, and background
are systematically varied—are presented.

 6. DISCUSSION
Although the present calculations were performed using natural hafnium, without isotopically enriched
samples, the results confirm that SAMMY can distinguish isotopes through their unique resonance sig-
natures. This work therefore establishes a qualitative basis for isotope identification, while quantitative
determination of isotopic fractions can also be achieved with SAMMY, as the updated version of the code
includes this capability. The success of this approach depends critically on several factors: the availabil-
ity and quality of evaluated nuclear data, the experimental energy resolution, and the capability to resolve
overlapping resonance features.
The methodology presented here advances the state-of-the-art in non-destructive nuclear material charac-
terization by combining high-resolution neutron transmission spectroscopy with sophisticated R-matrix
analysis. The demonstrated ability to extract isotopic compositions from complex, multi-component sam-
ples can support applications in nuclear safeguards verification, forensic analysis of nuclear materials, and
comprehensive post-irradiation examination protocols.
Future developments will focus on expanding the automated isotope scanning capabilities and integrating
complementary measurement types, such as neutron capture data, to enhance isotopic discrimination. The
extension of this methodology to samples with completely unknown compositions represents a significant
research objective that will further strengthen its utility for nuclear security and materials characterization
applications.

 7. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This investigation documented in this report demonstrates a novel application of SAMMY for non-destructive
isotopic analysis utilizing high-resolution transmission data from the SNS/VENUS facility. The method-
ology successfully combines R-matrix theory with Bayesian statistical inference to extract quantitative
isotopic information from neutron resonance signatures, advancing capabilities for nuclear material char-
acterization. The key contributions of this work include the following:
 • Development and validation of an accurate experimental resolution function for the VENUS beam-
 line.
 • Demonstration of SAMMY’s effectiveness for inverse analysis of multi-isotopic samples.
 • Implementation of enhanced automation features that streamline the analysis workflow for complex
 materials.
Future research efforts will focus on the following areas:
 • Expanding the automation framework to support fully unknown sample compositions.
 • Integrating additional measurement modalities such as neutron capture spectroscopy to improve iso-
 topic discrimination. While transmission and capture cross-section data provide distinct but comple-
 mentary information, they are often performed together to obtain a more complete understanding of
 a material. Transmission measurements are particularly valuable for identifying the isotopes present,
 determining resonance energies, and characterizing general interaction probabilities. In contrast,
 capture cross-section measurements yield reaction-specific information, such as the precise proba-
 bility of neutron capture at individual resonances, and can provide additional insight into spin and
 parity assignments. When combined, the two types of measurements enable a more reliable evalu-
 ation of nuclear data, supporting both fundamental studies and applied analyses in reactor physics,
 criticality safety, and nondestructive assay.
 • Extending the methodology to materials of interest for nuclear security applications. This work can
 be extended to materials of interest for nuclear security applications, particularly those whose reso-
 nance structures begin above the keV region. Such an extension would broaden the applicability of
 the methodology, enabling the analysis of a wider class of isotopes relevant to safeguards, nonprolif-
 eration, and nuclear forensics.
The development of real-time analysis capabilities and the integration of machine learning techniques for
pattern recognition represent additional avenues for enhancing the methodology’s operational utility. This
approach represents a significant advancement in non-destructive nuclear material characterization and has
the potential for broad application in such domains as nuclear safeguards verification, nuclear forensics
analysis, and fuel cycle monitoring.

In this application of SAMMY, the resonance parameters provided in the ENDF evaluations are not var-
ied during the fitting process. That is, the resonance energies, widths, and spin/parity assignments are
taken as fixed inputs and are not subject to optimization. Instead, the fitting focuses on experimental and
sample-specific parameters that strongly influence the shape and normalization of the transmission spec-
trum. These include:
 • the areal density (atoms/barn) of the isotope in the sample,
 • the sample temperature, which affects Doppler broadening,
 • the normalization factor, to account for experimental scaling (e.g., source strength, detector effi-
 ciency),
 • background correction, to address residual effects not captured by the model.
By constraining the fit to these parameters, the analysis retains fidelity to the evaluated nuclear data while
allowing the model to adjust for real-world experimental conditions. This approach is particularly well-
suited to non-destructive analysis, where the objective is to characterize the sample composition without
modifying or interpreting the fundamental nuclear structure data.
In practice, the additional parameters used in the fitting process are specified in the SAMMY input fol-
lowing the resonance data section. These include blocks for broadening, miscellaneous experimental cor-
rections, and normalization/background. Each of these sections provides control over key experimental
variables that influence the shape and position of the transmission spectrum. Since the resonance parame-
ters themselves are not varied, accurate modeling of these quantities is critical for achieving a meaningful
fit. Below is an example of such parameter blocks used in this analysis:

 section. Each line includes numerical values followed by a series of integer flags located in the far-right
 columns. These flags indicate whether a given parameter will be varied (“searched”) during the Bayesian
 fitting process. A flag value of 1 denotes that the corresponding parameter is active in the fit, while 0 means
 it is held constant. For example, see Line 2 of Listing 6 under the title “BROADENING PARAMETERS
 FOLLOW".
 The third parameter (0.00010121) represents the areal density of the sample in atoms/barn. The 1 in the
 third position among the trailing flags instructs SAMMY to vary this value during the fit, while all other
 parameters in this line remain fixed. This mechanism allows the user to precisely control which physical
 parameters are optimized in each analysis, ensuring that only experimentally meaningful quantities are
 adjusted.
 The procedure described above for 177 Hf must be repeated for each of the remaining naturally occurring
 hafnium isotopes. Each isotope requires its own set of resonance parameters to be retrieved, processed, and
 formatted appropriately. These individual parameter sets must then be combined into a single composite
 file that represents the full isotopic composition of natural hafnium. This process is inherently repetitive
 and prone to human error, as it involves manual editing of multiple SAMMY input and parameter files.
 The risk of introducing inconsistencies—such as overlapping resonance ranges, incorrect isotope ordering,
 or formatting issues—is significant, particularly when dealing with complex multi-isotope systems. This
 challenge motivated the development of enhancements to SAMMY aimed at automating the multi-isotope
 processing workflow and improving user interaction, thereby reducing the potential for error and stream-
 lining the non-destructive analysis of complex samples.
