The relationship between multidimensional parenting practices and oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety in a regional Turkish paediatric population
Authors: Hatice Yıldırım, Ayça Kurt, İrem Okumuş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between multidimensional parenting practices and children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety. This cross-sectional study included 605 children aged 6–12 years attending their first dental visit and their parents. Demographic data, oral health behaviours, and dietary habits were obtained from parent reports. Parenting practices were assessed using the Multidimensional Parenting Assessment Scale (MAPS). Children’s dental anxiety was evaluated using the Modified Child Dental Anxiety Scale-Faces (MCDAS-f) and a Dental Anxiety Questionnaire. Oral hygiene parameters were recorded through clinical examination. Group comparisons were performed using Mann–Whitney U and Kruskal–Wallis tests, and correlations were analyzed with Spearman’s test (p < 0.05). Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between independent and dependent variables. Positive parenting behaviours were found to be significantly associated with lower DMFT/dmft (Decay (D), Missing (M), and Filled (F) teeth), lower Plaque Index (PI), Gingival Index (GI), and reduced dental anxiety (p < 0.05). Poor oral health was linked to younger age, lower parental education, inadequate brushing, and higher sugar intake (p < 0.001). Multivariable analyses revealed that general positivity was significantly associated only with DMFT/dmft status (OR = 1.031, 95% CI 1.004–1.058, p = 0.022). General negativity showed significant associations with PI (OR = 1.045), PUFA/pufa (OR = 1.051), and BOP (OR = 1.030). While the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire was a strong independent predictor for PUFA/pufa (OR = 1.774), GI (OR = 2.034), and BOP (OR = 1.885), the MCDAS-f did not emerge as a significant predictor for any clinical index. No significant relationship was found between dental anxiety and DMFT/dmft or PI. Parenting practices are significantly associated with children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety. Supportive parenting is linked to better oral outcomes and lower anxiety, highlighting the need to consider family dynamics in paediatric dental care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-63942-w.

Introduction

Parents are crucial caregivers who play a vital role in their children’s lives, providing health support, acting as role models, and educating them1. The roles of parents within a family involve shaping their children’s behaviour and attitudes through parenting practices and interactions2–4. Parents are their children’s primary caregivers and therefore their knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices regarding oral health are directly related to their children’s oral health4. The parental role in a child’s oral health can be considered from two perspectives. The first is the parents’ role in influencing their child’s oral health status and behavior, such as their decision-making regarding brushing habits, the content of toothpaste used (e.g., fluoride toothpaste use), and healthy and balanced nutrition5. The second is the impact of the parental role on the child’s behaviour during dental treatment and their coping with stress5,6.

Dental caries, traumatic injuries, and related oral health issues requiring treatment can be considered a direct result of parenting practices7. The environment they create for their children affects oral hygiene (brushing habits, etc.), eating habits, and dental visit routines. Low parental self-efficacy, little dental health knowledge, and parenting stress are associated with an increased rate of caries development in children8–10. Since parents are responsible for almost all aspects of a child’s oral health status, their parenting practices are thought to influence their child’s oral health status and oral health behaviours7,10,11.

Dental anxiety, defined as a patient’s response to stress specific to their dental condition, is a global challenge in the field of oral health12. Dental anxiety can negatively impact oral health practices and dental practice2,5,13. Dental anxiety is a multifactorial condition13, and parenting practices can be one of these factors. Parenting practices are specific behaviours that parents use to socialize their children, and while there is increasing acceptance in paediatric dentistry of the role of family in dental anxiety2,7,14, the mechanisms by which parenting practices contribute to children’s anxiety and oral health outcomes have not been sufficiently explored7.

Although previous studies have provided evidence supporting a potential link between parenting practices, child behaviour, and dental caries, studies that comprehensively investigate this issue are quite limited5,7,15,16. The limited literature on parenting practices indicates that little is known about the effects of parenting on dental behaviours7,17. Similarly, studies specifically evaluating parenting practices using behavioural tools applicable in paediatric dentistry are limited7. As far as we could see from our literature review, it is possible to state that the applicability of the Multidimensional Parenting Assessment Scale (MAPS) on children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety has not been previously studied.

In light of all this information, our study aims to investigate the relationship between multidimensional parenting practices and children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety. Our null hypothesis (H₀) suggests that parenting practices have no effect on children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety, while the alternative hypothesis (H₁) suggests that parenting practices do have an effect on children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety.

Methods

Study setting and ethics approval

The study was conducted at the Department of Paediatric Dentistry of Recep Tayyip Erdogan University in Rize, Türkiye, between May 2023 and May 2024, and the study population reflects the demographic characteristics of this regional population. This survey-based study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Recep Tayyip Erdogan University (Protocol No: 2024/230). Children and their parents were provided with both verbal and written information regarding the study’s duration, content, and potential benefits. Parents who consented to participate were asked to sign a voluntary informed consent form. The study protocol is adhered to the principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki, including all relevant amendments and revisions.

Patient sample

Given that the MAPS produces ordinal data ranging from 1 to 5, and the dental anxiety questionnaire yields ordinal scores ranging from 0 to 124, comprising 31 items each scored between 0 and 318, it was anticipated that the relationship between these variables would be analysed using Spearman’s rank correlation. Spearman’s rank correlation was planned to describe bivariate associations among study variables. Because no prior studies reported effect sizes for associations between MAPS parenting dimensions and paediatric dental anxiety, we used a conservative expected small-to-moderate association for the a priori power analysis. Specifically, we assumed an expected correlation of ρ = 0.25 between MAPS parenting scores and Modified Child Dental Anxiety Scale-Faces version (MCDAS-f)19. Using G*Power for a two-tailed correlation test (α = 0.05, power = 0.80, H1: ρ = 0.25), the minimum required sample size was 124 participants. To increase precision for multivariable analyses and to account for incomplete questionnaires or missing clinical recordings, we enrolled 605 eligible parent–child dyads. The questionnaire forms were prepared electronically using an application and were self-administered by parents via a tablet device. The clinician performing the oral examination and the researcher responsible for questionnaire administration were different individuals to minimize potential interviewer bias. The examiner performing the clinical assessment was blinded to questionnaire responses.

Data collection and variables

The first phase of the study focused on gathering data regarding participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, as well as the oral health and nutritional habits of the children. In this context, basic demographic information was collected, including the child’s gender and age, the parent’s gender and age, and the parent’s level of education. Oral health behaviours were assessed using parent-reported questionnaires. Toothbrushing status was categorized as does not brush, brushes irregularly, or brushes regularly. Daily toothbrushing frequency was recorded as once daily, twice daily, or three times daily. Brushing before bedtime was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no). Dietary behaviour related to oral health was evaluated by recording the daily frequency of sugary food consumption (including candy and chocolate), categorized as once daily, twice daily, or three or more times daily.

Selection criteria

Children aged 6 to 12 years and their parents were included in the study if the child had no systemic disease, no physical or mental disability, no prior dental treatment, and no need for emergency dental care. Children who did not meet these conditions, and children and parents who were unwilling to participate in the study, were excluded.

Measurements

In the second part of the study, MAP18, MCDAS-f19, and the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire20 were administered. All questionnaires (MAPS, MCDAS-f, and the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire) were administered prior to the clinical oral examination to minimize potential procedural confounding of children’s anxiety responses.

The MAPS includes subscales representing comprehensive positive parenting, comprising positive reinforcement, warm relationship, supportive approach, and proactive parenting; as well as comprehensive negative parenting, which encompasses hostility, low control, and physical control18. Higher scores on each dimension indicate a greater presence of the corresponding parenting quality18. The scale is measured using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from ‘Always’ (5 points) to ‘Never’ (1 point). Comprehensive positive parenting is grouped under the main heading of ‘General positivity’, while comprehensive negative parenting is grouped under the main heading of ‘General negativity’.

The MCDAS-f is an 8-item self-report measure of dental anxiety, featuring five pictorial response options for each item19. Total scores range from 8 to 4019.

The Dental Anxiety Questionnaire assesses the child’s response to the question ‘Are you afraid of going to the dentist?’ with possible answers: No, A little, Yes, a lot20. Dental anxiety was assessed using both the Modified Child Dental Anxiety Scale-Faces (MCDAS-f) and a single-item Dental Anxiety Questionnaire. The MCDAS-f was used to obtain a multidimensional evaluation of children’s dental anxiety levels, while the single-item questionnaire was included to provide a rapid global assessment and complementary validation of dental anxiety perception.

Clinical indexes

In the final stage of the study, children’s oral examinations were conducted using the Community Periodontal Index (CPI) probe and mirror (WHO 973/80; Martin, Solingen, Germany), following the standards established by the World Health Organization. For permanent teeth, the DMFT index score was calculated as the total number of Decayed (D), Missing (M), and Filled (F) teeth. The same method was applied to determine the dmft index score for primary teeth21. The PUFA index was utilized for both permanent and primary teeth to assess the status of untreated caries, with the following categories: P (pulpal involvement due to severe caries), U (ulceration caused by sharp tooth fragments), F (fistula), and A (abscess)22. Because the study population consisted of children in the mixed dentition period, caries experience was assessed using the combined DMFT/dmft and PUFA/pufa scores, which represent the total number of decayed, missing, and filled teeth in both permanent and primary dentitions. A score was assigned to each tooth, and the individual’s total PUFA score was calculated cumulatively, similar to the DMFT index. For each index, primary and permanent dentition scores were summed to obtain a total score representing the overall disease burden.

The Silness and Löe Modified Plaque Index (PI) was employed to assess plaque accumulation on the teeth of the participating children23. Plaque assessment was conducted on four surfaces of each evaluated tooth: mesial, distal, labial, and lingual, with each surface scored on a scale from 0 to 324. Individual PI scores were calculated by dividing the total plaque score by the number of tooth surfaces examined. The Gingival Index (GI) was employed to assess gingival inflammation, with all evaluations performed on Ramfjord teeth. Clinical bleeding and gingival appearance were assessed by gently moving a periodontal probe held parallel to the tooth axis with a force not exceeding 20 g across the four gingival surfaces (mesial, distal, labial, and lingual) of the selected teeth. Each surface was scored on a scale from 0 to 3, and these scores were used to calculate the overall GI25. Gingival bleeding was evaluated using the Bleeding on Probing Index (BOP). To determine the index, a periodontal probe was gently inserted into the gingival pocket, and bleeding was assessed within 10–15 s of probing at the mesial, distal, buccal, and lingual sites of all teeth. A positive score was recorded if bleeding occurred. The BOP was calculated as the percentage of bleeding sites relative to the total number of sites examined26. For children in the mixed dentition period, PI, GI, and BOP scores were recorded for both primary and permanent teeth, and a mean score was calculated to represent the participant’s overall oral hygiene and gingival health status.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi software (version 2.3.28). The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of data distribution. As the data were not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were utilized. Results are presented as means and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare median differences between two independent groups, while the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied to assess differences among more than two independent groups. Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between independent and dependent variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with the clinical indices. For this purpose, each clinical index was dichotomized as follows: dmft ≤ 6 versus dmft > 6, plaque index ≤ 1 versus plaque index > 1, pufa ≤ 1 versus pufa > 1, gingival index ≤ 1 versus gingival index > 1, and bleeding index ≤ 1 versus bleeding index > 1. Separate logistic regression models were constructed for each clinical outcome. Independent variables included in the multivariable logistic regression models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, previous literature, and their potential role as confounders in the relationship between parenting practices, dental anxiety, oral health behaviours, and clinical oral health indices. The adjusted models included child age, child sex, parental education level, oral hygiene-related behaviours, dietary variables, General Positivity, General Negativity, MCDAS-f score, and Dental Anxiety Questionnaire score. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis; participants with missing values for variables required in a given model were excluded from that specific model, and no statistical imputation was performed.First, unadjusted odds ratios were calculated; subsequently, adjusted odds ratios were obtained by including potential confounding variables such as age, sex, parental education level, and oral hygiene/dietary behaviors in the models. The results were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals and p-values. To assess the reliability of oral health indices, data were collected from twenty randomly selected patients at two-week intervals. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to assess the associations among MCDAS-f, the Dental Anxiety Scale, General Positivity, and General Negativity scores, and to examine the convergent relationship between the two dental anxiety measures. Since the DMFT/dmft variable did not show a normal distribution and the primary aim of the analysis was to identify factors associated with a higher caries burden rather than to model changes in the score on a continuous scale, DMFT/dmft was dichotomized according to the sample median as DMFT/dmft ≤ 6 and DMFT/dmft > 6. Similarly, PUFA/pufa, PI, GI, and BOP scores were dichotomized using a threshold value of 1 (≤ 1 vs. > 1) to distinguish participants with lower and higher levels of oral disease severity and oral hygiene impairment. These categorizations facilitated the interpretation of the logistic regression models and enabled the identification of factors associated with clinically less favorable oral health outcomes. Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by repeated questionnaire evaluations performed by the same examiner on 60 participants at a two-month interval. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as r > 0.80, indicating excellent reliability. Detailed intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals are presented in the Supplementary Information (SI) 1 and 1.

Discussion

In our study examining the relationship between multidimensional parenting practices and children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and dental anxiety, we found that positive parenting practices were significantly associated with lower DMFT/dmft, lower PI, GI, and reduced dental anxiety. However, negative parenting patterns—particularly hostility, physical control, and lack of control—showed a strong positive correlation with poor oral health, higher dental anxiety, and prevalent negative parenting patterns. The multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated that parenting-related psychosocial characteristics and dental anxiety measures were differentially associated with oral health indices after adjustment for demographic and behavioral confounders. In particular, higher general negativity scores were associated with poorer oral hygiene and increased signs of oral disease activity. In contrast, dental anxiety showed stronger associations with inflammatory and untreated dental conditions. These findings suggest that psychosocial factors may contribute to children’s oral health through different behavioral and emotional pathways. We hypothesized that multidimensional parenting practices would be related to children’s oral health outcomes and dental anxiety levels. Consistent with our hypothesis that multidimensional parenting practices would be associated with children’s oral health outcomes and dental anxiety levels, the multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated that parenting-related psychosocial characteristics and dental anxiety measures were differentially associated with oral health indices after adjustment for demographic and behavioural confounders. In particular, higher general negativity scores were associated with poorer oral hygiene and increased signs of oral disease activity. In contrast, dental anxiety showed stronger associations with inflammatory and untreated dental conditions. An unexpected finding was a positive association between overall positivity and DMFT/dmft status in the adjusted regression model. This result differed from that observed in univariate analyses and should therefore be interpreted with caution. The association emerged only after adjusting for demographic and behavioral variables, suggesting that residual confounding or suppressive effects between associated variables may have influenced the findings. Some behavioral variables included in the model, such as toothbrushing habits and dietary behaviors, may also lie on the pathway between parenting practices and oral health outcomes. Therefore, adjustment for these variables may partly attenuate or alter the observed association. Moreover, DMFT/dmft reflects cumulative lifetime caries experience rather than current disease activity. Thus, higher parental positivity may partly reflect increased parental support following prior caries experience rather than being a causal risk factor for caries. This result should therefore be interpreted with caution.

According to the literature review, the fact that the effect of parenting practices on children’s dental anxiety, nutritional habits, and oral dental health has not been examined before using MAPS makes this study unique.

Although there is a study in the literature reporting that boys have higher PI averages than girl27, there is also a study reporting that there is no significant difference in the distribution of oral hygiene status by gender28. These differences may be due to age and socio-cultural differences between the research groups. The literature has reported that the risk of dental caries decreases with increasing age29. Similarly, another study found the highest prevalence of PUFA/pufa in the 7-year-old age group, while the lowest prevalence was in the 12-year-old age group30. Changes in dietary habits and increased effectiveness of oral care practices may explain the improved plaque control and decrease in dental caries. Furthermore, the literature has reported a negative correlation between increasing parental education and children’s experience of dental caries31. In the current study, children’s DMFT/dmft and PI values decreased as their tooth-brushing pattern and frequency increased. The frequency of brushing before bedtime was also found to be effective in reducing DMFT/dmft scores. Literature reports a significant and negative correlation between brushing teeth more than once a day and DMFT/dmft index scores31. The current study found that children’s oral health index values increased significantly with their daily consumption of products like candy, chocolate, etc. Similarly, it has been reported in the literature that the rate of tooth decay is higher in those who consume sugary foods between meals32.

Parenting practices are defined as specific behaviours that parents use to socialize their children14 Dental anxiety is a multifactorial condition that negatively impacts the effectiveness of oral and dental health services33, and therefore, it is crucial for dentists to recognize risk factors, particularly parenting practices, and take the necessary precautions. The impact of parenting practices on children’s eating habits, oral and dental health practices, behaviour in the dental office, and dental anxiety remains insufficiently studied34. In this study, paediatric patients’ oral health index data were recorded. Patients were administered the MCDAS-f and the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire. The MCDAS-f is a widely validated self-report instrument designed to capture children’s subjective emotional responses to dental situations in an age-appropriate manner35. In contrast, the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire provides complementary information by assessing anxiety-related behaviours and situational responses, offering a broader contextual perspective36. These instruments were used in conjunction to increase the comprehensiveness of anxiety assessment and to strengthen the validity of the findings through multi-method evaluation. In the present study, A strong positive correlation was observed between the two measures, supporting the consistency of the anxiety assessments. However, in the adjusted analyses, only the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire remained significantly associated with certain clinical oral health indices. This difference may be related to the distinct dimensions of dental anxiety assessed by the two instruments. While the MCDAS-f primarily evaluates children’s subjective emotional responses to dental situations, the Dental Anxiety Questionnaire may better reflect anxiety-related behaviours and perceptions that are more directly associated with oral health outcomes.

Age has been considered an important determining factor in the level of dental anxiety37. Previous studies have consistently reported that dental anxiety decreases as children grow older37,38. In the current study, parental education level was related to child anxiety, and children of parents younger than 35 years of age were found to have higher anxiety levels. Younger parents may have less experience in establishing consistent oral health routines and managing children’s emotional responses during dental care procedures. In addition, lower parental education levels may be associated with reduced oral health literacy, which could negatively influence preventive oral health behaviours and coping strategies related to dental visits. These factors may partly explain the higher dental anxiety levels observed in this group.

Given the limited number of studies utilizing the MAPS18,39–42, comparisons with the existing literature are restricted. The literature has reported strong correlations with the child’s gender41,43, the parent’s gender, and the child’s age according to the MAPS41–43. The current study found that scores for intimate relationships and low control on the MAPS were higher in girls, while scores for positive reinforcement, hostility, and physical control were higher in boys. Pervasive positive parenting scores were also higher in girls. These differences may reflect variations in parental expectations and communication styles according to child gender within family and sociocultural contexts. Similarly, parenting practices have been reported to vary by child age group44. The current study noted that scores for intimacy, low control, physical control, and hostility were higher in children aged 9 and under, and pervasive negative parenting scores were also higher in this age group. Younger children may require more direct parental supervision and behavioural control in daily routines, which could partly explain the higher scores observed in both controlling and negative parenting practices.

The literature suggests that mothers adopt a more democratic approach than fathers and exhibit a more peaceful and constructive approach toward their children. In contrast, fathers have been shown to exhibit more aggressive attitudes and are therefore perceived as more authoritarian than mothers45. In the current study, women scored higher than men on MAPS for proactive parenting, intimate relationships, positive reinforcement, supportive approach, low control, and comprehensive positive parenting. A similar study noted that mothers, compared to fathers, adopted a more democratic approach and exhibited a more peaceful and constructive approach toward their children45. One study reported that newer generations of parents tend to be more sensitive to their children’s needs, as reflected in greater flexibility and decreased rigidity in their expectations and behavioural attitudes toward children46.

There are studies in the literature showing the relationship between parents’ education levels and the parenting practices they have47,48. The current study found that, according to the MAPS, higher parental education levels were associated with higher scores in proactive parenting, supportive approach, positive reinforcement, intimate relationship, and overall positive parenting. Conversely, lower education levels were associated with higher scores in hostility, physical control, low control, and overall negative parenting. Furthermore, being a firstborn parent was associated with higher scores in proactive parenting, intimate relationships, supportive approach, and overall positive parenting. The literature suggests that this is because parents generally tend to exhibit more positive and supportive behaviours toward their firstborn. This tendency is attributed to their first child’s initial experience with parenthood and the parents’ higher expectations and hopes for their firstborn49. Parents who tend to give more attention, care, and praise to their firstborns often spend more time and energy on these children49.

Previous studies have reported significant associations between parenting practices, children’s caries experience, and dental anxiety9,50–52. The current study found that children of parents with high scores on proactive parenting, intimacy, positive reinforcement, and supportive approaches, which are included in the comprehensive positive parenting group, had lower rates of dental caries, untreated teeth, and signs of poor oral hygiene, such as plaque and gingival bleeding. The current study found that parents of children with high dental anxiety and MCDAS-f scores had lower proactive parenting scores. In contrast, higher overall negative parenting scores were associated with poorer oral health outcomes and higher levels of dental anxiety. These findings suggest that parental attitudes associated with children’s emotional states may play an important role in paediatric dental anxiety and oral health outcomes. In particular, negative parenting practices may be associated with adverse emotional outcomes in children, and these emotional difficulties may in turn be related to increased dental anxiety9.

One study indicated that parenting practice is not a static relationship between the child and the parent, but rather the result of a long-term and dynamic process53. This assumption was supported in the same study, where the child’s actions and parenting practices interacted53. The increased variety of questions posed to parents can lead to differences in parenting practice classifications. While a child’s behaviour can be a response to parenting practice, parenting practice can also be influenced by the child’s behaviour. In addition, over time, the child’s behaviour can change the parents’ influence on the child, leading to a two-way interaction53.

A limitation of this study is the potential for social desirability bias, as parents may have responded to survey questions in a manner reflecting more socially acceptable and idealized parenting behaviours rather than their actual practices and personality traits. Previous research has suggested that parents may tend to conceal aspects of their own personality traits54. In particular, responses to items related to physical control or violence may have been underreported to avoid creating a negative impression. Oral health behaviours were parent-reported and may have been affected by recall and social desirability bias. Additionally, this study was conducted in a relatively limited population with specific sociocultural characteristics, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the inclusion criterion requiring no prior dental treatment may have resulted in a selected study population with potentially higher caries experience and dental anxiety levels than the general paediatric population. This criterion was intentionally applied to minimize the potential effect of previous dental experiences on dental anxiety and to provide more homogeneous baseline conditions for evaluating the association between parental attitudes and children’s dental anxiety. However, this approach may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, although the regression models were adjusted for several demographic and behavioural variables, the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Another limitation is that the clinical indices were dichotomized for logistic regression analyses. Although this approach improved clinical interpretability and enabled the identification of children with higher oral disease burden, it may have reduced statistical power and led to some information loss compared with analyses using continuous or ordinal outcomes. Another limitation of this study is the dichotomization of several continuous outcome variables for logistic regression analyses. Although this approach facilitated model interpretation and the identification of factors associated with less favorable oral health outcomes, it may have resulted in some loss of information, reduced statistical power, and potential underestimation of more subtle associations. Additionally, due to the cross-sectional design of the study, causal relationships cannot be established, and the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than cause–and–effect relationships.

Clinical ımplications

The findings of this study suggest that parenting practices may influence children’s oral health behaviours and dental anxiety. Supportive and proactive parenting may contribute to better oral hygiene routines and reduced stress during dental care, whereas punitive or negative parenting approaches may be associated with increased anxiety and poorer oral health outcomes. Understanding parenting practices can help paediatric dentists tailor their communication and behaviour management strategies. Parents who exhibit permissive behaviours may benefit from the dentist’s oral hygiene guidance, while those who use punitive approaches may need supportive counseling focusing on positive reinforcement and anxiety-reducing communication. These approaches can contribute to improved cooperation in children, reduced dental anxiety, and better long-term oral health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study contributes to the literature by examining the relationships between parenting practices and children’s oral health status, oral health behaviours, and levels of dental anxiety. The findings suggest that several dimensions of parenting practices are associated with children’s oral health behaviours, oral health status, and dental anxiety levels. The findings suggest that proactive and supportive parenting approaches are associated with more favourable oral hygiene behaviours and lower levels of dental anxiety in children. Additionally, parenting practices are correlated with demographic variables such as child age, gender, and parental education level. By evaluating the applicability of MAPS in this context, this study provides preliminary information supporting its use in paediatric oral health research. These results highlight the potential importance of considering parenting practices and psychosocial factors in paediatric dental care and suggest that awareness of family-related factors can help dentists adopt more individualized and supportive approaches in managing children’s oral health. However, due to the cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as correlations rather than causal relationships. Further longitudinal studies involving larger and more diverse populations are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms between parenting practices, dental anxiety, and oral health outcomes.
