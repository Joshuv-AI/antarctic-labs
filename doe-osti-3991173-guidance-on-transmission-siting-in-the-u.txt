INTRODUCTION
The Chiefs' Trail, also known as Trail of the Chiefs, is a project proposed by the National Tribal Energy
Association (NTEA): connecting thirteen Native American Reservations across the Western United States
with the Western Electricity Coordinating Council (WECC) bulk power system through a new electric
transmission corridor. This initiative promotes energy self-determination and economic development
among participating tribal communities by allowing them to develop their own energy portfolios and par-
ticipate in energy markets. The proposed transmission corridor is shown in Figure 1.

 Figure 1. Proposed Chiefs’ Trail transmission corridor.

The Chiefs’ Trail will allow Native American Reservations to connect to existing transmission infrastruc-
ture, therefore, being able to provide various energy services: alleviating congestion and increasing WECC
reliability with a constructed transmission corridor; enabling participation in energy markets using their
diverse energy generation portfolios, including the commissioning of new energy generation sites; and
providing more affordable electricity to millions of people across the region.
This document serves as a guide for a Chiefs’ Trail phased-approach feasibility plan, providing a roadmap
for transmission siting processes that NTEA could follow, to enable the successful accomplishment of pro-
ject objectives. The roadmap outlines key considerations, best practices, and regulatory requirements for
identifying suitable transmission line routes and integrating new energy generation sites in tribal commu-
nities into the WECC system.
A generalized step-by-step approach was designed to help NTEA navigate the complex permitting and
approval processes of state and federal agencies—necessary procedures and protocols for engaging with
key stakeholders were outlined, which depend on federal- and state-level public utility commissions, and
environmental and other regulatory agencies. Additionally, the first step in developing and presenting com-
pelling cases to secure funding opportunities—through the Department of Energy (DOE), the Federal En-
ergy Regulatory Commission (FERC), or other relevant agencies—is provided here, ensuring that tribal
communities are well-equipped to effectively communicate the value of the Chiefs’ Trail and prepared to
begin its implementation.

BACKGROUND: TRANSMISSION SITING IN THE UNITED STATES
The term “transmission siting” refers to the process of identifying and evaluating potential locations for
new electric transmission lines, towers, and/or substations—facilitating the transmission of electricity from
power plants to consumers through the transmission infrastructure.
Transmission siting is critical to ensure the reliable and cost-effective transmission of electricity, as it ena-
bles the construction of new routes (while balancing grid reliability, public safety, project costs, and envi-
ronmental concerns), therefore, allowing remote communities to connect to the national bulk electric sys-
tem. A transmission siting process typically involves the analyses of land use, zoning regulations, environ-
mental impact, visual and noise impacts, community engagement, and alternative routing options to identify
suitable locations and develop proposals for construction or modification of existing infrastructure.
In general, siting new transmission lines is considered one of the most difficult but most important processes
of the electric power industry. It involves multiple stakeholders (including federal and state regulators,
electric utilities, local communities, and environmental groups) who hold jurisdiction over who, what, and
where can build, and who pays for it. It is a complex process, which has evolved over time, but is still
considered to be lengthy as it may entail disputes between interested parties at times.

State vs. Federal Jurisdiction
The authority for transmission siting resides primarily with the states. The 2005 Energy Policy Act [1]
established a limited federal role, where siting authority is shared between the DOE and FERC. The 2018
FERC Federal Power Act [2], amended in 2021, gives DOE the power to study transmission congestion to
provide authorization for designating National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors, commonly known
as “national corridors”. This Act grants FERC the authority to issue permits to construct or modify trans-
mission lines and facilities within a national corridor if one of the following statements is true:
  1. The state(s) do not have the authority to approve the transmission siting project or do not consider
 interstate or interregional benefits.
  2. The interested entity (e.g., transmission utility) does not qualify for siting approval in a particular
 state.
  3. The state(s) have not decided on the application within 1 year.
  4. The state(s) have conditioned the approval of the project such that it will not significantly reduce
 transmission capacity constraints or congestion or makes it economically infeasible.
  5. The state(s) deny the application.
Another point of contention between state and federal jurisdictions is related to electricity rates. For in-
stance, the federal government has jurisdiction over wholesale electricity rates, while states have jurisdic-
tion over retail rates. This dichotomy could create a challenge for transmission siting, as it affects both
wholesale and retail aspects of electricity sales due to its effect in congestion and capacity constraints.
State-level public utility commissions (PUCs) have jurisdiction over intrastate transmission lines and must
approve or deny requests to construct new ones within their respective states. PUCs consider factors such
as need for the transmission line, impact on local communities and environment, and consistency with state
energy policies. State regulators determine whether the construction plan of the transmission siting project
is done properly and adequately; if considered inadequate, they can forbid the electric utility from charging
the costs associated with the project to customers.

 4

Other Considerations
Transmission siting often involves public engagement and participation. Local communities may have con-
cerns about the impact of a new transmission corridor and equipment on their environment, health, quality
of life, and energy rates. FERC and PUCs typically provide opportunities for public comment and partici-
pation in the transmission siting process by having representatives from the community.
Transmission siting often involves environmental considerations, such as potential impacts on wildlife hab-
itats, water quality, and air quality. Both FERC and PUCs must consider these factors when evaluating a
request for construction of a new transmission corridor. Federal and state environmental regulators may
become involved in the transmission siting process to address environmental issues arising during a project.
The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) requires federal agencies to consider the environmental
impacts of their actions, including the siting of new transmission lines. State health officials may also be-
come involved in populated areas to assess the effects caused by the electromagnetic fields produced by the
current flowing through the transmission lines proposed.

Court Challenges
Transmission siting decisions can be challenged in court by affected parties (including local communities,
environmental groups, and electric utilities). These challenges can slow down the transmission siting pro-
cess and create uncertainty for developers and stakeholders.

Generic Transmission Siting Process
I. Planning: Developers and/or electric utilities perform planning studies to understand the choices they
have for delivering reliable and cost-effective electricity to customers. These choices include the construc-
tion of new power plants, addition of distributed generation, constructing new transmission lines, imple-
menting demand response programs, developing aggressive energy efficiency programs, or a combination
of these. Planners focus on using the proposed transmission infrastructure to reliably deliver power from
locations that may have excess capacity to areas with reduced capacity.

II. Cost Analysis: Cost is one of the most important aspects that developers and planners must consider in
a transmission siting process—building and commissioning a transmission corridor is a very expensive
undertaking. Typical capital cost ranges from $300K per mile to over $2M per mile, depending heavily on
the voltage class (e.g., 765 kV, 345 kV), cable placement (underground or overhead), and other costs related
to land acquisition and the permitting process. Terrain, related to land acquisition, is also a factor that affects
cost; for instance, it is more difficult to maneuver construction equipment through mountainous terrain, far
from roads, than it is on flat plains with nearby roads. Other factors affecting capital cost include the cost
to acquire rights-of-way, the cost of upgrading existing equipment (such as substations), and the cost of
interconnecting the new transmission project with the existing grid.

III. Route Evaluation: Planners must perform studies and evaluate multiple possible routes for a trans-
mission corridor proposed. Local interested groups may become involved in a project and may advocate
for a particular transmission line route over another. Some issues planners may encounter are related to
environmental considerations and the “not in my backyard” syndrome [3], where local opposing groups
focus on potential negative impacts a project may cause in their communities, considering recreational land,
scenic and historic trails, and parks.

 5

IV. State and Federal Permitting: Developers and/or electric utilities (or simply petitioners) seek per-
mission from state and federal agencies to continue with their transmission siting project. As previously
discussed, each state has its own process for granting permission to construct and commission a new trans-
mission line; these processes need to be verified by the federal government. A generic step-by-step descrip-
tion of this follows:
 1. Petitioners file an application with the state agency(ies) or local government(s).
 2. Once the application is complete, the state agency is given a period to review and rule on the appli-
 cation, which period can last between three months to two years or more (depending on state).
 3. Some states require a “certificate of need,” which clearly describes the specific need for the new
 transmission project; reliability and congestion are “needs” traditionally used for these cases.
 4. Some states require a certain number of public hearings to be held that should encompass the affected
 towns, cities, and counties.
 5. Some states require the application to be filed with different alternative routes with an analysis done
 for each one of them.
 6. A procedure is presented to appeal a decision.

V. Financing: Developers and/or electric utilities need to obtain financing for their transmission siting
project—this is not a simple task as financing is significant challenge in the transmission sector. One of the
reasons why building a new transmission corridor is considered a difficult and time-consuming process is
attributed to difficulties in raising capital for large investments, particularly due to the major changes in
electric power markets and the financial uncertainties existing in those markets. Higher returns on trans-
mission rates to attract more capital have been proposed, nonetheless, bulk energy rates are still regulated
by FERC while retail rates are regulated by the states.
Regulators are exploring solutions to address existing financing issues. One option is the use of perfor-
mance-based ratemaking plans, where regulators set performance goals and targets, and if those are met,
then electric utilities would earn higher returns. This approach aims to incentivize electric utilities to invest
in new transmission infrastructure while ensuring that customers pay only for necessary costs [4]. However,
it remains unclear who will ultimately bear the costs associated with transmission siting, including regula-
tory approvals and permitting processes. In some cases, these costs are borne by the developer and/or elec-
tric utility itself, while in others they may be passed on to consumers through increased rates. In any case,
financing is a critical part of any proposal, which needs to be addressed before the start of a project.

Step 1: Land Use Planning
The first step—which should happen concurrently with Step 2—is developers ensuring that their proposed
transmission siting project complies with existing federal, state, and local land use plans and zoning regu-
lations. Federal and states agencies use Land Use Plans (LUPs) to manage the use of their lands and dictate
what can and cannot be done in the land. In addition to LUPs, state and local governments have zoning
regulations that may restrict development activities: dictate building types, transmission line locations, set-
backs from streets, and other boundaries (such as the size and height of buildings).
States often delegate land use planning and zoning oversight to local governments (such as counties and
municipalities), with some states having a cooperative process between state and local governments. Level
of control over land use planning varies by state, ranging from statewide plans to local government control.

 8

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
In Montana, LUPs are delegated primarily to local governments pursuant to the Local Planning Enabling
Act (M.C.A. secs. 71-1-01 to 71-1-606). Local governments are given authority to regulate and restrict the
use of land and require permits for any construction project within their jurisdiction. The Montana Munic-
ipal Zoning Enabling Act (Montana Code 76-2-301, 302) [8] allows cities and towns to control zoning and
building, while the County Planning and Zoning Commission Act (Montana Code 76-2-201 [9]) enables
counties to adopt their own zoning regulations. Any transmission siting project will need to obtain land use
permits from each local government involved, but the specific regulations may differ depending on the
local jurisdiction.

Developers must comply with regulations established by the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), who is
responsible for managing federal natural resources and sustaining the health, diversity, and productivity of
public lands. BLM is in charge of roughly 258 million acres of public lands and 700 million acres of mineral
estates. They develop and maintain their own LUPs, called Resource Management Plans (RMPs), which
outline areas with limited/restricted/exclusive use, and designate areas of critical environmental concern
where development actions are prohibited.
A transmission siting project that passes through BLM-managed lands requires environmental review pro-
cesses, which include compliance with specific RMPs and with the National Environmental Policy Act
(NEPA). NEPA is a federal law, enacted in 1970, which requires federal agencies to assess the potential
environmental impacts of major federal actions before making decisions.
Another review that developers must complete checks whether the transmission siting project is proposed
to be constructed on land managed by the United States Forest Service (USFS). USFS manages approxi-
mately 192 million acres of land in accordance with the requirements of the 1976 National Forest Manage-
ment Act. USFS must also develop LUPs, called Land Management Plans (LMPs), that are prepared and
implemented individually by each national forest. Transmission siting projects are required to comply with
specific forest LMP(s).

Step 2: Site Considerations
The second step is considered to be the most technical one as developers must perform site selection anal-
yses and studies that involve factors like:
  • pre-existing uses of the land, which is proposed to be used for the project;
  • land access requirements;
  • proximity to existing transmission lines and rights-of-way;
  • proximity to existing land and highway rights-of-way;
  • transmission siting and interconnection requirements;
  • pre-existing recreational resources near the proposed project site; and
  • cultural and environmental resources near the proposed project site that may be impacted (i.e., cul-
 tural resources, water resources, ecological resources, geological resources, biological resources).

2.1 Pre-existing Land Restrictions
Developers must consider whether the location of their proposed transmission siting project has pre-existing
land use restrictions, particularly if that land is managed by a federal or state agency; certain lands have
statutory or regulatory limitations (such as federally designated wilderness areas and primitive areas man-
aged by USFS). Before making substantial investments, developers should research whether a proposed
site is available for development, and review all guidance from federal agencies, including the Bureau of
Reclamation (BOR), USFS, and other agencies that may have stipulations in place to protect environmental
resources. Restricted areas include national parks, national monuments, recreational areas, national wildlife
refuges, fish hatcheries, wild and scenic rivers, Native American Reservations, historical/cultural centers,
areas with threatened and endangered species, military areas, and water conservation areas.
Federal agencies are required by Executive Order 11988 [10] to minimize the risk of flood loss and protect
human safety, health, and welfare in floodplain areas. If a proposed transmission siting project is located
within a floodplain, agencies must take specific actions during the environmental review under NEPA,
including considering alternatives that avoid adverse effects and incompatible development in these sensi-
tive areas. This review process should go hand in hand with Step 1.

2.2 Land Access Requirements and Proximity to Existing Rights-of-Way
When selecting the location for their transmission siting project, developers must consider two key factors:
(1) land access requirements, and (2) proximity to existing rights-of-way. A project will likely require ac-
cess to multiple types of lands (including federal, state, local, tribal, and private property), which necessi-
tates to obtain rights-of-way, leases, or use authorizations, or purchase the land outright, depending on land
ownership status.

2.3 Siting and Interconnection Requirements
Developers must consider the site location’s proximity to existing transmission lines and interconnection(s),
and what approvals will be required to continue with the project. During this process, the siting team needs
to work with system planners.

System Planning Studies
Developers are required to justify the need for the transmission siting project, identify alternatives for the
interconnection, and select the voltage level(s) and terminal locations of the proposed transmission line(s).
Power flow studies are the foundation of every study during the planning phase of the project [11].
Transmission System Planning Studies provide the technical foundation for siting a transmission line(s),
and guide project decisions from start to finish. These studies determine the optimal voltage level(s) and
terminal locations for new line(s), ensuring reliable power flows to loads at minimal cost. The goal is to
create a resilient power grid interconnection capable of withstanding disruptions. While planners drive the
planning process, they do not oversee the physical design, operation, or maintenance of a line(s). Planners
focus on running a series of electrical studies (i.e., power flows) to analyze the transmission network as it
currently exists, and then evaluate the effect that the new transmission line(s) or whole project will have.
They evaluate multiple generation and loading scenarios (e.g., worst case peak loading) with computer
simulations, which use models capable of predicting the operation of the bulk power system during the
simulated conditions.

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
I. Transmission Line Interconnection Feasibility Study: The technical viability of constructing and inte-
grating the proposed transmission corridor into the Western and the Eastern Interconnections (managed by
WECC and MRO, respectively) must be assessed with computer simulations and power flow studies. These
studies need to address grid stability, congestion, voltage, and other potential technical issues that could
arise from the construction and integration of new transmission infrastructure. At this step, system planners
must select the voltage for the new transmission line(s) and facilities, and need to make assumptions about
other details such as ampacity and conductor size of the line(s).

II. Loading Analysis: Detailed analyses of the current and the future electricity demands of each Native
American Reservation must be conducted to understand their energy needs, energy consumption patterns,
anticipated growth, and the potential energy markets they could participate within the WECC and/or MRO.
At this step, historical electricity consumption data from the Reservations must be gathered, and load pro-
files generated based on these data to understand their energy usage patterns; load information from the
WECC and the MRO systems also need to be obtained to model the entire representative bulk power sys-
tem. Discussions with tribal representatives will be needed to understand and project their future energy
needs based on factors such as population growth, economic development, and electrification efforts.

III. Energy Resource Assessment: An assessment of energy resources available within the tribal territories
must be conducted. This study needs to be done using information on existing generation resources that
tribes have access to and are planning to connect to the bulk power system through the proposed transmis-
sion corridor—furthermore, the technical viability of integrating new energy generation sites needs to be
assessed. A full Interconnection process, somewhat similar but separate from the Transmission Siting pro-
cess, will be necessary.
New generation sites are planned to be managed by individual tribal communities, considering energy re-
source potential within their own territories. The assessment should evaluate the potential for connecting

Siting Studies
In the Transmission Design process, developers must select companies specialized in designing and con-
structing overhead or underground transmission lines. The three main components that need to be designed
are the wires (conductor and shield wire), the structure, and the foundation [11]; this is a complex task as
transmission lines must meet adequate electrical, strength, and aesthetic requirements.
For wire design, the conductor wires need to meet the requirements set by the System Planning Studies
and the design standards set by the electric utility. The choice of conductor type and size will determine
electrical capacity, electrical losses, thermal capacity, and corona noise—it must be noted that this selection
will directly affect the total capital cost of the transmission siting project as it will determine the structure
configuration and spacing of the new transmission infrastructure. Mechanical and electrical properties (such
as wire sag, tension, and clearance) must be taken into consideration during the design.
For structure design, an appropriate structure for the poles or towers, which will support the transmission
line(s), needs to be selected. Structure configuration is influenced by the System Planning Studies require-
ments, siting recommendations, geography, utility standards, land requirements, and permitting require-
ments. Some of the most used structures for transmission lines are poles or lattice towers made up of various
materials (such as wood, concrete, or steel)—Figure 6 presents commonly used structure designs.

 Figure 6. Transmission line structure designs [12].

For foundation design, the decision typically occurs near the end of the project's final design phase, follow-
ing a thorough sub-surface investigation; however, it may be necessary to make the decision earlier to meet
regulatory requirements. Foundations can range from simple direct burial systems to more complex designs
(such as reinforced concrete drilled shafts), which are among the most commonly used ones in the United
States—other examples include grillage, driven piles, vibratory foundations, and micro-piles [11].
It is beyond the scope of this document to provide a comprehensive overview of the large number of deci-
sions involved in a Transmission Design process. Designed transmission lines should meet specific project
requirements and must satisfy all applicable design codes, the most important being the latest National

Electric Safety Code standard that dictates minimum design requirements—however, most utilities and
regulators have more stringent design criteria of their own.

In the Transmission Line Route Analysis and Assessment process, developers must evaluate potential
transmission line routes that can connect the new transmission infrastructure with the bulk power system.
First, analysts determine the area that needs to be studied for the transmission siting project; this must be
large enough to encompass all possible alternative routes that should be considered. Next, the site of the
substation(s) needed for the project must be selected—the siting of a new substation(s) has significant im-
pact on transmission line routing options, which in turn affects its approval. Careful consideration should
be given to the selection of the substation site(s) to ensure that they align not only with regulatory and
environmental requirements, but also comply with engineering factors as well; the location of the substa-
tion(s) ensures that the system can operate as planned in the System Planning Studies.
In parallel to the substation site selection, an expert team needs to identify, evaluate, and determine the
route of the proposed transmission line(s). These routes should, as much as possible, minimize: route length
and cost; interference with existing residential and non-residential structures, historical/cultural or recrea-
tional facilities, public resource lands (such as forests and parks), and economic activities; and high-visual
impact on residential or public areas. To perform these studies and develop alternative routes, Geographic
Information System (GIS) data needs to be collected; software can be used to map the proposed transmis-
sion siting project to help decision-making. Multiple alternative routes must be identified and refined based
on routing constraints (identified using collected data) and public involvement. Figure 7 shows a generic
method that determines how potential routes of a network can be defined between substations.

Existing transmission corridors offer significant opportunities for optimizing new transmission line routes.
Various federal and state regulatory agencies have shown a strong preference for utilizing existing corridors
when constructing new transmission infrastructure. By taking advantage of these corridors, projects can
minimize potential opposition and ensure smoother regulatory approvals [11].

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
Potential transmission line routes, which can connect all three Native American Reservations to the WECC
and/or MRO systems, need to be identified. Both overhead and underground transmission lines (with both
AC and DC options) need to be assessed, considering key factors such as geographic obstacles and land
ownership. GIS and geospatial data need to be used to identify potential obstacles such as protected areas,
water bodies, and sensitive habitats. Optimal connection points to the WECC and/or MRO systems need to
be evaluated, considering existing transmission infrastructure and its corresponding capacity derived from
models.

Approvals
Any new transmission infrastructure related to the proposed transmission siting project needs approvals
(from federal, state, and/or local authorities) before construction can begin. It is usually in this phase of a
project when most proposed projects fail to be realized—this is why it is important to obtain all necessary
approvals before continuing with the project. Some of the common questions developers may use as guid-
ance when obtaining approvals [5]:
 1. Does the transmission siting project require federal authorizations?
 2. Is the project proposed in a National Interest Electric Transmission Corridor?
 3. Do the state(s) have a comprehensive transmission siting process?
 4. Does the project require approval from a state utility regulatory authority?
 5. Does the project require any additional approvals?
 6. Does the project require local approvals?

In Montana, high-voltage transmission line siting is managed by the Montana Department of Environmental
Quality (MDEQ). The state-administered siting act, called Montana Major Facility Siting Act (MFSA)
(Montana Code 75-20 [13]), requires that developers file an application, called Certificate of Compliance
(CoC), with MDEQ who has the authority to approve or deny their request; Montana law requires that all
proposed siting projects with a voltage over 69 kV must obtain a CoC.
The definition of transmission infrastructure does not include (per Montana Code 75-20-104 [14]):
 • transmission lines of < 230 kV and <= 10 miles in length;
 • transmission lines of > 69kV for which right-of-way agreements have been reached with more than
 75% of landowners who collectively own more than 75% of the property along the right-of-way;
 • transmission lines that are < 150 miles in length and are required under state or federal regulations
 (due to reliability, interconnection to regional transmission grid, or secure firm transmission service);
 • upgrades to an existing transmission line of a design capacity of > 50 kV to increase capacity; and
 • transmission substations, switchyards, or other control equipment facilities (including energy storage
 facilities).
A CoC application contains three sections: (1) Alternative Siting Study, (2) Baseline Study, and (3) Impact
Assessment—MDEQ provides detailed requirements for these in their "Application Requirements for

 15

Linear Facilities" circular. The transmission siting process also involves public meetings and an overview
of public issues and concerns related to each alternative transmission line route.
MDEQ reviews and notifies an applicant of the completeness of their CoC application and whether revi-
sions are necessary within 30 days of the submission. Nine months after a complete application is submitted,
MDEQ issues a report detailing alternatives, environmental findings, and opinions; the general format of
the report is an environmental impact statement. Once the proposed transmission siting project is approved
through the CoC, the applicant can use eminent domain to secure easement rights because the project is
deemed a public necessity.
In terms of local requirements, counties and municipalities—per the Montana Municipal Zoning Enabling
Act (Montana Code 76-2-301) and the Montana County Planning and Zoning Commission Act (Montana
Code 76-2-201)—have zoning authorities to regulate land use and require permits for constructions that lie
within their jurisdictions. However, MDEQ can refuse to apply any local laws or regulations that are con-
sidered unreasonably restrictive in view of public necessity (Montana Code 75-20-301).

Step 3: Land Access
The third step is developers obtaining approvals (licenses) and/or purchasing the land for constructing the
proposed transmission siting project—land access is a critical stage as it determines feasibility and success-
ful implementation. Securing access to land through negotiations with property owners, easements, or
rights-of-way agreements—where owners can be private owners, local agencies, or governmental agen-
cies—allows developers to build the transmission infrastructure necessary for realizing their project. With-
out land access, a project may be delayed or abandoned altogether, resulting in significant financial losses
and potential adverse impacts to the long-term reliability of the bulk power system.
Some of the common questions developers may use as guidance:
  1. Does the transmission siting project require access to private land?
 YES: A review of the private land must be conducted before signing the easement or purchasing the
 property.
  2. Does the transmission siting project require access to local government land?
 YES: Developers should contact the local government with jurisdiction over the land.
  3. Does the transmission siting project require access to state land?
 YES: Developers should obtain a lease, right-of-way, or other approvals from the state agency with
 jurisdiction over the land.
  4. Will the transmission siting project encroach on a local and/or state highway right-of-way?
 YES: Developers should obtain a lease, right-of-way, or other approvals from the local and/or state
 agency with jurisdiction for projects that encroach on a local and/or state highway.
  5. Does the transmission siting project require access to tribal land?
 YES: Developers should obtain approvals from the respective tribe(s) through Tribal Energy Re-
 source Agreement(s), an authorization from the United States Department of Interior.
  6. Does the transmission siting project require access to federal land?
 YES: Developers should contact the federal agency in charge of the land, including, but not limited
 to: BLM, USFS, BOR, National Park Service (NPS), the United States Fish and Wildlife Service
 (FWS), and the United States Department of Defense (DOD)—each may have different application
 processes to obtain right-of-way approvals.

  7. Is the transmission siting project within the right-of-way of federal aid or direct federal highway
 projects?
 YES: Developers should obtain permission from the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) to
 use federal aid highway rights-of-way. Before approval, FHWA (1) ascertains the impacts the project
 would have on safety, (2) determines environmental and economic impacts on agriculture, and (3)
 considers environmental and economic impacts the project would have on the use of the highway.

Step 4: Environmental Review Process
The fourth step is developers ensuring that all appropriate environmental approvals have been obtained
before continuing with the proposed transmission siting project. This encompasses the following:

I. Environmental Review: Developers must comply with federal and state environmental review pro-
cesses. Any environmental issue that requires permitting and/or regulatory approval (such as construction,
operation, or maintenance activities) must be addressed before continuing with the project. To initiate a
review, developers must determine whether the project meets the “major federal action” criteria, defined as
“an action that the agency carrying out such action determines is subject to substantial federal control and
responsibility” (42 U.S.C. 4336e (10)). If applicable, the project must comply with the NEPA review pro-
cess—the scope and depth of this will depend on the project and the level of federal involvement; in some
cases, projects may qualify for a categorical exclusion, while others may require a comprehensive EIS.
In addition to federal environmental regulations, the proposed project must also comply with state environ-
mental laws, which could include evaluations related to noise disturbances, vehicle emissions, and direct
and indirect impacts to cultural resources, ecological resources, and water quality.

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
Montana does not have a state-specific environmental review process for transmission siting projects.

II. On-site Evaluation: Developers should conduct on-site evaluations, designed to identify potential land
use restrictions and site-specific resource protection concerns, which may impact the proposed project. The

 17

assessment involves consulting with relevant federal, state, and local governmental agencies to determine
the necessary regulations to meet and permits to obtain. Multiple factors—including biological, ecological,
paleontological, archaeological, geological, hydrological, and aesthetic impacts—can affect the site's usa-
bility, which must be considered when performing the evaluation.

III. Cultural Resources: Developers must consider the potential impacts of the proposed project on his-
torical/cultural resources (including artifacts, human remains, historic sites, and locations with cultural im-
portance); federal and state laws protect these. It is essential to conduct an initial assessment to identify any
protected resources that may be affected by the project, ensuring compliance.

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
Developers should closely coordinate with the Montana State Historic Preservation Office to avoid any
unintended disruption to historic sites. In case cultural resources are discovered on a project site, developers
must comply with Montana state law, which requires obtaining a permit and providing public notice.

IV. Biological Resources: Developers must consider the potential impacts of the proposed project on
biological resources, including flora and fauna, as well as their habitats. Federal and state laws mandate a
comprehensive assessment—evaluating the impacts of access road development, transmission line instal-
lation, or other related site work—as construction may lead to wildlife habitat losses, reduction in plant
diversity, increased erosion, increased dust deposition, and elevated risk of wildfires.

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
Developers must consult with the Montana Fish, Wildlife, and Parks Division (MFWPD) to determine
whether protected wildlife habitats will be affected by the proposed project. MFWPD will provide measures
to avoid or mitigate damage to local ecosystems.

V. Pre-existing Land Use: Developers must assess existing land uses within and surrounding the proposed
project site. Federal and state laws may impose restrictions on altering or interfering with certain pre-exist-
ing land uses (such as farmland, livestock operations, nearby airports, military bases, navigable waters, and
other sensitive areas). For a comprehensive description, see Step 1: Land Use Planning.

VI. Water Quality: Developers must evaluate the potential impacts of the proposed project on water
quality. Water quality is protected by the Clean Water Act, aimed to reduce water pollution and ensure that
surface waters are safe for human consumption, recreation, and aquatic ecosystems. Development activities
associated with a transmission siting project can pose significant risks to water quality, including soil ero-
sion (creating increased turbidity and sediment transport), weathering of newly exposed soils (causing
leaching and oxidation), discharges of waste or sanitary water, and the use of untreated groundwater (de-
positing dissolved salts on the surface and entering surface water systems). To mitigate risks, developers
must implement measures to prevent water pollution and ensure compliance with federal regulations.

In Montana, a transmission siting project is required to obtain several water quality permits: a Nonpoint
Source Pollution permit, a National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permit, and a Sec-

ulatory Nonpoint Source Pollution Management Program, to check whether their project complies with the
program. Developers must also comply with NPDES requirements, a permit granted by the United States
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), if the proposed project requires the discharge of pollutants into
water systems (including wetlands). Last, an MDEQ Section 401 Water Quality Certification must be ob-
tained if the project requires federal licenses or permits to construct or operate a facility that may result in
any fill or discharge into water systems.

VII. Air Quality: Developers must comply with air quality standards set by federal, state, and local agen-
cies. In the United States, EPA is in charge of administering the Clean Air Act, which empowers states,
territories, and tribes to share authority in regulating and providing air quality permits. This Act sets strin-
gent standards to safeguard public health from pollutants (such as carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides, ozone,
particulate matter, and lead), and imposes limitations on both stationary and mobile sources of pollution
(such as from vehicles, heavy construction equipment, diesel generators, fugitive dust emissions, blasting
activities), requiring the implementation of operational measures that restrict air pollution. Before initiating
the proposed project, developers must assess how the project may generate pollution.

Montana Chiefs’ Trail
Developers must adhere to specific air quality standards related to particulate matter (PM), visibility, and
PM10 emissions, as outlined in Montana Administrative Rules 17.8.220, 17.8.221, and 17.8.223 [15]. De-
velopers must comply with opacity restrictions governed by 17.8.304 [16], which mandates that emissions
do not exceed 20% opacity over a 6-minute period. Developers should also be aware of local regulations in
Montana's particulate matter nonattainment areas, where consultations with county health departments are
recommended to ensure compliance with dust rules.
Under Montana state regulations, the proposed transmission siting project would be generally exempt from
requiring an Air Quality Permit from MDEQ Air Quality Bureau [5]. For the construction phase specifi-

and 17.8.744(1)(b).

geological and paleontological resources. These impacts can be caused directly by construction activities
(such as excavating, blasting activities, construction of access roads, altering natural drainage, and cutting
slopes, or indirectly by soil erosion).

Montana Chiefs’ Trail

Construction activities are likely to produce solid and industrial waste, including non-hazardous items (such
as containers and packaging materials), as well as hazardous substances (such as fuels, lubricating oils,
hydraulic fluids, and compressed gases used for welding and cutting).

Step 5: Water Access Rights
The fifth step is developers assessing the water access and water right needs of the proposed transmission
siting project—these are primarily regulated by state law. Depending on project requirements for dust sup-
pression and construction activities, developers may need to secure water from a leaseholder, municipality,
or permanent water right holder. Water rights can be divided into groundwater and surface water rights,
with different allocation systems in place across the Western and Eastern United States. In the West, it is
primarily based on prior appropriation (i.e., first-in-time, first-in-right), while in the East, it is based on
riparian rights (i.e., property ownership adjacent to a body of water).

During the research stage of preparing this document, a few alternatives were found as potential funding

Permitting and Siting Assistance Programs
In 2024, the DOE Grid Deployment Office announced two programs to advance critical transmission pro-
jects by accelerating siting and permitting processes [17]:
  • Transmission Siting and Economic Development Grants Program: This program provided up to
 $371 million to 20 selected projects across 16 states to accelerate permitting of high-voltage trans-
 mission projects and support community infrastructure projects along major new and upgraded trans-
 mission lines.
  • Transmission Acceleration Grants Program: This program offered $10 million to states, tribes, and
 non-profits to build capacity for transmission deployment and streamline transmission siting and per-
 mitting processes.

Public-Private Partnership Programs
The Bipartisan Infrastructure Law gave the DOE flexibility to partner with private companies on trans-
mission projects, making it easier to develop transmission infrastructure in areas where local regulations
could create obstacles. The DOE's Power Marketing Authorities (i.e., the Southwestern Power Administra-
tion and the Western Area Power Administration) have the authority to form partnerships for transmission
development projects within their regions.

Transmission Facilitation Programs
The Federal Power Act authorizes the United States Secretary of Energy to designate certain areas National
Interest Electric Transmission Corridors, helping expedite transmission projects in regions where con-
sumers are frequently harmed from the lack of transmission infrastructure, which directly contributes to
higher electricity prices, frequent power outages from extreme weather, and longer outages. The designa-
tion of national corridors can streamline permitting and transmission siting projects, making it easier for
companies to develop new or upgrade existing transmission infrastructure. In December 2024, the DOE
announced that it is moving forward with three potential National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors,
including the Tribal Energy Access Corridor (part of North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, and 5 Tribal
Reservations) that partially overlaps with the proposed Chiefs’ Trail transmission corridor [18].
Through the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act, the DOE has created a $2.5 billion revolving funds
program called the Transmission Facilitation Program [19] to support the development of new interre-
gional transmission lines across the United States. The program aims to help overcome financial obstacles
in building large-scale transmission projects, upgrading existing infrastructure, and connecting microgrids
in select areas.

CONCLUSION
The complex process of transmission siting involves several key steps and considerations that should
be closely followed to ensure that a proposed project is completed successfully and timely:
First, the need for the new transmission line(s) must be identified, and a preliminary route corridor—which
considers land use planning and pre-existing land restrictions—must be developed.
Second, a comprehensive review of the proposed route corridor—using advanced software technologies,
such as GIS and power flow programs—must be conducted. This review should include (power) system
planning studies and siting studies, which analyze electrical impacts on the existing bulk power system
upon connection, and address potential impacts on biological resources, cultural resources, and other sen-
sitive areas—these studies should be done in an iterative manner to guarantee the optimal selection of the
transmission line route(s) considering all relevant factors.
Third, once the initial review has been completed, a more detailed evaluation of the proposed transmission
route—using on-site investigations and consultations with relevant federal, state, and local government
agencies—must be conducted. This evaluation should identify any potential land use restrictions or site-
specific resource protection concerns that may impact the proposed project.
Last, all necessary permits and approvals from regulatory agencies must be obtained before the construction
can begin. This includes complying with federal environmental regulations and state laws protecting sensi-
tive areas and resources.
