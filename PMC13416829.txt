Mining and engineering of active PB transposons in Hymenoptera
Authors: Bingqing Wang, Bo Gao, Quan Wang, Kuilin Xiang, Mengyu Li, Naisu Yang, Chengyi Song
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
PiggyBac (PB) transposons, such as the canonical transposon from Trichoplusia ni and the more recently discovered Mage from Lepidoptera, are “cut-and-paste” mobile genetic elements widely harnessed as non-viral vector systems for gene therapy and transgenesis. Despite the ecological and economic significance of the insect order Hymenoptera, the evolutionary history and functional diversity of PB transposons within this group remain largely unexplored. To address this gap, we conducted a systematic investigation of PB transposons across 287 hymenopteran species. Our analysis revealed a widespread distribution, with PB elements detected in nearly half (137/287) of the genomes examined. Phylogenetic reconstruction identified four distinct clades, indicating a significant and previously unappreciated evolutionary diversity among hymenopteran PB transposases, and suggesting they have been highly active and dynamic. From this bioinformatic screen, we selected three PB transposons from Holcopasites calliopsidis (hcPB), Macropis europaea (mePB), and Andrena dorsata as strong candidates for recent activity. Functional validation using a binary transposition assay demonstrated that hcPB and mePB possess remarkable activity, comparable to the engineered hyperactive hyPB transposase. In contrast, adPB exhibited minimal transposition activity under the same conditions. We further engineered these systems based on successful strategies used for hyPB, which involves deleting an inhibitory N-terminal phosphorylation region and appending a second C-terminal domain. While this modification enhanced hyPB activity, it had variable effects on the new transposases: hcPB activity was maintained, but mePB activity decreased by approximately 20%. Intriguingly, merely deleting the N-terminal region severely impaired or abolished the activity of both hcPB and mePB, indicating distinct regulatory mechanisms. In summary, we have uncovered a rich diversity of active PB transposons in Hymenoptera and characterized two powerful new transposases (hcPB and mePB). These findings significantly advance the genome engineering toolkit, providing highly efficient tools with great promise for basic research and therapeutic applications.

Introduction

The piggyBac (PB) transposon is a highly versatile genetic element found in a diverse range of organisms, from protozoa and fungi to plants and mammals [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Its functional utility is underscored by this widespread evolutionary distribution. Originally isolated from the cabbage looper moth, Trichoplusia ni, the first active PB transposon has since been joined by newly discovered family members. These include Mage (Lepidoptera) [8], piggyBat (Myotis lucifugus) [4] and PLE-wu (Spodoptera frugiperda) [9], whose discovery—along with hyperactive variants—has significantly expanded the toolkit for efficient gene delivery in mammalian cells.

Substantial engineering efforts have enhanced the PB system's performance. Codon optimization of the transposase boosts its activity in mammalian cells [10,11], while structural analyses have identified key functional domains, enabling the rational design of mutants [12]. Directed evolution and high-throughput screening have yielded hyperactive transposases (e.g., hyPB) with dramatically improved excision and integration efficiencies, establishing them as standard tools for gene delivery [13,14].

Further refinements have increased both activity and precision. For instance, transposition is inhibited by N-terminal phosphorylation and asymmetric transposon ends; conversely, deleting the inhibitory phosphorylation sites enhances activity, especially when paired with symmetric left ends—a strategy effective in both PB and piggyBat systems [15]. Activity can also be augmented by appending a second C-terminal domain [16]. To improve targeting accuracy, chimeric transposases fused with specific DNA-binding domains enable targeted genomic integration [17]. Another innovative approach involves engineering an excision-competent but integration-defective (Exc+ Int-) PB transposase. This mutant, created by altering the catalytic DDD motif, efficiently excises the transposon but cannot reintegrate it, facilitating precise excision. The uniqueness of this system lies in combining the broad targeting flexibility of Cas9 (via fusion with the Exc+Int− transposase) with the clean, scarless excision characteristic of PB transposons. In practice, the Exc+Int− transposase is fused to a catalytically inactive Cas9 (dCas9); this fusion protein excises a transposon carrying a therapeutic gene cassette from a donor plasmid and facilitates its targeted integration into a genomic site specified by a guide RNA. This enables targeted DNA removal without introducing double-strand breaks or leaving exogenous sequences at the excision site. When fused with Cas9, this Exc+ Int-transposase enables efficient gene knock-in with reduced off-target effects, highlighting its potential for advanced genome engineering [18,19]. This system holds therapeutic promise for a range of applications, including the excision of integrated viral genomes (e.g., HIV-1 provirus, hepatitis B virus DNA), elimination of dominant disease-causing mutations, correction of pathogenic repeat expansions, and removal of transgenes after their function is no longer needed.

While the engineered hyperactive hyPB has become a gold standard for non-viral gene delivery, its heavily modified nature may obscure native regulatory mechanisms. Exploring naturally active PB transposons from understudied lineages such as Hymenoptera offers a complementary approach: it can reveal how transposition is natively restrained in wild elements, potentially uncovering novel auto-inhibitory strategies that could inspire alternative engineering avenues.

In this study, we characterize the evolutionary profile of PB transposons across 287 hymenopteran species and identify three with genomic signatures of recent activity. We demonstrate high transposition activity for PB transposons from Holcopasites calliopsidis (hcPB) and Macropis europaea (mePB). We further engineered their transposases and terminal sequences to optimize them for gene delivery applications. Our findings provide powerful new tools for genome engineering, with significant promise for both basic research and therapeutic development.

Methods and materials

Mining of PiggyBac (PB) transposons

A total of 287 well-assembled hymenopteran genomes were retrieved from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Using the conserved RNase H-like domain of known PB transposases as a reference, TBLASTN (v.2.12.0) was employed to identify homologous PB transposons with an E-value threshold of 1e−4 and query coverage >80%. For each identified DNA sequence, 4 kb of flanking regions were extracted, and sequences with a total length greater than 3 kb were retained. The obtained sequences were clustered using MAFFT (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software) and USEARCH with an identity threshold of 50%.

Phylogenetic analysis of PiggyBac (PB)

A total of 20 reference transposase sequences of PB and IS1380 families were downloaded from the Repbase (https://www.girinst.org/repbase) and ISfinder (https://www-is.biotoul.fr/blast.php) databases. The selection criteria were as follows: all sequences were full-length (PB > 500 aa, IS1380 > 400 aa); all contained an intact DDD/DDE catalytic domain; the PB sequences covered known phylogenetic diversity, including the well-characterized hyPB; and IS1380 sequences from prokaryotes were included as an outgroup for tree rooting. The G–INS–I algorithm of MAFFT was used to perform multiple sequence alignment between these reference sequences and the identified full-length PB transposases (>500 aa). The resulting alignment was used to construct a phylogenetic tree via the maximum likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE (v. 1.6.12), with ultrafast bootstrap support based on 1000 replicates. The best-fit amino acid substitution model, JTT + G4, was determined according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) using ModelFinder integrated within IQ-TREE.

Sequence analysis of PiggyBac (PB)

Sequence alignments were performed using the ClustalW program in BioEdit (v7.8.0) to manually identify terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) and target site duplications (TSDs). Transposase open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted using BioEdit and the online tool Genscan (http://hollywood.mit.edu/GENSCAN.html). Transposons containing full TIRs and transposases longer than 500 aa were selected for further structural analysis. Schematic diagrams of transposon structures were generated using IBS (http://ibs.biocuckoo.org). Sequence logos illustrating TIR variability were created with WebLogo (https://weblogo.berkeley.edu). Functional domains including the N-terminal domain (NTD), dimerization and DNA-binding domain (DDBD), the catalytic DDD domain, and the cysteine-rich C-terminal domain (CRD) were identified through HMMERscan (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/hmmer/search/hmmscan) and multiple alignments via MAFFT, with visualization conducted in Jalview (v2.11.3.2). Sequence similarity among transposase domains was computed using BioEdit and visualized as heatmaps in GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2).

Evolutionary dynamics of PiggyBac (PB)

The evolutionary dynamics of PB transposons were assessed by annotating genomes with RepeatMasker using consensus or representative PB sequences [20]. Kimura (K) divergence was calculated with the calcDivergenceFromAlign.pl script from RepeatMasker. Insertion time (T) of each transposon was estimated using formula T = K/2r [21], where K represents the Kimura divergence and r denotes the neutral mutation rate. A substitution rate (r) of 1 × 10−8 substitutions per synonymous site per year was applied [22].

Vector construction and functional validation of PiggyBac (PB)

To validate piggyBac (PB) function in mammalian cells, we employed a binary transposition system with separate donor and helper plasmids, as previously described [23,24]. In this system, the donor plasmid carries the transposon cassette (including the TIRs and a PGK-neomycin resistance gene), while the helper plasmid separately encodes the transposase under a CAG promoter. Transposition occurs only when both plasmids are co-transfected into cells, allowing independent manipulation of the transposon and transposase. Three representative PB transposons, hcPB from Holcopasites calliopsidis, mePB from Macropis europaea, and adPB from Andrena dorsata, were selected for functional validation. All full-length copies of these transposons were aligned to identify highly conserved 5′ and 3′ terminal inverted repeats (TIRs) and untranslated regions (UTRs). Consensus sequences were constructed using the EMBOSS explorer online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.nl/emboss-explorer). KpnI and SalI restriction sites were introduced at the ends of TIR and UTR fragments. The sequences were synthesized by Genewiz and then cloned into the PLB vector (Amp+; a standard plasmid backbone for transposon donor construction) to generate PLB-hc-TIR, PLB-me-TIR, and PLB-ad-TIR. The phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK)-Neo-bGHpA cassette was subcloned from a lab-stored PGK-Neo-bGHpA-TA vector into these plasmids, resulting in PLB-hc-TIR-PGK-Neo, PLB-me-TIR-PGK-Neo, and PLB-ad-TIR-PGK-Neo.

PLB-hyPB-short-LR–RE, PLB-hyPB-LE–LE, and PLB-hyPB-RE–RE were constructed by replacing the full-length TIRs of PLB-hyPB-LE–RE with truncated left end (LE) and/or right end (RE) TIRs. Similarly, PLB-hcPB-LE–LE and PLB-mePB-LE–LE were generated by substituting the full-length TIRs of PLB-hcPB-LE–RE and PLB-mePB-LE–RE, respectively, with shorter LE sequences. PCAG-74PB was created by deleting N-terminal amino acids 1–74 via PCR, while retaining the initiating methionine. PCAG-74PB2CD was produced by further introducing the C-terminal CRD domain of PB following the 74 PB. Standard molecular biology techniques were employed for all cloning procedures, and the integrity of all constructs was verified by DNA sequencing.

To enhance expression in human cells, transposase coding sequences from the three species were codon-optimized using the IDT online tool (https://sg.idtdna.com/pages/tools/codon-optimization-tool). The optimized ORFs were inserted into a modified pCAG vector to produce helper plasmids designated pCAG-hcPB-CDS, pCAG-mePB-CDS, and pCAG-adPB-CDS. For comparative analysis, the highly active hyPB transposase, which was previously optimized for enhanced transposition efficiency, was included as a positive control in cellular assays (all sequences and primers were listed in File S1 and Table S2).

HeLa cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS; Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Transfection was performed using FuGENE® HD (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. A total of 3 × 105 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates one day before transfection. At approximately 80% confluency, cells were co-transfected with 1 μg of total DNA (donor and helper plasmids at 1:1 ratio) using a reagent:DNA ratio of 3:1. Each experimental group was repeated in triplicate. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were trypsinized, replated at 1% density in 6 cm dishes, and selected with G418 (600 μg/mL; Gibco) for 14 days. Resistant colonies were stained with Giemsa (Promega) and counted using ImageJ.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses and comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 10. Details regarding reproducibility and statistical methods are described in the figure legends.

Results

Evolution of PB in hymenopterans (287 species)

The evolutionary profile of PB transposons was systematically investigated in 287 hymenopteran species using the local TBlastN search protocol, as described in the methods section. PB transposons were identified in 137 species across 26 orders, with the highest prevalence found in Apidae (30/62 species), Formicidae (21/58 species), Vespidae (11/15 species), and Halictidae (13/24 species). These orders also contain a large number of species, whereas other orders had fewer detections (Fig. 1a, Table 1, and Table S1). The IQ-tree program was used to construct a phylogenetic tree for the extracted PB transposases (≥500 aa) using reference sequences of active hyPB from eukaryotes, with IS1380 transposases from prokaryotes serving as an outgroup. Intact PB transposons ranged in length from 1939 bp to 6143 bp and contained a single open reading frame (ORF) varying from 500 to 768 aa. All detected PB transposons exhibited a consistent target site duplication (TSD) of TTAA. Most PB transposons have short TIRs, however, significant variations in terminal inverted repeat (TIR) lengths were observed, ranging from 5 to 2638 bp (Fig. 1c and Table 1). The 5′ ends of nearly all TIRs were highly conserved, with notably higher C and T contents (CCCTTT). An additional conserved motif (CCA/CCGA) was identified between 12 and 15 bp of the left and right TIRs (Fig. 1d).

The conservation levels of PB transposase domains varied: the DDD domain is highly conserved (Fig. 2a), and showed high sequence identity both within (≥40%) and between clades (23-30%) (Fig. 2b), followed by DDBD1 and DDBD2, and CRD. In contrast, the N-terminal domain (NTD) exhibited remarkably low sequence identity across different clades, all below 10% (Fig. 2c–f, and Fig. S2). It should be noted that our TBLASTN search used the conserved DDD domain as the query, which may have introduced ascertainment bias by preferentially recovering elements with detectable catalytic core conservation. Therefore, while our data indicate that the DDD domain is the most highly conserved region among the identified PB transposases, this conclusion may be partly influenced by the screening strategy. hcPB, mePB, and adPB activity in cells

Over 80 species harbor intact piggyBac (PB) transposons (Table 1), suggesting that these elements may remain active in some species. Insertion age analysis of PB transposons with high copy numbers (>6) and intact copy proportions exceeding 60% of total copies revealed that most copies in these species such as Holcopasites calliopsidis exhibited very young ages (Fig. S3), supporting recent transpositional activity. In contrast, PB transposons in some species (Anoplius nigerrimus) display both ancient and recent amplification patterns, suggesting the coexistence of both young and old PB copies within hymenopterans (Fig. S3).

Three putatively high active PB transposons from genomes of Holcopasites calliopsidis (hcPB), Macropis europaea (mePB), and Andrena dorsata (adPB), with intact structures and very young ages (Fig. 3a), were selected and submitted for activity test in mammalian cells. These three were not the only candidates that simultaneously satisfied all three selection criteria (high copy number, high intact-copy proportion, and young insertion age). We prioritized hcPB for its extremely young insertion ages (virtually no ancient copies), mePB for its highest copy number and intact-copy proportion, and adPB for its notably shorter TIRs to assess the effect of TIR length on transposition. Their sequences are provided in File S1. The binary transposition system, including a donor plasmid and a helper plasmid (Fig. 3b), was utilized to investigate the transposition capabilities of these PB elements, and hyPB was used as a control. Transposition enhancement of PB by transposase and transposon end engineering

Previous studies suggested that N-terminal phosphorylation of the transposase and asymmetric transposon ends inhibit piggyBac (PB) transposition in mammalian cells [15]. We analyzed the N-terminal sequences of hyPB, piggyBat, hcPB, and mePB for the presence of predicted CKII phosphorylation motifs (S/T-D/E-X-E/D). As shown in Fig. 4a, hyPB contains four such motifs within the first 74 amino acids, whereas hcPB contains three and mePB contains two within the same region. Fig. 4b shows the amino acid sequences of the C-terminal cysteine-rich domain (CRD) of the four transposases, with conserved cysteine residues marked by black dots. Alignment analysis showed that the conservation level is very low, and we could not detect the transposase binding motifs in the LE/RE of hcPB and mePB, which were identified in hyPB and piggyBat (Fig. S4). To further investigate the functional significance of these motifs, we designed and constructed donor vectors containing either asymmetric transposon ends (LE-RE) or symmetric left ends (LE-LE) (Fig. 4c, left). We also prepared helper vectors encoding either the hyPB transposase or engineered variants (74hyPB and 74hyPB2CD), which carries a deletion of the 74 aa N-terminus of hyPB (yielding 74hyPB), and another with an additional CRD domain (yielding 74hyPB2CD, Fig. 4c, right). We found that the hyPB transposon system's activity significantly improved when using short symmetric transposon left ends in combination with transposase engineering. Specifically, deleting the 74 aa N-terminus of hyPB (yielding 74hyPB) and adding a CRD domain (74hyPB2CD) enhanced transposition by 27% with short LE-LE. In contrast, the engineered hyPB transposase (74hyPB2CD) showed no change in activity when used with short asymmetric ends (LE-RE) compared to the wild-type hyPB. Notably, the non-engineered hyPB transposase exhibited a significant decrease in activity when paired with short symmetric ends (short LE-LE or short RE-RE) (Fig. 4d).

For the engineered transposases hcPB (74hcPB, 74hcPB2CD) and mePB (74mePB, 74mePB2CD), the activity of 74hcPB2CD and 74mePB2CD with long asymmetric ends (LE-RE) was substantially reduced (Fig. 4e). However, when using short 40 bp symmetric left ends (LE-LE), 74hcPB2CD maintained wild-type-level activity, while 74mePB2CD showed a ∼20% reduction. In contrast, the activities of 74hcPB and its wild-type counterpart (hcPB) were substantially reduced, and the activities of 74mePB and its wild-type counterpart (mePB) were almost abolished (Fig. 4f).

Discussion

Systematic evolutionary analysis of a specific family or superfamily of DNA transposons can help illustrate the diversity, distribution, and activity of these transposons, and aid in mining highly active members. Based on systematic bioinformatic data mining and evolutionary dynamics (K divergence) predictions, highly active Spy transposons from the PHIS superfamily and Passer transposons from the pogo superfamily were obtained and developed into gene delivery tools [24,25]. The evolutionary landscape of PB transposons in Hymenoptera remains largely unexplored, despite this insect order being one of the largest and most well-known. To address this, we systematically investigated PB transposons across 287 hymenopteran species. Our analysis revealed their extensive distribution, with copies detected in approximately half (137/287) of the species. Phylogenetic reconstruction identified four distinct clades, demonstrating significant evolutionary diversity among these transposases. Furthermore, we detected intact piggyBac (PB) transposons in over 80 species. Combined with evolutionary dynamics (K divergence) analysis, these findings indicate that some of these elements are young genomic invaders that remain highly active within the hymenopteran lineage. This activity is consistent with the known behavior of the superfamily, as at least three active PB transposons have been isolated from other insects, including Trichoplusia ni [26], Spodoptera frugiperda [9], and another lepidopteran [8].

Based on K divergence analysis, three PB transposons from Holcopasites calliopsidis, Macropis europaea, and Andrena dorsata were selected as strong candidates for activity. Experimental measurement by a binary transposition assay revealed that two of them (hcPB and mePB) displayed activity comparable to the engineered hyPB transposase, which represents a significantly improved system. These findings suggest that evolutionary dynamics (K divergence) or insertion age serves as an important indicator of transposition activity—a criterion that has been applied in the mining of multiple active DNA transposons, such as Passer [24], Buster [27], Spy [25], ZB [23], and Mutator [28]. Furthermore, they provide additional evidence that PB transposons are highly active in Hymenoptera.

DNA transposons, which function via a direct “cut-and-paste” mechanism, are widely used as non-viral vector systems for gene delivery in gene therapy, transgenesis, and mutagenesis. Compared to viral vectors, transposon-based systems offer several advantages: low cost, large gene cargo capacity, stable genomic integration, reduced immunogenicity, and simpler manipulation with a lower risk of immunosuppression. However, a primary limitation is their relatively low delivery efficiency [29]. To address this, most native transposons require extensive engineering to enhance their transposition activity for therapeutic applications. Notable examples from the Tc1/mariner family, such as Sleeping Beauty [30,31], and MAG [32], as well as TcBuster from the hAT superfamily [33,34], have been significantly optimized with substantial improvements in activity. Similarly, three naturally active piggyBac transposons—PB from Trichoplusia ni [26], Mage from Lepidoptera [8], and piggyBat from Myotis lucifugus [4]—have been extensively engineered to boost their performance.

Recent studies have identified molecular mechanisms that inhibit piggyBac transposition. For instance, N-terminal phosphorylation of the transposase and the use of asymmetric transposon ends can suppress activity. In mammalian cells, the PB transposase is multiply phosphorylated at three casein kinase II (CKII) motifs. Mass spectrometry revealed that Ser17 and Ser35 are fully phosphorylated, while Ser41 is phosphorylated in approximately 20% of peptides. Mutating these serine residues significantly increased transposition activity [16]. This finding was corroborated in piggyBat, where mutating serines in its four CKII motifs also enhanced activity [15]. Consequently, a simple engineering strategy involving the deletion of the N-terminal region containing these inhibitory sites can enhance activity, particularly when using symmetric transposon left ends [16]. The C-terminal CRD of piggyBac transposases plays essential roles in transposase–DNA binding and in dimerization-induced conformational changes of the transposon ends, both critical for assembling the active synaptic complex. Duplication of the CRD is hypothesized to enhance transposition by increasing dimerization efficiency or stabilizing the functional conformation of the transpososome [13,14]. Our data confirm that deleting the N-terminal 74-amino acid phosphorylation region and appending a second C-terminal domain increased the transposition activity of the hyPB transposase with symmetric ends. In contrast, the activity of hcPB was maintained, while a ∼20% reduction was observed for mePB compared to their wild-type counterparts. Interestingly, merely deleting the N-terminal 74-amino acid region in hcPB and mePB (creating variants 74hcPB and 74mePB) significantly impaired or abolished their activity. This stands in contrast to 74hyPB, where the same deletion strategy caused a significant improvement, indicating that this engineering approach is not universally applicable across all piggyBac transposons. This differential outcome can be partially explained by the distinct CKII motif configurations among these transposases: hyPB contains four CKII motifs within its N-terminal 74 amino acids, whereas hcPB contains three and mePB contains only two (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, engineering of piggyBat showed that mutating the four CKII serine residues (S8, S24, S32, S37) to alanine, combined with duplication of the C-terminal domain, improved transposition activity on asymmetric LE88/RE100 ends but was less effective on symmetric LE88/LE88 ends [15].

The differential responses to our engineering strategies (N-terminal deletion and C-terminal duplication) among hyPB, hcPB, and mePB reveal an unappreciated mechanistic diversity within the PB family. For hyPB, the N-terminal 74 amino acids act as a removable inhibitory module, consistent with previous reports of phosphorylation-dependent suppression. In contrast, the same deletion severely impaired hcPB and mePB, suggesting that their N-terminal regions are not merely inhibitory but may be essential for transposase folding, DNA binding, or dimerization. This finding carries two important implications. Firstly, from an evolutionary perspective, different PB lineages have solved the problem of “self-restraint” through divergent molecular architectures, highlighting the plasticity of this transposase family. Secondly, from a practical perspective, there is no “one-size-fits-all” engineering recipe for PB transposons. Our results underscore the value of mining diverse natural transposons, as each may possess unique properties that can be either harnessed directly (as in the case of wild-type hcPB and mePB, which rival hyPB in activity) or optimized through tailored engineering. Identifying the interaction structures of hcPB and mePB transposases with their DNA ends using AI-based protein modeling tools like AlphaFold could help pinpoint surface residues critical for DNA binding. This represents a promising avenue for rational design in future studies.

Our finding that the same N-terminal deletion and TIR truncation strategies that enhance hyPB either impair or fail to improve hcPB and mePB carries an important implication for future PB engineering. Specifically, it suggests that engineering rules are not universally transferable across different PB orthologs. Newly discovered PB systems may therefore require ortholog-specific optimization rather than relying on design principles derived from hyPB. In this context, data-driven or machine learning-guided strategies represent a promising alternative to traditional rational engineering. Recent work by Ivančić et al. successfully demonstrated the use of protein language models to generate hyperactive PB variants, an approach that does not require detailed structural or mechanistic knowledge of the transposase [35]. Given the unpredictable responses of hcPB and mePB to standard engineering modifications, such AI-guided methods may prove particularly valuable for optimizing newly isolated natural transposases. We anticipate that combining bioprospecting of diverse natural PB elements with machine learning-guided optimization will be a powerful paradigm for expanding the genome engineering toolkit.
