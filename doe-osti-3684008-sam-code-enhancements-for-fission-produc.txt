 ABSTRACT

 This report documents fiscal year 2026 enhancements to the System Analysis Module (SAM) for mod-
eling fission product transport in liquid-fueled molten salt reactors (MSRs). The work advances three prin-
cipal areas: noble gas transport, noble metal deposition, and user interface improvements. The noble gas
transport capability integrates drift-flux gas transport, Henry’s law two-film interphase mass transfer with
pressure-based nucleation suppression, Knudsen-regime pore diffusion into porous graphite with a conju-
gate salt-graphite interface constraint, built-in material properties, five Sherwood-number mass transfer cor-
relations including three derived from high-fidelity NekRS simulations, and xenon-135 reactivity feedback
through SAM’s point-kinetics model. This work also presents a comprehensive verification test suite, in-
cluding new analytically verified cases for pressure-dependent onset of interphase gas transfer in a stagnant
vertical pipe, a postulated FLiBe-graphite Xe extraction permeator, a gravity riser with a fission-product
source, and a descending pipe with gas redissolution driven by hydrostatic pressure. A machine learn-
ing framework for bubble rise velocity prediction in molten salt systems is developed and benchmarked
on molten-salt and diverse aqueous bubble datasets. The best-performing fine-tuned transfer-learning net-
works achieve an 82% reduction in RMSE relative to the Clift correlation, and is implemented directly in
SAM. The noble metal transport capability is developed, including a liquid-wall deposition model and a
gas-surface flotation mechanism that transfers insoluble particles entrained by sparging gas to wetted struc-
tures. Verification tests and demonstration cases cover the surface deposition, flotation efflux, and flotation
shedding. Finally, a new [SpeciesTransport] input structure replaces positional global vectors with self-
contained, order-independent, named species blocks, simplifies the specification of multiphase species and
decay chains, and remains fully compatible with existing SAM input files. Together, these developments
improve the physical fidelity, verification basis, and usability of SAM for system-level analyses of fission-
product behavior in MSRs.

1 Introduction

 There has been renewed and growing interest in molten salt reactor (MSR) technology as a candidate for
advanced nuclear power generation. Liquid-fueled MSRs, in which fissile and fertile materials are dissolved
directly in a circulating fluoride or chloride salt, offer potential advantages in fuel flexibility, passive safety,
and high-temperature operation. However, the use of a liquid fuel introduces unique modeling challenges that
are not encountered in solid-fueled reactor systems. Chief among these is the transport of fission products
throughout the primary loop: because the fuel salt continuously circulates, gaseous and insoluble fission
products are no longer confined to the fuel matrix but are instead free to migrate, deposit, and accumulate
throughout the reactor system. Accurate system-level predictions of fission product behavior are essential for
reactor safety analysis, including source-term estimation, neutron poison accounting, and the assessment of
material degradation from deposition.

 Noble gas fission products—primarily 135 Xe and 85 Kr—present a particularly complex modeling
challenge. In solid-fueled reactors, noble gases are retained within the fuel matrix and their effect on
reactivity is captured through simple buildup and decay equations. In liquid-fueled MSRs, however, these
species are released directly into the fuel salt and can exist simultaneously in multiple phases: dissolved in
the liquid salt, transported as a dispersed bubble phase, and diffused into the porous graphite moderator in
thermal-spectrum designs. 135 Xe is a dominant neutron poison with a thermal absorption cross section of
approximately 2.6 × 106 barns, and the distribution of Xe-135 among its multiple transport pathways has
a direct and transient-dependent impact on core reactivity. Understanding and predicting this distribution
requires a fully integrated, system-level transport model that resolves multi-phase flow, species interphase
transfer, porous diffusion, and reactivity feedback simultaneously.

 Insoluble noble metal fission products—including molybdenum, technetium, ruthenium, rhodium,
palladium, and tellurium—represent a second class of important fission products in MSRs. Unlike noble
gases, noble metals are chemically inert and generally lyophobic; they tend to form particulates that plate
out on structural surfaces or accumulate at gas-liquid interfaces. Their deposition behavior has implications
for both the reactor source term and for the integrity of structural materials that experience accumulation of
noble metal deposits over time. Fouling of heat exchangers can potentially introduce flow blockages and
plate out of short-lived fission products may also add undesired decay heating of structural components.

 Under the U.S. Department of Energy Nuclear Energy Advanced Modeling and Simulation (NEAMS)
program, SAM has been progressively extended to address the unique modeling requirements of MSR systems.
A comprehensive overview of these integrated capabilities can be found in Hu et al. [2]. Because liquid-fueled
MSRs involve tightly coupled interactions among reactor kinetics, thermal-hydraulics, species transport, and
salt chemistry, building a credible system-level analysis tool requires that each of these phenomena be treated
in an integrated and physically consistent way. The MSR-specific developments in SAM include:

 • a modified point kinetics model that accounts for the circulation of delayed neutron precursors (DNPs)
 with the flowing fuel and the associated reduction in the effective delayed neutron fraction;

 • flowing decay heat precursor groups, based on the ANS decay heat standard, that redistribute decay
 heating spatially as fission products are advected through the primary loop;

 • a general species transport model based on an advection-diffusion-decay formulation that can track an

 1

 arbitrary number of species simultaneously in the liquid and gas phases;

 • a tritium transport model in which species can also diffuse, permeate, or become trapped in solid
 structural materials;

 • a salt corrosion model grounded in mixed-potential theory and Butler-Volmer interfacial kinetics for
 redox-driven degradation of structural alloys;

 • a one-dimensional fluid solidification (freezing) model and freeze plug component; and

 • a built-in library of temperature-dependent molten salt equations of state, along with an interface to the
 Molten Salt Database Thermophysical (MSD-TP) properties through Saline.

The integrated model has been validated against a suite of MSRE experiments, including pump startup and
coastdown, natural convection, reactivity insertion, and frequency-response tests. Building on this framework,
FY2024 work established a four-equation drift-flux gas transport model and basic species interphase transfer
capability [3], and FY2025 saw major expansions to the noble gas transport model including Knudsen-regime
pore diffusion into graphite, a conjugate salt-graphite interface flux constraint, five Sherwood-number mass
transfer correlations, species reactivity feedback coupling to point kinetics, and a system-level demonstration
using the MSRE primary loop [4]. The present report documents FY2026 developments, which extend and
deepen this capability in four areas.

 First, the noble gas verification test suite is expanded with new analytically-verified cases that exercise
the pressure-dependent interphase transfer with an added nucleation suppression model that is demonstrated
in vertical pipe geometries representative of MSR primary loop configurations. Second, a machine learning
framework for predicting bubble rise velocity in molten salt systems is developed, benchmarked, and
integrated into SAM. The framework employs neural networks, physics-informed neural networks, and
transfer learning trained on a molten-salt bubble dataset, with deep-ensemble uncertainty quantification to
identify data gaps for future experiments. Third, the noble metal transport model is developed with a flotation
deposition mechanism that accounts for noble metal particles carried in the gas phase and subsequently
deposited on wetted surfaces. Fourth, a new [SpeciesTransport] input syntax is introduced to improve
the usability of the species transport framework for problems with large numbers of species and complex
decay chains.

 The remainder of this report is organized as follows. Section 2 presents the complete noble gas transport
model, covering theory, implementation, and verification tests. The machine learning framework for bubble
velocity prediction and its integration into SAM is also presented here. Section 3 presents the noble metal
transport model including the new flotation deposition mechanism and demonstration cases. Section 4
describes the new [SpeciesTransport] input block syntax and its implementation.

2 Noble Gas Transport Model

 Noble gases xenon and krypton are produced continuously as fission products during nuclear reactor
operation. In solid-fueled reactors these species remain embedded in the fuel matrix; in liquid-fueled
MSRs they are released directly into the circulating fuel salt and transported throughout the primary loop.
135 Xe is of particular importance because of its extraordinary thermal neutron absorption cross section

(σa ≈ 2.6 × 106 barns), making it a dominant neutron poison that significantly affects core reactivity during
both steady-state operation and transients. Its low solubility in fluoride salts means it readily partitions
between the dissolved liquid phase and any gas bubble phase present, and in thermal-spectrum MSRs with
graphite moderators it also migrates into the porous graphite where it accumulates and further perturbs the
neutron balance [5, 6].

 Beginning in FY2024, the species and gas transport frameworks in SAM were extended to provide a
system-level noble gas transport capability [3]. In FY2025 and FY2026, this capability was substantially
extended to include noble gas migration into porous graphite with built-in material property functions for
MSR-relevant species-material pairs, species reactivity feedback coupling to SAM’s point-kinetics model, an
expanded set of mass transfer correlations derived from NekRS high-fidelity CFD data, and a comprehensive
verification test suite [4]. This section presents the complete noble gas transport model as implemented
in SAM, covering the governing equations, MOOSE-based implementation, and verification tests against
analytical solutions. System-level MSRE demonstrations reported in the FY25 NEAMS M2 report [4] are
not repeated here.

2.2 Implementation in SAM

2.2.1 System Thermal-Hydraulics Implementation

 SAM uses a component-based approach to allow users to build representations of whole reactor systems
for integrated thermal-fluid and structural heat conduction simulation. The noble gas transport capability
has been fully integrated into this component system, allowing flexibility in defining system-wide and
component-specific model parameters. SAM is built on the MOOSE finite-element framework, and the
noble gas transport capability integrates several MOOSE objects: PSPhaseTransferAux computes the
volumetric liquid–gas interphase transfer rate using Eq. (2-6) with the pressure-based nucleation suppres-

2.4 Machine Learning Prediction of Gas Velocity

 Accurate prediction of gas (bubble) velocity in molten salt systems is essential for modeling two-phase
flow and void fraction in liquid-fueled MSRs. Bubble velocity governs residence time, transport, and extrac-
tion efficiency of gaseous fission products; inaccurate predictions therefore affect void-fraction estimates,
reactivity feedback, and source-term calculations. Existing terminal-velocity correlations (Clift [18], Har-
mathy [19]) were derived primarily from air–water data and exhibit large errors when extrapolated to molten
salts [1]. Although SAM already contains bubble-transport models based on these correlations, they inherit
the same water-based empiricism. Machine learning provides a data-driven route to capture the nonlinear
dependence of rise velocity on fluid properties and geometry, while physics-informed and transfer-learning
strategies mitigate the scarcity of molten-salt measurements.

 This work [20] develops and benchmarks neural networks (NNs), physics-informed neural networks
(PINNs), and transfer-learning (TL) models on the MSR Campaign salt-bubble dataset [1] and the large
Cioncolini–Magnini compilation [21]. Transfer learning between the two datasets yields marked gains in
accuracy and reduced uncertainty on the sparse molten-salt data. Deep-ensemble uncertainty quantification
identifies critical data gaps that can guide the next experimental campaign. The best-performing model was
implemented inside SAM for direct use in reactor simulation workflows.

2.4.1 Datasets

 Two complementary datasets were employed. The MSR Campaign dataset [1] comprises 83 high-fidelity
optical measurements of solitary bubbles (He, N2 , Ar, Kr) rising in clean LiCl–KCl eutectic at 500 ◦ C, shown
in Figure 2.8. Equivalent diameters range from 0.45 to 3.2 mm. The salt is free of fissile material and
particulates, providing an idealized baseline representative of MSR sparging conditions.

 15

Figure 2.8: Proportional distribution of experimental data in the MSR Campaign dataset by gas species [1].

 The second dataset is the Cioncolini & Magnini compilation [21], which contains >7 000 points from 58
independent studies covering 46 liquid–gas pairs (predominantly water/aqueous solutions and air). Property
ranges largely overlap the MSR Campaign data, but surface tension near 0.124 N m−1 and higher gas densities
are underrepresented in the MSR Campaign set. This large, diverse dataset serves as the pretraining source
for transfer learning.

 All models use the same six inputs: bubble equivalent diameter de , liquid and gas densities ρℓ and ρg ,
liquid viscosity µℓ , surface tension σ , and tube diameter D. The prediction target is terminal bubble rise
velocity vb . Only solitary-bubble flow was considered.

2.4.2 Methodology

 The MSR Campaign dataset was partitioned into training, validation, and test sets with a 60% / 20% / 20%
split. Four complementary ML strategies were developed.

Neural networks. Multilayer perceptrons (illustrative architecture 6-10-10-1, Figure 2.9) transform the six
input features into a velocity prediction through successive weighted layers. Hyperparameters—activation
function (ReLU, LeakyReLU, ELU, GELU), number of hidden layers (1–4), neurons per layer (10–100),

16

learning rate, and epochs—were optimized with Optuna. Models were trained either on the MSR set alone
(“MS”) or on an integrated Cioncolini+MSR set (“I”). Prior to training all features were standardized; a fixed
random seed (42) ensured reproducibility.

Figure 2.9: Illustrative NN architecture (6-10-10-1). The six physical input features are transformed through
two hidden layers of 10 neurons each to produce the terminal rise velocity prediction.

Physics-informed neural networks. A classical correlation (Clift [18] or Harmathy [19]) supplies a
baseline prediction; the network is trained solely on the residual between that baseline and the experimental
measurements. During training, an input is fed to both the physics model and the network; the network
parameters are updated to minimize the residual mean-squared error. In prediction the final output is the sum
of the physics baseline and the learned correction.

Transfer learning. Networks pretrained on the large Cioncolini–Magnini source set were adapted to the
smaller MSR target either by freezing the early layers and training only a new output head (feature extraction,
FE) as shown in Figure 2.10, or by allowing all weights to update under a reduced learning-rate schedule
(fine-tuning, FT). In the feature-extraction approach the pretrained layers remain fixed so that only the final
head adapts to molten-salt conditions, while fine-tuning permits modest adjustments throughout the network.
On the MSR test set, the transfer-learning models delivered very good predictive performance, with the
fine-tuning variant achieving the lowest overall RMSE among all architectures examined and substantially
reducing epistemic uncertainty relative to networks trained from scratch on the limited molten-salt data alone.

 17

Figure 2.10: Transfer-learning feature extraction process. Networks pretrained on the Cioncolini–Magnini

Uncertainty quantification. Deep ensembles of 15 identically architected networks were trained inde-
pendently from different random initializations (and optionally bootstrapped samples) using a negative-log-
likelihood loss [22]. This loss simultaneously encourages accurate mean predictions and well-calibrated
variance estimates. The ensemble variance is decomposed into an aleatoric component (average of the
individual predictive variances, reflecting irreducible data noise) and an epistemic component (variance of
the ensemble means, reflecting model uncertainty due to limited data). Particular attention is paid to the
epistemic uncertainty, which is used to identify data-sparse regions and thereby guide the design of the next
experimental campaign.

training set is available. Deep-ensemble uncertainty quantification cleanly separates irreducible experimental
noise from reducible model uncertainty and has already pinpointed the diameter and gas ranges that should
be prioritized in the next experimental campaign. The integration of the best model into SAM provides an
immediate pathway for more reliable two-phase-flow predictions in MSR system analyses. Future work
will extend the framework to multi-bubble interactions and forced-flow conditions, and will incorporate the
effects of noble-metal particulates and fission-product impurities that will be present in real fuel salts.

3 Noble Metal Transport Model

 Noble metal fission products, such as molybdenum, technetium, ruthenium, rhodium, palladium, and
tellurium behave in a manner distinct from that of noble gases, and represent another class of important
fission products in MSRs. These noble metals are generally chemically inert and lyophobic (i.e., liquid-
repelling), and their relatively low degree of attraction to the liquid medium (i.e., fuel salt) causes them
to form particulates within the liquid medium. These particulate fission products thereby act as passively
advecting species within the reactor system.

 Due to their lyophobic nature, the noble metal fission products tend to deposit onto non-liquid surfaces
as they encounter them, such as onto structural surfaces (i.e., liquid-solid interface) or onto gas bubbles (i.e.,
liquid-gas interface) [23]. These two types of deposition processes are referred to in this work as surface
deposition and flotation, respectively. The deposition behavior of noble metals has implications for both the
reactor source term and the performance of reactor components that experience the accumulation of noble
metals over time. For example, the fouling of heat exchangers by these species can potentially introduce
blockages, reduce heat exchanger performance, and add undesired decay heating to the component. Therefore,
it is important to model the generation, transport, and extinction of noble metal fission products within an
MSR.

3.2 Verification Tests

 During the SAM code feature development for modeling noble metal transport, verification tests are
developed to ensure that the code behaves as designed. These tests are split into unit tests which break up the
test scenarios into reasonably isolated phenomena so that each sub-feature can be tested. These unit tests also
contain built-in analytical solutions which are used to compare against the SAM simulation results to ensure
that the simulation error is within acceptable levels. A summary of unit tests for noble metal transport in
SAM is listed in Table 3.1. In the table, the discrepancy and description of each unit test is provided.
 In Table 3.1, all unit tests reported errors of less than 1e-3%, with the exception of PS to Flot MTC
and Flot Shedding. In PS to Flot MTC, the analytical solution is approximated using the average mass
transfer coefficient (MTC) of the pipe while SAM calculates the MTC at every node in the pipe. This
slight difference contributes to the error of the test. In Flot Shedding, the larger error magnitude is due to
the shedding process applying a large transfer rate constant (i.e., λmax ) to the species concentration, which
contributes to a larger numerical error. However, the precise flotation concentration is not as important
during the shedding process as the flotation species rapidly returns to the liquid-dispersion phase. The more
important metric would be the total species conservation between the flotation and liquid-dispersion phases,
which is conserved to an error of less than 1e-10%.

4 User Interface Enhancements

4.1 Motivation

 SAM’s species transport framework — originally introduced to model flowing delayed neutron precur-
sors (DNPs) in liquid-fueled MSRs — has expanded in scope to encompass dissolved fission products, noble
gases, decay-chain species, and noble metals relevant to MSR safety analysis. As the number of modeled
species grows, so does the complexity of specifying their properties in the input file.

 The legacy input syntax collects all species transport parameters inside the [PBModelParams] sub-
block of [GlobalParams] as a set of parallel, positionally ordered vectors. A representative excerpt for a
two-species system consisting of liquid and gas-phase xenon is shown below:

[GlobalParams]
  [PBModelParams]
 passive_scalar = 'Xe_l Xe_g'
 passive_scalar_phase = 'liq gas'
 passive_scalar_volatility = 'high high'
 passive_scalar_transfers_to = 'Xe_g Xe_l'
 passive_scalar_mass_transfer_option = UserFunction
 passive_scalar_mass_transfer_coefficient = '4.617e-2 4.617e-2'
 passive_scalar_henry_coefficient = '1.85e-4 1.85e-4'
 passive_scalar_eos = 'none xenon_eos'
 passive_scalar_scaling_factor = '1e1 1e2'
  []
[]

 Every property — phase assignment, EOS name, volatility, mass transfer coefficients, scaling factors —
must be entered as a positional vector whose ordering is globally consistent across all parameters. Inserting,
removing, or reordering a species requires simultaneous updates to every vector, which is error-prone and
becomes increasingly difficult to maintain as the number of modeled species grows to include DNPs, decay
heat precursors, noble metals, and noble gases across multiple phases.

4.2 New [SpeciesTransport] Block Syntax

 To address these usability challenges, a new [SpeciesTransport] top-level input block was intro-
duced. The block provides a per-species sub-block structure in which each species is declared by name and
all of its parameters are self-contained within that sub-block, independent of ordering or any other species
definition. The equivalent of the two-species example above is:

34

[SpeciesTransport]
  input_as_volumetric = true
  [Xe]
 phase = 'liq gas'
 gas_species_eos = xenon_eos
 volatility = high
 mass_transfer_option = UserFunction
 mass_transfer_coefficient = 4.617e-2
 henry_coefficient = 1.85e-4
 scaling_factor = 1e1
  []
[]

 Specifying phase = 'liq gas' for a species causes SAM to automatically create two internal entries
named Xe_l and Xe_g and wire their phase-transfer linkage, eliminating the need to manually declare both
phase variants and set passive_scalar_transfers_to in both directions. When only a single phase is
required, phase = 'liq' or phase = 'gas' is specified and a single entry is created.

 Each sub-block supports a full set of per-species parameters. Parameters that may differ between phases
— scaling_factor, initial_value, and from_fission — accept either a single value that broadcasts
uniformly to all phases, or a list of values in the same order as the entries in phase. The block-level
input_as_volumetric parameter controls whether initial concentrations are interpreted as volumetric
(mol m−3 ) or mass (mol kg−1 ) concentrations and applies uniformly to all species in the block. The complete
list of available parameters and their descriptions is provided in the updated SAM User’s Guide [27].

 The new syntax also simplifies the specification of radioactive decay chains. The decay_constant
parameter sets the radioactive decay constant (λ , s−1 ) for a species, and the decays_to parameter specifies
the sub-block name of the daughter species into which the parent decays. The following example configures
a two-species liquid-phase decay chain in which 137 Cs decays into 137 Ba at the appropriate decay constant:

4.4 Implementation

 The new syntax is realized through two MOOSE Action classes: AddSpeciesTransportAction and
FinalizeSpeciesTransportAction. The former is executed once per species sub-block and collects
each species’ parameters into a registry held by the SAMSimulation object. The latter executes after all
sub-blocks have been processed and translates the named-block representation into the existing flat-vector
passive_scalar_* parameter arrays consumed by the SAM solver. This translation layer provides full
backward compatibility: existing input files using the legacy [PBModelParams] syntax continue to function
without modification.

 A separate refactoring effort extracted a shared PassiveScalarHelper utility class to consolidate
species-wiring logic that had previously been duplicated between the PointKinetics and ReactorPower
classes. This eliminated approximately 270 lines of duplicated code from the reactor power source files and
ensures that DNP and decay heat precursor species registered through those classes are correctly recognized
by the new action framework. An associated fix was applied to the DNP pre-check in both reactor power
classes to ensure compatibility with species registered through the new block syntax. An additional correction
was made to the finalization logic to ensure that species participating in Noble Metal flotation and interphase
transfer are correctly assigned when used alongside the [SpeciesTransport] block.

5 Conclusions

 This report documents FY2026 enhancements to the SAM code for fission product tracking in liquid-
fueled molten salt reactors, spanning noble gas transport, machine learning-assisted bubble velocity prediction,
noble metal deposition, and user interface improvements.

 The noble gas transport capability in SAM provides a comprehensive system-level framework for
modeling the multi-phase behavior of xenon and krypton fission products in MSR primary loops. The
model encompasses a drift-flux gas transport formulation with Henry’s law two-film interphase transfer and
smooth pressure-based nucleation suppression, Knudsen-regime pore diffusion into graphite moderators
with a conjugate salt-graphite interface constraint enforcing Henry’s law solubility continuity, five geometry-
specific Sherwood-number mass transfer correlations including three derived from NekRS high-fidelity CFD
data for pipe, rod bundle, and pebble bed geometries, and direct coupling to SAM’s point-kinetics model
for Xe-135 reactivity feedback. Built-in temperature-dependent material property functions for Xe-135 in
FLiBe, IG-11 graphite, and NaF-ZrF4 -UF4 eliminate the need for manual property specification in common
MSR configurations. The FY2026 verification test suite adds four new analytically-verified cases: pressure-
dependent onset of interphase gas transfer, the FLiBe-graphite Xe extraction permeator, a gravity riser with
fission-product source mimicking noble gas buildup in an MSR core channel, and a descending downcomer
in which gas redissolves under increasing hydrostatic pressure. Together these cases confirm the correct
behavior of the nucleation suppression model, the porous graphite diffusion interface with built-in material
properties, and the conservation properties of the coupled two-phase transport equations in both upward and
downward flow configurations.

 A machine learning framework for bubble rise velocity prediction was developed, benchmarked, and
integrated into SAM. Neural networks, physics-informed neural networks, and transfer-learning architectures
were trained on the MSR Campaign dataset of 83 optical measurements of solitary bubbles in LiCl-KCl at
500 ◦ C, with the large Cioncolini-Magnini compilation of over 7,000 points from 58 independent studies used
as a pretraining source. The fine-tuned transfer-learning network achieved the lowest RMSE of 5.66 mm s−1 —
an 82% reduction relative to the Clift correlation—and was successfully implemented and verified in SAM.
Deep-ensemble uncertainty quantification identified the transition-diameter region (1 < de < 2.5 mm) and
small-diameter ranges for argon and nitrogen as the highest-priority targets for future experimental campaigns.
This work provides a direct path to more reliable two-phase-flow and void-fraction predictions in MSR
system analyses.

 The noble metal transport model was developed including a gas-surface flotation deposition mechanism
and a liquid-wall deposition model. In MSR primary loops with a sparging gas phase, insoluble noble
metal particulates can be entrained in gas bubbles and subsequently transferred to wetted surfaces when the
bubbles contact or rise past structural materials. The new flotation model captures this transfer pathway with
sticking-coefficient-based deposition kinetics, and demonstration cases confirm the expected behavior for
surface deposition, flotation efflux, and flotation shedding scenarios.

 The new [SpeciesTransport] input block syntax substantially improves the usability of the species
transport framework for complex MSR problems. By replacing positionally ordered global vectors with
self-contained, named per-species sub-blocks, the new syntax eliminates a major source of input errors when
working with large numbers of species across multiple phases and decay chains. The implementation is
fully backward compatible, and the action-based translation layer ensures that existing input files continue to

 37

function without modification. The refactoring also reduces code overlap and improves maintainability going
forward.

 Taken together, the FY2026 developments represent a significant maturation of SAM’s MSR fission
product modeling capability. Future work will focus on validation against experimental data from the MSRE
xenon poisoning and Kr-85 measurements, extension of the noble gas model to additional salt systems,
and the effects of noble metal particulates and fission-product impurities on the reactor system. Continued
integration of high-fidelity CFD-derived closure correlations and further expansion of the machine learning
framework to cover a wider range of gas species and salt compositions will further strengthen the predictive
fidelity of system-level MSR analyses.
