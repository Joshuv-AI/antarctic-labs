A Stochastic–Geometric Theory of Scaling Laws in Grokking
Róisín Luo, Christian Gagné, Jonas Ngnawé, Ihsan Ullah, Karyn Morrissey

Abstract
Delayed generalization ( i.e. grokking) refers to the phenomenon in which a neural network fits its training data early in training but only begins to generalize after a prolonged delay, often through an abrupt transition. Despite extensive empirical study, its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood. In this work, we first theoretically characterize a shell–core topological configuration of the reachable solution space induced by Adam’s optimization dynamics with weight-shrinkage regularization, supported by empirical evidence. This optimization-induced topological configuration gives rise to grokking. In model’s parameter space, random initialization solutions concentrate on a thin outer spherical shell, enclosing another spherical shell of memorization solutions, which in turn contains a core corresponding to the generalization solutions. Leveraging stopping-time theory, we then analyze the geometry of this topological configuration and the solution transition time at which optimization trajectories escape the memorization manifold and first reach the boundary of the generalization manifold. Our theoretical analysis derives grokking scaling laws for the learning rate, batch size, and ℓ 2 \ell_{2} regularization coefficient, which are further validated through experiments and shown to recover results from prior literature.

(

(

(

(

)
, including the fraction of training data, learning rate, batch size, and regularization coefficient
(
19
;
12
)
. Despite extensive empirical investigation, however, its underlying mechanism remains poorly understood.

Empirical findings consistently suggest that training dynamics in the grokking regime exhibit three-stage behavior
(
19
;
17
;
9
)
. In the initial stage, the model rapidly interpolates the training set and reaches the memorization regime. In the second stage, the trajectory remains near the memorization solutions for an extended period before escaping and eventually reaching the generalization regime. The length of this extended period scales with the task, learning rate, batch size, and regularization coefficient. In the third stage, drift and diffusion equilibrate in the optimization-induced dynamics, and the trajectory settles within the generalization solution region centered at its minimizer.

Under the regime with
\ell_{2}
weight-shrinkage regularization, these observations imply the existence of two distinct classes of solutions:
memorization solutions
, which only interpolate the training data and does not generalize, and
generalization solutions
, which additionally achieve low loss with respect to the underlying data distribution. Their topological configuration is thus induced by optimization dynamics, and characterized into three stages: the
early stage
, the
late stage
, and the
equilibrium
, as shown in Figure
1(a)
. The
early-stage
dynamics are driven by deterministic drift from large gradients, which dominates the optimization trajectory and rapidly drives it toward the memorization manifold. At this stage, the gradient fluctuations are anisotropic. The
late-stage
dynamics are driven more slowly by decaying but nonzero gradients, together with isotropic gradient fluctuations, which guide the optimization trajectory across the trivial memorization solutions. Once the trajectory reaches the generalization set, the drift and diffusion in the optimization dynamics equilibrate and confine the trajectory within the generalization manifold, referred to as the
equilibrium
. Figure
1(b)
–
1(c)
provide empirical evidence consistent with this optimization-induced topological configuration by visualizing optimization trajectories via isometric manifold learning. This optimizer-induced topological configuration, as illustrated in Figure
1(a)
, gives rise to grokking and forms a shell–core configuration: initialization solutions concentrate on a thin hyperspherical shell, which encloses a shell of memorization solutions containing a compact core of generalization solutions. Detailed empirical results of Adam–induced shell–core radii are provided in Appendix
A.2
.

We theoretically analyze the geometry of this topological configuration and derive scaling laws for the manifold radii and the solution transition time from memorization to generalization through a
stopping-time analysis

(
4
)
of stochastic differential equations (SDEs)
(
23
)
. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
3
presents initialization concentration, and Section
4.1
establishes the joint-state SDE for Adam
(
8
)
designed to analyze grokking dynamics. Building on this formulation, Section
4.2
derives the scaling law for the memorization-manifold radius. Under the late-stage reduction observations of grokking, Section
4.3
derives a closed-form preconditioned radius SDE for the evolution of manifold radii. Sections
4.4
–
4.5
derive the scaling laws for the generalization-manifold radius and the solution transition time from memorization to generalization. Finally, Section
5
validates the theory through experiments on modular arithmetic and group-theoretic learning tasks, and by comparison with the grokking literature. The contributions are summarized below:

1.

Optimization-Induced Shell–Core Topology.
We introduce an optimizer-induced shell–core prior for grokking, where initialization concentrates on a thin hyperspherical shell enclosing memorization solutions, which in turn contain a compact core of generalization solutions.

2.

Stochastic–Geometric Characterization.
Leveraging the topological prior, we formulate grokking as a stochastic transition problem and characterize the geometry by the first stopping time at which stochastic gradient flows escape the memorization manifold and reach the generalization manifold.

3.

Empirical, Literature, and Symbolic-Algebra Validation.
We validate our theory through experiments on group-theoretic learning and modular arithmetic tasks, and by comparison with the literature, recovering known scaling laws for the learning rate, batch size, and regularization coefficient. We additionally machine-check all closed-form and asymptotic results appearing in the proofs by a symbolic algebra system (SAS) with Julia.

Initialization Concentration

We first demonstrate the topological concentration of network-parameter initialization under normal and uniform distributions. Consider that
\theta_{0}=(\theta_{0}^{(1)},\cdots,\theta_{0}^{(k)})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
consists of
k
sub-vectors such that
\theta_{0}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}}
where
\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}=p
. Define effective dimension
\tilde{p}
such that
\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}
. For coordinate-wise initialization
[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2})
, the initialization radius square

\displaystyle\rho_{\Theta}^{2}(\theta_{0})=\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Biggl(\sum_{i=1}^{p_{j}}\;\Bigl|[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\Bigr|^{2}\Biggr)\sim\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}\chi_{p_{j}}^{2},

(1)

follows a sum of scaled chi-square distributions, where each
\sigma_{j}^{2}\chi_{p_{j}}^{2}
has
p_{j}
degrees of freedom. Hence, the radius of
\Theta
admits the closed-form expression

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}^{2}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\bigl[\rho_{\Theta}^{2}(\theta_{0})\bigr]\;=\;\tilde{\sigma}^{2}p=O(k),\quad\mathrm{Var}_{\theta_{0}}\bigl[\rho_{\Theta}^{2}(\theta_{0})\bigr]=2\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{4}=O(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}),

under a coordinate scaling
\sigma_{j}=O(1/\sqrt{p_{j}})

(
10
;
5
)
. This suggests that initialization solutions concentrate on a thin annulus of radius
O(k)
with thickness
O(\sqrt{k/\tilde{p}})
in parameter space. Similar concentration also holds for coordinate-wise uniform initialization distribution
[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2},\quad\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}}\bigl[\rho_{\Theta}^{2}(\theta_{0})\bigr]\;=\;\frac{p\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}}{3}=O(k),\quad\mathrm{Var}_{\theta_{0}}\bigl[\rho_{\Theta}^{2}(\theta_{0})\bigr]=\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}=O(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}),

under a coordinate scaling
\varepsilon_{j}=O(1/\sqrt{p_{j}})
. Proofs for the normal and uniform concentration results are provided in Appendices
A.3
and
A.4
, respectively.

4
Manifold Radius and Solution Transition Analysis

We characterize Adam’s dynamics through its continuous-time limit, formulated as an SDE on the joint state of the parameters and first- and second-moment estimates. Based on this joint-state SDE, we derive the scaling law for the
memorization radius

\rho_{M}
, defined as the parameter norm at the first-hitting time of the empirical-loss boundary
\partial M
, via perturbation method
(
1
)
. Further leveraging two reduction properties of the grokking dynamics, we reduce the intractable joint-state SDE for Adam (Section
4.1
) to a tractable radial SDE. This reduction enables an analytical characterization of the
generalization radius

\rho_{G}
, defined as the parameter norm at the first-hitting time of the generalization boundary
\partial G
, and the
solution transition time

\mathbb{E}[\tau_{M\to G}]
, defined as the mean time required to transition from memorization boundary
\partial M
to generalization boundary
\partial G
. A high-level sketch for theoretical analysis framework is illustrated as in Figure
6
of the appendix.

4.1
Adam’s Joint-State Continuous-Time SDE Limit

We consider Adam dynamics in the small learning rate and large batch size regime
\eta\to 0,\,b\to\infty
, with exponential moving average (EMA) coefficients
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})\in(0,1)^{2}
and an
\ell_{2}
regularizer
R(\theta)=\tfrac{1}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}
with coefficient
\lambda>0
. Let
g_{t}:=\nabla[\mathscr{L}^{*}_{f}(\xi_{t};\theta_{t})+\lambda R(\theta_{t})]
denote the regularized mini-sample gradient flow, with per-sample mean
\bar{g}_{t}:=\mathbb{E}_{s}[\nabla\ell_{f}(s;\theta_{t})]
and covariance
\Sigma_{t}:=\mathrm{Cov}_{s}[\nabla\ell_{f}(s;\theta_{t})]
. For sufficiently large batch size
b
, the mini-batch gradient admits the distributional limit
g_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t},\Sigma_{t}/b\bigr)
. We refer to this setting as
coupled weight decay
, since the
\ell_{2}
regularizer contributes to the gradient used to update Adam’s first- and second-moment estimates. In contrast, the
decoupled weight decay
scheme applies weight decay as a separate parameter-shrinkage step and is commonly referred to as
AdamW

(
13
)
.

Adam’s Parameter Update Rules.

Let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be model’s parameters. Let
m,v\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the first- and second-moment estimates, respectively. Let
\hat{m},\hat{v}
be the
m,v
with bias corrections. Adam’s discrete update rules at iteration

are given as

\displaystyle m_{k+1}=\beta_{1}\,m_{k}+(1-\beta_{1})\,g_{k},\qquad v_{k+1}=\beta_{2}\,v_{k}+(1-\beta_{2})\,(g_{k}\odot g_{k}),

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{k+1}=m_{k+1}\,/\,(1-\beta_{1}^{k+1}),\qquad\hat{v}_{k+1}=v_{k+1}\,/\,(1-\beta_{2}^{k+1}),

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\eta\,\hat{m}_{k+1}\oslash\Bigl(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{k+1}}+\varepsilon\,\mathbf{1}_{p}\Bigr),

with initial values
\theta_{0}\in\Theta,m_{0}=0
,
v_{0}=0
, where
\odot
is element-wise product and
\oslash
is element-wise division.
\hat{m}
and
\hat{v}
are Adam’s bias corrections, which compensate the warm-up underestimation of
(m_{k},v_{k})
caused by the zero initialization
m_{0}=v_{0}=0
. We drop Adam’s numerical-stability constant
\varepsilon
throughout the analysis as it does not affect the analysis. Defining the Adam joint state as
S_{t}:=(\theta_{t},m_{t},v_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3p}
and using the continuous-time interpolation

, we state the resulting joint-state SDE for Adam in Lemma
1
. The proof sketch is illustrated in Figure
7
. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.5
. The technical correctness is verified through an induced radius SDE with Itô’s lemma in Appendix
A.6

Lemma 1

(Adam’s Joint-State Continuous-Time SDE Limit)
.

Let
S_{t}:=(\theta_{t}^{\top},m_{t}^{\top},v_{t}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3p}
denote the joint state of Adam, where
\theta_{t}
,
m_{t}
, and
v_{t}
are the parameters, first-moment estimates, and second-moment estimates, respectively. Taking the continuous-time interpolation
t=\eta k
, the discrete Adam updates admit the following Itô SDE limit on the joint state

\displaystyle\differential S_{t}\;=\;\mu(S_{t})\,\differential t\;+\;\sqrt{\frac{\eta}{b}}\,\sigma(S_{t})\,\differential W_{t},

(2)

where
\differential W_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the infinitesimal increment of a Wiener process adapted to the filtration generated by the mini-batch sampling process
\{\xi_{t}\}
, with drift factor
\mu(S_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3p}
and diffusion factor
\sigma(S_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{3p\times p}

\displaystyle\mu(S_{t})\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}-\,B(t)\,m_{t}\oslash\sqrt{v_{t}}\\[2.0pt]
-\,\alpha_{1}\,\bigl(m_{t}-\bar{g}_{t}\bigr)\\[2.0pt]
-\,\alpha_{2}\,\bigl(v_{t}-\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\bigr)\end{pmatrix},\quad\sigma(S_{t})\;=\;\begin{pmatrix}0\\[2.0pt]
\alpha_{1}\,\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\\[2.0pt]
\alpha_{2}\,D_{t}\end{pmatrix},\quad B(t)\;:=\;\frac{\sqrt{\,1-e^{-\alpha_{2}t}\,}}{\,1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}\,},

(3)

where
\alpha_{i}:=(1-\beta_{i})/\eta
and
B(t)
is the bias-correction factor in continuous time
and

\displaystyle\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\;:=\;\bar{g}_{t}\odot\bar{g}_{t}\;+\;\frac{1}{b}\,\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\quad D_{t}\;:=\;2\,\mathrm{diag}\left(\bar{g}_{t}\right)\,\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}.

(4)

In particular, to decompose this SDE into a tractable form, we define
\pi(\theta_{t}):=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\right)^{-1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as Adam’s preconditioner. We write
\theta_{t}(\theta_{0}),\theta_{t}(S_{0})
to denote the Adam’s
\theta
-evolution starting from initial states
\theta_{0}\in\Theta,S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}
, respectively, where
\Theta_{S}
is the initialization distribution of
S
.

Remark 1
.

While the continuous-time SDE limit of ordinary stochastic gradient descent (SGD) is now well established
(
11
;
16
;
14
)
, a rigorous continuous-time treatment of Adam that explicitly accounts for stochasticity remains, to the best of our knowledge, comparatively less developed. For example, the joint
(m,v)

(

)
, and approximations for adaptive methods such as RMSprop and Adam were established in

Theorem 1

(Scaling Law of Memorization Radius (Perturbation Solution))
.

The memorization radius approximately admits the scaling law with respect to

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}\;=\;(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}\;+\;\frac{\eta}{b}\,c_{M}\;+\;\left(\frac{\eta}{b}\right)^{\!2}\!c_{M}^{(2)}\;+\;O\!\left(\!\left(\frac{\eta}{b}\right)^{\!3}\right),

depends on the deterministic gradient flow
\nabla\mathscr{L}_{f}(\theta_{t})
, when the task gradient
\nabla\mathscr{L}_{f}^{*}(\theta_{t})

\differential\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}=2\theta_{t}^{\top}\differential\theta_{t}\approx-2\lambda\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\,\differential t\Rightarrow\frac{1}{(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}\differential(\rho_M^{(0)})^{2}=-2\lambda\,\differential t

Remark 2
.

In the radius-shrinkage regime, diffusion first shifts the hitting point inward, giving

. This U-shaped effect is observed in Figure

, the first- and second-moment estimates
m_{t},v_{t}
rapidly converge to its mean-field limit

\displaystyle m_{t}\;\to\;\bar{g}_{t}\,\bigl(1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}\bigr)\to\bar{g}_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad v_{t}\;\to\;\overline{g\odot g}_{t}(1-e^{-\alpha_{2}t})\to\overline{g\odot g}_{t},

(8)

respectively, at an exponential rate
\exp(-O(t))
. The proof for this mean-field limit is provided in Lemma
7
of Appendix
A.7
.

Observation 2

(Small-Isotropic Gradient Covariance)
.

In Adam’s late-stage dynamics, the gradient covariance
\Sigma_{t}
rapidly becomes small and approximately isotropic as
t\to\infty
. With this isotropy and by equation (
4
), the Adam’s preconditioner is therefore approximated by
\pi(\theta)\approx s(\theta)^{-1}I_{p}
, as
t\to\infty
, where the state-dependent scalar
s(\theta)>0
is defined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s(\theta)}:=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\pi(\theta)\bigr),\qquad\pi(\theta):=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}(\theta)\right)^{-1/2},

(9)

where
p
is the parameter dimension. In particular, let
G(\theta):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}B(t)\pi(\theta)\Sigma(\theta)^{1/2}
be the preconditioned diffusion factor, which admits
G(\theta)G(\theta)^{\top}\approx I_{p}
. See Appendix
A.10
.

Under Observations
1
–
2
, Lemma
2
gives the approximate closed-form dynamics of
r_{t}^{2}=\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.11
, where we also experimentally verify that the lemma accurately characterizes the late-stage dynamics of manifold radii in Figure
10
.

Lemma 2

(Reduced Late-Stage Radius SDE)
.

Let
r_{t}^{2}:=\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}
. In Adam’s late-stage regime underlying grokking, assume that the diffusion covariance becomes isotropic
G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p}
as
t\to\infty
, then the reduced late-stage squared-radius SDE admits

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}^{2}\approx\left[-\frac{2\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}r_{t}^{2}-\frac{2}{s(\theta_{t})}\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)+\eta\,p+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)\right]\differential t+2\sqrt{\eta}\,r_{t}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)},

(10)

where slow-manifold residual
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)
and preconditioner residual
\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)
are

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)

\displaystyle:=-2B(t)\theta_{t}^{\top}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[m_{t}\oslash\sqrt{v_{t}}\right]-\bar{g}_{t}\bigl(1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}\bigr)\oslash\sqrt{\overline{g\odot g}_{t}}\right],

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)

\displaystyle:=-2\theta_{t}^{\top}\left[\pi(\theta_{t})-s(\theta_{t})^{-1}I_{p}\right]\bar{g}_{t},

(12)

respectively, and a one-dimensional Wiener process
W_{t}^{(r)}:=\int_{0}^{t}e_{r}(\theta_{u})^{\top}G(\theta_{u})\differential W_{u}
with
e_{r}(\theta):=\frac{\theta}{\|\theta\|_{2}}
by Lévy’s characterization:
\langle W_{t}^{(r)}\rangle_{t}=t
. Empirically, in the memorization regime, the residual sum
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)
becomes negligible. Hence, we set
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)=0
in the subsequent analysis, see Appendix
A.11
.

Theorem 2

(Scaling Law of Generalization Radius)
.

The mean squared generalization manifold radius admits the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}\;\approx\;\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}\;+\;\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\,c_{G}\;+\;O\!\left(\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

a landscape constant and

a task-determined constant independent of
\eta
and
\lambda

; a weak batch size

4.5
Solution Transition Time

Starting from
S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}
, the transition time on the trivial annulus
M\setminus G
is given as

\displaystyle\tau_{M\to G}(S_{0})\;:=\;\tau_{G}(S_{0})-\tau_{M}(S_{0}),

(16)

where
\tau_{M}(S_{0})
and
\tau_{G}(S_{0})
are the stopping times that the trajectory first hits
\partial{M}
and
\partial{G}
, respectively, defined in Sections
4.2
–
4.4
. Taking
\theta_{\tau_{M}}(S_{0})\in\partial M
as the initial condition (Theorem
1
), we formulate the transition from
\partial M
to
\partial G
as a
mean first-passage problem

(
7
)

for the radial process
r_{t}:=\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}
on the annulus
M\setminus G
. Its equilibrium distribution characterizes the equilibrium around the generalization region, while the mean first-passage time from the memorization radius to the generalization radius gives the solution transition time. Solving this first-passage problem yields Theorem
3
. The proof is provided in Appendix
A.14
. Experimental verification is provided for
S_{5}
in Figure

in Appendix

Theorem 3

(Scaling Law of Solution Transition Time)
.

The mean number of optimizer iterations between the first hit at
\partial M
and the first hit at
\partial G
admits the asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\tau_{M\to G}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\bigl[\tau_{M\to G}(S_{0})\bigr]\;\approx\;\frac{\bar{s}}{\eta\,\lambda}\,\log\!\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}\;+\;\frac{c_{\tau}}{b\,\lambda}\;+\;\frac{c_{\tau}^{(2)}}{\lambda^{2}}\;+\;O\!\left(\frac{\eta}{\lambda^{3}}\right),

(17)

where
\bar{s}
is determined by the harmonic mean of the effective deterministic gradient flow, while
c_{\tau}
and
c_{\tau}^{(2)}
collect the trajectory corrections, as defined in Appendix
A.14
. All three are task-determined constants independent of
\eta
and
\lambda
; a weak batch-size
b
-dependence enters through the scalar preconditioning scale
\bar{s}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}})
, and hence
c_{\tau}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}})
and
c_{\tau}^{(2)}=O(\frac{1}{b})
.

5
Validation

We validate the three scaling laws in Section
5.1
by directly measuring
\rho_{M}^{2}
,
\rho_{G}^{2}
, and
\tau_{M\to G}
on the group-theoretic learning task
S_{5}
and modular arithmetic tasks over
\mathbb{Z}_{127}
, and further compare them with published results from the grokking literature in Section

.

5.1
Experimental Validation

Experimental Setting.

We use a two-layer MLP: each input token is embedded into
256
dimensions, the two embeddings are concatenated, passed through a width-
512
ReLU hidden layer, and projected to
|\mathcal{Y}|
output logits, where
|\mathcal{Y}|=120
for
S_{5}
and
127
for
\mathbb{Z}_{127}
. The embedding layers are initialized from a standard normal distribution, while the linear layers use
Kaiming
initialization. Training is performed in
32
-bit floating-point precision. We find that
16
-bit precision can introduce numerical instability in grokking, suggesting that grokking dynamics are sensitive to numerical precision errors. We use base hyperparameters
\eta=10^{-3}
,
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.9,0.999)
,
\ell_{2}
regularization coefficient
\lambda=10^{-4}
, and batch size
100
. These base values are varied as needed to study scaling laws.

Learning Tasks.

We adopt two structured learning tasks: a group-theoretic learning task on
S_{n}
and a modular arithmetic learning task on
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. For
S_{n}
, each input is an ordered pair of permutations
(f,g)\in S_{n}^{2}
, and the model is trained to predict their group product
f\circ g
under permutation composition. For
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, each input is a pair
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{2}
, and the model is trained to predict the modular sum
a+b\bmod p
. In both tasks, the dataset consists of all possible input–output pairs, with a randomly sampled subset used for training and the remaining pairs used for evaluation. More details are provided in Appendix
A.1
.

Results.

Figures
2
and
4
validate the predicted scaling laws of the manifold radii
\rho_{M}^{2}
and
\rho_{G}^{2}
with respect to the learning rate
\eta
, batch size
b
, and regularization coefficient
\lambda
, as stated in Theorems
1
and
2
. Figure
5
validates the predicted scaling law of the solution transition time
\tau_{M\to G}
with respect to the same hyperparameters, as stated in Theorem
3
. Additional results on
\mathbb{Z}_{127}
are provided in Appendices
A.15
,
A.16
, and
A.17
. Overall, the experiments support the theoretical predictions; in particular,
\rho_{M}^{2}
exhibits the predicted U-shaped dependence on
\eta/b
.

Discussions and Limitations.

The scaling laws originate from how
\eta
,
b
, and
\lambda
modulate Adam’s SDE:
\eta
and
b
module the stochastic diffusion by scaling the gradient covariance with a factor
\sqrt{\eta/b}
, whereas
\lambda
modulates the deterministic gradient drift flow by regularization. Our results inherit the conditions required by Adam’s SDE limit, including an adapted Wiener-process approximation. These conditions require a sufficiently small learning rate and a sufficiently large batch size; thus, our results do not directly apply to the large-
\eta/b
regime.

5.2
Literature Validation

Theorem
1
characterizes the scaling of the memorization radius
\rho_{M}
with respect to
\eta/b
, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been derived in prior work. Theorem
2
derives the scaling law of the generalization radius
\rho_{G}
, showing that it decomposes into the landscape-dependent term
\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta_{0}\in\Theta}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(\theta_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right]
and an
O(\eta/(\sqrt{b}\lambda))
fluctuation correction; this is consistent with the weight-norm “Goldilocks zone” reported in prior studies
(
12
;
21
)
. Theorem
3
recovers the inverse dependence of the grokking delay on the regularization coefficient, including the scaling
\tau\propto 1/(\eta\lambda)
observed or implied in prior empirical and mechanistic studies
(
12
)
. Together, these results show that the proposed stopping-time analysis recovers known scaling behavior while refining it into separate contributions from learning rate, batch size, and regularization coefficient.

6
Conclusions

Optimization dynamics induce a topological configuration of solution manifolds underlying grokking. In this work, we characterize this configuration through a shell–core prior of the reachable solution space, in which training first reaches memorization solutions and then transitions, after a prolonged delay, toward generalization solutions. We derive scaling laws for the manifold radii and the solution transition time with respect to the learning rate, batch size, and regularization coefficient through stopping-time and equilibrium-distribution analyses of Adam’s joint-state SDE. These results connect the geometry of solution manifolds with the stochastic dynamics of Adam and provide a theoretical framework for understanding grokking through optimization dynamics.

Proof: Concentration of Normal Initialization

Lemma 3

(Gaussian Initialization Concentration)
.

Let

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=\left(\theta_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\theta_{0}^{(k)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\qquad\theta_{0}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}=p,

(18)

where the subvectors are independent and

[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{j}^{2})
. Define the effective
coordinate variance
\tilde{\sigma}^{2}
and the effective dimension parameter

\tilde{p}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}^{2}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2},\qquad\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}.

(19)

Then the squared initialization radius satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}p,\qquad\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=2\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{4}.

(20)

In particular, for initialization schemes used for numerical stability, each
block-wise coordinate scale is often chosen so that

\sigma_{j}=O(p_{j}^{-1/2})
. Under this scaling,

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{k}{p}\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=O(k),\qquad\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=O\!\left(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}\right).

(21)

Proof.

We consider a Gaussian initialization with block-wise coordinate scales. Write

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=\left(\theta_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\theta_{0}^{(k)}\right),\qquad\theta_{0}^{(j)}=\sigma_{j}Z^{(j)},\qquad Z^{(j)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p_{j}}),

(22)

where the subvectors
Z^{(j)}
are independent. Then

\displaystyle\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|\theta_{0}^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}\|Z^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2},

(23)

and it suffices to study the distribution of each
\|Z^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}
. Let

\displaystyle R_{j}:=\|Z^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}.

(24)

The random variable
R_{j}
follows a chi-square distribution with
p_{j}
degrees of freedom,
whose density is

\displaystyle f_{R_{j}}(r)=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)}r^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}-1}e^{-r/2},\qquad r\geq 0.

(25)

Computing
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]
.

The expected squared Euclidean norm of the initialization is therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}[R_{j}]

(26)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}rf_{R_{j}}(r)\,\differential r

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\sigma_{j}^{2}}{2^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}}e^{-r/2}\,\differential r.

(28)

Applying the change of variables
u=r/2
yields

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}}e^{-r/2}\,\differential r=2^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1\right).

(29)

Substituting back, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}2\sigma_{j}^{2}\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)}

(30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}p.

(32)

Computing
\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]
.

We next compute the fluctuations of the squared initialization radius. Since the
subvectors are independent and

\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}R_{j}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{4}\mathrm{Var}[R_{j}].

(33)

To compute
\mathrm{Var}[R_{j}]
, we first compute the second moment:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{j}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{2}f_{R_{j}}(r)\,\differential r

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1}e^{-r/2}\,\differential r.

(35)

Applying again the change of variables
u=r/2
gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1}e^{-r/2}\,\differential r=2^{\frac{p_{j}}{2}+2}\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}+2\right).

(36)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{j}^{2}]

\displaystyle=4\frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}+2\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)}

(37)

\displaystyle=4\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=p_{j}(p_{j}+2).

(39)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[R_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[R_{j}^{2}]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[R_{j}]\bigr)^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle=p_{j}(p_{j}+2)-p_{j}^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle=2p_{j}.

(42)

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{4}\mathrm{Var}[R_{j}]

(43)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{4}.

(44)

Coordinate Scaling.

Finally, in common initialization schemes used for numerical stability, each
block-wise per-coordinate variance is scaled inversely with its block dimension.
Equivalently, one may write

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{j}}}\right),\qquad\sigma_{j}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{p_{j}}\right).

(45)

Substituting this scaling into the definition of the effective coordinate variance gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{k}{p}\right).

(46)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}=\tilde{\sigma}^{2}p=O(k),

(47)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\sigma_{j}^{4}=O\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}\right).

(48)

Using the definition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}},

(49)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\bigl[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=O\!\left(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}\right).

(50)

∎

A.4
Proof: Concentration of Uniform Initialization

Lemma 4

(Uniform Initialization Concentration)
.

Let

\displaystyle\theta_{0}=\left(\theta_{0}^{(1)},\ldots,\theta_{0}^{(k)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\qquad\theta_{0}^{(j)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{j}},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}=p,

(51)

where the subvectors are independent and the coordinates of each subvector satisfy

[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
. Define the
effective coordinate variance
\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}
and the effective dimension
parameter
\tilde{p}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}:=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2},\qquad\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}:=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}.

(52)

Then the squared initialization radius satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}]=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}p}{3},\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2})=\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}.

(53)

Moreover, by the central limit theorem,

\displaystyle\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\;\overset{d}{\approx}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}p}{3},\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}\right),\qquad p\to\infty.

(54)

In particular, for initialization schemes with block-wise coordinate half-widths

\varepsilon_{j}=O(p_{j}^{-1/2})
, the squared initialization radius satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{k}{p}\right),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}]=O(k),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2})=O\!\left(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}\right).

(55)

For example, a common numerically stable block-wise choice is

\varepsilon_{j}=p_{j}^{-1/2}
, in which case

\displaystyle\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\;\overset{d}{\approx}\;\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{k}{3},\frac{4k}{45\tilde{p}}\right).

(56)

Thus uniform initialization concentrates on a thin hyperspherical shell with
squared radius
k/3
and squared-radius variance
4k/(45\tilde{p})
.

Proof.

For each coordinate
X_{i}^{(j)}:=[\theta_{0}^{(j)}]_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j})
,
the density of
X_{i}^{(j)}
is
f_{X_{i}^{(j)}}(x)=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{j}}
for

x\in[-\varepsilon_{j},\varepsilon_{j}]
. Then the second moment is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{2}]=\int_{-\varepsilon_{j}}^{\varepsilon_{j}}x^{2}f_{X_{i}^{(j)}}(x)\,\differential x=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{j}}\int_{-\varepsilon_{j}}^{\varepsilon_{j}}x^{2}\,\differential x=\frac{\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}{3}.

(57)

The fourth moment is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{4}]=\int_{-\varepsilon_{j}}^{\varepsilon_{j}}x^{4}f_{X_{i}^{(j)}}(x)\,\differential x=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon_{j}}\int_{-\varepsilon_{j}}^{\varepsilon_{j}}x^{4}\,\differential x=\frac{\varepsilon_{j}^{4}}{5}.

(58)

Defining
S_{i}^{(j)}:=(X_{i}^{(j)})^{2}
, we have

\mathbb{E}[S_{i}^{(j)}]=\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{2}]
and

\mathbb{E}[(S_{i}^{(j)})^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{4}]
, so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}((X_{i}^{(j)})^{2})=\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}^{(j)})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(S_{i}^{(j)})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[S_{i}^{(j)}]^{2}

(59)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{4}]-\bigl(\mathbb{E}[(X_{i}^{(j)})^{2}]\bigr)^{2}

(60)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon_{j}^{4}}{5}-\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{j}^{2}}{3}\right)^{2}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{4\varepsilon_{j}^{4}}{45}.

(62)

Now set

\displaystyle R^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{p_{j}}(X_{i}^{(j)})^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{p_{j}}S_{i}^{(j)}.

(63)

Since the random variables
S_{i}^{(j)}
are independent, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\,\mathbb{E}[S_{i}^{(j)}]=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}p}{3},

(64)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(R^{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\,\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}^{(j)})=\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}.

(65)

Moreover, by the central limit theorem,

\displaystyle R^{2}\overset{d}{\approx}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\,\mathbb{E}[S_{i}^{(j)}],\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\,\mathrm{Var}(S_{i}^{(j)})\right)=\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}p}{3},\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}\right),\qquad p\to\infty.

(66)

For numerically stable uniform initialization, the coordinate half-width of each block is often
chosen to scale as

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{j}}}\right).

(67)

Substituting this scaling into the preceding identities gives

\displaystyle\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{2}=O\!\left(\frac{k}{p}\right),

(68)

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}]=\frac{\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}p}{3}=O(k),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2})=\frac{4}{45}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p_{j}\varepsilon_{j}^{4}=O\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}}\right).

(69)

Using the definition

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{p}}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{j}},

(70)

we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2})=O\!\left(\frac{k}{\tilde{p}}\right).

(71)

For example, taking the common block-wise choice

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{j}}},

(72)

we obtain

\displaystyle R^{2}\overset{d}{\approx}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{k}{3},\frac{4k}{45\tilde{p}}\right).

(73)

∎

(102)

respectively. For a batch
\xi_{k}:=\{s_{i}\}_{i=1}^{b}
of size
b
, the gradient at time
k
is given by:

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}g_{k}{(s_{i})},

(103)

by central limit theorem, so that
g_{k}
distributionally converge to

\displaystyle g_{k}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bar{g}_{k},\frac{1}{b}\Sigma_{k}\right),

(104)

as batch size is sufficiently large
b\to\infty
. We therefore can model mini-batch gradient
g_{k}
as a Wiener process

\displaystyle g_{k}=\bar{g}_{k}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\Sigma_{k}^{1/2}\zeta_{k},\qquad(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2})(\Sigma_{k}^{1/2})^{\top}=\Sigma_{k},

(105)

where
\zeta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})
is Wiener process adapted to the filtration generated from mini-batch sampling sequence
\{\xi_{k}\}
. Because gradient descent uses negative gradient, we take a negative sign on
\zeta_{k}
for simplifying the later analysis algebraically.

For the adapted noise vector
\zeta_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
at iteration
k
(time
t=k\eta
) corresponds to the Wiener increment over
[t,t+\eta]
:

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\zeta_{k}\sqrt{\eta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\zeta_{k}\sqrt{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\eta}}\Delta W_{k},

(106)

where
\Delta t=\eta
and the increment follows the property of Brownian motion:

\displaystyle\Delta W_{k}=\zeta_{k}\sqrt{\Delta t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Delta t\;I_{p}).

(107)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t):=-2B(t)\theta_{t}^{\top}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[m_{t}\oslash\sqrt{v_{t}}\right]-\bar{g}_{t}\bigl(1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}\bigr)\oslash\sqrt{\overline{g\odot g}_{t}}\right].

(267)

Combining equations (

)–(

), the exact induced squared-radius has a drift

\displaystyle\left[-2a(t)\theta_{t}^{\top}\pi(\theta_{t})\bar{g}_{t}+\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)\right]\differential t.

(268)

Effective Diffusion.

Finally, in the reduced preconditioned diffusion approximation, the mini-batch gradient fluctuations induce the
\theta
-diffusion term

(270)

This proves equation (
249
).

A.10
Proof: Memorization-Regime Preconditioner and Effective Diffusion Identities

The validation for Lemma
10
is provided in Figure
9
. The experiment shows that, in the memorization regime, the approximated identities

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{c}\pi(\theta_{t}):=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\right)^{-1/2}\\

hold in the memorization regime.

Lemma 10

(Memorization-Regime Preconditioner and Effective Diffusion Identities)
.

Let

\displaystyle\pi(\theta_{t}):=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\right)^{-1/2},

(274)

and define

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}B(t)\pi(\theta_{t})\Sigma_{t}^{1/2},\qquad B(t):=\frac{\sqrt{1-e^{-\alpha_{2}t}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}}.

(275)

Assume that, in the memorization regime,

\displaystyle\overline{g\odot g}_{t}=\bar{g}_{t}\odot\bar{g}_{t}+\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\approx\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t}),

(276)

and that the diagonal Adam preconditioner admits the trace-matched scalar approximation

\displaystyle\pi(\theta_{t})\approx s(\theta_{t})^{-1}I_{p},\qquad\frac{1}{s(\theta)}:=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\pi(\theta)\bigr).

(277)

Equivalently, under the corresponding scalar covariance approximation,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\Sigma_{t}\approx s(\theta_{t})^{2}I_{p}.

(278)

Then the following two late-stage identities hold:

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\pi(\theta_{t}))

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{b}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\bigl[\left(\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\right)^{-1/2}\bigr],

(279)

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\!\left(G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle\approx p.

(280)

Moreover,

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(281)

Proof.

From equation (
276
),

\displaystyle\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\approx\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t}).

(282)

Therefore

\displaystyle\pi(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}_{t}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\frac{1}{b}\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b}\,\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\right)^{-1/2}.

(283)

Taking traces gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\pi(\theta_{t}))

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{b}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\bigl[\left(\mathrm{diag}(\Sigma_{t})\right)^{-1/2}\bigr].

(284)

This proves equation (
279
).

Next, by definition,

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}B(t)\pi(\theta_{t})\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}.

(285)

Hence

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b}\pi(\theta_{t})\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\left(\Sigma_{t}^{1/2}\right)^{\top}\pi(\theta_{t})^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b}\pi(\theta_{t})\Sigma_{t}\pi(\theta_{t})^{\top}.

(286)

Since
\pi(\theta_{t})
is diagonal, it is symmetric. Thus

\displaystyle\pi(\theta_{t})^{\top}=\pi(\theta_{t}),

(287)

and equation (
286
) becomes

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}=\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b}\pi(\theta_{t})\Sigma_{t}\pi(\theta_{t}).

(288)

Using the trace-matched scalar approximation

\displaystyle\pi(\theta_{t})\approx s(\theta_{t})^{-1}I_{p},

(289)

we obtain

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b}\left(s(\theta_{t})^{-1}I_{p}\right)\Sigma_{t}\left(s(\theta_{t})^{-1}I_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b\,s(\theta_{t})^{2}}\Sigma_{t}.

(290)

By

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\Sigma_{t}\approx s(\theta_{t})^{2}I_{p},

(291)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t}\approx b\,s(\theta_{t})^{2}I_{p}.

(292)

Substituting equation (
292
) into equation (
290
) gives

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{B(t)^{2}}{b\,s(\theta_{t})^{2}}b\,s(\theta_{t})^{2}I_{p}

\displaystyle=B(t)^{2}I_{p}.

(293)

Finally,

\displaystyle B(t)^{2}=\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{2}t}}{\left(1-e^{-\alpha_{1}t}\right)^{2}}\to 1\qquad(t\to\infty).

(294)

Therefore,

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(295)

Taking traces yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\!\left(G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\right)\approx\mathrm{tr}(I_{p})=p.

(296)

This proves equations (
280
) and (
281
).
∎

Learning rate
\eta
and batch size
b
modulate infinitesimal generator

Remark 11
.

The structure of the infinitesimal generator
\mathcal{L}_{S}f(S)
shows that
this operator contains a term modulated by the learning rate
\eta
and the
batch size
b
through the coefficient
\eta/b
. This motivates us to treat

\mathcal{L}_{S}f(S)
as an operator expansion with respect to the coefficient

\eta/b
, so that we can apply a regular perturbation expansion to study the
corresponding expansion of the memorization radius with respect to
\eta/b
.

Dirichlet Boundary PDE.

Since
u(S)
is the expected boundary value of the process stopped at
\partial M
, it solves the Dirichlet boundary value problem
(
23
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}u(S)

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\theta\in M^{\circ},

(344)

\displaystyle u(S)

\displaystyle=S^{\top}E_{\theta}S,\qquad

\displaystyle\theta\in\partial M.

(345)

Here equation (
344
) states that
u(S_{t})
is harmonic with respect to the stopped Adam diffusion before hitting the boundary, while equation (
345
) assigns the squared radius as the boundary payoff.

Let
\varepsilon:=\eta/b
. We split the generator into its deterministic and stochastic parts:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}=\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}f:=\mu^{\top}\nabla_{S}f,\qquad\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}f:=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{S}\nabla_{S}^{2}f\right).

(346)

Thus the Dirichlet PDE becomes

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}\right)u(S)=0,\qquad\theta\in M^{\circ},

(347)

with boundary condition

\displaystyle u(S)=S^{\top}E_{\theta}S,\qquad\theta\in\partial M.

(348)

Solving PDE via Regular Perturbation Expansion.

We solve this PDE by a regular perturbation expansion in the small parameter
\varepsilon
. Write

\displaystyle u(S)=u_{0}(S)+\varepsilon u_{1}(S)+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}(S)+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(349)

Substituting equation (
349
) into equation (
347
) gives

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}\right)\left(u_{0}+\varepsilon u_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{0}+\varepsilon\left(\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{0}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{2}+\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{1}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(350)

Since this identity must hold for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, each coefficient of
\varepsilon
must vanish. Therefore,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{0}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{0}=0,

(351)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{1}=-\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{0},

(352)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}:\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}u_{2}=-\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{1}.

(353)

The boundary condition is expanded in the same way:

\displaystyle u_{0}+\varepsilon u_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}+O(\varepsilon^{3})=S^{\top}E_{\theta}S,\qquad\theta\in\partial M.

(354)

Matching powers of
\varepsilon
on the boundary gives

\displaystyle u_{0}\big|_{\theta\in\partial M}

\displaystyle=S^{\top}E_{\theta}S,

(355)

\displaystyle u_{1}\big|_{\theta\in\partial M}

\displaystyle=0,

(356)

\displaystyle u_{2}\big|_{\theta\in\partial M}

\displaystyle=0.

(357)

Zero-Order Expansion.

We now solve these equations by characteristics. The characteristic curves of
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}=\mu^{\top}\nabla_{S}
are the deterministic Adam trajectories

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}\Phi_{t}(S_{0})=\mu(\Phi_{t}(S_{0})),\qquad\Phi_{0}(S_{0})=S_{0},

(358)

so that

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-m^{(0)}\oslash\sqrt{v^{(0)}},

\displaystyle\dot{m}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{1}\bigl(m^{(0)}-\bar{g}(\theta^{(0)})\bigr),

\displaystyle\dot{v}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{2}\bigl(v^{(0)}-\overline{g\odot g}(\theta^{(0)})\bigr).

(359)

For any smooth function
w
, along the deterministic flow we have

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}w(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))=\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(0)}w(\Phi_{t}(S_{0})).

(360)

Let
\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})
be the deterministic hitting time

\displaystyle\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0}):=\inf\{t\geq 0:(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))_{\theta}\in\partial M\}.

(361)

At order
\varepsilon^{0}
, equation (
351
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}u_{0}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))=0.

(362)

Thus
u_{0}
is constant along the deterministic trajectory. Evaluating it at the deterministic hitting time and using the boundary condition in equation (
355
), we obtain

\displaystyle u_{0}(S_{0})

\displaystyle=u_{0}(\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0}))

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0})^{\top}E_{\theta}\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0})

\displaystyle=\left\|\bigl(\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0})\bigr)_{\theta}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(363)

Define the deterministic exit-radius-squared map

\displaystyle R^{2}(S_{0}):=\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0})^{\top}E_{\theta}\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0}).

(364)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle R^{2}(S_{0})=\left\|\bigl(\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0})\bigr)_{\theta}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(365)

Then
u_{0}(S_{0})=R^{2}(S_{0})
.

First-Order Expansion.

At order
\varepsilon^{1}
, equation (
352
) and the transport identity give

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}u_{1}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))=-\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{0}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0})).

(366)

Integrating from
0
to
\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})
gives

\displaystyle u_{1}(\Phi_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}(S_{0}))-u_{1}(S_{0})=-\int_{0}^{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{0}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\,\differential t.

(367)

Since
u_{1}=0
on
\partial M
, the first term on the left vanishes. Therefore,

\displaystyle u_{1}(S_{0})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{0}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\,\differential t.

(368)

Using the definition of
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}
and
u_{0}=R^{2}
along the deterministic flow,

\displaystyle u_{1}(S_{0})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{S}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\nabla_{S}^{2}R^{2}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\right)\,\differential t.

(369)

Second-Order Expansion.

At order
\varepsilon^{2}
, the same argument gives

\displaystyle\frac{\differential}{\differential t}u_{2}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))=-\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{1}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0})).

(370)

Since
u_{2}=0
on
\partial M
, integrating along the deterministic characteristic yields

\displaystyle u_{2}(S_{0})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{(1)}u_{1}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\,\differential t.

(371)

Thus,

\displaystyle u_{2}(S_{0})=\int_{0}^{\tau_{M}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\Sigma_{S}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\nabla_{S}^{2}u_{1}(\Phi_{t}(S_{0}))\right)\,\differential t.

(372)

Produce Claims.

Combining the three orders, we have

\displaystyle u(S_{0})=R^{2}(S_{0})+\varepsilon u_{1}(S_{0})+\varepsilon^{2}u_{2}(S_{0})+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(373)

Finally, the mean squared memorization radius is obtained by averaging this exit-value function over the initialization distribution:

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\theta_{\tau_{M}}\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[S_{\tau_{M}}^{\top}E_{\theta}S_{\tau_{M}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[u(S_{0})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[R^{2}(S_{0})\right]+\varepsilon\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[u_{1}(S_{0})\right]+\varepsilon^{2}\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[u_{2}(S_{0})\right]+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(374)

Define

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{(0)\,2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[R^{2}(S_{0})\right],

\displaystyle c_{M}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[u_{1}(S_{0})\right],

\displaystyle c_{M}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\sim\Theta_{S}}\!\left[u_{2}(S_{0})\right].

(375)

Substituting these definitions into equation (
374
) gives

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}=\rho_{M}^{(0)\,2}+\varepsilon c_{M}+\varepsilon^{2}c_{M}^{(2)}+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(376)

Since
\varepsilon=\eta/b
, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}=\rho_{M}^{(0)\,2}+(\eta/b)c_{M}+(\eta/b)^{2}c_{M}^{(2)}+O\bigl((\eta/b)^{3}\bigr),

(377)

which proves the theorem.
∎

The leading constant

is determined by the deterministic

\displaystyle\nabla\mathscr{L}_{f}^{*}(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\approx 0,

(378)

the deterministic flow is dominated by the
\ell_{2}
drift:

\displaystyle\differential\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\approx-\lambda\theta_{t}\,\differential t.

(379)

Hence

\displaystyle\differential\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\theta_{t}^{\top}\differential\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\approx-2\lambda\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\,\differential t.

(380)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\differential\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx-2\lambda\,\differential t.

(381)

Integrating along the deterministic memorization path gives

\displaystyle\|\theta_{\tau_{M}^{(0)}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\|\theta_{0}\|_{2}^{2}\exp\!\left(-2\lambda\tau_{M}^{(0)}\right).

(382)

Therefore,

\displaystyle(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(-O(\lambda\tau_{M}^{(0)})\right).

(383)

When
\tau_{M}^{(0)}
is weakly dependent on
\lambda
in the considered regime,

\displaystyle(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(-O(\lambda)\right).

(384)

Since

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}=(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}+O(\eta),

(385)

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\!\left(-O(\lambda)\right).

Proof: Scaling Law of Generalization Radius

Theorem 5

(Scaling Law of Generalization Radius, restated)
.

We do not assume a global minizer, for each initialization
S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}
, let

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(S_{0}):=\arg\min_{\theta(S_{0})}\left\{\mathscr{L}^{*}_{f}(\theta(S_{0}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}\in G

(387)

denote the regularized local minimizer selected by the trajectory starting from initial state
S_{0}
, and
\mathscr{L}^{*}_{f}(\theta(S_{0}))
is the task loss. The trajectory
\{S_{t}\}
will be confined in the basin centered at
\theta^{\star}(S_{0})
. Define

\displaystyle\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right].

(388)

Let

\displaystyle\tau_{G}(S_{0}):=\inf\{t\geq 0:\theta_{t}(S_{0})\in\partial G\},

(389)

and define the generalization radius by

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\|\theta_{\tau_{G}(S_{0})}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]\right].

(390)

Let

\displaystyle s^{\star}(S_{0}):=s(\theta^{\star}(S_{0})),\qquad D^{\star}(S_{0}):=G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))^{\top},

(391)

where the effective scalar preconditioner at the selected minimizer is defined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s^{\star}(S_{0})}=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\pi(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))\bigr),\qquad\pi(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))=\mathrm{diag}\!\left(\overline{g\odot g}(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))\right)^{-1/2}.

(392)

Then, generalization radius approximately admits,

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}\approx\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\,c_{G}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

(393)

where

\displaystyle c_{G}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\tfrac{1}{2}s^{\star}(S_{0})\,\mathrm{tr}\!\left(D^{\star}(S_{0})\right)\right]>0.

(394)

Proof.

For a trajectory
\{S_{t}\}
starting from
S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}
, write

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}

\displaystyle:=\theta^{\star}(S_{0}),

\displaystyle\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\theta^{\star}+\delta_{t}.

(395)

By definition of the local minimizer,

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})+\lambda\theta^{\star}=0,

(396)

where
\bar{g}^{*}(\bullet)
denotes the task loss mean. By Lemma
11
(
Lemma 11

(Reduced Late-Stage Radius SDE with Slow-Manifold, Preconditioned Residual (Restated))
.
), together with Lemma
10
(
Lemma 10

(Memorization-Regime Preconditioner and Effective Diffusion Identities)
.
),

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p},

(397)

and the residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)\approx 0,

(398)

the reduced late-stage squared-radius SDE gives

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}^{2}\approx\left[-\frac{2}{s(\theta_{t})}\theta_{t}^{\top}\left(\bar{g}^{*}_{t}+\lambda\theta_{t}\right)+\eta p\right]\differential t+2\sqrt{\eta}\,r_{t}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)},

(399)

where

\displaystyle r_{t}^{2}=\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(400)

Linearization Near Local Minizer.

The Taylor expansion of
\bar{g}^{*}
around
\theta^{\star}
gives

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{*}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star}+\delta_{t})

\displaystyle=\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})+\nabla\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})\delta_{t}+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(401)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{*}_{t}+\lambda\theta_{t}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})+\nabla\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})\delta_{t}+\lambda\theta^{\star}+\lambda\delta_{t}+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right)

(402)

where equation (
396
) was used. Under the dominant-regularization approximation,

\displaystyle\nabla\bar{g}^{*}(\theta^{\star})+\lambda I_{p}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda I_{p},

(403)

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{*}_{t}+\lambda\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\approx\lambda\delta_{t}+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(404)

Using
\theta_{t}=\theta^{\star}+\delta_{t}
,

\displaystyle\theta_{t}^{\top}\left(\bar{g}^{*}_{t}+\lambda\theta_{t}\right)

\displaystyle\approx(\theta^{\star}+\delta_{t})^{\top}\left[\lambda\delta_{t}+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda\theta^{\star\top}\delta_{t}+\lambda\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(405)

Local Stationary Approximation.

Under the local stationary approximation,

\displaystyle s(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle=s^{\star}(S_{0})+O\!\left(\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}\right),

\displaystyle s^{\star}(S_{0})

\displaystyle:=s(\theta^{\star}(S_{0})).

(406)

Taking conditional expectation in equation (
399

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{2}{s^{\star}(S_{0})}\mathbb{E}\left[\theta_{t}^{\top}\left(\bar{g}^{*}_{t}+\lambda\theta_{t}\right)\mid S_{0}\right]+\eta p+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

(407)

since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[r_{t}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)}\mid S_{0}\right]=0.

(408)

The local stationary fluctuation is centered, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\delta_{t}\mid S_{0}]

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\theta^{\star\top}\delta_{t}\mid S_{0}]

\displaystyle=0.

(409)

Combining equations (
405
),
(
407
), and
(
409
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{2\lambda}{s^{\star}(S_{0})}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]+\eta p+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(410)

Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]\approx\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(411)

Also,

\displaystyle\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\theta^{\star}+\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\theta^{\star}\|_{2}^{2}+2\theta^{\star\top}\delta_{t}+\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.

(412)

Taking conditional expectation gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\mathbb{E}\left[\|\delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(413)

Solving Generalization Radius.

Under the local stationary approximation in the generalization basin,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\theta_{\tau_{G}(S_{0})}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\theta_{\tau_{G}(S_{0})}\|_{2}^{2}\mid S_{0}\right]

Averaging over
S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right]+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p\right]+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(416)

By definition,

\displaystyle\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(417)

\displaystyle c_{G}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p\right].

(418)

Thus,

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}\approx\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}c_{G}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

(419)

which proves the claimed scaling law.

Weak Batch-Size
b
-Dependence of

.

The weak batch-size dependence of
c_{G}
enters through the effective scalar
preconditioner
s^{\star}(S_{0})
. From the definition of

,

\displaystyle c_{G}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p\right].

(420)

By the late-stage preconditioning identity,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s^{\star}(S_{0})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\left(\pi(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))\right),

(421)

\displaystyle\pi(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{diag}\left(\overline{g\odot g}(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))\right)^{-1/2}.

(422)

In the memorization regime,

\displaystyle\overline{g\odot g}(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))

(423)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\pi(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s^{\star}(S_{0})}

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{b}),

\displaystyle=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\right).

Using the late-stage identity

, the diffusion trace

. Hence

\displaystyle c_{G}=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p\right]=O\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}}\right).

(427)

Proof: Scaling Law of Solution Transition Time

Theorem 6

(Scaling Law of Solution Transition Time, restated)
.

Assume the reduced late-stage radius SDE in
Lemma
11
(
Lemma 11

(Reduced Late-Stage Radius SDE with Slow-Manifold, Preconditioned Residual (Restated))
.
), with conditions in Lemma
10
(
Lemma 10

(Memorization-Regime Preconditioner and Effective Diffusion Identities)
.
)

\displaystyle G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p},

(428)

and the residual approximation

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)\approx 0.

(429)

Let
\bar{g}(\theta)
be the population mean gradient and let

s(\theta)>0
denote the scalar preconditioning factor satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s(\theta)}=\frac{1}{p}\mathrm{tr}\bigl(\pi(\theta)\bigr).

(430)

Let
\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})
be the deterministic late-stage trajectory from

\partial M
to
\partial G
starting with initial state
S_{0}
, and let
\tau_{M\to G}^{(0)}(S_{0})
be its
deterministic transition time.
For each deterministic late-stage trajectory

\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})
from
\partial M
to
\partial G
, define its pathwise
effective radial contraction rate by

\displaystyle\kappa(S_{0}):=\frac{1}{\tau_{M\to G}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\int_{0}^{\tau_{M\to G}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\left[\frac{\lambda}{s(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))}+\frac{\theta_{t}^{(0)\top}(S_{0})\bigl(\bar{g}(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))-\lambda\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})\bigr)}{s(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))\|\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\differential t.

(431)

The scalar
\bar{s}
is defined through the ensemble-averaged effective
contraction rate

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\kappa(S_{0})\right].

(432)

Let
\rho_{M}
and
\rho_{G}
be the memorization and generalization radii.
Assume the asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}+\frac{\eta}{b}c_{M}+O\bigl((\eta/b)^{2}\bigr),

(433)

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}c_{G}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

(434)

where
c_{M}
is the memorization-radius correction from
Theorem
4
, and

\displaystyle\bigl(\rho_{G}^{(0)}\bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(435)

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(S_{0})

\displaystyle:=\arg\min_{\theta(S_{0})}\left\{\mathscr{L}^{*}_{f}(\theta(S_{0}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right\},

(436)

\displaystyle c_{G}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}\mathrm{tr}\!\left(D^{\star}(S_{0})\right)\right],

(437)

\displaystyle s^{\star}(S_{0})

\displaystyle:=s(\theta^{\star}(S_{0})),\qquad D^{\star}(S_{0}):=G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))^{\top}.

(438)

Under the late-stage identity

G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))G(\theta^{\star}(S_{0}))^{\top}\approx I_{p}
,
equation (
437
) reduces to

\displaystyle c_{G}=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\frac{s^{\star}(S_{0})}{2}p\right].

(439)

Then, in iteration time, the expected transition time from
\partial M
to

\partial G
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\bigl[\tau_{M\to G}\bigr]\approx\frac{\bar{s}}{\eta\,\lambda}\log\!\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}+\frac{c_{\tau}}{b\,\lambda}+\frac{c_{\tau}^{(2)}}{\lambda^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta}{\lambda^{3}}\right),

(440)

where

\displaystyle c_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\bar{s}\,\frac{c_{M}}{2(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}},

(441)

\displaystyle c_{\tau}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=-\bar{s}\,\frac{c_{G}}{2(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}+\frac{\bar{s}^{2}(p-2)}{4}\left[\frac{1}{(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}\right].

(442)

The constants
\bar{s}
,
c_{\tau}
, and
c_{\tau}^{(2)}
are independent of

\eta
and
\lambda
in the considered scaling regime; a weak batch-size
b
-dependence enters through the scalar preconditioning scale
\bar{s}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}})
, and hence
c_{\tau}=O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{b}})
and
c_{\tau}^{(2)}=O(\frac{1}{b})
.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle r_{t}:=\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}.

(443)

By Lemma
11
(
Lemma 11

(Reduced Late-Stage Radius SDE with Slow-Manifold, Preconditioned Residual (Restated))
.
), under the late-stage observations

\displaystyle a(t)\to 1,\qquad G(\theta_{t})G(\theta_{t})^{\top}\approx I_{p},\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SM}}(t)+\mathcal{R}_{\pi}(t)\approx 0,

(444)

the squared-radius process satisfies

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}^{2}\approx\left[-\frac{2\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}r_{t}^{2}-\frac{2}{s(\theta_{t})}\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)+\eta\,p\right]\differential t+2\sqrt{\eta}\,r_{t}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)}.

(445)

Effective Deterministic Radius Flow.

The leading deterministic radial flow is obtained from
equation (
445
) by keeping the

O(1)
drift terms and dropping the
O(\eta)
Itô correction. Thus,

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}^{2}

\displaystyle\approx\left[-\frac{2\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}r_{t}^{2}-\frac{2}{s(\theta_{t})}\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)\right]\differential t.

(446)

Since

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}^{2}=2r_{t}\,\differential r_{t}+O(\eta)\differential t,

(447)

the leading deterministic radial drift is

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{t}

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}r_{t}-\frac{1}{s(\theta_{t})r_{t}}\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)

\displaystyle=-\left[\frac{\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}+\frac{\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)}{s(\theta_{t})\|\theta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}}\right]r_{t}.

(448)

For each deterministic late-stage trajectory

\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})
from
\partial M
to
\partial G
, define its pathwise
effective radial contraction rate by

\displaystyle\kappa(S_{0}):=\frac{1}{\tau_{M\to G}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\int_{0}^{\tau_{M\to G}^{(0)}(S_{0})}\left[\frac{\lambda}{s(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))}+\frac{\theta_{t}^{(0)\top}(S_{0})\bigl(\bar{g}(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))-\lambda\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})\bigr)}{s(\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0}))\|\theta_{t}^{(0)}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}}\right]\differential t.

(449)

The scalar
\bar{s}
is defined through the ensemble-averaged effective
contraction rate

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}:=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\kappa(S_{0})\right].

(450)

Thus, in the averaged effective radial model, the leading deterministic
late-stage radius flow is

\displaystyle\dot{r}_{t}^{(0)}=-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}r_{t}^{(0)}.

(451)

Therefore, along the averaged late-stage transition dynamics,

\displaystyle-\frac{\lambda}{s(\theta_{t})}r_{t}-\frac{1}{s(\theta_{t})r_{t}}\theta_{t}^{\top}\bigl(\bar{g}_{t}-\lambda\theta_{t}\bigr)\approx-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}r_{t}.

(452)

Effective Radius SDE.

We now pass from the squared-radius SDE to the radius SDE. Applying Itô’s
lemma to
r_{t}=(r_{t}^{2})^{1/2}
gives

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}=\frac{1}{2r_{t}}\differential r_{t}^{2}-\frac{1}{8r_{t}^{3}}\differential\langle r^{2}\rangle_{t}.

(453)

From the martingale term in equation (
445
),

\displaystyle\differential\langle r^{2}\rangle_{t}=4\eta r_{t}^{2}\,\differential t.

(454)

Substituting equations (
445
)
and (
454
) into
equation (
453
), and using
equation (
452
), yields

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}r_{t}+\eta\frac{p}{2r_{t}}-\eta\frac{1}{2r_{t}}\right]\differential t+\sqrt{\eta}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\left[-\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}}r_{t}+\eta\frac{p-1}{2r_{t}}\right]\differential t+\sqrt{\eta}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)}.

(455)

Let

\displaystyle\kappa:=\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}},\qquad\varepsilon:=\eta.

(456)

Then equation (
455
) becomes

\displaystyle\differential r_{t}=\left[-\kappa r_{t}+\frac{\varepsilon(p-1)}{2r_{t}}\right]\differential t+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,\differential W_{t}^{(r)}.

(457)

Mean First-Passage Time PDE.

Let
T(r)
be the expected continuous time for the process in
equation (
457
), initialized at radius
r
, to hit
the absorbing boundary
r_{G}
, defined as

\displaystyle T(r):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tau_{G}\mid r_{0}=r\right],\qquad r>r_{G}.

(458)

The transition starts at the memorization boundary and ends at the generalization boundary:

\displaystyle r_{0}

\displaystyle:=\rho_{M},

\displaystyle r_{G}

\displaystyle:=\rho_{G}.

(459)

Its infinitesimal generator is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}[\bullet]=\left(-\kappa r+\frac{\varepsilon(p-1)}{2r}\right)\frac{\differential}{\differential r}[\bullet]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{\differential^{2}}{\differential r^{2}}[\bullet].

(460)

Then
T(r)
solves a Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{r}T(r)=-1,\qquad T(r_{G})=0.

(461)

Solving Absorbing Boundary Dirichlet PDE.

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}T^{\prime\prime}(r)+\left(-\kappa r+\frac{\varepsilon(p-1)}{2r}\right)T^{\prime}(r)=-1.

(462)

Writing

\displaystyle q(r):=T^{\prime}(r),

(463)

we obtain

\displaystyle q^{\prime}(r)+\left(-\frac{2\kappa}{\varepsilon}r+\frac{p-1}{r}\right)q(r)=-\frac{2}{\varepsilon}.

(464)

The integrating factor is

\displaystyle I(r)=r^{p-1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon}r^{2}\right).

(465)

Using

\displaystyle I(r)q(r)\to 0\qquad\text{as }r\to\infty,

(466)

we obtain

\displaystyle q(r)=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\frac{1}{I(r)}\int_{r}^{\infty}I(y)\differential y.

(467)

Since
T(r_{G})=0
, for an initial radius
r_{0}>r_{G}
,

\displaystyle T(r_{0})=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\int_{r_{G}}^{r_{0}}\frac{1}{I(x)}\int_{x}^{\infty}I(y)\differential y\,\differential x.

(468)

Define

\displaystyle\beta:=\frac{\kappa}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}\,\eta}.

(469)

Then

\displaystyle I(r)=r^{p-1}e^{-\beta r^{2}},

(470)

and

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}y^{p-1}e^{-\beta y^{2}}\differential y=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-p/2}\Gamma(p/2,\beta x^{2}),

(471)

where

\displaystyle\Gamma(a,z):=\int_{z}^{\infty}t^{a-1}e^{-t}\differential t.

(472)

Thus,

\displaystyle T(r_{0})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\beta^{-p/2}\int_{r_{G}}^{r_{0}}\frac{e^{\beta x^{2}}}{x^{p-1}}\Gamma(p/2,\beta x^{2})\differential x.

(473)

Small-Learning-Rate Regime.

The learning rate
\eta\to 0
is small, so

\displaystyle\beta x^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{\bar{s}\,\eta}x^{2}\gg 1.

(474)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Gamma(p/2,\beta x^{2})=(\beta x^{2})^{p/2-1}e^{-\beta x^{2}}\left[1+\frac{p-2}{2\beta x^{2}}+O\!\left((\beta x^{2})^{-2}\right)\right].

(475)

Substituting equation (
475
) into
equation (
473
) gives

\displaystyle T(r_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varepsilon\beta}\int_{r_{G}}^{r_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{x}+\frac{p-2}{2\beta x^{3}}+O\!\left((\beta x^{2})^{-2}x^{-1}\right)\right]\differential x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\log\frac{r_{0}}{r_{G}}+\frac{\varepsilon(p-2)}{4\kappa^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{r_{G}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\right]+O\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\kappa^{3}}\right).

(476)

Substituting
\kappa=\lambda/\bar{s}
and
\varepsilon=\eta
yields

\displaystyle T(r_{0})=\frac{\bar{s}}{\lambda}\log\frac{r_{0}}{r_{G}}+\eta\frac{\bar{s}^{2}(p-2)}{4\lambda^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{r_{G}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}\right]+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{3}}\right).

(477)

Asymptotic Expansions.

The transition starts at the memorization radius and ends at the generalization radius.
By Theorem
4
,

\displaystyle\rho_{M}^{2}=(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}+\frac{\eta}{b}c_{M}+O\bigl((\eta/b)^{2}\bigr).

(478)

Taking the square root gives

\displaystyle r_{0}=\rho_{M}=\rho_{M}^{(0)}+\frac{\eta}{b}\frac{c_{M}}{2\rho_{M}^{(0)}}+O\bigl((\eta/b)^{2}\bigr).

(479)

Similarly, by Theorem
5
,

\displaystyle\rho_{G}^{2}=(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}c_{G}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right),

(480)

where

\displaystyle(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}\left[\|\theta^{\star}(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(481)

and

\displaystyle\theta^{\star}(S_{0})=\arg\min_{\theta(S_{0})}\left\{\mathscr{L}^{*}_{f}(\theta(S_{0}))+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta(S_{0})\|_{2}^{2}\right\}.

(482)

Therefore,

\displaystyle r_{G}=\rho_{G}=\rho_{G}^{(0)}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\frac{c_{G}}{2\rho_{G}^{(0)}}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(483)

Using

\displaystyle\log(x+\Delta x)=\log x+\frac{\Delta x}{x}+O(\Delta x^{2}),

(484)

we have

\displaystyle\log r_{0}

\displaystyle=\log\rho_{M}^{(0)}+\frac{\eta}{b}\frac{c_{M}}{2(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}+O\bigl((\eta/b)^{2}\bigr),

(485)

\displaystyle\log r_{G}

\displaystyle=\log\rho_{G}^{(0)}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\frac{c_{G}}{2(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(486)

Hence

\displaystyle\log\frac{r_{0}}{r_{G}}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}+\frac{\eta}{b}\frac{c_{M}}{2(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{\eta}{\lambda}\frac{c_{G}}{2(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\right).

(487)

In the explicit
O(\eta)
Itô correction term of
equation (
477
), it is sufficient to use
the leading asymptotics:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{G}^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{0}^{2}}=\frac{1}{(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}+O(\eta/b)+O(\eta/\lambda).

(488)

Substituting equations (
487
)
and (
488
) into
equation (
477
) gives

\displaystyle T_{M\to G}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bar{s}}{\lambda}\log\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\eta}{b\lambda}\bar{s}\frac{c_{M}}{2(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\eta}{\lambda^{2}}\left\{-\bar{s}\frac{c_{G}}{2(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}+\frac{\bar{s}^{2}(p-2)}{4}\left[\frac{1}{(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{3}}\right).

(489)

Define

\displaystyle c_{\tau}

\displaystyle:=\bar{s}\,\frac{c_{M}}{2(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}},

\displaystyle c_{\tau}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=-\bar{s}\,\frac{c_{G}}{2(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}+\frac{\bar{s}^{2}(p-2)}{4}\left[\frac{1}{(\rho_{G}^{(0)})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\rho_{M}^{(0)})^{2}}\right].

(490)

Then

\displaystyle T_{M\to G}\approx\frac{\bar{s}}{\lambda}\log\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}+\frac{\eta}{b\lambda}c_{\tau}+\frac{\eta}{\lambda^{2}}c_{\tau}^{(2)}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta^{2}}{\lambda^{3}}\right).

(491)

Computing Number of Iterations.

We compute the number of iterations, since the continuous-time interpolation is
t=\eta k
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}[\tau_{M\to G}]=\frac{T_{M\to G}}{\eta}.

(492)

Dividing equation (
491
) by
\eta
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{S_{0}\in\Theta_{S}}[\tau_{M\to G}]\approx\frac{\bar{s}}{\eta\,\lambda}\log\frac{\rho_{M}^{(0)}}{\rho_{G}^{(0)}}+\frac{c_{\tau}}{b\,\lambda}+\frac{c_{\tau}^{(2)}}{\lambda^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{\eta}{\lambda^{3}}\right),

(493)

which proves equation (
440
).

\ell_{2}
Regularization Coefficient Dependence.

In the late-stage radial SDE, the diffusion scale is
\varepsilon=\eta
and is independent
of batch size; the leading first-passage time is obtained by setting
\varepsilon=0
and is
determined only by the deterministic radial drift
-\lambda r/\bar{s}
. Batch size enters the
leading term through the scalar preconditioning scale
\bar{s}=O(1/\sqrt{b})
, and the
corrections through the memorization asymptotic
\rho_{M}
— whose
O(\eta/b)

correction becomes
O(1/b)
after converting to iteration time — and through

c_{\tau}=O(1/\sqrt{b})
and
c_{\tau}^{(2)}=O(1/b)
.
∎
