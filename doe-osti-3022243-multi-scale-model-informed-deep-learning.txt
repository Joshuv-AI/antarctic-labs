 Summary
The goal of this project is to achieve data-enabled predictions for plasma-nanoparticle (NP)
interaction effects on promoting a higher energy efficiency of carbon-neutral hydrogen (H2)
production from ammonia (NH3) cracking for maritime decarbonization.

The hypothesize that (1) the NPs will be negatively charged heterogeneously under non-thermal
plasma via hot electrons and the charged site will lower the binding energetics of key species; (2)
the vibrational excited species and radicals will both interact with the charged NPs through both
Langmuir–Hinshelwood and Eley–Rideal mechanisms to promote the energy efficiency of plasma
kinetics; and (3) the proposed active and deep learning model will be transferrable and physically
interpretable to accurately predict the plasma-NPs interactions, plasma kinetics and energy
distribution functions for vibrational excited species and radicals.

To further assess the hypotheses, integrating PI Che's expertise in quantum chemistry, non-thermal
plasma kinetics, multi-scale kinetic models, and physics-informed deep learning, three key pillars
of research innovation are proposed:

Aim 1: Probe the charge distribution of metallic NPs under non-thermal plasma conditions and
quantify the impacts of the surface charges on the catalytic activity.

Aim 2: Quantify the contributions of vibrationally excited species, and radical interactions with
charged NPs on altering the activity of plasma catalysis.

Aim 3: Discover the optimal plasma-NP interaction effects on catalysis actively.

Our novelty lies on:

(1) First-time setting up comprehensive multi-scale simulations, from atomic-scale DFT level to
micro-scale MKM, to both understand and quantify the contribution of each low temperature
plasma excited species on overall plasma kinetics over NPs.

(2) First-time developing a pioneering approach that leverages the multi-scale simulations,
physics-informed, and interpretable deep learning to bridge the gap between plasma-NP interfacial
properties (NP characteristics, plasma excited species) and the performance of kinetic properties
of chemical reactions under non-thermal plasma.

(3) Introducing an active learning model that synergizes theory, AI, and experiments to optimize
plasma-NP interfaces to maximize catalytic conversion and plasma kinetics.

(4) The novel catalysts predicted by our deep and active learning models will be validated from
existing experimental collaboration at the lab scale.

The proposed research activities are in resonant with the central components of the DOE basic
plasma science and engineering, including (1) innovative, predictive control and manipulation of
plasma kinetics and energy distribution functions; (2) the predictive understanding of self-
organization or pattern formation in plasmas interacting with surfaces. The integration of ab-initio

simulation, microkinetic model, and machine learning algorithms will facilitate materials
development and enter a new paradigm in plasma material design based on fundamentals rather
than trial-and-error. If NH3 cracking can generate H2 under plasma with high energy efficiency
and low cost, it can be used in purpose-built new ships, or can be integrated into retrofits.

We have accomplished:

Aim 1: Probe the charge distribution of metallic NPs under non-thermal plasma conditions.

Under plasma conditions, catalyst surfaces become charged either by the negative hot electrons or
local electric field, profoundly altering reactant–active site interactions. For example, NH3 binds
more strongly to positively charged sites via electrostatic attraction, whereas negatively charged
sites repel NH3 back into the gas phase. To assess how charge distribution affects catalytic activity,
we examine ammonia cracking on two theoretical models: a Ru(1013) flat surface, representing
catalysts larger than 2 nm, and a Ru45 cluster, representing sub-nanometer catalysts (<1 nm).

On the Ru(1013) surface, we examined the influence of charged Ru surfaces on the elementary
steps in the NH3 decomposition reaction network. It was observed that negatively charged surface
enhanced bond dissociation processes, particularly the dehydrogenation of NH x* intermediates
(Figure 2a). Meanwhile, positively charged surface promoted bond formation, such as the
formation of N-N and H-H bonds (Figure 2b). To understand the properties of bond-forming and
bond-dissociating elementary steps during NH3 cracking over different charged surfaces, we
examined the electronic structure of the charged catalytic surface. Specifically, the electronic
charge distribution at the B5 site (the most active site for ammonia decomposition) was
quantitatively assessed via Bader charge analysis (Figures 2 c-e). The cumulative oxidation state
of the B5 site exhibited a monotonic increase from -0.35 e to +0.01 e. Negatively charged surfaces
were found to create an electron-rich environment at the B5 site. This increased local electron
density facilitates N-H bond cleavage by providing electrons to stabilize the products of
dehydrogenation. Positively charged surfaces, however, reduce surface electron density. This
makes the surface more electrophilic, drawing electrons from adsorbed molecules. Such electron
withdrawal encourages adsorbates to form new bonds (like N-N and H-H, yielding N2 and H2
products) to stabilize the now electron-deficient species.

Using DFT combined with a group-additivity framework, we examined how surface charging
modulates species binding on a Ru45 cluster. As shown in Figure 3a, applied fields drive
significant electron accumulation at the tip and edge atoms. At an electric field of –0.6 V/Å, the
tip atom along the z-axis bears a pronounced negative charge, which electrostatically repels NH 3
yet strengthens N adsorption by enhancing the Ru–N bond via charge transfer. Figure 3b plots the
distribution of N-adsorption energies, revealing that adsorption is strongest at these electron-rich
sites under field. Moreover, top-site adsorption becomes more favorable than bridge or hollow
sites, highlighting the site charges’ ability to tune binding strength across different surface sites.
Next, we performed microkinetic modeling simulations to evaluate site‐specific activity under
with different site charges. As shown in Figure 3c, sites bearing greater negative charge exhibit
higher turnover frequencies than less‐charged sites. These findings reveal how surface charges,
induced by plasma conditions, modulate the catalytic performance.

Aim 2: Quantify the contributions of vibrationally excited species and radical interactions with
NPs on altering the activity of plasma catalysis.

To quantify the contributions of vibrationally excited species and radical interactions to plasma
catalytic activity, we developed a multi-scale modeling framework that integrates plasma effects
(Figure 4a) into a DFT-based microkinetic model. The baseline thermal model (Model 0) captures
conventional surface reactions without plasma influence. In Model I, we incorporated vibrational
excitation of NH3 (NH3v, v = 1–4) using an anharmonic oscillator framework, reducing the
activation energy of the first N–H bond cleavage via an efficiency factor. Model II added reactive
radicals (∙NH₂, ∙NH, ∙N, ∙H) as gas-phase species generated by plasma, contributing to surface
coverage through Langmuir–Hinshelwood adsorption. Model III further included Eley–Rideal
(ER) reactions, allowing direct radical–surface interactions. Our results showed that vibrational
excitation alone (Model I) significantly increases TOFs for metals with weak nitrogen binding
energy (EN > –0.5 eV), although the rate-limiting step remains N2 formation (2N* ⇌ N2* + *)
across all catalysts at 400 °C (Figure 4b–d). Including radical adsorption (Model II) modestly
improves TOFs for moderately active catalysts and substantially boosts activity for less active
metals like Cu, Ag, and Au due to increased N* surface coverage. When ER-type radical–surface
reactions are introduced in Model III, a major mechanistic shift occurs, the rate-limiting step
transitions from N≡N coupling to the dehydrogenation of N2Hx* (x = 1–3) intermediates (Figure
4e–f). This shift dramatically enhances activity on previously poor catalysts and highlights the
critical role of ER mechanisms in expanding the catalytic applicability of non-thermal plasma
systems.

To better understand the densities of reactive species (e.g., hot electrons, excited species, ions), we
developed a multi-scale 2D axisymmetric CFD model in COMSOL for a lab-scale packed bed
reactor used in ammonia decomposition under thermal and plasma conditions (Figure 5a). The
thermal model, validated against experimental data, treats the packed bed as a homogeneous
porous medium and incorporates a detailed micro-kinetic model based on a Langmuir-
Hinshelwood mechanism, with parameters informed by DFT calculations. Figures 5b–c show
steady-state fluid velocity and H₂ mole fraction for Ru/MgO, with similar comparisons for Co, Fe,
and Ni. Experimental validation confirms Ru’s superior conversion efficiency (Figure 5d).

Aim 3: Discover the optimal plasma-NP interaction effects on catalysis actively.

To actively identify optimal plasma–nanoparticle interaction effects, we developed an
interpretable machine learning framework trained on over 3,300 bimetallic surfaces (Figure 6).
The model integrates DFT-derived features, such as d-band filling, electron affinity, and spatial
extent, with plasma-specific descriptors, including nitrogen adsorption energy (EN) under plasma
catalysis. SHAP analysis revealed d-band filling as the most critical feature governing adsorption
behavior under non-thermal plasma conditions. By coupling machine learning predictions with
multi-scale kinetic modeling, we identified an optimal binding energy of nitrogen of –0.51 eV for
maximum catalytic activity under plasma conditions, which differs from the –0.9 eV optimal for
thermal catalysis. This workflow enabled rapid screening and down-selection of plasma-optimized,

earth-abundant alloys such as Fe3Cu, Ni3Mo, Fe15Ni, and Ni7Cu, which were subsequently
validated through experiments (Figure 7). These findings demonstrate a closed-loop, data-driven
discovery strategy that accelerates the identification of high-performance NP catalysts tailored to
specific plasma environments.

To tackle the challenge of identifying promising catalysts within a vast chemical space, we develop
a generative machine learning approach to design low-cost, high-performance bimetallic alloys for
hydrogen production via ammonia decomposition. This work establishes a framework for applying
diffusion models to catalyst discovery, highlighting their potential to accelerate the search for
novel materials.

The inverse design workflow (Figure 8a) integrates two pre-trained machine learning models for
bimetallic alloy design. The first is a generative diffusion model trained to produce unconditional
samples from a given data distribution. The second is a predictive model trained to estimate
nitrogen adsorption energy (EN), which serves as a key descriptor of catalytic performance based
on our prior multiscale simulation studies in Aim 2. The training data for both models are generated
and optimized using DFT simulations. The predictive model functions both as a scoring
mechanism and as a guiding signal for sampling from the diffusion model. During generation,
intermediate outputs from the diffusion model are evaluated using the predictive model to estimate
EN. Gradients of the predicted adsorption energy are used as a guiding signal by introducing a
correction term into the diffusion model’s loss function. This strategy steers the generative process
toward bimetallic alloys with EN values close to the target (approximately –0.9 eV). Using this
inverse design framework, we identified 15 bimetallic alloy candidates with promising predicted
performance for ammonia decomposition (Figure 8b-d). Emphasis was placed on earth-abundant
elements to ensure both environmental sustainability and cost-effectiveness. Among the generated
candidates, compositions such as CoFe, NiFe, CoMo, NiMo, and FeCu—as well as 10 additional
alloys with diverse elemental ratios and adsorption sites—exhibited nitrogen adsorption energies
near the optimal target.

Graduate Student Training:

Two Ph.D. students were recruited to this research, Saleh AhmatIbrahim (Ph.D. candidate) and
Pragyansh Singh (Ph.D. candidate).

Saleh Ahmat Ibrahim (Ph.D. candidate) is the first author of a paper on plasma-assisted ammonia
decomposition that was accepted for publication in Nature Chemical Engineering in May 2025.
He has completed a second manuscript focused on Co-based carbides and nitrides for ammonia
decomposition, which he plans to submit to ACS Catalysis in June 2025. Through this project,
Saleh has developed strong expertise in plasma catalysis by building multi-scale simulation
frameworks that integrate density DFT, microkinetic modeling (MKM), and plasma kinetics to
quantify vibrational and radical effects on catalytic performance. He gained experience in
simulating gas-phase plasma chemistry using zero-dimensional solvers to track species densities.
Saleh has actively presented his plasma catalysis research at five domestic conferences: ACS Fall
2024, AIChE 2024, NECS Spring 2024, NECS Winter 2025 and NECS Spring 2025. He delivered
oral presentations at 2024 ACS Fall, 2024 AIChE, and 2025 NECS Spring, and presented posters
at NECS Spring and NECS Winter. For his contributions, he received the ACS CATL
ChemCatBio Graduate Student Travel Award at ACS Fall 2024, second place in the poster
competition at NECS Winter 2025 and at NECS Spring 2025.
Pragyansh Singh, a Ph.D. candidate, recently published his first-author paper in ACS Catalysis in
April 2025. This research focused on quantifying the effects of surface charge effects on the
mechanism, thermodynamics, and kinetics of ammonia cracking over metallic surfaces. Through
this project, he has developed expertise in modeling multi-scale simulations via integrating DFT
and MKM. His DFT modeling work involved estimating the adsorption, reaction, and activation
energies of elementary steps involved in ammonia cracking, and importantly, analyzing the
influence of surface charge effects on these energetics. Furthermore, he explained the influence of
surface charge effects on reaction and activation energies, estimated through Bader charge
calculations. The microkinetic modeling aspect of his work was used to estimate the turnover
frequency (TOF), identify the rate-limiting step, and determine the apparent activation barrier of
the reaction over the catalyst. In addition to his research, Pragyansh has attended three domestic
conferences: the NECS Spring Symposium in May 2024, the NECS Winter Symposium in January
2025, and the NECS Spring Symposium in May 2025. He presented posters at the NECS Spring

2024 and NECS Winter 2025 meetings and delivered an oral presentation at the NECS Spring
2025 symposium.

research focuses on advancing fundamentals of non-thermal plasma discharges; plasma-surface
