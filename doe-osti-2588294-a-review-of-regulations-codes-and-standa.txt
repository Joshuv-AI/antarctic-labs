ABSTRACT
Transit authorities are exploring integrating hydrogen fuel cell electric buses
(FCEBs) into ground transportation fleets, including for potential use on commercial
airports. This report reviews regulations, codes, and standards to be considered for
FCEB operations on airports governing vehicle safety, hydrogen infrastructure, and
airport operations. Documents were reviewed to consider the operation of FCEBs on
public roadways, airport aprons, and for emergency power generation. Overall, the
review found the existing regulatory framework largely suitable for FCEB operations
on airports, with only minor gaps identified pertaining to heavy-duty vehicle fueling,
operation and fire safety on airport aprons, and authorization for emergency power
use. Airport authorities may need to reassess how codes and standards are
implemented in their internal airport planning and training protocols to ensure the
unique risks associated with hydrogen fuel cells are properly considered.

1. INTRODUCTION

Hydrogen is a potential alternative to diesel fuel for bus and mass transit
applications, and various municipal transit authorities are exploring the use of
hydrogen fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs). Hydrogen fuel cell electric buses combine
electric propulsion with the extended range and refueling capabilities of traditional
combustion engine vehicles. These buses utilize hydrogen fuel to generate
electricity through an electrochemical process, emitting only water vapor as a
byproduct [1].

California has seen the greatest adoption of FCEBs in the United States driven by
state level goals to convert all its public transit agencies to a fully electric fleet by
2040 [2]. AC Transit, the municipal transit authority for Alameda County, California,
were early adopters of FCEBs and now have one of the largest fleets in the United
States [3]. SamTrans, the San Mateo County Transit District, placed an order with
New Flyer in October 2024 for 108 FCEBs, marking the largest order of its kind in
North America [4]. At least 14 other California transit agencies had also placed
orders for FCEBs as of October 2022 [5].

Transit agencies elsewhere in the United States are also moving to adopt FCEBs
into their bus fleets. King County in Washington (the metro Seattle area) is planning
on adding four FCEBs as a pilot project in early 2026 to complement its battery
electric bus fleet and improve fleet reliability [6]. The National Renewable Energy
Laboratory identified four additional transit agencies currently using FCEBs and
seven more planning to do so [7].

Transit agencies implementing hydrogen vehicles also introduces novel settings for
the technology that have not been thoroughly explored. In 2024, the Port of Portland
completed feasibility studies on converting the shuttle bus fleet for Portland
International Airport (PDX) to battery electric and/or fuel cell electric buses [8].
Using FCEBs on airports has not been comprehensively studied, and this use case
may impact how existing regulations for transportation, buildings, and fire safety are
revised and implemented to account for the unique risks of FCEBs, such as high-

 10

pressure hydrogen releases [9]. As local building codes are regarded as the primary
regulations which should be followed, localities (and likewise airports) call on
national regulations, codes and standards documents for guidance. Local building
codes, including those applied to airports, draw upon and reference national codes
and standards, emphasizing their importance across the U.S. and facilitating
consistency between municipalities. For these reasons, this report aims to review
existing regulations, codes, and standards to inform the Port of Portland and other
airport agencies on details pertinent to implementing FCEBs by identifying:

 • What relevant regulations, codes, and standards apply to FCEB operation on
 public airports
 • How these regulations, codes, and standards may be implemented in practice
 by airport authorities, and
 • What gaps exist in these regulations, codes, and standards that could be
 updated to better reflect the use of FCEBs at airports

2. APPLICABLE REGULATIONS, CODES, AND STANDARDS
Based on discussions with the Port of Portland about the current and future
potential use for their shuttle bus fleets, this review considers the following
applications for FCEB operations on airports:

 • Transportation of passengers between terminal buildings and parking areas
 on publicly accessible roadways
 • Transportation of passengers between terminals on roadways closed to the
 public
 • Emergency use of onboard fuel cells to supply backup power generation to
 airport buildings

Table 1 provides an overview of the regulations, codes, and standards considered in
this report and their general purpose. Several of these documents do not directly
pertain to transportation vehicles or fueling but have implications for FCEBs on
airports if the buses are operating in airport aprons (the private roads owned by the
airport connecting the passenger terminals to the airstrip where aircraft are parked,
boarded, and refueled1). Various authorities responsible for airport regulations have
more stringent requirements for operations on airport aprons compared to publicly
accessible roadways due to the presence of passenger aircraft and their associated
risks. In the case of PDX, passengers arriving on international flights use a shuttle
bus to travel directly to the U.S. Customs area, which includes driving on the airport
apron [10]. Additionally, the prospects of using FCEBs as emergency backup power
in the event of a disaster event explicitly introduces additional fire safety
considerations and may require operations on airport aprons to reach certain power
modules.

3. APPLICATION AND GAP ANALYSIS OF RELEVANT DOCUMENTS
This chapter reviews each identified document that relate to FCEB airport use,
summarizes the purpose of the document and how they relate, and the gaps that
may exist in the documents that could limit their application.

3.1. SAE J2601-2 - Fueling Protocols for Gaseous Hydrogen Surface Vehicles -
 Part 2: Heavy Duty Vehicles

SAE J2601-2, last published in 2023, outlines the requirements for hydrogen
dispensing systems used for 35 MPa heavy-duty hydrogen transit buses and
vehicles. SAE J2601-2 was written to fill gaps in guidance for heavy duty vehicles in
SAE J2601 [25] and thus largely applies to FCEBs operating on airports. Specifically,
the standard expands upon Compressed Hydrogen Storage System (CHSS)
Category D (designated for hydrogen tanks greater than 10 kg or 248.6 L) by
providing fueling rates, pressures, and operating conditions for these larger tank
sizes, aligning with existing guidance provided in SAE J2601 for smaller, light-duty
hydrogen vehicle tanks.

While the standard applies to FCEB use on airports, the lack of specificity of
Category D may not provide sufficient guidance for FCEB tank sizes. Category D
only provides a lower bound of tank volume of 10 kg/248.6 L, which is far smaller
than many FCEB tank sizes [26]. As SAE J2601 includes lookup tables for target
pressures for Category D and requires dispensers to have a fuel target pressure
algorithm (Section 6.2 of the standard), greater granularity for a range of larger tank
sizes could provide more precise safety guidelines for a variety of FCEB tanks.

3.2. SAE J2578 - General Fuel Cell Vehicle Safety

SAE J2578, last updated in 2023, establishes general safety requirements for fuel
cell systems, hydrogen fuel storage, and high-voltage electrical systems in fuel cell
vehicles. SAE J2578 has several areas that could be clarified for FCEB applications.
Appendix A of the document references FMVSS 303 for post-crash criteria, which
applies to vehicles under 10,000 lbs. This creates ambiguity as to whether the same

 16

requirements should apply to heavier commercial buses, requiring clarification to
ensure appropriate safety measures are in place for these larger vehicles.
Additionally, the guidance for conducting discharge evaluations in enclosed spaces
(the safety testing protocol for discharging the fuel cell battery to ensure the
discharge process remains in safe operating parameters, detailed in Section 4.2.4
and Appendix C of the standard) uses dimensions for a light duty vehicle (4.5m x
2.6m x 2.6m). While the standard allows the enclosure to be adjusted for larger
vehicles (no more than 1m longer/wider and 0.5m taller than the vehicle), more
comprehensive detail for larger vehicles could be valuable if they are used at a
larger scale. Additionally, the discharge rate limits (<26.7 L/minute after the initial
10 minutes for shutdown) are based on the assumption that smaller vehicles can
afford brief exceedance of the 25% Lower Flammability Limit (LFL) hydrogen
threshold (1 vol% hydrogen) so long as the 3-second rolling average does not
exceed 25% LFL. The selection of a 3-second rolling average is based on the time
until the environment in the enclosure exceeds 25% based on the discharge rate of a
“standard size vehicle”. It is unclear if this assumption remains valid for vehicles
with large tanks such as FCEBs, which may have higher power ratings and
discharge rates than a “standard size vehicle”. This could be reevaluated to
determine if more stringent safety measures are needed for discharge testing for
FCEBs. to mitigate the risk of hydrogen accumulation in enclosed spaces.

3.3. SAE J2579 - Hydrogen Fueling System Component Standards

SAE J2579, last published in 2023, focuses on hydrogen and hydrogen handling
systems onboard vehicles, specifically addressing hydrogen fuel storage and
handling. The 2023 revision of the standard explicitly addresses gaps related to
heavy duty vehicles that were present in the original 2018 publication by updating
the storage system mounting, installation requirements, and localized fire test
methods suitable for larger vehicles. With these revisions, the standard would seem
applicable to FCEBs operating on airports without the need for further changes.

 17

3.4. SAE AIR8466 - Hydrogen Fueling Standardization Guidelines for Aircraft

SAE AIR8466, last published in 2024, provides standardization guidelines for
hydrogen fueling of aircraft. While not directly applicable to FCEBs, the standard
applies to small aircraft, and therefore may offer insight into potential guidance for
heavy duty ground vehicle airport operations and FCEB fueling infrastructure in a
setting with wider hydrogen fuel usage. For example, the standard specifically
mentions that gaseous hydrogen fueling flow rates should not exceed 120 g/s,
“similar to hydrogen bus fueling for 35 MPa”, suggesting synergy for fueling
infrastructure across different airport vehicles. The standard also provides
information on liquid hydrogen (LH2) fueling connector types for aircraft (Section
8.2.3.1), including A-sLH2 and B-LH2 connector types, stating their appropriateness
for heavy duty trucks, which likely extends to FCEBs. As hydrogen usage at airports
increases in the future, this standard is helpful when building hydrogen fueling
infrastructure for several co-located vehicle types including buses, trucks, and
aircraft.

3.5. SAE TIR J2601/5_202502 - High-Flow Prescriptive Fueling Protocols for
 Gaseous Hydrogen Powered Medium and Heavy-Duty Vehicles

SAE TIR J2601/5_202502 is a technical information report last published in 2025 and
is therefore not yet an SAE standard. It aims to establish high flow fueling protocols
for medium- and heavy-duty vehicles including process limits for fueling
compressed hydrogen at flowrates between 60 and 300 g/s for vehicles with storage
system capacities between 248.6 and 7500 L. This standard includes lookup tables
as well as formula-based fueling protocol methods for H35 (35 MPa) and H70 (70
MPa) pressure classes as well as several flow rate classes. If adopted as a standard
after adequate field testing, it can provide design requirements for vehicle
manufacturers and insight on fueling times for FCEBs based on vehicle flow rate
class. Such information would be useful for scheduling refueling sequences during
normal operations and during critical emergency service operation. Depending on

 18

how the final published standard is written, these fueling protocol requirements may
change. As written currently, they should be appropriate for FCEB use on airports.

3.6. NFPA 2 - Hydrogen Technologies Code

NFPA 2, last updated in 2023, presents fire safety guidance pertaining to the
storage, transfer, and use of gaseous and liquid hydrogen. It is applicable for all
vehicular infrastructure applications, and therefore applies to fueling for FCEBs.
While much of the code applies without issue to FCEB use on airports, there are a
few sections that could be clarified or updated:

 • In Chapter 6, NFPA 2 restricts gaseous hydrogen use by building occupancy,
 only allowing use in industry and storage occupancies or laboratory work
 areas of business occupancies. Airport terminals are classified under
 Assembly Group A-3, which is not included in the allowed areas. Aircraft
 hangars and motor vehicle repair garages are classified under Storage Group
 S-1, which do qualify. This should not present issue for airport hydrogen
 fueling stations so long as the hydrogen storage location is sufficiently
 separated from the airport terminals by the proper setbacks detailed in NFPA
 2 Chapter 7. Additionally, these classifications and their restrictions are
 important to consider for airport fueling station layout, siting, and operations.
 • Buses may be in enclosed or semi-enclosed areas for refueling and
 maintenance, in which case Maximum Allowable Quantities (MAQ) and
 ventilation rates for maintenance facilities requirements from Chapter 6 must
 be followed. This may necessitate retrofitting of site facilities to
 accommodate these requirements.
 • The setback distances for bulk outdoor storage of gaseous hydrogen included
 in the tables of Section 7.3.2.3 may need to be revised for the large amount of
 hydrogen storage necessary for a large fleet of buses, as these setback
 distances were derived for hydrogen storage amounts more appropriate for
 lighter duty vehicles. Likewise, these setback distances could be revised to
 account for exposures to airport-specific hazards such as aircraft.

 19

 • The maximum fueling flowrates in Section 10.5.1.5 are not specified for
 medium and heavy-duty hydrogen motor vehicles. The basis and assumptions
 behind any quantitative guidance could be re-examined for applicability to
 FCEB refueling.
 • The maximum allowable quantity of fuel dispensed (4.2 kg of gaseous
 hydrogen) in Section 10.5.3.4 prohibits full refueling of FCEBs indoors.
 Additionally, the maximum refueling rate of 1 kg/min may result in long
 refueling times for the size of tanks used by hydrogen buses. These refueling
 limitations should be considered when deciding on layouts and siting for
 FCEB maintenance facilities, and when deciding on the number of dispensers
 needed when refueling multiple FCEBs on airports.
 • The bonding and grounding requirements, electrical equipment setback
 distances, and emergency shutdown device locations in Chapter 10, among
 other requirements, are applicable to FCEBs on airports, though this may
 require retrofitting and installation of additional equipment. If FCEBs are
 adopted on airports, other regulations such as NFPA 407 and 14 CFR Part 139
 should refer to NFPA 2 for these requirements.

Additionally, NFPA 2 presents guidance regarding the use of hydrogen fuel cell
power systems, including fuel cell vehicles, for temporary power generation such as
during an outage. Some aspects of NFPA 2 would need to be modified to allow for
this application:
 • When used for emergency power, FCEBs need to meet Level 2 system
 requirements from NFPA 110 (2-hour minimum operating time at rated load,
 36-hour starting equipment maximum recharge time). Current FCEBs may not
 satisfy these requirements and therefore could not be used for emergency
 power generation [27].
 • The provisions of Section 12.3.4 could be extended to FCEBs that can provide
 emergency backup power on airports by modifying the language so the use of
 fuel cell vehicles to power buildings is not limited to “small dwellings”.

 20

3.7. NFPA 407 - Standard for Aircraft Fuel Servicing

NFPA 407, last published in 2022, only applies to fueling for aircraft requiring liquid
fuel, and thus does not apply to current FCEB fueling (which uses gaseous
hydrogen). However, if FCEBs adopt liquid fuel, the requirements in this standard
would need to be evaluated and rewritten to encompass liquid hydrogen fuel.
Nevertheless, the relevance to airport fueling offers insights into safety practices
relevant to FCEBs in this setting, as aviation fueling facilities that likely already exist
at airports must comply with the code, and this could inform restrictions for FCEBs
operating alongside and nearby aircraft. For example, Section 4.2.11 and Chapter 5
designate setback distances from terminal buildings, hangars, service buildings, and
enclosed passenger concourses other than a loading walkway. Hydrogen fueling
equipment for FCEBs could ascribe to similar fueling distances in conjunction with
other additional setback distance requirements from NFPA 2.

3.8. NFPA 415 - Standard on Airport Terminal Buildings

While largely not a relevant standard for transportation vehicles, NFPA 415, last
published in 2022 includes requirements for drainage systems on airport aprons.
Referred to as the “fueling ramp” in this standard, airport aprons are designated
areas where aircraft are parked, unloaded, loaded, refueled, and maintained, and
these areas are required to have drainage systems in place to mitigate liquid aircraft
fuel spill accidents. Due to the gaseous fuel type and design of FCEBs, these
drainage systems are less relevant, and this standard could be revised to reflect
more appropriate fuel release mitigation safety systems for hydrogen releases. For
example, this standard requires automated fire shutter systems which, when heat or
smoke is detected, trigger the automatic closing of openings to a building. These
system requirements would need to consider the risk of hydrogen fires, which may
be less visible, propagate more quickly, and burn hotter than fires from traditional
fuels.

The fire test guidelines for jet fuel fires for aircraft loading walkways in Chapter 6
may also require updates to address hydrogen fire risks in areas where FCEBs are in

 21

the vicinity of walkways, potentially adapting guidance from SAE J2579. Specifically,
the time-temperature curves (Section 6.4.2) and temperature limits on the walkway
surfaces (Section 6.4.4) used for the fire tests may need to be adjusted to reflect
the unique characteristics of hydrogen fires, such as the rapid upward diffusion of
hydrogen in air, higher flame temperatures, and faster flame velocities. Additionally,
the placement and design of fire suppression systems on loading walkways may
need to be evaluated to ensure they are effective in mitigating hydrogen fires and
protecting passengers and airport personnel.

3.9. ISO 19880-1 - Gaseous hydrogen — Fueling stations — Part 1: General
 requirements

International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 19880-1, last updated in 2020,
is an international standard providing requirements for gaseous hydrogen fueling
stations. Though some ISO standards tend to mirror SAE standards, ISO 19880-1
establishes safety and performance requirements for refueling stations themselves
including station design, layout, commissioning, installation, operation, inspection
and maintenance, whereas the SAE J2601 series of standards focus on vehicle
fueling protocols. This standard provides useful guidance for station equipment and
components as well, such as production systems, compression, buffer storage,
dispensing, and fueling interface. If fueling stations are commissioned on airports,
this standard may be useful to establish a safe baseline design and should be
consulted alongside fueling protocols provided in standards such as SAE J2601-2.
The newest revision of ISO 19880-1 includes specifications for medium- and heavy-
duty hydrogen-powered vehicles, including process limits for temperature and
pressure ranges, flow rate limits, and pause durations. There are no apparent gaps
in the standard that prevent it from being applied to FCEBs operating on airports
and can be referenced as written when developing hydrogen refueling processes.

3.10. 14 CFR Part 139 - Certification of Airports

14 CFR Part 139, last published in 2004, is the federal regulation administered by
the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) that documents the process for certifying

 22

airports for public operation and commercial flights. The regulation is non-
prescriptive but refers to FAA Advisory Circular (AC) documents for more
prescriptive guidance on FAA requirements. These AC guidelines are covered in
Sections 3.11 - 3.13 below.

Several sections in the regulation are pertinent to FCEBs operating on airports:

 • §139.203 requires that the airport certification manual includes elements
 regarding “procedures for protecting persons and property during the storing,
 dispensing, and handling of fuel and other hazardous substances and
 materials” and “procedures for controlling pedestrians and ground vehicles in
 movement areas and safety areas”
 • §139.321 outlines requirements for airport operators and fueling agents to
 establish safety procedures, training programs, and compliance measures to
 prevent accidents and ensure safe handling of hazardous substances, which
 includes gaseous hydrogen.
 • §139.329 requires airport certificate holders to separate private and public
 vehicle areas to avoid interference with aircraft operations, and require
 training for ground vehicle operators
These sections and their application to FCEBs are considered in the AC guidelines
review in the following sections.

3.11. AC 150/5230-4C - Aircraft Fuel Storage, Handling, and Dispensing on
 Airports

AC 150/5230-4C provides specifications and guidance for the storage, handling, and
dispensing of aviation fuel on airports and relates to §139.321 of 14 CFR Part 139
discussed in Section 3.10 above. While this advisory circular focuses on aircraft
fueling, it references NFPA 407 for detailed training requirements for personnel
involved in aircraft fueling operations. While the AC itself does not provide specific
guidance on hydrogen fueling or FCEB operations, the training requirements
outlined in NFPA 407, and other requirements relevant to NFPA 407 included in

 23

Section 3.7 of this report, could serve as a valuable starting point for developing
training programs for airport personnel involved in FCEB fueling and maintenance.

3.12. AC 150/5210-20A - Ground Vehicle Operations to Include Taxiing or
 Towing an Aircraft on Airports

AC 150/5210-20A focuses on ground vehicle operations within the aircraft
operations area (AOA) and protocols to facilitate proper training and maintaining
proper restriction of movement to minimize the risk of runway incursions. This
Advisory Circular relates to §139.329 and §139.203 of 14 CFR Part 139 discussed
in Section 3.10 above. The airport apron is a part of the AOA, so this guidance
applies to FCEB operations in airports assuming the FCEBs may be operating on the
apron. This guidance is sufficiently general to apply to FCEB airport use as written,
though the specific implementation of this guidance should be reassessed by
airports if they are adjusting their ground vehicle training programs for FCEBs to
ensure they still apply. For example, the introduction of hydrogen fuel cell systems
could impact the ground vehicle inspection programs (Section 3.3.1) or emergency
response plan (Section 4.2.2) used at a given airport.

3.13. AC 150/5360-13A - Airport Terminal Planning

AC 150/5360-13A provides guidance on the planning process for airport passenger
terminals, covering various aspects of terminal design, passenger flow, and ground
transportation. Chapter 8 of the document includes guidance for planning ground
transportation, particularly regarding design for airport roadways and parking areas.
The information in this chapter applies to FCEBs, particularly the discussion on
terminal lane designation and curbside requirements, though this guidance applies
identically to all buses regardless of fuel type. Section 8.7 of the document
specifically discusses considerations for public transportation on airports, though it
only explicitly discusses transportation modes that require significant infrastructure
such as rail and automated people movers.

 24

Chapter 9 of the document discusses guidance for sustainability considerations in
terminal planning, with particular emphasis on minimizing the amount of travel
required between airport locations. The provisions in this chapter are written
assuming internal combustion engine vehicles and the impact of vehicle emissions.
Emissions are not a sustainability concern for fuel cell electric vehicles, but using
hydrogen fuel as efficiently as possible is still relevant. Therefore, language in
Chapter 9 could be modified to include use of FCEBs and not just carbon-emitting
vehicles.

3.14. ACRP Report 146 - Commercial Ground Transportation at Airports: Best
 Practices

ACRP Report 146, published in 2015 by the Airport Cooperative Research Program
(ACRP), examines best practices in commercial ground transportation at airports,
including transportation planning, selection of transportation options for different
uses, and operational strategies. Regarding bus use, the report includes guidance on
the design for bus loading/unloading zones and how to facilitate convenient access
for bus passengers to the terminal. Since this applies all bus types on airports,
including FCEBs, no changes should be needed.

4. SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

This report reviews relevant regulations, codes, and standards pertaining to the use
of hydrogen fuel cell electric buses (FCEBs) on airports. Overall, the existing
regulatory framework seems largely applicable as written to apply to FCEBs
operating on airports, with minor gaps and potential revisions summarized in Table 2
below. Airport authorities should consider that these regulations, codes, and
standards are often written broadly to be applicable for many technologies and may
not prescribe explicit guidance. Due to this, airport authorities should consider
reviewing their implementation of the guidance given in these documents to ensure
their internal protocols and training properly account for the unique hazards and
risks of hydrogen that would be introduced by adding FCEBs into their ground
vehicle fleet.
