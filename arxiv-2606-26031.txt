Geometrically convex return risk measures on AM-algebras
Christian Laudagé

Abstract
. Monetary risk measures quantify the risk of uncertain monetary payoffs (or losses), whereas in time series analysis risk is typically assessed using logarithmic returns. Return risk measures (RRMs) provide an axiomatic foundation for this latter approach, which relies crucially on the positive cone of the space of essentially bounded random variables. We extend RRMs to general ordered vector spaces and characterize positive homogeneity via the geometric epigraph. To investigate geometric convexity and establish connections with monetary risk measures, we specialize the domain to AM-algebras, encompassing Euclidean spaces and spaces of multidimensional essentially bounded random variables. The latter is novel in the context of RRMs and leads to the new classes of systemic and vector-valued RRMs. We establish results on finiteness, continuity, separability, as well as dual and aggregation-based representations. Keywords: AM-algebras, monetary risk measures, return risk measures, separability, systemic risk, vector-valued risk measures



1.
Introduction

Monetary risk measures have become a standard tool in finance and actuarial science. While their original purpose — evaluating the risk of a monetary payoff or loss — is well understood, an axiomatic framework for their application to logarithmic payoffs, losses or returns was not available until the introduction of return risk measures (RRMs) in
Bellini et al. 2018
. In this manuscript, we contribute to the theory of RRMs by studying multivariate extensions — both in the domain and the codomain — of geometrically convex (GG-convex) RRMs.

GG-convex RRMs are the analogous concept to convex monetary risk measures. As pointed out in
(
Laeven and Rosazza Gianin 2022
, Section 3)
, while convex monetary risk measures reward diversification between buy-and-hold trading strategies, GG-convex RRMs reward diversification between continuously rebalancing trading strategies. GG-convex RRMs are also studied in
Aygün et al. 2024
. Their outlook highlights multivariate extensions of GG-convex RRMs as a promising direction for future research, which we, as already mentioned, pursue in this manuscript.

Moreover, our approach unifies earlier works on RRMs, namely approaches focusing on
L^{\infty}
or
(0,\infty)
, while simultaneously extending it to the multivariate framework. This is achieved by using AM-algebras. Although the application of AM-algebras in finance is rather limited, they provide a natural setting for our purposes, which leads to new results for risk measurement based on logarithmic transformations of the underlying quantities.

We now recall the state-of-the-art on RRMs. In a dynamic time framework they have been studied in
Laeven et al. 2025
. RRMs using multiple eligible assets to hedge uncertain losses are defined and analyzed in
Laudagé et al. 2025
. Elicitability of RRMs is discussed in
Aygün et al. 2023
. Another framework for RRMs that goes beyond
L^{\infty}
is the one in
Laeven et al. 2024
. However, this work focuses on locally convex topological vector spaces between
L^{\infty}
and
L^{1}
, which do not exploit the specific structure of AM-algebras. Moreover, their main intention is to obtain representation results for stochastically consistent (but not necessarily cash-additive) functionals. In contrast, we focus on GG-convexity and its connection to monetary risk measures. In addition, we clarify several important technicalities that are essential for working with RRMs at a deeper mathematical level.

Important examples of multivariate risk measures are systemic risk measures, which are axiomatically characterized on finite state spaces in
Chen et al. 2013
and later extended to locally convex-solid Riesz spaces in
Kromer et al. 2016
. Conditional versions of systemic risk measures can be found in
Hoffmann et al. 2016
. We orientate ourselves on these expositions and introduce systemic RRMs. Finally, our discussion of separability results for vector-valued RRMs is inspired by
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
. Together with the creation of systemic optimal allocations in
Biagini et al. 2020
via the dual representations in
Biagini et al. 2019
, their results motivate the study of vector-valued risk measures. Note that
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
appeared recently, and the existing literature focuses more on set-valued maps, although vector-valued risk measures constitute a natural intermediate step between scalar-valued and set-valued risk measures.

We aim to identify a small domain on which an RRM can admit properties like GG-convexity or representations via monetary risk measures, while at the same time being sufficiently rich to support a multivariate RRM framework. Our answer to this question is the class of AM-algebras, which has several advantages. First, they unify different RRM frameworks in the literature, thereby clarifying structural features of RRMs and eliminating the need to develop analogous results for special cases separately. Second, AM-algebras are general enough to encompass more demanding domains, such as families of vector-valued random variables. Third, developing a theory of RRMs on AM-algebras helps to clarify both the possibilities and limitations of applying RRMs. Finally, there is also an intrinsic mathematical motivation for developing a unified framework for RRMs.

Our study addresses the following three main topics:

•

GG-convex RRMs on AM-algebras:
We state the definition of GG-convex RRMs on AM-algebras and characterize the defining properties of positive homogeneity and GG-convexity via the geometric epigraph. Using the connection to convex monetary risk measures, we prove that GG-convex RRMs are only locally Lipschitz-continuous in general. Then, we establish two dual representations. The first representation admits a dual maximizer, whereas this is not guaranteed for the second one. Moreover, the second representation only holds under a “Kômlos”-type condition (C-property). Although this property holds in the most important situations, we provide a concrete AM-algebra in which it fails.

•

GG-convex systemic RRMs:
Evaluating the risk of a vector of losses is important to measure systemic risk. Therefore, we introduce systemic RRMs, which map vector-valued losses to positive real numbers. We show that this is equivalent to the existence of an RRM and a return aggregation function such that their composition yields the systemic RRM. We illustrate that this decomposition crucially relies on the assumption of preference consistency. Furthermore, a GG-convex and positively homogeneous systemic RRM satisfies risk GG-convexity, a property closely related to GG-convexity. Without positive homogeneity, a counterexample shows that GG-convexity of a systemic RRM is not sufficient for risk GG-convexity. We conclude with a dual representation for GG-convex systemic risk measures, which clarifies that the impact of the RRM and the return aggregation function can be fully separated from each other.

•

Separability of GG-convex RRMs:
Separability means that a vector-valued evaluation — where the codomain of the RRM is
(0,\infty)^{n}
— can be reduced to single-valued evaluations; that is, the
i
-th component of the RRM depends only on the
i
-th loss. We derive sufficient conditions for such a reduction. Our first result is obtained under pointwise positive homogeneity, which is stronger than positive homogeneity. Under this assumption, RRMs defined on the interior of the cone of positive, essentially bounded random vectors are separable. Finally, we extend these results to larger domains and present examples based on well-known monetary risk measures, namely Expected Shortfall and entropic risk measures.

The structure of the manuscript is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the unifying framework based on AM-algebras, define GG-convexity and study RRMs at this general level. Section
3
is devoted to systemic risk. Here, we specialize to vectors of positive, essentially bounded random variables and
(0,\infty)
as codomain.
In Section
4
, we study separability of GG-convex RRMs in the vector-valued setting, where the codomain is
(0,\infty)^{n}
.
In Section
5
, we conclude and outline two directions for future research.

2.
Return risk measures and AM-algebras

Domains for RRMs appearing in the current literature are subsets of positive cones in
L^{p}
-spaces. Nonetheless, risk measure theory has an inherent tendency towards unifying frameworks, allowing for more complex settings, such as stochastic processes to account for risk evaluation over entire trajectories. The unification we present below covers the cases of
(0,\infty)^{n}
and
L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

2.1.
Ordered vector spaces and return risk measures

The concepts presented next are standard and taken from
Rudin 1987
and
Aliprantis and Burkinshaw 1985
. First of all, in addition to a vector space structure, we need a partial order. A pair
(V,\leq)
, where
V
is a real vector space and
\leq
is a partial order, is an
ordered vector space
, if the partial order
\leq
is compatible with the algebraic structure of
V
, i.e., the following properties are satisfied for all
x,y,z\in V
and
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
:

(1)

If
x\leq y
, then
x+z\leq y+z
;

(2)

If
x\leq y
, then
\alpha x\leq\alpha y
.

For brevity, we often write
V
instead of
(V,\leq)
. The positive cone of
V
is defined by

V_{+}:=\{x\in V\mid 0\leq x\}.

Now we define a return risk measure, for which it is enough to work on an ordered vector space.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
V
be an ordered vector space. A map
\widetilde{\rho}:E\rightarrow[0,\infty]^{m}
with
E\subseteq V_{+}
being a cone, is called a
return risk measure (RRM)
, if it satisfies the following two properties:

(1)

For all
x,y\in E
with
x\leq y
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(x)\leq\widetilde{\rho}(y)

(monotonicity)
;

(2)

For all
x\in E
and
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(\lambda x)=\lambda\widetilde{\rho}(x)

(positive homogeneity)
.

The corresponding acceptance set is
\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\rho}}:=\{x\in E\mid\widetilde{\rho}(x)\leq 1\}
.

Properties like positive homogeneity and convexity for monetary risk measures with codomain
[-\infty,\infty]
are defined via the epigraph. The epigraph excludes
-\infty
by definition. Analogously, this would mean to exclude zero for RRMs, which is not obtained by applying the epigraph. Therefore, it is important to introduce the notion of a geometric epigraph and compare its properties with the classical epigraph and our definition of positive homogeneity of an RRM.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
V
be an ordered vector space and
E\subseteq V_{+}
. The
geometric epigraph
of a map
f:E\rightarrow[0,\infty]^{m}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
is

\epiG(f):=\{(x,\alpha)\in E\times(0,\infty)^{m}\mid f(x)\leq\alpha\}.

Remark 2.3
.

The geometric epigraph admits the following properties:
\epiG(f)\subseteq\epi(f)
, because

(2.1)

\displaystyle\epiG(f)=\epi(f)\cap\big(E\times(0,\infty)^{m}\big).

For
m=1
, the latter shows that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\epi(f)\setminus\epiG(f)=\{x\in E\mid f(x)=0\}\times\{0\}.

Since
(x,f(x))\in\epi(f)
iff
x\in\textnormal{dom}(f)
, the previous observation yields

(x,f(x))\in\epiG(f)\implies x\in\textnormal{dom}(f).

The inclusion
\epiG(f)\subseteq\epi(f)
in Remark
2.3
can be strict as the following example shows.

Example 2.4
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty),x\mapsto\max\{x_{2}-x_{1},0\}
. Then, by (
2.2
) we obtain that

\epi(f)\setminus\epiG(f)=\big\{x\in(0,\infty)^{2}\,\big|\,x_{2}\leq x_{1}\big\}\times\{0\}.

Next, we analyze the relation between positive homogeneity of a map

and conicity of its (geometric) epigraph. Since, additional assumptions are needed to ensure strict positivity of an RRM, see e.g., the discussion in
(

Proposition 2.5

2.2.
AM-algebras and geometric convexity

Next, we aim to define geometric convexity, which relies on powers and products of elements in the positive cone of an ordered vector space. Therefore, additional structure on the ordered vector space is required. As a first step, recall that an ordered vector space
V
is called a
Riesz space
if, for every
x,y\in V
, the infimum and supremum of the set
\{x,y\}
exist in
V
. In this case, for all
x,y\in V
we write

x\vee y:=\sup\{x,y\},\quad x\wedge y:=\inf\{x,y\},

and

x^{+}:=x\vee 0,\quad x^{-}:=(-x)\vee 0,\quad|x|:=x\vee(-x).

A helpful representation holds for all

(
Aliprantis and Burkinshaw 1985
, Theorem 1.2 (2))

(2.3)

\displaystyle x+y=x\wedge y+x\vee y.

This implies

x=x\wedge y+x\vee y-y=x\wedge y+(x-y)^{+}.

is called an

, or simply a

.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let

(i)

For all
x,y,z\in V_{+}
with

xz\leq yz
and

.

(ii)

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in V_{+}
with
x\leq y
it holds that
x^{n}\leq y^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in V_{+}
with
x\leq y
and set
w:=y-x\geq 0

which implies

.

is also an algebraic identity.

AM-algebras provide a unified framework for the two most common settings in which geometrically convex RRMs are studied, namely
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
(equipped with the supremum norm). To the best of our knowledge, the existing literature on geometrically convex RRMs considers only the case of
n=1
and moreover treats these two settings separately, see e.g.,
Aygün et al. 2024
.

Example 2.9
.

The following are AM-algebras:

(1)

\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the pointwise multiplication and the maximum norm;

(2)

The space of essentially bounded random variables
L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
with pointwise multiplication.

Note that
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
p\in[1,\infty)
is even not an algebra based on pointwise multiplication, because
X,Y\in L^{1}_{+}
does not imply
XY\in L^{1}_{+}
in general. Even the standard
L_{p}
-norm does not satisfy the property of an AM-space, i.e., it does not hold for all
X,Y\in L^{p}
that
\lVert X\vee Y\rVert_{L_{p}}\leq\lVert X\rVert_{L^{p}}\vee\lVert Y\rVert_{L^{p}}
.

On our way to define geometric convexity, we need to know if powers of positive elements in an AM-algebra are well-defined. This is indeed the case, as the next result shows. For its proof, we use the standard notation
C(X)
to denote the collection of continuous functions on a subset
X
of a topological space. If
X
is a compact Hausdorff space, then
C(X)
is an AM-space and even an AM-algebra when equipped with its usual pointwise multiplication.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra with unit
\unit
. Then, for all
x\in V_{+}
and
t\in[0,\infty)
,
x^{t}
exists and lies in
V_{+}
. Furthermore, the following intuitive properties hold:

(i)

x^{ts}=(x^{t})^{s}
,
x\in V_{+}
,
t,s\in[0,\infty)
;

(ii)

x^{t}z^{t}=(xz)^{t}
,
x,z\in V_{+}
,
t\in[0,\infty)
;

(iii)

x^{t}x^{s}=x^{t+s}
,
x\in V_{+}
,
t,z\in[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

We apply a version of the famous Krein-Kakutani Theorem for AM-algebras, which goes back to
Martignon 1980
.
4
4

4

The original Krein-Kakutani Theorem is stated for AM-spaces and not for AM-algebras, see
Kakutani 1941
.
It states that the AM-algebra
V
with unit
\unit
is lattice and algebraic isometric to
C(K)
for some compact Hausdorff space
K
with unit
\mathbf{1}_{K}
. By
\mathbf{1}_{K}
being the unit in
C(K)
, we can apply
(
Martignon 1980
, Proposition 1.4)
to obtain that the algebraic operation on
C(K)
has to be the pointwise multiplication. We denote the corresponding surjective lattice and algebraic isometry by
\Phi:V\rightarrow C(K)
and for short, we also write
\hat{x}=\Phi(x)
. Then, for
x\in V_{+}
and
y\in[0,\infty)
, the expression
(\hat{x})^{t}
is well-defined in the pointwise sense and it is contained in
C(K)
. Indeed, by using that
\Phi
is a lattice homomorphism we can apply
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.15 (3))
to obtain
\hat{x}=\Phi(x)=\Phi(x^{+})=\Phi(x)\vee 0=\hat{x}^{+}\geq 0
. Moreover,
(\hat{x})^{t}
as composition of continuous functions is again continuous
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Lemma 2.30)
. Hence,
(\hat{x})^{t}\in C(K)
. Then, set
x^{t}=\Phi^{-1}((\hat{x})^{t})
and analogously to before we obtain that
x^{t}\in V_{+}
.

To prove (i), let
x\in V_{+}
and
t,s\in[0,\infty)
. By

\widehat{x^{t}}=\Phi(x^{t})=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\hat{x}^{t}))=\hat{x}^{t},

we obtain

\Phi(x^{ts})=\Phi(\Phi^{-1}(\hat{x}^{ts}))=\hat{x}^{ts}=(\hat{x}^{t})^{s}=\left(\widehat{x^{t}}\right)^{s}=\widehat{(x^{t})^{s}}=\Phi((x^{t})^{s}),

which implies — by
\Phi
being bijective — that
x^{ts}=(x^{t})^{s}
. Finally, we only prove (ii), because (iii) follows in a similar manner. To do so, let
x,z\in V_{+}
and
t\in[0,\infty)
. First, note that

\hat{x}\hat{z}=\Phi(x)\Phi(z)=\Phi(xz)=\widehat{xz},

where we used that
\Phi
is also an algebraic isometry. This then gives us that

\displaystyle\Phi(x^{t}z^{t})

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(\hat{x}^{t})\Phi^{-1}(\hat{z}^{t})\big)=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(\hat{x}^{t}\hat{z}^{t})\big)

\displaystyle=\hat{x}^{t}\hat{z}^{t}=(\hat{x}\hat{z})^{t}=\widehat{xz}^{t}

\displaystyle=\Phi\big(\Phi^{-1}(\widehat{xz}^{t})\big)=\Phi((xz)^{t}).

Hence,
x^{t}z^{t}=(xz)^{t}
.
∎

Finally, we are able to define geometric convexity for maps on AM-algebras, for which we call a subset
A
of an AM-algebra
geometrically (or GG-)convex
, if for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
it holds that
A^{\lambda}\cdot A^{1-\lambda}\subseteq A
, where for two subsets
B,C
of the same AM-algebra and
\lambda\in(0,1)
we used that
B\cdot C:=\{bc\mid b\in B,c\in C\}
(Minkowski product) and
B^{\lambda}:=\{b^{\lambda}\mid b\in B\}
. Furthermore, we impose the convention that for each
\lambda\in(0,1)
it holds that
\infty^{\lambda}=\infty
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra. For a GG-convex set
E\subseteq V_{+}
, we assume a map
f:E\rightarrow[0,\infty]^{m}
. The function
f
is called
geometrically (or GG-)convex
, if for all
x,y\in E
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
it holds that

f\big(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda}\big)\leq f(x)^{\lambda}f(y)^{1-\lambda}.

Next, as for positive homogeneity it is indispensable to discuss the connection of GG-convexity of a map to GG–convexity of the corresponding (geometric) epigraph.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra. For a GG-convex set
E\subseteq V_{+}
we assume a map
f:E\rightarrow[0,\infty]^{m}
. Then, consider the following statements:

(a)

f
is GG-convex;

(b)

\epi(f)
is GG-convex;

(c)

\epiG(f)
is GG-convex.

Then,
(a)\implies(b)\Longleftrightarrow(c)
. For
f:E\rightarrow[0,\infty)^{m}
it holds that
(b)\implies(a)
.

Proof.

First, we prove that (a) implies (b). To do so, let
(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in\epi(f)
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then, it holds that

f(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda})\leq f(x)^{\lambda}f(y)^{1-\lambda}\leq\alpha^{\lambda}\beta^{1-\lambda}.

Hence,
(x,\alpha)^{\lambda}\cdot(y,\beta)^{1-\lambda}=(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda},\alpha^{\lambda}\beta^{1-\lambda})\in\epi(f)
.

To prove that (b) implies (c), let
(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in\epiG(f)
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then,
\alpha^{\lambda}\beta^{1-\lambda}\in(0,\infty)^{m}
and assumption (b) implies that

(x,\alpha)^{\lambda}\cdot(y,\beta)^{1-\lambda}=(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda},\alpha^{\lambda}\beta^{1-\lambda})\in\epi(f)\cap(E\times(0,\infty)^{m})=\epiG(f).

Next, we prove that
(c)
implies
(b)
. In doing so, assume
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in\epi(f)
. If
(x,\alpha),(y,\beta)\in\epiG(f)
then by assumption
(x,\alpha)^{\lambda}\cdot(y,\beta)^{1-\lambda}\in\epiG(f)\subseteq\epi(f)
. Otherwise, for every
\gamma\in[0,\infty)^{m}
we set
I_{\gamma}:=\{i\in[m]\mid\gamma_{i}=0\}
. Then, for every
i\in I_{\alpha}\cup I_{\beta}
we obtain that

f_{i}(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda})\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\eta,\kappa\in(0,\infty)^{m}\\
f(x)\leq\eta,f(y)\leq\kappa\end{subarray}}\eta_{i}\,\kappa_{i}=0.

This implies that
f_{i}(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda})=0
for all
i\in I_{\alpha}\cup I_{\beta}
and hence,
(x,\alpha)^{\lambda}\cdot(y,\beta)^{1-\lambda}\in\epi(f)
.

Finally, assume
f:E\rightarrow[0,\infty)^{m}
. To prove that then (b) implies (a), let
x,y\in E
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then,
(x,f(x)),(y,f(y))\in\epi(f)
, which by GG-convexity of
\epi(f)
implies that

(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda},f(x)^{\lambda}f(y)^{1-\lambda})\in\epi(f).

Hence,
f(x^{\lambda}y^{1-\lambda})\leq f(x)^{\lambda}f(y)^{1-\lambda}
, which shows that
f
is GG-convex.
∎

Surprisingly, in contrast to Proposition
2.5
, we do not obtain that (b) implies (a) when
m=1
, as the next counterexample shows.

Example 2.13
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
be given by

f(x)=\begin{cases}0,&x=1,\\
\infty,&x\neq 1.\end{cases}

Then,
\epi(f)=\{1\}\times[0,\infty)
. Choose an arbitrary
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\infty)
. Then, we obtain that
(1,\alpha^{\lambda}\beta^{1-\lambda})\in\epi(f)
and hence,
\epi(f)
is geometrically convex. However,

f(2^{1-\lambda})=\infty>0=f(1)^{\lambda}f(2)^{1-\lambda}.

2.3.
Connection to monetary risk measures

There is a one-to-one correspondence between RRMs on the interior of the positive cone
\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(L_{+}^{\infty})
and monetary risk measures on
L^{\infty}
. We show that the same correspondence holds for AM-algebras, enabling us to transfer a variety of results from the theory of monetary risk measures to RRMs.

For this, it is important to note that the interior of the positive cone of an AM-algebra with order unit is always non-empty. The next result shows that this interior refers to an exponential transform of the underlying AM-space. In particular, we show explicitly that the exponential of an element in an AM-space with unit is well-defined.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
V
be an AM-space with unit
\unit
. Then, for every
x\in V
,
\exp(x)
exists and lies in
V
. Then, set
\exp(V)=\{\exp(x)\mid x\in V\}
. The following statements hold:

(i)

\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})=\{x\in V_{+}\mid\exists r>0:x\geq r\unit\}
;
5
5

5

For the interior of
C(K)_{+}
, see Corollary
A.1
.

(ii)

\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})=\exp(V)
. In particular,
\unit\in\exp(V)
;

(iii)

For every
y=\exp(x)\in\exp(V)
,
\log(y)
exists and lies in
V
with
\log(y)=x
.

If
V
is an AM-algebra with unit
\unit
, then the following holds:

(iv)

For each
x\in V
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
we have
\exp(x+m\unit)=\exp(m)\exp(x)
.

Proof.

By the Krein-Kakutani Theorem, see
Kakutani 1941
and
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.32)
we obtain that the AM-space
V
with unit
\unit
is lattice isometric to
C(K)
for some compact Hausdorff space
K
. The latter is unique up to homeomorphism. The underlying lattice isometry is denoted by
\Phi:V\rightarrow C(K)
. For short, we also write
\hat{x}=\Phi(x)
. Then, for each
x\in V
, the expression
\exp(\hat{x})
is well-defined in the pointwise sense and is contained in
C(K)
. Indeed,
\exp(\hat{x})>0
by definition of the exponential function and, as composition of continuous functions, it is again continuous
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Lemma 2.30)
. So,
\exp(x):=\Phi^{-1}(\exp(\hat{x}))
exists for all
x\in V
. Part (iii) follows with analogous arguments.

Now, let us prove (i). First, assume
x\in V_{+}
and
r>0
with
x\geq r\unit
. Then, let
y\in V
satisfying
\lVert x-y\rVert_{\infty}<r
. We obtain

-r\unit<y-x<r\unit,

which in turn yields

y=x+(y-x)\geq r\unit-r\unit=0.

So,
y\in V_{+}
, which gives us that
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(x,r)\subseteq V_{+}
and hence,
x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
.

For the remaining inclusion let
x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
. Then, there exists
r\in(0,\infty)
such that
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(x,r)\subseteq V_{+}
. Towards a contradiction, assume that for all
s>0
we have
x\ngeq s\unit
. Then, it holds that
x-\frac{r}{2}\unit\notin V_{+}
. However,

\left\lVert x-\left(x-\frac{r}{2}\unit\right)\right\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{r}{2}\lVert\unit\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{r}{2}<r,

which implies that

x-\frac{r}{2}\unit\in B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(x,r)\subseteq V_{+},

a contradiction and hence,
\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\subseteq\{x\in V_{+}\mid\exists r>0:x\geq r\unit\}
.

Next, let us prove (ii). To do so, let
x\in V
. By using the lattice isometry
\Phi
from the Krein-Kakutani representation, we obtain that
\exp(\hat{x})
attains a strictly positive minimum
\delta>0
, because it is a real-valued lower semicontinuous function on a compact space, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 2.43)
. Furthermore, by
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.17)
,
\Phi^{-1}
is a positive operator, from which we conclude that

\exp(x)=\Phi^{-1}(\exp(\hat{x}))\geq\delta\Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{1}_{K})=\delta\unit.

Thus,
\exp(x)\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
, which gives us that
\exp(V)\subseteq\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
. For the inverse inclusion, assume
y\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
. Then, by part (i) we know that
y\geq r\unit
for some
r>0
. Using again
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.17)
, we have

\hat{y}=\Phi(y)\geq r\mathbf{1}_{K}>0.

This means that the expression
\log(\hat{y})
is well-defined with
\log(\hat{y})\in C(K)
. Set
x:=\Phi^{-1}(\log(\hat{y}))\in V
, and by
\exp(\hat{x})=\hat{y}
we get

y=\Phi^{-1}(\hat{y})=\Phi^{-1}(\exp(\hat{x}))=\exp(x)\in V_{+}

Hence,
\exp(V)\subseteq\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
.

Then,
\unit\in\exp(V)
is a direct consequence of (ii) together with
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.40)
.

Finally, for part (iv), by
\unit
being an order unit in
V
, for every
x\in V
there exists
\lambda>0
such that
|x|\leq\lambda\unit
. By
\Phi
being a lattice isomorphism, by applying
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.15 (5) and Theorem 9.17)
we obtain that

|\hat{x}|\leq\lambda\hat{\unit}.

Hence,
\hat{\unit}
is an order unit in
C(K)
. The only order unit recovering the supremum norm in
C(K)
is
\mathbf{1}_{K}
, i.e.,
\hat{\unit}=\mathbf{1}_{K}
. Thus,

\Phi(\exp(x+m\unit))=\exp(m)\exp(\Phi(x))\mathbf{1}_{K}=\exp(m)\exp(\Phi(x)).

This shows that
\exp(x+m\unit)=\exp(m)\exp(x)
.
∎

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we write
\mathbf{1}_{n}:=(1,\dots,1)^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, or simply
\mathbf{1}
if the context is clear. Now we are in a position to introduce monetary risk measures.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
V
be a Riesz space with order unit
\unit
. A map
\rho:V\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty]^{m}
is called a
(monetary) risk measure
(for
\unit
), if it satisfies the following two properties:

(1)

For all
x,y\in V
with
x\leq y
it holds that
\rho(x)\leq\rho(y)

(monotonicity)
;

(2)

For all
x\in V
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
it holds that
\rho(x+m\unit)=\rho(x)+m\mathbf{1}

(cash-additivity)
.

The corresponding acceptance set is
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}:=\{x\in V\mid\rho(x)\leq 0\}
.

The next result establishes the connection between monetary and return risk measures. Before stating the result, recall from Lemma
A.2
(i) that if
\tau
is a topology on an AM-algebra
V
, then
\exp(\tau):=\{\exp(U)\mid U\in\tau\}
is a topology on
\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
, the so-called pushforward topology.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra with unit
\unit
. Given a map
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]^{m}
and set
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
.
6
6

6

Note, for
\exp
, we use the construction given in the proof of Lemma
2.14
.
Then, the following statements hold:

(i)

\widetilde{\rho}
is an RRM if and only if
\rho
is a risk measure (for
\unit
);

(ii)

\widetilde{\rho}
is GG-convex if and only if
\rho
is convex;

(iii)

For a topology
\tau
on
V
,
\widetilde{\rho}
is lower semicontinuous with respect to
\exp(\tau)
if and only if
\rho
is lower semicontinuous with respect to
\tau
;

(iv)

For
m=1
,
\widetilde{\rho}
is proper if and only if
\rho
is proper.

Proof.

We start by proving (i). The equivalence for monotonicity is an immediate consequence of the monotonicity of
\log
and
\exp
. Then, let
\widetilde{\rho}
be positively homogeneous. For all
x\in V
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
we obtain by applying Lemma

\rho(x+m\unit)=\log\Big(\widetilde{\rho}\big(\exp(x+m\unit)\big)\Big)=\log\Big(\exp(m)\widetilde{\rho}\big(\exp(x)\big)\Big)=\rho(x)+m\mathbf{1}.

Positive homogeneity of
\widetilde{\rho}

\lambda\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\rho_{i}(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)

\displaystyle=(\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}_{i}\circ\exp)(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)

\displaystyle\leq\log\Big(\widetilde{\rho}_{i}(\e^{x})^{\lambda}\,\widetilde{\rho}_{i}(\e^{y})^{1-\lambda}\Big)=\lambda\rho_{i}(x)+(1-\lambda)\rho_{i}(y).

Proving the “if” part works analogously.

To prove (iii), let
\tilde{\rho}
be lower semicontinuous with respect to the pushforward topology

and let
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net in

. Moreover,

2.4.
Continuity and dual representations

In the case of
m=1
, the following result shows that an RRM defined on the interior of the positive cone can only attain values in
(0,\infty)
and it is locally Lipschitz-continuous.

Theorem 2.18
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra with unit
\unit
and assume an RRM
{\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]}
.
Then,
\widetilde{\rho}\in(0,\infty)
and
\widetilde{\rho}
is locally Lipschitz-continuous.

In particular, if
\widetilde{\rho}
is GG-convex, then
\widetilde{\rho}
is
\tau|_{\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})}
-lower semicontinuous, where
\tau
is the topology induced by
\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Set
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
. By Proposition
2.16
(i),
\rho
is a risk measure (for
\unit
). Hence, we can use the cash-additivity of
\rho
to represent it as follows:

\rho(x)=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\mid X-m\unit\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\}.

By
\unit
being an interior point of
V_{+}
, we can apply
(
Farkas et al. 2014
, Proposition 3.1)
to obtain that
\rho
is finite-valued and Lipschitz-continuous (with Lipschitz constant
L>0
). Hence,
\widetilde{\rho}<\infty
, by Proposition
2.16
(iv), and therefore,
\widetilde{\rho}\in(0,\infty)
. To prove that
\widetilde{\rho}
is locally Lipschitz-continuous, choose an arbitrary
x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
with
x\in\left[\varepsilon\unit,c\unit\right]
for some
\varepsilon,c>0
. Then, as neighborhood of
x
choose
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)\subseteq\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
. Using again the lattice isometry of the Krein-Kakutani representation between
V
and
C(K)
for the corresponding compact Hausdorff space
K
, we can note that for every
y\in B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
we have
\log(y)\in\left[\log(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\unit),\log((\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+c)\unit)\right]
. Thus, by the fact that
\log^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}
on
\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\infty\right)
— i.e.,
|\log(u)-\log(s)|\leq\frac{2}{\varepsilon}|u-s|
for all
u,s\in\left[\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\infty\right)
— the Lipschitz-continuity of
\rho
implies for all
y\in B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
that

\lVert\rho(\log(y))-\rho(\log(x))\rVert_{\infty}\leq L\lVert\log(y)-\log(x)\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{2L}{\varepsilon}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\infty}.

So,
\rho\circ\log
is Lipschitz-continuous on
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
. Furthermore,
\rho\circ\log
is bounded on
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
. Indeed, for all
y\in B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
we have

\displaystyle\lVert\rho(\log(y))\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\rho(\log(y))-\rho(\log(x))\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert\rho(\log(x))\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2L}{\varepsilon}\lVert y-x\rVert_{\infty}+\lVert\rho(\log(x))\rVert_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq L+\lVert\rho(\log(x))\rVert_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus, by the fact that the exponential function is also Lipschtz-continuous on bounded sets, we obtain that the composition
\widetilde{\rho}=\exp\circ\rho\circ\log
is Lipschitz-continuous on
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(x,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big)
and hence,
\widetilde{\rho}
is locally Lipschitz-continuous.

If
\widetilde{\rho}
is GG-convex, then
\rho
is convex. Recall, Lipschitz-continuity of
\rho
implies continuity of
\rho
with respect to the norm topology
\tau
. Moreover,
\tau
and
\sigma(V,V^{\prime})
are consistent to the dual pair
\langle V,V^{\prime}\rangle
. By
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Corollary 5.99)
, the set of lower semicontinuous convex maps is the same in all topologies consistent with respect to a fixed dual pair. Hence,
\rho
is
\sigma(V,V^{\prime})
-lower semicontinuous. The claim then follows by applying
\widetilde{\rho}=\exp\circ\rho\circ\log
and Lemma
A.3
.
∎

The following example shows that
\widetilde{\rho}
is not globally Lipschitz-continuous in general.

Example 2.19
.

We make use of the certainty equivalent for logarithmic utility. To do so, we choose
V=L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
and
\rho(X)=\mathbb{E}[X]
. Then, for a set
A\in\mathcal{F}
with
\mathbb{P}(A)=\frac{1}{2}
set
X_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{A}+\mathbf{1}_{A^{\complement}}
as well as
Y_{n}=\frac{2}{n}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{A}+\mathbf{1}_{A^{\complement}}
. Then,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(X_{n})

\displaystyle=(\exp\circ\rho\circ\log)(X_{n})=\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\log(n)\Big)=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(Y_{n})

\displaystyle=(\exp\circ\rho\circ\log)(Y_{n})=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(\frac{2}{n}\Big)\Big)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}.

Together with
\lVert X_{n}-Y_{n}\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{2}{n}-\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n}
we obtain

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|\widetilde{\rho}(X_{n})-\widetilde{\rho}(Y_{n})|}{\lVert X_{n}-Y_{n}\rVert_{\infty}}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(\sqrt{2}-1)n^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{1/n}=(\sqrt{2}-1)\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sqrt{n}=\infty.

Hence, no Lipschitz-constant exists, and therefore,
\widetilde{\rho}
is not globally Lipschitz-continuous.

By exploiting the connection to monetary risk measures, we derive dual representations for RRMs. To motivate the study of dual representations, we note that they play a central role to obtain many remarkable results concerning monetary risk measures. For instance, see the proof of the separability result in
(
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
, Theorem 3.3)
and the proof of the existence of an equilibrium for the risk sharing problem in
(
Liebrich and Svindland 2019
, Theorem 3.5)
. They can also be the starting point to define risk measures, see e.g., the approch in
(
Liebrich and Svindland 2017
, Section 4)
or the definition of systemic optimal allocations via dual representations in
Biagini et al. 2020
. However, only a few dual representations for RRMs are available and all rely on domains of random variables, see e.g.,
Laeven and Rosazza Gianin 2022
and
Laudagé et al. 2025
. Therefore, we extend these results to the interior of the positive cone of an AM-algebra as domain.

To do so, given the dual
V^{\prime}
of a normed space
V^{\prime}
, we write
V_{+}^{\prime}=\{\psi\in V^{\prime}\mid\forall x\in V_{+}:\psi(x)\geq 0\}
. If
V
is a Riesz space and
E\subseteq V
, then
E^{\disjointComplement}:=\{x\in V\mid\forall y\in E:|x|\wedge|y|=0\}
is the
disjoint complement
of
E
. The order dual and the order continuous dual of
V
are denoted by
V^{\sim}
and
V_{n}^{\sim}
, respectively. For brevity, the linear space of singular functionals is denoted by
V_{s}^{\sim}:=(V_{n}^{\sim})^{\disjointComplement}
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra with unit. Our dual representations rely on the following geometric transformations of the sets
(V_{n}^{\sim})_{+}
and
(V_{s}^{\sim})_{+}
:

\displaystyle V_{n,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim}

\displaystyle:=\big\{l_{c}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty)\mid\log\circ l_{c}\circ\exp\in(V^{\sim}_{n})_{+}\big\},

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad V_{s,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim}

\displaystyle:=\big\{l_{s}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty)\,\big|\,\log\circ l_{s}\circ\exp\in(V^{\sim}_{s})_{+}\big\}.

Note that a functional
l
contained in one of those sets is positively homogeneous and satisfies
l(x^{a})=l(x)^{a}
for all
x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
and
a\in(0,\infty)
, i.e.,
l
is geometrically linear.

Theorem 2.20
.

Let
V
be an infinite-dimensional AM-algebra with unit
\unit

and assume a proper map
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]
. Then,
\widetilde{\rho}
is a GG-convex RRM if and only if it admits the representation

(2.4)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(x)=\max\limits_{l\in L}\frac{l(x)}{\tilde{a}\big(l\big)},\quad x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+}),

where

(2.5)

\displaystyle L=\Bigg\{l_{c}\cdot l_{s}\,\Bigg|\,l_{c}\in V_{n,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim},l_{s}\in V_{s,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim},\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\geq 1\\
\lVert\log(x)\rVert_{\infty}=1\end{subarray}}\Big(l_{c}(x)l_{s}(x)\Big)=e\Bigg\},

and
\tilde{a}:L\rightarrow(0,\infty]
is given by

(2.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}\big(l\big)=\sup\limits_{x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})}\frac{l(x)}{\widetilde{\rho}(x)}=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}l(x),\quad l\in L.

Before proving this result, we would like to mention that the proof relies on
V^{\prime}=V^{\sim}
. Therefore, let us collect some facts about the dual space
V^{\prime}
of an AM-space
V
, which implicitly also occurs in the definition of the set

in Theorem
2.20
, because
V^{\prime}=(V^{\sim}_{n})\oplus(V^{\sim}_{s})
. First,
V^{\prime}
is an AL-space, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.27)
. Second,
V^{\prime}
is lattice isometric to an
L^{1}(\mu)
-space for some measure
\mu
, see
(
Aliprantis and Burkinshaw 1985
, Theorem 12.26)
. Finally,
V^{\prime}=(V^{\prime\prime})^{\sim}_{n}
, i.e.,
V^{\prime}
is equal to the order continuous dual of
V^{\prime\prime}
, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.34)
.

Proof of Theorem
2.20
.

We first prove the “only if” part. By Proposition
2.16
and the proof of Theorem
2.18
, we have that
\rho=\exp\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\log
is a proper, convex and Lipschitz-continuous risk measure. Hence,
\rho
is also
\sigma(V,V^{\prime})
-lower semicontinuous. By
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.38)
and
V
being infinite-dimensional, we obtain that
V
is non-reflexive. By

being an AM-algebra it is also a Banach space. Concluding,
V
is a non-reflexive Banach space, which allows for the application of
(
Kountzakis 2011

, from which we obtain the dual representation

(2.7)

\displaystyle\rho(x)=\sup_{\psi\in B_{\unit}}(\psi(x)-\rho^{\star}(\psi)),

where
\rho^{\star}(\psi)=\sup\limits_{x\in V}\big(\psi(x)-\rho(x)\big)
for
\psi\in V^{\prime}
is the Fenchel-Legendre transform of
\rho
and
B_{\unit}=\left\{\psi\in V^{\prime}_{+}\mid\hat{\unit}(\psi)=1\right\}
with
\hat{\unit}
being the embedding of
\unit
in
V^{\prime\prime}
, i.e.,
\hat{\unit}(\psi)=\psi(\unit)
for all
\psi\in V^{\prime}
. Next, note that cash-additivity of
\rho
implies

\rho(x)=\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,x-m\unit\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\},\quad x\in V.

Using a standard argumentation
8
8

8

The ansatz to show that the Fenchel-Legendre conjugate
\rho^{\star}

\rho

\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho}}

(

.

\displaystyle\rho^{\star}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sup\limits_{x\in V}\Big(\psi(x)-\inf\{m\in\mathbb{R}\,|\,x-m\unit\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{A}_{\rho}}\psi(y)+\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}}\big(\psi(\unit)-1\big)m

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho}}(\psi)+\infty\cdot(1-\mathbf{1}_{\{1\}}\big(\psi(\unit)\big)\big).

In particular, if
\psi\in B_{\unit}

. Now, we show that the maximum is attained. To do so, let

. By

Remark 2.21
.

The proof of Theorem
2.20
relies on the dual representation for monetary risk measures on non-reflexive Banach spaces in
Kountzakis 2011
, which does not ensure that the supremum is attained. We established this by following the hint in
(
Farkas et al. 2015
, Remark 18)
. We could also use other dual representations, provided the domain covers AM-spaces with unit, e.g.,
(
Farkas et al. 2015
, Theorem 3)
. However, their framework includes multiple eligible assets, which makes the treatment more involved. We also do not apply
(
Biagini and Frittelli 2009
, Theorem 1)
, since
\rho
is not assumed to be cash-additive, so the condition
\hat{\unit}(l)=1
does not appear explicitly in the dual representation. Moreover, the results in
(
Konstantinides and Kountzakis 2011
, Section 3)
are not applicable, as an AM-space cannot be both reflexive and infinite dimensional, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 9.38)
.

For illustration, let us specify the order duals from Theorem
2.20
for the case of
V=L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For this, we define
\mathbf{ba}_{\mathbb{P}}:=\{\mu\in\mathbf{ba}\,|\,\mu\ll\mathbb{P}\}
, where
\mathbf{ba}
denotes the linear space of all finitely additive measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
whose total variation is finite. Analogously, we define
\mathbf{ca}_{\mathbb{P}}:=\{\mu\in\mathbf{ca}\,|\,\mu\ll\mathbb{P}\}
, where
\mathbf{ca}\subseteq\mathbf{ba}
is the set of countably additive set functions.

Example 2.22
.

If
V=L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
V^{\prime}=\big(\mathbf{ba}_{\mathbb{P}}\big)^{n}
, and by the Yosida-Hewitt decomposition of
\mathbf{ba}_{\mathbb{P}}
, see
Yosida and Hewitt 1952
, we obtain that
V^{\prime}=L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\oplus L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\disjointComplement}=\mathbf{ca}_{\mathbb{P}}\oplus L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\disjointComplement}
. Note,
L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\disjointComplement}

refers to the set of purely additive measures absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathbb{P}
. The fact that the linear spaces in the Yosida-Hewitt decomposition are disjoint complements allows us to apply Lemma
A.4
to obtain
V_{+}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{ca}_{\mathbb{P}})_{+}\oplus(L^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\disjointComplement})_{+}
.

The connection to the dual representation of monetary risk measures in
(
Föllmer and Schied 2016
, Theorem 4.16)
becomes apparent by noting that, for each
l\in L
there exists
\mu\in\big(\mathbf{ba}_{\mathbb{P}}\big)^{n}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}(\Omega)=1
such that
l(x)=\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\log(x_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}\Big)
and therefore,

\tilde{a}(l)=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathcal{B}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}\exp\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\log(x_{i})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu_{i}\Big).

The next natural step is to determine conditions under which singular functionals, i.e.,
l_{s}\in V_{s,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim}
, no longer appear in the dual representation. This is possible under further continuity assumptions on the RRM. However, the price to pay is that the supremum in the resulting dual representation does not need to be attained. We begin by studying a continuity property of RRMs.

Lemma 2.23
.

Let
V
be an AM-space with unit. The map
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]
is an RRM and
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
. The following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\rho
is
\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})
-lower semicontinuous;

(b)

\widetilde{\rho}
is
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})\big)
-lower semicontinuous.

Proof.

\rho
being
\sigma\big(V,V_{n}^{\sim}\big)
-lower semicontinuous is equivalent to
\{x\in V\mid\rho(x)\leq c\}
being
\sigma\big(V,V_{n}^{\sim}\big)
-closed for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. By
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
, this is equivalent to
\big\{x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\,\big|\,\widetilde{\rho}(x)\leq\tilde{c}\big\}
being
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})\big)
-closed for all
\tilde{c}\in(0,\infty)
. By
\widetilde{\rho}>0
, for all
\tilde{c}\in(-\infty,0]
we have

\big\{x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\,\big|\,\widetilde{\rho}(x)\leq\tilde{c}\big\}=\emptyset,

and hence, those sets are
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})\big)
-closed. This proves the claim.
∎

For brevity, we keep the proof of the our final dual representation as short as possible.

Corollary 2.24
.

Let
V
be an AM-algebra with unit
\unit
and let
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]
be a proper, GG-convex and
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})\big)
-lower semicontinuous map. Then,
\widetilde{\rho}
admits the dual representation

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(x)=\sup\limits_{l\in L}\frac{l(x)}{\tilde{a}\big(l\big)},\quad x\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+}),

where

L=\Bigg\{l\in V_{n,+}^{\text{GG,}\sim}\,\Bigg|\,\sup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\geq 1\\
\lVert\log(x)\rVert_{\infty}=1\end{subarray}}l(x)=e\Bigg\},

and
\tilde{a}:L\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is given as in (
2.6
).

Proof.

Use the transform
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
, the imposed assumptions and Lemma
2.23
to obtain that
\rho
is finite-valued, convex and
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
-lower semicontinuous. Thus, by applying the Fenchel-Moreau theorem
(
Zălinescu 2002
, Theorem 2.3.3)
and using analogous arguments as in the proof of Theorem
2.20
, the desired claim follows.
∎

Finally, we ask for a weaker lower semicontinuity assumption such that the dual representation in Corollary
2.24
still holds. We need the following “Kômlos”-type condition. Moreover, in a Riesz space
V
, we say that a net
(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq V

order converges
to
X\in V
, denoted by
X_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{o}X
, if there exists a net
(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq V
such that
Z_{\alpha}\downarrow 0
and for all
\alpha
it holds that
|X_{\alpha}-X|\leq Z_{\alpha}
.

Definition 2.25
.

A linear topology
\tau
on a Riesz space
V
has the
C-property
if for all nets
(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq V
with
X_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\tau}X
for some
X\in V
there exists a subsequence
(X_{\alpha_{n}})_{n}\subseteq(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
and convex combinations
Z_{n}\in\text{conv}(X_{\alpha_{n}},X_{\alpha_{n+1},\dots})
such that
Z_{n}\xrightarrow{o}X
.

For a Riesz space
V
, a map
f:V\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is
order lower semicontinuous
, if for any net
(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq V
and
X\in V
with
X_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{o}X
it holds that
f(X)\leq\liminf\limits_{\alpha}f(X_{\alpha})
. The following result shows that order lower semicontinuity of the underlying monetary risk measure is weaker than
\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})
-lower semicontinuity.

Proposition 2.26
.

Let
V
be an AM-space with unit
\unit
,
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})\rightarrow(0,\infty]
an RRM and set
\rho=\log\circ\widetilde{\rho}\circ\exp
. Consider the following statements:

(a)

\widetilde{\rho}
is
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})\big)
-lower semicontinuous;

(b)

\widetilde{\rho}
is logarithmically order lower semicontinuous, i.e., for all nets
(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
and
X\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})
with
\log(X_{\alpha})\xrightarrow{o}\log(X)
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(X)\leq\liminf\limits_{\alpha}\widetilde{\rho}(X_{\alpha})
;

(c)

\rho
is order lower semicontinuous.

Then, the implications
(a)\implies(b)\Longleftrightarrow(c)
hold. If
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
satisfies the C-property, then in addition it holds that
(b)\implies(a)
.

Proof.

The implications “
(a)\implies(b)
” and — if
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
satisfies the C-property — “
(c)\implies(a)
” follow by applying Lemma
2.23
and subsequently using
(
Biagini and Frittelli 2009
, Proposition 24)
. This result is applicable, because, by being a Banach lattice,
V
equipped with its induced sup-norm topology is a locally convex Fréchet lattice. To prove that (b) implies (c), assume a net
(X_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq V
and
X\in V
with
X_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{o}X
. Then, by the definition of order convergence, there exists a net
(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\downarrow 0
such that
|X_{\alpha}-X|\leq Z_{\alpha}
for each
\alpha
. Hence,

\Big|\log\big(e^{X_{\alpha}}\big)-\log\big(e^{X}\big)\Big|\leq\log\big(e^{Z_{\alpha}}\big)

for each
\alpha
. Then, by
\widetilde{\rho}
being logarithmically order lower semicontinuous, we obtain that

\liminf\limits_{\alpha}\rho(X_{\alpha})=\log\Big(\liminf\limits_{\alpha}\widetilde{\rho}\big(e^{X_{\alpha}}\big)\Big)\geq\log\Big(\widetilde{\rho}\big(e^{X}\big)\Big)=\rho(X),

which implies
(c)
. The implication “
(c)\implies(b)
” follows analogously.
∎

Proposition
2.26
tells us that if the C-property is satisfied, we can substitute
\exp\big(\sigma(V,V_{n}^{\sim})\big)
-lower semicontinuity by logarithmically order lower semicontinuity of the RRM to obtain the dual representation in Corollary
2.24
.

In the next example, we demonstrate that
\sigma\big(L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n}),L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
has the C-property.

Example 2.27
.

Note, for each
p\in[1,\infty]
the linear space
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with its induced
L^{p}
-norm topology is a locally convex Fréchet lattice. Furthermore, the identity function injectively maps
L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
and it is a linear lattice homomorphism. Additionally, it holds that

\big(L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)^{\prime}=(L^{\infty})^{n}=\big(L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)^{\sim}_{n}.

Thus, by applying
(
Biagini and Frittelli 2009
, Lemma 22)
we obtain that the weak topology
\sigma\big(L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n}),L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
satisfies the C-property.

Based on the previous example, one might conjecture that, for every AM-algebra
V
, the weak topology
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
satisfies the C-property. However, this intuition is misleading, as the following counterexample shows.

Example 2.28
.

Let
V=C([0,1])
, which is an AM-space and equipped with its induced norm topology it is also a locally convex Frêchet lattice. By
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Example 8.29)
we have that
V_{n}^{\sim}=\{0\}
. Hence,

\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})=\sigma(V,\{0\})=\{\emptyset,V\},

i.e.,
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
is the trivial topology in which every sequence
(f_{n})_{n}\subseteq C([0,1])
converges to every point
f\in V
. Now, set

f_{n}(x)=\begin{cases}1-nx,&x\in[0,n^{-1}),\\
0,&x\in[n^{-1},1],\end{cases}

and choose an arbitrary convex sequence
Z_{n}\in\text{conv}(f_{n},f_{n+1},\dots)
. In particular,
Z_{n}\xrightarrow{\sigma(V,\{0\})}0
. However, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\lVert Z_{n}\rVert_{\infty}=Z_{n}(0)=1
. This contradicts that for any sequence
(h_{n})_{n}\subseteq C([0,1])
with
h_{n}\xrightarrow{o}0
we obtain that
\lVert h_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}0
. Indeed, by order convergence, there exists a sequence
(g_{n})_{n}\subseteq C([0,1])
with
g_{n}\downarrow 0
and
|h_{n}|\leq g_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Dini’s Theorem, see
(
Rudin 1976
, Theorem 7.13)
, we obtain that the pointwise convergence of
g_{n}
towards
0
is even uniformly and hence,
\lVert g_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which implies that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert h_{n}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert g_{n}\rVert_{\infty}=0.

Concluding,
(Z_{n})_{n}
is not order convergent to
0
and hence,
\sigma(V,V^{\sim}_{n})
does not satisfy the C-property.

3.
Systemic return risk measures

Now, we represent RRMs for multivariate losses as compositions of RRMs for one-dimensional losses and aggregation functions. Our decompositions are inspired by the theory of systemic risk measures. For simplicity, we impose the following assumption throughout the rest of this section.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
I\subseteq(0,\infty)
be an interval. Moreover,
V=L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
E\subseteq L^{\infty}_{++}
is a GG-convex cone with
(0,\infty)\subseteq E
, i.e.,
E
contains all positive constants. Also the set

E_{I}:=\{X\in E\mid X(\omega)\in I\ \omega\text{-a.s.}\}

is assumed to be a GG-convex cone.

Remark 3.2
.

The important cases for
I
are
(0,\infty)
and
[1,\infty)
. If
I=(0,\infty)
, then
E_{I}=E
. If
I=[1,\infty)
, then
E_{I}
contains all random variables
X\in E
with
X\geq 1
a.s., i.e.,
E_{I}
refers to random variables leading to pure logarithmic losses (
\log(X)\geq 0
a.s.).

Note that also
E^{n}
is a GG-convex cone. For our result, we adapt the definition of systemic risk measures to the framework of RRMs.

Definition 3.3
.

An increasing map
\widetilde{\rho}:E^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is a
systemic RRM
, if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\widetilde{\rho}\big((0,\infty)^{n}\big)=I

(
I
-surjectivity on constants)
;

(2)

\widetilde{\rho}|_{(0,\infty)^{n}}(E^{n})=E_{I}
;

(3)

For all
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in E^{n}
with
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}(\omega))\leq\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y}(\omega))

\omega
-a.s. it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})\leq\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y})

(preference consistency)
.

A systemic RRM
\widetilde{\rho}
is a
GG-convex systemic RRM
, if it is GG-convex and satisfies the following:

(4)

For all
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z}\in E^{n}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
with
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Z}(\omega))=\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}(\omega))^{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y}(\omega))^{1-\lambda}

\omega

\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Z})\leq\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})^{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y})^{1-\lambda}

(risk GG-convexity)
.

If
\widetilde{\rho}
is a positively homogeneous GG-convex systemic RRM, then we call it a
GG-coherent systemic RRM
.

Definition 3.4

An increasing and surjective map

A map

Remark 3.6
.

Theorem
3.5
is the RRM counterpart of similar decompositions for classical systemic risk measures. The starting point is
(
Chen et al. 2013
, Theorem 1)
, where the domain of the systemic risk measure is finite-dimensional and both convexity and positive homogeneity are imposed. Then,
(
Kromer et al. 2016
, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1)
are the extensions of this result to systemic risk measures defined on locally convex-solid Riesz subspaces of the space of all measurable functions on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
.

Theorem
3.5
allows us to effectively construct systemic RRMs, by choosing an RRM on
E_{I}
and a return aggregation function. The next result shows that classical aggregation functions can be used to obtain return aggregation functions, thereby providing a wide range on possibilities to design return aggregation functions.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\log(I)

\Lambda(\log(E^{n}))=\log(E_{I})
. Then,

\tilde{\Lambda}:(0,\infty)^{n}\rightarrow I,\ x\mapsto(\exp\circ\Lambda\circ\log)(x)

is a (GG-convex) return aggregation function.

The map
\tilde{\Lambda}
as composition of three increasing functions is increasing. Moreover,

\tilde{\Lambda}\big((0,\infty)^{n}\big)=\exp\big(\Lambda(\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)=\exp(\log(I))=I

and

\tilde{\Lambda}(E^{n})=\exp\bigg(\Lambda\big(\log(E^{n})\big)\bigg)=\exp(\log(E_{I}))=E_{I}.

Hence,
\tilde{\Lambda}
is a return aggregation function. If
\Lambda
is convex, then for all

and

The proof of Theorem
3.5
heavily relies on the untypical conditions of preference consistency and risk GG-convexity. To obtain a sophisticated picture, we illustrate situations in which GG-convex RRMs do not satisfy either preference consistency or risk GG-convexity. We start with a GG-convex RRM, which do not satisfy preference consistency. Such an example is absent even from the seminal paper of
Chen et al. 2013
, thereby filling an important gap in the literature.

Example 3.8
.

Assume
I=(0,\infty)
and
\widetilde{\rho}:(L_{++}^{\infty})^{2}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, defined by
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\mathbb{E}[X_{2}]}
. Obviously, this map is increasing and positively homogeneous. In addition, for all
x\in(0,\infty)
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}((x,x)^{\intercal})=x
, i.e.,
\widetilde{\rho}\big((0,\infty)^{2}\big)=(0,\infty)
, and for every
X\in L_{++}^{\infty}
we have
\widetilde{\rho}|_{(0,\infty)^{2}}((X,X)^{\intercal})=X
, i.e.,
\widetilde{\rho}|_{(0,\infty)^{2}}\big((L_{++}^{\infty})^{2}\big)=L_{++}^{\infty}
. Thus, properties (1) and (2) in Definition
3.3
are satisfied. Next, for
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in(L_{++}^{\infty})^{2}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
it holds by Hölder’s inequality that
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}^{\lambda}\mathbf{Y}^{1-\lambda})\leq\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})^{\lambda}\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y})^{1-\lambda}
. Thus,
\widetilde{\rho}
is a GG-convex RRM. However,
\widetilde{\rho}
does not satisfy preference consistency. Indeed, let
\mathbf{X}
satisfy
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathbf{X}=(10,0.1)^{\intercal}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mathbf{X}=(0.1,10)^{\intercal}\big)=0.5
and let
\mathbf{Y}=(1,1)^{\intercal}
. Then, for almost every
\omega\in\Omega
it holds that

\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}(\omega))=\sqrt{10\cdot 0.1}=1=\sqrt{1\cdot 1}=\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y}(\omega)),

but
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})=\sqrt{5.05\cdot 5.05}=5.05>1=\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{Y})
, showing that
\widetilde{\rho}
is not preference consistent. The latter property is used in the proof of Theorem
3.5
to ensure that
\rho_{0}
defined in (
3.1
) is indeed well-defined.

In this example, losses are not aggregated before the information contained in the random variables is reduced to real values. Instead, the order is reversed: the reduction is performed first via the expectation operator, yielding scalar quantities, which are then aggregated by taking their product. Consequently, preference consistency necessitates an accumulation step through a return aggregation function
\tilde{\Lambda}
prior to reducing the information contained in the random variables via
\widetilde{\rho}_{0}
.

Our next goal is to show that every GG-convex and positively homogeneous RRM satisfies risk GG-convexity. This expands the observation for systemic risk measures in
Chen et al. 2013

satisfies risk GG-convexity.

Proposition 3.12

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\inf\{m\in(0,\infty)\mid\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})\leq m\}=\inf\{m\in(0,\infty)\mid(\widetilde{\rho}_{0}\circ\tilde{\Lambda})(\mathbf{X})\leq m\}.

4.
Separability of vector-valued return risk measures

Finally, we study maps
\widetilde{\rho}:E^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty]^{n}
with
E\subseteq L^{\infty}_{+}
. Motivated by the study of
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
, we are focusing on the discussion of sufficient conditions such that
\widetilde{\rho}
can be written as
\rho(\mathbf{X})=\big(\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\dots,\widetilde{\rho}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\big)^{\intercal}
, where each
\widetilde{\rho}_{i}
is also an RRM, i.e., measuring the risk of the multivariate position
\mathbf{X}
simplifies to measuring the risk of each component of
\mathbf{X}
separately. Hence, in such situations, the multidimensionality of the codomain can de facto be ignored.

We formalize this situation by the separability property of an RRM, which we define next.

Definition 4.1
.

Assume a GG-convex cone
E\subseteq L^{\infty}_{+}
with
(0,\infty)\subseteq E
and an RRM
{\widetilde{\rho}:E^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty]^{n}}
. Then, we introduce the following properties:

(1)

\widetilde{\rho}
is
separable
, if there exist RRMs
\tilde{r}_{1},\dots,\tilde{r}_{n}
on
E
such that
\widetilde{\rho}=(\tilde{r}_{1},\dots,\tilde{r}_{n})^{\intercal}
.

(2)

\widetilde{\rho}
is
pointwise positively homogeneous
, if for all
\mathbf{X}\in E^{n}
and
z\in(0,\infty)^{n}
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(z\mathbf{X})=z\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})
, where multiplications are performed pointwise.

(3)

\widetilde{\rho}
satisfies the
marginal domination property
, if for every
i\in[n]
there exists a function
\tilde{f}_{i}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
with
\widetilde{\rho}_{i}(z)\leq\tilde{f}_{i}(z_{i})
for all
z\in(0,\infty)^{n}
.

Under pointwise positive homogeneity, separability of the RRM follows immediately, even without assuming GG-convexity or any additional continuity assumption. Our result also clarifies that — in case of a monotone and (pointwise) cash-additive functional — the additional assumptions of convexity and lower semicontinuity in
(
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
, Corollary 3.6)
are superfluous.

Corollary 4.2
.

A pointwise positively homogeneous RRM
\widetilde{\rho}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)\rightarrow(0,\infty)^{n}
is separable.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{X}\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
. Without loss of generality, we prove the statement for the first component of
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})
. By monotonicity and pointwise positive homogeneity of
\widetilde{\rho}
, we obtain that

\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(\mathbf{X})\geq\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\big((\mathbf{X}_{1},\essinf\mathbf{X}_{2},\dots,\essinf\mathbf{X}_{n})^{\intercal}\big)=\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\big((\mathbf{X}_{1},1,\dots,1)^{\intercal}\big).

Analogously, we have that

\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(\mathbf{X})\leq\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\big((\mathbf{X}_{1},\esssup\mathbf{X}_{2},\dots,\esssup\mathbf{X}_{n})^{\intercal}\big)=\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\big((\mathbf{X}_{1},1,\dots,1)^{\intercal}\big).

Hence,
\widetilde{\rho}_{1}(\mathbf{X})=\tilde{r}_{1}
, where
\tilde{r}_{1}:\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L^{\infty}_{+}\big)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
is defined by
\tilde{r}_{1}(X)=\widetilde{\rho}_{1}\big((\mathbf{X}_{1},1,\dots,1)^{\intercal}\big)
.
∎

The proof strongly relies on pointwise positive homogeneity, which is stronger than positive homogeneity. On the other hand, even under GG-convexity, positive homogeneity does not imply pointwise positive homogeneity, as the next example shows.

Example 4.3
.

For all
\mathbf{X}\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
, we set

\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})=\Big(\max_{i\in[n]}\,\esssup X_{i}\Big)\mathbf{1},

where
\mathbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We directly see that this is a positively homogeneous RRM. In addition, it is also GG-convex. However, let
n=2
and assume the constant (random) vector
\mathbf{X}=(1,2)^{\intercal}
. Then, for
z=\Big(2,\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{\intercal}
it holds that

\widetilde{\rho}(z\mathbf{X})=\widetilde{\rho}\big((2,1)^{\intercal}\big)=(2,2)\neq(4,1)=\Big(2,\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{\intercal}\widetilde{\rho}\big((1,2)^{\intercal}\big)=z\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}).

Thus,
\widetilde{\rho}
is not pointwise positively homogeneous.

, we have that
L_{\tilde{r}_{i}}
equipped with its Minkowski norm is a Banach lattice, from which (i) follows.

We prove the properties in (ii) one by one. Monotonicity of
\tilde{f}
is a direct consequence of monotonicity of
\widetilde{\rho}
. Next, let
\mathbf{X}\in L^{\infty}_{++}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\lambda>0
. Then, we obtain that

\tilde{f}(\lambda\mathbf{X})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\rho}\big((\lambda\mathbf{X})\vee m^{-1}\big)=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\lambda\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{X}\vee(\lambda m)^{-1}\big).

For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
it holds that

\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{X}\vee(\lceil\lambda m\rceil)^{-1}\big)\leq\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{X}\vee(\lambda m)^{-1}\big)\leq\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{X}\vee(\lfloor\lambda m\rfloor)^{-1}\big),

which shows that
\tilde{f}(\lambda\mathbf{X})=\lambda\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X})
. For GG-convexity, let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\in L_{++}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then,

\displaystyle\tilde{f}\big(\mathbf{X}^{\lambda}\mathbf{Y}^{1-\lambda}\big)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\rho}\big((\mathbf{X}^{\lambda}\mathbf{Y}^{1-\lambda})\vee m^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\rho}\Big(\big(\mathbf{X}\vee m^{-1}\big)^{\lambda}\big(\mathbf{Y}\vee m^{-1}\big)^{1-\lambda}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{X}\vee m^{-1}\big)^{\lambda}\,\widetilde{\rho}\big(\mathbf{Y}\vee m^{-1}\big)^{1-\lambda}=\tilde{f}\big(\mathbf{X}\big)^{\lambda}\tilde{f}\big(\mathbf{Y}\big)^{1-\lambda}.

Hence,
\tilde{f}
is GG-convex. Then, by separability of
\widetilde{\rho}
, we obtain that

\tilde{f}(\mathbf{X})=\begin{pmatrix}\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{r}_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{1}\vee m^{-1})\\
\vdots\\
\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{r}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n}\vee m^{-1})\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{X}\in L_{++}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Analogously to before, we see that for each
i\in[n]
, the map

X\mapsto\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{r}_{i}(X\vee m^{-1}),\quad X\in L_{++}^{\infty},

is a (GG-convex) RRM, which gives us that
\tilde{f}
is separable.

Finally, given
\mathbf{X}\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L_{+}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)
, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
large enough, we obtain that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
with
i\geq m
it holds that
\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X}\vee i^{-1})=\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})
, which proves that
\tilde{f}\big|_{\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big)}=\widetilde{\rho}
.

For (iii), separability and GG-convexity of
\tilde{f}\big|_{\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}
follows by (i) and (ii). For all
\mathbf{X}\in\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}}
it holds

\big(\tilde{f}\big|_{\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}\big)_{i}(\mathbf{X})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\tilde{r}_{i}(\mathbf{X}_{i}\vee m^{-1})=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\exp\Big(r_{i}\big(\log(\mathbf{X}_{i})\vee-\log(m)\big)\Big),

where
r_{i}=\log\circ\,\tilde{r}_{i}\circ\exp
. By defining
r_{i}^{E}:=\log\circ\,\tilde{r}_{i}^{E}\circ\exp
,
(
Liebrich and Svindland 2017
, Theorem 4.5)
implies that

\big(\tilde{f}\big|_{\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}\big)_{i}(\mathbf{X})=\exp\big(\inf\big\{m\in\mathbb{R}\,\big|\,r_{i}^{E}\big(\log(\mathbf{X}_{i})-m\big)\leq 0\big\}\big),\quad\mathbf{X}\in\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}},

where the infimum is larger than
-\infty
, as follows from
(
Liebrich and Svindland 2017
, Theorem 4.4)
. Hence,
\tilde{f}\big|_{\tilde{L}_{\widetilde{\rho}}}>0
. To prove Equation (
4.2
), note that the infimum can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\inf\big\{m\in\mathbb{R}\,\big|\,r_{i}^{E}\big(\log(\mathbf{X}_{i})-m\big)\leq 0\big\}

\displaystyle=\inf\Bigg\{m\in\mathbb{R}\,\Bigg|\,\tilde{r}_{i}^{E}\bigg(\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}}{e^{m}}\bigg)\leq 1\Bigg\}

\displaystyle=\log\Bigg(\inf\Bigg\{m>0\,\Bigg|\,\frac{\mathbf{X}_{i}}{m}\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{\tilde{r}_{i}}\Bigg\}\Bigg).

∎

Example 4.7
.

For parameters
\gamma_{i}>0
,
i\in[n]
, assume the following separable, GG-convex RRM:

\widetilde{\rho}(\mathbf{X})=\big(\tilde{r}_{1}(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\dots,\tilde{r}_{n}(\mathbf{X}_{n})\big)^{\intercal}=\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X_{1}}^{\gamma_{1}}]^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}},\dots,\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{X_{n}}^{\gamma_{n}}]^{\frac{1}{\gamma_{n}}}\Big)^{\intercal},\quad\mathbf{X}\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}\big(L_{+}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\big).

By
(

r_{i}(X):=(\log\circ\,\tilde{r}_{i}\circ\exp)(X)=\gamma_{i}^{-1}\log(\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma_{i}X)]),\quad X\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(L_{+}^{\infty}),

which is an entropic risk measure with parameter

. Then,
(
Liebrich and Svindland 2017
, Example 5.4)
, allows us to calculate for
i\in[n]
that

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\tilde{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\big\{X\in L_{++}^{\infty}\,\big|\,\exists\lambda>0:\exp\big(\big|\log\big(X^{\lambda}\big)\big|\big)\in L^{1}\big\}

\displaystyle=\big\{X\in L_{++}^{\infty}\,\big|\,\exists\lambda>0:X^{\lambda}\mathbf{1}_{\{X\geq 1\}}+X^{-\lambda}\mathbf{1}_{\{X<1\}}\in L^{1}\big\}.

Now, let
Y
be a random variable with density function
f_{Y}(y)=(1+y)^{-2}
,

. Then, set

a.s., from which we obtain that

\mathbb{E}\Big[X^{\lambda}\mathbf{1}_{\{X\geq 1\}}+X^{-\lambda}\mathbf{1}_{\{X<1\}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[X^{-\lambda}\Big]=\mathbb{E}[\exp(\lambda Y)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{\lambda y}}{(1+y)^{2}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\infty,

Example 4.8
.

We use the RRM counterpart of the Expected Shortfall as final example, i.e., for given probability levels
p_{i}
,
i\in[n]

\widetilde{\rho}
for every

\displaystyle(\log\circ\widetilde{\rho})(\mathbf{X})

\displaystyle=\big((\log\circ\,\tilde{r}_{1})(\mathbf{X}_{1}),\dots,(\log\circ\,\tilde{r}_{n})(\mathbf{X}_{n})\big)^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{1-p_{1}}\int_{p_{1}}^{1}\log(q_{\mathbf{X}_{1}}(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u,\dots,\frac{1}{1-p_{n}}\int_{p_{n}}^{1}\log(q_{\mathbf{X}_{n}}(u))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\Big)^{\intercal},

(

\displaystyle\tilde{L}_{\tilde{r}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\Big\{X\in L_{++}^{\infty}\,\Big|\,\forall\lambda>0:\tilde{r}_{i}\Big(\exp\big(\big|\log\big(X^{\lambda})\big|\big)\Big)<\infty\Big\}=\Bigg\{X\in L_{++}^{\infty}\,\Bigg|\,\int_{p_{i}}^{1}q_{|\log(X)|}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u<\infty\Bigg\}.

Motivated by this representation, assume a random variable

5.
Conclusion and outlook

We revisit our main results and highlight two directions for future research. Addressing the latter would require substantial modifications of the current framework as well as the development of new technical methods, and is therefore beyond the scope of this manuscript.

5.1.
Conclusion

In this manuscript, we extend the definition of RRMs to multivariate frameworks. Thereby, we model the domain as an AM-algebra, a novel approach for RRMs. This benefits from treating the possible domains of
(0,\infty)^{n}
and
L^{\infty}_{++}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
in a unifying manner. Moreover, instead of focusing on a codomain of
(0,\infty)
, we use
[0,\infty]^{m}
.

We initiate the study with epigraphic characterizations of positive homogeneity and GG-convexity. We then relate RRMs to monetary risk measures, yielding a characterization of GG-convexity via convexity of the corresponding monetary risk measure. Under suitable assumptions, RRMs take values in
(0,\infty)
and they are only locally Lipschitz-continuous; a counterexample shows that Lipschitz-continuity does not hold in general. Then, we obtain two dual representations with different sets of potential optimizers, only one of which guarantees the existence of an optimizer.

We then discuss two specific situations. First, for domain
L^{\infty}_{++}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and codomain
(0,\infty)
, we introduce the notion of a systemic RRM. We show that every GG-convex systemic RRM admits a representation as a composition of a GG-convex RRM and a GG-convex return aggregation function, a result that strongly relies on preference consistency and risk GG-convexity. We clarify the roles of these assumptions via two examples, in which either preference consistency or risk GG-convexity are not satisfied. Notably, such examples do not appear in the current literature. However, under positive homogeneity, GG-convexity implies risk GG-convexity.

Finally, we introduce vector-valued RRMs and study separability, i.e., the case in which each RRM component depends only on the corresponding loss component. Restricting to the interior of
L^{\infty}_{+}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as domain, we show that pointwise positive homogeneity implies separability, while the weaker notion of positive homogeneity is insufficient in general; as shown by a counterexample. Finally, we discuss possibilities to extend this result to domains beyond this interior.

5.2.
Outlook: Beyond AM-algebras

Even if the logarithm of the domain of an RRM does not yield a desirable vector space structure — such as an
L^{p}
-space with
p\in[1,\infty]
— it may still be possible to define the RRM via corresponding formulas for monetary risk measures. For instance, even if
\log(X)\notin L^{1}
, for
X\in L^{1}_{++}
, it is still possible to calculate the Expected Shortfall (ES) of
\log(X)
at level
p\in[0,1)
as

\es_{p}\big(\log(X)\big):=\frac{1}{1-p}\int_{p}^{1}q_{\log(X)}(u)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}{u}.

However, for
X,Y\in L^{1}_{++}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
we do not obtain
X^{\lambda}Y^{1-\lambda}\in L^{1}_{++}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
in general. Hence, the problem of extending beyond the framework of AM-algebras while maintaining GG-convexity well-defined remains open.

A starting point to answer this question is the fact that every AM-algebra with (approximate) unit is an f-algebra
(
Muñoz-Lahoz and Tradacete 2024
, Corollary 2.13)
. An
f-algebra
is a Riesz algebra with the additional property that for all
x,y\in V_{+}
with
|x|\wedge|y|=0
it follows that
(xz)\wedge y=(zx)\wedge y=0
for all
z\in V_{+}
. A sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in a Riesz space
V
is called relatively uniformly convergent to
x\in V
, whenever there exist some
u\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
in
\mathbb{R}
such that
|x_{n}-x|<\varepsilon_{n}u
for all
n
. A sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
in Riesz space
V
is called uniformly Cauchy whenever there exists some
u\in(0,\infty)
such that for each
\varepsilon>0
we have
|x_{n}-x_{m}|<\varepsilon u
for all
n
and
m
. A Riesz space
V
is then called uniformly complete if every uniformly Cauchy sequence is relatively uniformly convergent.

To define GG-convexity, we need to introduce powers of positive elements. The following result from
(
Beukers and Huijsmans 1984
, Corollary 6)
allows us to define
x^{r}
for a positive element
x
and
r\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
. For this, recall that a Riesz space
V
is Archimedean, if for all
x\in V_{+}
it holds that
n^{-1}x\downarrow 0
.
12
12

12

A Riesz space
V
is Dedekind complete, if every nonempty subset that is bounded from above has a supremum. Every Dedekind complete Riesz space is automatically Archimedean, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Lemma 8.4)
.
By
(
Aliprantis and Burkinshaw 1985
, Theorem 8.21)
, every Archimedean f-algebra is commutative.

Corollary 5.1
.

Let
V
be an Archimedean uniformly complete f-algebra with unit element. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
y\in V_{+}
, there exists a unique
x\in V_{+}
such that
x^{n}=y
. The element
x
from the previous result is called the
n
-th root of
y
and we define
y:=x^{\frac{1}{n}}
.

Consequently, for
r=\frac{q}{p}
with
p,q\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
set
x^{r}=(x^{p})^{\frac{1}{q}}
. However, by using f-algebras we still lose track of powers for irrational numbers. Therefore, one needs to find out which additional assumptions an f-algebra needs to satisfy such that
y^{t}
is defined for all
y\in V_{+}
and
t>0
and perform an analysis for RRMs on corresponding f-algebras. In conclusion, studying risk measures on f-algebras appears to be a promising direction for future research to better understand the compatibility between risk measures and algebraic multiplications.

5.3.
Outlook: Set-valued RRMs

The study of systemic and vector-valued RRMs in Sections
3
and
4
motivates the study of corresponding set-valued maps. Instead of capital reserves as outputs, they yield allocations that render the vector-valued loss acceptable, thereby focusing on managerial actions required after measuring the risk.
The theory of set-valued risk measures is quite large and it started with the seminal work of
Jouini et al. 2004
. Consequences of separability results on set-valued risk measures are discussed in
Ararat and Feinstein 2024
. Studying dual representations of set-valued RRMs can complement our RRM dual representations. Dual representations of set-valued risk measures can be found in
Hamel and Heyde 2010
;
Hamel et al. 2011
;
Molchanov and Cascos 2016
. The importance of set-valued maps in the context of systemic risk is demonstrated in
Feinstein et al. 2017
. Recent developments focuses on star-shaped set-valued risk measures and the consistency between set-valued risk measures and stochastic orders, see
Nie et al. 2025
and
Mastrogiacomo and Tarsia 2026
.

Corollary A.1
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space. Then,

\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(C(K)_{+})=\{f\in C(K)\mid\forall x\in K:f(x)>0\}.

Proof.

Let
f\in\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(C(K)_{+})
. There exists
\varepsilon>0
with
B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(f,\varepsilon)\subseteq C(K)_{+}
. By a proof of contradiction, assume there exists
x_{0}\in K
with
f(x_{0})=0
. Then for sufficiently small
\delta>0
the map
g=f-\delta\,\mathbf{1}_{K}
satisfies
\lVert g-f\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, i.e.,
g\in B_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(f,\varepsilon)
and hence,
g\in C(K)_{+}
. However,
g(x_{0})<0
implies that
g\notin C(K)_{+}
, a contradiction. Thus,

\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(C(K)_{+})\subseteq\{f\in C(K)\mid\forall x\in K:f(x)>0\}.

Now, let
f(x)>0
for all
x\in K
. By
K
being compact and
f
being (lower semi-)continuous,
f
attains a minimum on
K
, see
(
Aliprantis and Border 2006
, Theorem 2.43)
. Hence,

r:=\min_{x\in K}f(x)>0.

Then, for
\varepsilon=\frac{r}{2}
and for each
g\in C(K)
with
\lVert g-f\rVert_{\infty}<\varepsilon
, we obtain for all

g(x)\geq f(x)-\varepsilon\geq r-\frac{r}{2}=\frac{r}{2}>0,

∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\Phi:X\rightarrow Y
be a bijection between two nonempty sets
X
and
Y
and
\tau
is a topology on
X
. Then, for
\Phi(\tau)=\{\Phi(U)\mid U\in\tau\}
the following statements hold:

(i)

\Phi(\tau)
is a topology on
Y
;

(ii)

For a net
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}\subseteq X

x_{\alpha}\xrightarrow{\tau}x
it holds that
\Phi(x_{\alpha})\xrightarrow{\Phi(\tau)}\Phi(x)
.

Proof.

For part (i), we verify the defining properties of a topology. Obviously,
\Phi(\emptyset)=\emptyset\in\Phi(\tau)
and by
\Phi
being surjective, we have
\Phi(X)=Y\in\Phi(\tau)

(V_{i})_{i\in I}\subseteq\Phi(\tau)
. Then, for every
i\in I

U_{i}\in\tau

. We obtain that

\bigcup_{i\in I}V_{i}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\Phi\left(U_{i}\right)=\Phi\left(\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}\right)\in\Phi(\tau).

So,
\Phi(\tau)
is closed under arbitrary unions. Finally, let
V_{1}=\Phi(U_{1})
and
V_{2}=\Phi(U_{2})
with
U_{1},U_{2}\in\tau
. Then,
\Phi
being bijective implies that

V_{1}\cap V_{2}=\Phi(U_{1})\cap\Phi(U_{2})=\Phi(U_{1}\cap U_{2})\in\Phi(\tau).

A simple induction shows that
\Phi(\tau)
is being closed under finite intersections.

To prove (ii), let
N
be an arbitrary open neighborhood of
\Phi(x)
in
(Y,\Phi(\tau))
. There exists
U\in\tau
with
N=\Phi(U)
. By
\Phi(x)\in\Phi(U)
and
\Phi
being injective, it holds that
x\in U
. By assumption, there exists
\alpha_{0}
such that for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
we obtain that
x_{\alpha}\in U
. Applying
\Phi
, we obtain for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
that
\Phi(x_{\alpha})\in N
. This means that
\Phi(x_{\alpha})\xrightarrow{\Phi(\tau)}\Phi(x)
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

Let
V
be an AM-space with order unit and

\exp(\tau)=\tau|_{\interior_{\lVert.\rVert_{\infty}}(V_{+})}.

.

Appendix B
Positive real line as domain

We add two findings for GG-convex functions
f:(0,\infty)^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
. The first one is based on Proposition
2.16
(ii), from which we obtain the following characterization as counterpart to analogous representations under so-called logarithmic convexity.

Corollary B.1
.

Let
f:(0,\infty)^{n}\rightarrow(0,\infty)
be GG-convex and differentiable. For all
x,y\in(0,\infty)^{n}
it holds that

\exp\left(\frac{\nabla f(x)}{f(x)}\log\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{\intercal}\right)\leq\frac{f(y)}{f(x)},

where
\frac{y}{x}
refers to pointwise division.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in(0,\infty)^{n}
. By Proposition
2.16
,
f
is GG-convex if and only if
g=\log\circ f\circ\exp
is convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This means, for
\tilde{x}=\log(x)
and
\tilde{y}=\log(y)
it holds that

(\tilde{y}-\tilde{x})^{\intercal}\nabla g(\tilde{x})\leq g(\tilde{y})-g(\tilde{x}).

By noting that
\nabla g(\tilde{x})=\frac{\nabla f(x)}{f(x)}
, we obtain

\big(\log(y)-\log(x)\big)^{\intercal}\frac{\nabla f(x)}{f(x)}\leq\log(f(y))-\log(f(x)),

from which the claim follows.
∎

GG-convexity — as well as convexity — is a weaker property than logarithmic convexity, due to the AM-GM inequality. For the AM-GM inequality we refer to
Aldaz 2009
. In the following, we state examples of functions satisfying only convexity or GG-convexity. Those examples complement the ones in
(
Aygün et al. 2024
, Example 8)
.

Example B.2
.

We seek functions
f:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
that satisfy either GG-convexity or convexity, but not both.

(1)

GG-convex, but not convex:
Choose
f(x)=x^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then,
f
is obviously GG-convex and not convex.

(2)

Convex, but not GG-convex:
Choose

f(x)=\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}=x^{2}-x+1.

Then
f
is obviously convex. However, for
g=\log\circ f\circ\exp
it holds for all
y\in\mathbb{R}
that

g^{\prime\prime}(y)=\frac{\e^{y}(-\e^{2y}+4\e^{y}-1)}{(\e^{2y}-\e^{y}+1)^{2}}.

Set,
z=\e^{y}
. Then, for
z>2+\sqrt{3}
it holds that
g^{\prime\prime}(\log(z))<0
, giving that
g
is not convex and hence, by Proposition
2.16
(ii)
f
is not GG-convex.
