Pediatric extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support at a single center: Outcomes by indication, prognostic factors, and duration
Authors: L. Lily Rosenthal, Andreas W. Flemmer, Kai Martin Förster, Mathias Bruegel, Jürgen Hörer, Nikolaus A. Haas, Christian Hagl, Sebastian Sadoni, Oliver J Muensterer, Sebastian Georg Michel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pediatric extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (pECMO) provides life-saving cardiopulmonary support for children with severe cardiac or respiratory failure. Identifying prognostic factors may improve risk stratification and clinical decision-making.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 149 patients (<18 years) who received pECMO at a single center between 2003 and 2025. Patients with multiple extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) runs during the same hospitalization were excluded. Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and outcome data were collected. The primary endpoint was 30-day survival.Results:Among 149 patients, the median age was 245 days, and the median weight was 6.3 kg. Indications for ECMO were acute cardiac failure (56%), respiratory failure (37%), and extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR; 11%). Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated significant survival differences among groups (log-rank P = 0.010). Mean survival times were 24.07 days (95% confidence interval [CI] 20.72–27.41) for cardiac failure, 28.99 days (95% CI 24.64–33.35) for respiratory failure, and 14.13 days (95% CI 7.72–20.55) for ECPR. In multivariable Cox regression analysis, pre-ECMO lactate (hazard ratio [HR] 1.042, 95% CI 1.008–1.078; P = 0.017) and post-ECMO lactate (HR 1.067, 95% CI 1.024–1.110; P = 0.002) were independent predictors of mortality. Pre- and post-ECMO aspartate aminotransferase and pH were not significantly associated with mortality.Conclusions:Elevated lactate levels before and after ECMO initiation independently predicted mortality in pediatric patients receiving ECMO. Lactate may serve as a useful biomarker for prognostication and risk stratification in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has become an essential life-support modality for children with severe cardiac or respiratory failure refractory to conventional therapy. Since its introduction, ECMO technology and clinical protocols have evolved significantly, resulting in improved survival rates, yet substantial morbidity persists. Children undergoing ECMO are particularly vulnerable due to immature organ systems, small body size, and complex underlying pathologies, which can lead to complications including acute kidney injury (AKI), neurological injury, and hepatic dysfunction.[1234] Improvements in circuit maintenance, nutritional support, and clinical decision-making have reduced many complications once considered insurmountable.[5] Cardiogenic shock, particularly post-cardiotomy, presents a high-risk scenario where survival is closely tied to pre-ECMO organ dysfunction, timing of initiation, and adequacy of perfusion. In contrast, neonatal respiratory failure, including conditions such as meconium aspiration syndrome or pulmonary hypertension due to diaphragmatic hernia, may have more favorable outcomes if ECMO is initiated before irreversible hypoxemia or multi-organ damage occurs,[67891011] and neurological events such as seizures, stroke, and intracranial hemorrhage. Despite advances, ECMO remains associated with significant complications. Renal failure, defined according to Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria, occurs in 25%–45% of patients and frequently necessitates renal replacement therapy (RRT). Neurological injury, including seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke, affects up to one-third of pediatric patients. Hepatic dysfunction, characterized by elevated transaminases or coagulopathy, reflects systemic hypoperfusion, inflammation, or ischemia-reperfusion injury. Understanding these complications in the context of specific indications is critical for improving outcomes.[1213141516] Förster et al. previously reported our clinical results in a subgroup of patients with meconium aspiration syndrome. In the present study, we provide an updated analysis of these data.[17]

Single-center analyses provide valuable insight by allowing standardized assessment of clinical protocols, cannulation strategies, anticoagulation practices, and supportive care. Large heterogeneous cohorts also enable identification of prognostic factors such as pre-ECMO lactate, acid-base status, and organ function markers that can guide patient selection and timing of ECMO initiation. The findings aim to inform best practices, refine risk stratification, and improve care strategies for critically ill children requiring ECMO support.

METHODS

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Ludwig Maximilian University (LMU) University Hospital Munich (approval number 25-0892) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patient data were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Study design

This retrospective observational study included pediatric patients (<18 years of age) who received ECMO support at LMU University Hospital Munich between January 2003 and July 2025. Patients were identified through the institutional ECMO registry and electronic medical records.

A total of 178 patients were screened for eligibility. Twenty-nine patients were excluded because they (1) underwent complex tracheal or esophageal repair with cardiopulmonary bypass without ECMO support, (2) required multiple ECMO runs during the same hospitalization, or (3) represented atypical cases previously reported separately, including one patient bridged to lung transplantation and one patient treated with a surgical Potts shunt in combination with ECMO. The final study cohort consisted of 149 patients.

Patients were categorized according to the primary indication for ECMO support into three groups: (1) cardiac failure, including cardiogenic shock and dilated cardiomyopathy; (2) respiratory failure, including neonatal respiratory failure and perioperative support for congenital tracheal or esophageal malformations; and (3) cardiorespiratory failure, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), severe pneumonia or airway infection, drowning, and patients requiring extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR). These groups were analyzed to reflect the major clinical indications for pediatric ECMO at our institution.

The primary outcome was 30-day survival following ECMO initiation. Secondary outcomes included renal failure according to KDIGO criteria, hepatic dysfunction defined by elevated serum transaminases (aspartate aminotransferase [AST] and alanine aminotransferase), and neurological complications including seizures, intracranial hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke occurring within 30 days of ECMO initiation.

Demographic characteristics, underlying diagnoses, ECMO modality and duration, laboratory parameters, complications, and clinical outcomes were collected for analysis.

Cannulation and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation management

The choice of ECMO modality (veno-arterial [V-A] or veno-venous [V-V]) was based on the underlying pathology, degree of cardiorespiratory compromise, and anticipated duration of support. V-A ECMO was primarily used for patients with cardiac failure or combined cardiorespiratory failure, whereas V-V ECMO was preferred for isolated respiratory failure.

Cannula size and configuration were selected according to patient age, weight, and institutional protocols. In neonates, peripheral V-A ECMO was typically established through cannulation of the right carotid artery and internal jugular vein. In older children, femoral cannulation with distal limb perfusion was the preferred approach when anatomically feasible. V-V ECMO was most commonly established using a dual-lumen Avalon® cannula inserted through the right internal jugular vein. Central ECMO support was established through median sternotomy with cannulation of the ascending aorta and right atrium. Cannula position was routinely confirmed by echocardiography and chest radiography.

ECMO initiation was considered in patients with severe cardiac and/or respiratory failure refractory to maximal conventional medical management, including vasoactive support, mechanical ventilation, and adjunctive therapies, following multidisciplinary team assessment.

Anticoagulation

Systemic anticoagulation was achieved using unfractionated heparin according to institutional protocols. Anticoagulation monitoring included activated partial thromboplastin time, activated clotting time, anti-factor Xa activity, platelet count, and routine coagulation parameters. Target ranges were adjusted according to patient age, ECMO modality, and individual bleeding or thrombotic risk.

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation explantation

Weaning from ECMO was performed following evidence of adequate cardiac and/or pulmonary recovery, as determined by clinical, hemodynamic, laboratory, and echocardiographic assessment. Flow reduction trials were conducted before decannulation whenever clinically appropriate.

For V-V ECMO, percutaneous cannula removal was followed by manual compression to achieve hemostasis. For V-A ECMO, arterial decannulation involved removal of the arterial cannula with subsequent vascular reconstruction using direct suturing or patch repair when required. Venous cannulas were removed with direct vessel closure. Central ECMO explantation was performed surgically through median sternotomy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Survival was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier analysis from the time of ECMO initiation, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of mortality. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Variables considered clinically relevant or associated with mortality in univariable analyses were entered into multivariable models.

The multivariable model included clinically relevant pre-ECMO and early post-ECMO laboratory variables, specifically serum lactate, pH, and AST. These variables were selected based on biological plausibility and prior literature linking tissue hypoperfusion and organ dysfunction to mortality in ECMO patients.

A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

As shown in Table 2, adjunct pharmacological therapies during ECMO weaning and explantation: Adjunct pharmacological therapies varied according to underlying indication and were used to support organ recovery during ECMO weaning and preparation for explantation. In patients with cardiogenic shock, inotropic and vasoactive agents such as dobutamine, milrinone, levosimendan, noradrenaline, and vasodilators were used to optimize cardiac output, maintain systemic perfusion, and reduce afterload. Diuretics and antiarrhythmic agents were administered as clinically indicated. In neonatal respiratory failure (including meconium aspiration syndrome and diaphragmatic hernia), pulmonary vasodilators such as inhaled nitric oxide and sildenafil, in addition to milrinone and corticosteroids, were used to improve oxygenation and reduce pulmonary hypertension. In patients with tracheal or esophageal malformations, therapy consisted primarily of supportive care, antibiotics when infection was present, and pulmonary vasodilators when required. In dilated cardiomyopathy, pharmacological support included inotropes, afterload-reducing agents (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers), beta-blockers after stabilization, diuretics, and anticoagulation as a bridge to ventricular assist device implantation. In ARDS, and other respiratory failure states, sedatives, neuromuscular blockade, corticosteroids, inhaled nitric oxide, diuretics, and antibiotics were used to optimize oxygenation and treat underlying infection or inflammation. In drowning-associated cardiorespiratory failure, supportive therapy included corticosteroids, antibiotics when indicated, and temperature management. Controlled rewarming was performed when hypothermia was present to avoid hemodynamic instability and support safe ECMO weaning.

DISCUSSION

In this two-decade single-center study of 149 pediatric patients receiving ECMO, survival varied significantly according to underlying diagnosis, with the highest outcomes observed in respiratory failure and elective surgical indications, and the lowest in cardiogenic shock and drowning-related injury. Importantly, pre- and post-ECMO lactate levels were independently associated with mortality, highlighting the prognostic value of tissue hypoperfusion markers in this population.

Our overall survival rate of 58% is consistent with previously reported pediatric ECMO outcomes, which range from 40% to 60% in heterogeneous cohorts.[12] Zens et al. reported in their review that vessel reconstruction did not appear to significantly impact short-term morbidity.[18] Lactate has been repeatedly identified as a marker of severity and mortality in ECMO patients, reflecting global tissue hypoxia and inadequate perfusion.[12] In addition, Wheeler and Bullock reported improved survival in patients with ARDS treated with ECMO combined with continuous RRT.[19] Better outcomes in respiratory failure likely reflect the reversibility of pulmonary pathology and the possibility of initiating ECMO before irreversible multiorgan dysfunction occurs.[202122] The heterogeneity of ECMO indications in our cohort highlights a major challenge in pediatric ECMO research, as differences in underlying diagnosis and clinical severity limit direct comparisons and the development of universal prognostic models. Our cohort included both emergent cardiogenic shock and elective perioperative cases, reflecting the wide spectrum of disease acuity encountered in clinical practice.

Similar to Raj et al., who emphasized the importance of patient-specific factors such as underlying diagnosis, pre-ECMO physiological status, and comorbidities, our findings support the need for individualized risk assessment in pediatric ECMO patients.[23] In our cohort, markers of illness severity, including pre-ECMO acidosis and prolonged ECMO duration, as well as neurological complications, were associated with worse outcomes, underscoring the importance of early recognition and tailored management strategies. Future research and clinical protocols should focus on indication-specific risk stratification to better optimize the timing, modality, and duration of ECMO support. AKI and the need for RRT remain common complications during pediatric ECMO and contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality. In our cohort, renal complications occurred in 35% of patients, all of whom required dialysis, which is consistent with reported incidences of 27%–46% in the literature.[12] AKI during ECMO may result from multiple interrelated mechanisms, including pre-existing shock, hypoperfusion, hemolysis, and inflammation associated with extracorporeal circulation. The ECMO-induced systemic inflammatory response may further contribute to renal endothelial injury, capillary leak, and tissue oedema.[242526] These complications reflect multifactorial mechanisms, including hypoperfusion, inflammation, hemolysis, and anticoagulation-related effects. Although combined ECMO and RRT has been suggested to improve fluid and metabolic control in selected patients, this approach was not routinely used in our cohort.[272829] Neurological injury remains a major complication of ECMO, with multifactorial pathogenesis including altered cerebral perfusion, embolic events, and anticoagulation-related hemorrhage.[30] Hepatic dysfunction is similarly associated with hypoperfusion, congestion, and systemic inflammation.[10202729] In our cohort, prolonged ECMO duration was associated with increased complication rates and mortality, consistent with prior studies suggesting that longer support reflects higher disease severity and increased exposure to circuit-related risks.[10212830] Our data indicate an association between prolonged ECMO duration and increased complication rates and mortality. This finding is consistent with previous reports suggesting that extended ECMO support reflects greater illness severity or delayed recovery and increases exposure to complications such as bleeding, thrombosis, and infection.[242530] Despite advances in ECMO technology and management, renal, hepatic, and neurological complications remain common. In our cohort, renal injury occurred in 35% of patients, hepatic dysfunction in 28%, and neurological complications in 35%. These findings are consistent with published data, in which pediatric ECMO-associated AKI has been reported in 27%–46% of cases.[12252627] Early initiation of ECMO, before the development of severe metabolic derangements such as acidosis and hyperlactatemia, appears to be associated with improved outcomes. In our cohort, pre-ECMO pH was identified as an additional predictor of mortality, supporting the importance of timely cannulation strategies. However, the optimal timing of ECMO initiation remains challenging and requires balanced clinical judgment, potentially supported by standardized protocols incorporating biomarkers and physiological thresholds.

Anticoagulation strategies vary considerably between centers due to differences in patient populations and institutional protocols. Careful individualized monitoring is therefore essential to balance bleeding and thrombotic risks in pediatric ECMO patients.[122324252630]

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective single-center design, long study period with evolving ECMO practices, and heterogeneity of indications. In addition, the predictive value of laboratory markers requires prospective validation.

CONCLUSION

Survival in pediatric ECMO varies significantly by indication and severity of illness. Serum lactate is an independent predictor of mortality and may aid in early risk stratification. Despite advances in management, organ dysfunction remains common, underscoring the need for timely initiation and individualized ECMO strategies.
