Executive Summary of CRADA Work:
 Additive manufacturing, including direct ink writing (DIW), enables fabrication of complex
 structures with tailored properties while reducing waste compared to conventional
 manufacturing. However, broader adoption requires expanded access to specialized feedstock
 inks with controlled rheology and reliable printability. This CRADA focuses on advancing and
 commercializing 3D printable low-cost catalyst ink suitable for decarbonization-relevant
 electrochemical applications, emphasizing hydrogen production via the hydrogen evolution
 reaction (HER).
 LLNL developed a DIW-printable catalytic ink based on graphene oxide (GO) and MoS2 with 10
 wt.% MoS2 catalyst loading, formulated with cellulose and water, and demonstrated
 laboratory-scale printability. This project is designed to bridge the gap between bench-scale
 development and broader industrial and research use by scaling the ink to intermediate batch
 volumes, measuring time-dependent rheology and printability to support shelf-life
 requirements, fabricating and testing 3D printed catalytic electrodes, and iteratively refining
 formulations using user feedback gathered through participant distribution channels. Sigma-
 Aldrich’s role is to prepare the material for distribution, increase exposure through
 catalog/website availability and marketing, and collect structured feedback from users to guide

Summary of Research Results:
 Technical Objectives:
 The objective of this CRADA was to advance a laboratory-developed direct ink writing (DIW)
 catalytic ink based on graphene oxide and molybdenum disulfide (GO/MoS2) from bench-scale
 demonstrations toward broader external use and commercialization readiness. The work
 focused on improving reproducibility, characterizing stability, demonstrating printed-electrode
 performance, and iteratively refining the formulation based on user feedback.
 The project was guided by one central hypothesis: that a properly tuned, shear-thinning,
 viscoelastic GO/MoS2 ink could be scaled from small laboratory batches to intermediate
 volumes while retaining printability through fine nozzles, maintaining sufficient shelf life for
 distribution and quality control, and enabling fabrication of free-standing 3D catalytic
 electrodes with competitive electrochemical performance relative to non-3D printed baselines.

 To test this hypothesis, the Parties executed a staged approach over the full period of
 performance. LLNL scaled ink synthesis from prior 20 mL batches to 100 mL batches, with scale
 bounded by the DIW syringe capacity of approximately 55 cm³ maximum. LLNL documented
 synthesis, mixing, handling, storage, and post-processing procedures for transfer to the
 Participant. LLNL characterized ink physical properties tied to DIW performance, including
 viscosity and rheological parameters such as viscosity and storage modulus as functions of
 shear rate and shear stress, on a monthly cadence over a 12-month observation window (Fig.1).

 The approach assumed that consistent rheological trends over time correlate with acceptable
 shelf life for downstream packaging and distribution. Printability was evaluated by extrusion
 through a 400 µm nozzle and monitoring extrusion pressure monthly over the same period to
 quantify age-related changes in flow behavior.

 In parallel, LLNL established application relevance by printing 3D catalytic electrodes with
 feature sizes down to 400 µm and thicknesses of approximately 1 mm and evaluating
 electrochemical performance in custom electrochemical cells (Fig 2). Testing used standard
 three-electrode and two-electrode configurations to assess activity, active surface area, and
 durability. The electrochemical toolbox included cyclic voltammetry, chronoamperometry,
 chronopotentiometry, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, conducted at quarterly
 intervals to evaluate whether aging-related rheology changes translated into performance
 changes.

  Actual Accomplishments:
  The project established an integrated scale-up, validation, and user-feedback workflow to
  transition LLNL’s direct ink writing (DIW) GO/MoS2 catalytic ink from bench-scale
  demonstrations toward external use and commercialization readiness. The effort aligned ink
  manufacturing and documentation, stability and printability characterization, application-
  relevant 3D electrode fabrication and electrochemical testing, and a participant-led pathway
  for distribution and structured user feedback to guide second-generation refinement.
  Relative to the Statement of Work, the project scope was organized as follows: Task 1 defined
  scale-up to ~100 mL batches (bounded by DIW syringe capacity) and established monthly
  rheology and 400 µm nozzle extrusion-pressure tracking for stability and printability. Task
  2 defined participant activities for safety/trade assessment, sample packaging (10 mL units
  with expansion to 100 mL), catalog/awareness efforts, and monthly collection of user profiles
  and feedback for LLNL. Task 3 defined printing of free-standing 3D electrodes (down to 400 µm
  features, ~1 mm thick) and quarterly electrochemical evaluation (CV, chronoamperometry, GC,
  chronopotentiometry) against non-3D printed and tape-cast baselines. Task 4 optional N2
  conversion testing was demonstrated, and results were shared with the Participant. Task
  5 defined joint development of user protocols, and Task 6 defined final reporting deliverables.
  All the tasks and deliverables on this CRADA was completed. The technical objective of this
  CRADA was afdavqnce the ink from TRL 4 to 6 through a global launch the ink in MilliporeSigma
  website and this was successfully completed.
