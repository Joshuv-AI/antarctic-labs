Peritonitis and Mortality Risks of Gastric Acid Suppressants in Patients Undergoing Peritoneal Dialysis
Authors: Min Fan, Kenneth K.C. Man, X. Li, Celine S.L. Chui, X.N. Tong, Esther W.Y. Chan, Sydney C.W. Tang, Ian C.K. Wong, Franco W.T. Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeritonitis is a critical complication in patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD). The association between gastric acid suppressants (GAS), specifically proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), and peritonitis risk remains controversial, with conflicting evidence regarding their safety.MethodsWe conducted a target trial emulation using Hong Kong electronic health records (2006–2019), applying a 168-month sequential design to minimize immortal time bias. Using inverse probability of treatment weighting and discrete hazards regression, we estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for peritonitis, all-cause mortality, and peritonitis-related mortality among PPI initiators, H2RA initiators, and non-initiators (95% confidence interval [CI] calculated using 500-resample bootstrapping). We also performed a PPI versus H2RA head-to-head comparison and predictive approaches to treatment effect heterogeneity analysis.ResultsAmong 11,693 individuals (240,431 person-trials), PPI initiation (vs. non-initiation) was associated with higher risks of peritonitis (HR 1.53, 95% CI 1.31–1.80), all-cause mortality (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.43–1.67), and peritonitis-related mortality (HR 1.39, 95% CI 1.05–1.58). H2RA use was also associated with increased risk of peritonitis (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.08–1.35) and all-cause mortality risks (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.06–1.20). Head-to-head analysis showed no significant increase in peritonitis risk with PPIs versus H2RAs, but revealed an association between PPI use and higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.38, 95% CI 1.23–1.54) and peritonitis-related mortality (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.07–3.19). Predictive approaches to treatment effect heterogeneity analysis indicated that risk elevations were more pronounced in individuals with higher baseline risk.ConclusionsIn patients on PD, GAS initiation is associated with increased peritonitis and mortality compared with non-initiation. Furthermore, PPIs were associated with higher mortality risks than H2RAs. These findings highlight the need for cautious use of acid-suppressive therapy.

Peritonitis is a major complication of peritoneal dialysis (PD) in patients with kidney failure, leading to increased hospitalizations and mortality.1,2 Reported peritonitis rates vary widely across sites, ranging from 0.06 to 1.66 episodes per patient-year.3 In recent years, there has been growing concern and ongoing debate regarding the risks associated with gastric acid suppressants (GAS), including proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), but the evidence remains inconclusive.4, 5, 6, 7

A significant paradox exists between current clinical guidelines and established mechanisms. The 2022 International Society for Peritoneal Dialysis issued guidelines explicitly caution against H2RAs due to the potential risk of enteric peritonitis, while notably exempting PPIs from this recommendation.8 Biologically, both drug classes elevate gastric pH and cause dysbiosis of the gut microbiome, microbial translocation across the intestinal barrier, and promote intestinal colonization by enteric pathogens with subsequent bacterial translocation.9, 10, 11 Paradoxically, despite PPIs exhibiting more pronounced acid suppressant effects, prior clinical evidence has predominantly implicated H2RAs but not PPIs as the primary risk driver for peritonitis.8,12

Recent evidence from observational studies and systematic reviews remains conflicting. A 2024 meta-analysis reported a 61% increased enteric peritonitis risk with GAS exposure (risk ratio [RR], 1.61; 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.26–2.05), with risk concentrated in H2RA users.4 Conversely, another updated systematic review in 2025 argued that the risk was overestimated after including a larger contemporary cohort.13 However, that study may be subject to prevalent user bias leading to an underestimated risk.14 Crucially, traditional observational designs evaluating GAS often fail to adequately handle the misalignment of time zero between initiators and non-initiators, leading to immortal time bias. This necessitates a rigorous re-examination of GAS-associated peritonitis risk using advanced causal inference frameworks.

To overcome these methodological limitations, we implemented a target trial emulation framework using territory-wide electronic health records (EHRs) in Hong Kong. Our estimand of interest was the difference in the 5-year cumulative incidence of incident peritonitis, all-cause mortality, and peritonitis-specific mortality among patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis if they had been assigned to initiate PPIs, H2RAs, or refrain from GAS at the start of follow-up. By applying a sequential trial approach, we explicitly minimized the misalignment of time-zero in non-initiators in observational studies through a series of index anchoring times. Furthermore, an active-comparator newuser design was conducted for a robust head-to-head comparison between PPIs and H2RAs. This study design enables us to evaluate the peritonitis risk between GAS initiators and non-initiators, alongside a detailed comparative evaluation among GAS agents themselves.

Methods

Study Design and Cohort Identification

We applied a TTE framework to a healthcare database to simulate a hypothetical randomized controlled trial evaluating the risk of peritonitis among GAS initiators versus non-initiators who underwent PD from 2006 to 2019. PD patients were identified using the dispensing records of peritoneal dialysis fluid in outpatient settings. The specification for emulation with observational data and the target trial is illustrated in Supplementary Table S1.

To minimize the impact of the misalignment of time zero for the non-initiators, a sequential trial approach was adopted, aligning with the target trial framework. Eligibility criteria were dynamically reassessed monthly, and the first day of each month was assigned as time zero. Each trial included individuals who: (1) initiated PD before time zero, (2) were not on haemodialysis after PD, (3) had no history of kidney transplant, and (4) had no history of peritonitis within 6 months before time zero. Individuals with a history of GAS prescription within 6 months before time zero or those with both PPI and H2RA prescription records in the index month were excluded. From January 2006 to December 2019, a total of 168 sequential trials were conducted, each representing a monthly eligibility assessment window. The detailed illustration of the design is shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Data Source

The EHR system in Hong Kong is managed by the Hospital Authority (HA). More than seven million Hong Kong citizens have access to public healthcare services provided by the HA. The EHR stores records of demographic characteristics and clinical information, including clinical diagnoses, prescription details, laboratory tests, accident and emergency, inpatient, and outpatient visits, since 1995. The death information, including the cause of death, is provided by the Hong Kong Death Registry and merged into the EHR. In the EHR, the patient records are anonymised to protect patient privacy, and a unique patient reference number is generated for each patient to link all data sources and facilitate the retrieval of data. This data source has a verified positive predictive value of over 90%.15, 16, 17 It has been utilized for numerous impactful studies, including research on PPIs and studies involving the chronic kidney disease population.18, 19, 20, 21, 22

Exposure Assessment and Outcome Assessment

Those who received PPI or H2RA prescriptions within the index month were considered as PPI initiators and H2RA initiators, respectively, while the others were treated as non-initiators. Non-initiators in each trial remained eligible for subsequent trials. The intention-to-treat effect of incident peritonitis after a 6-month washout period was the primary outcome of interest. All-cause mortality and peritonitis-specific mortality were the secondary outcomes. Peritonitis-specific mortality was determined based on the primary cause of death listed in the death records. All events were identified through ICD-9-CM diagnosis codes for peritonitis or ICD-10-CM codes for mortality. The codes used in this study are illustrated in the Supplementary Table S2. Individuals were followed from each index month until the first occurrence of the outcome, hemodialysis, kidney transplant, death, the end of 2019, or 5 years after the index month, whichever occurred first.

Statistical Analysis

We used inverse propensity weighting to mimic the randomization process in a hypothetical emulation, addressing potential confounding at baseline. The multinomial regression model with stabilized weights was used for the propensity score model, with inclusion of demographic variables (sex, age at the index month, and time from first peritoneal dialysis to time zero); baseline comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index, diabetes, hypertension, congestive heart failure, peripheral vascular disease, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, dementia, paralysis, mild liver disease, moderate-to-severe liver disease, peptic ulcers, stroke or systemic embolism, asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, malignancy, metastatic solid tumor, type I and type II diabetes, severe constipation, smoking, drinking, and obesity); drug prescription of antiplatelet agents, injectable anticoagulants, oral anticoagulant use, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, statins, antihypertensives, antidepressants, antipsychotics, immunosuppressants, antidiabetic agents, iron supplements, and calcium supplements before time zero. The weights were calculated with 99th-percentile trimming for extreme weights. The balance of covariates was assessed with maximal standardized mean difference (SMD) among the three arms, and unbalanced covariates (SMD > 0.1) after weighting were included in the outcome model for doubly robust adjustment. The directed acyclic graph for this study is shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

Hazard ratios and risk differences for each outcome were estimated using discrete hazard (pooled logistic) regression models with adjustment for indicators of month of follow-up and unbalanced baseline covariates after weighting. The time-related variables (follow-up time, calendar period, age, and their quadratic form with time since time zero) were included for secular trend. Time-varying Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were included as time-varying covariates. Pooled logistic regression is a flexible method for estimating risks that considers time-varying confounders and has been widely applied in recent decades.23 Nonparametric bootstrapping with 500 resamples was used to construct 95% CIs. We visualized the temporal trend of the outcome using g-formula cumulative incidence plots based on the estimated hazards, incorporating a treatment-time interaction term to relax the proportional hazards assumption and allow the treatment effect to vary over follow-up.24 Statistical significance was determined using a two-sided test with a significance level of 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0.

Subgroup, Sensitivity, and Treatment Effect Heterogeneity Analysis

Subgroup analyses, including those by sex and age group (≥ 65 years and < 65 years), were conducted, and interaction effects were tested for effect modification using Wald tests. Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the results, including (i) changing the washout period from 6 months for prior GAS exposure to 1 year; (ii) using an overlap weighting approach to test the robustness of the propensity score weight construction25; (iii) conducting a per-protocol analysis by censoring at drug switch to estimate the effects of sustained GAS exposure; (iv) using two-tailed trimming at both the 1st and 99th percentiles for weights construction; (v) including pneumonia as a positive outcome since PPIs were considered a higher risk factor for pneumonia, while cataract was included as a negative outcome in the sensitivity analysis. Both outcomes were chosen based on prior literature and biological plausibility, not as proof of causation26, 27, 28, 29; (vi) using a model adjusting only for baseline confounders using a time-fixed intention-to-treat framework with no post-baseline covariate adjustment; and (vii) using a marginal structural model with additional time-varying censoring weights based on the primary analysis to estimate the causal total effect under standard marginal structural model assumptions. Finally, a head-to-head active comparator new-user design between H2RAs and PPIs was also conducted to minimize the impact of confounding by indication.

We also conducted a predictive approache to treatment effect heterogeneity analysis to investigate variation in treatment effects across patient populations.30 We fitted a prognostic model without treatment to predict baseline outcome risk for each patient using all covariates. The population was then classified into four risk strata (quartiles) based on the distribution of predicted baseline risk. Within each risk stratum, we conducted the main analysis to estimate treatment effects for PPIs and H2RAs compared with non-initiators. This approach allowed us to estimate treatment effects across different baseline risk subgroups and visualize how the expected benefit or risk of GAS agents varied by baseline risk profile.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong—the HA Hong Kong West Cluster (reference number UW20-110). Since all data used in this study were anonymised and obtained from the clinical management system database mentioned above, patients’ consent to participate was not required.

Results

Discussion

Our findings suggest that both PPI initiations and H2RA initiations are associated with significantly increased risks of adverse outcomes in PD patients compared with non-initiators. Specifically, PPI initiator were associated with a 53% increased risk of peritonitis, a 55% increased risk of all-cause mortality, and a 39% increased risk of peritonitis-specific mortality. Moreover, H2RA-initiators also showed a 21% higher risk of peritonitis and a 13% higher risk of all-cause mortality. Age plays an important effect modifier in peritonitis and mortality risk. Furthermore, in the head to head comparison, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in peritonitis risk between PPI initiators and H2RA initiators, whereas PPI initiators demonstrated higher all cause and peritonitis specific mortality. The predictive approaches to treatment effect heterogeneity analysis showed that peritonitis risk was more evident among populations with a higher baseline risk. These results suggest that the benefits of GAS in PD patients should be carefully weighed against the potential increased risks of peritonitis and mortality, particularly for PPIs.

Biological Plausibility

Several biological mechanisms support the observed association between the use of GAS and an increased risk of peritonitis. First, GAS therapy fundamentally alters the gastrointestinal environment. By reducing gastric acidity, these agents raise the intestinal pH, which disrupts the normal gut flora and promotes bacterial overgrowth. This dysbiosis can lead to the colonization of the gut by opportunistic pathogens, including the translocation of oral bacteria to the intestinal tract.31 Although both PPIs and H2RAs are associated with these changes, evidence suggests that PPIs induce a significantly greater degree of oral-to-gut transmission.32 Such microbial imbalances are a known risk factor for enteric infections and have been linked to spontaneous bacterial peritonitis in other vulnerable populations, such as patients with cirrhosis.33 Second, GAS may directly modulate host immunity, further increasing susceptibility to infection. In vitro studies have demonstrated that PPIs can directly affect the function of inflammatory cells, including macrophages and neutrophils.34 More specifically, GAS has been shown to inhibit the oxidative burst in human neutrophils and alter the response of dendritic cells to microbial ligands.35,36 This impairment of the innate immune system could compromise the host defense against translocating bacteria, thereby increasing the risk of peritonitis. The more profound disruption of the gut microbiota associated with PPIs, compared to H2RAs, may explain the more pronounced effects observed for PPIs in our study. Lastly, the effect modification by age observed in our study may be attributed to a combination of immunosenescence and diminished physiological reserve in older patients. The pharmacological effects of GAS may disproportionately exacerbate age-related gut dysbiosis and impaired mucosal defense.37,38 Elderly patients, who frequently exhibit reduced baseline gastric acid secretion, are likely more susceptible to bacterial translocation, enteric peritonitis, and subsequent mortality.

Comparison With Previous Studies

Our findings build upon, yet also diverge from, previous epidemiological studies examining this association. A notable paradox in the existing literature is that while biological evidence suggests PPIs provide a stronger acid-suppressive effect than H2RAs, some large observational studies have reported a stronger association between H2RA use and enteric peritonitis. For instance, the Peritoneal Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (PDOPPS) found that while overall GAS use was not significantly associated with all-cause peritonitis, H2RA users had a stronger link to enteric peritonitis than PPI users.14 Other foundational evidence driving this conclusion comes from smaller, methodologically limited studies. This included a regional Spanish study with just over 600 participants (119 H2RA and 207 PPI users), which reported a higher risk for H2RAs, but not PPIs.39 Another piece of evidence is from a meta-analysis with over 800 participants, but it combined the results from different study designs: case-control, cross-sectional, and cohort designs.12 Both studies were reported with small sample sizes, inadequate confounding control, and/or inappropriate statistical methods.

Furthermore, compared with non-initiators, PPI initiation was associated with a 55% higher 5-year mortality risk, highlighting substantial clinical relevance given the high baseline mortality in PD populations. This excess mortality appears partially driven by non-peritonitis pathways. For both PPIs and H2RAs, the associations with all-cause mortality were stronger than those for peritonitis-specific mortality. Beyond increasing susceptibility to non-peritoneal infections, as supported by our positive control showing a strong association with pneumonia, PPIs might also influence mortality through altered nutrient absorption, cardiovascular risk, or immune modulation.40,41 Notably, our estimated mortality risks, while directionally consistent with PDOPPS, are higher in magnitude than their primary finding (adjusted HR 1.14).14 Differences in study design may partly account for this gap. Our sequential target-trial emulation reassesses GAS exposure monthly, assigning each individual, including non-initiators, an explicit and updated index date, which may allow for more complete capture of exposure changes over follow-up compared with approaches that classify exposure primarily at study enrollment. Interestingly, PDOPPS’s own sensitivity analysis incorporating updated exposure classification yielded a somewhat higher PPI-mortality hazard ratio of approximately 1.2, suggesting that exposure-time handling may contribute to part of the difference in magnitude we observe, although this alone does not fully account for the gap between the two studies. Other unmeasured factors, including potential confounding and selection bias, may also play a role. For example, PPI initiation could be more common among patients with greater underlying frailty or anticipated need for palliative care than measured covariates fully capture.

Our study addresses this methodological gap by employing a TTE framework. By using a sequential approach with robust propensity score weighting, we systematically minimized time-zero misalignment and baseline confounding. The adverse effects we identified, especially for PPIs, may therefore represent a more accurate estimate of the causal risk, an estimate that was potentially obscured by residual confounding and structural biases in previous research.

Nonetheless, caution is warranted in interpreting any observational association involving PPIs as definitively causal, given the broader track record of this literature. Beyond peritonitis and mortality, PPIs have been linked in observational studies to a range of other adverse outcomes, including chronic kidney disease, pneumonia, and Clostridioides difficile infection42, 43, 44; however, this body of evidence has faced substantial methodological scrutiny, and causal inference remains contested despite its size. For pneumonia specifically, Othman et al.45 applied analytic methods designed to isolate confounding from a true drug effect and found that the apparent excess risk in conventional cohort analyses actually preceded PPI initiation, consistent with confounding rather than a causal effect of the drug itself; once this was accounted for, no excess pneumonia risk attributable to PPI use remained. These critiques illustrate that, despite an extensive observational literature associating PPIs with numerous adverse outcomes, definitive evidence of causality remains elusive, and our own findings regarding peritonitis and mortality should be interpreted with this broader context of unresolved confounding in PPI pharmacoepidemiology in mind, notwithstanding the methodological safeguards employed in our design.

Clinical Implications

Our findings have several important implications for the clinical management of patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis. Clinicians should exercise caution and critically re-evaluate the risk-benefit profile before initiating or continuing GAS therapy, particularly without a clear indication. The data suggest a potential hierarchy of risk, which could inform the choice of agent when acid suppression is deemed necessary, as PPIs were associated with more significant adverse outcomes than H2RAs. For patients who are prescribed GAS, especially PPIs, heightened clinical vigilance and closer monitoring for early signs of peritonitis are warranted. Ultimately, these results should encourage the consideration of non-pharmacological strategies or H2RA with a lower risk of adverse reactions to manage acid-related symptoms, thereby reducing reliance on agents linked to this increased infection risk. Further studies with greater ethnic diversity and larger samples are needed to support our findings.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study benefits from several methodological advantages, including a substantial sample size and extended observation period that provided sufficient statistical power to identify clinically relevant differences in peritonitis and mortality rates. The research design utilized propensity score weighting methods to minimize confounding factors and implemented various sensitivity analyses, which enhanced the reliability of our estimates regarding acid suppression therapy effects in observational settings. Furthermore, we conducted both positive and negative control analyses to strengthen the validity of our conclusions.

Several potential limitations warrant consideration. First, unmeasured confounding remains a concern, as some variables, such as socioeconomic indicators, were not captured in our administrative dataset. Nevertheless, our study utilized population-wide health records covering the entire Hong Kong population and the dataset has been extensively used to explore pharmacoepidemiology questions, supporting the validity of our approach. Second, outcome identification depended on diagnostic codes within administrative databases, which introduces the possibility of misclassification errors. However, the healthcare database employed in this study has demonstrated high accuracy in previous validation research for key clinical outcomes. Third, because specific prescribing indications were not recorded, our findings are susceptible to confounding by indication that underlying gastrointestinal disease may prompt GAS use while independently increasing peritonitis risk. Furthermore, comparing GAS initiators to non-initiators introduces potential selection bias, as the non-initiators may be generally healthier. To mitigate these inherent limitations, we applied propensity score weighting to balance most covariates and used doubly robust estimation to adjust for any remaining unbalanced covariates in the outcome model. We also employed negative and positive outcome controls to evaluate residual confounding. In addition, we conducted a secondary head-to-head comparison between active users to further minimize the impact of confounding by indication. Additional research is needed to explore the impact of medication adherence patterns, dosing variations, and drug interactions on these findings, and prospective randomized trials would provide valuable confirmation of these observational results. Fourth, the cumulative incidence curves for H2RAs suggested a non-proportional treatment effect over follow-up, whereas our primary HR assumes a constant effect across the study period. This summary measure should therefore be interpreted as a time-averaged estimate rather than reflecting the effect size at any specific time point. We retained the constant-effect HR as our primary measure for comparability with prior pharmacoepidemiologic literature and ease of clinical interpretation, while the cumulative incidence curves are presented to capture the full temporal pattern of risk. Lastly, because our current dataset lacked microbiological details (e.g., causative organisms and Gram stain categories), we were unable to evaluate whether the distribution of causative organisms differed across exposure groups, including potential differences among non-GAS initiators or between the two GAS classes.

Conclusion

This target trial emulation demonstrates that PPI and H2RA initiation increased peritonitis and all-cause mortality risk in PD patients compared with non-initiators. Although the head-to-head analysis showed no significant difference in peritonitis risk between the two GAS agents, PPIs were associated with higher all-cause and peritonitis-specific mortality. These findings suggest caution in prescribing and the timely withdrawal of GAS in this population, with H2RAs potentially representing a safer alternative when GAS is necessary.
