ABSTRACT
This report assesses whether private insurance and existing federal authorities would likely provide
meaningful financial protection to private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators after a
major sealed-source release. It does not answer this question quantitatively because publicly
observable data on premiums, limits, uptake, and claims outcomes for this risk class appear
sparse. Instead, it uses a qualitative structural analysis based on prior literature, review of relevant
federal authorities, limited observable insurance-market evidence, expert outreach, and historical
analogs. The analysis finds that available public and private mechanisms do not combine into a
clear, dependable, or readily verifiable compensation structure for ordinary private sealed-source
operators. Institutions therefore should not assume that either insurance or government response
will make them financially whole after a severe incident. Source reduction and replacement remain
more dependable than post-event financial mechanisms for reducing institutional exposure and
broader radiological risk.

Executive Summary

Question. If a major sealed-source release involving a self-shielded gamma irradiator were to occur,
whether accidental or malicious, would private insurance and existing federal authorities likely
provide meaningful financial protection to a private hospital, university, or research laboratory?

Bottom-line answer. Not reliably.

Important public and private mechanisms do exist, but they do not combine into a clear, dependable,
and readily verifiable structure for protecting ordinary private sealed-source operators against the
categories of loss most likely to matter for institutional continuity. Private institutions should not
assume that either commercial insurance or government response would make them financially
whole after a severe incident.

What this report does and does not do. This report presents a qualitative structural assessment
rather than a quantitative market study. The analysis did not identify a sufficiently broad and
transparent body of insurance-market evidence specific to private operators of self-shielded gamma
irradiators to support quantitative estimation of premiums, limits, uptake, or claims outcomes.
Accordingly, the report does not attempt to quantify the size of the insurance gap.

Instead, it examines whether currently visible insurance structures and existing federal authorities
appear well aligned with the categories of loss most likely to threaten institutional continuity after
a sealed-source release large enough to produce substantial contamination, prolonged interruption,
or significant liability exposure. It draws on prior literature, review of relevant federal authorities,
limited observable insurance-market evidence, expert outreach, and historical analogs.

The limited transparency of this market is itself part of the finding. This limited transparency is one
way in which the present analysis extends prior work focused primarily on the insurance-liability
gap: if meaningful financial protection exists only through difficult-to-observe or institution-specific
arrangements, then insurance cannot be expected to serve as a clear or scalable substitution signal
for the broader population of source-owning institutions.

Main findings

 1. Private operators may retain meaningful residual financial exposure after a major
 sealed-source release. The available evidence does not support the assumption that radi-
 ological losses large enough to threaten institutional continuity would be absorbed cleanly
 by either private insurance or federal assistance. Some losses may be covered, shifted, or
 publicly managed, but the overall structure remains incomplete and uncertain.

 2. The losses most likely to threaten institutional continuity also appear to be among the
 least predictably protected. These losses include radiological cleanup, waste management,
 restoration, prolonged business interruption, certain third-party claims, and longer-term op-
 erational or reputational effects such as delayed research activity, reduced service demand,
 or loss of institutional confidence. Those categories do not map cleanly onto ordinary com-
 mercial insurance structures and are not comprehensively addressed by federal authorities.

 13

  3. Existing federal authorities may reduce portions of the post-event burden, but they do
 not form a standing compensation regime for ordinary private sealed-source operators.
 The Price-Anderson Act, CERCLA, the Stafford Act, and TRIA may each matter under
 particular conditions, but they serve different purposes and do not combine into a single,
 predictable recovery structure for private hospitals, universities, and research laboratories
 operating self-shielded irradiators.

  4. Government response should not be equated with institutional indemnification. A radi-
 ological incident large enough to produce substantial contamination, prolonged interruption,
 or significant liability exposure could trigger substantial public-sector involvement in re-
 sponse, cleanup, coordination, or public health protection. That involvement does not mean
 the affected institution would be reimbursed for its full financial loss.

  5. The absence of transparent market evidence is itself policy-relevant. The analysis did not
 identify a broad, observable market record showing that private operators can readily purchase
 and verify meaningful financial protection for the losses most central to this problem. That
 opacity weakens the idea that insurance currently provides a strong or broadly interpretable
 substitution signal.

Implications for ORS. The principal value of this analysis for ORS is not that it changes ORS’s
mission, but that it sharpens how ORS can explain, target, and support that mission. The report
provides additional analytical support for why elimination, removal, replacement, and protection
efforts remain important even when insurance and federal response mechanisms exist.

Recommendations for ORS

  1. Use this analysis to treat private insurance conditions as a contextual factor rather than
 as a primary substitution lever. Available evidence does not show a clear and broadly
 visible premium signal that would reliably drive technology transition across the broader
 population of private operators.

  2. Use this analysis to strengthen the institutional-risk case for source elimination and re-
 placement. The report adds a complementary argument to existing security and consequence-
 based rationales by showing that institutions may remain financially exposed even when public
 and private recovery mechanisms exist.

  3. Use this analysis to distinguish public response from private financial recovery in stake-
 holder engagement. ORS can use this report to clarify that visible federal involvement after
 an incident does not necessarily imply that an institution will be made financially whole.

  4. Use this analysis to encourage more realistic review of financial and continuity as-
 sumptions among institutions that retain irradiators. At a minimum, institutions should
 understand contamination exclusions, terrorism endorsements, sublimits, business interrup-
 tion triggers, contractor and vendor indemnities, and continuity assumptions for prolonged
 loss of use.

5. Use this analysis to highlight the relevance of financial uncertainty in research, labo-
 ratory, and clinical settings. This recommendation may be especially useful where insti-
 tutional missions depend on specialized laboratory space, clinical continuity, time-sensitive
 work, or grant-funded activity.
6. Use this analysis to inform future decision-support and prioritization tools. ORS may
 benefit from materials that help distinguish where retained source use poses the greatest
 institutional and recovery uncertainty across different sectors.

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background and Problem Statement

Self-shielded gamma irradiators containing 137Cs or 60 Co remain in use across U.S. hospitals,
universities, and research laboratories because they are technically mature, operationally reliable,
and familiar to end users. At the same time, they present a distinct security and safety concern:
theft, sabotage, or breach of a high-activity sealed source could produce radiological contamination
large enough to impose major cleanup costs, interrupt operations for extended periods, and trigger
broader public and institutional consequences, including disruption of clinical services, loss of
access to specialized research space, and interruption of grant-funded work.
Those studies help illustrate the scale of potential consequences, but they do not fully answer the
more practical question facing source-owning institutions and policymakers: who would actually
pay? For institutions deciding whether to retain or replace source-based irradiators, the key issue
is not only the magnitude of possible losses, but also how those losses would be distributed across
the operator, insurers, contractors, and public authorities after an event.[1, 2, 3].

1.2. Economic Cost vs. Financial Liability

That distinction is central to this paper. The total economic cost of a radiological release is not
the same as the financial exposure of the institution that owns or operates the source. Economic
cost refers to the total losses imposed on society, including direct response and cleanup costs as
well as indirect losses such as business interruption, reputational damage, and regional economic
effects. Financial exposure, by contrast, depends on how those costs are allocated through insurance
contracts, regulatory authorities, tort claims, statutory cleanup regimes, and discretionary public
spending [4, 2, 5, 3]. This distinction is also consistent with Sandia’s 2024 report Historical
Perspective on the Economic Consequences of a Radiological Dispersal Event, which separates
direct event-related costs from broader economic losses and emphasizes that different categories of
burden may scale very differently across incidents [6].
A release that appears catastrophic in an economic model may still leave a private institution
underinsured or exposed to disputed losses, while an event that prompts substantial federal response
activity may still fall well short of full institutional indemnification [1, 2, 5, 3]. Insurance and federal
statutes do not pay losses in the same way that consequence studies count them. What is actually
paid depends on contract triggers and exclusions, the legal characterization of the event, and the
authorities activated after the event. As a result, two contamination incidents that appear similar
from a consequence perspective can produce very different financial outcomes for the institution
[4, 7, 2, 3, 6].

 17

1.3. Research Question and Scope

This distinction is especially relevant to the Office of Radiological Security (ORS), whose mission
includes reducing the risks posed by high-activity radioactive sources. One possible argument
for technology transition is that if gamma irradiators create large tail risks, insurance markets
might be expected to price those risks and thereby provide institutions with a financial incentive to
substitute lower-consequence alternatives such as X-ray irradiators. In principle, higher expected
loss could translate into higher premiums, tighter underwriting, or both. In practice, however,
losses associated with radiological dispersal or major source contamination are often difficult to
insure because they are rare, potentially very large, and difficult to bound. Contamination cleanup,
regulatory clearance, business interruption, and reputational effects may all extend well beyond the
initiating event [4, 8, 9].

This concern was framed directly in prior work by Kamen, who examined the insurance-liability gap
for blood and research gamma irradiators using the 2019 University of Washington / Harborview
sealed-source breach as a practical warning case [1, 10]. In that incident, breach of a 2,900-Ci
137
 Cs source during decommissioning contaminated multiple individuals and all seven floors of the
affected building, with estimated recovery costs exceeding $150 million in published and internal
analyses of the event [1, 10, 11]. Kamen’s central argument is that commercially available insurance
is unlikely to provide sufficient limits to fully protect healthcare or research institutions from direct
financial liability in a major radiological contamination event, particularly where nuclear, chemical,
biological, or radiological exclusions or restrictions apply [1].

Public reporting and the available discussion of the Harborview case establish the scale of realized
loss more clearly than they establish the final distribution of costs across all parties. However,
reporting by International Isotopes indicates that the company was indemnified under the Price-
Anderson Act for the 2019 cesium release, underscoring that the event occurred in a federal
recovery and contractor context rather than in the ordinary course of private institutional operation
[12]. Sandia’s later assessment of the incident estimated total recovery costs of approximately $158
million and concluded that Harborview represented an extreme-case recovery scenario shaped by
case-specific factors, including the requirement to return the building to use and the unusually
stringent cleanup standard adopted for the affected medical environment [11]. The case is therefore
important as evidence of severe realized loss, but it should not be assumed to mirror the financial
position of an ordinary private sealed-source operator outside that framework. In that sense, this
paper extends prior work on irradiator insurance gaps by asking whether existing federal post-
event authorities materially reduce the residual financial exposure left when private coverage is
incomplete.

This paper takes up a related but narrower question: if insurance is often inadequate to cover
radiological contamination losses large enough to threaten institutional continuity, to what extent
would government authorities actually offset those costs after a sealed-source release? The purpose
of the analysis is not to restate the case for source substitution in general, but rather to examine
the post-event allocation of financial responsibility among the institution, private insurers, and
government. In particular, it considers whether existing federal authorities may support cleanup,
compensation, or recovery in ways that materially reduce institutional exposure, and where those

 18

authorities still fall short of providing dependable protection for the categories of loss most important
to private operators [1, 13, 2, 3, 7].
The analysis is limited to U.S. institutions operating self-shielded irradiators using 137Cs or 60 Co.
It does not attempt to estimate national aggregate losses from an RDD event or model the full
societal consequences of radiological contamination. Instead, it focuses on a narrower and more
decision-relevant set of questions: whether private insurance provides meaningful protection for
source-owning institutions, whether existing federal authorities are likely to offset a meaningful
share of post-event costs, and how residual financial exposure is likely to be distributed when neither
mechanism fully responds.
Throughout the paper, financial outcomes are understood to depend in part on whether a release
is treated as accidental, malicious, or certified terrorism, because those distinctions can materially
affect insurance response and the availability of federal authorities.
For purposes of this paper, “meaningful financial protection” does not mean full reimbursement of
every category of loss. It refers instead to whether private insurance and existing federal authorities
appear capable of materially reducing the losses most likely to threaten institutional continuity
after a severe sealed-source release, including cleanup and restoration costs, prolonged business
interruption, significant third-party liability, and other retained losses large enough to destabilize
the affected institution.
A central feature of this problem is limited market transparency. The analysis did not identify
a broad, publicly observable body of evidence on premiums, limits, uptake, or claims outcomes
specific to private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators. For that reason, this paper does
not present a quantitative market estimate. Instead, it asks a narrower and more decision-relevant
question: whether currently visible insurance structures and existing federal authorities appear
sufficiently clear, dependable, and well aligned with likely loss categories to support confident
assumptions of post-event financial protection. If meaningful protection cannot be readily verified
in advance, then it cannot function as a clear or broadly interpretable substitution signal. On the
evidence reviewed here, that is the more relevant problem.

1.4. Organization of the Paper

The analysis reaches three broad conclusions:
 1. The largest loss components associated with a sealed-source release, particularly radiological
 cleanup, long-duration business interruption, and related third-party claims, are often among
 the most difficult categories to insure under standard commercial policies [4, 8, 9].
 2. Existing federal authorities such as the Price-Anderson Act (PAA), the Comprehensive En-
 vironmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), the Robert T. Stafford
 Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act (Stafford Act), and the Terrorism Risk In-
 surance Act (TRIA) may support response, cleanup, or claims administration in specific
 circumstances, but they do not form a standing, comprehensive liability-and-compensation
 system for private sealed-source operators [13, 2, 5, 3, 7].

 19

 3. Because both private insurance and federal post-event support are limited and highly depen-
 dent on how the event is classified (e.g., accidental, malicious, or certified terrorism), as well
 as on the institutional setting, operators cannot assume that either mechanism will reliably
 protect them from major post-event financial exposure [1, 13, 2, 3, 8, 9].
The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows:
 1. Chapter 2 presents the analytical framework used to distinguish modeled consequence from
 institutional financial exposure and to identify the principal channels through which post-
 event costs may be allocated.
 2. Chapter 3 examines the commercial insurance context relevant to sealed-source operators,
 including standard coverage lines, common exclusions, and the limitations of observable
 specialty-market options.
 3. Chapter 4 reviews the principal federal authorities that may shape post-event response,
 cleanup, and cost allocation after a severe sealed-source release.
 4. Chapter 5 uses historical analogs to illustrate how catastrophic losses have been allocated
 under different liability and institutional settings.
 5. Chapter 6 summarizes the main findings, implications for institutional planning, and recom-
 mendations for ORS.
 6. Chapter 7 concludes by summarizing the paper’s central argument and reinforcing the impli-
 cations for ORS and for institutional expectations regarding post-event financial protection.

1.5. Basis of Analysis

This paper presents a qualitative structural assessment of post-event financial exposure for U.S.
operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators using 137 Cs or 60 Co. It does not estimate the size
of the insurance gap quantitatively and does not attempt to calculate expected insured recovery,
premiums, or market-wide uptake rates for this risk class.
That limitation reflects the limited market transparency described above. The analysis included
review of available literature and market materials and outreach to insurance and risk professionals,
but it did not identify a broad, transparent dataset sufficient to support a quantitative market
assessment. The absence of transparent market evidence is therefore treated as part of the analytic
problem rather than merely as a data shortfall.
Given those constraints, the paper evaluates a narrower question: whether currently visible insur-
ance structures and existing federal authorities appear well aligned with the categories of loss most
likely to threaten institutional continuity after a severe sealed-source release. The analysis draws
on four main source types:
 1. Prior literature on the consequences of radiological dispersal and source-related contamina-
 tion events;

 20

 2. Literature and practitioner discussion relevant to insurance-market structure, exclusions, and
 coverage limitations;
 3. Review of relevant federal statutory and regulatory authorities; and
 4. Comparative historical analogs used to examine how catastrophic losses have been allocated
 under different institutional arrangements.
The analysis was also informed by outreach to insurance and risk professionals, including consulta-
tion with an actuary and insurance-risk professional regarding catastrophe pricing, specialty-market
structure, capital requirements, and the treatment of low-frequency, high-severity risks in commer-
cial insurance and reinsurance markets [14]. These consultations informed the paper’s structural
interpretation of the problem, but they do not substitute for a broad empirical dataset on this specific
market.
The purpose of this approach is not to predict the exact financial outcome of any single future
incident. Instead, it is to identify likely structural constraints and post-event pathways through
which costs may be distributed among private institutions, insurers, contractors, and government
following a severe sealed-source release. The emphasis is therefore on alignment and verifiability
rather than on precise quantification.
Confidence in the paper’s conclusions is not uniform. It is highest where the analysis concerns
structural mismatches between likely loss categories and available insurance or federal recovery
pathways. It is lower where stronger claims would require direct evidence on premiums, limits,
uptake, or claims outcomes across the relevant institutional insurance market, because those data
remain difficult to observe publicly.
Several limitations follow from that approach. First, insurance outcomes are highly dependent
on institution-specific policy language, endorsements, exclusions, retentions, limits, and claims
handling. Broad conclusions about structural uncertainty cannot substitute for review of any one
institution’s actual insurance program. Second, the universe of directly comparable events is limited,
which means some conclusions necessarily rely on structural reasoning and analog evidence rather
than repeated observation of closely matched historical cases. Third, public-sector cost absorption
after severe incidents may depend on event-specific legal, political, and administrative decisions
that cannot be known in advance. Accordingly, the findings in this paper should be understood as a
qualitative assessment of likely financial pathways under conditions of limited market transparency,
not as a deterministic forecast of how any particular future event would be financed.

2. ANALYTICAL FRAMEWORK

2.1. Why Modeled Consequence and Institutional Exposure Diverge

Studies of radiological dispersal and sealed-source incidents often report very large economic
losses. These estimates are useful because they convey the potential scale of disruption associated
with contamination, response, decontamination, disposal, and longer-term social and economic
effects. However, they do not directly answer the question most relevant to institutions that own or
operate high-activity gamma irradiators: what portion of those losses would actually become the
institution’s responsibility [15, 16, 1].
That distinction is central to the present analysis. Economic cost is a measure of total societal
burden. It may include direct cleanup expenditures, emergency response, medical evaluation,
facility shutdown, lost output, reputational harm, regional business disruption, and other spillover
effects. Institutional financial exposure is different. It refers to the costs actually borne by the
operator after accounting for insurance coverage, contractual risk transfer, regulatory obligations,
cost recovery actions, and government assistance [4, 2, 5, 3, 17, 18].
This divergence matters because insurance contracts, statutory cleanup authorities, and disaster-
assistance mechanisms do not pay losses in the same way that consequence studies count them.
A consequence model may treat contamination, interruption, and social disruption as components
of one aggregate loss estimate. In practice, however, different portions of that estimate may
follow entirely different financial pathways. Some costs may be borne by the institution directly,
some may be covered only partially or conditionally by insurance, some may be shifted through
cleanup liability regimes, and some may be absorbed by government response without creating any
enforceable right to indemnification for the operator [4, 2, 5, 3, 17, 18]. The central analytical
problem is therefore not simply the magnitude of possible harm, but the allocation of that harm
after an event.

2.2. Major Categories of Post-Event Loss

For institutions operating self-shielded gamma irradiators, a sealed-source breach or release can
generate multiple categories of loss, each with different implications for liability and insurability.
The first category is response and stabilization cost. These costs include immediate expenses
associated with emergency response, hazmat operations, incident command, perimeter security,
contamination control, monitoring, initial protective actions, and temporary closure of affected
spaces. Some of these expenses may initially be borne by public agencies, but that possibility does
not necessarily determine who ultimately bears them or whether the institution remains exposed to
related internal and external costs [3, 17, 18].

 23

The second category is decontamination, waste management, and restoration. In many radi-
ological contamination scenarios, these are among the largest and most visible costs. They may
include radiological characterization, decontamination of rooms and equipment, demolition, pack-
aging and transport of contaminated material, waste disposal, confirmatory surveys, environmental
remediation, and reconstruction or restoration of affected spaces. These costs are highly sensitive
to the scale of contamination, the physical form of the radionuclide, the built environment, and the
cleanup endpoint selected by regulators or decision-makers [15, 16, 1, 2, 5].
The third category is business interruption and operational loss. An institution may lose
clinical capacity, laboratory access, research continuity, teaching functions, grant performance,
contracts, or other mission-essential activity for months or years after an event. For universities and
research laboratories, such disruption may also mean loss of access to specialized laboratory space,
interruption of time-sensitive experiments, delayed grant milestones, and disruption of funded
research programs. Even where contamination is geographically limited, operational disruption
can be extended by remediation sequencing, access restrictions, confirmatory monitoring, and
reputational concerns. These losses are often among the most financially significant for institutions,
but they are also among the most difficult to define and insure cleanly [16, 4, 9].
The fourth category is third-party bodily injury and property damage. Off-site contamination
or alleged exposure may give rise to claims by employees, patients, neighboring property owners,
visitors, students, contractors, or members of the public. Regardless of whether such claims
ultimately succeed, they can generate significant defense costs, claims-management burdens, and
uncertainty regarding the applicable theory of liability and available insurance response [19, 8, 20,
21].
The fifth category is reputational, demand, and long-tail institutional loss. A contamination
incident may produce loss of confidence, reduced service demand, delayed research activity, im-
paired recruitment, reduced occupancy or enrollment, fundraising impacts, or other consequences
that persist beyond technical cleanup. These losses are often recognized in RDD consequence
studies because they can materially increase the total burden of an event, but they are also among
the least likely to fit comfortably within standard insurance structures [16, 4, 9].
These categories should not be treated as interchangeable. They differ in timing, insurability, legal
treatment, and the likelihood that they will be borne by the institution rather than shifted elsewhere.
Those differences are why a single total-loss figure, while useful for showing scale, is insufficient
for evaluating actual post-event financial exposure.

2.3. Main Channels of Financial Allocation

Following a sealed-source release, costs may be allocated through several overlapping channels.
No single mechanism governs all losses for private irradiator operators, and no channel reliably
absorbs the full burden.
Figure 2-1 summarizes the principal channels through which post-event losses may be allocated
after a severe sealed-source release. The figure is conceptual rather than predictive. Its purpose is
to show that financial allocation is not governed by a single mechanism, but instead may involve

several channels operating at once, with some costs shifted, some publicly managed, and some
retained by the institution.
The first and often most important channel is direct institutional retention. The operator may
bear uninsured cleanup expenses, deductibles and retentions, internal labor costs, equipment re-
placement, interruption losses, legal costs, and losses that fall outside coverage or outside eligible
reimbursement categories. Regulatory compliance does not eliminate this possibility. A facility
can be fully compliant with applicable source-security and radiation-safety requirements and still
remain financially exposed after a major contamination event [1, 4, 8].
The second channel is private insurance. In principle, commercial property insurance, business
interruption coverage, commercial general liability, excess liability, pollution legal liability, and
terrorism-related endorsements may shift portions of the loss to insurers [19, 20, 21]. In practice,
however, the availability and adequacy of that transfer depend on policy wording, exclusions,
definitions of covered loss, contamination language, causation disputes, and whether the event is
characterized as accidental, malicious, or certified terrorism [22, 4, 8]. As Kamen’s work suggests,
the mere existence of insurance does not mean that the operator is meaningfully protected against
the losses that matter most in a severe radiological contamination event [1].
The third channel is contractual transfer to other private parties, including contractors, vendors,
subcontractors, transporters, maintenance providers, or waste-handling entities. In some cases,
contract terms, indemnity provisions, negligence findings, or contractor-specific insurance may
shift a portion of the burden away from the source-owning institution. But these arrangements
are highly fact-specific and cannot be assumed in advance as a stable solution to the broader
financial-exposure problem.
The fourth channel is government response, assistance, and public expenditure. Federal, state,
local, and tribal governments may fund emergency response, public health actions, environmental
cleanup, technical support, debris or waste management, or other elements of event response. Yet
public spending is not the same as institutional indemnification. Government may absorb some costs
because of public health necessity, statutory cleanup authority, disaster declarations, or discretionary
appropriations without thereby eliminating the institution’s own exposure to interruption, uninsured
loss, or residual liability [2, 5, 3].
The fifth channel is litigation, enforcement, and statutory cost recovery. Depending on the
facts, losses may be allocated through tort claims, contribution actions, regulatory enforcement,
or environmental cost-recovery mechanisms. These pathways may shift responsibility after the
fact, but they are often slow, contested, and dependent on legal classification, causation, and party
solvency [2, 5, 23]. They therefore do not provide the same kind of predictable protection that a
dedicated compensation regime would provide.

close to full financial protection for the private operator.

2.4. Why Event Classification Matters

Financial outcomes after a sealed-source release depend not only on the scale of contamination, but
also on how the event is classified legally and administratively. That classification can determine
which insurance provisions apply, which federal authorities are triggered, and whether the institution
is treated primarily as a victim, a responsible party, or some combination of the two [24, 25, 26].

One important distinction is accident versus malicious act. Many insurance provisions and
federal authorities distinguish among accidental contamination, negligent handling, sabotage, and
terrorism. A policy that might respond to accidental physical damage may exclude intentional acts
or radiological contamination associated with malicious conduct. Likewise, a federal mechanism
tied to terrorism may not apply to an accidental release, even if the resulting contamination looks
similar from a consequence standpoint [27, 28, 29].

A second distinction is certified terrorism versus non-certified violence or sabotage. The
Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA), a federal program that backstops certain insured terrorism
losses, is the clearest example. Even if an event is widely understood or publicly described as
terrorism, TRIA applies only if it is formally certified and only with respect to covered insured
losses under applicable policies [24, 25]. If certification does not occur, or if the underlying policy
excludes the relevant radiological loss, then the existence of TRIA may do little for the affected
institution. Those limitations mean that even a deliberate attack involving a radioactive source may
fail to trigger meaningful federal-backed insurance protection for the operator.

A third distinction is institutional setting and licensing status. Liability and indemnification
structures developed for reactors, U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) facilities, or federal contractors
do not necessarily map onto the hospitals, universities, and research laboratories that operate self-
shielded gamma irradiators. Whether a source is held by a private institution, a federal contractor,
or an entity operating under a particular licensing or contractual framework can materially affect the
availability of indemnification, cleanup authority, or federal support pathways [30, 31, 13]. Similar
contamination events may therefore produce different financial outcomes depending on who owns
the source, who was handling it, and under what legal framework the activity occurred.

financial analysis. They help explain why operators may be fully compliant and still face uncertain

2.5. Implications of the Framework for This Analysis

This framework yields three implications for the remainder of the paper. First, the relevant question
is how losses from sealed-source releases may be distributed once insurance limitations, liability
rules, and government response mechanisms are taken into account. Prior literature and real-world
incidents have already established that such releases can produce large cleanup costs, prolonged
interruption, and other serious consequences [15, 16, 1].

 28

Second, the loss categories most likely to threaten institutional continuity, including cleanup, waste
disposal, long-duration interruption, disputed third-party claims, and reputational effects, also
appear to be among the hardest to allocate predictably through ordinary commercial insurance and
the least likely to be fully offset by existing federal mechanisms [4, 8, 9, 2, 3]. These coverage and
compensation limits mean that private operators may face meaningful residual exposure even when
public response is substantial.
Third, if both private insurance and government support remain uncertain, incomplete, and highly
dependent on event classification, then institutions cannot assume that a severe sealed-source
release will be absorbed by either market mechanisms or public backstops. That uncertainty is
itself part of the policy problem. The next chapter therefore turns to the private insurance side
of the question: what insurance lines are relevant in principle, why they often fail or narrow in
radiological contamination scenarios, and why the existence of insurance does not necessarily
translate into meaningful financial protection for sealed-source operators [1, 4, 8, 33, 9].

3. INSURANCE MARKET REALITY FOR SEALED-SOURCE
 OPERATORS

3.1. Why Insurance Matters to This Question

Private insurance is the principal non-governmental mechanism for transferring financial risk
after a major accidental release, contamination event, or third-party liability claim. For institutions
operating self-shielded gamma irradiators, it is therefore the first place to look when asking whether
a serious sealed-source incident would become a direct institutional financial burden or whether a
meaningful portion of that burden could be shifted to private markets [34, 35, 36]. That role of
private insurance is the practical context for Kamen’s concern that commercially available insurance
may be insufficient to protect healthcare and research institutions from the largest contamination-
related losses associated with gamma irradiators [1].
That concern is plausible for two related reasons. First, the Harborview incident demonstrated
that even a non-terrorism breach of a sealed source can produce very large remediation and in-
terruption costs at a single facility, with Sandia’s later assessment estimating total recovery costs
of approximately $158 million [1, 10, 11]. At the same time, the case does not provide a fully
transparent or easily generalizable account of final payment allocation, particularly because it arose
in a federal recovery and contractor context rather than in the ordinary course of private facility
operations. Sandia’s later analysis of the incident also concluded that Harborview represented an
extreme-case recovery scenario and that much of the resulting cost was driven by case-specific fac-
tors, including the requirement to return the building to use, unusually stringent cleanup standards,
and other factors outside ordinary ORS control [11]. Second, the types of loss that dominate severe
radiological contamination events, including cleanup, waste disposal, prolonged interruption, and
related third-party claims, are precisely the categories that tend to create the greatest difficulty for
conventional commercial insurance [4, 8, 9].
The relevant question, then, is not whether insurance exists in the abstract, but whether it provides
meaningful and dependable protection against the categories of loss most likely to threaten insti-
tutional continuity. This distinction matters because insurance can fail in more than one way. The
most obvious is complete noncoverage: the policy does not respond because the event is excluded,
falls outside the insuring agreement, or is subject to a sublimit too small to matter. A second and
more subtle problem is partial or unstable coverage: some protection may exist on paper, but the
terms may be narrow, the limits low, or the claims questions sufficiently uncertain that the policy
does not function as reliable financial protection when a major contamination event occurs [4, 8, 33,
9]. In both cases, an institution may discover after an event that the mere existence of “insurance”
was not the same thing as meaningful risk transfer.
For that reason, this section does not treat insurance as a binary question of whether a licensee
is insured or uninsured. Instead, it asks three narrower questions. First, which commercial

 31

insurance lines would ordinarily be expected to respond to the kinds of losses created by a sealed-
source release? Second, why do those lines often narrow, fail, or become highly uncertain when
radiological contamination is involved? Third, does currently visible market evidence suggest a
clear enough price signal to encourage substitution away from gamma irradiators, or does it more
often suggest a coverage boundary that leaves institutions with residual uncertainty rather than a
predictable premium incentive [1, 4, 8, 9]?

3.2. Why Radiological Contamination Losses Are Difficult to Insure

Insurance functions best when losses are sufficiently frequent to model, sufficiently independent to
pool, and sufficiently definite to define in policy language and claims practice [34, 35, 37]. Severe
radiological contamination losses are a poor fit for those conditions. They are rare, potentially
catastrophic, and difficult to bound in both physical and financial terms. Severity depends not only
on the amount and form of material released, but also on building characteristics, contamination
pathways, cleanup standards, waste-disposal requirements, operational dependencies, and public
perception. These factors make both loss estimation and contract design unusually difficult [4].

One major difficulty is that contamination losses do not stop at the point of initial release. A
single sealed-source breach may lead to immediate response costs, multi-phase decontamination,
long-term monitoring, disposal of large volumes of waste, reconstruction of affected space, and
extended interruption of clinical or research operations. Small changes in the cleanup endpoint or
the operational standard required for reoccupation can materially change the total cost of the event
[4]. That sensitivity to cleanup standards and reoccupation requirements creates a level of severity
uncertainty that is very different from more conventional insured events such as discrete property
damage or short-duration operational outages.

A second difficulty is that some of the most important losses are not easily defined within standard
insurance concepts. Business interruption, loss of use, reputational damage, patient or research
diversion, delayed grants, and long-tail institutional disruption may dominate the real financial
burden of a contamination event, yet these losses often depend on indirect causation, judgment-
based reopening decisions, or social response rather than a simple, time-bounded repair process
[16, 4, 9]. These characteristics make them harder to underwrite, harder to value, and more likely
to generate dispute.

A third difficulty is correlation. In a dense urban area or complex institutional setting, a single
radiological contamination event may produce overlapping first-party and third-party claims across
multiple lines of coverage. Property, business interruption, liability, cleanup, and civil-authority
type losses may all arise from a common event. This kind of correlated, high-severity exposure can
strain insurer and reinsurer capacity and can encourage exclusions, narrow terms, and conservative
underwriting rather than broad, competitively priced coverage [4].

For these reasons, radiological contamination risk is not simply “high loss” risk. It is a form of risk
that interacts poorly with the usual conditions that support stable pooling and predictable pricing.
This poor fit with stable pooling and predictable pricing helps explain why market responses often

 32

take the form of exclusions, carvebacks, specialty underwriting, and limited visible uptake rather
than a smooth premium schedule that reliably communicates risk to operators [4, 8, 9].

3.3. Commercial Insurance Lines That Matter in Principle

In a non-radiological setting, several standard commercial insurance lines would ordinarily be
relevant to the kinds of losses that follow a major facility incident.
Commercial property insurance is relevant because a sealed-source release may contaminate
buildings, equipment, and other physical assets. If contamination is treated as direct physical loss
or damage, property coverage may in principle help finance repair, replacement, or restoration of
the affected space.
Business interruption coverage is relevant because a hospital, laboratory, or research facility may
lose the ability to operate for a prolonged period after contamination. In conventional settings,
business interruption insurance is often designed to reimburse lost income or continuing expenses
associated with a covered property loss.
Commercial General Liability (CGL) is relevant because third parties may assert claims for bodily
injury, property damage, or related harms arising from the institution’s operations or premises [19].
Where outside parties allege contamination or exposure, CGL would ordinarily be one of the first
liability lines considered.
Pollution Legal Liability (PLL) or related environmental coverage is relevant because contam-
ination incidents can produce cleanup obligations, environmental response costs, and third-party
claims associated with hazardous substances [20, 21]. In non-radiological contamination settings,
these policies can provide a more tailored response than either property or general liability forms.
Terrorism endorsements or specialty CBRN products are relevant because some radiological
scenarios may involve sabotage, coercive threats, or acts intended to resemble or function as terror-
ism. Limited specialty products may be available in some parts of the market, but their existence
should not be confused with broad, dependable access to meaningful radiological contamination
coverage for the institutional operators considered here [33, 38, 39, 40, 9].
At the level of principle, then, the insurance architecture appears to offer multiple pathways
through which a serious sealed-source loss might be financed. The problem is that radiological
contamination often falls at the boundary where these lines narrow, exclude, or become less
predictable in practice.

3.4. Why Standard Lines Often Fail in Radiological Contamination Scenarios

The main practical problem for sealed-source operators is that the policies most relevant in the-
ory often become less reliable or more contested in practice once radiological contamination is
involved.

 33

For CGL, the core issue is that many forms contain nuclear or radiological exclusions broad
enough to bar coverage for losses caused by or contributed to by ionizing radiation or radioactive
contamination [8]. Where that language is present, even claims that would otherwise look like
ordinary bodily injury or property damage claims may be denied or narrowed because radioactive
material is part of the causal chain. Those exclusions mean that one of the most important liability
lines available to hospitals and research institutions may not function as expected in the very
scenario for which it seems most needed [33].
For PLL and related environmental products, the difficulty is similar. These policies are
designed to respond to contamination events, but many carriers carve back or exclude radioactive
materials, nuclear hazards, or ionizing-radiation-related loss even while providing broad protection
for chemical or conventional pollution events [20, 21, 41]. As a result, an institution may carry
environmental coverage and still discover that the sealed-source scenario of greatest concern sits
outside the policy’s practical reach.
For property and business interruption coverage, the challenge is often one of trigger and
definition. Coverage may depend on whether radioactive contamination qualifies as direct physical
loss or damage, whether contamination is itself excluded, whether the interruption resulted from a
covered peril, and how the “period of restoration” is defined. In a radiological incident, reopening
may depend not on ordinary repair but on decontamination sequencing, confirmatory monitoring,
regulatory review, or operational caution. Those features make business interruption harder to
value and easier to dispute [4]. Even where some coverage exists, it may not align well with the
true duration or character of institutional disruption.
For terrorism-related coverage, the issue is not simply whether an institution has purchased the
right endorsement, but whether meaningful underlying coverage is available at all, whether the
event falls within the covered form, and whether radiological or CBRN-related exclusions have
been addressed. Even then, the presence of TRIA does not itself guarantee that the institution’s
actual losses will be covered, because TRIA backstops insured losses; it does not create coverage
where the underlying policy does not provide it [7, 42]. This limitation is especially important for
radioactive contamination because underlying coverage structures often narrow the very losses that
would matter most after an RDD-like event [33, 38, 39, 40, 9].
Taken together, these problems mean that standard commercial lines often prove incomplete or
uncertain not because institutions are careless or uninsured, but because radiological contamination
sits outside the assumptions under which many standard forms were designed. The result is often a
gap between the institution’s intuitive expectation of protection and the narrower protection actually
available under policy language.

3.5. Specialty Markets and the Problem of Limited Capacity

Where standard commercial lines narrow or exclude radiological contamination, the next question
is whether specialty markets provide a meaningful alternative. The remaining visible options do not
appear to constitute a broad and dependable market, but rather a limited set of specialty terrorism,
CBRN, or manuscripted coverage arrangements discussed in parts of the literature and practitioner

 34

market commentary [43, 44, 2, 5, 32]. The existence of such products in principle should not be
confused with the existence of a stable, scalable market for meaningful radioactive contamination
coverage for private hospitals, universities, and research laboratories operating self-shielded gamma
irradiators.
Federal and market evidence is more consistent with this narrower view. The U.S. Government
Accountability Office (GAO) reported in 2008 that property/casualty insurers generally continued
to seek exclusion of CBRN coverage from commercial policies and that commercial policyholders,
including owners of high-value properties, generally reported that they could not obtain CBRN
coverage. GAO further found that insurers remained unwilling to offer such coverage because of
uncertainty regarding the severity and frequency of attacks and the potential for catastrophic losses
that could threaten insurer solvency [43].
The structural problem can be summarized simply. Specialty radiological and CBRN markets do
not appear limited only because the relevant products are niche; they also appear limited because
the underlying risk is difficult to bound, difficult to price, and difficult to scale across a broad
population of buyers. Figure 3-1 illustrates this logic at a high level.

analysis did not identify evidence of broad, routine use of radioactive contamination insurance by
private irradiator operators [32].
For purposes of this paper, the main implication is that the visible specialty market does not clearly
resolve the limitations of standard commercial coverage. Products may exist, but the analysis did
not identify evidence of a broadly usable and readily verifiable specialty-market solution for the
institutions most relevant here.

3.6. Demand Constraints and Low Uptake in Specialty Radiological and CBRN
 Coverage

The apparent weakness of this market is not simply a supply problem or simply a demand problem;
it is also a problem of interpretation. Even if some specialty terrorism or CBRN-related insurance
products are offered in limited parts of the market, limited visible uptake should not be mistaken
for evidence that private irradiator operators have found coverage unnecessary or that the market
is functioning well [1, 33, 40, 9]. The more important question is whether institutions encounter
coverage options that are sufficiently available, understandable, and valuable to influence real
financial planning. The available evidence reviewed here does not provide a clear basis for
concluding that they generally do.
Part of this constrained market appears to reflect supply-side constraints. GAO found that commer-
cial property/casualty insurers generally remained unwilling to provide CBRN coverage because
of extreme uncertainty regarding risk frequency and severity, the wide range of possible attack
scenarios, and the catastrophic scale of potential losses. Without confidence in their ability to
estimate losses and price the risk, insurers focused on worst-case scenarios rather than on sta-
ble premium-setting, helping sustain an exclusion-driven market rather than a broad underwriting
market [33].
Limited visible uptake should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that institutions have
assessed the risk and found coverage unnecessary. The same GAO report found that commercial
policyholders generally reported being unable to obtain CBRN coverage in the first place, which
suggests that limited uptake may reflect limited availability as much as buyer preference. Even
where specialty products can be identified, they may be expensive, narrow, difficult to interpret,
or poorly matched to the categories of loss institutions most care about. If the buyer reasonably
believes that the product will still leave major cleanup, interruption, reputational, or continuity
losses uninsured, then even a technically available policy may not appear worth purchasing. In
that setting, limited demand is not evidence of low risk; it may instead indicate that the available
product does not deliver enough value to justify the premium [33, 39, 40].
The structure of TRIA reinforces this problem rather than solving it. Treasury’s terrorism-insurance
framework requires insurers to make terrorism coverage available without material difference from

 37

other covered losses, but the implementing regulation also makes clear that insurers are not required
to cover risks they typically exclude. The regulation explicitly notes that if a state permits exclusions
for certain losses, such as nuclear, biological, or chemical events, insurers are not required to make
that coverage available under TRIA. In other words, TRIA’s make-available requirement does not
itself create underlying radiological coverage where exclusions remain in place [45].

Demand may also be shaped by institutional expectations about government response. If operators
believe that an extreme radiological event would trigger substantial public cleanup or disaster
involvement, they may be less inclined to pursue expensive specialty products, especially where
those products appear incomplete. Whether or not that belief is fully justified, it could weaken
already-thin demand and contribute to a market equilibrium characterized by underinsurance rather
than by effective risk-based pricing.

The analysis also did not identify sufficient evidence of a broad, transparent market for radiological
contamination coverage for typical private sealed-source operators to support a formal demand
estimate. That limitation should not be understood simply as a research shortfall. It is also consistent
with the underlying market structure described in this section: sparse observable availability, heavy
reliance on exclusions, niche specialty offerings, limited visible uptake, and uncertain practical
value for the institutions most relevant to this paper. In that sense, the absence of a robust demand
signal is itself part of the finding, not merely an absence of data.

Available evidence also provides only limited transparency into pricing for the relevant institutional
market. However, the combination of constrained capacity, specialized placement, and expert
consultation suggests that where bespoke radiological or CBRN-related coverage can be assembled,
premiums may be high relative to available limits. Expert consultation further suggested that
institutions would rationally hesitate to purchase products that appear too expensive or too narrow
to justify the cost.

For ORS’ purposes, the key implication is that limited visible uptake of radiological and CBRN-
related coverage should not be interpreted as evidence that the market has solved the problem or
that the underlying risk is minor. It more likely reflects some combination of exclusions, limited
availability, underwriting difficulty, uncertain value, and limited transparency into practical pricing
and coverage terms.

3.7. Why Premiums Often Do Not Provide a Strong Substitution Signal

A separate question is whether insurance pricing itself creates a meaningful incentive for institutions
to substitute away from sealed-source irradiators. This subsection addresses that narrower policy
question directly: even if some coverage can be identified, does the structure of the market produce
a clear and interpretable financial signal favoring lower-consequence alternatives?

A conventional insurance story would predict that if gamma irradiators impose larger tail risk than
substitute technologies, insurers might be expected to charge more for institutions that retain them.
That higher premium would then create a market-based incentive to adopt lower-risk alternatives.
This logic is appealing, but it depends on a market in which the peril is routinely underwritten, the

 38

loss can be modeled with some confidence, and premiums can be adjusted incrementally across a
wide population of buyers.
The available evidence reviewed here suggests that radioactive contamination risk often does not
behave this way in practice. Instead of smoothly pricing the difference between higher- and lower-
consequence technologies, insurers often appear to respond to radioactive contamination through
exclusions, narrow triggers, restrictive endorsements, or limited specialty capacity [4, 8, 9]. This
creates what is better understood as a coverage cliff than a continuous price signal. Institutions
are less likely to encounter gradually rising premiums that reward substitution and more likely to
encounter some combination of noncoverage, uncertain coverage, or high-priced specialty products
with limited availability.
That structure helps explain why insurance alone does not appear to have produced widespread
substitution away from sealed-source irradiators. Where the relevant losses are excluded or only
thinly covered, the financial signal reaching the operator may be weak, inconsistent, or delayed.
An institution may not see an actuarially meaningful premium penalty for retaining a gamma
irradiator because the market may have addressed the problem by narrowing coverage rather than
fully pricing it. In that setting, insurance should not be expected to function as a robust policy lever
for technology transition [1, 4, 8, 9].
This limited ability of insurance to drive substitution does not mean insurance is irrelevant. Policy
review, removal of restrictive endorsements where possible, and purchase of tailored coverage may
still reduce exposure at the margin, as Kamen argues [1]. But the broader implication is that
insurance markets, as currently structured and publicly observable, do not appear to provide a
dependable stand-alone solution to post-event financial risk or a clear stand-alone policy lever for
technology substitution.

3.9. Implications for the Government-Backstop Question

The limits of private insurance matter not only because they may leave institutions exposed, but
also because they sharpen the next question: what happens to the remaining losses? If commercial
insurance does not reliably cover the most consequential categories of radiological contamination
loss, then institutions and policymakers may reasonably expect that some portion of the burden
would shift to government response, cleanup authorities, or discretionary public spending. That
expectation is plausible, but it should not be confused with the existence of a standing public
compensation regime.
In other words, the inadequacy of private insurance does not by itself establish that government
will fully absorb the difference. It establishes only that residual exposure may remain real and that
public mechanisms become more important to analyze. The next chapter therefore turns to the
principal federal authorities that may shape post-event cost recovery, cleanup, or assistance after a
sealed-source release, and asks whether those authorities actually function as meaningful financial
backstops for private irradiator operators [13, 2, 5, 3, 7].

4. FEDERAL POST-EVENT COST PATHWAYS: WHAT
 GOVERNMENT CAN AND CANNOT COVER

4.1. Why Federal Authorities Matter After Insurance Gaps

The limits of private insurance make federal post-event cost pathways an essential part of the
analysis. If commercial coverage does not reliably protect private irradiator operators against
the most consequential categories of loss, then the next practical question is whether government
authorities may materially reduce the institution’s financial burden after a major sealed-source
release. That question is especially important because radiological incidents are likely to trigger
some level of public-sector involvement, whether through emergency response, technical support,
environmental cleanup authority, public health measures, or disaster coordination [13, 2, 5, 3, 7].
At the same time, federal involvement should not be confused with institutional indemnification.
Government may step in for reasons of public health, environmental protection, emergency man-
agement, or national security without thereby creating a right to full reimbursement for the private
operator. Public agencies may fund or direct response activities, coordinate cleanup, or provide op-
erational support while leaving the institution exposed to interruption losses, uninsured restoration
costs, legal expenses, or residual liability. In other words, federal action does not by itself answer
the question of who ultimately pays [2, 5, 3].
This distinction is particularly important in radiological scenarios because the United States does
not maintain a single, standing compensation regime for private operators of self-shielded gamma
irradiators. Instead, post-event cost allocation is shaped by a patchwork of authorities designed for
different purposes: nuclear liability management, environmental cleanup, disaster response, and
terrorism-insurance stabilization. Some of these authorities may be highly relevant in a given event.
None, however, appears designed specifically to provide comprehensive and predictable financial
protection for private hospitals, universities, or research laboratories operating sealed sources [13,
2, 5, 3, 7].
The remainder of this chapter therefore examines the principal federal authorities most likely to
matter after a major sealed-source release: the Price-Anderson Act, CERCLA, the Stafford Act,
and TRIA. The question throughout is not merely whether each authority could become relevant,
but whether it is likely to reduce meaningfully the post-event financial exposure borne by a private
irradiator operator.

4.2. Price-Anderson Act

The Price-Anderson Act (PAA) is the closest thing the United States has to a dedicated liability and
compensation framework for catastrophic nuclear incidents. It was designed to ensure the avail-

 43

ability of funds for public claims arising from covered nuclear incidents while limiting catastrophic
exposure for covered entities through a structured liability regime [25, 30, 13, 46]. For commercial
reactors, that structure includes a primary private insurance layer and a secondary retrospective
premium pool across reactor owners; together, these mechanisms provide a multi-tiered funding
system for public claims, with Congress retaining authority to consider additional compensation if
losses exceed the available pool. In that sense, PAA provides an important point of comparison
for this paper because it shows what a purpose-built federal liability architecture looks like when
government and industry have concluded that private insurance alone is insufficient [13, 46].
At first glance, PAA may appear relevant to the financial risks associated with a major sealed-
source release. The event involves radioactive material, may produce third-party claims, and could
generate large cleanup and disruption costs. Statutory and regulatory definitions are broad enough
that some radiological releases may appear relevant on first reading, especially where off-site
damage, dispersal, or substantial public claims are involved. In addition, Kamen’s discussion of the
2019 Harborview cesium-source breach, which generated unusually large recovery costs in a federal
recovery and contractor context, helps explain why institutions might ask whether a radiological
event involving an irradiator would benefit from a federal liability framework of this type [1, 13,
46].
The practical problem, however, is that PAA is not a general compensation regime for all radiological
incidents. Its core structure is tied to covered nuclear activities, especially commercial reactors and
certain DOE-related operations, rather than to the ordinary operation of self-shielded irradiators
at private hospitals, universities, and research laboratories [13, 47, 46]. This limitation matters
because most private sealed-source operators fall outside the Act’s ordinary center of gravity. For
those institutions, the existence of PAA does not mean that a major contamination event would
automatically trigger a reactor-style claims process, a dedicated compensation pool, or a federal
backstop for restoration costs.
Price-Anderson may still become relevant in narrower circumstances, particularly where a fed-
eral contractor, DOE activity, or other covered relationship is involved. The 2019 Harborview
cesium-source breach during a federally supported source recovery operation is illustrative in that
respect. Because the source breach occurred during a recovery operation conducted under the
National Nuclear Security Administration’s Off-Site Source Recovery Program, the institutional
and liability setting differed from that of a purely private licensee managing its own source with-
out federal involvement [1, 10]. International Isotopes, Inc. was the recovery contractor whose
personnel breached the cesium source during source-removal operations. Public company dis-
closures indicate that the firm was indemnified under the Price-Anderson Act in connection with
the 2019 cesium release, reinforcing that the financial consequences of the event were shaped by
federal recovery-program and contractor-indemnification arrangements rather than by the ordinary
liability environment facing a typical private irradiator operator [12].
Even where PAA is relevant, it should not be assumed to function as a general restoration fund for all
categories of institutional loss. PAA is principally a liability and claims-management framework.
Historical precedent shows that this distinction matters. After the 1979 Three Mile Island reactor
accident, PAA supported compensation for public claims such as evacuation, lost wages, and
property damage, while the much larger plant decontamination and restoration burden remained a
separate financial problem rather than a fully socialized cleanup cost [25, 13, 43, 48, 47]. In other

 44

words, even the federal regime most closely associated with nuclear liability does not map neatly
onto the categories of loss that matter most to private sealed-source operators.
For purposes of this paper, the main implication is straightforward. PAA matters less because it is
likely to solve the private irradiator problem in most cases than because it highlights the absence
of an equivalent regime for sealed-source operators. It demonstrates that the federal government
knows how to create a dedicated nuclear liability framework when it chooses to do so. The fact
that most private gamma irradiator operators do not sit clearly within such a framework is therefore
analytically significant. Private operators should not assume the existence of a reactor-style federal
backstop for post-event financial exposure simply because the event involves radioactive material.

4.3.        CERCLA

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) is one
of the most important federal authorities potentially relevant to a serious sealed-source release
because it provides a legal framework for environmental response to hazardous substance contam-
ination, including radioactive contamination in appropriate circumstances [44, 2, 5]. Unlike PAA,
which is a specialized liability regime for covered nuclear activities, CERCLA is fundamentally a
cleanup authority. Its central function is to enable response to contamination, impose liability on
responsible parties, and recover cleanup costs where statutory conditions are met [2, 5, 49].
CERCLA’s broad response and cost-recovery framework makes the statute highly relevant to the
question of whether government may step in after a significant radiological release from a gamma
irradiator. If contamination of buildings, land, or surrounding areas were severe enough to trigger
environmental response authority, CERCLA could support or direct federal cleanup activity, either
through government-led action or through enforcement mechanisms requiring responsible parties
to perform or finance the work [2, 5]. In practical terms, the availability of CERCLA authority
means that a radiological event at a private institution would not necessarily be left entirely to the
operator’s own resources. Government could become actively involved in remediation.
At the same time, CERCLA does not function as a general compensation mechanism for the
institution experiencing the event. Its focus is environmental response and cost recovery, not full
financial restoration of the affected operator. It can help answer the question of who has authority
to address contamination and who may be legally responsible for cleanup costs, but it does much
less to resolve who will bear business interruption, lost revenue, reputational damage, third-party
compensation outside the cleanup framework, or other long-tail institutional harms [2, 5, 23]. For
that reason, CERCLA may reduce some dimensions of public and environmental risk without
materially reducing the operator’s broader financial exposure.
This distinction matters because CERCLA can operate in a way that heightens liability rather than
relieving it. The statute is built around strict liability for cleanup costs and, in many circumstances,
may expose responsible parties to joint and several liability. Strict liability means that a party may
be required to pay for remediation even without conventional negligence. Joint and several liability
means that, in some cases, one party may be pursued for the full cleanup cost even if other parties
also contributed to the contamination [2, 5]. For a private irradiator operator, government “stepping

 45

in” through CERCLA may therefore mean that government asserts or coordinates cleanup authority
and later seeks cost recovery, rather than absorbing the loss on the institution’s behalf. In that sense,
CERCLA is as much a mechanism for assigning financial responsibility as it is a mechanism for
delivering assistance.
CERCLA is also a poor fit for some of the loss categories most likely to matter to hospitals,
universities, and research laboratories after a sealed-source breach. A facility may face prolonged
loss of use, interruption of patient or research activity, equipment replacement costs, delayed grant
or contract performance, reputational damage, and internal continuity losses that are financially
severe but do not fall naturally within CERCLA’s environmental cleanup structure [2, 5, 23]. Even
if CERCLA is highly relevant to contamination of the physical environment, it still leaves major
gaps from the standpoint of institutional continuity.
There is also a timing issue. Environmental response under CERCLA can involve urgent removal
actions, but longer-term remediation may proceed through assessment, investigation, and remedy-
selection processes that are not well aligned with the operational pressures facing an institution
trying to reopen facilities, restore services, or resume research. A mechanism that is well suited
to environmental governance is not necessarily well suited to rapid institutional financial recovery.
That mismatch reinforces the point that federal cleanup authority should not be mistaken for
predictable institutional protection [50, 51].
In addition, CERCLA engagement in radiological settings is driven less by the mere existence
of radioactive material than by risk-based considerations such as exposure pathways, migration
potential, and cleanup standards. That risk-based response structure means that smaller-scale
dispersals may still generate significant institutional loss without necessarily producing the kind of
large, visible Superfund response that some operators might assume [52, 53].
CERCLA’s practical value is also constrained by funding realities. Although Superfund remains an
important federal cleanup mechanism, its financial base has historically depended on a mix of taxes
and appropriations, and large or multi-jurisdictional events could still strain available resources
or delay response. In that sense, even where CERCLA is legally relevant, its existence does not
guarantee rapid or comprehensive financial relief for affected institutions [54].
For purposes of this paper, the main takeaway is that CERCLA is one of the clearest examples of
how government may become heavily involved after a radiological contamination event without
creating anything like a comprehensive bailout for the affected private operator. It matters greatly
for cleanup. It may matter much less for the operator’s full financial recovery. In some cases, it
may even sharpen the operator’s liability exposure by providing the legal basis for cost recovery
rather than compensation [2, 5, 23]. That limited compensatory value makes CERCLA a central
part of the post-event landscape, but not a substitute for a dedicated compensation regime.

4.4. Stafford Act and the National Response Framework

The Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act is the principal federal
framework for providing assistance after major disasters and emergencies, and it is therefore one
of the most plausible pathways through which government may become heavily involved after a

 46

severe radiological incident [3, 18]. Through presidential emergency or major disaster declarations,
the Stafford Act can support federal coordination, emergency protective measures, debris removal,
infrastructure-related assistance, and other forms of disaster response. In operational terms, the
National Response Framework (NRF) provides the structure through which that federal support is
organized across agencies [18, 55].
For purposes of this paper, the Stafford Act matters because it is often the mechanism readers have
in mind when they ask whether “the government would step in” after a catastrophic event. In a
sufficiently large or disruptive sealed-source release, especially one involving visible contamination,
major public concern, or multi-jurisdictional consequences, it is plausible that Stafford authorities
could be engaged in some form [3, 17, 18]. The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)
and other federal agencies could coordinate response, support emergency protective actions, and
help affected jurisdictions manage the incident [17, 18]. In that sense, Stafford-based involvement
could materially reduce the overall burden of response and recovery at the community level.
However, the Stafford Act does not function as a general liability or compensation regime for private
institutions. Its orientation is toward disaster response, emergency support, and reimbursement of
eligible costs, particularly for public entities and certain eligible nonprofit organizations, rather than
comprehensive indemnification of private operators for the full range of post-event losses [3, 18].
Unlike CERCLA, it does not create a general liability regime for responsible parties or a direct third-
party claims mechanism for private victims [3]. Even where a radiological event triggers substantial
federal activity, Stafford assistance does not automatically translate into reimbursement for a private
hospital, university, or research laboratory’s business interruption, lost revenue, reputational harm,
uninsured cleanup obligations, or third-party liability exposure.
This gap matters because the losses most likely to threaten institutional continuity after a sealed-
source release are often not the same losses most directly addressed by disaster-assistance frame-
works. Stafford can be highly effective at mobilizing federal operational support and reducing harm
to the public. It is much less effective as a mechanism for restoring a private institution’s balance
sheet. A facility could therefore experience extensive federal presence and still remain financially
exposed to the costs that matter most from its own perspective.
Another important limitation is that Stafford assistance is declaration-dependent. Federal disaster
involvement under this framework is not automatic; it depends on thresholds, requests, assessments,
and presidential action [3, 18]. A major wide-area event could plausibly satisfy those conditions. A
serious but more localized irradiator breach, even one that produces substantial contamination and
institutional loss, may not. This declaration dependence creates an additional layer of uncertainty
for private operators: even if disaster assistance would be helpful in a major event, it may not be
available in smaller but still financially devastating incidents.
The National Response Framework reinforces this point. The NRF is essential for organizing
federal roles and coordinating response to radiological incidents, but it is fundamentally a response-
management framework, not a compensation mechanism [55]. It helps answer who will respond
and how federal capabilities will be coordinated. It does much less to answer who will absorb
private institutional losses after the response phase [55].
Historical practice underscores this distinction. Stafford authorities have supported large-scale
federal response across events such as Hurricane Katrina (2005), Superstorm Sandy (2012), and

 47

the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), demonstrating the statute’s capacity to mobilize significant op-
erational and fiscal support at the public level [56, 57, 37]. But those examples show response
capacity, not a standing mechanism for making private institutions whole after a complex radiolog-
ical contamination event.

For purposes of this paper, the Stafford Act and NRF illustrate a recurring theme in the federal
landscape: government may become heavily involved at the operational level without providing
comprehensive financial protection at the institutional level. That distinction is easy to miss in
public discussions of disaster response, but it is central to realistic institutional planning. A
private irradiator operator should not assume that a large federal response presence means that the
institution itself has been made financially whole [3, 55].

4.5. Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA)

The Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) is the federal authority most likely to be raised when a
radiological dispersal device scenario is discussed in insurance terms, because it was specifically
designed to stabilize terrorism-insurance markets after the September 11 attacks and to create a
shared public-private mechanism for insured terrorism losses [58, 7].In broad terms, TRIA does
not operate as a standalone compensation fund. Instead, it backstops insured losses arising from
certified acts of terrorism under qualifying commercial insurance policies. At the policy level,
insured parties remain responsible for applicable deductibles, retentions, and uncovered losses,
while insurers pay covered claims subject to policy terms and limits. At the program level,
participating insurers are responsible for losses up to their applicable TRIA deductibles, after
which federal loss sharing may apply, subject to the statute’s thresholds and recoupment provisions
[58, 7].

At a surface level, TRIA may appear highly relevant to an RDD event. Radiological dispersal
is often discussed in terrorism terms, and TRIA is the main federal mechanism associated with
terrorism-related insurance losses. But that apparent fit is narrower than it first seems. TRIA
does not automatically apply to every deliberate or politically motivated act involving radioactive
material. Its protections are conditioned first on formal certification and second on the existence
of underlying commercial insurance coverage that actually responds to the event [7, 42]. Those
certification and coverage requirements mean that TRIA may matter greatly in theory while doing
much less in practice for private irradiator operators.

The certification requirement is the first major limitation. For TRIA to apply, the event must be
formally certified as an act of terrorism by the Secretary of the Treasury, in consultation with the
Secretary of Homeland Security and the Attorney General. That certification is not automatic, and
it functions as a binary gate. If an incident is certified, TRIA may become available, subject to
the policy and program conditions. If it is not certified, TRIA does not apply, even if the event
is widely described in public discourse as terrorism. In that case, financial recovery depends on
the underlying insurance policies and any other applicable legal authorities rather than on TRIA’s
federal backstop [42].

 48

The second major limitation is that TRIA only backstops insured losses. It does not create coverage
where none exists. This point is not simply a matter of implication; it is reflected directly in
the implementing regulation. Under Title 31 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR), Section
50.22, insurers are not required to cover risks they typically exclude in order to satisfy TRIA’s
make-available requirement, and the regulation specifically notes that where permitted exclusions
exist for nuclear, biological, or chemical events, insurers are not required to make such coverage
available [45]. That limitation means the existence of TRIA does not override underlying CBRN
exclusion structures. If the commercial policy excludes the relevant radiological loss, TRIA does
not cure that problem. This dependence on underlying coverage is especially important in the
present context because a terrorism endorsement is only useful if the underlying policy has not
already removed the relevant radiological peril from coverage.
This limitation is consistent with GAO’s findings regarding the post-TRIA market. In 2008, GAO
reported that property/casualty insurers generally continued to seek exclusion of CBRN coverage
from commercial policies, and that commercial policyholders generally reported being unable to
obtain such coverage. GAO also noted that TRIA applied only where the underlying policy covered
the peril in the first place [33].
This continuing dependence on underlying policy coverage matters because radiological contam-
ination losses are often among the hardest to insure in the first place. A localized event, an
ambiguous act of sabotage, or even a certified act of terrorism may still leave the affected institution
with major uncovered losses if the underlying policy excludes radioactive contamination or if only
narrow specialty coverage is available. A severe event can therefore fall within the broad public
imagination of “terrorism” while still failing to generate meaningful insurance recovery for the
institution most affected.
TRIA is also a poor fit for several of the loss categories most important to private irradiator operators.
Workers’ compensation may respond to employee injury in some circumstances, but that does little
to address evacuation costs, contamination cleanup, business interruption, reputational harm, or
long-duration loss of use. More broadly, TRIA’s architecture is designed to stabilize terrorism-
insurance markets, not to ensure full restoration of private institutions affected by radioactive
contamination. That distinction matters because the losses most likely to threaten institutional
continuity are not necessarily the losses most likely to be captured by terrorism endorsements and
federal insurance-sharing mechanisms.
No domestic U.S. event has been certified under TRIA, and the federal backstop has therefore never
been triggered in practice. Its repeated reauthorization nevertheless reflects the continuing view
that terrorism risk is difficult for private insurers to carry without some form of public support
[59].
For purposes of this paper, the main implication is that TRIA may be relevant to a narrow subset
of deliberate radiological events, but it does not reliably solve the post-event financial problem
facing private gamma irradiator operators. Its usefulness depends on certification, the presence
of underlying coverage, and sufficient insured loss to activate the program’s backstop features [7,
42, 45]. In many plausible sealed-source scenarios, those conditions may not all be met. TRIA
therefore reinforces the broader conclusion emerging across the federal landscape: government
may provide important support in specific circumstances, but private operators should not assume

 49

the existence of a broad or automatic federal financial backstop for radiological contamination
losses.

4.7. Comparative Takeaway: Federal Involvement Is Real but Fragmented

Taken together, the principal federal authorities relevant to a major sealed-source release do not
add up to a single, standing compensation regime for private operators of self-shielded gamma
irradiators. Instead, they address different parts of the post-event problem for different reasons.
PAA provides a specialized liability framework for covered nuclear activities, but it does not reliably
extend reactor-style protection to ordinary private sealed-source operators [13, 47]. CERCLA
can support or compel environmental cleanup and establish cost-recovery pathways, but it is a
remediation authority rather than a mechanism for comprehensive institutional financial recovery
[2, 5, 23]. The Stafford Act and the NRF can mobilize major federal response resources, but they are
oriented toward emergency management and eligible assistance rather than private indemnification
[3, 55]. TRIA can backstop certain insured terrorism losses, but only if the event is certified and
the underlying commercial policies actually provide covered losses [7, 42].
This fragmentation has two important consequences. First, the answer to whether government
may step in after a serious radiological incident is often yes in an operational, regulatory, or
administrative sense. Federal agencies may respond, coordinate, remediate, support public health
measures, or share insured terrorism losses under particular conditions. Second, the answer to
whether government would restore the private operator’s financial position is much less favorable.
None of the authorities discussed above appears designed to provide comprehensive and predictable
protection for a private hospital, university, or research laboratory facing the full range of losses
associated with a sealed-source release.
For ORS and similarly situated stakeholders, the practical lesson is that federal post-event pathways
should be understood as conditional tools rather than as dependable substitutes for risk reduction.
They may reduce some categories of harm and shift some share of cost under specific circumstances,
but they do not provide a clear basis for assuming that private sealed-source operators will be
protected from catastrophic financial exposure. This conclusion also sharpens the implications of
Kamen’s insurance-gap argument. If private insurance often fails to cover the most consequential
radiological losses, and federal authorities do not reliably close that gap, then residual exposure may
remain substantial for source-owning institutions [1]. The next chapter turns to historical analogs
to illustrate how differences in liability architecture, solvency, and public involvement shape who
ultimately bears catastrophic losses.

5. HISTORICAL ANALOGS: WHAT REAL CASES SUGGEST ABOUT
 WHO PAYS

5.1. Why Historical Analogs Are Useful

There is no perfect historical analog for a large sealed-source release at a private hospital, univer-
sity, or research laboratory. The combination of radiological contamination, uncertain insurance
response, fragmented federal authorities, and institutional continuity losses is too specific to be
matched cleanly by any single prior event. For that reason, the cases in this section are not presented
as direct precedents for what would happen after a future irradiator-related incident. Instead, they
are used as comparative examples of how catastrophic losses have been financed under different
liability architectures, insurance conditions, and government roles.

This comparative approach is useful because the central question of this paper is not simply
whether radiological events can be costly; that point is already well established [15, 16, 1]. The
more important question is how costs are allocated after severe events. Historical cases help
illuminate that question by showing that financial outcomes depend heavily on institutional setting.
This logic is also consistent with prior Sandia analysis emphasizing that each historical dispersal
event is unique and that situational context can greatly affect the scale and composition of resulting
costs [6]. In some cases, a dedicated liability regime channels claims and creates a relatively legible
compensation structure. In others, a clearly identifiable and solvent corporate actor bears much of
the burden through cleanup liability, insurance, and retained risk. In still others, catastrophic harm
occurs without a viable private recovery pathway, and support becomes more contingent, public,
special-purpose, or charitable.

The cases below are therefore selected not because they are identical to a severe irradiator breach
or RDD event, but because each highlights a different financing pattern relevant to the present
analysis. Together, they show that the question of who pays after a catastrophic event depends less
on the existence of large losses alone than on whether a workable post-event financial architecture
exists in the specific setting.

Unless otherwise noted, selected historical dollar figures in the case studies are reported both
in nominal dollars and in approximate 2025 dollars using CPI-U data from the U.S. Bureau of
Labor Statistics. Because the East Palestine case involves very recent costs, dollar figures there
are presented in nominal terms as reported and are already close to 2025 dollars. For multi-year
charitable distributions associated with the Oklahoma City bombing, 1995 CPI-U scaling is used

5.2. Three Mile Island: A Dedicated Liability Regime and Its Limits

The Three Mile Island accident is relevant to this paper because it illustrates what cost allocation
can look like when a major radiological event occurs within a setting already covered by a purpose-
built federal liability framework. Following the 1979 accident, the event triggered public claims,
extensive cleanup obligations, and long-duration recovery costs, but these costs were addressed
within an institutional environment fundamentally different from that facing most private sealed-
source operators [60, 25, 13].

The key difference is the existence of the PAA framework. Three Mile Island was not handled solely
through ordinary commercial insurance and conventional tort litigation. Instead, claims arising
from the accident were addressed through a federally established nuclear liability regime designed
for covered nuclear incidents [25, 13]. That system provides a structured mechanism for handling
eligible public claims for personal injury and property damage rather than relying exclusively
on fragmented tort proceedings or ad hoc settlement processes. In that sense, Price–Anderson
establishes an ex ante liability architecture for a defined class of nuclear risks, even though it does
not extend to full site restoration costs.

At the same time, Three Mile Island also shows the limits of even a specialized regime. Cleanup
at TMI-2 extended for roughly 14 years and ultimately cost approximately $973 million by the
time decontamination concluded in 1993 ($2.2 billion in 2025 dollars) [61]. Available accounts
indicate that insurance and utility responsibility absorbed most direct physical recovery costs, while
federal involvement was concentrated in indirect expenditures associated with emergency response,
oversight, and technical support. GAO estimated that those federal indirect costs would total about
$275 million through 1981 ($1.1 billion in 2025 dollars), and reported that, with few exceptions,
cleanup costs had been paid through insurance proceeds rather than direct federal financial assistance
[62]. As a result, even under a dedicated nuclear liability regime, claims compensation and full
restoration costs remained distinct financial categories. Broader reputational and perception-related
consequences were likewise not the same as statutory claims compensation, reinforcing the point
that even a dedicated liability regime does not necessarily absorb the full institutional and social
burden of a radiological event.

Three Mile Island useful less as a direct analog to a private irradiator breach and more as a
contrast case. It illustrates what a comparatively mature post-event liability architecture looks like
when government and industry have established in advance that catastrophic nuclear risk requires
a dedicated statutory claims framework. Most hospitals, universities, and research laboratories
operating self-shielded gamma irradiators do not occupy that kind of legal and financial environment
[13, 47]. They may handle radioactive material, but they generally do not operate within a reactor-
specific compensation regime.

The relevance to this paper is therefore straightforward. Three Mile Island suggests that more
predictable post-event cost allocation depends heavily on the existence of a specialized institutional
framework, not simply on the presence of radiological harm. Where such a framework exists,
certain categories of financial responsibility can be clearly channeled and administered. Where
it does not, institutions are more likely to face the fragmented combination of limited insurance

 54

coverage, uncertain public-sector backstop, and residual self-retention that this paper identifies as
the core problem for private sealed-source operators.

5.3. East Palestine: Clear Responsible-Party Liability and Insurable
 Contamination Loss

The East Palestine train derailment is not a radiological event, but it is a useful analog for this paper
because it illustrates how post-event costs may be allocated when a primary liable party is iden-
tifiable, cleanup authority is clearly established under federal environmental law, and commercial
insurance and corporate balance-sheet capacity play a measurable role in recovery. In February
2023, a Norfolk Southern freight train carrying hazardous materials derailed in East Palestine,
Ohio, resulting in contamination, evacuation, environmental remediation, and prolonged public
concern [63, 64]. The event generated substantial environmental response costs, public health con-
cerns, litigation, and long-tail recovery issues, making it a useful contrast case for examining how
catastrophic contamination losses are financed when institutional responsibility is comparatively
well defined.

A central feature of the East Palestine case is the identification of Norfolk Southern as the pri-
mary liable carrier. Under CERCLA, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) exercised
enforcement authority to direct and oversee remediation activities, structuring cleanup obligations
within an established federal liability framework [65, 66, 67]. Government involvement therefore
reinforced private financial responsibility rather than substituting for it.

Available corporate disclosures indicate that Norfolk Southern reported more than $1.1 billion in
total incident-related costs, including environmental remediation, legal expenses, and community
support commitments, with insurance recoveries of approximately $101 million reported at the
time of disclosure [68, 69]. In addition, a 2024 federal settlement with the EPA and Department of
Justice established a package of civil penalties, environmental remediation funding, and long-term
monitoring and safety commitments totaling approximately $310 million [70]. A separate class-
action settlement of approximately $600 million addressed property damage, personal injury, and
related claims [71].

Insurance and retained corporate risk therefore played a measurable role in absorbing costs asso-
ciated with the derailment, alongside regulatory enforcement and litigation-based recovery mech-
anisms. While the event still required significant public-sector involvement, government action
primarily operated through enforcement and structuring of obligations rather than direct substitu-
tion for private financial responsibility.

This financing pattern is analytically important because it highlights how catastrophic contamination
costs may be allocated when liability is identifiable, enforcement mechanisms are active, and a
large corporate entity has the capacity to absorb and finance recovery. In such cases, costs may be
distributed across insurance, corporate balance-sheet exposure, and legally enforced obligations.

The contrast with private sealed-source operators is therefore significant. Hospitals, universities,
and research laboratories operating irradiators may face severe contamination or interruption risks

 55

without a comparable combination of clearly consolidated liability, meaningful insurance partici-
pation, and corporate-scale financial capacity. Even where regulatory authority exists to mandate
cleanup, that authority does not guarantee the presence of sufficient financial resources or insurance
structures to support full recovery.
For this paper, East Palestine thus serves as a case in which post-event cost allocation was shaped by
identifiable responsibility and enforceable financing channels. It underscores that catastrophic loss
recovery is more tractable when liability, insurance participation, and enforcement authority are
aligned. The uncertainty facing irradiator operators stems in part from the absence of this degree
of structural financial and legal integration.

5.4. Oklahoma City: Catastrophic Intentional Harm Without a Viable Private
 Recovery Pathway

The Oklahoma City bombing is not a radiological or contamination analog for the problem examined
in this paper. It is useful in a narrower sense: it illustrates how post-event financing can become
detached from ordinary liability and insurance recovery pathways when catastrophic intentional
harm is caused by actors who are not viable sources of meaningful financial recovery. In such cases,
the existence of severe loss does not automatically translate into a workable private compensation
mechanism [72].
One reason the case is relevant is that the responsible parties were not realistic sources of payment.
In 1998, Terry Nichols was ordered to pay approximately $14.5 million in restitution for federal
reconstruction costs associated with the Murrah Building ($28.6 million in 2025 dollars); however,
his limited personal assets made the order largely symbolic in practical terms [73]. Timothy
McVeigh’s execution in 2001 eliminated any practical prospect of recovery from him [74]. As
a result, although legal responsibility was clearly established, it did not translate into meaningful
financial recovery from the perpetrators.
Post-event support therefore relied on alternative mechanisms. Immediately following the attack,
state and federal emergency declarations under the Stafford Act enabled FEMA-led response and
reimbursement of emergency expenditures, including police, fire, and medical response costs
[75, 76]. FEMA provided more than $2.8 million in emergency response reimbursements ($5.9
million in 2025 dollars) to support immediate response activities [75]. A subsequent major disaster
declaration extended federal involvement into longer-term recovery support, although the available
sources do not provide a single comprehensive accounting of all later federal assistance.
Charitable and special-purpose funding also played a central role in recovery. The Oklahoma
City Community Foundation’s Disaster Relief Fund received approximately $14–$14.7 million in
donations in the aftermath of the bombing ($31 million in 2025 dollars) and has reported cumulative
distributions exceeding that amount over time to more than 1,200 survivors [77]. The Survivor
Education Fund provided approximately $5.9 million in educational support to 217 eligible students
($12.4 million in 2025 dollars), and Project Recovery OKC distributed approximately $3.7 million
in direct assistance to victims’ families ($7.8 million in 2025 dollars) through a coordinated state
and local effort [77, 78].

 56

This pattern is analytically important because it demonstrates that even in the presence of a well-
developed public emergency response system, post-event financing can shift away from private
liability recovery when responsible actors lack the capacity to pay. Recovery is then distributed
across federal disaster assistance, victim compensation programs, and charitable funding mecha-
nisms rather than through enforceable private restitution.
The relevance to sealed-source operators is limited but still instructive. Oklahoma City does not
inform radiological cleanup or regulatory liability structures directly. Its contribution is narrower:
it shows what catastrophic recovery can look like when losses are severe but private responsible
parties cannot meaningfully finance restitution, causing reliance to shift toward public and charitable
channels rather than private liability recovery. In that sense, it serves as a financing-architecture
contrast case rather than a direct analog for radiological contamination.

5.5. Comparative Synthesis: What the Analogs Show

Taken together, these cases show that catastrophic losses are not allocated according to severity
alone. What matters most is the surrounding financial and legal architecture: whether a dedicated
liability regime exists, whether there is a clearly identifiable and solvent responsible party, whether
the relevant losses are insurable, and whether government involvement takes the form of structured
compensation or more contingent public, special-purpose, or charitable support. The central lesson
is that “who pays” depends less on the mere fact of catastrophic harm than on the institutional
pathways available after the event.
Three Mile Island illustrates the value and limits of a dedicated liability framework. Because
the event occurred within a setting already governed by a specialized nuclear claims architecture,
compensation and liability were more legible than they would have been under ordinary commercial
arrangements alone [25, 13]. The existence of that specialized framework did not eliminate the
broader financial burden of cleanup and restoration, but it did provide a clearer structure for handling
at least some categories of claims. The relevance to this paper is not that private irradiator operators
can expect the same treatment, but the opposite: most private irradiator operators do not operate
within anything comparable.
East Palestine illustrates a different pattern. There, a clearly identifiable and solvent corporate
actor, combined with strong cleanup authority and visible insurance participation, created a much
more legible pathway for allocating large contamination-related losses [65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71].
Government intervention was significant, but it largely reinforced corporate responsibility rather
than substituting for it. The case therefore shows that when liability, solvency, enforcement, and
coverage are aligned, catastrophic contamination losses may be financed through a combination of
regulatory action, private insurance, and retained corporate risk. That alignment is often missing
in the sealed-source context.
Oklahoma City illustrates a third pattern: catastrophic intentional harm in the absence of a viable
private recovery pathway. Where the responsible actors cannot finance recovery, post-event sup-
port shifts toward public assistance, victim-support programs, and charitable or special-purpose
mechanisms rather than ordinary liability recovery [73, 75, 77, 78, 79, 76, 74]. That shift does not

 57

mean support is absent. It means support is less predictable from an ex ante planning perspective
and less clearly embedded in a mature preexisting compensation structure. For this paper, that
pattern is relevant because it highlights the difference between strong public response and reliable
pre-event financial protection.
Viewed together, the analogs support the broader conclusion of this paper. The financial outcome
after a severe sealed-source incident would likely depend less on the fact that radioactive contami-
nation occurred than on whether a usable compensation architecture existed in that specific setting.
Prior Sandia analysis likewise shows that the scale and composition of radiological event costs can
vary sharply with situational conditions and are not determined by severity alone [6]. For most
private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators, that architecture remains fragmented. They
generally do not benefit from a reactor-style liability regime, may not have access to broad and
dependable insurance for the most consequential loss categories, and cannot assume that public
response authorities will operate as a complete financial backstop. The likely result is a mixed
and uncertain allocation of costs across the institution, insurers, government, and possibly other
supporting mechanisms rather than a clear, pre-established answer to the question of who pays.

6. FINDINGS AND IMPLICATIONS FOR ORS

6.1. Main Findings

The preceding analysis suggests that private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators may
remain financially vulnerable after a major sealed-source release not because no public or private
mechanisms exist, but because the available mechanisms do not combine into a clear, dependable,
and readily verifiable structure for addressing the categories of loss most likely to matter for
institutional continuity. Five findings follow from that assessment.
Finding 1: Private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators may retain meaningful
residual financial exposure after a major sealed-source release.
The available evidence does not support the assumption that catastrophic radiological losses would
be absorbed cleanly by either private insurance or federal assistance. Standard commercial coverage
may leave important portions of loss uninsured, disputed, or only partially recoverable, while
federal authorities may support response or cleanup without functioning as comprehensive financial
protection for the institution itself [1, 2, 5, 3, 7]. As a result, a private operator may remain exposed
to substantial direct costs even in an event that prompts significant public-sector involvement.
Finding 2: The losses most likely to threaten institutional continuity also appear to be among
the least predictably protected.
The losses most likely to destabilize a hospital, university, or research laboratory after a severe
sealed-source incident are not limited to immediate contamination response. They include decon-
tamination and restoration, waste handling and disposal, prolonged business interruption, disrupted
operations, certain third-party claims, and longer-tail institutional effects such as delayed research
activity, loss of access to specialized laboratory space, interrupted grant performance, and reputa-
tional damage [15, 16, 4, 9]. These loss categories are also the categories least likely to fit neatly
within ordinary commercial insurance structures. In many cases, the problem is not the complete
absence of a policy, but exclusions, narrow triggers, contested definitions of physical loss, limited
specialty-market capacity, or low visible limits [1, 8, 33, 9].
Finding 3: Existing federal authorities may reduce portions of the post-event burden, but
they do not form a standing compensation regime for private sealed-source operators.
The federal government has important tools that may become relevant after a severe radiological
incident, including nuclear liability structures in covered settings, environmental cleanup authority,
disaster-response mechanisms, and terrorism-insurance backstopping [13, 2, 5, 3, 7]. But these
authorities were designed for different purposes and do not add up to a single, predictable financial
architecture for private hospitals, universities, and research laboratories operating self-shielded
irradiators. Some of these authorities may facilitate claims handling, some may support cleanup,

 59

some may reimburse eligible public-sector costs, and some may backstop insured terrorism losses.
None appears designed to guarantee full institutional recovery across the categories of loss most
likely to matter after a sealed-source release.

Finding 4: Government response should not be treated as guaranteed institutional indemni-
fication.

A major radiological incident could trigger substantial public-sector response at some level, par-
ticularly if contamination, public concern, or jurisdictional complexity were significant. But
government response and government indemnification are not the same thing. Public agencies may
fund emergency actions, coordinate recovery, or exercise cleanup authority without making the
affected institution financially whole [2, 5, 3, 7]. In some cases, government involvement may even
sharpen institutional liability rather than relieve it, as in cleanup cost-recovery contexts. Private
operators therefore should not assume that strong federal presence after an event would eliminate
their own retained financial exposure.

Finding 5: Under currently visible market conditions, insurance does not appear to provide
a strong or consistent substitution signal.

One possible policy expectation is that institutions retaining higher-consequence gamma irradiators
would face clearly higher insurance costs than institutions using substitute technologies, thereby
creating a market-based incentive to transition away from sealed sources. The analysis does not sup-
port that expectation in any strong form. In practice, radiological contamination risk often appears
to be managed through exclusions, narrow endorsements, limited specialty capacity, or uncertain
claims treatment rather than through a smooth and visible premium gradient [4, 8, 33, 40, 9]. The
signal reaching institutions is therefore often weak, inconsistent, or discontinuous. Insurance may
still matter at the margin, particularly through policy review and targeted coverage decisions, but it
does not appear to function as a dependable primary lever for technology substitution [1].

Taken together, these findings suggest that the central financial problem for private irradiator
operators is not the complete absence of legal or financial mechanisms after a major event. It is the
absence of a single, dependable structure that aligns insurance coverage, federal authorities, and
institutional recovery needs in a predictable and readily verifiable way. That gap is what leaves
substantial residual uncertainty in the post-event allocation of costs.

6.2. What Institutions Should Realistically Assume

For private institutions that continue to operate self-shielded gamma irradiators, the most impor-
tant practical implication of this analysis is that post-event financial protection should be treated
cautiously and realistically. Institutions should not assume that large modeled economic losses
will translate into equally large insured recoveries. They should not assume that compliance with
source-security and radiation-safety requirements guarantees meaningful financial protection after
a severe contamination event. And they should not assume that substantial government response
would necessarily make the institution whole [1, 2, 5, 3, 7].

 60

A more realistic planning assumption is that a severe sealed-source release could produce a mixed
outcome in which some costs are publicly managed, some are addressed through cleanup authorities,
some are partially insured, and some remain with the institution. That retained exposure may include
uninsured restoration costs, prolonged interruption, legal expenses, disputed claims, reputational
effects, and operational losses that fall outside both commercial coverage and federal reimbursement
pathways [4, 8, 9]. For universities and research laboratories, those retained losses may also include
delayed grant performance, interruption of time-sensitive experiments, loss of access to specialized
laboratory space, and disruption of funded research programs. The practical challenge is therefore
not simply whether help would exist after an event, but whether the available help would align with
the institution’s actual loss profile.

Institutions should also assume that event classification will matter. The financial consequences of
an accidental source breach, a malicious act, and a certified act of terrorism may differ substan-
tially even where physical contamination outcomes appear similar. Insurance triggers, exclusion
language, terrorism endorsements, cleanup authorities, and public-response pathways all depend
in part on how the event is characterized legally and administratively [7, 42, 8]. For institutional
planning purposes, this legal and administrative dependence means that broad assumptions such as
“terrorism insurance would respond” or “the federal government would step in” are too imprecise
to support serious risk management.

This broader structural problem does not mean that policy review and insurance review are unim-
portant. To the contrary, institutions operating irradiators should understand their actual coverage
terms, exclusions, sublimits, business interruption language, terrorism endorsements, and any
relevant contractor or vendor indemnities. But the value of such review should be understood
realistically. It may reduce uncertainty at the margins and identify avoidable gaps, but it is unlikely
by itself to change the broader structural problem identified in this paper [1, 8, 33, 9].

In practical terms, institutions should plan on the possibility of significant retained financial expo-
sure even in compliant settings and even in events that generate meaningful public response. That
assumption is more consistent with the available evidence reviewed here than the alternative view
that either private insurance or government intervention will predictably absorb the full burden of
a major sealed-source release.

6.3. Recommendations for ORS

The most useful implication of this paper for ORS is not that it changes ORS’s mission, but that it
sharpens how ORS can explain, target, and support that mission. The analysis does not suggest that
insurance and federal response mechanisms are irrelevant. It suggests that these mechanisms do not
combine into a clear, dependable, and readily verifiable structure for protecting institutions against
the categories of loss most likely to matter after a severe sealed-source release. That conclusion
has several practical uses for ORS.

Recommendation 1: ORS can use this analysis to treat private insurance conditions as a
contextual factor rather than as a primary substitution lever.

 61

Available evidence does not show a clear and broadly visible premium signal that would reliably
drive technology transition across the broader population of private operators. ORS can therefore
use this analysis to explain why insurance conditions may matter, but should not be assumed to
function as a scalable or dependable primary lever for substitution.

Recommendation 2: ORS can use this analysis to clarify that public response is not the same
as institutional financial recovery.

Institutions may assume that a major radiological event would trigger enough government involve-
ment to protect them financially. This report provides ORS with a clearer basis for explaining
why that assumption is too broad. ORS can use the distinction between operational response
and institutional financial recovery in stakeholder engagement, briefing materials, and transition
discussions, particularly when engaging institutions that may overestimate the protective effect of
federal involvement.

Recommendation 3: ORS can use this analysis to strengthen the institutional-risk case for
source elimination and replacement.

Much of the case for eliminating or replacing high-activity sources rests on security risk, public
safety, and consequence reduction. This report adds a complementary institutional-risk argument.
Even compliant operators may face severe and difficult-to-bound financial exposure after a major
sealed-source release, and neither commercial insurance nor existing federal authorities provide a
dependable mechanism for making them whole. ORS can use that point to strengthen discussions
with institutions that are less responsive to security arguments alone.

Recommendation 4: ORS can use this analysis to focus engagement on institutions whose
missions depend heavily on continuity.

The consequences examined here may be especially salient for institutions whose operations depend
on clinical continuity, specialized laboratory space, time-sensitive research, or grant-funded work.
ORS can use this analysis to tailor discussions with hospitals, universities, and research laboratories
where prolonged interruption, delayed reopening, or loss of access to specialized space would create
outsized institutional consequences relative to the source itself.

Recommendation 5: ORS can use this analysis to encourage more realistic institutional review
of insurance, continuity, and contractor assumptions.

This report does not suggest that ORS should underwrite institutional risk management. It does
suggest that ORS can encourage more realistic planning by helping institutions identify the right
questions. At a minimum, institutions retaining irradiators should understand their contamination
exclusions, terrorism endorsements, sublimits, business interruption triggers, contractor and vendor
indemnities, and continuity assumptions for prolonged loss of use. For research institutions,
that review should also consider disruption to specialized laboratory space, grant performance,
time-sensitive experiments, and other mission-critical activities that may be difficult to recover
financially.

Recommendation 6: ORS can use this analysis to inform future decision-support and priori-
tization tools.

 62

Because financial exposure is shaped by institution type, mission dependence, continuity require-
ments, and uncertain insurance structures, this report may help ORS identify where additional
decision-support materials or institution-specific follow-on analysis would be most useful. Such
tools could help distinguish where retained source use poses the greatest institutional and recovery
uncertainty and could support more targeted engagement across different sectors.

Taken together, these recommendations suggest that ORS should treat insurance and federal cost
pathways as contextual factors that help explain why elimination, removal, replacement, and protec-
tion efforts remain important. The report does not redefine ORS’s mission. It provides additional
analytical support for how ORS can communicate and apply that mission in the face of persistent
post-event financial uncertainty.

6.4. Limits of the Analysis

This analysis has several limitations. First, insurance outcomes are highly policy-specific. Even
where broader structural patterns appear reasonably clear, actual recovery would depend on the
language of individual policies, endorsements, exclusions, limits, retentions, and claims handling.
Publicly observable market evidence for this specific risk class also remains limited. For that
reason, the conclusions of this paper are best understood as describing likely structural constraints
and financial pathways rather than predicting how any single institution’s insurance program would
respond.

Second, the universe of directly comparable cases is limited. There are few historical events
that closely match the combination of private ownership, high-activity sealed sources, severe
contamination, uncertain insurance response, and possible federal involvement that motivates this
paper. The case analogs used here are therefore illustrative rather than determinative. They are
valuable for showing different post-event financing patterns, but they do not eliminate uncertainty
about how a future sealed-source event would unfold in a specific institutional context. One example
is the Harborview case, which is highly relevant because it demonstrates that a sealed-source breach
can generate very large realized costs, but less definitive as a model of final cost allocation because
public reporting does not fully resolve which parties ultimately bore which portions of the financial
burden. Sandia’s later assessment of the incident likewise estimated recovery costs of approximately
$158 million and found that Harborview represented an extreme-case recovery scenario, reinforcing
the need for caution in treating it as a general model of cost allocation or consequence severity for
ordinary private operators [11].

Third, public cost allocation after severe events can depend on event-specific facts, political vis-
ibility, jurisdictional choices, and discretionary government decisions that are difficult to predict
in advance. This dependence on appropriations, declarations, certifications, and interagency judg-
ments is especially important because those factors shape the practical availability of federal
support. Accordingly, the absence of a standing compensation regime should not be read to mean
that government would never absorb meaningful costs; it means only that such absorption is not
sufficiently predictable to treat as assured ex ante.

 63

The conclusions of this paper would be more favorable to reliance on post-event financial mecha-
nisms if future evidence were to show a broad, transparent, and institutionally relevant market for
meaningful radiological contamination coverage, or if policymakers were to create a more explicit
compensation architecture for private sealed-source operators.
These limitations do not undermine the paper’s core conclusion. They reinforce it. The central
problem identified here is not simply that losses could be large, but that the post-event financial
architecture for private sealed-source operators remains difficult to verify in advance, incomplete
in important respects, and uncertain in application after a severe event.

 64

7. CONCLUSION

This paper examined a practical question for private operators of self-shielded gamma irradiators: if
a major sealed-source release were to occur, would private insurance and existing federal authorities
likely provide meaningful financial protection to the affected institution? The analysis suggests that
the answer is only partially, and not predictably. Important public and private mechanisms do exist,
but they do not combine into a clear, dependable, and readily verifiable structure for protecting
institutions against the categories of loss most likely to matter for institutional continuity.
On the private side, insurance remains an important part of the risk picture, but not a comprehensive
solution. The losses most likely to drive serious institutional harm, including radiological cleanup,
restoration, prolonged interruption, disputed third-party liability, and longer-tail reputational or
operational effects, also appear to be among the least predictably protected. In many cases, the
practical problem is not simply that no policy exists, but that exclusions, narrow triggers, limited
specialty capacity, and contested claims treatment weaken the value of coverage precisely where
losses are most severe [1, 8, 33, 9].
On the public side, the federal government has authorities that may shape response, cleanup, and
selected categories of loss after a serious incident. But PAA, CERCLA, the Stafford Act, and
TRIA do not combine into a standing, comprehensive compensation regime for private hospitals,
universities, and research laboratories operating sealed sources [13, 2, 5, 3, 7]. Government may
become heavily involved operationally, regulatorily, or financially in particular circumstances, but
that is not the same as a reliable promise to make an affected institution whole.
For ORS, the core implication is straightforward: private insurance should be treated as a contextual
factor, not as a dependable primary lever for substitution away from source-based gamma irradia-
tors. Under currently visible market conditions, source elimination and replacement remain more
dependable tools for reducing both societal risk and institutional financial exposure than reliance
on either commercial insurance markets or after-the-fact federal assistance.
The broader lesson is equally clear. For private sealed-source operators, post-event financial
uncertainty is itself a meaningful part of the risk. Institutions that retain irradiators should do so
with realistic expectations about the limits of both insurance and government support.
