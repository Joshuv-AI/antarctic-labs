Adaptation before and one year after liver transplantation: a longitudinal grounded theory study
Authors: Anna Brantmark, Anna Forsberg, Annette Lennerling, My Engström
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLiver transplant recipients undergo emotional, social and existential changes. Adaptation begins before surgery and continues long after discharge. However, no longitudinal grounded theory study has described this process across the transplantation trajectory. This study explored how individuals adapted by addressing their main concerns.MethodsTwelve adults (mean age 49.3 years, range 25–70) were interviewed pre- and post-transplantation. Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently using Charmaz’s constructivist grounded theory and the constant comparative method.ResultsThe grounded theory conceptualised adaptation as striving for a life in social belonging. This process was explained through five interrelated categories: Comprehending, Accepting, Adapting, Coping and Balancing. Before transplantation, participants sought to maintain social belonging by preserving continuity in everyday life despite disruption, uncertainty and hope, reflecting existential concerns and conceptualised as optimising a disruptive situation. One year after transplantation, the process shifted from maintaining to reconstructing social belonging by rebuilding stability while managing vulnerability and ongoing uncertainty, reflecting existential concerns related to vulnerability and the future, conceptualised as managing an uncertain presence. Although the categories persisted across time, their meanings evolved throughout the transplantation trajectory.ConclusionAdaptation after liver transplantation involves creating a stable new normality, that accommodates uncertainty, gratitude, vulnerability and hope.Keywords: Adaptation psychological, grounded theory, liver transplantation, longitudinal studies, psychosocial factors, qualitative research

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) offers patients with end-stage liver disease the possibility of survival and improved health. Advances in surgical techniques, immunosuppressive regimens and perioperative care have transformed LT into a standard treatment with steadily improving survival rates (Lucey et al., 2023; Terrault et al., 2023). However, while medical outcomes have advanced, the psychosocial challenges and symptom distress of LT remain profound (Serper et al., 2022; Taber et al., 2025). Liver transplant recipients (LTRs) must not only recover physically but also adapt emotionally, socially and existentially (Blanch et al., 2004; Dengsø et al., 2024). This process, known as psychosocial adaptation, represents a complex, ongoing transformation that begins before surgery and extends long after discharge (Serper et al., 2022). Understanding how LTRs engage in this adaptation process provides a crucial insight for holistic, person-centred transplant care. LT constitutes a major adaptive challenge and while quantitative research has documented psychosocial outcomes such as quality of life, depression and adherence after LT; (Åberg, 2020; Blanch et al., 2004; Dengsø et al., 2024; Taber et al., 2025) it often fails to capture the dynamic, processual nature of adaptation.

In the pre-transplant period, qualitative research offers insight into how patients interpret and negotiate illness, uncertainty and hope while waiting for a liver (Bjørk & Nåden, 2008; Brown et al., 2006). Baker and McWilliam’s grounded theory (GT) study (Baker and McWillam, 2003) is the sole attempt to map how people navigate waiting—how they manage confinement as well as the disciplining and surrendering of the self. The absence of process-focused, longitudinal studies limits our in-depth understanding of adaptation, particularly across the transition from waiting for a liver to post-transplant recovery. Research on individuals with advanced liver disease describes the illness as a threatening and morally charged experience marked by fear, guilt and loss of control (Ghasemniaye Namaghi et al., 2022). Studies also indicate that psychological distress, including post-traumatic stress symptoms, is a common and significant concern prior to transplantation (Annema et al., 2017; Bjørk & Nåden, 2008; Brown et al., 2006; Ghasemniaye Namaghi et al., 2022), reinforcing the importance of understanding the pre-transplant phase as an integral part of the adaptation trajectory.

Post-transplant studies highlight diverse challenges and coping strategies (Blanch et al., 2004; Dengsø et al., 2024; Nåden & Bjørk, 2023; Serper et al., 2022; Taber et al., 2025; Taher et al., 2021). LTRs describe ongoing struggles with self-care, fear of complications and a constant fluctuation between hope and despair (Serper et al., 2022; Taher et al., 2021). Post-transplant life is depicted as a paradoxical experience marked by gratitude and renewal, but also new burdens such as physical changes and uncertainty. Long-term follow-up studies (Nåden & Bjørk, 2023) reveal how existential reflection continues years after transplantation, as patients move from suffering and uncertainty towards humility and acceptance.

However, existing literature remains fragmented. Most studies focus on one phase of the journey rather than its full trajectory and few apply a theoretical framework to interpret the psychosocial processes involved. To date, no longitudinal GT has been developed to explain how LTRs adapt across the pre- and post-transplant continuum. Thus, the aim of this study was to explore the processes through which individuals adapt before and after liver transplantation focusing on how they address their main concerns.

Participants and methods

The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research checklist (COREQ) (Tong et al., 2007) was used to ensure the quality of the reporting of the findings (See Supplementary File 1).

Design and selection

We applied the previously described (Lundmark et al., 2016) procedure for our GT approach, which aims to generate data-driven explanatory theory grounded in participants’ experiences. The inclusion criteria were medically stable adults on a waiting list for LT who considered themselves fit enough for the interview. Medically unstable patients or those with poor Swedish were excluded. Participants were selected in a stepwise manner from the largest transplant unit in Sweden, as described by Morse (2011):

1)Convenience sampling: Adult candidates accepted for LT were consecutively recruited, either face-to-face during outpatient visits or through telephone contact, to identify the scope, major components and trajectory of the overall process involved in waiting for a LT. A total of six males and six females with a mean age of 49.3 years (range 25-70 years) were included in the study. All participants received deceased-donor liver grafts, as living-donor liver transplantation is uncommon at our centre, particularly among adult recipients. At one year post-transplantation, all participants had functioning grafts. Although some participants had experienced postoperative complications, including leukopenia, infections and biliary complications, no serious complications had occurred. The interviews took place digitally as the participants came from a wide geographical area. Post-transplant, one participant was interviewed face to face at the out-patient clinic. No other persons were present during any of the interviews. The participants had no prior relationship to the interviewer at the time of recruitment or during the initial pre-transplant interviews. Two participants received their routine care at the outpatient clinic where the interviewer is employed. However, they were not dependent on her for their follow-up and the interviews were not linked to clinical care. This distinction between clinical and research roles was maintained during the whole research process to minimise potential role-related influence on the data collection.

Data collection

Data were collected from 2022–2025. An author constructed interview guide with open ended questions was used. No pilot interviews were performed. The open-ended interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim. Thoughts, emotions and actions from the time the participant became seriously ill, to LT and the first year afterwards were recalled during the interview. The open-ended questions enabled the participants to more vividly relate memories and elaborate on their experiences. The pre-transplant interviews lasted on average 38 minutes (range 26–73 minutes), while those after one year lasted 44 minutes (range 26–57 minutes). The whole data set comprised 249 pages of transcribed text.

Reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by the first author (AB), a nurse specialist and PhD student with clinical experience in transplantation care. AB had no prior relationship with participants at the time of the pre-transplant interviews. Two participants later received follow-up care at the outpatient clinic where AB was employed, thus AB was involved in their clinical care. To minimise potential role-related influence, a clear distinction between clinical and research roles was maintained throughout the study and participation or non-participation had no impact on clinical care.

Reflexivity was ensured through continuous discussions within the research team during data collection and analysis. All authors contributed to the interpretation of the data and development of the emerging theory. The research team members have extensive experience of qualitative research (ME, AL & AF), comprehensive experience of transplantation nursing (AL & AF), as well as perspectives from surgical care (ME), which supported critical reflection on emerging interpretations and challenged assumptions during the analysis process. This professional background contributed contextual understanding of LT and may have facilitated depth in the interviews, while also implying a risk of preunderstanding influencing data generation and interpretation.

Data analysis

Initially a literature review, as recommended by Hallberg (2010), was conducted to establish whether previous prospective studies of LT with a GT approach had been conducted, but no such study was found. One prospective GT-study of lung transplantation (Lundmark et al., 2019) and one about heart transplantation performed with a different qualitative method (Lindberg et al., 2020) were identified, both covering the intermediate post-transplant period.

Following Charmaz’s constructivist grounded theory approach (Charmaz, 2010) the research team performed initial line by line coding to find words or phrases indicating important categories, qualities, or contexts related to the research question. Reflections and questions that emerged during the analysis and coding process were logged for each interview. We then conducted focused coding to detect and explain the most frequent and significant codes. This process illuminated the main concern experienced by the participants both pre-transplant and after one year. Theoretical coding indicated relationships between the codes generated from the focused coding by means of the driver for moving forward in the process at each time point. The constant comparative method was used simultaneously for data, codes and categories (Charmaz, 2010). Finally, the change process and the ways the participants dealt with it were identified.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (Dnr 2020-07079, approved 3rd February, 2021; Dnr 2021-02526, approved 19th May, 2021). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee as well as with the Declarations of Helsinki. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Findings

The participants’ overarching main concern throughout the transplantation trajectory was Leading a life in social belonging. However, the content of the adaptation process changed over time. Before LT, the core category was striving to maintain social belonging, reflecting participants’ efforts to preserve everyday continuity and social belonging while living with a progressive illness and uncertainty related to the waiting time and the future. After LT, the core category shifted towards Striving to re-construct social belonging, reflecting participants’ efforts to rebuild continuity and stability in everyday life while adapting to an altered sense of self. Across both phases, participants engaged in an ongoing and recursive adaptation process in which they continuously negotiated uncertainty, meaningful relationships and their place in everyday life before and after LT. The adaptation process was conceptualised as dynamic and recursive, involving the interrelated categories of comprehending, accepting, adapting, coping and balancing (Figure 1).

Rather than representing fixed or sequential stages, the categories constituted interrelated and recursive processes through which participants continuously negotiated and re-negotiated social belonging while managing changing physical, emotional, social and existential conditions over time. Movement within and between the categories was fluid and shaped by different enabling mechanisms. Before transplantation, coherence facilitated participants’ efforts to comprehend and accept their situation, while endurance supported ongoing adaptation to uncertainty and bodily decline. Resilience enabled participants to cope with emotional and social challenges, whereas perseverance contributed to sustaining balance and continuity in everyday life despite ongoing disruption.

While awaiting transplantation, participants described living in a disruptive and uncertain life situation characterised by physical decline, dependency and an unpredictable future. Within this context, the adaptation process reflected existential concerns related to uncertainty, hope and meaning making, conceptualised as optimising a disruptive situation. Setbacks, deteriorations, or complications often disrupted the adaptation process and required participants to re-evaluate and renegotiate their situation, leading them to revisit earlier processes of comprehension and acceptance.

One year after transplantation, the participants continued striving for a life in social belonging, although the content and conditions of adaptation had changed. During this phase, the adaptation process reflected existential concerns related to vulnerability, uncertainty and everyday stability, conceptualised as Managing an uncertain presence Although the same overarching categories remained relevant post-transplant, their meanings and expressions differed from the pre-transplant phase through altered experiences, priorities and challenges (Figure 1).

Post-transplant adaptation was characterised by an ongoing negotiation between gratitude for survival and awareness of continued uncertainty. Coherence remained important in facilitating comprehension and acceptance, while confidence supported adaptation to the new life situation. Effort enabled coping with everyday demands and self-management responsibilities, whereas experience and learning through daily life contributed to periods of balance. Similar to the waiting period, setbacks and complications could disrupt the process and reactivate uncertainty, requiring participants to revisit and renegotiate earlier phases in the adaptation process. Rather than progressing through fixed stages, movement within and between comprehending, accepting, adapting, coping and balancing was facilitated by coherence, confidence, effort and experience, while uncertainty could disrupt stability and require participants to revisit and renegotiate earlier ways of managing everyday life and social belonging.

Comprehending

Comprehension enables acceptance, which in turn promotes adaptation and is thus a foundation for the ongoing adaptation process. From pre-transplant to one year post-transplant, participants move from actively seeking information—often online and by observing other LTRs—to becoming more attentive to their own symptoms and well-being as well as everyday experiences. Experience-based learning develops over time through reflection on their process, changing circumstances and personal progress, as well as their altered understanding of themselves and their future. When experiences become understandable and meaningful, participants reach a sense of coherence that facilitates acceptance.

“You end up googling, reading and trying to get a grasp of it, looking at how many transplants are done each year and the survival rates and things like that. At least that’s how I react—I google and look up those kinds of things. And then the doctors have explained that it’s a major operation and that it takes a great toll on the body and so on. And my attitude is that I’d be satisfied just surviving an operation like that.” (I:1 pre-transplant)

During the waiting period, comprehending primarily involves trying to understand the transplantation process, the implications of severe illness and the uncertainty surrounding the future. One year after LT, the process of comprehending involves recognising that the period of waiting has ended and reflecting on the experiences that led to the present situation. It also entails developing an understanding of, and attentiveness to, the new circumstances of everyday life, encompassing physical, emotional and existentially challenging dimensions, while reflecting on both the present and the past. Through ongoing reflection on both past and present experiences, participants continuously renegotiate their understanding of themselves and their life situation. As participants increasingly understood and made sense of their situation, comprehension gradually enabled acceptance of both present limitations and future uncertainty.

Accepting

Acceptance enables a smoother adaptation process than resisting the reality of the situation. Both pre-transplant and one year post transplant, participants described the importance of accepting that transplantation is, or was, the only option while attempting to remain mindful and present in their current life situation. Across the transplantation trajectory, they also come to accept varying degrees of dependence on others for emotional and instrumental support in their everyday life, initially due to the end-stage liver disease and later as a result of experienced vulnerability despite transplantation. During the waiting period, acceptance primarily involves reconciling with progressive illness, uncertainty and dependence on others. One year after transplantation, acceptance increasingly relates to adjusting to a changed everyday life while acknowledging that vulnerability, the risk of complications and uncertainty remain. Post-transplant acceptance therefore becomes important in enabling participants to live more fully in the present moment without becoming overwhelmed by concerns about uncontrollable setbacks, future complications, or threats to their regained stability and everyday life. Through acceptance, participants became better able to reorganise everyday life and actively adapt to changing circumstances.

“I had to try to deal with it in my own way. I kind of—eventually I had to accept the idea that… that it’s—there’s nothing I can really control. I mean, I’m already doing what I can and the rest is sort of out of my hands and I just have to try to let it go and live in the here and now.” (4 post-transplant)

Adapting

Adaptation involves ways of managing the disruptive situation by creating structure, order and a sense of control in everyday life. From pre-transplant to one year post-transplant, participants move from actively reorganising their daily lives in response to deteriorating health, towards continuously adjusting to new and changing bodily conditions after transplantation. Across this trajectory, adaptation is also shaped through communication within their social networks, as well as through shifts in self-identity, sense of a new normality, performance and how they relate to others.

Pre-transplant, participants describe taking preventive actions to protect themselves from complications and making physical adjustments to cope with declining health and reduced functional capacity.

“You must listen to your body. If you’re tired, well, then it’s okay to lie down and rest for a while. Then you’ll feel more energetic afterwards... But like recently, when I’ve had more energy than in a long time, I’ve been doing a lot at home. Housework, you could say. I didn’t do that before—I didn’t have the strength. But that was just in the beginning, when you had to adjust to everything and maybe hadn’t fully accepted it yet. But now I take care of the laundry and cooking, and I can vacuum a bit. But one can feel it—I don’t have the energy I used to. Before, I could clean both floors at once, but that’s not possible anymore. There’s just no way.” (I:10 pre-transplant)

One year after LT, adaptation primarily involves managing ongoing medical follow-up, complications such as biliary problems and infections, and frequent hospital visits. In parallel with physical recovery, participants must integrate medication routines and other health-promoting practices into everyday life, while continuously organising and re-organising daily activities to maintain stability and reduce the risk of setbacks. As practical and emotional adjustments became integrated into everyday life, participants increasingly developed coping strategies for managing ongoing uncertainty and vulnerability.

Coping

Coping during the waiting period represents a crucial part of the process of adaptation, as the participants navigate uncertainty, vulnerability and hope. This phase involves ongoing efforts to manage an unpredictable illness trajectory and maintain a sense of control and meaning in everyday life. Participants described how coping includes re-evaluating priorities, maintaining routines, fostering supportive relationships and nurturing inner resilience to preserve a sense of normality. Through coping processes, they cultivate the emotional strength and flexibility needed to endure the challenge of waiting and prepare themselves for future challenges.

Coping remains a central element of adaptation even after LT, though its nature and demands often shift over time. From pre-transplant to one year post transplant, participants move from coping primarily with uncertainty and waiting toward coping with the realities of recovery and living with a transplanted organ. While the pre-transplant phase is characterised by coping with unpredictability and gradual loss of control, the post-transplant phase involves ongoing adjustment to medication regimens, medical surveillance and potential complications. In this phase, relief and gratitude due to receiving a transplant coexist with continued anxiety about organ rejection or other complications. Coping therefore becomes less about enduring an uncertain waiting period and more about continuously managing and renegotiating everyday life in the context of a “new normality,” where stability is actively maintained rather than assumed. Across both phases, coping remains a dynamic process through which participants seek to balance emotional demands, bodily experiences and everyday responsibilities. Over time, these coping processes contributed to periods of balance, where participants experienced greater stability and continuity in everyday life despite ongoing uncertainty.

“I am the way I am. I tend to withdraw when things get tough. I never went and talked to anyone or anything like that—I don’t know, deep down I kept thinking that this would somehow get better and I tried to look forward to the idea that it probably would get better. I just got through those months, and in a way, I withdrew. That was my way of dealing with it. It’s not the best way.” (I:3 post-transplant)

Balancing

Balancing, which builds upon coping represents a processual shift from managing disruption toward establishing and maintaining stability within it. Rather than a final stage, balancing reflects an ongoing and dynamic process in which participants who have developed coping strategies to endure uncertainty increasingly focus on restoring a sense of order in their daily lives. This involves continuous perseverance to optimise a life constrained by illness and transplant-related distress. Through this process, balancing represents a strive not for perfection or cure, but for a workable harmony that preserves identity, meaning and a life in social belonging.

From pre-transplant to one year post-transplant, balancing evolves in meaning and expression. During the waiting period, balancing is oriented toward enduring uncertainty and maintaining a fragile sense of everyday order while living with progressive illness. One year after LT, balancing remains central to the adaptation process but is shaped by the ongoing realities of post-transplant life.

“No, but you don’t go around thinking about it every day. But you still feel that you must plan your days based on what you’re able to do. Before, you could do spontaneous things after coming home from work, but now you can’t—instead you have to rest first. And it’s the same if I’m going somewhere; I must think about it. Go home a bit earlier, rest for a while and then I can go out in the evening or do something like that. So, it affects me in that way—that you’re always kind of thinking about it. But it’s nothing… I’ve got used to it and I’m still extremely happy with how everything has turned out. And that’s a thought you probably live with more often—just how lucky and happy you actually are. It could have been so much worse.” (I:5 post-transplant)

Having navigated the uncertainty of illness and transplantation, the participants draw on their accumulated knowledge, emotional insights and self-awareness to maintain balance. This involves integrating their experiences into the sense of self, while managing the ongoing risks and responsibilities of a chronic condition. Through lived experiences, participants learn to recognise their limits and adapt routines to maintain stability. This understanding becomes the foundation for managing an uncertain presence—living meaningfully despite ongoing vulnerability, with social belonging and hope for the unknown future.

Discussion

By developing a conceptual understanding grounded in patients lived experiences, this study illustrates the dynamic nature of adaptation and contributes to more theory-informed, holistic transplant care. Across the transplantation trajectory, adaptation emerged as a dynamic and ongoing process in which participants continuously negotiated uncertainty, vulnerability, dependence, identity and hope in order to sustain a meaningful everyday life and maintain social belonging. Although the content and focus of the categories changed from pre- to post-transplant, the underlying process remained directed toward leading a life in social belonging. Setbacks, complications and changing life conditions could disrupt periods of stability and lead participants to revisit earlier processes of comprehension, acceptance, adaptation and coping. Thus, adaptation was not experienced as a linear progression, but as a recursive and evolving process shaped over time by bodily changes, existential concerns, social relationships and everyday experiences.

Participants faced persistent symptoms, fatigue and functional decline pre-transplant that triggered the need for comprehension. As demonstrated by prior research, waiting for a LT is experienced as a physically draining and emotionally turbulent period marked by uncertainty, loss of control and the need to surrender to medical teams and available support. Patients navigate confinement, fear and fluctuating hope, while coping with severely impaired quality of life, including fatigue, depression and diminished physical and sexual function (Bagheri Lankarani and Honarvar, 2022; Baker and McWillam, 2003; Bjørk & Nåden, 2008; Brown et al., 2006; Fidan et al., 2021; Ghasemniaye Namaghi et al., 2022; Stine et al., 2020). Overall, the experience reflects a profoundly disruptive situation. Being able to comprehend the bodily deterioration and urgency of LT was a pre-requisite for acceptance. Post-transplant, this mode remained central as a starting point for recovery, reflecting on the waiting time and understanding the importance of post-transplant self-management, in terms of medication management as well as monitoring symptoms and complications, later shaping their ability to accept, adapt and cope. This is partly in line with the process adopted by lung recipients (Lindberg et al., 2020) in their strive for a new normality by means of comparing, accepting and adjusting. The findings highlight how adaptation is closely intertwined with person-centred care, as patients emerge as co-creators of their care when they develop self-management strategies. This illustrates how promoting comprehensibility, reflection and coping forms a foundation for strengthening both self-management and the patient’s sense of control in the long-term adaptation process, in line with person-centredness.

Before LT, illness often disrupted their sense of identity and belonging, making acceptance a crucial step in reconciling who they had become with who they once were. After LT, their self-concept was re-formulated around the identity of being an organ recipient, with uncertainty about the future influencing how they adapted to this new self. Heart recipients also experience a profound sense of uncertainty one year post-transplant and their adaptation process seems to take up to three years (Lindberg et al., 2020).

Participants described a compromised ability to fulfil family, occupational and social roles pre-transplant, which motivated a strong desire to optimise life and preserve normality. Post-transplant role function entailed re-evaluating life roles, such as returning to work and engaging in missed activities, with the theme of managing an uncertain presence. Similar findings were revealed one year after lung transplantation, where “reconstructing daily occupations” emerged as the core category based on the process of regaining, re-organising and enriching daily occupations as part of a transition from illness to health (Tong et al., 2007).

The differences between pre- and post-transplant experiences illustrate shifting adaptation levels. Pre-transplant, participants functioned largely within compensatory processes, attempting to maintain social belonging despite significant physiological and social disruptions. Post-transplant, although improved physical health allowed movement towards adaptation, the persistent uncertainty related to an unpredictable future and ongoing vulnerability due to the risk of infections and graft rejection created a new form of psychosocial disequilibrium. This highlights that LT does not simply enable adaptation, but instead reconfigures the process, requiring ongoing adjustments that extend well beyond the initial postoperative period.

Our GT suggests that adaptation during the first post-transplant year is a progressive process. When viewed alongside the GT of adaptation three years after lung transplantation (Lundmark et al., 2019), a meaningful distinction emerges between short- and intermediate-term adaptation. The first year appears to be dominated by the need for understanding the implications of being a LTR, managing uncertainties and developing initial coping strategies. In contrast Lundmark et al. (2019), describe a later phase in which these strategies have become more stable and embedded in everyday life. Several years post-transplant, their study illustrates how being transplanted involves maintaining these strategies through low, moderate or high adjustments, reconstructing a sense of normality and integrating the transplant into one’s identity.

Our study contributes to the field of transplantation by offering a process-oriented understanding of pre- and post-transplant adaptation that extends beyond static descriptions of well-being or quality of life. The concept of adaptation as a pathway to social belonging and hope for the unknown future provides insight into how individuals deal with ongoing concerns as part of the transplant journey. Although grounded in the context of liver disease and transplantation, our findings align with existing knowledge on adaptation to chronic illness (de Ridder et al., 2008; Naus et al., 2009; Ordin et al., 2013; Rosengren et al., 2021). Symptoms, treatment demands, complications and the broader disruptions that accompany chronic illness can act as stressors that influence a person’s ability to adapt, which may, in turn, affect the trajectory of the disease in both positive and negative ways. Furthermore, the emergence of balancing as the culminating step enriches theoretical discussions about long-term survivorship. This emphasises that the endpoint of adaptation is not a return to pre-illness identity but the creation of a new, continuous stability that accommodates uncertainty, gratitude, vulnerability, altered self-understanding and hope, i.e., a new normality.

Clinical implications

Recognising which phase of the adaptation process a patient is experiencing can guide tailored support. Healthcare teams should be positioned to facilitate these processes through continuous assessment and relational care that responds to both physiological and psychosocial needs. These insights also support the value of specialised, person-centred, long-term follow-up that addresses not only medical stability but also identity reconstruction, social functioning and coping strategies. Future research should examine how clinical factors, such as postoperative complications and rejection episodes, influence patient-reported outcome measures and adaptation trajectories, thereby complementing the process-oriented understanding generated by the present grounded theory study.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its longitudinal constructivist GT design, enabling exploration of adaptation following LT as a dynamic and evolving process over time. Interviews conducted both pre-transplant and one year post-transplant enhanced the explanatory power and conceptual depth of the emerging theory by capturing changes in participants’ experiences and meaning-making across the transplantation trajectory. The iterative analytic process, including constant comparison and concurrent data collection and analysis, further strengthened the conceptual development of the findings. Limitations include the study being conducted at a single transplant centre within the Swedish healthcare system, which may limit transferability. Digital interviews, while enabling broad geographical inclusion and longitudinal follow-up, may have reduced access to non-verbal data. Although the sample size was appropriate for GT, no negative cases were identified. As a result, the analysis may have had fewer opportunities to challenge or refine emerging categories through comparison with divergent experiences, potentially limiting the theoretical variation and complexity within the developed theory. Finally, despite efforts to maintain a clear separation between clinical and research roles, the interviewer’s dual role as researcher and clinician may have influenced data generation in the case of two participants.
