Liberia: What If Taylor

Departs the Scene? (b)(3)

Summary -

Charles Taylor--the single most powerful leader involved in
Liberia's civil war--has survived several recent attempts on his
life by disgruntled rebels and he continues to be at some risk of
assassination or overthrow. In our view, Taylor's departure

could dramatically change the political and military situation in
Liberia. He has no obvious successor and his departure

probably would lead to a major--and potentially violent--power
struggle within his rebel group. In the most likely scenario,
National Patriotic Front of Liberia (NPFL) rebel military

forces would split into various armed factions whose military
focus would shift from attacking the West African regional

In our view, there is no clear successor to Taylor, and his departure by any means
probably would spark an intense power struggle within the NPFL. Taylor has limited the
sphere of authority of all of his lieutenants and has eliminated--or reined in--those,
including civilians, whom he viewed as potential challengers.

Potential Military Leaders: We believe a Gio or Mano would have the best chance of
quickly seizing and consolidating power given that the NPFL is dominated by Gio and
Mano troops. Based on our knowledge of past schemes to oust Taylor, we believe that

‘Interior Minister Dokie, who is a Gio, would be best positioned to attempt a power grab

from the rebel leader. Dokie probably would have an edge over other rebel commanders
given his contacts in both NPFL military and political circles and reputed connections in
Burkina and Libya, | He probably would be
able to count on Burkina and Libya to continue providing materiel or other support in an
effort to maintain ties to the NPFL established by Taylor.’ Dokie also would likely have
support from senior NPFL commanders, including Chief of Staff Musa, a Gio, and his

widely popular within the NPFL, Dokie, however,
lacks Taylor's charisma and probably the leadership skills needed to hold the fractious

sophistication and probably would have major problems maintaining Taylor's effective

Alternatively, Musa, who has often expressed anger over Taylor's apparent indifference

What are Some Potential Post-Taylor Scenarios?

If the NPFL Splinters: In our view, regardless of who initially succeeded Taylor, the
latter's departure most likely would set off the fragmentation of the NPFL within three
months or so. Key indicators that no new leader was taking hold and building broad
support among Taylor's political inner circle and rebel commanders would include:

-- Signs that local rebel commanders were establishing autonomous fiefdoms
throughout Taylorland in an effort to protect their ethnic and economic
interests. We would expect to receive increased reporting of regional
_rebel leaders independently "taxing" local civilians and refusing to obey
orders given by top commanders.
-- Rival, and increasingly contentious, NPFL leadership claims and
statements on the desirability of negotiating peace from Taylor's various
former lieutenants.

-- Outbreaks of fighting between NPFL troops associated with different
commanders.

The splintering of the NPFL probably would cause economic, political, and security

conditions in Taylorland to worsen considerably. Without the authority imposed by the

charismatic Taylor, local commanders would gradually slip from central control, and

each such armed faction could develop its own agenda, leading to potential conflict

among them. Moreover, without Taylor at the helm, a number of the foreign firms

currently exporting timber and other raw materials from Taylorland under contract with

Taylor probably would withdraw, leaving the rebels with little access to foreign currency.

In sum, any semblance of economic or political structures in Taylorland almost certainly

would disintegrate, increasing prospects for local anarchy. (b)(3)

Moreover, NPFL rebels probably would shift from a common military strategy of fending
off the West African security force near Monrovia toward a more fragmented approach,
with each rebel leader concentrating on protecting his individual fiefdom. Fighting also
would likely become more ethnic-based, as Gio and Mano rebels moved to avenge the
deaths of kinsmen at the hands of Krahn rivals, who currently predominate in the
Liberian anti-Taylor forces fighting alongside the West African security force. The

without a leader capable of reliably securing military and other supplies and organizing
military campaigns. This would make these rebels more vulnerable to defeat by
ECOMOG should Nigeria decide to take the potentially risky step of penetrating rebel

The impact of NPFL fragmentation on peace talks would be uncertain. Without a focal
point, the number of players from the NPFL would multiply, limiting the prospects for a
coordinated peace effort. As their economic and military situations worsened, some
individual commanders would show more flexibility than Taylor has in negotiating a
settlement. Nonetheless, more militant rebel commanders probably would not agree to

give up local autonomy to the UN or ECOMOG unless militarily forced to do so.

If A New Military Strongman Took Over: In the less likely event that a succession
struggle was producing a forceful leader capable of maintaining NPFL unity, we would
probably see reports that regional and local rebel commanders were beginning to defer to
one of their peers on military tactics and strategy. Other indicators would be such a
leader's ability to maintain Taylor's external supply network and to gain agreement from
fellow rebels on a coordinated approach toward negotiations with the regional security
force, other armed factions, and outside mediators.

Even under this scenario, we believe a shift in the military strategy of NPFL forces would:
_be likely; the new leader probably would focus on protecting rebel territory rather than

pressing for military victories against ECOMOG. Gio rebel commanders, who have long

questioned Taylor's costly attempts to take Monrovia, probably would urge the new

commander to consolidate troops in rebel-held territory to minimize casualties and

protect rebel strongholds from other armed factions. Such a strategy probably would

shift the focus of the fighting away from the outskirts of Monrovia to western Liberia,

where United Liberation Movement of Liberia for Democracy (ULIMO) forces--the

greatest indigenous threat to the NPFL--are concentrated.

A quick succession from Taylor to a Gio or Mano military leader probably would not
increase the likelihood of a negotiated peace settlement within the next year. Like
_ Taylor, his military leaders appear bent on maintaining their military options.

Dokie would seek a negotiated settlement only if the NPFL

were about to break up into factions and no longer could counter ECOMOG. Musa also
has indicated on numerous occasions that he would be unwilling to negotiate away his
rebels' arms and freedom of movement. Should Dokie--or some other Gio or Mano
military leader--eventually decide to negotiate, however, his ethnic ties to the troops
would make him better able than Taylor to maintain the confidence and cooperation of
the rank and file while negotiating their encampment and disarmament.

If A Moderate Political Leader Emerged: Indicators that this least likely scenario was
emerging might include moves by Taylor's military leaders to choose a politician to
represent them in negotiations with West African leaders and other outsiders and to
broker disagreements among factions within Taylor’s organization. In our view, such a
scenario would become more likely if Taylor's military successors determined that their
military prospects were dim. Under such circumstances, the rebels probably would be
looking primarily for assurances of personal safety and economic incentives--including

What are the Opportunities and Risks for the United States?

' The scenario most favorable to US interests--although also the least likely--would be for

the NPFL to hold together and a moderate political leader to emerge from the leadership
struggle. Washington, however, probably would be unable to exercise much influence on
the succession process within the NPFL in the immediate aftermath of Taylor's departure.
Although NPFL leaders maintain contact with US officials--and, with the possible
exception of Musa, probably would continue to do so--they are rarely swayed by US
advice, believing most US officials are biased against the NPFL. Washington would
have a better chance of influencing rebel behavior with political and economic
inducements at a later stage--particularly if the NPFL fragmented and continued
ECOMOG military pressure and sanctions worsened the military and economic situation
for individual commanders. In the less likely event that a new strongman such as Dokie
emerged, the US could encourage him to negotiate by assuring him that the United States

and UN would work to protect NPFL fighters from retribution at the hands of ECOMOG -°

and its Liberian allies.

Fragmentation of the NPFL would greatly increase the potential for further humanitarian
problems in Liberia and in neighboring countries. A complete breakdown in NPFL
discipline, internecine strife, and increasingly ethnic-based fighting among warring
Liberian groups would displace more citizens within the country, complicate
international relief efforts there, and boost refugee flows into neighboring countries. The
deteriorating security situation would cause Liberians seeking refuge to flood into the
crowded capitol, exacerbating food and water shortages. Without Taylor and his inner
circle as pegpoints, international relief workers probably would run into many of the

fending off, local warlords bent on procuring their share of relief provisions.

As refugee outflows increased and fighting by NPFL splinter groups expanded to border
areas, neighboring countries such as Sierra Leone, Guinea, and Ivory Coast probably
would request increased US military assistance to minimize potential spillover of the
conflict. Local NPFL commanders probably would be tempted to expand their activities
across borders and to sell off large weapons caches for personal gain, increasing banditry
and potentially arming anti-regime elements, particularly in Sierra Leone and Guinea.

Although the risk to the roughly 100 US citizens in Monrovia probably would not
increase much above present levels as long as ECOMOG remained, the 14 to 30
Americans still residing in rebel-held territory would be in significantly greater danger.
Without Taylor to foster some sense of discipline among the rebels, US citizens living
and traveling in the countryside almost certainly would run an even greater risk of
suffering the same fate as the five nuns allegedly killed by Taylor's troops in October
1992.
