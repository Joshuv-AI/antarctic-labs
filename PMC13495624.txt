Wohlfahrtiimonas chitiniclastica induced leg wound infection in a diabetic patient: a case report
Authors: Jing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wohlfahrtiimonas chitiniclastica (W. chitiniclastica) is an emerging zoonotic Gram-negative pathogen. Over 40 human infections have been documented worldwide. Poor hygiene conditions and chronic wounds are recognized as high-risk factors for infection. We report a case of W. chitiniclastica infection in a patient with a sprain complicated by type 2 diabetes mellitus. Due to suboptimal glycemic control during the early hospitalization period, the wound progressed to an active infection. This case indicates that W. chitiniclastica may possess virulence capable of causing severe local and systemic infections in diabetic patients. Clinically, diagnosis should be confirmed through pathogen detection, and early intervention may reduce the occurrence of serious complications.

Introduction

Wohlfahrtiimonas chitiniclastica (W. chitiniclastica) is a Gram-negative bacillus that was first isolated from third-stage fly larvae of the obligate parasitic fly Wohlfahrtia magnifica in 2008 in Hungary [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. This bacterium is strictly aerobic, non-motile, non-spore-forming, and non-fermentative [6], [7]. Both catalase and oxidase tests are positive [8], [9]. The bacterium harbors specific chitinase genes that enable the degradation of chitin in the larval body wall of flies. It stably colonizes the intestinal tracts of various fly species, such as Lucilia sericata and Chrysomya bezziana, and serves as a natural symbiont of these flies [10], [11]. Transmission occurs primarily through contact with fly larvae. After eggs are laid on open human wounds, the bacteria carried by hatching larvae invade subcutaneous tissue, muscle, and even the bloodstream, leading to skin and soft tissue infections, wound infections, and bacteremia. In rare cases, severe complications such as osteomyelitis, sepsis, cellulitis, and gangrene may develop [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Human infections caused by this organism are rarely documented, primarily occurring in tropical or subtropical regions [17]. Clinical reports of this bacterium are scarce, and its clinical manifestations lack specificity. Moreover, it is readily misidentified as other species by automated identification systems (such as Vitek 2 and API 20 NE). In previous reports, nearly one-third of cases were misidentified when using the Vitek 2 system or API 20 NE for identification: five cases were identified as Acinetobacter lwoffii[18], [19], [20], one as Rhizobium radiobacter[19], one as Comamonas testosteroni[21], and another one as both Brevundimonas diminuta and Oligella urethralis[3]. All of these were ultimately confirmed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) or 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Kopf et al. simultaneously employed MALDI-TOF MS, the VITEK 2 system, WGS, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing to identify 14 isolates, and the results showed that the Vitek 2 system misidentified all isolates as A. lwoffii[22]. Furthermore, in polymicrobial infections, W. chitiniclastica is readily overlooked due to overgrowth by other microorganisms, posing considerable challenges for microbiology laboratories in its identification [18], [19], [20], [23]. Currently, the biochemical reaction profile of W. chitiniclastica has not yet been incorporated into the VITEK 2 database [22]. With advances in pathogen detection technologies, the clinical significance of this organism as an emerging zoonotic pathogen has received increasing attention; therefore, it is imperative to enhance the awareness and diagnostic capability of clinicians and laboratory personnel regarding this bacterium.

Case information

A 59-year-old male patient was admitted to the Department of Orthopedics at Chengdu Xinhua Hospital on January 31, 2025, with a one-day history of right lower limb pain and limited mobility following a sprain. He had a five-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus and poor personal hygiene. On admission, his vital signs were as follows: body temperature 36.9℃, pulse 100 beats/min, respiration 20 breaths/min, and blood pressure 139/90 mmHg. Physical examination revealed a 2 cm× 2 cm sinus tract on the anterior aspect of the right lower leg, with no obvious discharge, local tenderness (+), and palpable bony crepitus. Laboratory findings showed a white blood cell count of 13.54 × 109/L, neutrophil percentage of 82.6%, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein < 0.5 mg/L. Glycemic parameters were as follows: fasting blood glucose 13 mmol/L, urine glucose (++), urine ketone bodies (+), and glycated hemoglobin 9.6%. Liver and renal function tests, electrolytes, lactate, and β-hydroxybutyrate were all unremarkable. On February 1, 2025, orthopedic examination revealed marked tenderness over the sinus tract on the anterior aspect of the right lower leg with palpable bony crepitus. In conjunction with the history of tibial fracture, chronic suppurative osteomyelitis following fracture surgery was suspected. On February 5, 2025, the skin temperature of the patient's left lower leg increased, and the sinus discharge changed from bloody to purulent with a pronounced foul odor, indicating worsening local infection (Fig. 1). On February 5, purulent secretions from the sinus tract of the patient's right lower limb were collected and inoculated onto blood agar and MacConkey agar plates (Zhengzhou Autobio Biotech Co., Ltd.), which were then incubated at 35 ± 2 ℃ in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for 18–24 h. Two distinct colony morphologies were observed on blood agar. After subculture and purification, the isolates were identified as Streptococcus agalactiae and W. chitiniclastica. The target bacterium was identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Zhongyuan Huiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) with two replicate tests performed on pure culture isolates. Both tests identified the organism as W. chitiniclastica, with identification scores exceeding 2.0. According to the manufacturer's guidelines, a score> 2.0 indicates reliable identification at both the genus and species levels, and the scoring criteria of this system are consistent with those of Bruker. The physician chose to administer cefazolin sodium empirically at 1 g intravenously every 12 h. After one day of treatment with limited efficacy, the dosing frequency was adjusted to 1 g intravenously every 8 h. The initial specimen showed mixed bacterial growth, which could not be excluded as related to inadequate wound debridement. Thorough debridement with re-collection of specimens for culture was therefore recommended. On February 7, culture of the specimen collected following repeated thorough debridement yielded only a single bacterial species. On blood agar, moist, convex, grayish-white, medium-sized colonies were observed (Fig. 2a); on MacConkey agar, colorless, translucent colonies were seen (Fig. 2b). Gram staining of the pure culture revealed short Gram-negative bacilli without spores or capsules (Fig. 3), consistent with the morphological characteristics of W. chitiniclastica. To confirm the species identification, an automated microbial mass spectrometry system was used to analyze the pure culture, yielding an identification result of W. chitiniclastica with a score of 2.38. The strain was subjected to 16S rRNA gene sequencing at an external testing laboratory; comparison against the EzBioCloud database yielded 99.01% similarity to the reference sequence of W. chitiniclastica, confirming this isolate as the causative pathogen of the wound infection (Fig. 4). After the pathogen was definitively identified, routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed as shown in Table 1. In vitro antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the SAST60 system (Zhuhai Dirui Bio-engineering Co., Ltd.) with the CHICARD NMIC−150 Gram-negative bacillus susceptibility panel. Results were interpreted according to the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints for other non-Enterobacterales specified in the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) Performance Standards for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (M100, 35th edition, 2025). The results indicated that the isolate was susceptible to all clinically administered antibiotics; however, the routine non-fermenting bacteria susceptibility panel did not include the antimicrobial agents used for empirical clinical therapy. The laboratory therefore supplemented the testing with a broth microdilution susceptibility assay for cefazolin (Wenzhou Kangtai Biological Technology Co., Ltd.). On February 5, the physician initiated empirical therapy with cefazolin sodium 1 g intravenously every 12 h; after one day, the dosing frequency was adjusted to 1 g intravenously every 8 h, and treatment was continued for 20 days until discontinuation on March 5. For wound management, daily dressing changes were performed from February 5 to February 21, after which the frequency was reduced to every other day starting February 22 until March 3. Following treatment, the patient's clinical symptoms gradually improved. Purulent discharge from the sinus tract resolved on February 10, with concurrent alleviation of local erythema, swelling, and tenderness. On February 27, repeat bacterial culture of wound secretions yielded no growth. On March 1, the patient's condition had stabilized, the sinus tract had diminished in size, and wound granulation tissue was growing well; discharge was approved with instructions to continue outpatient dressing changes and standardized glycemic management. Follow-up in May 2025 revealed complete wound crusting of the right lower limb, with no erythema, pain, or discharge (Fig. 5), good glycemic control, and no recurrent infection; the patient remains under follow-up without relapse to date.Fig. 5Wound healing status of the patient after treatment (follow-up in May 2025). Discussion

W. chitiniclastica is an emerging zoonotic Gram-negative pathogen. Currently, it is believed that the transmission route of W. chitiniclastica involves direct contact of adult flies or fly larvae with skin wounds or mucosal surfaces of human hosts [9]. It readily causes infections in immunocompromised populations, such as those with poor sanitation conditions or chronic wounds. In this case, a diabetic patient developed chronic wound infection secondary to osteomyelitis following tibial fracture surgery. Etiological examination definitively identified W. chitiniclastica infection through both MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The isolate proved fully susceptible to antimicrobial agents. Following comprehensive treatment including debridement, anti-infective therapy with cefazolin sodium, and glycemic control, the patient's infection was successfully eradicated with favorable wound healing, consistent with therapeutic outcomes reported in some published cases [5], [7], [17], [24].

Clinical recognition of W. chitiniclastica infection faces multiple challenges. The clinical manifestations are non-specific and difficult to distinguish from common Gram-negative bacteria. Conventional identification systems based on miniaturized biochemical reactions, such as Vitek 2 and API 20 NE, cannot correctly identify this organism due to overlapping biochemical profiles with other Gram-negative bacilli, leading to potential misidentification [2], [5], [9], [18], [25]. MALDI-TOF MS and 16S rRNA sequencing have been proven to be reliable identification methods for W. chitiniclastica[3], [9], [18], [19], [21]. In polymicrobial infections, the organism may be overlooked due to overgrowth by other microorganisms. In this case, the colony morphology of this strain showed no significant differences from common Gram-negative bacilli, making differentiation by morphological features alone difficult. The DL−96A automated bacterial identification system failed to complete species identification. The final diagnosis of W. chitiniclastica was established through repeated verification by MALDI-TOF MS. This suggests that for patients with a history of fly larva exposure, active communication of epidemiological information to the microbiology laboratory is essential. Molecular methods such as MALDI-TOF MS or 16S rRNA gene sequencing should be employed when necessary to assist identification and improve the accuracy of etiological diagnosis.

In terms of treatment, a comprehensive regimen combining debridement, anti-infective therapy, and management of underlying diseases should be adopted. Surgical debridement is the cornerstone of treatment. Antimicrobial selection should strictly follow susceptibility results, while concurrent glycemic control and immune function improvement are essential to enhance therapeutic efficacy [17], [26], [27]. Currently, no standardized antimicrobial selection criteria exist, and available experience derives primarily from case reports. Given that this organism is a non-fermentative Gram-negative bacillus, individualized antimicrobial regimens based on susceptibility testing are recommended [28]. In this case, surgical debridement was performed with topical amikacin ointment applied to the wound. Simultaneous glycemic management combined with cefazolin sodium-based anti-infective therapy led to clinical stabilization, reduction of the wound sinus tract, and favorable granulation tissue proliferation, demonstrating significant efficacy of comprehensive treatment. This highlights that early recognition and timely intervention are critical for improving prognosis.

Furthermore, several drug-resistant strains of W. chitiniclastica have been identified in recent years. Studies have indicated that trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, levofloxacin, and cephalosporins (such as ceftazidime) may be the preferred antimicrobial agents for treating W. chitiniclastica infections, whereas this bacterium exhibits resistance to fosfomycin, amikacin, and tobramycin [22]. Strains carrying the bla VEB−1 extended-spectrum β-lactamase gene exhibit resistance to ceftazidime and ampicillin, while those harboring tet(H) and aph(3'')-Ib demonstrate resistance to tetracyclines and aminoglycosides, suggesting that clinical laboratories should strengthen resistance surveillance for this pathogen, establish regional resistance monitoring systems, select antimicrobial agents rationally based on susceptibility results, and prevent the spread and dissemination of resistant strains [29]. Meanwhile, according to current case reports, effective treatment regimens should be tailored to the specific conditions of individual patients. For severe cases (particularly those complicated by sepsis or soft tissue infections), an initial empirical regimen combining β-lactams with β-lactamase inhibitors (such as piperacillin-tazobactam) may be considered to control disease progression. Once pathogen identification and antimicrobial susceptibility data are available, the treatment regimen should be adjusted to more targeted antibiotics (such as cefepime, ciprofloxacin, or gentamicin), with timely modifications based on susceptibility results to minimize the risk of resistance development [15], [30], [31]. Therefore, further in-depth research involving more clinical cases is warranted regarding the pathogenic mechanisms, virulence factors, and host-pathogen interactions of this bacterium, with particular attention to regional differences and environmental temperature factors, to provide a theoretical basis for clinical prevention and control.

As a single case report, this study has the following limitations: (1) the evidence level from a single case is limited, precluding assessment of the overall incidence, risk factors, and prognostic patterns of infection caused by this organism; (2) in-depth molecular characterization such as whole-genome sequencing was not performed, leaving insufficient understanding of virulence factors and resistance mechanisms of this bacterium; (3) susceptibility testing results reflect only the characteristics of this particular strain and cannot be extrapolated to the antimicrobial sensitivity patterns of other strains. Future studies should accumulate additional clinical cases and conduct multicenter retrospective studies or prospective surveillance to systematically elucidate the epidemiological features, clinical spectrum, and optimal treatment strategies for this organism.

Conclusion

In summary, W. chitiniclastica is a rare yet clinically significant emerging zoonotic pathogen. The present case is particularly uncommon, occurring during winter-a non-breeding season for flies. Accurate diagnosis depends on physician awareness of this organism and precise identification by the microbiology laboratory. Heightened vigilance for this bacterium in fly larva-associated infections is essential to avoid delayed diagnosis and improve patient prognosis.
