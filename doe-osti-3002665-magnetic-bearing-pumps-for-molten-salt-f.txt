 1. PROBLEM STATEMENT
The Commonwealth Fusion Systems (CFS) ARC reactor will employ a novel liquid immersion blanket
design that uses a molten lithium salt as both the tritium-breeding material and the vacuum vessel coolant.
fluoride lithium beryllium (FLiBe) (a lithium fluoride–beryllium difluoride mixture) is currently the lead-
ing salt candidate. Its low-Z components provide good moderation, it has adequate heat transfer properties
and low electrical conductivity, and it has good neutron multiplication properties, which further enhance
tritium production. The use of FLiBe enables a thinner blanket without compromising tritium-breeding
abilities (Sorbom et al. 2015; Mumgaard et al. 2017; Kuang et al. 2018).
Figure 1 illustrates a schematic of ARC’s vacuum vessel (VV) geometry and pumping system. “Cool” 800
K FLiBe is pumped directly into the bulk tank for overflow/reserve (black), around the inboard VV main
chamber (yellow), through the upper divertor leg and foot (purple), around the outboard VV main chamber
(orange), and through the lower divertor leg and foot (blue). The red upward arrow represents “hot” FLiBe
exhausted to the heat exchanger.

 Figure 1. Schematic of ARC’s VV geometry and pumping system (Kuang et al. 2018).

The lifetime and reliability of the molten salt pumps are vital to an ARC fusion power plant achieving high
uptime and competitive economics. No existing commercially mature pumps have demonstrated the life-
time and reliability in FLiBe service to satisfy ARC’s requirements. The most challenging aspect is mak-
ing the pump’s bearings and seals compatible with high-temperature molten salt and have sufficient life-
times in that environment. Roller bearings are expected to have serious material degradation issues due to
high temperatures and corrosion. Fluid journal bearings demonstrate better high-temperature behavior but
feature a small gap, which can be fouled by the molten salt, particularly if it recrystallizes and becomes
abrasive. This risk is heightened for ARC because FLiBe corrosion processes and corrosion control mea-
sures are not fully understood or developed and are complicated by shifting salt operating points.

 1

Canned rotor pumps based on magnetic bearings are an attractive solution to these problems because they
do not require exposing seals or bearings to the molten salt. The molten salt coolant is contained in the gap
between the rotor and stator, which form an effective “can” that protects the rotor and stator components
from corrosion. The terms can and corrosion barrier are used interchangeably in this report. This pump
design eliminates rotating seals and roller element bearings. Reliability and maintenance risks are shifted
away from salt-wetted materials, uncertain salt properties, and sealing hardware to power electronics and
control software, which are informed by standard industrial techniques and can be more easily developed.
Safety mechanisms and backup systems including dual redundant controls can be built into pump electron-
ics to reduce electronics and control risks. This approach opens a near-term pathway to achieve required
pump performance and lifetime.
This Innovation Network for Fusion Energy (INFUSE) program leverages the expertise at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) in high-temperature canned rotor magnetic bearing pumps. Under the Ad-
vanced Sensors and Instrumentation program and the Nuclear Energy Enabling Technologies program,
which ran from 2013 to 2019, ORNL developed a conceptual 970 K pump design with active magnetic
bearings. A low-temperature pump test bed was created for developing and testing embedded instrumenta-
tion and controls during pump operation, as shown in Figure 2 (Melin and Kisner 2016).

 Figure 2. Low-temperature magnetic bearing pump control system test bed (Melin and Kisner
 2016).

Subsequently, from 2019 to 2022, ORNL carried out an Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy
(ARPA-E) Modeling-Enhanced Innovations Trailblazing Nuclear Energy Reinvigoration (MEITNER)
project to explore magnetic bearing molten salt pumps for small modular reactors (Melin and Hines 2021),
which produced results relevant to this INFUSE program:
 • Designed a high-temperature (970 K) 10 kW molten salt pump with magnetic bearings
 • Investigated insulation and encapsulation techniques for high-temperature low-voltage (<450 V)
 windings, which are necessary for the motor, magnetic bearings, and sensors, and tested the electri-
 cal performance of different combinations of fabrication techniques

 2

 • Investigated fabrication methods and materials for creating the canned rotor and stator corrosion
 barriers
 • Developed and tested position sensors that can sense the rotor position through the protective can
 and operate at temperatures greater than 970 K
The objectives of this INFUSE project are to scale and adapt the ORNL pump design with a magnetically
suspended rotor to the ARC device and to determine if there are any fundamental barriers to adapting the
design to the requirements of the ARC device. The work was carried out in two parallel channels: a design
study and insulation experiments. The design study consisted of the following actions:
 1. Establish notional requirements for the ARC pump, which drive the motor design, particularly motor
 power and shaft speed.
 2. Design a canned motor that can operate at the required power (1 MW scale) while immersed. De-
 termine technical feasibility, particularly regarding conductor cooling and insulation. Estimate the
 motor rotor mass, which drives weight and imbalance loads on the magnetic bearings.
 3. Design magnetic bearings for the canned motor pump. Estimate the winding voltages, which drive
 the required dielectric strength for the insulation experiments.
 4. Assess material options for the high-temperature electrical conductors and corrosion barriers.
The insulation experiments tested different materials and methods for applying a ceramic coating to metal
wire and then measured the dielectric strength of the resulting insulation. The objective of these tests was
to demonstrate insulation with a breakdown voltage at least 4 times higher than the nominal operating volt-
age of the motor and bearing coils at over 900 K determined in the design study.

1.1 WORK SCOPE
The overall objectives of CRADA NFE-22-09139 were to (1) perform a design study to adapt ORNL’s
magnetic molten salt pump design and evaluate it for use in ARC and (2) concurrently retire one of the key
risks for the pump design, the dielectric strength of insulation materials, through a combination of mod-
eling and experiments. The two channels of work proceeded in parallel and reinforced each other. The
following tasks were executed by CFS and ORNL:
 1. CFS provided design specifications for ARC’s FLiBe pumps, expanding on the high-level specifi-
 cations shown in Table 1. To do so, CFS applied ARC reference design bounds to create bounds on
 temperature, pressure, pressure drop, and throughput. From these, the pump power requirements
 were estimated.
 2. ORNL and CFS jointly performed a design study to identify modifications needed to achieve ARC’s
 pump power requirements:
 • ORNL analyzed its existing pump design to identify and execute modifications needed to achieve
 ARC’s higher pump power requirements.
 • ORNL used physics-based coupled multiphysics fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and electromag-
 netic models in MATLAB, STAR-CCM+, and GE MotorSolve to assess (1) the power, torque,
 voltages, and losses of the motor and (2) the cooling of the motor stator coils. The output of
 this work was a set of design options for the motor stator coils and corrosion barrier.
 • ORNL and CFS jointly reviewed and evaluated the design options and down-selected the most
 promising design (based on design complexity, manufacturing feasibility, and cost projections)
 for the motor stator coils. A promising design for the corrosion barrier was not identified.

 • A detailed design study of the magnetic bearings was de-scoped; only a simple feasibility/sizing
 study of the radial bearing was conducted.
3. CFS and ORNL jointly performed a material analysis study to ensure FLiBe compatibility:
 • CFS (lead) and ORNL together analyzed the material compatibility of pump components that

 corrosion experiments were de-scoped.
 • CFS analyzed the material properties, cost, and availability of high-temperature electrical con-

4. ORNL and CFS jointly investigated the high-voltage insulation risk through experiments. This task

2.1 PUMP REQUIREMENTS
ARC will require pumps at several locations in the FLiBe loop. Each location will have different pressure
rise requirements and may split the flow between multiple parallel pumps. The exact pressure rise and flow
rate requirements are not available, but the notional requirements in Table 2 are fairly close and can pro-
vide a starting point to assess the technical feasibility of a canned motor pump.
The specific speed of 2.4 indicates an axial flow pump, for which an efficiency of about 0.8 is achievable
(Brennen 2011). Thus, the motor should be designed to a shaft power of 1 MW to provide a power margin
to overcome hydraulic losses.
The pumps will need to be resistant to gamma radiation. Although the pumps can be well shielded from
neutron and gamma radiation from the fusion plasma, they will inevitably be exposed to gamma radia-
tion from short-lived activation products in the FLiBe. However, the high temperature precludes the use of
polymer insulation or seal materials that are susceptible to gamma radiation damage, so radiation dose is
not expected to be a design driver for the canned motor pump.

2.2 MOTOR DESIGN
This section presents the results of the motor design study for a 1 MW-scale canned motor pump under-
taken to identify technical risks in the development of a large motor that could operate near 1,000 K while
flooded with molten salt. The study selected a synchronous reluctance motor configuration, which can be
applied in high-temperature operation due to the absence of permanent magnets. The study identified a
concept for the stator windings that is feasible in terms of cooling, AC losses, and insulation. The winding
concept relies on exotic conductor materials to provide creep resistance and electrical conductivity at high
temperatures (dispersion-strengthened copper; see Section 2.4.1) and an unproven gas-ceramic insulation
method.
The study revealed that, for a large motor, a simple nickel-alloy corrosion barrier would have unaccept-
ably high eddy current losses in the stator can (around 20% of the motor’s shaft power). The study iden-
tified several mitigation concepts for the can eddy current losses (a corrugated can, laminated can, or ce-
ramic can). However, these mitigation concepts require additional validation and development and would
be more difficult to manufacture than a typical can.
In summary, the motor for the canned motor pump appears to be physically feasible. However, maturing
the design
 • would require significant additional research and development (R&D) because vital components are
 at a technology readiness level (TRL) (windings and can are TRL 2 by the National Aeronautics and
 Space Administration (NASA) definition) and
 • requires specialized manufacturing processes.

2.2.1 Selection of a Synchronous Reluctance Motor
This design study selected a synchronous reluctance motor. The operating temperature (about 1,000 K)
ruled out motor concepts that use permanent magnets because no permanent magnetic materials retain
their magnetization at that temperature. Brushed motors were ruled out due to the difficulty of making
moving electrical contacts survive immersion in molten salt. Separately excited motors and squirrel-cage
induction motors were ruled out to avoid the complexity of high-temperature conductors within the rotor.
Reluctance motors avoid these issues because they have only ferromagnetic material in the rotor. Of the
two common reluctance motors, synchronous and switched, the synchronous reluctance motor was se-
lected for its lower torque ripple. Another advantage of the synchronous reluctance motor is the decreased
eddy current losses in the rotor can, which make the rotor easier to cool.

 6

2.2.2 Selected Motor Design
Multiple motor topologies were simulated, and a synchronous reluctance motor with 4 poles and 24 sta-
tor slots was selected as the motor topology. This permanent magnet–free motor is capable of operating
at high temperatures. This motor operates on the principle of magnetic reluctance and can be fed from a
standard three-phase voltage source inverter. Nonlinear magnetic B-H curves were used in the simulation
study and were modified to represent the degradation in saturation flux density and permeability at high
temperatures (Takeuchi et al. 2017).
A geometric parametric model for the synchronous reluctance motor was developed. The motor parame-
ters to obtain 1 MW of power output at 1,800 rpm are shown in Table 3. The motor cross section is shown
in Figure 3. Time-stepped transient finite element analysis (FEA) with motion was conducted in Ansys
Maxwell. The motor model was excited with sinusoidal currents. The phase angle of the current resulting
in the highest torque was determined. The motor voltage waveforms are shown in Figure 4.

Hairpin windings will be used for the motor. Because of the high temperature, this motor will use ce-
ramic insulation that is thicker and has a lower allowable voltage than typical room-temperature insula-
tion. Fewer and thicker conductors are preferred to reduce the area to be insulated and reduce the voltage
across the insulation. This comes at the cost of higher AC losses and requiring a higher current power sup-
ply. Further, a limitation of hairpin windings is that rules related to slot-pole combinations and winding
layers must be satisfied (Berardi and Bianchi 2018). Analysis to determine AC loss was conducted for 2,
4, 6, 8, and 12 conductor layers per slot and showed that 6 layers of conductors per slot would result in the
minimum AC loss, with an AC/DC loss ratio 1.29. The motor will require four parallel paths so that it can
operate from a 700 to 800 V DC bus.
The losses in different conductors were calculated considering that the material for the conductors was
dispersion-strengthened copper. The losses were calculated for different grades of this material (GLID-
COP AL-15, GLIDCOP AL-25, and GLIDCOP AL-60) with IACS (International Annealed Copper Stan-
dard) values of 92%, 87%, and 78% , respectively. A linear variation of resistance with temperature was
assumed, and the loss was calculated at 700◦ C. The distribution map of losses in the conductors is shown
in Figure 5. The highest loss occurs in the conductor closest to the air gap and is about 65% higher than
the loss in the conductor farthest from the air gap.
Multiple options for stator windings and stator cooling were considered. (See Section 2.2.3.) In the se-
lected design, the hairpin conductors are directly cooled by coolant gas (helium) flowing in channels be-
tween the conductors (Figure 6). Helium flowing at bar pressure and an average velocity of a few meters
per second provides adequate cooling. Ceramic spacers within the coolant passages hold the conductors
apart at the desired distance. The coolant gas thus forms part of the insulation between the conductors and
must resist arcing. Initial Paschen’s law calculations using room-temperature gas properties indicate that
arcing should not occur. However, the elevated temperature and ionizing radiation (from decay within the
FLiBe) may make the gas more prone to arcing, and these effects were not assessed.

2.2.3 Alternative Stator Winding Options
The design study explored these stator winding options:
 • Solid hairpin windings with coolant flow between the conductors (selected)
 • Hollow hairpin windings with coolant flow through the conductors
 • Hollow concentrated windings with coolant flow through the conductors
Thermal analysis showed that thermal conduction through the relatively thick ceramic insulation between
the windings and core would not remove sufficient heat from the conductors. Thus, only designs with di-
rect cooling of the windings were considered.

 9

In addition to hairpin windings with coolant flow between the conductors, motor designs based on hollow
conductors, which would enable direct cooling by allowing fluid to pass through them (Figure 7), were
also explored. Hollow conductors are commonly used for direct cooling of megawatt-scale alternators with
de-ionized water. Using hollow hairpin windings for the direct cooling of traction motors is proposed in
Reinap et al (Reinap et al. 2019). Two-dimensional FEA modeling revealed that the use of hollow con-
ductors increased the conductor loss by 16% compared with solid conductors due to the increase in DC
resistance. However, the difficulty in manufacturing hollow hairpin windings caused those designs to be
put aside.
As an alternative to hollow hairpin windings, a motor design using fractional-slot concentrated windings
was studied. The idea was that a hollow tooth winding would be easier to prototype. To evaluate the fea-
sibility of this approach, a 2D FEA was conducted for a 12-slot 8-pole synchronous reluctance machine.
This design provided power output comparable with that of the hairpin winding design but with a 10%
higher stack length as well as a substantially higher winding loss, stator loss, rotor loss, and torque ripple.
(See Table 4.) This was due to the non-sinusoidal magnetomotive force in the air gap created by the tooth
windings as well as the lower winding factor of the tooth winding design. This approach was not pursued
due to the higher losses and larger geometry.
Finally, a design with solid hairpin windings having three turns per coil and six conductors per slot with
cooling channels incorporated between the conductors was studied. The impact on loss of using different
materials for the cooling channels was evaluated using FEA. In addition, different shapes, numbers, and
sizes of cooling channels were studied. Using a conducting material for the cooling channels was found
to lead to electrical losses in the cooling channels. Conversely, the FEA showed that making the cooling
channels from a nonconducting material would lead to the least overall loss. Therefore, a nonconducting
material for the cooling channels was selected.

2.2.4 Stator Can Eddy Current Issues
One challenge was the power loss due to eddy currents in the stator and rotor cans. The stator and rotor
cans were initially considered to be made from Hastelloy C276 with a thickness of 0.25 mm. The losses in
the stator and rotor cans were found to be 212 and 34 kW, respectively, for 1 MW of output power. The can
eddy current loss was found to scale proportionally to
 • the cube of the stator bore radius,
 • the radial thickness of the can, and
 • the can material electrical conductivity.
These scaling laws were confirmed by multiple finite element studies and an analytical treatment. Further,
the ratio of loss to useful work scales in proportion to the stator bore squared. Thus, the stator can losses
are a particular challenge for large, high-power motors like the one considered here.
Three methods were considered to reduce the eddy current losses in the can:
 • Corrugating the can
 • Making the can from metal laminations separated by a non-conductive ceramic.

2.4 MATERIALS COMPATABILITY ANALYSIS
2.4.1 High-Temperature Electrical Conductors for Motor Windings
The canned motor’s windings will operate near 1,000 K. This poses a materials challenge because the
electrical-grade copper typically used for motor windings has poor creep resistance at that temperature.
A search was conducted for an alternative conductor material with the following properties:
 • High electrical conductivity at an operating temperature in the 950 to 1,000 K range
 • Moderate creep resistance at an operating temperature in the 950 to 1,000 K range, roughly quanti-
 fied as withstanding stress of 10 MPa for 10 years with <1% strain
 • Commercially available as bar stock at an acceptable price
Several factors were explicitly excluded from being constraints on the material search. The conductor ma-
terial will not be in contact with the FLiBe, so fluoride corrosion resistance is not a concern. Oxidation is
another factor that often limits the use of copper windings at high temperatures. However, the windings for
this motor concept can be kept in an inert gas atmosphere while hot, so oxidation resistance above room
temperature is not a limiting factor. Finally, the pump/motors will be shielded from the tokamak’s neutron
flux, so neutron activation is not a concern.
The following categories of materials were considered:
 • Silver alloys
 • Dispersion-strengthened copper composites
 • Copper–nickel alloys
A dispersion-strengthened copper, UNS C15715,3 was selected as the conductor material for this design
study. Its electrical conductivity is superior compared with the copper–nickel alloys but is lower than the
electrical conductivity of silver. Compared with silver, it is cheaper and stronger.4 Silver diffusion at the
operating temperatures is also a concern.

2.4.2 Corrosion Barriers
Materials corrosion in high-temperature FLiBe salt plays a crucial role in the development of the salt-
facing components of the molten salt pumps. During the Molten Salt Reactor Experiment programs at
ORNL, the nickel-based Hastelloy N (UNS N10003) was developed specifically for a combination of cor-
rosion resistance in molten fluoride salts and air-side oxidation resistance. Recently, vanadium alloys have
attracted more attention because they are high-performance low-activation advanced alloys for fusion de-
vices, but their compatibility with FLiBe salt has not been fully evaluated yet. In addition to the relatively
highly corrosion-resistant alloys, such as Alloy C-276, Haynes 230, Inconel 617, and stainless steel 316H,
some coating materials (i.e., silicon nitride and nickel-tungsten carbide) have been investigated recently
to further enhance the performance of the salt-facing components by combining corrosion-resistant ma-
terials with advanced coating technologies (Hensel et al. 2023; Handy-Cardenas 2024). Assuming a 0.25
mm thick can and a 30 year life, corrosion rates (in micrometers per year) need to be limited to single dig-
its. Although attainable, these are very low corrosion rates. Applying a corrosion barrier to the can base
material would support meeting this low–corrosion rate requirement.

The technology for fabricating and installing metallic corrosion barriers in low-temperature pumps is well
developed. The primary risk of metallic corrosion barriers is uncertainty in the corrosion rates of the dif-
ferent materials. The corrosion rates are highly dependent on both the molten salt chemistry and any im-
purities in the molten salt. Uncertainty in corrosion rates can be mitigated by conducting experiments ad-
ditional corrosion experiments, employing salt cleaning in the coolant loop, and monitoring the salt chem-
istry.
Fabricating a metallic can using additive manufacturing is a lower-TRL process but has worked well dur-
ing prior research at ORNL. The can is applied directly to the magnetic core material using laser powder
deposition welding. This corrosion barrier can then be machined or ground to the final corrosion barrier
thickness.
Ceramic corrosion barriers are an attractive option because they can be approximately 10 times thinner
than metallic barriers and would eliminate eddy currents in the can. Ceramic tubes are commercially man-
ufactured at the diameters needed for this application, but the wall thicknesses available commercially off
the shelf are larger than required, so efforts to find a commercial source would be necessary. Another chal-
lenge is bonding the ceramic tube to a metallic frame. Active metal brazing is one option. Additionally,
compliant structures would be required in the metallic frame to accommodate differential thermal expan-
sion and prevent overstressing the ceramic can.
In summary, to develop practical corrosion barriers, additional R&D is needed to
 • reduce the uncertainty in corrosion rates,
 • quantify the effects of salt impurities on corrosion rates,
 • identify alternative failure modes like grain boundary corrosion and embrittlement,
 • determine suitable metal-to-ceramic brazing techniques that are salt corrosion resistant,
 • design compliant features,
 • evaluate coating integrity under thermal cycling, and
 • investigate ceramic coatings.

2.5 RETIRING OF INSULATION RISK
A substantial challenge to developing practical electromagnetic devices that can operate above 350◦ C–
400◦ C is high-temperature electrical insulation. Operating at >650◦ C greatly limits the materials that can
be used as electrical insulation. Suitable insulation includes glasses, ceramics, gases, and vacuum. The
CFS canned pump has four insulation applications:

 • Magnetic bearing coils
 • Motor coils
 • Sensor coils
 • Coil to the pump chassis
For all four applications, feasible solutions have been developed based on the insulation voltage limitations
that were determined both experimentally and theoretically. The solutions are at TRL 2 or 3 based on the
NASA definition, so additional R&D is needed to improve the insulation TRL. Continued development
will require programs to

 16

 • study and optimize fabrication methods,
 • develop a better understanding of conductor diffusion into the insulation at high temperatures,
 • determine radiation impacts on the insulation,
 • perform techno-economic analysis of competing solutions, and
 • perform component reliability and lifetime testing.

2.5.1 Proposed Insulation Solutions
When scaling up the magnetic bearings to the ARC pump, the voltage does not need to increase from the
10 kW pump designed during the ARPA-E project. The larger force required can be generated using the
same voltage by increasing the cross-sectional area of the magnetic flux between the rotor and stator. The
key drivers of the magnetic bearing voltage requirement are the rotational velocity and the mass imbalance
of the shaft. Table 6 provides details on the design of the radial magnetic bearing.
The larger motor required by the CFS pump necessitates an increase in voltage because a high-power elec-
tric motor operating at low voltages is very inefficient. A balance between the motor voltage and efficiency
was achieved, and the motor design values are given in Table 3. Emphasis was placed on using a small
number of turns in the motor coil so that larger conductors could be used. The larger conductors are me-
chanically stiff, so they will be mechanically separated even without the insulation. A novel insulation
method was developed using a machinable ceramic as detailed in Section 2.2.2. This insulation physi-
cally separates the turns of the motor coils while also providing a pathway for gas cooling the coils. This
design allows an arbitrary separation between the coils. The primary concern is arcing through the helium
coolant. Fundamentally, any concerns about the design can be addressed through engineering and testing.
The rotor position sensors are small air-core inductive sensors. The sensor coils operate at 5 V and are
wound on a ceramic bobbin, so their insulation is not a concern.
The final insulation application is to insulate the pump magnetic cores from the magnetic bearings and
motor coils. The motor operates at a higher voltage than the magnetic bearings, so the highest voltage dif-
ference occurs between the motor coils and the motor stator core. The coil and the core need to have suffi-
cient mechanical separation to prevent arcing. This could be achieved by spraying or coating the inside of
the motor’s stator slots. Another potential solution is to use solid machined ceramics to provide separation
that would allow helium gas flow for cooling, similar to the insulation designed to separate the coils in the
motor.

2.5.2 Potential Electrical Insulation Materials
The base temperature of the conductors is the molten salt temperature of 975 K. Due to electrical losses
during operation, the conductor temperature will be above the molten salt temperature. The motor thermal
design must keep the conductor temperature under 1,000 K, but for a safety margin, we assume that the
electrical insulation needs to withstand a minimum of 1,025 K (752◦ C), and preferably it will be usable up
to 800◦ C.
The potential solid insulation materials fall into two categories: ceramics and glasses. Although ceramics
and glasses are chemically similar, ceramics have a crystalline structure, making them hard, brittle, and
inflexible, whereas glasses have an amorphous structure that allows them to be flexible, particularly in fiber
or thread form.
A range of commercially available pure ceramics along with ceramic adhesives, epoxies, and putties will
work in this application. The primary design considerations are the

 17

 • dielectric strength,
 • coefficient of thermal expansion,
 • density,
 • thermal conductivity,
 • green ceramic wettability,
 • green ceramic adhesion,
 • green ceramic toughness, and
 • green ceramic flexibility.
Two companies produce green ceramic coated nickel, coated copper, silver, and pure nickel conductors:
California Fine Wire and T-Axel. Both companies have a 24 American Wire Gauge (AWG) maximum
conductor diameter, and both green ceramic coatings are robust. Impulse testing and polarization index
testing showed that the California Fine Wire product is suitable for magnetic bearings and sensors. The
T-Axel product did not demonstrate sufficient dielectric strength for magnetic bearings, but more testing
is required. Some commercially available machinable ceramics also allow for novel insulation techniques
like the synchronous reluctance motor design proposed in Section 2.2.2.
Porcelain is a specific ceramic formulation that is used for insulators on high-voltage power transmission
lines (138–765 kV AC) and should be considered for this application. Green porcelain is easily shaped.
High-voltage porcelain insulators are glazed so that the surface is not porous, increasing their voltage resis-
tance. Glazing could also be used for solid and machinable ceramics after they are at net shape to improve
voltage resistance.
Quartz glass (silicon dioxide) can be obtained commercially and has a 1,600◦ C softening temperature
and a 950◦ C–1,050◦ C continuous working temperature that depends on the commercial product (Her-
aeus Conamic; Technical Glass; Saint-Gobain). Quartz glass comes in multiple form factors: plates, tubes,
yarns, roving, chopped strands, threads, wool, and felt (Saint-Gobain). One commercial product—Quartzel,
made by Saint-Gobain—has a 1 × 10−8 Ω · m resistivity at 1,000◦ C.
Gases can also be used as insulators. In this case, the separation between conductors needs to be main-
tained either through the stiffness in the conductors, nonconducting mechanical spacers, or a combination
of both. The conductor geometry, maximum voltage differential, and space between conductors of different
voltages need to be considered when using a gas as the insulator. Paschen’s law can be applied to deter-
mine the minimum space between conductors of different voltages to prevent the gas from ionizing and
arcing. Another important factor when using a gas as an insulator is oxidation, which can cause conductor
deterioration.
Vacuum is the most effective insulator. However, removing heat from the coils is limited to conduction and
thermal radiation, and the conductors still need to be mechanically separated.
An alternative to traditional ceramics like aluminum oxide or silicon dioxide that sinter at high tempera-
tures is low-temperature cofired ceramic (LTCC), which are typically used to create radio frequency elec-
tronics and application-specific integrated circuits. They sinter at a temperature low enough to be cofired
with the electronics and in this case would sinter below the melting temperature of the conductor. The ce-
ramic substrate is usually a tape-cast ceramic borosilicate glass (although quartz glass can be used) or a
ceramic powder that is doped to reduce the sintering temperature. Recently, LTCCs have been investigated
for high-temperature wire insulation (Wang et al. 2015). In this work, a powder mixture was suspended
in a binder, and the wire was dipcoated. The sintered ceramic had a dielectric breakdown strength of 17.6
MV/m, approximately twice the estimated breakdown voltage of the current high-temperature wire insula-
tion.

2.5.3 Insulation Design and Fabrication
Because ceramics/glasses will be important in the electrical insulation, this section discusses ceramic fab-
rication along with insulation designs and fabrication methods, beginning with ceramic fabrication. Ce-
ramics like pottery have been fabricated by humans for over 20,000 years (Wu et al. 2012). Methods for
ceramic fabrication have evolved over the past twenty centuries. Originally, pottery was made from clay
consisting of alumina, magnesia, silica, water, and impurities (Kodama and Grim 2024). The clay was
modified by adding water or drying the clay to a shapable consistency, shaping the clay to the desired
shape, and then firing the clay at high temperatures. Many modern ceramic fabrication techniques follow
the same methods. The shaped ceramic before firing or after being partially fired is a green ceramic.
Sintering occurs when a green ceramic is heated below its melting point (800◦ C–1,200◦ C), which depends
on the ceramic’s chemical composition. Sintered ceramics have an ordered crystalline atomic structure. It
is densified but still slightly porous and can absorb a small amount of water (between 0.5% and 3%) per
the ASTM International C373 standard (ASTM International 2021). Vitrification heats a green ceramic
above its melting temperature (1,100◦ C–1,600◦ C). Vitrified ceramics have a glassy, unordered amorphous
atomic structure. The ASTM C373 standard requires that they must have water absorption less than 0.5%
to be considered vitrified. Ceramics can be glazed to add a protective coating to the surface that is less
porous, increasing the effectiveness of the ceramic as an insulator.
The methods used to shape green ceramics depend on the consistency of the green ceramics. Slip casting
uses a slurry of water and clay called a slip to form hollow ceramics with complex shapes like vases and
toilets. The slip is poured into a plaster mold. The plaster absorbs water from the clay slurry, which forms
a shell on the inside of the mold. After some time, the remaining liquid slip is poured out of the mold,
leaving only the shell. The thickness of the shell is determined by the time the slip is left in the mold.
Greater thicknesses can be built up by repeating the process.
Molding can also be used to form complex shapes. The green ceramic must be stiff enough to hold its
shape without support but pliable enough to be formed. A green ceramic this stiff can also be easily ma-
chined, although dimensional tolerances are not particularly precise.
When green ceramics dry, they shrink. They shrink further during sintering and even further during vitrifi-
cation. Finite element software can be used to predict the shrinkage, but manufacturing ceramics with high
tolerances using slip casting or molding is a trial-and-error process, so initially the cost of developing the
component is high, but for high component volumes, the cost per component is low.
Cold isostatic pressing compresses a ceramic powder to a near net shape using a water bladder to provide
even surface pressure. This improves the mechanical properties and density of the ceramic after sintering
(Akimov 1998). Cold isostatic pressing is typically used to produce plates and cylinders.
Some commercially available ceramics can be machined, such as Shapal Hi M Soft, Macor, boron nitride,
aluminum nitride, zirconia-toughened alumina, forsterite, and soft talc ceramics. These ceramics can be
machined to much higher tolerances than those possible with molding or slip casting. Economically, ma-
chined ceramics are more suitable for low production volumes.
Some companies like Cotronics and Aremco make ceramic adhesives and coatings. These products can be
used to dipcoat conductors. Different products from Cotronics and Aremco were tested to determine their
dipcoating characteristics qualitatively. All products adhered well to the conductor and built up a uniform
layer. However, they showed surface cracking when the conductor was bent after the coating dried, unlike
the green ceramic–coated wire from California Fine Wire and T-Axel. These coatings were also used to
encapsulate commercial green ceramic–coated wire to provide mechanical stiffness and additional insula-
tion. This worked well in practice.

 19

The maximum-radius green ceramic–coated wires from California Fine Wire and T-Axel are 24 AWG.
For flexible conductors larger than this, fabrication techniques need to be explored experimentally. One
possible solution is to braid or wind quartz fibers around the conductor prior to dipcoating the wire in a
ceramic coating. The ceramic-coated conductor is then immediately wound onto a bobbin, and then the
coil is fired. The insulation thickness is limited by the thickness of the quartz fiber winding or braid.
If additional thickness is required, multiple layers of quartz fiber could be braided around the conductor.
The quartz fiber would provide mechanical separation as the ceramic coating cures. One concern with this
technique is that shrinkage in the ceramic coating during curing could create cracks or voids, which would
reduce the resistance of the insulation. Additionally, ceramic shrinkage may cause gaps between the con-
ductor and the ceramic, which would impede heat transfer from the coil into the coolant, raising the con-
ductor temperature.
Another possible technique for insulated coils with gauges larger than 24 AWG is to braid a layer of quartz
fiber around the conductor that is thick enough to provide the required breakdown voltage. Then, the con-
ductor is wound around the bobbin, and the ceramic coating is vacuum molded to backfill the voids in the
quartz fiber with the ceramic coating.
For even larger conductors that are self-supporting, using machined ceramic spacers as described in Sec-
tion 2.2.2 is an attractive option because it allows for better cooling of the coils. These spacers would be
relatively simple to fabricate.
Finally, an unexplored insulation fabrication method using ceramic additive manufacturing could be used.
Green ceramic coil insulation is printed with a void where the conductor will be located. Then, after sinter-
ing or vitrification of the ceramic, molten conductor material is poured into the ceramic’s void, forming the
coils. One challenge with this technique is collapse of the void in the green ceramic. Internal voids have
been fabricated using ceramic additive manufacturing, but there is a maximum void size that can be fabri-
cated, which would limit the conductor cross section. Another challenge is joining the ends of the coils to
other conductors outside of the ceramic encapsulation to complete the conductor geometry.

2.5.4 Insulation Breakdown and Aging
At high electric potential differences between two conductors, the insulating material may break down;
this creates a short-circuit arc where current flows between the conductors. The short-circuit arc current
can be extremely high and damage the insulation and conductor. This insulation breakdown can occur be-
tween two turns of a coil and between the coil and the grounded chassis of the pump. As the solid insula-
tion ages, the voltage needed to initiate an arc decreases. Insulation using gases or vacuum does not age,
but changes to gas chemistry, oxygen inclusion, and moisture change the breakdown voltage.
When the insulating medium is a gas or vacuum, arcing occurs where the gas is ionized into a plasma,
which allows a current arc to flow between the conductors. Paschen’s law can be used to determine the
breakdown voltage of gases based on the gap between the conductors. The current arc through the ionized
gas starts with either thermionic emission or field emission. Conductor geometry affects the voltage dif-
ferential needed to initiate arcing. Sharp corners or points create a higher voltage gradient that reduces the
breakdown voltage.
A solid insulator’s breakdown voltage depends on the density, coating, types of impurities, water absorp-
tion, and aging. One concern is the absorption of water into the ceramic or glass insulation, which reduces
the insulation breakdown voltage. Less water is absorbed when a ceramic has been more densified through
sintering or vitrification, giving it a higher breakdown voltage. Glazing also reduces the water absorption
of the insulator.

 20

For the hybrid insulation proposed for the CFS pump motor in this work, to improve the breakdown volt-
age, the gas used as a coolant may need to be dried before flowing through the cooling channels.
Over time, solid insulation ages, reducing its effectiveness. When insulation is subjected to a voltage dif-
ferential, it causes electrons to tunnel into the insulation using Fowler–Nordheim tunneling. This electron
is trapped in the insulation. The energy required to trap the electron must be dissipated, and this dissipated
energy damages the insulation. Over time, the cumulative damage reduces the insulation resistance.

 3. CONCLUSION
In this work, we investigated whether any fundamental barriers to scaling up a canned rotor pump from a
10 kW design to 1 MW exist.
A synchronous reluctance motor was designed with a lower operating voltage and a unique coil insulation
design that improves cooling. The motor cooling was simulated, and a helium gas coolant removed enough
heat to cool the conductors. A radial magnetic bearing that meets the frequency and slew rate requirements
of the pump was designed. This magnetic bearing operates at voltages lower than the experimentally deter-
mined maximum useful voltage for a commercially available green ceramic–coated wire. The rotor posi-
tion sensors operate at 5 V, so sensor coil insulation is not an issue.
One of the major challenges is the design of the stator and rotor cans, which act as the corrosion barrier.
Materials that can resist molten salt corrosion over the lifetime of the pump were identified. The potential
options are Alloy C-276, Haynes 230, Inconel 617, vanadium alloys, and stainless steel 316H. Silicon ni-
tride ceramic is another potential corrosion barrier. The silicon nitride could be a solid corrosion barrier or
a coating on a metallic corrosion barrier. Finally, nickel cladding reinforced with tungsten carbide applied
to a metallic substrate is a possibility.
Metallic corrosion barriers are a well-established technology but would need to be about 10 times thicker
than a silicon nitride corrosion barrier. Compared with a silicon nitride corrosion barrier, the thicker metal-
lic corrosion barrier would decrease the motor efficiency, increase the size of the magnetic bearings, limit
the rotational speed of the motor, reduce the bandwidth of the rotor position sensors, and increase eddy
current heating of the corrosion barrier. On the other hand, silicon nitride corrosion barriers would improve
electromagnetic component performance and reduce eddy current heating and losses. A silicon nitride can
would be more challenging to fabricate and bond to the metallic pump structure. A silicon nitride corro-
sion barrier would also require the design of compliant features at the metal–ceramic interface to prevent
large tensile stresses on the ceramic.
In conclusion, no fundamental physics barriers to developing a canned rotor pump with a magnetically lev-
itated rotor exist. The proposed designs for the CFS molten salt pump and its components are at TRL 2–4
based on the NASA definition, so significant additional R&D is required to develop a pump that will have
the lifetime and performance necessary to meet the pump requirements. The primary challenges can be
solved through good engineering design and testing.
