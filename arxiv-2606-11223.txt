Scenario Constraints with Memory: A Finite-State Approach to Quantitative Financial Analysis
Vitaly Nürnberg

Abstract
Quantifying worst-case and best-case performance under complex market scenarios is a persistent challenge in financial risk management and the verification of path-dependent financial instruments, such as exotic options and structured products. Simulation-based methods are well suited for probabilistic estimation, but they do not directly provide exhaustive guarantees over all admissible scenarios or explicit witnesses for extremal outcomes. To address this, we introduce a quantitative automata-based framework for the exact extremal analysis of financial systems under declarative scenario constraints. At the core of our approach are event history automata (EHAs), a new formal model that integrates regular-expression event patterns with admissible numerical intervals to represent constrained event histories with memory. Quantitative payoffs are represented by weighted finance finite automata (WFFAs), which allow transition weights to depend on observed market values. By computing the synchronized product of EHAs and WFFAs, our framework enables the exact calculation of upper and lower payoff bounds. Furthermore, the method automatically extracts interpretable witness event histories that realize these extremal outcomes. We demonstrate the practical viability of the approach through a case study of an autocallable structured product with path-dependent mechanisms. The case study analyzes how different scenario constraints affect coupon accumulation, early redemption, and protection-loss outcomes. Scalability experiments indicate that the framework’s execution remains computationally feasible for practical contract horizons and nontrivial constraint configurations. Overall, this approach provides a mathematically rigorous complement to standard financial simulation methods.

Introduction

Path dependence is a central feature of many financial instruments and
risk-management problems. In such settings, value and risk may depend not only on
terminal market states but also on the temporal structure of observed events and
the evolution of market quantities over time.
Examples include path-dependent derivatives such as barrier and Asian
options
[
10
]
, as well as autocallable structured products with early
redemption features
[
8
]
.

In practice, such systems are often analyzed using simulation-based approaches
such as Monte Carlo methods
[
7
]
. These techniques are effective for estimating
expected values and probabilistic risk measures under stochastic assumptions.
However, they explore sampled subsets of the admissible scenario space and therefore
provide limited guarantees about extremal outcomes. Rare scenarios with significant payoff impact may remain undiscovered by sampling.

At the same time, analysts and risk managers often formulate market assumptions in
qualitative and sequential terms: prolonged stress, delayed recovery, repeated
adverse event patterns such as consecutive downward movements, or restrictions on
certain events after specific observation steps; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
.
Such assumptions are naturally expressed as constraints on event histories, but
they are difficult to combine with quantitative payoff models in a way that
supports exact best-case and worst-case analysis.

Formal methods provide a natural foundation for this perspective. Automata and
regular expressions
[
9
,
12
]
are mathematically precise models for
specifying and analyzing discrete event structures. They also form a standard
basis for verification techniques such as model checking
[
2
]
. Weighted automata
[
5
]
extend finite automata with quantitative semantics
over semirings. Weighted finance finite automata (WFFAs), introduced recently in
[
6
]
,
extend this quantitative automata perspective to financial payoff modeling by
allowing payoff contributions to depend on observed market values.

The main goal of this paper is to complement simulation-based financial analysis
with a finite-state framework for rigorous extremal reasoning. Instead of assigning
probabilities to market paths, we describe which paths are admissible under a given
set of scenario assumptions. This non-probabilistic viewpoint is intended for settings such as stress testing, scenario design, and worst-case analysis, where explicit constraints and extremal outcomes are of primary interest; see, e.g.,
[
3
]
. It also provides a transparent mechanism for incorporating investor views or
expert judgments by expressing qualitative market beliefs as constraints on
admissible scenarios, in the spirit of view-based approaches such as the
Black–Litterman model
[
4
]
.

Contributions.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

•

We introduce pattern-based scenario constraints as a declarative specification
language for admissible financial scenarios. These constraints combine two components: event patterns specified by regular expressions and admissible numerical intervals. They allow qualitative market
beliefs, stress assumptions, and temporal restrictions to be expressed directly at
the level of event histories.

•

We define event history automata (EHAs) as finite-state mechanisms for
compiling and resolving pattern-based scenario constraints. EHAs are closely related to Moore machines
[
13
]
: after reading an event history prefix, their state
summarizes the relevant prefix information, and their output determines the
admissible data interval associated with the current history prefix.

•

We develop synchronized product constructions that combine EHAs with the
WFFA-based payoff-modeling formalism of
[
6
]
. The resulting product automaton assigns to each admissible finance word the same payoff as the original WFFA, while words violating the EHA scenario constraints are excluded from the domain of evaluation.

•

We derive effective procedures for exact best-case and worst-case payoff
analysis under scenario constraints, adapting graph-based extremal-value techniques
for weighted automata
[
1
]
to WFFAs with data-dependent transition
expressions. These procedures compute exact extremal payoff bounds and automatically extract witness event histories that realize them.

•

We implement the proposed constructions in a Java research prototype
supporting an end-to-end workflow from declarative scenario specification to
scenario-constrained extremal payoff analysis. The prototype translates input
models into automata, constructs the required synchronized products, and computes
extremal payoff values together with witness histories.

•

We evaluate the framework on a stylized autocallable structured-product case
study with typical payoff mechanisms of such products, including coupon
accumulation, early redemption, and downside protection features; see,
e.g.,
[
8
]
. The experiments demonstrate the expressiveness of our declarative scenario specification, the interpretability of extracted witness histories, and the computational scalability of the framework with respect to contract horizon and the number of scenario constraints.

Scenario Constraints and Event History Automata

2.1
Finance Words and Scenario Languages

We focus on discrete financial scenarios, observing a market over a finite number
of time steps. At each step, we simultaneously capture a discrete market event and
its corresponding quantitative value. Events represent qualitative triggers, such
as market shocks or corporate earnings announcements, while the associated
numerical observations record market quantities such as asset prices or index
returns at that moment.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet of discrete market events, and let
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the quantitative data domain.
1
1

1

More general choices, such as
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, could model negative returns or several market quantities. For simplicity and clarity of presentation, we restrict attention to the one-dimensional non-negative setting

\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

A
finance word
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
is a finite sequence
w=(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})
, where
n\geq 0
,
a_{i}\in\Sigma
, and
d_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. The set of all such sequences is denoted by
(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}
. These words serve as formal representations of the discrete financial scenarios introduced above.
For instance, the sequence

w=(\texttt{earnings\_announcement},100.0)(\texttt{market\_shock},85.5)

represents a scenario in which an earnings announcement is observed at a market
value of
100.0
, followed by a market shock with the market value reduced to

85.5
.

In financial analysis, assumptions about future market behavior usually describe
a set of possible scenarios rather than a single future outcome. We represent such
sets as scenario languages. Formally, a
scenario language

\mathcal{L}

over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
is a set of finance words, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}
.

Example 1

Consider the share price of a publicly traded company observed over a finite
sequence of market events.
We use the alphabet
\Sigma
with event labels
normal
,

earnings_miss
, and
macro_shock
, denoting an ordinary trading
day, weaker-than-expected company earnings, and an adverse macroeconomic event,
respectively.
By default, admissible prices are required to lie in the interval
[98,102]
.
This default is overridden by the following history-dependent cases, listed in
order of priority:

•

after an
earnings_miss
followed later by a

macro_shock
, the admissible interval becomes
[40,80]
for the
remainder of the scenario;

•

after a
macro_shock
followed later by an

earnings_miss
, the admissible interval becomes
[90,104]
.

The first case has higher priority. Hence, if both ordered patterns occur, as in

\texttt{earnings\_miss}\;\dots\;\texttt{macro\_shock}\;\dots\;\texttt{macro\_shock}\;\dots\;\texttt{earnings\_miss},

the first case determines the admissible interval, namely
[40,80]
.
These rules define a scenario language: a finance word is admissible if each
observed share price lies in the interval prescribed by the event history prefix.
For example,

(\texttt{normal},100)(\texttt{earnings\_miss},99)(\texttt{macro\_shock},70)(\texttt{normal},d)

is admissible exactly when
d\in[40,80]
.

2.2
Pattern-Based Scenario Constraints

Example
1
suggests a convenient way to specify scenario assumptions. Relevant event histories are described by regular expressions. Numerical ranges are then assigned to observations whenever such patterns apply.

Regular expressions.

Recall that
\Sigma
denotes the finite alphabet of discrete market events.
Let
\Sigma^{*}
denote the set of all words
w=a_{1}\dots a_{n}
, where

n\geq 0
and
a_{i}\in\Sigma
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. The unique word of
length zero is denoted by
\varepsilon
. We identify each symbol
a\in\Sigma
with
the corresponding one-letter word in
\Sigma^{*}
. Any set

L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is called a language over
\Sigma
.
For languages
L_{1},L_{2}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, their concatenation is

L_{1}\cdot L_{2}=\{uv\mid u\in L_{1},\ v\in L_{2}\}
. The Kleene star of

L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
is
L^{*}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}L^{k}
, where

L^{0}=\{\varepsilon\}
and
L^{k+1}=L^{k}\cdot L
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

We model event patterns by regular expressions
[
12
]
over
\Sigma
. Let

\mathrm{Reg}(\Sigma)
be the set of
regular expressions
over
\Sigma
generated by
the grammar

E::=\varepsilon\mid A\mid E+E\mid E\cdot E\mid E^{*},

where
A\subseteq\Sigma
. In particular,
A=\emptyset
denotes the empty
language and
A=\Sigma
matches any single event.

The
semantics
of a regular expression
E\in\mathrm{Reg}(\Sigma)
is the language
{[\![E]\!]}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
defined inductively by
{[\![\varepsilon]\!]}=\{\varepsilon\}
,
{[\![A]\!]}=A
for
A\subseteq\Sigma
,

{[\![E_{1}+E_{2}]\!]}={[\![E_{1}]\!]}\cup{[\![E_{2}]\!]}
,

{[\![E_{1}\cdot E_{2}]\!]}={[\![E_{1}]\!]}\cdot{[\![E_{2}]\!]}
, and

{[\![E^{*}]\!]}={[\![E]\!]}^{*}
.

Example 2

Let
\Sigma
be the alphabet from Example
1
. Consider the regular expression

E=\Sigma^{*}\cdot\texttt{earnings\_miss}\cdot\Sigma^{*}\cdot\texttt{macro\_shock}\cdot\Sigma^{*}.

Its semantics is

{[\![E]\!]}=\Sigma^{*}\cdot\{\texttt{earnings\_miss}\}\cdot\Sigma^{*}\cdot\{\texttt{macro\_shock}\}\cdot\Sigma^{*}.

Thus,
{[\![E]\!]}
contains exactly those event histories in which an

earnings_miss
occurs before a later
macro_shock
, not necessarily in consecutive steps.
For example,

w_{1}=\texttt{normal}\cdot\texttt{earnings\_miss}\cdot\texttt{normal}\cdot\texttt{macro\_shock}\in\Sigma^{*}

satisfies the pattern, i.e.,
w_{1}\in{[\![E]\!]}
, whereas

w_{2}=\texttt{normal}\cdot\texttt{macro\_shock}\cdot\texttt{earnings\_miss}\in\Sigma^{*}

does not, i.e.,
w_{2}\notin{[\![E]\!]}
.

Numerical intervals.

When a pattern applies, it restricts the numerical values that are considered possible under the current scenario assumptions. In this paper, we use
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and restrict attention to the following interval types: the empty set
\emptyset
, the full quantitative domain
\mathbb{D}
, bounded intervals
[a,b]
with rational endpoints
0\leq a\leq b
, and unbounded intervals
[a,\infty)
with rational endpoint
a\geq 0
.
We write
\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
for the set of all such intervals.

The empty interval
\emptyset
means that any finance word matching the associated pattern is excluded from the scenario language. The full domain
\mathbb{D}
means that there is no numerical restriction. A bounded interval
[a,b]
imposes lower and upper bounds, while
[a,\infty)
imposes only a lower bound.

Pattern-based scenario constraints.

We now combine event patterns and numerical intervals into a rule-based specification language. The goal is to describe how admissible numerical observations depend on the previously observed event history.

A
pattern-based scenario constraint
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
is a pair

(E,J),

where
E\in\mathrm{Reg}(\Sigma)
is an event pattern and
J\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
is a numerical interval. Its intended meaning is that, whenever the event history matches
E
, the current numerical observation must lie in
J
. We write

\mathcal{C}(\Sigma,\mathbb{D})

for the set of all pattern-based scenario constraints over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
.

Several constraints may match the same event history. For example, one pattern may describe a broad market regime, while another describes a more specific stress case. We therefore need a mechanism that selects the interval to be applied.
For a finite set
X
, we write
2^{X}
for the set of all subsets of
X
.

Definition 1

A
pattern-based scenario constraint system
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
is a pair

\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{C},\operatorname{Res}),

where
\mathbb{C}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
is a finite sequence of constraints from
\mathcal{C}(\Sigma,\mathbb{D})
, and

\operatorname{Res}:2^{\{1,\dots,m\}}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})

is an
interval resolver
.

Given an event history
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, the set of
active constraint
indices
is

{\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u)=\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\mid u\in{[\![E_{i}]\!]}\},}

where
c_{i}=(E_{i},J_{i})
. Thus, a constraint is active after
u
precisely when
its event pattern matches the history
u
.

The interval resolver then determines which interval is applied for the current
history. The constraint system induces a mapping

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})

defined by

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}(u)=\operatorname{Res}\bigl(\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u)\bigr).

Hence, every event history
u
is assigned the interval of numerical values
admissible after observing
u
.

For a finance word

w=(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})

and
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we call

{u_{i}=a_{1}\dots a_{i}}\in\Sigma^{*}

the
prefix history
at step
i
. The scenario language defined by

\mathcal{S}
consists of all finance words whose observed values satisfy the
intervals assigned to their prefix histories:

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S})=\left\{(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})\in(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\;\middle|\;d_{i}\in{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}(a_{1}\dots a_{i})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\right\}.

Example 3
(Priority Resolver)

Scenario specifications often contain both baseline assumptions and more specific
stress conditions. A priority resolver selects the first active constraint in a
given priority order, so that specific stress conditions can override generic
baseline assumptions.

Assume that constraints are ordered by decreasing priority, so that
c_{1}
has
the highest priority. Let
J_{0}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
be a default interval used
when no constraint is active. The priority resolver

\operatorname{Res}_{\mathrm{prio}}^{J_{0}}:2^{\{1,\dots,m\}}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})

is defined as follows. For every non-empty set
X\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
, let

\operatorname{Res}^{J_{0}}_{\mathrm{prio}}(X)=J_{k},

where
k=\min X
and
c_{k}=(E_{k},J_{k})
. For the empty set, let

\operatorname{Res}^{J_{0}}_{\mathrm{prio}}(\emptyset)=J_{0}.

Thus, among all active constraints, the highest-priority one determines the
interval to be applied. This is useful when stress constraints are intended to
override normal-market assumptions.

Example 4
(Intersection Resolver)

A conservative alternative is to require all active constraints to be satisfied
simultaneously. This gives the
intersection resolver

\operatorname{Res}_{\cap}:2^{\{1,\dots,m\}}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
, defined by

\operatorname{Res}_{\cap}(X)=\bigcap_{i\in X}J_{i}
, where

{c_{i}=(E_{i},J_{i})}
. For the empty set of active constraints, we set

\operatorname{Res}_{\cap}(\emptyset)=\mathbb{D}
.

Thus, only numerical values contained in every active interval remain admissible.
If the active constraints are inconsistent, the resulting interval is

\emptyset
.

2.3
Event History Automata (EHA)

Let
\Sigma
be a finite event alphabet and let
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the data domain. We now
introduce event history automata, a finite-state model that assigns admissible
data intervals to event histories. EHAs can be specified directly, for instance
to represent sequential interval forecasts or tree-like scenario structures, and
they also serve as the compilation target for pattern-based scenario constraints.

Definition 2

An
event history automaton
(EHA) over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
is a tuple

\mathcal{H}=(Q,q_{0},\delta,\rho),

where
Q
is a finite set of states,
q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state,
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is a deterministic transition function, and
\rho:Q\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
assigns an interval to each state.

An EHA can be viewed as a deterministic Moore machine
[
13
]
whose outputs are intervals over the data domain. Each state summarizes the relevant information contained in the event history processed so far, and the output function specifies the admissible numerical values after that history.

For an event history
u=a_{1}\dots a_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
, the unique run of
\mathcal{H}
on
u
is the sequence

q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}

such that

q_{i}=\delta(q_{i-1},a_{i})

for all

1\leq i\leq n
.
We write

\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)=q_{n}

for the last state of this run. The
behavior
of
\mathcal{H}
is the mapping

{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})

defined for all
u\in\Sigma^{*}
by

{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}(u)=\rho\bigl(\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)\bigr).

The scenario language induced by
\mathcal{H}
is

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})=\left\{(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})\in(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\;\middle|\;d_{i}\in{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}(a_{1}\dots a_{i})\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq n\right\}.

The previous example illustrates how a single pattern constraint can be represented by an EHA. The same idea extends to arbitrary finite constraint systems: each pattern is compiled into a deterministic automaton, and the resulting automata are combined by a synchronous product.

Theorem 2.1
(Equivalence of pattern constraints and EHAs)

Let
\Sigma
be a finite event alphabet and let
\mathbb{D}
be the quantitative domain.

(a)

For every pattern-based scenario constraint system
\mathcal{S}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
, there exists an EHA
\mathcal{H}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
such that

{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}.

(b)

For every EHA
\mathcal{H}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
, there exists a pattern-based scenario constraint system
\mathcal{S}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
such that

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}.

For part (a), each event pattern is translated into a deterministic finite automaton; their
synchronous product tracks the active constraints, and the EHA output is obtained
by applying the resolver. Part (b) follows by using one pattern for each EHA
state.

Remark 1
(Complexity)

If the system has
m
constraints and each pattern automaton has
O(N)

states, the product construction may have
O(N^{m})
states in the worst case.
After construction, the interval for a history
u\in\Sigma^{*}
is computed in
time
O(|u|)
by following the unique run.

3
Quantitative Payoff Evaluation via Weighted Automata

In this section, we recall the quantitative automata model used for payoff
evaluation. Our presentation is based on weighted finite finance automata
(WFFAs)
[
6
]
, which provide a finite-state formalism for modeling
financial payoff structures over event-data sequences.

We only recall the parts of the framework that are needed in the present paper.
For the general theory, further examples, and the connection with weighted finance regular expressions, we refer the reader to
[
6
]
.

Finance Semirings.

We first recall the algebraic setting used for quantitative payoff evaluation. The role of the algebraic structure is to specify how local payoff contributions are accumulated along a run and how alternative runs are compared.

A
semiring
(cf., e.g.,
[
5
]
) is a structure

\mathbb{S}=(S,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbb{0},\mathbb{1}),

where
(S,\oplus,\mathbb{0})
is a commutative monoid,
(S,\otimes,\mathbb{1})
is a monoid, multiplication distributes over addition, and

s\otimes\mathbb{0}=\mathbb{0}\otimes s=\mathbb{0}

for all
s\in S
.
The main example used in this paper is the
max-plus semiring

\mathbb{S}_{\max,+}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\},\max,+,-\infty,0).

Here,
+
accumulates values along a path, while
\max
selects the best value among alternatives.

To incorporate quantitative market observations, we use an additional data-binding operation. Let
\mathbb{D}
be the data domain. A
data-binding function
over
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{D}
is a mapping

\mathbb{b}:\mathbb{D}\times S\to S.

It describes how an externally observed numerical value, such as an asset price, is combined with a semiring value during the evaluation of a transition.

A
finance semiring
is a triple

\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{D},\mathbb{b}).

Thus,
\mathbb{S}
specifies the algebra used for accumulation and optimization,

\mathbb{D}
specifies the domain of external quantitative observations, and
\mathbb{b}

specifies the operation by which observations are combined with semiring weights.

In the present paper, we restrict attention to the
linear finance semiring

{\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}=(\mathbb{S}_{\max,+},\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\mathbb{b}_{\mathrm{lin}}),}

where

\mathbb{b}_{\mathrm{lin}}(d,s)=d\cdot s

for
s\in\mathbb{R}
, and
{\mathbb{b}_{\mathrm{lin}}(d,-\infty)=-\infty}
for all
d\in\mathbb{D}
. Thus, finite scalar
weights are transformed by multiplication with the observed data value. For instance, if
d
is an observed price and
s
is the number of units held, then
\mathbb{b}_{\mathrm{lin}}(d,s)
is the market value of the position.

Local Payoff Expressions

Weighted finance automata evaluate finance words step by step. When the automaton reads a pair
(a,d)
, the event
a
determines which transitions are available, while the quantitative value
d
is used to evaluate the payoff expression attached to the chosen transition. These expressions are local in the sense that they depend only on the current data value.

Definition 3

Let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{D},\mathbb{b})
be a finance semiring with

\mathbb{S}=(S,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbb{0},\mathbb{1})
. The set
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
of

local payoff expressions
over
\mathbb{F}
is generated by the grammar

e::=s\mid{\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle}\mid e\oplus e\mid e\otimes e\mid e=e\mid e\neq e\qquad(s\in S).

These expressions correspond to the
\mathbb{F}
-expressions introduced in
[
6
]
; we use the term local payoff expressions here to emphasize their role as transition-level payoff specifications.
Let
e\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
be a local payoff expression. The
semantics
of
e
is the mapping

{[\![e]\!]}:\mathbb{D}\to S

defined inductively as follows. For every
d\in\mathbb{D}
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl@{\qquad}rcl}{[\![s]\!]}(d)&=&s\hfil\hskip 17.00024pt&{[\![{\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle}]\!]}(d)&=&\mathbb{b}(d,s)\\[5.69054pt]
{[\![e_{1}\oplus e_{2}]\!]}(d)&=&{[\![e_{1}]\!]}(d)\oplus{[\![e_{2}]\!]}(d)\hfil\hskip 17.00024pt&{[\![e_{1}\otimes e_{2}]\!]}(d)&=&{[\![e_{1}]\!]}(d)\otimes{[\![e_{2}]\!]}(d)\\[5.69054pt]
{[\![e_{1}=e_{2}]\!]}(d)&=&\begin{cases}\mathbb{1},&\text{if }{[\![e_{1}]\!]}(d)={[\![e_{2}]\!]}(d),\\
\mathbb{0},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\hfil\hskip 17.00024pt&{[\![e_{1}\neq e_{2}]\!]}(d)&=&\begin{cases}\mathbb{1},&\text{if }{[\![e_{1}]\!]}(d)\neq{[\![e_{2}]\!]}(d),\\
\mathbb{0},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{array}

Constants provide fixed semiring values, terms of the form
{\langle\!\langle s\rangle\!\rangle}
combine
the current data value with
s
through
\mathbb{b}
, and comparison expressions act
as guards. For the linear finance semiring
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
, equality and
inequality guards can encode the usual order comparisons
<,\leq,>,\geq
; see
Example 2.9 in
[
6
]
.

For example, over
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
, the expression

e=\bigl({\langle\!\langle 1\rangle\!\rangle}\otimes(-100.0)\bigr)

has the semantics

{[\![e]\!]}(d)=d-100.0

for all
d\in\mathbb{D}
. Hence, the expression

e^{\prime}=\bigl(({\langle\!\langle 1\rangle\!\rangle}\otimes(-100.0))\oplus 0.0\bigr)

has the semantics

{[\![e^{\prime}]\!]}(d)=\max(d-100.0,0.0).

Here,
0.0
is a real-valued constant, not the semiring zero
\mathbb{0}=-\infty
.

Weighted Finite Finance Automata (WFFA)

Let
\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{S},\mathbb{D},\mathbb{b})
be a finance semiring with
\mathbb{S}=(S,\oplus,\otimes,\mathbb{0},\mathbb{1})
.
We now recall weighted finite finance automata from
[
6
]
. They extend the weighted automata of Schützenberger by allowing transition weights to be local payoff expressions from
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
rather than fixed semiring constants from
S
. In this way, transition weights can incorporate the quantitative input value observed at the current step.

Definition 4

A
weighted finite finance automaton
(WFFA) over an event alphabet
\Sigma
and a finance semiring
\mathbb{F}
is a tuple

\mathcal{W}=(Q,I,T,F,\mathrm{wt}_{I},\mathrm{wt}_{T},\mathrm{wt}_{F}),

where
Q
is a finite set of states,
I,F\subseteq Q
are the initial and final states,

T\subseteq Q\times\Sigma\times Q

is the transition relation,
\mathrm{wt}_{I}:I\to S
and
\mathrm{wt}_{F}:F\to S
are initial and final weight functions, and

\mathrm{wt}_{T}:T\to\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}

assigns a local payoff expression to each transition.

Let

w=(a_{1},d_{1})\ldots(a_{n},d_{n})\in(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}

be a finance word. A
run
of
\mathcal{W}
on
w
is a sequence

\varrho=(q_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}q_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{2}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{n}}q_{n})

such that
q_{0}\in I
,
q_{n}\in F
, and
t_{i}=(q_{i-1},a_{i},q_{i})\in T
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. The
accumulated payoff
of the run
\varrho
on
w
is

\mathrm{wt}_{\mathcal{W}}(\varrho,w)=\mathrm{wt}_{I}(q_{0})\otimes{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t_{1})]\!]}(d_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t_{n})]\!]}(d_{n})\otimes\mathrm{wt}_{F}(q_{n}).

Thus, each transition contributes a local payoff value, and these contributions are accumulated along the run using the semiring multiplication
\otimes
.

The
behavior
of
\mathcal{W}
is the mapping

{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}:(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\to S

defined by

{{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)=\bigoplus\left(\mathrm{wt}_{\mathcal{W}}(\varrho,w)\mid\varrho\in\mathrm{Run}_{\mathcal{W}}(w)\right),}

where
\mathrm{Run}_{\mathcal{W}}(w)
is the set of all runs of
\mathcal{W}
on
w
.
Thus, local payoff expressions are evaluated on the data values of the input word. Their values are accumulated along each run using
\otimes
, and the accumulated payoffs of all compatible runs are aggregated using
\oplus
. In the max-plus setting, this means that local payoff values are summed along a run, and the behavior selects the maximal accumulated payoff over all compatible runs.

Scenario-Restricted Payoff Evaluation

A WFFA assigns a payoff value to every finance word, whereas an EHA specifies which finance words are admissible under the chosen scenario assumptions. We now combine these two components.

A
quantitative finance language

[
6
]
over
\Sigma
,
\mathbb{D}
, and
\mathbb{S}
is a mapping

{\mathcal{F}:(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\to S.}

It assigns a semiring value to each finance word. In particular, the behavior
{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}
of a WFFA is a quantitative finance language. Let

\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}

be a scenario language. The
restriction
of
\mathcal{F}
to
\mathcal{L}
is the quantitative finance language

(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L}):(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\to S

defined by setting
(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L})(w)=\mathcal{F}(w)
for
w\in\mathcal{L}
, and

(\mathcal{F}\cap\mathcal{L})(w)=\mathbb{0}
otherwise.
Thus, finance words outside the scenario language are assigned the semiring zero and do not contribute to the payoff evaluation.

To enforce such restrictions within a WFFA, interval membership must be encoded by
local payoff expressions. Let
J\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
. A local payoff expression
g_{J}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
is an
interval guard
for
J
if, for every
d\in\mathbb{D}
, we have

{[\![g_{J}]\!]}(d)=\mathbb{1}
when
d\in J
, and
{[\![g_{J}]\!]}(d)=\mathbb{0}
otherwise.
We call
\mathbb{F}

interval-complete
if every interval
J\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
has an interval guard
g_{J}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
. For instance, the linear finance semiring
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
is interval-complete (cf. Example 2.9 in
[
6
]
).

Theorem 3.1
(Restriction by event history automata)

Let
\mathbb{F}
be interval-complete. For every EHA
\mathcal{H}
over
\Sigma
and

\mathbb{D}
, and every WFFA
\mathcal{W}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
, there exists
a WFFA
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
such that

{[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}\cap\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),

where
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
denotes the scenario language induced by
\mathcal{H}
.

The construction is a synchronized product of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{W}
. The
interval-completeness assumption ensures that the EHA interval output can be
encoded as a local payoff-expression guard on the corresponding WFFA transitions.

Extremal Payoff Analysis

We now define the extremal payoff quantities used below. We
restrict attention to the linear finance semiring
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
, where payoffs
are real values and infeasible words have value
-\infty
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be a quantitative finance
language. Its feasible domain is

\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{F})=\{w\in(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\mid\mathcal{F}(w)\neq-\infty\}.

Given a scenario language
\mathcal{L}\subseteq(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}
, the
best-case
and

worst-case payoffs
over
\mathcal{L}
are

\mathrm{best}_{L}(\mathcal{F})=\sup\left\{\mathcal{F}(w)\mid w\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{F})\right\}

and

\mathrm{worst}_{L}(\mathcal{F})=\inf\left\{\mathcal{F}(w)\mid w\in\mathcal{L}\cap\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{F})\right\}
,
respectively. If
{\mathcal{L}\cap\mathrm{dom}(\mathcal{F})=\emptyset}
, then the scenario is infeasible.

Let
\mathcal{W}=(Q,I,T,F,\mathrm{wt}_{I},\mathrm{wt}_{T},\mathrm{wt}_{F})
be a WFFA over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
.
A transition
t\in T
is
enabled
by
d\in\mathbb{D}
if

{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t)]\!]}(d)\neq\mathbb{0}.

The WFFA
\mathcal{W}
is called
deterministic
if it has a unique initial state and,
for every
q\in Q
,
a\in\Sigma
, and
d\in\mathbb{D}
, at most one transition

t=(q,a,q^{\prime})\in T
is enabled by
d
.

The following result adapts graph-based extremal-value techniques for weighted
automata
[
1
]
to WFFAs with data-dependent transition expressions and
scenario constraints, building on the threshold-analysis perspective
of
[
6
]
.

Theorem 3.2
(Polynomial-time extremal payoff analysis)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an EHA over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
, and let
\mathcal{W}
be a WFFA over

\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
. Assume that all numerical constants occurring in

\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{H}
are rational numbers encoded in binary. Then the best-case value

\mathrm{best}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
can be computed in polynomial time in the
size of the product automaton and the bit-size of the numerical constants. If

\mathcal{W}
is deterministic, then the worst-case value

\mathrm{worst}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
can also be computed in polynomial time in
the size of the product automaton and the bit-size of the numerical constants.

declarative model specification to extremal payoff analysis. Contract models and
scenario assumptions are specified by JSON task descriptions; pattern-based
scenario constraints are then compiled into EHAs.
The resulting components are combined using synchronized product constructions.

The evaluation considers two aspects. First, we compare the extremal payoffs

Apple MacBook Pro with an Apple M1 Max processor and 64 GB of memory. No custom JVM
tuning parameters were used. Reported

construction, product construction, extremal payoff computation, and witness

Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduced an automata-based framework for exact extremal payoff
analysis under declarative scenario constraints. The central idea is to separate
the deterministic contract model from assumptions about future market evolution:
payoffs are represented by WFFAs, while admissible scenarios are specified by
event history automata or compiled from pattern-based constraint systems.

The resulting product construction enables best-case and worst-case payoff
analysis over complete languages of admissible event histories, rather than over
sampled paths. In addition to extremal values, the framework produces witness
histories that make the corresponding outcomes interpretable.

From a computational perspective, the scalability experiments quantify the cost of
the synchronized product constructions underlying the framework. As expected,
product sizes and runtimes increase when horizons grow or when multiple scenario
constraints interact. However, the prototype still computes exact extremal values
and witness histories across all tested configurations, showing that the approach
is practically tractable for moderately large horizons and nontrivial scenario
systems.

Future work can proceed in several directions. First, the current EHA model assigns
admissible intervals from finite-state event histories. Many realistic market
assumptions, however, involve quantitative dependencies between observations, such
as bounded relative changes, reversals, drawdown constraints, or dynamically
evolving reference levels. Extending the framework toward multi-valued finance
words and models such as weighted register automata
[
15
]
may provide a way to capture
such dependencies while preserving algorithmic analyzability.

Second, the synchronized product constructions used in this paper are explicit.
An important algorithmic direction is the development of lazy or on-the-fly
product constructions that generate only those combined states needed during
extremal payoff analysis. This could improve scalability for scenario systems
with many interacting constraints.

Finally, the same methodology could be applied beyond structured products. One
promising direction is the analysis of rule-based investment or trading strategies,
where technical-analysis patterns
[
14
]
and event-driven signals can be represented as
formal scenario constraints and evaluated against quantitative payoff objectives.

such that

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}.

Proof

(a)

Let
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{C},\operatorname{Res})
be a pattern-based scenario
constraint system over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
, with

\mathbb{C}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
and
c_{i}=(E_{i},J_{i})
for
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

We construct an event history automaton
\mathcal{H}
in two steps.

(i)

For each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, choose a complete deterministic finite
automaton

\mathcal{D}_{i}=(Q_{i},q_{0,i},\delta_{i},F_{i})

over
\Sigma
such that

L(\mathcal{D}_{i})={[\![E_{i}]\!]}.

(ii)

Define the EHA

\mathcal{H}=(Q,q_{0},\delta,\rho)

as the synchronous product of the automata
\mathcal{D}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{D}_{m}
.
Let

Q=Q_{1}\times\cdots\times Q_{m}

and

q_{0}=(q_{0,1},\dots,q_{0,m}).

The transition function
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to Q
is defined by

\delta((q_{1},\dots,q_{m}),a)=(\delta_{1}(q_{1},a),\dots,\delta_{m}(q_{m},a))

for all
(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})\in Q
and
a\in\Sigma
. Finally, define the output
function
\rho:Q\to\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
by

\rho(q_{1},\dots,q_{m})=\operatorname{Res}\bigl(\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\mid q_{i}\in F_{i}\}\bigr).

It remains to show that
{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}
. Let
u\in\Sigma^{*}
.
By construction of the product automaton, the
i
-th component of

\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)
is precisely

\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}(u)
.
Hence,

\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}(u)\in F_{i}\iff u\in{[\![E_{i}]\!]}.

Therefore,

\{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\mid\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}(u)\in F_{i}\}=\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u).

It follows that

{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}(u)=\rho(\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u))=\operatorname{Res}(\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u))={[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}(u).

Since this holds for all
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, we obtain

{[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}.

(b)

For the converse direction, let
\mathcal{H}=(Q,q_{0},\delta,\rho)
be an EHA over

\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
Q=\{1,\dots,m\}
.
For each
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, let

L_{j}=\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)=j\}.

This language is recognized by the deterministic finite automaton

(Q,q_{0},\delta,\{j\})
, obtained from
\mathcal{H}
by taking
j
as the only final
state. Hence, choose a regular expression
E_{j}\in\mathrm{Reg}(\Sigma)
such that

{[\![E_{j}]\!]}=L_{j}
.

Define
\mathcal{S}=(\mathbb{C},\operatorname{Res})
by taking

\mathbb{C}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
, where
c_{j}=(E_{j},\rho(j))
, and letting

\operatorname{Res}=\operatorname{Res}_{\mathrm{prio}}^{J_{0}}
for an arbitrary
interval
J_{0}\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
.

For every
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, exactly one constraint is active, namely the one
corresponding to
\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)
. Hence, if

\operatorname{last}_{\mathcal{H}}(u)=j
, then

\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u)=\{j\}
.
Since the active set is a singleton, the priority resolver returns

\operatorname{Res}(\{j\})=\rho(j)
.
Therefore,

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}(u)=\operatorname{Res}(\operatorname{Act}_{\mathcal{S}}(u))=\rho(j)={[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}(u).

Since this holds for all
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, we obtain

{[\![\mathcal{S}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{H}]\!]}
.

Theorem
3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be interval-complete. For every EHA
\mathcal{H}
over
\Sigma
and

\mathbb{D}
, and every WFFA
\mathcal{W}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
, there exists
a WFFA
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
such that

{[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}\cap\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),

where

denotes the scenario language induced by

.

Proof

Let:

•

\mathcal{H}=(P,p_{0},\delta,\rho)
be an EHA over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
;

•

\mathcal{W}=(Q,I,T,F,\mathrm{wt}_{I},\mathrm{wt}_{T},\mathrm{wt}_{F})
be a WFFA over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}
.

Since
\mathbb{F}
is interval-complete, for every state
p\in P
there exists a

g_{p}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
representing membership in the
interval
\rho(p)\in\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{D})
, i.e., for all
d\in\mathbb{D}

{[\![g_{p}]\!]}(d)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{1},&d\in\rho(p),\\
\mathbb{0},&d\notin\rho(p).\end{cases}

We define
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
as the product automaton whose states record both the current
state of
\mathcal{H}
and the current state of
\mathcal{W}
. The EHA component determines the
interval guard to be applied after reading the next event. Formally, let

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=(Q^{\prime},I^{\prime},T^{\prime},F^{\prime},\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{I},\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{T},\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{F})
, where:

•

Q^{\prime}=P\times Q
,
I^{\prime}=\{p_{0}\}\times I
,
F^{\prime}=P\times F
;

•

T^{\prime}
consists of all transitions

such that

and
(q,a,q^{\prime})\in T
;

•

\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{I}(p_{0},q)=\mathrm{wt}_{I}(q)
for all
q\in I
;

•

\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{F}(p,q)=\mathrm{wt}_{F}(q)
for all
p\in P
and
q\in F
;

•

for
t^{\prime}=((p,q),a,(p^{\prime},q^{\prime}))\in T^{\prime}
, with

\mathrm{wt}^{\prime}_{T}(t^{\prime})=\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t)\otimes g_{p^{\prime}}.

Here
\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t),g_{p^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
, so the expression is well-defined by closure
of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{F}}
under
\otimes
.

Let
w=(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})
. The EHA has a unique run

p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
on
a_{1}\dots a_{n}
, where

p_{i}=\delta(p_{i-1},a_{i})
. Runs of
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
on
w
are in one-to-one

\mathcal{W}
on
w
: a run

of
\mathcal{W}
corresponds to

(p_{0},q_{0}),\dots,(p_{n},q_{n})
in
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.

If
w\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
, then

for every

. Hence

{[\![g_{p_{i}}]\!]}(d_{i})=\mathbb{1}
, and every corresponding run in

If

, then for some

we have

{[\![g_{p_{i}}]\!]}(d_{i})=\mathbb{0}

Consequently,

{[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]}(w)=\begin{cases}{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w),&w\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}),\\
\mathbb{0},&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

for all

Proof of Theorem
3.2

Theorem
3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be an EHA over
\Sigma
and
\mathbb{D}
, and let
\mathcal{W}
be a WFFA over

\Sigma
and
\mathbb{F}_{\mathrm{lin}}
. Assume that all numerical constants occurring in

\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{H}
are rational numbers encoded in binary. Then the best-case value

\mathrm{best}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
can be computed in polynomial time in the
size of the product automaton and the bit-size of the numerical constants. If

\mathcal{W}
is deterministic, then the worst-case value

\mathrm{worst}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
can also be computed in polynomial time in
the size of the product automaton and the bit-size of the numerical constants.

Proof

We give the argument for the best-case value; the worst-case value is analogous
under determinism. By Theorem
3.1
, we can construct a WFFA
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
such
that

{[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]}={[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}\cap\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}).

Therefore,

\mathrm{best}_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})=\sup_{w\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\cap\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})}{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)=\sup_{w\in\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]})}{[\![\mathcal{W}^{\prime}]\!]}(w).

Thus, it suffices to compute the unrestricted supremum of the
scenario-constrained WFFA
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
. In the following, write this WFFA as

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=(Q,I,T,F,\mathrm{wt}_{I},\mathrm{wt}_{T},\mathrm{wt}_{F}).

For the linear finance semiring, Lemma 9.7 in
[
6
]
gives an effective
procedure for computing

\sup_{d\in\mathbb{D}}{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t)]\!]}(d)

for each transition expression
\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t)
.
For each transition
t\in T
, compute

M_{t}=\sup_{d\in\mathbb{D}}{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t)]\!]}(d).

If
M_{t}=-\infty
, then
t
is infeasible and can be removed. If

M_{t}=+\infty
, then the best-case value is
+\infty
whenever
t
lies on
some feasible initial-to-final path. This condition is checked by standard graph reachability after removing infeasible transitions.

It remains to consider transitions with finite
M_{t}
. Replacing each such
transition expression by the constant weight
M_{t}
yields a classical max-plus
weighted automaton
\mathcal{W}_{\max}
over the feasible finite-weight part of the same
underlying graph. Its supremum value can then be computed by the standard
graph-based analysis for weighted automata, as in
[
1
]
.

After removing infeasible transitions,
\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})=\emptyset
iff

\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}_{\max}]\!]})=\emptyset
. If the domains are empty, the scenario is
infeasible. Otherwise, let

S=\sup\{{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)\mid w\in(\Sigma\times\mathbb{D})^{*}\text{ and }{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)\neq-\infty\}

and

S_{\max}=\sup\{{[\![\mathcal{W}_{\max}]\!]}(u)\mid u\in\Sigma^{*}\text{ and }{[\![\mathcal{W}_{\max}]\!]}(u)\neq-\infty\}.

We show that
S=S_{\max}
.

First,
S\leq S_{\max}
. Indeed, every accepting run of
\mathcal{W}
on a finance word

w=(a_{1},d_{1})\dots(a_{n},d_{n})
determines an accepting path

\pi=t_{1}\dots t_{n}
in
\mathcal{W}_{\max}
. Since

{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t_{i})]\!]}(d_{i})\leq M_{t_{i}}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
d_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
, the payoff of this run is at
most the weight of
\pi
in
\mathcal{W}_{\max}
.

Conversely, let
\pi=t_{1}\dots t_{n}
be an accepting path in
\mathcal{W}_{\max}
with
finite weight and let
\eta>0
. We show that there is a finance word whose
payoff is greater than
\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{W}_{\max}}(\pi)-\eta
. If
n=0
,
then the empty finance word realizes the same initial-final weight, so the claim
is immediate. Assume
n>0
. Write
t_{i}=(q_{i-1},a_{i},q_{i})
. By the definition of

M_{t_{i}}
, for each
i
there exists
d^{\eta}_{i}\in\mathbb{D}
such that

{[\![\mathrm{wt}_{T}(t_{i})]\!]}(d^{\eta}_{i})>M_{t_{i}}-\frac{\eta}{n}.

Here the choices of
d^{\eta}_{i}
are independent: once the event path

a_{1}\dots a_{n}
is fixed, automaton states and scenario guards are fixed by the
event history, and each transition expression depends only on the current value

d^{\eta}_{i}
. Hence the finance word

w=(a_{1},d^{\eta}_{1})\dots(a_{n},d^{\eta}_{n})

admits the corresponding run in
\mathcal{W}
, whose payoff is greater than the weight
of
\pi
minus
\eta
. Therefore

S\geq\operatorname{wt}_{\mathcal{W}_{\max}}(\pi)-\eta
. Since
\pi
and

\eta
were arbitrary,
S\geq S_{\max}
.

Thus
S=S_{\max}
.

The value
S_{\max}
is computed by standard graph analysis for max-plus
automata. We first restrict the graph to states and transitions that occur on some
path from an initial state to a final state. If this accepting part of the graph
contains a cycle of positive total weight, then
S_{\max}=+\infty
. Otherwise,
the finite value is computed in polynomial time by standard path-optimization
algorithms, such as a Bellman–Ford variant on the remaining weighted graph.

For the worst-case value, assume that
\mathcal{W}
is deterministic. Then every finance
word induces at most one accepting run, so the value
{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)
is not affected
by maximization over competing runs. We use the identity

\mathrm{worst}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})=-\sup_{w\in\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})}(-{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)).

It remains to observe that, for the linear finance semiring, transition
expressions can be effectively negated. By Lemma 6.23 in
[
6
]
, the
semantics of local payoff expressions can be reduced to finitely many affine
pieces. Negating each affine piece yields an effective representation of the
negative transition contribution. Hence we can construct a deterministic WFFA

-\mathcal{W}
such that, for every
w\in\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
,

{[\![-\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)=-{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w).

Applying the best-case procedure to
-\mathcal{W}
computes

\sup_{w\in\mathrm{dom}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})}(-{[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]}(w)).

Taking the negative of this value yields
\mathrm{worst}({[\![\mathcal{W}]\!]})
. Thus the worst-case
value is computable by the same polynomial-time graph analysis, after the
transition-level negation and extrema have been computed. ∎

Appendix 0.C
Prototype Architecture and Reproducibility

Prototype Architecture.

We implemented a Java research prototype supporting the end-to-end workflow used
in the case study. The prototype separates declarative model specification,
automata construction, scenario compilation, product construction, and extremal
payoff analysis.

Input models are provided as JSON task descriptions. A task specifies the payoff
WFFA, the temporal contract structure, the pattern-based scenario constraint
system, and the analysis to be performed. The workflow is as follows. The JSON
specification is deserialized into internal DTOs and translated into automata-level
objects. The payoff component
\mathcal{P}
is synchronized with the temporal
structure
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. The scenario system
\mathcal{S}
is compiled into an
EHA
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{S}}
, which is then synchronized with the contract
model. The resulting scenario-constrained WFFA is analyzed to compute exact
best-case and worst-case payoffs, witness event histories, automaton sizes, and
runtime statistics.

The implementation consists of four main layers: an automata layer for deterministic
automata, WFFAs, and product constructions; a scenario layer for pattern-based
constraints and EHAs; a JSON layer for declarative input specifications; and a
command-line layer for running analysis tasks. This modular organization allows
payoff models, temporal structures, and scenario assumptions to be varied
independently and recombined through the workflow shown in
