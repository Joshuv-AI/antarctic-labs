Patient-centered and coordinated therapy with RNA interference-based givosiran for acute hepatic porphyria: real-life experience for an ultra-rare disease from Romania
Authors: Anamaria Bancos, Ciprian Jitaru, Andrada Uhl, Diana Roibu, Maria Santa, Madalina Nistor, Andrei Ivancuta, Cristian Silviu Moldovan, Delia Dima, Maria Puiu, Ciprian Tomuleasa
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Symptomatic acute hepatic porphyria (AHP) is a debilitating hereditary disease that greatly hinders the affected patient’s quality of life. Mutational enzymatic defects lead to the accumulation of intermediate heme biosynthesis compounds delta aminolevulinic acid (ALA) and porphobilinogen (PBG). The 4 main types of porphyria are acute intermittent (AIP), variegate porphyria (VP), hereditary coproporphyria (HCP), and ALA dehydratase (ALAD) deficiency porphyria (ADP). Of all of the types of clinical porphyria, this clarification refers only to AHP. Latent carriers of pathogenic mutations are considered more common, with a prevalence of approximately 1 in 1700 individuals. However, the prevalence of symptomatic AHP is reported to be around 1 in 75,000 individuals in most European countries1–4 (Fig. 1). Most affected individuals only experience a few attacks in their lifetime1; however, a reported 8% of symptomatic patients present with recurrent attacks (>4 per year by definition). Acute attacks are defined as porphyria-specific acute symptomatic disease, confirmed by a healthcare professional, in all cases, being a certified physician. RNA interference (RNAi) therapeutic drug givosiran targets aminolevulinic acid synthase 1 (ALAS1), thereby degrading ALAS1 mRNA and reducing the overproduction of ALA and PBG5,6 (Fig. 2). Approved by the European Commission in March 2020, givosiran was shown to reduce acute attacks by 50% to 74% compared to placebo and improved quality of life and number of hospitalizations in affected patients.2 This study aims to review the real-world setting and efficiency of givosiran in patients presenting with AHP at the Department of Hematology of the Ion Chiricuta Oncology Institute in Cluj-Napoca, Romania, the largest porphyria center in Romania.

Since February 2021, 10 patients have received subcutaneous givosiran injections. The 5 remaining patients declined treatment, receiving hemin-based therapy and symptomatic treatment as a substitute. The full cohort (n = 15) had a mean age of 48.66 years, with 5 males (33.3%) and 10 females (66.6%). Treatment-related analyses refer only to the 10 patients who received givosiran. Of the 10 patients included in the cohort, 30% had a positive family history of porphyria, with 10% reporting an affected daughter and 20% reporting an affected sibling. Of special interest, we report 2 families with important genetic aggregation of either the mutated PPOX gene (family 1) or mutated HMBS gene (family 2), as shown in Figure 3. A total of 70% reported no family history. Beyond familial aggregation, we also analyzed the territorial distribution of patients who tested positive for porphyria. Six patients reported gastrointestinal disturbances during attacks such as stabbing throat pain and intermittent, high-intensity abdominal pain. Eight patients experienced neurological manifestations: morning headache and dizziness, paresthesia at the level of the joints and extremities, as well as tetraparesis. Furthermore, 6 of the 10 patients complained of psychiatric disturbances: generalized fatigue, insomnia, depression, and sensations of anxiety and restlessness. A single patient reported dermatological manifestations, and the same patient was diagnosed with congenital erythropoietic porphyria. All patients who initially experienced more than 3 attacks showed complete resolution of high-frequency attacks following treatment. Moreover, the proportion of attack-free patients more than doubled, rising from 30% before treatment to 70% after givosiran administration (Supplementary Figure 1, https://links.lww.com/BS/A175).

The frequency of attacks was assessed on an ordinal 3-level scale (0 = <1 attack/y, 1 = 1–3 attacks/y, 2 = >3 attacks/y). In the givosiran-treated cohort, attack frequency significantly decreased after treatment initiation (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, one-sided p = 0.027). All patients receiving Givosiran with a higher debut rate of attacks and >3 hospitalizations showed a complete reduction in attacks for 70% of patients, while 30% experienced a decrease from more than 3 attacks to between 1 and 3 attacks. Patients reported a decrease in overall attack rates and symptomatology improvement as soon as 1 month after treatment. All patients with AIP (30%) experienced a complete reduction in attacks post-treatment. One of them had multiple hospitalizations, but the others had no further hospitalizations after treatment. When analyzing the key secondary end-points, givosiran significantly reduced the levels of ALA, PBG, and days of hemin use, as shown in Table 3. Among the patients with AIP, levels of urinary ALA (at 3 and 6 months) and PBG (at 6 months) were significantly lower in the givosiran group than in the control group of untreated patients (p < .001), in comparison with published data.1,2

1. DISCUSSION

Currently, AIP has only a few treatment options, with little effect to prevent the long-term worsening symptoms of the patients. However, the past 5 years have been marked with significant change with the emergence of givosiran, a double-stranded, small interfering RNA (RNAi) injection, that targets the silencing of ALAS1 enzyme mRNA, with consequences on the reduction of ALA and PBG, 2 neurotoxic heme precursors. Givosiran, with its ability to target the disease at its source, has become an emerging treatment primarily prescribed to reduce the burdenous symptoms of the AIP patients, like acute, recurrent, neurovisceral crises. The ENVISION trials were designed to focus on a population of AIP patients treated with givosiran and investigate its long-term effect, moving forward with an open-label extension after 6 months. However, several AIP patients experience chronic symptoms with sporadic attacks, which leads to a decrease in health-related quality-of-life (HR-QoL), while also coping with other “hidden” symptoms like nausea, tiredness, anxiety, sleep disturbances, and pain. Therefore, the trial warrants further exploration of givosiran in a broader dynamic range of AIP patients.

One important aspect of the trial is the in-depth investigation of genetic factors and their impact on the clinical outcomes, providing a more detailed analysis of givosiran’s effects beyond the commonly reported “attack incidence.” The ENVISION study associated givosiran administration with a number of AEs, such as nausea, fatigue, injection site reactions, and chronic kidney disease. Nonetheless, the study reveals a permanent gap from optimal health in patient-reported overall health scores, among those treated with givosiran, over an expanded period of time. Qualitative data collected on patient experience highlight that the majority of the individuals reported a sustained lack of energy while on givosiran. Therefore, further studies are required to clarify whether this side effect is a consequence of givosiran treatment or is linked to their underlying disease.

The real-life experience with givosiran was previously reported in limited real-life cohorts, with the largest cohort being reported by the Japanese national cohort.7 Still, the real-life use of givosiran reported in Europe was published by the German national registry, with 28 patients being described.8 The present study describes the second largest cohort in Europe, with 15 cases being diagnosed, out of which 10 were treated. The results presented in the Romanian cohort are consistent with the international data presented both by the German and Japanese cohorts, as reported by the reference porphyria centers in Tokyo and Chemnitz. Our data confirm that patients with AHP experience a high clinical burden related to chronic symptoms, acute intermittent attack rates, and physical and psychological long-term complications, in comparison to similar demographic populations without AHP diagnosis.

In our 2 presented families, the HMBS gene mutation sites differ between the proband and the children. In AHP, specifically AIP, the hydroxymethylbilane synthase gene mutation sites might differ between the proband and the children due to several genetic mechanisms. The most common explanation is de novo mutation in the proband, meaning the proband carries a pathogenic HMBS variant that arose spontaneously and is not present in either parent. In such cases, the children may inherit a different mutation if the other parent is also a carrier of a separate HMBS mutation, or the children may not inherit the proband’s mutation at all. Additionally, germline mosaicism in a parent can result in siblings having different HMBS mutations if multiple mutational events occurred in the parental germline, though this is rare. The GeneReviews guideline from the University of Washington notes that if a proband’s HMBS pathogenic variant cannot be detected in the leukocyte DNA of either parent, the risk to siblings is estimated at 1% due to the possibility of parental germline mosaicism, which could theoretically result in different mutations in offspring if multiple germline events occurred.9 Another mechanism, which will be the purpose of future investigations, is allelic heterogeneity. The HMBS gene is highly heterogeneous, with over 400 different mutations reported, and most families have private (unique) mutations.9–11 If the proband and children are not biologically related (eg, adoption, non-paternity, or assisted reproduction), or if there is a laboratory error or sample mix-up, different mutations may be detected. Still, the most likely reasons for differing HMBS mutation sites between a proband and their children are de novo mutation in the proband, parental germline mosaicism, or non-biological relationship.

An early diagnosis and start of therapy are essential in avoiding attack triggers, in a better clinical management of the disease, reduced disease burden, and improved overall quality of life. Givosiran is formally approved and authorized for use in Romania through the European Medicines Agency’s centralized procedure, and the first patient was treated in the reference center of Cluj-Napoca, the most widely known reference center in the country. The cost is approximately 190,000 to 240,000 RON per vial, reimbursed by the national insurance. Being a single-center, observational, non-controlled study, the report has limitations, and we emphasize that we primarily demonstrate the feasibility, safety, and preliminary effectiveness in the real-life setting of Romania.
