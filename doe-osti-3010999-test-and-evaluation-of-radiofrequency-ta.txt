EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Advanced and small modular reactors (A/SMRs), due to their versatile nature, are likely to be used
in remote locations to provide electrical power or other services in regions that are difficult to
access or have limited transportation infrastructure. This will result in limited on-site staff,
therefore driving A/SMR vendors to consider remote monitoring as a solution to support nuclear
security. Maintaining Continuity of Knowledge (CoK) of nuclear material quantities and locations is
vital to nuclear security, and remote monitoring of active tamper indicating devices (TIDs) has been
well established as a component of International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Safeguards since the
early 2000s. Active TIDs, such as radiofrequency TIDs (RFTIDs), immediately alarm upon
unauthorized access attempts, promoting timely detection. In contrast, passive TIDs require a
surveillance regime and offer delayed detection. Active TIDs deter insiders and enable prompt
detection of malicious acts. They can be used on nuclear material containers and controlled entry
points like vaults and toolboxes. Therefore, the implementation of RFTIDs into security programs
bolsters overall nuclear material control, and provides a visible deterrent, with primary efficacy in
mitigating the insider threat and potentially allowing for Security by Design considerations. They
are a strong candidate technology for maintaining nuclear security of A/SMRs but need to be
evaluated for feasibility and implementation into the wider physical protection system.
There are multiple sources of guidance on the use of TIDs to support the control and accountability
of nuclear material, as well as authenticating or verifying that physical containers have not been
accessed across the IAEA, Department of Energy (DOE), and Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC).
These guides are generally broad in their use of TIDs. Therefore, no succinct list of guidance exists
for the use of active TIDs, or more specifically, RFTIDs. This report synthesizes general guidance on
TIDs and seals programs to provide recommendations for implementing RFTIDs in nuclear security
applications at A/SMRs.
Additionally, this report summarizes the testing of two RFTIDs – the Remotely Monitored Sealing
Array (RMSA) and Transportation Sealing Monitor (TSM) – to provide proof of concept of the use of
RFTIDs for remotely monitored nuclear security of A/SMRs. Tests include: 120 attempted tampers
of the RMSA in 4 operating environments and all successfully alarmed at station under 2 seconds;
RMSA submersion test and physical intrusion test; 10 attempted tampers of the TSM under high
temperature with indication received at remote review station within average of 20 seconds; and
over 30 narrowband and wide band interference of TSM with indication at remote station within 3
minutes. Relying on that testing, the report presents potential applications of the RFTIDs in nuclear
security of A/SMRs, as well as improvements and recommended follow-on work to fully implement
RFTIDs into a nuclear security system for A/SMRs. The key recommendations include developing or
enhancing RFTID enclosures, augmenting RFTIDs with a water sensor, and integration of alarm
signals into the alarm communication and display system.

1. INTRODUCTION
Advanced and small modular reactors (A/SMRs), due to their adaptability, are likely to be used in
remote locations to provide electrical power, support desalination activities, or enable other
services in regions that are difficult to access or have serious obstacles for transportation.
Simultaneously, there is minimal experienced human capital to support these A/SMRs, due to the
lack of early to mid-career nuclear- trained professionals. Both factors are driving A/SMR vendors
to consider remote monitoring as a solution to support nuclear security. Maintaining continuity of
knowledge (CoK) of nuclear material quantities and locations is vital to nuclear security, and
remote monitoring of active tamper indicating devices (TIDs) has been well established as a
component of International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Safeguards. Active TIDs, such as
radiofrequency TIDs (RFTIDs), promote timely detection by alarming when unauthorized access of
an enclosure is attempted or accomplished. Passive TIDs, on the other hand, provide delayed
detection, and require the implementation of a surveillance regime provide timely detection.
Therefore, the use of active TIDs is also considered as a surveillance measure. The presence of
active TIDs will deter potential insider adversaries but also promotes the timely detection of a
malicious act by an insider adversary. Active TIDs can be applied to nuclear material containers or
other entry points (e.g., vaults, drawers, toolboxes, etc.) where routine access may or may not be
required, and authorized access is governed by strict controls. Therefore, the implementation of an
RFTID program enhances overall nuclear material control, and provides a visible deterrent, with
primary efficacy in mitigating the insider threat. In the application of remote A/SMR nuclear
security, passive TIDs that have historically been used for nuclear security are unsuited to provide
timely detection of potential adversary attempts at theft or sabotage. RFTIDs provide an excellent
tool to fill this gap, as well as augment other physical protection sensors and systems.
There are multiple sources of guidance on the use of TIDs to support the control and accountability
of nuclear material, as well as authenticating or verifying that physical containers have not been
accessed across the IAEA, Department of Energy (DOE), and Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC).
These guides are generally broad in their use of TIDs. Therefore, no terse list of guidance exists for
the use of active TIDs, or more specifically, RFTIDs. This report provides a fusion of general
guidance for developing a TID program and specific recommendations on the implementation of
RFTIDs, to develop a singular, concise reference for utilizing RFTIDs for nuclear security purposes
at A/SMRs, as presented in section 2. Note that this report is specific to nuclear security, and the
terms: “TIDs”, “safeguards seals”, and “seals”, are used interchangeably throughout.
One example of an RFTID is the Remotely Monitored Sealing Array (RMSA), which is an active fiber-
optic loop seal that stores, forwards, and communicates data (tamper events, open/close events,
seal status) via a customized data communication protocol over a no-license, low power
radiofrequency (RF) link. The data can be stored and transferred remotely and securely
(authenticated and encrypted) to the RMSA Translator that collects data from all the RMSA seals in
a particular location. The data are made available on the RMSA Translator in an easily accessible
format either for data retrieval by an authorized inspector at a local review station or for remote
data communication via Ethernet and virtual private networks (VPN) link. The data collected by the
RMSA seals can then be verified and analyzed on-site or worldwide with the RMSA review software.
It is designed to be part of a large array of seals. The system is comprised of the seal, a translator

 2

and a data review station. Hundreds of seals can be connected simultaneously with a single
translator. State of health (SoH) data are transmitted periodically, and security significant events
are transmitted at the time of the event. The review station provides decryption, data
authentication, data storage and data review capabilities. The seal was developed by Sandia
National Laboratories (SNL) and is based on the secure sensor platform (SSP). The RMSA was
originally developed under contract to the IAEA and has been used at multiple sites worldwide
including the Savannah River Site (SRS). The RMSA uses Advanced Encryption Standard (AES)
symmetric key encryption and message authentication code (MAC) symmetric key authentication.
SNL developed a newer version of the seal called the Chain of Custody Item Monitor (CoCIM) that
has been used in nuclear weapons monitoring and verification exercises. The CoCIM uses an
asymmetric public key algorithm for data authentication in addition to the AES symmetric
encryption. Additionally, the DOE uses a Transportation Sealing Monitor (TSM) 1 for shipments of
some nuclear waste, which utilizes the same RMSA and translator, however the translator sends
data to a remote review station via satellite or direct IP connection. These different devices offer
value to nuclear security, but additional guidance is needed for effective implementation.
Furthermore, because these devices have not been used specifically for remote monitored nuclear
security applications, testing was needed to confirm applicability and identify any improvements
that remain before full implementation.

1.1. REPORT ORGANIZATION OVERVIEW
Ultimately, this testing and evaluation project seeks to provide proof of concept for remotely
monitored TIDs for the nuclear security of A/SMRs worldwide, which are remotely monitored by the
operator. This report first provides a cohesive list of recommended guidance for implementing
RFTIDs into a nuclear security regime. It then provides a description and conclusion of the testing
conducted to prove the applicability of RFTIDs in a nuclear security system of A/SMRs. And finally,
it provides a description of recommended applications of RFTIDs and improvements for full
implementation.
This study assessed the technical requirements for implementing RFTIDs, compiling over 300
requirements from nearly a dozen references into single document that guides the application and
operation of the RFTIDs for A/SMRs, including data transmission, tamper indication, and setup.
Some categories of requirements include overarching requirements for RFTID application for
nuclear security (e.g., remote data transmission, monitoring, communication, power, and data
security), nuclear security front end requirements for TID application (e.g., procurement,
verification, information barriers, and computer security), and unique A/SMR
challenges/constraints (e.g., environmental and operational constraints). This is presented in
Section 2.
In Section 3, the project sought to determine RFTIDs’ suitability for use as security devices with
A/SMRs in remote environments with a series of tests. The ultimate goal of these tests is to
confirm response times, alarms/notifications, and observations of the system during various tests.

nuclear security subject matter experts. At the conclusion of FY25, the project intends to
demonstrate that RFTIDs can be utilized in A/SMRs as a part of the physical security system (PPS)
to provide real time alarms. The study provides possible applications of the RTFIDs (Section 4) and
identifies potential improvements and addresses follow-on questions and paths forward for remote
monitoring of TIDs for A/SMRs to fully clear RFTIDs for remote deployment application and
incorporated into a PPS (Section 5).

2. RFTID IMPLEMENTATION GUIDANCE FOR A/SMRS
This section seeks to compile general guidance for developing a TID program, as well as specific
recommendations on the implementation of RFTIDs, to develop a short and pointed list for utilizing
RFTIDs for nuclear security purposes at A/SMRs, Section 2.1 describes the role that TIDs serve in a
physical protection system and provides guidance on the different types of TIDs available. Section
2.2 outlines the structure of a TID Program and provides required elements. And Section 2.3
provides guidance around the appropriate use, application, and day-to-day operations of RFTIDs in
nuclear security.

2.1. INFORMATION
Seals are employed in nuclear facilities to detect tampering and unauthorized access, but they do
not prevent abrupt theft and must show clear evidence if tampering occurs. An effective seal
program enhances nuclear material management by reducing verification efforts, minimizing costs,
and ensuring material integrity through comprehensive monitoring. Proper selection and
application of TIDs, in conjunction with other security measures, contribute to robust nuclear
security by ensuring CoK and enabling efficient auditing and surveillance.

2.1.1. Overview
Seals can be, and have been, used to support IAEA Safeguards, nuclear security (e.g., nuclear
material accounting and control (NMAC)), and facility operations purposes. Facilities must utilize
TIDs to the extent practical to detect tampering or unauthorized access of containers. Seals are

 4

used on items such as containers and doors to reveal violations of containment and integrity due
to their uniqueness in design or structure. However, seals are not useful in deterring adversaries,
such as an insider and/or external adversary, from undertaking abrupt theft. The role of a seal is to
support the detection of attempted or actual unauthorized access of a container, package, door, or
other object to which a seal has been affixed. Therefore, TIDs should not be capable of being
removed without creating indications of tampering within the device. Additionally, the use of a TID
with unique identification features provides a level of confidence that the item protected by the TID
has not been opened without detection. (DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(a); DOE
Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.1, 1.2, IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.130)
TIDs not only enable detection of unauthorized access from adversaries but can also deter an
insider because they may perceive that the TID is an obstacle that decreases the chances of
undetected access to the material. Much like cameras, fences, and security doors, TIDs may serve
as a psychological deterrent by indicating a robust nuclear security regime. A TID by itself does not
protect against the removal of nuclear material from a container or location. The only delay time
associated with a TID is the time it takes an adversary to reapply or substitute it. Additionally,
indiscriminate and uncontrolled use of TIDs can reduce nuclear security and operational
effectiveness by giving a false sense of security. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.30; DOE Safeguards Seals
Reference Guide 1.3, 1.5)

2.1.2. Designing an Effective TID Program
DOE nuclear materials are either in item or bulk form. TIDs allow for more cost–effective
management, control, and accountability of items; specifically, by indicating that container
contents have not been altered since the TID was applied, reducing the frequency of time-
consuming nuclear material verification measurements and physical inventory takings (PITs), and
supporting special nuclear material (SNM) assessments and audits. (DOE Safeguards Seals
Reference Guide 1.3, 1.4)
Further, the use of TIDs can enhance efficiency, reduce costs, and minimize radiation exposure. A
comprehensive TID program and the use of remote monitoring for nuclear materials can result in
efficient inventory taking and reduced radiation exposure of inspectors and facility personnel, and
reduce the support required from the facility operator for external inspections. The required
measurement intervals for containerized nuclear material, with a properly applied and intact TID,
can be sufficiently extended. Proper inspection of correctly applied and intact TIDs can provide a
quick and easy means of conducting item integrity verification. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference
Guide 1.5; Policy Paper 16, page 95)

2.1.3. Surveillance Inherently Satisfied by RFTIDs
Use of TIDs in conjunction with other material control measures, e.g., surveillance to detect
unauthorized access to the TIDs, can provide additional assurance that unauthorized removal of
nuclear material has not occurred. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.133)
In standard practice, TIDs must be maintained under an effective material surveillance program,
which provides the assessment of the sensing to result in overall detection ensuring they are
effective indicators of container tampering. However, RFTIDs intrinsically provide surveillance while
simultaneously achieving their TID functionality. An intact TID in conjunction with a material
surveillance program allows the custodian to verify the integrity of an item, reducing the effort

 5

needed to conduct PITs by lowering the number or frequency of required measurements. However,
they need to be implemented in conjunction with other technical measures including video
surveillance, weight sensors, radiation portal monitors and other radiation monitoring equipment,
X-ray equipment and metal detectors, and TIDs with radio transmitters. Therefore, TIDs that
incorporate radio transmitters are inherently considered a surveillance measure, and are a single
component of a greater nuclear security regime, consisting of key control, tie-downs, access
control, surveillance, etc. (DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(b); DOE Safeguards
Seals Reference Guide 1.1, 1.3, 1.5; IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.66, 4.71, 4.87; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.54)

2.1.4. Continuity of Knowledge
The TID preserves the CoK of the material, especially during storage and transport, by indicating
whether or not the container or location has been accessed. Measures to maintain CoK of nuclear
material can enhance the system’s ability to detect unauthorized activities in a timely manner.
(IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.30 NSS 25-G; 4.125)
Verification measurements must be used to validate the CoK when necessary, e.g., at time of
physical inventory for non-tamper-indicating items or in response to a security anomaly that could
have resulted in a theft or diversion of SNM. Automated TID dispensing systems and automation of
TIDs information pertaining to bar code labels, part numbers, nomenclature, serial numbers, dates,
and part classifications are common and are considered enhancements to the efficiency and
effectiveness of the TID program. (DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 4.c.(2); DOE Safeguards
Seals Reference Guide 1.3)

2.1.5. TID Types
TIDs are available in many different physical forms, having a broad range of characteristics and
capabilities, and using a variety of means to indicate tampering. Ample consideration should be
applied in determining which TID is best suited for use at a specified facility. RFTIDs can be a good
selection choice if their application occurs in remote locations, frequent access is needed to the
item under seal, or any other factors constitute challenges for continuous verification activities.
(IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.131; IAEA NSS32-T 3.33)

2.1.6. Seal Choice
The selection of a TID should be appropriate for the given application, meaning that when applied
properly, unauthorized access or attempts at access should be clearly indicated by the TID.

 6

Deciding which TID to use requires a variety of considerations to be made and translated into
performance and procurement requirements. These include the following: purpose, threat, type of
container, durability, durability of TID during transit, length of storage, ease of application/removal,
detection capabilities, uniqueness, size and shape, cost effectiveness, interface with other
safeguard and security elements, disposition of the sealed container, and reliability. (DOE
Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 3.1)
TID programs should be considered for the following: items in long-term storage, off-site shipments
and transfers between material balance areas (MBAs), security doors, waste containers, items or
containers in process or working vaults if it would save on manpower, cost of performing
inventories, or reducing radiation exposure. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 3.2, 5.3, 1.5;
DOE O 474.2A Attachment 2 Chapter I; 3.e.(5)(C); NRC 10 CFR Part 73; 73.46 (c)(5)(ii))
Typical uses of TIDs include:
  Empty shipping containers, drums, and cans of SNM in storage vaults
  Drums and box containers for waste in material access area
  Drums and "bird cages" of SNM and other radioactive materials
  Small containers of SNM stored in 5, 10, 30, and 55-gallon drums
  Casks and caskets containing fuel or target material in transit or storage
  Various small containers containing nuclear materials
  Door hasps and cages for fuel
  Containers of numerous smaller items/pieces for PIT purposes

2.2. TID PROGRAM MANAGEMENT
A TID control program is essential for effective and appropriate use of RFTIDs, incorporating clear
organizational structure, personnel responsibilities, and documented procedures, including
protocols from acquisition to destruction and security measures. Effective implementation requires
the program to integrate seamlessly with other facility systems, ensuring synchronization with
nuclear material accountability databases, inspection procedures, and operational protocols while
maintaining security without disrupting normal operations. Establishing and organizing roles within
the TID program is crucial, with defined responsibilities for positions, supported by comprehensive
training to ensure personnel are qualified to secure and properly manage TIDs.

2.2.1. Program Structure and Requirements
A TID control program is essential for effective and appropriate use of RFTIDs. The program should
incorporate clear organizational structure, personnel responsibilities, and documented procedures.
The TID control program should specify, as a minimum, the following elements:
  Acquisition, procurement, and destruction protocols
  TID selection criteria, TID inventory controls, and the TIDs themselves
  Assurance of unique TIDs (i.e., no two TID labels the same) and unique identifiers (to
 mitigate counterfeiting)
  Secure storage, issuance and control measures

 7

  TID authorized personnel and procedural documentation
  Required forms and documentation for application/removal
  Techniques, training requirements, modules, and devices necessary for their effective
 use
  Verification schedules and methods, including audits and inventories by administrators
 and custodians
  TID violation criteria and response procedures for violation
  Assurance of TID destruction
  Containers or objects to be protected
Facilities should document their organizational structure in both their NMAC Plan and Site Security
Plan to enhance clarity and accountability. Identification of the organizational structure aids in the
effective control of TIDs. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 2.1, 2.3, 2.4; IAEA NSS 25-G
4.131, 4.133)
Separation of duties is a concept to be incorporated within the TID program to ensure an insider
adversary cannot abuse their access, authority, and/or knowledge. The key areas to be kept
separate are the control of records (the TID administrator and TID custodian) and the application of
TIDs (the TID users). (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 2.2)

2.2.2. Integration with Other Systems
A TID program will interface with other facility systems. Effective implementation of RFTID requires
consideration of these touchpoints. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.7)

 Training Requirements
A trained and knowledgeable person should be designated by the facility or the State’s competent
authority to test and approve each type of TID for use on a case-by-case basis. Selection of TIDs
should be overseen by a trained individual, designated by the facility or competent authority
(regulatory body). (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.34)
The facility should identify personnel (i.e., TID Users or TID Custodians) responsible for use and
control of TIDs and document completion of training requirements and qualifications to ensure they
can conduct their assigned duties. Training should be done in accordance with site procedures.
Personnel working with TIDs are trained in all aspects of the program, including the use,
application, removal, destruction, storage, issuance, and verification of TIDs. It is recommended
that a periodic re-evaluation be conducted to determine their current knowledge of the TID program
in a manner and frequency to be determined by the site, which may include testing of their ability
to correctly apply, remove, and verify TIDs. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.41; DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2;
Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c)1; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 4.3)

2.2.4. Procedures
All TID activities are governed by formal procedures and should be maintained on a need-to-know
level. Any deviation from established procedures must be approved and documented. Procedures
are developed for facility personnel to follow when implementing all parts of the TID program,
including audits, inventories, and inspections of the TID program. (IAEA NSS 32-T 3.37, 3.41; DOE
O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 5.c.(2)(a)3; NRC 10 CFR Part 74; 74.43; (c)(3), 74.59 f(2)(i);
DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 4.2, 7.1)
  The physical inventory procedure must verify, at a minimum: Item/container
 identification numbers and locations, TID integrity (i.e., verification should be of TID
 for indications of tamper as well as ensuring TID unique identification), serial
 number and locations. Inventories should be performed on applied and unused TID
 inventory.
  Implement and adhere to tamper-sealing protocols for SNM containers or vaults
 when using TIDs to validate previous measurements. These protocols must include
 strict controls over unused TID inventory and documentation of TID application
 details, including dates and times.
The two-person rule is a security measure that requires two authorized personnel to be present
during critical TID activities to prevent unauthorized access. The two-person rule should be used
when applying, verifying, removing and destroying TIDs, and should be incorporated in facility
procedures. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.41)
Facility procedures for installing and verifying installed TIDs should consider manufacturer
specifications for conditions of use and only use the devices under those conditions. (IAEA NSS 32-
T; 3.37)
Facilities should maintain removal and destruction procedures that specify how to render TIDs
ineffective and prevent their reuse. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 5.7; NRC RG 5.80; C.
f., IAEA NSS 32-T 3.41; DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c)8)

2.2.5. Documentation
When applying, verifying, or removing TIDs, the TID users should record appropriate data. TID
programs should maintain destruction records identifying who removed the TID, date of removal,
who witnessed the removal, and the container/item from which it was removed. (DOE O 474.2A;
Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c)4, 9; IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.50, 4.42; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.39, 3.41; NRC
RG 5.80; C. g; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 5.1)
 Data recorded may include the following information:
  Container or material identification
  TID number and type of TID
  Information on whether the TID was applied, voided, broken, or removed
  Location of the container
  Identification of the TID user (signature and/or identification number)
  Identification of verifier/witness (signature and/or identification number)
  Date entries were made
  If applicable, the date and status of the final disposition of the TID or container (TID
 removed, item shipped or transferred)
Movements of nuclear material, including shipments, receipts, transfers and relocations, should be
recorded in the accounting system and include item and TID identification due to its vulnerable
state. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.147)
Facility operators should maintain control over computerized or handwritten data associated with
TIDs, in addition to requisition forms and purchase order forms, to prevent or detect any attempt at
unauthorized alteration and falsification of those data. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.40; DOE Safeguards
Seals Reference Guide 4.1, 4.2)

2.2.6. Audits/Self Assessments
A continuous internal review/self-assessment program and audit process is essential to
maintaining the effectiveness of any seals program. The program may include both scheduled and
unscheduled assessments of the program. Periodic checks should be conducted by the facility
operator to confirm that measures are applied according to the established procedures and that
equipment is used correctly, e.g., that TIDs on doors and containers do not reveal irregularities and
are properly implemented. All assessments should be documented, identifying deficiencies and
corrective actions taken to address the deficiencies and weaknesses in the seals program. (IAEA
NSS 25-G; 4.135; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.5, 4.2)
The most common problems found during assessments are non-compliance with procedures,
failure to identify items/containers to which TIDs are applied, failure to sign and date
application/removal records, improper application of TIDs, and inadequate response procedures.
Checklists developed for the review and evaluation of a site’s seals program may be of value during
an assessment of the seals program. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.135; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference
Guide 1.5, 4.2)

The effectiveness of seals programs should be validated by performance testing. (DOE Safeguards
Seals Reference Guide 7.3)

2.3. USE AND APPLICATION
This section presents the recommended appropriate use, application, and day-to-day operations of
RFTIDs in nuclear security. The use of uniquely identified TIDs ensures that tampering on protected
items can be detected, with requirements for unique serial identities and controlled procurement
to prevent unauthorized duplication. Procedures must be followed for the chain of custody,
application, verification, and destruction of TIDs to maintain security and detect any unauthorized
access or tampering, including prompt investigations into any irregularities. TIDs should be
designed to withstand environmental conditions, integrate with the overall security system, provide
reliable data transmission and cybersecurity, and operational requirements to support minimal
staff and remote monitoring, particularly in the context of A/SMRs.

2.3.1. Detection
The objective of a TID is to ensure that no undetected tampering, unauthorized access, or
attempted entry occurred while it was affixed to the item. A TID would fail to perform its function if
it could be replaced, removed and reapplied, or altered without leaving any indications of
tampering. The design and use of TIDs should make obvious any alterations to recorded data on
the TID, including alterations to the unique identifier. If required, a TID should include a tamper
indicating enclosure (TIE) to make obvious any alterations or attempted alterations to the interior.
Results should be conclusive and not difficult to interpret. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.130; NSS 32-T 3.35;
3.38; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.2)
Seals programs should be cost-effective (capital and manpower), capable of indicating violations of
the container and tampering with safeguards devices and designed to minimize impact to facility
operations. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.5)
The verification of the integrity of a TID is meant to indicate the existence, or not, of an irregularity.
Damaged, voided, or missing TIDs are irregularities, and an investigation to determine whether or
not nuclear material has been removed should be carried out immediately and resolved. The
correct implementation of a TID program is meant to increase the probability of detection of
irregularities that require investigation. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.161; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.72, 5.11)

2.3.2. Uniqueness/Supply Chain
The use of a TID with unique identification features provides a level of confidence that the item
protected by the TID has not been opened without detection. Additionally, the TID should bear a
unique serial identity combined with unique information that identifies the licensed facility using
the TID. Both the serial identity and the logo or other identifying information should be applied in a
manner that makes undetected removal difficult. If an alphanumeric code sequence is used for a
unique label, then the sequence should contain enough characters to allow it to remain unique for
longer than the likely lifetime of the TID design. **Note: In this context, the term unique label is
used for TID identifiers, while unique identifier denotes the feature that makes a TID resistant to
counterfeit; for example, some TIDs use speckle patterns. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.130; NSS 32-T 3.36,
3.41; NRC RG 5.80; C. a)

 12

At a minimum, the procurement process should require steps to ensure the TIDs are truly unique,
and their means of production are also controlled. This includes the provision, in writing, of a
vendor-certified statement that TIDs bearing the unique characteristics of the supplied TIDs will not
be provided to another customer without approval from the purchaser. Other specific security
requirements at the vendor’s facility, such as stock control, die and mold control, access to
product, and item accounting may be included in the contract. Background investigations and
formal facility clearance may be requested of the vendors as well. (DOE Safeguards Seals
Reference Guide 4.1)

2.3.3. Unused TIDs
The TID Administrator should maintain chain of custody documentation for each transfer of TIDs.
Upon receipt, all TIDs should be inspected to assure that they are of acceptable quality,
inventoried, entered into the nuclear materials accountability database (if applicable), and
secured. Unused and unissued TIDs should be secured under controlled access, as are TIDs that
have been removed but not yet destroyed, and the inventory of unused TIDs is periodically verified.
Positive control over TIDs internal to the facility must be provided to prevent unauthorized
substitution or modification. Previously used RFTIDs should be controlled in the same manner as
unissued TIDs. (DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c)5; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.41; DOE
Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 4.1, 4.2)
The facility operator should maintain and follow procedures for applying TIDs to containers or
vaults containing SNM, which include control of access to, and distribution of, unused TIDs and to
records showing the date and time of TID application. (NRC 10 CFR Part 74; 74.43; (c)(3))

2.3.4. Regulatory Considerations
In addition to the guidance herein, the State’s competent authority may require that TIDs meet
certain specifications and may require certification of specific TID types for use. (IAEA NSS 32-T;
3.34)
The TID Administrator, who serves as central authority for TID management, is responsible for
verifying that TIDs meet regulatory requirements. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 2.6, 4.2;
DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c))
The TID Selection Authority should be designated by the facility or State’s competent authority.
They are responsible for evaluating and approving TIDs for specific applications, testing and
approving each type of TID for use, evaluating appropriateness for case-by-case applications, and
verifying TIDs meet security and operational requirements (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.34).

2.3.5. Engineering and Vulnerability Assessment
TIDs should be appropriate for the intended use (e.g., ensuring the integrity of an item, a storage
cabinet or a room) and should be capable of withstanding the actual conditions of use (e.g.,
temperature, moisture, repeated handling, high radiation from gamma rays and neutrons,
chemicals) without any degradation that could allow tampering or be mistaken for evidence of
tampering. Examples for A/SMRs applications include extreme weather events, a wide range of
temperature/humidity applications (i.e., desert, arctic, Pacific Island conditions), and marine based
conditions. Additionally, most SMR designs are based underground and are susceptible to flooding
and inhibited access in emergencies. Therefore, the RFTID must be able to operate or send signal if

 13

immersed in water, after a seismic event, and be able to send data to its translator from
underground; this may require sending a specific alarm when wetting or flooding occurs.
Additionally, RFTIDs should be able to withstand and operate according to specification within 5-
95% humidity and -40°C to +85°C expected in operation or storage. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.34; IAEA-
SM-367/7/07; Ghimire, et al; Black et al; Hussein)
The acceptability of a TID should be based on engineering studies and vulnerability testing that
demonstrate its appropriateness for the conditions of use. These studies should also assure
performance requirements are met through an evaluation of the attributes of the device in relation
to the time and means needed to defeat the tamper indicating features (e.g., evaluation of
manufacturer data and field tests) and provide assurance the seal can only be used one time.
RFTIDs are an exception to the one-time use, however, acceptability should assure reuse meets
performance requirements. The TIDs being used should withstand the conditions of use without
degradation that could allow tampering or be mistaken for evidence of tampering. (IAEA NSS 32-T;
3.34, 3.41; DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 4.1)

2.3.6. Application
A TID should be properly applied immediately after the nuclear material is packaged in a container
while the container is still under the surveillance of the two-person concept. TIDs should be applied
in a manner that makes undetected removal difficult and ensures the contents cannot be removed
from the sealed container without compromising the integrity of the TID, the TIE housing of the TID,
or the container and could be detected during a visual inspection. (DOE Safeguards Seals
Reference Guide 1.5, 5.2; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.37, 3.41; NRC 10 CFR Part 71; 71.43 (b); NRC RG
5.80; C. b.)
TIDs can be used on containers or storage vaults to ensure the integrity of nuclear material either
when it is in transit or being stored. TIDs are particularly effective for maintaining CoK of an
inventory of nuclear material items, especially when factors preclude individual verification (such
as a remote location of nuclear material storage). At the facility, TIDs may be applied to objects
providing containment for nuclear materials (e.g., containers, doors, safes) when the material is in
storage or when immediate access to it is not needed. TIDs can also be used when storage
characteristics preclude verifying each individual item and, if radiation testing is conducted, it
could also be used to preclude periodic inspection. Equipment used for all activities involving
nuclear material, including handling, measuring, application of TIDs or processing (e.g.,
gloveboxes, empty containers, sample vials, cranes), should be controlled, since it could potentially
be used for unauthorized removal or misuse of nuclear material. (IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.66, 4.131; NSS
32-T; 3.14, 3.15, 3.31)

2.3.7. Verification
TID verification should be accomplished at a variety of different times to ensure unauthorized
access to material has not occurred. (DOE O 474.2A; Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.e.(5)(d); DOE
Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 5.6, 6.1; IAEA NSS 25-G; 4.138)
 The identity and integrity of TIDs should be verified at the following times:
  Immediately before and after TID application
  Prior to removing, breaking, or voiding a TID

 14

  Prior to transferring a sealed container into an MBA
  Prior to removing a sealed container from an MBA
  When sealed containers are received
  During physical inventory
  When confirmation measurements are made
  During inspections and internal reviews
In addition, to ensure that operators do not miss alarms or notifications and to allow for any trend
tracking of SoH, RFTIDs should be verified on the review station on a routine basis, recommended
not to exceed 2 hours. Additionally, they should be incorporated into the PPS to allow for
automated verification to alert the CAS operator of an alarm condition.
The verification of TIDs will be accomplished according to their design, which means it may require
postmortem examination. Verification can be accomplished for some TIDs by visual inspection;
other TIDs require a postmortem examination even though there is no apparent evidence of
tampering. Verification activities should include determining if TID is properly in place, reading the
TID number, and comparing the TID number and location with facility records. (DOE Safeguards
Seals Reference Guide 6.1)
RFTIDs’ verification should include physical examination for signs of tamper, followed by electronic
verification on the review station to review fiber open/closed authorized notifications, any tamper
events, and SoH. Additionally, the unique identifier should be checked to ensure the TID is the one
expected and there has been no counterfeit or substitution. For RFTIDs, it is typically sufficient to
receive an authenticated message with the signed electronic ID.
Destruction is an important component of a complete TID program and should be required after the
removal of a TID. This precludes design information, in the form of the physical TID, from becoming
uncontrolled information. Any TID removed from a container or a shipping vehicle should be
examined and disposed of according to approved site procedures. When a TID is removed, it must
be intentionally damaged sufficiently to preclude its reuse or should be protected by TID
custodians using the same procedures as those for unused TIDs to prevent unauthorized persons
from obtaining the TID. Destruction is not required for active TIDs, including RFTIDs, which are
intended to be reused, unless they are no longer functional. When an RFTID is taken out of use,
follow the appropriate user manual for disconnecting it from its associated translator. (DOE
Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 5.7; NRC RG 5.80; C. f., IAEA NSS 32-T 3.41; DOE O 474.2A;
Attachment 2; Chapter I; 3.f.(1)(c)8)

2.3.8. Response
If a TID is violated, a verification measurement is required to detect possible loss of material. A
violated TID signals facility personnel to begin investigative and corrective actions in accordance
with established procedures. The possibility of theft or diversion of nuclear material should be
evaluated and corrective actions determined and executed. The assumption when finding a broken
TID is that potential tampering may have occurred. It is important that prompt action be taken to
determine whether or not tampering occurred. Some conditions that may require a response are a
missing TID; a violated TID; a TID number discrepancy; a damaged TID; an improperly applied TID;

 15

an unauthorized TID; uncontrolled TIDs or TID records; obvious tampering with TID or TID markings.
(DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 1.3, 6.2, 6.3)
If a TID becomes broken, damaged, or improperly applied during application of the TID, the TID
should be removed, and a new one applied immediately according to appropriate TID application
and removal procedures. Retraining of the TID user may be needed, with no other action required.
(DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 6.3)
A suspect seal, which are those that show signs of tamper, should be retained until an
investigation has resolved the situation involving the seal. Once the investigation is complete and
the report has been issued, the seal administrator can authorize destruction and disposal of the
suspect seal. (DOE Safeguards Seals Reference Guide 6.4)

2.3.9. Operational
A/SMRs are expected to utilize limited staff and autonomous controls, with minimal operational
and security personnel, due to their remote location. Therefore, TIDs should remotely transmit
status and tamper, as well as provide for appropriate reaction time from responding personnel.
(Lee et al.)
TIDs should have the ability to deal with nuisance alarms, e.g., filtering out or compensating for
nuisances or spurious alarms. (Evans et al)
With respect to SeBD, active TIDs should be included in the design phase to provide economic
competitiveness. (SMR SeBD – Evans)
TIDs should be included in any access point that provides power and communication from any
portions of the intrusion detection system (IDS) to the central alarm station (CAS) to protect
against insider threats. (SMR SeBD – Evans)
TIDs should be designed to sense adversary incursions and ensure that the CAS operator can
adequately assess and communicate the cause of alarms. Specifically, the alarm should include
the unique label, associated item, and alarm condition. (SMR SeBD – Evans)
A/SMR operators should consider how TIDs integrate with the PPS and facilitate an effective
response to mitigate adversaries, considering integration of detection, delay, and response
throughout the design of the PPS. (SMR SeBD – Evans)
The design of physical security structures, systems, and components, including TIDs, relied on for
interior and exterior intrusion detection functions should provide assurance of detecting
unauthorized access into vital and protected areas. The design should apply the principle of
diversity necessary for the reliability and availability of systems and components to achieve the
intended intrusion detection functions. (NRC Non-LWR Security Design Considerations)
The expected operational life of an RFTID, such as RMSA or TSM, is 4+ years with two lithium
batteries at 1 interrogation per day, i.e., providing at least one SoH update per day. This will be
considerably shorter for the number of interrogations required for RFTID use in nuclear security.
Therefore, the operational life should be long enough to fit in a routine maintenance cycle (i.e.,
approximately six months). RFTIDs should consider energy harvesting to prolong operational life.
RFTIDs should have low power mode and local data storage. If a communication link or transceiver
goes down or loses power, local storage should keep messages long enough to resolve any power

 16

or communications (or other) issues that prevent message transmission; typically, at least 7 days is
sufficient. (RMSA Data Sheet; SAND2022-4273)
RFTIDs should include internal non-volatile memory for a recommended minimum of 1000 real-time
stamped events, recording unique serial numbers, events such as tamper, case open/close, cable
open/close, SoH messages, and inspector actions. They should include a cable diameter sized to
thread through items of interest of ample length with high tamper sensitivity. (*Note, the RMSA
uses 1 mm Plastic Fiber ≤50 m long). The TID reader/review station should be based on a
commercial portable computer or desktop PC, Windows OS and facilitate easy data transfer and
evaluation. Additionally, they should have the potential to connect multiple TIDs in party-lines and
interrogate automatically in unattended or remote monitoring systems. (IAEA-SM-367/7/07; RMSA
Data Sheet)

2.3.10. Transmission
Communication links should be highly reliable with less than 2% downtime for the primary link.
Communication links should consider automatic, seamless handover to a secondary independent
communication link if the primary goes down. (SAND2022-4273)
System should have sufficient bandwidth to transmit required data. For example, RMSA specs are:
  RF frequency operation: 900 MHz ISM Band (902 to 928 MHz) (with other ISM/SRD
 bands available) (**Note ISM band not allowed in some countries)
  RF sensitivity: -110 dB (typical)
  RF power output: -30 to +7 dBm typical at antenna input (internal or external)
  ~10 dB Effective Isotropic Radiated Power (EIRP) improvement with 1/4 wave vertical
 external antenna (transmit and receive) (SAND2022-4273; RMSA Data Sheet)
Alarms or relevant information should be sent immediately from RFTID to alarm station. The time
from alarm at the RFTID to review station should be short enough to ensure adequate response
time. This should include an alarm if the RFTID is “Not Heard” within a specified time,
recommended two minutes, to account for intentional or inadvertent RF interference. It is expected
that the facility operators develop appropriate response times and measures for each type of
alarm. (SAND2022-4273)
Measures should be taken to ensure the authenticity and encryption of the transmitted and stored
data from system to alarm station. Encryption should be applied during data transmission. Reliable
authentication of all TID data should use certified algorithms and key lengths of 128 bits or more,
private/public key system preferred. Cryptographic keys should be erased upon tamper, as agreed
with the State (SAND2022-4273; Policy Paper 16, page 97)
Remote monitoring systems may utilize any communication means that have been agreed on
between the operator and the State, such as telephone lines, ethernet cables, internet or other
public network utilizing VPNs, or satellites. The choice should be based on reliability, secure
transmission, installation cost and transmission cost. Environmental factors determine if
communication lines should be trenched or wireless (satellite vs internet) (Policy Paper 16, page
97)
RFTID systems should be reliable with minimal maintenance required. Remote maintenance should
be considered if needed systems may be deployed unattended for long periods. (SAND2022-4273)

 17

Recommended communication range with translator should be 500* meters with external antenna,
200* meters with internal antenna. The RFTID shall connect to the corresponding translator.
(*Based on outdoor test with no obstacles) (RMSA Data Sheet, IAEA-SM-367/7/07)

2.3.11. Cyber Security
Endpoint Detection and Response (EDR) is a cybersecurity solution that continuously monitors
endpoints for suspicious activity, analyzes data to detect threats, and provides automated or
manual response capabilities to mitigate those threats. EDR solutions should be deployed on all
devices downstream of the RFTID receiver to continuously monitor for threats and provide real-time
visibility. TID data protection should be instituted for computerized, transmitted, and handwritten
data, to protect against falsification. (NRC RG 5.71; B.1.16)
Enhance Receiver Security: Secure the RFTID receiver against spoofing, falsification, interception,
jamming, and unauthorized access by implementing strong authentication mechanisms and
regularly updating firmware/software. Additionally, an alarm condition should be sent if indications
of these cyber-attacks are sensed. (IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.63; NRC RG 5.71; B.1.16; SAND2022-4273)
Conduct Asset Inventory and Vulnerability Assessment: Maintain an inventory of all assets and
services running on them. Regularly assess known vulnerabilities related to hardware, firmware,
and software. Maintain control over computerized/handwritten data associated with TIDs to protect
against unauthorized alteration. Protect audit records. (NRC RG 5.71; B.2.10; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.40)
Assess Consequences of Compromise: Evaluate and document the potential consequences if any
devices are compromised, including impacts on safety, operations, and data integrity.
Establish Comprehensive Security Controls: Develop a suite of complementary security measures
including:
  Detection Measures: Use IDS to monitor for suspicious activity.
  Protection Measures: Implement firewalls, anti-malware solutions, and network
 segmentation.
  Response Measures: Create incident response plans for security breaches. Employ and
 document mitigation techniques to secure CDAs through media access control address
 locking, physical or electrical isolation, static tables, encryption, or monitoring. (NRC RG
           5.71; B.1.15, B.3.1)
VPN Security Enhancements: Ensure secure storage of VPN credentials and implement multi-factor
authentication (MFA) for VPN access.
Address Attack Vectors: Develop controls to protect, detect, and respond to the following attack
vectors (up to and including the design basis threat as described in § 73.1.) (NRC RG 5.71;
A.4.2.3, B.1.1, B.1.17, B.1.18, B.1.19, B.2.8, B.3.4, B.3.6, B.3.7, B.3.18; NRC 10 CFR Part 73;
73.54 (a); NEI 08-09):
  Wired: Use network segmentation and monitoring to detect unauthorized access.
  Wireless: Implement strong encryption and secure wireless protocols.
  Utilize the following technical controls for securing both wired and wireless
 communications, as outlined in NEI 08-09 and recommended by DOE:

 18

 o Wired Network: time stamps, application partitioning/security function isolation,
 shared resources, denial-of-service (DoS) protection, resource priority, transmission
 integrity, transmission confidentiality, trusted path, cryptographic key establishment
 and management (to recognize changes to information during transmission and
 upon receipt, unless otherwise protected by alternative physical measures,
 implementing mechanisms to prevent “man-in-the-middle” (MITM) attacks), and
 session authenticity.
 o Additional controls for wireless monitoring devices: Radio resource management, RF
 monitoring, RF-Restricted zones.
  Direct Access (Insider): Enforce strict access controls and conduct background checks.
  Portable Media/Mobile Devices: Implement policies for the use of portable media and
 mobile devices, including encryption and access controls.
  Supply Chain: Assess and monitor third-party vendors for security practices and
 vulnerabilities. Establish, implement, document, and maintain a process that ensures that
 modifications to CDAs are evaluated. Protect integrity of transmitted information by
 employing cryptographic mechanisms to recognize changes to information during transmit.
Documentation and Compliance: Maintain thorough documentation of all security measures and
controls, ensuring alignment with NRC requirements. Prepare to demonstrate how these measures
protect against identified attack vectors. Keep records of actions performed protected. (NRC RG
5.71; B.2.10; IAEA NSS 32-T; 3.40)
Regular Training and Awareness: Conduct regular training sessions for personnel on cybersecurity
best practices and the importance of securing RFTIDs and associated systems.
Incident Response Planning: Develop and regularly update an incident response plan for RFTID-
related security incidents, conducting tabletop exercises to test its effectiveness. (NRC 10 CFR Part
73; 73.77 (a)(1), (2))
Continuous Monitoring and Improvement: Establish a continuous monitoring program to assess the
effectiveness of security controls and make improvements as necessary. Stay informed about
emerging threats and vulnerabilities related to RFTIDs and adjust security measures accordingly.
They may also utilize automated support tools to facilitate near real-time cybersecurity
management for TIDs. (NRC RG 5.71; A.4.1)

3. TEST REPORT SUMMARY

3.1. OVERVIEW
This section delineates the evaluation of RFTIDs, carried out to replicate operations in a range of
environmental conditions for A/SMRs. It describes the testing parameters and summarizes the
results obtained at both SRNL and SNL, employing RMSAs and TSMs as available surrogates to
represent RFTIDs in a broader context, again emphasizing that this project does not aim to endorse
any specific RFTID; rather, it aims to illustrate RFTIDs as a crucial element within a comprehensive
detection system to bolster nuclear security objectives, facilitated by remote monitoring
capabilities. Detailed outcomes are documented separately in the A.1.3 RFTID Test Report.

It is imperative to highlight that these tests are designed to evaluate RMSA’s sufficiency for
nuclear security and not safeguards, and that none of the findings invalidate their usage in
safeguards, which have different requirements. For example, the lack of SoH is important in
safeguards regimes, where the lack of a message over some period is sufficient for detection, while
for nuclear security, the lack of a single message should initiate some level of response.
SRNL and SNL completed the following tests:
  (SNL/SRNL) RMSA “Open-Alarm” Testing in 4 environments.
 o The purpose of this testing was to assess the “timely detection” capability of the RMSA
 in standard, indoor conditions (e.g., room temperature, minimal humidity, etc.),
 warehouse conditions (higher temperature and humidity), hot conditions (e.g., 55-65 C
 simulating hot climates and reactor compartment), and cold conditions (e.g., -10 C
 simulating cold climates).
 o It was observed that across 120 trials in the four environments, the open alarm was
 received by the review station within one to two seconds for every test.
  (SNL) RMSA Physical Intrusion Testing
 o The purpose of this test was to determine if physical penetration of the RMSA casing
 will generate an alarm, either through tamper-indicating switches or vibrational effects
 on the electronic components. Of note, the types of tampering that could occur once an
 adversary has access to the inner electronics is not within the scope of this paper.
  (SNL) RMSA Water Intrusion and Submersion Test
 o The purpose of this test is to simulate the actuation of a fire suppression system (e.g.,
 sprinkler system), or a flash flood condition, and assess the impact of gradual water
 intrusion or complete submersion on the performance of the RFTID (if an alarm is
 generated solely based on short circuiting, or other electronic impacts due to contact
 with water).
  (SRNL-SNL) TSM “Hot-Open Alarm” and Communication from SRNL to SNL Testing
 o SRNL subjected a TSM to a high-heat environment, and in coordination with SNL,
 assessed the overall time for an open event to be communicated to the alarm interface
 at SNL. Once again, the goal was to determine if high-heat exposure will degrade the
 performance of the RFTID (in this case, a TSM).
 o The review station at SNL received an open alarm within an average of 20 seconds
 across ten tests with no correlation between temperature and time to receipt.
  (SRNL-SNL) TSM “Interference-Open Alarm” and Communication from SRNL to SNL Testing
 o The purpose of this testing was to determine if radiofrequency interference, either
 incidentally or malicious, would disrupt communications between the TSM and the
 coordinator (equivalent to the translator for the RMSA), as well as determine the time
 for the review station to receive indication of the interference.

3.1.1. RMSA and TSM Operation
The RMSA is an active fiber-optic loop seal that stores, forwards, and communicates data (tamper
events, case and fiber open/close events, seal status) via a customized data communication
protocol over a no-license, low power RF link. The data can be stored and transferred remotely and
securely (authenticated and encrypted) to the RMSA Translator that collects data from all the RMSA

 20

seals in a particular location. (Note that translator and coordinator are used interchangeably
throughout external references.) The data is made available on the RMSA Translator in an easily
accessible format either for data retrieval by an authorized inspector, typically with an SD Card, or
for remote data communication via Ethernet or VPN link. The data collected by the RMSA seals can
then be verified and analyzed on-site or worldwide with the RMSA Review Software. It is designed
to be part of a large array of seals. As shown Figure 1 below, the system is comprised of the seal, a
translator and a data review station. Hundreds of seals can be connected simultaneously with a
single translator. SoH data are transmitted periodically, and security significant events are
transmitted at the time of the event. The review station provides decryption, data authentication,
data storage and data review capabilities. The seal was developed by SNL and is based on the SSP,
which is a standardized architecture for power, communications, and security. The RMSA uses AES
symmetric key encryption and message authentication code (MAC) symmetric key authentication.

3.1.2. RMSA Open Alarm Testing
For the SNL portion, the components of the test setup were placed within an office environment,
where a constant temperature and humidity were maintained. The temperature reading of each
RMSA was held between 20 to 21 °C (~68 to 70 °F). The RMSAs were collocated with the
coordinator, which receives RF communications from the RFTIDs. These specific RMSAs were set to
an automatic SoH check on a 15-minute interval. As previously mentioned, this can be modified
and would likely need to be modified for nuclear security purposes. The coordinator had a corded
connection with a laptop, which was running RMSA review software that displayed the RF
communications received by the coordinator, with setup similar to Figure 1.
To simulate an “open event,” one of the two fiber optic cable leads was disconnected from the
RMSA. Each disconnection led to the generation of an alarm code that said “Seal_Open.” The test
was conducted by removing one lead and simultaneously starting a stopwatch. The time was
stopped on the watch once an alarm was generated. A total of 30 tests were performed on the
RMSA technology, which spanned 4 different individual seals. The purpose of including additional
seals within the 30 trials was to verify that seal performance was, in general, consistent across the
seals and not significantly different from seal to seal.
The conclusion of the testing showed that the RMSAs correctly alarmed each time the seal fiber
was opened. The only variance was the time to alarm. The distribution of alarm times for the 4
seals was 1.05, 0.94, 1.03, and 1.04 seconds, respectively. In summary, the average alarm time
was 1.02 seconds.
For the SRNL portion, The RMSA was tested at SRNL for a series of 3 tests at varying temperatures
– a “room temperature” test, “hot” test, and a “cold” test – to provide three typical expected
operating environments. It is expected that A/SMRs will operate in a wide variety of environments –
from Arctic, cold regions to hot and humid locales. As the RMSA operates on a local network, the
computer was set up with the NGMS program and opened in accordance with the operating
instructions. The maximum ambient temperature was approximately 90°F and the humidity was
approximately 54%. The first test was the room temperature. The environment was the package
test facility, a warehouse-like environment with insulation. The RMSA was placed inside a large
environmental chamber that contains a single window and simulates a very small-scale reactor
compartment (Figure 4 below) on a containment vessel approximately 50 feet from the coordinator
and review station. The temperature reading of each RMSA was held constant between 30 to 31 °C
(~86 to 88 °F). To simulate an “open event,” one of the two fiber leads was disconnected from the
RMSA, and a stopwatch was simultaneously started. The time was stopped on the watch once an
alarm was generated. Each disconnection led to the generation of an alarm code that said
“Seal_Open.” The time was recorded from the disconnection to the receipt of the “Seal_Open” at
the review station. A total of 30 tests were performed on the RMSA, with an average time of 1.27
seconds.

 23

 Figure 04. RMSA setup in environmental chamber

This was followed by the hot testing on a single RMSA. The RMSA was placed in the environmental
chamber, which was heated to 65 °C (150 °F) and allowed to heat up for 30 minutes. The RMSA
was then “opened” in the same format for a total of 30 tests. Once again, the time for the review
station to receive the message was recorded, with an average time of 1.36 seconds.

The RMSA testing was concluded with the cold testing. The RMSA was placed in a chill box at
approximately -18°C (0°F) approximately 125 feet from the coordinator in a separate room and
allowed to cool for 30 minutes. The RMSA was then retrieved from the chill box, immediately
“opened” in the same format for a series of 6 to 8 tests, then allowed to cool down more and
repeated for a total of 30 tests. Once again, the time for the review station to receive the message
was recorded, with an average alarm time of 1.53 seconds.

In this case, the review station received the SoH messages every 5 minutes per settings in all
locations. The conclusion of the testing showed that the RMSAs correctly alarmed each time the
seal was opened. The only variance was the time to alarm. In summary, the average alarm time was
1.39 seconds for all 90 tests, with every alarm under 2.5 seconds, proving that environmental
constraints within the operating window of the RMSA have no effect on time to alarm.

3.1.3. RMSA Water Intrusion and Submersion Test
The RMSA was placed in the bottom of a 5-gallon bucket to enable the conduct of the water
intrusion and submersion test. The initial phase of the test is the water intrusion component, which
is meant to represent an event in which a fire suppression system is actuated to extinguish a fire.
Consequently, the deployed water would impact the RMSA. According to (Ret.) Deputy Chief James
Smith, of the Philadelphia Fire Department, as written in Firehouse Magazine, the requisite water
flowrate, to effectively combat a fire covering a given square footage, can be found by dividing the
square footage by a factor of 3. The square footage of a standard 5-gallon bucket was calculated to
be 0.77 𝑓𝑡 , which corresponds to around 0.25 gallons/min. A 1-gallon milk jug was modified,
through the creation of holes in the bottom of the jug, such that it leaked at a flow rate of around

 24

0.25 gallons/minute (Figure 5). The RMSA was in contact with water, until it was eventually
submerged, around 3 minutes after the water began pouring into the bucket.

 Figure 05. Water intrusion and submersion test

Following the initial submersion, a SoH query was sent to the RMSA, upon which it indicated it was
in a good state. Following this query, a fiber optic lead was disconnected from the RMSA, upon
which an alarm was immediately generated (~1-2 seconds). More water was added to the bucket
such that the water level was 3, 4, and 11 inches respectfully. Recall that the submersion of the
RMSA occurred once the water level reached 1.625 inches. At each of these water levels, the SoH
queries were promptly answered, and open-alarms were generated upon the removal of the fiber
optic lead.
In reality, the application of such an RFTID would likely occur on top of containers, attached to
doors, etc. This means that the testing conditions will likely exceed any amount of water
accumulation, surrounding the RFTID, that would occur as a result of fire suppression or flooding.
However, as a caveat, note that the number of trials performed are far below the requisite number
(30) to generate acceptable probabilities of sensing and confidence levels. The test performed
shows no indication that water intrusion and submersion would immediately cause an impact to
RFTID performance. Additional tests, to meet performance evaluation thresholds, may be
warranted.

3.1.5. SRNL to SNL TSM RF Interference Testing
With the aim of determining the possible interference of the TSM system to potential intentional or
unintentional in-band emitters, SRNL performed testing in an operationally relevant environment at
an open-area test site using realistic distances ranging from two to 10 meters between devices.
The testing followed general best practices based on the team’s history with electromagnetic
compatibility (EMC) testing, radio communication testing, and waveform development backgrounds.
An ad-hoc procedure was developed vice utilizing MILSTD 461 and relevant commercial guidance,
since the project goals did not appear to justify that level of formality.
The TSM system consists of one or more TIDs that transmit sensor-related data to a coordinator
node that receives the local TID transmissions and relays the information either via satellite or the
Pendant, which is directly connected to the coordinator and provides a visual indication of TID
communication status. The specific objective of this test was to determine the power levels at
which RF energy interrupted the functions of the coordinator node. The scope did not involve
testing the RF susceptibility of the TIDs since they are transmit-only and do not receive signals.
The possible RF interference of the coordinator (or DUT) was assessed based on its ability to
successfully receive messages from the TIDs at different frequencies and power levels. The
interference source consisted of a software-defined radio (SDR) attached to a transmitting antenna
(called TX antenna). The TX antenna and TSM 1 were positioned in a manner that allowed them to
both be in the main beam of the coordinator without blocking each other. TSM 1 was placed 10
meters from the DUT, which was assessed to be the maximum typical operational distance. The TX
antenna was placed roughly 30 degrees off the line of sight from TSM 1 located 2 meters from the
DUT. A second “control” TID (TSM 2) was placed 2 meters from the DUT, outside of the beamwidth
of the TX antenna to provide a baseline for comparison. The setup is shown in Figure 8.
The tests were performed in the following order:
1. Functional verification of test articles using spectrum analyzer captures.
2. Cabled calibration of power measurements for narrowband 902/915/928 MHz and
wideband 902-928 MHz interference transmission signals.
3. Over-the-air calibration of power measurements for narrowband 902/915/928 MHz and
wideband 902-928 MHz interference transmission signals.
4. Narrowband interference tests, 902/915/928 MHz at transmitted power levels 0-20 dBm
at the TX antenna.
5. Wideband interference tests, 902-928 MHz at power levels 0-20 dBm at the Tx antenna.

3.1.6. Testing Conclusions
Overall, the testing offers compelling evidence that RFTIDs can serve as a legitimate element in the
remote monitoring of nuclear security for A/SMRs. It effectively showcases RFTIDs as an integral
part of a broader detection system that supports nuclear security objectives while enabling remote
monitoring capabilities.
Specifically:
  The RMSA “Open-Alarm” testing revealed that, across four different operating environments
 ranging from -10°C to 65°C and minimal humidity to approximately 60% humidity, there
 was no effect on time to alarm. Across 120 trials, the open alarm was received by the
 review station within one to two seconds for every test.
  The RMSA Physical Intrusion Testing highlighted the importance of RFTIDs utilized for
 remotely monitored nuclear security to generate an alarm if there is a physical penetration
 of the RFTID casing. This could be accomplished by either tamper-indicating switches,
 vibrational effects on the electronic components, or some other TIE. Again, the types of
 tampering that could occur once an adversary has access to the inner electronics is not
 within the scope of this paper.
  The RMSA Water Intrusion and Submersion Test revealed that neither water intrusion nor
 submersion of the RMSA prevented alarm generation when the RMSA was opened.
  The TSM “Hot-Open Alarm” and Communication from SRNL to SNL Testing again showed no
 correlation between high temperature and time to alarm receipt at the review station. In
 this case, the RFTID sends a message via satellite to the review station, which is more
 realistic of application for A/SMRs. The average time to alarm was within 20 seconds,

 providing ample time for a security response, and bounding the tests conducted locally with
 the RMSAs.
Each of these tests provides insight into the potential applications and also revealed improvements
for full and effective implementation into an A/SMR remotely monitored PPS.

4. APPLICATIONS OF RFTIDS
TIDs signal if access to an item has been attempted or achieved, and they can be either passive or
active, such as RFTIDs mentioned throughout this report. RFTIDs improve security by reporting
unauthorized access attempts to a central system, thus providing continuous surveillance, and are
especially useful in hazardous or inaccessible areas and decrease the need for in-person
verification. They are particularly beneficial for securing nuclear material containers, reactor
vessels, doors to reactor compartments, and critical asset cabinets in A/SMRs. TIDs are not meant
to prevent access, as they are not locks, or other forms of obstruction. Locks can be removed or
defeated with a key, code, or other kinds of tools. This alludes to the idea that TIDs are not meant
to be removable without damage, otherwise a TID could be removed and replaced during the
commission of a malicious act. However, it is important to clearly distinguish between passive and
active TIDs.
To reiterate, passive TIDs are simple, physical mechanisms that are meant to be applied and
observed. Once removed, a passive TID is discarded, as it is no longer useable. Active TIDs, such
as RFTIDS, are physical mechanisms that have communication capabilities. This means that active
TIDs, if implemented effectively, promote CoK by not only indicating that unauthorized access has
been attempted, but by communicating that fact to a centralized location, such as a CAS, that
enables a response to be initiated. Therefore, active TIDs inherently include the surveillance
component that makes a TID program effective.
The application of RFTIDs is as broad as that of passive TIDs, with the exception that in some cases
RFTIDs are more advantageous, or in others where they are not as feasible. In general, TIDs can be
applied to any type of access point, such as doorways, lids, drawers, containers, etc. that contain
critical assets or objects that could be sabotaged or utilized by an adversary. Examples of these
assets and objects would include nuclear material; measurement equipment; internal electronics
of computing systems and large facility equipment (e.g., cranes); and hand tools.
As previously inferred, the timely detection capability of passive TIDs is derived from surveillance
(either by means of visual observation by people, tactile examination, and for some passive TIDs,
the use of in-situ readers, as well as through the use of cameras). If using cameras, the resolution
would have to be such that indications of tamper could be discernable, and in any case, some
frequency of in-person verifications would be warranted. Therefore, the application of passive TIDs
is limited by the safe and accessible access of people to conduct in-person verification of presence
and integrity. The benefit of RFTIDs is that, due to the communications capability, they can be
placed on access points that are inaccessible to people, exist in environments that are hazardous
to human health, or remote locations with minimal or infrequent on-site presence.
Anecdotally, the use of RFTIDs in areas with a background radiation that is elevated above normal,
outside conditions is well tested. In these circumstances, the RFTIDs have performed well.
However, if the desired use is in locations with exceptionally high background radiation, further

 29

testing would be warranted to determine the survivability of the RFTIDs, specifically its internal
electronics.
In addition to enabling continuous monitoring of the integrity of whichever access point is secured
with an RFTID, the widespread use of these components decreases the required effort for
maintaining CoK. With passive TIDs, the CoK is contingent on the conduct of surveillance, which
could require the deployment of personnel to physically locate the TID and verify its presence and
integrity. With active TIDs, the surveillance information is routed to a central location and
configured to generate an alarm when communication is disrupted, or the RFTID is damaged or
opened. This means that aside from periodic audits, the verification of RFTID integrity is assumed
intact on the basis that any impacts to component integrity would be immediately known to the
personnel responsible for monitoring alarms. Additionally, in a remotely monitored nuclear security
environment, passive TIDs historically used for nuclear security applications (e.g. nuclear material
containers) would be unable to accomplish their standard objectives effectively.

4.1. POSSIBLE A/SMR APPLICATIONS OF RFTIDS
The general applications of RFTIDs feed into specific A/SMR related applications of RFTIDs to
enhance the remote monitoring of their security, one of the key objectives of these tests and
evaluation. While there are many intrusion detection devices currently, they have specific
applications and cannot be used on material or containers. Additionally, because of the expected
limited staff present at A/SMRs, periodic in-person verification may not be feasible as typical with
traditional nuclear power and nuclear material sites. Based on the evaluation herein, as well as the
purpose of RFTIDs, the following applications are envisioned:
  Nuclear material containers at the A/SMR – Traditionally sealed with passive TID and
 integrity verified periodically and enhanced with in-person security. However, with remote
 monitoring, RFTIDs provide a better, more flexible and quick response solution for nuclear
 security.
  The reactor vessel – Again, historically sealed with passive TIDs and CoK maintained with
 periodic in-person verification. This would provide an excellent application for RFTIDs as an
 area that would expect little to no in-person verification but is a key asset of the entire
 system.
  Doors into the reactor compartment – Typically uses a Balanced Magnetic Switch (BMS) for
 intrusion detection. A BMS is a magnetically operated switch using a balanced magnetic
 field, designed to detect and initiate an alarm upon the opening of a secured door, window,
 or other point of entry, which primary purpose is to allow detection of attempted or actual
 unauthorized access. This could be augmented with RFTIDs to provide defense in depth.
  Cabinets or drawers containing critical assets – Again, BMS can be augmented or replaced
 with RFTIDs, depending on the specific asset.

5. IMPROVEMENTS REMAINING FOR FULL IMPLEMENTATION IN
 REMOTELY MONITORED A/SMRS
The purpose of this test and evaluation of an RFTID, particularly ones utilized for safeguards
applications, was to assess its applicability for nuclear security in remote A/SMRs. This section
highlights the potential improvements identified during the course of the testing. It also presents
several scenarios of probable lines of attacks. Specifically, it considers immediate, or direct, attack
or a “lone attack” with a single adversary. Most notably, the most pressing improvements that this
work identified facilitate full implementation into an A/SMR nuclear security PPS are:
  Refine RFTID TIEs, either with enhanced material, vibration sensors, fiber optic or
 conductive meshes. A vibration sensor in the RFTID could also be coupled to the
 containment of a nuclear material container itself.
  Ensure augmentation of RFTIDs with a water/flooding sensor.
  Integration of alarm signals into the alarm communication and display system that would
 be present in the central alarm station CAS.
This project does not intend to endorse any particular RFTID, rather it intends to demonstrate
RFTIDs as a valuable component within a greater detection system to support nuclear security
objectives, with remote monitoring capabilities; the RMSA and TSM were chosen because they were
the most accessible to the project contributors and had systems in place at both SNL and SRNL.
The RMSA is designed as a TID suited for application to international nuclear safeguards scenarios.
As such, it securely records, stores, and eventually transmits SoH and alerts (open/close fiber optic
cable, case open/tamper) to a nearby transceiver and on to a review station. The RMSA system is
not designed nor configured to prioritize real-time alerts, and thus there are several situations
where alerts may be delayed. In safeguards, this delay is not significant, as safeguards
inspectorates only need to know that a tamper happened within a defined window based on the
timeliness goal of a facility; in this case, an “RMSA not heard” message is sent if the RMSA does
not send a SoH during a predefined interval. Conversely, security applications require immediate
alerts.
In these circumstances, adversaries having gained access to critical assets, protected with an
RFTID, were envisioned. In particular, the goal of the adversary is to undertake unauthorized
removal clandestinely. It could be that external adversaries circumvented all other physical
protection measures and encountered the RFTID (for our purposes, an RMSA or TSM was utilized as
a representative for RFTIDs at large). The other scenario, which is more likely, is the attempted
defeat of the RFTID by an insider adversary with authorized access to the critical asset.
An insider adversary would: undertake an immediate, and brief attack on the RFTID (during a two-
person rule activity); defeat access control restrictions to enable lone access to the critical asset;
or gradually impact the integrity of the RFTID over a longer period of time.
The tests that were conducted involved assessing the timely detection of the RFTIDs, in terms of
how quickly they resulted in an alarm generation when tampered, and the performance of the
RFTID when subjected to physical penetration and water intrusion. These tests correlate to a
couple of the adversary attack methods, postulated above, whereas the other impacts can be
inferred (and possibly be the subject of future analysis).

5.1. GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS
Assessment of the location(s) where RFTIDs may be implemented should consider environmental
factors and how existing infrastructure could impact signal strength (e.g., reinforced concrete) or
interfere with other equipment utilizing radio waves (or adversarial interference). Even when
communications are confirmed to be constant, contingencies need to be built into the configuration
of the system such that a loss of communication results in an immediate alarm. The reason for this
is because communication coupled with the TID is what provides CoK. Improperly applied RFTIDs or
a disruption in communication compromises the CoK. A message may need to be sent to indicate
changing environmental conditions. Through testing, the RMSA appears resilient to extreme
temperatures, but it might be beneficial to incorporate more accurate detection of conditions – for
example, the RFTID might send an alert if the temperature begins to change dramatically or outside
normal ranges (SoH will send temperature, but on predefined intervals and the change in
conditions will need to be parsed out of SoH rather than as an alert). A flood sensor may also need
to be incorporated such that if humidity changes rapidly or if the TID detects water in the case, an
alert is sent immediately.
The following are differences between RFTIDs used for security and safeguards:
  Alerts need to be sent immediately. Since there are activities that render the RMSA unable
 to send an “alert” message, “RMSA not heard” may need to be sent more quickly and be
 qualified as a higher tier alarm. This might be configured within the SoH interval or
 inclusion of a separate emergency battery with the ability to power a rapid alert message
 for specific scenarios.
  RMSA can detect opening of its case through two oppositely configured tamper switches.
 There are other RFTIDs that employ active tamper-indication, and that is suggested for
 RFTIDs in security applications. Fiber optics can be embedded within the plastic case walls,
 connected to the electronics, and used to send an alert if the case is penetrated. Other
 methods are conductive meshes/fabrics/wires used in a similar fashion. Active tamper-
 indication of the case should be relatively straight forward to implement.
The modification of the RFTID to include longer lengths of fiber optic cable, weight sensors, and
global positioning system (GPS) trackers or inertial measurement units would require an
assessment of the increased battery consumption that results. Additionally, integration would need
to be carefully accomplished so as not to jeopardize the overall performance of the RFTID with the
addition of these new features.
Additionally, the project recognized the following potential future work that would address follow-on
questions and paths forward to fully clear RFTIDs for remote deployment application and
incorporated into a PPS. Additional analysis and testing would include:
  Comparison of similar cyber testing and analysis of cybersecurity recommendations for
 A/SMRs to bound the transmission of data from a coordinator station to a review station.
  Long term testing (approximately one month) of RFTIDs at higher radiation exposure to
 ensure no effect on electronic internals, loss in efficiency, or timeliness to alarm.
  Integration of alarm signals into the alarm communication and display system that would
 be present in the CAS.

5.2. IMMEDIATE ATTACK
The immediate attack scenario would occur through the disconnection or damage of the fiber optic
leads that loop through the hasp or hole of the access point. When conducting “timely detection”
assessments on the RFTIDs, alarm generation occurred within 1-2 seconds of the disconnection.
This is sufficiently short enough that an insider adversary would likely not have time to even begin
the process of entering the access point before a response is initiated.

5.2.1. Immediate Attack – Potential Improvements
However, as an additional measure, the implementation of a specialized lock key (requiring a
specific tool) would be useful to prevent an insider adversary from disconnecting the fiber optic
lead in an attempt to create an intentional alarm and response, possibly for the purposes of
diverting attention from another malicious act. The special tool would only be attainable during
operations that required the application and removal of RFTIDs. This, however, does not prevent
the intentional breaking of the fiber optic lead by other means. Therefore, in addition to protecting
against the unauthorized removal of a lead, metallic conduit could also be used to shroud the fiber
optic leads to protect it from cutting. This addition would complement existing contraband
detection, as metallic scissors may be identified but ceramic (or other non-metallic tools) would
likely defeat these detection methods.

5.3. LONE ATTACK
The lone attack scenario could occur in one of two ways – attacking the RFTID or removing the
containment. The attack on the RFTID would likely occur through the penetration of the RFTID
casing in an attempt to gain access to the internal electronics. In this case of removing the
containment, the critical asset is most likely nuclear material in a storage container, as
containment in general could refer to a vault door and hardened walls. In this scenario, the
concern is that an insider adversary, defeating various controls, is able to physically remove a
container of nuclear material from the storage facility, or relocate the container to a location that
is advantageous for a future abrupt theft. This type of attack, in which the container is removed
from the facility, would require sophisticated planning and complex creativity. The relocation of
containers to an advantageous location for abrupt theft is less complicated but would still require
the defeat of different measures (e.g., two-person rule, etc.). Again, this scenario is reliant on the
failure of upstream control measures.

5.3.1. Lone Attack – Potential Improvements
 Lone Attack – On RFTID
Potential improvements to mitigate attacks on the RFTID include the configuration of the RFTID and
the coordinator and alarm dashboard to alarm (visibly) when communications are disrupted. In
situations where large numbers of RFTIDs are deployed, it would be very difficult to identify the
RFTID that is not providing updates without some sort of highlighted alarm or notification. Current
RFTID software does allow the operator to set up notifications, e.g., some change the color to red,
or some applications send a pop-up message the operator must acknowledge depending on the tier
of the condition.

 33

Additionally, the application of a shield to protect the batteries from removal would compel the
adversary to attack the circuitry, which is a more complex attack pathway. The battery shield would
be made of a material that has differing properties than that of the RFTID face plate, such that the
tool used to penetrate the base plate is less effective on the battery shield.
The routing of the fiber optic leads or a conductive mesh throughout the casing would also create a
TIE, since the severing of the lead or mesh would result in the generation of an alarm. Therefore,
any physical penetration into the RFTID, not through the access point, would result in an alarm
generation. Alternatively, a sensitive vibration sensor could also provide an effective tamper
indication.

 Lone Attack – Removing Containment
As previously mentioned, the act of removing the containment (which, in this case, is a nuclear
material container) is likely challenging due to the range of PPS elements that would need to be
defeated for this to go undetected. However, an additional modification of the RFTID would provide
additional defense in depth and transform the component into one with multiple applications for
mitigating unauthorized removal.
In this case, the application of a GPS tracker, if located outside, or an inertial measurement unit, if
located inside, within the RFTID would provide another data point against which an alarm can be
generated. If the container is removed, with the RFTID intact, the location change of the RFTID
would indicate that an unauthorized removal or relocation of the container is being attempted. The
addition of the GPS tracker or inertial measurement unit would need to be integrated into the
communication system of the preexisting RFTID, and battery consumption would need to be
assessed. Several RFTIDs, such as the TSM, already provide a GPS link that could be modified
depending on the needs of the facility and can alarm once outside the authorized area or indicate
out of the authorized area in the SoH message.

6. CONCLUSION
Overall, the project provides valuable proof of concept that RFTIDs can provide a valid component
to remotely monitored nuclear security of A/SMRs. It effectively demonstrates RFTIDs as a valuable
element within a greater PPS to support nuclear security objectives, with remote monitoring
capabilities. Utilizing both a series of TID reference material and the testing results, the report
provided a concise list of recommended guidance for implementation of RFTIDs. It also identified
key applications in the nuclear security realm of A/SMRs, as well as specific improvements that will
need to be addressed to allow full implementation into an A/SMR PPS. However, with adequate
follow-on testing, design, and early incorporation into the PPS development of A/SMRs, RFTIDs can
play both a key role in specific applications and augment other applications for defense-in-depth,
ensuring adequate security of remotely monitored A/SMRs.
