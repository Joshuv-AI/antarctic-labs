Occurrence of hepatitis A virus in Philippine cupped oysters (Magallana bilineata) and green mussels (Perna viridis) collected in the Philippines
Authors: Abigail S Rustia, Leslie Michelle M Dalmacio, Erniel B Barrios, Patricia Mae M Lagazon, John Carlo G Gosilatar, Dennis D Tanay, Anne Marie M Matubis-Binuya, Christrhea N Udang, Minerva M Manaog, Roel Anthony S Bayangos, Denise Anne B Dela Cruz, Crisanto M Lopez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesHepatitis A virus (HAV) causes acute hepatitis through ingestion of contaminated food and water, with bivalve molluscs as common vehicles of transmission. As filter feeders, HAV concentrates in their digestive tissues when grown in contaminated waters. In 2020, the Philippines produced nearly 75 MT of shellfish. However, maintaining proper sanitation in growing areas remains challenging, and data on the occurrence of HAV in these commercially significant shellfish is lacking. This study aimed to detect HAV in Philippine cupped oysters (Magallana bilineata), green mussels (Perna viridis), and seawater from their growing areas. Samples were collected in Pangasinan (August 2022), Cavite (April 2023), and Capiz (June 2023), and HAV detection was conducted using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR).ResultsHAV RNA was not detected in the shellfish and seawater samples, consistent with zero reported cases of Hepatitis A during the collection periods. However, a disparity was noted between these findings and potential sewage run-off linked to net positive rainfall. The Department of Health reported 111 and 127 Hepatitis A cases nationwide in 2022 and 2023, respectively, indicating that HAV remains endemic. Identifying sources, transmission modes, and environmental factors is crucial for integrating HAV risks into the food safety risk assessment framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13104-025-07481-z.Keywords: Hepatitis a virus, HAV, Green mussels, Perna viridis, Oyster, Magallana bilineata, Philippines

Introduction

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) is a non-enveloped positive-stranded RNA virus from the genus Hepatovirus in the Picornaviridae family. It causes acute hepatitis, with symptoms including jaundice, dark urine, abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, malaise, diarrhea, poor appetite, and fever. Transmission occurs via the fecal-oral route through contaminated food and water or person-to-person contact [1].

A 2024 report on HAV seroprevalence in Metro Manila, Philippines, found an overall seropositivity rate of 62.3%, with anti-HAV positivity increasing with age [2]. Another study on the overall seropositivity in the Philippines suggested that the country had low HAV endemicity, which was in line with the trend observed in other countries [3]. Given the possible risks associated with HAV, it is imperative to analyze its occurrence in highly-consumed commodities such as bivalve molluscs (e.g. Philippine cupped oysters and green mussels).

In 2020, the Philippines produced nearly 75 MT of oysters and mussels, with top-producing regions including Aklan, Capiz, Iloilo, Negros Occidental, Bulacan, Cavite, Samar, Leyte, Biliran, Pangasinan, La Union, Ilocos Norte, and Ilocos Sur. The National Shellfish Sanitation Program (NSSP) was established to protect consumer health, facilitate trade, and maintain the country’s reputation for safe seafood, while providing guidance on microbiological and marine biotoxin monitoring for food safety [4].

Quality assessments in other countries have detected HAV in bivalve molluscs using molecular techniques [5–8]. However, there is limited published data on virus occurrence in Philippine shellfish, despite the existence of NSSP. A recent study noted pathogens in the microbiological quality of bivalve molluscs along the market supply, but it did not include viruses [9].

In 2018, a town in Iloilo was declared under a state of emergency due to rising Hepatitis A cases in a particular high school with the identified culprit being HAV-positive food handlers. An old Hepatitis A outbreak was reported where raw oysters were identified as the source of the virus [10]. Aside from seroprevalence studies, HAV was detected in Philippine waters. HAV subgenotype IA and IIIA were present in water samples collected from rivers in Metro Manila [11]. Moreover, HAV was also detected in scallops exported from the Philippines to Hawaii [12].

These cases warrant for HAV surveillance in foods and water to protect public health. This is aligned with a recent study about monitoring of HAV in local shellfish products and their culturing sites as one of the crucial gaps in addressing food safety [13]. Thus, this study aimed to establish baseline information on the occurrence of HAV in local oysters and mussels.

Materials and methods

Sampling and sample preparation

Philippine cupped oysters (Magallana bilineata) and green mussels (Perna viridis) were collected from the three shellfish-producing provinces in the Philippines, namely, Capiz, Cavite, and Pangasinan. Three coastal bivalve commercial cultivation sites were identified in each province, and at least three sampling sites per province were determined for biological replication. In the same manner, seawater from where oysters and green mussels were being cultured was collected. Sampling was done from August 2022 to June 2023. Collected samples were maintained in the cold chain (≤ 4 °C) during transportation to the laboratory for analysis. Samples were immediately processed or stored at -20 °C until processing (Table 1).

The bivalves were rinsed, shucked, and their digestive glands dissected out. The tissues of corresponding shellfish from each sampling site were pooled and chopped manually with a sterile scalpel to paste-like consistency. Approximately 0.5 g of digestive tissue was added with 4 µL of internal extraction control RNA, provided in the TaqMan®-based Hepatitis A Virus 5’ NCR genesig® Advanced Kit (Primerdesign™ Ltd.). Then, 0.75 mL proteinase K solution was added to the mixture for pre-treatment [7]. The tissue samples were digested for one (1) hour at 37 °C and 15 min at 60 °C. After digestion, suspensions were centrifuged at 800 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. Homogenate supernatants were collected for RNA extraction. Seawater samples were treated with proteinase K solution as described above but with a 500 µL-sample. Proteinase K is a serine protease that breaks down proteins by hydrolyzing peptide bonds [14]. It is a broad-spectrum protease that can digest various proteins, including those resistant to other proteases [15].

Rainfall data collection

The rainfall data in sampling sites during the month prior to and in the month of sampling were requested from the Department of Science and Technology-Philippine Atmospheric, Geophysical, and Astronomical Services Administration (DOST PAG-ASA).

RNA extraction

RNAs from homogenate supernatants or seawater were extracted using the MagMAX Viral/Pathogen Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, 400 µL of sample was added with 550 µL binding bead mix. Samples were allowed to stay on the magnetic rack for 10 min or until all the beads had been collected. Supernatants were then collected and discarded. The magnetic beads were added with 1.0 mL wash buffer and vortexed for 1 min. The suspension was again placed on the magnetic rack until all the beads had collected. Supernatants were then discarded. The magnetic beads were washed with 80% ethanol twice. Finally, RNAs were obtained using 50 µL of elution buffer.

Detection and quantification of HAV

The detection of HAV was done using the OneStep RT-qPCR detection protocol, which uses the oasig One Step 2X RT-qPCR Master Mix and the TaqMan®-based Hepatitis A Virus 5’ NCR genesig® Advanced Kit (Primerdesign™ Ltd.), which can detect less than 100 copies of the target template. A reaction mix was prepared for every RNA sample according to the manual from the manufacturer. The reaction mix consisted of a 10 µL master mix, 1 µL HAV primer/probe mix, 1 µL internal extraction control primer/probe mix, and 3 µL nuclease-free water. The 15-µL reaction mix was added with 5 µL of RNA template to achieve a final volume of 20 µL. The amplification consisted of the following protocol: reverse transcription (10 min, 55 °C), enzyme activation (2 min, 95 °C), denaturation (10 s, 95 °C), and fluorogenic data collection using FAM channel (60 s, 60 °C). The denaturation and data collection were done in 50 cycles. A positive control template (PCT) included in the kit was used for positive control. A no-template control (NCT) was included by adding nuclease-free water instead of sample RNA.

Quantitative analysis was done using six standards (from 2 × 105 copy numbers/µL to 2 copy numbers/µL) prepared by 10-fold dilution of the positive control provided in the kit. RT-PCR assays were done on the CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-rad). Data collection and analysis were done using the CFX Manager™ software v3.0.

Collection of reported HAV cases

To determine if there were any reported cases of Hepatitis A in the regions during the sample collection months, data from the Department of Health Epidemic-prone Disease Case Surveillance (EDCS) weekly report for Hepatitis A was obtained.

Based on the obtained data, Pangasinan received the highest amount of rainfall, followed by Capiz and Cavite. The occurrence of HAV in the environment could be due to run-off from sewage systems. Run-off occurs during rain events [16]. However, HAV RNA was not detected in any of the collected oyster, mussel, and seawater samples (Supplementary Fig. 1).

No Hepatitis A cases were reported in August 2022 in Region I (Ilocos Norte), where Pangasinan is located. In addition, there were no reported cases of Hepatitis A in April and June 2023 in CALABARZON and Region VI (Western Visayas), where Cavite and Capiz are located, respectively.

In recent years, there have been no reported Hepatitis A outbreaks linked to the consumption of bivalve mollusks in the Philippines. The only documented outbreak associated with raw oysters occurred in 1983 [10]. However, the virus was not directly detected in the raw oysters, but the report was based on the positive reactivity of the sera of the patients to HAV antibody (anti-HAV) and anti-HAV IgM.

In this study, the virus was not detected in the seawater of the shellfish culturing sites. Previous studies have shown that the virus can be detected using real-time RT-PCR in seawater [17, 18]. However, several factors affect the detection of HAV in seawater, such as temperature, the presence of organic matter, and rain events [19].

Despite the zero reported cases of Hepatitis A in the regions of the collection sites in specific months, the Philippines still recorded 111 and 121 cases in 2022 [20] and 2023 [21], respectively. The reported cases came mostly from Regions VI, VII, IX, NCR, and VIII. This information indicates that the HAV is indeed endemic in the Philippines. However, information on the sources, exposure, and other factors that affect the prevalence of the virus in the Philippine population is still lacking. Since HAV is known to be transmitted via the fecal-oral route [1], it is worthwhile to conduct a food safety risk assessment and establish a mechanism that integrates risk analysis, risk management, and risk communication that, in turn, will support the Food Safety Act 2013 of the Philippines to make sure that the public health is protected, and trade of shellfish commodities is fair and sustainable.

Limitations

The study is limited to:

Samples were collected during specific months, potentially missing seasonal variations that could influence HAV contamination, such as increased rainfall or sewage run-off during monsoon periods.

Sampling was limited to the three regions (Pangasinan, Cavite, and Capiz) through targeted sampling. Though consulted with the statistical science expert, this may not fully capture HAV occurrence across all shellfish-growing areas in the Philippines.

Sampling was limited to the three regions (Pangasinan, Cavite, and Capiz) through targeted sampling. Though consulted with the statistical science expert, this may not fully capture HAV occurrence across all shellfish-growing areas in the Philippines.

Sample size of Philippine cupped oysters, green mussels, and seawater collected in each sampling site was limited from 6 to 12. While this may not accurately depict the status of HAV in the entire Philippines, the method has been validated by a statistical science expert.

Financial and logistical limitations restricted the number of samples and the frequency of testing, limiting the ability of the study to identify trends comprehensively. However, this study provided baseline information regarding the occurrence of HAV in bivalve molluscs in the Philippines.
