Predictors of Decompressive Craniectomy in Patients with Acute Subdural Hematoma:
A Retrospective Study
Ryogo IKEMURA1), Naohide FUJITA1), Yasuaki NAKAO1),
Takuji YAMAMOTO1), Akihide KONDO2)
1)Department of Neurosurgery, Juntendo University Shizuoka Hospital, Shizuoka, Japan
2)Department of Neurosurgery, Juntendo University School of Medicine, Tokyo, Japan

Purpose: Appropriate indications for decompressive craniectomy (DC) in patients with acute subdural hematoma (ASDH)
remain controversial. This study aimed to identify clinical factors associated with postoperative brain swelling after hematoma
evacuation and to explore predictors that may support surgical decision-making regarding the need for DC.
Materials and Methods: This retrospective study included 74 patients with ASDH who underwent hematoma evacuation via
craniotomy between 2017 and 2021. Patients were classified into swelling and non-swelling groups according to postoperative
clinical and radiological findings. Clinical, radiological, and treatment-related variables were compared using univariate and
multivariate analysis. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R²), corrected Akaike informa­
tion criterion (AICc), and C-index.
Results: Twenty-one patients (28.4%) were classified into the swelling group and 53 patients (71.6%) into the non-swelling
group. Univariate analysis demonstrated significant associations between postoperative brain swelling and younger age, severe
consciousness disturbance (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] 3-8), concomitant cerebral contusion, and shorter time from injury to
surgery. Multivariate logistic regression identified cerebral contusion as the only independent factor associated with brain
swelling (OR, 20.95; 95% confidence interval [CI], 3.81-206.51; P < 0.001). The predictive model showed an R² of 0.47 and a
C-index of 0.90, suggesting good discriminative ability.
Conclusions: Postoperative brain swelling following surgery for ASDH was strongly associated with severe traumatic brain
injury, particularly in patients with concomitant cerebral contusion. These findings may contribute to improved preoperative
risk stratification and support surgical decision-making when considering decompressive craniectomy in high-risk patients.
Key words: acute subdural hematoma, cerebral contusion, decompressive craniectomy, surgical indication, predictors

Introduction
Intracranial pressure (ICP) management using
various therapeutic strategies is a cornerstone of
treatment for patients with severe traumatic brain
injury (TBI). Intracranial hematoma and brain
swelling can cause brain herniation, leading to poor
neurological outcomes. When ICP cannot be adequ­
ately controlled with conservative therapy, surgical

intervention becomes an important treatment option.
The initial surgical management of acute subdural
hematoma (ASDH) involves evacuation of the
hematoma responsible for intracranial mass effect.
In clinical practice, decompressive craniectomy (DC),
often performed in addition to hematoma evacua­
tion, is an important surgical option for patients with
marked brain swelling or cerebral contusion1-4).
However, few high-quality clinical studies have

Evidence supporting DC has accumulated, inclu­

Guidelines for the Management of Severe TBI, 4th
Edition, recommend DC with a level IIA recom­
mendation9), and Japanese guidelines also support
its efficacy (recommendation; grade B)10). However,
strict indication criteria for DC have not been
established. In clinical practice, surgical indications
are often determined based not only on objective
indices such as level of consciousness and hema­
toma volume, but also on institution-specific criteria
and the neurosurgeon’s experience, including qual­
itative assessments of cerebral contusion severity
and concomitant cerebral ischemia4, 5).
The present study retrospectively evaluated
clinical factors associated with postoperative brain
swelling in patients who underwent conventional
craniotomy for ASDH, with the aim of identifying
clinical indicators that may support decision-making
regarding the appropriate use of decompressive
craniectomy in severe traumatic brain injury.
Materials and Methods
Patient population
This retrospective study included 74 patients
who underwent hematoma evacuation via crani­
otomy for ASDH at Juntendo University Shizuoka
Hospital between January 1, 2017, and December
31, 2021. Patients younger than 18 years, those with
ASDH at atypical locations, and those with coexisting
active diseases under treatment were excluded. As
this study was a retrospective observational study,
the sample size was determined by the number of
eligible patients during the study period, rather
than by a priori power calculation. All consecutive
eligible patients were included. The characteristics
of the overall cohort and those stratified by DC

conducted in accordance with the ethical principles
of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the
Juntendo University Ethics Committee (Approval
No. E24-0076-S01; approval date: May 27, 2024).
The requirement for informed consent was waived
because of the retrospective study design and the
use of anonymized data.
Surgical indications, procedures, and perioperative
management
Before surgery, all patients received appropriate
respiratory and hemodynamic management, and
osmotic therapy (e.g., mannitol) was administered
when intracranial hypertension was suspected.
The standard procedure consisted of appropriately
sized craniotomy, hematoma evacuation, identifica­
tion of the bleeding source, and meticulous hemo­
stasis. Based on intraoperative findings, such as
acute brain swelling during surgery, difficulty
achieving adequate hemostasis, or obvious paren­
chymal brain injury, decompressive craniectomy
(DC) with expansive duroplasty using artificial dura
was performed at the discretion of the attending
neurosurgeon. Postoperatively, ICP was managed
in the intensive care unit with continuous ICP moni­
toring. Patients with severe TBI received compre­
hensive care, including respiratory and hemody­
namic support and pharmacological therapy. In
patients managed primarily with ICP monitoring,
secondary DC was considered when ICP exceeded
25 mmHg for more than 30 minutes.
For this study, brain swelling was defined as early
radiological signs of cerebral herniation: compres­
sion or obliteration of the basal cisterns, midline
shift > 5 mm, extracranial herniation of brain paren­
chyma > 10 mm, or diffuse effacement of the cere­
bral sulci.
Cranioplasty using autologous bone was performed
after normalization of ICP, typically ≥ 3 weeks after
the initial surgery. Temporary ventricular drainage
or permanent cerebrospinal fluid shunting was
performed in patients who developed hydroceph­
alus. The study followed a normothermia-based
management protocol and excluded patients treated
with therapeutic hypothermia or barbiturate therapy.

Statistical analysis
Differences in continuous variables between the
swelling and non-swelling groups were assessed
using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test,
as appropriate. The time from injury to surgery,
which was significant in the t -test, was dichoto­
mized using an exploratory cutoff value determined
from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve
analysis. Categorical variables were compared using
the chi-square test.
Variables significant in univariate analysis were
entered into a multivariable logistic regression model
to identify independent factors associated with
postoperative brain swelling. Model performance
was evaluated using the coefficient of determina­
tion (R2), the corrected Akaike information criterion
(AICc), and the c-statistic. Results are presented
as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals
(CI) and P values.
All analyses were performed using JMP® Pro
18.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). All tests
were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statis­
tically significant.
Results
The baseline characteristics of the overall cohort
are summarized in Table 1. The study cohort
comprised 74 patients with a median age of 80
years (interquartile range [IQR], 72-85), of whom
26 (35.1%) were female.
According to the predefined classification algo­
rithm (Table 2 and Figure 1), patients were cate­
gorized into swelling and non-swelling groups.
Twenty-one patients (28.4%; male : female = 13 : 8)
were classified into the swelling group and 53
patients (71.6%; male : female = 35 : 18) into the
non-swelling group.
Clinical parameters for each group are presented
in Table 3. The median age was 75 years (IQR,
58-80) in the swelling group and 82 years (IQR,
75-86) in the non-swelling group, with the latter
being significantly older (P < 0.001).
Univariate analysis demonstrated that brain
swelling was significantly associated with age < 65
years (odds ratio [OR], 0.060; 95% confidence
interval [CI], 0.01-0.34; P < 0.001), GCS 3-8 (OR,
5.78; 95% CI, 1.90-17.61; P = 0.002), and concomitant
cerebral contusion (OR, 11.84; 95% CI, 3.40-41.27; P
< 0.001). Cerebral contusion severity was assessed

using the Adams’ Regional Contusion Index, and a
total contusion index ≥ 3 was also significantly asso­
ciated with brain swelling (OR, 4.56; 95% CI, 1.5513.38; P = 0.006).
Time from injury onset was clearly documented
in 55 of the 74 patients; cases without exact time
records were excluded from this analysis. The
mean time from injury to surgery was significantly
shorter in the swelling group (280.1 ± 120.5 min)
than in the non-swelling group (482.4 ± 366.2 min)
(P = 0.007). Exploratory receiver operating charac­
teristic (ROC) curve analysis identified a cutoff
value of 351 minutes (sensitivity 58.3%, specificity
84.2%). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was
0.66 (95% CI, 0.47-0.85). Based on this cutoff, patients
were dichotomized into < 350 minutes and ≥ 350
minutes groups, which showed a significant associ­
ation with brain swelling (OR, 0.13; 95% CI, 0.030.54; P = 0.004).
No significant differences were observed for sex,
“talk and deterioration,” vital sign abnormalities,
anisocoria, hematoma side, hematoma thickness,
midline shift, or antithrombotic medication use.
However, anisocoria showed a tendency toward
association with brain swelling (OR, 0.37; 95% CI,
0.13-1.06; P = 0.073). All patients in the swelling
group had poor outcomes at 30 days (P = 0.0001).
Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were
entered into multivariate logistic regression anal­
ysis. Multivariate analysis showed cerebral contu­
sion as the only independent factor associated with
brain swelling (OR, 20.95; 95% CI, 3.81-206.51; P <
0.001), whereas age, GCS, and time from injury to
surgery were not significant. The model showed a
coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.47, indicating
that approximately 47% of the variance associated
with brain swelling was accounted for by the
model. Model selection using the corrected Akaike
Information Criterion yielded an AICc of 48.93. The
C-index was 0.90, suggesting good discriminative
ability of the model. Multivariate analysis results
are presented in Table 4.

underwent primary DC and were later classified in
Group III due to brain swelling are shown in Table 5.

Discussion
This retrospective study evaluated patients with
ASDH who underwent craniotomy for hematoma
evacuation, with or without decompressive craniec­
tomy (DC), to identify factors associated with post­
operative brain swelling that may help guide deci­
sion-making regarding decompressive craniectomy.
ASDH is caused by intracranial hypertension due
to hematoma mass effect and focal injury such as
cerebral contusion14-16). The molecular mechanisms
underlying poor outcomes remain complex17, 18),
making treatment decisions challenging.
Normalization of ICP remains a fundamental
therapeutic strategy, second only to circulatory
and respiratory stabilization. When conservative
measures such as hyperventilation or osmotic
therapy fail, surgical decompression—including DC
or removal of intraparenchymal mass lesions—
becomes necessary. However, predicting the need
for additional decompression based solely on preop­
erative findings is often difficult and may result in
delayed or unnecessary intervention.
Younger patients were more likely to develop
postoperative brain swelling, suggesting that careful
consideration of DC may be warranted in such
high-risk patients. Age-related brain atrophy in
elderly patients may increase ICP buffering capacity
through enlargement of the cerebrospinal fluid
space6, 19-21), thereby reducing the need for DC. In
contrast, lower intracranial compliance in younger
patients22) predisposes them to rapid ICP elevation
and a higher likelihood of requiring DC. Poor preop­
erative level of consciousness was also associated
with increased DC requirement, likely reflecting
greater injury severity, consistent with previous
findings23).
Early surgery has been associated with improved

outcomes in ASDH, particularly when performed
within 2-4 hours after injury24, 25). Accordingly,
patients meeting surgical criteria should undergo
prompt treatment26). In this study, time from injury
to surgery was significantly shorter in the swelling
group, and DC was more frequently required in
patients operated on within 350 minutes (P = 0.004).
This likely reflects confounding by injury severity,
as more severe cases undergo earlier surgery and
are also more likely to require DC.
Although surgical treatment for cerebral contu­
sions associated with ASDH is established27, 28), clear
criteria for DC remain undefined. A TCI ≥ 3 was
significantly associated with DC requirement, sugge­
sting that the presence of contusion itself may influ­
ence DC indication more than its absolute severity.
In addition, delayed deterioration, including “talk
and deteriorate” phenomena, underscores the impor­
tance of careful preoperative evaluation and consid­
eration of proactive DC in high-risk patients.
No significant association was observed between
DC and traditional markers of herniation such as
pupillary abnormalities or midline shift. Pupillary
irregularities caused by cerebral ischemia may not
always correlate with ICP elevation29), suggesting
that continuous ICP monitoring is valuable. Conver­
sely, localized brainstem compression may adversely
affect prognosis even without global ICP elevation,
highlighting the need to integrate ICP monitoring
with detailed imaging assessment.
The RESCUE-ASDH randomized trial reported
comparable long-term disability and quality-of-life
outcomes between craniotomy and DC30). However,
it did not evaluate predictors of DC necessity.
Previous studies2, 4) have examined factors related
to DC requirement without definitive conclusions.
The present findings may assist in refining preop­
erative decision-making regarding consideration of
DC. Interestingly, re-examination of the five patients
classified in Group III showed that four patients
had severe consciousness disturbance (GCS ≤ 8),
and all patients had concomitant cerebral contu­
sion. Although statistical analysis was not feasible
because of the limited number of cases, these find­
ings suggest that some of the risk factors identified
in the present model were also present in patients
who subsequently developed postoperative brain
swelling despite not undergoing primary DC.

Limitations
This single-center retrospective study is subject
to inherent selection bias. Although DC decisions
were made by board-certified neurosurgeons, the
influence of clinical judgment cannot be excluded,
particularly in primary DC cases.
The DC group may have included patients in
whom early DC prevented subsequent swelling,
whereas some non-DC cases later required secon­
dary DC. The swelling group included 21 events
while four predictors were entered into the multi­
variable model, raising concern for overfitting and
instability of estimates.
Cerebral contusion was shown as an indepen­
dent factor but is intrinsically related to surgical
indication and injury severity, limiting outcome
comparisons between groups. Furthermore, the
95% confidence interval for cerebral contusions is
wide; this may be due to the limited number of
cases or potential bias resulting from insufficient
data. Therefore, caution is required when inter­
preting the results.
Functional outcome was assessed at 30 days using
GOS, which may underestimate later recovery.
Finally, the AUC of 0.66 indicates modest discrimi­
native performance of the TCI cutoff and warrants
cautious clinical interpretation.
Conclusions
This retrospective study identified several factors
associated with postoperative brain swelling in
patients with ASDH, including younger age, severe
impairment of consciousness, concomitant cerebral
contusion, and shorter time from injury to surgery.
Although these findings do not establish definitive
criteria for DC, they may contribute to improved
preoperative risk stratification and support surgical
decision-making when considering DC in high-risk
patients.
