Agreement between markerless and marker-based motion capture for knee kinematics and kinetics during functional activities in knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Rhodora Therese Gumabao Torres, Benjamin Senderling, Ehyun Kim, Michael Rose, Mary Gheller, Tuhina Neogi, Deepak Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo assess the agreement between markerless and marker-based motion capture for measuring knee kinematics and kinetics during functional activities in people with knee osteoarthritis.MethodData from 22 participants (age 63.2 ± 7.6 years; BMI 28.2 ± 4.7 kg/m2; 64% women) with mild-to-moderate osteoarthritis related disability were analyzed. Synchronized marker-based motion capture (Qualisys Oqus, 250 Hz), color videos (Qualisys Miqus, 50 Hz), and force platform data (2000 Hz) were collected during self-selected & fast walking and sit-to-stand. Video data were processed using an artificial intelligence-based pose estimation algorithm (Theia3D v2022.1.0.2309). Knee kinematics and kinetics (discrete measures and waveforms) were derived from both systems for the stance phase of walking and ascent phase of sit-to-stand. Agreement was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients, Pearson's correlation coefficients, and Bland-Altman analyses.ResultsAcross tasks, RMSD ranged from 3.8° to 5.7° for kinematics and 0.2 to 1.2%BW∗ht for kinetics, with joint center differences <5 cm. Agreements on knee adduction moment (KAM) impulse during both walking speeds (ICC = 0.94) and 2nd KAM peak during fast walking (ICC = 0.91) were excellent. Agreements on peak knee adduction angle (ICC = 0.78–0.83), sagittal knee range of motion (ICC = 0.78–0.83), 1st peak of knee flexion moment (KFM) (ICC = 0.76–0.81), and KAM (ICC = 0.87–0.90) were good, as well as, 2nd KAM peak during self-selected walking (ICC = 0.84), KFM impulse during fast walking (ICC = 0.84), and peak KFM during sit-to-stand (ICC = 0.78).ConclusionThe evaluated markerless system is a potential efficient alternative for estimating knee kinematics and kinetics for people with knee osteoarthritis, particularly for sagittal range of motion and frontal kinetics during walking, and sagittal moments in sit-to-stand.

Introduction

Objective biomechanical assessments are essential for monitoring knee osteoarthritis (OA) progression and evaluating treatments designed to modify joint loading, such as muscle strengthening and gait re-training [1,2]. Tasks like walking and sit-to-stand (STS) capture real-world knee function relevant to OA-related mobility limitations [3], with parameters such as knee range of motion (ROM), peak knee adduction moment (KAM) and knee flexion moment (KFM) strongly linked to disease progression, symptoms, and treatment response [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Thus, biomechanical assessments during these tasks have therefore become an important tool for characterizing knee OA, with growing interest in translating such assessments to clinical settings.

Despite this relevance, biomechanical assessments in knee OA have largely been confined to specialized laboratories, hindering clinical translation or collection of biomechanical data in large cohorts. Traditional biomechanical assessments rely on marker-based motion capture systems (MBmocap), which present limitations due to the requirement for trained personnel, specialized equipment, extensive setup time, and potential variability due to marker placement inconsistency [11]. Emerging markerless motion capture systems (MLmocap) offer as a potential alternative by eliminating the need for manual marker placement, automating anatomical landmark identification and reducing operational barriers [12,13]. However, most validation studies have focused on healthy populations [14]. A previous study [15] compared gait kinematics and kinetics between systems in mixed healthy and clinical populations, not including OA, and comparisons during other functional tasks like STS, remain limited. Recent studies in OA population showed that MLmocap can detect kinematic abnormalities and provide repeatable gait outcomes, yet direct comparisons to MBmocap, particularly for kinetic variables which are particularly relevant to this population remains lacking [16,17]. Comprehensive biomechanical assessment of knee OA requires both kinematic and kinetic data to fully capture movement dynamics and joint loading, which are crucial for evaluating disease progression and clinical monitoring [18]. Furthermore, the validity of MLmocap for tasks beyond overground walking, such as STS, remains lacking in this population. Therefore, studies comparing MLmocap and MBmocap for estimating knee kinematics and kinetics in knee OA population during daily activities like walking and STS are needed.

Our objective was to compare knee joint kinematics and kinetics during walking and STS between MLmocap and MBmocap in people with knee OA, providing insights into the feasibility of MLmocap for clinical and research applications.

Methods

Participants

This study was a secondary analysis of data collected from 22 participants (Table 1) enrolled in a clinical trial (NCT04243096) who completed a single study visit. The clinical trial included individuals with age ≥50 years, confirmed clinical diagnosis of knee OA based on American College of Rheumatology criteria, ≥3 score on weight-bearing questions from the Knee injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS) Pain Subscale, BMI ≤40 kg/m2, and ability to walk without assisted devices for at least 20 min. Exclusion criteria were health conditions that limit the ability to walk except for knee OA pain, exercise contraindications, ongoing cancer therapy except for non-melanoma skin cancer, inflammatory joint diseases, knee surgery in the previous 6 months, and pain in the lower back or legs that is greater than knee pain. KOOS was collected and index knee was defined as the more painful knee, or a knee selected at random if the pain was similar in both knees. Participants were on average over 60 years of age and in the overweight category, with majority being women. Participants had mild-to-moderate OA related disability based on KOOS pain and activities of daily living scores.

Experimental setup and procedure

Participants wore study-provided shorts, tank-top, and neutral shoes (Gel-Cumulus 19, Asics, Kobe, Japan). We attached fifty-two 12.5 mm retroreflective markers to bony landmarks of the trunk (manubrium, spinous process of the seventh cervical vertebrae (C7), spinous process of the eighth thoracic vertebrae (T8), and acromia), pelvis (sacrum, superior aspects of iliac crests, anterior superior iliac spines, and posterior superior iliac spines), and both lower extremities (first and fifth metatarsal heads, lateral and medial malleoli, lateral and medial femoral epicondyles, and greater trochanters of the femur). These markers were used to identify joint centers during a static standing trial. We also attached rigid clusters of four markers to both thighs and shank segments. To track feet segments, we placed additional markers on shoes corresponding to the posterior aspect of the heel, the medial and lateral mid-feet, the second metatarsal heads, and the lateral aspects of the fifth metatarsal heads. Motion data were captured concurrently by synchronizing 12 infrared cameras (Qualisys Oqus, Qualisys, Sweden) recording at 250 Hz for MBmocap and 8 color video cameras (Qualisys Miqus, 3-megapixel resolution video cameras, Qualisys, Sweden) recording at 50 Hz for MLmocap. Cameras were mounted on an overhead railing 2.5 m from the floor, and the capture volume was centered over the force plates (Supplementary Figure 1). Calibration of both systems were performed synchronously in Qualisys Track Manager using an L-frame positioned at the origin of the capture volume and a wand-based dynamic calibration procedure. Ground reaction force (GRF) data were captured using floor-embedded force platforms (AMTI Inc., Watertown, MA, USA) at 2000 Hz synchronously with the motion data.

Participants walked at self-selected and fast speeds along a 12 m walkway and performed STS on an 18 inch-high armless chair. For self-selected speed, participants were instructed to walk at a purposeful, usual pace. For fast speed, participants were instructed to walk as quickly as possible while maintaining comfort and safety, and without running. Speeds were determined prior to collection of motion capture data. We collected 4–6 trials per speed that were within 5% of the pre-determined pace and had clean foot contacts on the force platforms. Walking speed (m/s) was measured using a timing system (Brower Timing Systems, Draper, UT, USA). For STS, participants sat with hands on their chest and one foot on each force platform. Participants completed five chair stands without using their arms while standing up fully each time, at their self-selected pace. We excluded the last chair stand from each trial due to variations between participants in terms of taking a step after reaching a fully standing posture.

Data processing

For MBmocap, we labeled the marker trajectories within the motion capture software (Qualisys Track Manager 3D; Qualisys AB Inc., Gothenburg, Sweden). Any gaps in data were filled using polynomial splines for gaps of less than 10 frames and a trajectory matching for larger gaps. We exported the data in c3d file format for subsequent processing in Visual3D (HAS-Motion, Inc., ON, Canada). In Visual3D, MBmocap data was downsampled to 50 Hz to match the MLmocap data. Marker trajectories and GRF data were filtered using fourth-order low-pass Butterworth filters with cutoff frequencies of 6 Hz and 12 Hz, respectively [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Subject-specific models were created based on the manually defined markers from the static trial for the MBmocap data. The MBmocap model was defined to have six degrees-of-freedom (DOF) at the pelvis, and three at the hips, knees, and ankles.

For MLmocap, key point identification and pose estimation were completed in Theia3D (Theia3D v2022.1.0.2309, Theia Markerless Inc., ON, Canada). Theia3D's implementation of a generalized cross-validatory spline function was used to filter the data with an 8 Hz cut-off frequency [16], and were exported in c3d format for further processing in Visual3D. Theia3D is an artificial intelligence-based pose estimation algorithm that automatically generates skeletal models from video data for further analysis. By default, Theia3D version used has six DOF at the pelvis, three at the hips, two at the knees, and three at the ankles. To match with the MBmocap model, we then constrained the knee to have three DOF, and data using this model were reported here. Comparisons between MBmocap and MLmocap with 2 and 3 DOF at the knees were reported in the supplementary data.

A total of 129 gait cycles during self-selected walking speed, 108 gait cycles during fast walking, and 156 ascents during STS were used for analysis (Supplementary Figure 2).

In Visual 3D, hip, knee, and ankle joint angles were calculated using the default segment coordinate systems following the Cardan sequence of rotations (x-y-z, representing flexion/extension, abduction/adduction, and axial rotations) and expressed in degrees (°). Joint moments were derived using inverse dynamics and normalized to percentage of body weight x height (%BW∗ht). Impulse of joint moments were computed as the time integral of the joint moment and expressed in %BW∗ht∗s. All joint moments were presented as external moments.

For walking trials, the stance phase of gait was identified using the GRF data applying a threshold of 20 N [23,24]. For STS, the ascent phase was defined using the angular velocity of the trunk around the medial-lateral axis to identify the start and end of the trunk movement in the sagittal plane (Supplementary Figure 3). All measures and analyses were conducted on the index side. These included sagittal and frontal plane angle and moment waveforms, as well as discrete kinematic and kinetic measures. Discrete measures included peak knee flexion and adduction angles during stance; sagittal and frontal plane knee ROM during stance and ascent; peak external KFM and KAM during ascent and in the first and second halves of stance; and KFM and KAM impulses during stance. Joint center positions (JCP) for the MLmocap represent the direct estimates of the position from the deep-learning algorithm. JCP for the MBmocap were estimated using the midpoint between medial and lateral malleoli markers for the ankles, midpoint between the medial and lateral femoral condyles for the knee, and at 25% distance from each greater trochanter marker along the line connecting the two greater trochanters [25,26].

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean and standard deviation. We plotted the time-normalized knee angle and moment waveforms over the stance phase for walking tasks and ascent phase for STS. The differences between systems (MLmocap-MBmocap) and the root mean square differences (RMSD) were also computed and plotted throughout the phases of interest [26,27]. The average RMSD between systems over the entire phase of interest was also computed and reported [26,27]. Waveform comparisons were qualitative, with the average RMSD used as a summary metric of waveform difference. For discrete metrics, we used two-way mixed effects intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC 3,1), Pearson's correlation (r), and Bland-Altman analysis to examine the agreement between MLmocap and MBmocap. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of <0.50 = poor, 0.50–0.75 = moderate, 0.75–0.90 = good, and >0.90 = excellent [18,28,29]. Normality of all discrete measures were assessed using skewness and kurtosis statistics, with acceptable ranges defined as ±2 and ± 7, respectively [30,31]. No violations of normality were observed. For JCP, differences between systems (MLmocap-MBmocap) were computed for each component of the global coordinate system, then 3D Euclidean distance differences were calculated and plotted for each lower extremity joint across tasks. The average root-mean square values of the 3D Euclidean distance differences were also reported [26].

For this study, feasibility is defined as achieving ICC point estimate of ≥0.75 [18,29], as well as RMSD of ≤5° for kinematic measurements [11] and ≤1%BW∗ht for kinetic parameters [19,27].

Results

Kinematic waveforms

Average knee kinematic RMSDs across all measures and tasks ranged from 3.8 to 5.7° using 3 DOF (Fig. 1), and from 3.8 to 6.2° when using 2 DOF (Supplementary Figure 4). Except for frontal plane knee angle during STS, average RMSDs were <5°. Sagittal plane RMSDs during walking and STS were consistently ≤5°. Visual inspection suggested that MLmocap tended to estimate smaller peak knee flexion in early stance than MBmocap in both walking tasks. In the frontal plane, plots suggested that RMSDs during walking were ≤5° on average, but shows relative increase toward the end of stance, where MLmocap tended to lean towards abduction. During STS, plot showed that MLmocap estimated greater flexion in early ascent and less flexion in late ascent, although average RMSDs remained relatively stable across the ascent phase. Frontal plane knee angle RMSDs during STS were consistently ≥5° with larger differences observed during late ascent. Kinetic waveforms

Average knee kinetic RMSDs ranged from 0.2 to 1.2%BW∗ht (Fig. 2) and were similar for MLmocap with 2 or 3 knee DOF (Supplementary Figure 7). Average RMSDs were lower for KAM (0.4–0.5%BW∗Ht) than for KFM (1.1–1.2%BW∗Ht) during walking, and similar for both in STS (0.2–0.3%BW∗Ht). Plots suggested that during walking tasks, KFM RMSDs were greater in the 2nd half of stance, but relatively consistent for KAM. It was also observed that MLmocap showed lower KFM during 1st half, while higher moment during 2nd half of stance in both walking tasks. KAM estimates were also seen to be slightly larger in MLmocap than MBmocap. During STS, KFM estimates appeared larger for MLmocap; but the RMSD remained consistently <1%BW∗ht. Discrete kinetic and kinematic measures

For discrete measures (Table 2), ICC point estimates were excellent for KAM impulse during stance at self-selected and fast walking (ICC = 0.94) and for 2nd peak KAM during fast walking (ICC = 0.91). ICC point estimates were also good for the peak knee adduction angle (ICC = 0.78–0.83), sagittal knee ROM (ICC = 0.78–0.83), 1st peak of KFM (ICC = 0.76–0.81) and KAM (ICC = 0.87–0.90) during self-selected and fast walking; for 2nd peak of KAM during self-selected walking (ICC = 0.88); for KFM impulse during fast walking (ICC = 0.84); and for peak KFM during STS (ICC = 0.78). The rest of the measures had moderate (ICC = 0.5–0.75) or poor (ICC<0.50) agreement. Discussion

MLmocap demonstrated excellent-to-good ICC point estimates relative to MBmocap for most knee kinematic and kinetic measures during walking and STS in people with knee OA, meeting our predefined feasibility thresholds for several clinically important outcomes. Specifically, ICC point estimates exceeded 0.75 for peak knee adduction angle, sagittal knee ROM, first KFM peak, KAM, and KAM impulse during walking, as well as peak KFM during STS. These findings suggest the potential of MLmocap as an accessible alternative to MBmocap for capturing biomechanically relevant measures in OA populations. While MBmocap are traditionally used, they are not exempt from errors, including challenges in marker placement and identification, particularly in individuals with higher BMI, as well as inherent skin-to-bone motion [27,32]. Eliminating the need for marker placement can improve efficiency and accessibility of motion analysis in clinical and research settings. Further improvements in MLmocap estimations are expected as the algorithm improve over time.

Average RMSDs for knee angles in the sagittal (3.8–4.8°) and frontal (3.8°) planes during walking stance, and sagittal plane knee angle during STS ascent (4.3°) were all <5° which are considered minimal and acceptable [11], and aligned with previous findings [15,26,27] using the same MLmocap in healthy and other clinical population. The increase in RMSD in the frontal plane during the last 12.5% of stance phase is likely due to increased angular and linear velocities of the limb segments during the transition to swing, challenging MLmocap accuracy due to motion blur, particularly at 50 Hz sampling rate [33,34]. The change in velocities may have also influenced the frontal plane RMSD during STS from early to late ascent. Supplementary waveform comparisons showed largest sagittal plane RMSDs at the hip potentially due to challenges with determining hip joint center reliably. Ankle RMSDs were also higher than knee RMSDs, particularly during early stance, likely due to larger angular velocity at the ankle relative to other joints. Accordingly, hip and ankle estimates should be interpreted with greater caution, and further system improvements for these joints are needed.

For discrete measures of knee kinematics, ICC point estimates indicated moderate-to-good for all measures except frontal knee ROM across all tasks. Mean differences for peak knee flexion and adduction were ≤2.5°; and ≤1° for sagittal and frontal ROM during walking. During STS, mean differences for sagittal and frontal knee ROM were 4.2° and 1.4°, respectively. The differences observed between systems may partly reflect their distinct modeling approaches. MLmocap uses automatically generated models with JCPs derived from deep-learning-based pose estimation, whereas MBmocap uses marker-based models with JCPs defined using established anatomical landmark methods within the segment coordinate system [25,26]. Differences in the JCP can contribute up to 3° of error in lower limb joint kinematics [35,36]. Our results showed that the average RMSD in JCP estimations are comparable to those reported by previous study [26], with the hip exhibiting the largest RMSD. This partly reflects the challenges of accurately tracking this joint [27], particularly in individuals with higher BMI [37], which is common in knee OA population. Prior studies suggest both MBmocap and MLmocap face difficulties when there is greater adiposity due to marker placement difficulties and reliance on surface contours, respectively [27,37,38]. The larger hip JCP RMSD may have contributed to knee kinematic differences, as errors in the proximal JCP estimation can alter thigh segment orientation and affect downstream knee kinematic estimates [39,40]. Frontal plane knee angles are also sensitive to the mediolateral knee JCP, and even small offsets may shift the axis around which abduction and adduction are calculated [41]; these systematic differences may then produce a consistent directional bias in frontal plane angles, as seen in the plotted waveforms, where MLmocap estimates were biased toward abduction. The poor agreement for frontal knee ROMs across tasks may also reflect the inherent limitations of both systems in measuring small, subtle movements precisely [37,42]. Additionally, although mean biases were small across measures, the LoA were wide, indicating substantial inter-individual variability that may be clinically meaningful. This variability is likely rooted in the varied characteristics and OA severity among participants, suggesting caution when applying these at the individual level. Nevertheless, meta-analysis has shown that reduced knee flexion excursion is the only consistent kinematic finding during walking in knee OA population [43]. Taken together, our findings suggest that the evaluated MLmocap may be useful for efficiently assessing knee ROM, specifically in the sagittal plane, during walking in people with knee OA.

Average RMSDs for sagittal (0.3–1.2%BW∗ht) and frontal (0.2–0.5%BW∗ht) knee kinetics were ≤1%BW∗ht which are considered minimal, and similar to previous study in healthy individuals using the same MLmocap [27]. Average hip and ankle kinetic RMSDs were also below 1%BW∗ht, except for hip sagittal kinetics during self-selected (1.4%BW∗ht) and fast (1.5%BW∗ht) walking. Comparative to walking, STS exhibited lower RMSDs across all joints. Consistent with the kinematic results, knee kinetics had the lowest RMSDs across tasks. The greater hip-related differences may reflect the combined influence of JCP estimation and soft tissue artifact; whereas ankle estimates may be more variable because distal segment motion is smaller and more sensitive to tracking challenges particularly for task like walking that are dynamically complex [14]. Ankle estimates may also be influenced by footwear, as shoes can affect foot tracking and may yield different results compared with barefoot conditions [44].

For discrete measures of knee kinetics during walking, ICC point estimates were excellent-to-good for KAM impulse and peak KAM with mean differences of 0.1%BW∗ht∗s and 0–0.4%BW∗ht, respectively. These measures are of substantial interest given their associations with risk of OA worsening [[45], [46], [47], [48]] and are often targeted in biomechanical interventions for knee OA [[49], [50], [51]]. KAM is a key predictor of medial-lateral distribution of knee contact forces [52]. Since GRF measures were identical for both systems, kinetic differences arise primarily from pose estimation differences. KAM is more influenced by lever arm between the GRF and knee joint than the magnitude of the GRF [53]. Hence, small difference in the JCP between the systems may explain the excellent agreement observed for this measure despite relatively large RMSD for frontal plane knee angle. The mean difference is smaller than what is typically considered as clinically meaningful (i.e., 0.5–2.2%BW∗ht) for detecting differences between people with knee OA and controls for estimating risk of OA progression [47,54,55], suggesting that the evaluated MLmocap may be used for assessing peak KAM and KAM impulse during walking in people with knee OA. ICC point estimates were good for peak KFM (mean difference of 0.4%BW∗ht) during STS and 1st KFM peak (mean difference of −0.8 to −1%BW∗ht) during walking, but poor on the 2nd peak. KFM impulse was also good during fast walking and moderate during self-selected speed. KFM reflects net muscle contraction, particularly of the quadriceps [46], but is also influenced by other factors such as pain and compensatory strategies. As a constrained sagittal-plane task, STS yields more consistent KFM measures across systems. Walking, however, introduces greater variability from differences in speed, stride length, and individual movement patterns; particularly at self-selected speeds where people with knee OA may demonstrate more variable gait adaptations related to pain. This variability is reduced at faster walking speeds, which produce stronger, more consistent kinetic signals, explaining the better agreement observed in KFM impulse. In addition, the better agreement of the 1st KFM peak is likely due to its larger, more distinct magnitude, whereas the 2nd peak, although linked to knee OA progression [5], is more subtle and variable. As KFM changes of as much as 1%BW∗ht are clinically relevant [46], caution is warranted when interpreting MLmocap-derived KFM data, particularly during walking.

We observed differences in ICCs, bias, and waveform agreement between self-selected and fast walking speeds; thus, the agreement metrics reported here should be interpreted as speed-specific rather than directly interchangeable across walking conditions. Because walking speed can influence lower-extremity kinematics and kinetics, these differences may reflect both system agreement and true speed-related changes in movement mechanics [[56], [57], [58]]. In people with knee OA, walking speed may influence gait parameters such as joint moments; and accounting for this in the analysis has been shown to significantly reduce observed differences [58]. Walking at self-selected speed better represents typical gait and symptom-related adaptations, whereas fast walking may be particularly useful for assessing functional capacity under increased demand and may yield more stable signals for some measures. Formal between-speed comparisons were beyond the scope of this study and these observations should be considered hypothesis-generating and an area for future work.

Strengths of our study include relevant clinical sample, and assessment of both kinematic and kinetic parameters. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, differences in biomechanical models between the MLmocap and MBmocap present challenge to comparability. Although model definition standards have been proposed [59,60], modifications are often made specific to a study. We explored both MLmocap models with 3 and 2 DOF at the knee, and reported results here based on 3 DOF model to match the MBmocap model used. As technology advances, model customizations are expanding; ultimately, the selection will remain dependent on the specific research question and application. Theia3D v2022.1.0.2309 was used for MLmocap processing; newer versions may include improvements in pose estimation algorithms and joint center estimation that could enhance accuracy beyond what was observed. In addition, as a secondary analysis, the available sample was limited to participants with BMI ≤40 kg/m2, which may not reflect modeling accuracy in individuals with greater adiposity. Kellgren-Lawrence grade was also not collected, leaving structural severity unknown, although, KOOS scores showed mild-to-moderate disability among participants; results may however differ in those with severe disability or deformity. The limited sample size inherent to this secondary analysis may have also reduced the precision of the ICC point estimates as observed in the width of the ICC confidence intervals, therefore these findings should be considered preliminary rather than definitive. Also, we assessed agreement during stance phase only. MLmocap data were collected at 50 Hz, which limits accuracy during rapid swing-phase movements due to motion blur; while MBmocap data were downsampled to 50 Hz prior to time-normalization to match the MLmocap sampling rate, which may influence the agreement estimates relative to analysis at the original MBmocap sampling rate. Additionally, we focused only on sagittal and frontal planes considering the challenges of reliably assessing transverse plane motions [38,61]. Our study also focused on knee kinematics and kinetics, as these are the most clinically relevant outcomes in knee OA biomechanics research and the intended application of the MLmocap being validated. To provide additional biomechanical context, we included supplementary hip and ankle waveform comparisons (Supplementary Figures 6–8). Also, because only the index side was analyzed, the present findings may not fully capture within-subject variability or generalize to the contralateral limb. Evaluation of contralateral-limb differences was beyond the scope of this study and may warrant future investigation. Lastly, participants wore standardized clothing with most body segments exposed to facilitate MBmocap. As such, our findings may not be generalizable to use of MLmocap with participants wearing other types of clothing.

In conclusion, the evaluated MLmocap showed generally favorable agreement with MBmocap for several knee kinematic and kinetic measures during walking and STS in people with knee OA, suggesting its potential for efficient group-level biomechanical research use. Its current limitations, especially in the frontal plane and during high movement variability, underscore the need for continued refinement. Future studies examining test-retest reliability and sensitivity to clinically meaningful change are needed before individual-level clinical applications can be endorsed.
