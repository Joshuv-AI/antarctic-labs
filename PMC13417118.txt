Nanostructured ZnO, CuO and Zn–Cu mixed metal oxides for enhanced photocatalytic performance
Authors: Rayhan Hossain, Alice Bella Baxter, Bing Gao, Victoria Valeria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Semiconductor metal oxides, particularly zinc oxide (ZnO) and copper oxide (CuO), have attracted considerable attention as photocatalysts for the degradation of organic pollutants because of their favorable electronic properties, chemical stability, and low cost. In this study, ZnO and CuO nanoparticles were synthesized by wick-assisted solution combustion, while Zn–Cu mixed oxide composites were prepared by thermal decomposition of oxalate precursors. Their structural, morphological, and photocatalytic properties were systematically compared with those of commercial materials. X-ray diffraction analysis showed that the synthesized ZnO possessed a highly crystalline hexagonal wurtzite structure, whereas the commercial ZnO exhibited a predominantly amorphous nature. The photocatalytic activity was evaluated by the degradation of Orange G dye under UV irradiation. Commercial ZnO exhibited superior photocatalytic performance compared with the synthesized ZnO, while the synthesized CuO showed enhanced activity relative to commercial CuO. The Zn–Cu mixed oxide composite demonstrated improved photocatalytic performance owing to the synergistic interaction between ZnO and CuO, which promotes charge separation and suppresses electron–hole recombination. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy confirmed the generation of hydroxyl (˙OH) and superoxide (˙O2−) radicals during UV irradiation, and radical trapping experiments identified ˙OH as the dominant reactive species responsible for Orange G degradation. These findings provide direct experimental evidence for the proposed photocatalytic mechanism and establish a clear relationship between crystallinity, reactive oxygen species generation, and photocatalytic performance. The results provide useful insights into the design of efficient ZnO–CuO-based photocatalysts for environmental remediation applications.

Introduction

Semiconductor nanoparticles, particularly zinc oxide (ZnO) and copper oxide (CuO), are highly attractive for applications in photocatalysis, optoelectronics, solar energy conversion, and environmental remediation.1–3 Their favorable electronic properties, including suitable band gaps, enable efficient charge carrier generation under light irradiation, making them ideal candidates for photocatalytic degradation of toxic organic dyes, such as Orange G (OG). OG, commonly used in biological staining and electrophoresis, presents environmental risks due to its persistence and toxicity in wastewater. Photocatalysis driven by ZnO and CuO under solar irradiation generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) that efficiently break down these pollutants into less toxic byproducts, offering an eco-friendly solution for large-scale environmental remediation.

ZnO, a wide-bandgap (3.37 eV) semiconductor, is known for its high photocatalytic activity, non-toxicity, and stability under UV light, while CuO, a narrow-bandgap (1.2 eV) p-type semiconductor, is recognized for its chemical stability and ease of synthesis.4,5 When combined in binary oxide composites, ZnO and CuO can synergistically enhance photocatalytic performance by improving charge carrier separation, reducing recombination rates, and expanding the absorption spectrum to include visible light.6,7 This combination enhances their potential for solar-driven environmental remediation applications.

The synthesis of ZnO, CuO, and their mixed oxide composites can be achieved through various methods such as sol–gel, evaporative decomposition, wet chemical synthesis, and gas-phase synthesis.8,9 Among these, solution combustion is particularly favored for its simplicity, cost-effectiveness, and ability to produce high-purity products at relatively low temperatures.10 However, this method may lead to non-uniform particle sizes, difficulties in temperature control, and environmental concerns due to the exothermic nature of the reaction. Thermal decomposition of oxalate precursors, in contrast, offers better control over nanoparticle morphology, size, and phase purity, ensuring more consistent results.11 While solution combustion is better suited for large-scale production and rapid synthesis, thermal decomposition excels in precision and reproducibility, making the choice of method dependent on synthesis requirements such as speed, size uniformity, and production scale.12

The photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants, such as toxic organic dyes, is a significant application for semiconductor nanoparticles like ZnO and CuO. These materials have demonstrated excellent photocatalytic activity for the degradation of model dyes under UV light.13 The mechanism involves the absorption of UV light by the semiconductor, generating electron–hole pairs. The photogenerated electrons reduce oxygen molecules to form superoxide radicals (O2−), while the holes oxidize water or hydroxide ions to produce hydroxyl radicals (OH−). These highly reactive species break down organic pollutants into simpler, non-toxic compounds. However, the photocatalytic efficiency of ZnO and CuO is often limited by rapid electron–hole recombination, which reduces their overall performance. To overcome this limitation, one effective approach is the formation of binary oxide composites, such as ZnO–CuO systems. These composites can enhance charge carrier separation, thereby improving photocatalytic efficiency.14 The photocatalytic activity of semiconductor nanoparticles is typically evaluated by monitoring the degradation of model pollutants, with OG being a widely used dye in many studies. The degradation of OG under UV irradiation in the presence of ZnO, CuO, or Zn–Cu binary oxide composites provides a valuable measure of the photocatalytic performance of these materials.15

This study aims to investigate the synthesis, characterization, and photocatalytic performance of ZnO, CuO, and Zn–Cu binary oxide nanoparticles. The objectives include: (1) synthesizing individual ZnO and CuO nanoparticles and their binary Zn–Cu oxide composites using solution combustion and thermal decomposition of oxalate precursors; (2) characterizing the synthesized nanoparticles in terms of structural, morphological, and compositional properties using techniques such as Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy, X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD), and Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM); and (3) evaluating the photocatalytic performance by investigating the degradation of OG dye under UV irradiation. The results will enhance understanding of the relationship between synthesis conditions, nanoparticle properties, and photocatalytic performance, with implications for applications in water purification, dye degradation, and environmental remediation.

Beyond dye degradation, photocatalysis using ZnO and CuO nanoparticles has applications in the degradation of other hazardous organic pollutants, including pharmaceuticals, pesticides, and volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which pose significant environmental and health risks. ROS generated during photocatalytic processes, such as superoxide (O2−) and hydroxyl (OH−) radicals, play a crucial role in the mineralization of these pollutants, converting them into less toxic byproducts. Studies have shown that ZnO and CuO nanoparticles are effective in the photocatalytic degradation of pharmaceuticals like ibuprofen and diclofenac, which are commonly found in wastewater and contribute to pollution.16,17 These materials also show promise in the photocatalytic degradation of VOCs, which are responsible for poor air quality.18

ZnO and CuO-based photocatalysts also hold potential for environmental remediation in air purification applications. For example, they can be employed to remove indoor air pollutants such as formaldehyde and benzene, which are emitted by building materials and furnishings. The photocatalytic degradation of these volatile organic compounds using ZnO and CuO under UV light has shown significant effectiveness in reducing their concentrations, contributing to cleaner indoor environments.19–21 Furthermore, the long-term stability and low cost of ZnO and CuO make them attractive materials for scalable applications, particularly in developing regions where access to clean water and air remains a critical challenge.22

Another promising application of ZnO and CuO in photocatalysis is in the field of solar energy conversion.23–25 These materials can be integrated into photocatalytic water splitting systems for hydrogen production, a clean and sustainable energy source. The photocatalytic water splitting process involves the generation of electron–hole pairs under UV light, leading to the separation of water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen. ZnO, with its wide bandgap, is particularly effective in capturing UV light, making it suitable for this application. Moreover, ZnO–CuO composite systems enhance the photocatalytic activity by broadening the absorption spectrum to include visible light, thereby improving the efficiency of hydrogen production.26,27 CuO, with its narrow bandgap, contributes to efficient charge carrier separation and the stabilization of the photocatalyst, further boosting the overall hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) efficiency.

Although ZnO, CuO, and ZnO/CuO photocatalysts have been widely investigated, systematic comparisons between wick-assisted solution combustion and oxalate precursor thermal decomposition remain limited. In this study, ZnO, CuO, and Zn–Cu mixed oxides were synthesized using these two simple and cost-effective routes and evaluated for the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G under UV irradiation. Structural, morphological, and optical properties were characterized by SEM, STM, FT-IR, and XRD, while the photocatalytic mechanism was investigated using EPR spectroscopy and radical trapping experiments. The results provide insight into the relationship between synthesis method, crystallinity, reactive oxygen species generation, and photocatalytic performance, offering guidance for the development of efficient ZnO–CuO photocatalysts for environmental remediation.

Results and discussion

Characteristic results of nano ZnO

The particle size analysis of ZnO through Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM) and Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) reveals notable differences, reflecting the distinct capabilities of these techniques. The STM images, as shown in Fig. 1a and b, clearly indicate that the individual particle diameters are below 10 nm, with higher magnification enabling precise resolution of individual particles. In contrast, the SEM images are presented in Fig. 1c and d show a particle size distribution for ZnO ranging between 60–70 nm. This variation in observed particle sizes underscores the inherent differences in the spatial resolution and sensitivity of the two methods.

(a) and (b) high-resolution STM images of ZnO showing individual particle diameters below 10 nm with distinct particle boundaries observed at high magnification. (c) and (d) SEM images of ZnO illustrating a broader particle size distribution in the range of 60–70 nm, highlighting the differences in resolution and particle size measurements between the two techniques.

STM is renowned for its exceptional resolution, capable of measuring particle dimensions with sub-nanometer accuracy, extending to an impressive lower limit of 0.01 nm. This high precision is attributed to its quantum tunneling mechanism, which provides atomic-scale sensitivity and enables detailed topographical mapping of nanoscale materials. On the other hand, SEM, while a robust tool for morphological characterization, operates at a lower resolution threshold. It is optimally suited for analyzing larger scale structures and provides reliable size measurements for particles exceeding 500 nm, but its ability to accurately resolve features in the nanometer range is limited.

The discrepancy in particle size measurements between STM and SEM reflects their respective instrumental capabilities and limitations. While SEM offers a broader overview of particle morphology, its lower resolution may lead to an overestimation of particle sizes in nanoscale materials, especially when fine structural details are not adequately resolved. Conversely, STM provides a more accurate and precise measurement of particle size in the nanometer regime, making it an indispensable tool for characterizing nanostructured materials with high fidelity. The particle size measurements obtained via STM are inherently more reliable for ZnO at the nanoscale due to its superior resolution and precision. The findings underscore the critical importance of selecting appropriate characterization techniques tailored to the size regime of the material under investigation. STM emerges as the preferred method for nanoscale characterization, offering unparalleled accuracy and precision for detailed structural analysis.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the prepared nano ZnO sample reveals a predominantly hexagonal wurtzite structure, as indicated by the distinct peaks corresponding to various crystallographic planes. The prominent peak at 36.285° (2θ) with a d-spacing of 2.473 Å, associated with the (101) plane, exhibits the highest intensity (100.00), suggesting that this crystallographic orientation is most abundant and well-ordered within the sample. Additionally, the peaks observed at 31.789° (2θ) and 34.463° (2θ), corresponding to the (100) and (002) planes, demonstrate significant intensity (58.64 and 46.92, respectively), further confirming the crystallinity and structural integrity of the material. However, the peaks at higher angles (47.561°, 56.539°, 62.898°, and 67.911°) exhibit progressively lower intensities, indicative of a reduced abundance or less perfect ordering of these higher–index planes such as (102), (110), (103), and (112). These variations in intensity suggest that the sample is predominantly composed of grains oriented along specific crystallographic directions, with preferential growth along the (101) direction, while other planes exhibit lesser crystallographic alignment, which may be attributed to defects or grain boundary scattering. Overall, the XRD data reflect a high degree of crystallinity in the sample, with specific planes exhibiting more prominent ordering, aligning with typical characteristics of ZnO nanostructures.

Fig. 3 presents the FT-IR spectra of ZnO, revealing distinct vibrational bands that confirm the structural composition and surface chemistry of the synthesized nanoparticles. The prominent absorption band at approximately 437 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of Zn–O bonds, indicating the presence of ZnO nanoparticles. Within the 1000 to 4000 cm−1 range, a series of absorption peaks emerge, which are associated with carboxylate (COO−) and hydroxyl (OH) functional groups present on the nanoparticle surface.

FT-IR spectra of ZnO nanoparticles. The spectrum shows a peak at 437 cm−1 for Zn–O stretching, and bands at 1630 cm−1 and 3432 cm−1 corresponding to surface-bound carboxylate and hydroxyl groups, respectively.

A specific peak observed at 1630 cm−1 corresponds to the asymmetric stretching vibrations of zinc carboxylate, suggesting the presence of surface-bound carboxylate groups. Meanwhile, a broad band centered at approximately 3432 cm−1 is attributed to the O–H stretching vibrations of adsorbed water molecules, confirming the hygroscopic nature of ZnO nanoparticles and the presence of water absorption on their surface. As the size of the nanoparticles increases, there is a noticeable reduction in the intensity of the carboxylate and hydroxyl bands, indicating a decrease in the content of surface-bound impurities. It suggests that as the size of the ZnO nanoparticles grows, the number of surface-bound impurities (carboxylate and hydroxyl groups) becomes less pronounced. The carboxylate groups are likely derived from reactive carbon-containing species during the synthesis process, while the hydroxyl groups originate from the inherent hygroscopic nature of ZnO nanoparticles. Thus, the FT-IR spectra confirm the presence of surface-bound impurities, including carboxylate and hydroxyl groups, on ZnO nanoparticles.

Photocatalytic degradation of Orange Green dye using CuO catalyst: a comprehensive study

Step 1: adsorption phase (Dark Phase)

The adsorption phase is conducted in the absence of light, allowing the dye molecules to adhere to the surface of the CuO catalyst. This phase is critical, as it sets the stage for the subsequent photocatalytic degradation by providing initial adsorption of the dye onto the catalyst surface.

Initial Absorbance (0 minutes): 0.450, corresponding to the dye concentration in the solution before any adsorption.

Absorbance at 60 minutes: 0.375, signifying the equilibrium point, where the dye has adsorbed onto the CuO catalyst. During this phase, a decrease in absorbance indicates the gradual uptake of the dye molecules by the catalyst surface.

The adsorption equilibrium is attained after 60 minutes, where the rate of dye adsorption onto the CuO catalyst equals the rate of desorption, resulting in a stable absorbance value of 0.375. This phase occurs in the dark, ensuring that no degradation takes place during this time.

Step 2: reference dark adsorption run (No Light)

In this phase, no light is exposed to the solution, and only adsorption occurs. After 60 minutes, the absorbance remains unchanged at 0.375, confirming that no degradation happens in the absence of UV light. This serves as a baseline for comparison with the photocatalytic degradation phase.

Step 3: photocatalytic degradation under UV light

Once the adsorption equilibrium is reached, the dye solution is exposed to UV light, initiating the photocatalytic degradation process. Copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles are known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals (*OH) and superoxide anions (O2−), upon UV excitation. These reactive species facilitate the breakdown of the dye molecules, leading to a decrease in absorbance over time.

Initial absorbance (at 0 minutes): 0.375, following the adsorption phase, marking the starting point for photocatalytic degradation.

The photocatalytic degradation of Orange Green dye was found to be highly efficient with CuO as the photocatalyst, achieving significant dye removal (85.11%) after 120 minutes of UV exposure. The initial decrease in absorbance during the adsorption phase indicates a strong interaction between the dye molecules and the CuO catalyst surface. As the photocatalytic process progresses under UV light, the CuO catalyst generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), which interact with and degrade the dye molecules, leading to their reduction in concentration in the solution.

The data also highlight the essential role of light in enhancing the degradation process. The lack of degradation in the absence of light (reference dark adsorption run) confirms that the observed dye removal is predominantly due to the photocatalytic action of CuO under UV irradiation. This observation is consistent with the well-established understanding that photocatalytic degradation is a light-dependent process that significantly enhances the removal of organic contaminants in aqueous solutions.

The CuO photocatalyst demonstrates substantial efficacy in the photocatalytic degradation of Orange Green dye under UV light. The experimental data clearly indicate that the degradation occurs via a two-step process: initial adsorption of the dye onto the catalyst followed by photocatalytic degradation under UV irradiation. The degradation efficiency, reaching 85.11% after 120 minutes, underscores the potential of CuO as an effective photocatalyst for wastewater treatment applications, particularly for the removal of synthetic dyes. This study provides valuable insight into the role of CuO in environmental remediation, specifically in the context of photocatalytic processes for the degradation of organic pollutants in water. The dark adsorption experiment confirms that adsorption contributes to the initial removal of OG before UV irradiation. The subsequent decrease in dye concentration under UV irradiation in the presence of CuO is consistent with photocatalytic degradation.

To directly verify the reactive oxygen species involved in the photocatalytic process, EPR measurements were performed using DMPO as a spin-trapping agent. As shown in Fig. 13, no significant DMPO–˙OH or DMPO–˙O2− signals were detected under dark conditions. In contrast, characteristic EPR signals assigned to hydroxyl radicals and superoxide radicals appeared under UV irradiation. These results provide direct experimental evidence that both ˙OH and ˙O2− are generated during photocatalytic degradation and support the proposed reaction mechanism.

To further identify the dominant reactive species involved in the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G, radical trapping experiments were carried out using ammonium oxalate (AO), benzoquinone (BQ), and isopropanol (IPA) as scavengers for photogenerated holes (h+), superoxide radicals (˙O2−), and hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), respectively. As shown in Fig. 4, the photocatalytic degradation efficiency decreased in the presence of all scavengers, confirming that these reactive species participate in the degradation process. Among the three scavengers, IPA caused the greatest suppression of photocatalytic activity, indicating that hydroxyl radicals are the primary oxidative species responsible for Orange G degradation. The reductions observed with AO and BQ further demonstrate that photogenerated holes and superoxide radicals also contribute to the photocatalytic reaction, although to a lesser extent than hydroxyl radicals. These results are in good agreement with the EPR measurements and provide direct experimental evidence supporting the proposed photocatalytic mechanism (Fig. 14).

Mechanism

The proposed photocatalytic mechanism is experimentally supported by both EPR spectroscopy and radical trapping experiments.A plausible photocatalytic mechanism is proposed based on the experimental results obtained in this study together with the well-established photocatalytic behavior of ZnO and CuO reported in the literature. The proposed mechanism is intended to illustrate the possible generation of reactive oxygen species during the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G under UV irradiation and should not be interpreted as direct experimental evidence for a specific charge-transfer pathway or band alignment.

In the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G (OG) using CuO under UV light, the photocatalyst absorbs photons, generating electron–hole pairs (e−/h+). The photogenerated electrons (e−) react with oxygen (O2) adsorbed on the CuO surface, forming superoxide radicals (˙O2−), while the photogenerated holes (h+) oxidize water molecules, producing hydroxyl radicals (˙OH). These reactive oxygen species, particularly hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), attack the OG dye molecules, leading to their degradation. Superoxide radicals (˙O2−) and photogenerated holes (h+) also contribute to the process but to a lesser extent. The CuO catalyst remains relatively stable over multiple cycles, though minor surface deactivation may occur.

Comparison with existing manuscripts and research in the literature

We observed that commercial ZnO outperforms the prepared ZnO in photocatalytic degradation of Orange Green (OG), a result that aligns with findings from previous studies in the literature. Specifically, it was highlighted that amorphous ZnO, which typically has a lower crystallinity and higher surface area, shows superior photocatalytic activity compared to crystalline forms.28 The enhanced surface area of amorphous ZnO facilitates better adsorption of organic pollutants, which is crucial for efficient photocatalysis. On the other hand, it was emphasized that the crystalline ZnO directly impacts its photocatalytic performance, with highly crystalline ZnO exhibiting reduced surface area and fewer surface defects, which hinder adsorption and limit degradation efficiency.29 This observation is consistent with the lower photocatalytic activity of the prepared ZnO.

Similarly, the bimetallic ZnO–CuO composites in our study showed lower effective photocatalytic degradation compared with commercial ZnO–CuO mixture. This trend is consistent with previous work, where ZnO–CuO composites with amorphous or lower-crystallinity ZnO–CuO systems exhibited improved photocatalytic activity due to enhanced charge separation and surface reactivity.30,31 In contrast, this finding demonstrated that bimetallic ZnO–CuO composites with higher crystallinity and more ordered structures had lower photocatalytic efficiencies, similar to what we observed with the prepared bimetallic ZnO–CuO composite.32 The reduced porosity and lower adsorption capacity of the crystalline phases likely contribute to the diminished photocatalytic activity in our prepared composite, highlighting the importance of controlling the crystallinity and phase composition to optimize photocatalytic performance.

The photocatalytic behavior of CuO in our study is also comparable with previous findings, where highly crystalline CuO exhibits poor photocatalytic performance due to its reduced surface area and limited active sites for adsorption.33 In contrast, smaller and less crystalline CuO particles exhibited superior photocatalytic activity for organic dye degradation, confirming that structural modifications, such as controlling particle size and crystallinity, significantly affect photocatalytic efficiency.34,35 These studies support our observation that the commercial CuO, with its more crystalline form, is less effective in degrading OG, while the prepared CuO, which featured monoclinic phase or smaller particle sizes, shows better catalytic performance. This comparison underscores the critical role of material design, particularly in terms of crystallinity and phase composition, in enhancing photocatalytic performance. Overall, these comparisons suggest that material characteristics, particularly crystallinity, particle size, and phase composition, play important roles in determining photocatalytic behavior. However, further investigations, such as electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements and radical scavenging experiments, are required to directly verify the dominant active species and clarify the detailed photocatalytic mechanism.

Experimental

Synthesise of ZnO nanoparticles by using lampwick

ZnO nanoparticles were prepared using Zn(OAc)2·2H2O as the source of Zn, and a mixture of ethanol and ethylene glycol (60 : 40 V/V) as the solvent in presence of atmospheric O2. In an experiment 1.6462 g of Zn(OAc)2·2H2O was dissolved in 250 mL of solvent and the resulting solution was taken inside a spirit lamp having a cotton lampwick. The lamp was fired and after a few moments some ZnO nanoparticles were produced on the top of the lampwick as yellow substance. When the fire blew out, the yellow colour turned white. The product was collected with some incompletely burned carbon in a crucible.

Carbonaceous residue may form during combustion because of the incomplete oxidation of the cotton lampwick under ambient atmospheric conditions. To minimize these residues, the collected product was calcined in a muffle furnace at 600 °C for approximately 5 h under an oxygen atmosphere until a constant mass was obtained. This post-calcination treatment promotes oxidation of residual carbon and improves the purity of the ZnO product. The weight of the product was 0.523 g. This process was repeated several times to prepare ZnO nanoparticles.

Oxidation of carbon residue:C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g)

The final product after heating is ZnO with removed carbon residue.

Preparation of nano Bi metallic oxide (mixture of ZnO & CuO)

The starting materials were Copper (II) nitrate, axalic acid (OA), zinc acetate, ethanol. Zinc acetate and copper nitrate were added to ethanol in a two-neck flask giving a 0.3 M blue solution. The temperature was raised to 50 °C and after 30 min of continuous stirring, oxalic acid (OA) was rapidly added to the solution. The molar ratio Zn/Cu : OA was 1. The system was kept at 50 °C under reflux for 2 h and a blue precipitate was obtained; then the resulting viscous gel (viscous gel arises from both the chemical nature of the precipitate and the presence of solvent within it before it's dried and calcined) was dried at 80 °C overnight. The dried Zinc/Copper oxalate was ground and calcined at 450 °C for 2 h. The prepared sample has been labeled as a mixture of ZnO & CuO.

Thermal decomposition to form oxides:

After calcination, the resulting mixture is composed of zinc oxide (ZnO) and copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticles.

Synthesise of CuO nano particle by using lampwick

CuO nanoparticles were prepared using Cu(OAc)2.H2O as the source of Cu, and a mixture of ethanol and ethylene glycol (60 : 40 V/V) as the solvent in presence of atmospheric O2. In an experiment 1.6322 g of Cu(OAc)2·H2O was dissolved in 250 mL of solvent and the resulting solution was taken inside a spirit lamp having a cotton lampwick. The lamp was fired and after a few moments some CuO nanoparticles were produced on the top of the lampwick as black substance. The product was collected with some incompletely burned carbon in a crucible. Carbonaceous residue may form during combustion because of the incomplete oxidation of the cotton lampwick under ambient atmospheric conditions. The collected product was therefore calcined at 600 °C for approximately 5 h under an oxygen atmosphere until a constant mass was reached to oxidize and substantially reduce residual carbon impurities before characterization. Finally, 0.496 g CuO was obtained. This process was repeated several times to prepare CuO nanoparticles.

Photocatalytic degradation of OG by different photocatalysts

A specific mass of the prepared nano metal oxide catalyst (e.g., ZnO, CuO, etc.) was added to the 100 mL of OG dye solution. The amount of catalysts can vary depending on the catalyst used, typically ranging from 0.1 g to 1 g per 100 mL solution. To investigate the photocatalytic degradation of OG using prepared nano metal oxide and corresponding commercial metal oxide, 100 mL of the experimental solution was taken in a beaker, and it was placed under UV lamp enclosed in a wooden box with continuously stirred by a magnetic stirrer. The solution was irradiated for 1–1.5 hours. During irradiation, a definite volume of irradiated solution was withdrawn after a definite interval of time to determine the concentration of dye and pH was measured. The sample was analyzed by UV-visible spectrophotometer. The same experimental procedure was repeated using a commercial metal oxide under identical reaction conditions. The photocatalytic degradation efficiency and rate were compared to those of the synthesized nano metal oxides.

Prior to UV irradiation, the catalystdye suspension was stirred in the dark to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The suspension was then exposed to UV light to evaluate the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G. The actual power density of the light source used during the photocatalytic experiments was determined to be 0.10 W cm−2. This was measured using a Thorlabs S120VC power meter positioned at a distance of 10 cm from the sample. The total power output of the light source was 10 W, and the illuminated area was 100 cm2. These measurements ensure accurate assessment of the photocatalytic efficiency under controlled irradiation conditions.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements

Reactive oxygen species generated during the photocatalytic degradation of Orange G were detected by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) as the spin-trapping agent. The catalyst suspension was prepared under the same conditions as those used for the photocatalytic degradation experiments. After the addition of DMPO, the suspension was irradiated with UV light and the EPR spectra were recorded under both dark and UV-irradiated conditions at room temperature to identify hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) and superoxide radicals (˙O2−).

Conclusion

In the present investigation, copper oxide (CuO), zinc oxide (ZnO), and mixed copper–zinc oxides were synthesized using various methods and characterized using techniques including Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and photocatalytic degradation experiments. ZnO nanoparticles were successfully synthesized via a solution combustion method utilizing lampwick as a fuel source. STM analysis revealed particle diameters below 10 nm, while SEM imaging indicated a particle size range of 60–70 nm. FTIR analysis identified the presence of surface impurities. XRD analysis established a hexagonal structure with a zincite phase.

The bimetallic oxide was synthesized through the thermal decomposition of an oxalate precursor. STM and SEM analyses showed particle sizes below 20 nm and within the range of 50–70 nm, respectively. XRD analysis revealed the coexistence of the hexagonal structure of ZnO and the monoclinic structure of CuO. Nano-sized CuO was prepared using the combustion of copper acetate with lampwick as fuel. STM analysis indicated particle diameters below 10 nm, while SEM revealed cluster formations with particle sizes ranging from 100–180 nm. XRD analysis identified the material's primary phase as monoclinic CuO.

The crystal structure and morphology of ZnO and CuO can significantly influence photocatalytic performance. Commercial ZnO may have more favorable crystalline structures that help with efficient charge separation and transport, while synthesized materials may have more defects or less optimized structures, leading to lower photocatalytic efficiency. Morphological differences, such as the size and shape of the nanoparticles or the presence of certain crystal facets, can also affect the surface area available for dye adsorption, as well as the charge carrier dynamics, both of which are crucial for effective photocatalysis.

The photocatalytic degradation performance of the synthesized oxides for Orange Green (OG) dye was compared with that of commercial oxides under identical experimental conditions. Commercial ZnO exhibited superior photocatalytic efficiency compared to the synthesized ZnO, while commercial CuO was ineffective in degrading OG. Structural analysis revealed that commercial ZnO was highly amorphous, whereas synthesized ZnO was predominantly crystalline. The observed difference in photocatalytic performance may be associated with variations in crystallinity, morphology, and surface characteristics. The prepared bimetallic oxide showed less efficiency on degradation of OG within an hour, whereas the commercial oxide demonstrates superior performance under similar conditions. On the other hand, synthesized CuO exhibited superior photocatalytic efficiency compared to the commercial CuO. The combined XRD, EPR and radical trapping results provide direct experimental evidence for the proposed photocatalytic mechanism and demonstrate that hydroxyl radicals are the dominant reactive species responsible for Orange Green degradation.
