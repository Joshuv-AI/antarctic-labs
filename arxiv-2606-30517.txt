Convergence towards Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations with a focus on Diestel–Leader graphs
Matteo D’ACHILLE & Ali KHEZELI

Abstract
We provide necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence towards a unique IPVT on any proper pointed measured metric space. The conditions are that the volume function, when composed with log \log , is regularly varying and that the limit of the uniform probability measure on a large ball exists in the horocompactification. As an application we prove convergence towards a unique IPVT for higher rank symmetric spaces, which solves an open problem of [ 18 ] . Versions of this theorem are provided for graphs and edge-measured graphs, where a natural parameter ξ \xi appears. We prove independence on ξ \xi in a specific sense under mild assumptions, which answers an open problem of [ 13 ] . As a main example, we show that the latter holds for the IPVT of Diestel-Leader graphs. We also focus on further properties of this example, in particular, that its IPVT cells are distinguishable, providing the first Cayley graph with this property. Keywords: Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellation (IPVT); Diestel–Leader graphs; regularly varying function; horoboundary MSC (2020): 60D05, 60G55, 51F99

Ideal Poisson–Voronoi tessellations.

In this section, we provide the general definition of IPVT and the horoboundary. These definitions build on the abstract deterministic framework of

2.1
Boundary and Extended Boundary

Let

‌ be a boundedly-compact (sometimes called proper or Heine-Borel, see

30

) metric space; i.e., every bounded closed subset of

‌ is compact. Fix an element

‌ and call it the

A‌

‌ and

denote the space of shifted distance functions, which is a subset of the space

of 1-Lipschitz functions on

\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(E)
by regarding every

equivalent to
f+t
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathrm{SD}(E)/\mathbb{R}
‌ and
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(E)/\mathbb{R}
denote the quotients of
\mathrm{SD}(E)
‌ and
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(E)
by this equivalence relation and equip them with the quotient topology. For every

f(\cdot)-f(\mathbf{o})
is the unique function in the equivalence class of
f
‌ whose value at
\mathbf{o}
is zero. So,
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}(E)/\mathbb{R}
is homeomorphic to

The mapping

Remark 2.1

(
Non-Quasi-Isometry-Invariance
)
.

The horoboundary is not invariant under quasi-isometries. For example, if
E
is the vertex set of a graph, then
\partial E
is totally disconnected (since the space of integer-valued elements of
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}(E)
is totally disconnected). When
E
is the graph of a mosaic of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
by congruent regular
p

\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}<1/2
, then
\partial E
is different from
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
(the latter being homeomorphic to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
). This might seem counter intuitive since a regular mosaic is quasi-isometric to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. As a result, the horoboundary is not quasi-isometry-invariant.

As another example, the horoboundary of a Cayley graph depends crucially on the choice of the generating set of the group. For instance, if

and
G_{2}
are the Cayley graphs of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
corresponding to the generating sets

and
\{(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\}
respectively, then

has 4 non-isolated points, while
\partial G_{2}
has 6 non-isolated points.

2.2
Limits of the Poisson-Voronoi diagram

Now, assume a boundedly-finite (i.e., Radon) Borel measure

is given on

such that
\mu(E)=\infty
. Throughout, we will make use of the
volume function
: it is the non-decreasing, right-continuous non-negative function defined by

F(r)\coloneqq\mu(B_{r}(\mathbf{o})),

where
B_{r}(\mathbf{o})
is the closed
d
-ball of radius

centered at
\mathbf{o}
.

Definition 2.2

(
Nuclei Process
)
.

For each
0<\lambda<\infty
, the
nuclei process
is a marked Poisson point process

with intensity measure
\lambda\mu
and with uniform i.i.d. marks
5
5

5

We don’t use a symbol for the marks because they are only used to break the ties shortly.
in the unit interval
[0,1]
. Given the origin
\mathbf{o}
, sort the points of
X^{(\lambda)}
‌ by increasing distance from
\mathbf{o}
; i.e.,
X^{(\lambda)}_{1}
is the point nearest to
\mathbf{o}
,
X^{(\lambda)}_{2}
is the second nearest point to
\mathbf{o}
, and so on. If there are points with equal distance to

In fact, one can forget the marks in the unordered version. Given the nuclei process
X^{(\lambda)}
, our goal is to study the limit as
\lambda\downarrow 0
of its Voronoi diagram, whose definition we recall now.

Definition 2.3

(
Voronoi cells and Diagram
)
.

Given a sequence
\Phi\coloneqq(f_{i})_{i\geq 1}
in
\widehat{\partial E}
, the
Voronoi cells

(C_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are defined by

C_{i}\coloneqq\left\{y\in E\,;\;f_{i}(y)\leq f_{j}(y),\;\forall j\geq 1\right\}\;.

The
Voronoi diagram
of

is
\mathrm{Vor}(\Phi)\coloneqq(C_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.

Note that Voronoi cells can overlap in general. The
strict Voronoi cells
are disjoint sets defined by the strict inequality, namely
C_{i}^{\circ}\coloneqq\{y\in E\,;\;f_{i}(y)<f_{j}(y),\forall j\neq i\}
.
6
6

6

Another option is to break the ties using the marks of the points, as in
[

14
]

. The Voronoi diagram (or just

.
Note that the strict Voronoi cells are open provided that
(f_{i})_{i}
is

13
]

. Admissibility also implies that the cells
(C_{i})_{i\geq 1}

(but may have large overlaps).

To study limits of Voronoi diagrams, as in
[
24

\mathcal{F}(E)

denotes the space of nonempty closed subsets of

[
28

for more detail on this topology. In addition, one might consider the pair

(together with the mapping between the nuclei and the cells, which is omitted in the notation) as a

(see

).

Alternatively, as in
[
13
]

as a random element of

(while keeping the order of
(f_{i})_{i\geq 1}
) and use the notion of convergence in the product topology on
(\mathcal{F}(E)\cup\{\emptyset\})^{\mathbb{N}}

2.5
below). Actually, under some natural conditions, the two topologies are identical; see
3.7
and
3.8
.

Definition 2.4

(
IPVT
)
.

Let
X^{(\lambda)}
be the nuclei process as in
2.2
. Assume
\lambda_{n}\to 0
is a sequence for which
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda_{n})})
is convergent (as a point process of closed subsets). Then, the limit is called an
ideal Poisson-Voronoi tessellation (IPVT)
of
(E,\mu)
. If, in addition,
\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})

of

.

For example, in

(equipped with the Lebesgue measure), the IPVT exists and is trivial: It contains a single cell that occupies the whole

. But this is not the case in hyperbolic spaces, as it is recalled later.

Lemma 2.5

(
Automorphism-Invariance
)
.

Any IPVT is a random weak tessellation whose distribution is invariant under the automorphisms of
(E,\mu)
.

Proof.

Under the topology described above, it can be seen that the set of weak tessellations is closed. It implies that any IPVT is a random weak tessellation. Also, the distribution of (the unordered version of)
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
is invariant under the automorphisms of
(E,\mu)
, even if
\mathbf{o}
is not preserved. This implies that any weak limit of
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
is also automorphism-invariant.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

If
E
is a geodesic space,
\theta
is a horofunction and

, one can show that there exists an infinite geodesic
\gamma
starting from

such that
\forall t\geq 0\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\theta(\gamma(t))=\theta(x)-t
. However,

might not always converge to

. This geodesic might be helpful for proving or disproving non-degeneracy (see e.g.,

).

Remark 2.7

(
Star-Like cells
)
.

In every Voronoi diagram, the cells are star-like in the following sense: If
\theta
is the center of a cell
C
and
x\in C
, then
C
contains the set
[x,\theta]\coloneqq\{y\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d(x,y)+d_{\theta}(y)=d_{\theta}(x)\}
.
In particular, if
E
is a graph or a geodesic space and
\theta\in\partial E
, then

2.6
implies that
C
contains an infinite geodesic in

starting from
x
. However,
C
might or might not contain a bi-infinite geodesic.

Remark 2.8
.

It is useful to mention some statements that do not necessarily hold for Voronoi diagrams, even if
E
is a geodesic space: The cells are not necessarily geodesically convex not even connected (see the example in
Section

2.4
). Even if the cells are connected, the intersection of two cells might be disconnected. The closure of a cell might contain points in

[

]
.

2.3
Limits of the Nuclei Process

As mentioned in the introduction, Poisson–Voronoi diagrams may have a nontrivial limit despite that the nuclei escape to infinity. In this subsection, we describe how to
shift
the distance functions, as in
[
13
,
18
]
, to obtain a nontrivial limit of the nuclei process. A detailed description of the topology is given in
Section

3.1
.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the embedding
\iota_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}E\to\mathrm{SD}(E)
defined by

\iota_{t}(x)\coloneqq(y\mapsto d(x,y)-t).

It is clear that
\mathrm{Vor}(\iota_{t}(X^{(\lambda)}))=\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
for all
t
. So, if there exists some (possibly random)
t=t(\lambda)
such that
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})
converges weakly to a nontrivial point process on
\widehat{\partial E}
(see
Section

3.1
for the description of the topology), possibly along a subsequence, this limiting point process might be used as a way to study the limiting behavior of
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
. Note that adding a constant to
t(\lambda)
does not affect the behavior of the tessellation, but it affects the limit of
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})
.

For the above goal, it is important to choose the shift suitably to keep track of the closest point of
X^{(\lambda)}
to the origin. There are two natural choices for
t(\lambda)
:

•

In
[
13
]
, the choice in the general case is
t(\lambda)\coloneqq d(X^{(\lambda)}_{1},\mathbf{o})
, where
X^{(\lambda)}_{1}
is the nucleus nearest to
\mathbf{o}
;

•

In
[
18
]
, and in the present paper,
t(\lambda)
is deterministic and is defined by
t(\lambda)\coloneqq F^{-1}(\lambda)\coloneqq\inf\{r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda\cdot F(r)\geq 1\}
.

The benefit of the first choice is that the delay of
X^{(\lambda)}_{1}
is controlled (preventing it from escaping to
\pm\infty
). The second choice is also natural since
\iota_{t}(X^{(\lambda)})
is a Poisson point process on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
with intensity measure
\lambda\iota_{t}^{*}\mu
, and hence, the measure of
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{\leq 0}
is controlled by the second choice. More importantly, since the second choice does not depend on
\mathbf{o}
, the limiting distributions are automatically invariant under the automorphisms of
(E,\mu)
; see also
2.5
. However, this choice does not a priori prevent the delays from escaping to
-\infty
(which is the case in the Euclidean space).

Nevertheless, it is easy to see that, under nice growth conditions (see
3.4
), the two choices are not much different since
d(X^{(\lambda)}_{1},\mathbf{o})-\inf\{r\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda\cdot F(r)\geq 1\}
is tight. In this case, precompactness of
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})
holds in either definition (
3.4
). Also, under the stronger assumptions of
Theorem

1.2
, full-convergence holds with both definitions, see also
3.12
.

Remark 2.9
.

There are some examples where
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
converges while
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})
cannot fully converge for any choice of
t(\lambda)
, see
Theorems

1.5
and
4.14
.

2.4
On the relation between horofunctions, Busemann Functions and the Visual Boundary of CAT(0) Spaces

In any metric space
E
, if
\gamma
is an infinite geodesic, then the function
x\mapsto\lim_{t\to\infty}d(x,\gamma(t))-t
is well defined and is called the
Busemann function
corresponding to
\gamma
. If the limit is uniform on bounded subsets of
E
(which holds in graphs and in CAT(0) spaces), then every Busemann function is a horofunction. But not necessarily all horofunctions are Busemann functions, even if
E
is a proper geodesic metric space. In the last case, for every
\theta\in\partial E
, there exists an infinite geodesic
\gamma
such that
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{o}
and
d_{\theta}(\gamma(t))=-t,\forall t
, but
\gamma
does not necessarily converge to
\theta
; e.g., when
E=\mathbb{R}^{2}
is endowed with the
L^{1}
metric. Another example is given by
E=\cup_{n\geq 1}C_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
C_{n}
is the square whose opposite corners are the points
(0,0)
and
(n,n)
, and the distance is given by the shortest-path metric. Clearly, there is no geodesic that converges to
\theta\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}(n,n)\in\partial E
. In this case,
d_{\theta}
is not a Busemann function.

However, in a complete CAT(0) space, the two notions are equivalent:
[
9
, Theorem II.8.13]
shows indeed that the horofunctions are precisely the Busemann functions and the horoboundary
\partial E
, defined in
Section

2
, coincides with the
visual boundary
of
E
. The latter is the set of equivalence classes of infinite geodesic rays
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,\infty)\to E
, where
\gamma
is equivalent to
\gamma^{\prime}
if
\sup d(\gamma(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t))<\infty
. See
Section

5.1
for more on the IPVT of CAT(0) spaces.

General Convergence results

3.1
Subsequential Convergence and Pre-compactness

As discussed in
Section

2.3
, the shifted nuclei process
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})
is used to study the limiting tessellations.
As mentioned therein, we need to prevent the delays from escaping to
-\infty
. For this goal, we
compactify
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}\equiv\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times\mathbb{R}
at delay
-\infty

\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[-\infty,\infty)

\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[-\infty,\infty)
(e.g.,
\iota_{t(\lambda)}(X^{(\lambda)})

2.2

[
13

5
]
consider the map from
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times\mathbb{R}
to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[0,\infty)
defined by
(f,\delta)\mapsto(f,e^{\delta})
and consider the vague topology for measures on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[0,\infty)
.

We always study the limits of the shifted nuclei processes when they are regarded as point processes on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[-\infty,\infty)
. This is described further as follows. Let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of Borel measures
\varphi
on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
such that
\varphi(\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{\leq t})<\infty,\forall t
. Let
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
be the space of Radon measures on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[-\infty,+\infty)

‌ to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}\times[-\infty,+\infty)

can be identified with a subset of

(those elements of

that vanish on

).

Definition 3.1

(
Topology on
\mathcal{M}
)

We always equip
\mathcal{M}
with the topology induced from the vague convergence on
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.

Roughly speaking, this is the vague topology at

and the weak topology at
-\infty
.
This allows one to define the weak convergence of probability measures on
\mathcal{M}
or
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
and weak convergence of point processes on

Proposition 3.2

(
Converging Subsequences
)
.

Assume that
t_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\lambda_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lambda_{n}\to 0
. Then, the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

The shifted Poisson point process
\iota_{t_{n}}(X^{(\lambda_{n})})
converges weakly as
\lambda\downarrow 0
to a point process on
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
with respect to the topology of
\mathcal{M}
, and its limit is nonempty with positive probability.

(ii)

The sequence of measures
\lambda_{n}\iota_{t_{n}}^{*}\mu
‌ converges in
\mathcal{M}
and its limit is nonzero.

If these conditions hold, then:

(iii)

t_{n}\to\infty
and the measure
\nu\coloneqq\lim_{n}\lambda_{n}\iota_{t_{n}}^{*}\mu
is supported on
\widehat{\partial E}
.

(iv)

The weak limit of
\iota_{t_{n}}X^{(\lambda_{n})}
is a (marked) Poisson point process on
\widehat{\partial E}
with intensity measure
\nu
, namely
\Phi\coloneqq(\Theta_{i},\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.

(v)

[Convergence of Ordered Points]
Assuming that either
E
is a graph (with the graph-distance metric) or that the delays
(\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are distinct a.s., if the points
(\Theta_{i},\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are ordered with the same rule as in
2.2
, then the joint distribution of the ordered sequence of points
\left(\iota_{t_{n}}X^{(\lambda_{n})}_{i}\right)_{i\geq 1}
converges to that of
(\Theta_{i},\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

The equivalence of
(i)
and
(ii)
is implied by the fact that
\iota_{t_{n}}X^{(\lambda_{n})}
is a Poisson point process with intensity measure
\mu_{n}\coloneqq\lambda_{n}\iota_{t_{n}}^{*}\mu
. Assume that these conditions hold.

(iii)
. If
t_{n}\not\to\infty
, one might pass to a subsequence and assume
t_{n}\leq t,\forall n
. For all
t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
\mu_{n}\left(\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{\leq t^{\prime}}\right)=\lambda_{n}F(t_{n}+t^{\prime})\leq\lambda_{n}F(t+t^{\prime})
. This implies that
\nu=0
, which is a contradiction.
One also has
\mu_{n}(B_{r}(\mathbf{o})\times\mathbb{R})=\lambda_{n}\mu(B_{r}(\mathbf{o}))\to 0
for every

. This implies that
\nu
is supported on
\widehat{\partial E}
.

(iv)
. Since
\mu_{n}
converges weakly to
\nu
, one obtains that
\mathrm{Poiss(\mu_{n})}
also converges weakly to
\mathrm{Poiss}(\nu)
, where
\mathrm{Poiss(\cdot)}
denotes a Poisson point process with intensity measure
(\cdot)
.

(v)
. The claim is implied by the fact that the marks of the points are distinct a.s. and the ordering procedure is continuous on the event where the delays are distinct (note that, for the last continuity to hold, it is important that we have considered the vague topology on
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
to prevent points from escaping to
-\infty
). If
E
is a graph, the argument is similar and there is no need for having distinct delays (for the mentioned continuity, it is enough that the marks are distinct).
∎

Proposition 3.3

(

)
.

In

(i)

If either

Proof.

(ii)

(i)
.
By Skorokhod’s representation theorem, there exists a coupling of

and
\Phi

\iota_{t_{n}}X^{\lambda_{n}}\to\Phi
a.s. If

For the next proposition, we choose the natural shift mentioned in

3.2

In this subsection, we prove
Theorem

1.2
, which provides a criterion for full-convergence of the shifted Poisson point process

as

(not along an unknown subsequence). This criterion builds on (a logarithmic version of) the theory of regularly varying functions (see

[

Definition 3.10

(
Log-regularly-varying functions
)
.

A function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is
log-regularly-varying
if

\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n+1)/f(n)
exists and is positive. This clearly implies that
\forall r_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n+r_{0})/f(n)=e^{br_{0}}
for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, which is called the
index
of
f
. We also use
p\coloneqq e^{b}
in the formulas.

Similarly, a measurable function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is called
log-regularly-varying
(at
+\infty
) if
f\circ\log
is regularly-varying; i.e., for every
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, the limit
\lim_{r\to\infty}f(r+r_{0})/f(r)
exists and is positive.
By well-known results about regularly-varying functions, the limit is equal to
e^{br_{0}}
‌ for some
b\in\mathbb{R}
, which is called the
index
of
f
. Hence,
f(r)=l(r)e^{br}
, where
l
is a
long-tail
function (see
[
17
, Definition 2.14]
); i.e.,
\lim_{r\to\infty}l(r+r_{0})/l(r)=1,\forall r_{0}
.

\displaystyle p_{1}^{j}\leq

Remark 4.8

Lemma 4.9

(
Poisson VS Bernoulli
)
.

Let
(G,\mu)
be a vertex-measured graph that satisfies the growth conditions of
3.4
. For each
\lambda>0
, let
X^{(\lambda)}
be a Poisson point process with intensity measure
\lambda{\mu}
and let
Y^{(\lambda)}
be a Bernoulli point process on
G
with probabilities
x\mapsto\min\{1,\lambda\mu(x)\}
. Then,
d_{P}(\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)}),\mathrm{Vor}(Y^{(\lambda)}))\to 0
as
\lambda\to 0
(
d_{P}
being the Prokhorov distance), provided that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu^{2}(B_{n}(\mathbf{o}))/\mu(B_{n}(\mathbf{o}))^{2}=0
, where
\mu^{2}(\cdot)\coloneqq\left[\sum_{x\in\cdot}\mu(x)^{2}\right]
. As a result,
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
and
\mathrm{Vor}(Y^{(\lambda)})
have the same subsequential weak limits.

Note that if
\mu
is the counting measure on
G
, then the assumption of this lemma always holds (assuming the growth conditions of
3.4
).

Proof.

We construct a coupling of
X^{(\lambda)}
and
Y^{(\lambda)}
as follows. Let
(U(x))_{x\in G}
be i.i.d. uniform random variables in the interval
[0,1]
. For each
x\in G
, let
x\in Y^{(\lambda)}
if
U(x)>1-\min\{1,\lambda\mu(x)\}
. Let the multiplicity of
x
in
X^{(\lambda)}
be zero if
U(x)<e^{-\lambda\mu(x)}
, be 1 if
e^{-\lambda\mu(x)}\leq U(x)<(1+\lambda\mu(x))e^{-\lambda\mu(x)}
, and be larger than 1 otherwise. It follows that the multiplicities of
x
in
X^{(\lambda)}
and
Y^{(\lambda)}
are different with probability at most
c_{1}(\lambda\mu(x))^{2}
, for some constant
c_{1}
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
and let
A_{\epsilon}
be the event that the restrictions of
\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda)})
and
\mathrm{Vor}(Y^{(\lambda)})
to the ball
B_{1/\epsilon}(\mathbf{o})
are identical. We will prove that
\mathbb{P}\left[A_{\epsilon}\right]\geq 1-2\epsilon
for small enough
\lambda
. This implies the claim. For this, it is enough to show that, with probability at least
1-2\epsilon
, the closest points of
X^{(\lambda)}
and
Y^{(\lambda)}
to
\mathbf{o}
are identical, namely at distance
\Delta_{1}
, and that
X^{(\lambda)}
and
Y^{(\lambda)}
are identical in
B_{\Delta_{1}+2/\epsilon}(\mathbf{o})
.

Let
t_{\epsilon}(\lambda)\coloneqq\min\{n\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda F(n)\geq-\ln\epsilon\}
and
t^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)\coloneqq t_{\epsilon}(\lambda)+2/\epsilon
. Assume for simplicity that the growth conditions of
3.4
hold with
r_{0}=1
(the general case is similar). One gets for small enough
\lambda
that
\lambda F(t^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\lambda))\leq\lambda F(t_{\epsilon}(\lambda))(p_{2}+\epsilon)^{2/\epsilon}\leq-(\ln\epsilon)(p_{2}+\epsilon)^{1+2/\epsilon}
. So, the assumption in Lemma
4.9
implies that
\lambda^{2}\mu^{2}(B_{t^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)})
converges to zero as
\lambda\to 0
. Hence, the previous paragraph implies that
X^{(\lambda)}
and
Y^{{(\lambda)}}
are identical in
B_{t^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(\lambda)}(\mathbf{o})
with high probability. Also, one has
\mathbb{P}\left[\Delta_{1}>t_{\epsilon}(\lambda)\right]\leq e^{\ln\epsilon}=\epsilon
. This implies that
\mathbb{P}\left[A_{\epsilon}\right]\geq 1-2\epsilon
for small enough
\lambda
, and the claim is proved.
∎

In this subsection, we prove an extended version of
Theorem

1.4
, which gives a convergence criterion for
weighted edge-measured graphs
(not necessarily bipartite).
Here is the formal definition of the latter. Let

be a given undirected connected locally-finite graph in which every edge

. We allow multiple edges and self-loops here. For each edge

be the quotient of the disjoint union

when gluing an endpoint of

with an endpoint of

with the quotient metric. Let also

corresponding to

To avoid ambiguity, let

centered at

and

The above paragraph implies that

Theorem

1.2

cannot exist for any function

‌ and

4.3.1
The relation of
\partial E
and
\partial G
for edge-measured graphs

It can be seen that a sequence in

converges in
\overline{G}
‌ if and only if it converges in
\overline{E}
. This induces a topological embedding of
\partial G
into
\partial E
. Assume
(x_{n}y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a sequence of edges such that
\iota(x_{n})\to f\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}
and
\iota(y_{n})\to g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0}
. If
d(\mathbf{o},y_{n})=d(\mathbf{o},x_{n})\eqqcolon m_{n}
(i.e.,
x_{n}y_{n}
is a
same-level edge
), then the image
\iota_{m_{n}}(x_{n}y_{n})
of this edge converges to the set of functions of the form
\mathcal{E}_{0}^{f,g,t}\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
,

, where

\mathcal{E}_{0}^{f,g,t}\coloneqq\left[(f+t)\wedge(g+1-t)\right]\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{t\wedge(1-t)}\;.

If
d(\mathbf{o},x_{n})=d(\mathbf{o},y_{n})-1\eqqcolon m_{n}
(i.e.,
x_{n}y_{n}
is an
inter-level edge
), then
\iota_{m_{n}}(x_{n}y_{n})
converges to the set of functions of the form
\mathcal{E}_{1}^{f,g,t}
, where

\mathcal{E}_{1}^{f,g,t}\coloneqq\mathcal{E}_{0}^{f,g+1,t}=\left[(f+t)\wedge(g+1+(1-t))\right]\in\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{t}\;.

By regarding
\mathcal{E}_{0}
and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
as maps from
(\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{0})^{2}\times[0,1]
to
\mathrm{Lip}_{1}
, we have obtained:

Lemma 4.10

(
\partial E
vs
\partial G
)
.

Let
K_{0}\subseteq(\partial G)^{2}
(resp.
K_{1}\subseteq(\partial G)^{2}
) be the set of
(f,g)\in(\partial G)^{2}
such that there is a sequence of same-level edges (resp. inter-level edges)

in which
\iota(x_{n})\to f
and
\iota(y_{n})\to g
as

. Then,
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
are compact subsets of
(\partial G)^{2}
and

\partial E=\partial G\cup\pi\left(\mathcal{E}_{0}(K_{0}\times[0,1])\right)\cup\pi\left(\mathcal{E}_{1}(K_{1}\times[0,1])\right),

(4.10)

where

is the projection

.

Note that

is a subset of
\{(f,g)\in(\partial G)^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\sup\left|f(\cdot)-g(\cdot)\right|\leq 1\}

We now study the limiting measures on
\partial E
. Let
\mathrm{edges}(x,y)
denote the set of edges with endpoints
\{x,y\}
.
Let
\alpha_{1}^{n}
be the measure on

defined by

\alpha_{1}^{n}(x,y)\coloneqq\sum_{e\in\mathrm{edges}(x,y)}w(e).

(4.11)

Let also
\alpha_{0}^{n}
be the measure on
S_{n}(G,\mathbf{o})^{2}
defined by

\alpha_{0}^{n}(x,y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in\mathrm{edges}(x,y)}w(e),&\text{$x\neq y$},\\
\sum_{e\in\mathrm{edges}(x,x)}w(e),&\text{$x=y$.}\end{cases}

(4.12)

By pushing forward under the map

(\overline{G})^{2}
.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
E
be the weighted edge-measured graph corresponding to a graph
G
. Assume
\nu=\lim_{n}\lambda_{n}\iota_{t_{n}}^{*}\mu
, where
\mathbb{Z}\ni t_{n}\to\infty
.
Then, by passing to a subsequence if necessary, the measures
\alpha_{0}\coloneqq\lim_{n}\lambda_{n}\iota^{*}\alpha_{0}^{t_{n}}

K_{1}
and

respectively, and

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\nu\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{[0,1]}}}=\mathcal{E}_{0}{}^{*}\left(\alpha_{0}\otimes\mathrm{Leb}\right)+\mathcal{E}_{1}{}^{*}\left(\alpha_{1}\otimes\mathrm{Leb}\right).

(4.13)

Roughly speaking,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\nu\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{[0,1]}}}
is a combination of the Lebesgue measure on the intervals of the form
\{(f,g)\}\times[0,1]
, where
(f,g)
is an element of
K_{0}
or

. Also, unless in exotic examples, there is no need to pass to a subsequence.

Proof.

By pre-compactness and passing to a subsequence, we may assume that

and
\alpha_{1}\coloneqq\lim_{n}\lambda_{n}\iota^{*}\alpha_{1}^{t_{n}}
exist.
Since

is continuous and linear on the intervals

, so is

. Therefore,
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\nu\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{[0,1]}}}
is the limit of
\lambda_{n}{\left.\kern-1.2pt(\iota_{t_{n}}^{*}{\mu})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathrm{Lip}_{1}^{[0,1]}}}
. The latter is the sum of
\lambda_{n}\mathcal{E}_{0}{}^{*}\left(\iota^{*}(\alpha_{0}^{t_{n}})\otimes{\mathrm{Leb}}\right)
and
\lambda_{n}\mathcal{E}_{1}{}^{*}\left(\iota^{*}(\alpha_{1}^{t_{n}})\otimes{\mathrm{Leb}}\right)
.

∎

Theorem 4.14

(

)

\mathbb{R}
with intensity measure
\beta^{(\xi)}
. Note that
\nu_{1}
is supported on
\partial G
, and hence,
\Theta_{i}\in\partial G
a.s.
In addition, a point
x\in E
is in the
i
’th cell if and only if
\forall j\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}d_{\Theta_{i}}(x)+\Delta_{i}\leq d_{\Theta_{j}}(x)+\Delta_{j}
. If

{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathrm{Vor}(\Phi)\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{G^{1/2}}}
is determined by

and

. Thus, the claim is implied by
4.15

The last claim is implied by
Theorem

4.12
as follows. Let

have the same distribution as
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathrm{Vor}(\Phi)\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{G^{1/2}}}
which does not depend on
\xi
. If the claim is false, then there exists a sequence
\lambda^{\prime}_{n}\to 0
such that
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda^{\prime}_{n})})\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{G^{1/2}}}

We now provide the main ingredient needed to complete the proof of
Theorem

1.5
.
The key is that the terms depending on
\xi
are cancelled in a
“magic telescopic sum” (see (
4.16
) below), which explains the independence on
\xi
under our hypotheses.

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
\tilde{\varphi}
be a continuous function such that
\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{\varphi}(s)ds=1
.
Let
(\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}
with intensity measure
\beta^{(\xi)}
, where the latter is defined in (
4.15
). Then, the distribution of
(\lfloor\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}\rfloor)_{i,j\geq 1}
does not depend on
\xi
.

Proof.

We first prove that the distribution of the difference of the delays w.r.t. the first point, that is,
\bar{\Delta}_{1}\coloneqq(\lfloor\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{1}\rfloor)_{i\geq 1}
, does not depend on
\xi
. The latter is determined by the probability of the events of the form

A\coloneqq\left\{\forall 0\leq k<m\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\left|(\Delta_{i})_{i\geq 1}\cap(\Delta_{1}+k,\Delta_{1}+k+1)\right|=n_{k}\right\},

where
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{0},\ldots,n_{m-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq 0}
are given integers. By conditioning on
\Delta_{1}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}\left[(\Delta_{i})_{i}\cap(-\infty,t)=\emptyset\right]\times\mathbb{P}\left[\left|(\Delta_{i})_{i}\cap(t,t+dt)\right|=1\right]

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{k}\mathbb{P}\left[\left|(\Delta_{i})_{i}\cap(t+k,t+k+1)\right|=n_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int e^{-\beta^{(\xi)}(-\infty,t)}\beta^{(\xi)}(t,t+dt)\prod_{0\leq k<m}e^{-\beta^{(\xi)}(t+k,t+k+1)}\frac{\left(\beta^{(\xi)}(t+k,t+k+1)\right)^{n_{k}}}{n_{k}!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{1}\int\beta^{(\xi)}(t,t+dt)e^{-\beta^{(\xi)}(-\infty,t+m)}\prod_{0\leq k<m}\left(\beta^{(\xi)}(t+k,t+k+1)\right)^{n_{k}},

where
c_{1}\coloneqq\prod_{k}1/{n_{k}!}
. In the following lines, each time a variable
c_{i}
is used, it means a constant that does not depend on
\xi
(but depends on the other variables).
If
t=j+s
, where
j\in\mathbb{Z}
‌ and
0\leq s<1
, then
\beta^{(\xi)}(t,t+dt)=(p-1)p^{j+\xi}\tilde{\varphi}(s)ds
and
\beta^{(\xi)}(-\infty,t+k)=p^{j+k+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))
, where
H(s)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{\varphi}(u)du
. So,
\beta^{(\xi)}(t+k,t+k+1)=(p-1)p^{j+k+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))
, and thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{1}p^{j+\xi}e^{-p^{j+m+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))}\prod_{0\leq k<m}\left(p^{j+k+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))\right)^{n_{k}}\tilde{\varphi}(s)ds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{3}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(p^{j+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))\right)^{n}e^{-p^{j+m+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))}p^{j+\xi}\tilde{\varphi}(s)ds,

where
n\coloneqq\sum_{k}n_{k}
.
Use the change of variables
u\coloneqq p^{j+m+\xi}(1+(p-1)H(s))
, which is valid since
H
has a continuous derivative by assumption. By
du=(p-1)p^{j+m+\xi}\tilde{\varphi}(s)ds
, one gets

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[A\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{4}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{p^{j+m+\xi}}^{p^{j+m+1+\xi}}u^{n}e^{-u}du=c_{4}\int_{0}^{+\infty}u^{n}e^{-u}du=c_{4}\cdot(n!).

(4.16)

This clearly does not depend on
\xi
. So, it is proved that the distribution of
\bar{\Delta}_{1}
does not depend on
\xi
.

To complete the proof, it remains to show that the distribution of
\bar{\Delta}\coloneqq(\lfloor\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{j}\rfloor)_{i,j\geq 1}
conditional on
\bar{\Delta}_{1}
does not depend on
\xi
. First, we study the conditional distribution of
\bar{\Delta}
given
\Delta_{1}
and
\bar{\Delta}_{1}
. Assume
\Delta_{1}=j+s
, where
j=\lfloor\Delta_{1}\rfloor
, and
\forall i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}z_{i}\coloneqq\lfloor\Delta_{i}-\Delta_{1}\rfloor
.
Note that, given this information, one has
\forall i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\Delta_{i}\in[z_{i}+j+s,z_{i}+j+1+s)
. By the definition of
\beta^{(\xi)}
, the restriction of
\beta^{(\xi)}
to this interval is a multiple of the measure
\beta_{s}
shifted by
z_{i}+j
, where
\beta_{s}
is the measure on
[s,s+1)
with density function
\tilde{\varphi}(t)1_{[s,1]}(t)+p\tilde{\varphi}(t-1)1_{[1,s+1)}(t)
(note that
\beta_{s}
does not depend on
\xi
). Now, construct
s_{2},s_{3},\ldots\in[s,s+1)
i.i.d. with distribution
\beta_{s}
. For each
m\geq 0
, shuffle
\{s_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}z_{i}=m\}
by sorting them in increasing order, and let
s^{\prime}_{2},s^{\prime}_{3},\ldots,
be the result. Finally, define
\Delta_{i}\coloneqq\Delta_{1}+z_{i}+s^{\prime}_{i}
for every
i\geq 2
. It can be seen that this constructs
\Delta_{2},\Delta_{3},\ldots,
with the right distribution conditionally on
\Delta_{1}
and
\bar{\Delta}
. Unfortunately, the distribution of
(s^{\prime}_{2},s^{\prime}_{3},\ldots)
depends on
s
and the distribution of
s=\{\Delta_{1}\}
depends on
\xi
. However, note that the order of
s^{\prime}_{2},s^{\prime}_{3},\ldots
(from smallest to largest) determines
\bar{\Delta}
. Since
(s_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. with a diffuse distribution, one obtains that, for every
M
, the order of
(s^{\prime}_{i})_{\{i\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}z_{i}\leq M\}}
is uniform among all possible orders (given
\Delta_{1}
and
(z_{i})_{i\geq 1}
), and this distribution does not depend on the triplet
(j,s,\xi)
. This implies that the distribution of
\bar{\Delta}
conditionally on
\bar{\Delta}_{1}
does not depend on
\xi
, and the proof is completed.
∎

Remark 4.16
.

Here is the problem with the off-diagonal points of
K_{0}
and
K_{1}
. Assume
f,g\in\widehat{\partial G}
such that
\sup\left|f(x)-g(x)\right|\leq 1
,
f\neq g
,
f\neq g+1
and
f\neq g-1
. So, there exist adjacent vertices
x
and
y
such that
f(x)-g(x)\neq f(y)-g(y)
. In this case, it can be seen that, for
h_{t}\coloneqq(f+t)\wedge(g+(1-t))
, the function
t\mapsto h_{t}(x)-h_{t}(y)
is not constant, and hence,
h_{t}
depends on
t
. So, if
\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha_{1}
are not supported on the diagonal, we cannot deduce that
\nu_{t}
is independent of

in general, which is essential in the proof of
Theorem

4.14
.

The next lemma gives an intuitive criterion for proving that
\alpha_{1}
is supported on the diagonal. In the statement, by a
1\times n
rectangle
, we mean vertices
(x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2})
such that
d(x_{1},y_{1})=d(x_{2},y_{2})=1
,
d(x_{1},x_{2})=d(y_{1},y_{2})=n
and
d(x_{1},y_{2})=d(x_{2},y_{1})=n+1
.

Lemma 4.17

(Thin Rectangles)
.

In the setting of
Theorem

4.12
, assuming
\alpha_{0}=0
,
\alpha_{1}
is supported on the diagonal if and only if for every edge
e
, the number of
1\times n
rectangles with side
e
, divided by
F(n)
, converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

For simplicity of the proof, we prove the claim in the bipartite case.
For every edge
e=(x,y)
, let
R_{n}(e)
be the set of edges
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
that form a
1\times n
rectangles together with
e
, and let
R(e)
be the closure of
\cup_{n}R_{n}(e)
. It can be seen that the set of off-diagonal elements of
K_{1}
can be written as
\cup_{e}R(e)
. So,
\alpha_{1}
is supported on the diagonal if and only if
\forall e\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\alpha_{1}(R(e))=0
. Since
R(e)
is clopen, the latter is equivalent to
\forall e\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lim_{n}\alpha_{1}^{n}(R(e))=0
. This easily implies the claim.
∎

Examples and Applications of the General Results

In this section, we apply the general results of

3
and
4
to prove convergence towards IPVT in various settings. We also study in

further properties of the IPVT of regular trees and their products. The case of Diestel-Leader graphs will be studied in

To use the general theorems, usually the more difficult part is proving that the normalized restriction of
\mu
to a large ball converges. This task is highly model-dependent. For this goal, we will use the following lemma frequently, which helps proving the convergence by partitioning the ball into some
layers
or
regions
:

Lemma 5.1

(
Weak Convergence via Linear Decomposition
)
.

(i)

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mu_{n}
be a probability measure on a separable metric space
E_{1}
. Assume
\mu_{n}=\sum_{i}c_{n,i}\mu_{n,i}
, where
c_{n,i}\geq 0
and
\mu_{n,i}
is a probability measure. Assume also that, for all
i
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{n,i}=c^{\prime}_{i}
and
\lim_{n}\mu_{n,i}=\mu^{\prime}_{i}
in the weak topology. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}=\mu^{\prime}\coloneqq\sum_{i}c^{\prime}_{i}\mu^{\prime}_{i}\;.

(ii)

In the previous part, assume
\mu_{n}=\int k_{n}(y,\cdot)\alpha_{n}(dy)
, where
\alpha_{n}
is a probability measure on a measurable space
E_{2}
and
k_{n}
is a Markov kernel from
E_{2}
to
E_{1}
. Assume also that
||\alpha_{n}-\alpha||_{\mathrm{TV}}\to 0
and
\lim_{n}k_{n}(y,\cdot)=k(y,\cdot)
(in the topology of weak convergence) for all
y\in E_{2}
. Then,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mu_{n}=\mu^{\prime}\coloneqq\int k(y,\cdot)\alpha(dy)\;.

Proof.

The first statement is a special case of the second one.
We use the Prokhorov metric
d_{P}
for weak convergence.
Fix
\epsilon>0
. For Borel sets
A\subseteq E_{1}
, let
A_{\epsilon}\coloneqq\cup_{x\in A}B_{\epsilon}(x)
. It is enough to show that, for large enough
n
, one has
\forall A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mu^{\prime}(A_{\epsilon})\leq\mu_{n}(A)+\epsilon
. Let
\epsilon_{n}(y)\coloneqq d_{P}(k_{n}(y,\cdot),k(y,\cdot))
. One has

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(A_{\epsilon})=\int k(y,A_{\epsilon})\alpha(dy)\leq\int\left(k_{n}(y,A)+\epsilon_{n}(y)\right)\alpha(dy).

Since
\epsilon_{n}(y)\leq 1
, bounded convergence implies that
\int\epsilon_{n}(y)\alpha(dy)\to 0
. For the other term, since
k_{n}(y,A)\leq 1
, one has

\displaystyle\int k_{n}(y,A)\alpha(dy)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int k_{n}(y,A)\alpha_{n}(y)+||\alpha_{n}-\alpha||_{\mathrm{TV}}=\mu_{n}(A)+||\alpha_{n}-\alpha||_{\mathrm{TV}}.

This implies the claim.
∎

One can extend

, assuming that the kernels

5.1
CAT(0) Spaces

Recall from
Section

2.4
that, in complete CAT(0) spaces, the horoboundary coincides with the visual boundary. We now obtain the following useful criterion for non-degeneracy of Voronoi diagrams. In this result, a metric space is called
non-branching
if every geodesic has at most a unique prolongation, see
e.g.

[
2
, Definition 2.1]
.

Lemma 5.3

(
Non-degeneracy in CAT(0) spaces
)
.

Let
E
be a proper non-branching CAT(0) space (in particular, any CAT(0) Riemannian manifold). If
(f_{i})_{i\geq 1}
is an admissible sequence of distinct horofunctions, then
\mathrm{Vor}((f_{i})_{i\geq 1})
is non-degenerate.

Proof.

If
x\in C_{i}
, let
\gamma_{i}
be the unique geodesic starting from
x
such that
f_{i}(\gamma_{i}(t))=f_{i}(x)-t
. The triangle inequality implies that, if
f_{i}\neq f_{j}
, then
f_{j}(\gamma_{i}(t))\geq f_{j}(x)-t\geq f_{i}(x)-t
. If equality holds for some
t>0
, then the geodesic
{\left.\kern-1.2pt\gamma\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{[0,t]}}
has two different extensions, which violates the assumption. This implies that
\gamma(t)\in C_{i}^{\circ}
for every
t>0
and the claim is proved.
∎

Corollary 5.4

(
Convergence towards IPVT in CAT(0) Spaces
)
.

Let
E
be a proper non-branching CAT(0) space (in particular, any CAT(0) Riemannian manifold). Under the assumptions of
3.2
, if
\nu
is diffuse, then
\mathrm{Vor}(\Phi)
is non-degenerate a.s. Hence,
\lim_{n}\mathrm{Vor}(X^{(\lambda_{n})})
exists and is equal to

.

Proof.

If
\nu

are distinct a.s. So, the proof is implied by

.
∎

(ii)

\theta^{\prime}
to
\partial T\setminus\{\omega_{0}\}\subseteq\partial DL(p,q)
.
Define the
trunk
of
C^{\prime}
as the set of
(x,x^{\prime})\in C^{\prime}
that have a unique geodesic towards
\theta^{\prime}
; i.e.
x^{\prime}\in[\theta^{\prime}\omega^{\prime}_{0}]
. These geodesics make a family tree structure in the trunk. If we choose
C^{\prime}
as a typical cell that contains
(\mathbf{o},\mathbf{o}^{\prime})
from the beginning (as described in the proof of
6.9
), then the trunk becomes a unimodular random family tree. We should prove that the trunk is one-ended. By the general classification of unimodular family trees provided in
[
3
]
, it is enough to show that the level-sets of the trunk are infinite a.s. This is also implied by the stationarity of
\Psi
under the action of the stabilizer of
h
similarly to the proof of
6.9
.

Finally, if
C^{\prime}
contains a bi-infinite geodesic between two other points of
\partial DL(p,q)
, one can deduce that
C^{\prime}
contains a geodesic between
\theta^{\prime}
and another ideal point. So, by the previous arguments, no such geodesics exist.
∎

7
Problems and future directions

Question 7.1
.

Is there a transitive graph (or a Cayley graph) with log-regularly-varying growth, where full convergence of
Theorem

1.3
does not hold? Recall that the volume function of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}*\mathbb{Z}_{3}
is not log-regularly-varying, see
[
6
, Figure 2.7]
.

Question 7.2

(
Other point processes of nuclei
)
.

Is it possible to build a non-trivial random ideal Voronoi diagram via low-intensity limits (in some sense) of Voronoi tessellations of other point processes different from the IPVT? A natural candidate to consider in dimension 2 is the
\alpha
-hyperbolic ensemble considered in
[
11
]
in the limit
\alpha\downarrow 0
.

Question 7.3

(
Product with
L^{1}
metric more general than
5.9
)
.

Without the condition in item
(i)
of
5.9
, what can be said about full convergence towards
{\rm IPVT}
? It seems that the limiting behavior of
F(r)/F^{\prime}(r)
would play an important role to address this question.

Question 7.4

(
Product with
L^{2}
metric
)
.

Under what conditions the produc of two metric spaces endowed with the
L^{2}
metric satisfies
Theorem

1.2
or
Theorem

1.3
? And what would be the limiting measure in this case? We believe that under some mild conditions the limit is identical to the limit with the weighted
L^{1}
metric discussed in
5.9
.

Question 7.5

(
Edge-measured product graphs
)
.

When does the edge-weighted version of the product of two graphs
G\times G^{\prime}
satisfy full convergence of
Theorem

4.12
? It seems that the limit of the uniform measure on large balls in both vertex measured and edge measured versions play a role.
