Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation for Periviable Pregnancy With a Fetus In Situ
Authors: Mohit Pahuja, Khushboo Agarwal, Abby Russell, Benjamin Chou, Koichi Nomoto, Nana Toyoda, Shinobu Itagaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal cardiac arrest at periviable gestation presents unique physiologic and logistical challenges. Evidence guiding the use of extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) in pregnancy remains limited, particularly relative to emergent cesarean delivery, a Class I guideline recommendation.Case SummaryA 32-year-old woman at 24 weeks and 5 days’ gestation with newly diagnosed intrauterine fetal demise presented in refractory cardiogenic shock and suffered pulseless electrical activity arrest. Resuscitative hysterotomy was not feasible in the catheterization laboratory. Given absent return of spontaneous circulation and preexisting large-bore arterial access from an Impella, venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation was initiated for ECPR. After stabilization, the pregnancy was managed expectantly with coagulopathy monitoring. She was decannulated on hospital day 7, delivered spontaneously on day 11, and discharged home on day 60 with improved left ventricular function.Take-Home MessageWhen rapid cannulation is feasible, ECPR may be lifesaving while maternal stabilization and individualized obstetric planning remain paramount.

History of Presentation

A 32-year-old previously healthy woman at 24 weeks and 5 days’ gestation presented to an outside hospital with a 5-day history of progressive nausea, vomiting, and dyspnea. On arrival she was in supraventricular tachycardia to the 200s. Multiple doses of adenosine were ineffective; procainamide and amiodarone were administered, after which she developed hemodynamic compromise. She was intubated and started on multiple vasopressors. Bedside echocardiography demonstrated severe biventricular dysfunction with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 5% to 10%. An Impella CP was placed for presumed cardiogenic shock. Fetal cardiac activity had been present earlier in the day but was absent immediately before Impella placement; repeat bedside obstetric ultrasound confirmed absence of fetal cardiac activity consistent with new intrauterine fetal demise (IUFD). She was transferred to our institution for further management. On arrival she remained hypotensive with persistent Impella suction alarms and minimal forward flow. Shortly thereafter she developed pulseless electrical activity (PEA) arrest.

Past Medical History

Her pregnancy had been uncomplicated until the diagnosis of IUFD. She had no prior cardiac disease.

Differential Diagnosis

At the time of presentation, the differential diagnosis included tachyarrhythmia-induced cardiomyopathy, fulminant myocarditis, peripartum cardiomyopathy, massive pulmonary embolism, sepsis-related cardiomyopathy.

Management

Cardiac arrest and decision regarding resuscitative hysterotomy

Following the initial PEA arrest, cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was performed and transient return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) achieved. She remained profoundly hypotensive in cardiogenic shock. The team elected to transport the patient to the cardiac catheterization laboratory to initiate extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) cannulation. On arrival she suffered a second PEA arrest on the stretcher; ROSC was not achieved within 4 minutes. Although she met conceptual American Heart Association criteria for resuscitative hysterotomy (≥24 weeks and no ROSC by 4 minutes), the procedure was not feasible in the cath-lab environment because obstetric personnel and equipment were not immediately available, the team was not prepared to perform an emergent hysterotomy in that setting, and ongoing CPR on a narrow stretcher made safe operative exposure impractical. In addition, severe biventricular failure suggested that mechanical circulatory support would be required for maternal survival.

Resuscitative hysterotomy (perimortem cesarean delivery) is recommended because rapid evacuation of the gravid uterus can relieve aortocaval compression and improve venous return and cardiac output, and thereby enhance the effectiveness of chest compressions and the likelihood of ROSC; it also offers the opportunity to deliver a potentially viable fetus.

Extracorporeal CPR and Venoarterial ECMO cannulation

Because large-bore femoral arterial access was already present from the Impella CP, the team transitioned to venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) for extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR). Left femoral venous access was obtained during ongoing CPR on the stretcher; the venous cannula was advanced without fluoroscopic or echocardiographic guidance (blind wire technique) into the right atrium. ECMO flow was established promptly and ROSC followed shortly thereafter.

Although a gravid uterus can compress the inferior vena cava and theoretically impede venous return or wire passage, standard wire and cannulation techniques permitted passage into the right atrium and establishment of effective ECMO flows. This practical feasibility, combined with preexisting arterial access in our patient, facilitated execution of ECPR in an anatomically challenging case.

Post-ECMO management and decision regarding delivery

After stabilization, a multidisciplinary team including cardiology, cardiac surgery, obstetrics, maternal-fetal medicine, critical care, and hematology reviewed management options. Given confirmed IUFD, periviable gestation, the bleeding risk associated with systemic anticoagulation on ECMO, and absence of obstetric indications for immediate delivery such as disseminated intravascular coagulation, uterine hemorrhage, or infection requiring extraction, expectant management was chosen. Systemic anticoagulation was maintained with unfractionated heparin targeting an activated partial thromboplastin time of 50 to 70 seconds. Heparin was selected over direct thrombin inhibitors to allow rapid reversibility in the event of hemorrhagic complications, which was a key consideration given the presence of IUFD and the anticipated risk of coagulopathy. Given the risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation associated with retained IUFD, close laboratory surveillance was performed, including serial platelet count, fibrinogen levels, and coagulation parameters, along with frequent clinical assessment for bleeding.

The patient remained hemodynamically supported on ECMO while serial laboratory monitoring for coagulopathy and infection was performed. Cardiac function recovered gradually and she was decannulated on hospital day 7.

Spontaneous delivery and recovery

On hospital day 11 she experienced spontaneous labor and delivered the fetus in the intensive care unit without hemorrhagic or infectious complications. She was extubated on hospital day 12. Her cardiac function continued to improve with initiation of guideline-directed medical therapy; she was discharged home on hospital day 60 with LVEF 43%.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 3-month follow-up she reported NYHA functional class II symptoms with stable hemodynamics and no neurologic deficits. Echocardiography demonstrated near-normal LVEF (50%-55%) and normal chamber sizes (Video 2). Genetic evaluation performed during follow-up revealed a pathogenic TTN truncating variant, a known risk factor for dilated and peripartum cardiomyopathy, providing a plausible substrate for her fulminant biventricular failure.

Discussion

Cardiac arrest in pregnancy is one of the most challenging emergencies; it carries disproportionately high mortality and is infrequently encountered by most providers.1,2 Importantly, many causes of maternal cardiac arrest are potentially reversible with common etiologies being pulmonary embolism, amniotic fluid embolism, hemorrhage, sepsis, and cardiomyopathy, and these conditions are often amenable to temporary mechanical cardiopulmonary support. ECMO provides effective cardiopulmonary support for many of these reversible causes and has been associated with favorable maternal outcomes in systematic reviews and registry reports, although reported cases remain heterogeneous and subject to reporting bias.3 In the present case, a cardiomyopathic process was felt to be the primary driver of arrest. Although fulminant myocarditis and tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy were initially considered, the absence of inflammatory findings, subsequent recovery of ventricular function, and discovery of a pathogenic TTN truncating variant made peripartum cardiomyopathy with tachyarrhythmia-triggered decompensation the most likely working diagnosis. Although cardiac arrest has been reported among indications for ECMO in pregnancy, true ECPR with an ongoing pregnancy, particularly in the presence of IUFD or periviable gestation, remains exceedingly uncommon.3 As a result, data guiding optimal timing of delivery and resuscitative strategy in these scenarios are limited.

The 2015 American Heart Association Scientific Statement emphasizes maternal-first resuscitation and recommends emergent cesarean delivery when no ROSC is achieved within minutes at a viable gestation,4 whereas the contemporary 2025 Extracorporeal Life Support Organization guidance endorses consideration of ECPR in pregnant and peripartum patients and suggests ECPR may be performed during or immediately after perimortem cesarean delivery.5 These recommendations are grounded in physiologic rationale and should remain central to institutional protocols.

Real-world constraints matter. Simultaneous execution of high-quality chest compressions, an emergent hysterotomy, and rapid ECMO cannulation can be chaotic and technically challenging, particularly in nonobstetric environments lacking prepared personnel and equipment. If an emergent cesarean delivery, frequently reported to take longer than the guideline-recommended 5 minutes,6 would meaningfully delay definitive mechanical circulatory support, that delay could compromise maternal resuscitation. When an ECMO team is immediately available and large-bore vascular access can be obtained without undue delay, prompt initiation of VA-ECMO may preserve maternal perfusion more reliably than attempting an emergent delivery that would postpone mechanical support. From a fetal perspective, remaining in utero while the mother is rapidly and stably supported by ECMO may be preferable to an emergent, premature, and unsterile delivery when the fetus is periviable or nonviable.

Timing for ECPR activation is context dependent. In general practice, ECPR is considered within the first 30 minutes of high-quality CPR when reversible causes are suspected and resources permit; earlier activation is associated with better outcomes. In pregnant patients with suspected reversible cardiopulmonary causes and immediate ECMO capability, earlier consideration may be reasonable to avoid prolonged hypoperfusion. These thresholds must be individualized to the clinical scenario and institutional capabilities.

Expectant management of delivery after initiation of ECMO is appropriate when the fetus is nonviable or periviable and there are no obstetric indications for immediate extraction7; this approach prioritizes maternal stabilization while maintaining vigilant multidisciplinary surveillance for evolving hemorrhagic, infectious, or coagulopathic indications to intervene.

Prospective study of these scenarios is unlikely to be feasible. Conclusions

This case demonstrates successful utilization of ECPR in a periviable pregnancy with IUFD, resulting in full maternal recovery. When emergent cesarean delivery is not feasible or would delay definitive mechanical circulatory support, prompt initiation of VA-ECMO may be a reasonable, lifesaving strategy. Institutional preparedness, multidisciplinary coordination, and individualized decision-making are essential.
