Towards a Doubly Efficient 𝖨𝖯 = 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{IP}=\mathsf{PSPACE}
Liyan Chen, Matthew M. Hong, Yael Tauman Kalai, Zoe Xi

Abstract
We show that every language in 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} decidable by a Turing machine in time T ⁡ ( n ) = n O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) T(n)=n^{O(\log n)} admits a doubly efficient interactive proof system: the prover runs in time 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( T ⁡ ( n ) ) \mathsf{poly}(T(n)) , and the verifier runs in time 𝗉𝗈𝗅𝗒 ⁡ ( n ) \mathsf{poly}(n) . This extends the best previously known regime for such proof systems from T ⁡ ( n ) = n O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ​ n ) T(n)=n^{O\!\left(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}\right)} , established by Berger, Goyal, Hong, and Kalai (FOCS 2025), to T ⁡ ( n ) = n O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) T(n)=n^{O(\log n)} . Beyond improving the range of T T , our protocol is substantially simpler than previous doubly efficient proofs for time-bounded 𝖯𝖲𝖯𝖠𝖢𝖤 \mathsf{PSPACE} . Earlier constructions proceed indirectly: they first build batch interactive proofs and then invoke them as a black box to obtain doubly efficient protocols. In contrast, we give a direct construction. This not only simplifies the proof but also points to a more promising route for future improvements.

Introduction

Verification is one of the most fundamental concepts in computer science, and motivated the study of the complexity classes
NP

[
10
]
,
{\mathsf{I}P}

[
13
,
5
]
and
{\mathsf{M}IP}

[
6
,
4
]
, among others. This study has led to foundational concepts in cryptography such as zero-knowledge proofs
[
13
]
, which are used as building blocks in many cryptographic protocols used today. It has also inspired fundamental notions such as probabilistically checkable proofs (PCPs)
[
3
,
11
,
2
,
1
]
,
interactive PCPs
[
14
]
,
and interactive oracle proofs
[
7
,
17
]
, which in turn have led to breakthrough results in hardness of approximation and to the construction of
{\mathsf{S}NARG}
s (succinct non-interactive arguments), which are used in many blockchain applications.

Interactive proofs (
IP
s)

The power of interactive proofs was demonstrated by the celebrated

\textsf{IP}=\mathsf{PSPACE}
theorem
[
15
,
21
]
.
Specifically, it was proven that the correctness of any time-
T
, space-
S
computation can be verified by a
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S,n)
-time verifier, via an interactive proof. However, this comes at a price: The time required by the prover to convince the verifier of the correctness is
2^{\Omega(S\cdot\log S)}
. While in those works the runtime of the prover was not a parameter of interest, and the prover was thought of as being all-powerful (and was even named after the famous wizard Merlin
[
5
]
), this blowup in the prover’s runtime makes these proof systems completely impractical.

Doubly efficient
IP
s

The work of
[
12
]
initiated the study of
doubly efficient

IP
s, in which the prover’s runtime is required to be at most polynomial in
T
(the time it takes to run the underlying computation), and the verifier is required to run in time significantly less than
T
(otherwise, such interactive proofs are trivial).
They constructed a doubly efficient
IP
for any computation that can be performed by a (log-space uniform) circuit of depth
D

and size
T
, where the verifier’s runtime is

D\cdot\mathrm{polylog}{(}T)+\tilde{O}(n)
, and the communication complexity is
D\cdot\mathrm{polylog}{(}T)
, where
n
is the input length.
In particular, this implies an improved

\textsf{IP}=\mathsf{PSPACE}
theorem where the verifier runs in time

{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)+\tilde{O}(n)
, the communication complexity is
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)
, and the prover runs in time

2^{O(S)}
.
This result yields a doubly efficient
IP
for log-space computations.

The next significant advancement was due to Reingold, Rothblum, and Rothblum
[
17
]
, who constructed a doubly efficient
IP
, where the prover’s runtime is
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}T)
and the verifier’s runtime is
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}n)
, for every language computable in polynomial space and time
T=n^{O((\log n)^{\delta})}
for a sufficiently small constant
\delta>0
.
Very recently,
[
8
]
improved this result by constructing a doubly efficient
IP
for every language in polynomial space and time
T=n^{O\!\left(\sqrt{\log n/\log\log n}\right)}
.

The main technical contribution in both of these works is an efficient way to batch unambiguous interactive proofs. An unambiguous interactive proof is an interactive proof with the guarantee that if the prover ever deviates from the unique prescribed strategy, then it will be rejected with high probability. Both results mentioned above
[
17
,
8
]
show that if membership in a language
\mathcal{L}
can be proven via a doubly efficient unambiguous
IP
, then one can prove that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{L}
via a doubly efficient unambiguous
IP
where the verifier runs in time significantly less than running these
k
proofs.
These works then use this batch unambiguous
IP
in a black-box way to construct a doubly efficient
IP
.
This latter part is quite straightforward.
Indeed, the improvement in
[
8
]
is in constructing a more efficient batch unambiguous
IP
, which immediately yields a more efficient doubly efficient
IP
.

1.1
Our Result

We construct a doubly efficient
IP
for every language that is computable in polynomial space and time
T=n^{O(\log n)}
, thus improving both previous results.

Theorem 1

(Our Doubly Efficient
IP
, Informal)
.

There exists a doubly efficient
IP
(i.e. an interactive proof with a
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}T)
prover and a
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}n)
verifier) for every language that is computable in polynomial space and time
T=n^{O(\log n)}
.

More generally, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Our General Doubly-Efficient
IP
, Informal)
.

There exists an
IP
for every language that is computable in time
T
and space
S
, where the prover runs in time
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}T)
and the verifier runs in time
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S,2^{\sqrt{\log T}})
.

Our construction is significantly simpler than the constructions in both of these prior works. In particular, the construction is direct. As opposed to previous works, which use a batch unambiguous
IP
as a black box, we directly batch
{\mathsf{D}TISP}
computations. We believe that this direct construction has the potential to lead to further improvements, while constructions that use batch (unambiguous)
IP
as a black box are subject to barriers. Specifically, to get a doubly efficient
IP
for languages computable in time
T=n^{\omega(\log n)}
and polynomial space using a batch (unambiguous)
IP
as a black box, one would need a “rate-1” batch (unambiguous)
IP
, i.e., one where the communication complexity for proving
k
statements is
(1+o(1))\cdot{\mathsf{c}c}
, where
{\mathsf{c}c}
is the communication complexity of proving a single statement.
Constructing such a rate-1 batch (unambiguous)
IP
seems challenging.
On the other hand, we believe that our protocol points to a more promising route for future improvements.

1.2
Additional Related Work

Our protocol builds on two sub-protocols from prior works. The first is the doubly efficient
IP
for bounded depth circuits from
[
12
]
which we mentioned above.
The second is an “instance reduction” protocol from
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
, which roughly says that if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{L}
is far from being true (i.e., one needs to change, say
d
, of the statements
x_{i}
to make it true), then there is a so-called “instance reduction” protocol that reduces checking that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathcal{L}
to checking that
\tilde{O}(k/d)
of these instances are in
\mathcal{L}
(with an additional linear test on these
\tilde{O}(k/d)
instances). Such an instance-reduction protocol is special case of an interactive proof of proximity (
IPP
)
[
20
]
, which is an efficient protocol that, given an input
x
far from satisfying some predicate
P
, i.e., one needs to change at least
d
coordinates in
x
to satisfy
P
, reduces
verifying that
P(x)=1
to verifying that
P^{\prime}(x_{S})=1
, where
P^{\prime}
is a related predicate and
S
is a subset of
indices of size
\tilde{O}(|x|/d)
.

We also mention that there is a large body of work, starting with
[
9
,
16
]
, on constructing computationally sound proofs, where soundness is guaranteed to
hold only against computationally bounded cheating provers. We do not elaborate on these works
here, since they are less relevant for our work.

2
Technical Overview

In this section, we give an overview of our approach for proving membership in a
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
language, where the verifier is given a Turing machine
M
and two configurations
\mathsf{cf}_{0}
and
\mathsf{cf}_{T}
of size bounded by
S
, and it wishes to verify whether running
M
for
T
steps from
\mathsf{cf}_{0}
reaches
\mathsf{cf}_{T}
.

One natural idea is for the prover to send intermediate configurations: let
\lambda
be a fixed parameter,
1
1

1

We will later discuss how to set this parameter.
and the prover sends

\mathsf{cf}_{T/\lambda},\mathsf{cf}_{2T/\lambda},\ldots,\mathsf{cf}_{(\lambda-1)T/\lambda}

as the
\lambda
intermediate configurations after
\frac{T}{\lambda},\frac{2T}{\lambda},\ldots,\frac{(\lambda-1)T}{\lambda}
steps, respectively. This reduces the task of verifying a
T
-time statement (
\mathsf{cf}_{0}\overset{T}{\to}\mathsf{cf}_{T}
) to the task of verifying
\lambda
many
T/\lambda
-time statements

\mathsf{cf}_{i\cdot T/\lambda}\overset{T}{\to}\mathsf{cf}_{(i+1)\cdot T/\lambda}~\mbox{ for all }~0\leq i<\lambda.

Prior works, starting with
[
17
]
, and continuing with
[
18
,
19
,
8
]
, showed that verifying
k
statements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
is easier than verifying each of them separately. Roughly speaking, this is based on the following initial observation: if we are guaranteed that a constant fraction of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are false, then it suffices to randomly sample a small subset of
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
and perform verification on that subset. Following this observation, previous works on batching (unambiguous)
NP
statements
[
18
,
19
]
and batching (unambiguous) interactive proofs
[
17
,
8
]
manage to cleverly reduce the number of instances by a constant factor, even when initially only a single claim is false, by running sub-protocols that add sufficiently many constraints on the corresponding witnesses
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{k})
, or on the transcripts in the case of (unambiguous)
IP
, such that with these additional constraints many of the statements become false. They then repeat this recipe until they are left with a constant number of instances, which the verifier can verify on its own or via an interactive proof.

In order to obtain a doubly efficient interactive proof for
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
, previous works, as well as this work, first break down the statement into
\lambda
statements, each in
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T/\lambda,S)
. They then apply a batching protocol for these
\lambda
statements, reducing the task to verifying a constant number of statements in
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T/\lambda,S)
. These works then break down each of the remaining statements in
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T/\lambda,S)
into
\lambda
statements, each in
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T/\lambda^{2},S)
, and apply the batching protocol again. This recipe is repeated until
T=O(1)
(or until it is polynomial), in which case the verifier can verify the statements on its own.

What differentiates this work from previous works is the batching protocol used. Previous works used a batch protocol for unambiguous
interactive proofs
, which is conceptually complex and brings a lot of technical difficulties. This work, on the other hand, directly batches
{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements. Our batching protocol recursively reduces both
k
and
T
, and is surprisingly simple.

Core Ingredient: Interactive Proof of Proximity

Before describing our protocol, we first introduce an important ingredient: an Interactive Proof of Proximity (
IPP
).

An
IPP
is a proof system that combines interactive proofs with property testing: a sublinear-time verifier, who can only query a few bits of a huge input
x
, interacts with a prover to decide whether
x
satisfies some property
\Pi
or is far (in Hamming distance) from every string that satisfies property
\Pi
.
Completeness requires that if
x
satisfies property
\Pi
, then the honest prover convinces the verifier to accept with probability
1
. Soundness requires that if
x
is far from satisfying
\Pi
in Hamming distance, no prover strategy will make the verifier accept with more than a small probability.
Previous work
[
19
]
constructs an
IPP
for any low-depth property with communication complexity
\tilde{O}(d)
and query complexity
\tilde{O}(n/d)
, where
d
is the distance threshold corresponding to the soundness guarantee. Furthermore, the
IPP
verifier makes
non-adaptive
queries: it never queries the instance
x
until the end of the interaction, at which point it samples a random set of query indices
Q
(depending on its randomness in previous rounds and this end phase), and the final decision is a low-depth predicate that depends only on
x_{Q}
.

This work uses a generalization of
IPP
from
[
8
]
, called row-
IPP
, which treats the huge input
x
as a large matrix
M
with many rows. The verifier can only query a few rows in
M
, and soundness holds for any
M
with a large row-distance from the property
\Pi
, i.e., for any
M
for which every
M^{\prime}
that satisfies
\Pi
differs from
M
in many rows. Row-
IPP
is known for parameters similar to those of standard
IPP
; specifically, for
n\times m
matrices and distance parameter
d
,
[
8
]
adapts the
IPP
from
[
19
]
into a row-
IPP
for any low-depth property with communication complexity
\tilde{O}(dm)
and query complexity
\tilde{O}(n/d)
. The row-
IPP
verifier also makes
non-adaptive
queries: the final decision is a low-depth predicate that depends on a few rows in
M
.

Batching TISP: A 4-Step Construction.

Our protocol
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k)
for proving
k

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements is as follows.
Assume that we are proving
k
statements
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
, where

x_{i}:\mathsf{cf}_{0}^{i}\overset{T}{\to}\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{T}

is a time-
T
computation. An immediate idea is to apply the famous GKR protocol
[
12
]
to verify the correctness of all
k

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements. However, the verification time of the GKR protocol depends on the
depth
of the computation, which is
O(T{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)\log k)
for the union of
k

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements: The main overhead for applying GKR is
T
. Our main motivation for the following steps is to reduce
T
of the
{\mathsf{TISP}}
statements we need to verify.

We let
w_{i}
be the
\lambda
intermediate configurations, i.e.,

w_{i}=(\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{T/\lambda},\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{2T/\lambda},\ldots,\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{(\lambda-1)T/\lambda}).

Consider the following
k
-by-
\lambda
matrix, denoted by
M
, where the
i
-th row consists of
w_{i}
, and to simplify exposition, we also include in the
i
-th row the statement
x_{i}=(\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{0},\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{T})
in the first and last columns, respectively:

M=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{cf}^{1}_{0}&\mathsf{cf}^{1}_{T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{1}_{2T/\lambda}&\cdots&\mathsf{cf}^{1}_{(\lambda-1)T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{1}_{T}\\
\mathsf{cf}^{2}_{0}&\mathsf{cf}^{2}_{T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{2}_{2T/\lambda}&\cdots&\mathsf{cf}^{2}_{(\lambda-1)T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{2}_{T}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\mathsf{cf}^{k}_{0}&\mathsf{cf}^{k}_{T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{k}_{2T/\lambda}&\cdots&\mathsf{cf}^{k}_{(\lambda-1)T/\lambda}&\mathsf{cf}^{k}_{T}\end{pmatrix}.

The matrix
M
defines
k\lambda

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T/\lambda,S)
statements, by taking each pair of consecutive elements in a row as the start and end configurations. We denote the
k\lambda
statements defined by
M
as
{\bm{x}}_{M}
.

Let
\lambda,d
be two global parameters. The protocol
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k)
is as follows:

1.

Sending Checksums.
The prover computes checksums of
M
, denoted by
\mathsf{cksum}
, such that any two matrices that are consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
have row-distance at least
(2d+1)
(i.e., they differ in at least
(2d+1)
rows).
It sends
\mathsf{cksum}
to the verifier.

2.

Reducing
T
.
The prover and verifier engage in
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
over
{\bm{x}}_{M}
. However, we delay the verification of
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
: the verifier does not access
{\bm{x}}_{M}
directly; instead, it outputs a low-depth predicate
\psi
about
{\bm{x}}_{M}
such that
\psi({\bm{x}}_{M})=0
if
{\bm{x}}_{M}
is not correct. Define
\Psi
as
\Psi(M)=\psi({\bm{x}}_{M})
.

Remark 1
.

The purpose of these two steps is to create distance: Either every
M^{\prime}
that is consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
is
d
-far from
M
, or there exists an
M^{\prime}
consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
that is
d
-close to
M
for which we will show
\Psi(M^{\prime})=0
.

3.

IPP.
The prover and verifier engage in a row-
IPP
proving that
M
is close in row-distance to satisfying the following property (checkable by a low-depth circuit):

•

M
is consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
;

•

\Psi(M)=1
.

The distance parameter of this row-IPP is
d
, and the verifier at the end needs to query
k/d
rows
M[\mathcal{S},:]
in
M
. Note that
M[\mathcal{S},:]
are the
\lambda
intermediate configurations of
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
. The output of the IPP verifier is a low-depth predicate
{\Phi}
about
M[\mathcal{S},:]
.

4.

Reducing
k
.
The prover and verifier engage in
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k/d)
over
k/d

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
, with the goal of checking that
M[\mathcal{S},:]
, defined by the
k/d
statements
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
, satisfies
{\Phi}
, and that the
k/d
statements
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
are valid. We recurse over a batched protocol
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k/d,{\Phi})
that checks a low-depth predicate
{\Phi}
on the
middle configurations
and follows exactly the same recursive construction that we are describing. In this technical overview, we forget
{\Phi}
for simplicity, and only say we want to check if all of
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
are valid.

The base case is when
T=O(1)
or
k=O(1)
.

•

T
is small
. In this case, all
k

{\mathsf{TISP}}(T,S)
statements can be verified by a low-depth circuit. Thus, the prover and verifier can engage in the GKR protocol, which returns a low-degree extension check on the statements.

•

k
is small
. In this case, the prover sends the entire
M
in the clear, and the prover and verifier engage in
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
.

The above is the full description of our construction. We analyze it and provide concrete parameters below.

Protocol Analysis: Win-Win Argument

In what follows, we say that the “unique” witness (or true configurations) corresponding to the
i
-th statement
x_{i}=(\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{0},\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{T})
consists of the middle configurations obtained by computing honestly from
\mathsf{cf}^{i}_{0}
(even if the statement
x_{i}
is false). Thus,
M
is well-defined even if some statements are false.
Our analysis goes through the four steps in the protocol.

Step 1: Sending Checksums.

In the first step of the protocol, the prover sends checksums, denoted by
\mathsf{cksum}
, for all the columns of
M
. The size of each column of
\mathsf{cksum}
is set to be
\tilde{O}(d)
so that one can (uniquely) decode any deviation in at most
d
rows. It is convenient to think of
d=\lambda
since we will indeed set these two parameters to be equal. The total size of
\mathsf{cksum}
is
\tilde{O}(d)\cdot|w_{i}|=\tilde{O}(d\cdot\lambda\cdot S)
, where
S
is the size of each configuration.

We distinguish between two cases, both of which take us a step closer to catching the cheating prover:

•

Case 1:
One needs to change more than
d
rows of the unique witnesses
M
to be consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
. In this case,

M
already has a large row-distance to the property proven by row-
IPP
in step 3. This is an easy case as we can use the soundness guarantee of the row-
IPP
. In this case step 2 of the protocol is not necessary since we already have the distance needed for the IPP soundness.

•

Case 2:
One needs to change fewer than
d
rows of the unique witnesses
M
to be consistent with
\mathsf{cksum}
. In this case, we cannot directly say that the distance condition for row-
IPP
soundness is satisfied.
However, this case still offers us a win:
\mathsf{cksum}
uniquely determines the witnesses sent by the prover, since for each column,
\mathsf{cksum}
uniquely decodes up to
d
deviations. In other words, if we haven’t created distance then the prover has committed to a matrix
M^{\prime}
that is
d
-close to
M
through
\mathsf{cksum}
.

Note that the committed matrix
M^{\prime}
is a fixed
k
-by-
\lambda
matrix that contains
k\lambda
configurations, corresponding to
k\lambda
statements
{\bm{x}}_{M^{\prime}}
, where each statement corresponds to a
T/\lambda
-time computation. We can assume that
M^{\prime}
and
M
have the same starting and ending configurations in each row, since the verifier can efficiently check this.
This implies that one of these
k\lambda
statements in
{\bm{x}}_{M^{\prime}}
must be false.

Step 2: Reducing
T
.

The purpose of this step is to create the distance needed for the IPP if we are in Case 2 in the last step.
At step 2, the prover and verifier run the protocol
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
. Recall that the verifier does not access the instance, but only outputs a predicate
\psi
about this instance. In our soundness analysis, we regard this
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
as executed w.r.t.
{\bm{x}}_{M^{\prime}}
, the
k\lambda
statements defined by
M^{\prime}
. Therefore, by soundness of
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
, with high probability
\psi({\bm{x}}_{M^{\prime}})=0
, i.e.
\Psi(M^{\prime})=0
.

Step 3: IPP.

At step 3, the prover and verifier engage in a row-
IPP
proving that
M
is close in row-distance to the property defined by the
\mathsf{cksum}
from step 1 and
\Psi
from step 2. Recall that in step 1 we distinguished between two cases:
M
is close (in row distance) to
\mathsf{cksum}
or not. We next claim that in both cases
M
is far (in row distance) from the property that row-
IPP
is proving:

•

Case 1:

M
is already far from the satisfying checksums.

•

Case 2:
The only possible matrix close to
M
and satisfying the checksums is
M^{\prime}
. However,
\Psi(M^{\prime})=0
.

Therefore, by soundness of row-IPP, with high probability, the verifier obtains a set
\mathcal{S}
and predicate
{\Phi}
such that
{\Phi}(M[\mathcal{S},:])=0
.

Step 4: Reducing
k
.

We are left with checking
{\Phi}(M[\mathcal{S},:])=1
. If we don’t need to check
{\Phi}
but need only check whether
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
are correct, then simply running
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k/d)
is sufficient. In our actual construction, we instead check if
{\Phi}
is satisfied by the intermediate configurations
M[\mathcal{S},:]
uniquely determined by
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
. This turns out to be extremely close to checking correctness of
\left\{x_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
, and we recursively build
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k,{\Phi})
for the above condition with the same 4-step construction.

Efficiency analysis

In the construction above, the
\mathsf{Batch}(T,k)
protocol runs a
\mathsf{Batch}(T/\lambda,k\lambda)
protocol and a
(T,k/d)
protocol with additional
\tilde{O}(\lambda\cdot d\cdot S)
communication and
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}\lambda,d,S)
verification time. Taking
\lambda=d
, the verification time satisfies the following transition (below we use notation
O_{\lambda,S}
to hide
{\mathrm{poly}{(}}\lambda,S)
terms):

•

{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{1,k}=O_{\lambda,S}(1)
;

•

{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,1}={\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T/\lambda,\lambda}+O_{\lambda,S}(1)
;

•

{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,k}={\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T/\lambda,k\lambda}+{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,k/\lambda}+O_{\lambda,S}(1)
.

Standard calculation shows that
{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,1}
equals
O_{\lambda,S}(C_{\log_{\lambda}T})
, where
C_{\ell}\approx 2^{2\ell}/{\mathrm{poly}{(}}\ell)
is the
\ell
-th Catalan Number. Taking
\lambda=2^{\sqrt{\log T}}
gives us the desired
{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,1}=O_{\lambda,S}(2^{\sqrt{\log T}})
, and in particular
{\mathsf{Vtime}}_{T,1}={\mathrm{poly}{(}}n)
when
T=n^{O(\log n)}
.

3.1
Finite Fields and Distances on Matrices

All finite fields
\mathbb{F}
considered in this work are always
constructible
in the following sense.

Definition 1

(Constructible Field Ensemble)
.

We say a field ensemble
\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{F}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
constructible
if every element in
\mathbb{F}_{n}
has an

O(\log{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert})
-bit representation,
and addition, multiplication, and inverses can be computed in
\mathrm{polylog}{(}\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert)
time given the representations.

It is well known that for every
S=S(n)
,
constructible field ensembles
\mathbb{F}=(\mathbb{F}_{n})
with
\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert=\Theta(S)
exist.
Moreover,
we make the (mild) assumption that addition, multiplication and inverses can be computed in
\widetilde{O}(\log\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert)
time,
where
\widetilde{O}
omits
{\mathrm{poly}{\log}}\log(\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert)
factors.
This implies that degree-
d
polynomials in
\mathbb{F}[X]
can be evaluated in
\widetilde{O}(d\log\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert)
time (with
\widetilde{O}
omitting
\mathrm{polylog}{(}d,\log\left\lvert\mathbb{F}_{n}\right\rvert)
factors).
These properties are satisfied in fields that support FFT. (See table
8.6
in
[
22
]
)

An important metric defined on matrices, denoted by
\Delta_{c}
, is as follows.

Definition 2

(
\Delta_{c}
-distance)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field.
For matrices
\bm{A}=({\bm{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{a}}_{{L}})\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times{{L}}}
and
\bm{B}=(\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{{L}})\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times{{L}}}
,
the
\Delta_{c}
-distance
between
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
, denoted by
\Delta_{c}(\bm{A},\bm{B})
, is the maximum Hamming distance between corresponding columns of
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
,
i.e.,

\Delta_{c}(\bm{A},\bm{B})=\max_{i\in[{{L}}]}\Delta({\bm{a}}_{i},\bm{b}_{i}).

If
\Delta_{c}(\bm{A},\bm{B})\leq d
,
we say
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
are
\Delta_{c}
-
d
-close
. If
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
are not
\Delta_{c}
-
d
-close, then we say they are
\Delta_{c}
-
d
-far
.

Furthermore, given a distance parameter
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times{{L}}}
,
define
\Delta_{c}
-
d
-ball
as

{{\mathcal{B}_{d,\mathbb{F}}}}(\bm{A})\coloneqq\left\{\bm{A}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times{{L}}}:\Delta_{c}(\bm{A},\bm{A}^{\prime})\leq d\right\}.

Remark 2
.

Observe that
\Delta_{c}
is a lower bound on how many rows have to be modified to transform one matrix into another.

3.2
Succinct Descriptions of Sets and Functions

Definition 3

(Uniform Arithmetic Circuits)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
Let
C=\left\{C_{n}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{m}\right\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of arithmetic circuits,
consisting of fan-in 2
ADD
and
MULT
gates over a field
\mathbb{F}
.
For any
f=f(n)
, we say that
C
is
f
-space uniform if there exists a fixed
O(f(n))
-space Turing machine
\mathcal{M}
that, on input
1^{n}
, outputs the full description of the circuit
C_{n}
.
When
n
is clear from the context, we omit the subscript
n
and write
C
instead of
C_{n}
.

An important special case is when
f(n)=\log(n)
,
in which case we say that
C
is log-space uniform.

Remark 3
.

Any
f
-space uniform Boolean circuit
C
can be trivially extended to a
f
-space uniform arithmetic circuit
C^{\prime}
of the same size and depth over any field
\mathbb{F}
.

The following
succinct descriptions
of sets can be used to recover the entire set.

Definition 4

(Descriptions of Sets)
.

A bit string
\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle\in\{0,1\}^{B}
is a description of a set
\mathcal{S}=\left\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\right\}\subset\{0,1\}^{p}

if there exists a (multi-output)
p
-space uniform
circuit
G:[k]\times\{0,1\}^{B}\to\{0,1\}^{p}
of fan-in 2,
called its
implementation circuit
,
such that
G(i,\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle)=s_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
.
The description is succinct if
\left\lvert\langle\mathcal{S}\rangle\right\rvert=B<k\cdot p
.

Similarly,

succinct descriptions
of functions can be used to configure a uniform circuit family to implement the function.

Definition 5

(Descriptions of Functions)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
We say that
\langle{\Phi}\rangle\in\{0,1\}^{B}
is a description of a function
{\Phi}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{m}
if there exists a log-space uniform circuit
C:\mathbb{F}^{n+B}\to\mathbb{F}^{m}
of fan-in 2,
called its
implementation circuit
,
such that
C(x,\langle{\Phi}\rangle)={\Phi}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
.
The description is succinct if
\left\lvert\langle{\Phi}\rangle\right\rvert=B<{\mathsf{size}}({\Phi})
.

When we write
\langle{\Phi}\rangle=\top
,
we mean that
{\Phi}
is the trivial predicate that outputs 1 on all inputs (i.e., it imposes no constraint on
x
).

The Turing machines that generate the uniform
G
and
C
in
Definitions

4
and
5
take in their “shape parameters”
(k,1^{B},1^{p})
and
(1^{n},1^{B})
as input, respectively.

3.3
Unique-Decoding Checksums

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
be a
deviation radius
and
{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}\in\mathbb{N}
be a checksum-length parameter.
We use syndromes of linear error-correcting codes as checksums.

Definition 6

(Unique-Decoding Checksums)
.

Let
k,\rho,{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
A function
\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}
is a
\rho
-unique decoding checksum function

if for any
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
,
and for any
\bm{m}^{\prime},\bm{m}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
that are both
\rho
-close to
\bm{m}
and
\bm{m}^{\prime}\neq\bm{m}^{\prime\prime}
,

\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime})\neq\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime\prime})
.

The term
unique decoding
refers to the fact that if we know
\bm{m}^{\prime}
is
\rho
-close to some (fixed)
\bm{m}
,
then we can uniquely determine
\bm{m}^{\prime}
given
\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime})
.

Proposition 1

(Linear-code checksums)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{k}
be a linear code of minimum distance
D
and codimension
{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}
,
and let
H\in\mathbb{F}^{{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}\times k}
be a parity-check matrix for
\mathcal{C}
.
For any
\rho\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
2\rho<D
,
the syndrome map

\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m})\coloneqq H\bm{m}

is a
\rho
-unique decoding checksum function.
Moreover, with
\widetilde{O}
hiding polylogarithmic factors,
\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}
can be evaluated in
\widetilde{O}(k{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}({\mathrm{polylog}{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}}))
,
and its implementation circuit over
\mathbb{F}
has size
O(k{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}})
and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

Proof.

Fix
\bm{m}\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
and let
\bm{m}^{\prime},\bm{m}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
be distinct vectors that are both
\rho
-close to
\bm{m}
.
Then
\bm{z}=\bm{m}^{\prime}-\bm{m}^{\prime\prime}
is nonzero and has Hamming weight at most
2\rho
.
If
\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime})=\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime\prime})
,
then
H\bm{z}=H\bm{m}^{\prime}-H\bm{m}^{\prime\prime}=\bm{0}
by linearity,
so
\bm{z}\in\ker(H)=\mathcal{C}
.
This is a nonzero codeword of Hamming weight at most
2\rho<D
,
contradicting the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
.
Hence
\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime})\neq\mathsf{cksum}_{\rho}(\bm{m}^{\prime\prime})
.
∎

Lemma 1

(Reed–Solomon checksums)
.

Let
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbb{F}
be a finite field with
\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert\geq k
.
Set
{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}\coloneqq 2d
.
Then a
d
-unique decoding checksum function
\mathsf{cksum}_{d}:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}}
exists.
With
\widetilde{O}
hiding
\mathrm{polylog}{(}k)
factors,

\mathsf{cksum}_{d}:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}
can be evaluated in
\widetilde{O}(k{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}{\mathrm{polylog}{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}})
time,
and its implementation circuit over
\mathbb{F}
has size
O(k{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}})
and depth
\widetilde{O}(1)
.

Proof.

If
2d<k
,
let
H\in\mathbb{F}^{2d\times k}
be a parity-check matrix of a Reed–Solomon code over
\mathbb{F}
with block length
k
,
dimension
k-2d
,
and minimum distance
2d+1
.
Define
\mathsf{cksum}_{d}(\bm{m})\coloneqq H\bm{m}
.
By the previous proposition, this is a
d
-unique decoding checksum function.

If
2d\geq k
,
let
\mathsf{cksum}_{d}
be the identity map on
\mathbb{F}^{k}
padded with
2d-k
zero coordinates.
Then
\mathsf{cksum}_{d}
is injective, so it is
d
-unique decoding.

The evaluation bound follows by computing the
{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}
linear forms defining
\mathsf{cksum}_{d}
.
∎

Generalizing the notion of checksums to matrices,
given a matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{F}^{k\times L}
with columns
(\bm{m}_{1},\ldots,\bm{m}_{L})
,
we use

\mathsf{cksum}_{d}(\bm{M})
to denote
\chi=(\mathsf{cksum}_{d}(\bm{m}_{1}),\ldots,\mathsf{cksum}_{d}(\bm{m}_{L}))\in\mathbb{F}^{{R_{\mathsf{cksum}}}\times L}
.

3.4
Interactive Protocols

An
(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}},\Sigma)
-protocol is a
public-coin,

\ell
-round interactive protocol, with alphabet
\Sigma
,
per-round message length
a
, and prover runtime
{\mathsf{Ptime}}
and verifier runtime
{\mathsf{Vtime}}
.
Specifically, such a protocol consists of a pair of interacting Turing machines
({\mathsf{P}}{(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{(y)}){{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathsf{pp}]}}
,
where the
{\mathsf{P}}
has input a string
x\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
and the
{\mathsf{V}}
has input a string
y\in\{0,1\}^{*}
,
and both parties have explicit access to the input parameters
\mathsf{pp}
.
We omit
x
,
y
, and
\mathsf{pp}
from the notation when they are not important to the context.
The machines may also take in other private parameters as additional input
(in particular, the prover might have some extra information that makes it more efficient),
but we omit them from the notation for simplicity.
The machine
{\mathsf{P}}
is deterministic and runs in time
{\mathsf{Ptime}}
, and is called
the prover
, while
{\mathsf{V}}
is probabilistic, runs in time
{\mathsf{Vtime}}
, and is called
the verifier
.

{\mathsf{P}}
and
{\mathsf{V}}
are the
two parties
of the protocol.
We omit the specification of
\Sigma
when
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.

In each round
j\in[\ell]
of the protocol:

1.

{\mathsf{V}}
sends a random message
q_{j}\leftarrow_{R}\Sigma^{a}
to
{\mathsf{P}}
, referred to as a
query
.

2.

{\mathsf{P}}
responds with a message
a_{j}\in\Sigma^{a}
determined by the prescribed next-message function, referred to as an
answer
, which (abusing notation) is denoted by

a_{j}\coloneqq{\mathsf{P}}(x,j,(q_{1},\ldots,q_{j}))\in\Sigma^{a}.

We make the simplifying assumption that both parties send messages of equal length,
and that
{\mathsf{V}}
never rejects in the middle of an execution.
We denote the sequence of verifier random coins by
\bm{q}\coloneqq(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell})
.
Finally,

{\mathsf{P}}(x,\bm{q})\coloneqq(a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell})
, where the
a_{j}={\mathsf{P}}(x,j,\bm{q}_{\leq j})
are the prescribed messages,
and the notation
\langle{\mathsf{P}}{(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{(y;\bm{q})}\rangle{{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathsf{pp}]}}\in\{0,1\}^{*}\cup\left\{\bot\right\}
represents the output of the verifier at the end of the interaction,
which contains a special symbol
\bot
to indicate that the verifier rejected.
Note that the randomness is only over the random coins
\bm{q}
of
{\mathsf{V}}
,
and we omit
\bm{q}
and
\mathsf{pp}
from the notation when they are not important in the context.

The
total communication complexity
of the protocol is the number of bits exchanged between the prover and the verifier, i.e.
2a\ell\cdot\log(\left\lvert\Sigma\right\rvert)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\{0,1\}^{*}
be a language. We next define the notion of an
interactive proof of
\mathcal{L}
.

Definition 7

(
\epsilon
-Sound
{(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}})}
-
IP
)
.

An
{(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}})}
-protocol
({\mathsf{P}}{(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{(y)}){{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathsf{pp}]}}
,
where the public parameter is
\mathsf{pp}
and
y=x
,
is an
interactive proof
(
IP
) with soundness error
\epsilon

for a language
\mathcal{L}
,
if it satisfies the following completeness and soundness conditions.

-

Completeness:

For any
x\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists a prover strategy
{\mathsf{P}}
such that

\Pr[\langle{\mathsf{P}}(x),{\mathsf{V}}(x)\rangle_{\mathsf{pp}}\neq\bot]=1.

-

\epsilon
-Soundness:

For any
x\notin\mathcal{L}
and any (computationally unbounded) prover strategy
{\mathsf{P}}^{*}
,

\Pr[\langle{\mathsf{P}}^{*}(x),{\mathsf{V}}(x)\rangle_{\mathsf{pp}}\neq\bot]\leq\epsilon.

3.4.1
Low-depth-predicate Interactive Proof and the
GKR
protocol

We consider the following special type of protocol where the verifier does not query the input
x
but instead outputs a low-depth predicate to be checked against the input.

Definition 8

(
\epsilon
-Sound
{(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}})}
-
LDP-IP
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field.
An
low-depth-predicate-interactive-proof
(
LDP-IP
) with respect to a field
\mathbb{F}
for a language
\mathcal{L}
is a protocol
with public parameters
\mathsf{pp}
, where the prover is given some input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
,
and the verifier receives no input (except the protocol’s parameters).
At the end of the protocol, the verifier either rejects (outputs
\bot
) or outputs the description of a low-depth predicate
\Psi
,
which takes as input
x
and outputs 0 or 1,
satisfying the following:

-

Completeness:
If
x\in\mathcal{L}
, there exists a prover strategy
{\mathsf{P}}
such that

\Pr[\langle{\mathsf{P}}{(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{}\rangle{{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathsf{pp}]}}=\langle\Psi\rangle\text{ s.t. }\Psi(x)=1]=1,

-

Soundness:
If
x\notin\mathcal{L}
, then for any (computationally unbounded) prover strategy
{\mathsf{P}}^{*}
,

\Pr[\langle{\mathsf{P}}{{}^{*}(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{}\rangle{{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathsf{pp}]}}=\langle\Psi\rangle\text{ s.t. }\Psi(x)=1]\leq\epsilon.

Importantly, the verifier never accesses
x
throughout the protocol.

We restate the main result from
[
12
]
in terms of
LDP-IP
.

Theorem 3

(The
GKR
Protocol, as an
LDP-IP
)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field,
and let
{\Phi}:\mathbb{F}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}
be an arithmetic circuit with addition and multiplication gates of fan-in 2 over
\mathbb{F}
,
with description
\langle{\Phi}\rangle
.
Let
G(x,\langle{\Phi}\rangle)
be the log-space uniform circuit that outputs
{\Phi}(x)
on input
x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
and
\langle{\Phi}\rangle\in\{0,1\}^{\left\lvert\langle{\Phi}\rangle\right\rvert}
.
Denote the depth and size of
G
by
D=D(n)\geq\log n
and
S=S(n)\geq n
.

For some constant
C_{\textsf{GKR}}>0
, there exists an
\epsilon
-sound
{(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}})}
-
LDP-IP
,
abbreviated as
\textsf{GKR}\coloneqq({\mathsf{P}}{(x)},{\mathsf{V}}{}){{}_{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}[\mathbb{F},\langle{\Phi}\rangle]}}
,
for the language
\mathcal{L}_{{\Phi}}=\{x\in\mathbb{F}^{n}:{\Phi}(x)=1\}
,
with the following complexity (with
\widetilde{O}
ignoring poly-logarithmic factors in
D,\log S
):

•

The soundness error is bounded by
\epsilon\leq C_{\textsf{GKR}}\cdot\left(\frac{D\log S}{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}\right)=O(\frac{D\log S}{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert})
.

•

\ell=O(D\cdot\log S)
.

•

a=O({\log{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}})
.

•

{\mathsf{Ptime}}=\widetilde{O}({\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)\cdot{\mathrm{polylog}{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}})
.

•

{\mathsf{Vtime}}=\widetilde{O}(D\log S\cdot{\log{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}}+\left\lvert\langle{\Phi}\rangle\right\rvert\cdot{\log{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}})

(and
{\mathsf{V}}
does not access
x
).

\left\lvert\langle\Psi\rangle\right\rvert=O({\log{\left\lvert\mathbb{F}\right\rvert}})
, and the implementation circuit
C
for
\Psi
satisfies

•

{\mathsf{size}}(C)=\widetilde{O}(n)
.

•

0pt(C)=\widetilde{O}(1)
.

3.4.2
Interactive Proof of Proximity with Row Reduction

An Interactive Proof of Proximity with Row Reduction
allows us to reduce checking a predicate
{\Phi}
over a matrix
\bm{M}
to checking a related predicate
\Psi
over a subset of rows of
\bm{M}
.
It is a generalization of the standard
IPP
,
which is defined over bit strings and with respect to Hamming distance.

Definition 9

(
\epsilon
-Sound
{(\ell,a,{\mathsf{Ptime}},{\mathsf{Vtime}})}
-
IPP
with Row Reduction)
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field,

k,L\in\mathbb{N}
,
and
{\Phi}:\mathbb{F}^{k\times L}\to\{0,1\}
be a predicate.
An
\epsilon

{\mathsf{Vtime}}^{\prime}(i,j)={\mathsf{Vtime}}^{\prime}(i-1,j+1)+{\mathsf{Vtime}}^{\prime}(i,j-1)+\mathsf{poly}(S,\lambda)
.
Consider the recursion tree for
\mathsf{Vtime^{\prime}}
rooted at node
(\rho,0)
. Our goal is to upper bound the number of nodes in this recursion tree, which would yield an upper bound on
{\mathsf{Vtime}}(T,0)
.

In this recursion tree, every node
(i,j)
that has more than one child has exactly two children, which correspond to two moves that we can make: a
\nwarrow
-move from
(i,j)
to
(i-1,j+1)
, and a
\downarrow
-move from
(i,j)
to
(i,j-1)
. Starting at node
(\rho,0)
, we must make exactly
\rho

\nwarrow
-moves to reach a node
(0,j)
, i.e., the base case. Since after making
\rho

\nwarrow
-moves we are at node
(0,\rho)
, and we can never reach a node
(i,j)
such that
j<0
, this implies that we can make at most
\rho

\downarrow
-moves. Suppose at some step we have made exactly
a

\nwarrow
-moves and
b

\downarrow
-moves; then we are at node
(\rho-a,a-b)
, and since we never reach a node
(i,j)
such that
j<0
, this implies that
a
and
b
must satisfy
a-b\geq 0
. The number of paths starting from
(\rho,0)
that make
\rho

\nwarrow
-moves and
\rho

\downarrow
-moves and at every step satisfy
a-b\geq 0
, is exactly the number of Dyck paths of size
\rho
, which is given by the
\rho
th Catalan number
C_{\rho}={2\rho\choose\rho}/(\rho+1)
. Since the number of nodes in the recursion tree is at most the number of such paths, we have that
{2\rho\choose\rho}/(\rho+1)\cdot{\mathrm{poly}{(}}\lambda,S)\leq 2^{2\rho}\cdot{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)
is an upper bound on
{\mathsf{Vtime}}(T,0)
.
Since
\lambda=2^{\sqrt{\log T}}
,
\rho=\log_{\lambda}T=\sqrt{\log T}
,
and thus
{\mathsf{Vtime}}(T,0)=2^{O(\sqrt{\log T})}\cdot{\mathrm{poly}{(}}S)
.

We reduce the number of transition claims

This predicate

To implement this, we introduce the

Definition 10
