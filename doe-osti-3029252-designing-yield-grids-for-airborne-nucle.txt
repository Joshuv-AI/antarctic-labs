11 Abstract
12 This document details a method for selecting grid points that sample the yields of airborne, low
13 yield nuclear explosions that constrains the relative size of seismic waveforms sourced by
14 explosions that are parameterized by this grid. In detail, we impose this constraint so that the
15 vertical component of ground displacement that is sourced by explosions with a fixed height of
16 burst (HoB) at grid point 𝑘 change by a fixed factor of 𝛼 when compared to the waveform
17 amplitudes sourced by explosions at grid point 𝑘 + 1, as observed at a fixed standoff distance. This
18 gridding thereby preserves amplitude ratios along the grid so that they are agnostic to 𝑘. We assert
19 that this method is useful in tests that apply algorithms against synthetic or augmented datasets for
20 which the performance of the algorithm must be measured against source size, and waveform
21 amplitudes must change uniformly (e.g., Berg et al, 2025).

22 Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)
23 We iteratively compute a set of points along a yield grid for airborne nuclear explosions to maintain
24 a fixed amplitude ratio between vertical component, Rayleigh waveform displacements at adjacent
25 grid points. We design the interior points of this grid to range from 10 to 250 tons so that yields at
26 neighboring points produce waveforms with amplitudes that are 9/10 of the amplitudes of
27 waveforms at adjacent, larger yield grid points. We calculate these grid points in three steps. First,
28 we select simple, reduced order models (ROMs) for the ground displacement sourced by airborne
29 explosions in which the amplitudes of the Airy phase contribution to Rayleigh waveforms are
30 implicitly parameterized by explosion yield and height of burst (HoB). Second, we impose an
31 objective function that requires that the amplitudes of waveforms that sample adjacent grid points
32 meet our constant, grid agnostic, relative amplitude criteria. Third, we iteratively invert for yields
33 that satisfy our objective function by initializing our grid with a 250-ton yield point. Our algorithm
34 terminates after it outputs a grid point that is less than our equal to our minimum yield of 10 tons.
35 We use this process to build yield grids for explosions at 10 distinct HoBs between 0 m and 20000
36 m. We compartmentalize the parameters that we used to build these results in Table 1, the grid
37 points for 0 HoB explosions in Table 2, and display our results in Figure 1.

39 Background
40 Rayleigh waveforms sourced by events like atmospheric explosions that travel near their minimum
41 group velocity form a type of nearly nondispersive signal that appears in late-time seismograms
42 known as the Airy phase (Press et al., 1950). The amplitudes of these surface waves generally
43 dominate those of body waves when their sources do not immediately couple into the ground. The
44 HoB, effective yield, and site conditions below such sources (at ground zero) largely determine
45 observable Rayleigh waveforms parameter like amplitude, frequency, and signal dispersion
46 (Harkrider, 1970; Harkrider et al., 1974). The stationary phase approximation for the predicted
47 Airy phase contribution to vertical component Rayleigh waveform that is sourced by such an
48 airborne explosion is (Murph and Shah, 1988):

102 Reduction of CRUST1.0 Layers
103 We model the loading site for any explosion over land as a one-dimensional profile of six or fewer
104 layers within the crust that is tabulated by CRUST1.0, per one degree cell (Laske et al., 2013).
105 This model specifies layer descriptions, the depth of their interfaces, the body wave speeds in each
106 layer, and their densities. We replace the (up to) six-element layer from CRUST1.0 at the source
107 location with a simplified layer-over-half space model using the method of Xu et al. (2009). This
108 method substitutes the density of the layered system with its arithmetical average and replaces the
109 body wave speeds with their harmonic averages. The new layer is then characterized by these
110 harmonic and arithmetic averages while the half-space retains the elastic parameters of the
111 shallowest layer that defines the half space. We selected this depth as the first layer in which the
112 body wave speeds each change by at least five percent that was not within a low velocity layer.
113 Our algorithm then sets the reduced model layer interface to be 5 m below the deepest layer
114 interface that we averaged over and then computes each body wave and density average.
115
116 Reduction of and Rapid Calculation of Dispersion Curves
117 The reduced layer over half-space system a has simple secular equation (Bolt and Butcher, 1960;
118 Equation 2) when compared to multi-layered systems and takes the form of a determinant 𝐹𝑅 (𝜔).
119 The roots of 𝐹𝑅 (𝜔) define the dispersion curve for the phase velocity 𝑐(𝜔). We compute these
120 roots from a monotonic transform on the determinant that has equivalent roots:

121 Our transform stabilizes the root-finding algorithm for the higher frequencies at which phase
122 velocities approach those of the top layer. We truncate these calculations to frequencies below the
123 long-period cutoff value for the first higher-order mode (Xu et al., 2009; Eq. 3). We again use these
124 resultant phase velocities to compute the Rayleigh group velocity 𝑈. The minimum of 𝑈 over
125 angular frequency 𝜔 defines the characteristic Airy phase frequency 𝜔0 and 𝑘0 = 𝜔0⁄𝑐(𝜔0 ). We
126 used the results of Murphy and Shah (Murphy and Shah, 1988; Eq. 3) to initialize our minimum-

128 upper layer of thickness 𝐿. Our waveform model uses these reduced fidelity estimates of phase
129 velocity, group velocity, its derivatives with respect to frequency, and 𝜔0 to calculate the
130 approximate Airy phase contribution to the Rayleigh wave seismograms (Equation 1).

170 Results
171 Our inversion scheme (Equation 14) output a set of grid points for each HoB that range in sample
172 number from 23 points (3300 m HoB) to 36 points (20000 m HoB) (Figure 1). The grid that we
173 calculate for surface explosions (0 m HoB) quantitatively resembles both near ground explosions
174 (20 m and 100 m HoBs) as well as several higher altitude explosions (e.g., 10000 m and 15000 m
175 HoBs). Intermediate altitude explosions show sparser grids that are coarser at higher yields. All
176 grid specifications show increased sampling density with decreasing yields, as we expect from
177 crude scaling laws (cube-root yield).
178
179 The maximum and minimum amplitudes of Rayleigh waveforms sourced by surface explosions
180   that sample our 0 HoB grid show a ratio of 1.74×106 (Pa m2)/ 4.96×104 (Pa m2) ≈ 35 (Table 1)
181 that spans our 35 grid points (Figure 1). Forward calculations of the source terms at adjacent grid
182 points with Equation 2 show measurable error in the vertical component amplitude ratios that we
183   measure as 𝑒 = |𝑆𝑘+1 (𝜔0 )|⁄|𝑆𝑘 (𝜔0 )| − 1⁄𝛼. We report a mean error 0.09 and standard deviation
184 of 0.02. We conclude that our method produces grid points with a positive bias of roughly 8% and
185 a bias-adjusted uncertainty of less than 2%.

187 Discussion and Summary
188 We provide a simple process to compute a yield grid for airborne explosions that is subject to
189 amplitude constraints on the Rayleigh waveforms that such explosions trigger. Our electronic
190 supplement (a .JSON file) includes a calculation summary that specifies yield grid values at 10
191 HoBs that non-uniformly sample a U.S. Standard Atmosphere (United States, 1976). We
192 determined that high altitude explosions (20000 km) require the maximum number of grid points
193 (36 in number) among grids for distinct HoBs to maintain an approximate, relative amplitude
194 scaling constraint. The density of these grid points increases as explosion size decreases across all
195 HoBs (Figure 1). We further determine that some high-altitude explosions (> 10000 m HoB)
196 require a similar grid specification to the 0 HoB case to maintain a uniform scaling between
197 Rayleigh waveform amplitudes. This observation is consistent with historical, more sophisticated
198 model studies (Harkrider et al., 1974). We propose that such a gridding method is useful for efforts
199 like the Dynamic Network Experiments (DNEs) that quantify the performance of algorithms
200 versus source parameters, like yield (Berg et al., 2025).
201
202 Lastly, we note that other signatures output by a nuclear explosion, such as acoustic,
203 electromagnetic waveforms, or optical power likely provide more physically defensible constraints
204 to construct similar HoB grids. Reports that use simple ROMs to predict features of those

 7

205 signatures (e.g., Whitaker and Symbalisty, 2009) provide analogous equations to those that we
206 used here.
