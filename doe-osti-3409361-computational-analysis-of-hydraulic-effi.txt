1. Introduction
Drainage structures are used in urban street and highway systems to capture stormwater runoff.
These structures typically consist of catch basins fitted with grates, inlets, or combination
grate/inlet configurations that collect runoff and convey it through buried drainage systems.
They are strategically placed within curb-and-gutter systems to enhance public safety by
efficiently removing water from roadways and thereby reducing the risk of hydroplaning.

The performance of drainage structures is commonly evaluated in terms of hydraulic efficiency,
defined as the percentage of flow captured by the basin relative to the total flow reaching the
structure. Understanding the hydraulic performance of these structures allows designers to
properly space inlets, resulting in cost-effective designs that also help ensure the safety of the
traveling public.

MDOT uses a variety of drainage structures for runoff capture, as documented in Michigan
Department of Transportation (MDOT) Drainage Manual [1]. Many of these structures
incorporate sinusoidal-type grates that are not addressed in HEC-22 [2]. Because physical
modeling of these structures has been limited, further analysis is needed to verify their capture
efficiency. Under current MDOT practice, the capture efficiency of these grates is estimated by
assuming performance similar to that of a comparably sized reticuline grate described in HEC-
22.

The first phase of this effort, titled Computational Analysis of Hydraulic Efficiency of Michigan
DOT Cover C, focused on evaluating the hydraulic performance of MDOT’s Cover C grate. Cover
C was selected as the initial test candidate because its sinusoidal pattern is representative of
other MDOT grates, while it is typically used in high-volume, higher-speed applications. A
similar version, Cover CX, is used on interstate highways but does not include transverse bars
for bicycle safety.

The current second phase of the study expands this work to evaluate MDOT’s Covers J and K.
These grates were selected for additional analysis to further assess the hydraulic performance of
MDOT drainage structures that are not directly represented by grate configurations in HEC-22.
The results of this phase will build on the findings from the Cover C analysis and support
improved understanding of the capture efficiency of MDOT’s standard drainage grates.

This report is intended to serve as a companion document to the earlier study, Computational
Analysis of Hydraulic Efficiency of Michigan DOT Cover C [3]. The present work applies the
same overall CFD-based evaluation approach to MDOT Covers J and K and compares the
resulting performance trends with those previously identified for Cover C. In particular, both
studies assess on-grade interception efficiency, sag-location hydraulic capacity, and the effects of
partial obstruction relative to HEC-22-based design estimates.

2. Scope of Work
The goals of the study are to use computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling to evaluate the
hydraulic performance of Covers J and K and to determine the hydraulic efficiency of catch
basins equipped with these covers at on-grade locations. Hydraulic efficiency is defined as the
percentage of intercepted flow relative to the total approaching flow. The study also examines
frontal flow, side flow, and bypass flow for on-grade applications. In addition, the hydraulic
capacity of the inlet under sump conditions is evaluated as a function of ponding depth above
the inlet, with flow depth varied up to 18 in. above the center of the inlet. Sump performance is
assessed for several scenarios, including the catch basin operating with full grate capacity, with
the grate obstructed, and with the curb opening obstructed.

For comparison with the CFD results, hydraulic performance was also evaluated using the HEC-
22 procedures referenced in current MDOT practice. For on-grade conditions, HEC-22
represents inlet interception as a combination of frontal and side interception and relates inlet
efficiency to gutter flow characteristics, pavement cross slope, longitudinal slope, and inlet
geometry. For sag conditions, HEC-22 represents inlet capacity using weir-flow and orifice-flow
equations, with the controlling regime depending on the degree of submergence over the inlet.
As in the earlier Cover C study [3], these equations were used as a baseline design reference for
comparison with the CFD predictions. Because HEC-22 does not explicitly represent the
detailed geometry of MDOT Covers J and K, the comparison is intended to indicate general
conservatism and trend agreement rather than exact geometric equivalence.

Covers J and K are grate inlets with curb boxes used by MDOT on local roads. Both grates
include transverse bars to provide bicycle-safe operation. Cover J is designed for installation in
concrete curb and gutter Detail B, whereas Cover K is intended for use with concrete curb and
gutter Details C, E, and F. Screenshots of the cover geometries are shown in Figure 2-1.

4. CFD Modeling
The unsteady Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (URANS) solver with the SST k-ω turbulence
model was used in combination with a Eulerian two-phase formulation consisting of air as the
gas phase and water as the liquid phase. The Volume of Fluid (VOF) model was applied to
represent the open-channel flow, including the free water surface and the air region above it.
Surface tension at the air-water interface was also included. Additional details regarding the
governing formulations and model implementation are provided in the STAR-CCM+
documentation [4].

Model verification and solution quality checks for the present CFD analyses were conducted
following the same procedures established in earlier studies [3], where the numerical approach
was developed and documented in greater detail. In the current work, mesh independence and
time-step sensitivity were checked to confirm that predicted interception efficiency and sag-
capacity results were not materially affected by further grid refinement or smaller time
increments. The near-wall mesh treatment, including prism-layer resolution and rough-wall
representation, was selected consistently with prior studies and verified to provide stable
predictions of the boundary-layer flow. Convergence was monitored using residual reduction,
flow-rate stabilization, and global mass-balance checks, with acceptable mass imbalance
maintained throughout the simulations. Stability of the free-surface solution was also confirmed
by examining the temporal behavior of water-surface elevation and discharge histories to ensure
that no nonphysical oscillations influenced the reported results.

In hydraulic design, the Manning roughness coefficient is used to account for flow losses
associated with pavement texture, debris, and other surface conditions. In the CFD models,
pavement roughness was represented using rough-wall functions based on the equivalent
roughness height, ks. This roughness height can be related to the grain-related component of the
Manning coefficient, n, through the Colebrook-White relationship, which is commonly
illustrated by the Moody diagram [6].

The analysis considered flow conditions associated with both on-grade and sag locations.
Separate CFD models were developed for these two cases to account for differences in geometry,
boundary conditions, and initial conditions.

The CFD model used to evaluate interception by catch basins installed on a continuous grade
including a section of roadway containing an inlet and catch basin, as shown in Figure 4-1. The
considered covers have different shape, and they are installed in different concrete curb and
gutter types. Figure 4-2 presents cross sectional view of the curb and gutter Detail B, used with
Cover J and Detail C used with Cover K. The roadway width was assumed to exceed the
maximum anticipated flow spread. The longitudinal roadway slope was represented by applying
a component of gravitational acceleration in the longitudinal direction along the travel lanes.
Each simulation was initialized with the computational domain filled with air. Water was then
introduced through a vertical boundary at the upstream end of the domain, located across the
roadway upstream of the catch basin. A velocity inlet condition was assigned to this boundary.
Flow was allowed to leave the domain through the grate inlet, the curb opening, or by bypassing
the drainage structure downstream. These outlet surfaces were assigned pressure outlet
boundary conditions with zero-gauge pressure. A pressure outlet condition was also applied to
the top surface of the domain. The boundary corresponding to the roadway median side, located
entirely within the air region, was modeled using a symmetry condition. This boundary
treatment enforces a free-slip condition on the air flow while preventing mass flux across the
boundary.

The CFD geometry and boundary conditions used in this study follow the same general
approach established in the earlier MDOT Cover C analysis [3], with updates only to represent
the specific geometry of Covers J and K and their corresponding curb-and-gutter details. For the
on-grade cases, the roadway width in the computational domain was selected to be greater than
the maximum expected flow spread so that lateral boundary effects did not influence the inlet
hydraulics. The upstream and downstream boundaries were placed sufficiently far from the
catch basin to allow development of the approaching gutter flow and uninhibited discharge of
bypass flow, and sensitivity checks confirmed that the outlet location did not materially affect
the predicted interception results. A symmetry boundary condition was applied along the
median-side boundary because this surface remained entirely within the air region and outside
the zone of hydraulic interaction. The roadway longitudinal slope was represented by resolving
the gravitational acceleration vector into longitudinal and vertical components, consistent with
standard CFD treatment of shallow open-channel flow over sloped pavement. Pavement
roughness was modeled using equivalent sand-grain roughness, ks, applied through rough-wall
functions, with ks selected to be consistent with the Manning’s n values used in roadway
drainage practice and with the methodology adopted in the previous Cover C study.

and 3 inches above the gutter level. The simulations were performed for one flow case, with the
objective of qualitatively illustrating the impact of such an obstruction on pavement flow spread
and inlet interception.

The computational model used for the sump-condition analysis included a section of roadway
upstream and downstream of the catch basin, together with the catch basin itself. In addition,
the longitudinal pavement grade was conservatively assumed to be zero. The domain height was
made substantially greater than that used in the on-grade analysis in order to capture orifice-
flow behavior at higher ponding depths. During the simulation, water drains through the inlet
until the domain is emptied. The discharge is recorded as a function of time on a horizontal
cross-section through the inlet, and average water depth is recorded as a function of time over a
small area of the water surface located away from the inlet. These two datasets are then
combined to define the relationship between discharge capacity and ponding depth.

5. Simulation Results
This section presents the results of the three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
simulations conducted to evaluate the flow capacity of catch basins with Cover J and K under
both on-grade and sag conditions.

The analysis examines catch basin hydraulic efficiency, including the fractions of grate flow
captured at the upstream edge and along the side of the grate, as well as flow entering through
the curb opening. These quantities are evaluated as functions of relevant hydraulic and
geometric parameters. The modeling results are presented using surface plots and line graphs.

For catch basins operating under sump conditions, three clogging scenarios were considered:

 1. Unobstructed grate and unobstructed curb opening

 2. Unobstructed grate and fully obstructed curb opening

 3. Fully obstructed grate and unobstructed curb opening

The dependence of drainage capacity on key parameters was analyzed for each scenario. Results
are presented in the form of data tables and water depth–discharge curves.

5.1. On-Grade Locations

Catch basins located on a grade intercept surface runoff while flow is moving longitudinally
along the gutter and roadway cross slope. Under these conditions, the hydraulic performance of
the inlet depends on the approaching flow characteristics, including gutter discharge, flow
spread, longitudinal slope, cross slope, and inlet geometry. For on-grade installations, only a
portion of the approaching runoff is captured by the grate and curb opening, while the
remainder bypasses the inlet and continues downstream.

Figure 5-1 presents an example of the steady-state solution for Covers J and K at a flow rate of
0.4 cfs and a longitudinal slope of 1%, while Figure 5-2 shows the corresponding results for a
flow rate of 2.0 cfs and a longitudinal slope of 5%. In both figures, the water surface is colored
according to vertical position, Z. Under these representative flow conditions, Cover J exhibits
higher hydraulic performance than Cover K. The computed hydraulic efficiencies are 0.96 and
0.90, respectively, for the lower-flow case, and 0.72 and 0.58, respectively, for the higher-flow
case. Although the upstream flow conditions are identical, the flow spread in the vicinity of the

                                                                                                Page | 9

drain is greater for Cover K, resulting in a larger proportion of flow bypassing the inlet. The
curb-and-gutter geometry also influences the local flow pattern. For curb-and-gutter Detail B,
the flow can partially extend onto the curb because of the smaller curb slope.

The longitudinal grade ranges from 0.003 ft/ft to 0.05 ft/ft, which is typical for roads in
Michigan. The cross slope is 0.02 ft/ft. At lower longitudinal grades, flow velocity is relatively
low, allowing the drain inlet to intercept most of the flow: all or most of the frontal flow and part
of the side flow enter the inlet. In some cases, backflow occurs when flow separates at the
downstream roadside corner of the grate and recirculates downstream, returning toward the
grate. At steeper longitudinal grades, the grate intercepts less side flow, backflow does not occur,
and a larger share of the flow bypasses the inlet.

The following figures provide a detailed view of the on-grade interception behavior of the
combination inlet covers, Cover J and Cover K, over a range of gutter flow rates and longitudinal
slopes.

In Figure 4-5, the intercepted discharge through the grate and curb-opening window is plotted
as a function of total gutter flow for longitudinal slopes ranging from 0.003 ft/ft to 0.05 ft/ft. For
both Cover J and Cover K, the intercepted flow through the grate increases steadily as gutter
flow increases, demonstrating that the inlet captures a larger absolute discharge as more runoff
approaches the structure. The curb-opening window also intercepts flow, but its contribution is
consistently much smaller than that of the grate. The plots further show that, for a given gutter
flow, interception performance tends to decrease as longitudinal slope increases. This trend is
expected because higher slopes increase flow velocity in the gutter, reducing the residence time
of water over the inlet and increasing the fraction of runoff that bypasses the opening.
Comparing the two subfigures suggests that Cover J achieves slightly higher grate and total
interception than Cover K at similar flow and slope conditions, indicating somewhat better
hydraulic performance.

Figure 5-6 presents the ratio of grate interception and window interception to the total
intercepted flow for each test case. These plots make clear that the grate is the dominant
interception pathway for both covers across all tested conditions. In most cases, the grate
accounts for approximately 85 to 95 percent of the total intercepted discharge, while the window
contributes only a small remainder. The relative contribution of the window varies somewhat
with flow and slope, but it remains secondary throughout the test matrix. This finding indicates
that the hydraulic performance of the inlet is governed primarily by grate behavior rather than
by the curb-opening window. The comparison between Cover J and Cover K also shows only
modest differences in these ratios, implying that although total captured flow differs somewhat
between the covers, the internal distribution between grate and window interception remains
broadly similar.

Figure 5-7 isolates the front interception component of grate capture and plots it against total
gutter discharge. Front interception represents the fraction of approaching flow captured along
the upstream edge of the grate, and it is shown to increase significantly with increasing gutter
flow for both covers. The results also indicate that front interception becomes more pronounced
as longitudinal slope increases. Under steeper slopes, the gutter flow arrives at the inlet with
greater momentum and is more strongly directed toward the front edge, making this portion of
the grate increasingly important in controlling overall capture. Cover J appears to sustain
somewhat greater front interception than Cover K over much of the tested range, which helps
explain its slightly higher total hydraulic efficiency. These results confirm that the leading edge
of the grate is the primary capture zone for on-grade flow conditions.

In Figure 5-8, the side interception component is plotted as a function of gutter discharge. Side
interception is much smaller than front interception for both covers and across all slopes, which
is consistent with the flow approaching the inlet predominantly from upstream rather than
laterally. Although side interception generally increases with discharge, its absolute magnitude
remains limited, indicating that only a relatively small portion of the total runoff is captured
along the sides of the grate. The effect of longitudinal slope is also evident: as slope increases,
the proportion of flow reaching the side of the grate decreases because more of the runoff is
concentrated and intercepted at the front edge. Thus, side interception plays a secondary role in
the overall hydraulic behavior of the inlet, and differences between Cover J and Cover K in this
component are relatively minor compared with those observed in front interception.

5.2. Sag Locations

The catch basin analyses for sag locations considered three inlet conditions: (1) an unobstructed
grate and curb opening, (2) obstruction of either the grate or the curb-opening section, and (3) a
50% reduction in the open area of both the grate and curb opening. These scenarios were
selected to evaluate how blockage and reduced inlet capacity affect drainage performance under
sump conditions.

Flow under sag conditions was simulated using a model that included a section of roadway,
sufficient vertical space above the road to allow formation of a deep pond with an air layer above
the water surface, the grate and curb opening, and the catch basin itself. The computational
domain was initially filled with water from the road surface to a depth of 0.9 m (about 3 ft),
which exceeds the maximum design water depth. This setup ensured that the full range of inlet
flow behavior could be captured as the pond drained.

The simulations were performed as unsteady-flow analyses. At the start of each simulation,
water began to drain through the grate and curb opening and exited the system through a
pressure-outlet boundary condition at the bottom of the catch basin, at atmospheric pressure.
As the water depth decreased over time, the hydraulic behavior of the inlet evolved through the
expected sequence of flow regimes: initially orifice flow, followed by transition flow, and
eventually weir flow. This progression reflects the changing degree of submergence and the
corresponding shift in controlling hydraulic mechanism.

During each simulation, the average ponded water depth away from the inlet and the mass flow
rate through the inlet were recorded at regular time intervals. In postprocessing, these time
histories were combined to develop the relationship between ponding depth and discharge. This
approach made it possible to quantify inlet capacity over a range of water depths and to compare
the performance of the different obstruction scenarios on a consistent basis.

and ponded water depth, reported in U.S. customary units as discharge in cubic feet per second
(cfs) and water depth in feet (ft). The results were fitted using the same functional forms as in
HEC-22 to obtain a hydraulically meaningful rating curve:

differences are most evident under partially obstructed conditions, where the influence of
blockage geometry on flow contraction and internal circulation becomes more pronounced.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that clogging significantly reduces inlet hydraulic
capacity, increases surface ponding, and prolongs drainage, thereby elevating the potential for
localized flooding. The comparison also indicates that the location and type of blockage matter,
with window obstruction appearing more detrimental than grate clogging for the conditions
represented in these simulations.

6. Summary and Conclusions
This study used three-dimensional computational fluid dynamics to evaluate the hydraulic
performance of Michigan DOT Covers J and K for both on-grade and sag inlet applications. The
analyses examined inlet interception efficiency, the distribution of captured flow between the
grate and curb-opening window, the relative importance of front and side grate interception, the
effects of debris blockage, and the hydraulic capacity of the inlets under sag conditions. In
addition, CFD results were compared with HEC-22-based design methods to assess the
adequacy of current design assumptions.

For on-grade conditions, the simulations showed that hydraulic efficiency depends strongly on
gutter discharge, longitudinal slope, and inlet geometry. For both covers, interception efficiency
decreased as flow rate and longitudinal slope increased, since higher approach velocities and
larger spreads increased the proportion of bypass flow. Across the full range of simulated cases,
Cover J consistently performed better than Cover K, intercepting a larger fraction of the
approaching runoff under comparable hydraulic conditions. At lower flow rates, both covers
were capable of nearly complete interception, but Cover J maintained high efficiency over a
broader range of conditions. At steeper slopes and higher discharges, the difference between the
two covers became more pronounced, with Cover J showing greater robustness and lower
bypass.

The on-grade flow partitioning results further showed that the grate is the dominant
interception pathway for both cover types, while the curb-opening window contributes a smaller
but still measurable portion of the total intercepted discharge. In most cases, the grate captured
the large majority of the intercepted flow, and the internal distribution within the grate
demonstrated that front interception is the controlling mechanism. The upstream edge of the
grate accounted for most of the grate capture, while side interception remained comparatively
small. This trend became more pronounced as longitudinal slope increased, indicating that
higher approach momentum shifts the hydraulic burden increasingly toward the front edge of
the inlet. These findings suggest that inlet geometry influencing frontal capture has the greatest
effect on on-grade performance.

The debris simulations showed that localized blockage upstream of the grate can severely
degrade inlet effectiveness. A cylindrical obstruction placed immediately upstream of the inlet
diverted flow away from the grate, increased bypass, and caused a substantial reduction in
interception efficiency for both covers. Under the tested conditions, Cover J efficiency dropped
from 96% to 67% and 33%, while Cover K efficiency decreased from 90% to 56% and 25%,
depending on the size of the obstruction. These results demonstrate that even partial blockage of
the approach flow path can significantly reduce hydraulic performance and increase the risk of
surface ponding. They also indicate that Cover J is somewhat more resilient than Cover K under
obstructed conditions. Nevertheless, vertical curb-openings like in Cover K, are often less prone
to clogging because floating debris tends to pass by rather than accumulate directly over the
opening.

For sag conditions, the CFD simulations captured the full drainage process as ponded water
above the inlet receded over time. The results confirmed that inlet flow transitions through the
expected sequence of hydraulic regimes, beginning with orifice flow at high ponding depths,
passing through a transitional regime, and ending in weir flow as the water depth decreases. For
both Cover J and Cover K, the unobstructed configuration provided the greatest hydraulic
capacity, while blockage of either the grate or the curb opening reduced discharge, increased
ponding depth, and prolonged drainage time. Among the blockage scenarios considered, grate
obstruction generally produced the lowest flow rates and the deepest ponding, indicating that

                                                                                         Page | 32

the grate opening is the most hydraulically important component under the modeled sag
conditions.

Comparison of the two cover types under sag conditions showed that they follow the same
overall trends, but Cover J again performed somewhat better than Cover K. In the stage-
discharge relationships, Cover J generally conveyed slightly higher flow rates for the same
ponding depth. The time-series results also showed that Cover J drained more quickly, with
lower residual ponding and shorter recession periods, while Cover K exhibited a slower
hydraulic response and longer drawdown, particularly under obstructed conditions. These
differences indicate that Cover J has slightly greater hydraulic capacity and somewhat lower
sensitivity to partial obstruction than Cover K.

These findings are broadly consistent with those reported previously for MDOT Cover C. In both
studies, CFD results show that on-grade interception decreases with increasing discharge and
longitudinal slope, that inlet performance is governed primarily by grate interception and
especially front-edge capture, and that obstruction can substantially reduce hydraulic
effectiveness. Relative to Cover C, Covers J and K exhibit the additional hydraulic behavior
associated with the curb-opening contribution, but the overall trends with flow rate, slope, and
sag-depth remain similar.

The comparison between CFD and HEC-22 predictions showed that the empirical design
equations reproduce the general hydraulic trends observed in the simulations, including the
increase in discharge with ponding depth and the relative ranking of the unobstructed and
obstructed cases. However, the CFD results also revealed deviations in the magnitude of
predicted discharge, especially under partially obstructed conditions, where detailed three-
dimensional flow contraction, local recirculation, and geometry-specific blockage effects become
important. For on-grade conditions, the current MDOT practice of using HEC-22 relationships
for a comparable reticuline grate was found to be conservative, generally underpredicting inlet
efficiency relative to CFD by roughly 10 to 20%. For sag conditions, the HEC-22 approach
provided a reasonable first-order estimate of capacity, and the results suggest that when both
the grate and curb opening are reduced by the same percentage, inlet capacity may be
approximated by proportionally reducing the HEC-22 flow rate.

Overall, the results of this study show that both Covers J and K provide effective hydraulic
performance, but Cover J is consistently the stronger design across the range of on-grade and
sag conditions examined. It achieves higher interception efficiency, greater sag capacity, faster
drainage, and somewhat better resilience to blockage. The study also confirms that grate
capture, and especially frontal grate capture, governs inlet performance in on-grade
applications, while in sag conditions the available open area of the grate and curb opening
strongly controls the transition between weir and orifice behavior. Finally, the results highlight
the importance of debris management and suggest that CFD can provide useful insight beyond
standard design equations, particularly for inlet geometries and blockage scenarios not directly
addressed by HEC-22.

For design purposes, the current MDOT practice of applying HEC-22 methods using a
comparable reticuline grate remains conservative for on-grade analysis of Covers J and K.
Where inlet performance is critical, Cover J is preferred due to its consistently higher
interception efficiency and sag capacity. For sag locations and maintenance-sensitive areas, a
partial-blockage sensitivity check is suggested.
