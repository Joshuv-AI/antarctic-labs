Effects of age, sex, and renal function on bowel movement‐improving effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum G9‐1 on constipation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease: A prespecified subgroup analysis of the BIRDIE study
Authors: Fuki Ikeda, Junko Sato, Hidenori Yoshii, Hiroaki Satoh, Hirotsugu Uzawa, Daisuke Sugimoto, Reina Muto, Yuya Nishida, Hirotaka Watada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionType 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease are associated with increasing risk of constipation. We previously reported that Bifidobacterium bifidum G9‐1 improved stool consistency, bowel movement, and quality of life of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and constipation. However, the effect of patients' background characteristics on the efficacy of B. bifidum G9‐1 has not yet been investigated.Materials and MethodsThis prespecified subgroup analysis of the BIRDIE study investigated the effect of age, sex, and baseline renal function on the efficacy of B. bifidum G9‐1.ResultsThe primary endpoint, the percentage of participants with normal stool consistency at week 12, significantly increased in participants aged ≥65 years, male, and those with estimated glomerular filtration rate of ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2. Regarding the secondary endpoints, although the improved variables differed according to subgroup, the constipation score on the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale and abdominal bloating among the abdominal symptoms significantly improved in most of the subgroups, except for the subgroup including only a few participants. Metformin users were more likely to experience relief from constipation, whereas insulin users were less likely to experience improvement.DiscussionPatients aged ≥65 years, male, and those with estimated glomerular filtration of ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 are more likely to benefit from treatment with B. bifidum G9‐1. These results may provide useful guidance for considering treatment options based on patients' background characteristics upon treatment of constipation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease.Keywords: Constipation, diabetic nephropathies, probiotics

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus 1 and chronic kidney disease (CKD) 2 are associated with increasing risk of constipation. As constipation lowers quality of life (QOL) 3 , 4 and increases the financial burden on patients, 5 , 6 it remains an important clinical issue.

The probiotic Bifidobacterium is a useful pharmacological option for the treatment of constipation. For example, Bifidobacterium lactis‐containing fermented milk for constipated women, 7 Bifidobacterium infantis‐containing synbiotics for chronic constipation, 8 Bifidobacterium breve‐containing probiotics for healthy volunteers with evacuation disorders and hard stools, 9 and Bifidobacterium bifidum strain CCFM16 for chronic constipation 10 improve constipation. Another strain, B. bifidum G9‐1 (BBG9‐1), also improves gastrointestinal function in patients with chronic constipation 11 and in those with diabetes mellitus treated with metformin (Big STAR study) 12 without worsening glycemic control. Moreover, in our previous study, the “Efficacy of probiotics (BIofermin) in patients with Renal dysfunction (DIabetic kidney disease) complicated with constipation; an Exploratory, multicenter, open‐label, single‐arm before‐after study” (BIRDIE study), the administration of the tablets containing BBG9‐1 (brand name: Biofermin® tablet) for 12 weeks significantly increased the proportion of participants with normal stool consistency and improved bowel movement and QOL in participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus, CKD, and constipation. 13

However, the effect of participants' background characteristics on the efficacy of the probiotic BBG9‐1 has not yet been investigated. To address this issue, this prespecified subgroup analysis of the BIRDIE study aimed to investigate the effects of age, sex, and renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR]) at baseline on the efficacy of the probiotic BBG9‐1.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study overview

The BIRDIE study was designed as an exploratory, multicenter, open‐label, single‐arm, before‐and‐after study and conducted at four medical institutions in Japan. The primary and secondary outcomes of this study have been previously described. 13 Briefly, 30 participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus, renal dysfunction, and constipation were enrolled. All enrolled participants orally received tablets of the BBG9‐1 for 12 weeks. The participants were subsequently observed at week 0 (baseline), 6, and 12.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint in this study was the percentage of participants with normal stool consistency at week 12. The stool consistency was measured using the Bristol Scale score, which classifies stool consistency into seven types as follows; type 1: separate hard lumps, type 2: lumpy and sausage like, type 3: a sausage shape with cracks in the surface, type 4: like a smooth, soft sausage or snake, type 5: soft blobs with clear‐cut edges, type 6: mushy consistency with ragged edges, and type 7: liquid consistency with no solid pieces. Types 1 and 2 are generally considered as constipation, types 3–5 as normal, and types 6 and 7 as diarrhea. 14 Especially, the Bristol Scale score of 4 is considered as an ideal stool consistency, as QOL in patients with chronic constipation was highest when the Bristol Scale score was 4. 15 The previous Big STAR study 12 employed the Bristol Scale score of 3.5 or higher but less than 4.5 as an index of the ideal fecal condition. More recent clinical trial conducted in the same group using BBG9‐1 in patients with gastrointestinal symptoms and type 2 diabetes mellitus (Binary STAR study) also employed this criterion as the index of normal stool. 16 According to the Big STAR study, this study defined that stool consistency was normal when the average of the Bristol Scale score for the two weeks prior to each observation point was 3.5 or higher but less than 4.5. The secondary endpoints included stool consistency, frequency of bowel movement, Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) score, and abdominal symptoms (straining, feeling of incomplete bowel movement, abdominal bloating, and abdominal discomfort). The GSRS is a 7‐point Likert scale with scores ranging from 1 (no discomfort at all) to 7 (very severe discomfort). Abdominal symptoms were recorded daily by the participants themselves on a 5‐point Likert scale with scores ranging from 0 (no) to 4 (very severe). The mean score for each abdominal symptom for the two weeks prior to each observation point was defined as the abdominal symptom score at the observation point.

Subgroup analysis

The statistical analysis plan of this study, that was developed before the database lock, prespecified the subgroup analyses of the primary and secondary endpoints. The subgroups were defined as follows: by age (younger than 65 years, 65 years or older), sex (male, female), and eGFR at baseline (less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2, 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher but less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 or higher).

Exploration of factors associated with the primary endpoint

The prespecified exploratory endpoints included the exploration of factors associated with the primary endpoint. Responders and nonresponders were defined as the participants whose stool consistency was normal (the average of the Bristol Scale score for the two weeks prior to week 12 was 3.5 or higher but less than 4.5) or abnormal at week 12, respectively, and baseline characteristics in each population were compared. Additionally, correlation analysis was performed to explore the factors associated with the primary endpoint.

Statistical analyses

Detailed methods of the statistical analyses have been previously described. 13 Briefly, for the primary endpoint, McNemar's test was used for within‐group comparison of the percentage of participants between baseline and week 12. For the secondary endpoints, one‐sample t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests were used for within‐group comparisons. Subgroup analyses were performed using Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and two‐sample t‐test for continuous variables for between‐group comparison. Correlation analysis to explore the factors associated with the primary endpoint was conducted using Pearson's product–moment and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient by transforming the classification of responder/nonresponder to an ordinal variable, with nonresponder = 0 and responder = 1.

RESULTS

Baseline participant characteristics

Detailed baseline participant characteristics have been previously described. 13 Most of the enrolled participants were male (21 [70.0%] participants). The mean (±standard deviation) age, duration of diabetes, eGFR at baseline, and median ([first quartile, third quartile]) urinary albumin to creatinine ratio (UACR) at baseline were 65.5 ± 8.3 years, 16.6 ± 9.1 years, 59.4 ± 25.8 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 382.1 (230.0, 846.0) mg/g·Cre, respectively.

Percentage of participants with normal stool consistency

The primary endpoint in this study was the percentage of participants with normal stool consistency at week 12 (the average of the Bristol Scale score for the two weeks prior to week 12 was 3.5 or higher but less than 4.5). Although this study enrolled participants with constipation, as indicated by an average of the Bristol Scale score of less than 3.5, because of a violation to the eligibility criterion, one (3.3%) participant had a normal stool consistency at baseline. Among all enrolled participants, the percentage of participants with normal stool consistency significantly increased from baseline to week 12 (13 [44.8%] participants) (McNemar's P = 0.002) (Table 1).

The total GSRS score significantly decreased from baseline to weeks 6 and 12 in participants aged ≥65 years (P = 0.007 and <0.001, respectively), female participants (P = 0.007 and 0.002, respectively), and those with eGFR of ≥30 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (P = 0.004 and 0.013, respectively) and significantly decreased from baseline to week 12 in male participants and those with eGFR of ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (P = 0.018). Participants aged <65 years (P = 0.06) and participants with eGFR of <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 (P = 0.07) showed a tendency to decrease from baseline to week 12.

Participants aged ≥65 years, male participants, and those with eGFR of ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 showed a significant decrease or a tendency to decrease in all four abdominal symptoms at least one week in weeks 6 and 12. Participants aged <65 years and those with eGFR of ≥30 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 showed a significant decrease or a tendency to decrease in incomplete bowel movement, abdominal bloating, and abdominal discomfort at least one week in weeks 6 and 12, but not in straining. Female participants showed a significant decrease or a tendency to decrease in abdominal bloating and abdominal discomfort in at least one of the weeks 6 and 12, but not in straining or incomplete bowel movement. Participants with eGFR of <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 did not show a significant change or a tendency to change in all four abdominal symptoms.

Factors associated with the primary endpoint

Baseline characteristics were compared between responders (the participants whose stool consistency was normal at week 12 [the average of the Bristol Scale score for the two weeks prior to week 12 was 3.5 or higher but less than 4.5]) and nonresponders. Baseline characteristics did not significantly differ between responders and nonresponders; however, the proportion of participants who used biguanide at baseline tended to be higher in responders (P = 0.05) and the proportion of participants who used insulin at baseline tended to be higher in nonresponders (P = 0.07) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This subgroup analysis of the BIRDIE study demonstrated that the efficacy of the probiotic BBG9‐1 for 12 weeks in participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus, CKD, and constipation may differ according to the participants' background characteristics (age, sex, and renal function). The primary endpoint, the percentage of participants with normal stool consistency, significantly increased in participants aged ≥65 years, male participants, and those with eGFR of ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2. To the best of our knowledge, investigations into the effect of individual background characteristics on the efficacy of B. bifidum are limited. A possible explanation for the effect of age is that, as the relative abundance of B. bifidum gradually decreased by age, 17 supplementation with B. bifidum was more effective in the elderly population. However, no study had investigated the effects of sex or renal function. Therefore, although the main analysis of the BIRDIE study has already demonstrated that BBG9‐1 improved stool consistency, bowel movement, and QOL, 13 this subgroup analysis showed novel findings regarding the different efficacies of BBG9‐1 according to the participants' background characteristics. These results may contribute to the optimization of the treatment effects of BBG9‐1 in participants with type 2 diabetes mellitus, CKD, and constipation. Particularly patients aged ≥65 years, male, and those with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 might be more responsive to treatment with BBG9‐1.

On the contrary, regarding the secondary endpoints, although the variables improved by the BBG9‐1 differed according to subgroup, the constipation score in the GSRS and abdominal bloating among the abdominal symptoms were significantly improved in all subgroups, except for the subgroup with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2, which included only a few (three) participants. Even in the subgroups in which the primary endpoint did not improve, various endpoints, including stool consistency, GSRS score, and abdominal symptoms, improved. These results suggest that BBG9‐1 is clinically effective in terms of overall symptom relief and improved QOL, even if the primary endpoint is not achieved. Recent Binary STAR study using BBG9‐1 in patients with gastrointestinal symptoms (constipation or diarrhea) and type 2 diabetes mellitus reported that the GSRS total score significantly improved in participants aged ≥65 years, female, and participants with BMI < 25 kg/m2. 16 , 18 This is consistent with the results in this study, showing that the GSRS total score significantly improved from baseline to weeks 6 and 12 in participants aged ≥65 years, female, and those with eGFR of ≥30 and < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, although this study did not include BMI as a factor for the prespecified subgroup analysis. These results in the Binary STAR study and this BIRDIE study demonstrate the robustness of the effectiveness of BBG9‐1 in these subgroups on the patients' QOL. It is reported that, although both stool consistency and bowel movement are important for QOL in patients with constipation, normal stool form was important for improving the QOL in patients with constipation. 15 This is consistent with the results in this study as the stool consistency and participants' QOL improved or tended to improve in many subgroups, regardless that improvement in bowel movement was limited.

The responder/nonresponder analysis showed that metformin users were more likely to experience relief from constipation, whereas insulin users tended not to show an improvement in constipation with BBG9‐1 administration. The finding that the administration of BBG9‐1 to patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with metformin improved abdominal symptoms in this study was consistent with a previous report demonstrating the efficacy of BBG9‐1 12 and showed the robustness of the results. Several studies have suggested an association between insulin use and constipation. 19 , 20 However, few studies have demonstrated that insulin directly causes constipation. Rather, constipation is a common complication in patients with diabetes mellitus. 1 , 21 High blood glucose levels are believed to cause nerve damage, which slows intestinal peristalsis. As insulin users generally tend to have more advanced diabetes and a longer duration of diabetes, they are more prone to constipation. One possible explanation for the lack of improvement in constipation in insulin users is that BBG9‐1 did not improve glycemic control.

This study has some limitations. First, the subgroup analyses included a small sample size, because number of total enrolled participants was 30. 13 Therefore, the results in the subgroup analyses in this article may be considered as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, and further confirmatory clinical trials with enough larger sample size are required for definitive evidence in the future. In particular, the subgroup with eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m2 included only three participants. The statistical power is far less in this subgroup, and we could not conclude whether the BBG9‐1 has the benefit or less benefit in this subgroup. Second, age, sex, and eGFR may be interrelated, and confounding factors or interactions cannot be completely ruled out.

In conclusion, this subgroup analysis suggests that the efficacy of B. bifidum G9‐1 (BBG9‐1) in improving constipation varies according to age, sex, and renal function. In particular, patients aged ≥65 years, male, and those with eGFR ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2 are more likely to benefit from treatment with BBG9‐1. These results may provide useful guidance for considering treatment options based on patients' background characteristics upon treatment of constipation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus and CKD.
