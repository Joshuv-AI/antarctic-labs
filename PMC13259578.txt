Preparing Canada’s health system for conflict: beyond resilience toward sumud
Authors: Emmanuel Stip
Published: 2026

Grushka and colleagues highlight the urgent need to prepare Canada’s health systems for conflict through strengthened civilian–military medical readiness.1 Their framework appropriately emphasizes coordination, surge capacity, and system-level resilience. However, it leaves largely implicit the psychological assumptions underpinning preparedness in contexts of sustained adversity.

Much of contemporary mental health practice is grounded in resilience, understood as an individual capacity to adapt and recover. Yet in situations of protracted conflict, displacement, or structural instability, adversity is not temporary. The expectation of recovery toward equilibrium may therefore be unrealistic. This gap becomes particularly visible in contexts of prolonged conflict. In such contexts, an alternative framework — sumud (an Arabic word that is difficult to translate fully but conveys forms of steadfastness, perseverance, rootedness, and collective endurance in the face of ongoing adversity) — offers a useful complement.

Sumud describes a collective, relational form of endurance that prioritizes continuity, dignity, and meaning rather than recovery. It is not about “bouncing back,” but about persisting without the expectation of resolution. This distinction is clinically relevant: When distress is ongoing, interventions focused solely on individual coping and restoration risk misalignment with lived realities. For example, in primary care and mental health settings, clinicians may encounter patients whose distress persists despite appropriate interventions, reflecting ongoing structural or transnational stressors. This is particularly relevant for family physicians, who often provide longitudinal care to patients living with unresolved or ongoing adversity.

These considerations are not limited to distant settings. In Canadian clinical practice, many patients — including refugees and members of diasporic communities affected by ongoing conflicts — live with forms of adversity that remain active across borders and over time, as reflected in national guidance on immigrant and refugee health.2

Preparing health systems for conflict, therefore, is not only a logistical and organizational challenge. It also requires rethinking the psychological models that guide care. Are we preparing systems to restore health — or to sustain dignity where restoration is not possible?
