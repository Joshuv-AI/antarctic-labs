Abstract
. In the context of the Dirac equation with L 2 ​ ( ℝ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{R}) -potential, we study the Jost solutions and prove that the maximal function associated with the argument of the transmission coefficient is unbounded. We also prove that the strong version of the Nonlinear Carleson Conjecture (NCC) fails for Dirac equations and Krein systems.



1.
Krein systems, maximal functions, and the NCC

Consider the fundamental matrix
X(x,k,A)
for the Krein system which is defined as the solution to the Cauchy problem

(1.1)

\partial_{x}{X}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}ik&-\overline{A}\\
-A&0\end{array}\right)X,\quad X(0,k,A)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right)\,,

where
k=\xi+i\eta\in\mathbb{C}
and
A(x)\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.
We can write (see
[
3
]
, p.38)

X=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathfrak{A}^{*}&\mathfrak{B}^{*}\\
\mathfrak{B}&\mathfrak{A}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
where
\mathfrak{A}(x,k,A)
and
\mathfrak{B}(x,k,A)
, as functions in
k
, are entire functions of exponential type at most
x
and they satisfy

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}\mathfrak{A}^{*}-\mathfrak{B}\mathfrak{B}^{*}=e^{ikx},\quad k\in\mathbb{C},

(1.3)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{A}|^{2}=1+|\mathfrak{B}|^{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{R},

(1.4)

\displaystyle|\mathfrak{A}|^{2}\geqslant 1+|\mathfrak{B}|^{2},|\mathfrak{A}|^{2}\geqslant 1+|\mathfrak{B}^{*}|^{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\,.

Here, we use convention
f^{*}(k):=e^{ikx}\overline{f(\bar{k})}
for a function
f
defined in
\mathbb{C}
and such operation is
x
-dependent.
Given
A\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, we have two limits as
x\to+\infty
:

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}(x,k,A)\to\mathfrak{a}(k,A),\quad\mathfrak{B}(x,k,A)\to\mathfrak{b}(k,A)

and the convergence is locally uniform in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
(see
[
3
]
, Section 12). In folklore, the question of existence of limits
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathfrak{A}(x,\xi,A)
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathfrak{B}(x,\xi,A)
for a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
often goes under the name
Nonlinear Carleson Conjecture
(NCC). See
[
10
,
11
]
for related results and discussion. This problem is motivated by, e.g., studying the stationary and non-stationary scattering in Dirac and Schrödinger equations. In fact, existence of such limits often implies the existence of wave operators for the corresponding Schrödinger dynamics
[
2
,
5
]
. The weaker version of NCC asks to study the existence of the limit
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathfrak{B}(x,\xi,A)/\mathfrak{A}(x,\xi,A)
, so we will address existence of
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathfrak{A}(x,\xi,A)
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathfrak{B}(x,\xi,A)
for a.e.
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
as
a strong version of NCC
. In the case when
A\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{+}),p\in[1,2)
, the convergence and the corresponding estimates for maximal functions are known
[
2
,
11
,
8
]
. For
p>2
, there are examples for which the limits do not exist (check
[
7
]
, where this problem was addressed for the Schrödinger operators). In the current work, we will study the borderline case
p=2
. The note
[
4
]
(see also
[
12
,
9
]
) contains discussion of NCC in the context of polynomials orthogonal on the unit circle (OPUC) which has some advantages. We, however, prefer to address NCC for Krein systems and Dirac equations because these two models exhibit more scaling properties than the OPUC setup and that makes our analysis less technical.

Q1

Do the limits

Q2

Clearly,
\mathfrak{M}_{w}(\xi,A)\leqslant\mathfrak{M}(\xi,A)
.

Are the bounds
\mathfrak{M}(\xi,A)<\infty
or
\mathfrak{M}(\xi,A)<\infty

?
If so, do we have weak-

(1.6)

|\{\xi:\mathfrak{M}(\xi,A)\geqslant\lambda\}|\lesssim\frac{\|A\|_{2}}{\lambda}\quad{\it or}\quad|\{\xi:\mathfrak{M}_{w}(\xi,A)\geqslant\lambda\}|\lesssim\frac{\|A\|_{2}}{\lambda},\quad\forall\lambda>0

at least in the perturbative regime when
\|A\|_{2}\leqslant\delta_{0}
where
\delta_{0}
is some small positive number?

Our main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Both questions Q1 and Q2 have negative answers.

Proof.

We will need the following two scaling properties of the fundamental matrix

1. Modulation.
Let

\mathfrak{A}(x,k,A)=\mathfrak{A}(x,k-\ell,Ae^{i\ell x}),\mathfrak{B}(x,k,A)=\mathfrak{B}(x,k-\ell,Ae^{i\ell x})

Hence,

(1.9)

X(\mu x,\mu^{-1}k,A)=X(x,k,A^{(\mu)})

and
\mathfrak{A}(\mu x,\mu^{-1}k,A)=\mathfrak{A}(x,k,A^{(\mu)}),\mathfrak{B}(\mu x,\mu^{-1}k,A)=\mathfrak{B}(x,k,A^{(\mu)})
.

2.
Dirac operator formalism, inverse scattering theory approach, and negative answers to Q1 and Q2

We recall some basics about the Dirac equation on the line closely following
[
1
]
, Section 2 (see also
[
6
]
). Given a complex-valued function
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, define the differential operator

(2.1)

\mathcal{L}_{q}=i\sigma_{3}\partial_{x}+i(q\sigma_{-}-\overline{q}\sigma_{+}),

where
\sigma_{3}
,
\sigma_{\pm}

(2.2)

\sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\sigma_{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let us also define

E(x,k)=e^{\frac{k}{2i}x\sigma_{3}}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\frac{k}{2i}x}&0\\
0&e^{-\frac{k}{2i}x}\end{pmatrix}.

In the free case when
q=0
, the matrix-function
E
solves
\mathcal{L}_{0}E={\frac{k}{2}}E,\,E(0,k)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Since
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, it decays at infinity fast and therefore one can find two solutions

T_{\pm}=T_{\pm}(x,\xi,q)
such that

(2.3)

\mathcal{L}_{q}T_{\pm}=\tfrac{\xi}{2}T_{\pm},\qquad T_{\pm}=E(x,\xi)+o(1),\qquad x\to\pm\infty,

for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.
These solutions are called the
Jost solutions
for
\mathcal{L}_{q}
. Since both
T_{+}
and
T_{-}
solve the same ODE, they must satisfy

(2.4)

T_{-}(x,\xi,q)=T_{+}(x,\xi,q)T(\xi,q),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\xi\in\mathbb{R},

where the matrix
T=T(\xi,q)
does not depend on
x\in\mathbb{R}
. One can show that it has the form

(2.5)

T(\xi,q)=\begin{pmatrix}a(\xi,q)&\overline{b(\xi,q)}\\
b(\xi,q)&\overline{a(\xi,q)}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det T=|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1.

The matrix
T
is called the
reduced transition matrix
for
\mathcal{L}_{q}
, and the ratio
{\bf r}=b/a
is called the
reflection coefficient
for
\mathcal{L}_{q}
and
1/a
is called the
transmission coefficient
. One can obtain
T
in a different way: let
Z=Z(x,k,q)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
k\in\mathbb{C}
be the fundamental matrix for
\mathcal{L}_{q}
with spectral parameter
\tfrac{k}{2}
, that is,

(2.6)

\mathcal{L}_{q}Z=\tfrac{k}{2}Z,\qquad Z(0,k,q)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Then, we have

(2.7)

Z(x,k,q)=T_{\pm}(x,k,q)T^{-1}_{\pm}(0,k,q)

and the pointwise limits

(2.8)

T^{-1}_{\pm}(0,\xi,q)=\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}E^{-1}(x,\xi)Z(x,\xi,q)

exist for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, we have
T(\xi,q)=T_{+}^{-1}(0,\xi,q)T_{-}(0,\xi,q)
on
\mathbb{R}
.

The coefficients
a,b
, and
{\bf r}
were defined for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and they satisfy
|a|^{2}=1+|b|^{2}
,
1-|{\bf r}|^{2}=|a|^{-2}
for these
\xi
. However, one can show that
a(\xi,q)
is the boundary value of the outer function defined in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
by the formula

a(k,q)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\xi-k}\log|a(\xi,q)|\,d\xi\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

which, in view of identity
1-|{\bf r}|^{2}=|a|^{-2}=(1+|b|^{2})^{-1}
on
\mathbb{R}
, can be written as

(2.9)

a(k,q)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\xi-k}\log(1-|{\bf r}(\xi)|^{2})d\xi\right)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\xi-k}\log(1+|b(\xi,q)|^{2})d\xi\right)\,.

That shows, in particular, that
b
defines both
a
and
{\bf r}
, and
{\bf r}
defines
a
and
b
. The
sum rule
for the Dirac equation reads

\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}}|q(x)|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log|a(\xi,q)|d\xi\,.

The map
q(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[r]}}{{\mapsto}}{\bf r}(k,q)
is called the direct scattering transform and its inverse is called the inverse scattering transform. These maps are well-studied when
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. In particular, we have the following result (see Theorem 2.1 in
[
1
]
, the proof can be found in
[
6
]
):

Theorem 2.1
.

The map
q\stackrel{{\scriptstyle[r]}}{{\mapsto}}{\bf r}
is a bijection from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
onto the set of complex-valued functions
\{{\bf r}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}),\|{\bf r}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}<1\}
.

In particular, each
b(k)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
uniquely defines
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
such that
b(k)=b(k,q)
.
That scattering transform has some symmetries (see
[
1
]
and
[
8
]
, p.241):

Lemma 2.1
.

If
q\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(dilation):

\displaystyle\qquad{\bf r}(\xi,\mu q(\mu x))={\bf r}(\mu^{-1}\xi,q(x)),\quad\mu>0\,,

(conjugation):

\displaystyle\qquad{\bf r}(\xi,\overline{q}(x))={\overline{{\bf r}(-\xi,q)}}\,,

(translation):

\displaystyle\qquad{\bf r}(\xi,q(x-\ell))={\bf r}(\xi,q(x))e^{-i\xi\ell},\quad\ell\in\mathbb{R}\,,

(modulation):

\displaystyle\qquad{\bf r}(\xi,e^{-i\beta x}q(x))={\bf r}(\xi+\beta,q(x)),\quad\beta\in\mathbb{R}\,,

(rotation):

\displaystyle\qquad{\bf r}(\xi,\zeta q(x))={\zeta}{\bf r}(\xi,q(x)),\quad\zeta\in\mathbb{C},\;|\zeta|=1\,.

They imply the corresponding formulas for
a(\xi,q)
and
b(\xi,q)
, too.
One advantage of working with Dirac operators on the whole line is the presence of translation symmetry, which we do not have for the Krein system on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
The other benefit of the Dirac model is Theorem
2.1
which does not require the scattering data
{\bf r}
to be analytic in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

We need to outline the connection between the Krein systems and the Dirac operator
\mathcal{L}_{q}
(see
[
1
]
, p.225). Given
q
, let

A_{+}(x)=-\tfrac{1}{2}\overline{q(\tfrac{x}{2})},\quad A_{-}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}{q(-\tfrac{x}{2})}\,,\quad x\geqslant 0\,.

For these two Krein systems, we consider
\mathfrak{a}(k,A_{+}),\mathfrak{b}(k,A_{+})
and
\mathfrak{a}(k,A_{-}),\mathfrak{b}(k,A_{-})
. Then, we have (check
[
1
]
, p.227 and
[
8
]
, p.241)

(2.10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a({2}k,q)=\mathfrak{a}(k,A_{+})\mathfrak{a}(k,A_{-})-\mathfrak{b}(k,A_{+})\mathfrak{b}(k,A_{-}),\\
b({2}\xi,q)=\mathfrak{a}(\xi,A_{-})\overline{\mathfrak{b}(\xi,A_{+})}-\mathfrak{b}(\xi,A_{-})\overline{\mathfrak{a}(\xi,A^{+})}\,,\end{array}\right.

where
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+},\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.

The existence of Jost functions can be proved for
q\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, too. We will need two results.

Lemma 2.1
.

Consider
v_{1},v_{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, both supported inside
[-r,r],r>0
. Take
R\geqslant 2r
and let
Q=v_{1}(x)+v_{2}(x-R)
. Then, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, we get

(2.11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a(\xi,Q)=a(\xi,v_{1})a(\xi,v_{2})+b(\xi,v_{1})\overline{b(\xi,v_{2})}e^{-\xi R/i},\\
b(\xi,Q)=\overline{a(\xi,v_{2})}b(\xi,v_{1})+{a(\xi,v_{1})}b(\xi,v_{2})e^{\xi R/i}\,.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

This is immediate from the definition and the group property of the fundamental matrix. Alternatively, one can see it from (
1.14
), where
\ell=2
, and (
2.10
).
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Consider
v_{1},v_{2}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Take
R\geqslant 0
and let
Q=v_{1}(x)+v_{2}(x-R)
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a(\xi,Q)=a(\xi,v_{1})a(\xi,v_{2})+b(\xi,v_{1})\overline{b(\xi,v_{2})}e^{-\xi R/i}+O(\varepsilon^{*}(R))\\
b(\xi,Q)=\overline{a(\xi,v_{2})}b(\xi,v_{1})+{a(\xi,v_{1})}b(\xi,v_{2})e^{\xi R/i}+O(\varepsilon^{*}(R))\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{*}(R):=\left(\int_{|x|>R/2}|v_{1}(s)|ds+\int_{|x|>R/2}|v_{2}(s)|ds\right)\exp(C(\|v_{1}\|_{1}+\|v_{2}\|_{1}))

uniformly in
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We can take
v_{1}^{(R)}:=v_{1}\cdot\chi_{|x|<R/2},v_{2}^{(R)}:=v_{2}\cdot\chi_{|x|<R/2}
and use (
3.4
) to write

(2.15)

|a(\xi,v_{j}^{(R)})-a(\xi,v_{j})|\lesssim\varepsilon_{j}(R),\quad|b(\xi,v_{j}^{(R)})-b(\xi,v_{j})|\lesssim\varepsilon_{j}(R),\quad j\in\{1,2\}

uniformly in
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, where

\varepsilon_{j}(R):=\left(\int_{|x|>R/2}|v_{j}(s)|ds\right)\exp(C\|v_{j}\|_{1})\,.

Similarly, if one defines
Q^{(R)}:=v_{1}^{(R)}(x)+v_{2}^{(R)}(x-R)
, then (
3.4
) provides

(2.16)

|a(\xi,Q^{(R)})-a(\xi,Q)|\lesssim\varepsilon^{*}(R),\quad|b(\xi,Q^{(R)})-b(\xi,Q)|\lesssim\varepsilon^{*}(R)\,.

We can apply the previous lemma to
Q^{(R)}
. Then, substituting the obtained bounds along with (check (
3.8
) and (
3.9
)) the estimates

|a_{j}(\xi,v_{j})|\lesssim\exp(C\|v_{j}\|_{1}),\quad|b_{j}(\xi,v_{j})|\lesssim\exp(C\|v_{j}\|_{1})\,,

into (
2.11
), we arrive at (
2.2
).
∎

The strong maximal function can be defined for the Dirac operator
\mathcal{L}_{q}
as follows. Let
T_{1},T_{2}\in\mathbb{R},T_{1}<T_{2}
. Then,

\mathrm{M}(\xi,q):=\sup_{k\in S_{\xi},T_{1},T_{2}}|\arg\,a(k,q\cdot\chi_{T_{1}\leqslant x\leqslant T_{2}})|\,,

where
S_{\xi}
is a Stolz angle at
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. We can let
q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and ask the same questions we asked for Krein systems, i.e., whether the maximal function
\mathrm{M}
is well-defined and whether
a(k,q\cdot\chi_{T_{1}\leqslant x\leqslant T_{2}})\to a(k,q)
pointwise when
T_{1}\to-\infty,T_{2}\to+\infty
. In fact, taking
q=0
for
x\leqslant 0
in the formula (
2.10
), we already see that the weak-type bound for
\mathrm{M}(\xi,q)
fails due to Theorem
1.2
.
If we consider another maximal function

\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)=\sup_{T_{1},T_{2}}|\arg\,a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{T_{1}\leqslant x\leqslant T_{2}})|\,,\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}\,,

then it is clear that
\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)\leqslant\mathrm{M}(\xi,q)
.
The next result shows that these maximal functions
\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)
and
\mathrm{M}(\xi,q)
might not be even defined for
q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Theorem 2.3
.

There is
q\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)=+\infty
for
\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
.

Proof.

We do the proof in two steps.

First step: building a sample.
Unlike in the first section, we use inverse scattering theory as a stepping stone in our construction. Consider a function
b(\xi)\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with support inside the interval
(0,1)
which is not identically equal to zero. Take
\delta\in(0,1)
and consider
b_{[\delta]}(\xi):=\delta b(\xi)
. It will define
a_{[\delta]}(k)
by (
2.9
),
{\bf r}_{[\delta]}(\xi)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
and
q_{[\delta]}(x)\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. For
q_{[\delta]}
, we have a bound

(2.17)

|\partial^{p}q_{[\delta]}(x)|\leqslant_{\ell,p}(x^{2}+1)^{-\ell/2},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N},\,p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\,.

By the sum rule,

(2.18)

2\pi\int_{\mathbb{R}}|q_{[\delta]}|^{2}dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log(1+|b_{[\delta]}|^{2})d\xi\sim\delta^{2}\,.

Take
\nu\geqslant 1
and let
q_{[\delta]}^{(1/\nu)}(x):=q_{[\delta]}(x/\nu)/\nu
. Then,

b(\xi,q_{[\delta]}^{(1/\nu)})=b(\nu\xi,q_{[\delta]})=\delta b(\nu\xi)\,.

For every
j\in\{0,\ldots,\nu-1\}
, we let
q_{j}(x):=q_{[\delta]}^{(1/\nu)}(x)e^{i\xi_{j}x}
. Notice that

(2.19)

|q_{j}(x)|\leqslant_{\ell}\nu^{-1}((x/\nu)^{2}+1)^{-\ell/2},\quad\forall\ell\in\mathbb{N}\,.

The modulation property of the scattering transform yields
b(\xi,q_{j})=\delta b(\nu(\xi-\xi_{j})),a(\xi,q_{j})=a_{[\delta]}(\nu(\xi-\xi_{j})),\xi_{j}:=j/\nu
. Take
\rho>0
, let

Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta}(x):=\sum_{j=0}^{m}q_{j}(x-\rho j),\,m\in\{0,\ldots,\nu-1\}

and choose
\rho=e^{\nu^{2}}
.
By construction, we have

(2.20)

\displaystyle b(\xi,q_{j})b(\xi,q_{\ell})=0,\quad j\neq\ell\,,

(2.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}|b(\xi,q_{j})|\lesssim\delta,\quad\sum_{j=0}^{m}|b(\xi,q_{j})|^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2},\,\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}\,.

We can now apply the previous Lemma
\nu
times recursively taking each time
v_{1}=Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta}
,
v_{2}=q_{m+1}(x)
, and
R=\rho(m+1)
. To estimate the errors, we notice that
\|Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta}\|_{1}\lesssim m\leqslant\nu
and
\|q_{m+1}\|_{1}\lesssim 1
. The strong decay (
2.19
) of Schwartz-class functions
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
gives

\varepsilon^{*}\leqslant e^{-2\nu^{2}}

for each iteration and
\nu\geqslant\nu_{0}
with large enough
\nu_{0}
. Rewrite (
2.2
) as

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m+1,\rho,\delta})&\bar{b}(\xi,Q_{\nu,m+1,\rho,\delta})\\
b(\xi,Q_{\nu,m+1,\rho,\delta})&\bar{a}(Q_{\nu,m+1,\rho,\delta})\end{array}\right)=\hskip 142.26378pt

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\xi,q_{m+1})&\bar{b}(\xi,q_{m+1})e^{-\xi\rho(m+1)/i}\\
b(\xi,q_{m+1})e^{\xi\rho(m+1)/i}&\bar{a}(\xi,q_{m+1})\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta})&\bar{b}(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta})\\
b(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta})&\bar{a}(Q_{\nu,m,\rho,\delta})\end{array}\right)+O(\varepsilon^{*})\,.

Recall that
\rho=e^{\nu^{2}}
. Applying Lemma
3.2
, we get

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})&\bar{b}(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})\\
b(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})&\bar{a}(Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})\end{array}\right)=\hskip 113.81102pt

\displaystyle\prod_{n=0}^{m}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a(\xi,q_{n})&\bar{b}(\xi,q_{n})e^{-\xi ne^{\nu^{2}}/i}\\
b(\xi,q_{n})e^{\xi ne^{\nu^{2}}/i}&\bar{a}(\xi,q_{n})\end{array}\right)+O(e^{-\nu^{2}})\,.

We multiply the matrices in the product using (
2.20
) to get

(2.26)

a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})=a(\xi,q_{0})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(\xi,q_{m})+O(e^{-\nu^{2}})

uniformly in
\xi\in\mathbb{R},\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\nu\geqslant\nu_{0}
.
Moreover,

\displaystyle 1\leqslant|a(\xi,q_{0})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(\xi,q_{m})|=\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\log(1+|b(\xi,q_{j})|^{2})\right)\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{m}|b(\xi,q_{j})|^{2}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{pool2}}}{{\leqslant}}\exp(C\delta^{2})\leqslant 1+C\delta^{2}\,.

Hence, we get

(2.27)

\displaystyle 1\leqslant|a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})|\leqslant 1+C\delta^{2}+e^{-\nu^{2}}

and an identity

|b(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})|^{2}=|a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})|^{2}-1

implies

(2.28)

\displaystyle|b(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})|\lesssim\delta+e^{-\nu^{2}/2}

for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R},\delta\in(0,1)
, and
m\in\{0,\ldots,\nu-1\}
. Recall that
|a(k,V)-1|\to 0
when
\Im k\geqslant 0,|k|\to\infty
and
V\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
. Therefore, the application of the maximum principle to the function

a(k,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})-a(k,q_{0})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(k,q_{m})\,,

analytic in
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, gives

|a(k,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})-a(k,q_{0})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(k,q_{m})|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{choi}}}{{\leqslant}}Ce^{-\nu^{2}}\,,

when
k\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
By the reasoning used in the proof of Lemma
1.3
, we get (now we have that the height of each bump is
\sim\delta^{2}
and its width, as before, is
\sim\nu^{-1}
):

(2.29)

\max_{\nu/2\leqslant m\leqslant\nu-1,\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]}|\arg(a(\xi,q_{0})\cdot\ldots\cdot a(\xi,q_{m}))|\geqslant C_{1}\delta^{2}\log\nu

and, therefore,

(2.30)

\max_{\nu/2\leqslant m\leqslant\nu-1,\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]}|\arg a(\xi,Q_{\nu,m,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta})|\gtrsim\delta^{2}\log\nu\,,

provided that both
\delta^{2}\log\nu
and
\nu
are large enough and the bound

(2.31)

C_{1}\delta^{2}\log\nu-Ce^{-\nu^{2}}>C_{2}\delta^{2}\log\nu

holds with some positive constant
C_{2}
.
We will call
Q_{\nu,\nu-1,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta}

a sample
. This is not a compactly supported function but it is essentially localized to the interval, e.g.,
[-\nu^{2},\nu e^{\nu^{2}}]
in a sense that its size is negligible outside that range when
\nu\to\infty
. For the
L^{2}
-norm of the sample, we recall (
2.18
) and sparseness of bumps within a sample to get

(2.32)

\|Q_{\nu,\nu-1,e^{\nu^{2}},\delta}\|_{2}^{2}\sim\delta^{2}\,.

Remark.
By taking
\nu\to+\infty
, our argument already shows that the weak-type bound

(2.33)

|\{\xi:\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)\geqslant\lambda\}|\lesssim\frac{\|q\|_{2}}{\lambda},\,\forall\lambda>0

fails even when
q
is restricted to
\|q\|_{2}\leqslant\delta
and
\delta
is an arbitrarily small number.

Second step: putting samples together.
In the previous construction of samples, we choose

(2.34)

\nu_{n}=\exp(\exp(n^{2})),\quad\delta_{n}=\exp(-n),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}

where
n\geqslant n_{0}
. This choice of parameters will satisfy (
2.31
) for

, where

is sufficiently large, because

as

. We also take

t_{n}=\exp(\exp(\nu_{n}))

and construct the potential
q
by spreading the samples over the line at distance

from the origin:

q(x):=\sum_{m\geqslant n_{0}}Q_{m}(x),\quad Q_{m}(x):=Q_{\nu_{m},\nu_{m}-1,e^{\nu_{m}^{2}},\delta_{m}}(x-t_{m}),\quad q^{(n)}(x):=\sum_{n_{0}\leqslant m\leqslant n}Q_{m}(x)\,.

Given that
t_{n+1}/t_{n}\to\infty
and

as

, our

is a very sparse configuration of samples. Moreover, the

to control the

. Indeed, (

\|q\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sum_{n\geqslant n_{0}}\exp(-2n)\lesssim e^{-2n_{0}}.

Notice that we can make
\|q\|_{2}
as small as we want by choosing
n_{0}
large enough.

Next, we use (
2.2
) again to analyze the fundamental matrix after combining the samples in
q
.
In fact, after adding a new sample
Q_{n}
to those already collected in

, the resulting recurrence becomes (see Lemma

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a(\xi,q^{(n)})=a(\xi,q^{(n-1)})a(\xi,Q_{n})+b(\xi,q^{(n-1)})\overline{b(\xi,Q_{n})}e^{-\xi t_{n}/i}+O(\varepsilon^{*}_{n}),\\
b(\xi,q^{(n)})=\overline{a(\xi,Q_{n})}b(\xi,q^{(n-1)})+{a(\xi,q^{(n-1)})}b(\xi,Q_{n})e^{\xi t_{n}/i}+O(\varepsilon^{*}_{n})\end{array}\right.

uniformly in
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
. To bound

, one uses

(2.36)

\|q^{(n)}\|_{1}\lesssim n\nu_{n-1}\,,\quad\|Q_{n}\|_{1}\lesssim\nu_{n}

and (

\varepsilon^{*}_{n}\lesssim\exp(C(n\nu_{n-1}+\nu_{n}))t_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}\leqslant t_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{3}}\,.

According to (

) and (

(2.37)

1\leqslant|a(\xi,Q_{n})|\leqslant 1+Ce^{-n},|b(\xi,Q_{n})|\leqslant Ce^{-n}.

|a(\xi,q^{(n)})|+|b(\xi,q^{(n)})|\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n})(|a(\xi,q^{(n-1)})|+|b(\xi,q^{(n-1)})|)+O(t_{n}^{-\tfrac{1}{3}}),\,n\geqslant n_{0}\,.

|a(\xi,q^{(n)})|\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n})|a(\xi,q^{(n-1)})|+Ce^{-n},\quad|b(\xi,q^{(n)})|\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n})|b(\xi,q^{(n-1)})|+Ce^{-n},\,n\geqslant n_{0}\,.

Using Lemma
3.3

(2.38)

1\leqslant|a(\xi,q^{(n)})|\leqslant 1+Ce^{-n_{0}},\quad|b(\xi,q^{(n)})|\leqslant Ce^{-n_{0}}

for all

.

|a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{(-\infty,t_{n}+(m+0.5)e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})-a(\xi,q_{n-1})a(\xi,Q_{\nu_{n},m,e^{\nu_{n}^{2}},\delta_{n}})|\lesssim e^{-n_{0}}\,.

|a(k,q\cdot\chi_{(-\infty,t_{n}+(m+0.5)e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})-a(k,q_{n-1})a(k,Q_{\nu_{n},m,e^{\nu_{n}^{2}},\delta_{n}})|\lesssim e^{-n_{0}}\,,

when

. Therefore, for
n_{0}
large enough, one has

\arg a(k,q\cdot\chi_{(-\infty,t_{n}+(m+0.5)e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})=\arg a(k,q_{n-1})+\arg a(k,Q_{\nu_{n},m,e^{\nu_{n}^{2}},\delta_{n}})+O(e^{-n_{0}})

where
k\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
.
Subtracting these equations from each other for two different values of
m
:
m=r
and
m=0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{\lfloor\nu_{n}/2\rfloor\leqslant r\leqslant\nu_{n}-1,\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]}|\arg a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{(-\infty,t_{n}+(r+0.5)e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})-\arg a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{(-\infty,t_{n}+0.5e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})|

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102pt\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{lotg}}}{{\gtrsim}}\delta_{n}^{2}\log\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{choice}}}{{\gtrsim}}e^{-2n+n^{2}}\,.

Taking
n_{0}
large enough, that bound implies

(2.39)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\max_{\lfloor\nu_{n}/2\rfloor\leqslant r\leqslant\nu_{n}-1,\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]}|\arg a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{[0,t_{n}+(r+0.5)e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})-\arg a(\xi,q\cdot\chi_{[0,t_{n}+0.5e^{\nu_{n}^{2}}]})|\gtrsim e^{-2n+n^{2}}\,.

Arguing by contradiction, we get
\mathrm{M}_{w}(\xi,q)=+\infty
for
\xi\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
.
∎

Corollary 2.4

In this Appendix, we collect some auxiliary statements used in the main text.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose we have square matrices
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu}\}
and
\{\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{\nu}\}
. Denote
\Psi_{j}=X_{j}\cdot\ldots\cdot X_{1}
. Assume that
\|X_{j}\|\leqslant C
and
\|\Delta_{j}\|\leqslant\epsilon
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\}
. Then, we have

(3.1)

\|(X_{n}+\Delta_{n})\cdot\ldots\cdot(X_{1}+\Delta_{1})-\Psi_{n}\|\leqslant C_{1}^{n}\epsilon,\quad n\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\}\,,

where
C_{1}=1+2C+2\epsilon
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction in
n
. For
n=1
, the bound is correct. Take
n\geqslant 2
and suppose we have our inequality for
n-1
, i.e.,
\|\Sigma_{n-1}\|\leqslant C_{1}^{n-1}\epsilon
, where
\Sigma_{n-1}:=(X_{n-1}+\Delta_{n-1})\cdot\ldots\cdot(X_{1}+\Delta_{1})-\Psi_{n-1}
. Then,

\Sigma_{n}=X_{n}\Sigma_{n-1}+\Delta_{n}(X_{n-1}+\Delta_{n-1})\cdot\ldots\cdot(X_{1}+\Delta_{1})

and

\|\Sigma_{n}\|\leqslant CC_{1}^{n-1}\epsilon+(C+\epsilon)^{n-1}\epsilon\leqslant C_{1}^{n}\epsilon
,
provided that
C_{1}=1+2C+2\epsilon
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose we have square matrices
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\nu}\}
and
\{\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{\nu-1}\}
. Denote
\Psi_{j}=X_{j}\cdot\ldots\cdot X_{1},\,j\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\}
. Assume that
\|X_{j}\|\leqslant C,1\leqslant C
, and
\|\Delta_{j}\|\leqslant\epsilon
for each
j
. Let matrices
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{\nu}\}
be defined inductively by:

Y_{n+1}=X_{n+1}Y_{n}+\Delta_{n},\quad Y_{1}=X_{1},\quad n\in\{1,\ldots,\nu-1\}\,.

Then, we have

(3.2)

\|Y_{n}-\Psi_{n}\|\leqslant\epsilon(n-1)C^{n-1},\quad n\in\{1,\ldots,\nu\}\,.

Proof.

We use induction, again. For
n=1
, the statement holds. Suppose it holds for
n\geqslant 1
. Denote
E_{n}=Y_{n}-\Psi_{n}
. Then,

E_{n+1}+\Psi_{n+1}=X_{n+1}(E_{n}+\Psi_{n})+\Delta_{n}

and

E_{n+1}=X_{n+1}E_{n}+\Delta_{n},\,\|E_{n+1}\|\leqslant C\|E_{n}\|+\epsilon\leqslant(n-1)C^{n}\epsilon+\epsilon\leqslant\epsilon nC^{n}\,,

as required.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose the numerical sequence
\{x_{n}\}
satisfies

|x_{n+1}|\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n})|x_{n}|+Ce^{-n},\quad n\geqslant n_{0}\,,

then (with a different choice of
C
) we have

|x_{n}|\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n_{0}})|x_{n_{0}}|+Ce^{-n_{0}},\,\quad n\geqslant n_{0}\,.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{n}:=\prod_{j=n_{0}}^{n}(1+Ce^{-j})
and
z_{n}:=\psi_{n}^{-1}|x_{n}|
. We have
1\leqslant\psi_{n}\leqslant 1+Ce^{-n_{0}}
. Then,

z_{n+1}\leqslant z_{n}+Ce^{-n},\quad z_{n_{0}}\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n_{0}})|x_{n_{0}}|\,.

The summation in
n
yields
z_{n}\leqslant(1+Ce^{-n_{0}})|x_{n_{0}}|+Ce^{-n_{0}}
. Multiplication with

gives the required estimate.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Suppose
