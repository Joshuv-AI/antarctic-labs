 Abstract
 This study benchmarks a simplified Geant4 NIF Target Chamber (TC) model against foil
 measurements in two instruments: the HTOAD and HNED Snout. The number of product
 atoms per source neutron per gram (N0 /n/g) is compared across multiple locations and
 material configurations. The model reproduces high energy threshold reactions dominated
 by 14 MeV neutrons to within a few percent, depending on location. Discrepancies occur
 where scattering and moderation are significant for low energy threshold reactions. The
 defeatured Geant4 model achieves comparable statistical precision with ∼400 times less
 CPU time than a full fidelity TC model in MCNP. The results can be produced locally
 on a laptop without the need for high performance computing. Low energy biasing can
 be improved by preserving room return pathways and implementing IRDFF cross section
 evaluations.

1 Introduction
This report documents a replication of Chapter 7: Improvements and Experimental Valida-
tion of the Monte Carlo Model of the Target Chamber in Sandra Bogetic’s PhD thesis titled
Improvements, Validation, and Applications of a Metaheuristic Optimization Method for
Neutron Spectra Tailoring at the National Ignition Facility, using Geant4 [1]. The two cam-
paigns involved are the HTOAD assembly and the HNED Snout. The goal is to reproduce
activation in the foils with a simple, heavily defeatured Monte Carlo model Geant4 model
to benchmark experimental values.
 Deuterium-tritium (DT) shots produce ≈14.1 MeV neutrons at the Target Chamber
Center (TCC). Foils at known distances are installed to produce an activation signal from
known reactions. Analyzing the gamma signal after irradiation constrains the local neutron
spectrum and fluence. Values are reported as atoms per neutron per gram (atoms/n/g),
which removes the effects of shot yield and foil mass and allows for direct comparison across
locations and campaigns.
 The HTOAD tests place four identical foil packs of 50 mm diameter along the axis of
the HTOAD at distances of 512.3, 529.3, 548.3, 566.3 mm from TCC. Each pack contains
gold, indium, titanium, and zirconium disks with thin aluminum separators on a tantalum

 1

backing. Three 17 mm long internal fills are placed in between the foils: empty, high den-
sity polyethylene (HDPE), and stainless steel. The fills modify moderation and attenuation
within the body, changing the spectrum seen by each foil pack. In the empty case, neutrons
stream directly through the housing with minimal interactions, so the foils primarily see the
unmodified 14 MeV DT spectrum. When the cavity is filled with HDPE, elastic scattering
on hydrogen slows neutrons down, producing a softer spectrum and lower fluence. The stain-
less steel fill introduces a high Z medium where inelastic scattering and (n, 2n) reactions
dominate. This lowers the 14 MeV peak while generating additional secondary neutrons at
lower energies.
 The HNED Snout is a long aluminum tube aimed at TCC with three fixed foil stations
at the Pinhole (7 cm), Filter Basket (41 cm), and Kinematic Base (110 cm). The four HNED
experiments performed were S1-S4. S1 uses a baseline foil set (Au, In, Zr, Al), S2 expands the
set with nickel and tantalum to add threshold coverage, and S3 and S4 repeat S2 but include
aluminum inserts of different lengths (30 cm and 60 cm) inside the tube. As neutrons travel
through the 30 or 60 cm of Al-6061, they undergo elastic and inelastic scattering that alters
the shape of the transmitted spectrum. The shorter insert produces moderate softening of
the high energy peak, while the longer insert increases the effect by removing more neutrons
near 14 MeV and generating a broader distribution of down scattered neutrons.

1.2 Simulation Approach
The simulation in Geant4 uses the QGSP BERT HP physics list. This physics list uses the
Bertini cascade model [2] with the high precision NeutronHP models. This provides excellent
accuracy for low energy and thermal neutron transport, designed for activation studies. The
limitations of this model are its increased computation cost, which can be several times
higher than simpler models and its dependence on the quality of evaluated nuclear data.
 The target chamber is modeled as a concrete world volume with a 4.5 meter radius
spherical vacuum region centered around the source. The HNED Snout is built as a set of
aluminum 6061 and stainless steel 304 parts placed along the z-axis. The geometry was heav-
ily defeatured to improved simulation efficiency. Bolts, threaded features, vent holes, fillets,
and chamfers were removed and only the general shape of the snout tube were retained.
For example, the HNED tube was modeled with just the main tube as a hollow cylinder
(60.33 diameter outer radius, 52.5 mm diameter inner radius, 389.8 mm length). Figure 4
Top shows the engineering drawings of the HNED Snout and HTOAD and for comparison,
Figure 4 Bottom shows the defeatured geometries used in the Geant4 simulations. Bench-
marking against the experimental data verify that these simplifications still accurately model
particle transport at NIF and significantly reduce computing time.

 The source is a 14.1 MeV monoenergetic neutron point source and each primary neutron
carries a statistical weight that is propagated through transport. A monoenergetic source is
a good approximation because the DT fuel layer is thin for these experiments, so neutron
down scatter within the target is negligible. Thin (0.1 mm thick) vacuum foil volumes are
defined at each foil location for scoring and the transport records every step a neutron takes
inside any foil volume. For each step, the event ID, foil ID, kinetic energy, step length, and
particle weight are stored and written to an output CSV file. This CSV file is then post-
processed in Matlab to bin neutron interactions in logarithmic energy bins. The cross section
data is taken from ENDF/B-VIII.0 and is folded onto the step length per bin to calculate
fluence, number of nuclei produced, and N0 /n/g . For details, see Calculations.
 As the source is at 14.1 MeV, a very low fraction of neutrons scatter and subsequently
interact with the foils. These low energy interactions are required to assess the number
of nuclei produced for the low/no threshold reactions show above. To keep the run time
reasonable while reducing error bars in the foils, variance reduction techniques are used.
The neutron is killed when it goes beyond the radius of the target chamber where it has less
than 1 percent chance of returning with the probability P ∼ Ae−x , where A is the albedo
fraction from a concrete surface and x is the distance. With Geant4 simulations a 14 MeV
neutron has λmfp =2.18 cm, which sets the particle kill distance at 11.8 cm beyond the
concrete. Additionally, Monte Carlo roulette is used to push the computation towards the
foils without biasing the results. In roulette, survivors have their statistical weight scaled by
1/p(E ) so the tally remains unbiased, while the high energy contributions are discarded. The
roulette is gated by energy where neutrons above 8 MeV have a 2% survival chance, between
3 MeV and 8 MeV have a 10% survival chance, between 0.5 MeV and 50 keV have a 80%
survival chance, and below 50 keV always survive. This improves statistics on track length
inside the thin scoring volumes for the low energy reactions. It is estimated a combination
of these techniques reduces the runtime by 4-5 times.
 The Monte Carlo simulation is run locally on twenty 11th Gen Intel Core i7-1185G7
CPUs. It is estimated that each CPU core can calculate transport for 5×106 neutrons per
core per hour. With 4×108 particles, error bars of 1.4% can be obtained in the 197 Au(n,
γ )198 Au reaction in the pinhole foils. This reaction was chosen as statistics for low threshold
energy reactions are most difficult to obtain. Assuming a perfect speedup and assuming
 √
error scales as 1/ N , errors of <1% can be achieved in ≈2 times the duration. This would
take 196 core hours, or 9.8 hours when run on twenty cores.
 In contrast to the simple variance reduction methods in this study, Botegic uses surface
sources around the instruments and weight windows with importance splitting to drive
statistics toward the detectors. This approach also puts emphasis on room return neutrons
to optimize for low energy reactions without biasing. However, these methods are much
more computationally expensive. Botegic reports that it takes 168 hours to run 9 million
histories on a LBNL Savio high performance computer using 8 threads in order to achieve
1% statistics. This corresponds to 1.34×104 neutrons per core per hour. Assuming similar
speeds per core, the defeatured Geant4 model is able to produce statistically similar results
with ∼400 times less computing time. This clearly shows the advantage of the simplified

target chamber approach. The defeatured Geant4 model is ideal for rapid analyses, whereas
the MCNP model is preferable when high accuracy is required and computational cost is
not a concern.

3 Discussion
Across all configurations, the N0 /n/g value from Geant4 were compared against the mea-
sured values and propagated uncertainties. For direct line of sight locations and high
threshold reactions (197 Au(n,2n)196 Au, 90 Zr(n,2n)89 Zr, 48 Ti(n,p)48 Sc), Geant4 typically
agrees with experiment to within a few tens of percent. For example at the HNED pinhole,

 16

Geant4 overpredicts 196 Au by ∼ 10% (Table 4), while at HTOAD Pack 1 in the empty con-
figuation it underpredicts by ∼ 5% (Table 1). These are encouraging results given the heavily
defeatured geometry and the monoenergetic DT source assumption used in the simulation.
 Once significant scattering and moderation is involved, such as in downstream stacks of
HTOAD and deeper along the filled HNED, the spectrum in Geant4 begins to deviate from
the measured spectrum. Typical differences for 198 Au are −80% to −99% at all three HNED
stations (Tables 4-6), and 116m In is similarly low where there is more considerable scatter
(Table 4). Threshold channels such as 48 Ti(n,p)48 Sc and 47 Ti(n,p)47 Sc are often 70% high
at the Filter Basket and can be an order of magnitude different at the Kinematic Base in
the aluminum HNED shots (Tables 5–6). These trends indicate that the simulated spectrum
is generally too hard once significant scatter is present.
 Within the HTOAD, the qualitative trends mostly match expectations, with small dif-
ferences in the low energy reactions. The bar plot comparisons of fractional production
(Figures 5–7) show good qualitative agreement in shape and a much better error can
be achieved in the simulations for high energy reactions. Quantitatively, the reduction in
nuclides produced from one stack to the next was 85.5% for the HDPE case and 78.8% for
the stainless steel case. This is an improvement to the MCNP model, as Botegic reports
that it is expected that 86% of the 14.1 MeV neutrons are transmitted through the HDPE
per stack level and the MCNP model only reported a 90% reduction.
 Many of the discrepancies may come from limits in nuclear data, especially for channels
with isomers. Outside of fission neutron energy ranges (2-5 MeV), evaluated nuclear data
is sparse because neutrons in this energy range have historically been difficult to produce.
Botegic notes that ENDF/B-VIII does not consistently treat isomeric branching, causing
biases in 115 In(n,n’)115m In, 115 In(n,2n)114m In, and 197 Au(n,2n)196 Au unless the IRDFF file
is used. Adopting IRDFF improved several comparisons by ∼ 10% relative to ENDF/B-VIII.
The large differences in these channels are consistent with the issues in the thesis. For
58
 Ni(n,p)58 Co, Botegic notes inconsistencies in experimental cross sections near 14 MeV,
with EXFOR values differing by 30%. Nickel channels are a known source of high energy
nuclear data uncertainty that can account for a large bias. Importantly, the thesis concludes
that the largest uncertainties are in the epithermal (10 keV up to a few MeV) where many
activation foils have limited sensitivity and the evaluated data carry sizable uncertainties.
This is the region where the reactions that produce 198 Au and 116m In have the largest cross
section.
 This model intentionally trades physical fidelity for computational speed, allowing it to
run efficiently on a local system, without the need for high performance computing. The
results also highlight several areas for improving accuracy. First, the neutron kill threshold
beyond the target chamber boundary should be increased to preserve more scattered neu-
trons and improve statistics in low energy reactions. This adjustment is possibly the largest
factor affecting accuracy. However, it also has a large effect on runtime, since many neu-
trons that exit the chamber do not return. Therefore, a balance must be struck between
computationally efficiency and a high room return neutron fraction. For activation analy-
ses, IRDFF v2.0 cross-section data should be used wherever available to reduce biases in

 17

isomeric and threshold channels. In addition, covariance data from ENDF should be propa-
gated to help distinguish discrepancies arising from modeling limitations versus those caused
by deficiencies in the underlying nuclear data.
 Overall, the Geant4 predictions reproduce the high energy part of the spectrum very
well. Channels dominated by 14 MeV neutrons are typically within 10% of the measurements
across all configurations. The largest biases arise in low energy and epithermal capture and
inelastic channels, especially where scattering and moderation are significant. In these cases,
the combination of spectrum softening and limited evaluated nuclear data above 6 MeV
increases uncertainties.
