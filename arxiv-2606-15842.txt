A small noise approximation for Muller’s Ratchet
Carola Sophia Heinzel, Peter Pfaffelhuber, Anton Wakolbinger

Abstract
We consider an infinite system of SDEs with Fleming-Viot noise indexed by k = 0 , 1 , 2 , … k=0,1,2,... , whose parameters α , λ \alpha,\lambda , and ν \nu are the (deleterious) selection coefficient, the (uni-directional) mutation rate, and a quantity which determines the size of the system’s fluctuations. The SDE’s unique weak solution X ⁡ ( t ) = ( X k ​ ( t ) ) k = 0 , 1 , 2 , … X(t)=(X_{k}(t))_{k=0,1,2,...} models what is known in population genetics as Muller’s ratchet . Here, X k ​ ( t ) X_{k}(t) stands for the frequency of individuals carrying k k deleterious mutations. Since the mutation process is uni-directional, t ↦ inf { k : X k ​ ( t ) > 0 } t\mapsto\inf\{k:X_{k}(t)>0\} is non-decreasing for almost every path of X X , and we refer to an increase as a click of Muller’s ratchet. A long standing question concerns the clicking rate of Muller’s ratchet. Using Duhamel’s principle for semigroups, we give a partial answer by approximating 𝔼 ⁡ ( ∑ k = 1 ∞ k ​ X k ​ ( t ) ) \mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kX_{k}(t)\Big) and 𝔼 ​ ( X 0 ​ ( t ) ) \mathbb{E}\big(X_{0}(t)\big) up to 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / ν 2 ) \mathcal{O}(1/\nu^{2}) for fixed α \alpha , λ \lambda and t > 0 t>0 . Our results suggest that ψ := ν α e − λ / α \psi:=\nu\alpha e^{-\lambda/\alpha} is a crucial quantity also when the mutation/selection ratio θ = λ / α \theta=\lambda/\alpha is moderately large: for large ν ​ α \nu\alpha , clicking of the ratchet on the time scale 1 α ​ log ⁡ θ \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\log\theta becomes rare as soon as ψ \psi becomes large.

1
Introduction and main results

In
[
22
]
, Muller has introduced the following genetic model: A population of fixed size
N
reproduces randomly. Each individual carries a number of mutations, all of which are assumed to be deleterious. Fitness (measured by the expected number of offspring) of an individual carrying
k
mutations is proportional to
(1-\mathfrak{s})^{k}
for some
\mathfrak{s}\geq 0
. The offspring of an individual has a small chance
\mathfrak{u}\geq 0
to gain a new deleterious mutation. In particular, offspring of an individual has at least as many mutations as the parent and mutation is an irreversible process. Hence, there is a chance that, eventually, all individuals will have at least one mutation and we say that the ratchet has clicked. So, mutations drive the population to a larger number of deleterious mutations while selection acts in the opposite direction. This means that Muller’s ratchet describes a form of mutation-selection quasi-balance and the question is if selection is strong enough in order to prevent the accumulation of deleterious mutations, at least for some time. In the biological literature, Muller’s ratchet is a textbook model for the evolutionary advantage of reproduction under recombination
[
7
]
, has many different applications (e.g.
[
20
,
14
]
) and huge simulation studies try to answer how fast fitness of a population decreases by Muller’s ratchet
[
18
]
.

Variants of Muller’s ratchet have been studied in the past years.
[
25
]
allowed for compensatory back-mutations, and
[
8
]
considered a spatial version of the ratchet.
[
10
]
considered a modification of the fitness function leading to the so-called tournament ratchet: here, selection between individuals of type
k
and of type
\ell
only depends on whether
k>\ell
or
k<\ell
and not on the difference
k-\ell
. With this fitness function,
(X_{0},...,X_{n})
is an autonomous process for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which makes the analysis of the tournament ratchet more accessible than that of the classical one. Recently,
[
15
]
managed to compute the tournament ratchet’s click rate for a parameter regime leading to metastability.

1.1
A Fleming-Viot model of Muller’s ratchet

Here, we study a version of Muller’s ratchet described by the infinite system of stochastic differential equations

\displaystyle dX_{k}=\Big(\alpha\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j-k)X_{j}\Big)X_{k}+\lambda(X_{k-1}-X_{k})\Big)dt+\sum_{\ell\neq k}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{k}X_{\ell}}\,dW_{k\ell},

(1)

for
k=0,1,\ldots
with
X_{-1}:=0
,
\sum X_{k}=1
,
\lambda\geq 0
(the mutation rate),
\alpha\geq 0
(the selection coefficient) and
\nu>0
(the effective population size on the chosen time-scale), where
(W_{k\ell})_{k>\ell}
is a family of independent Brownian motions and
W_{k\ell}=-W_{\ell k}
. Existence and uniqueness of a (weak) solution of (
1
) was shown in
[
25
]
(under the assumption of exponential moments) and
[
2
]
(under the weaker assumption of moments of order
2+\delta
). The system (
1
) and the Wright-Fisher (WF) model of Muller’s ratchet (sketched above and described in more detail in Sec
2.1
) are “synchronized” by measuring time
t
in (
1
) in units of WF generations and putting
(\alpha,\lambda,\nu):=(\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{u},N)
; with this choice, expectation and covariance of the increments of
X
in (
1
) mimic those of the type frequencies in the WF system.

One of the benefits of (
1
) is its time-scaling property: With
X^{(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)}
denoting the solution of (
1
), one has for all
c>0

\big(X^{(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)}(tc)\big)_{t\geq 0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(X^{(c\alpha,c\lambda,\nu/c)}(t)\big)_{t\geq 0}.

(2)

Thus, measuring time
t
in (
1
) in
N
generations of the WF system would lead to the choice
(\alpha,\lambda,\nu):=(\mathfrak{s}N,\mathfrak{u}N,1)
. Below, we will work with yet another timescale, turning
(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)
into
(1,\lambda/\alpha,\nu\alpha)
, which is appropriate in the case of
moderate selection
, i.e.
1/N\ll\mathfrak{s}\ll 1
as
N\to\infty
.

The
mutation-selection ratio

\theta:=\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}

will play a key role for the frequency of clicks of (
1
). If
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
,
X(t)=(X_{k}(t))_{k=0,1,2,...}
is the solution of (
1
), let

\displaystyle K^{\ast}(t):=\inf\{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:X_{k}(t)>0\},

(3)

which we refer to as the
fittest class
(or
least-loaded class
) by time
t
, because selection favors individuals with a small number of deleterious mutations. Since mutation is irreversible,
t\mapsto K^{\ast}(t)
is non-decreasing, and any time
\tau>0
with
K^{\ast}(\tau)-K^{\ast}(\tau-)>0
is a
click
of Muller’s ratchet. In
[
2
]
it was proved that the ratchet clicks a.s. in finite time, and
[
19
]

analysed
properties of quasistationarity of
X
. We believe (even though we are not aware of a proof) that for all
(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)
both the a.s. limit of
\tfrac{1}{t}K^{\ast}(t)
and that of
\tfrac{1}{t}\sum_{k}kX_{k}(t)
(as
t\to\infty
) exist and are equal to some constant
\mathfrak{r}=\mathfrak{r}(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)
, independent of the initial condition
X(0)
.

A formula for
\mathfrak{r}(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)
seems out of reach. However,
\mathfrak{r}
(provided it exists) inherits from (
2
) the scaling property

\mathfrak{r}(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)=\alpha\,\mathfrak{r}(1,\theta,\nu\alpha),

i.e.
\mathfrak{r}/\alpha
only depends on
\theta
and
\nu\alpha
rather than on the full triple
(\alpha,\lambda,\nu)
; see also Remark
1.4
below.

In the case
\nu=\infty
, the system (
1
) has an explicit solution; see Proposition 4.1 in
[
5
]
, Theorem 2 in
[
25
]
, or Theorem
3.5
below. In particular, if
X_{0}(0)>0
, the solution
X(t)
of this system converges as
t\to\infty
to
\pi:=\text{Poi}(\theta)
, the Poisson distribution with parameter
\theta
. Thus, for
\nu=\infty
one clearly has
K^{\ast}(t)=K^{\ast}(0)
.

Since the clicking rate
\mathfrak{r}
is unavailable for finite
\nu
, but (
1
) is solvable for
\nu=\infty
, here we study asymptotic properties of
X
as
\nu\to\infty
. In particular, as a first rigorous quantitative step in exploring the asymptotics of the rate of Muller’s ratchet, for fixed
\alpha
,
\lambda
and
t
we derive in Corollary
1.3
the expected values of quantities as

m_{1}(X(t)):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}kX_{k}(t)

and
X_{k}(t)
, and their asymptotics as
\nu\to\infty
, given the system is started in
\pi
.

Heuristic approaches

We briefly recall the history of quantitative approaches for Muller’s ratchet. The first mathematical description was given by
[

]
. It was followed by some other investigations; see
[

]

However, all of these papers rely on approximations in order to study the full system (

X_{0}

solves the SDE

\displaystyle dX_{0}=(\alpha m_{1}(X)-\lambda)X_{0}dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{0}(1-X_{0})}\,dW

(4)

for some Brownian motion

solves (

m_{1}(X)\approx\widehat{m}_{1}(X_{0})
leads to a closed system for

and it is possible to study the resulting one-dimensional diffusion by classical theory. Using some heuristics and calculations,
[

]
,
[

]
,
[

suggest the approximate functional relationship

\displaystyle\widehat{m}_{1}(X_{0})=\theta+\kappa\Big(1-\frac{X_{0}}{e^{-\theta}}\Big)

(5)

for
\kappa=0.6,\kappa=0.7
and
\kappa=0.58
, respectively.
It was shown that this approximation gives reasonable results for the average time between clicks of the ratchet for various parameter combinations when compared to simulations. However – see Table 6.1 of
[

– some other parameter combinations lead to large errors up to a factor of 2, even for large populations.
[

,

,

estimated the expected time of first click with different approximations, but still using a 1d-heuristics for the evolution of

.
In
[

]
, linear perturbations, an eigen-decomposition and path integrals are used for analysing the evolution of Muller’s ratchet. Breaking the analysis down to the 1d-case, they find a better approximation for the rate of the ratchet when letting

Remark 1.1

(Yet another heuristics on the rate of the ratchet)
.

Given
D(t):=1-X_{0}(t)-X_{1}(t)
, we find that

\displaystyle dX_{0}

\displaystyle=(\alpha m_{1}(X)-\lambda)X_{0}\,dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{0}X_{1}}dW_{01}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{0}D}dW_{0},

\displaystyle dX_{1}

\displaystyle=\left((\alpha m_{1}(X)-\alpha-\lambda)X_{1}+\lambda X_{0}\right)dt-\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{0}X_{1}}dW_{01}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{1}D}dW_{1}.

for independent Brownian motions,
W_{0},W_{1}
, and
W_{01}
. Itô’s lemma applied to
Q:=q(X_{0},X_{1}):=X_{0}/X_{1}
yields

\displaystyle dQ

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{X_{1}}dX_{0}-\frac{X_{0}}{X_{1}^{2}}dX_{1}-\frac{1}{X_{1}^{2}}d\langle X_{0},X_{1}\rangle+\frac{X_{0}}{X_{1}^{3}}d\langle X_{1}\rangle

(6)

\displaystyle=Q(\alpha-\lambda Q)dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}}\left(\sqrt{Q}dW_{01}+\sqrt{\frac{D}{X_{1}}}\sqrt{Q}dW_{0}+Q^{3/2}dW_{01}+\sqrt{\frac{D}{X_{1}}}QdW_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{\nu}\left(Q^{2}+\frac{D}{X_{1}}Q+Q\right)dt.

The fluctuation term in (
6
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\sqrt{Q}(1+Q)dW_{01}+\sqrt{\frac{D}{X_{1}}}\sqrt{Q}dW_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{D}{X_{1}}}QdW_{1}=\sqrt{Q(1+Q)\left(\frac{D}{X_{1}}+1+Q\right)}dW.

for some standard Brownian motion
W
. Since
\frac{D}{X_{1}}+1+Q=\frac{1}{X_{1}}
, (
6
) gets the interesting form

\displaystyle dQ=Q\Big(\alpha-\lambda Q+\frac{1}{\nu X_{1}}\Big)dt+\sqrt{\frac{Q(1+Q)}{\nu X_{1}}}dW.

(7)

This means that the evolution of
Q
only depends on
X_{1}
(and
Q
).
Now, set
\widetilde{Q}(t):=\theta Q(t/\alpha)
and
\widetilde{X}_{1}(t)=X_{1}(t/\alpha)
. Then, a little calculation gives

\displaystyle d\widetilde{Q}=\widetilde{Q}\Big(1-\widetilde{Q}+\frac{1}{\nu\alpha\widetilde{X}_{1}}\Big)dt+\sqrt{\widetilde{Q}\Big(1+\frac{\widetilde{Q}}{\theta}\Big)\frac{\theta}{\nu\alpha\widetilde{X}_{1}}}d\widetilde{W}

(8)

for a standard Brownian motion
\widetilde{W}
. Note that the last calculations do not require that
X(t)
is close to
\pi
. However, observe that
\widetilde{Q}(t)=1
if
X(t)=\pi
and if
X(t)
is close to
\pi
, we see that the last term becomes
\sqrt{\widetilde{Q}\Big(1+\frac{\widetilde{Q}}{\theta}\Big)\frac{1}{\psi}}\,d\widetilde{W}
, with
\psi=\nu\alpha e^{-\theta}
. Since we rescaled time by
1/\alpha
, this suggests that the click time of the ratchet is much larger than
1/\alpha
if
\psi\gg 1
and is of order
1/\alpha
if
\psi=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Simulations and outlook

2.1
Simulations

In order to assess the scope of our findings, we implement a Wright-Fisher model as follows: For fixed
\mathfrak{s}\in[0,1)
,
\mathfrak{u}\geq 0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, start
X^{N}(0)\sim\text{Multinomial}(N,\text{Poi}(\mathfrak{u}/\mathfrak{s}))
. In order to generate
X^{N}(n+1)
from
X(n)
, set
P(n+1):=\tfrac{1}{N}X(n)\cdot S\cdot M
, where
S=(s_{ij})_{ij}
with
s_{ij}=(1-\mathfrak{s})^{j}
and
M=(m_{ij})_{ij}
is a matrix with
m_{ij}=\text{Poi}(\mathfrak{u})(j-i)=1_{j\geq i}e^{-\mathfrak{u}}\mathfrak{u}^{j-i}/((j-i)!)
. After normalizing
P(n+1)
, pick
X(n+1)\sim\text{Multinomial}(N,P(n+1))
.

As can be seen from Figure
2
, up to some time, which is related to the mean extinction time of
X_{0}
(the “mean first loss” of the fittest class), the first order prediction from Corollary
1.3
fits well with the simulations for

Remark 2.3

(Outlook)
.

Two extensions of the analysis of Muller’s ratchet offer themselves, based on the techniques used and developed in this paper:

•

Approximations of higher order: For small sampling rate

3.1
Notation and a well-posed martingale problem

We first introduce some notation, before we can name a theorem about martingale problems for the ratchet.

Remark 3.1

(Notation)
.

For a complete and separable metric space
(E,r)
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}(E)
the space of probability measures on (the Borel sets of)
E
. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}(E)
(
\mathcal{B}(E)
) is the space of real-valued, measurable (and bounded) functions. If
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}
, we let
\mathcal{C}^{k}(E)
(
\mathcal{C}_{b}^{k}(E)
) be the (bounded),
k
times partially continuously differentiable functions (with bounded derivatives).

Definition 3.2

(Martingale problem)
.

Let
(E,r)
be a complete and separable metric space,
\mathbb{P}_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(E)
,
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{C}(E)
, and
G
a linear operator on
{\mathcal{C}}(E)
with domain
\mathcal{F}
. A (distribution
\mathbb{P}
of an)
E
-valued stochastic process
X=(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
is called a solution of the
(E,\mathbb{P}_{0},G,\mathcal{F})
(local) martingale problem if
X_{0}
has distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
,
\mathcal{X}
has paths in the space
{\mathcal{D}}_{E}([0,\infty))
, almost surely, and for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

M_{f}:=\Big(f(X(t))-f(X_{0})-\int_{0}^{t}Gf(X(s))ds\Big)_{t\geq 0}

(25)

is a (local)
\mathbb{P}
-martingale with respect to the canonical filtration. Moreover, the
(E,\mathbb{P}_{0},G,\mathcal{F})
(local) martingale problem is said to be well-posed if there is a unique solution
\mathbb{P}.

In order to state the martingale problem for Muller’s ratchet, we need a state space, the domain of the generator, and the generator itself.

Definition 3.3

(Ingredients of the Martingale problem for Muller’s ratchet)
.

1.

State space: We write, for some
\chi>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}_{0}}:\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}=1\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{\chi}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{x\in\mathbb{S}:\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x_{k}e^{\chi k}<\infty\Big\}.

Note that
\mathbb{S}_{\chi}
is a suitable state space because there is a metric
r_{\chi}
(defined by
r_{\chi}(x,y):=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{{\chi}k}|x_{k}-y_{k}|)
that turns the space
\mathbb{S}_{\chi}
into a complete and separable metric space (see Remark 3.2 of
[
25
]
).

2.

Domain of the generator: For
x\in\mathbb{S}
and
\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
, set

\displaystyle f_{\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{n}}(x):=\langle x,\varphi_{1}\rangle\cdots\langle x,\varphi_{n}\rangle,\qquad\langle x,\varphi\rangle:=\sum_{k}x_{k}\varphi(k).

(26)

We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}:=

\displaystyle\text{algebra generated by }\{f_{\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N},\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{n}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{N}_{0})\}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{b}(\mathbb{S}).

(27)

3.

Generator:
We define the operator
G
with domain

by

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=G^{(1)}+G^{(2)},

\displaystyle G^{(1)}f(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Big(\lambda(x_{k-1}-x_{k})+\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j-k)x_{j}x_{k}\Big)\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x_{k}},

(28)

\displaystyle G^{(2)}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\nu}\sum_{k,\ell=0}^{\infty}x_{k}(\delta_{kl}-x_{\ell})\frac{\partial^{2}f(x)}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{\ell}}.

with

Theorem 3.5

(Solution of (

.

Started in

\displaystyle x_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g_{\alpha t}(x)}\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}e^{-\alpha it}\frac{\theta^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}(1-e^{-\alpha t})^{k-i}\cdot\exp\Big(-\theta(1-e^{-\alpha t})\Big).

(32)

Proof.

We use a particle system in our proof. Precisely, let
(K_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be an
\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\dagger\}
-valued, pure Markov jump process starting in
K_{0}\sim x(0)
. Here
,\dagger
is an absorbing state, and if in state
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it

1.

jumps to
k+1
at rate
\lambda
,

2.

gets absorbed (i.e. jumps to
\dagger
) at rate
k\alpha
.

Then, it is straight-forward to see that

\displaystyle x_{k}(t):=\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger)

(33)

solves (
30

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(K_{t+dt}=k|K_{t+dt}\neq\dagger)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k)(1-dt(\lambda+\alpha k))+\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k-1)\lambda dt}{\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=j)(1-dt\alpha j)}+\mathcal{O}(dt^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k)+dt\lambda(\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k-1)-\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k))-dt\alpha k\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k)}{\mathbb{P}(K_{t}\neq\dagger)(1-dt\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=j)/\mathbb{P}(K_{t}\neq\dagger)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger)\Big(1+dt\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}j\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=j|K_{t}\neq\dagger)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+dt\lambda(\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k-1)-\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k))-dt\alpha k\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger)

Rearranging gives

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(K_{t+dt}=k|K_{t+dt}\neq\dagger)=-\lambda\Big(\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger)-\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k-1|K_{t}\neq\dagger)\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\alpha\sum_{j=0}(j-k)\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=j|K_{t}\neq\dagger)\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger),

i.e.
\mathbb{P}(K_{t}=k|K_{t}\neq\dagger)
solves (
30
).

The right hand side of (
33
) can be computed directly. When starting with
K_{0}=i\leq k
, a total of
k-i
mutations must be gained by time

\alpha

\displaystyle x_{k}(t)

\displaystyle\sim\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}(0)e^{-\alpha it}\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\cdots\int_{t_{k-i-1}}^{t}dt_{k-i}\lambda^{k-i}e^{-\alpha(t-t_{1})}\cdots e^{-\alpha(t-t_{k-i})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}(0)e^{-\alpha it}\frac{\lambda^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}ds\Big)^{k-i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}(0)e^{-\alpha it}\frac{\theta^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}(1-e^{-\alpha t})^{k-i}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}x_{i}e^{-\alpha it}\frac{\theta^{k-i}}{(k-i)!}(1-e^{-\alpha t})^{k-i}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}e^{-\alpha it}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}}{k!}(1-e^{-\alpha t})^{k}

\displaystyle=\exp\Big(\theta(1-e^{-\alpha t})\Big)\cdot g_{\alpha t}(x),

Lemma 4.1
.

Let (
1

\tau\geq 0
. For all

,

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}m_{1}(X(t))-m_{1}(X_{0})\geq\lambda\tau+c\right)\leq\exp(-2\alpha\nu c).

Proof.

Using Itô’s Lemma in (
1
), we see that

\displaystyle dm_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}kdX_{k}

(44)

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{j,k=0}^{\infty}k(j-k)X_{j}X_{k}+\lambda\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k(X_{k-1}-X_{k})\Big)dt+\sum_{k,\ell\atop k\neq\ell}k\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{k}X_{\ell}}dW_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=(\lambda-\alpha\bar{m}_{2}(X))dt+\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}\bar{m}_{2}(X)}dW

for some Brownian motion
W

\displaystyle\Big\langle\sum_{k,\ell\atop k\neq\ell}k\!\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{k}X_{\ell}}dW_{k\ell},\sum_{k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}\atop k^{\prime}\neq\ell^{\prime}}k^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}X_{k^{\prime}}X_{\ell^{\prime}}}dW_{k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}\Big\rangle\!=\!\frac{1}{\nu}\sum_{k,\ell\atop k\neq\ell}k^{2}X_{k}X_{\ell}-k\ell X_{k}X_{\ell}\!=\!\frac{1}{\nu}\bar{m}_{2}(X).

So, we find

\displaystyle m_{1}(X(t))-m_{1}(X_{0})=\int_{0}^{t}dm_{1}(X(s))=\lambda t\underbrace{-\alpha\int_{0}^{t}\bar{m}_{2}(X(s))ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\frac{\bar{m}_{2}(X(s))}{\nu}}\,dW_{s}}_{=:Z_{t}}.

Moreover, (see e.g.
[
17
]

\big(\exp\bigl(2\alpha\nu\,Z_{t}\bigr)\big)_{t\geq 0}=\Big(\exp\Bigl(\int_{0}^{t}2\alpha\sqrt{\nu\bar{m}_{2}(X(s))}dW_{s}-\int_{0}^{t}2\alpha^{2}\nu\bar{m}_{2}(X(s))ds\Big)\Big)_{t\geq 0}

In particular, using Doob’s inequality, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}(m_{1}(X(t))-m_{1}(X_{0}))>\lambda\tau+c\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\lambda t+Z_{t}>\lambda\tau+c\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}Z_{t}>c\Big)=\mathbb{P}\!\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\exp\Big(2\alpha\nu Z_{t}\Big)\geq e^{2\alpha\nu c}\right)\leq e^{-2\alpha\nu c}.

∎

A
Martingales along the Duhamel formula

The main result of this section, Proposition
A.1
, provides the martingales that are used for the proof of Theorem
1.2
, see also the explanation in Remark
1.6
. Because of its independent interest we will phrase Proposition
A.1
in a framework that is more general than that of paper’s main part. In Remark
A.2
we will point to the connection between the martingales appearing in (
62
) and the Duhamel formula for semigroups.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
(E,r)
be a complete and separable metric space,
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{M}(E)
linear, and
G^{(1)},G^{(2)}:\mathcal{D}\to\mathcal{M}(E)
. Assume
G^{(1)}
is the generator in the sense of bounded pointwise limits of the semigroup
S
(i.e.
(S_{h}f(x)-f(x))/h\xrightarrow{h\downarrow 0}G^{(1)}f(x)
boundedly for all
f\in\mathcal{D}
,
t\geq 0
, and
x\in E
).

Let
X
be a solution of the
(G^{(1)}+G^{(2)},\mathcal{D})
local martingale problem with càdlàg paths, and
\tau\geq 0
. Fix
f\in\mathcal{D}
and assume

(i)

S_{t}f\in\mathcal{D}
and continuous, for all
t\in[0,\tau]
,

(ii)

t\mapsto G^{(2)}S_{t}f(x)
is right-continuous for all
x
on
[0,\tau]
,

(iii)

there exist
L\in\mathcal{M}(E)
, such that for all
t\in[0,\tau]
and
x\in E

\displaystyle|S_{t}f(x)|+|G^{(1)}S_{t}f(x)|+|G^{(2)}S_{t}f(x)|\leq L(x),

(60)

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}L(X(t))\big)<\infty.

(61)

Then

M_{f}(t):=S_{\tau-t}f(X(t))-\int_{0}^{t}G^{(2)}S_{\tau-s}f(X(s))\,ds,\qquad 0\leq t\leq\tau

(62)

is a martingale.

Remark A.2
.

For the martingale
M_{f}
as in (
62
), with
X(0)=x
, the equality

\mathbb{E}(M_{f}(\tau))=\mathbb{E}(M_{f}(0))
translates into

\mathbb{E}_{x}(f(X(\tau)))-\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{x}(G^{(2)}S_{\tau-s}f(X(s)))\,ds=S_{\tau}f(x).

(63)

This relates the semigroup of
X
(associated with the operator
G^{(1)}+G^{(2)}
) with the semigroup
S
(generated by
G^{(1)}
), and thus (
63
) can be seen as an instance of
Duhamel’s formula
. See e.g. Section 3.1, eq. (1.2) in
[
24
]
.

Here is (the core of) an argument for the martingale property of
M_{f}
.
Since we know that
X
satisfies a martingale problem
involving the operator
G^{(1)}+G^{(2)}
, it is fair to hope that

\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial s}+G^{(1)}+G^{(2)}\right)S_{\tau-s}f(X(s))\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}G^{(2)}S_{\tau-s}f(X(s))\,ds

is the compensator whose subtraction from
S_{\tau-t}f(X(t))
yields a martingale, namely
M_{f}
.
Proposition
A.1
puts this reasoning on firm grounds.
