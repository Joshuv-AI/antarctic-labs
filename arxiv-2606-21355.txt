Efficient Verification of Entangled Measurements with Local States
Kun Wang, Masahito Hayashi

Abstract
We develop a framework for quantum measurement verification (QMV) using only local state preparations. For locally transitive and irreducible projective measurements, we prove that symmetry reduces locality constrained QMV to quantum state verification of a single basis state, thereby reducing protocol design to the optimization of homogeneous verification operators. We apply the framework to generalized Bell measurements, single-parameter measurements on two qubits, elegant joint measurements, and stabilizer state induced measurements, and derive explicit local protocols together with closed form verification operators, success probabilities, and sample complexities. We further show that homogeneous QMV protocols can estimate measurement fidelity directly from observed passing frequencies.

Preliminaries

II.1
Quantum state verification

Here we review the quantum state verification (QSV)
task
Pallister et al. 2018
;
Yu et al. 2022
.
A quantum device is designed to produce a multipartite pure state
|{\psi}\rangle
.
However, it might work incorrectly and outputs
independent states
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N}
in
N
runs.
It is guaranteed that either
\sigma_{j}=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
for all
j
(good case)
or
\langle{\psi}|\sigma_{j}|{\psi}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon
for all
j
(bad case).
We aim to identify the case with the worst-case failure probability
\delta
.
To achieve this, a verifier performs binary measurements randomly chosen
from a set of available measurements.
Each measurement
\{T_{\ell},\mathbbm{1}-T_{\ell}\}
is specified by some operator
T_{\ell}

and is performed with probability
p_{\ell}
, corresponding to “passing” the test.
Let
\Omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\ell}p_{\ell}T_{\ell}
denote the corresponding verification strategy. In the bad case,
the single-round worst-case passing probability that
\sigma_{j}
passes the
test satisfies
Pallister et al. 2018

\displaystyle P(\Omega,\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\max_{\langle{\psi}|\sigma_{j}|{\psi}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon}\tr[\Omega\sigma_{j}]=1-[1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega)]\varepsilon,

(1)

where
\lambda_{2}(\Omega)
is the second largest eigenvalue of
\Omega
.
Correspondingly, all
N
quantum states in the bad case can pass the test
with probability at most
[1-(1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega))\varepsilon]^{N}
.
To achieve the failure probability
\delta
, it suffices to take

\displaystyle N(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln[1-[1-\lambda_{2}(\Omega)]\varepsilon]}\right\rceil,

(2)

where
\ln
denotes the natural logarithm.
We call
N(\Omega)
the sample complexity of
\Omega
by abuse of notation.

II.2
Quantum measurements

The most general kind of measurements in quantum mechanics is called the

positive-operator-valued measures (POVMs)
.
A POVM is a set of operators
\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
satisfying
\forall\theta\in\Theta,M_{\theta}\geq 0

and
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}M_{\theta}=\mathbbm{1}
, where
\Theta
is a finite alphabet,

\theta\in\Theta
records the measurement outcome, and
\mathbbm{1}
is the identity operator
Hayashi 2017
.
Performing the measurement on a quantum state
\rho
,
the probability of outcome
\theta
is given by the Born rule:
p_{\theta}=\tr[M_{\theta}\rho]
.
If every operator
M_{\theta}
of the POVM is a rank-one projector,
then the corresponding measurement is also called a
von Neumann measurement
.

An equivalent characterization of quantum measurements from the quantum channel perspective
is to regard the resulting states as purely classical states.
Mathematically, the induced measurement channel of a POVM
\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

is defined as
Hayashi 2017

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(\rho)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[M_{\theta}\rho]|{\theta}\rangle\!\langle{\theta}|,

(3)

where
\{|{\theta}\rangle\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is the computational basis of the underlying Hilbert space.
Thus, a measurement channel takes a quantum system to a classical one.

The QSV task reviewed above is closely related to QMV, but it cannot be applied
directly. In QSV, the untrusted object is a state source; in QMV, it is an
entire measurement apparatus, described by a faulty POVM

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
whose elements are jointly constrained by

\sum_{\theta}M_{\theta}=\mathbbm{1}
. Hence the adversary may redistribute probability
among outcomes, and the worst-case passing probability is not the expectation
value of one state against one verification operator.
Verifying the Choi state of the measurement channel in Eq. (
3
)
would require process verification, while directly testing a von Neumann measurement

\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
by preparing

|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle
is often unavailable when these states are entangled. These
obstacles motivate a dedicated QMV framework with local input states, which we
now formulate.

III
Quantum Measurement Verification

We first establish a general framework for quantum measurement verification.
Then, by leveraging the group symmetry within von Neumann measurements,
we show when the QMV problem constrained by locality can be reduced to a
QSV problem for one representative basis state,
where existing tools facilitate the design of efficient and even optimal verification strategies.

III.1
General framework

Consider a quantum measurement device
\mathscr{D}
that is supposed to implement a
von Neumann measurement on
n
parties
\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
,
where
\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}

and
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space of dimension
d
.
The total number of measurement outcomes is
|\Theta|=d^{n}
.
However,
\mathscr{D}
might not work correctly and actually
realizes a POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
that is different from
\mathcal{P}
.
In order to distinguish these two cases by making a reasonable number of uses of
\mathscr{D}
,
we are promised that the noisy measurement
\mathcal{M}
is sufficiently far from the target
\mathcal{P}
.
That is, we need to distinguish between the following two cases with the worst-case failure probability
\delta
:

Good

\mathscr{D}
accurately implements
\mathcal{P}
;

Bad

\mathscr{D}
implements
\mathcal{M}
, such that
F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon
, where

F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|M_{\theta}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle

is the measurement fidelity quantifying the closeness between
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{P}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
is
the precision.

The ideal measurement
\mathcal{P}
naturally assigns to each input state a
set of outcomes that may occur with nonzero probability. More precisely,
for an input state
\rho
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\rho)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\theta\in\Theta\;\middle|\;\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\rho|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle>0\right\}.

(4)

This set is the support of the outcome distribution generated by the
ideal measurement
\mathcal{P}
on the input
\rho
, and it will be used as the
accept set for that input.

Once the accept set is fixed in this way by the target measurement,
a QMV protocol is specified by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}))\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}},

(5)

where
\rho_{x}
is chosen with probability
p_{x}

and
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})
denotes the associated accept set of measurement outcomes.
A single round of the protocol proceeds as follows.
The verifier samples
x
according to

p_{x}
, prepares
\rho_{x}
, and feeds it into the device
\mathscr{D}
.
If the returned outcome
\theta
belongs to
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})
, the device
passes this round; otherwise it fails.
The rounds are repeated until either a failure occurs or
the prescribed number
N
of rounds has been reached.
If any round fails, the verifier stops and declares that

\mathscr{D}
is in the
Bad
case.
If all
N
rounds pass, the verifier declares that
\mathscr{D}
is in the
Good
case.
The verification procedure is illustrated in Figure
1
.
The choice of
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})
enjoys two crucial advantages:
1) it guarantees that if the device
is
in the
Good
case, it always passes the test; and
2) it is optimal in the sense that any strictly larger set than
\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})
can only increase
the chance of being fooled by an adversary
Pallister et al. 2018
.

Given a protocol
\mathcal{S}
, we associate with each outcome
\theta\in\Theta

the operator

\displaystyle\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}p_{x}\rho_{x}.

(6)

We call
\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})
the verification operator associated with
outcome
\theta
. It collects, with their sampling probabilities, all
test states for which the ideal outcome
\theta
is accepted.

Now fix an actual POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
implemented
by
\mathscr{D}
. The single-round passing probability of the protocol

\mathcal{S}
against
\mathcal{M}
is

\displaystyle p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\tr[\rho_{x}M_{\theta}]

(7a)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr\!\left[\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})M_{\theta}\right].

(7b)

The second equality is just a rearrangement of the sum according to the
reported outcome.

The relevant figure of merit for QMV is the largest value of
this single-round passing probability over all POVMs in the

Bad
case.
We therefore define the single-round worst-case passing probability as

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\;\max_{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\scriptscriptstyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})

(8a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\max_{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\scriptscriptstyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr\!\left[\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})M_{\theta}\right].

(8b)

We repeat the above verification round
N
times independently.
An error occurs if the quantum device is in the
Bad
case
but we incorrectly decide that it is in the
Good
case.
The worst-case probability of this error is
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)^{N}
,
since the rounds are carried out independently.
Thus, to achieve confidence
1-\delta
, it suffices to take

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)^{N}\leq\delta
, yielding the minimum number of tests

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon,\delta)=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)}\right\rceil,

(9)

assuming that
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)<1
.
Our task is to construct good verification protocols that minimize
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
.

For completeness, Appendix
A
collects several
basic properties of the maximal passing probability

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
. These observations clarify the behavior of the
figure of merit under refinement of test states, variation of

\varepsilon
, mixing of protocols, and local equivalence of target
measurements.

III.2
Optimal verification without constraints

If there were no constraints on the set of test states,
the optimal QMV protocol has been proposed in
Liu et al. 2020

and works as follows. We randomly prepare an input state
|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle

with probability
1/|\Theta|
and measure it with
\mathscr{D}
.
If the measurement outcome is
\theta
,
\mathscr{D}
passes the test;
otherwise
\mathscr{D}
fails the test. Namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm opt}=\left\{1/|\Theta|,(|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|,\{\theta\})\right\}_{\theta\in\Theta}.

(10)

The maximal probability of this protocol reads

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\rm opt},\varepsilon)=\max_{\scriptscriptstyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\psi_{\theta}M_{\theta}]=1-\varepsilon,

(11)

where
\psi_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|
and the upper bound follows from the definition of
F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})
and is
tight by mixing the ideal measurement with a fixed point free relabeling of the
outcomes.
Then, Eq. (
9
) implies that we can verify
\mathscr{D}
with the number of tests

\displaystyle N_{\rm opt}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln(1-\varepsilon)}\right\rceil\approx\left\lceil\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil,

(12)

where we have used the fact that
\lim_{x\to 0}\ln(1+x)=x
.

The above protocol requires that we are able to prepare possibly highly
entangled states
\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\}_{\theta}
, which is experimentally challenging.
It is thus of practical interest to devise efficient or optimal QMV protocols
under reasonable physical constraints.
Nevertheless, it will serve as the benchmark against which we compare the
more constrained protocols investigated below.

III.3
Symmetries simplify QMV with locality constraint

We now impose the locality constraint on the verification protocol: every test state
in
\mathcal{S}
must be a local product state. This constraint is essential for
experimental implementability, but it also makes the optimization in
Eq. (
8
) difficult. The main point of this
section is that a natural symmetry of the target measurement removes most of
this difficulty: under suitable conditions,
locality constrained QMV reduces to
locality constrained QSV for one representative basis state.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a von Neumann measurement.
We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
locally transitive
if there exist a finite group

G
acting transitively on
\Theta
and a local unitary representation
V
of

G
on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
such that

\displaystyle V(g)|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|V^{\dagger}(g)=|{\psi_{g\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{g\theta}}|,\quad\forall g\in G,

(13)

where
V(g)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}(g)
and each
V_{i}(g)
acts on the local Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.

Given a local protocol
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}))\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}
, its group symmetrization is

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{S}}=\left\{\frac{p_{x}}{|G|},\left(V(g)\rho_{x}V^{\dagger}(g),\,g\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})\right)\right\}_{x\in\mathcal{X},g\in G},

(14)

where
g\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{g\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})\}
. Since
V(g)
is local,

\bar{\mathcal{S}}
is still a local protocol. We call
\mathcal{S}

symmetric
if
this symmetrization leaves the protocol unchanged up to relabeling.
We prove the following in Appendix
B.1
.

Proposition 1
.

If
\mathcal{P}
is locally transitive, then a local protocol
\mathcal{S}
can be replaced
by a symmetric local protocol
\bar{\mathcal{S}}
satisfying

\displaystyle P(\bar{\mathcal{S}},\varepsilon)\leq P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon).

(15)

Consequently, optimal local QMV protocols may be searched for within the class
of symmetric protocols.

The second symmetry condition concerns the stabilizer of one outcome.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be locally transitive with
respect to
G
. For each
\theta\in\Theta
, define the stabilizer

H_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{g\in G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}g\theta=\theta\}
. We say that
\mathcal{P}
is

irreducible
if the one dimensional space spanned by

|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle
is an irreducible subspace of the representation of

H_{\theta}
, and no irreducible subspace of its orthogonal complement is
equivalent to this one dimensional subspace. This condition is independent of
the choice of
\theta
.

These two symmetry conditions play complementary roles. Local transitivity
allows any local protocol to be symmetrized without increasing the worst-case
passing probability, while irreducibility ensures that the symmetrized
verification operator for any one outcome has the same spectral structure as a
pure state verification strategy. Together, they reduce the design of efficient
local QMV protocols to the design of efficient local QSV protocols for a single
representative state
|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle
.
This reduction is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a von Neumann
measurement that is both locally transitive and irreducible.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be a symmetric local QMV protocol of
\mathcal{P}
, and define

\displaystyle T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\Theta|\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

(16)

The following statements hold:

1.

For every
\theta\in\Theta
,
T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})
is a valid
verification operator for the state
|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle
; namely,

\displaystyle 0\leq T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})\leq\mathbbm{1},\qquad T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle.

(17)

2.

The QMV worst-case passing probability is equal to the QSV
worst-case passing probability for any fixed outcome
\theta
;
namely, for any
\theta\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\geq 0,\ \tr[\sigma]=1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tr[T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})\sigma].

(18)

In particular, the value of the maximization on the right
hand side is independent of the choice of
\theta
.

3.

The second largest eigenvalue of
T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})
is independent
of
\theta
. Denoting this common value by
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{S})
, we have

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)=1-\bigl[1-\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{S})\bigr]\varepsilon.

(19)

Equivalently, for every
\theta\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}(T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}))=\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{S}).

(20)

Theorem
2
is the main reduction result and is shown in
Appendix
B.2
.
It turns the original optimization over faulty POVMs in
Eq. (
8
) into the familiar QSV problem
of minimizing the second eigenvalue of a single operator
T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})
.
The representative outcome
\theta
is arbitrary, so all outcomes are verified
with the same efficiency.

A particularly useful case is when the symmetric protocol is homogeneous:

\displaystyle\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})=\frac{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|+\beta(\mathbbm{1}-|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|)}{d^{n}},\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(21)

Then
T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})=|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|+\beta(\mathbbm{1}-|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|)
and
Theorem
2
immediately gives

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)=1-(1-\beta)\varepsilon.

(22)

Accordingly, the number of tests required to achieve significance
\delta
is

\displaystyle N(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon,\delta)=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln[1-(1-\beta)\varepsilon]}\right\rceil.

(23)

Thus, after symmetry reduction, designing an efficient local QMV protocol
amounts to constructing local test states that make
\lambda_{2}(T_{\theta})
,
or equivalently
\beta
in the homogeneous case, as small as possible.

IV
Applications

In this section, we apply the symmetry viewpoint of
Theorem
2
to several representative measurements.
For each example, we first identify the local symmetry that
permutes the measurement outcomes, then construct a local protocol and display
its verification operators. When both preconditions of
Theorem
2
are satisfied, this reduces the
QMV problem constrained by locality to verification of a single representative
state; otherwise, we give a direct calculation of the homogeneous operator.

IV.1
Generalized Bell measurement

The generalized Bell measurement (GBM) is the canonical joint measurement of two qu
d
its

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}\equiv\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
onto a maximally entangled basis.
It is a fundamental primitive in quantum information theory, most notably in quantum
teleportation
Bennett et al. 1993
.
We use the symmetry reduction of Theorem
2
to obtain the
optimal local state verification protocol for the GBM.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,\cdots,d-1\}
.
The canonical maximally entangled state in a Hilbert space of dimension
d^{2}
,

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
, is formally defined as

\displaystyle|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|{jj}\rangle,

(24)

where
\{|{j}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\}
is the computational basis of
\mathcal{H}
.
Let
\{W_{x,z}\}_{(x,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}}
be the set of discrete Weyl operators on
\mathcal{H}
;
see Appendix
C
for the detailed definition.
The GBM on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes 2}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\left\{|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{x,z}|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\right\},

(25)

where
|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbbm{1}\otimes W_{x,z})|\Psi^{\star}\rangle
.

Now we show that the GBM satisfies the two symmetry conditions required by
Theorem
2
. Let
G
be the finite local Weyl group;
after suppressing irrelevant phases, its action is represented by

\displaystyle V((x,z),(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W_{x,z}\otimes W_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}}

(26)

for
((x,z),(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}))\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
.
This action permutes the
d^{2}
Bell states transitively. The stabilizer of

|\Psi^{\star}\rangle
is generated, up to phases, by

\{W_{x,z}\otimes W_{x,-z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(x,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}\}
, under which the Bell basis
decomposes into one dimensional irreducible subspaces. Hence the GBM is locally
transitive and irreducible.

By Theorem
2
, the QMV problem constrained by locality
therefore reduces to a single symmetric pure state verification problem. For the
reference outcome
(0,0)
, set
T=d^{2}\Omega_{0,0}(\mathcal{S})
.
The optimal single-round worst-case passing probability can be written as

\displaystyle P^{\star}_{\mathcal{B},\mathrm{loc}}(\varepsilon)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{\mathcal{S}\,\mathrm{local}}P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)=\min_{T}\max_{\scriptscriptstyle\langle{\Psi^{\star}}|\sigma|{\Psi^{\star}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon}\tr[T\sigma],

(27)

where the minimization is over separable verification operators satisfying

0\leq T\leq\mathbbm{1}
and
T|\Psi^{\star}\rangle=|\Psi^{\star}\rangle
.
Thus the QMV problem is reduced to separable verification of the maximally
entangled state
|\Psi^{\star}\rangle
. The remaining task is to solve this
minimax problem and lift the optimal representative operator back to all
GBM outcomes. The following corollary states the optimal local
verification protocol and sample complexity; the proof and explicit MUB
construction are given in Appendix
C
.

Corollary 3
.

Assume that
d
is prime. The generalized Bell measurement
\mathcal{B}
defined in
Eq. (
25
) admits an optimal local verification protocol
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
with
verification operators

\displaystyle\Omega_{x,z}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\Bigl[|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\langle\Psi_{x,z}|

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{d+1}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\langle\Psi_{x,z}|\right)\Bigr].

(28)

Consequently,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}},\varepsilon)=P^{\star}_{\mathcal{B},\mathrm{loc}}(\varepsilon)=1-\frac{d}{d+1}\varepsilon,

(29)

and the exact number of tests needed to verify
\mathcal{B}
within infidelity

\varepsilon

\delta

IV.2
Single-parameter measurement on two qubits

We next consider a single-parameter family of measurements on two qubits that interpolates
between product basis measurements and the Bell measurement. Define the four
orthonormal states on two qubits

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0,0}^{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sin\gamma|{00}\rangle+\cos\gamma|{11}\rangle,

(31a)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{0,1}^{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sin\gamma|{01}\rangle+\cos\gamma|{10}\rangle,

(31b)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{1,0}^{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\cos\gamma|{01}\rangle-\sin\gamma|{10}\rangle,

(31c)

\displaystyle|\Psi_{1,1}^{\gamma}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\cos\gamma|{00}\rangle-\sin\gamma|{11}\rangle,

(31d)

where
\gamma\in[0,\pi/2]
. The corresponding von Neumann measurement is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}=\{|\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}|\}_{x,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}}.

(32)

At
\gamma=\pi/4
,
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
is the Bell measurement on two qubits; at

\gamma=0
and
\gamma=\pi/2
, it reduces to a product basis measurement up to
relabeling.

We first show that the family
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
is locally transitive.
Up to irrelevant phases, the local unitaries generated by

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\otimes X,\quad Z\otimes Z,\quad ZX\otimes\mathbbm{1}

(33)

permute the four projectors in Eq. (
32
). This allows us
to use a symmetric local protocol. Appendix
D

constructs such a protocol
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}
explicitly. The corollary below gives
the resulting verification operators, success probability, and sample
complexity.

Corollary 4
.

For every
\gamma\in[0,\pi/2]
, the single-parameter measurement on two qubits

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
defined in Eq. (

) admits a local
verification protocol
\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}
with verification operators

\displaystyle\Omega_{x,z}(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma})=\frac{1}{4}\left[|{\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}}|+\frac{1}{3}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|{\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}}\rangle\!\langle{\Psi_{x,z}^{\gamma}}|\right)\right].

(34)

Consequently,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma},\varepsilon)=1-\frac{2}{3}\varepsilon,

(35)

and the exact number of tests required by this protocol is

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln P(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma},\varepsilon)}\right\rceil\approx\left\lceil\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil.

(36)

The protocol has the same asymptotic sample complexity as the Bell measurement

\gamma=\pi/4
, it

IV.3
Elegant joint measurement on two qubits

The elegant joint measurement (EJM) on two qubits is a partially entangled
measurement with tetrahedral one qubit marginals. It was introduced as a
joint measurement different from the Bell measurement
Gisin 2019

and later used in bilocal network nonlocality tests

Tavakoli et al. 2021
;
Huang et al. 2022
. This makes the EJM a
representative target on two qubits beyond both product basis measurements and
maximally entangled Bell measurements.

In its general form, the EJM on two qubits is itself a single-parameter family. Let

\kappa\in[0,\pi/2]
and
r_{\pm}^{\kappa}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1\pm e^{i\kappa})/\sqrt{2}
.
Define four orthonormal states
Huang et al. 2022
:

\displaystyle|\Phi_{0}^{\kappa}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}|{00}\rangle-r_{+}^{\kappa}|{01}\rangle-r_{-}^{\kappa}|{10}\rangle+e^{-\frac{3i\pi}{4}}|{11}\rangle\right),

(37a)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{1}^{\kappa}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}|{00}\rangle+r_{-}^{\kappa}|{01}\rangle+r_{+}^{\kappa}|{10}\rangle+e^{\frac{3i\pi}{4}}|{11}\rangle\right),

(37b)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{2}^{\kappa}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{3i\pi}{4}}|{00}\rangle+r_{-}^{\kappa}|{01}\rangle+r_{+}^{\kappa}|{10}\rangle+e^{-\frac{i\pi}{4}}|{11}\rangle\right),

(37c)

\displaystyle|\Phi_{3}^{\kappa}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(e^{\frac{3i\pi}{4}}|{00}\rangle-r_{+}^{\kappa}|{01}\rangle-r_{-}^{\kappa}|{10}\rangle+e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}}|{11}\rangle\right).

(37d)

The single-parameter two-qubit EJM is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}=\{|\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}\rangle\!\langle\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}j=0,1,2,3\}.

(38)

At
\kappa=0
this recovers the original EJM in
Ref.
Gisin 2019
, while
\kappa=\pi/2
gives a maximally
entangled endpoint.

For
0\leq\kappa<\pi/2
,
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}
is essentially different from
the single-parameter family
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
considered in
Section
IV.2
. Indeed, every state in
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
has
the same Schmidt basis up to local relabeling, whereas the states in

\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}
have tetrahedrally arranged reduced states. Hence the verification
protocol constructed for
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}
cannot be imported by local covariance,
and a separate local protocol is required.

The local Pauli operators
X\otimes X
and
Z\otimes Z
act on the four
EJM projectors as the permutations
(01)(23)
and
(03)(12)
,
respectively, up to irrelevant phases of the underlying state vectors.
Hence the group generated by them acts transitively on the four outcomes
for every
\kappa\in[0,\pi/2]
, including the maximally entangled endpoint

\kappa=\pi/2
. Thus
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}
admits a symmetric local verification
protocol. Appendix
E

constructs this protocol explicitly; the corollary below gives
its verification operators, success probability, and sample complexity.

Corollary 5
.

For every
\kappa\in[0,\pi/2]
, the elegant joint measurement
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}

defined in Eq. (
38
) admits a local verification protocol

\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}
with verification operators, for
j=0,1,2,3
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{j}(\mathcal{S}_{\kappa})=\frac{1}{4}\left[|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle\!\langle{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}|+\beta_{\kappa}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle\!\langle{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}|\right)\right],

(39)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{\kappa}=\frac{4-3\sin\kappa}{3(2-\sin\kappa)}.

(40)

Consequently,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\kappa},\varepsilon)=1-(1-\beta_{\kappa})\varepsilon=1-\frac{2}{3(2-\sin\kappa)}\varepsilon,

(41)

and the exact number of tests required by this protocol is

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln P(\mathcal{S}_{\kappa},\varepsilon)}\right\rceil\approx\left\lceil\frac{3(2-\sin\kappa)}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil.

(42)

Compared with the homogeneous protocol in Corollary
4
,
the EJM protocol has a homogeneous parameter that depends on
\kappa
. It equals
2/3
for the original
EJM at
\kappa=0
and decreases to
1/3
at the maximally entangled endpoint

\kappa=\pi/2
. Thus the original EJM requires twice as many tests as the
Bell measurement scaling, while the endpoint recovers the same asymptotic sample
complexity as the Bell measurement.

IV.4
Stabilizer state induced measurements

We now consider measurements induced by stabilizer bases on
n
qudits, assuming
that the local dimension
d
is prime. Let
\mathbb{F}_{d}
be the finite field with
d

elements, and define the Weyl representation on
n
qudits

\displaystyle W^{n}(\vec{x})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W(x_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes W(x_{n})

(43)

for
\vec{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2})^{n}\simeq\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}
, where

x_{i}=(a_{i},b_{i})
and
W(x_{i})\equiv W_{a_{i},b_{i}}
is a single qudit Weyl operator;
see Appendix
C
.

Let
N\subset\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}
be a commutative subgroup with dimension
n
.
For this subgroup, let
|{S}\rangle
be the stabilizer state satisfying

\displaystyle W^{n}(\vec{x})|{S}\rangle=|{S}\rangle,\qquad\forall\vec{x}\in N.

(44)

The orbit

\{|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=W^{n}(\vec{a})|{S}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\vec{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N\}

forms an orthonormal basis, and the induced measurement is

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{S}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\!\langle{S,\vec{a}}|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\vec{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N\}.

(45)

The Weyl translations
W^{n}(\vec{x})
are local and act
transitively on the outcomes of
\mathcal{M}_{S}
, because

W^{n}(\vec{x})|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle

is proportional to
|{S,\vec{x}+\vec{a}}\rangle
, with the label understood
modulo
N
. The stabilizer of the reference outcome is precisely
N
,
up to irrelevant phases. For
\vec{n}\in N
, the basis state

|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle=W^{n}(\vec{a})|{S}\rangle
is an eigenvector of the stabilizer
operator
W^{n}(\vec{n})
with character

\displaystyle\chi_{\vec{a}}(\vec{n})=\omega^{[\vec{n},\vec{a}]},

(46)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the standard symplectic form over

\mathbb{F}_{d}
and
\omega=\exp(2\pi i/d)
.

These characters are distinct on different cosets of
N
. Indeed, if

\chi_{\vec{a}}=\chi_{\vec{b}}
on
N
, then

\displaystyle[\vec{n},\vec{a}-\vec{b}]=0\qquad\forall\,\vec{n}\in N.

(47)

Thus
\vec{a}-\vec{b}\in N^{\perp}
. Since
N
is maximal isotropic,

N^{\perp}=N
, and hence
\vec{a}
and
\vec{b}
represent the same coset in

\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N
. Therefore different cosets give inequivalent
one-dimensional characters of the stabilizer. The reference line has no
equivalent copy in its orthogonal complement, so the irreducibility
condition in Definition
2
is satisfied.
Therefore Theorem
2
applies.
Appendix
F

constructs the local protocol; the corollary below
summarizes its verification operators, success probability, and sample complexity.

Corollary 6
.

The stabilizer state induced measurement
\mathcal{M}_{S}
defined in
Eq. (
45
) admits the local
verification protocol
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}}
.
For each outcome
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N
, its verification operator is

\displaystyle\Omega_{\vec{a}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{n}}\left[|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\!\langle{S,\vec{a}}|+\beta_{S}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\!\langle{S,\vec{a}}|\right)\right],

(48)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{S}=\frac{d^{n-1}-1}{d^{n}-1}.

(49)

Consequently,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=1-(1-\beta_{S})\varepsilon=1-\frac{d^{n}-d^{n-1}}{d^{n}-1}\varepsilon,

(50)

and the exact number of tests required by this protocol is

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{M}_{S}}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}},\varepsilon)}\right\rceil\approx\left\lceil\frac{d^{n}-1}{d^{n}-d^{n-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil.

(51)

V
Measurement fidelity estimation

A QMV protocol
\mathcal{S}
for the measurement device
\mathscr{D}
can be
utilized to estimate the device’s fidelity, as in the cases of quantum state and
process verification
Zhu and Hayashi 2019b
;
Jiang et al. 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2022
;
Wollmann et al. 2023
.
In particular, because the constructed verification operators are homogeneous,
we can estimate the measurement fidelity in a more direct manner.
By the definition of homogeneous operator in Eq. (
21
),
QMV can be related to measurement fidelity estimation via

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|M_{\theta}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle

(52)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\Omega_{\theta}M_{\theta}]-\beta}{1-\beta},

(53)

where the average passing probability
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\Omega_{\theta}M_{\theta}]
can be evaluated
from the experimental data. Specifically, we consecutively perform the
measurement verification protocol
\mathcal{S}
for
N
rounds and record the number of passing instances
f
.
Then
\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\Omega_{\theta}M_{\theta}]\approx f/N
, as guaranteed
by the law of large numbers. However, for finite
N
, the standard deviation is bounded by

\displaystyle\Delta(\widehat{F})\leq\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)\sqrt{N}},

(54)

meaning that the number of rounds required must satisfy

N=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2})

in order to achieve estimation precision
\varepsilon_{\rm est}
,
which is quadratically worse than that of verification.
Details regarding measurement fidelity estimation based on QMV
can be found in Appendix
G
.

Nevertheless, using QMV as an estimation procedure,
one can directly obtain the value of measurement fidelity rather than a bound.
In addition, performing fidelity estimation only needs to know the
frequency
of pass instances
rather than the number of
successive
pass cases, which is much more experimentally robust.

VI
Conclusions and outlook

We developed a framework for verifying entangled measurements using only local
state preparations. We formulated the QMV task in terms of the worst-case
passing probability of faulty POVMs and proved that, for locally transitive and
irreducible von Neumann measurements, symmetry reduced the QMV problem
constrained by locality to a QSV style optimization for one representative basis state. We
then constructed homogeneous local protocols for generalized Bell measurements,
single-parameter measurements on two qubits, elegant joint measurements, and
stabilizer state induced measurements, and derived their verification operators,
success probabilities, and sample complexities. We also showed how homogeneous
QMV protocols could be used to estimate measurement fidelity directly from the
frequency of passing events.

Two problems deserve further study. First, it is important to extend the
present symmetry reduction method beyond locally transitive and irreducible von
Neumann measurements. Second, a fully realistic theory should incorporate
imperfect test state preparation, device behavior that is not independent and identically distributed, and finite sample
confidence bounds, so that QMV protocols can be deployed robustly on quantum platforms.

Appendix A
Properties of the maximal probability

In this section, we collect several basic properties of the maximal
passing probability
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
defined in
Eq. (
8
) of the main text.
These properties are used when designing efficient and optimal
verification protocols.

Proposition 7
.

It suffices to consider pure test states to minimize

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary QMV protocol

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\{p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}))\}_{x\in\mathcal{X}}

(55)

for the target measurement
\mathcal{P}
. For each test state
\rho_{x}
, choose
a spectral decomposition

\displaystyle\rho_{x}=\sum_{y}q_{y|x}v_{y|x},\qquad v_{y|x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|{v_{y|x}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{y|x}}|,

(56)

where only eigenvectors with
q_{y|x}>0
are included. Zero
eigenvalue components are omitted, and the corresponding eigenvectors
may be completed to an orthonormal basis of the underlying Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
.

We refine the original protocol by replacing each mixed test state with
the pure states appearing in its spectral decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{p_{x}q_{y|x},\left(v_{y|x},\mathcal{P}(v_{y|x})\right)\right\}_{x,y}.

(57)

This protocol consists only of pure test states. It also accepts the
ideal measurement
\mathcal{P}
with certainty, because the accept set is again
defined as the support of the ideal outcome distribution.

It remains to compare the worst-case passing probabilities. Take any
POVM
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
satisfying

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon.

(58)

For every pair
(x,y)
appearing in the above decomposition,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(v_{y|x})\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}).

(59)

Indeed, if
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(v_{y|x})
, then

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|v_{y|x}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle>0.

(60)

Since
q_{y|x}>0
, Eq. (
56
)
implies

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\rho_{x}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\geq q_{y|x}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|v_{y|x}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle>0.

(61)

Thus
\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})
.

For this fixed POVM
\mathcal{M}
, we therefore have

\displaystyle p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x,y}p_{x}q_{y|x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(v_{y|x})}\tr[v_{y|x}M_{\theta}]

(62)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x,y}p_{x}q_{y|x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\tr[v_{y|x}M_{\theta}]

(63)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\tr\!\left[\left(\sum_{y}q_{y|x}v_{y|x}\right)M_{\theta}\right]

(64)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\tr[\rho_{x}M_{\theta}]=p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}).

(65)

Maximizing over all POVMs satisfying

F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon
gives

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\varepsilon)\leq P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon).

(66)

Hence any protocol with mixed test states can be refined to a pure-state
protocol without increasing the maximal passing probability.

When the test states are required to be local product states, the same
argument is compatible with the locality constraint for product mixed
states: it suffices to take local spectral decompositions, thereby obtaining
pure local product test states.
∎

Proposition 8
.

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
is nonincreasing in
\varepsilon
. Namely, for

\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,1)
with

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\geq P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon^{\prime}).

(67)

Proof.

Increasing
\varepsilon
makes the bad-measurement condition more
restrictive. More precisely, since
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
, the
feasible set

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}\}

(68)

is contained in

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon\}.

(69)

The same objective function
p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
is maximized over
these two feasible sets. Maximization over the larger feasible set
cannot give a smaller value, and hence

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\geq P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon^{\prime}).

(70)

∎

Proposition 9
.

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
is concave in
\varepsilon
. Namely, for

\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\lambda\varepsilon_{1}+(1-\lambda)\varepsilon_{2})\geq\lambda P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{1})+(1-\lambda)P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{2}).

(71)

Proof.

The maximum in the definition of
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
is attained:
the POVM set is compact in finite dimension, and both the objective
function and the fidelity constraint are continuous.

Choose maximizing POVMs

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{(i)}=\{M_{\theta}^{(i)}\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\qquad i=1,2,

(72)

for
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{1})
and
P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{2})
, respectively.
Thus

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}^{(i)})\leq 1-\varepsilon_{i},\qquad P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{i})=p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}^{(i)}).

(73)

Their convex combination

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\ast}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{\lambda M_{\theta}^{(1)}+(1-\lambda)M_{\theta}^{(2)}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

(74)

is again a POVM. By linearity of the fidelity,

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\lambda F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}^{(1)})+(1-\lambda)F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}^{(2)})

(75)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda(1-\varepsilon_{1})+(1-\lambda)(1-\varepsilon_{2})

(76)

\displaystyle=1-\{\lambda\varepsilon_{1}+(1-\lambda)\varepsilon_{2}\}.

(77)

Therefore
\mathcal{M}^{\ast}
is feasible for

P(\mathcal{S},\lambda\varepsilon_{1}+(1-\lambda)\varepsilon_{2})
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\lambda\varepsilon_{1}+(1-\lambda)\varepsilon_{2})

\displaystyle\geq p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}^{\ast})

(78)

\displaystyle=\lambda p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}^{(1)})+(1-\lambda)p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}^{(2)})

(79)

\displaystyle=\lambda P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{1})+(1-\lambda)P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon_{2}),

(80)

where the middle equality uses the linearity of

p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})
in the POVM elements.
∎

Convex mixing of verification protocols.

Consider two verification protocols for the same target measurement
\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}))\}_{x},\qquad\mathcal{S}_{2}=\{q_{y},(\sigma_{y},\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{y}))\}_{y}.

(81)

For
\lambda\in[0,1]
, their mixture is the protocol

\displaystyle\lambda\mathcal{S}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{S}_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{\lambda p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}));\ (1-\lambda)q_{y},(\sigma_{y},\mathcal{P}(\sigma_{y}))\right\}_{x,y}.

(82)

Proposition 10
.

P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
is convex in the protocol
\mathcal{S}
.
More precisely, for
\mathcal{S}=\lambda\mathcal{S}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{S}_{2}
, one has

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\leq\lambda P(\mathcal{S}_{1},\varepsilon)+(1-\lambda)P(\mathcal{S}_{2},\varepsilon).

(83)

Proof.

For every POVM
\mathcal{M}
, the passing probability is affine in the protocol:

\displaystyle p_{\rm pass}(\lambda\mathcal{S}_{1}+(1-\lambda)\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{M})=\lambda p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{M})+(1-\lambda)p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{M}).

(84)

Hence

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}\left[\lambda p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{M})+(1-\lambda)p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{M})\right]

(85)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\max_{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{M})+(1-\lambda)\max_{\mathcal{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon}p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{M})

(86)

\displaystyle=\lambda P(\mathcal{S}_{1},\varepsilon)+(1-\lambda)P(\mathcal{S}_{2},\varepsilon).

(87)

∎

Local equivalence of target measurements.
Suppose that

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta},\qquad\mathcal{Q}=\{|{\phi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\phi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}

(88)

are two projective measurements on
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}
. They are called
locally equivalent if local unitaries
U_{1},\ldots,U_{n}
exist such that,
with
U\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=U_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes U_{n}
,

\displaystyle|{\phi_{\theta}}\rangle=U|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle,\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(89)

The next proposition states that local equivalence preserves the maximal
passing probability. To avoid ambiguity,
P_{\mathcal{P}}
and
P_{\mathcal{Q}}
denote
the maximal passing probabilities evaluated with respect to the target
measurements
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, respectively.

Proposition 11
.

For any protocol
\mathcal{S}
verifying
\mathcal{P}
, there is a corresponding protocol

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
verifying
\mathcal{Q}
such that

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\varepsilon)=P_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon).

(90)

Proof.

Start from a protocol
\mathcal{S}=\{p_{x},(\rho_{x},\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x}))\}_{x}
for
\mathcal{P}
.
The corresponding protocol for
\mathcal{Q}
is obtained by applying
the same local unitary to every test state:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{p_{x},\left(U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger},\,\mathcal{Q}(U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger})\right)\right\}_{x}.

(91)

This protocol accepts the ideal measurement
\mathcal{Q}
with certainty.

The accept sets are transported by the same unitary. Indeed, for every

x
and
\theta
,

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\rho_{x}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=\langle{\phi_{\theta}}|U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}|{\phi_{\theta}}\rangle,

(92)

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})=\mathcal{Q}(U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}).

(93)

Now consider an arbitrary POVM

\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
in the
\mathcal{Q}
-verification problem.
After conjugation by
U
, define

\displaystyle M_{\theta}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=U^{\dagger}M_{\theta}U,\qquad\mathcal{M}^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{M_{\theta}^{\prime}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}.

(94)

The family
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
is a valid POVM. Moreover,

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{M})=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle{\phi_{\theta}}|M_{\theta}|{\phi_{\theta}}\rangle=\frac{1}{|\Theta|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|U^{\dagger}M_{\theta}U|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}).

(95)

The passing probabilities are also preserved:

\displaystyle p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\mathcal{M})=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}(U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger})}\tr[U\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}M_{\theta}]=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\tr[\rho_{x}U^{\dagger}M_{\theta}U]=p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M}^{\prime}).

(96)

Thus conjugation by
U
gives a bijection between the feasible POVMs for

P_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\varepsilon)
and those for

P_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)
, while preserving the objective value.
Consequently,

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{Q}}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},\varepsilon)=P_{\mathcal{P}}(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon).

(97)

∎

B.1
Proof of Proposition

\mathcal{M}

(102)

(103)

B.2
Proof of Theorem
2

This appendix first proves a lifting lemma for stabilizer-twirled states
and then uses it to reduce the QMV analysis to QSV for one representative
outcome.

B.2.1
Preparation of a key lemma

To prove Theorem
2
from the main text,
the following key lemma is needed to lift a stabilizer-twirled state to a POVM.

Lemma 12
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be locally transitive
and irreducible with respect to
G
and
V
. Fix

\theta_{0}\in\Theta
, and let

\displaystyle H_{\theta_{0}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{g\in G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}g\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}\}.

(104)

For any density operator
\sigma
, define its stabilizer twirl by

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{|H_{\theta_{0}}|}\sum_{h\in H_{\theta_{0}}}V^{\dagger}(h)\sigma V(h).

(105)

Choose one representative
g_{\theta}\in G
for each

\theta\in\Theta
such that
g_{\theta}\theta_{0}=\theta
, and set

\displaystyle M_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V(g_{\theta})\sigma_{H}V^{\dagger}(g_{\theta}),\qquad\theta\in\Theta.

(106)

Then
\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is a POVM; that is,

\displaystyle M_{\theta}\geq 0,\qquad\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}M_{\theta}=\mathbbm{1}.

(107)

Proof.

Positivity of each
M_{\theta}
is immediate. It remains to prove

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}M_{\theta}=\mathbbm{1}.

(108)

Let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}M_{\theta}
.
Since
\sigma_{H}
is invariant under the stabilizer
H_{\theta_{0}}
, the
definition of
M_{\theta}
is independent of the choice of the
representative
g_{\theta}
. Therefore the covariance relation

\displaystyle V(k)AV^{\dagger}(k)=A,\qquad\forall k\in G,

(109)

holds.
Thus
A
commutes with the full group representation. In particular,

A|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle
transforms under
H_{\theta_{0}}
in the same
representation as the line spanned by
|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle
. By the
irreducibility assumption, no equivalent irreducible subspace occurs in
the orthogonal complement of this line. Hence

\displaystyle A|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle=c|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle

(110)

for some scalar
c
.

Since
A
commutes with all
V(g)
and
G
acts transitively on

\Theta
, the same scalar
c
is obtained on every basis vector

|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle
. Therefore

\displaystyle A=c\mathbbm{1}.

(111)

Taking traces gives

\displaystyle c|\Theta|=\tr A=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr M_{\theta}=|\Theta|,

(112)

because each
M_{\theta}
is unitarily equivalent to the density operator

\sigma_{H}
and hence has trace one. Thus
c=1
, and
A=\mathbbm{1}
.
Hence
\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
is a POVM.
∎

B.2.2
Reduction to quantum state verification

Fix an arbitrary outcome
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
.
The proof of Theorem
2
proceeds in five steps:

•

Step 1
records the covariance and normalization properties forced by symmetry.

•

Steps 2 and 3
prove matching upper and lower bounds for a fixed representative outcome
\theta_{0}
:
an arbitrary faulty POVM is averaged into a single density operator, and, conversely,
the lifting lemma converts any admissible density operator back
into a POVM with the same objective value.

•

Step 4
uses the same lifting argument to
prove that
T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})
is a valid verification operator.

•

Step 5
removes the dependence on
\theta_{0}
by transitivity and
applies the standard QSV eigenvalue formula.

Step 1: Covariance and normalization.

We first establish the covariance relations

\displaystyle V(g)\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})V^{\dagger}(g)=\Omega_{g\theta}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad g\in G,\ \theta\in\Theta,

(113)

and

\displaystyle V(g)T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})V^{\dagger}(g)=T_{g\theta}(\mathcal{S}),\qquad g\in G,\ \theta\in\Theta,

(114)

together with the normalization

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=1,\qquad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(115)

Indeed, Eq. (
113
) follows directly
from the symmetry of
\mathcal{S}
. Since

\displaystyle T_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=|\Theta|\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S}),

(116)

Eq. (
114
) follows immediately. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=\sum_{x}p_{x}\sum_{\theta\in\mathcal{P}(\rho_{x})}\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\rho_{x}|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=\sum_{x}p_{x}\tr[\rho_{x}]=1.

(117)

By Eq. (
113
) and transitivity, all
terms on the left-hand side are equal. Therefore, for every

\theta\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle|\Theta|\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle=1,

(118)

which is Eq. (
115
).

Step 2: From a POVM to a density operator.

This step proves the upper bound

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\geq 0,\ \tr[\sigma]=1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma].

(119)

Let
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be an arbitrary POVM. Define

\displaystyle\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}g\in G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
g\theta_{0}=\theta\end{subarray}}V^{\dagger}(g)M_{\theta}V(g).

(120)

This operator is positive. Its trace is one because each

\theta\in\Theta
has
|H_{\theta_{0}}|
preimages under the map

g\mapsto g\theta_{0}
and

\displaystyle|G|=|\Theta|\,|H_{\theta_{0}}|.

(121)

Thus
\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})
is a density operator.

Using

\displaystyle V(g)|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|V^{\dagger}(g)=|{\psi_{g\theta_{0}}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{g\theta_{0}}}|,

(122)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle=F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M}).

(123)

Similarly, using Eq. (
113
), we have

\displaystyle p_{\rm pass}(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{M})=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\Omega_{\theta}(\mathcal{S})M_{\theta}]=\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})].

(124)

Therefore, if
\mathcal{M}
satisfies
F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})\leq 1-\varepsilon
,
then
\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})
satisfies

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{M})|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon.

(125)

Taking the maximum over all such POVMs
\mathcal{M}
gives
Eq. (
119
).

Step 3: The reverse inequality.

We next prove the reverse bound

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\geq 0,\ \tr[\sigma]=1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma].

(126)

Let
\sigma
be any operator satisfying

\displaystyle\sigma\geq 0,\qquad\tr[\sigma]=1,\qquad\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon.

(127)

Twirl it over the stabilizer
H_{\theta_{0}}
:

\displaystyle\sigma_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{|H_{\theta_{0}}|}\sum_{h\in H_{\theta_{0}}}V^{\dagger}(h)\sigma V(h).

(128)

Since
h\theta_{0}=\theta_{0}
for
h\in H_{\theta_{0}}
, Eq. (
114
)
implies that
T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})
is invariant under the stabilizer.
The projector
|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|
is also invariant under the
stabilizer. Hence

\displaystyle\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma_{H}]

\displaystyle=\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma],

(129)

\displaystyle\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma_{H}|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle.

(130)

Choose one representative
g_{\theta}\in G
for each
\theta\in\Theta

such that
g_{\theta}\theta_{0}=\theta
. By
Lemma
12

\displaystyle M_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=V(g_{\theta})\sigma_{H}V^{\dagger}(g_{\theta}),\qquad\theta\in\Theta,

(131)

For this POVM, covariance gives

\displaystyle F(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{M})

\displaystyle=\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma_{H}|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon,

(132)

(133)

Therefore

(134)

Since this holds for every
\sigma
satisfying the constraints in
Eq. (
126
), Eq. (
126
) follows.

Combining Eqs. (
119
) and (
126

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S},\varepsilon)=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\geq 0,\tr[\sigma]=1\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\\
\langle{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}|\sigma|{\psi_{\theta_{0}}}\rangle\leq 1-\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tr[T_{\theta_{0}}(\mathcal{S})\sigma].

(135)

Thus Eq. (

of

Appendix C
Generalized Bell measurement verification

C.1
Introduction to generalized Bell measurements

We first define the Weyl operators, which generalize the qubit Pauli operators.
For every positive integer
d
, define the set
\mathbb{Z}_{d}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{0,\cdots,d-1\}
.
Whenever elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
appear in arithmetic expressions, we assume that the operations are to be taken modulo
d
.
The
discrete Weyl operators
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
d

are defined as follows. Set

\displaystyle\zeta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{d}\right),

(142)

the root of unity of order
d
, and define the unitary operators

\displaystyle X\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}|{c+1}\rangle\!\langle{c}|,\quad Z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\zeta^{c}|{c}\rangle\!\langle{c}|.

(143)

When
d=2
,
X
and
Z
are the standard Pauli operators.
For each index pair
(x,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\times\mathbb{Z}_{d}
, the discrete Weyl operator
W_{x,z}
in
\mathcal{H}
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{x,z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=X^{x}Z^{z}=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}}\zeta^{zc}|{c+x}\rangle\!\langle{c}|.

(144)

When
d=2
, we recover the complete set of Pauli operators.

From the canonical maximally entangled state of two qudits
|\Psi^{\star}\rangle

defined in Eq. (
24
) of the main text,
the generalized Bell states
\{|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\}

are defined via the Weyl operators by

\displaystyle|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbbm{1}_{R}\otimes W_{x,z})|\Psi^{\star}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}|{i}\rangle\otimes W_{x,z}|{i}\rangle.

(145)

Based on the generalized Bell states, the
generalized Bell measurement
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\{|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{x,z}|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}x,z\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}\}.

(146)

Since all the Bell states are entangled,
the generalized Bell measurement is an entangling quantum measurement.
For
d=2
, this construction gives the standard Bell measurement.

C.2
Symmetries of generalized Bell measurements

Let
G
be the local Weyl group, including phases. After quotienting out phases,
its action is represented by
W_{a,b}\otimes W_{c,e}
with

(a,b),(c,e)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
. This action maps Bell states to Bell states and is
transitive on
\{|{\Psi_{x,z}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(x,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}\}
, so the GBM is locally
transitive.

The stabilizer of
|{\Psi^{\star}}\rangle
is, up to phases,

\displaystyle G_{\Psi^{\star}}=\{W_{a,b}\otimes W_{a,-b}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}\}.

(147)

The Bell states are simultaneous eigenvectors of this stabilizer:

\displaystyle(W_{a,b}\otimes W_{a,-b})|{\Psi_{x,z}}\rangle=\chi_{a,b}(x,z)|{\Psi_{x,z}}\rangle,

(148)

where
\chi_{a,b}(x,z)
is a phase depending on
(a,b)
and
(x,z)
. For prime

d
, different pairs
(x,z)
give different characters. Hence the Bell basis
decomposes into one dimensional irreducible subspaces under the stabilizer, and
the irreducibility condition in Definition
2
holds.

C.3
Proof of Corollary
3

We prove Corollary
3
by constructing a local protocol and showing
that its representative operator is the optimal separable verification operator
for
|\Psi^{\star}\rangle
.

C.3.1
Verification protocol

Assume that
d
is prime, so that
\mathbb{Z}_{d}
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{d}
. Introduce
the equivalence relation on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
by

(x,z)\sim(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
if there exists
a\in\mathbb{F}_{d}\setminus\{0\}
such that

(ax,az)=(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
. For a Weyl operator
W
, denote by
\mathscr{B}_{W}
an
eigenbasis of
W
. If
(x,z)\sim(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
, then

\mathscr{B}_{W_{x,z}}=\mathscr{B}_{W_{x^{\prime},z^{\prime}}}
. A convenient set of
representatives is

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{W_{0,1}\equiv Z,\;W_{1,0}\equiv X,\;W_{1,1}\equiv XZ,\;W_{1,2}\equiv XZ^{2},\;\cdots,\;W_{1,d-1}\equiv XZ^{d-1}\}.

(149)

The eigenbases associated with
\mathscr{M}
form a complete set of
d+1

mutually unbiased bases
DURT et al. 2010
. They satisfy the identity

Hayashi 2008
;
Zhu and Hayashi 2019b

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d+1}\sum_{W\in\mathscr{M}}\sum_{|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}}|{u,\overline{u}}\rangle\!\langle{u,\overline{u}}|=|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|+\frac{1}{d+1}(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|),

(150)

where
|{\overline{u}}\rangle
is the complex conjugate of
|{u}\rangle
in the
computational basis.

For each
W\in\mathscr{M}
, let

\mathscr{B}_{\overline{W}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{|{\overline{u}}\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}\}
be the
conjugate basis. Define the local input ensemble

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\frac{1}{d^{2}(d+1)},\left(|{u,u^{\prime}}\rangle\!\langle{u,u^{\prime}}|,\mathcal{B}(|{u,u^{\prime}}\rangle\!\langle{u,u^{\prime}}|)\right)\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\middle|\;|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W},\ |{u^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\overline{W}},\ W\in\mathscr{M}\right\}.

(151)

Thus
\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}}
contains
(d+1)d^{2}
local test states. In each round, one
uniformly samples
W\in\mathscr{M}
, then uniformly and independently samples

|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}
and
|{u^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\overline{W}}
, prepares

|{u}\rangle\!\langle{u}|\otimes|{u^{\prime}}\rangle\!\langle{u^{\prime}}|
, and accepts exactly the outcomes in

\mathcal{B}(|{u,u^{\prime}}\rangle\!\langle{u,u^{\prime}}|)
.

C.3.2
Performance analysis

By local transitivity, it is enough to compute the verification operator for the
reference outcome
(0,0)
. For

|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}
and
|{u^{\prime}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\overline{W}}
,

\displaystyle\left|\langle{\Psi_{0,0}}|u,u^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{d}\left|\langle\overline{u}|u^{\prime}\rangle\right|^{2}.

(152)

Therefore the local inputs that can produce the ideal outcome
(0,0)
are exactly

\displaystyle\Xi_{0,0}=\bigcup_{W\in\mathscr{M}}\left\{|{u,\overline{u}}\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\middle|\;|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}\right\}.

(153)

It follows that

\displaystyle\Omega_{0,0}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}(d+1)}\sum_{W\in\mathscr{M}}\sum_{|{u}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{W}}|{u,\overline{u}}\rangle\!\langle{u,\overline{u}}|

(154)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|+\frac{1}{d+1}(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|)\right)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left(|\Psi_{0,0}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{0,0}|+\frac{1}{d+1}(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi_{0,0}\rangle\!\langle\Psi_{0,0}|)\right),

(155)

where Eq. (
155
) follows from Eq. (
150
). Equivalently,
the representative operator
T^{\star}_{\mathcal{B}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=d^{2}\Omega_{0,0}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}})
is

\displaystyle T^{\star}_{\mathcal{B}}=|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|+\frac{1}{d+1}(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi^{\star}\rangle\!\langle\Psi^{\star}|).

(156)

Covariance then gives, for every
(x,z)\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{2}
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{x,z}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}})=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left[|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\langle\Psi_{x,z}|+\frac{1}{d+1}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|\Psi_{x,z}\rangle\langle\Psi_{x,z}|\right)\right].

(157)

Thus the homogeneous parameter is
\beta=1/(d+1)
, and
Theorem
2
gives

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{B}},\varepsilon)=1-\frac{d}{d+1}\varepsilon.

(158)

Substituting this value into Eq. (

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{B}}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln\left(1-\frac{d}{d+1}\varepsilon\right)}\right\rceil

(159)

and, for small

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{B}}\approx\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{d}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil,

(160)

which is the claimed scaling in Corollary
3
.

It remains to justify optimality. By the symmetry reduction in
Theorem
2
, any local protocol for the GBM induces a
separable verification operator
T
for the maximally entangled state

|{\Psi^{\star}}\rangle
. The separable verification bound in
Ref.
(

states that the smallest possible second
eigenvalue of such a

above.
Hence no local GBM verification protocol
can improve on the passing probability or sample complexity above.

E.3.1
Verification protocol

The protocol
\mathcal{S}_{\kappa}
consists of three symmetry orbits of local product

First, for each unordered pair
0\leq j<\ell\leq 3

the two dimensional subspace

\operatorname{span}\{|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle,|{\Phi_{\ell}^{\kappa}}\rangle\}
of
the form

\displaystyle|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle+z|{\Phi_{\ell}^{\kappa}}\rangle.

(185)

Such a vector is a product vector if and only if the determinant of its

2\times 2
coefficient matrix in the computational product basis
vanishes. Direct substitution of Eq. (
37
) shows that,
for every unordered pair
\{j,\ell\}

equivalent, up to an irrelevant nonzero scalar factor, to

\displaystyle(e^{2i\kappa}-3)z^{2}+2(e^{2i\kappa}+1)z+(e^{2i\kappa}-3)=0.

(186)

Let

denote the two roots of this equation, counted with

\displaystyle|{\eta_{j\ell,\pm}^{\kappa}}\rangle=\frac{|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle+z_{\pm}^{\kappa}|{\Phi_{\ell}^{\kappa}}\rangle}{\sqrt{1+|z_{\pm}^{\kappa}|^{2}}}.

(187)

Since
|{\eta_{j\ell,\pm}^{\kappa}}\rangle
lies in

\operatorname{span}\{|{\Phi_{j}^{\kappa}}\rangle,|{\Phi_{\ell}^{\kappa}}\rangle\}

it is automatically orthogonal to the other two EJM basis states.

\{j,\ell\}
.

For

\kappa=0

\displaystyle-2(z-1)^{2}=0,

(188)

so the two roots coincide. In this degenerate endpoint, the two labels

\eta_{j\ell,+}^{0}
and
\eta_{j\ell,-}^{0}
represent the same product
test. The protocol is then understood with the duplicate tests merged,
or equivalently with their probabilities added.

Set

\displaystyle a_{\kappa}=\frac{1+\sin\kappa}{2(2-\sin\kappa)},\qquad b_{\kappa}=\frac{1-\sin\kappa}{2-\sin\kappa},\qquad c_{\kappa}=\frac{1-\sin\kappa}{2(2-\sin\kappa)}.

(189)

The protocol is:

•

prepare each

and accept the outcomes
\{j,\ell\}
;

•

prepare each
|{\bm{t}_{j},\bm{t}_{j}}\rangle
and

b_{\kappa}/8
and accept all outcomes
except

•

prepare each
|{\bm{t}_{j},-\bm{t}_{j}}\rangle
with probability

and
accept all four outcomes.

The probabilities sum to one because
a_{\kappa}+b_{\kappa}+c_{\kappa}=1
. At

\kappa=0
, the two roots in Eq. (

and the first orbit reduces to the six Pauli tests with opposite local
directions used for the original EJM.

, Section X)
.

F.2
Symmetries of stabilizer state induced measurements

The Weyl operators
W^{n}(\vec{x})
are tensor products of local Weyl operators.
They map

\displaystyle|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\mapsto W^{n}(\vec{x})|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle=|{S,\vec{x}+\vec{a}}\rangle,

(202)

up to phases and modulo the stabilizer subgroup
N
. Hence the action is
locally transitive on
\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N
.

The stabilizer of the reference outcome is
N
.
Each basis
state
|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle=W^{n}(\vec{a})|{S}\rangle
is a simultaneous eigenvector of
the stabilizer operators. More explicitly, the relation

\displaystyle W^{n}(\vec{n})|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle=\chi_{\vec{a}}(\vec{n})|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle,\qquad\chi_{\vec{a}}(\vec{n})=\omega^{[\vec{n},\vec{a}]}

(203)

holds for
\vec{n}\in N
. If two
cosets
\vec{a}+N
and
\vec{b}+N
define the same character on
N
, then

\displaystyle[\vec{n},\vec{a}-\vec{b}]=0\qquad\forall\,\vec{n}\in N.

(204)

Hence
\vec{a}-\vec{b}\in N^{\perp}
. Since
N
is maximal isotropic,

N^{\perp}=N
, and therefore
\vec{a}+N=\vec{b}+N
. Thus different cosets in

\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N
define inequivalent one-dimensional characters of the
stabilizer. Consequently, the stabilizer representation on the basis

\{|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\}
is multiplicity free, and the irreducibility
condition in Definition
2
is satisfied.

F.3
Proof of Corollary
6

We prove Corollary
6
by
constructing a local stabilizer test protocol and evaluating the associated
homogeneous verification operator.

F.3.1
Verification protocol

Choose stabilizer generators
\{W^{n}(\vec{x}_{r})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}r=1,\ldots,n\}
for
|{S}\rangle
.
For
\vec{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n}
, define

\displaystyle S_{\vec{k}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{r=1}^{n}W^{n}(\vec{x}_{r})^{k_{r}}.

(205)

There are
d^{n}
such stabilizers, and
\vec{k}=\vec{0}
gives the identity.
Let
\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}
be a local product eigenbasis of
S_{\vec{k}}
, and
let
\mathscr{B}^{1}_{\vec{k}}
be the subset with eigenvalue
1
. Then

|\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}|=d^{n}
and
|\mathscr{B}^{1}_{\vec{k}}|=d^{n-1}
for

\vec{k}\neq\vec{0}
. The stabilizer state identity
Zhu and Hayashi 2019a

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{n}-1}\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n}\backslash\{\vec{0}\}}\;\sum_{|{\vec{u}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}^{1}_{\vec{k}}}|{\vec{u}}\rangle\!\langle{\vec{u}}|=|{S}\rangle\!\langle{S}|+\frac{d^{n-1}-1}{d^{n}-1}(\mathbbm{1}-|{S}\rangle\!\langle{S}|)

(206)

is used below.

Define the local protocol

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}}=\left\{\frac{1}{d^{n}(d^{n}-1)},\left(|{\vec{u}}\rangle\!\langle{\vec{u}}|,\mathcal{M}_{S}(|{\vec{u}}\rangle\!\langle{\vec{u}}|)\right)\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\middle|\;|{\vec{u}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}},\;\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n}\backslash\{\vec{0}\}\right\}.

(207)

That is, one samples a nonidentity stabilizer uniformly, samples a local product
eigenstate of that stabilizer uniformly, and accepts exactly the ideal outcomes
that can occur for this input state.

F.3.2
Performance analysis

Fix an outcome
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{F}_{d}^{2n}/N
and define

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}(\vec{a})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{W^{n}(\vec{a})|{\vec{u}}\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\middle|\;|{\vec{u}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}^{1}_{\vec{k}}\right\}.

(208)

This set is exactly the set of test states from the
\vec{k}
-basis that
are accepted by the outcome
\vec{a}
. To see this, first consider the
reference outcome
\vec{0}
. Since
|{S}\rangle
is stabilized by every
nonidentity stabilizer
S_{\vec{k}}
, it lies in the eigenvalue-one
eigenspace of
S_{\vec{k}}
. Hence, in the eigenbasis

\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}
, only the vectors in

\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}^{1}
can have nonzero overlap with
|{S}\rangle
.
Therefore the states in
\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}^{1}
are precisely the
states in
\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}
accepted by the reference outcome.

Conjugation by
W^{n}(\vec{a})
maps eigenstates of
S_{\vec{k}}
to eigenstates
of the same stabilizer, possibly with a shifted eigenvalue. Hence

\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}(\vec{a})
is a subset of the test states in
Eq. (
207
). These are exactly the test
states selected by the protocol that have support on the ideal outcome

\vec{a}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\Omega_{\vec{a}}(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{n}(d^{n}-1)}\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n}\backslash\{\vec{0}\}}\sum_{|{\vec{u}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}^{1}}W^{n}(\vec{a})|{\vec{u}}\rangle\!\langle{\vec{u}}|(W^{n}(\vec{a}))^{\dagger}

(209)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{n}}W^{n}(\vec{a})\left(\frac{1}{d^{n}-1}\sum_{\vec{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{d}^{n}\backslash\{\vec{0}\}}\sum_{|{\vec{u}}\rangle\in\mathscr{B}_{\vec{k}}^{1}}|{\vec{u}}\rangle\!\langle{\vec{u}}|\right)(W^{n}(\vec{a}))^{\dagger}

(210)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{n}}W^{n}(\vec{a})\left(|{S}\rangle\!\langle{S}|+\frac{d^{n-1}-1}{d^{n}-1}(\mathbbm{1}-|{S}\rangle\!\langle{S}|)\right)(W^{n}(\vec{a}))^{\dagger}

(211)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{n}}\left[|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\!\langle{S,\vec{a}}|+\frac{d^{n-1}-1}{d^{n}-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}-|{S,\vec{a}}\rangle\!\langle{S,\vec{a}}|\right)\right].

(212)

Thus
\beta=(d^{n-1}-1)/(d^{n}-1)
. Substitution into
Eq. (
22
) gives

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}},\varepsilon)=1-\frac{d^{n}-d^{n-1}}{d^{n}-1}\varepsilon.

(213)

Substitution into Eq. (
23
) gives

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{M}_{S}}=\left\lceil\frac{\ln\delta}{\ln P(\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{M}_{S}},\varepsilon)}\right\rceil\approx\left\lceil\frac{d^{n}-1}{d^{n}-d^{n-1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil,

(214)

which gives the asymptotic sample complexity in
Corollary
6
. The exact formula is
the ceiling expression above. The protocol is
the standard homogeneous stabilizer test strategy. Theorem
2

reduces the relevant optimality question to the second eigenvalue of the
representative stabilizer state verification operator; within the stabilizer test
construction above, this eigenvalue is fixed by the stabilizer identity in
Eq. (
206
), so no different weighting of these
nonidentity stabilizer eigenspaces improves the homogeneous parameter.

Appendix G
Discussion on measurement fidelity estimation

This appendix provides the details and proofs for measurement fidelity estimation based on QMV.
Assume that a homogeneous verification
protocol
\mathcal{S}=\{\Omega_{\theta}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\theta\in\Theta\}
for the target quantum measurement
\mathcal{P}

has been constructed, where each
\Omega_{\theta}
has the form of
Eq. (
21
).

To use this homogeneous QMV strategy to achieve fidelity estimation,
the protocol is performed for
N
rounds.
In round
i
, a test state is sampled and fed into the measurement device.
Based on the outcome, define the random variable
X_{i}
by

\displaystyle X_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{The state passes the test},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(215)

By construction, each
X_{i}
is independent and has a Bernoulli distribution
B(p)
with

\displaystyle p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr[\Omega_{\theta}M_{\theta}]=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\tr\left[\left(\frac{|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|+\beta(\mathbbm{1}-|{\psi_{\theta}}\rangle\!\langle{\psi_{\theta}}|)}{|\Theta|}\right)M_{\theta}\right]=(1-\beta)F+\beta,

(216)

where
F
is the fidelity between the target measurement
\mathcal{P}
and the actual measurement
\mathcal{M}
.
Accordingly, the variance of
X_{i}
is
\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]=p(1-p)
,
where
\mathbb{V}[\cdot]
denotes variance.
By the law of large numbers,
\widehat{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}

approximates the ideal target
p
as
N
becomes large, and

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}]=p
,
where
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation value.
From
\widehat{p}
, the estimate
\widehat{F}
is obtained directly from Eq. (
216
).

It remains to analyze how quickly the estimate
\widehat{F}
converges. First,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\widehat{p}]=\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right]=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{V}[X_{i}]=\frac{p(1-p)}{N},

(217)

where independence of the rounds is used in the third equality. Using Eq. (
216
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\widehat{F}]=\frac{\mathbb{V}[\widehat{p}]}{(1-\beta)^{2}}=\frac{p(1-p)}{(1-\beta)^{2}N}.

(218)

Thus, the sample standard deviation of the estimated fidelity
\widehat{F}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta(\widehat{F})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[\widehat{F}]}=\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}{(1-\beta)\sqrt{N}}=\frac{\sqrt{(1-F)(F+(1-\beta)^{-1}-1)}}{\sqrt{N}}\leq\frac{1}{2(1-\beta)\sqrt{N}},

(219)

where the second equality follows from Eq. (
216
)
and the last inequality follows from the arithmetic-mean–geometric-mean inequality.

This bound gives the sample complexity of fidelity estimation. Since

\widehat{F}=(\widehat{p}-\beta)/(1-\beta)
and

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{p}]=p
, the estimator
\widehat{F}
is unbiased:

\mathbb{E}[\widehat{F}]=F
. If the estimation precision is quantified by the
root-mean-square error and denoted by
\varepsilon_{\rm est}
, then it suffices
to require

\displaystyle\Delta(\widehat{F})\leq\varepsilon_{\rm est}.

(220)

Using Eq. (
219
), this condition is guaranteed whenever

\displaystyle N\geq\left\lceil\frac{1}{4(1-\beta)^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2}}\right\rceil.

(221)

Therefore the number of rounds needed for fidelity estimation scales as

\displaystyle N_{\rm est}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{(1-\beta)^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2}}\right),

(222)

and, for a fixed homogeneous parameter
\beta<1
,

N_{\rm est}=\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2})
. Equivalently, Chebyshev’s
inequality gives the confidence bound

\displaystyle\Pr\!\left[|\widehat{F}-F|\geq\varepsilon_{\rm est}\right]\leq\frac{1}{4(1-\beta)^{2}N\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2}},

(223)

so confidence
1-\delta
is ensured by

\displaystyle N\geq\left\lceil\frac{1}{4(1-\beta)^{2}\delta\,\varepsilon_{\rm est}^{2}}\right\rceil.

(224)
