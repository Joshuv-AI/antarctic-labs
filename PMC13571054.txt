Migraine in the cradle of civilization: Evaluating ancient Mesopotamian texts through the lens of ICHD‐3
Authors: Bulent Diri, Bilge Piri Cinar, Zeynep Sena Diri, Okan Bolukbasi, Peter J. Goadsby, Aynur Ozge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigraine is a neurological disorder with well‐established diagnostic criteria. In its current formulation, migraine is a modern‐day diagnosis, whereas descriptions consistent with migraine may date back to ancient civilizations.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate whether symptoms consistent with migraine without aura, as defined by criteria of ICHD‐3, are present in cuneiform medical texts from ancient Mesopotamia.MethodsA total of 37 Akkadian‐language cuneiform medical texts dated between the second and first millennia bce were analyzed. Texts containing references to secondary headache or aura symptoms were excluded. Each remaining text was examined for descriptions corresponding to ICHD‐3 diagnostic criteria (B: duration; C: pain characteristics; D: associated symptoms).ResultsAmong the 37 texts, 15 included expressions equivalent to continuous or prolonged pain, satisfying criterion B. Criterion C (unilateral, throbbing, and severe pain) was met in 37 texts (26 completely +11 partially). Criterion D (nausea, photophobia, phonophobia) was present in 21 of 37 texts.ConclusionThis study presents compelling evidence that the fundamental features of migraine without aura were described in Mesopotamian medical texts nearly 3000 years ago. These findings suggest that the diagnostic framework of migraine, as captured by ICHD‐3, reflects the earliest documented medical traditions.

INTRODUCTION

Migraine constitutes a prevalent and disabling primary headache disorder, affecting an estimated 12%–15% of the global population and imposing a significant public health burden. 1 Although its modern diagnostic classification has been refined through centuries of systematic clinical inquiry, descriptions consistent with migraine may extend far deeper into human history. Archaeological and written records suggest that head pain has preoccupied ancient healers, philosophers, and scribes since antiquity. 2

Among the earliest traces of such interest are trepanations—surgical perforations of the skull—for which the purposes remain debated. Although Paul Broca's late 19th‐century publication drew scholarly attention to these ancient interventions, 3 the oldest known example, discovered at Taforalt, Morocco, dates to the Epipaleolithic era and provides evidence of postoperative survival. 4 Whether these procedures were performed to relieve intracranial pressure, as Broca hypothesized, or as components of spiritual rituals remains uncertain. The later discovery that cranial bone fragments were used as amulets 5 lends support to the latter interpretation.

As investigations into trepanation continue, scholarly attention has increasingly focused on the rich corpus of cuneiform medical texts from ancient Mesopotamia. Yet, despite this growing interest, comprehensive analyses of headache descriptions—particularly those resembling migraine—remain scarce. Mesopotamia, situated between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, constituted a cradle of urbanization, literacy, and institutional medicine 6 , 7 (Figure 1). Within this setting, writing systems developed by the Sumerians and subsequently adopted by the Akkadians, Babylonians, and Assyrians laid the foundation for an extensive medical tradition. 8

The most significant medical corpus originates from the library of King Ashurbanipal. It includes diagnostic–prognostic series such as Sakikku, attributed to the healer Esagil‐kin‐apli in the 11th century bce. Although Esagil‐kin‐apli is credited as the author, the content reflects a substantially older accumulation of knowledge. 9 In this magico‐medical system, illness was often attributed to metaphysical causes—gods, ghosts, or demons—requiring the intervention of priest‐healers (āšipu), who combined diagnostic acumen with ritual practices. In certain instances, they collaborated with asû‐healers, who performed minor surgical procedures or administered remedies derived from plants, minerals, and animals. 10 , 11 Notably, several headache descriptions in Mesopotamian texts include hemicranial location, throbbing quality, photophobia, nausea, and vestibular‐type sensations such as tinnitus or roaring in the ears—symptoms that align closely with the modern clinical profile of migraine. 9 , 12

This study seeks to investigate whether cuneiform medical texts from Mesopotamia contain symptom descriptions consistent with the diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura, as defined by ICHD‐3 (Headache Classification Committee of the International Headache Society [IHS]) 13 (Table 1). Although anachronistic diagnoses must be approached with appropriate caution, a careful textual and symptom‐based comparison offers valuable insights into historical conceptualizations of migraine.

METHODS

Text selection and inclusion criteria

Thirty‐seven Akkadian‐language cuneiform medical texts were identified and selected for their references to primary headache symptoms, dating primarily to the second and first millennia bce. An initial keyword search related to headache and its associated symptoms yielded 84 texts. Twenty of these pertained to secondary headache disorders. Ten texts were classified as relating to other primary headache types. Consistent with Mesopotamian medical understanding, 12 texts were designated as fatal headaches because they indicated a prognosis of death. Five texts were excluded due to substantial similarity to those already included. Consequently, 47 of the 84 texts were excluded, whereas 37 texts considered relevant to at least one of ICHD‐3 criteria B, C, or D were retained for analysis. Texts suggestive of aura, secondary causes, or systemic illness were also excluded.

These texts were retrieved from authoritative editions of Mesopotamian medical compendia, particularly the works of Scurlock. 9 , 14 In ancient medical texts, references to “bloody vomiting” are generally interpreted as indications of gastrointestinal diseases; such tablets have previously been analyzed under the category of digestive system disorders. 15 Therefore, any tablets pointing to a gastrointestinal condition or lacking additional headache‐related characteristics were excluded from the present analysis. In this context, the expression in question was understood not as literal hematemesis but as a description of severe or bile‐related vomiting. Although ancient medical texts do not explicitly specify headache duration, short‐lasting headaches rarely appear in general medical texts. Instead, these records typically emphasize continuous or paroxysmal headache patterns, reflecting an effort to describe clinically meaningful symptom clusters. To prevent diagnostic ambiguity, tablets describing fatal headaches were excluded from the study.

Within this framework, references to “continuous headache” were interpreted only after fatal conditions had been ruled out and accompanying symptoms were evaluated holistically; thus, the concept of prolonged pain in these texts was considered potentially compatible with the 4–72 h duration defined in modern criteria. Continuous headaches reported without associated symptoms were not included. Only texts containing descriptions consistent with primary headache features were retained. Symptom‐to‐criterion mapping

Each text was examined for descriptors aligned with the diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura from the ICHD‐3 (Table 1). A predefined coding framework was developed to map symptom expressions to ICHD‐3 criteria (Figure 4).

Coding of symptoms in texts according to ICHD‐3 criteria. ICHD‐3. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Interdisciplinary review and reliability assessment

To ensure interpretive accuracy and minimize subjectivity, all symptom‐to‐criterion assignments were independently reviewed by two clinical neurologists (AÖ, bpÇ) with expertise in migraine, in conjunction with a medical historian (o.b.). Discrepancies in coding were discussed until a complete consensus was achieved. To assess interrater agreement, a subset of 10 randomly selected texts was recoded independently, yielding a Cohen's kappa coefficient of 0.87, indicating strong reliability.

Handling ambiguity and missing data

Texts lacking sufficient detail for definitive classification were retained but coded as partial (e.g., “C/2: meets half of the criteria” or “B + C/2 + D”) to reflect incomplete correspondence. No imputation or speculative reconstruction of symptoms was performed. Although metaphysical concepts such as ghost's hand or wandering spirits were frequently encountered in the texts, they were excluded from the analysis because they fell outside the study's criteria.

RESULTS

Symptom distribution across texts

A total of 37 cuneiform medical texts were analyzed for features corresponding to ICHD‐3 diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura. The frequencies of each ICHD‐3 criterion were calculated, and their distribution was analyzed. The most frequently observed symptom was temporal pain, present in 34 of 37 texts. Pulsating or throbbing quality was described in 20 texts, often with expressions such as “the blood vessels of the temple afflict him” or “the temple throbs.” Hemicranial localization (right/left) was explicitly mentioned in 10 texts. Severe or stabbing pain, inferred from terms such as “intensely,” “stings,” or “jabbing pain,” was noted in 10 texts. Figure 5 presents the symptoms mentioned in the texts that correspond to migraine without aura. We also calculated the frequency with which texts fulfilled ICHD‐3 criteria B, C, and D individually. These rates were determined as follows: criterion B: 41%, criterion C: 83%, and criterion D: 57%.

Using ICHD‐3 diagnostic criteria for migraine without aura, four texts containing items B, C, and D were identified. There were 11 texts that met only items C + D. Considering that criteria C and D capture the core symptomatic features of migraine without aura, a total of 15 Mesopotamian texts in which these two items were fully met were identified. Because three texts meeting items B + C/2 + D and three texts meeting items C/2 + D also fulfilled 50% of item C, these six texts can be considered partially related to migraine without aura. The number of texts that met items C and D of migraine without aura was 21 in total: 15 fully and six partially. The number of texts lacking the key C + D combination in ICHD‐3 criteria is 16 (B + C: five texts, B + C/2: three texts, C: six texts, C/2: two texts).

In the C + D combination, it is posited that the patient's pain in criterion C, together with autonomic or nausea/vomiting symptoms in criterion D, supports the diagnosis. Therefore, it was determined that the 16 texts that did not include criterion D or that fell outside the C + D combination did not contain sufficient symptoms to diagnose migraine without aura according to ICHD‐3 criteria, although they were related to migraine without aura.

To further illustrate the internal patterns within the dataset, symptom co‐occurrence analysis was performed. Texts were grouped based on overlapping symptom clusters consistent with ICHD‐3 subcriteria. These symptom clusters are visualized in Figure 8, which highlights the intersections among the most dominant migraine features across texts. This visual exploration reveals that temporal pain functions as a central hub symptom, frequently overlapping with other symptoms. Such internal structure supports the hypothesis that Mesopotamian healers could distinguish complex headache syndromes with overlapping features, which is remarkably consistent with modern approaches to migraine.

DISCUSSION

Headache is among the oldest documented ailments, and its classification has evolved from the earliest recorded descriptions to the modern ICHD‐3 criteria. Whether trepanation—an ancient intervention dating to the Epipaleolithic and Neolithic periods—was employed specifically to treat headache remains a matter of scholarly debate. Given the prevailing belief that headaches were caused by evil spirits, demons, or ghosts, it is plausible that trepanation was performed to alleviate symptoms by expelling such metaphysical entities from the skull. 17 , 18 However, current archaeological evidence indicates that a definitive link between trepanation and headache treatment has not yet been established. 19

Medical records documenting headaches also exist in ancient Egyptian civilization. Several Egyptian papyri, including the Hearst Papyrus (c. 2000 bce), the Edwin Smith Papyrus (c. 1600 bce), the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 bce), and the Berlin Papyrus (c. 1350 bce), contain prescriptions for treating headaches. 20 Headaches are also mentioned in magical papyri from the New Kingdom Period (c. 1550–1200 bce). 21 However, because these texts do not specify the location, nature, or accompanying symptoms of the headache, they cannot be retrospectively classified as migraine. 22 Although unilateral headache is described in some Egyptian papyri, diagnosing migraine based on these documents remains impossible. 21

Subsequently, Hippocrates—frequently regarded as the source of the earliest descriptions of many diseases in modern medical understanding—explained disease physiology using specific theoretical frameworks. His medical doctrine, the theory of the four humors, defined health and illness in terms of balance or imbalance among phlegm, blood, yellow bile, and black bile. For example, cold and damp phlegm was thought to explain brain‐related diseases, whereas vomiting associated with migraine was linked to an excess of yellow bile. 22 , 23 Hippocrates' description—“he seemed to see something shining before him like a light, usually in part of the right eye; at the end of a moment, a violent pain supervened in the right temple, then in all the head and neck …”—includes symptoms compatible with migraine, primarily addressing visual features and pain location. 20 , 24 Despite this, migraine does not appear as a defined disease entity in the Hippocratic Corpus. 22 Nonetheless, Hippocrates is credited with being the first to recognize phonophobia and photophobia in association with migraine. 23 Cornelius Celsus (25 bce–50 bce) distinguished between symptomatic and idiopathic headaches. 22 He emphasized that migraine was a nonfatal chronic disorder triggered by various factors. 20 , 25

Aretaeus of Cappadocia, who lived in the first half of the second century ce, distinguished among different types of headache. 25 He referred to a short‐lasting, mild headache as “cephalalgia” and to a more severe, prolonged headache as “cephalea.” The third type he described, “heterocrania,” was characterized as unilateral, nonbenign, and associated with dizziness, nausea, and vomiting. 20 His observation that avoiding light alleviated symptoms suggests that Aretaeus was describing migraine; however, he also noted that if certain accompanying symptoms worsened, the disease could become fatal. 22 This view likely contributed to confusion between fatal headaches and migraine. Aretaeus is also known to have provided the first documented description of a deadly headache. 25 Nevertheless, Aretaeus of Cappadocia is often credited with providing the first description that closely resembles the modern definition of migraine 26 , 27 —although our findings suggest Mesopotamian texts may predate this by over a millennium.

Galen of Pergamon (129–c. 216 CE) continued Aretaeus's classification of headache types into cephalea, cephalalgia, and heterocrania. 22 He introduced the term hemicrania to describe pain restricted to one‐half of the head, a concept later revisited by Van Opdorp in the 18th century. 22 , 24 , 25 In keeping with the Hippocratic tradition, Galen attributed the etiology of hemicrania to the accumulation of yellow bile. 2

The Latin term hemicranium later evolved into emigranea, migranea, and eventually migrana. The modern term “migraine” has been used in English—adapted from the French migraine—since 1777. 23 , 26 Avicenna (980–1037 CE) described a migraine‐like disorder known as shaghighe. 27 He characterized shaghighe as a unilateral headache with temporal throbbing and a recurrent pattern. He also classified migraine into “hot” and “cold” types and recommended massage, steam, and bathing as symptomatic treatments. 27

In this study, we examined texts from the earliest written era, specifically those inscribed on clay tablets. When tracing the earliest indications of migraine, we observed that no explicit historical diagnosis of migraine exists in Egyptian sources. Our findings suggest that the earliest written descriptions of migraine originate in the Mesopotamian texts analyzed in this study. Thus, the historical trajectory of migraine—from early symptom descriptions in Mesopotamian texts to the rational medicine of Hippocrates and ultimately to modern IHS classifications—reflects a long developmental continuum.

The large number of descriptions and studies related to migraine have resulted in diagnostic confusion throughout history. To resolve this confusion and enable appropriate treatment, the IHS published its first official classification, the ICHD‐1 28 in 1988. The most recent version, published in 2018, established diagnostic criteria for migraine with and without aura. 13

The ancient Mesopotamian cuneiform texts used in this study were inscribed on clay tablets that have undergone millennia of physical deterioration, and their linguistic structure is considerably more limited than that of modern languages. Even in literary texts, sentence construction is simple and constrained. These factors represent important methodological limitations, and the texts’ restricted expressive range naturally limits the extent to which strict alignment with ICHD‐3 criteria can be assessed. For this reason, we adopted a flexible rather than a rigid interpretive approach when evaluating criteria B, C, and D, while remaining attentive to the material's historical and cultural context. In attempting to trace elements of modern rational medicine in ancient sources, we were careful to avoid overly definitive interpretations and to consider the conceptual world and linguistic constraints of the period.

Despite the inherent challenges of examining ancient materials, this study investigated the presence of diagnostic features corresponding to the modern ICHD‐3 criteria for migraine without aura. Consistent with the general characteristics of Mesopotamian medical texts, metaphysical concepts such as ghosts and evil spirits are frequently encountered; however, because this study is anchored in ICHD‐3 criteria, such metaphysical attributions were deliberately excluded from the analytical framework. In Mesopotamian medical thought, metaphysical concepts were frequently invoked to explain diseases of unknown origin. 10 The phrase “ghost's hand” found in our texts reflects this cultural tradition. Rather than denoting a specific disease associated with a particular ghost, this expression appears to represent a cultural framework for describing a clinical condition perceived as complex and qualitatively different from an ordinary headache. Thus, the phrase suggests that those affected recognized their experience as a constellation of symptoms extending beyond a simple complaint. For this reason, we interpreted “ghost's hand” as a conceptual descriptor reflecting an awareness of the headache's severity and multidimensionality. Although this interpretation cannot be directly aligned with ICHD‐3 criteria, approaching the texts in their cultural and medical‐religious context allows us to situate ancient understandings of headache within a modern analytical perspective.

No information regarding sex was found in the examined texts. This suggests that, with the exception of gynecological contexts, Mesopotamian medical texts generally did not specify sex and were not organized along sex lines. For this reason, the well‐known female predominance of migraine cannot be inferred from these sources.

However, across all texts, criterion C was found to be a consistent standard for the location and characteristics of headache. In clinical practice, although diagnostic criteria are considered when evaluating a patient with a headache, the location and quality of the pain remain central to establishing a diagnosis. In a 2015 taxometric analysis of migraine pain characteristics, the most reported features were a pulsating quality and unilateral localization. 29 The predominance of these same features in ancient texts suggests that the descriptions we observed may represent some of the earliest documented attempts to characterize migraine.

Pain localized to the temple—appearing in over 70% of the texts—emerges as a central node in the symptom network. This finding mirrors the centrality of unilateral, pulsating temporal pain in modern ICHD‐3 migraine definitions. Although some degree of metaphor and symbolic language is inherent in Akkadian medical texts, the convergence of descriptors such as “head sting,” “striking pain,” and “jabbing pain” suggests consistent phenomenological grounding. These expressions reflect not only location but also character and intensity—anticipating core elements of modern migraine typology. 30

This study demonstrates that a significant subset of second‐millennium bce Mesopotamian medical texts contains headache descriptions that are remarkably consistent with the diagnostic framework for migraine without aura as defined by the ICHD‐3. Of the 37 texts analyzed, four met the complete ICHD‐3 diagnostic combination (B + C + D), whereas 11 additional texts fulfilled the key symptomatic criteria (C + D). When combined with those that partially met these criteria, this number rises to 21. This finding challenges the notion that symptom‐based classification is an entirely modern concept. 10 , 14 Rather than representing scattered or mythological accounts, these clay tablet records suggest an early attempt to systematically describe recurring headache episodes through a recognizable triad of symptom patterns.

Associated symptoms—nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and eye tearing—appeared in over 56% of all analyzed records. This suggests that Mesopotamian healers, although operating within a theological–astrological framework, understood headache as a systemic condition. Their references to “his eyes contain tears,” “eye muscles sting,” “ears ring,” and “ears roar” support the interpretation that they recognized visual and autonomic symptoms, albeit metaphorically framed. Importantly, these descriptors align with the symptoms now used to distinguish migraine from tension‐type headache. 13

In addition, dizziness, one of the accompanying symptoms mentioned in the texts, was observed in approximately 8% of the focal cases. This link between migraine and dizziness is even more evident in epidemiological studies conducted on patients with migraine. Although 30%–50% of patients with migraine describe dizziness, 31 , 32 it has been found that 36% of these patients may meet the clinical criteria for migraine. 33 Although direct rate comparisons are not possible, the persistence of this association from ancient writings to the present day is remarkable. Our findings support the hypothesis of a long‐recognized comorbidity between migraine and cochleovestibular disorders. The other symptoms that accompany migraine will be evaluated in detail and addressed in a subsequent paper.

Although the parallels with modern diagnostic frameworks are compelling, the risk of anachronistic retrodiagnosis remains. Cuneiform texts are rooted in metaphysical and spiritual interpretations of illness, and our modern analytic lens might oversimplify or distort their intent. 10 , 34 Furthermore, the extent to which Akkadian scribes intended these records to reflect empirical observations versus ritualized language cannot be fully determined. Nonetheless, the internal consistency of symptom clusters and their recurrence across distinct tablets suggests that these descriptions were neither random nor purely symbolic.

The results support the proposition that migraine has been a consistent phenomenological entity for thousands of years. Although the taxonomic sophistication of the ICHD‐3 is unprecedented in history, its core components—unilateral pulsating pain, duration, and associated symptoms—appear to resonate with ancient narrative medicine. Thus, the cuneiform texts may reflect not merely “prescientific” observations but perhaps represent the earliest scaffolding of a clinical syndrome that persists in modern neurology.

CONCLUSION

This analysis suggests that Mesopotamian cuneiform medical texts from the second millennium bce contain rich, structured symptom descriptions that are consistent with ICHD‐3 migraine criteria. These findings contribute to a growing body of evidence that supports the continuity of core clinical phenomena across history and geography. Migraine, in its essence, appears to be both a biological and a civilizational constant.

The analysis of Mesopotamian cuneiform medical texts reveals structured symptom constellations that align with the core criteria of modern migraine diagnosis. Far from being vague or mystical, these records suggest early medical cognition capable of distinguishing symptom patterns, inferring causality, and articulating episodic disorders. Migraine emerges not only as a neurological condition but as a historical constant—written into clay long before it was codified in classification manuals. As preparations for ICHD‐4 move forward, these ancient messages remind us that the roots of diagnostic reasoning may lie deeper in human history than previously imagined.
