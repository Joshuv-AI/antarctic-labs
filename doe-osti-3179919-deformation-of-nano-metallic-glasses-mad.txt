Introduction
Metallic glasses (MGs) are a class of materials which display high strength, hardness, corrosion,
and wear resistance, that are promising for structural applications ranging from medical implants
to microgears. Despite decades of intensive research, the deformation mechanisms that govern the
mechanical behavior of metallic glasses remain mysterious. It has been challenging to use
conventional metallic glasses to investigate deformation mechanisms because of the difficulty of
tuning key structural parameters that control mechanical behavior. The major scientific innovation
of this project is the use of colloidal synthesis to make nano-metallic glasses (Figure 1). This allows
the formation of highly-out-of-equilibrium microstructures, which cannot be achieved using
conventional metallic glass forming methods. In addition, the nano-metallic glasses exhibit a high
level of plasticity, which is desirable for practical applications, and important to understand at a
mechanistic level to rationally design materials with similar properties. The knowledge gained
from project is critical for developing alloys in which structural disorder is key to achieving
unprecedented mechanical performance (e.g. metallic glasses and high-entropy alloys).

Figure 1. Overview of approach.
The nano-metallic glasses studied in this project have potential as strong, hard and tough coatings,
nanolaminates and structural materials. Superior structural and protective materials are limiting
factors in the design of energy systems from batteries to nuclear reactors, so the proposed work
supports DOE Basic Energy Sciences’ missions in fundamental research that addresses challenges
in energy, environment and national security. In this course of the project, we found that the
colloidal synthesis, sintering and mechanical testing techniques developed in this project are also
applicable to additional structural materials, antiferromagnetic thin films, and battery materials,
which are also used in energy technologies of interest to DOE.

Results

The first phase of this project focused on the development of synthetic methods to fabricate
colloidal metallic glass nanoparticles, and the structural characterization and testing of these
nanoparticles. This includes the fabrication of iron boride nanoparticles, the high-pressure
compression and X-ray diffraction of nickel boride nanoparticles to investigate the size-
dependence of structural and mechanical properties, and the synthesis of amorphous shell-
crystalline core nanoparticles (Figure 2). The magnetic properties of the core-shell nanoparticles
were characterized, and their structural stability was investigated through high pressure studies
that showed that the coatings are highly resistant to crystallization and are strongly bonded to the
crystalline core. Defect-free Al nanocubes were also synthesized. In situ scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) mechanical compression of the nanocubes found a high linear strain hardening
rate of 4.1 GPa and a high strength of 1.1 GPa. Post-compression transmission electron microcopy
(TEM) imaging reveals stable prismatic dislocation loops and the absence of stacking faults. MD
simulations relate the formation of dislocation loops and strain hardening to the surface oxide.
These results indicate that slight modifications to surface and interfacial properties can induce
enormous changes to mechanical properties in high SFE metals. We provided nickel boride
metallic glass nanoparticles to Dr. Changyong Song’s group at Postech, Korea for single-pulse,
time-resolved X-ray free-electron laser coherent diffraction imaging. This resulted in
measurements of nanoscale density maps that indicate structural changes that lead to mechanical
failure.

The second phase of this project focused on forming the nanoparticles into bulk materials. This
involved the scale up of the nanoparticle synthesis. For instance, colloidal synthesis of iron boride

metallic glass nanoparticles was achieved at the gram-scale, which is enough for the fabrication of
several ~8 mm x 0.5 mm sized nano-glass discs. Compaction and low-temperature sintering was
used to form the nanoparticles into bulk nanoglasses with grain sizes of 116 nm, 258 nm and 576
nm with mechanical properties (e.g. hardness, yield strength, and ultimate compressive strength)
that are on par with bulk metallic glasses. Plasticity of up to ~4% was achieved. Similar techniques
were use to form low density foams out of colloidal CoS nanoboxes. Mechanical characterization
of these foams found similar behavior as granular materials. Porous iron foams were formed from
metal by using ultra-fast high temperature sintering (UHS), which involves Joule heating of the
samples through a conductive felt. In UHS, the short sintering time at high temperature preserved
the pores but also formed strong bonds between iron powders. 3-point bending was used to
characterize the flexural modulus and strength of the porous iron samples.

The in-situ nano-mechanical testing methods developed in this project was used in collaborative
work on nanotwinned alloys, metal bi-crystals, antiferromagnetic thin films and battery materials.
In the nanotwinned work, we collaborated with Prof. Andrea Hodge and Prof. Huajian Gao to
compress nanotwinned copper-nickel and Inconel alloys at high pressure (Figure 4). We found de-
twinning within the copper-nickel alloy, but more stability within the Inconel alloy. Simulations
were used to understand how shear stress at grain boundaries can lead to atomic rearrangements
and detwinning. In-situ TEM tension testing was performed at the Center for Integrated
Nanotechnologies (CINT) at Sandia National Laboratory on gold bi-crystals. This allowed us to
understand the influence of grain boundary misorientation angle on plastic deformation.
Nanoindentation was performed on antiferromagnetic thin films to understand their elastic
properties.

Our battery research has focused on materials for lithium metal solid-state batteries. A major
challenge for lithium metal solid-state batteries is the formation of lithium dendrites across the
solid electrolyte during cycling, which leads to short-circuiting and mechanical failure of the cell.
The reason that dendrites form is not fully understood, but evidence shows that dendrites could
initiate either at the surface or within the interior of solid electrolytes such as lithium lanthanum
zirconate (LLZO). We used in-situ indentation and electrochemical testing to demonstrate that Ag-
doped LLZO has dendrites under elevated mechanical loads due to compressive stress effects at
the LLZO surface. We also investigated the effect of biaxial compressive stress on dendrite
initiation and propagation in LLZO. The biaxial compressive stress is applied orthogonal to the
electric field generation, and serves to close cracks that extend from the anode, through the
electrolyte, to the cathode. This allows lithium symmetric cells to be cycled at current densities up
to 100 mA/cm2, for >10,000 cycles, and provides evidence that lithium plating occurs within the
interior of LLZO when surface dendrite initiation is suppressed.
These results have been published in peer reviewed journals, including Acta Materialia, Nano
Letters, and Nature Materials, and presented at conferences such as the TMS, MRS and SES
conferences, and department seminars at Brown University, Northwestern University and MIT,
among others. This project resulted in significant workforce development. Students and postdocs
supported by this project are now assistant professors at FAMU-FSU (Florida State University)

and the University of Waterloo, a staff scientist at Livermore National Laboratory, a postdoc at
Northwestern University, and engineers at Applied Materials, Lam Research, and Keysight.

Conclusions

In summary, this project developed synthetic methods for the fabrication of colloidal amorphous
metallic glass and crystalline nanoparticles. The mechanical properties, structural stability and
functional properties of the resulting nanoparticles were investigated. We found that these colloidal
nanoparticles can have strength beyond that of conventional, bulk-scale metals, and that interfaces
play a large role in governing their properties. Nanoparticles and metal powders were formed into
nanostructured, bulk materials. Bulk nanoglasses were found to have high strength and some
plasticity. Other projects that were completed was the high-pressure compression of nanotwinned
alloys to understand the thermodynamics of detwinning, in-situ TEM mechanical testing of metal
bicrystals to understand plasticity mechanisms at a single grain boundary, and in-situ mechanical
and electrochemical testing of solid electrolytes for use in solid state batteries.
