 Executive Summary

The rapid growth of distributed energy resources (DERs), especially photovoltaic (PV) systems,
has introduced new complexities in maintaining grid reliability, stability, and cost-effective
operation. This project addresses these challenges by developing and demonstrating a scalable
GridOS Distributed Energy Resource Management System (DERMS) that enables secure, real-
time optimization and control of DERs at the distribution feeder level.

Key accomplishments include the ingestion and conversion of ComEd’s Common Information
Model (CIM) version 16—an industry-standard representation of grid assets and topology—and
the implementation of unbalanced three-phase optimal power flow (OPF) with 24-hour, day-ahead
planning. The GridOS DERMS platform was enhanced with core functional modules such as
Forecasting, Optimization, and Gateway dispatch, and was integrated with SCADA systems,
feeder controllers, and grid-interactive sensors to support real-time control. The system supports
critical DER optimization use cases including cost minimization, peak shaving, and conservation
voltage reduction (CVR), all under real-world constraints.

The platform’s scalability and performance were validated through simulations involving up to 10
feeders and 10,000 nodes, demonstrating reliable operation in high-DER-penetration scenarios.
DERMS was deployed in ComEd’s test environment and integrated into a control hardware-in-
the-loop (CHIL) setup for ongoing testing, laying the foundation for future field deployment.

In addition to software development, the project involved the deployment of a 250 kW / 356 kWh
battery energy storage system (BESS) and grid-interactive line sensors to improve telemetry
resolution and system responsiveness. These assets were integrated into the DERMS control
loop to enhance forecasting accuracy and dispatch coordination.

The project delivered measurable outcomes: reduced operational costs through economic
dispatch, decreased peak demand via load shifting, and improved voltage regulation across
feeder lines. These benefits demonstrate the DERMS platform’s potential to enable utilities to
integrate DERs cost-effectively, improve grid efficiency and resilience, and accelerate
decarbonization efforts. Moreover, the project defined a clear roadmap for DERMS deployment
and operational use, providing a replicable model for other utilities seeking to modernize their
distribution systems.

1. Background

As the deployment of photovoltaic (PV) distributed energy resources (DERs) accelerates, electric
utilities and grid operators face mounting challenges in maintaining system reliability, voltage
stability, and operational efficiency [1]. High-penetration PV scenarios introduce complex
dynamics such as reverse power flows, voltage excursions, and protection coordination issues,
particularly in low-voltage distribution networks. These impacts are further compounded by the
intermittent nature of solar generation and the increasing diversity of DER technologies, including
battery energy storage systems (BESS), electric vehicles, and smart inverters [2].

Recent research has explored advanced inverter control strategies—such as Volt/VAR
optimization, dynamic reactive power dispatch, and droop-based coordination—to mitigate
voltage fluctuations and enhance hosting capacity [3]. Parallel efforts in distribution-level optimal
power flow (D-OPF) have demonstrated scalable optimization techniques for real-time DER
dispatch, leveraging linearized power flow models and decentralized algorithms to reduce
computational complexity and improve responsiveness [4]. However, many of these approaches
remain confined to simulation environments or pilot-scale implementations, lacking full integration
with utility-scale operational platforms.

This project directly addresses that gap by developing a comprehensive Distributed Energy
Resource Management System (DERMS). The DERMS platform enables security-constrained
economic dispatch of DERs, co-optimizing network constraints, cost objectives, and host-site
benefits under real-world conditions. It incorporates telemetry, forecasting, and control
capabilities to dynamically manage DER contributions across diverse grid scenarios, including
abnormal switching configurations, contingency events, and market participation.

This project advances a scalable, utility-ready DERMS platform designed to manage high-
penetration PV integration at the distribution level. The system enables coordinated voltage
regulation using smart inverters and battery energy storage systems (BESS) and incorporates
unbalanced three-phase optimal power flow (OPF) with integrated forecasting. Through
simulation and testing (see section 3.2.3 DERMS Use Cases), the platform demonstrated the
ability to maximize the value of PV generation by dynamically adjusting DER dispatch to reduce
operational costs, shift load away from peak periods, and improve voltage stability. For example,
in the summer-low/high-PV scenario, the system successfully dispatched BESS to absorb excess
generation and maintain voltage within acceptable limits, while in winter-peak/low-PV conditions,
it prioritized cost-efficient DER utilization. These use cases illustrate how the DERMS platform
enhances grid reliability, efficiency, and resilience while supporting cost-effective renewable
integration.
Ultimately, this work provides a critical foundation for future grid modernization efforts, enabling
a transition toward more flexible, decarbonized, and customer-centric energy systems. It aligns
with broader industry goals to integrate distributed renewables at scale, reduce carbon emissions,
and empower prosumers through intelligent grid-edge technologies.

2. Project Objectives

The Security Constrained Economic Optimization of PV and Other Distributed Assets project is
intended to demonstrate a comprehensive, integrated solution that co-optimizes aggregated
distributed energy resources (DERs) of all types to maximize host site energy benefits and utility
grid services and features. It also optimizes for physical distribution grid constraints, performance,
needs, and cost reduction, achieving a distribution grid-level security-constrained economic
dispatch of the DERs. The combined optimization maximizes the value of PV output and mitigates
PV integration impacts at least cost. The project aims to demonstrate cost-effective integration of
high penetrations of distributed PV in the power grid, while meeting key objectives of reliability,
resiliency, and affordability. Traditionally, PV generators operate as set-and-forget resources that
export energy to the grid without coordination. This project introduces an innovative approach by
actively managing these PV resources to optimize their output and maintain grid constraints,
thereby enabling greater PV penetration and reducing costs for Utility. The technology is expected
to benefit both Utilities and prospective PV adopters, supporting broader deployment of distributed
solar generation and contributing to a more sustainable, greener future. This project will be
demonstrated at a distribution utility ComEd – hereafter “Utility”.

2.1 Summary of SOPO Tasks
The project is organized into multiple work streams that span budget periods, each focusing on
distinct components of the solution. These work streams are designed to progressively enhance
the integration of distributed energy resources (DERs) and demonstrate their economic
optimization in real-world utility applications.

 2.1.1 Development of Microgrid Optimization Algorithm Solution
This task focuses on simulating and testing a microgrid-level optimization solution that integrates
photovoltaic (PV) systems, batteries, sensors, and other distributed energy resources (DERs).
Smaller network models were used to represent microgrid configurations, enabling the team to
evaluate DER coordination under localized grid conditions. The objective of these simulations
was to optimize the collective performance of microgrid assets—improving grid stability, enabling
grid services such as voltage support and peak shaving, and evaluating the potential for market
participation. Representative DER types and loading conditions were used to simulate real-world
microgrid behavior. Beyond demonstrating acceptable solve times, the optimization engine
successfully achieved its objective functions (e.g., minimizing cost, regulating voltage) in each
scenario, producing viable, optimized control strategies for the modeled microgrids. Initial
algorithm development and requirement definition occurred in BP1, with implementation, testing,
and commissioning through Factory Acceptance Testing (FAT) and Site Acceptance Testing
(SAT) completed in BP2 and BP3.

 2.1.2 Develop Feeder-level Power Flow Optimization
This task aims to develop and validate a feeder-scale Distributed Energy Resource Management
System (DERMS) that interfaces with the Utility’s Distribution Management System (DMS) and
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) system. Larger network models—
incorporating multiple feeders and diverse DERs—were used to represent distribution-scale grid

 Page 8 of 68

segments. These models enabled power flow and optimal power flow (OPF) analyses under
representative loading scenarios, including high DER penetration. In addition to verifying
computational scalability and run-time performance, the DERMS optimization engine met key
objective functions, including cost reduction, peak demand management, and voltage stability.
These outcomes demonstrate the system's ability to generate effective control strategies for
complex feeder environments. Modeling and simulation began in BP1, followed by system
integration and field testing in BP2 and BP3.

 2.1.3 Simulated Scalability Demonstration
This task aims to simulate the scalability of the solution to a larger system with 10,000 active
nodes, multiple microgrids, feeders, and substations, and a high penetration of PV. The
development of new software to enable faster scaling is crucial for commercialization. In BP2, the
scalability model will be developed, and in BP3, it will be used to analyze potential cost-benefits
for high-PV penetration scenarios, supporting the broader deployment of microgrids.

 2.1.4 Integration of Grid Services
This task demonstrates how the solution reduces the cost of deploying and integrating PV in the
utility by optimizing grid services such as voltage optimization (VVO), conservation voltage
reduction (CVR), and other distribution services. The task focuses on enhancing the functionality
of the GridOS system to provide real-time grid operations, including the integration of new assets
like battery storage and microgrids. By improving asset value, the task supports the
commercialization of DERs.

 2.1.5 Develop Enhanced Grid-interactive Sensors
This task involves adding field sensors to collect real-time telemetry, such as frequency
measurements, system inertia compensation, and voltage control. The sensors will communicate
with the DERMS to improve grid stability and optimization capabilities. The sensors will be
deployed at various locations in the Utility’s grid and will play a crucial role in the hardware-in-the-
loop demonstration.

 2.1.6 Deploy Utility Microgrid Energy Storage System
This task involves identifying and deploying a battery-centric microgrid within the host Utility’s
territory. It will be initiated in BP1, setting the stage for further integration and testing of energy
storage systems as part of the overall microgrid solution.

 2.1.7 Data and Software Curation
The curation of project data and software is essential for maintaining organized, accessible, and
secure project information. This task includes collecting, indexing, and protecting digital data and
software modules. Additionally, it includes the development of Open Source, Interoperability, and
Cyber Security Plans, in compliance with DOE’s ENERGISE FOA requirements.

2.2 Project Periods
The project is structured into three distinct budget periods (BP1, BP2, BP3), each focusing on
critical milestones necessary for achieving the overall objectives. These periods are designed to
progressively build upon each other, ensuring a smooth transition from planning and initial
development to deployment, testing, and final reporting.

 2.2.1 Budget Period 1 (BP1)
The first budget period is dedicated to finalizing the Project Management Plan and establishing
baseline conditions at the microgrid site. The initial tasks focus on setting up the feeder-level
optimization system, deploying sensors, and integrating the microgrid. Furthermore, the OO team
will initiate the Stakeholder Engagement program, ensuring the project aligns with utility needs.

 2.2.2 Budget Period 2 (BP2)
In BP2, the focus shifts to field testing and the physical deployment of microgrid systems. OO will
integrate and conduct performance testing for the microgrid’s battery system, along with the
security-constrained economic optimization of the grid. The scalability demonstration will also be
initiated, and further development of grid services will take place.

 2.2.3 Budget Period 3 (BP3)
The third period will involve the operation of hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) systems, as well as
extensive testing of scalability simulations. OO will refine the grid services and further optimize
DERMS. Additionally, letters of interest from additional stakeholders will be secured, and a
comprehensive Final Report will be prepared detailing the results from the post-deployment
analysis.

3. Project Results and Discussion
3.1 High Level Summary of Project Results
The project successfully demonstrated the capabilities of the DERMS Simulation platform and the
GridOS Optimization Engine (OE) in optimizing DERs for grid applications. Key milestones
include the integration of 10 feeders and 3 substations, achieving scalable simulations with time-
series power flow for 100 DERs across 10 feeders, and successful cost optimization (OPF) that
reduced operational costs by optimizing DER dispatch. Peak shaving was effectively implemented,
reducing peak demand by shifting load to off-peak times, and Conservation Voltage Reduction
(CVR) optimization improved voltage stability and reduced active power consumption. The OE
showed a 50% improvement in solve times compared to prior versions, validating its efficiency.
The project met the objectives outlined in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO),
showcasing the OE’s ability to optimize cost, manage peak load, and improve voltage regulation,
all while maintaining scalability for larger network models.

3.2 DERMS Solution Development
This section describes accomplishments aligned with the high-level task categories outlined in
Sections 2.1.1 through 2.1.4.

 3.2.1 DERMS Architecture

This section covers the updated design of the GridOS DERMS architecture. With organizational
and roadmap changes to Opus One due to the acquisition by General Electric, the DERMS
software is comprised of a suite of modules: Forecasting, Optimization, Gateway and eTerra
SCADA. These modules provide the following functionality:

 • Forecasting provides a platform for network and DER measurement data to be received
 and passed to a forecast algorithm for near-future predictions
 • Optimization performs the optimal power flow (OPF) algorithms using the network load
 and generation data provided from Forecasting
 • Gateway sends dispatch signals externally. It will receive the OPF results from
 Optimization
 • eTerra SCADA will convert the dispatch signals to DNP3 protocol to send externally to the
 DER controller (RTAC). It will also receive measurements via DNP3 from the DER and
 pass it back to Forecasting.

 Figure 1 DERMS Architecture Overview

                                         Page 14 of 68

As part of this project, the DERMS architecture was enhanced to support utility-grade deployment
and real-time integration with ComEd systems. Key contributions include: the implementation of
a modular control loop across Forecasting, Optimization, and Gateway modules; integration with
eTerra SCADA to enable DNP3 protocol dispatch; and adaptation of internal APIs and data flows
to support 24-hour interval OPF and time-series power flow simulations. The architecture was
also validated for scalability across large feeder models, and configured for deployment in test
and CHIL environments, providing a foundation for future field implementations.

 3.2.2 DERMS Functionalities
DERMS is a modern microservices-based platform solution that provides grid wide end to end
Front of Meter (FTM) and Behind the Meter (BTM) DER orchestration and management and
addresses connections, visibility, operations, monitoring, and control of DERs, along with the
ability to optimize and dispatch these to maximize their value in the energy market.
The DERMS is a modular platform that is designed to address a wide range of DER management
use cases, from interconnection and simulation to grid operations and markets. The platform is
built on Kubernetes, a modern container orchestration solution, and GE’s Digital Energy
Foundation, a set of secure, curates and production-tested common services. The DERMS
platform includes eight integrated business modules that are presented to users using GE’s Digital
Energy Experience (DEX) to provide a common look-and-feel with a modern HTML5 web browser
client. A description of these eight integrated business modules is shown in Figure 2 below.

 Figure 2 DERMS Modular Capabilities

For this ENERGISE project which focuses on optimization, the modules deployed include
Forecasting, Gateway and DER Optimization along with GE’s SCADA to facilitate DNP3
communication. The data will transit through GE’s SCADA server via DNP3 and translated via
internal APIs and adaptors into DERMS server. The measurement data will be used to generate

forecasts, which are passed to DER Optimization which will generate the hourly 24-hr battery
dispatch schedule to the DER Gateway which will send it to SCADA for output to RTUs.

 3.2.3 DERMS Use Cases

This subsection presents one of the core demonstrations of the project: the evaluation of three
key DERMS optimization functions—Cost Optimization, Peak Shaving, and Conservation Voltage
Reduction (CVR)—using time-series simulations. Each function was designed to coordinate the
dispatch of DERs to improve system performance under real-world constraints, supporting the
project’s objectives of cost-effective PV integration, improved grid reliability, and operational
efficiency.

The performance of these functions was benchmarked against a baseline scenario in which DERs
operated passively without optimization. Subsequent optimization runs demonstrated how
DERMS dynamically adjusted DER dispatches to minimize costs, reduce peak demand, and
stabilize voltage, while maximizing the value of distributed PV and mitigating integration
challenges. A detailed description of the test feeder, DER configuration, and baseline scenario
setup is provided in Section 3.2.3.1.

These use cases also demonstrate how coordinated dispatch of DERs can deliver utility-
grade grid services such as peak load reduction, energy cost minimization, and improved
voltage regulation—traditionally achieved using centralized infrastructure. By leveraging
local DER flexibility, the DERMS platform supports a distributed, data-driven approach to
grid optimization. Across all use cases, PV plays a pivotal role not only as a renewable
energy source but also as a contributor to both challenges (e.g., midday voltage rise,
surplus generation) and opportunities (e.g., free midday energy, grid support through
BESS coordination). DERMS helps unlock this potential by enabling smarter, real-time
utilization of solar assets in a way that aligns with operational and economic priorities.

  3.2.3.1 Baseline Scenarios
To evaluate the effectiveness of the DERMS optimization strategies, a 24-hour baseline scenario
was established to represent unoptimized grid operation. In the baseline, DERs operate in default
“set-and-forget” mode: the PV system exports based on solar availability without coordination,
and the BESS remains inactive. This scenario serves as a control for quantifying the incremental
benefits of DERMS-enabled optimization.
The simulation was conducted on ComEd's host feeder, which includes over 1,000 nodes and
was modeled in CIM format for integration into the GridOS DERMS Simulation platform. Two
DERs were placed on the feeder:
 • A 250 kW / 356 kWh BESS, uniquely identified by its model resource identifier (mRID)
 hostFeeder_F1, subject to typical state-of-charge and charge/discharge constraints.
 • A 144 kW PV unit, with mRID: hostFeeder_F1_PV1, sized to reflect rooftop or small
 commercial solar installations and modeled using historical irradiance and production
 profiles.

                                          Page 16 of 68

In Illinois, winter-peak periods typically occur during early mornings and evenings due to heating
and lighting demand, while summer-peak demand is driven by cooling loads during hot
afternoons. Conversely, winter-low and summer-low conditions represent off-peak periods with
reduced load, allowing for higher PV utilization and fewer grid constraints.
Four baseline scenarios were created using historical ComEd data to reflect a range of seasonal
load and generation conditions:
 • Winter-low with high PV
 • Winter-peak with low PV
 • Summer-low with high PV
 • Summer-peak with low PV
For each of these scenarios, the DERMS platform performed a full 24-hour unbalanced three-
phase power flow simulation at hourly resolution (see Figure 3 as an example), recording system
metrics such as nodal voltages, substation power draw, and energy costs. These results provided
the reference point for evaluating the improvements made by cost optimization, peak shaving,
and CVR strategies.

 Figure 3 Summer-low with High PV Scenario

The baseline also revealed integration challenges associated with high PV output, including
voltage rise along the feeder and periods of underutilized solar energy due to lack of load or
storage dispatch. These conditions served as a critical benchmark for demonstrating how DERMS
enhances PV utilization, stabilizes grid performance, and provides measurable operational value
to the utility and its customers.

  3.2.3.2 Cost Optimization
The Cost Optimization strategy aims to minimize the total operational cost of supplying energy
over a 24-hour period, while adhering to grid constraints such as voltage limits and current ratings.
The DERMS Optimization Engine performs this function by leveraging time-varying price
schedules for both active and reactive energy, calculating dispatch schedules that reduce total
cost while maintaining system feasibility.

                                           Page 17 of 68

This function was tested on the ComEd’s host feeder with two DERs: a 250 kW / 356 kWh battery
energy storage system (BESS) and a PV unit sized according to real ComEd production data.
The optimization engine coordinated these assets to shift energy consumption and supply to
periods of lowest marginal cost. In high-PV scenarios, DERMS prioritized charging the BESS
during solar production peaks and discharging during higher-priced evening hours—thereby
improving PV utilization and reducing net grid purchases.
Figure 4 shows the BESS charging and discharging schedule under the optimization regime. The
battery charges during low-price (and high PV generation) hours and discharges strategically
during cost peaks.

optimized case. The optimized dispatch flattened the cost curve and eliminated high-cost peaks,
resulting in a smoother, lower-cost operational profile.

  3.2.3.3 Peak Shaving Optimization
Peak Shaving aims to reduce the highest power demand during peak periods by managing
controllable assets like batteries. The DER Optimization module identifies the time points with the
highest active power demand and adjusts asset behavior to flatten these peaks. For example,
batteries can be charged during off-peak periods and discharged during peak demand periods to
reduce the strain on the transmission system. The objective function for peak shaving uses a
quadratic approach to minimize the peak demand (P_slack_peak), while simultaneously reducing

                                            Page 19 of 68

the average demand (P_slack_average) for improved efficiency. This control strategy helps in
reducing peak demand charges and balancing power distribution.

  3.2.3.4 Voltage Regulation (CVR)
The Conservation Voltage Reduction (CVR) strategy optimizes voltage stability by reducing active
power consumption across the network. The objective of CVR optimization is to reduce voltage
levels to the point where load reduction outweighs the additional system losses caused by the
reduced voltage. The optimization engine calculates the dispatch schedules that minimize the
active power drawn from the slack bus, ensuring voltage stability while maintaining efficient power
distribution. The strategy helps in reducing energy consumption without compromising service
quality.

Conversely, when the capacitor output increases, the BESS charges to maintain voltage stability.

 3.3.8 Testing and Demonstration of the DERMS in CHIL Test Environment

Prior to deploying the DERMS on Exelon’s central server system, its functionalities are thoroughly
tested and demonstrated using control hardware in the loop (CHIL) setup at ComEd’s Grid
Integration and Technology (GrIT) lab. This setup leverages a real time digital simulator (RTDS)
that simulates the dynamics of the host feeder, the DERs such as solar PV system and BESS,
line sensors, reclosers, and associated communication protocols.

The CHIL testing of the DERMS offers several key benefits:

 • It provides a secure, efficient, and economic environment for testing and validating new
 technologies such as DERMS. This process helps to validate functionalities of the
 proposed solution and identify potential software variances early, reducing the risk of
 issues being transferred to the production environment.
 • The CHIL setup accurately mimics the dynamics of the real feeder and DERs, allowing
 the DERMS solution to respond as if it were operating with live field data. This enables
 testing under edge-case scenarios that may be difficult to replicate in the field.
 • The setup serves as a valuable environment for training the DERMS operators and
 facilitating knowledge transfer.
 • The setup also provides a controlled environment for testing supporting components. For
 example, the same setup has been leveraged for testing the RTAC settings that were
 developed for BESS and PV sites.

  3.3.8.1 RTDS-CHIL Test Setup

 Figure 37 RTDS-CHIL test setup at ComEd's GrIT laboratory

                                          Page 47 of 68

The ComEd team developed the CHIL test setup illustrated in Figure 37. The model of the host
feeder and DERs were developed and implemented in RTDS. The PV system is modeled as a
current source and its output current is adjusted dynamically based on solar irradiance profile.
The BESS is implemented as a voltage source and its output voltage is calculated based on the
active and reactive power setpoints received from the DERMS.

A physical server in the lab network hosts the DERMS solution, which communicates with the
RTDS to send/receive real-time data. An RTAC interface between the RTDS and the DERMS.
This RTAC serves 3 key functions:

 • Receives PV data via MODBUS, BESS, line sensor, and recloser data via DNP from
 RTDS.
 • Organizes the incoming data and parses into three remote terminal units (RTUs): PV RTU,
 BESS RTU, and PI RTU.
 • Serves as a protocol converter enabling DERMS to interpret the data as if it were coming
 from actual field.

This architecture mirrors the field deployment, ensuring that the DERMS receives data in a format
consistent with its live operation. As a result, minimal configuration changes will be required when
rolling out the DERMS from lab environment to the production environment.

  3.3.9.1 Quality Assurance and Staging (QAS) Environment
The DERMS in Exelon Production system has access to real-time data from the field devices and

the production environment in terms of server configuration and system architecture. Moreover,
the QAS has connectivity to the PI Historian servers and SCADA/ADMS system enabling realistic
integration testing. By identifying and resolving variances during this stage, the risk of transferring
issues into the production environment is significantly reduced.
Figure 57 illustrates the QAS test setup. The PV and BESS DNP points are modeled within
Triangular Microworks (TMW) plat form that support SCADA communication protocols. The
DERMS solution communicates with the simulated RTUs to send/receive real-time data. The
DERMS also retrieves the status and measurements from the reclosers and line sensors from PI
Historian via a Web API. Unlike the RTDS-CHIL setup, the TMW does not simulate the power
system dynamics. Therefore, the QAS environment is primarily used for 1) comprehensive testing
and verification of DERM integration with the ADMS and PI Historian, 2) IT security failover tests,
and 3) basic functional validation by injecting operating points into the RTU simulator.

4. Significant Accomplishment and Conclusions

The deployment of GridOS technology in this project served as a foundational proof of concept
that delivered key technical and product development outcomes. The project successfully
integrated real-world utility data, SCADA systems and DER assets into a unified optimization
framework, validating critical workflows such as CIM-based model ingestion, DER scheduling,
and SCADA-interfaced control. Through extensive testing across feeder and substation scales,
the project demonstrated the effectiveness of the DERMS platform in supporting cost
optimization, voltage regulation (CVR), and DER dispatch coordination under realistic
operational constraints. While the project encountered several execution challenges, the team
gained critical lessons on deployment planning, stakeholder alignment, and cross-functional
coordination. These learnings are essential for informing future GridOS DERMS deployments and
improving project management strategies under evolving technical and organizational
environments.
This initiative significantly enhanced GE Vernova’s GridOS DERMS platform. The feedback and
results from this deployment informed multiple roadmap decisions and resulted in tangible
improvements to performance, modularity, and interoperability. For example, the Optimization
Engine (OE) was enhanced across multiple versions to improve scalability and solve time, while
maintaining support for unbalanced three-phase OPF and flexible objective functions. The project
also validated integration workflows with SCADA systems and feeder-level assets, helping to
shape a more robust architecture that meets utility-grade performance and security standards.
Beyond technical validation, this project also deepened the understanding of evolving utility
operational needs, directly influencing new product features in subsequent GridOS DERMS
releases. For example, through close collaboration with ComEd, the team identified the need for
higher-resolution forecasting capabilities to support dynamic grid operations. As a result, the
forecasting module was enhanced from a fixed 24-hour interval to configurable 1-hour, 30-minute,
and 15-minute intervals, enabling utilities to perform more granular, near-real-time prediction and
control. This improvement not only enhances DER scheduling accuracy but also aligns the
product more closely with diverse utility requirements across different grid conditions and
operational strategies.
As a part of this project, ComEd successfully deployed line sensors and a BESS system, with
DERMS deployment and PV system integration currently in progress. The line sensors have
improved situational awareness by feeding real-time measurements to SCADA/ADMS, while also
enabling faster fault detection and restoration. The BESS, a dispatchable asset connected to the
host feeder, demonstrated capabilities for peak shaving, load shifting, and resilience
enhancement. Once the PV system is fully integrated with DERMS, it will provide additional
visibility and control over distributed generation, further enhancing system observability and
reliability. Collectively, these deployments provide a scalable and modular foundation for future
grid modernization and DER integration across ComEd’s network.
Key Challenges and Lessons Learned
 • The initial start of the project was conceptualized pre-Covid and was implemented during
 the beginning stages of the lockdown. This caused many disruptions in the start of the
 project that delayed beginning activities such as utility company onboarding to the project.
 • Opus One was acquired by GE midway through the project. This posed challenges to the
 overall solution as Opus One’s DERMS solution was integrated within the wider GE
 platform. This caused many delays as the forward development of the individuals modules
                                          Page 62 of 68

 needed to be integrated into the wider platform. Though making the overall solution more
 robust and with enhanced stability and features, overall integrations were complex and
 required significant review for compatibility.
• Deployment of new technologies in the utility sector requires close coordination with
 multiple stakeholders, including legal, supply, and IT/OT security architects—to review the
 system architecture and data sharing protocols. This process is critical to ensuring
 compliance with safety and cybersecurity standards. Once the architecture is approved,
 any subsequent changes trigger a re-engagement process, which can impact on the
 project timeline. For example, during this project, the replacement of Oracle’s legacy
 system with a SQL-based solution triggered an architecture re-review. Additionally, the
 GE team required elevated server privileges during DERMS deployment, and securing
 such access within the Exelon network involved multiple layers of approvals. To mitigate
 these challenges in future projects, it is essential to identify all impacted stakeholders early
 in the planning phase, clearly define IT security and software/hardware requirements,
 breakdown tasks into manageable milestones with assigned ownership. This proactive
 approach will help ensure early alignment, reduce cascading delays, and streamline the
 deployment of advanced technologies like DERMS across utility operations.
• The development, testing, deployment, and long-term ownership of advanced
 technologies such as DERMS and BESS require resources with advanced skillset. High
 turnover can disrupt the project continuity. Moreover, it is critical to identify and secure
 resources early in the project and to establish robust change management and knowledge
 transition plans.
• DERMS and BESS are relatively new technologies in the power industry. Their evolving
 nature introduces uncertainty in project planning and execution.
• Moving forward, Opus One/GE has implemented a more streamlined approach to
 generating requirements to help monitor and facilitate GridOS deployments. A shift toward
 more stable, non-static releases will also enable better long-term support. For complex
 programs with multiple concurrent tracks, task scoping and resource allocation should be
 detailed at the milestone level. In future efforts, more granular task breakdowns, clearly
 assigned owners, and structured cross-time-zone sync points will be critical to managing
 deliverables and adapting to uncertainty. These improvements aim to enhance execution
 efficiency and reduce project risk in future engagements.

5. Path Forward

Building on the achievements of this collaborative project, GridOS DERMS (Opus One/GE) will
continue working to advance the solution from a proven pilot to a fully production-ready, scalable
platform. Future research and development (R&D) efforts will focus on enhancing system
scalability, improving computational performance, and expanding support for multi-substation and
multi-slack bus configurations. Additionally, there will be continued refinement of optimization
algorithms, including dynamic cost optimization, adaptive peak shaving, and advanced CVR
strategies, to ensure the solution can flexibly adapt to evolving grid needs and market conditions.

Both parties have recognized the challenges of deploying the solution across different customer
environments. Moving forward, a key priority will be to optimize deployment processes, develop
clearer and more detailed instructions, and increase efficiency across all teams. Once full end-to-
end testing is completed, ComEd and GridOS DERMS may plan to work together to define
additional use cases and test scenarios to validate the solution’s resilience under a wider variety
of grid conditions. This may also include algorithm modifications to better tailor the platform to
specific customer requirements and operational contexts.

From a commercialization perspective, there is significant potential to transfer this technology to
other utility partners and markets seeking to manage high DER penetration while improving grid
stability and operational efficiency. Joint efforts in engaging other utilities, regulatory bodies, and
industry stakeholders will help refine the value proposition and support broader adoption.

Remaining technical risks include integration with diverse legacy systems, ensuring cybersecurity
within increasingly interconnected infrastructures, and addressing evolving regulatory
requirements. By continuing their strong collaboration, ComEd and GridOS DERMS are well
positioned to overcome these challenges through ongoing R&D, targeted demonstrations, and a
shared focus on operational readiness. Together, they will enable the GridOS DERMS platform
to support modern, resilient, and sustainable distribution networks that maximize the value of
DERs and support long-term decarbonization goals.
