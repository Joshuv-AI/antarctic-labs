Toward a Scientific Discovery Engine for Weather and Climate Data: A Visual Analytics Workbench for Embedding-Based Exploration
Nihanth W. Cherukuru, Matt Rehme, Kirsten J. Mayer, David John Gagne, John Schreck, John Clyne, Charlie Becker

Abstract
Earth system science is producing increasingly large, high-dimensional datasets from physics based Earth system models to AI-based weather and climate models. Embedding-based representations can make these data searchable through similarity search and analog retrieval, but nearest neighbors in latent space are not automatically scientifically meaningful: it may reflect real weather structure, or preprocessing, geography, or model bias. Researchers therefore need ways to inspect how embeddings organize meteorological data, compare representation models, develop retrieval strategies, and verify results against physical evidence. We present an open-source visual analytics workbench for each of these steps. The system links embedding experiments to source data, metadata, spatial context, and model configurations, so latent-space results can be traced back to the physics. Users can explore latent spaces for different models, issue global or localized queries, and inspect analogs through familiar meteorological views. This enables a discovery workflow in which scientists characterize a phenomenon of interest in a well-understood dataset, identifying its signature in latent space, and then use that signature to probe larger, less-labeled archives or ensembles for similar events. We demonstrate the workbench through tropical-cyclone retrieval using ERA5-derived embeddings and IBTrACS metadata, and evaluate its out-of-core retrieval backend to show that large embedding collections can be searched beyond in-memory limits on commodity workstation hardware.

Introduction

keywords
Embeddings, Latent Space Visualization, Ensembles, Meteorology, Weather, Climate

Numerical weather prediction and Earth system models already produce petabyte-scale, multivariate, spatiotemporal datasets. AI-based weather and climate models compound this: they enable far larger ensembles and cheaper experimentation, opening the door to broader exploration of rare and extreme events. As dataset size grows and the field moves toward combining machine-learning methods with physics-based simulation, the bottleneck increasingly shifts from technical feasibility of producing forecasts to searching, interpreting, and validating the resulting data.

Embedding-based representations offer a promising way to make these datasets searchable
[
3
]
. By mapping complex atmospheric states, spatial regions, or model outputs into vector spaces, embeddings support similarity search, analog retrieval, clustering, and discovery of recurring patterns. This is attractive for researchers in Earth systems science, where there is often a need to find structurally related events across large archives or ensembles. However, embedding-based search functionality might not be inherently scientifically meaningful. A nearest neighbor in latent space may reflect a physically relevant weather structure. It may also reflect artifacts of preprocessing, geography, seasonality, model bias, or the particular representation used to generate the embeddings. This raises a trustworthiness challenge for scientific search: researchers need to determine whether retrieved neighbors are meaningful enough to support scientific reasoning, hypothesis generation, or downstream analysis. Beyond a search interface, users must be able to inspect how embedding spaces are organized, compare alternative representations, formulate retrieval strategies, and verify results against physical meteorological evidence. In practice, these steps are difficult to combine. Embedding generation, dimensionality reduction, vector search, model auditing, geospatial inspection, and conventional meteorological visualization are often handled by separate scripts or tools. This fragmentation makes it hard to determine which embedding models and query strategies are appropriate for a given scientific task.

We present an open-source visual analytics workbench for developing and auditing embedding-based search workflows for weather and climate data. The system links each embedding experiment to shared source data, metadata, spatial context, model configuration, and retrieval parameters, allowing users to inspect latent-space organization and verify retrieved neighbors against meteorological evidence. This paper makes three contributions: (a)
A provenance-aware scientific retrieval

Interactive retrieval-strategy design for meteorological analog search
. The system supports image-level, localized patch-level, metadata-constrained, spatially constrained, and global-to-local retrieval, enabling users to test whether nearest neighbors reflect meaningful atmospheric structures rather than representation artifacts. (c)
A tropical-cyclone case study with scalable retrieval evaluation
. We demonstrate the workflow using ECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) derived vision transformer (ViT) embeddings and International Best Track Archive for Climate Stewardship (IBTrACS)

Background

1.1
Big Data Challenges in Earth System Modeling

Navigating petabyte-scale climate data was identified as a major challenge more than a decade ago
[
14
]
and remains a research priority
[
20
,
23
]
. Recent advances in AI-based Earth system modeling further intensify this challenge. AI weather prediction models now outperform operational numerical weather prediction systems on several metrics while requiring substantially fewer computational resources
[
2
]
. This also enables much larger ensembles
[
11
]
, which are important for uncertainty quantification, rare-event analysis, and characterizing variability in chaotic atmospheric systems. As Earth system models increasingly combine machine learning with physical modeling
[
4
]
, analysis workflows must support not only access to high-volume outputs, but also their interpretation, validation, and search.

Substantial work has addressed the technical challenge of accessing and visualizing large Earth science datasets. Multi-resolution progressive access
[
16
]
, cloud-native chunking strategies
[
1
]
, and scientific visualization tools remain essential for moving from raw data to verifiable visual evidence. However, access and rendering alone do not solve the problem of deciding what to inspect. Visual analytics systems address this gap by combining automated analysis with interactive exploration. Keim et al.
[
8
]
argue for an “analyze first” workflow in which systems identify potentially important structure before users visually inspect details. Our work follows this tradition but focuses on embedding-based search as the automated analysis layer for weather and climate data, while preserving links to meteorological evidence for interpretation and validation.

1.2
Embeddings, Search, and Latent Space Exploration Tools

Representation learning is increasingly used to identify structure in Earth science data
[
10
]
. Climate research has a long history of vector-space representations, including empirical orthogonal functions based on principal component analysis
[
13
]
; modern embeddings extend this idea using nonlinear architectures such as autoencoders, contrastive models, and foundation models. Recent examples include autoencoder-based ERA5 representations
[
24
]
, AlphaEarth Foundations and its embedding dataset for Earth observation
[
3
]
, analyses of Aurora model embeddings
[
19
]
, and mechanistic interpretation tools for weather and climate model latent spaces
[
22
]
.

Embedding-based search complements supervised event detection methods such as ClimateNet
[
17
]
by enabling users to query for similar atmospheric states or localized structures without relying only on predefined labels. This is important for discovery tasks involving rare events, hidden precursors, or “unknown unknowns,” where unsupervised and self-supervised methods can reduce dependence on expert labels
[
12
]
. Several recent systems address adjacent pieces of this problem. Embedding Atlas
[
18
]
provides a low-friction, general-purpose interface for inspecting large embedding projections through scalable rendering, density-based clustering, and metadata cross-filtering, with the projection itself as the primary analysis surface. ClimateSOM
[
7
]
targets climate ensembles, using a self-organizing map and LLM-assisted sensemaking to compare variability across ensemble runs by laying members onto a learned 2D grid.

Our workbench is designed for a different approach: embedding inspection and similarity retrieval are coupled stages of a single workflow. The system supports multiple coexisting embedding experiments over the same source data, with provenance back to the underlying meteorology, and the retrieval backend is built for out-of-core search over patch-level indexes that exceed workstation memory. The pace of representation learning also matters for system design. Different models, preprocessing choices, spatial scales, and similarity definitions yield different vector spaces and different retrieval behavior, and the right representation for a given scientific question is rarely known in advance. We therefore frame embedding-based retrieval as a visual analytics problem in which model comparison, search, interpretation, and scalable access are coupled requirements, and we organize the system around an embedding experiment abstraction that lets multiple representations of the same source data coexist and be evaluated against each other.

2
Design Goals

Embedding-based search is promising for exploring large weather and climate datasets, but it is difficult to use scientifically unless users can understand what is being searched, how similarity is defined and whether retrieved results correspond to meaningful physical phenomena. Drawing on prior work and conversations with domain scientists, we designed the workbench around three goals:

G1: Support provenance-aware embedding experiments.

Each representation is treated as an experiment tied to a shared source dataset, capturing model configuration, preprocessing, spatial sampling, timestamps, metadata, and source imagery. This lets researchers load and compare representations from different architectures; vision models, autoencoders, domain foundation models, latent spaces from AI weather emulators; while tracing any latent-space result back to the data and modeling choices that produced it.

G2: Support interactive, domain-grounded retrieval design.

What counts as meteorological similarity depends on spatial scale, metadata, geographic context, and the question being asked. Users should therefore be able to build and revise retrieval strategies; global-state, localized patch-level, metadata-constrained, spatially constrained, and staged global-to-local; and inspect results through familiar domain views: maps, timestamps, metadata, source imagery, derived fields, and highlighted matching regions. Together these let users judge whether nearest neighbors reflect real atmospheric structure.

G3: Support scalable out-of-core retrieval.

Embedding workflows can produce many vectors per timestep, ensemble member, variable, or region, quickly exceeding workstation memory. The system must support interactive retrieval at this scale without requiring all vectors in RAM or persistent access to high-memory HPC environments. This matters most during experimentation, when several embedding collections may coexist for the same dataset.

3
System Design And Workflow

The workbench is a reactive visual analytics application for exploring embedding-based search over weather and climate data. Its central abstraction is an embedding experiment: a set of vectors generated from a shared source reference using a particular model, preprocessing pipeline, spatial sampling scheme, and configuration (G1,
\cref
fig:teaser(a)). This abstraction keeps the system model-agnostic. In the case study, we instantiate this design using image-level and patch-level embeddings from vision foundation models, but the same structure can represent embeddings from other architectures as long as they can be linked back to source data and metadata.

The system separates source data from embedding experiments while preserving links between them (G2,
\cref
fig:teaser(b)). Source tables store the original or derived meteorological data, timestamps, spatial extent, and metadata. Experiment tables store model configuration, embedding vectors, spatial indexing information, and vector-search indexes. This separation allows multiple models or preprocessing choices to be evaluated over the same source dataset without duplicating the underlying data, while preserving the links needed to trace retrieval results from latent space back to their physical origin.

Users first load an experiment and inspect its associated source metadata, model configuration, embedding tables, index information, spatial extent, and available metadata fields. This inspection step lets users verify which dataset, representation, and spatial sampling scheme they are analyzing before using the embedding space for search.
The workbench supports two complementary forms of interaction. First, users can examine the organization of an embedding space through dimensionality-reduction views such as PCA and UMAP. These projections can be linked with metadata brushing, parallel coordinates, image galleries, and domain-specific views, allowing users to inspect whether latent-space structure corresponds to time, location, event labels, or other physical attributes.

Second, users can construct and evaluate retrieval strategies (G2,
\cref
fig:teaser(c)). A query may represent an entire atmospheric state, a localized spatial region, or a staged combination of both. Retrieval can be constrained by metadata or spatial filters, and the returned results are linked back to source imagery, maps, timestamps, similarity scores, and highlighted matching regions when available. This makes search an iterative visual process: users can adjust the query formulation and constraints, inspect the retrieved analogue, and decide whether the strategy captures the intended meteorological similarity.

4
Demonstration: Tropical-Cyclone Retrieval

We demonstrate the workbench through a tropical-cyclone retrieval
scenario over hourly ERA5 composites from the North Atlantic basin
(2016–2018), paired with IBTrACS storm-track metadata. ERA5 variables
are encoded as three-channel images of mean sea-level pressure anomaly,
10 m wind speed, and total column water vapor
(
\cref
tab:rgb-encoding; example composites in

\cref
fig:umapb–d). DINOv3 Base
[
21
]
, a vision foundation model, produces image-level and patch-level embeddings. IBTrACS observations are
spatiotemporally joined with each composite to provide storm labels and
intensity metadata. The workflow described here illustrates the core
retrieval loop; the full interaction sequence, including additional
views and features omitted for space, is shown in the supplemental
screen recording.

The user first inspects the DINOv3 latent space through a UMAP
projection colored by IBTrACS maximum sustained wind speed
(
\cref
fig:umapa). Storm-containing composites form a distinct
peripheral cluster; non-storm states remain broadly distributed.
Box-selecting the cluster populates a linked gallery of raw composites,
confirming that the structure is meteorological rather than a
latent-space artifact. This step grounds the embedding experiment as
suitable for retrieval before any query is issued.

The user then issues a localized query: a patch in Hurricane Matthew
near the Florida coast (2016-10-08) with a spatial filter restricting
matches to the Caribbean region around Puerto Rico (
\cref
fig:teaserc). As expected, the nearest matches are Matthew’s own track frames followed by Hurricane Irma north of Puerto Rico (2017-09-08 to 2017-09-12) and Hurricane Maria during its landfall on the island (2017-09-15 to 2017-09-27). More distant matches
include other 2017 and 2018 Atlantic systems crossing the filter
region. Patch locations, timestamps, and similarity scores are exposed
in the result gallery for export.

We do not attempt to quantify the accuracy of the retrieved results in
this paper. Doing so meaningfully requires task-specific definitions of
meteorological similarity, curated query sets, and interpretability of the embedding model - a study in its own right, and one for which the
workbench is the supporting infrastructure rather than the object of
evaluation. The workbench can isolate and export retrieval queries for
exactly this kind of subsequent model-level benchmarking.

This scenario illustrates how the workbench supports a practical
discovery workflow: a known phenomenon is used to characterize a region
of interest in the embedding space, and a constrained patch-level query
then surfaces meteorologically similar events across the archive. The
same query construction can be reused against embedding experiments
built over other corpora: for example, AI-emulator ensembles or
reanalysis archives to search for similar phenomena where storm-track
labels are not readily available.

5
Scalability Evaluation

We benchmarked memory-mapped IVF-PQ indexes over patch-embedding corpora ranging from 0.98M to 23.55M 768-dimensional vectors. IVF-PQ combines an inverted file index (IVF)
[
6
]
, which partitions the vector space so that search can probe only a subset of candidate regions, with product quantization (PQ)
[
5
]
, which compresses vectors to reduce storage and memory costs. Benchmarks were run on a HPC compute node configured with 16 GiB RAM and local NVMe SSD staging - simulating a commodity workstation. Each benchmark used exact nearest neighbors as ground truth. For each index, we ran 100 untimed warm-up queries followed by 1,000 timed queries, measuring mean latency for aggregate query cost, p95/p99 tail latency for worst-case interactive behavior, peak process Resident Set Size (RSS) for memory use, and Recall@10 for approximate-search quality. RSS captures the process memory high-water mark, while Recall@10 measures agreement between approximate search results and the exact top-10 neighbors.

\cref
fig:scalability_eval shows the backend supports out-of-core search well beyond available memory. The raw float32 vector footprint exceeds the 16 GiB budget at larger scales, but peak RSS stays at roughly 3 GiB even for the 23.55M-vector index. The full corpus is never loaded into process memory. This gap suggests headroom for substantially larger datasets on commodity hardware, bounded mainly by storage bandwidth and access patterns. Latency rises with corpus size but stays practical for exploratory use, and Recall@10 remains high; both can be traded against each other at query time via the number of IVF probes and refinement depth, without reindexing.

6
Conclusion and Future Work

We propose an Earth system data search and discovery workbench powered by vector embeddings, to address the growing challenge of exploring large, high-dimensional weather and climate datasets. Recognizing the diversity of embedding models and retrieval strategies, as well as the rapid pace of development in representation learning, we designed the system as an extensible environment for experimentation rather than a single-purpose search interface. This short paper demonstrates the first steps toward this goal: organizing embedding experiments around shared source data, supporting inspectable search workflows, and evaluating the technical feasibility of out-of-core retrieval over large embedding collections.

Building on ongoing collaboration with domain scientists, future work needs to refine the user experience for query formulation, retrieval interpretation, and integration with existing XAI workflows. Beyond qualitative visual verification of retrieved results, the workbench can isolate and export retrieval queries for subsequent benchmarking of embedding models. Such model-level evaluation is a study in its own right, requiring task-specific definitions of meteorological similarity, carefully designed query sets, appropriate retrieval strategies, and ground-truth labels. The current implementation supports image-level and patch-level similarity search using vision models; future work will explore richer query constructions, including vector combinations, vectors for segmented spatial regions, and multi-region queries.

Finally, embeddings provide a natural bridge to multi-modal and language-driven interfaces for Earth system data. As large language and vision-language models become increasingly integrated with scientific workflows, future versions of the workbench could explore richer query specification and cross-modal reasoning over data, metadata, and literature.
