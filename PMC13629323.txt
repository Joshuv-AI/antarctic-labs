Protocol for repeated Plasmodium chabaudi infection in a murine malaria model

Federica Bruno, Jean Langhorne, Cristina Lo Celso

Abstract

In malaria-endemic regions, individuals experience repeated malaria infection throughout life. Here, we present a protocol to investigate the immunological mechanisms triggered by repeated exposure using a murine model of sequential infections with the rodent parasite Plasmodium chabaudi chabaudi. We provide a detailed and standardized series of steps covering primary infection, natural recovery without treatment, and subsequent secondary infection. This approach offers a robust framework to investigate the effects and long-term consequences of repeated malaria infections on the host. For complete details on use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Stephen et al., Pérez-Mazliah et al., and Achtman et al.

Before you begin

Malaria, caused by the Plasmodium parasite, is a severe and life-threatening disease and it is still a global cause of mortality. Importantly, due to the polymorphism of Plasmodium, individuals living in endemic areas are often re-infected by antigenically different parasites which evade the immune response. Recently, it was demonstrated that individuals contracting malaria several times throughout their lifetime are protected against clinical disease. Importantly, an increasing body of evidence underscores how protective immunity develops after one or two episodes of malaria, resulting in a reduced risk of severe disease and control of parasitemia at low levels.,,, Moreover, this partial immunity has been shown to remodel the host immune response to prevent acute systemic activation, highlighting how the host is able to tolerate the parasite. While clinical immunity to malaria has been historically thought to be beneficial as it prevents severe disease, recently adverse outcomes have come to light. Specifically, it has been associated with maternal and neonatal mortality and a higher risk of co-infections with other pathogens due to immunosuppression. Incomplete development of clinical immunity has been shown to contribute to long-term hidden pathologies such as chronic anemia, cognitive impairment and bone loss.,,, Additionally, asymptomatic persistent malaria increases the transmission of the parasite and promotes the spread of drug-resistant strains. Importantly, while some level of exposure may provide immunity, ongoing malaria infections are a public health challenge due to their high costs in terms of human health and societal impact. The precise process beyond the establishment of clinical immunity against malaria is still unclear, and experimental models of repeated infection are ideal to investigate the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon. We established a murine model of repeated malaria whereby following recovery from first infection, mice undergo a second malaria episode. While we mainly use this model to study the hematopoietic response in repeated malaria, it can be readily applied to a broad range of research questions, including the investigation of immune responses, parasite-targeted interventions, and rescue of long-term effects.

Innovation

Most models of repeated murine malaria infection use Plasmodium strains with high severity and risk of death, requiring anti-malaria treatments to clear the parasite before initiating a second infection.,, By utilizing a self-recovery model of Plasmodium chabaudi chabaudi infection we have detailed a protocol whereby the immune system of mice is able to clear the parasite naturally followed by a second infection. This approach enables the study of biological systems without the confounding effects of pharmacological interventions, thereby more accurately reflecting real-world conditions, where re-infection is often associated with asymptomatic malaria that typically remains untreated.

Step-by-step method details

Initiation of first infection

Timing: 7–9 days

In this step, a first malaria infection is induced with Plasmodium chabaudi AS in an experimental cohort of naïve mice. This requires blood from one infected donor mouse. This can be either by pre-infecting a mouse with frozen stabilate (step 1) or by blood passage from a mouse in another experimental cohort (skip to step 3).CRITICAL: The majority of mice survive Plasmodium chabaudi AS infection without any major clinical signs, however rarely some mice might show signs of disease during the acute phase (day 9 to 15, or parasitaemia > 10%) of the primary infection, such as weight loss, blood in urine and pallor of the paws consistent with anaemia. From day 5 post-infection onwards, mice need to be monitored regularly for body weight, parasitaemia, and clinical signs to assess disease severity. Humane endpoints include the development of a hunched posture, reduced mobility, decreased strength, lethargy, or rapid weight loss exceeding 10% within 24 h or 15% over 48 h. Animals reaching any of these endpoints will be humanely euthanised. The likelihood of severe disease and mortality varies depending on the P. chabaudi strain used, as well as the age and sex of the host. The P. chabaudi CB strain is more virulent than the AS strain and is associated with much increased disease severity and reduced survival. We therefore recommend using the CB strain only after an initial AS infection is cleared. In addition, male and older mice have a slightly higher risk of developing severe infection. Monitor first infection and determine recovery

Timing: 30–50 days

In this step, monitor first infection by measuring mouse weight and parasitemia to determine the different phases of infection. We take undetectable parasitemia as the criterion to define cleared infection.11.Carry out blood smear from experimental cohort every 2–3 days in order to determine parasite growth curve.a.Once that the infection model is established and parasitemia growth curve is reliable, blood smear can be done less frequently.12.Employ parasite growth curve to determine acute and recovery phases and to choose timepoints of interest.Note: When using Plasmodium chabaudi AS, parasitemia is expected to peak at approximately 20%–30% between day 9 and day 12 post-infection and to become undetectable in the blood by approximately day 30 post infection. Peak weight loss typically occurs during the same period of peak parasitemia; however, this is not consistently observed across all mice.

Initiate and monitor second infection

Timing: 30 days

In this step, the second malaria infection is initiated in recovered mice with a different strain of parasite from the primary infection: e.g., primary infection with Plasmodium chabaudi AS and second infection with Plasmodium chabaudi CB.CRITICAL: In this model, the more virulent Plasmodium chabaudi CB strain is used only as the secondary challenge infection. Primary infection with P. chabaudi CB, compared with the less virulent AS strain, results in more severe disease, characterised by greater weight loss and higher parasitaemia. Animals are monitored closely throughout the study and will be humanely euthanised if predefined humane endpoints are reached, including hunched posture, reduced mobility, decreased strength, lethargy, or rapid weight loss. The use of the less virulent AS strain for primary infection reduces the risk of animals developing severe disease and reaching humane endpoints, while still allowing investigation of the immune response to subsequent challenge with the more virulent CB strain.13.At day 53 post AS infection, infect two donor mice with Plasmodium chabaudi CB as described in Step 1.14.At day 60 post AS infection, initiate second infection as described in Step 3–10.15.Determine phase of infection by monitoring mice weight and parasitemia as described in Step 13–14.CRITICAL: Infect some naïve mice to confirm that infection develops as expected; mice infected with Plasmodium chabaudi CB as a first infection are anticipated to develop severe infection and should be culled with a SK1 method once they reach 40% parasitaemia, approximately day 11 post-infection.

Expected outcomes

This protocol allows the establishment of a repeated murine malaria model. This involves two malaria episodes, where each is characterized by an acute infection and a recovery phase. Specifically, during first infection, parasites are detectable in the blood as soon as day 5 post infection, although at low levels (<3%). Parasitemia is expected to peak to approximately 20%–25% between day 9 to day 11 post infection followed by a gradual decrease (A and 3B). Peak infection is associated with some transient weight loss, although this is not consistenly observed in all mice (C and 3D). As a result, we defined the window of acute infection from day 9 to day 15 post infection, when parasitemia is >10%. With regards to second infection, the timing of re-infection should be determined based on the recovery of the biological system under investigation. Because we employed this model to investigate hematopoietic system in response to infection, different hematopoeitc populations were assessed during first infection using phenotypic and proliferation assays, which indicated recovery of the system by day 60 post infection. As a result, a second infection was initiated at day 60 post infection with Plasmodium chabaudi CB. As expected, naïve mice experienced high parasitemia, marked body weight loss and reduced survival (A and 6B). In contrast, mice that had recovered from the first infection displayed a markedly reduced parasitemia burden (at around 1%) from day 7 to day 14 post infection and with parasite becoming undetectable from day 17 onwards until the end of infection (A and 6B).Figure 6Infection dynamics of Plasmodium chabaudi CB in previously exposed or naïve mice(A) Parasitemia levels during P. chabaudi CB infection in naïve or mice that had previosly cleared P. chabaudi AS infection. Limitations

One limitation of this protocol is variability in the timing of peak of first infection, which generally occurs within a 2–3-days span. Once the model is established, the parasitemia growth curve can be used to anticipate the peak and coordinate culling or sampling at the right time points. A further limitation of this protocol is the length of the experimental protocol, which is approximately 97 days. However, depending on the research question asked, the time between first and second injection could be adjusted. For instance, we determined that the hematopoietic system recovers at day 60 post first infection, and therefore to study the effects of a second infection on the haemtopoietic system, P. chabaudi CB could be administered at day 60. If the requirement to initiate the second infection is cleared parasitaemia, P. chabaudi CB could be administered as early as day 30 post first infection. Finally, AS and CB are not the only Plasmodium chabaudi strains available to the community. We selected them here because their combination as as follows by CB provides an effective and safe strategy to induce two subsequent waves of parasitemia in C57BL/6 mice. Other strains and strain combinations could be used instead, however the timing and intensity of each parasitemia wave could vary and would have to be carefully established before proceeding to analyse the response of the system or organ of interest.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Donor mice do not have adequate parasitemia to start an infection in experimental cohort.

Potential solution

Allow one or two more days for the development of infection.

Problem 2

Variability in infection development across mice.

Potential solution

To account for potential variability in infection development, 1–2 additional mice should be infected to ensure sufficient numbers for statistical analysis. In the rare event that no parasites are detected in the blood by day 11 post-infection, those mice should be excluded from the experiment.

Problem 3

The quality of the thin blood films is poor due to anemia.

Potential solution

Always make sure that there is at least a drop of blood to smear, if required, heat the mice for 5 minutes at 37°. If no fields with 200 RBC can be found, count smaller fields containing 100 RBC but increase their number to a minimum of 6 fields. If the blood is very thin, then increase the angle of the second slide used to make the smear to more than 45°.
