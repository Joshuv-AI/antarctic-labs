II. Accomplishments:

 1. What are the major goals of the project?

 The Overarching goal of this work is to characterize mechanisms of extracellular electron
 uptake in three electrochemically active microbial systems. System one/Aim 1 is
 in Shewanella oneidensis MR-1 where we propose to characterize two previously identified
 genes, from a high throughput genetic screen that contain redox active features. For these
 two genes (SO0400 and SO3662), a deficiency for extracellular electron uptake is the only
 phenotype that has been observed and we hypothesize these genes play a role in
 connecting electron flow from the known EET pathway (MtrCAB), to the canonical aerobic
 electron transport chain. System two/Aim 2 investigates EET in an environmentally isolated
 microbe, a Pseudomonas stutzeri sp., that we showed is also capable of bi-directional
 extracellular electron transfer. Work in this organism will focus on performing a genome

 wide screen, first generating a pooled transposon library in this strain and then screening
 the population of transposon mutants after exposure to different selective conditions using
 high throughput sequencing. The last system we are studying (Aim 3)
 is Methanosarcina where we are working on applying genetic techniques to test genes we
 previously identified as being important to EET using proteomic techniques. As one of the
 proteomic approaches used targeted extracellular proteins, we hypothesize that one or
 more of these genes highlighted is involved in allowing electron flow across the cell’s
 envelope. This work will focus on previously constructed mutants from a collaborators lab in
 M. acetivorans.

2. What was accomplished under these goals?
 a. Aim 1: Graduate student Jin-Sang Yu has generated several plasmids containing
 SO0400 and SO3662 fusion proteins including: CFP C and N terminal fusions, CFP
 C and N terminal fusions with His tags, His tag only fusion proteins, and strep tag
 only fusion proteins. Unfortunately, mid-way through the year we identified a 6 base
 pair deletion in the promotor of the main plasmid we were using for cloning, and so
 several of Jin’s previous constructs needed to be remade and previous expression
 studies needed to be repeated, including the arabinose induction overexpression
 work. This pushed back our projected timeline, however this work has been taken
 over by MSU student Chris Chavteur and he has, both purified the proteins in
 question and begun biochemical characterization.
 b. Aim 2: Over the summer of 2024, I worked with an undergraduate student (Justin
 Mackey) interested in characterization of novel EET mechanisms. He started
 working on developing EET assays, using reduced iron, iron oxide and AQDS to
 screen P. stutzeri. This assay will be used to screen mutants generated via Tn-seq.
 I’m hoping to take on a rotation student this fall who can work on developing a Tn-
 seq library in P. stutzeri and take it over as a PhD project.
 c. Aim 3: I also had the opportunity to take an REU student through the NSF-Realize
 2030 REU program. Kenedy Whiters, and undergraduate from Albany State
 University in Georgia, worked in my lab for 10 weeks on developing genetic
 techniques for both M. acetivorans and M. barkeri. We started out plating these two
 organisms on solid media (i.e. plates), following protocols developed by Dan
 Lessner’s lab at University of Arkansas. We had better efficiency in M. acetivorans
 compared with M. barkeri which likely resulted in the better genetic transformation
 outcomes in this organism. In the end we were able to genetically transform M.
 acetivorans with a control plasmid, but not M. barkeri—again likely due to platting
 efficiency, which is something we are currently trouble shooting. Postdoc Saranya
 Sriram, an electrochemist, also worked on Aim 3 for part of 2024. Since we had
 success transforming M. acetivorans, we decided to move forward trying to test
 mutants in M. acetivorans capable of EET. Saranya was able to generate some
 promising electrochemical results for M. acetivorans, however the results were not
 consistently reproducible. Investigating the electrodes post microscopy suggested
 that perhaps the issue is variability in attachment of cells to the electrode, which is
 something we are working on optimizing for future experiments, using different
 electrode materials and pre-treatment regimes.

V. Impact
1. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?
At present, our mechanistic understanding of microbes that interact in a redox capacity with
electronic/electrode interfaces focuses on a few well characterized model systems. In particular,
our understanding of microbial systems that are capable of taking electrons from an electrode
(i.e., EEU) and converting these to cellular reducing equivalents for biosynthesis remains limited.
As will be detailed in this work, even in the best studied model systems (i.e., Shewanella) we are
still uncovering mechanisms and proteins involved in EEU. The proposed work will have major

 impacts on understanding EEU in both model systems (Shewanella and Methanosarcina), as well
 as an environmentally isolated microbe (Stutzerimonas). This work also has important
 implications for our understanding of the physiologic and ecologic relevance of bi‐ directional
 electron flow in microbes capable of both, as well as its potential applications. In general,
 understanding the underlying mechanism behind redox interactions with electrical interfaces is
 critical for application of natural or engineered microbes for applied electrochemical
 technologies. For example, expanding our understanding of the organisms capable of EEU for
 electrosynthesis and providing a diversity of mechanisms and organisms capable of these
 processes will help support the longevity and scalability of these technologies.
 2. What is the impact on other disciplines?
 Microbes that interact with electronic interfaces have the potential to be technologically
 transformative. Microbes are programable, encode the ability to regenerate themselves and can
 process and transduce signals from incredibly low concentration molecules. They also catalyze
 the most diverse array of biochemical reactions. As such, they have the potential to address
 several challenges facing our electronic and energy future, possibly providing solutions to
 sustainable use and recovery of rare earth elements, or mitigating our demand for these costly
 to acquire materials. From an energy perspective, they are often critical components of the
 most promising carbon‐neutral biofuel production platforms. An example of one such
 technology is electrosynthesis. Though renewable energy platforms like wind and solar are
 imperative to sustaining our future, the inability to efficiently store large amounts of energy is a
 major hurdle to the practical, widespread use of these platforms. Understanding the mechanics
 of EEU in diverse systems has the potential to improve and expand technologies in these areas.

7. What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?
 a. One of the long-term goals of this work is to help develop technologies that will
 efficiently help develop sustainable fuel production. Fossil fuels are a finite resource and
 replacing them with current CO2 capturing processes is a heavily desired pursuit for
 both climate and economic stability. One example of the potential technologies
 supported by this is the polishing of methane gas from anaerobic digestion—specifically
 converting the last percentages of CO2 to methane to gain concentration of methane
 >95% so it is suitable for use in natural gas infrastructure. This would convert a widely
 generated waste product into a usable fuel source.
