WT1 peptide vaccines of post–allogeneic HSCT maintenance immunotherapy for pediatric acute leukemias: a phase 2 study
Authors: Yoshiko Hashii, Yoshihiro Oka, Naoki Sakata, Masashi Hirayama, Yusuke Oji, Naoki Hosen, Satoshi Hattori, Hiroko Nakajima, Jun Nakata, Fumihiro Fujiki, Soyoko Morimoto, Shigeo Fuji, Sumiyuki Nishida, Maiko Noguchi, Moeko Hino, Atsushi Sato, Ken Tabuchi, Yoshiko Atsuta, Keiichi Ozono, Akihiro Tsuboi, Haruo Sugiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•The 3-year OS rate (70.6%) for WT1 peptide–vaccinated patients was much higher than 30% for historical control patients.•Maintenance cancer immunotherapy using WT1 peptide vaccines was suggested to be effective and safe for pediatric refractory acute leukemias.

Introduction

Recently, the prognosis of patients with acute leukemias has considerably been improved by risk-directed chemotherapy and allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). In pediatric patients, the 5-year event-free survival (EFS) rates for acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) were ≥80% and ≥60%, respectively.1 However, most of patients with refractory acute leukemias ultimately die, despite having undergone allo-HSCT, presumably due to (1) the regrowth of minimal residual disease (MRD) caused by the poor responsiveness or resistance of leukemic cells to conditioning regimens,2 or (2) difficulty achieving the graft-versus-leukemia effect without causing graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)3,4 because donor-derived immune cells attack leukemic and normal cells of the host.5 Therefore, the ideal therapeutic approach is to develop post–allo-HSCT maintenance cancer immunotherapy capable of eliciting the graft-versus-leukemia effect without aggravating GVHD. Cancer immunotherapies using peptide-based or dendritic cell (DC)–based vaccines are generally well tolerated, because they induce antigen-specific immune responses (IR) against tumor-associated antigens with limited effects on normal tissues.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Wilms tumor 1 (WT1), a stem cell antigen,11 is of critical immunological relevance12 among target antigens for vaccines. WT1-specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) damage malignant cells but not normal hematopoietic stem cells.13 WT1 ranked first in cumulative score among 75 cancer antigens12 and is therefore considered a superior target antigen of immunotherapy for leukemias.14 We previously reported on WT1 peptide vaccine (modified peptide235-243) treatment after allo-HSCT for 3 pediatric patients with refractory ALL or AML who were at high risk for relapse and had an obviously poor postrelapse prognosis,15 suggesting the feasibility of maintenance cancer immunotherapy.

In this phase 2 clinical study, we enrolled a greater number of pediatric patients with refractory acute leukemias including these 3 patients to obtain clinical evidence on the efficacy and safety of post–allo-HSCT maintenance cancer immunotherapy using adjuvant-emulsified, WT1-modified and natural peptide vaccines (hereafter referred to as MCI).

Methods

Patients and study design

Between 1 August 2008 and 31 March 2013, this phase 2, multicenter, uncontrolled, open-label clinical study recruited pediatric patients with refractory acute leukemias at 3 medical institutions (Osaka University Hospital, Kindai University Hospital, and Nakadori General Hospital) in Japan. The study protocol was approved by the respective institutional ethics committees (08091-3, 24-246, and 26-011, respectively). The study period end date was 31 March 2016.

WT1 peptide vaccines and vaccination procedures

Good manufacturing practice (GMP)-grade, HLA-A∗24:02-restricted, 9-mer–modified WT1 peptide235-243 (CYTWNQMNL), which more effectively elicits CTLs than a natural WT1 peptide (CMTWNQMNL),16 and HLA-A∗02:01-restricted, natural WT1 peptide126-134 (RMFPNAPYL)17 were prepared and provided in lyophilized form by Peptide Institute (Osaka, Japan). The pharmaceutical details of the vaccines have been published previously.13,18 Because HLA-A∗24:02 and HLA-A∗02:01 are present in ∼60%16 and ∼10%19 of the Japanese population, respectively, priority was to be given to the former; however, none of studied patients had both alleles.

According to his/her HLA allele type, each patient received intradermal injections of the appropriate vaccine emulsified with an adjuvant, Montanide ISA 51, at a weight ratio of 1:1 according to the following dosing schedules based on the findings of previous studies:20, 21, 22, 23 once weekly for the first 24 weeks into 4 sites (right and left shoulders and thighs) of each patient, once biweekly at week 25 to year 1, and once monthly thereafter. The doses used were as follows: 0.5, 1, 2, and 3 mg for body weights of <10, 10-19, 20-39, and ≥40 kg, respectively. At the twelfth vaccination (∼3 months after the first vaccination), the data monitoring committee determined whether to proceed with the study based on the predefined criteria for suspension or discontinuation of MCI due to nonhematologic, hematologic, or other toxicities. Continuation of MCI for up to 3 years was permitted upon approval by the committee.

Inclusion criteria

Pediatric patients with refractory acute leukemias were considered eligible when (1) being diagnosed as having hematologic malignancies in bone marrow (BM) examination; (2) achieving hematologic remission after allo-HSCT; (3) being nonresponders to induction chemotherapy; (4) having an obviously poor prognosis after allo-HSCT ([a] showing a poor response to induction chemotherapy using prednisolone, and [b] showing a poor prognosis as described in the protocol of the revised supplemental Material); (5) WT1-mRNA was detected in leukemia cells collected from peripheral blood (PB) or BM at the time of initial diagnosis or relapse (≥50 and ≥1000 copies per microgram RNA in the PB and BM, respectively24); (6) both the donor and the recipient having either of HLA-A∗24:02 and HLA-A∗02:01, namely, each eligible pediatric patient was required to match at least 1 of the HLA-A∗ alleles (24:02 and 02:01) with those of his or her donor; (7) vaccination could be initiated within 6 months after allo-HSCT to the extent possible; and (8) toxicities should fall under grade 1 or below according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 2.0; the remaining inclusion criteria are provided in the protocol of the revised supplemental Material.

Exclusion criteria

The details of the exclusion criteria are provided in the protocol of the revised supplemental Material.

Definitions

Immune responders were pediatric patients whose WT1-specific CTL frequency per 2.5 mL of PB at week 6 after the initiation of vaccination presented a ≥1.455-fold increase from the baseline frequency determined by the receiver operating characteristics analysis or whose WT1-specific CTL count was ≥10 when the baseline count was 0. The cutoff (a 1.455-fold increase from baseline) was determined using receiver operating characteristic curve analysis based on 3-year survival, the primary end point of this study. The optimal threshold was defined by the Youden index (sensitivity, 90.0%; specificity, 71.4%; area under the curve, 0.793; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.538-1). Fold change was used to account for interindividual variability in baseline WT1-specific CTL frequency. We acknowledge that this cutoff was derived from the same cohort without external validation and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies with independent cohorts will be required to validate this threshold. In contrast, immune nonresponders did not exceed the relevant frequency or count. To assess the clinical efficacy of MCI, clinical responders were defined as pediatric patients who sustained complete response (CR) at year 1 after the initiation of vaccination, and clinical nonresponders as pediatric patients who did not meet the relevant criterion.

Efficacy and safety

The primary end point of efficacy was the 3-year overall survival (OS) rate after allo-HSCT and was compared with 30%, a historical control benchmark described in a clinical study on pediatric and adult AML conducted by Fung et al.25 The secondary end points of efficacy were as follows: (1) the efficacy of the vaccine based on the 3-year EFS (events: relapse and all-cause death, whichever developed earlier) rates after allo-HSCT; (2) the incidences of adverse events (AEs); (3) the incidences and deterioration rates of acute GVHD (aGVHD) and chronic GVHD (cGVHD); as well as (4) the associations of immune signatures with the 3-year OS and EFS rates and clinical responses. The safety of MCI was evaluated according to the updated Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. aGVHD and cGVHD were assessed as described previously by Jacobsohn26 and Filipovich et al.27 The immunological mechanisms of MCI were investigated by comparing immunological profiles between clinical responders and nonresponders.

Patient backgrounds differed between this phase 2 clinical study and the historical control study,25 which made it difficult to precisely assess the primary end point. To address this statistical concern and to clarify the clinical efficacy of MCI, Japanese Data Center for Hematopoietic Cell Transplantation (U2021-13) matched patients in this study (approval number at Osaka International Cancer Institute: 22080-4) with patients who had been registered with the Transplantation Registry Unified Management Program (TRUMP), an external, nationwide, real-world, population-based database on clinical information obtained before and after allo-HSCT.28 Subsequently, we conducted the propensity score matching analysis on TRUMP-registered patients (“TRUMP cohort”) and WT1-vaccinated patients in this study (“WT1 cohort”) with respect to the time frame (2008-2013) of allo-HSCT and important propensity scores.

Immunomonitoring, sampling, and sample storage

Individual patients were assessed for their IR as assessed by flow cytometry (WT1-specific CTL frequency and immunophenotyping of CTLs), serum anti-WT1 peptide immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody titer determination, interferon gamma (IFN-γ) enzyme-linked immunospot assay to determine the IR index (number of spot-forming cells under antigen-stimulated testing conditions per number of spot-forming cells under antigen-free, control conditions), and delayed-type hypersensitivity (DTH).

Flow cytometry, DTH, and monitoring of MRD quantification of WT1 mRNA expression levels

The details of flow cytometry, DTH, and monitoring of MRD quantification of WT1 mRNA expression levels are provided in the supplemental Material.

Statistical analyses

The primary analysis was to evaluate whether the 3-year OS rate in this study exceeds a historical control benchmark of 30%.25 Sample size was determined to be 20 in consideration of the estimated annual incidences of refractory acute leukemias and HLA compatibility (70%) in Japan. Values are expressed as mean ± standard error, median (95% CI), or hazard ratio (HR). A 2-tailed P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted with the Easy R software version 1.41.29

The estimates for the survival of patients in the prospective and retrospective studies30, 31, 32 were compared according to the Kaplan-Meier method and the log-rank test. The details of the propensity score matching analysis are provided in the supplemental Material.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants aged ≥16 years and their guardians. This study was registered with University Hospital Medical Information Network (UMIN00005319) and was conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Patients

Twenty pediatric patients were enrolled. Patient disposition is shown in Figure 1. Patient characteristics at baseline are summarized in Table 1. Six patients had not achieved CR, but 11 had achieved the first to third CRs at the time of allo-HSCT. The details of patient characteristics at baseline are provided in supplemental Table 1.1-1.4, and patient characteristics by cohort at baseline are summarized in supplemental Table 2. The period between the day of allo-HSCT and the initiation of vaccination ranged from 41 to 293 days (median, 87 days). Three patients were excluded before enrollment due to their primary disease of lymphoma, hematologic relapse, as well as increased alanine and aspartate aminotransferases, respectively. Each of 17 enrolled patients (median age, 8.3 years [range, 2-18]), who were at high risk for relapse, received either of the peptide vaccines. The median WT1 mRNA expressions at the time of initial diagnosis or hematologic relapse in the BM and PB were 14 500 (2100-430 000) copies per microgram RNA and 20 000 (190-160 000) copies per microgram RNA, respectively (Table 1; supplemental Tables 1-3). Efficacy

Strong associations between immune responsiveness in the PB and clinical prognosis:

The 3-year OS rate was significantly higher for 12 clinical responders who did not show relapse after year 1 (91.7% [95% CI, 53.9-98.7]) than for 5 nonresponders (20.0% [95% CI, 0.8-58.2]; HR, 0.040 [95% CI, 0.005-0.358]; P = .004). WT1-specific CTL frequency in the PB from 12 clinical responders increased significantly after vaccination, reaching the maximum by week 12 of vaccination (before, 0.25% ± 0.08%; after, 1.07% ± 0.24%; P < .001; Figure 2D), whereas that from 5 nonresponders remained unchanged after vaccination (before, 0.12% ± 0.2%; after, 0.20% ± 0.25%; P = .424; data not shown). The maximum frequency of WT1-specific CTLs in the PB by week 12 of vaccination was significantly higher for 12 clinical responders than for 5 nonresponders (responder, 1.08% ± 0.24%; nonresponder, 0.32% ± 0.18%; P = .015; Figure 2E).

The proportion of immune responders was 68.8% (11/16). The proportions of WT1-specific CTLs with memory phenotypes at the maximum frequency were significantly higher for 12 clinical responders than for 5 nonresponders (for central memory T cells [Tcm]: responders, 3.93% ± 1.66%, and nonresponders, 0.08% ± 0.08%, P = .008; and for effector memory T cells [Tem], responders, 45.21% ± 6.80%, and nonresponders, 17.06% ± 13.45%, P = .026; Figure 2F); namely, the proportion of WT1-specific central and effector memory CTLs increased significantly in clinical responders compared to clinical nonresponders. This finding strongly suggested that an increased proportion of WT1-specific memory CTLs led to favorable clinical outcomes in patients treated with MCI. Ten of 13 patients who could be measured for the IR index were clinical responders (red circles in Figure 2G), and 10 of 12 clinical responders were immune responders who showed a ≥1.455-fold increase in WT1-specific CTL frequency from the baseline value; 7 of 9 clinical responders showed WT1-specific IFN-γ production (IR index: ≥1.09).

According to the Kaplan-Meier method with a landmark analysis at week 6, the estimated 3-year OS rate was significantly higher for 11 immune responders (who showed a ≥1.455-fold increase from the baseline value (supplemental Table 1); 90.9%; [95% CI, 50.8-98.7]) than for 5 immune nonresponders (40.0% [95% CI, 5.2-75.3]) (HR, 0.083 [95% CI, 0.009-0.751]; P = .027) (Figure 2H); and the estimated 3-year EFS rate was significantly higher for 11 immune responders (90.9% [95% CI, 50.8-98.7]) than for 5 nonresponders (20.0% [95% CI, 0.8-58.2]) (HR, 0.07 [0.008-0.636]; P = .018; Figure 2I). UPN 19, who had a relapse before week 6, could not be assessed for immunological responses.

All of 17 patients were negative for DTH before vaccination, and 16 were examined for DTH throughout the vaccination. A statistically significant difference was found not in OS (Figure 2J) but in EFS (Figure 2K; P = .041) between 11 patients with DTH positivity and 5 with DTH negativity.

The anti-WT1 IgG antibody titers were below the detection limit in all 16 patients examined before vaccination. Notably, 2 (12.5%) and 3 (18.8%) became seropositive at as early as weeks 4 and 8 of vaccination, respectively (supplemental Figure 1), with a statistically significant difference between the IgG titers at the onset and month 24 of vaccination (P = .031). Five patients who turned seropositive by week 24 of vaccination were clinical responders, 4 (80%) of whom survived 3 or more years (supplemental Figure 2).

WT1-specific CTL frequency in PB from healthy individuals is usually ∼0.1%.33,34 The 3-year OS (Figure 2L; HR, 0.104; 95% CI, 0.018-0.597; P = .011) and EFS (Figure 2M; HR, 0.117; 95% CI, 0.020-0.678; P = .017) rates estimated by the Kaplan-Meier method with a landmark analysis and the log-rank test at the initiation of vaccination were significantly higher for 12 patients whose WT1-specific CTL frequency in PB before the initiation of vaccination was ≥0.1% than for their 5 counterparts (<0.1%). WT1-specific CTL frequency at the initiation of vaccination and IR at week 6 of vaccination were significantly associated with clinical responses, respectively (P = .004 each). These findings strongly suggested that the high frequencies of WT1-specific CTLs at the initiation and early phase of vaccination were significantly associated with the clinical efficacy of MCI. Regarding pediatric patients whose BM was analyzable, in contrast, WT1-specific CTL frequency in BM from 5 clinical nonresponders did not increase significantly (before, 0.05% ± 0.009%; after, 0.445% ± 0.6%; P = .25). Safety

MCI was well tolerated by pediatric patients who had undergone allo-HSCT, and its toxicity profiles were not unfavorable compared with those who had not undergone previous allo-HSCT.22,34 Vaccination-related injection site reactions occurred in all patients (grade 1, n = 15; grade 2, n = 1; and grade 3 skin ulceration, n = 1). Moreover, the following treatment-related AEs were noted: 1 case each of grade 2 wheezing and pruritus; and 2 cases of grade 2 urticaria. During the study period, 6 patients experienced relapse; 5 subsequently died of relapse, whereas 1 remained alive at the end of the study.

In total, 6 patients died during the study period: 5 due to relapse and 1 due to acute pancreatitis, which was judged unrelated to MCI by the data and safety monitoring committee. Consequently, the nonrelapse mortality rate was 0%.

A total of 9 patients developed GVHD: 6 and 3 had aGVHD and cGVHD, respectively (Table 3). Six patients with aGVHD showed the complete resolution of signs and symptoms with minimal or without treatment, except UPN19 who showed the partial resolution of aGVHD signs owing to aGVHD treatment and eventually died of relapsed primary disease. Discussion

This phase 2 clinical study of the vaccine evaluated the efficacy and safety of MCI for pediatric patients with refractory acute leukemias. Overall, the 3-year OS rate for 17 patients was 70.6% and met the predefined historical control benchmark of 30%.25 Moreover, this rate was much higher than 31.6% reported by Kitazawa et al35 who had examined pediatric patients with ALL (n = 18) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (n = 1), and 12% to 24% reported by Goemans et al36 who had examined pediatric patients with relapsed AML.

We consider that the following findings contributed to the favorable outcomes of MCI: 12 clinical responders showed increases in WT1-specific CTL frequency; and the 3-year OS rate was notably high. These findings suggest that MCI may prolong survival. Furthermore, both the 3-year OS and EFS rates were significantly higher in the WT1 cohort than in the TRUMP cohort, further supporting the clinical relevance of MCI in improving survival through relapse prevention.

Markedly longer OS for clinical responders than nonresponders appears to be attributable to the following immunological findings in BM: a significant increase in WT1-specific CTL frequency after vaccination (Figure 3A,C), and a significantly higher proportion of Tem (Figure 3D). Our understanding of immunological mechanisms by which donor-derived WT1-specific CTLs expand efficiently after allo-HSCT with WT1 peptide vaccination is as follows: conditioning regimens promote antigen release from killed leukemia cell–derived fragments37 and induce profound host lymphoablation, thereby providing a permissive platform for homeostatic expansion of donor-derived, nonanergic WT1-specific CTLs after allo-HSCT.7 Conventional DCs can take up killed leukemia cell–derived fragments,37 including WT1. WT1-specific CTLs can then recognize and eliminate WT1-expressing leukemia cells.13 Antigen recognition by immune cells induces the clonal expansion of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells,38 which may contribute to the formation of restricted T-cell receptor repertoires in BM-resident tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.38 Our data support the following speculations: CD4+ T-cell help amplified the relevant formation by licensing DCs via major histocompatibility complex class II presentation, enhancing DCs’ cross-presenting capacity and enabling robust CD8+ T-cell priming; and CD4+ T-cell help enhanced the intrinsic capacity of CD8+ T cells to produce IFN-γ,39,40 maintaining Tcm with self-renewal potential and preserved cytotoxicity, as well as regulating the size and function of Tem that can more efficiently expand and exert effector functions.41 Within the BM microenvironment, which constitutes TME for leukemia, WT1-specific CTLs comprised both Tcm and Tem. Tcm are maintained in a CD4+ T-cell help-dependent manner, sustaining their self-renewal capacity and cytotoxic potential.40 Consistent with this concept, the titer of class-switched IgG-type anti-WT1 peptide antibody increased after vaccination in clinical responders, in line with previous evidence that immunoglobulin class switching from IgM to IgG requires CD4+ T-cell help (supplemental Figures 1 and 2).42,43 Notably, 4 of 5 (80%) patients who exhibited increased IgG-type antibody production at month 6 survived, suggesting that this mechanism may be associated with improved long-term outcomes.

Tem possess an intrinsic ability to expand efficiently and exert effector functions. In clinical responders, MCI induced a long-lasting presence of WT1-specific CTLs within the BM TME at weeks 50 through 54, likely reflecting repeated antigen encounters through continued vaccination44 and subsequent clonal reexpansion. Tem frequency was significantly higher in clinical responders than in nonresponders at week 25 of vaccination. Taken together, MCI increased WT1-specific CTL frequency and promoted IFN-γ production, indicating functional maturation of the WT1-specific IR. This immune activation may contribute to relapse suppression, consistent with the significantly longer EFS observed in patients with DTH positivity than those with DTH negativity. MCI may therefore act synergistically with allo-HSCT and sustain antileukemic immunity in patients with ALL or AML.

Leukemia relapse was completely prevented through WT1-specific T-cell receptor–transduced T-cell therapy for post–allo-HSCT AML adult patients.45 We consider that MCI is more feasible than their therapy for pediatric AML in the real-world clinical settings in the following aspects: (1) less patient burden because these peptide vaccines are already manufactured in compliance with GMP, allowing for speedy vaccination; (2) lentiviral vector construction is not required, which provides patients with greater safety; (3) less regulatory hurdles because of not needing the review and approval by the regulatory authorities; and (4) less manufacturing hurdles because of not necessitating a GMP-compliant cell processing facility.

Among 17 patients, 7 were positive for MRD, an independent risk factor of tumor relapse46; 3 of these 7 pediatric patients (42.9%) became immune responders at week 6 of vaccination, supporting the potential of MCI to exert clinical effects also on pediatric patients at high risk for leukemia relapse. Our data infer that the determination of WT1-specific CTL frequency before vaccination may serve for the optimal selection of acute leukemia patients who are expected to show favorable clinical outcomes from MCI.

Regarding safety, 6 and 3 of 17 pediatric patients developed aGVHD and cGVHD, respectively; therefore, the incidence rates of both aGVHD and cGVHD were not higher than those reported for patients who had undergone allo-HSCT alone.47,48 MCI did not seem to increase the incidence and severity of aGVHD and cGVHD, especially cGVHD that is a developmental issue for pediatric patients,49 and other AEs. Taken together, these data suggest that MCI is safe and has the potential to improve post-HSCT survival of pediatric patients with high-risk leukemia.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not confirm the presence of activated CD4+ T cells, although anti-WT1 antibody titers were determined. Second, the phenotyping of rare antigen-specific T cells is challenging in immune nonresponders. However, we addressed the challenge as described in flow cytometry of the supplemental Materials. Third, sample size was relatively small due to the nature of the study design (phase 2). We consider that another randomized clinical study is required to obtain robust clinical evidence for our findings.

Conclusion

MCI was suggested to be very effective (as manifested by the 3-year OS and EFS rates of ≥90% in immune responders) and safe for pediatric patients with refractory acute leukemias, indicating its potential to improve their survival through relapse prevention. However, another clinical study of randomized design enrolling a greater number of the relevant patients who are screened for WT1-specific CTL frequency and/or CD4+ T-cell count is warranted to obtain robust clinical evidence on our findings.
