 Abstract
 240
 This report documents an evaluation of Pu prompt fission neutron spectra (PFNS) including
 Chi-Nu experimental data. This work is a FY2025 Q4 NCSP (Nuclear Criticality Safety Program)
 milestone and named a “strategic priority”. This evaluation is new; it differs substantially from
 the most recent evaluation included in the ENDF/B library, which was for ENDF/B-VII.1 and
 then carried over unchanged to ENDF/B-VIII.1. The key difference between ENDF/B-VIII.1 and
 the new evaluation is that we have, for the first time, realistic experimental 240 Pu PFNS covering
 a broad incident and outgoing neutron energy range. These data were measured for NCSP by the
 LANL/LLNL Chi-Nu team for continuous incident neutron energies of 1–20 MeV and outgoing
 neutron energies from 800 keV–10 MeV. The data were provided along with a detailed assessment of
 experimental uncertainties. Also, the prior of ENDF/B-VIII.1 and the new evaluation is different:
 While both use the Los Alamos model, an extended version implemented in the code CoH3 was used
 along with the exciton model describing pre-equilibrium neutron emission for the new evaluation.
 In addition to that, the energy dependence of the average total kinetic energy release and average
 total γ-ray energy released were modeled based on previous NCSP work for evaluating minor Pu
 isotope average prompt fission neutron multiplicities. The resulting evaluated data correspond
 well to Chi-Nu PFNS across most energies, and also produce average mean energies in agreement
 with associated Chi-Nu data. The new evaluated data also agree with ENDF/B-VIII.1 PFNS
 until incident neutron energies of 4 MeV, but differ distinctly above. If one uses the new evaluated
 data to predict the effective neutron multiplication factor, keff , of selected critical assemblies and
 neutron leakage spectra of LLNL pulsed spheres, the simulated values differ for most experiment
 within their 1-σ Monte Carlo uncertainties. The only exception is the keff value of the Dirty
 Jezebel critical assembly where simulated keff values differ by 3-σ. Currently, the Chi-Nu PFNS
 are off-set by 500 keV in incident neutron energy from the evaluation grid, which causes evaluation
 artifacts, especially at second-chance fission. A new representation of the Chi-Nu experimental
 data is expected to be delivered next FY to correct for that.
 Keywords: 240 Pu, Prompt Fission Neutron Spectrum, ENDF/B-IX.0.

1 Introduction
This report serves as documentation for an evaluation of 240 Pu prompt fission neutron spectra (PFNS)
that includes Chi-Nu experimental 240 Pu PFNS as published in Ref. [1]. In addition, the report shows
that we completed an FY2025 Q4 NCSP (Nuclear Criticality Safety Program) milestone at LANL
that required: “240 Pu: Update evaluation to include new LANSCE / Chi-Nu prompt fission neutron
spectra”, which was also a strategic priority.
 The evaluation described here is new. It was independently undertaken from the most recent
ENDF/B evaluation, which was published as part of ENDF/B-VII.1 [2] and carried over unchanged to
ENDF/B-VIII.1 [3]. The underlying approach for our new evaluation builds upon previous evaluations
undertaken for 239 Pu and 235 U for ENDF/B-VIII.0 [4] in as far as the same extended Los Alamos
model, exciton model, evaluation algorithm, and codes were used. However, new model curves had to
be calculated to form the prior mean values and covariances, and experimental covariances had to be
estimated for Chi-Nu experimental data.
 In fact, the availability of Chi-Nu experimental 240 Pu PFNS were a driving factor for re-evaluating
the PFNS. These experimental data were measured by the LANL/ LLNL Chi-Nu team for NCSP and
published in FY24 [1] and are here now included subsequently in the evaluation. This experiment
is the first one that provides credible 240 Pu PFNS spanning a broad incident and outgoing neutron
energy range. Also, carefully quantified uncertainties are reported. This information allows for more
definitive evaluated 240 Pu PFNS than was possible for ENDF/B-VIII.1. Instead, the latter evaluation
had to rely on 240 Pu average prompt fission neutron multiplicities to fit PFNS model parameters—these
experimental data are only weakly linked to those parameters crucial for PFNS modeling.
 The report here serves to document this new evaluation and its input. Section 2 discusses sim-
ilarities and differences to ENDF/B-VIII.1, while Section 3 focuses on the model and experimental
input for the new evaluation. Evaluated results for PFNS, mean energies and covariances are shown in
Section 5.1. The impact of this new evaluated PFNS on the simulated neutron multiplication factor of
selected critical assemblies and LLNL pulsed sphere neutron leakage spectra is discussed in Section 5.2.
Section 6 summarizes the main findings and provides conclusions.

2 Differences Compared with the ENDF/B-VIII.1 240 Pu PFNS
 Evaluation
The evaluated 240 Pu PFNS in the current U.S. nuclear data library ENDF/B-VIII.1 [3] was carried
over unchanged from ENDF/B-VII.1 [2]. Our new evaluation differs in input distinctly from the input
for the ENDF/B-VIII.1 PFNS, namely:
 • Model: The ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation used as input the Los Alamos model as described in
 Ref. [5]. It included multiple-chance fission processes via the GNASH code [6] and did not
 model the pre-equilibrium neutron emission process. This latter process leads to a sharp peak
 in evaluated spectra above incident energies of ≈10 MeV and outgoing neutron energy ranges
 above 8 MeV. For this new evaluation, an extended version of the Los Alamos model was used as
 described in [4, 7] and implemented in CoH3 [8]. CoH3 was also used to model multiple-chance
 fission processes. In addition to that, we also include pre-quilibrium neutron emission via the
 exciton model implemented in CoH3 .
 • Experimental Data: No experimental PFNS were used to constrain the ENDF/B-VIII.1 or earlier
 evaluations. Model parameters were instead informed by experimental average prompt fission
 neutron multiplicities. For the new evaluation, we also used model parameter systematics in-
 formed by average prompt fission neutron multiplicities. However, and this is a key feature of
 this evaluation, the LANL/ LLNL Chi-Nu team provided recently for the first time realistic
 experimental 240 Pu PFNS that were used as direct input for the new evaluation.
 • Evaluation technique: ENDF/B-VIII.1 PFNS were evaluated with the Kalman filter technique
 in the model parameter space, while we evaluate here the PFNS with generalized least squares
 in PFNS observable space.

The impact of these differences is discussed in Section 5.1.

3 Evaluation Input
3.1 Modeling
The same models as described at length in Refs. [4, 7] were used here. All model calculations were
undertaken with the code CoH3 [8].
 Here, we provide only a concise summary: An extended version of the Los Alamos model [5]
was used to model PFNS. Multiple-chance fission probabilities were calculated with Hauser-Feshbach
theory based on a 240 Pu input deck that was also used for Ref. [9]. These were used as input for CoH3
to calculate multiple-chance fission contributions to the PFNS. The exciton model was used to model
the pre-equilibrium neutron emission component to the PFNS.

and 2 following the nomenclature of Ref. [4]. Most parameter values were extracted either from previous
evaluations for 239 Pu, see Refs. [4, 7], or the original Los Alamos model formulation [5].

3.2 Experimental Data
Two experimental data sets are available in EXFOR for 240 Pu PFNS, namely, by Smith et al. [12] at
incident neutron energies of 0.85 MeV and LANL/LLNL Chi-Nu data [1] which span a larger energy
range as shown schematically in Fig. 2.
 Smith’s PFNS were not included in this nor the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation. We took this decision
given their highly unphysical shape in the right-hand side of Fig. 2.
 Hence, Chi-Nu PFNS are the only experimental data left. They cover an incident-neutron energy
range from 1.5–20 MeV, and outgoing-neutron energy range from 0.8–9 MeV. Previous Chi-Nu mea-
surements for 235 U or 239 Pu [13, 14] covered a larger outgoing neutron energy range because a Li-glass
and liquid scintillator array were used to measure PFNS in lower and higher outgoing neutron energy
ranges, respectively.
 For their 240 Pu PFNS measurement, the neutron counts coming from the sample were systemat-
ically lower. The neutron counts were lower due to a sample mass that was approximately a factor
five smaller than for instance for their 239 Pu measurement [14, 15]. This lower mass was chosen due
to the much larger α decay rate of 240 Pu (10 times that of 239 Pu) which would have led to an other-
wise prohibitively large background. This resulted in overall increased background-to-foreground ratio
and a decreased neutron counting statistics. Hence, the 240 Pu PFNS have higher uncertainties than
those for 235 U or 239 Pu. In addition to that, it was not possible to measure with the Li-glass array.

 4

This array has a distinctly lower overall detector efficiency than the liquid scintillators restricting the
outgoing neutron energy range. Given the lower mass of the 240 Pu experiment and the lower Li-glass
detector efficiency, it would have taken two run-cycles to measure with this array. Instead, Chi-Nu
used a “dual gain” set up to expand the range of the liquid scintillator data. “Dual gain” means each
signal was sent to two digitizer channels, with the high versus low gain used for the lower versus higher
energy outgoing neutrons. Despite these challenges, Chi-Nu PFNS are the first reliable experimental
data for this quantity and, thus, enable a more realistic evaluation than was previously possible.

4 Evaluation
The same evaluation methodology was employed for this evaluation and 235 U and 239 Pu PFNS for
ENDF/B-VIII.1 above thermal incident neutrons, namely, generalized least squares in PFNS space.
The equations for the evaluation technique are given in Eqs. (11)–(14) of Ref. [4]. The same code
developed by D. Neudecker for the evaluation in Ref. [4] was used.
 Several test evaluations were performed that are combined with the one that was accepted here:
 • Filename: DataEvaluatedAccepted Upto5 13-20MeV.txt. This evaluation factors in the entire
      model prior and Chi-Nu PFNS for Einc = {1.54–5.81, 10.6, 13.56–19.56} MeV. This evaluation
 was accepted for Einc ≤ 5.5 MeV and for 13–20 MeV. Experimental data for Einc = 6.5–9.5
 and 11.5–12.5 MeV were not included as the model values for second (6.5–9.5 MeV) and third
 chance fission (11.5–12.5 MeV) were too far from experimental data, while they agreed reasonably
 well at other Einc . If experimental data would have been included for Einc = 6.5–9.5 and 11.5–
 12.5 MeV, resulting evaluated data would pull away from experimental data at Einc = {1.54–5.81,
 10.6, 13.56–19.56} MeV. So, this evaluation focuses on providing evaluated data up to 5.5 MeV
 and above 13 MeV.
 • Filename: DataEvaluated6500keV.txt was accepted for Einc = 6.5 MeV. This evaluation uses as
 prior an arithmetic mean of model means and covariances at Einc = 6 and 7 MeV. No correlations
 of model values to other Einc are considered which allowed the evaluation to closely match Chi-Nu
 data at Einc = 6.5 MeV.

 • Filename: DataEvaluated7500keV.txt was accepted for Einc = 7.5 MeV. The same as DataEval-
 uated7500keV.txt except for using model values for Einc = 7 and 8 MeV and Chi-Nu data at
      Einc = 7.5 MeV.
 • Filename: DataEvaluated7-9MeV.txt was accepted for Einc = {8–10} MeV. The entire model
 prior was fit to Chi-Nu PFNS for Einc = {7.5–9.5} MeV.
 • Filename: DataEvaluated6 10-12MeV.txt was accepted for Einc = {11–12} MeV. This evaluation
 factors in the entire prior and Chi-Nu PFNS for Einc = {6.5–12.48} MeV.
This pragmatic approach of piecewise evaluations had to be taken because the model agreed with
Chi-Nu data at some Einc well, while it differed strongly at others. This varying degree of experiment
and model discrepancy paired with strong model correlations did not allow to fit all experimental data
in the same evaluation.

5 Results
5.1 Evaluation Results
The new evaluated mean values agree with most experimental Chi-Nu PFNS in Figs. 3–7, but distinct
differences compared to ENDF/B-VIII.1 can be observed. It should be highlighted that Chi-Nu data
are on a grid that is off-set by 500 keV from the evaluation grid. That is Chi-Nu data are often given
for, e.g., Einc = 3–4 MeV with a mean Einc of 3.5 MeV, while the evaluation is given for 3 and 4 MeV.
The evaluated values were transformed to be closer to Chi-Nu PFNS’ Einc grid after the evaluation to
facilitate the visual comparison between evaluated and Chi-Nu PFNS. Chi-Nu PFNS will be provided
in a future iteration on the grid of the evaluation.

 For thermal incident neutrons to 5 MeV (Figs. 3 and 4), the new evaluation is distinctly softer
than ENDF/B-VIII.1; that means the PFNS is visibly larger in the peak region of the PFNS (ap-
proximately at 2 MeV outgoing neutron energy), while the new evaluation falls more rapidly off above
5 MeV outgoing neutron energies. This trend is induced by Chi-Nu data that also fall more rapidly
off compared to ENDF/B-VIII.1 This behavior is also visible for curves without experimental input
(thermal to 1 MeV incident neutron energy), because strong model correlations perpetuate the trend
from higher incident neutron energies where Chi-Nu data are available.
 It is interesting to observe a distinct downward trend of Chi-Nu experimental data for Einc = 3.5
and 4.5 MeV for the first four data points. While such a shape is expected for second-chance fission
spectra, there is no physics explanation for this effect at these lower incident neutron energies. The
model also does not follow this trend and we accepted an evaluation that does not follow it.
 At Einc = 6.5 and 7.5 MeV, the evaluated PFNS follow closely Chi-Nu data as only the experimental
data shown for the specific Einc were fit. The new evaluated data are very far from ENDF/B-VIII.1
that had no experimental data with which to calibrate. However, given the unphysical downward trend
of the first four Chi-Nu data points for Einc = 3.5 and 4.5 MeV, one could question if the observed
strong downward trend of Chi-Nu data (reproduced by the evaluation) below 2 MeV outgoing neutron
energies is really exclusively caused by the second-chance fission PFNS contribution, or if it is enhanced
by potential biases in the data.
 Also, the evaluated mean energies of the new evaluation in Fig. 5 have a surprisingly strong down-
ward trend for Einc =6.5–7.5 MeV. This trend agrees within uncertainties with Chi-Nu mean energy
data in the same outgoing neutron energy range, but the loss of mean energy at second chance fission
is quite dramatic. At this Einc , PFNS values can change distinctly. Hence, having Chi-Nu PFNS in
the future on the evaluation grid might help see if this trend holds when Chi-Nu data are given for
this new grid.
 The evaluated data for Einc =8–10.5 MeV agree with Chi-Nu well within their uncertainties but
differ distinctly from ENDF/B-VIII.1 These different trends are also reflected in a distinct drop of the
mean energy in the new versus the ENDF/B-VIII.1 evaluation (Fig. 5).
 The evaluated data for Einc =11.5–20 MeV (Figs. 6 and 7) agree well with experimental data and
ENDF/B-VIII.1. The one major difference between the two evaluations is the sharp pre-equilibrium
peak at higher outgoing neutron energies (approximately close to 10 MeV) that is only modeled for
this evaluation.

a factor, sU , given in Table 3. This factor was chosen such that the evaluated uncertainties shown in
the left-hand side of Figs. 8–9 are close to Chi-Nu uncertainties.

developed for ENDF/B-VIII.0 235 U and 239 Pu PFNS evaluations [7, 21]. These new evaluated PFNS

 The mean energies computed from the new evaluated PFNS are close to ENDF/B-VII.1=ENDF/B-
VIII.1 for Einc ≤ 4 MeV, but than differs distinctly from ENDF/B-VIII.1 mean energies except around
12.5–14.5 MeV, where the mean energies are closer again. Due to that, we see only minor changes in
neutron-leakage spectra of LLNL pulsed spheres simulated with ENDF/B-VIII.1 versus the new PFNS
evaluation. The keff values of most selected critical assemblies differ well within their respective 1-σ
Monte Carlo uncertainties. The only exception is Dirty Jezebel keff , where the differences is within
three σ of the MC uncertainties.
 Currently a new evaluation of 240 Pu in the fast energy range is ongoing at LANL. This new PFNS
is included in the testing of the effort. Changes in simulated Dirty Jezebel keff values away from unity
due to the PFNS can be counter-balanced with the prompt average neutron multiplicity or the cross
sections.
 One issue in this evaluation is that the experimental data are offset in incident neutron energy by
500 keV from the evaluation grid. While the evaluated PFNS agree with the experimental values when
transformed after the evaluation to the experiment grid, it would be cleaner to have the experimental
data on an incident neutron energy grid that agrees better with the evaluation grid. This statement is
especially true at the second-chance fission threshold where the PFNS changes rapidly. Right now, we
see a deep decline in the mean energy at this incident neutron energy. K.J. Kelly is re-running data to
be on the same grid as the evaluation to see if the grid mismatch could bias the evaluation at second
chance fission. If these new Chi-nu PFNS change the evaluation distinctly, an updated evaluation will
be provided. Otherwise, these new evaluated PFNS could be delivered to CSEWG.
