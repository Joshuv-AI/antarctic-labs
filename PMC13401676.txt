Chronic Diseases and Health Related Quality of Life in South and Southeast Asia: A Systematic Review of Burden, Interventions, and Measurement Tools
Authors: Fardina Rahman Omi, Farhana Zaman, SM Saidur Rahman Mashreky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn South and Southeast Asia, chronic diseases (CD) are a major public health concern and have a considerable impact on people's quality of life (QoL). CD and their ailments place a dual burden of sickness and socioeconomic difficulties on afflicted people and healthcare systems.AimThis review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of evidence on the relationship between chronic diseases and HRQoL in the region, focusing on study populations, disease types, methodologies, sample sizes, and assessment tools used.MethodsA systematic search was conducted to identify studies published between 2014 and 2024 that assessed HRQoL among patients with CD in South and Southeast Asia. Eligible studies included cross‐sectional, observational, prospective cohort, and economic evaluation designs involving conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus, sarcopenia, cardiovascular diseases, breast cancer, and hepatitis C. Data extracted included study design, sample size, participant characteristics, and validated HRQoL measurement instruments.ResultsTwenty‐five studies conducted across multiple South and Southeast Asian countries were included. Sample sizes ranged from 99 to 816 participants. A variety of validated instruments were used to assess HRQoL, including the St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ), Kidney Disease Quality of Life (KDQOL), WHOQOL‐BREF, and Short Form‐36 (SF‐36). Overall, CD were consistently associated with reduced HRQoL, with variations influenced by disease severity, comorbidities, and socioeconomic status. Specific challenges included reduced functional capacity among patients with sarcopenia and significant decisional conflict among patients with CKD. In addition, economic evaluations, particularly for hepatitis C treatments using direct‐acting antivirals, highlighted important cost‐effectiveness considerations.ConclusionCD negatively affect HRQoL, which is influenced by disease severity, sociocultural factors, and barriers to healthcare access. Community‐based disease management, telemedicine, culturally tailored health education, and financial protection policies may improve outcomes. Future studies should assess the effectiveness and scalability of these interventions across diverse regional settings.Keywords: chronic diseases, health related quality of life, review, South and Southeast Asia

Background

Noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), also known as chronic diseases, represent a major public health challenge in Southeast Asia, contributing substantially to morbidity, mortality, and economic burden [1, 2, 3]. The region has experienced a rapid epidemiological transition, leading to a marked increase in conditions such as cancer, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [4]. These conditions are largely driven by modifiable risk factors, including unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, tobacco use, and harmful alcohol consumption, which are further exacerbated by rapid urbanization and lifestyle changes [4, 5].

Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) is a critical multidimensional construct that reflects the physical, psychological, and social impacts of chronic diseases on individuals [6, 7]. Beyond individual health, the burden of NCDs extends to families, communities, and healthcare systems, placing sustained pressure on already resource‐constrained settings in Southeast Asia [8]. Understanding the relationship between chronic diseases and HRQoL is therefore essential for designing effective and context‐sensitive interventions, particularly in settings characterized by disparities in healthcare access, economic resources, and cultural perceptions of health.

According to estimates from the World Health Organization, cardiovascular diseases account for more than 60% of NCD‐related deaths in Southeast Asia [9]. Despite improvements in healthcare delivery, a substantial proportion of the population still lacks access to preventive, diagnostic, and long‐term management services [10]. Socioeconomic inequalities further compound this challenge, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations and intensifying the adverse consequences of chronic diseases [11, 12]. Moreover, the long‐term nature of NCD management imposes significant financial burdens, including high out‐of‐pocket expenditures and productivity losses [13].

A comprehensive assessment of the burden of chronic diseases and their impact on HRQoL requires an integrated approach that combines epidemiological evidence, patient‐reported outcomes, and economic evaluations [14, 15]. Such an approach facilitates the identification of high‐risk populations, informs intervention strategies, and supports the development of targeted health policies aimed at reducing risk factors, improving healthcare access, and enhancing quality of life.

This systematic review is conceptually grounded in the Wilson and Cleary Model of HRQoL, which conceptualizes health outcomes as a continuum linking biological and clinical variables with symptom status, functional status, and overall HRQoL. Within this framework, disease severity, multimorbidity, and clinical complications influence both physical and psychological functioning. To capture broader contextual influences, this review also incorporates the Social Determinants of Health framework, emphasizing the role of socioeconomic status, education, occupation, and healthcare access in shaping health outcomes. In the context of South and Southeast Asia, these structural determinants influence healthcare utilization, financial protection, and continuity of care.

In addition, cultural beliefs and health‐seeking behaviors play a significant role in shaping how individuals perceive illness and interact with healthcare systems. By integrating these theoretical perspectives, this review adopts a multidimensional framework in which clinical, behavioral, and structural factors interact to influence HRQoL outcomes. This integrated conceptual framework provides the foundation for understanding how the burden of chronic diseases is embedded within broader socioeconomic and contextual realities (Figure 1).

Integrated conceptual framework of determinants influencing health‐related quality of life (HRQoL).

Understanding the impact of NCDs on HRQoL is essential for informing public health policies in Southeast Asia. Strong and context‐specific evidence is needed to guide strategies that not only reduce the prevalence and severity of chronic diseases but also enhance resilience and overall well‐being. Considering the region's diverse and evolving healthcare landscape, this review aims to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the burden, determinants, and potential intervention pathways related to NCDs and HRQoL.

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the appropriate study‐specific risk of bias tools (e.g., Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for observational studies and relevant appraisal tools for qualitative studies). Each study was independently assessed by two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used for assessing the quality of the included cohort and cross‐sectional studies. We did not find any case control study matched with our outcome of interest. We used NOS as a foundation to evaluate the quality of cross‐sectional studies informed by earlier work. The tools included an assessment of selection, measurement and attrition bias, and comparability. Each item of the NOS was assigned one or two points. The maximum score possible for a given study was nine points.

Data Management and Synthesis

A systematic review management program (Microsoft Excel) was used for data extraction and organization. Relevant data were extracted independently by two reviewers to minimize bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer.

Given the expected heterogeneity in study designs, populations, chronic disease categories, and outcome measures, a narrative synthesis approach was adopted for data synthesis. Given the heterogeneity of included studies in terms of study design, chronic disease categories, and HRQoL measurement tools, a meta‐analysis was not considered appropriate. The included studies used different HRQoL instruments, including SF‐36, EQ‐5D, and disease‐specific tools such as KDQOL, which limited direct statistical pooling of results. This approach was considered appropriate as statistical pooling was not feasible due to variability in study characteristics and HRQoL measurement tools.

For synthesis, studies were first categorized according to type of chronic disease, geographical setting, and study design. This structured grouping allowed for systematic comparison of findings across different disease conditions and contexts.

Where qualitative or mixed‐methods data were available, thematic analysis was conducted to identify recurring patterns related to HRQoL, risk factors, and intervention outcomes. Key themes were compared across studies to identify consistency and variations in findings.

Differences in outcomes across studies were explored by considering variations in study quality, population characteristics, disease severity, and measurement instruments used to assess HRQoL.

The review was guided by an integrated conceptual framework combining the Wilson and Cleary model of HRQoL and the Social Determinants of Health framework. These frameworks informed both data extraction and thematic categorization by providing a structured approach to link clinical conditions, functional status, and broader socio‐economic and health system factors influencing HRQoL outcomes.

Results

Study Selection

The process of searching turned out 1550 documents in all, 1500 of which were from database searches and 50 more from other sources. There were still 1200 records for screening after 350 duplicate records were eliminated. Nine hundred records that did not fulfill the inclusion criteria were excluded from the first screening step, which involved reviewing 1200 records based on titles and abstracts.

Three hundred full‐text articles were scrutinized to determine eligibility. 200 of them were disqualified for the following reasons: (i) Not connected to results pertaining to HRQoL. (ii) not carried out in Southeast Asia, and (iii) Low quality research (lack of representativeness, insufficient outcome measurement), as judged by the standards for quality evaluation

Eventually, a total of 144 papers were included in the qualitative synthesis after meeting the inclusion criteria. Out of these, 25 studies provided sufficient data for inclusion in quantitative synthesis.

The study selection process was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [16], including identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion stages. Details are shown in Figure 2.

To enhance interpretability, studies were synthesized thematically based on disease category and HRQoL domain. Despite variability in study design and measurement tools, a consistent pattern of reduced HRQoL was observed across all chronic disease groups. Physical health was the most consistently affected domain, followed by psychological and social well‐being.

HRQoL Rankings and the Mean Scores

Studies use a range of HRQoL measures, including the EQ‐5D [19, 24, 27, 38], KDQOL‐36 [25, 32, 36], SF‐12, and SF‐36 [17, 23, 28]. WHOQOL‐BREF [18, 30], EORTC QLQ‐C30 [21, 29], and disease‐specific instruments like the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire [39, 41] and the St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire [31, 33, 35, 40].

The range of mean HRQoL scores is typically 54–75, which indicates that the quality of life varies according to the kind of chronic disease, research population, and scale employed. The lower ratings, especially for hemodialysis patients with CKD [34] and palliative patients [29], indicate significant effects on HRQoL because of treatment loads and disease severity.

Affected Domains

All chronic illnesses have an influence on physical health domains, and these limitations are frequently accompanied by pain or discomfort, functional ability, and mobility restrictions.

The psychological toll of chronic diseases is reflected in the emotional and mental health domains, which are commonly impacted by ailments including heart failure, breast cancer, and among caregivers.

Important HRQoL factors include social ties and support, particularly for patients and caretakers of illnesses requiring significant lifestyle modifications (e.g., COPD, sarcopenia).

Key Outcomes and Implications for Specific Diseases

Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary illness (COPD)

A number of research (e.g., on Thai men with COPD and patients who do not smoke) show that the most impacted domain is physical health, with low scores suggesting a higher burden of the illness and decreased functional ability [18].

Research on people with CKD regularly reports a worse HRQoL, especially in terms of overall health and physical functioning. These effects are compounded by age, anemia, and dialysis; elderly patients still have a worse quality of life even with therapy [25, 34, 41].

Heart Diseases

Comorbid disorders such as anemia significantly impair HRQoL ratings for heart failure patients, while caregivers of heart disease patients report a worse quality of life because of caring obligations [37].

Diabetes

The evidence suggests that HRQoL among patients with type 2 diabetes is moderately compromised, particularly in physical discomfort and psychological well‐being domains. Determinants span both clinical and socio‐demographic factors [38].

Cancer and Palliative Care

For cancer patients and those undergoing palliative care, physical symptoms and emotional functioning are significant issues [29]. This suggests that combined medical and psychological assistance is necessary.

Varicose Veins and Hepatitis C

Treatment treatments (e.g., VenaSeal for varicose veins and direct‐acting antivirals for hepatitis C) demonstrated increases in HRQoL, indicating the potential advantages of successful disease management on quality of life [28]. Sarcopenia: Inflammation was associated with a worse HRQoL in older people with sarcopenia because of a decrease in their functional ability [30]. This finding emphasizes the importance that systemic inflammation plays in health outcomes.

Cross‐Cultural Aspects and Regional Perspectives

The variations in HRQoL metrics and average scores throughout Southeast Asian nations (such as Thailand, Vietnam, Malaysia, Singapore, and Cambodia) are indicative of variations in healthcare accessibility, socioeconomic status, and culture. These elements probably affect how patients view their quality of life and react to treatment for chronic illnesses. Certain research (like those on breast cancer in Southeast Asia) span several nations, indicating that despite regional variations, these issues are similar. International research can assist in identifying common obstacles and guiding regional chronic illness treatment strategies.

Mortality and Prevalence

According to the available evidence, there is a notable prevalence of chronic illnesses such as cancer, diabetes, heart failure, CKD, and COPD in Southeast Asia. The incidence of COPD is reported to be approximately 15% among nonsmokers [19], while around 30% of patients present with heart failure and anemia [28]. In addition, CKD and varicose veins are also commonly reported (Table 3).

However, studies reporting mortality data are limited. Available evidence indicates that a proportion of patients receiving palliative care (40%) [41] and those undergoing dialysis (25%–30%) [24] show notable mortality‐related outcomes.

A key limitation identified in this review is the inconsistent reporting of mortality rates across included studies, as reflected by multiple instances of missing data. This gap likely reflects broader challenges in epidemiological data collection in South and Southeast Asia, including weak health information systems, underreporting, and limited longitudinal follow‐up. The absence of reliable mortality data restricts a comprehensive assessment of disease burden and highlights the need for improved surveillance systems and standardized reporting.

Major Risk Elements

Aging, poor diet, air pollution, comorbidities (such as heart disease and CKD), and limited access to healthcare services are examples of common risk factors. These elements increase the burden of disease, which lowers life expectancy and raises death rates.

Risk factors for diseases including hepatitis C and sarcopenia include age, inflammation, and intravenous drug use [29, 30].

Significant Burden Findings

The quality of life is sometimes greatly reduced by chronic illnesses because of physical restrictions, the financial strain of treatment (such as cancer treatments or dialysis), and the psychological effects of having a chronic disease.

The high prevalence of COPD in nonsmokers, decreased HRQoL because of problems from hemodialysis, and improvements in quality of life after antiviral medication for hepatitis C with VenaSeal are among the specific results.

Improvements in the Area to HRQoL

For all chronic illnesses, the quality of life connected to health improved because of the treatments. Enhanced mental and physical health, symptom relief, and improved illness management were typical results (Table 4).

Studies with weaker adjustment for confounding or limited sample representativeness may have influenced the magnitude of reported HRQoL associations. Discussion

This systematic review shows that chronic diseases substantially reduce HRQoL across South and Southeast Asia. The impact is seen across physical, psychological, and social domains, with physical functioning affected most consistently. Conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, CKD, cancer, COPD, and hepatitis C were repeatedly linked to poorer HRQoL. Overall, the findings reflect the long‐standing burden of chronic illness in health systems where care is often delayed, fragmented, and difficult to sustain.

The Wilson and Cleary model helps explain these patterns by linking biological dysfunction to symptoms, functional limitations, and ultimately quality of life [42]. In the included studies, worse disease severity and the presence of multiple conditions were closely associated with reduced physical function and overall well‐being [42, 43]. This pattern supports the idea that HRQoL declines gradually as illness progresses and daily functioning becomes more restricted.

Cultural beliefs also play an important role in shaping how people respond to chronic illness in this region. In many communities, long‐term conditions are accepted as part of life or understood through traditional explanations. This often influences how symptoms are interpreted and when medical care is sought. Several studies reported that people delay visiting formal health services and instead rely on informal or home‐based care. These delays were commonly linked with poorer HRQoL [44]. This shows that health outcomes are not only determined by disease itself but also by how illness is understood within families and communities.

Health system limitations further contribute to the problem. In many countries, chronic disease care depends heavily on out‐of‐pocket spending, as universal health coverage is still incomplete. This creates financial pressure for patients and families, often leading to delayed treatment or interruptions in care. The burden is not only economic; it also affects mental and social well‐being, which further reduces HRQoL. Evidence from several studies supports the link between weak financial protection and greater inequality in chronic disease outcomes [45].

Compared with high‐income countries, HRQoL outcomes in this region appear lower. This difference is likely related to stronger health systems elsewhere, with better access to continuous care and wider insurance coverage. In contrast, limited‐service availability and financial barriers in South and Southeast Asia make long‐term disease management more difficult, which affects overall quality of life.

Despite these challenges, some studies show that HRQoL can improve when appropriate interventions are in place. Treatments such as antiviral therapy for hepatitis C, improved dialysis approaches, and structured chronic disease management programs were associated with better quality of life outcomes [46, 47]. This suggests that while chronic diseases have a long‐term impact, their effects on quality of life can be reduced with timely and well‐organized care.

One limitation of this review is the variation in HRQoL measurement tools across studies, including SF‐36, EQ‐5D, and KDQOL. Because these tools differ in structure and scoring, it is difficult to directly compare results across studies. Some of the variations in HRQoL scores may therefore reflect measurement differences rather than true differences in health status. A formal certainty‐of‐evidence framework (such as GRADE) was not applied in this review, which may limit the ability to rate overall confidence in the findings. Future reviews should consider structured certainty‐of‐evidence approaches to further strengthen the robustness of synthesis.

Overall, the findings show that, HRQoL in South and Southeast Asia is shaped by a combination of disease severity, cultural practices, and health system constraints. The Wilson and Cleary model helps to explain how biological illness translates into functional and quality‐of‐life outcomes. Improving HRQoL will require not only better clinical care but also stronger health systems, improved financial protection, and approaches that take local cultural contexts into account.

Conclusion

The review found that chronic diseases have a negative impact on HRQoL in South and Southeast Asia. Drivers of this impact appear to include case severity, sociocultural perceptions of disease, and weaknesses in healthcare systems that hinder access to high‐quality services. The latter underscores that HRQoL is affected not just by disease characteristics, but also by the ways in which care is organized, financed, and delivered.

This speaks to larger issues in health system performance regarding access to services, continuity of care, and financial protection. In countries with incomplete universal health coverage systems and high out‐of‐pocket expenditure on health care, patients may face long wait times to receive care and fragmented care for ongoing treatment. When health care is accessible, these barriers may lead to poorer HRQoL.

Here are some examples of interventions with the potential to address these challenges and improve the HRQoL of people living with chronic diseases in Asia. Community‐based chronic disease management programs can help facilitate follow‐up, medication adherence, and self‐management for patients with single or multiple chronic conditions. Innovations like mobile clinics and telemedicine may increase access to care for marginalized populations and those living in remote areas, while reducing the time and cost associated with seeking care. Disease‐specific health education programs and interventions may also promote disease knowledge and healthier lifestyles. Health education can include community‐based awareness campaigns and provision of culturally tailored education materials to people living with chronic diseases. Task‐shifting approaches that utilize trained nurses, community health workers, and other lay health workers may be of particular importance for resource‐limited settings. Financial protection is also key for improving access to long‐term care and should be addressed through policies that aim to reduce out‐of‐pocket expenses. These interventions may improve disease management, healthcare access, and overall quality of life. Future research should evaluate the effectiveness and scalability of these strategies across diverse settings in South and Southeast Asia.
