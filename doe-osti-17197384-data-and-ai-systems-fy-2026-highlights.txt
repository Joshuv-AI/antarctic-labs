Drive Time: Mapping Access to Services and Care

 How long it takes to drive somewhere, and how that varies across the
country, underpins questions of access and equity. The Drive Time project
builds tables that answer a deceptively simple question, how far any location
in the continental United States is from the services its residents need.
 The first dataset is a general-purpose routing table for the continental
United States, giving drive times between locations at several levels of geo-
graphic detail. Areas with dense road networks and many destinations are
represented precisely, while long routes and remote areas with sparse infras-
tructure are summarized more coarsely, which keeps the dataset manageable
without losing accuracy where it matters. Five regional datasets covering key
 Regions colored by their nearest facility.
areas of interest are underway.
 The second measures access to Department of Veterans Affairs healthcare,
calculating the fastest drive time from every populated location in the con-
tinental United States to VA sites nationwide. These tables are refreshed
quarterly as facilities and roads change, so that researchers and planners can
see where veterans face long drives for care, and can model how opening
or closing a facility would affect coverage. Both rest on the H3 geospatial
indexing system, which divides the country into consistent hexagonal grids at Jeremy Logan

DataFed: The Data Spine for Biopreparedness Research

cause disease and spread, and predicting how a host and pathogen interact
means pulling together data from many disciplines and scales. NW-BRaVE, the

and ORNL’s DataFed service is its data spine. DataFed tracks, shares, and

Hybrid Learning for Spiking Neural Networks

 Spiking neural networks carry information in timed pulses, as biological
neurons do, and promise far lower energy use on suitable hardware. Training
them is the open problem, because the methods that work for conventional
networks do not transfer.
 This activity, part of the ENGAGE project led by Prasanna Date, tests a
hybrid approach on the Cora citation benchmark, where the task is to classify
a paper by topic from the papers it cites. Two mechanisms divide the work. An
evolutionary algorithm searches more than 10,000 synaptic weights, guided
by how well the network classifies, while spike timing dependent plasticity
adjusts about 20,000 further synapses locally, in response to the activity the
network itself produces. Global search and local adaptation therefore shape Evolution tunes some synapses, spike timing the
the network together. rest.

 The implementation couples the LEAP evolutionary computation frame-
work with SuperNeuroABM, ORNL’s open-source spiking network simulator,
and distributes evaluation across supercomputers including Frontier. It estab-
lishes a foundation for measuring how accuracy, optimization behavior, and
cost respond as the two mechanisms interact. Energy efficiency gains remain
to be demonstrated, but the methods matter for judging alternative computing Mark Coletti

ENLIGHT: Cutting the Energy Cost of Large Language Models

 Running large language models (LLMs) now accounts for much of the
energy AI data centers consume. Analog in-memory computing performs
their core arithmetic quickly and efficiently, but several steps of a modern
model do not map onto analog hardware, each forcing data back into digital
form at a cost that can exceed the computation itself.
 ENLIGHT designs algorithms, circuits, architecture, and simulation tools
together to keep as much of the model as possible analog. A new attention The analog Softmax barely changes model loss.
method (how a model relates distant parts of its input) keeps one compressed
memory of recent input and another of the beginning, scoring 0.68 on a hard
retrieval benchmark against 0.59 and 0.54 for existing methods. An analog
circuit for Softmax, a step that usually leaves analog hardware, cost a simu-
lated model only about 2.5% in validation loss, and analog normalization left
the accuracy of Llama-3.2-1B virtually unchanged. Full-system simulations
showed that converting between analog and digital accounts for 63 to 68%
of the energy, making conversion, not the analog core, the bottleneck. The
goal is a hundredfold cut in the energy of running an LLM, making capable
models practical at scientific facilities and on edge devices with scarce power.

Turning Operations Data into Intelligence

 Operational knowledge at a national laboratory is scattered across reports,
regulations, drawings, safety records, and aging computer systems. Finding
the right information can take hours, and turning it into a decision takes
expert effort that is always in short supply.
 AI4LabOps builds trustworthy AI tools and workflows that turn that frag-
mented material into clear guidance. The tools help staff locate engineering
information, understand chemical hazards, identify high-risk equipment and
property, assess changing regulations, improve procurement quality, and
surface safety lessons buried in field reports. They do not replace expertise,
because every result is traceable to its source and reviewed by someone who
 The AI4LabOps ecosystem, from data to decisions.
knows the subject.
 Six applications are already deployed and more are in development. By
cutting repetitive searching and paperwork, they return time to researchers,
safety professionals, engineers, and operations leaders, and faster access
to reliable evidence supports safer work, stronger compliance, and better
planning. The approach is spreading beyond ORNL through demonstrations
and partnerships with PNNL, SRNL, and INL, which are forming a community
of practice and consortium, SAILOR, for smart AI in laboratory operations. Sanjay Das

EESAV: Energy-Efficient Sensing for Autonomous Vehicles

 Autonomous vehicles see the world through conventional cameras and
dense neural networks, a combination that costs energy and computing
power a vehicle has little of to spare. Neuromorphic sensing takes its cue from
biological vision instead, treating change as sparse electrical events rather
than analyzing complete image frames over and over.
 Through the Energy Efficient Mobility Systems effort, we are co-designing
event-based sensing, neuromorphic algorithms, and edge hardware for lower-
power perception, and ObstacleSense shows what that buys. It turns the
asynchronous output of an event camera into a compact picture of the driving
corridor ahead. Leaky integrate-and-fire spiking neurons keep the timing
information, while lightweight spatial processing decides whether an obstacle
 Event-camera input with detections and distances.
sits in the vehicle’s path and how far away it is. The design follows what the
driving task actually needs rather than reconstructing the whole visual scene.
 Early experiments support driver-assistance tasks such as adaptive cruise
control with models roughly 35 to 40 times smaller than small camera-based
detectors, and point toward inference above 30 Hz on CPUs and edge hard-
ware rather than on power-hungry GPUs.

System-Centric Analytics for Veteran Mental Health

 The Department of Veterans Affairs treats veterans for major depressive
disorder and post-traumatic stress disorder. Standard metrics have severely
underestimated how well psychotherapy works, because sicker patients are
assigned more sessions, which makes the true effect of treatment hard to
measure.
 Our team applied causal modeling to records covering more than 172,000
major depressive disorder events and extensive PTSD care trajectories across
the Veterans Health Administration, separating workforce constraints from
clinical outcomes. What selection bias hid about psychotherapy.
 Expanding dedicated staffing increases how often acute sessions fall in
the critical window of 14 to 60 days after diagnosis, and that early density
translates into measurable relief. Each additional acute visit within 60 days
reduces six-month PTSD symptom scores by more than three points. Session
spacing is only an administrative buffer, since cumulative early volume drives
recovery.
 The VA can now set severity-adjusted staffing benchmarks and two-stage
performance metrics that remove structural bottlenecks.

Privacy-Preserving Federated Learning for Science

 Some of the most valuable scientific data in the country cannot be shared.
A manufacturer cannot release the process data behind a proprietary alloy,
and a user facility cannot export readings covered by agreements with the
researchers who produced them. Modern AI draws its power from scale, so
the rules that protect this data keep it out of the datasets AI depends on.
 Instead of gathering data in one place, we send the model to the data.
Each institution trains on its own records and shares only what was learned,
never the records themselves. The research is in making that trustworthy,
because what a model learns can still hint at what it saw, a released model What makes learning without sharing work.

can be copied without credit, and any system open to many participants can
be probed or manipulated. We build safeguards for each.
 Institutions that could never pool their data can now build models together,
each contributing to a result none could reach alone. Every participant sets
privacy protection to its own rules rather than one shared standard, guided
by a tool called PRESTO that won a 2025 R&D 100 Award. Models carry
a mark tracing them back to their source, so contributing earns credit, and
we find weaknesses before an adversary does. All of it runs at full scale on
Frontier, extending this year to European supercomputers, and everything is Olivera Kotevska

VisTrust: Showing Uncertainty in Scientific Visualization

 Scientists rely on visualizations to find important structures in their data.
But those pictures usually draw each structure as if its position and shape were
certain, even though measurements and simulations always carry noise. That
missing uncertainty can hide plausible alternatives and give false confidence.
 This Early Career Research Project develops methods for measuring and
showing that uncertainty in two- and three-dimensional visualizations. One
recent result, MAGIC, makes this practical at scale. Instead of estimating
uncertainty by running many random samples, MAGIC uses closed-form
models that also capture how nearby data values relate. Built into the widely
used Viskores library, it runs up to 585 times faster, fast enough to show
uncertainty in near real time. The team demonstrated it on urban airflow
simulations, where confidence in wind features around tall buildings bears
on engineering and safety decisions.
 The project is also building a community around trustworthy visualization.

CIPHER Phenotype Community Explorer

 The Centralized Interactive Phenomics Resource (CIPHER) is a knowledge-sharing platform developed by the U.S. Depart-
ment of Veterans Affairs to advance the use of electronic health record data in research and healthcare. Its knowledgebase
brings together phenotype definitions, metadata, data mappings, programming resources, and tools. By making these
resources easier to find, understand, and reuse, CIPHER promotes reproducibility, interoperability, and collaboration across
the health data community.
 My work extends this mission with a visual analytics tool that helps researchers understand relationships across CIPHER’s
phenotype definitions. Traditional search locates known items, but it may not reveal how definitions connect, where clinical
themes overlap, or which areas contain closely related work. The explorer transforms the collection into a connected,
interpretable landscape that users can examine at multiple levels.
 Researchers can identify groups of related phenotypes, examine neighboring records in context, and move from visual
patterns to detailed entries. Community summaries make themes easier to recognize, while accessible colors and flexible
interactions suit a broad range of users. These capabilities reduce the time needed to locate definitions, support more
informed selection and comparison, and reveal relationships hidden in record-by-record review. By improving discovery
and understanding across the knowledgebase, this work can reduce duplicated effort, encourage reuse of knowledge, and
strengthen collaboration. Ultimately, it supports more efficient phenotype development and more reproducible health research.

EVEREST: A Room Built for Seeing Big Data

 The supercomputers at Oak Ridge National Laboratory can simulate ev-
erything from fusion plasmas to the global climate, but their results arrive as
datasets far too large and too detailed for any ordinary monitor to show. A
scientist at a laptop sees only a keyhole view of a result spanning billions of
data points, and important features get lost.
 EVEREST, the Exploratory Visualization Environment for Research in Sci-
ence and Technology, is the laboratory’s answer. Part of the Oak Ridge Lead-
ership Computing Facility (OLCF), it is a room built for seeing big data. Its
primary display wall is a 32-by-9-foot expanse of eighteen panels showing
more than 37 million pixels at once, with a second wall of equal resolution
beside it. A dedicated cluster of GPU-rich nodes drives these walls in real
time, so teams can pan, zoom, and explore simulation output at a scale that The EVEREST wall showing a cosmology simulation.
reveals patterns a single screen would hide. The space is built for people to
work together, with cameras, microphones, and streaming that let on-site and
remote collaborators study the same visualization live.
 EVEREST is also a testbed for the next generation of visualization. Re-

Eight Dimensions of AI-Ready Scientific Data

 Simulations and experiments rarely produce data an AI workflow can use directly. Much of the scientific meaning lives in
specialized scripts or in an expert’s head rather than in machine-readable metadata, and data can be findable and accessible
while still lacking the quantities of interest, normalization, provenance, and quality controls a particular AI task needs.
 In the LLNL Digital Twin project we defined data readiness as eight measurable dimensions that extend FAIR while accepting
that readiness depends on the science and the intended use. We then built a three-layer workflow. Schema-driven shims enrich
the data with metadata, PULSE campaign management organizes runs and experiments as federated searchable collections,
and a final layer extracts the features and quantities of interest that make a task-specific, model-ready representation. Applied
to fusion fuel-cycle simulations and to heterogeneous diagnostics from laser experiments, it supported surrogate models and
a real-time controller, and cut the local data footprint by two to four orders of magnitude. Readiness, measured before and
after, is a property of the whole data lifecycle rather than a file format.

SIRIUS: Smaller Data Without Losing the Science

 Our data reduction research this year made scientific data smaller and faster to move without losing what scientists
need from it. CAESAR combines variational autoencoders, super-resolution, and conditional diffusion to compress large
spatiotemporal datasets, reaching compression up to ten times higher than rule-based compressors such as SZ3, with a GPU
postprocessing step providing rigorous error control.
 HP-MDR addresses a different need, letting scientists retrieve only the precision an analysis requires. Its portable GPU
implementation accelerated refactoring and progressive retrieval by up to 6.6 times, improved reconstruction under error
control by up to 10.4 times, and delivered up to a 4.2 times end-to-end speedup. Lossy compression of simulation checkpoints
is harder, because standard error controls can disturb physical invariants and let errors grow after a restart. Separating
kinetic and potential energy and applying appropriate MGARD error controls preserved stability across repeated restarts at
compression as high as 238 times, while speeding checkpoint reads and writes on Frontier.

ABOUND: Taming the Bursts That Damage Fusion Devices

 Large bursts at the edge of a fusion plasma, known as edge-localized modes, can batter the walls of a reactor. What decides
whether a device settles into small, benign bursts instead is one of the questions standing between today’s experiments and a
working power plant. ABOUND is building the physics understanding and the predictive tool to answer it. The DOE Office of
Science has highlighted the project’s work on DIII-D as a physics-based route to protecting future devices from damaging
bursts.
 Turning that understanding into something usable means simulating far more than a physicist can run by hand. The
project ran millions of simulations across tens of thousands of equilibrium states of the DIII-D experiment to build an AI-ready
database, and trained a surrogate model that gives uncertainty-aware predictions relevant to control. ORNL supplies the
computing behind that campaign: ADIOS to couple the separate codes that together simulate a whole device, EFFIS to run the
ensembles and extract and archive what matters, and HPC-CAMPAIGN to organize the data products for long-term storage,
public release, and remote access.

remain stable is central to designing one, and the detailed physics simulation

explore. The Edge Learning and Modeling Orchestrator (ELMO) uses the
EFFIS workflow system to replace most of those simulations with a surrogate,

around the reactor, and the level of physics detail. It tracked finished runs,
restarted failed ones, saved diagnostic images, and scheduled the analysis that
prepares the training data, so the campaign ran without anyone supervising
each step. From simulation campaign to trained surrogate.

SWARM: Distributed Intelligence for Resilient Workflows

 Scientific workflows now stretch from instruments at the edge to leadership-
class supercomputers, yet the strategies that keep them running through
failures are mostly static, rule-based, and dependent on a single central
controller. SWARM explores whether swarm intelligence, where many simple
agents coordinate without a leader, can keep DOE workflows fast and fault
tolerant across that continuum.
 This year, ORNL designed and evaluated a fully decentralized scheduler
for a federation of leadership computing facilities, in which an agent at each
site bids for incoming jobs and the sites agree on where each one runs. We
compared three bidding strategies, a hand-engineered heuristic, one based on
learned job embeddings, and one that reasons with a large language model, Agents at each site bid for incoming jobs.
in high-fidelity simulations of realistic facility workloads. The decentralized
scheduler matched the quality of a centralized one while removing its single
point of failure, and letting jobs migrate between sites removed queue wait
time and cut median turnaround more than threefold. The embedding and
language-model strategies also improved scheduling without hand-tuned
rules, pointing toward infrastructure that adapts on its own to failures.
