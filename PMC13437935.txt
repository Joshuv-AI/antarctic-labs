Compound Chamomile and Lidocaine Hydrochloride Gel in Oral Diseases: A Review of Current Evidence and Potential Applications
Authors: Zhongli Wang, Xuelian Zeng, Yingli Song, Miaomiao Li, Shuang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral diseases frequently entail microbial infections or mucosal damage, typically accompanied by pain and discomfort. Present pharmaceutical interventions for oral conditions in clinical settings are constrained by their singular therapeutic effects, insufficient pain relief, and practical inconveniences. The exploration of novel compound medications holds promise for augmenting clinical treatment outcomes. Compound chamomile and lidocaine hydrochloride gel (CCLHG) represents a composite formulation endowed with analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and wound healing properties, showcasing auspicious potential in oral disease management. This article summarizes the articles of CCLHG in treating various oral diseases. Electronic databases PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Wangfang, China National Knowledge Infrastructure and China Science and Technology Journal Database were searched from inception to August 30, 2025. A total of 1413 articles were searched. 27 articles were finally included. Our analysis reveals preliminary therapeutic potential of CCLHG in managing periodontal diseases, oral mucosal conditions, pericoronitis, dry socket syndrome, peri-implantitis, and related maladies. However, given the overall limited sample sizes, variability in methodology, and generally moderate-to-low quality of the included studies, these findings warrant caution. Further high-quality investigations are therefore essential to substantiate its clinical utility.

Introduction

According to estimates by the World Health Organization, nearly 3.5 billion people globally are affected by oral diseases.1 Dental caries, periodontal diseases, lip and oral cancer, oral mucosal diseases, oral traumas, and other oral conditions represent public health challenges worldwide.2,3 These oral diseases often involve microbial infections or mucosal damage, typically resulting in varying degrees of pain, effective management necessitates controlling microbial infections or local inflammation, as well as utilizing medications to promote wound healing or provide pain relief during treatment.4–8

Medications such as Kangfuxin liquid and basic fibroblast growth factor gel are commonly used to facilitate local wound healing within the oral cavity.9–13 Glucocorticoids, such as triamcinolone acetonide,8,14–18 are frequently employed for local anti-inflammatory purposes, while chlorhexidine mouthwash and tetracycline hydrochloride ointment are often used for treating oral microbial infections.19–22 Despite the widespread clinical use of these medications, their efficacy is often limited, with suboptimal pain relief, thereby necessitating the combination of multiple drugs.4,13 While novel composite nanomaterials or compound medications are currently under development, they have not yet entered clinical use.23 Therefore, there is a pressing need for new compound medications to treat oral diseases.

Compound chamomile and lidocaine hydrochloride gel (CCLHG), also known as Kamistad gel, is a composite formulation that combines analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and wound healing properties.24–26 It has garnered increasing attention from oral health researchers in recent years and has been approved for marketing in several European and Asian countries, positioning it as a potential treatment for conditions such as periodontal diseases, oral mucosal lesions, pericoronitis, dry socket syndrome, peri‑implantitis, and related maladies. However, the existing literature on this gel is limited in quantity, diverse in focus, and lacks comprehensive reviews or systematic summaries of clinical usage methods and efficacy. This article aims to review preclinical and clinical studies of CCLHG in the field of oral health, identifying potential research directions.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies about composition, administration, and pharmacological research of CCLHG; (2) studies focusing on oral diseases; (3) CCLHG or CCLHG combined with conventional drugs were used; (4) data availability, including efficacy and safety; (5) preclinical or clinical studies and (6) English or Chinese publications.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, letters and public database analyses; (2) unavailable outcomes and (3) duplicate studies.

Composition, Administration and Pharmacological Research of CCLHG

Composition of CCLHG

CCLHG is manufactured by STADA Arzneimittel AG in Germany and presents as a homogeneous yellow gel. Its formulation primarily includes lidocaine hydrochloride (20mg/g), chamomile flower extract (185mg or 200mg/g), musk xylene (1.0 mg/g), benzalkonium chloride (1.0 mg/g), polyacrylic acid, sodium saccharin, propylene glycol, and cinnamon oil.

Clinical Process of CCLHG

To apply, expose the affected area, clean away any saliva, and ensure the site remains clear and dry. Squeeze out approximately 0.5 cm of the gel (about the size of a pea), and apply it evenly to the affected area, and gently massaging it in. Allow the medication to be fully absorbed before consuming water or food. It is recommended to use it three times daily, avoiding excessive frequency or dosage. It is advised for use by adults or older children, with caution taken to prevent accidental ingestion in younger children. The length of gel used should not exceed 0.5 cm, and the frequency should be strictly controlled (not more than three times daily). This product is contraindicated for patients with allergies to any of its component.27–29

Pharmacodynamics and Toxicology of CCLHG

The primary active ingredients of this gel are lidocaine hydrochloride gel and chamomile extract.

Lidocaine is a commonly used local anesthetic that primarily exerts its analgesic effects by blocking the generation and conduction of nerve impulses.30 Studies have shown that lidocaine can inhibit bacteria such as Neisseria species, Escherichia coli, Group A Streptococcus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.31–33 In gel form, it provides rapid pain relief and antibacterial effects. Existing literature supports the safety and reliability of lidocaine both as a local anesthetic or for oral use.34–36 Additionally, as CCLHG is intended for local application, the amount absorbed into the bloodstream is minimal, making the risk of lidocaine toxicity manageable when used according to clinical recommendation.

Chamomile tincture, also known as mother’s daisy or German chamomile, contains various pharmacologically ingredients, including bisabolol, chamazulene, and apigenin, all of which are essential. It demonstrates significant anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-allergic, antimicrobial, and analgesic properties.37,38 Research by Mohsenzadeh-Ledari et al has also indicated that chamomile has anxiolytic effects.39 Although no studies have pointed the primary therapeutic compounds in CCLHG. Its broad spectrum of activity suggests to a likely multicomponent synergy. Chamomile is considered a safe food ingredient, commonly used in teas and health products, with its essential oils and extracts classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the US Food and Drug Administration.37,40

Thymol, a phenol derivative, possesses disinfectant and preservative properties. Studies have demonstrated that thymol possesses significant anti-inflammatory, antibacterial (against Staphylococcus aureus, fungi), and antiviral activities.41–44 Thymol is considered safe as a natural flavoring or preservative and also has neuroprotective properties.41,45

Benzalkonium chloride, a quaternary ammonium compound, serves as a preservative and has broad-spectrum antibacterial activity.46 It significantly inhibits the growth of common pathogens such as yeast, Candida albicans, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus, Klebsiella, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa.47–51 It promotes metabolism and wound healing. However, long-term oral ingestion may disrupt the intestinal flora.52 Therefore, when using CCLHG locally, care should be taken to minimize swallowing the medication.

In vitro studies support the ability of CCLHG to inhibit the growth of bacteria and fungi. Strilets et al compared the antibacterial activity of CCLHG, Aloedental, and Metrogil Denta gels against Staphylococcus aureus, B. subtilis, Escherichia coli, and Candida albicans. And showed that CCLHG significantly inhibited the growth of these four bacterial strains, with inhibition zone diameters of 17.5 ± 0.2 mm, 16.6 ± 0.2 mm, 16.2 ± 0.5 mm, and 15.8 ± 0.3 mm, respectively.53 Kulik et al evaluated the antibacterial and antifungal activities of CCLHG, Holisal gel, and Asepta parodontal gel, finding that Staphylococcus aureus and Candida albicans were sensitive to CCLHG, showing inhibition zones of 22–23 mm and 19–21 mm, respectively, while the growth of Pseudomonas aeruginosa was unaffected.54

CCLHG has shown the ability to reduce inflammatory reactions in ulcer areas, promote fibrous tissue proliferation, and enhance healing in vivo. Miao et al constructed a mouse tongue traumatic ulcer model using circular plastic rods and compared the effects of observation (control group), CCLHG, and phycocyanin on vascular parameters of the ulcer surface. The researchers analyzed hemodynamics, vascular structure, vascular formation, inflammatory changes, ulcer healing rate, and weight on days 2, 3, 5, and 7. The study found that by day 7, blood circulation, vascular density, average diameter, and maximum diameter of the ulcer surface were significantly enhanced in the CCLHG group compared to the control group, with milder ulcer severity, reduced subepithelial inflammatory activity, and less weight loss. Furthermore, the ulcer healing rate in the CCLHG group was significantly higher than control and phycocyanin groups, demonstrating the efficacy of CCLHG in treating mouse tongue traumatic ulcers.55 Wang et al constructed a mouse oral ulcer model using chemical burns and divided the mice into four treatment groups: CCLHG, Beifuxin gel, lidocaine gel, and saline, applied to the ulcer surface three times daily. On the first day of treatment, the CCLHG groups showed a significant lower number of mouth wipes within 3 minutes compared to the Beifuxin gel and saline groups. By the third and fifth days of treatment, the ulcer area in the CCLHG and Beifuxin groups was significantly smaller than in the lidocaine and saline groups. Histological examination on day five indicated that the ulcer surfaces in the CCLHG and Beifuxin groups were essentially healed, exhibiting fibroblast proliferation and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration. Additionally, levels of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and interleukin (IL)-6 were significantly lower in the CCLHG and Beifuxin groups compared to the other two groups, while the expression of epidermal growth factor (EGF) was significantly elevated. In contrast, the lidocaine group and saline group still showed higher levels of inflammatory cell infiltration.56 Chen et al used a chemical burn method to create a male rabbit oral ulcer model and in which CCLHG or fibroblast growth factor was applied three times daily. They recorded ulcer area on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9, and collected ulcer tissue for pathological examination on days 3, 5, and 7. The results showed that CCLHG reduced inflammatory cell and cytokine infiltration, promoted fibrous proliferation at the wound edge, and exhibited significant inhibitory effects on Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. However, the rate of ulcer surface reduction, fibrous proliferation, and anti-inflammatory effects were less pronounced to those observed with fibroblast growth factor.57

CCLHG can also be used to treat animal periodontitis. Sun et al created a rat periodontitis model by ligating the first mandibular molar. Sixty rats were divided into four groups: a control group, an induced periodontitis group, a G1 group (periodontitis induced for 7 days, treated with CCLHG once daily), and a G3 group (periodontitis induced for 7 days, treated with CCLHG three times daily). The results showed that treatment with CCLHG resulted in a significant reduction in attachment loss and osteoclast numbers, as well as inhibition of inflammatory cytokine secretion and inflammatory cell phenotype transformation. It also promoted fibrous growth, bone volume to tissue volume ratio (BV/TV), and bone tissue formation. Notably, fiber density, BV/TV, trabecular thickness, and osteoclast numbers showed significant dose dependence, while attachment loss did not. The gel exhibits anti-inflammatory effects by modulating macrophage polarization via the Type II interferon pathway during Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) infection. It also regulates bone resorption by balancing the RANKL/OPG axis and promoting osteogenesis.24

Jie lv studied the role of CCLHG in tooth extraction surgeries. Twenty female rats underwent bilateral extraction of their mandibular third molars to create a tooth extraction model. After achieving local hemostasis at the extraction site, CCLHG was applied to twice daily in the experimental group, while the control group received only local hemostasis. Five rats from each group were euthanized and analyzed on days 3, 7, 10, and 14 postoperatively. The study found no significant difference in residual area of the extraction site between the two groups by day 3(P > 0.05); however, by days 7, 10, and 14 postoperatively, the residual areain the experimental group was significantly smaller than control group. By days 3, 7, and 10, the number of capillaries in soft tissue was significantly higher in the experimental group compared to the control group; and fibroblast proliferation and collagen fiber growth were more pronounced in the experimental group, with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration. By day 14, these trends became consistent. This study demonstrated that CCLHG promotes soft tissue healing in tooth extraction wounds by suppressing inflammatory reactions.58

Clinical Applications of CCLHG in Oral Diseases

CCLHG Effectively Reduces Bacteria in Periodontal Pockets, Controls Clinical Symptoms, and Alleviates Pain During Treatment

The experimental group received CCLHG injections into the gingival sulcus prior to ultrasonic scaling, while the control group received a simulated injection without any medication. Multiple randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that CCLHG, whether used alone or in combination with traditional antimicrobial agents (such as metronidazole, povidone-iodine mouthwash, or minocycline hydrochloride) can significantly control clinical symptoms, bacteria in periodontal pockets, and inflammatory cytokines in patients with chronic periodontitis. Chen et al randomized the left or right dental arch of 24 patients with chronic periodontitis into two groups. The experimental group (n=24) received CCLHG injections into the periodontal pockets, while the control group (n=24) received iodine glycerol, followed by subgingival scaling and root planning. Although there were no significant differences in probing depth (PD) and bleeding on probing (BOP) between two groups, VAS and treatment time were significantly lower in experimental group. This study confirmed the efficacy of intra-pocket injection of CCLHG in relieving pain and reducing operation time.60

Tian et al randomized 90 patients with chronic periodontitis into three groups, all of whom maintained good oral hygiene. After gingival scaling, subgingival scaling, or flap surgery, they were treated with oral metronidazole (control group), topical metronidazole (metronidazole group), or a combination of topical metronidazole and CCLHG (combination group). Results showed that the combination group exhibited significantly higher efficacy and increased level of transforming growth factor (TGF)-β compared to the metronidazole group and the control group. They also had significantly lower levels of IL-1β, IL-6, clinical attachment loss (CAL), PD, bleeding index (BI), and plaque index (PLI) compared to the metronidazole group and the control group. Notably, adverse reactions did not significantly differ among the groups. This study demonstrated the efficacy and safety of local application of metronidazole and CCLHG in the periodontal diseases’ treatment.61 He et al randomized 60 patients with moderate to severe periodontitis into two groups. After subgingival scaling, the experimental group received injections of minocycline hydrochloride (minocycline group) or CCLHG (gel group) into the gingiva of the left or right dental arch. At the 6 and 12weeks follow-ups, the experimental group showed significantly lower PD, AL, BI, detection rates of Porphyromonas intermedia (Pi) and Pg compared to the control group, but there was no significant statistical difference between the minocycline group and the gel group. The study confirmed the comparable efficacy of CCLHG to minocycline in moderate to severe periodontitis.62

Zheng et al divided 66 periodontitis patients into two groups, with no statistical differences in baseline indicators. The experimental group (n=33) applied CCLHG, 0.5 cm in length, to the gums three times daily, in conjunction with rinsing using povidone-iodine mouthwash, 10mL per rinse, four times daily for 10 seconds each time. The control group (n=33) only used povidone-iodine mouthwash following the same procedure. The experimental group exhibited a significantly higher efficacy rate, GSES, and CGRP levels along with improvement in symptoms such as gum pain, submandibular lymph node swelling, gum redness, halitosis, and loose teeth were significantly shorter in the experimental group. Additionally, OHIP-14, VAS, PD, SBI, CAL, PLI, CRP, IL-1β, IL-17, HMGB1, and IL-6 were significantly lower in the experimental group compared to the control group. No significant differences in adverse events were observed between the two groups.63

In summary, multiple clinical studies indicate that CCLHG significantly relieves pain during basic periodontal treatment and effectively manages pathogenic bacteria and various periodontal indices over different periods (such as 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month, 6 weeks, and 12 weeks). The current research findings support the use of this gel in foundational treatment for patients with periodontal diseases.

Discussion

Despite numerous preclinical and clinical trials have investigated the efficacy and safety of CCLHG in oral diseases, there remains a lack of systematic reviews and summarizations. This absence hampers clinicians’ understanding the scope of use and precautions of this medication in oral diseases and usage precautions. This study aimed to provide an overview of CCLHG. Additionally, it summarizes and analyzes the latest published studies on the application of this gel in oral diseases, revealing substantial advantages in various treatment contexts.

Current preclinical and clinical studies indicate that CCLHG possesses good healing promotion, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, angiogenesis induction, bone absorption inhibition, and bone formation promotion effects, supporting its clinical application for patients with gingivitis and chronic periodontitis (including those associated with fixed orthodontic treatment) as an antimicrobial agent. And it also suitable for managing other oral conditions such as RAU, elderly ulcers, denture-related oral mucositis, BMS, OLP, complications from tooth extraction, pericoronitis of wisdom teeth, dry socket syndrome, and peri-implantitis. Therefore, CCLHG shows promise as a therapeutic option for various oral diseases. However, there is lack of research regarding the ability of chamomile compound to penetrate oral biofilms, necessitating basic studies for explore this issue and its underlying mechanism. Due to the small sample sizes and the concentration of trial centers in China and Germany, further multicenter, large-sample, randomized controlled clinical trials are required for validation.

We found that the publications on CCLHG in mucosal diseases are more extensive and in-depth. Studies indicate that compared to blank control groups (or placebo control groups), the efficacy rate significantly increases with the use of single agent CCLHG, and the VAS significantly decreases; compared to the use of traditional medications (such as Xiguashuang and glucocorticoids), the efficacy rate significantly increases with the sole use of CCLHG, and the VAS significantly decreases; compared to the sole use of CCLHG, the combination with traditional medications (such as Kangfuxin Solution and glucocorticoids) significantly increases the efficacy rate, and decreases the VAS. Our findings suggest that CCLHG has potential benefits either as a monotherapy or as an adjunct to traditional medications. However, large and well-designed trials are needed to substantiate these observations.

Although CCLHG has been shown to be safe, it is important to note that it is a compound preparation. Long-term local use of lidocaine may lead to the pigmentation (78), so caution should be exercised when applying it to exposed areas, such as red lips, to avoid aesthetic concerns. Additionally, excessive exposure to benzalkonium chloride can disrupt intestinal flora, warranting careful consideration of its use in young children or disabled individuals. Dosage and frequency must be strictly adhered to in order to prevent unnecessary complications.

Prospects of CCLHG in Treating Oral Diseases

Published articles indicate that CCLHG has potential benefits in clinical treatment of various oral diseases, justify further investigation. While several relevant preclinical and clinical studies exist, most are case-control or cohort studies, possessing limitations in design, a high risk of bias, and a lack of level one evidence. Consequently, several unresolved issues remain.

Consistent treatment strategies, outcomes and follow-up duration are essential and should be tailored to the specific characteristics of each disease. This study found inconsistencies in published clinical trials regarding treatment strategies and the duration of follow-up for similar disease. In periodontal diseases, CCLHG was compared to various traditional strategies, including iodinated glycerol, tinidazole, and minocycline; however, the protocols varied and lacked comparison with chlorhexidine mouthwash. The duration of follow-up ranged from one week to five months, involving 16 different outcomes. Apart from the article of Chen et al64 who conducted follow-ups at 3 months and 5 months, the rest of the studies had different and short durations of follow-up, with different outcomes. Analogously, investigations concerning mucosal disorders, pericoronitis of wisdom teeth, and peri-implantitis exhibited substantial temporal incongruities, coupled with disparate follow-up durations and outcome indicators. These research gaps may hinder comparisons and combined analyses among different studies and make secondary research inconvenient, thus not providing sufficient favorable evidence for clinical application. Therefore, reasonable and consistent short-term and long-term follow-up durations and outcomes based on disease treatment and prognosis characteristics in studies of the same type may be more conducive to in-depth systematic research on this medication.

It is needed to further explore on the safety and precautions of using CCLHG in different populations. Most studies focus on healthy adults, with limited data on elderly population or vulnerable groups such as young children, pregnant or lactating women. Who may suffer from oral diseases linked by poor oral hygiene, systemic conditions, radiotherapy, denture pressure, viral infections, trauma, or hormonal changes. Establishing protocols for these populations could enhance the broader application of CCLHG.

The optimal combination therapy regimen with CCLHG is worth further exploration. This study suggests that in oral mucosal diseases, the efficacy of CCLHG combined with traditional medications (such as Kangfuxin solution or glucocorticoids) may be superior to using CCLHG alone. Therefore, comparing the efficacy of different combination therapy regimens within the same clinical trial study is a promising direction.

Further exploration of CCLHG’s efficacy and mechanism is warranted for other oral diseases, particularly in the context of postoperative wound healing after oral surgery and root canal therapy. Oral surgical procedures often involve significant soft tissue incisions, especially in tongue and lip surgeries, which can be affected by activities like eating and speaking, resulting to impaired wound healing and considerable postoperative pain. CCLHG, with its multifaceted effects, may help mitigate these issues during recovery. Animal studies have confirmed the application of CCLHG in tooth extraction surgeries,58 yet relevant clinical trials remain lacking. And there is a research gap concerning the use of CCLHG in other procedures, including the antibacterial therapy of root canal therapy and surgical sites. Therefore, investigating the effect of this gel on postoperative wound healing and antibacterial mechanism offers a clinically valuable avenue for future research.
