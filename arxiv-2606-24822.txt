Graded Betti numbers of generalized split–join graphs and applications
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
We determine the full graded Betti tables of graph families that subsume several classes studied recently in the literature, namely the generalized multiple complete split-like graphs and the generalized clique-star graphs with arbitrary clique block sizes. The method combines Hochster’s formula with a precise decomposition of the associated independence complexes into disjoint unions of simplices and iterated joins of discrete complexes. This reduces every graded Betti number to an explicit coefficient extraction formula and yields closed expressions for the linear strand, higher strands, Hilbert series, regularity, projective dimension, and extremal Betti numbers. In particular, we prove a sharp criterion for 2 \displaystyle 2 -linear resolution and identify the regularity corner in terms of the number of nontrivial clique blocks. As applications, we recover and extend earlier results on equal-block split-like graphs, obtain complete formulas for pineapple graphs, and derive consequences for power graphs of cyclic groups, elementary abelian groups, and prime-power dihedral groups.

Introduction

The interaction between combinatorics and commutative algebra through edge ideals has become one of the most active themes in modern algebraic combinatorics. To a finite simple graph
\displaystyle G
with vertex set
\displaystyle V(G)=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, we associates the squarefree quadratic monomial ideal

I(G)=(x_{i}x_{j}:{x_{i},x_{j}}\in E(G))\subseteq R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}],

and the quotient ring
\displaystyle R/I(G)
records in algebraic form many structural features of the graph. The graded Betti numbers

\beta_{i,j}(G):=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\Tor_{i}^{R}(\mathbb{K},R/I(G))_{j}

describe the minimal graded free resolution of
\displaystyle R/I(G)
, and hence control several fundamental invariants such as the Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity and the projective dimension. Classical references for this viewpoint include Villarreal, Jacques, Herzog and Hibi, and the works surrounding Hochster’s formula and Fröberg’s theorem
[
26
,
16
,
17
,
15
,
10
]
. More recent developments emphasize exact Betti tables for structured graph classes, regularity bounds, extremal Betti numbers, and computational consequences
[
3
,
8
,
14
,
9
,
27
,
11
,
12
]
.

From the graph-theoretic side, split graphs and graphs assembled by graph joins are especially attractive, since they sit between rigid and flexible behavior. They are simple enough to admit explicit combinatorial descriptions, yet rich enough to model algebraic graphs arising from groups and rings. In the recent paper
[
22
]
, initial Betti numbers of multiple complete split-like graphs, clique-star graphs, and some equal-block generalizations were determined, together with last-column extremal Betti numbers and projective dimensions. The same paper showed that these graph classes occur naturally in power graphs and commuting graphs of finite groups. However, the higher strands of the Betti table, the regularity, and the Hilbert series were left open there. This omission is substantial, knowing only the linear strand does not determine the full homological behavior, does not locate the regularity corner, and does not answer when the edge ideal has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution.

A second motivation comes from algebraic graphs attached to finite groups. Power graphs and commuting graphs have received sustained attention in both graph theory and algebra, see the surveys and foundational papers
[
1
,
5
,
18
,
19
]
. In
[
23
]
, homological invariants of edge ideals of power graphs of cyclic groups were investigated. That article established exact results for prime powers, for certain semiprime cases, and for cyclic groups with order equal to the product of three distinct primes. It also obtained inequalities for general values of
\displaystyle n
. Yet even there, the complete graded Betti table remained inaccessible in general, and the computations depended strongly on the special shape of the underlying power graph. Thus two parallel gaps were visible in the literature. First, the absence of full Betti tables for the graph families underlying many group graphs, and second, the lack of a structural mechanism explaining why apparently different algebraic graphs exhibit similar homological patterns.

The present paper fills both gaps. We introduce two heterogeneous graph families

\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})=\overline{K}_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})=K_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}},

where
\displaystyle a\geq 1
,
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
with each
\displaystyle b_{r}\geq 1
, and
\displaystyle*
denotes graph join (joining each vertex of first graph to every vertex of second graph). When all
\displaystyle b_{r}
are equal, these recover the multiple complete split-like graphs and clique-star graphs studied in
[
22
]
. The main point is that the independence complexes of these families admit a transparent decomposition into a disjoint union of a simplex or a discrete complex with an iterated join of discrete complexes. This decomposition is sufficiently precise to make Hochster’s formula completely explicit. In particular, every nonzero graded Betti number can be expressed either by a closed summation formula or, more compactly, by a coefficient extraction from certain elementary symmetric polynomials built from one-variable block polynomials. The resulting formulas simultaneously recover all previously known initial Betti numbers and produce every higher strand that was previously unavailable.

The significance of these formulas is both conceptual and computational. Conceptually, they show that the entire Betti table is governed by two pieces of data only, the size
\displaystyle a
of the distinguished part and the multiset of block sizes
\displaystyle\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\}
. This immediately yields sharp formulas for regularity, projective dimension, Hilbert series, and extremal Betti numbers. It also gives a transparent criterion for
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution, as the edge ideal is
\displaystyle 2
-linear precisely when there is at most one block of size at least
\displaystyle 2
. Computationally, the formulas bypass repeated Gröbner or free-resolution computations in software systems such as Macaulay2
[
13
]
, instead, they reduce the problem to coefficient extraction from short generating functions. This leads to a dynamic programming algorithm with polynomial complexity in the total number of vertices and clique blocks.

The real and technical significance of the problem is broader than the graph families themselves. Power graphs and commuting graphs encode algebraic generation and commutation, respectively, and therefore serve as combinatorial shadows of finite groups. Exact homological invariants of their edge ideals provide a refined measure of how these algebraic relations interact. For instance, the number and sizes of maximal cyclic or commuting pieces determine not only the shape of the graph but also the position of extremal Betti numbers. Such formulas are useful in symbolic computation, in the classification of graphs with low regularity, and in the systematic search for graph classes with prescribed homological behavior. They also offer a clean route from group-theoretic structure theorems to explicit free resolutions.

Our main results may be summarized informally as follows. First, we determine the full graded Betti numbers of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
for arbitrary block sizes. Second, we compute the Hilbert series of the corresponding Stanley–Reisner rings. Third, we show that the regularity of
\displaystyle R/I(G)
equals
\displaystyle\max\{1,\nu\}
, where
\displaystyle\nu
is the number of blocks with size at least
\displaystyle 2
, and hence
\displaystyle\reg I(G)=1+\max\{1,\nu\}
. Fourth, we identify both the regularity corner and the last-column extremal corner, thereby determining the projective dimension. Finally, we apply the theory to pineapple graphs, power graphs of cyclic prime-power groups, power graphs of elementary abelian groups, and prime-power dihedral groups, recovering known results from
[
22
,
23
]
and extending them substantially.

A further goal of the paper is methodological. The formulas are not presented as isolated identities but as consequences of a unifying topological mechanism. The independence complex of our graph families is a disjoint union in which one component is an iterated join of discrete complexes. Since the reduced homology of such joins is easy to control, Hochster’s formula becomes tractable in every bidegree. This perspective clarifies why equal-block and group-graph examples behave so regularly and why the top nonzero strand is governed by the number of nontrivial blocks.

The paper is organized as: Section
2

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})

7
applies the general theory to algebraic graphs coming from groups, including pineapple graphs, cyclic prime-power groups, elementary abelian groups, and prime-power dihedral groups. The paper ends with Section
8

Preliminaries and notation

Throughout the paper,
\displaystyle\mathbb{K}
denotes a field, all graphs are finite and simple, and for a graph
\displaystyle G
we write
\displaystyle V(G)
and
\displaystyle E(G)
for its vertex and edge sets. For
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(G)
, the induced subgraph on
\displaystyle W
is denoted by
\displaystyle G[W]
. We write
\displaystyle\overline{K}_{a}
for the independent graph on
\displaystyle a
vertices and
\displaystyle K_{a}
for the complete graph on
\displaystyle a
vertices. The symbol
\displaystyle\sqcup
denotes disjoint union of graphs.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a graph on
\displaystyle N
vertices and let
\displaystyle R=\mathbb{K}[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]
be the polynomial ring indexed by the vertices of
\displaystyle G
. The edge ideal of
\displaystyle G
is

I(G)=(x_{i}x_{j}:{x_{i},x_{j}}\in E(G))\subseteq R.

We write

\beta_{i,j}(G)=\beta_{i,j}(R/I(G))=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\Tor_{i}^{R}(\mathbb{K},R/I(G))_{j}.

The Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity of
\displaystyle R/I(G)
is defined by

\reg(G):=\reg(R/I(G))=\max\{j-i:\beta_{i,j}(R/I(G))\neq 0\}.

The projective dimension of
\displaystyle R/I(G)
is defined by

\pd(G):=\pd(R/I(G))=\max\{i:\beta_{i,j}(R/I(G))\neq 0\text{ for some }j\}.

Equivalently, if

0\to\bigoplus_{j}R(-j)^{\beta_{p,j}}\to\cdots\to\bigoplus_{j}R(-j)^{\beta_{1,j}}\to R\to R/I(G)\to 0

is the minimal graded free resolution of
\displaystyle R/I(G)
, then
\displaystyle\reg(G)
is the largest value of
\displaystyle j-i
for which
\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}\neq 0
, and
\displaystyle\pd(G)
is the length
\displaystyle p
of the minimal graded free resolution.

Definition 2.2
.

A simplicial complex
\displaystyle\Delta
on a vertex set
\displaystyle V
is a collection of subsets of
\displaystyle V
such that if
\displaystyle F\in\Delta
and
\displaystyle G\subseteq F
, then
\displaystyle G\in\Delta
. A facet of
\displaystyle\Delta
is a face
\displaystyle F\in\Delta
that is maximal under inclusion, meaning there is no face
\displaystyle H\in\Delta
with
\displaystyle F\subsetneq H
. A facet is a maximal face of
\displaystyle\Delta
under inclusion.

The independence complex of a graph
\displaystyle G
is the simplicial complex

\mathrm{Ind}(G)=\{F\subseteq V(G):F\text{ is an independent set in }G\}.

If
\displaystyle\Delta
is a simplicial complex and
\displaystyle W\subseteq V(\Delta)
, then
\displaystyle\Delta_{W}
denotes the induced subcomplex on
\displaystyle W
.

Definition 2.3
.

For integers
\displaystyle a\geq 1
,
\displaystyle m\geq 1
, and
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
with
\displaystyle b_{r}\geq 1
, we define

\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})=\overline{K}_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})=K_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}.

When
\displaystyle b_{1}=\cdots=b_{m}=b
, these become the equal-block families studied in
[
22
]
. In that notation,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;(b,\dots,b))=MCS^{a}_{b,m},
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;(b,\dots,b))=S^{a}_{b,m}.

For later use, we set

\displaystyle B=\sum_{r=1}^{m}b_{r},

\displaystyle N=a+B,

\displaystyle\nu=\#\{r:b_{r}\geq 2\},
and
\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{b_{r}\geq 2}b_{r}.
We also define

M_{s}(\mathbf{b})=\binom{B}{s}-\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{b_{r}}{s}.

So,
\displaystyle M_{s}(\mathbf{b})
counts
\displaystyle s
-subsets of the union of the clique blocks that meet at least two distinct blocks.

The next theorem is the basic bridge between graded Betti numbers and simplicial topology.

Theorem 2.4

(Hochster,
[
16
,
15
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta
be a simplicial complex on the vertex set
\displaystyle[N]
. Then for every
\displaystyle i,j\geq 0
,

\beta_{i,j}(\mathbb{K}[\Delta])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq[N]\\
|W|=j\end{subarray}}\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{j-i-1}(\Delta_{W};\mathbb{K}).

In particular, if
\displaystyle\Delta=\mathrm{Ind}(G)
, then
\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}(G)
is computed by the reduced homology of induced subcomplexes of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)
.

The next criterion identifies the
\displaystyle 2
-linear case and is the standard benchmark for edge ideals.

Theorem 2.5

(Fröberg,
[
10
,
12
]
)
.

For a finite simple graph
\displaystyle G
, the edge ideal
\displaystyle I(G)
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution if and only if the complement graph
\displaystyle\overline{G}
is chordal.

The homology of joins turns the combinatorics of clique blocks into explicit higher strands.

Theorem 2.6

(Homology of joins,
[
4
,
15
]
)
.

Let
\displaystyle\Delta
and
\displaystyle\Gamma
be simplicial complexes on disjoint vertex sets. Then

\widetilde{H}_{q}(\Delta*\Gamma;\mathbb{K})\cong\bigoplus_{p+\ell=q-1}\widetilde{H}_{p}(\Delta;\mathbb{K})\otimes_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{\ell}(\Gamma;\mathbb{K}).

The existing literature makes clear that the linear strand is only the beginning of the homological story. Equal-block formulas from
[
22
]
show that certain split–join graphs are tractable in the first strand, but they do not reveal the higher syzygies, and hence they do not determine regularity or the full Betti table. Similarly,
[
23
]
identifies interesting cyclic power-graph cases and proves sharp results in some special arithmetic regimes, but the method there remains tied to the specific subgroup pattern of the cyclic group under consideration. What is missing is a structural theorem covering arbitrary block sizes and all strands simultaneously. The purpose of the present paper is precisely to provide such a theorem and then to push it back to the algebraic graph classes that motivated the problem.

3
Homological decomposition and Hilbert series

In this section, we identify the exact simplicial structure behind the graph families
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
. This is the key point on which all later formulas depend.

Let
\displaystyle A
denote the distinguished part of size
\displaystyle a
, and let
\displaystyle B_{r}
be the vertex set of the
\displaystyle r
-th clique block
\displaystyle K_{b_{r}}
. Thus, we derive

V(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))=A\sqcup B_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_{m}\qquad\text{and}\qquad V(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))=A\sqcup B_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_{m}.

We also write
\displaystyle D_{s}
for the discrete simplicial complex on
\displaystyle s
vertices.

The next proposition shows that the independence complexes split into a very small number of topological pieces.

Proposition 3.1
.

The independence complexes of the two graph families satisfy

\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\langle A\rangle\sqcup(D_{b_{1}}*\cdots*D_{b_{m}}),

and

\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))=D_{a}\sqcup(D_{b_{1}}*\cdots*D_{b_{m}}),

where
\displaystyle\langle A\rangle
denotes the simplex on the vertex set
\displaystyle A
.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
, every vertex of
\displaystyle A
is adjacent to every vertex of every block
\displaystyle B_{r}
, so no independent set can meet both
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle\bigcup_{r}B_{r}
. Inside
\displaystyle A
there are no edges, and hence every subset of
\displaystyle A
is independent, thereby giving the simplex
\displaystyle\langle A\rangle
. Inside each block
\displaystyle B_{r}
every pair of vertices is adjacent, so an independent set may contain at most one vertex from each block. Thus, across different blocks there are no edges, so one may choose independently from distinct blocks. This is exactly the iterated join
\displaystyle D_{b_{1}}*\cdots*D_{b_{m}}
. Hence the first formula follows.

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
, the same join restriction between
\displaystyle A
and the blocks remains valid, but now the induced graph on
\displaystyle A
is a clique, so independent sets in
\displaystyle A
are only the empty set and the singletons. Therefore, the component contributed by
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle D_{a}
, and the second formula follows.
∎

The next lemma computes the entire reduced homology of the block-join component.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{t})
with each
\displaystyle w_{i}\geq 1
, and let

\displaystyle J(\mathbf{w})=D_{w_{1}}*\cdots*D_{w_{t}}.

Then the following hold.

(i)

If some
\displaystyle w_{i}=1
, then
\displaystyle J(\mathbf{w})
is a cone, and hence contractible.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle w_{i}\geq 2
for every
\displaystyle i
, then

\widetilde{H}_{q}(J(\mathbf{w});\mathbb{K})\cong\begin{cases}\mathbb{K}^{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(w_{i}-1)},&q=t-1,\\
0,&q\neq t-1.\end{cases}

Proof.

If some
\displaystyle w_{i}=1
, then
\displaystyle D_{w_{i}}
is a point, and the join with a point is a cone. This proves (i).
For (ii), each
\displaystyle D_{w_{i}}
has reduced homology concentrated in degree
\displaystyle 0
, with

\widetilde{H}_{0}(D_{w_{i}};\mathbb{K})\cong\mathbb{K}^{w_{i}-1}.

Applying Theorem
2.6
inductively, the only nonzero reduced homology of the join occurs in degree
\displaystyle t-1
, and the rank multiplies at each step. Hence, we have

\widetilde{H}_{t-1}(J(\mathbf{w});\mathbb{K})\cong\bigotimes_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{H}_{0}(D_{w_{i}};\mathbb{K})\cong\mathbb{K}^{\prod_{i=1}^{t}(w_{i}-1)},

while all other reduced homology groups vanish.
∎

The next proposition reduces every induced subcomplex to one of a few explicit topological models.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle W\subseteq A\sqcup B_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_{m}
, and let

\displaystyle u=|W\cap A|,

\displaystyle w_{r}=|W\cap B_{r}|,
and
\displaystyle t=\#\{r:w_{r}>0\}.
Then the induced subcomplexes are described as follows.

(i)

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
, we have

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong\begin{cases}\langle W\cap A\rangle,&t=0,\\
D_{w_{r}},&u=0,\ t=1,\\
J(w_{r_{1}},\dots,w_{r_{t}}),&u=0,\ t\geq 2,\\
\langle W\cap A\rangle\sqcup D_{w_{r}},&u>0,\ t=1,\\
\langle W\cap A\rangle\sqcup J(w_{r_{1}},\dots,w_{r_{t}}),&u>0,\ t\geq 2.\end{cases}

(ii)

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
, we have

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong\begin{cases}D_{u},&t=0,\\
D_{w_{r}},&u=0,\ t=1,\\
J(w_{r_{1}},\dots,w_{r_{t}}),&u=0,\ t\geq 2,\\
D_{u}\sqcup D_{w_{r}},&u>0,\ t=1,\\
D_{u}\sqcup J(w_{r_{1}},\dots,w_{r_{t}}),&u>0,\ t\geq 2.\end{cases}

Proof.

This is an immediate induced-subcomplex version of Proposition
3.1
. One simply restricts the components there to the vertices in
\displaystyle W
. For the split case the
\displaystyle A
-part remains a simplex whenever
\displaystyle u>0
. For the clique-star case it remains a discrete complex on
\displaystyle u
vertices.
∎

The next theorem computes the Hilbert series explicitly before any Betti-number calculation is attempted.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=(1+x)^{a}+\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1+b_{r}x)-1

and

\displaystyle F_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=1+ax+\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1+b_{r}x)-1.

Then

\Hilb_{\mathbb{K}[\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))]}(t)=F_{\mathcal{M}}\Big(\tfrac{t}{1-t}\Big)=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{a}}+\prod_{r=1}^{m}\Big(1+\frac{b_{r}t}{1-t}\Big)-1,

and

\Hilb_{\mathbb{K}[\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))]}(t)=F_{\mathcal{C}}\Big(\tfrac{t}{1-t}\Big)=1+\frac{at}{1-t}+\prod_{r=1}^{m}\Big(1+\frac{b_{r}t}{1-t}\Big)-1.

Proof.

For a simplicial complex
\displaystyle\Delta
, we have

\Hilb_{\mathbb{K}[\Delta]}(t)=\sum_{F\in\Delta}\Big(\frac{t}{1-t}\Big)^{|F|}.

Thus it is enough to determine the face enumerators. By Proposition
3.1
, independent sets in
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
are either arbitrary subsets of
\displaystyle A
or choices of at most one vertex from each block, but not both simultaneously. This fact gives us

F_{\mathcal{M}}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{a}\binom{a}{i}x^{i}+\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1+b_{r}x)-1=(1+x)^{a}+\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1+b_{r}x)-1.

Similarly, in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
the independent sets in
\displaystyle A
are only the empty set and singletons, and we have

F_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=1+ax+\prod_{r=1}^{m}(1+b_{r}x)-1.

Now with
\displaystyle x=\tfrac{t}{1-t}
, we obtain the required series.
∎

The decomposition in Proposition

4
Exact graded Betti numbers for generalized split–join graphs

We now turn to the family

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})=\overline{K}_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}.

The equal-block case was treated only on the initial strand in
[
22
]
. We derive the full graded Betti table.
For convenience, define the block polynomials

P_{r}(z)=\sum_{q=2}^{b_{r}}(q-1)\binom{b_{r}}{q}z^{q},\qquad 1\leq r\leq m,

and for
\displaystyle t\geq 1
, let

\mathcal{E}_{t}(z;\mathbf{b})=e_{t}(P_{1}(z),\dots,P_{m}(z)),

which is the
\displaystyle t
-th elementary symmetric polynomial in the polynomials
\displaystyle P_{1}(z),\dots,P_{m}(z)
.

The next theorem gives the complete linear strand of
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
\displaystyle j\geq 2
,

\beta_{j-1,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-1)\binom{b_{r}}{j}+\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-1}}\binom{a}{u}\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-u)\binom{b_{r}}{j-u}+\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-2}}\binom{a}{u}M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b}).

Proof.

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
, Hochster’s formula implies

\beta_{j-1,j}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq V(G)\\
|W|=j\end{subarray}}\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K}).

We therefore classify all
\displaystyle j
-subsets
\displaystyle W
for which
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
is disconnected, and consider the following cases.

Case 1:
If
\displaystyle W\subseteq B_{r}
for some fixed
\displaystyle r
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{j}
, so we have

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=j-1.

There are
\displaystyle\binom{b_{r}}{j}
such choices inside
\displaystyle B_{r}
, and hence the total contribution of this case is

\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-1)\binom{b_{r}}{j}.

Case 2:
If
\displaystyle W
meets
\displaystyle A
and exactly one block
\displaystyle B_{r}
, then for
\displaystyle u=|W\cap A|
with
\displaystyle 1\leq u\leq j-1
, so we get
\displaystyle|W\cap B_{r}|=j-u
. By Proposition
3.3
, we have

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong\langle W\cap A\rangle\sqcup D_{j-u}.

It has
\displaystyle(j-u)+1
connected components, and hence we have

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=j-u.

For fixed
\displaystyle u
, the number of such subsets is
\displaystyle\binom{a}{u}\binom{b_{r}}{j-u}
, so the total contribution is

\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-1}}\binom{a}{u}\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-u)\binom{b_{r}}{j-u}.

Case 3:
If
\displaystyle W
meets
\displaystyle A
and at least two distinct blocks. Again For
\displaystyle u=|W\cap A|
, with
\displaystyle 1\leq u\leq j-2
, and by Proposition
3.3
, we have

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong\langle W\cap A\rangle\sqcup J(\mathbf{w}),

where
\displaystyle J(\mathbf{w})
is connected, since it is the join of at least two nonempty complexes. Hence,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
has exactly two connected components, so we obtain

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=1.

For fixed
\displaystyle u
, the number of ways to choose the remaining
\displaystyle j-u
vertices in at least two blocks is precisely
\displaystyle M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b})
. SO, in this case, the total contribution is

\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-2}}\binom{a}{u}M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b}).

No other subset contributes to
\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))
. Indeed, if
\displaystyle W\subseteq A
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
is a simplex, if
\displaystyle W
is contained in at least two blocks and avoids
\displaystyle A
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
is connected, and contributes null. Now, summing the cases, we obtain the stated formula for
\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})).

∎

The next theorem gives every higher strand in one formula.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\displaystyle t\geq 2
. Then for every
\displaystyle j\geq 2t
,

\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}[z^{j-u}]\mathcal{E}_{t}(z;\mathbf{b}),

where
\displaystyle[z^{s}]
denotes coefficient extraction, or equivalently,

\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r_{1}<\cdots<r_{t}\leq m\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}=j-u\\
2\leq q_{i}\leq b_{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}~\prod_{i=1}^{t}(q_{i}-1)\binom{b_{r_{i}}}{q_{i}}.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and
\displaystyle j\geq 2t
with
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
, Hochster’s formula implies that

\beta_{j-t,j}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq V(G)\\
|W|=j\end{subarray}}\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{t-1}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K}).

By Proposition
3.3
, the only subsets
\displaystyle W
that can contribute to
\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{t-1}
are those for which the block part of
\displaystyle W
meets exactly
\displaystyle t
blocks, each in at least
\displaystyle 2
vertices. Any selected block of size
\displaystyle 1
would make the relevant join a cone by Lemma
3.2
(i), and hence no contribution. Thus, for non zero contribution to
\displaystyle\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})),
we choose: (i)
\displaystyle u
vertices from
\displaystyle A
, where
\displaystyle 0\leq u\leq a
, (ii)
\displaystyle t
distinct blocks
\displaystyle B_{r_{1}},\dots,B_{r_{t}}
, (iii) integers
\displaystyle q_{i}\geq 2
with
\displaystyle q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}=j-u
, and (iv)
\displaystyle q_{i}
vertices from each selected block
\displaystyle B_{r_{i}}
. For such a choice, Proposition
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
(ii) implies that

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{t-1}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(q_{i}-1).

The number of such subsets realizing these numbers is

\binom{a}{u}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\binom{b_{r_{i}}}{q_{i}}.

Therefore, we obtain

\beta_{j-t,j}(G)=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r_{1}<\cdots<r_{t}\leq m\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}=j-u\\
2\leq q_{i}\leq b_{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}~\prod_{i=1}^{t}(q_{i}-1)\binom{b_{r_{i}}}{q_{i}},

which is exactly the second displayed formula. The coefficient form follows, as selecting one monomial from each of
\displaystyle P_{r_{1}}(z),\dots,P_{r_{t}}(z)
and summing over all
\displaystyle t
-subsets of blocks produces the elementary symmetric polynomial
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(z;\mathbf{b})
.
∎

The next corollary packages all higher strands into a single generating function.

Corollary 4.3
.

For each fixed
\displaystyle t\geq 2
,

\sum_{j\geq 0}\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))\lambda^{j}=(1+\lambda)^{a}\mathcal{E}_{t}(\lambda;\mathbf{b}).

Proof.

Multiply the coefficient formula in Theorem
4.2
by
\displaystyle\lambda^{j}
and sum over
\displaystyle j
. The factor
\displaystyle(1+\lambda)^{a}
records the choice of vertices from
\displaystyle A
, while
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{t}(\lambda;\mathbf{b})
records the selected blocks and their internal multiplicities. Now, with these facts, the formula follows.
∎

The next corollary recovers the equal-block formulas from
[
22
]
and extends them to every strand.

Corollary 4.4
.

Assume
\displaystyle b_{1}=\cdots=b_{m}=b
. Then for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
,

\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;(b,\dots,b)))=\binom{m}{t}\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}[z^{j-u}]P_{b}(z)^{t},

where

P_{b}(z)=\sum_{q=2}^{b}(q-1)\binom{b}{q}z^{q}.

Moreover, the linear strand is given by Theorem
4.1
with all
\displaystyle b_{r}=b
.

Proof.

If all blocks have the same size
\displaystyle b
, then every
\displaystyle t
-fold elementary symmetric polynomial in
\displaystyle P_{1}(z),\dots,P_{m}(z)
equals
\displaystyle\binom{m}{t}P_{b}(z)^{t}
. The linear strand is immediate from Theorem
4.1
.
∎

5
Exact graded Betti numbers for generalized clique-star graphs

We now consider
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})=K_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}.

The topology of the block part is unchanged from Section
4
, but the
\displaystyle A
-part is now discrete. This changes the linear strand while leaving the higher strands intact.

The next theorem shows exactly how the linear strand changes when the independent part becomes a clique.

Theorem 5.1
.

For
\displaystyle j\geq 2
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(j-1)\binom{a}{j}+\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-1)\binom{b_{r}}{j}+\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-1}}(j-1)\binom{a}{u}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{b_{r}}{j-u}

\displaystyle\displaystyle+\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-2}}u\binom{a}{u}M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b}).

Proof.

For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
, Hochster’s formula gives

\beta_{j-1,j}(G)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\subseteq V(G)\\
|W|=j\end{subarray}}\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K}).

For non zero contribution to
\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,j}(G)
, we classify the contributing subsets.

Case 1:
If
\displaystyle W\subseteq A
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{j}
, so the contribution is
\displaystyle(j-1)\binom{a}{j}
.

Case 2:
If
\displaystyle W\subseteq B_{r}
for some
\displaystyle r
, then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{j}
, contributing

\sum_{r=1}^{m}(j-1)\binom{b_{r}}{j}.

Case 3:
If
\displaystyle W
meets
\displaystyle A
and exactly one block
\displaystyle B_{r}
, then for
\displaystyle u=|W\cap A|
and
\displaystyle j-u=|W\cap B_{r}|
, with
\displaystyle 1\leq u\leq j-1
, we have

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{u}\sqcup D_{j-u}.

So, the number of connected components is
\displaystyle u+(j-u)=j
, and hence

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=j-1.

The contribution in this case is

\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-1}}(j-1)\binom{a}{u}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\binom{b_{r}}{j-u}.

Case 4:
If
\displaystyle W
meets
\displaystyle A
and at least two distinct blocks, then for
\displaystyle u=|W\cap A|
, Proposition
3.3
implies that

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{u}\sqcup J(\mathbf{w}),

where
\displaystyle J(\mathbf{w})
is connected. Therefore the total number of connected components is
\displaystyle u+1
, and hence

\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=u.

For fixed
\displaystyle u
, the number of such subsets is
\displaystyle\binom{a}{u}M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b})
, so this case contributes

\sum_{u=1}^{\min{a,j-2}}u\binom{a}{u}M_{j-u}(\mathbf{b}).

The remaining subsets cases contribute nothing as
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
is a connected component. Now summing the four cases, the result follows.
∎

The next theorem shows that all higher strands are unchanged from the split–join case.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\displaystyle t\geq 2
. Then for every
\displaystyle j\geq 2t
,

\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}[z^{j-u}]\mathcal{E}_{t}(z;\mathbf{b}).

or equivalently,

\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{a,j-2t}}\binom{a}{u}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq r_{1}<\cdots<r_{t}\leq m\\
q_{1}+\cdots+q_{t}=j-u\\
2\leq q_{i}\leq b_{r_{i}}\end{subarray}}\,\prod_{i=1}^{t}(q_{i}-1)\binom{b_{r_{i}}}{q_{i}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.3
, the only difference between
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))_{W}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))_{W}
is that the simplex on
\displaystyle A
is replaced by a discrete set. But for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
we are computing
\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{t-1}
with
\displaystyle t-1\geq 1
. The discrete part on
\displaystyle A
has no positive-dimensional reduced homology, so the only contribution again comes from the join component of the selected blocks. Exactly the same block counting as in Theorem
4.2
therefore applies.
∎

The next corollary packages the higher strands of the clique-star family into the same generating function as before.

.

For each fixed
\displaystyle t\geq 2
,

\sum_{j\geq 0}\beta_{j-t,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))\lambda^{j}=(1+\lambda)^{a}\mathcal{E}_{t}(\lambda;\mathbf{b}).

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Corollary
4.3
, using Theorem
5.2
.
∎

and

to the complete graded Betti table.

\beta_{2,3}(G)=2\binom{2}{3}+\sum_{r=1}^{3}2\binom{b_{r}}{3}+\sum_{u=1}^{2}2\binom{2}{u}\sum_{r=1}^{3}\binom{b_{r}}{3-u}+\sum_{u=1}^{1}u\binom{2}{u}M_{3-u}=120.

For
\displaystyle\beta_{2,4}(G)
, Theorem
5.2
, gives

\beta_{2,4}(G)=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{2,4-4}}\binom{2}{u}[z^{4-u}]\mathcal{E}_{2}(z;\mathbf{b}).

As
\displaystyle\min\{2,0\}=0
, only
\displaystyle u=0
occurs, and

\displaystyle\beta_{2,4}(G)=[z^{4}]\mathcal{E}_{2}(z;\mathbf{b}).

Now

\mathcal{E}_{2}(z;\mathbf{b})=P_{1}(z)P_{2}(z)+P_{1}(z)P_{3}(z)+P_{2}(z)P_{3}(z),

so the coefficient of
\displaystyle z^{4}
comes only from choosing the
\displaystyle z^{2}
-term in each factor, and we have

[z^{4}]P_{1}P_{2}=1\cdot 3=3,~[z^{4}]P_{1}P_{3}=1\cdot 6=6,~[z^{4}]P_{2}P_{3}=3\cdot 6=18.

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{2,4}(G)=3+6+18=27.

For
\displaystyle\beta_{3,6}(G)
, Theorem
5.2
, gives

\beta_{3,6}(G)=\sum_{u=0}^{\min{2,6-6}}\binom{2}{u}[z^{6-u}],\mathcal{E}_{3}(z;\mathbf{b}).

Only
\displaystyle u=0
contributes, and since there are exactly three blocks, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}(z;\mathbf{b})=P_{1}(z)P_{2}(z)P_{3}(z).

Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{3,6}(G)=[z^{6}]\big(P_{1}P_{2}P_{3}\big).
The only way to obtain degree
\displaystyle 6
is to take the
\displaystyle z^{2}
-term from each factor. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{3,6}(G)=1\cdot 3\cdot 6=18.

Also,
\displaystyle 11-8=3
, so
\displaystyle\beta_{8,11}(G)
also lies on the third strand, and

\beta_{8,11}(G)=\sum_{u=0}^{2}\binom{2}{u}[z^{11-u}]\mathcal{E}_{3}(z;\mathbf{b}).

Since
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{3}(z;\mathbf{b})=P_{1}P_{2}P_{3}
has maximum degree

\displaystyle 2+3+4=9,

the terms with
\displaystyle u=0
and
\displaystyle u=1
vanish, as they ask for the coefficients of
\displaystyle z^{11}
and
\displaystyle z^{10}
. Hence only
\displaystyle u=2
survives, and

\beta_{8,11}(G)=\binom{2}{2}[z^{9}](P_{1}P_{2}P_{3})=[z^{9}](P_{1}P_{2}P_{3}).

The top-degree term is obtained by taking the largest-degree monomial from each factor

\displaystyle z^{2}\cdot 2z^{3}\cdot 3z^{4}=6z^{9}.

So, we get
\displaystyle\beta_{8,11}(G)=6.

For
\displaystyle\beta_{10,11}(G)
,
\displaystyle j=11
in Theorem
5.1
, and with
\displaystyle a=2
,the block part has only
\displaystyle 9
vertices. So, all terms involving
\displaystyle\binom{2}{11}
,
\displaystyle\binom{b_{r}}{11}
,
\displaystyle\binom{b_{r}}{10}
, and
\displaystyle\binom{b_{r}}{9}
vanish. Thus, we get

\beta_{10,11}(G)=\sum_{u=1}^{2}u\binom{2}{u}M_{11-u}.

AS

\displaystyle M_{10}=0,
since choose
\displaystyle 10
vertices from a set of size
\displaystyle 9
is absurd, while

M_{9}=\binom{9}{9}-\binom{2}{9}-\binom{3}{9}-\binom{4}{9}=1,

and hence

\beta_{10,11}(G)=1\cdot\binom{2}{1}M_{10}+2\cdot\binom{2}{2}M_{9}=0+2\cdot 1\cdot 1=2.

6
Regularity, extremal Betti numbers, and projective dimension

In this section, we extract the main homological invariants from the explicit Betti formulas.

The next theorem gives the regularity of the quotient ring for both graph families in a single closed formula.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be either
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
or
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
. Then

\displaystyle\reg(G)=\max\{1,\nu\},

where
\displaystyle\nu=\#\{r:b_{r}\geq 2\}
, and consequently,
\displaystyle\reg(I(G))=1+\max\{1,\nu\}.

Proof.

By Theorems
4.2
and
5.2
, the strand with difference
\displaystyle j-i=t
can be nonzero only if one chooses
\displaystyle t
distinct blocks each contributing at least two vertices. Therefore, no strand with
\displaystyle t>\nu
can occur. On the other hand, if
\displaystyle\nu\geq 2
, select exactly the
\displaystyle\nu
blocks of size at least
\displaystyle 2
and choose all their vertices. Then in the coefficient formula for
\displaystyle t=\nu
, the monomial
\displaystyle z^{\sigma}
appears with coefficient
\displaystyle\prod_{b_{r}\geq 2}(b_{r}-1)\neq 0.

Choosing all
\displaystyle a
vertices from the distinguished part contributes the term

\beta_{a+\sigma-\nu,\ a+\sigma}(G)\neq 0.

Hence,
\displaystyle\reg(G)\geq\nu
. Since no larger difference can occur, so
\displaystyle\reg(G)=\nu
.

If
\displaystyle\nu\leq 1
, then all higher strands vanish. The linear strand is nonzero, since every nontrivial graph in our families contains an edge. Thus,
\displaystyle\reg(G)=1
. Combining the two cases, we have

\reg(G)=\max\{1,\nu\}.

Finally, for any squarefree quadratic monomial ideal, we have

\reg(I(G))=\reg(R/I(G))+1.

And the second formula follows.
∎

The next corollary translates the regularity formula into a sharp criterion for
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution.

Corollary 6.2
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be either
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
or
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
. Then the following are equivalent.

(i)

\displaystyle I(G)
has a
\displaystyle 2
-linear resolution.

(ii)

\displaystyle\reg(I(G))=2
.

(iii)

\displaystyle\reg(G)=1
.

(iv)

\displaystyle\nu\leq 1
, that is, at most one block has size at least
\displaystyle 2
.

Proof.

The equivalence of (i), (ii), and (iii) is standard for quadratic monomial ideals. The equivalence with (iv) follows immediately from Theorem
6.1
.
∎

The next theorem identifies the regularity corner explicitly and shows where the top nonzero strand begins.

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume
\displaystyle\nu\geq 2
, and let
\displaystyle\sigma=\sum_{b_{r}\geq 2}b_{r}.

Then for both
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
and
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
,

\beta_{a+\sigma-\nu,\ a+\sigma}(G)=\prod_{b_{r}\geq 2}(b_{r}-1).

Moreover, this Betti number is extremal and realizes the regularity.

Proof.

In Theorems
4.2
and
5.2
, take
\displaystyle t=\nu
, choose all
\displaystyle\nu
nontrivial blocks, and choose all their vertices. This contributes degree
\displaystyle\sigma
in the block polynomial, with coefficient
\displaystyle\prod_{b_{r}\geq 2}(b_{r}-1)
. Choosing all
\displaystyle a
distinguished vertices, we have

\beta_{a+\sigma-\nu,\ a+\sigma}(G)=\prod_{b_{r}\geq 2}(b_{r}-1).

Since
\displaystyle\nu
is the largest possible value of
\displaystyle j-i
, no Betti number with larger difference can be nonzero. Also
\displaystyle a+\sigma
is the largest internal degree in the
\displaystyle\nu
-th strand, since one cannot choose more than all
\displaystyle a
distinguished vertices and all vertices of the nontrivial blocks. Hence, the displayed Betti number lies in the north-east corner of the nonzero region of the Betti table, and is therefore extremal. By Theorem
6.1
, its difference equals the regularity.
∎

The next theorem determines the last-column extremal entry and the projective dimension.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\displaystyle G
be either
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
or
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
on
\displaystyle N=a+\sum_{r=1}^{m}b_{r}
vertices. Then
\displaystyle\pd(G)=N-1
. More precisely, the following hold.

(i)

If
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
and
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=1.

If
\displaystyle m=1
, then
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=b_{1}.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
and
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=a.

If
\displaystyle m=1
, then
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=a+b_{1}-1.

Proof.

By Hochster’s formula, we have

\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G);\mathbb{K})=\comp(\mathrm{Ind}(G))-1.

Thus the problem reduces to counting connected components of the full independence complex.
For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})
, Proposition
3.1
implies that

\mathrm{Ind}(G)=\langle A\rangle\sqcup(D_{b_{1}}*\cdots*D_{b_{m}}).

If
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, the join component is connected, so
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)
has exactly two connected components, whence
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=1
. If
\displaystyle m=1
, then the join component is just
\displaystyle D_{b_{1}}
, so the total number of components is
\displaystyle b_{1}+1
, thereby giving
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=b_{1}
.
For
\displaystyle G=\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})
, Proposition
3.1
implies us

\mathrm{Ind}(G)=D_{a}\sqcup(D_{b_{1}}*\cdots*D_{b_{m}}).

If
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, the join component is connected, so
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)
has
\displaystyle a+1
connected components, whence
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=a
. If
\displaystyle m=1
, then the second component is
\displaystyle D_{b_{1}}
, so the total number of components is
\displaystyle a+b_{1}
, and
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)=a+b_{1}-1
. In every case
\displaystyle\beta_{N-1,N}(G)\neq 0
, so the projective dimension equals
\displaystyle N-1
.
∎

The next proposition shows that the Betti numbers are monotone with respect to enlarging blocks.

Proposition 6.5
.

Fix
\displaystyle a\geq 1
and let
\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\dots,b_{m})
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
satisfy
\displaystyle b_{r}\leq c_{r}
for every
\displaystyle r
. Then for every bidegree
\displaystyle(i,j)
and for both graph families,

\beta_{i,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b}))\leq\beta_{i,j}(\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{c})),\qquad\beta_{i,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b}))\leq\beta_{i,j}(\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{c})).

Proof.

Every formula in Sections
4
and
5
is a sum of nonnegative multiples of binomial coefficients in the variables
\displaystyle b_{r}
. Replacing
\displaystyle b_{r}
by a larger
\displaystyle c_{r}
can only increase these coefficients. Therefore, each graded Betti number is monotone under coordinatewise enlargement of the block sizes.
∎

7
Applications to algebraic graphs from groups

We now illustrate the general formulas on graph classes attached to finite groups. We begin with pineapple graphs, because prime-power dihedral power graphs fall into that class.

Let
\displaystyle P_{m}^{r}
denote the pineapple graph obtained from a clique
\displaystyle K_{m}
by attaching
\displaystyle r
pendant vertices to one distinguished vertex of the clique.

The next theorem computes the entire Betti table of the pineapple graph, not just its linear strand.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
\displaystyle G=P_{m}^{r}
with
\displaystyle m\geq 2
and
\displaystyle r\geq 1
. Then all nonzero graded Betti numbers lie on the linear strand, and for every
\displaystyle j\geq 2
,

\beta_{j-1,j}(G)=(j-1)\binom{m}{j}+\binom{r}{j-1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u+v=j-1\\
u,v\geq 1\end{subarray}}\binom{m-1}{u}\binom{r}{v}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\beta_{i,j}(G)=0
for
\displaystyle j-i\geq 2,

\displaystyle\reg(G)=1,\reg(I(G))=2,\pd(G)=m+r-1,

and

\displaystyle\beta_{m+r-1,m+r}(G)=1

is the unique extremal Betti number.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle v_{0}
be the distinguished clique vertex, let
\displaystyle C^{\prime}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{m-1}\}
be the remaining clique vertices, and let
\displaystyle L=\{\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{r}\}
be the leaves. Every induced subcomplex of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)
is a disjoint union of contractible pieces and isolated vertices, so higher reduced homology vanishes. Thus all nonzero Betti numbers lie on the linear strand.

To compute
\displaystyle\beta_{j-1,j}(G)
, use Hochster’s formula and count disconnected induced subcomplexes on
\displaystyle j
vertices.

Case 1:
If
\displaystyle W
lies entirely in the clique
\displaystyle K_{m}={v_{0}}\cup C^{\prime}
. Then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong D_{j}
, contributing
\displaystyle(j-1)\binom{m}{j}
.

Case 2:
If
\displaystyle W=\{v_{0}\}\cup T
with
\displaystyle T\subseteq L
and
\displaystyle|T|=j-1
. Then

\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}\cong{v_{0}}\sqcup\langle T\rangle,

so
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=1
, and its contribution is
\displaystyle\binom{r}{j-1}
.

Case 3:
If
\displaystyle W=\{v_{0}\}\cup U\cup T
, where
\displaystyle U\subseteq C^{\prime}
,
\displaystyle T\subseteq L
,
\displaystyle u=|U|\geq 1
,
\displaystyle v=|T|\geq 1
, and
\displaystyle u+v=j-1
. Then
\displaystyle\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W}
is the disjoint union of the isolated vertex
\displaystyle{v_{0}}
and a contractible component on
\displaystyle U\cup T
, and hence
\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{H}_{0}(\mathrm{Ind}(G)_{W};\mathbb{K})=1
. The number of such subsets is
\displaystyle\binom{m-1}{u}\binom{r}{v}
, and summing over
\displaystyle u+v=j-1
gives the third term.

All other subsets give connected induced subcomplexes, and hence contribute zero. This proves the linear formula. Since there are no higher strands,
\displaystyle\reg(G)=1
and
\displaystyle\reg(I(G))=2
. The full independence complex has exactly two connected components, namely the isolated facet
\displaystyle\{v_{0}\}
and the contractible component on
\displaystyle C^{\prime}\cup L
. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{m+r-1,m+r}(G)=\comp(\mathrm{Ind}(G))-1=1,

which is extremal, and
\displaystyle\pd(G)=m+r-1
.
∎

Recall that the undirected power graph
\displaystyle P(\Gamma)
of a finite group
\displaystyle\Gamma
has vertex set
\displaystyle\Gamma
and two distinct vertices adjacent whenever one is a positive power of the other
[
5
,
1
]
.

An important class comes from elementary abelian groups. If
\displaystyle\Gamma
has order
\displaystyle p^{z}
and every nonidentity element has order
\displaystyle p
, then the power graph is a join of one distinguished vertex with many copies of
\displaystyle K_{p-1}
, see
[
6
,
22
]
.

The next corollary gives a complete homogeneous formula for elementary abelian groups.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\displaystyle\Gamma
be an elementary abelian group of order
\displaystyle p^{z}
, and let

\displaystyle\ell=\frac{p^{z}-1}{p-1}.

Then

\displaystyle P(\Gamma)\cong\mathcal{C}(1;(\underbrace{p-1,\dots,p-1}_{\ell\text{ times}})),
and for
\displaystyle j\geq 2
,

\beta_{j-1,j}(P(\Gamma))=\ell(j-1)\binom{p-1}{j}+\ell(j-1)\binom{p-1}{j-1}+\binom{\ell(p-1)}{j-1}-\ell\binom{p-1}{j-1},

and for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
,

\beta_{j-t,j}(P(\Gamma))=\binom{\ell}{t}[z^{j}]P_{p-1}(z)^{t}+\binom{\ell}{t}[z^{j-1}]P_{p-1}(z)^{t},

where

P_{p-1}(z)=\sum_{q=2}^{p-1}(q-1)\binom{p-1}{q}z^{q}.

In particular,

\displaystyle\reg(R/I(P(\Gamma)))=\max\{1,\ell\},
and
\displaystyle\pd(R/I(P(\Gamma)))=p^{z}-1.

Proof.

By the standard subgroup count,
\displaystyle\Gamma
has exactly
\displaystyle\ell=\tfrac{p^{z}-1}{p-1}
subgroups of order
\displaystyle p
, each contributing a clique
\displaystyle K_{p-1}
among the nonidentity vertices, and the identity is adjacent to every vertex. Hence
\displaystyle P(\Gamma)\cong K_{1}*\ell K_{p-1}
, which is exactly
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(1;(\underbrace{p-1,\dots,p-1}_{\ell}))
; see
[
6
,
22
]
. Applying Theorems
5.1
,
5.2
, and
6.4
with
\displaystyle a=1
and all
\displaystyle b_{r}=p-1
yields the formulas.
∎

Finally, we return to the dihedral prime-power case already visible in
[

]
.

The next corollary shows that prime-power dihedral power graphs are pineapple graphs, so their full Betti tables are known.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\Gamma=D_{2n}
be the dihedral group of order
\displaystyle 2n
, and assume
\displaystyle n=p^{\alpha}
is a prime power. Then

\displaystyle P(\Gamma)\cong P_{n}^{n},
and consequently

\beta_{j-1,j}(P(\Gamma))=(j-1)\binom{n}{j}+\binom{n}{j-1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u+v=j-1\\
u,v\geq 1\end{subarray}}\binom{n-1}{u}\binom{n}{v},

all higher strands vanish, with

\displaystyle\reg(R/I(P(\Gamma)))=1,
and
\displaystyle\pd(R/I(P(\Gamma)))=2n-1.

Proof.

The structural description
\displaystyle P(D_{2n})\cong P_{n}^{n}
in the prime-power case is exactly the pineapple description obtained in
[
22
]
. The cyclic subgroup of rotations forms a clique of size
\displaystyle n
, and the
\displaystyle n
reflections become leaves attached to the identity. The formulas therefore follow from Theorem
7.1
.

8
Conclusion

This paper closes the main gap left by the recent literature on Betti numbers of split-like algebraic graphs and power graphs of finite groups. The central contribution is a complete computation of the graded Betti numbers of the heterogeneous graph families

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(a;\mathbf{b})=\overline{K}_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(a;\mathbf{b})=K_{a}*\bigsqcup_{r=1}^{m}K_{b_{r}},
with arbitrary block sizes. The method rests on a structural decomposition of the independence complex into a simplex or discrete component together with an iterated join of discrete complexes. Once this decomposition is identified, Hochster’s formula yields exact coefficient-extraction formulas for every graded Betti number. From these formulas we derived closed expressions for the linear strand, every higher strand, the Hilbert series, the regularity, the projective dimension, and two natural classes of extremal Betti numbers.

The results recover the initial equal-block formulas from
[
22
]
but go substantially further by determining the complete Betti table and the regularity. The paper also clarifies the role of the number
\displaystyle\nu
of nontrivial blocks, as it is exactly the quotient regularity unless
\displaystyle\nu=0
, in which case the regularity is
\displaystyle 1
. This leads to a sharp
\displaystyle 2
-linear criterion for the edge ideal. In addition, the top regularity corner and the last-column corner are identified explicitly, making the global shape of the Betti table transparent.

The group-theoretic applications show that the formulas are not merely abstract generalizations. Pineapple graphs, cyclic prime-power power graphs, elementary abelian power graphs, and prime-power dihedral power graphs all fit naturally into the framework. In particular, the present work gives complete Betti tables for classes for which only partial formulas or initial strands were previously available.

There are also limitations. The power graphs of arbitrary cyclic groups, and more generally of arbitrary finite groups, need not decompose into the block-join patterns studied here. For those graphs the subgroup lattice may force more complicated simplicial topology and genuinely new homological phenomena. Thus the main open direction is to identify additional structural templates beyond the split–join families treated in this paper. A second direction is to investigate multigraded refinements, Alexander dual interpretations, and asymptotic behavior under iterated joins or subgroup-growth operations. A third direction is to extend the present coefficient-extraction method to other algebraic graph classes, especially commuting and comaximal graphs whose block structures suggest partial compatibility with the methods developed here.
