Executive summary
Steel Thread is a NA-22 venture that seeks to build trustworthy, reliable AI models that can be
used in a wide variety of nonproliferation tasks. Identifying and developing appropriate
benchmarks and evaluation methods will enable the venture to select and adapt models to
provide the most value in this domain. This report:
 • Provides an overview of benchmark design, evaluation, and challenges
 • Reviews a variety of open benchmarks, with a focus on language models and tasks
 • Identifies benchmarks that are most relevant to Steel Thread
Several challenges for benchmark development and use are addressed in the report:
 • Data representation, quality, and coverage
 • Data contamination
 • Metric interpretability and validity
 • Reproducibility and overemphasis on leaderboard rankings
 • Benchmark saturation
 • Computation and resource requirements
 • Safety and security
The report provides detailed evaluations of 17 benchmarks and short reviews of an additional
21. We consider these in relation to four use cases identified by the Steel Thread Human-AI
Teaming team: 1) open-ended question answering, 2) information retrieval, 3) summarization,
and 4) consensus analysis. Several of the benchmarks evaluated are potentially relevant to
these tasks, although many are limited to multiple-choice question answering or have narrow
or inconsistent coverage of nonproliferation-relevant topics. Further investigation is needed to
establish whether performance on these benchmarks correlates with Steel Thread task
performance.
Benchmarks are rapidly evolving as researchers address their limitations. We expect to see
trends toward:
 • More extensive benchmarks that cover a variety of domains and evaluation modes
 • More realistic task-oriented benchmarks in specific domains
 • More systematic, capability-oriented benchmarks based on cognitive science and
 psychometric methods
Of the benchmarks reviewed, we identify the following as most promising for Steel Thread
models and applications:
 • HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models): Covers a range of relevant tasks
 • MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding): Particularly strong in science and
 mathematics
 • GPQA (Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q&A): Also strong in science and mathematics
 • TruthfulQA: Useful for capturing reliability in relation to hallucinations and
 confabulations

Introduction
Steel Thread is a NA-22 venture that seeks to build trustworthy, reliable AI models that can be
used in a wide variety of nonproliferation tasks. A key aspect of building these models is
developing appropriate benchmarks and evaluation methods, which will enable the venture to
identify and adapt models to provide the most value in the nonproliferation domain.
Benchmarks must be relevant to key tasks in this domain, such as question answering,
information retrieval, document summarization and classification, consensus analysis, and
image and data analysis. This report 1) provides an overview of benchmark design, evaluation,
and challenges; 2) reviews a variety of open benchmarks, with a focus on language models and
tasks; and 3) identifies benchmarks that are most relevant to Steel Thread. This report is
intended to serve as a basis for further efforts to classify and evaluate benchmarks and their
correlation with success on nonproliferation-specific tasks.

The Steel Thread venture has defined benchmarks to be a particular combination of a dataset
(or datasets) and a metric (or metrics) conceptualized as representing one or more specific
tasks or sets of abilities for a specific modality. It is adopted by a research community as a
shared framework for comparing methods.1 It includes 1) Data: Labeled (a designated subset
not used for training, which could be all the data), 2) Metric: A way to quantify performance, 3)
Task/Ability: What the benchmark is testing, 4) Protocol: A structured and repeatable
evaluation process, 5) Baseline/Reference Model: For comparison; could be statistical, rule-
based, SME-derived, or another model, and 6) Maintenance Plan: to update with new
information over time; important for long-term utility.

For further clarity, the definition includes what a benchmark, in this context, is not. It is not a
corpus of training data, specific to a model (it is intended to apply to a range of models), a
universal evaluation of performance, a guarantee that the ‘top’ model on the leaderboard will
be the best fit for every specific use case, an all-encompassing proof of a model’s universal
quality, nor is it a one-size-fits-all measure of success. It does not cover every real-world
constraint (like operational, ethical, or cost considerations), a systems integration test, or a unit
test.

This definition was inspired by [1] and resulted from discussions within the Steel Thread
Benchmarking Task Force. This group was formed to define what we would mean as a
benchmark within Steel Thread but persisted as the need to develop a thorough understanding
of the large and expanding existing benchmarking space. This technical report is a result of the
group’s divide and conquer approach to exploring this space.

The release of benchmarks might not be progressing as quickly as model development, but it is
moving very fast, as many benchmarks quickly become saturated, when state-of-the-art models
score so close to the benchmark’s ceiling that their results are virtually indistinguishable. At

Benchmarking challenges
Broadly, a benchmark functions as a shared, reproducible standard to assess the capabilities of
a model on a given task or set of tasks. Benchmark scores are often used to assess suitability for
deployment, to characterize a model’s strengths and limitations, and to compare competing
models. Benchmarks today are the de facto tool for measuring AI progress, and as such,
benchmarks serve a powerful role in shaping the social organization and research goals of the
field of machine learning [5].

In this section, we touch on some of the major challenges facing developers of benchmarks and
practitioners who seek to use benchmarks for model evaluation. The six components: data,
metric, task, protocol, baseline, and maintenance plan in the benchmarking task force’s
benchmark definition each present their own challenges and the following challenges discussed
here are related to one or many of the components.

Many benchmarking challenges stem from issues of construct validity, the extent to which a
benchmark accurately measures the capability it claims to assess [6]. A full treatment on
construct validity would require addressing how model capabilities should be defined and

 4

measured, a complex and often philosophical debate. While important, that discussion lies
beyond the scope of this report and we refer interested readers to [7, 8] and the references
therein. Here, we treat benchmarks as imperfect proxies for model capabilities and focus the
discussion on a few of the practical challenges facing benchmark developers and researchers
today outlined below.

 1. Data representation, quality, and coverage
 Data is a foundational component for both model training and benchmarking. The
 validity of a benchmark as an assessment depends on the extent to which the
 benchmark dataset represents the domain in which the model will be deployed.
 Curating such a dataset poses many challenges as benchmarks require high-quality,
 representative data and annotations. In many circumstances, data collection and
 annotation require domain-specific experts, making data collection costly and labor-
 intensive. As a result, benchmarks can be limited in scope, coverage, and diversity,
 limiting the ability of the benchmark to faithfully measure the capabilities they are
 designed to assess.

 Determining the sufficient coverage, and therefore appropriate size of a benchmark,
 presents a further difficulty. On one hand, it is impossible to include every potential
 instance. On the other, insufficient coverage can lead to model performance blind spots.
 McCoy et al. [9] demonstrated for LLMs that task performance is sensitive to the
 probability distribution of tasks and inputs, with low-probability, or low coverage,
 instances systematically producing poorer results, even for deterministic tasks or tasks
 of similar complexity. When benchmarks fail to have adequate coverage of the
 capability, they risk concealing performance gaps that may emerge only in deployment.

 Furthering these issues, data problems can be subtle and pervasive. Sambasivan et al.,
 [10] describes how data issues propagate through ‘data cascades’, in which flaws
 introduced during data collection or management compound over time, producing
 negative downstream effects. We can expect these dynamics to apply to benchmarks as
 issues with representation, quality, and coverage can cascade into misleading
 assessments of model performance. Moreover, [10] notes changes in deployment space
 e.g., shifts in sensor reliability or environmental conditions pose additional risks in terms
 of data cascades. In the case of benchmarks, any static test dataset may no longer
 provide an adequate measure of performance in such conditions, emphasizing the
 challenge of keeping an up-to-date benchmark in dynamic deployment scenarios.

 Ultimately, the credibility of a benchmark rests on the quality and scope of its data. That
 is, without sufficient coverage, quality control, and representation of the task it intends
 to measure, benchmarks risk failing their central purpose of providing a reliable
 measure of model capabilities.

 2. Data contamination

 5

 Data contamination, or data leakage, is a major concern in the benchmarking field
 today. While there are many potential issues arising from the leakage of benchmark
 data, Sainz et al., [11] identify two significant consequences of data contamination.

 First, models with access to benchmark data in their pretraining will likely have a
 comparative advantage over models that have not encountered the same data. This
 undermines the validity of the reported results and can lead to that model being
 preferentially adopted by the ML community. Secondly, scientific claims and hypothesis
 using contaminated LLMs risk being incorrect or misleading. Specifically, papers may
 draw incorrect conclusions or prematurely dismiss alternative hypotheses, driving
 research across academia, industry, and government toward unproductive directions
 that consume time, money, and resources.

 For LLMs, the most obvious form of data contamination occurs when benchmark data
 appears in the pretraining corpus of a model. However, other forms have been
 documented [11]. Balloccu et al. [12], discuss how data leakage can occur when LLMs
 interact with users. They report extensive data leakage to OpenAI models via OpenAI’s
 data usage policy which enabled calls to iteratively improve the model through the API.
 In total, they estimate that OpenAI models have been exposed to 4.7 million samples
 from 263 benchmarks.

 A further complication for data contamination is introduced by in context-learning, or
 the ability of LLMs to adapt to new tasks by conditioning on input-output examples
 provided directly by prompts and without any gradient updates or parameter changes
 [13]. The work of Min et al. [14], shows that even with random output examples paired
 with the real inputs improved performance significantly. This suggests that
 contamination does not require explicit ground-truth labels to distort the benchmark
 evaluation, significantly complicating how data contamination can occur.

 Finally, detection of contamination cases is becoming increasingly challenging. Data
 contamination has been traditionally done by directly analyzing the training data, but
 the current scale of the pre-training data makes this a computationally difficult task. For
 closed-source models, the challenges are even greater, as the pretraining data is not
 publicly available and contamination detection must rely on additional techniques e.g.,
 [15].

3. Metric interpretability and validity

 Metrics define what success on a given benchmark means and provide the quantitative
 basis for comparing models and interpreting results. Therefore, benchmarks should
 utilize clear, reliable, valid, and interpretable metrics. However, due to the scale of
 modern benchmarks, automatic metrics, metrics which can be computed
 algorithmically, are essential. However, the usage of automatic metrics introduces
 additional concerns about metric interpretability and validity.

 6

 In a recent article on analysis from NLG papers in ACL and INLG, for natural language
 generation, Schmidtová et al., [16] report 95% of recent papers use automatic metrics,
 yet fewer than 13% provide a rationale for why those metrics were chosen. Many
 studies default to BLEU [17] or ROUGE [18] even when these are known to correlate
 poorly with factuality, adequacy, or style. [16] also emphasize a surprising issue with
 reproducibility with parameterized metrics e.g., BLEU. That is, nearly two-thirds of
 papers omit implementation and parametrization details, meaning BLEU scores across
 papers can reflect entirely different computational choices. This highlights a field-wide
 problem of metric transparency and undermines the comparability of results.

 The question of metric validity is an increasing concern. For example, Reiter’s review
 [19] on BLEU aggregates 284 correlations between BLEU and human judgements,
 concluding that BLEU is only sensible if it has been validated for the given task
 appropriately. More recent approaches leverage learned embedding spaces e.g.,
 BERTScore [20], BLEURT [21] for automatic metric evaluation, while others use trained
 LLMs to provide evaluations, called LLM-as-a-judge. These methods promise better
 alignment with human judgements but bring their own challenges including bias,
 prompt sensitivity, and self-preference effects. In addition, the LLM to be used as a
 judge should be a more capable LLM, which, by definition, means that the most capable
 LLM cannot be evaluated. While there has been a lot of progress in utilizing LLM-as-a-
 judge, [22] recommends validating these methods against task-specific human
 annotations before using the metric for evaluation.

 No single metric can measure and capture all nuances of a problem. However, a
 benchmark metric should be well-motivated and align with the design of the metric and
 be validated across gold-standard instances for the given task(s) [16]. If a metric has
 limitations, is sensitive to hyperparameters, or should be interpreted in a certain
 manner, these need to be made as explicit.

4. Reproducibility and leaderboardism
 Leaderboardism is characterized by an over-emphasis on the importance of models
 attaining high rankings on a subset of common benchmark leaderboards and conflating
 general AI progress with the improvements in the leaderboards. Saxon et al., introduce
 the notion as benchmarks as microscopes, emphasizing that benchmarks should aim to
 yield meaningful scientific observations rather than act as competitive targets. Koch et
 al. [5], observe the perception that advancing on established benchmarks signals
 scientific progress push researchers toward design choices that maximize scores, rather
 than general capabilities. That is, if models are trained to merely improve on a select
 set of benchmarks, overfitting to the benchmark is likely. This weakens the ability of the
 benchmark to serve as a reliable proxy for a real-world capability.

 A 2022 survey of NLP researchers by Holtman et al. [23], found that 88% of participants
 believe there is too much focus on optimizing performance on benchmarks and

 7

 illustrates the growing concern that benchmark optimization may pose a risk to research
 incentives [5]. The oft-cited maxim “the map is not the territory” is apt here as the
 benchmark is a limited abstraction, and mistaking benchmark progress for capability
 progress can be a mistake.

 Reproducibility of benchmark scores is of increasing concern. It is recognized that in
 some benchmarks, small design choices, data processing steps, and evaluation tweaks
 can inflate scores [12]. If there is an over-incentivization to rise in the benchmark
 leaderboards, then we can expect methodological shortcuts, selective reporting, and
 metric overfitting to become more prevalent, eroding the trust and interpretability of
 benchmark results.

5. Benchmark saturation
 As noted previously, the phenomenon in which benchmarks are no longer challenging to
 state-of-the-art models is often described as benchmark saturation. Ott et al. [24], note
 that benchmark saturation is particularly problematic as saturated benchmarks can no
 longer be used for measuring or steering progress. In particular, saturated benchmarks
 may give the misleading appearance that the capability or problem it describes as more
 mature and developed than it is in practice.

 With that said, benchmark saturation is a well-known problem, and many benchmarks
 try to mitigate saturation by preferentially including problems that are known to be
 difficult for the best models at the time of release (e.g., TruthfulQA, SimpleQA, etc.).
 However, the pace at which frontier models are advancing is another concern and the
 lifecycle of a benchmark are increasingly short, particularly when considering the
 challenges of data acquisition previously discussed.

6. Computation and resource requirements
 Large-scale creation and evaluation of benchmarks for LLMs entail a substantial
 computation cost. This cost poses a barrier for participation, particularly for small
 research groups with limited resources. While we have discussed the resource
 challenges for developing a benchmark, we haven’t discussed the requirements to
 evaluate models with benchmarks. As the capabilities of models advance, researchers
 seek more comprehensive benchmark suites like HELM [25] to evaluate their models.
 However, in some cases, evaluating a single model can require over 500 GPU hours [25].
 Due to these costs, many research groups rely on third-party evaluation pipelines, or
 limit themselves to a smaller set of benchmarks.

 Koch et al. [5], recognize this trend and refer to it as the institutionalization of
 benchmark datasets, where benchmarking practices concentrate on a small number of
 datasets for a task community e.g., machine vision, and heavily concentrated on
 datasets created by a small number of well-resourced institutions. The consequences of
 benchmark dataset concentration include the usage of datasets not for their intended

 8

 purpose, despite new datasets created for that task specifically, but have yet to be
 adopted by the ML community [5]. The risk is that innovations outside of the core,
 adopted benchmarks, will be increasingly unincentivized and potentially leaving many
 domains and capabilities unexplored.

 7. Safety and security
 A prominent concern today is AI safety and security, leading to the development of AI
 safety benchmarks e.g., TruthfulQA, Human Jailbreaks, etc. These benchmarks are
 intended to validate that the language model is robust and trustworthy by assessing
 factors such as model reliability, honesty, fairness, and its propensity to produce
 hallucinations, which are incorrect, but oftentimes plausible sounding, statements.
 Additionally, some benchmarks evaluate whether a model is vulnerable to malicious
 use.

 From the AI safety perspective, benchmarks are not only evaluative tools but, ideally,
 are also informing deployment and safety risks, which is a challenge on its own. Ren et
 al. [26], introduce the concept of safetywashing where capability improvements are
 misrepresented as safety advancements. One of the primary concerns of benchmark
 performance being highly correlated with model capabilities, is that the models are not
 disproportionately safer relative to its increased capabilities and this can introduce new
 risks that scale or emerge with greater capabilities. That is, [26] suggest what matters
 for AI safety is whether safety improvements outpace capability growth. In their
 analysis, they find that many safety benchmarks are highly correlated with model
 capabilities, indicating safetywashing as a real concern.

 In high-consequence domains, AI safety and security is particularly critical as
 benchmarks are often used as one of the final assurance checks and therefore
 benchmarks may function as the ultimate safeguard prior to decisions that carry
 substantial impact. If a benchmark, or collection of benchmarks, serve this safeguard
 role, then the stakes associated with many of the discussed challenges are magnified.
 Hence, a distinct challenge for AI safety is to design benchmarks that not only test
 performance under idealized conditions, but also to probe for failure modes under
 realistic, or even adversarial, settings.

Practical benchmark design and evaluation
The recognition of benchmarking challenges has direct implications for how new benchmarks
should be designed and deployed. The challenges outlined above underscore that benchmark
require thoughtfulness and technical rigor if they are to accurately assesses and measure the
intended model capabilities. However, for there to be a direct impact on current benchmarking
practices, there likely needs to be a practical and systematic methodology for the creation and
evaluation of benchmarks.

 9

The need for systematic benchmark development and evaluation is increasingly recognized.
While some prior works [27, 28, 29] focus on recommendations and best practice
benchmarking standards in domain specific methods, Reuel et al. [1], operationalizes many of
the issues we discuss here in their BetterBench methodology.

BetterBench is exemplary of recent research focusing on practical implications on benchmark
design and evaluation in a general setting. The BetterBench methodology includes 46 best
benchmarking practices across the full benchmark lifecycle. The BetterBench checklist provides
a structured framework for translating benchmarking principles into verifiable practice and
covers defining tested capabilities, validating metrics, publishing reproducible evaluation code,
and documenting benchmark evolution, addressing many of the challenges like data
contamination, metric validity, and reproducibility discussed in the previous section.

While BetterBench covers a broad range of essential benchmarking practices, we note that
evaluating modern language models can also benefit from accounting for language model
specific behaviors that are not directly discussed in the BetterBench methodology. For instance,
as highlighted in Kalai et al. [30], hallucinations, a major issue with language models today, are
often the result of training-time incentives such as rewarding confident guessing, rather than
rewarding acknowledgement of uncertainty or abstention. Indeed, [30] argues that many of the
prominent benchmarks on leaderboards today unintentionally reward hallucinatory behavior
by relying on binary right-wrong evaluations that fail to credit responses that express
uncertainty or omit unnecessary details. The solution proposed in [30] is to realign the
evaluation incentives by rewarding appropriate expressions of uncertainty, rather than
penalizing them, and by considering other metrics beyond simple binary accuracy.

Implementing frameworks such as BetterBench or modifying current benchmarks to permit
more nuanced question and answering, ultimately requires the collective commitment from the
broader research community. Systematic benchmarking is resource intensive and benefits from
institutional support and collaborative standards to ensure methodological consistency. This
requirement is particularly important for high-consequence domains, where benchmarks may
be the final assurance check that could impact downstream decision making. Continued
investment and research into evaluation frameworks implementing best benchmarking
practices is essential for ensuring that progress in model capability is matched by progress in
benchmarking.

Methodology
Our benchmark assessment was conducted by individual benchmarking taskforce members.
The selection criteria for benchmarks were primarily based on the perceived popularity of the
benchmark within the machine learning community, either historically or currently. We also
emphasized benchmarks which have been reported in models recently released by frontier

 10

labs2. We note that the selection of benchmarks included in this report is not intended to be
comprehensive or systematic, but rather to provide practical insights into general
benchmarking methods which can be leveraged for Steel Thread use cases.

Benchmarking task force team members selected benchmarks in line with their own interests.
For consistent evaluation of the benchmarks, we created a standardized evaluation rubric (see
Appendix A) for evaluators to follow. The rubric main sections are given by general information,
a summary of the benchmark with high-level meta information, a detailed evaluation, and
guidelines and recommendations for usage within Steel Thread.

The general information is a summary of the benchmark with high-level meta information, e.g.,
authorship and benchmark publication date. The detailed evaluation is an assessment focused
on considering the strengths and limitations of various benchmark characteristics such as
dataset coverage and diversity, task difficulty, and evaluation design i.e., metrics and methods
used to evaluate performance. The guidelines and recommendations for usage within Steel
Thread considers potential use cases of the benchmark for the Steel Thread mission, a
recommendation for inclusion of the benchmark in an evaluation suite and cautions for
potential misuse or misinterpretations for the benchmark results.

 CLAPNQ 2024 Natural-Questions spin-off with gold passages for end-to-end
 RAG long-answer generation

Some high level takeaways are that most of the earlier benchmarks are saturated by the
frontier AI models. However, many of these benchmarks test general knowledge in a multiple-
choice style question and answering, including many that attempt to quantify an aspect of
common sense. The more modern benchmarks are better aligned with expert workflows and
should be emulated when creating bespoke Steel Thread relevant benchmarks.

Steel Thread relevance of open benchmarks
The Steel Thread Human-AI Teaming team has identified four key use cases to guide model
development for language models within the venture. These are:

 1. “Open-ended” QA
 o Input: Question(s) about nonproliferation subject area
 o Output: Answer(s) to question(s) in paragraph form with citations
 2. Information retrieval
 o Input: Keywords; general document corpus
 o Output: Subset of relevant documents, keywords highlighted
 3. Summarization
 o Input: Document corpus (preselected for relevance)
 o Output: Paragraph summary of corpus with document citations
 4. Classification/Categorization - “Consensus Analysis”
 o Input: Claim(s) to be evaluated (optional); Document corpus (preselected for
 relevance)
 o Output: Results showing the percentage of documents in support of different
 claims OR in support of claim(s) specified by user

The majority of benchmarks we evaluated in depth for FY25 were based on multiple-choice
question answering. The primary exception was the code generation/correction benchmarks,
which were based on summative evaluation of code performance. Of the tasks listed above,
only Task 1 is specifically a QA task. Tasks 2 and 4 ask the model to evaluate a document corpus
in relation to keywords and claims rather than questions per se, while task 3 asks for a summary
without explicit prompting from the user. Task 4 is a complex task that involves several
subtasks related to identifying and categorizing claims and documents. None of the tasks
involve simple factual retrieval or evaluation of options similar to a multiple-choice test, and
none have code as a primary output.

It is possible that model performance on multiple-choice QA benchmarks such as ARC,
OpenBookQA, MMLU, or MMMU could be correlated with performance on the open-ended,
long-form QA described in Task 1. Along with different QA formats, these benchmarks cover
general and scientific knowledge domains that may have some overlap with the
nonproliferation domain. Whether these issues are a significant barrier to use of these
benchmarks for Steel Thread requires further investigation.

 15

Performance on multiple-choice QA benchmarks is probably less likely to correlate with success
on Tasks 2-4, which all involve some form of information retrieval/summarization across a
known corpus. These tasks depend less on the knowledge encoded in a model’s training set and
are more related to a model’s general ability to summarize across an external corpus of
documents. This suggests there may be a need to identify additional benchmarks that test
general capabilities of a model in tasks like information retrieval, summarization, and reasoning
across information in a corpus.

Very broad benchmarks like BIG-bench and HELM cover a multitude of tasks and formats
beyond multiple-choice QA. Although these benchmarks are known to have inconsistent
coverage, the breadth of tasks they cover may provide better correlation with success on more
complex problems like Task 4 above that require information retrieval, summarization, and
reasoning capabilities. This is also an area where further investigation is required.

A benchmark like HealthBench Hard, which specifically focuses on medical interactions, is
unlikely to be directly useful for evaluating models for performance on nonproliferation tasks.
However, this type of benchmark may be useful to Steel Thread as a blueprint for how to create
a domain-specific benchmark featuring realistic professional problem-solving scenarios. The
recently released GDPval , which we have not yet evaluated, is another example of this type of
realistic task-oriented benchmark. One concern is that developing these sophisticated
benchmarks appears to be particularly time and labor-intensive.

These findings provide a path forward for identifying benchmarks that are correlated or
decorrelated with performance on Steel Thread tasks. In addition to testing the benchmarks
already identified, there is a need to identify additional benchmarks that capture performance
on more generic reasoning and linguistic processing tasks. Where existing benchmarks fail to
cover Steel Thread needs, developing in-house benchmarks similar to those that already exist
for other specialized scientific and professional domains is a potential option.

Conclusions
Benchmarks for LLMs and generative AI are still rapidly evolving as researchers continue to
identify and address concerns about coverage, saturation, construct validity, interpretability,
and other issues. In addition, there is increasing interest in moving beyond “leaderboardism”
into more nuanced benchmarking and evaluation approaches.

One clear trend is the movement from possibly simplistic benchmarks covering narrow tasks,
often limited to multiple-choice evaluation, toward more holistic benchmarks like HELM and
MMLU that compile tasks across a variety of domains and evaluation modes. In addition,
standards like BetterBench are beginning to bring a more rigorous and systematic approach to
the evaluation and comparison of benchmarks themselves.

Another recent trend in a different direction has been toward developing much harder and
more realistic benchmarks for narrowly focused professional domains such as medicine or

 16

engineering, such as HealthBench Hard and GDPval. Developing these detailed domain-specific
benchmarks is labor-intensive and often requires extensive input from human domain experts,
but they can address issues with saturation and interpretability and may provide a more
realistic gauge of real-world performance. In the future, we expect to see continuation of both
trends, as well as potentially an increasing emphasis on carefully designed capability-oriented
evaluations and benchmarks that draw more deeply from fields like cognitive science and
psychometrics.

For this report, we identified a broad range of benchmarks and reviewed a few in detail,
focusing mainly on language model-oriented benchmarks. In addition, we focused primarily on
question-answering (QA) benchmarks. These have been used extensively and have a good track
record, but it is not clear to what extent performance on QA will generalize to performance on
non-QA tasks. While QA dominates in many existing benchmarks, as we extend this effort we
will expand our scope to a broader range of capability-oriented benchmarks. However, we
believe the scope of our effort is sufficient to identify some key benchmarks for Steel Thread
use.

From the benchmarks we have evaluated in this phase of research, we identify HELM, MMLU,
and GPQA as the most promising open benchmarks for Steel Thread models and applications.
Although these are largely limited to multiple-choice question answering, they are quite
challenging and incorporate a wide range of tasks, with MMLU and GPQA in particular being
strong in science and mathematics questions that are highly relevant to Steel Thread. In
addition, TruthfulQA is a valuable benchmark for capturing baseline model reliability in relation
to hallucinations and confabulations. Moving forward, we may want to look into more general
capability-oriented evaluation tools that focus on areas like text summarization or information
retrieval rather than factual question-answering. These findings will provide a basis for further
Steel Thread efforts to test and evaluate benchmarks and their correlation with success on
nonproliferation-relevant tasks.

Beyond Nonproliferation
This report delineates the essential considerations for constructing benchmarks and proposes a
pragmatic framework for benchmark design, while also warning of common pitfalls. Although
the analysis centers on open benchmarks and was originally motivated by the need to survey
existing benchmarks for potential relevance to proliferation detection applications, the detailed
pitfalls of benchmark creation are broadly applicable to other specialized areas.

The Human‑AI teaming experts identified four tasks that are most relevant to the users of the
AI systems for proliferation detection applications: open‑ended question answering,
information retrieval, summarization, and consensus analysis. However, each of these
capabilities carries significant relevance far beyond the nuclear‑non‑proliferation arena and,
therefore, benchmarks deemed relevant for measuring capability on these topics could also be
relevant to other NNSA programs with similar requirements. For example, an AI technology
priority called out by NA-122 at the FY26 AI4NS workshop is analyzing large, complex

 17

documents quickly and efficiently, tasks we aim to benchmark for analysts, and NA-193
highlights that AI could assist with production and assembly in-process controls, testers, and
inspections, which could benefit from well-designed benchmarks to assess the accuracy and
groundedness of AI-assisted search and synthesis tools.

In this sense, the document can serve as a blueprint for systematically pinpointing existing,
relevant benchmarks and for deciding when a bespoke benchmark is required for a particular
application. As the conclusions note, the current trajectory of open‑benchmark development is
moving toward task‑oriented, domain‑specific evaluations, with less emphasis on information
recall and more on instruction following, tool use, and agentic demonstration of capability. This
trend reinforces the need for dedicated benchmarks tailored to NNSA applications and,
consequently, for a standardized, methodical approach to benchmark creation that anticipates
and mitigates the usual design pitfalls.

Appendix A: Evaluation outline
A copy of the outline that guided the evaluation of the benchmarks explored.

 Outline for Evaluating Benchmarks
 I. General Information
 1. Benchmark name
 2. Associated Paper/Manuscript Title
 3. Authors of Benchmark
 4. URL/DOI- link to benchmarks official website/repository
 5. Date Evaluated
 6. Reviewer(s) – Name(s) and affiliation(s) of evaluator(s)

 II. Summary of Benchmark
 1. Primary Goal/Purpose of Benchmark: concise statement on the intended
 objective/performance being evaluated by the benchmark e.g., reasoning, coding,
 multi-modality, etc.
 2. Metrics: Include which metrics are used to quantitatively measure performance on
 said objective(s).
 3. Dataset: Dataset name (if applicable), Include details on data type(s), and size of
 dataset. How was data created i.e., human annotated, synthetically generated, web-
 scraped, etc.
 4. Intended Use Cases: List the specific applications where this benchmark is likely to
 correlate with success
 5. Known Limitations: Brief account on the limitations for intended uses or tasks that
 would not be well-assessed by this benchmark

 III. Detailed Evaluation
Not all benchmarks will have the same analysis and detail. Redundancy should be avoided e.g.,
providing details on common metrics. The following are some considerations for the evaluation
of the benchmark:
 1. Benchmark Quality Assessment details
 a. Data quality- assess the accuracy and consistency of the data
 b. Data coverage and diversity- how well does the dataset cover and represents
 the diversity of real-world scenarios
 c. Task Difficulty and Model Performance: Describe the current state of the art
 (SOTA) performance for this model. Do current models easily achieve high
 performance, or does it still pose a challenge for models?
 d. Metrics Validity- provide details on what the metrics are measuring and
 discuss identifiable flaws and strengths of these metrics i.e., do the metrics

 21

 measure what they intend to measure. Are the metrics easily manipulated?
 What is considered good performance on this benchmark?
 e. Strengths- what does this benchmark do particularly well?
 f. Weaknesses- what are the shortcomings of this benchmark?
 g. Comparison to other benchmarks- detail benchmarks with similar intended
 uses and how they differ in data, metrics, difficulty, etc.
  2. Implementation and Maintainability
 a. Code Availability- is code publicly available, is it well-documented?
 b. Licensing and Restrictions- Any there any licensing terms or restrictions on
 usage?
 c. Computational and Storage Resources- Computational requirements, e.g.,
 needs GPUs and how much storage space is needed
 d. Maintenance Status- Is the benchmark being actively maintained? Is there a
 community of users contributing to its development?
 e. Adoption- How widely utilized is this benchmark? Are published papers using
 this benchmark in their reported results?

IV. Guidelines/Recommendations for Steel Thread
 1. Recommended Use Cases – based on your evaluation, what specific use cases is
 the benchmark most appropriate
 2. Recommendation for Evaluation Suite- should this benchmark be included in
 commonly used evaluation suite like LM evaluation harness?
 3. Cautions- What are potential misinterpretations or incorrect uses for this
 benchmark.
 4. Complementary Benchmarks- which other benchmarks could be used in
 conjunction with this benchmark to provide a more comprehensive assessment
 for similar use cases.
 5. Future Work- any commentary on suggestions to improve the benchmark

 compositional reasoning, context-sensitive rule application, and symbolic

 • Complementary Benchmarks
 o HellaSwag: Sentence completion tasks testing commonsense reasoning

 o TruthfulQA: 817 questions measuring factual accuracy and hallucination

 • Future Work: Require explanations alongside answers, expand beyond multiple-choice,

 ▪ Easy integration with `datasets` and `transformers` libraries

 • Recommended Use Cases
 o **Testing multi-hop reasoning** where the system must integrate a core
 science fact with external commonsense or world knowledge.
 o **Evaluating few-shot or zero-shot capabilities** of large language models,
 especially their ability to synthesize multiple knowledge types without external
 retrieval.
 o **Curriculum-aligned QA tasks**, particularly those centered on **grade school
 science education**.
 o **Benchmarking reasoning-focused models** (e.g., cognitive architectures,
 neuro-symbolic approaches) that go beyond surface-level text matching.
 o **Interpretable model development**, since the “open book” facts provide a
 constrained and inspectable reasoning base.
 • Recommendation for Evaluation Suite
 o **Yes**, OpenBookQA should be included in **commonly used evaluation
 suites**, such as **LM Evaluation Harness**, **HELM**, or **BIG-Bench-
 style** tests—**with caveats**:
 o **Justification**: Despite its limited size and domain, it is one of the few
 benchmarks specifically designed to test **reasoning with known and external
 knowledge**. It remains a valuable lightweight probe.
 o It can serve as a **stress test** for models that overly rely on memorized
 associations instead of logical reasoning.

 28

 o It **should not be used in isolation** to claim general QA or reasoning
 capabilities due to its narrow domain and fixed answer structure.
 • Cautions
 o **Overestimating general reasoning ability**: Success on OpenBookQA doesn’t
 imply broader QA capability, since questions are science-focused and
 constructed with specific fact chaining in mind.
 o **Training on test set**: The dataset is small, making it prone to overfitting.
 Some papers may inadvertently report inflated performance due to data
 leakage.
 o **Ignoring ambiguity**: Some questions depend on subtle commonsense
 nuances or ambiguous distractors. High accuracy does not guarantee
 **understanding**.
 o **Use with retrieval-augmented models**: The benchmark does not assume
 retrieval from a large corpus. Using it with such models may not reflect its
 intended task structure (reasoning from a small, known fact base).
 • Complementary Benchmarks
 o **ARC (AI2 Reasoning Challenge)** – Same domain (science), but with more
 diverse and complex questions; harder to “guess” answers.
 o **CommonsenseQA** – Tests similar integration of facts with commonsense
 reasoning in a broader domain.
 o **BoolQ** – Focused on yes/no questions from real-world queries; introduces
 more natural language variation.
 o **QASC** – Built by the same team as OpenBookQA, but focuses on **two-
 fact** composition reasoning for science QA.
 o **StrategyQA** – Designed to test **implicit multi-hop reasoning** without
 explicitly provided facts.
 • Future Work
 o **Include reasoning explanations**: Adding human-written rationales would
 support evaluation of explainability and alignment.
 o **Broaden the domain**: Introduce additional "books" from history, social
 science, or math to diversify the type of factual reasoning required.
 o **Increase size and difficulty**: Larger, adversarially filtered question sets could
 improve robustness and stress-test modern LLMs.
 o **Enable open-ended QA variants**: Allow for generative answers and rationale
 generation instead of just multiple choice.
 o **Incorporate adversarial examples**: Craft edge cases that foil shortcut
 strategies and probe deeper reasoning layers.

 typically only use 10% of their brains.”

 semantically equivalent answers, can be used to reduce the number of correct answers
 without any knowledge when there is a single correct response. However, when zero-
 shot prompted the Authors found that there is little evidence that models utilize such
 strategies. Finally, as observed in “Safteywashing: Do AI Saftey Benchmarks Actually

 36

 Measure Safety Progress?”, TruthfulQA performance in highly determined by general
 capabilities i.e., general knowledge and reasoning.

 true and false reference answers. The questions were adversarially selected i.e., written
 so that some humans would answer incorrectly and filtered based on performance by
 GPT-3-175B. Additional questions were created without the filtering on GPT-3-175B
 performance. Such selection of questions helps with saturation.
 • Benchmark Diversity: Benchmark contains questions from 38 categories and have

 • Metrics:

 o Multiple Choice: Accuracy for binary and single correct answer multiple choice

 • Recommended Use Cases: Evaluating general factual accuracy
 • Recommendation for Evaluation Suite: Yes

Appendix D: Statistical Comparisons of ChatGPT4o,
ChatGPT5, and ChatGPT5Pro for Evaluating Nuclear
Physics Knowledge
This appendix was written by Giri Gopalan and Henry Yuchi.

Given the recent release of ChatGPT5 and ChatGPT5Pro a natural nonproliferation question to
ask is: how knowledgeable in nuclear physics are new versions of chatGPT? In order to answer
this question, we use the NuclearQA benchmark developed by Steel Thread applied to OpenAI’s
state of the art large language models, using a set of general nuclear physics questions meant
to ascertain knowledge of basic nuclear physics.

Nuclear QA Summary: The nuclear physics basics question set consists of 38 yes or no
questions that are meant to evaluate factual knowledge of nuclear physics basics. Some
examples of these questions are:
 - Do photons carry momentum? (Yes.)
 - Is it true that hydrogen and deuterium have different resonance regions? (No.)
 - Is it true that the majority of the photon energy imparted on an atom, as a result of the
 photoelectric effect, is transferred to the kinetic energy of the ejected electron? (Yes.)

Data collection: The 38 questions were asked to be answered by ChatGPT4o, 5, and 5Pro using
LANL’s enterprise version. A prompt is entered along the lines: Answer the following questions
in batches of 10 with Yes or No. Put the questions and answers into a spreadsheet. For instance,
here are extracts from a session with ChatGPT5:

 69

 Figure: Two example extracts from a session with ChatGPT5.

We first asked ChatGPT4o and 5 to answer all of the questions at once instead of batching the
responses, but the quality of answers seemed to degrade, as ChatGPT4o and 5 were answering
them too quickly. Batching questions into 5 to 10 at a time seemed to improve the quality of
answers produced. Furthermore, we also tried inputting one question at a time (giving more
time per question), in which case the quality of responses was comparable to batching into
small sets.

Variation in Outputs: One important finding was that for distinct ChatGPT sessions (i.e., “New
Chats”), the same answer was not necessarily reproduced upon re-entering the same question.
This led us to consider a new source of variability/uncertainty in assessing the performance of
ChatGPT.

For example, when asked the question: Is it true that the alpha particle is equivalent to a highly
stable nucleus of a helium atom? All of ChatGPT4o, 5, and 5 Pro consistently answered Yes (the
correct answer).

On the other hand, when asked: Is it true that the masses of a hydrogen nucleus and a single
neutron are approximately equal? 1 of 6 replications from ChatGPT4o incorrectly answered no,
and the remaining 5 answered yes correctly. Therefore, we need to take into account
uncertainty in response when evaluating LLMs on QA benchmarks. For example, an evaluation
that includes the previously stated question with just a ‘1’ for correct ignores that the AI model
is less certain about its response than the question on stability of a helium atom nucleus.

 70

Study Design: We asked the 38 questions from the nuclear physics benchmark of Nuclear QAv2
developed by Steel Thread both one by one and in several batches to three ChatGPT models:
ChatGPT4o, ChatGPT5, and ChatGPT5Pro — and in all cases, we asked for yes or no answers.
Performance summary including both mean and standard deviation (sd):
Model Mean Performance Standard Deviation
ChatGPT4o                     73.7%                           1.7%
ChatGPT5                      76.3%                           2.6%
ChatGPT5Pro                   93.4%                           1.9%

These scores seem to indicate that ChatGPT5Pro does outperform ChatGPT5 and ChatGPT4o,
but that the performances of ChatGPT5 and ChatGPT4o are comparable. To more rigorously
corroborate these claims, we conducted an uncertainty analysis as follows.

Uncertainty Analysis via Simulation: We want to appropriately account for the following
sources of uncertainty when comparing different versions of ChatGPT:
 1. Test set sampling variability: While the 38 questions are chosen to be broadly
 representative of basic nuclear physics knowledge, this test set can be viewed as a
 sample from a large pool of potential nuclear physics questions. If, hypothetically, the
 versions of ChatGPT were privy to different test sets, there would be variation in overall
 scores. To address this source of uncertainty in evaluation we resample test items with
 replacement iteratively.
 2. Model output uncertainty: Another source of uncertainty discussed above is that
 ChatGPT’s response is sometimes uncertain (that is, even if it responds ‘Yes’, it could be
 unsure of its answer). We judge this level of uncertainty by repeatedly asking the same
 questions. In our uncertainty analysis, we randomly select one of the answers provided
 by ChatGPT with even probability – for example, if ChatGPT4o answered ‘Yes’ 5 times
 and ‘No’ 1 time for a particular question over six replications, in our resampled test set
 we simulate the ChatGPT4o answer to that question with ‘Yes’ with probability 5/6 and
 ‘No’ with probability 1/6.

We iteratively resampled test questions and responses 1000 times and compared the
distribution of differences in model performances below.
