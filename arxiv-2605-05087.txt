The top cohomology of principal congruence subgroups of special linear groups over Euclidean number rings
Urshita Pal

Abstract
For R R a Euclidean number ring, let Γ n ​ ( p ) \Gamma_{n}(p) be the level- p p principal congruence subgroup of SL n ​ ( R ) \text{SL}_{n}(R) . Borel–Serre showed that the cohomology of Γ n ​ ( p ) \Gamma_{n}(p) vanishes above a degree ν \nu that is quadratic in n n . Let K K be the fraction field of R R , and 𝒯 n ​ ( K ) \mathcal{T}_{n}(K) the Tits building of SL n ​ ( K ) \text{SL}_{n}(K) . For R = ℤ R=\mathbb{Z} , Lee–Szczarba asked when H ν ​ ( Γ n ​ ( p ) ) \text{H}^{\nu}(\Gamma_{n}(p)) is isomorphic to H ~ n − 2 ​ ( 𝒯 n ​ ( K ) / Γ n ​ ( p ) ) \widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p)) , which was answered by Miller–Patzt–Putman. We study a generalized version of Lee–Szczarba’s question. We prove that for a prime p p in a Euclidean number ring R R with fraction field K K , that a natural map H ν ​ ( Γ n ​ ( p ) ) → H ~ n − 2 ​ ( 𝒯 n ​ ( K ) / Γ n ​ ( p ) ) \text{H}^{\nu}(\Gamma_{n}(p))\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p)) is always surjective, and give a sufficent set of conditions on p ∈ R p\in R that guarantee when this map is an isomorphism.



1
Introduction

The cohomology of arithmetic groups plays a fundamental role in algebraic K-theory and number theory. Examples of arithmetic groups include special linear groups over a number ring and their finite-index subgroups.
An important class of finite-index subgroups are
principal congruence subgroups
, i.e. the kernel
\Gamma_{n}(J)
of the map
\text{SL}_{n}(R)\to\text{SL}_{n}(R/J)
that reduces coefficients mod
J
, where
J\subset R
is an ideal such that
R/J
is finite. In this paper, we study the high degree cohomology of
\Gamma_{n}(J)
when
R
is a Euclidean domain and
J=(p)
for
p\in R
a prime.

Borel-Serre Duality.

Borel–Serre
[
3
]
proved that when
R
is a number ring with fraction field
K
and
\Gamma
is a finite-index subgroup of
\text{SL}_{n}(R)
, then
\Gamma
is a rational duality group of some dimension
\nu
, which implies that

\displaystyle\text{H}^{\nu-i}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Q})\cong\text{H}_{i}(\Gamma;\mathfrak{D}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\text{ for all }i

for a
\Gamma
-module
\mathfrak{D}
called the
dualizing module
. This statement also holds with
\mathbb{Z}
-coefficients if
\Gamma
is torsion-free.
In particular, we have

\displaystyle\text{H}^{\nu}(\Gamma;\mathbb{Q})\cong\text{H}_{0}(\Gamma;\mathfrak{D}\otimes\mathbb{Q})\cong(\mathfrak{D}\otimes\mathbb{Q})_{\Gamma},

where the subscript indicates that we are taking coinvariants.
The dimension
\nu
is given by:

\displaystyle\nu=r\binom{n+1}{2}+cn^{2}-n-r-c+1

where

•

r
is the number of embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\text{R}

•

c
is the number of pairs of complex conjugate embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
that do not factor through
R
.

Steinberg Modules.

In the case of
\text{SL}_{n}(R)
for
R
a number ring, the dualizing module
\mathfrak{D}
has the following description.
For
K
a field, let
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
be the
Tits building
for
\text{SL}_{n}(K)
, that is, the simplicial complex whose
k
-simplices are flags

\displaystyle 0\subsetneq V_{0}\subsetneq\dots\subsetneq V_{k}\subsetneq K^{n}

The Solomon-Tits theorem
[
14
,
4
]
says that
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres of dimension
(n-2)
. The
Steinberg module
for
\text{SL}_{n}(K)
, denoted
\text{St}_{n}(K)
, is
\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K))
.
The action of
\text{SL}_{n}(K)
(and its subgroups) on
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
descends to an action on
\text{St}_{n}(K)
. When
K
is the field of fractions of a number ring, Borel–Serre proved that the dualizing module for a finite-index subgroup
\Gamma\subset\text{SL}_{n}(R)
is
\text{St}_{n}(K)
.

The cohomology groups we are interested in are thus isomorphic to
(\text{St}_{n}(K)\otimes\mathbb{Q})_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
, where
K
is the field of fractions of a Euclidean number ring
R
, and
p\in R
is prime. The
\otimes\mathbb{Q}
is unnecessary if
\Gamma_{n}(p)\subset\text{SL}_{n}(R)
is torsion-free.

Congruence subgroups of
\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
.

In
[
10
]
, Miller–Patzt–Putman studied the top cohomology of congruence subgroups of
\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, and answered a question of Lee–Szczarba
[
9
, remark on p. 28]
, which we now describe.
The quotient map
\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})\to\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})/\Gamma_{n}(p)
induces a
\Gamma_{n}(p)
-equivariant map

\displaystyle\text{St}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})=\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q}))\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})/\Gamma_{n}(p))

(1)

In
[
9
]
, Lee–Szczarba asked when this map induces an isomorphism on the
\Gamma_{n}(p)
-coinvariants of
\text{St}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
for primes
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Question 1.1

(Lee–Szczarba)
.

For a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\geq 2
, when is the map

\displaystyle\text{H}^{\binom{n}{2}}(\Gamma_{n}(p))\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})/\Gamma_{n}(p))

induced by (
1
) an isomorphism?

Lee–Szczarba proved this map is an isomorphism for
p=3
. Their proof also yields an isomorphism for
p=2
(in that case one has to tensor with
\mathbb{Q}
because of torsion in
\Gamma_{n}(2)\subset\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
). They used the fact that all units of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\mathbb{F}_{3}
can be lifted to units of
\mathbb{Z}
.

In
[
10
]
, Miller–Patzt–Putman gave a complete resolution of Question
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
10
, Theorem A]
)
.

For a prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\geq 2
, the map

\displaystyle\text{H}^{\binom{n}{2}}(\Gamma_{n}(p))\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})/\Gamma_{n}(p))

induced by (
1
) is a surjection. However, it is an injection if and only if
p\leq 5
. (For
p=2
one needs to tensor with
\mathbb{Q}
due to torsion in
\Gamma_{n}(2)
).

Their proof used the high-connectivity of certain simplicial complexes that they denoted as
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\pm}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. The isomorphism for
p\leq 5
in the above theorem came from a better connectivity range for these simplicial complexes for
p\leq 5
, which did not hold for
p>5
. They also used Church–Putman’s proof of the Bykovskii presentation for
\text{St}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
(see
[
6
]
).

In
[
8
]
, Kupers–Miller–Patzt–Wilson generalized Church–Putman’s proof of the Bykovskii presentation for
\text{St}_{n}(K)
when
K
is the fraction field of the Gaussian integers
\mathbb{Z}[\iota]
or the Eisenstein integers
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. They also posed the following question.

Question 1.3

(
[
8
, Question 5.5]
)
.

For what primes in the Gaussian or Eisenstein integers does the isomorphism of Theorem
1.2
hold? That is, for
R=\mathbb{Z}[\iota]
or
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
with fraction field
K
, for what primes
p\in R
is the map

\displaystyle(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))

an isomorphism?

We give an answer to this question in this paper.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
R
be a Euclidean domain with fraction field
K
and
p\in R
a prime. Examples of pairs
(R,p)
for which the map

\displaystyle(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))

is an isomorphism for
n\geq 2
include:
(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}],1+\sqrt{-2})
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\iota],3)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\iota],2\iota+1)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],4\omega+1)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],4\omega+3)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],3\omega+1)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],3\omega+2)
.

In fact, we prove a more general result, which forms the main theorem of this paper. Our proof generalizes the high-connectivity result of Miller–Patzt–Putman in the case
p=5
. Our proof also avoids their use of the Bykovskii presentation, which lets us compute the
\Gamma_{n}(p)
-coinvariants of
\text{St}_{n}(K)
for rings such as
\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}]
. Our result is as follows.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
R
be a Euclidean domain and
p\in R
a prime. Let
\mathbb{F}=R/(p)
and let
K
be the field of fractions of
R
.
Then the map

\displaystyle(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))

(2)

is always surjective for
n\geq 2
.

Let
\text{U}\subset\mathbb{F}^{\times}
be the image of
\text{R}^{\times}
under the map
R\to\mathbb{F}
.
Suppose in addition that either
\mathbb{F}^{\times}=\text{U}
, or that
\mathbb{F}
and
U
satisfy the following conditions:

1.

The multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}
is isomorphic to the cyclic group of order 2.

2.

For any two distinct
\lambda,\nu\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}\setminus\text{U}
, there exists a finite set of units
u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{k}\in\text{U}
such that
\lambda+u_{1}+\dots+u_{i}\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}\setminus\text{U}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
, and
\nu=\lambda+u_{1}+\dots+u_{k}
.

3.

\mathbb{F}
is additively generated by the units in
U
.

4.

Either 2
\in\mathbb{F}^{\times}\setminus\text{U}
, or there exist units
u_{1},u_{2}\in\text{U}
such that both
u_{1}+u_{2}
and
2u_{1}+u_{2}
are in
\mathbb{F}^{\times}\setminus\text{U}
, and
3u_{1}+u_{2}\in\text{U}
.

5.

\text{BDA}_{2}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
is 1-connected. (See Section
2.2
for the definition of
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
).

Then, the map (
2
) is in fact an isomorphism for
n\geq 2
.

Combining this result with Borel–Serre duality
[
3
]
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 1.6
.

Let
R
be a Euclidean number ring with fraction field
K
, and let
p\in R
be a prime. Let
F
be the quotient field
R/(p)
.

Then for
n\geq 2
, we have a surjection

\displaystyle\text{H}^{\nu}(\Gamma_{n}(p);\mathbb{Q})\twoheadrightarrow\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p);\mathbb{Q})

(3)

where
\nu
is given by

\displaystyle\nu=r\binom{n+1}{2}+cn^{2}-n-r-c+1

where

•

r
is the number of embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\text{R}

•

c
is the number of pairs of complex conjugate embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
that do not factor through
R
.

Let
\text{U}\subset\mathbb{F}^{\times}
be the image of
\text{R}^{\times}
under the map
R\to\mathbb{F}
.
Suppose in addition that either
\mathbb{F}^{\times}=\text{U}
, or that
\mathbb{F}
and
U
satisfy conditions (1)-(5) of Theorem
1.5
.
Then, the map (
3
) is in fact an isomorphism for
n\geq 2
. The statement also holds with
\mathbb{Z}
-coefficients if
\Gamma_{n}(p)
is torsion-free.

Theorem
1.4
follows from Theorem
1.5
since in the cases listed in Theorem
1.4
,
\mathbb{F}
and
U
either satisfy
\mathbb{F}^{\times}=\text{U}
, or the five conditions listed in Theorem
1.5
. That the pairs
(\mathbb{Z}[\iota],3)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],4\omega+1)
, and
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],4\omega+3)
satisfy conditions (1)-(5) of Theorem
1.5
is explained in Section
2.3.6
. I do not currently know if there are other examples of rings
R
and primes
p\in R
that satisfy these conditions. In particular, condition (5) which requires the simplicial complex
\text{BDA}_{2}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
to be 1-connected, seems to have to be checked by hand on a case-to-case basis.

On the other hand, there are many examples where the condition of
\mathbb{F}^{\times}=\text{U}
from Theorem
1.5
holds, including when
(R,p)
is
(\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-2}],1+\sqrt{-2})
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\iota],1+2\iota)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],2\omega+1)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],3\omega+1)
,
(\mathbb{Z}[\omega],3\omega+2)
, and so on.
A simple way to generate such examples is to take a Euclidean domain
R
with multiplicative norm, and take an element
p\in R
with minimal norm such that
p
is not a unit. Any such
p
will necessarily be prime, and will satisfy the condition of
\mathbb{F}^{\times}=\text{U}
from Theorem
1.5
.

A major advantage of the isomorphism of Equation (
3
) is that it is possible to compute the rank of
\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))
. Miller–Patzt–Putman
[
10
, Theorem C]
gave a recursive formula for the rank, which is summarised below. Though they considered the specific case where
R=\mathbb{Z}
and
K=\mathbb{Q}
, the same proof works more generally. Their proof proceeded by relating the quotient
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p)
to a simplicial complex similar to the Tits building, and used a discrete Morse theory argument to count the number of spheres in its homotopy type.

Proposition 1.7

(
[
10
, Theorem C]
)
.

Let
R
be a PID and
K
its field of fractions. Let
p\in R
be a prime and suppose
\mathbb{F}\coloneq R/(p)
is finite. Let
U
be the image of
R^{\times}
under the map
R\to\mathbb{F}
.
For
n\geq 1
, let
t_{n}
be the rank of
\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))
. We then have
t_{1}=1
and

\displaystyle t_{n}=\left((|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|-1)+(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|)|\mathbb{F}|^{n-1}\right)t_{n-1}+|\mathbb{F}/\text{U}|(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}|\mathbb{F}|^{k}|\text{Gr}_{k}(\mathbb{F}^{n-1})|t_{k}t_{n-k-1}

for
n\geq 2
.
Here
\text{Gr}_{k}(V)
denotes the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces of an
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
V
.

Remark 1.8
.

In particular, Proposition
1.7
above gives a loose lower bound on the rank of
t_{n}=\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)/\Gamma_{n}(p))
. In particular, the recursive formula implies that

\displaystyle t_{n}\geq|\mathbb{F}|^{\binom{n}{2}}|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|^{n-1}

for
n\geq 1
(with strict inequality for
n>1
).
Indeed, note that equality holds for
n=1
, and for
n>1
the formula of
1.7
recursively gives

\displaystyle t_{n}

\displaystyle>(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|)|\mathbb{F}|^{n-1}t_{n-1}\geq(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|)|\mathbb{F}|^{n-1}|\mathbb{F}|^{\binom{n-1}{2}}|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|^{n-2}

\displaystyle>|\mathbb{F}|^{\binom{n}{2}}|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|^{n-1}

This is analogous to Paraschivescu’s
[
12
]
lower bound on
\text{H}^{\binom{n}{2}}(\Gamma_{n}(p))
for
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Miller–Patzt–Putman also used the failure of certain complexes to be highly acyclic for
p>5
to produce better lower bounds for
(\text{St}_{n}(\mathbb{Q}))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
than the one given by Theorem
1.2
. One could probably use their methods more generally, but we expect this to produce highly non-optimal bounds. We have not pursued this approach in the present article.

Outline.

The first main ingredient in the proof of Theorem
1.5
is a high-connectivity result for certain simplicial complexes, that are generalizations of the complexes
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\pm}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
defined in
[
10
]
. This high-connectivity result is proven in Section
2
. Our arguments mimic those in
[
10
, Section 2]
. One point of difference is in Lemma
2.37
, that shows that a certain type of map from a sphere into our simplicial complexes is nullhomotopic. Miller–Patzt–Putman’s proof of the corresponding statement relied on a high-connectivity result for
\mathbb{Z}
that is a key ingredient in Church–Putman’s proof of the Bykovskii presentation for
\text{St}_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
in
[
6
]
. We give a different proof that avoids this assumption. Another point of difference is that these high-connectivity arguments require a base case for
n=2
, i.e. we need certain complexes
\text{BDA}_{2}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
to be 1-connected. In the case of
R=\mathbb{Z}
, these complexes have a simple description which easily yields the required base case, but for other rings one needs a case-specific argument. The desired 1-connectivity is proved in Section
2.3.6
.

The second main ingredient for Theorem
1.5
is a spectral sequence argument, that is described in Section
3
. This argument is relatively standard and is similar to the one used by Church–Putman
[
6
]
.
This is a second point where we avoid assuming the Bykovskii presentation for the Steinberg module. To do this we need a cyclicity result about
\text{St}_{n}(K)
as an
\text{SL}_{n}(R)
-module when
R
is a Euclidean domain with fraction field
K
, which was proved by Ash–Rudolph
[
1
]
, with a different proof later given by Church–Putman
[
6
]
, that used high-connectivity arguments on certain simplicial complexes.

Simplicial complexes associated to free
R
-modules

We start by defining several simplicial complexes that we will use in our proofs. Throughout all these definitions,
R
will denote a commutative ring, and
U
a subgroup of its group of units
R^{\times}
. We give the same definitions as Miller–Patzt–Putman in
[
10
]
– they work with the specific case when
\text{U}=\{\pm 1\}
, but all their definitions easily generalize.

2.1
Complexes of bases and augmented bases

2.1.1
Partial
U
-bases

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and let
R^{\times}
denote the set of units of
R
. Let
U
be a subgroup of
R^{\times}
. We make the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

A
U
-vector
in
R^{n}
is a set of the form
\{c\vec{v}|c\in\text{U}\}
for a nonzero vector
\vec{v}\in R^{n}
. Given a non-zero
\vec{v}\in R^{n}
, we will write
[\vec{v}]
for the associated
U
-vector.

Definition 2.2
.

A
partial-basis
for
R^{n}
is a set of elements of
R^{n}
that is a subset of a free basis for
R^{n}
.
A
partial
U
-basis
for
R^{n}
is a set
\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{k}]\}
of
U
-vectors such that
\{\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{k}\}
is a partial-basis for
R^{n}
. This does not depend on the choice of representatives
\vec{v_{i}}
.

We now turn these into a simplicial complex as follows. Here and throughout the rest of this paper, we will define simplicial complexes by specifying that their simplices are certain sets.
What we mean by this is that the
k
-simplices are such sets containing
(k+1)
-elements, and the face relations between simplices are simply inclusions of sets.

Definition 2.3
.

The
complex of partial
U
-bases
, denoted
\text{B}_{n}^{\text{U}}(R)
, is the simplicial complex whose simplices are partial
U
-bases for
R^{n}.

To understand
\text{B}_{n}^{\text{U}}(R)
in an inductive way, we will need to understand the links of its simplices. We thus make the following definition.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\{\vec{e}_{1},\vec{e}_{2},\dots,\vec{e}_{m+n}\}
denote the standard basis for
R^{m+n}
. Define
\text{B}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(R)=\text{Link}_{\text{B}_{m+n}^{\text{U}}(R)}\{[\vec{e}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{e}_{m}]\}
.

Recall that a simplicial complex
X
is
Cohen-Macaulay
of dimension
r
if it satisfies the following condition:

•

X is
r
-dimensional and
(r-1)
-connected.

•

For all
k
-simplices
\sigma
of
X
,
\text{Link}_{X}(\sigma)
is
(r-k-1)
-dimensional and
(r-k-2)
-connected.

Miller–Patzt–Putman
[
10
]
proved the following theorem for
\mathbb{F}
a field, and
\text{U}\subset\mathbb{F}^{\times}
a subgroup of its units. They state their result in the specific case where
\text{U}=\{\pm 1\}
, but the same proof adapts to the general case:

Proposition 2.5

(
[
10
, Proposition 2.21]
)
.

For a field
\mathbb{F}
and
\text{U}\subset\mathbb{F}^{\times}
a subgroup of its units, the complex
\text{B}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
is Cohen-Macaulay of dimension
(n-1)
for all
n,m\geq 0
.

2.1.2
Augmented
U
-bases

We now define the augmented version of
\text{B}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(R)
.

Definition 2.6
.

An
augmented partial
U
-basis
for
R^{n}
is a set
\{[\vec{v}_{0}],[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{k}]\}
of
U
-vectors that can be reordered so that

•

\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{k}]\}
is a partial
U
-basis for
R^{n}

•

There exist units
\lambda,\nu\in R^{\times}
such that
\vec{v}_{0}=\lambda\vec{v}_{1}+\nu\vec{v}_{2}
. The existence of
\lambda
and
\nu
is independent of the choice of representatives
\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2}
.

We will call the
U
-vectors
\{[\vec{v}_{0}],[\vec{v}_{1}],[\vec{v}_{2}]\}
the
additive core
of
\{[\vec{v}_{0}],[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{k}]\}
.

A subset of an augmented
U
-basis is either itself an augmented
U
-basis (if the subset contains the entire additive core) or is a partial
U
-basis. We can thus make the following definition:

Definition 2.7
.

The
complex of augmented partial
U
-bases
for
R^{n}
, denoted
\text{BA}_{n}^{\text{U}}
, is the simplicial complex whose simplices consist either of partial
U
-bases of
R^{n}
or of augmented partial
U
-bases of
R^{n}
.

Again, we will need to study the links of simplices in
\text{BA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(R)
. However, for technical reasons, we will not look at the entire link, but rather the following subcomplex of it:

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\sigma=\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{k}]\}
be a simplex of
\text{BA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(R)
. The
augmented link
of
\sigma
, denoted

Lemma 2.20

(
[
10

)

Suppose

Since we will make use of this retraction in Section

Proof.

Let

denote the standard basis for
\mathbb{F}^{m+n}
. It is enough to say how
\rho
maps a simplex of
\text{B}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
that is not in
\text{BD}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
. The only such simplices are

\sigma=\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]\}
such that the matrix with columns
(\vec{e}_{1},\dots,\vec{e_{m}},\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{n})
has determinant equal to

Let

be the result of subdividing

with a new vertex

. Thus the top-dimensional simplices of

are of the form

\displaystyle\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\widehat{\vec{v}}_{i}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}],x_{\sigma}\}\hskip 28.45274pt(1\leq i\leq n)

Define

\displaystyle\rho|_{\sigma}:\sigma\cong S(\sigma)\to\text{BD}_{n,m}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})

to be the map that fixes the vertices

and takes

to
\frac{1}{d}(\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+\vec{v}_{n})
.
The following calculation checks that this extends over the top-dimensional simplices of

\displaystyle\det\left(\vec{e_{1}},\dots,\vec{e}_{m},\vec{v}_{1},\dots\widehat{\vec{v}}_{i},\dots,\vec{v}_{n},\frac{1}{d}(\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+\vec{v}_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\det\left(\vec{e_{1}},\dots,\vec{e}_{m},\vec{v}_{1},\dots\widehat{\vec{v}}_{i},\dots,\vec{v}_{n},(\vec{v}_{1}+\dots+\vec{v}_{n})\right)=\pm d/d=\pm 1\in\text{U}

∎

Lemma 2.46

Let

Proof.

3.1
Preliminaries

3.1.1
The map-of-posets spectral sequence

In this section, we review some results about the homology of posets with coefficients in a functor and about the map-of-posets spectral sequence. Much of this is due to Quillen
[
13
]
and Charney
[
5
]
. We begin with some definitions concerning posets.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a poset and
x\in\mathbb{X}
. We say
x
has height
m
and write
\text{ht}(x)=m
if
m
is the largest integer such that there exists a chain

\displaystyle x_{0}<\dots<x_{m}=x\hskip 7.11317pt\text{ with }x_{i}\in\mathbb{X}\text{ for all }0\leq i\leq m.

We write
\mathbb{X}_{>x}
for the subposet of
\mathbb{X}
consisting of elements strictly larger than
x
. For a map
f:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{X}
of posets, we write
f_{\leq x}
for the subposet of
\mathbb{Y}
consisting of all
y\in\mathbb{Y}
such that
f(y)\leq x
.

A poset
\mathbb{X}
can be viewed as a category with an object for each
x\in\mathbb{X}
and a single morphism from
x\in\mathbb{X}
to
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
precisely when
x\leq x^{\prime}
. Letting
Ab
denote the category of abelian groups, we now recall the definition of the homology of a poset with coefficients in a functor
F:\mathbb{X}\to\text{Ab}
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a poset and let
F:\mathbb{X}\to\text{Ab}
be a functor. Define
C_{\bullet}(\mathbb{X};F)
to be the following chain complex. For
k\geq 0
, we set

\displaystyle C_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)=\bigoplus_{x_{0}<\dots<x_{k}}F(x_{0})

where the
x_{i}
are understood to be elements of
\mathbb{X}
. The differential
\partial:C_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)\to C_{k-1}(\mathbb{X};F)
is defined to be
\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{i}\partial_{i}
, where
\partial_{i}:C_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)\to C_{k-1}(\mathbb{X};F)
is as follows:

•

For
0<i\leq k
, the map
\partial_{i}
takes the
x_{0}<\dots<x_{k}
summand of
C_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)
to the
x_{0}<\dots<\widehat{x_{i}}<\dots<x_{k}
summand of
C_{k-1}(\mathbb{X};F)
via the identity map
F(x_{0})\to F(x_{0})
.

•

The map
\partial_{0}
takes the
x_{0}<\dots<x_{k}
summand of
C_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)
to the
x_{1}<\dots<x_{k}
summand of
C_{k-1}(\mathbb{X};F)
via the induced map
F(x_{0})\to F(x_{1})
.

We define
\text{H}_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)=\text{H}_{k}(C_{\bullet}(\mathbb{X};F)
.

Example 1
.

Fix a poset
\mathbb{X}
. For a commutative ring
R
, we write
\underline{R}
for the constant functor on
\mathbb{X}
with value
R
. We then have
\text{H}_{k}(\mathbb{X};\underline{R})\cong\text{H}_{k}(|\mathbb{X}|;R)
, where
|\mathbb{X}|
is the geometric realization of
\mathbb{X}
. We will often simply write this as
\text{H}_{k}(\mathbb{X};R)
.

These homology groups can be very difficult to calculate. One case where there is an easy formula is where the functor
F
is
supported on elements of height
m
, i.e. where
F(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{X}
with
\text{ht}(x)\neq m
. We then have the following lemma. See e.g.
[
11
]
for a proof.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a poset and let
F:\mathbb{X}\to\text{Ab}
be a functor that is supported on elements of height
m
. Then

\displaystyle\text{H}_{k}(\mathbb{X};F)\cong\bigoplus_{\text{ht}(x)=m}\widetilde{\text{H}}_{k-1}(|\mathbb{X}_{>x}|;F(x))

where the coefficients
F(x)
are simply regarded as an abelian group.

The reason we will need to consider the homology of a poset with coefficients in a functor is due to the following spectral sequence. See
[
11
]
for a proof.

Theorem 3.4

(Map-of-posets spectral sequence)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{X}
be a map of posets. Then there is a homologically graded spectral sequence

\displaystyle\text{E}_{kh}^{2}=\text{H}_{k}(\mathbb{X};[x\mapsto\text{H}_{h}(f_{\leq x})])\implies\text{H}_{k+h}(\mathbb{Y}).

Remark 3.5
.

Following Miller–Patzt–Putman
[
10
]
, we use the nonstandard indices
(k,h)
since for us
p
is always a prime, so we cannot use
(p,q)
, and
n
is always a dimension, so we cannot use
(n,m)
.

3.1.2
The quotient of the Tits building by a congruence subgroup

We will need a concrete description of the quotient of the Tits building by a congruence subgroup.

Definition 3.6

(Tits building)
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring and let
V
be a finite-rank free
R
=module. Define
\mathbb{T}(V)
to be the poset of proper nonzero direct summands of
V
, ordered by inclusion. Also, let
\mathcal{T}(V)
denote the geometric realization of
\mathbb{T}(V)
, viewed as a simplicial complex. For
n\geq 1
, we will write
\mathbb{T}_{n}(R)=\mathbb{T}(R^{n})
and
\mathcal{T}_{n}(R)=\mathcal{T}(R^{n})
.

Theorem 3.7

(Solomon–Tits,
[
14
]
,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
K
be a field. The Tits building
\mathcal{T}_{n}(K)
is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of spheres of dimension
n-2
. The reduced homology group
\text{St}_{n}(K)=\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}(K))
is generated by certain cycles called
apartment classes
, which are indexed by bases of
K^{n}
.
For a basis
\vec{v}_{1},\vec{v}_{2},\dots,\vec{v}_{n}
of
K^{n}
, the corresponding
apartment class
is the homology cycle determined by the chain

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\Sigma_{n}}(-1)^{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma)}[\langle\vec{v}_{\sigma(1)}\rangle,\langle\vec{v}_{\sigma(1)},\vec{v}_{\sigma(2)}\rangle,\dots,\langle\vec{v}_{\sigma(1)},\dots,\vec{v}_{\sigma(n-1)}\rangle]

where
\Sigma_{n}
denotes the Weyl group of permutations of the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
R
is a PID, and
K
its field of fractions. For
n\geq 1
, we have
\mathbb{T}_{n}(R)\cong\mathbb{T}_{n}(K)
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that there is a bijection between subspaces of
K^{n}
and direct summands of
R^{n}
taking a subspace
V\subset K^{n}
to
V\cap R^{n}
and a direct summand
W\subset R^{n}
to
W\otimes_{R}K
.
∎

We now decorate our buildings by appropriate versions of orientations.

Definition 3.9

(

Let
R

of units acts transitively on the set of orientations on

U

such that

Remark 3.10

Note that if

Definition 3.11

(

Let

Lemma 3.21

Let
R
be a Euclidean domain, let
p\in R
be a prime, and let
\mathbb{F}
be the quotient field
R/(p)
. Let
\mathcal{U}=R^{\times}
and let
U
be the image of
\mathcal{U}
under the map

. Then for all
n\geq 1
, we have isomorphisms

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\cong\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))\cong(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R)))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}

Proof.

The chain group
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})
is the free abelian group generated by all the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplices of
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
whose vertices span
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, and these are the same as the
(n-1)
-dimensional simplices of
\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
, so the first isomorphism follows.

For the second isomorphism, note that there is a map

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R))=\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R))\to\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

\mathcal{U}
-vectors
\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]\}
in
R^{n}
to the
U
-vectors in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
determined by the mod
p
reductions of the
\vec{v}_{i}
, and that this map is equivariant with respect to the
\Gamma_{n}(p)
-action on
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R))
. Now, note that the chain group
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
is freely generated by collections of
U
-vectors
\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]\}
in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
such that we can pick the representatives
\vec{v}_{i}
so that they are the columns a matrix in
\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})
. We only need matrices of determinant 1 (rather than having determinant in
U
) since we are free to multiply the
\vec{v}_{i}
with elements of
U
as needed. Similarly,
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R))
is freely generated by sets of
\mathcal{U}
-vectors
\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]\}
in
R^{n}
such that the
\vec{v}_{i}
are the columns of a matrix in
\text{SL}_{n}(R)
. So surjectivity follows from the classical fact that the map

\displaystyle\text{SL}_{n}(R)\longrightarrow\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})

that reduces matrices modulo
p
is surjective. For injectivity, suppose that there are two sets of
\mathcal{U}
-vectors
\{[\vec{V}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{V}_{n}]\}
and
\{[\vec{W}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{W}_{n}]\}
of
R^{n}
that both map to the same set of
U
-vectors
\{[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]\}
of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. We can choose these vector representatives such that

\displaystyle\det(\vec{V}_{1},\dots,\vec{V}_{n})=1=\det(\vec{W}_{1},\dots,\vec{W}_{n})=1

\displaystyle\det(\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{n})=1

\displaystyle\vec{V}_{i}\mapsto\vec{v}_{i}\text{ under the map }R^{n}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}\text{ for }1\leq i\leq n

Let
\vec{w}_{i}
be the mod
p

\vec{W}_{i}
. Thus

\displaystyle\det(\vec{w}_{1},\dots,\vec{w}_{n})=1

Since
[\vec{v}_{i}]=[\vec{w}_{i}]
for all

, we have

\displaystyle\vec{v}_{i}=u_{i}\vec{w}_{i}

for some
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\text{U}
. The determinant condition implies that

\displaystyle u_{1}u_{2}\dots u_{n}=1

Choose units
u_{1}^{\prime},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
that map to
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
respectively under the map
R\to\mathbb{F}
.
Then note that the matrices

\displaystyle(\vec{V_{1}},\dots,\vec{V}_{n})\text{ and }(u_{1}^{\prime}\vec{W}_{1},\dots,u_{n}^{\prime}\vec{W}_{n})

map to the same matrix, namely

\displaystyle\left(\vec{v_{1}},\dots,\vec{v}_{n}\right)

under the mod
p

A\in\Gamma_{n}(p)
that takes one matrix to the other. Since
[\vec{W}_{i}]=[u_{i}^{\prime}\vec{W}_{i}]
for all
i
, we deduce that the sets
\{[\vec{V}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{V}_{n}]\}
and
\{[\vec{W}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{W}_{n}]\}
are in the same coset of
(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R)))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
.
∎

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
R
be a Euclidean domain with field of fractions
K
. Let
p\in R
be a prime and let
\mathbb{F}
be the quotient field
R/(p)
. Let
\mathcal{U}=R^{\times}
and let
U
be the image of
\mathcal{U}
under the map
R\to\mathbb{F}
.
Consider the map

\displaystyle(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

(13)

induced by the natural map
\mathbb{T}_{n}(K)\cong\mathbb{T}_{n}(R)\to\mathbb{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
that sends a summand
V\subset R^{n}
to its mod
p
reduction equipped with the induced
U
-orientation on it. This map is always surjective for
n\geq 2
.
If in addition
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
is
(n-1)
-connected for all
n\geq 2
, then the map (
13
) is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Lemma
3.16
states that
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(R))\to\text{St}_{n}(K)
is surjective, and this implies that the induced map

\displaystyle(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(\text{R})))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}

is surjective.

Corollary
3.18
tells us that
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
is surjective.

It is not hard to check that the composition

\displaystyle(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(\text{R})))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\twoheadrightarrow(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

is the same as the composition

\displaystyle(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(\text{R})))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\cong\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BD}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))\twoheadrightarrow\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})),

where the isomorphism in the above expression comes from Lemma
3.21
. This implies surjectivity of
(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
for all
n\geq 2
.

Now, assuming that
\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})
is
(n-1)
-connected for all
n\geq 1
, we turn to showing that this map is an isomorphism.

By tracing the descriptions of the maps
\partial
and
\Psi
from Lemma
3.19
, we see that the composition

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\cong(\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{B}_{n}^{\mathcal{U}}(\text{R})))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

(14)

is the same as the composition

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to\text{coker}(\text{C}_{n}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\hskip 284.52756pt\cong\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})),

(15)

where the isomorphism in (
15
) is due to Corollary
3.20
. This (once again) implies surjectivity of
(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
.

We need to show that this map is also injective. Note that the equality of the compositions (
14
) and (
15
) implies that showing injectivity is equivalent to showing that anything in the kernel of the map

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

is also in the kernel of the map

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}

By Corollary
3.20
, we know that the kernel of

\displaystyle\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))

is equal to

\displaystyle\text{im}(\text{C}_{n}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime}))

This image is generated by sums of the form

\displaystyle[[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]]-[[\vec{v}_{0}],[\vec{v}_{2}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]]+[[\vec{v}_{0}],[\vec{v}_{1}],[\vec{v}_{3}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]]

(16)

where
[\vec{v}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{v}_{n}]
form a determinant-
U
basis of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
and
\vec{v}_{0}=\lambda\vec{v}_{1}+\nu\vec{v}_{2}
for some
\lambda,\nu\in\text{U}
. We can pick the representatives so that

\displaystyle\det(\vec{v}_{1},\dots,\vec{v}_{n})=1

and so the surjectivity of

\displaystyle\text{SL}_{n}(R)\to\text{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{F})

implies that for
1\leq i\leq n
, we can pick vectors
\vec{V}_{i}\in R^{n}
that map to
\vec{v}_{i}
under the mod
p
reduction map. Let
\vec{V}_{0}=\lambda^{\prime}\vec{V}_{1}+\nu^{\prime}\vec{V}_{2}
, where
\lambda^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
are preimages of
\lambda,\nu\in\text{U}
.
The map
\text{C}_{n-1}(\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}),\text{BDA}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F})^{\prime})\to(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
sends the sum (
16
) to the image in
(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
of the following sum of apartments of
\text{St}_{n}(K)
:

\displaystyle[[\vec{V}_{1}],\dots,[\vec{V}_{n}]]-[[\vec{V}_{0}],[\vec{V}_{2}],\dots,[\vec{V}_{n}]]+[[\vec{V}_{0}],[\vec{V}_{1}],[\vec{V}_{3}],\dots,[\vec{V}_{n}]]

(17)

The sum (
17
) is a relation among apartments in
\text{St}_{n}(K)
, i.e. it is 0 in
\text{St}_{n}(K)
– this can easily be verified using the description of apartment classes stated in Theorem
3.7
– and thus so is its image in
(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.23
.

Note that for the last step of the proof, we needed to know that the Bykovskii relation (
17
) always holds in
\text{St}_{n}(K)
, but we did not need to know that this type of sum generates
all
the relations between apartments in
\text{St}_{n}(K)
. The Bykovskii relation does give a full presentation for
\text{St}_{n}(K)
in certain cases, namely for

(see
[
6
]
), and for
R=\mathbb{Z}[\iota]
or
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
(see
[
8
]
). Since we don’t assume the Bykovskii presentation here, this allows us to compute the top cohomology of
\Gamma_{n}(p)
for primes in many other Euclidean rings.

3.2.2
Computational Result

In this section we state a recursive formula for the rank of
\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
when
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field. Thus in instances when the isomorphism of Theorem
3.22
holds, this lets us compute the rank of
(\text{St}_{n}(K))_{\Gamma_{n}(p)}
.

For
\mathbb{F}
a field, let
\text{Gr}_{k}(\mathbb{F}^{n})
denote the set of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
.
We state our result as follows. It can be proved using the same proof as
[
10
, Theorem C]
. They work in the specific case when
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
for some prime
p\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
\text{U}=\{\pm\}
, but the same proof works more generally. Their proof is inspired by Bestvina’s discrete Morse theory proof of the Solomon–Tits theorem in
[
2
]
.

Proposition 3.24
.

Suppose
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field, and let
U
be a subgroup of
\mathbb{F}^{\times}
. For
n\geq 1
, let
t_{n}
be the rank of
\widetilde{\text{H}}_{n-2}(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{\text{U}}(\mathbb{F}))
. We then have
t_{1}=1
and

\displaystyle t_{n}=\left((|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|-1)+(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|)|\mathbb{F}|^{n-1}\right)t_{n-1}+|\mathbb{F}/\text{U}|(|\mathbb{F}^{\times}/\text{U}|-1)\sum_{k=1}^{n-2}|\mathbb{F}|^{k}|\text{Gr}_{k}(\mathbb{F}^{n-1})|t_{k}t_{n-k-1}

for
n\geq 2
.

Note that Proposition
1.7
now immediately follows using this result and Proposition
3.14
.
