Identification of a “new” crystal/Su(Ste) locus on the Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome, resembling a piRNA cluster regulating Stellate repeats and transposons
Authors: Maria Dolores De Donno, Serafina Massari, Francesco Zangaro, Laura Fanti, Maria Berloco, Valeria Specchia, Sergio Pimpinelli, Maria Pia Bozzetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe crystal-Stellate interaction is the first known example of piRNA-mediated regulation between heterochromatin (the crystal/Suppressor of Stellate [Su(Ste)] locus on the Y chromosome) and euchromatin (the Stellate locus on the X chromosome) in Drosophila melanogaster. Here, we comprehensively annotate the Y-linked crystal/Su(Ste) sequences using Release 6 and heterochromatin-enriched scaffolds and contigs. In Release 6, we mapped 2 distinct crystal/Su(Ste) repeat regions with different molecular organizations. Beyond the ordered, tandem “classical” region, we identified a novel, disorganized region containing sequence fragments in both orientations derived from both crystal/Su(Ste) repeats and transposable elements. This disorganized locus displays structural features of a piRNA cluster that regulates both Stellate repeats and transposons. Furthermore, we identified at least 4 additional loci with organized crystal/Su(Ste)-like repeats within heterochromatin-enriched sequences not included in Release 6. We also conducted a detailed examination of the complex relationship between Release 6 sequences and more recent scaffolds and contigs. Alongside the classic crystal/Su(Ste) repeat containing hoppel/1360 fragments, we discovered 2 new repeat types structurally organized like crystal/Su(Ste) but carrying portions of HetA and gypsy12 transposons. Overall, these findings provide structural insights into the crystal-Stellate system, refining the Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome map.

Introduction

The crystal-Stellate system is one of the better-known examples of heterochromatin–euchromatin interaction in Drosophila melanogaster and the first described “natural case” of repetitive sequence silencing mediated by piRNAs (Aravin et al. 2001, 2003; Vagin et al. 2006; Bozzetti et al. 2012; Specchia et al. 2017; Chen and Aravin 2021). Males lacking either the Y chromosome (Meyer et al. 1961) or Suppressor of Stellate [Su(Ste)] locus—located between the breakpoints of the X–Y translocations P7-E1 and E15 (Hardy et al. 1981, 1984; Gatti and Pimpinelli 1983, 1992; Palumbo et al. 1994a)—exhibit needle- or star-shaped crystalline aggregates in spermatocytes, alongside chromosome condensation and segregation defects. The morphology of these crystals and the severity of meiotic phenotypes depend on the X-linked Stellate allele present: Ste+ (∼15 to 40 repeats) or the more severe Ste (∼50 to 400 repeats) (Palumbo et al. 1994a; Belloni et al. 2002). Homologous repetitive sequences drive this interaction at 3 main chromosomal positions: euchromatic Stellate (Steeu, 1250 bp units) at region 12D/E (Palumbo et al. 1994a; Tritto et al. 2003; Bozzetti et al. 2012; De Grassi et al. 2022), heterochromatic Stellate (Stehet, 1150 bp units) at region h27 (Shevelyov 1992; Palumbo et al. 1994a, 1994b; Tulin et al. 1997), both on the X chromosome, and the Y-linked crystal/Su(Ste) locus (2843 bp units) at position h11-h13. The Y-linked element contains a 1360/hoppel transposon fragment, a Stellate-like sequence, and a crystal-specific core (Livak 1984, 1990; Pimpinelli et al. 1986; Balakireva et al. 1992; Gatti and Pimpinelli 1992; McKee and Satter 1996; Tulin et al. 1997; Kalmykova et al. 1998; Kogan et al. 2000; Carvalho et al. 2001; Tritto et al. 2003; Bozzetti et al. 2012; Hoskins et al. 2015; Specchia et al. 2017). The locus corresponding to the so-called crystal/Su(Ste) deletion is the only site on the entire Y chromosome, believed to be responsible for this phenomenon (Gatti and Pimpinelli 1983; Hardy et al. 1984; Livak 1984; Palumbo et al. 1994a).

Molecularly, deleting the crystal region (hereafter crystal1) induces a testis-specific 750-nucleotide Stellate mRNA (Livak 1990; Palumbo et al. 1994a; Tritto et al. 2003). This mRNA translates into the Stellate protein—homologous to the β subunit of casein kinase II—which aggregates into the observed crystals (Bozzetti et al. 1995). Stellate silencing relies on Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs), 23 to 32 nucleotide noncoding RNAs that bind gonadal PIWI-subfamily proteins (Aubergine, Ago3, Piwi) to repress transposons and repeats (Aravin et al. 2001, 2003; Vagin et al. 2006). The silencing is regulated by the piRNA pathway predominantly active in the gonads (Vagin et al. 2006; Gunawardane et al. 2007; Nishida et al. 2007) although the piRNA pathway has recently been identified in the nervous system of both Drosophila and mice (Lee et al. 2011; Rajasethupathy et al. 2012; Peng and Lin 2013; Perrat et al. 2013; De Donno et al. 2023). This silencing mechanism involves piRNAs, a specific class of small noncoding RNA molecules that protect animal germ cells and their somatic precursors from transposons and other repetitive elements, thereby preserving genome stability (Gunawardane et al. 2007; Malone and Hannon 2009; Hayashi et al. 2016). The major sources of piRNAs are discrete transposon-rich genomic loci, known as “piRNA clusters,” which are located at various positions throughout the genome (Brennecke et al. 2007; Malone and Hannon 2009; Senti and Brennecke 2010; Czech and Hannon 2016; Huang et al. 2017). These clusters produce RNA precursors that enter somatic or germinal piRNA pathways depending on the specific cells in which they function (Aravin et al. 2003; Ishizu et al. 2012; Mohn et al. 2015; Chang and Larracuente 2019; Kotov et al. 2019; Sato and Siomi 2020).

In fly testes, the most abundant piRNAs associated with Aub and Ago3 are crystal-derived (Gunawardane et al. 2007; Nishida et al. 2007; Malone and Hannon 2009; Khurana and Theurkauf 2010). Genetic modifiers of this regulation include key piRNA pathway components, such as aubergine (aubsting and aubclash mutants) and hsp83 (hsp83scratch) (Schmidt et al. 1999; Zhang et al. 2001, 2004; Specchia et al. 2008, 2010, 2017, 2019; Specchia and Bozzetti 2009, 2021; Bozzetti et al. 2015; Sahin et al. 2016).

Although primarily heterochromatic, the Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome hosts fertility genes (eg Kl1, Kl2) (Bonaccorsi and Lohe 1991; Hoskins et al. 2015; Chang and Larracuente 2019; Adashev et al. 2020; Chen and Aravin 2021). The standard reference map by Hoskins et al. (2015) for Release 6, recently improved via long-read sequencing, annotates crystal region as a single tandem-repeat region at h11 (Livak 1984, 1990; Balakireva et al. 1992; Tulin et al. 1997; Adashev et al. 2020; Shao et al. 2024).

Here, by analyzing Release 6 alongside recent heterochromatin-enriched scaffolds and contigs, we resolved the structural distribution of crystal sequences on the Y chromosome. Unexpectedly, Release 6 contains 2 distinct crystal regions: the classical tandem array and a novel locus structurally resembling a typical piRNA cluster. Furthermore, we characterized structurally divergent repeats within the h13–h14 region of heterochromatin-enriched assemblies. These results refine the Y chromosome map and provide critical insights into the architecture regulating the crystal-Stellate system.

Methods

Drosophila strains and culture conditions

Bscry´ Yy+ (kindly provided by Pedro Ripoll and abbreviated as Y cry´ in the text) (Hardy et al. 1981) carries all of the fertility factors and the ribosomal DNA locus but elicits the formation of needle- or star-shaped crystals in primary spermatocytes in the presence of a Ste+ or Ste X chromosome, respectively. Oregon-R strain was obtained from Bloomington.

Flies were raised on a standard cornmeal agar medium and maintained at 25 °C.

Genome analyses

To analyze the structure and the organization of the crystal repeats, the z11734 repeat (Tritto et al. 2003) was used as a probe against the whole Y chromosome contig (RefSeq NC_024512.1; International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration INSDC CP007106.1) of the latest genome assembly of the Drosophila melanogaster species (Release 6) by the BLAST program (Hoskins et al. 2015).

Two regions of homology were identified in the contig: the first region spans 277,709 bp (pos. 846,141 to 1,123,850), the second is 170,807 bp long (pos. 2,496,221 to 2,667,028). The 2 regions were retrieved and aligned with the z11734 repeat using the pipMaker program (Schwartz et al. 2000).

The same program was also used to analyze for the presence of the z11734 repeat in the contigs and scaffolds from the heterochromatin region of the Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome (Chang and Larracuente 2019).

Sequence alignments

Sequence alignments were performed using the BLAST tool (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool), in particular, Blast2seq was utilized to determine the sequence identity between 2 sequences (Altschul et al. 1990). We used Standard BLAST parameter of the BLAST Align 2 sequences: Align Sequences Nucleotide Blast tool; the highly similar sequences program; Scoring parameters: Match 1, Mismatch 2.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

Chromosome metaphase preparations and FISH experiments were carried out as previously described (Fanti and Pimpinelli 2003). Probe was directly labeled with Cy3-dUTP (Amersham). Preparations were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Digital images were recorded using an Olympus epifluorescence microscope equipped with a cooled CCD camera. Fluorescent hybridization signals were pseudocolored and merged using Adobe Photoshop (Fanti and Pimpinelli 2004).

Phylogenetic analysis

Sequences were aligned using Clustal Omega, and the resulting multiple sequence alignment was used to infer phylogeny via the Maximum Likelihood method under the Jukes-Cantor (1969) substitution model. The tree with the highest log likelihood (−17,297.41) is shown. The proportion of replicate trees in which the associated sequences clustered together (999 replicates) is shown next to the branches (Felsenstein 1985). Initial tree(s) for the heuristic search were obtained automatically by applying the Maximum Parsimony method. The evolutionary rate differences among sites were modeled using a discrete Gamma distribution across 4 categories (+G, parameter = 1.9854). The analytical procedure encompassed 80 coding nucleotide sequences using first, second, third, and noncoding positions, with 4,522 positions in the final dataset. Evolutionary analyses were conducted in MEGA12 (Kumar et al. 2024) utilizing up to 8 parallel computing threads. The tree was displayed using the online tool Interactive Tree Of Life v7.4.2 (Letunic and Bork 2024).

Total RNA extraction

Total RNA was extracted from adult heads and larval brains (25 flies or larvae for each extraction) using RNAqueous-Micro Kit (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. The RNA concentration and purity were determined photometrically by measuring absorbance at 260 nm and A260/A280 ratio. To remove all the DNA in the preparation, samples were incubated with DNase I RNase free at 37 °C for 20 min and, after treatment, DNase was inactivated by DNase Inactivation Reagent on the kit.

cDNA synthesis from total RNA

For first-strand cDNA synthesis, up to 5 µg of total RNA was used as a template for oligonucleotides dT(20) primed reverse transcription using SuperScript III First-Strand Synthesis System (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Real-time PCR was performed in the SmartCycler real-time PCR (Cepheid, Sunnyvale, CA), and in StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Invitrogen, USA). Expression of transposable elements (TEs) was determined by real-time PCR using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For quantification of the transcripts, we used the 2ΔΔct method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001) using rp49 transcripts as control. Bars of errors correspond to the standard deviation of the 3 independent experiments. Primers are listed in Supplementary Table 7.

Results

Detailed annotation of Y-linked crystal repeats on the Release 6 of Drosophila genome assembly

The Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome Release 6 assembly (Hoskins et al. 2015) allowed detailed structural mapping of the Y-linked crystal repeats. We analyzed the 3,667,352 bp chromosome contig (CP007106.1) using the 2,843 bp z11734 repeat as a reference sequence (Tritto et al. 2003). The z11734 element is flanked by terminal 6-nucleotide palindromic CfoI/MluI sites (ACGCGT) and contains a Stellate-like sequence from position 979 to 2,666, corresponding to the Stellate coding sequence (Tritto et al. 2003; Bozzetti et al. 2012; Chang and Larracuente 2019) (Fig. 1). The flanking regions (positions 1 to 979 and 2,666 to 2,843) consist of a rearranged hoppel/1360 DNA transposon carrying a central deletion (positions 586 to 2,960) and intact 31 bp terminal inverted repeats (Fig. 1). This transposon sequence is oriented antisense relative to the Stellate-like sequence.

BLAST analysis retrieved 2 distinct crystal-containing regions within the Y chromosome contig (RefSeq NC_024512.1; International Nucleotide Sequence Database Collaboration INSDC CP007106.1) (Fig. 2a). The first region, that we called crystal1 (277,709 bp), spans nucleotides 846,141 to 1,123,850, located between the ARY (702,430 to 704,048), and Ppr-Y (1,636,574 to 1,884,846) genes; this corresponds to the classical crystal/Su(Ste) locus at the h11 heterochromatic band. The second region that we called crystal2 (170,807 bp), spans nucleotides 2,496,221 to 2,667,028, proximal to the WDY gene (2,273,422 to 2,487,777) at the h13-h14 band.

To resolve their genomic organization, we aligned both loci against z11734 using PipMaker platform (http://bio.cse.psu.edu) (Schwartz et al. 2000).

The alignment is expressed as a dot-plot sequence comparison graph. The PipMaker platform also computes alignments of similar regions in 2 DNA sequences furnishing detailed results about the arrangement of the analyzed sequences. We searched for homology between z11734 and the 2 specific regions, containing crystal-like sequences, of the Y chromosome. The output of this program is a matrix displaying the summarized alignments with the percent identity plot organization of the sequences compared with each other. Alignment of crystal1 (h11) revealed fragmented, multidirectional sequences of varying lengths with high similarity to z11734 (Fig. 2b). This fragmented architecture indicates that crystal1 is structurally disorganized, challenging previous models describing it as a simple tandem array (Balakireva et al. 1992; Tritto et al. 2003; Bozzetti et al. 2012; Specchia et al. 2017). Conversely, crystal2 (h13–h14) displayed uniform, co-directional alignment bars, confirming a highly ordered tandem-repeat structure (Fig. 2c).

Base-by-base BLAST analysis revealed that crystal1 contains 113 crystal-repeat fragments of varying lengths, structures, and orientations (Fig. 3a). This region includes only 4 complete, z11734 repeats oriented in both directions (genomic coordinates and orientations in Supplementary Table 1) and none are arranged as tandem repeats. Most repeats carry shared or unique SNPs, small deletions, and insertions (Supplementary Table 2). crystal1 also hosts numerous multidirectional transposon relics, including rearranged fragments of R1A1, micropia, 17.6, gypsy12, and GATE (Fig. 3a). Unsequenced or unclassified gaps remain within the Release 6 crystal1 assembly. This disorganized architecture structurally resembles known genomic piRNA clusters, located elsewhere in the genome (Han et al. 2015; Senti et al. 2015; Sato and Siomi 2020). Additionally, crystal1 contains novel, multidirectional crystal-like repeats that are sequence-distinct from z11734. We termed these XhoI-Y and HpaI-Y repeats based on their terminal restriction sites. In total, crystal1 comprises 95 z11734, 15 XhoI-Y, and 3 HpaI-Y fragments. These “new” repeats have not been previously described and are detailed in the following paragraphs.

Detailed analysis of crystal2 confirmed the regular tandem architecture predicted by PipMaker (Fig. 2c). As illustrated in Fig. 3b and detailed in Supplementary Table 3, crystal2 contains 39 z11734-like repeats, 35 of which are complete, ordered, and arranged in tandem. These repeats display specific sequence variations compared with the z11734 reference (Supplementary Table 4) and retain the flanking 6 bp palindromic MluI/CfoI restriction sites (ACGCGT). We also identified 3 XhoI-Y repeats in this region.

Like crystal1, crystal2 contains rearranged TEs in both orientation (Fig. 3b); some are shared (gypsy12, Batumi, hoppel/1360, roo, invader2), while others are locus-specific (mdg1, 1731, springer, copia, F-element). Characterized by head-to-tail crystal repeats with a regular organization of hoppel/1360 and Stellate-like sequences (Fig. 4), crystal2 structurally mirrors the euchromatic Stellate arrays on the X chromosome (Livak 1984; Palumbo et al. 1994b; Tritto et al. 2003).

Sequence analysis reveals that the most frequent variations observed in the ordered tandem repeats of the crystal 2 region are also present in the majority of rearranged repeats within the crystal1 region (Supplementary Tables 2 and 4).

To validate these 2 distinct Y-linked loci, we performed FISH using the CryMe3 clone (De Grassi et al. 2022). This clone covers a 380 bp segment of z11734 (positions 2,148 to 2,528) shared among the z11734, HpaI-Y, and XhoI-Y repeats. On standard mitotic chromosomes, FISH generated a broad signal spanning the h11–h14 heterochromatic region, consistent with the literature. However, on highly elongated chromosomes, this single signal resolved into 2 distinct hybridization spots at h11 and h14 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Arrangement of crystal-related sequences in recent Y scaffolds

Heterochromatin-enriched assemblies of the Drosophila melanogaster Y chromosome (Chang and Larracuente 2019) mapped crystal-related sequences primarily to scaffold 7—which contains the Y-linked genes PRY and Kl3—and scaffold 4, which contains ARY and WDY. These repeats are also present in several associated contigs (eg contigs 14, 17, and 20). PipMaker and BLAST alignments against the z11734 reference revealed both z11734-like arrays and novel, molecularly distinct crystal variants organized in regular tandem repeats, mirroring the crystal2 architecture of Release 6 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In scaffold 7, XhoI-Y repeats are arranged in tandem alongside z11734-like repeats; no HpaI-Y elements were detected (Fig. 5a, b; Supplementary Table 5). Conversely, scaffold 4 contains tandem HpaI-Y and z11734-like arrays but lacks XhoI-Y repeats (Fig. 5d, e; Supplementary Table 6).

Within scaffold 7, we mapped 3 regions hosting 146 crystal-like elements: 99 z11734-like, 45 XhoI-Y, and 2 hybrid repeats (z11734/XhoI-Y and XhoI-Y/z11734; Supplementary Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table 5). Scaffold 4 contains 2 regions with crystal-like repeats, including 1 z11734/HpaI-Y hybrid (Supplementary Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table 6). All identified crystal-like repeats (z11734, XhoI-Y, and HpaI-Y) contain pseudo-CK2β repeat sequences (PCKRs) (Chang and Larracuente 2019; De Grassi et al. 2022; Shao et al. 2024). Specifically, the PCKR sequence spans positions 1,065 to 1,481 in z11734 (Fig. 4b), 2,150 to 2,420 in XhoI-Y (Fig. 5b), and 1,250 to 1,690 in HpaI-Y (Fig. 5e), where the Stellate-like region matches the PCKR in length and sequence.

Excluding core Y-linked genes (WDY, Ppr-Y, Kl3), scaffolds 7 and 4 show minimal sequence overlap with Release 6 (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). Significant overlaps are restricted to contigs 14 and 17, which anchor the crystal2 region; notably, no overlapping sequences exist between the disorganized crystal1 locus at h11 and these newly assembled scaffolds.

Evolution of the crystal-like repeats on the Y chromosome

To comprehensively resolve the phylogenetic relationship among all crystal and crystal-like sequences identified on the Y chromosome and to place them within a broader evolutionary framework alongside X-linked Stellate sequences, we conducted a rigorous and systematic evolutionary analysis. Given the complex and repetitive nature of these sequences, as well as their known propensity to undergo concerted evolution, establishing their precise evolutionary affinities represents a significant challenge that requires careful selection of both the sequence dataset and the analytical approach. To this end, 68 crystal-like repeats drawn from crystal1 and crystal2 regions, characterized both in the present study and by Chang and Larracuente (2019), were included in the analysis. These were jointly aligned with 10 PCKRs previously reported by Chang and Larracuente (2019), whose evolutionary position within the broader crystal/Stellate superfamily has remained insufficiently resolved. To permit direct comparison with the X-linked counterparts of these Y-chromosomal elements, 2 functionally and structurally distinct Stellate sequences were incorporated. These represent the 2 major chromatin compartments in which Stellate elements are known to reside: the euchromatic Stellate sequence (AC no. X15899) and the heterochromatic Stellate sequence (AC no. X97134).

All sequences were subjected to multiple sequence alignments, which served as the basis for phylogenetic inference using the Maximum Likelihood method. Unlike distance-based approaches, Maximum Likelihood explicitly models the substitution process, making it particularly well-suited for recovering the evolutionary history of sequences that may have experienced varying selective pressures across genomic compartments. The resulting unrooted phylogenetic tree unambiguously partitions all sequences into 3 well-supported and clearly delineated evolutionary clades (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 4):

Expression of TEs in XY cry− males

Given its disorganized architecture and bidirectional transposon relics, we hypothesized that the crystal1 locus functions as a piRNA cluster, structurally resembling known uni-strand germline clusters (eg AT-X1like, AT-X1, cluster 2) or somatic clusters (eg flamenco) (Goriaux et al. 2014; Hirakata and Siomi 2016; Czech et al. 2018; Sato and Siomi 2020). To test whether this region silences TEs, we quantified the expression of crystal1-associated germline and somatic transposons in the testes of males lacking this locus (XYcry−).

qPCR analysis demonstrated that deleting the crystal1 region significantly upregulates all analyzed TEs compared with controls (Fig. 8). Specifically, I-element, Batumi, and HeT-A exhibited the highest expression increases (fold changes of 17.96, 15.74, and 9.32, respectively), followed by gypsy12 (4.94), ZAM (3.86), R1 (2.12), 412 (2.07), and mdg1 (1.95).

Discussion

Identification of 2 distinct Y-linked crystal/Su(Ste) loci on Release 6

The crystal-Stellate system represents one of the most intriguing examples of heterochromatin–euchromatin interaction regulated by the piRNA pathway in Drosophila melanogaster (Aravin et al. 2001, 2003; Vagin et al. 2006; Bozzetti et al. 2012; Specchia et al. 2017). Since its discovery, cytological and molecular studies have extensively mapped and characterized the Stellate euchromatic and heterochromatic loci on the X chromosome, alongside the crystal/Su(Ste) region on the Y chromosome (Livak 1984, 1990; Pimpinelli et al. 1986; Balakireva et al. 1992; Gatti and Pimpinelli 1992; McKee and Satter 1996; Tulin et al. 1997; Kalmykova et al. 1998; Tritto et al. 2003; Hoskins et al. 2015).

Building upon Release 6 of the Drosophila melanogaster genome (Hoskins et al. 2015), we analyze all Y genomic sequences and identified 2 distinct Y-chromosomal regions containing z11734-like repeats (Fig. 2):

The crystal1 region: Located at the h11 heterochromatic band (proximal to the ARY gene), this region exhibits a “disorganized” structure containing fragments of z11734-like repeats of varying lengths in both orientations, interspersed with transposon relics. We verified these fragmented crystal-like sequences (Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating that this disorganization represents the actual biological structure of the locus rather than an assembly artifact of incomplete sequencing.

We verified these fragmented crystal-like sequences (Supplementary Table 1), demonstrating that this disorganization represents the actual biological structure of the locus rather than an assembly artifact of incomplete sequencing.

The crystal2 region: Located at h14 (immediately to the right of the WDY gene), this region contains z11734-like tandem-repeat units (Fig. 2b). This locus likely corresponds to the classic tandem-repeat crystal/Su(Ste) region previously described in the literature (Livak 1984, 1990; Pimpinelli et al. 1986; Balakireva et al. 1992; Gatti and Pimpinelli 1992; Tulin et al. 1997; Kalmykova et al. 1998; Kogan et al. 2000; Carvalho et al. 2001; Hoskins et al. 2015).

Characterization of novel repeats in heterochromatin-enriched scaffolds and contigs of the Y chromosome

This study provides the first detailed structural characterization of distinct domains containing z11734-like tandem-repeat units. Crucially, in addition to the canonical 2843 bp repeat (z11734-like), we identified 2 new types of crystal/Su(Ste) repeat the Y chromosome by cross-referencing available scaffolds from Chang and Larracuente (2019): the XhoI-Y repeat (5328 bp) and the HpaI-Y repeat (2596 bp) (Figs. 5 and 6). Their structural organization resembles that of the z11734-like repeat, integrating sequences from different TEs: gypsy12 in the XhoI-Y repeat and HeT-A in the HpaI-Y repeat.

Interestingly, previous studies briefly noted a crystal/Su(Ste) repeat of approximately 2,500 nucleotides without further elaboration (McKee and Satter 1996; Kogan et al. 2000). These likely correspond to the 2,596 bp HpaI-Y repeats identified here, or to generic PCKRs derived from Ck2β-tes—also known as Ssl (Suppressor of Ste-like) (Gvozdev et al. 2005; Usakin et al. 2005).

Evolutionary origin and phylogeny of the crystal repeats

Some of these PCKRs map to the h15 band of the Y chromosome (Chang and Larracuente 2019; Chang et al. 2022; De Grassi et al. 2022; Shao et al. 2024). Previous models suggest that the evolutionarily conserved autosomal Ssl gene—which encodes the testis-specific β-subunit of casein kinase II and maps to the 60D region of chromosome 2—is the ancestral precursor of both Ste and Su(Ste) sequences (Kalmykova et al. 1997; Kogan et al. 2000). Following the initial duplication of Ssl to the Y chromosome, this gene family expanded onto both sex chromosomes, undergoing independent amplification to reach high copy numbers.

Although the current phylogenetic tree does not allow for exact divergence times, relative branch lengths suggest an ancient divergence of Group 1 and Group 2 from a common Ssl-like ancestor, followed by a recent diversification within Group 3. Furthermore, our data indicate that organized genomic regions evolved before disorganized ones, as exemplified by the derived position of crystal1 sequences in Group 3. A similar evolutionary trajectory has been proposed for uni-strand piRNA clusters, such as flamenco (Malone and Hannon 2009; Signor et al. 2023).

Crystal1 region regulates Stellate and transposable elements

Based on the detailed structure of crystal1 (Figs. 2b and 3a), we propose that it acts as a uni-strand piRNA cluster that silences euchromatic Stellate repeats on the X chromosome. Consequently, the absence of the crystal1 region leads to the deregulation of Stellate repeats, causing the formation of crystalline aggregates in the spermatocytes of crystal− males. This hypothesis is strongly supported by the fact that males mutant for most piRNA pathway genes exhibit identical crystalline aggregates (Specchia et al. 2017, 2019).

Several lines of evidence support the classification of crystal1 as a functional uni-strand cluster:

Sequence Orientation: The repetitive sequences and transposon relics within this cluster are oriented in both directions, meaning transcription generates RNA substrates compatible with the piRNA processing machinery.

Because hoppel/1360 sequences are present within both the z11734-like repeats and the newly characterized XhoI-Y repeats of the crystal1 region, the proposed mechanism—whereby Ste-like piRNA production depends on maternally inherited piRNAs targeting hoppel/1360 (Venkei et al. 2023)—remains valid. Additionally, other TEs likely participate in this process, such as HeT-A, a component of the HpaI-Y repeats that is also present in the crystal1 region and highly expressed in ovaries.

Although the crystal/Stellate system is functionally active in the male germline but silent in somatic tissues, we clarify that our chromatin state observations reflect larval neuroblasts rather than active germline transcription. Active chromatin marks do not inherently imply productive transcription, particularly on the Drosophila Y chromosome, which is largely heterochromatic but may display locally permissive features (Tritto et al. 2003; Xu et al. 2025).

However, further supporting the role of crystal1 as a regulatory piRNA cluster, we observed a marked upregulation of specific transposons (such as I, Batumi, and HeT-A, which increased more than gypsy12, ZAM, R1, 412, and mdg1) in X/Ycry− males relative to X/Y control males.

Collectively, all our findings provide critical structural insights into Y-linked loci. While recent progress has clarified the aspects of crystal-Stellate regulation, the precise mechanics of their interaction remain to be fully elucidated. Beyond the crystal1 and crystal2 regions, we characterized 4 additional Y-linked regions containing crystal-like repeats organized in tandem (Fig. 10). While it remains to be determined whether all identified regions contribute to Stellate silencing, our data strongly suggest that the crystal1 disorganized region serves as the primary piRNA cluster mediating X-linked Stellate repression.
