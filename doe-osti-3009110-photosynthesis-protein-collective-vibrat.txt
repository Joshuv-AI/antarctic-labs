1. Overview and Objectives

This project investigates how low‑frequency collective vibrations of photosynthetic proteins control
energy transfer and dissipation, using terahertz (THz) spectroscopy and advanced theoretical analysis.
The central hypothesis is that specific long‑range protein scaffold motions, rather than just local
chromophore dynamics, steer exciton transport and photoprotection by modulating energy gaps and
coupling between pigments.

The specific objectives are to:

   Directly determine the spectral density and directionality of intramolecular vibrations in Fenna–
 Matthews–Olson (FMO) protein and Orange Carotenoid Protein (OCP) at THz frequencies.

   Quantify how collective modes change with temperature, hydration, and functional state (e.g., OCP
 orange vs red), and how these changes relate to photoswitching and energy transfer efficiency.

   Develop and validate theoretical tools (Normal Mode Ensemble Analysis, NMEA) that link measured
 THz spectra to specific backbone motions and functional conformational changes.

   Establish robust, sharable THz microscopy and analysis infrastructure applicable to broader
 problems in biomolecular structural dynamics.

2. Methods and Approach

Experimental methods

The experimental platform combines time‑domain THz spectroscopy, near‑field THz detection, and
cryogenic/temperature‑controlled environments for both protein solutions and crystals. Two principal
spectroscopic modalities are employed:

Solution‑phase THz “dynamical transition” measurements: Temperature‑dependent THz absorption
of fully hydrated protein solutions is used to monitor the onset of anharmonic, collective motions, with
the transition temperature interpreted as a proxy for structural stability and global flexibility.
Measurements are performed over ~80–300 K with controlled hydration and concentration to separate
protein and water contributions.[3][1][2]

  Crystal‑phase anisotropic THz microscopy:

 o Anisotropic Terahertz Microscopy (ATM) and Polarization‑Varying ATM (PV‑ATM) quantify
 polarization‑dependent THz absorbance in protein crystals by rotating either the sample or
 the incident THz polarization.

 o Stationary‑Sample Anisotropic THz Microscopy (SS‑ATM/IATM) keeps the crystal fixed while
 rotating THz and probe polarization along calibrated iso‑response curves, producing rapid,
 artifact‑free anisotropic absorbance spectra directly comparable to calculations.

 o Near‑field THz micropolarimetry provides sub‑wavelength spatial resolution and full
 Stokes‑parameter characterization of THz polarization for biomolecular crystals and other
 anisotropic samples. Standard optical spectroscopies (UV–Vis, microspectroscopy) and X‑ray
 crystallography are used for sample characterization, orientation determination, and
 verification of photostate conversion.

Theoretical and computational methods

Normal Mode Ensemble Analysis (NMEA) is developed to bridge THz experiments and structural
dynamics. The method:

  Combines long equilibrium all‑atom molecular dynamics trajectories with repeated normal‑mode
 calculations on many snapshots, including explicit hydration shells.

  Computes isotropic and anisotropic THz spectra by summing contributions from thousands of
 modes per snapshot, then ensemble‑averaging over hundreds of configurations to mimic thermal
 sampling in experiments.

  Uses convergence tests to determine required water shell thickness (~20 Å) and number of modes
 (≥6000 per structure) to accurately cover the 0–100 cm⁻¹ region and capture hydration‑dependent
 vibrational density of states.

  Projects eigenvectors onto functional displacement vectors (e.g., inhibitor‑binding displacements,
 loop motions, domain separation) to identify “functional bands” where vibrational modes strongly
 overlap with known conformational changes.

Targets include FMO, OCP, lysozyme (CEWL), fluorescent proteins (TagRFP, mCherry, mOrange2), and
other benchmark systems where structural and functional data are available.

3. Results and Technical Progress

3.1 Instrument development and validation

  ATM → PV‑ATM → IATM evolution: Initial ATM measurements required long acquisition times
 and sample rotation, limiting temperature series and risking crystal aging. PV‑ATM reduced
 measurement time by rotating THz polarization instead, allowing FMO and OCP temperature series
 and photostate measurements. IATM/SS‑ATM then eliminated polarization‑dependent artifacts by
 operating the electro‑optic detector on optimized iso‑response curves and keeping the sample
 stationary, enabling high‑throughput, reproducible anisotropic spectra.

  Artifact identification and correction: Polarization‑dependent artifacts in early SS‑ATM were
 traced to steep‑slope regions of the detector response; selection of new iso‑response curves and
 automated etalon filtering removed these effects, as validated on sucrose single‑crystal standards.

  Benchmarking with lysozyme crystals: Tetragonal, monoclinic, triclinic, and orthorhombic CEWL
 crystals were grown reproducibly, and IATM measurements showed consistent anisotropic
 resonances across samples and apertures (e.g., bands near ~30, 45, 55, and 80 cm⁻¹), with
 well‑characterized aging effects. These benchmarks demonstrate that sample‑to‑sample variability
 is understood and controlled.

3.2 FMO: vibrational spectral density and temperature dependence

  Dynamical transition of intramolecular modes: PV‑ATM measurements on FMO crystals show
 structured anisotropic spectra at low temperature (5–60 cm⁻¹) that collapse rapidly above ~200 K,
 coincident with the dynamical transition of biological water; this is interpreted as release of
 water‑imposed steric constraints on certain collective motions.

  Persistent high‑frequency band and blue‑shift: A prominent anisotropic resonance near ~85
 cm⁻¹ at 150 K persists to 300 K while blue‑shifting to ~110 cm⁻¹, with weak sensitivity to the water
 dynamical transition, implying that the associated mode primarily involves internal scaffold
 motions. This supports the idea that modes near the 4.7 THz optical coherence frequency are
 genuine intramolecular vibrations that can modulate pigment energy gaps at biologically relevant
 temperatures.

3.3 OCP: structural stability and photoprotective state

   Solution‑phase dynamical transition: Temperature‑dependent THz absorption of OCP solutions
 reveals that the orange state (OCPO) exhibits a gradual dynamical transition beginning near ~180 K,
 while both photo‑activated and chaotrope‑induced red states (OCPR) show a sharper onset near
 ~240 K. This indicates that the photoprotective red state is globally more structurally stable and
 less flexible than OCPO, counter to the prior narrative of a globally “more flexible” red state.

   Frequency‑resolved changes and water artifacts: For OCPR, low‑frequency absorbance is
 suppressed or shifted to higher frequency, consistent with backbone stiffening, while an anomalous
 ~200 K feature in some datasets was investigated via high‑concentration CEWL controls and
 attributed to complex frozen‑water contributions rather than simple protein transitions.

   Crystal‑phase anisotropic vibrations and photostate dependence:

 o PV‑ATM measurements on OCP crystals from multiple collaborators demonstrate
 reproducible light‑induced blue‑shifts of intramolecular resonances (~2–3 cm⁻¹) and changes
            in angular dependence at selected bands (e.g., ~18–21, 27–30, 36–38, and ~100–17 cm⁻¹),
 indicating reorientation of collective motions in the red state.

 o IATM/SS‑ATM measurements on new OCP crystals grown at UB (Fremyella and Synechocystis
 constructs) confirm photostate‑dependent shifts and demonstrate that the observed
 resonances are molecular rather than lattice in origin.

 o Control measurements on CEWL crystals under identical illumination show no
 photostate‑dependent shifts, confirming that OCP effects arise from specific functional
 rearrangements rather than generic heating or optical artifacts.

Taken together, these solution‑ and crystal‑phase results support a model in which OCP photoprotection
is accompanied by reduced flexibility and increased vibrational directionality in the core domains,
facilitating robust interaction with phycobilisome components while localizing enhanced flexibility to
linker regions that mediate domain separation.

3.4 NMEA: linking spectra to function

   Convergence and hydration effects: NMEA calculations on CEWL and other proteins show that
 including at least ~20 Å of water and ≥6000 modes per snapshot is required to reproduce measured
 isotropic THz spectra between 0 and 100 cm⁻¹, and that both isotropic and anisotropic spectra
 exhibit non‑monotonic temperature dependence with peaks near ~200 K, consistent with
 experimental observations.

   Anisotropic spectra and inhibitor dependence: For CEWL, ensemble‑averaged anisotropic
 spectra show well‑defined resonant bands with low standard deviations when averaged over ~640
 spectra (8 replicas × 800 ns trajectories). These anisotropic spectra change with inhibitor binding in
 ways that mirror ATM/IATM measurements, while isotropic spectra and simple vibrational density
 of states remain comparatively insensitive, emphasizing that directional selection is essential for
 resolving functionally relevant modes.

   Identification of functional bands: By projecting eigenvectors onto structural displacement
 vectors associated with functional changes (e.g., substrate‑binding displacement of CEWL, loop
 motions), NMEA identifies narrow spectral regions (e.g., around ~8 and ~18 cm⁻¹) where modes
 show high overlap with functional displacements, even though the underlying vibrational density of
 states is broad and congested. This overturns the assumption that only the very lowest‑frequency
 modes are functionally important and suggests that higher‑frequency collective modes in selected
 bands could be targeted for frequency‑specific perturbations or control.

   Persistent motions in rugged landscapes: NMEA applied to several proteins (including
 fluorescent proteins and OCP) reveals persistent collective motions that recur across thermally
 sampled configurations, consistent with a rugged energy landscape in which functionally important
 vibrational motifs persist despite structural heterogeneity.

4. Impact, Significance, and Future Directions

Scientific and technical impact

This project establishes THz anisotropic spectroscopy as a practical, quantitative tool for probing
functional protein vibrations in both crystals and solutions, with demonstrated application to
photosynthetic energy transfer and photoprotection. The combination of IATM and NMEA provides a
general workflow to:

   Identify protein vibrational bands that are strongly coupled to functional conformational changes
 and energy transfer processes.

   Separate functionally relevant modes from a large dynamical background (“dynamical buffer”) that
 obscures them in conventional isotropic and density‑of‑states measurements.

   Reconcile dynamical transition phenomena, hydration effects, and functional motions in a unified
 framework applicable to a broad class of biomolecular systems.

Beyond photosynthesis, the instrumentation and theory developed here are directly applicable to
enzymes, signaling proteins, RNA–protein complexes, and soft materials where long‑range collective
motions mediate function.

Training and dissemination

The project has supported and trained multiple PhD students, postdoctoral researchers, and
undergraduates in THz optics, structural biology, and molecular simulation, with graduates moving into
research careers in industry, national labs, and academia. Results have been disseminated via
peer‑reviewed journal articles (including high‑impact journals in biophysics, physical chemistry, and
photonics), conference presentations (Biophysical Society, IRMMW‑THz, CLEO, APS, ACS), and invited
seminars. The group has also contributed community resources such as optics workbench CAD libraries,
materials databases, and analysis scripts for visualizing and assigning protein vibrational motions.

Planned future work (as outlined in the reports)

Planned or ongoing directions include:

 Completing and publishing manuscripts on OCP dynamical transition and IATM photostate results,
 and on foundational NMEA methodology and anisotropic spectral assignment.

 Extending NMEA to OCP mutants and FMO to test how targeted sequence changes alter
 intramolecular dynamics and functional vibrational bands.

 Integrating THz anisotropic spectroscopy with photo‑dependent crystallography and NMR to
 correlate vibrational bands with atomistic structural rearrangements in real time.

 Exploring alternative experimental strategies (e.g., pump‑probe schemes that selectively modulate
 dynamics in specific regions) to isolate functional vibrations in solution without crystallization.
