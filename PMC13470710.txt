Experimental Study on the Variation of Characteristic Temperatures and Activation Energy of Coal Oxidation under Thermal Airflow Erosion
Authors: Chang Liu, Baoshan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Coal spontaneous combustion (CSC) poses severe threats in deep mining environments with high ground temperature and thermal airflow. This study investigates thermogravimetric characteristics and activation energy variations of lignite subjected to thermal airflow erosion. Coal samples were pretreated at 35 and 65 °C with airflow rates of 0–200 mL/min for 30 days, followed by TG-DSC analysis. Eight characteristic temperatures and activation energies were determined using the Kissinger–Akahira–Sunose (KAS) and Ozawa–Flynn–Wall (OFW) methods. Results show that elevated temperature (65 °C) pretreatment universally reduced critical temperature (T 1), dry-cracking temperature (T 2), and activation temperature (T 3), significantly increasing CSC propensity. Temperature was the primary influencing factor, while the airflow rate was secondary. Notably, thermally airflow-eroded samples exhibited monotonically decreasing activation energy with conversion rate, indicating pronounced self-accelerating oxidation. The most significant “activation” effect occurred under 35 °C with a low airflow rate (100 mL/min), substantially lowering the activation energy barrier. These findings reveal that residual coal exposed to “low temperature with restricted ventilation” undergoes the most detrimental transformations, becoming extremely susceptible to spontaneous combustion.

Introduction

Coal remains a widely utilized traditional energy source worldwide. Safety assessment and disaster prevention during its mining, transportation, and storage processes have consistently been central topics of research in both industrial and academic communities. , Coal spontaneous combustion (CSC), as one of the most destructive disasters in coal mine production, not only causes substantial resource losses but also readily induces secondary disasters such as gas explosions and the emission of toxic and harmful gases. − Therefore, investigating CSC issues and preventing its occurrence hold significant importance for addressing challenges related to energy, the environment, and human health.

CSC is a complex physicochemical evolution process involving physical adsorption, , chemical adsorption, , and multistage pyrolysis reactions. , Subtle changes in the physicochemical properties of coal under different environmental conditions can significantly affect the CSC characteristics. In China, as overlying coal seams are becoming increasingly depleted, coal mining is gradually shifting to the underlying seams. The deep mining environment exhibits typical ″three-high″ characteristics, that is, high ground temperature, high stress, and high pore pressure. Such primary thermal erosion not only alters the micropore structure and surface chemical activity of the coal mass but also exposes residual coal in goaf areas to oxidation environments distinctly different from those in traditional shallow mining. After entering the goaf, these coal masses with initial temperatures encounter thermal airflow infiltrating through air leakage channels, resulting in significant deviations in the CSC behavior.

In terms of the influence of the goaf environment on coal oxidation characteristics, researchers have conducted systematic investigations from two dimensions: macroscopic kinetics and microscopic structural evolution. Wang et al. , found through temperature-programmed experiments that with increasing preoxidation temperature, the oxidizability and exothermicity of oxidized coal initially decreased and subsequently increased, which was correlated with variations in oxygen consumption rate, carbon monoxide emission rate, and carbon dioxide emission rate; through Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), they discovered that the aromatic structures of preoxidized coal were more susceptible to oxygen erosion during secondary oxidation. Wang et al. employed scanning electron microscopy and low-temperature liquid nitrogen adsorption, finding that macropore fractal dimensions increased with temperature while micropore fractal dimensions decreased. Duan et al. simulated the secondary oxidation process of oxidized coal through thermogravimetric-DSC (TG-DSC) and in situ FTIR experiments, measuring CSC characteristic parameters and functional group evolution, and found that aliphatic hydrocarbons, –COOH, and CO played predominant roles in promoting heat release during secondary oxidation. Similarly, Niu et al. experimentally analyzed the micropore structural characteristics of residual coal in deep wells, discovering that the deep high-temperature environment resulted in residual coal with larger specific surface areas and more developed pore structures, thereby enhancing coal-oxygen contact area and oxidation reaction activity. Shao et al. found that pore structure analysis revealed increased specific surface area and pore volume of intruded coal samples, with significant enhancement in pore surface roughness and network complexity, providing more active sites for coal–oxygen complex reactions; furthermore, igneous intrusion increased the proportion of aromatic carbon in coal, elevated the degree of branching, facilitated the transformation of aliphatic carbon to aromatic carbon, and increased oxygen-containing functional group content. Gao et al., through experimental research, found that compared with raw coal, secondary oxidized coal absorbed more heat and generated more oxygen-containing functional groups (−COOH, CO, –COO−), leading to higher CSC risks during secondary oxidation. Tang et al., through adiabatic oxidation and TG/DSC experiments, discovered that preoxidation steps weakened certain functional groups in coal to some extent, but the carbon–oxygen structure ratio fluctuated, resulting in decreased activation energy for secondary oxidation between 40 and 120 °C. Wang et al., utilizing infrared spectrometer and simultaneous thermal analyzer (STA) tests, found that residual coal during secondary oxidation exhibited lower characteristic temperatures and heat absorption, but higher heat release, mass loss rate, and comprehensive combustion index. Current research primarily explores the mechanisms of high-temperature or airflow effects on coal microstructure and focuses on secondary oxidation with initial oxidation temperatures exceeding 70 °C; studies simulating CSC under low-temperature influence approximating high-temperature mine conditions remain scarce.

In view of this, we designed an experimental method to simulate the effect of high-temperature airflow erosion on CSC in high-temperature mines. This method enables the investigation of variations in thermogravimetric characteristics and activation energy following such treatment, thereby providing theoretical support for evaluating changes in CSC behavior. Specifically, coal samples were oxidized under controlled temperatures (35 and 65 °C) with different airflow rates, after which their thermogravimetric and differential thermal characteristics were determined. Based on the theory of coal oxidation and CSC, characteristic temperatures and exothermic properties were identified, and the variation patterns of activation energy were calculated and analyzed using the Kissinger–Akahira–Sunose (KAS) and Ozawa–Flynn–Wall (OFW) isoconversional methods. Finally, the changes in CSC characteristics of coal subjected to high-temperature airflow erosion were predicted based on the evolution patterns of these parameters.

Experimental and Analytical Methods

Experimental Methods

Lignite from Zuoyun Donggucheng Coal Industry Co., Ltd., Shanxi Coal Import and Export Group, was selected for this study. Large lumps of fresh lignite were packed in sealed bags and transported to the laboratory. The selected coal lumps were crushed by using a press, and coal samples with particle sizes below 100 mm were sieved and packed in sealed bags for standby use.

The sieved coal samples were divided into nine portions. One portion was left untreated as raw coal, sealed in a bag, labeled R0, and stored at room temperature for 30 days. The remaining coal samples were treated and numbered according to Table . The pretreatment procedure is exemplified by T35-Q1: the sample was homogenized by quartering, placed in a quartz glass tube equipped with a one-way gas valve, immersed in a water bath maintained at 35 °C, and subjected to 200 mL/min airflow for 30 days of isothermal treatment. Following pretreatment, each sample was again subjected to quartering, and a portion was crushed and ground to below 120 mesh (<125 μm) for subsequent TG-DSC analysis.

Characteristic Temperatures during Coal Oxidation

(1)Critical temperature (T 1) is the temperature at which coal begins to undergo significant oxidation reactions. The critical temperature represents the onset temperature of the CSC; below this temperature, the oxidation reaction rate is extremely low, whereas above it, the reaction accelerates significantly. A lower critical temperature indicates a higher CSC propensity [the temperature corresponding to the first local maximum (peak) on the derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) curve in the initial mass-loss stage].(2)Dry-cracking temperature (T 2) is the temperature at which moisture and volatile matter in coal begin to evolve substantially. The dry-cracking temperature marks the initiation of coal pyrolysis and significant structural changes. It reflects the thermal stability of coal; a lower temperature indicates greater susceptibility to pyrolysis at low temperatures and higher CSC risk (the temperature at which the coal sample first reaches its minimum mass on the TG curve before the major combustion stage; correspondingly, the first zero-crossing point of the DTG curve after the initial mass-loss peak).(3)Activation temperature (T 3): the temperature at which the oxidation reaction rate increases markedly, typically associated with the activation energy. The activation temperature represents the energy threshold required for coal oxidation and serves as a key parameter for assessing the CSC tendency. A lower activation temperature indicates easier initiation of the CSC at low temperatures, necessitating enhanced monitoring and prevention measures [the temperature corresponding to the first local minimum (trough) on the DTG curve during the oxygen-chemisorption weight-gain stage, representing the onset of accelerated oxidation].(4)Temperature of maximum oxygen chemisorption weight gain (T 4) is the temperature at which oxygen chemisorption weight gain reaches its maximum during coal oxidation. This temperature reflects the most intense stage of oxidation and represents a critical point in the CSC process. Monitoring this parameter provides insight into oxidation intensity and informs prevention strategies [the temperature corresponding to the local minimum on the TG curve (or the maximum mass gain) before substantial mass loss occurs, also the second zero-crossing point of the DTG curve].(5)The ignition temperature (T 5) is the temperature at which coal begins to burn in air. The ignition temperature marks the transition from oxidation to combustion, indicating the onset of uncontrolled CSC. A lower ignition temperature corresponds to a higher CSC risk (determined by the tangent method: a vertical line is drawn through the maximum peak of the DTG curve to intersect the TG curve at point A; the tangent to the TG curve at point A intersects the horizontal line at the onset of mass loss, and the abscissa of this intersection gives the ignition temperature).(6)Temperature of maximum mass loss rate (T 6) is the temperature at which the mass loss rate reaches its maximum during pyrolysis or combustion. This temperature indicates the most intense stage of the pyrolysis or combustion reactions. Analysis of this parameter elucidates pyrolysis or combustion characteristics, informing process optimization or CSC prevention [the temperature corresponding to the global minimum (maximum negative peak) on the DTG curve].(7)Temperature of the maximum heat release rate (T i) is the temperature at which the heat release rate reaches its maximum during oxidation or combustion. This temperature marks the stage of the most concentrated heat release. Monitoring this parameter enables assessment of heat release intensity during CSC or combustion, guiding prevention or control measures (the temperature at the peak of the principal exothermic peak on the DSC curve).(8)Burnout temperature (T 7): the temperature at which coal combustion or pyrolysis is completed. The burnout temperature reflects the completeness of combustion or pyrolysis and can be used to evaluate combustion efficiency and determine the final stage of the CSC process − (the temperature at which the DTG curve returns to the baseline after the final combustion peak, or determined by the tangent method as the intersection of the TG-curve tangent with the horizontal line at the end of mass loss).

Results and Discussion

Variation Patterns of Characteristic Temperatures

Taking the heating rate of 10 °C/min as an example, the mass residual (Mass), DTG, heat flow (DSC), and integrated DSC curves of raw coal (R0) are presented in Figure . The characteristic temperatures T 1, T 2, T 3, T 4, T 5, T 6, T i, and T 7 were determined as 86.6, 163.2, 201.5, 233.6, 447.8, 462.6, 536.6, and 588.5 °C, respectively, as annotated in Figure . The CSC propensity can be assessed by determining the critical temperature (T 1), activation temperature (T 3), and ignition temperature (T 5); lower temperatures indicate higher CSC risk. Characteristic temperatures determined by thermogravimetric analysis (e.g., critical temperature, dry-cracking temperature, and activation temperature) serve as crucial parameters for evaluating CSC tendency and predicting CSC behavior. These temperature points are influenced by coal quality characteristics, experimental conditions, and environmental factors, and their variation patterns provide a scientific basis for the prevention and control of CSC. However, CSC in actual goaf environments is affected by multiple factors; therefore, characteristic temperature data must be integrated with field conditions for comprehensive analysis to enable more accurate prediction and prevention of CSC.

TG-DTG, DSC-Chr, and characteristic temperatures of R0 samples.

The factors influencing characteristic temperatures under experimental conditions primarily include the coal quality characteristics, particle size, and experimental conditions. Coal quality characteristics mainly refer to the coal rank, volatile matter content, ash content, and moisture content. A lower coal rank corresponds to lower critical, dry-cracking, and activation temperatures, indicating a higher CSC propensity. A higher volatile matter content results in lower dry-cracking and ignition temperatures, facilitating pyrolysis and combustion. Lower ash and moisture contents generally lead to lower characteristic temperatures; conversely, higher ash and moisture contents inhibit pyrolysis and combustion, resulting in higher characteristic temperatures.

Experimental conditions that affect characteristic temperatures primarily include heating rate and oxygen concentration. Higher heating rates prevent coal samples from having sufficient reaction time at low temperatures, typically leading to higher characteristic temperatures. Higher oxygen concentrations intensify coal oxidation reactions, generally reducing the critical, activation, and ignition temperatures. Smaller particle sizes increase the coal–oxygen contact area and accelerate the oxidation reaction rate, typically decreasing characteristic temperatures such as the critical and activation temperatures.

When investigating the thermogravimetric and differential thermal characteristics of coal using STA, experiments are typically conducted at heating rates of 10, 20, and 30 °C/min (at least three rates). Subsequently, the KAS and OFW methods are employed to calculate the activation energy, enabling further comparative analysis of its variation patterns.

Based on the influence patterns of heating rate and particle size on coal characteristic temperaturesspecifically, increasing heating rate elevates characteristic temperatures while decreasing particle size reduces themsynchronous thermal analysis experiments typically employ coal samples with particle sizes below 120 mesh. This particle size represents a significant reduction compared to actual goaf coal particle sizes. Therefore, when analyzing characteristic temperatures in this study, the lowest experimental heating rate was selected to minimize these effects. Characteristic Temperatures

Based on Table and Figure , the T 1 (critical temperature) of all pretreated coal samples showed varying degrees of reduction compared with R0, although the magnitude of reduction was relatively small. The most significant decrease was observed for sample T65-Q3, with T 1 reduced to 79.3 °C. Under identical pretreatment temperatures, the T 1 of samples without airflow (sealed heating) was the highest, whereas under thermal airflow conditions, T 1 decreased with decreasing airflow rate (except for T35-Q2). At 65 °C, although all samples exhibited reduced T 1 values, T 1 similarly decreased with decreasing airflow rate from sealed heating to the various airflow conditions.

Characteristic temperatures of coal samples under different pretreatment conditions.

The variation of T 2 (dry-cracking temperature) with temperature and airflow rate was relatively complex. At 35 °C, the T 2 of the sample without airflow (T35-Q0) increased slightly. Under thermal airflow conditions at this temperature, T 2 increased with decreasing airflow rate, with sample T35-Q3 (minimum airflow rate) exhibiting T 2 slightly higher than that of R0. At 65 °C, the T 2 of the sample without airflow also increased slightly, whereas T65-Q1 showed a significant decrease to 128.3 °C. With decreasing airflow rate, T 2 increased but remained significantly lower than those of R0 and the corresponding samples at 35 °C. Notably, the T 2 of R0 was 163.2 °C, while the T 2 values under sealed heating conditions at 35 and 65 °C were 165.6 and 165.5 °C, respectively, showing negligible difference. However, samples subjected to various airflow rates at 65 °C exhibited T 2 values substantially lower than those at 35 °C. These results indicate that temperature alone without airflow caused only a slight increase in T 2 without significant temperature-dependent variation; nevertheless, under thermal airflow conditions, T 2 varied markedly with temperature, with higher temperatures resulting in lower dry-cracking temperatures.

The T 3 (activation temperature) of R0 was 201.5 °C, while those of samples T35-Q0 and T35-Q1 were 201.3 and 201.9 °C, respectively, showing no significant variation. However, T35-Q2 exhibited a significant increase to 210.7 °C, whereas T35-Q3 (minimum airflow rate of 100 mL/min at 35 °C) showed a significant decrease to 182.2 °C. These results indicate that under 35 °C conditions, T 3 remained essentially unchanged for samples without airflow and at low airflow rates (100 mL/min), increased significantly at 150 mL/min, and decreased substantially when the airflow rate was reduced from 150 to 100 mL/min. At 65 °C, the T 3 of sample T65-Q0 (sealed heating) increased significantly to 210.5 °C; under thermal airflow conditions at this temperature, T 3 decreased significantly to 168.2, 184.4, and 169.9 °C at airflow rates of 200, 150, and 100 mL/min, respectively. Furthermore, under high-temperature airflow conditions, coal samples exhibited consistent T 3 variation patterns at both 35 and 65 °C, specifically, an initial increase followed by a decrease with decreasing airflow rate.

The T 4 (temperature of maximum oxygen chemisorption weight gain) of R0 was 233.6 °C. Under 35 °C pretreatment conditions, the T 4 values for samples without airflow and at airflow rates of 200 and 150 mL/min were 232.8, 233.1, and 232.3 °C, respectively, showing slight but insignificant decreases. However, under 35 °C and 100 mL/min airflow conditions, T 4 decreased to 227.3 °C, representing a relatively notable reduction. At 65 °C, the T 4 values under sealed heating and airflow rates of 200, 150, and 100 mL/min were 237.2, 232.4, 232.1, and 233.2 °C, respectively, exhibiting no significant variation.

The ignition temperature T 5 of R0 was 447.7 °C. Under 35 °C pretreatment without airflow, the T 5 of the coal sample was 436.4 °C, representing a significant decrease. At this temperature, the T 5 values under different airflow rates were 444.3, 435.8, and 439.6 °C for 200, 150, and 100 mL/min, respectively. The ignition temperature decreased slightly under high airflow rates, showed the maximum reduction at 150 mL/min, and also decreased to some extent under low airflow rates. At 65 °C, the T 5 values for R0 and samples under airflow rates of 200, 150, and 100 mL/min were 444.4, 436.6, 440.5, and 446.2 °C, respectively. Under this temperature, R0 and low airflow conditions showed essentially no significant variation; however, the ignition temperature decreased relatively substantially under high airflow rates at 65 °C.

The T 6 (temperature of maximum mass loss rate) was highest for R0, reaching 462.6 °C. Under 35 °C pretreatment conditions, T 6 decreased significantly for all samples, with T35-Q2 exhibiting the maximum reduction. Similarly, at 65 °C, all samples showed significant decreases, with T65-Q1 displaying the most substantial reduction.

Compared to R0, T 1 exhibited varying degrees of reduction across all samples. Under 35 °C pretreatment conditions, the sample without airflow (T35-Q0) showed the lowest reduction ratio of 0.6%. Following thermal airflow treatment at 35 °C, T 1 decreased to varying extents, with the sample subjected to the minimum airflow rate (T35-Q3) exhibiting the maximum reduction of 7.6%. At 65 °C, the reduction ratio of T 1 increased significantly with increasing airflow rate, with the lowest airflow rate consistently corresponding to the highest reduction ratio.

As shown in Table and Figure , T 2 increased significantly for samples without airflow at both 35 and 65 °C. At 35 °C, T 2 decreased by 2.6% at airflow rates of 200 and 150 mL/min, whereas it increased by 1% at 100 mL/min. At 65 °C, upon exposure to thermal airflow, the reduction ratio of T 2 decreased progressively with decreasing airflow rate. Compared with R0, T 3 decreased significantly by 9.6% only for T35-Q3 at 35 °C, while other samples showed essentially increased values. At 65 °C, T 3 increased significantly by 4.5% for T65-Q0 (sealed heating); under thermal airflow conditions at this temperature, T3 decreased significantly, with reduction ratios of 16.5%, 8.5%, and 15.7% for T65-Q1, T65-Q2, and T65-Q3, respectively. Following pretreatment under various temperatures and thermal airflow conditions, T4 decreased for all samples except T65-Q0, which increased by 1.5%. Pretreatment temperature (simulating ground temperature) represents the primary factor influencing the variation of coal characteristic temperatures, whereas airflow rate serves as a secondary factor. Following pretreatment at elevated temperature (65 °C), most key characteristic temperatures of coal samplesincluding the critical temperature (T 1), dry-cracking temperature (T 2), and activation temperature (T 3)decreased universally, indicating a significant increase in CSC hazard. Under identical pretreatment temperatures, decreasing airflow rate tended to induce further slight reductions in certain characteristic temperatures; however, this effect exhibited less pronounced regularity compared with the influence of temperature.

The critical temperature (T 1), representing the onset temperature of oxidation reactions, serves as a key indicator of CSC propensity: lower T 1 corresponds to higher CSC tendency. The T 1 values of coal samples pretreated at 65 °C were universally lower than those of counterparts pretreated at 35 °C under identical airflow conditions, demonstrating that elevated pretreatment temperature reduces the critical temperature and thereby increases CSC propensity. Under identical pretreatment temperatures, decreasing airflow rate tended to lower T 1, possibly attributable to the facilitated gradual oxidation and stabilization of active structures under low-flow conditions. Following pretreatment, particularly under the combined condition of high temperature (65 °C) and low airflow rate (100 mL/min), the critical temperature of coal decreased significantly, indicating enhanced susceptibility to oxidation initiation at lower temperatures and a marked increase in CSC hazard.

The dry-cracking temperature (T 2) and activation temperature (T 3) serve as indicators of pyrolysis acceleration and oxidation intensification, respectively. Lower T 2 and T 3 values indicate more rapid pyrolysis and oxidation reactions at lower temperatures, corresponding to higher CSC risk. T 2: The T 2 of R0 was 163.2 °C. The most pronounced variation occurred in samples pretreated at 65 °C: T65-Q1, T65-Q2, and T65-Q3 (128.3, 140.0, and 141.3 °C, respectively) were substantially lower than both R0 and the T35 series samples. This indicates that pretreatment at 65 °C substantially promoted the evolution of moisture and volatile matter from the coal matrix, initiating pyrolysis at markedly reduced temperatures. T 3: The T 3 of R0 was 201.5 °C. T65-Q1 and T65-Q3 (168.2 and 169.9 °C, respectively) exhibited significant reductions, whereas the T35 series samples showed inconsistent variations, with some slightly elevated (e.g., T35-Q2) and others marginally decreased. This further demonstrates that high-temperature (65 °C) pretreatment significantly reduces the energy threshold required for coal oxidation, facilitating the transition into rapid oxidation stages.

Pretreatment at 65 °C significantly reduced the critical temperature (T 1), dry-cracking temperature (T 2), and activation temperature (T 3) of coal, substantially increasing the CSC hazard. The influence of 35 °C pretreatment was relatively complex: certain characteristic temperatures decreased slightly, but the magnitude of reduction was considerably smaller than that observed at 65 °C. Although the CSC hazard exhibited an increasing trend, it was markedly less pronounced compared to high-temperature pretreatment. Under identical pretreatment temperatures, lower airflow rates generally resulted in further reductions in characteristic temperatures, particularly T 1. This phenomenon may be attributed to the facilitated formation and stabilization of active structures within the coal matrix under low-flow conditions, in contrast to high airflow rates that dissipate generated heat and evolved gases.

High-temperature pretreatment likely disrupted the macromolecular structure of coal, generating additional micropores and active functional groups that provided enhanced adsorption sites and reaction loci for oxygen, thereby reducing the energy barrier for oxidation initiation. Low-flow pretreatment potentially permitted the accumulation of oxidation intermediates (e.g., peroxides) within the coal matrix; these unstable intermediates subsequently promoted low-temperature oxidation reactions.

Coal subjected to high-temperature (65 °C) pretreatment, particularly when accompanied by low airflow rates (100–150 mL/min), exhibits significantly increased CSC hazard. This is manifested by a lower critical temperature (T 1) and substantially advanced dry-cracking temperature (T 2) and activation temperature (T 3), indicating accelerated pyrolysis and oxidation kinetics. Coal pretreated at low temperature (35 °C) demonstrates a marginally increasing trend in CSC risk; however, the magnitude of risk elevation remains considerably lower than that of high-temperature pretreated coal.

This trend suggests that the early stage of oxidation involves numerous unstable components that readily decompose. At this stage, low temperatures favor physical processes including moisture desorption and cleavage of weak bonds, while oxygen rapidly penetrates through pre-existing pores, collectively resulting in lower energy requirements. As temperature and conversion rate increase, chemical oxidation reactions become dominant, involving oxidation of aliphatic chains and decomposition of cross-linked structures that require cleavage of strong chemical bonds. Relatively stable components undergo decomposition, and despite the creation of pore space following moisture evolution, thermal expansion induces significant pore constriction. Oxygen must penetrate dense structural layers, collectively leading to elevated energy requirements and increased activation energy. Upon reaching high-temperature and high-conversion stages, the process enters a highly exothermic phase with accumulated free radicals and intermediates from preceding reactions. The oxidation mechanism transitions to diffusion-controlled oxidation, and with surface reactions largely complete, thermal cracking generates additional pore channels, significantly enhancing oxygen diffusion and reducing diffusion resistance. This stage exhibits relatively lower energy requirements, enabling spontaneous reaction or combustion of most stable substances. From the perspective of competitive reaction pathway transitions, the low-temperature stage likely involves parallel endothermic desorption and exothermic oxidation reactions. As temperature increases, endothermic pathways become suppressed while exothermic oxidation gradually dominates, contributing to reduced activation energy at medium-to-high temperatures. Additionally, considering the presence of various metals in coal, alkaline metal salts may catalyze oxidation reactions at medium temperatures (200–400 °C). At high temperatures (∼600 °C), deactivation of these catalytic species occurs. During high-temperature combustion (400–800 °C), Fe2O3 and related species may exhibit catalytic effects through redox cycling: Fe2O3 + CO → FeO + CO2. The Fe3+ ↔ Fe2+ valence transition facilitates electron transfer and accelerates oxygen activation. Partial reduction at high temperatures generates oxygen vacancies, enhancing oxygen adsorption capacity.

The activation energy calculation results reveal that at the low-temperature oxidation stage (α = 0–0.1), samples without airflow (R0, T35-Q0, and T65-Q0) exhibited significantly lower activation energies than those subjected to thermal airflow. This indicates that R0 and coal subjected to high temperature alone possess higher oxidation activity during the initial low-temperature stage, whereas thermal airflow treatment substantially increases activation energy and reduces oxidation activity. At, the activation energies followed the descending order: T35-Q1 > T65-Q1 > T65-Q2 > T35-Q2 > T65-Q3 > T35-Q3 > R0 > T35-Q0 > T65-Q0. Overall, these results demonstrate that coal subjected to high-temperature airflow exhibits significantly higher activation energy at the low-temperature oxidation stage compared to samples subjected to high temperature without airflow. Furthermore, under high-temperature airflow conditions, activation energy increased with increasing airflow rate; conversely, lower airflow rates resulted in reduced activation energy and enhanced oxidation activity. Specifically, at the high airflow rate of 200 mL/min, samples subjected to 35 °C airflow exhibited higher activation energy than those at 65 °C (T35-Q1 > T65-Q1), indicating that at high airflow rates, higher temperatures correlate with lower activation energy and enhanced coal activity. Conversely, at airflow rates of 150 and 100 mL/min, samples under 65 °C airflow exhibited higher activation energy than those at 35 °C, suggesting that under medium-to-low airflow conditions, the effect of temperature on activation energy diverges from the pattern observed at high airflow rates. Synthesizing these observations, lower activation energy (and consequently higher oxidation activity) occurs under two distinct scenarios: (1) high temperature (65 °C) combined with high airflow rates and (2) low temperature (35 °C) combined with low airflow rates. This demonstrates that the interaction between pretreatment temperature and airflow rate critically determines the energy barrier for coal oxidation.

Overall, across both 35 and 65 °C pretreatment conditions, T65-Q1 (high temperature + high airflow rate) and T35-Q3 (low temperature + low airflow rate) exhibited the lowest activation energies and consequently the highest oxidation activity.

In contrast, all thermally airflow-treated samples exhibited significant increases in activation energy, ranging from 32% to 56%, suggesting that thermal airflow treatment may exert an inhibitory effect on coal oxidation during the low-temperature stage. These findings regarding activation energy corroborate the conclusions drawn from the characteristic temperature analysis. Following pretreatment at 35 and 65 °C, the activation energy of coal decreased significantly during critical reaction stages (particularly at medium to high conversion rates), indicating a general increase in CSC hazard. Specifically, pretreatment involving thermal airflow, especially at 35 °C, exhibited the most pronounced “activation” effect, substantially lowering the activation energy barrier for oxidation reactions. In contrast, pretreatment at 65 °C, particularly under sealed conditions without airflow, exhibited greater complexity.

To most effectively elucidate the variation trends, three critical stagesthe initial stage (α = 0.2), the middle stage (α = 0.4), and the late stage (α = 0.6)were selected for comparative analysis. At the initial stage (α = 0.2), the activation energies of almost all pretreated coal samples were higher than or equal to that of R0. This suggests that pretreatment, particularly under sealed conditions (without airflow) or at high temperature without airflow, imposes a slightly higher energy barrier at the onset of oxidation. This phenomenon may be attributed to the removal of highly reactive yet unstable functional groups during pretreatment, or to slight passivation of the coal surface, both of which necessitate greater energy input to initiate the reaction. The middle stage (α = 0.4) represents the phase where the “activation” effect of thermal airflow is most pronounced. At 35 °C, the activation energy decreased continuously as the airflow rate decreased from Q1 to Q3. Low-flow environments are most conducive to the generation of abundant active sites within the coal matrix, significantly lowering subsequent oxidation barriers. Notably, the effect of low-flow treatment at 35 °C was even more pronounced than that observed at 65 °C. However, at 65 °C without airflow (T65-Q0), the activation energy was already reduced, whereas at 35 °C without airflow (T35-Q0), no such reduction was observed. At the late stage (α = 0.6), once oxidation progresses to the middle-to-late stages, all pretreated coal samples exhibit significantly higher susceptibility to intense oxidation compared to R0.

Discussion

Coal samples subjected to thermal airflow exhibited a monotonically decreasing trend in activation energy with reaction progress, indicating that once initiated, the reaction becomes progressively easier to proceed. The inherent self-accelerating nature of the system is pronounced, facilitating rapid heat accumulation and ultimately resulting in CSC. In contrast, samples treated without airflow displayed less smooth variation curves of activation energy compared to airflow-treated samples, suggesting a more complex reaction mechanism; however, the overall trend indicated that activation energy in the later stages remained lower than that of R0.1.Critical role of airflow rate: The introduction of thermal airflow represents a critical factor significantly reducing the coal activation energy and increasing the CSC hazard. Comparing samples without an airflow with their counterparts under identical temperature conditions with an airflow, the latter consistently exhibit lower activation energies during the middle and late reaction stages. Low airflow rates (100 mL/min) demonstrate superior “activation” effects compared with high airflow rates. At identical temperatures, the Q3 samples consistently exhibit the lowest activation energies within their respective series. This is attributed to the fact that low airflow rates provide sufficient oxygen for oxidation while avoiding the excessive removal of reaction heat and reactive intermediates, thereby creating favorable conditions for heat accumulation and chain reactions.2.Complex influence of pretreatment temperature: pretreatment at 65 °C effectively reduces activation energy during the middle and late reaction stages even under sealed conditions without airflow (T65-Q0). However, upon the introduction of thermal airflow, the reduction in activation energy is less pronounced than that observed for samples subjected to 35 °C pretreatment combined with airflow. Conversely, under 35 °C without airflow, the “activation” effect is limited; however, under thermal airflow conditions, the “activation” effect becomes extremely pronounced, yielding coal samples (T35-Q3) with the highest CSC hazard.

Residual coal in goaf areas subjected to prolonged exposure to conditions analogous to “low temperature (35 °C) with restricted ventilation (low airflow rate)” undergoes the most detrimental chemical transformations, characterized by substantial reductions in activation energy, thereby becoming extremely susceptible to CSC. While high-temperature (65 °C) sealed environments present obvious hazards, the combination of “low temperature + weak airflow” necessitates heightened vigilance due to its greater insidiousness.

Conclusions

1.Variation patterns of characteristic temperatures: following pretreatment at elevated temperature (65 °C), coal samples exhibited universal reductions in critical characteristic temperatures, including the critical temperature (T 1), dry-cracking temperature (T 2), and activation temperature (T 3), indicating a substantial increase in CSC hazard. Under identical pretreatment conditions, decreasing airflow rates tended to induce further reductions in certain characteristic temperatures; however, this effect exhibited less pronounced regularity compared to the temperature influence. Pretreatment temperature (simulating ground temperature) represents the primary factor governing the variation of coal characteristic temperatures, whereas airflow rate serves as a secondary factor.2.Activation energy variation patterns and kinetic mechanism: Thermal airflow erosion fundamentally altered the intrinsic kinetic behavior of coal oxidation. Samples subjected to thermal airflow pretreatment exhibited a monotonically decreasing trend in activation energy with conversion rate, indicating that once initiated, the reaction becomes progressively easier to proceed; the inherent self-accelerating nature of the system is pronounced. In contrast, samples treated without airflow displayed less smooth variation curves, showing an initial increase, followed by a subsequent decrease, suggesting a more complex reaction mechanism; however, the overall trend indicated that activation energy in the later stages remained lower than that of R0. Notably, the most severe reduction in the activation energy barrier occurred under the condition of relatively lower temperature (35 °C) with airflow, whereas pretreatment at 65 °C without airflow yielded comparatively greater complexity in activation energy evolution. These results demonstrate that the airflow rate represents the critical factor in controlling activation energy variation, and its coupling with thermal preconditioning governs the overall CSC propensity of residual coal.3.Engineering implications: these findings suggest that CSC risk assessment in deep mining should not focus exclusively on high-temperature goaf zones. Areas with moderately elevated temperature yet poor ventilation also warrant heightened attention, as sustained low-rate oxidation under such conditions can substantially lower the activation energy barrier and prime residual coal for accelerated self-heating. Accordingly, ensuring adequate airflow throughout goaf areasincluding those with only moderately elevated temperaturesis essential for effective CSC prevention. The characteristic temperature and activation energy data obtained in this study can provide a reference for setting monitoring thresholds and early warning criteria in underground CSC monitoring systems.
