S.B. and E.V. are partially supported by the Indam-GNAMPA project Esistenza, regolarità e studio delle proprietà qualitative per problemi nonlineari .
Stefano Biagi, Fabio Punzo, Eugenio Vecchi

Abstract
. We investigate finite-time blow-up for nonnegative solutions to the Cauchy problem associated with semilinear parabolic equations driven by a mixed local–nonlocal operator. The reaction term is assumed to satisfy suitable structural hypotheses, the prototype being f ⁡ ( u ) = u p f(u)=u^{p} with p > 1 p>1 . By adapting the Kaplan method to the present framework, we prove that solutions blow up in finite time whenever the initial datum is sufficiently large. In the prototype case f ⁡ ( u ) = u p f(u)=u^{p} , this conclusion holds for every p > 1 p>1 . As a particular case of our operator, we also include the fractional Laplacian; to the best of our knowledge, this type of result is new even in that special case.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we continue the study initiated in
[
8
]
,
concerning the finite-time blow-up of nonnegative solutions to the
following Cauchy problem:

(
\mathrm{CP}_{\mathcal{L}}
)

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u+\mathcal{L}u=f(u)&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\times(0,+\infty)$}\\
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$},\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{a}\geq 0
and
\mathbf{b}>0
are fixed parameters,
p\in(1,+\infty)
,

\mathcal{L}
is the (weighted) mixed local-nonlocal operator defined as

\mathcal{L}=-\mathbf{a}\Delta+\mathbf{b}(-\Delta)^{s}.

Furthermore, as for the
initial datum
u_{0}
, we always assume that it is continuous, bounded, and non-negative on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Concerning the
reaction term
f
,
we make the following structural assumption:

(H)_{f}

f:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is a locally Lipschitz
and
convex function such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle\text{$f(0)=0$ and $f(z)>0$ for all $z>0$};

\displaystyle\mathrm{ii)}\,\,

\displaystyle\exists\lim_{z\to+\infty}\frac{f(z)}{z}=+\infty

\displaystyle\mathrm{iii)}\,\,

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{f(z)}\,dz<+\infty

Clearly, assumption
(H)_{f}
is satisfied when
f(u)=u^{p}
with

p>1
.

The study of finite-time blow-up and global existence of solutions to
the problem (

CP
L

) with
\mathbf{a}=1
and
\mathbf{b}=0
has been widely developed in the literature, specially whenever
f(u)=u^{p},p>1
.
We refer to
[
8
]
for a detailed account of the various available
results. Here, we restrict ourselves to recalling the early results
in
[
20
,
21
,
25
]
, as well as the survey papers

[
1
,
14
,
16
,
29
,
31
,
35
]
, which also contain analogous
results for nonlinear problems. On the other hand, the case
\mathbf{a}=0
and
\mathbf{b}=1
was treated
in
[
37
,
28
]
. Moreover, for
p>1+\frac{2s}{N}
, global-in-time solutions were
investigated in
[
23
]
(see also
[
22
]
), where the
asymptotic behavior of solutions as
t\to+\infty
was also studied.

In recent years, mixed operators of the form
\mathcal{L}
have
emerged as a significant object of study in the analysis of partial
differential equations. A growing literature has been devoted to the
qualitative properties of solutions to equations driven by such
operators, especially in the elliptic setting, but also in the
parabolic one; see, for instance,
[
3
]
-
[
13
]
.

Part of this interest stems from the probabilistic interpretation of
these operators. Indeed, they naturally arise in connection with
stochastic dynamics combining different mechanisms, such as standard
diffusion and jump processes of Lévy type. In addition, mixed
operators have proved useful in the modeling of several phenomena from
the applied sciences. A notable example is provided by models for
optimal animal foraging strategies; see, e.g.,
[
17
,
18
]
and the
references therein.

As for problem (

CP
L

) in the general case of a mixed local–nonlocal
operator with
f(u)=u^{p}
, it was shown in
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
(see also
[
15
,
26
]
) that,
if
p\leq 1+\frac{2s}{N}
, then every solution corresponding to any
nontrivial initial datum
u_{0}\not\equiv 0
blows up in finite time.
On the other hand, if
p>1+\frac{2s}{N}
and
u_{0}
is sufficiently
small, then there exists a global-in-time solution.

We now focus on the case in which
p>1
is arbitrary, while the
initial datum
u_{0}
is sufficiently large. It is well known that, in
the case
\mathbf{a}=1
and
\mathbf{b}=0
(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
16
]
), if
u_{0}
is
big enough, then the corresponding solution blows up in finite for every
p>1
.

Some related results in this direction were obtained
in
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
for problem (

CP
L

) posed in a bounded domain,
by different methods relying heavily on the assumption
that
\Omega
is bounded.

In this paper, we show that, for problem
(

CP
L

), finite-time blow-up occurs provided
that the initial datum is sufficiently large. Note that the assumption on the initial datum will be stated explicitly. In particular, when

f(u)=u^{p}
, our result applies to every
p>1
. We emphasize that, to the best of our knowledge, the result
established here is new even in the special case
\mathbf{a}=0
, namely for
the fractional operator.

To obtain our result, we adapt to the present setting the
so-called Kaplan method (see
[
24
]
), which was used, for instance,
in
[
2
,
16
]
in the case
\mathbf{a}=1
,
\mathbf{b}=0
, and in
[
33
]
for the
semilinear heat equation posed on an infinite combinatorial graph.

We first derive a general condition ensuring finite-time blow-up of
the solution whenever
u_{0}
is sufficiently large; see
Theorem
3.4
. The proof of this theorem
requires particular care because of the nonlocal part of the
operator. The result relies on the existence of a special function
\kappa
,
which we call a
Kaplan function
. Besides satisfying suitable
regularity and integrability properties,
\kappa
is a positive
subsolution of equation

-\mathcal{L}\kappa+\lambda\kappa=0\qquad\text{pointwise in }\mathbb{R}^{N},

for some
\lambda>0
.

Given a solution
u(t)
of problem (

CP
L

), we define the function

\Phi\colon[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
by

\Phi(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\kappa u(t)\,dx.

We then show that

\Phi^{\prime}(t)+\lambda\Phi(t)\geq f(\Phi(t))\qquad\text{for all }t>0.

If the initial datum
u_{0}
is sufficiently large, then this property
is inherited by
\Phi(0)
. A suitable analysis of the above ordinary
differential inequality with initial condition
\Phi(0)
then yields finite-time blow-up.

A further crucial step is the explicit construction of the
subsolution
\kappa
(see Theorem
3.7
), which is carried out ad hoc by treating the
fractional part of the operator with special care, since explicit
computations are less straightforward in the nonlocal setting. By combining the general criterion with the construction of the
function
\kappa
, we deduce our main result, namely
Theorem
3.9
. In this connection, let us point out that, within our framework

\mathbf{a}\geq 0
and
\mathbf{b}>0
, the Kaplan function is of the form

\kappa(x):=C\varepsilon^{\frac{N}{2}}\bigl(1+\varepsilon|x|^{2}\bigr)^{-\beta},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

for some constants
C>0
,
\varepsilon>0
and
\beta>0
.

By contrast, in the case
\mathbf{a}=1
and
\mathbf{b}=0
,
which does not fall within the scope of our analysis, it is well known
(see
[
2
,
16
]
) that the Kaplan function is given by

\kappa(x):=Ck^{\frac{N}{2}}e^{-k|x|^{2}},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

for some constants
C>0
and
k>0
.

This difference in the form of
\kappa
is due to the presence or
absence of the fractional part in the operator
\mathcal{L}
.

In addition, using Theorem
3.9
, in which
u_{0}
is assumed to be
sufficiently large and
p>1
, we deduce a blow-up result under the
assumptions that
u_{0}
is arbitrary, as long as it is nontrivial, and
that
f(u)=u^{p}
with
p<1+\frac{2s}{N}
. In this way, we recover a result that, as
mentioned above, had already been established in
[
8
]
.

2.2.
Pointwise theory for the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

)

Having fixed the notation, we briefly discuss the
classical theory
for the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

) associated with
\mathcal{L}
.

1) The fractional Laplacian.

Let
s\in(0,1)
be fixed and let
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. The
fractional Laplacian
of order
s
of
u
at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is defined by

(2.4)

\begin{split}(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)&=C_{N,s}\,\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\\
&=C_{N,s}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{\{|x-y|\geq\varepsilon\}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dy,\end{split}

whenever the limit exists and is finite.

Here
C_{N,s}>0
is a normalization constant, explicitly given by

C_{N,s}=\frac{2^{2s}\Gamma((N+2s)/2)}{\pi^{N/2}|\Gamma(-s)|}.

In order for
(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)
to be well defined, suitable growth conditions on
u
are required, both as
y\to\infty
and as
y\to x
. In this direction we recall the following result (see
[
27
,
36
]
).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\varnothing\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open set and let
\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be defined as in (
2.1
).

Then, the following assertions hold.

i)

If
0<s<1/2
and
u\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2s+\gamma}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1-2s)
, then

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=C_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\qquad\forall\,\,x\in\Omega.

ii)

If
1/2<s<1
and
u\in C^{1,2s-1+\gamma}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\gamma\in(0,2-2s)
, then

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=-\frac{C_{N,s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u(x+z)+u(x-z)-2u(x)}{|z|^{N+2s}}\,dz\qquad\forall\,\,x\in\Omega.

Moreover, in both cases
(-\Delta)^{s}u\in C(\Omega)
.

Remark 2.2
.

Observe that the weight
\kappa(x)=(1+|x|^{N+2s})^{-1}
belongs to
L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
1\leq r\leq+\infty
. Consequently,

(2.5)

L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\quad\forall\,\,1\leq r\leq+\infty.

This fact will be used repeatedly in the sequel.

In the special case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
u\in\mathcal{S}
(the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions), an equivalent definition of
(-\Delta)^{s}u
can be given via the Fourier transform
\mathfrak{F}
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Then

(2.6)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(x)=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\big(|\xi|^{2s}\mathfrak{F}u\big)(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Remark 2.4
.

Since the Fourier transform extends to an isometry on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, identity (
2.6
) allows one to define the fractional Laplacian for less regular functions.

More precisely, one can set

(-\Delta)^{s}u=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\big(|\xi|^{2s}\mathfrak{F}u\big)\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

for every
u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that
|\xi|^{2s}\mathfrak{F}u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Due to its relevance in the sequel,
we state
in the next theorem the well-known fractional version of the
Leibniz formula
and of the
integration-by-parts formula

for the Laplacian.

Theorem 2.5
.

The following assertions hold.

(1)

Assume that
f,g\in\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfy
the following properties:

\displaystyle\mathrm{a)}\,\,

f,g\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2s+\gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for some
\gamma\in(0,1-2s)

0<s<1/2
,
or

\displaystyle\text{$f,g\in C^{1,2s+\gamma-1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$
for some
$\gamma\in(0,2-2s)$ if $1/2<s<1$};

\displaystyle\mathrm{b)}\,\,

\displaystyle fg\in\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N});

Then, the following
Leibniz formula
holds:

(2.7)

(-\Delta)^{s}[fg](x)=f(x)(-\Delta)^{s}g(x)+(-\Delta)^{s}f(x)g(x)-\mathcal{B}(f,g)(x),

where the bilinear form
\mathcal{B}
is defined as follows:

(2.8)

\mathcal{B}(f,g)(x):=C_{N,s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\dfrac{(f(x)-f(y))(g(x)-g(y))}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,dy\quad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

(2)

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set,
and let

satisfy the following properties:

\displaystyle\mathrm{a)}\,\,

u,v\in C_{\mathrm{loc}}^{2s+\gamma}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\text{$u,v\in C^{1,2s+\gamma-1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ for some

\displaystyle\mathrm{b)}\,\,

\displaystyle\text{$u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$

\displaystyle\mathrm{c)}\,\,

\displaystyle\text{$v\equiv 0$ on $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\Omega$ and $(-\Delta)^{s}v\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$}.

Then, the following formula holds:

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega}v(-\Delta)^{s}u\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u(-\Delta)^{s}v\,dx

In the proof of our main theorem, the above formulas (

2) Classical solutions to problem (

CP
L

).

Having reviewed the basic properties of the fractional Laplacian, we can now introduce the notion of
classical solution
to the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

). From now on, we tacitly assume that

\mathbf{a}\geq 0,\qquad\mathbf{b}>0,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\text{$f$ satisfies assumption $(H)_{f}$}.

We stress that the choice
\mathbf{a}=0
,
which corresponds to the purely nonlocal operator

\mathcal{L}=\mathbf{b}(-\Delta)^{s},

is fully admissible; therefore, all the results that follow remain valid in this case as well. However, since the main goal of this paper is to investigate the interplay between the local and the nonlocal parts of the operator, we formulate the theory having primarily in mind the genuinely mixed case
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}>0
. In particular, the definition of classical solution to (

CP
L

) is tailored to this setting (see Remark
2.8
-(3)).

Definition 2.7
.

Let
u_{0}\in C_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. A function

u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}

(for some
0<T\leq+\infty
) is called a
classical solution
to (

CP
L

) if:

(1)

u\in C_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T))\cap C^{2,1}_{x,t}(S_{T})
;

(2)

u\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,\tau])
for every
0<\tau<T
;

(3)

u
satisfies (

CP
L

) pointwise, namely

\displaystyle\mathrm{a)}\,\,

\displaystyle u_{t}(x,t)+\mathcal{L}[u(\cdot,t)](x)=f(u(x,t))\quad\forall(x,t)\in S_{T},

\displaystyle\mathrm{b)}\,\,

\displaystyle u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

If
T=+\infty
, we say that
u
is a
global solution
(to (

CP
L

)).

Remark 2.8
.

We make a few remarks concerning Definition
2.7
.

(1)

First, we point out that Definition
2.7
is
well posed
in the following sense: if

u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}

(with
0<T\leq+\infty
) satisfies properties (1)–(2), then the quantity

\mathcal{L}[u(\cdot,t)](x)

is well defined for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and every
t\in(0,T)
.

Indeed, fixing
t\in(0,T)
, properties (1)–(2) yield

0\leq u(x,t)\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,t])}\qquad\forall\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

so that
u(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
(see (
2.5
)) and

(2.10)

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,t])}<+\infty.

As a consequence, since we also have
u(\cdot,t)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
by property (1), from Proposition
2.1
we derive that
\mathcal{L}[u(\cdot,t)]
is well defined pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and

x\mapsto\mathcal{L}[u(\cdot,t)](x)\in C(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

(2)

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
be a
global solution
to the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

). Then, by (
2.10
) (and the arbitrariness of
t\in(0,+\infty)
), we have

u(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\quad\text{for every }t>0.

(3)

As already discussed, the above definition is
modeled on
the genuinely mixed case
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}>0
, since
C^{2}
-regularity in the space variable is not needed when
\mathbf{a}=0
. In that case, taking Proposition
2.1
into account, it is enough to require

•

u(\cdot,t)\in C^{2s+\gamma}_{\mathbf{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some
\gamma\in(0,1-2s)
if
0<s<1/2
;

•

u(\cdot,t)\in C^{1,2s-1+\gamma}_{\mathbf{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

for some
\gamma\in(0,2-2s)
if
1/2<s<1
.

3.
Kaplan functions for
\mathcal{L}
and the main results

Thanks to Definition
2.7
, we are now in a position to state and prove our main non-existence results for global solutions to the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

).
Before doing so, and in order to improve the readability of the statement, we introduce the following auxiliary
ad hoc
definition.
Throughout what follows, given
\beta>0
, we denote by
\Psi_{\beta}
the weight function

\Psi_{\beta}(x)=\frac{1}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}.

Furthermore, for every
\lambda>0
we define

(3.1)

\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda)=\inf\Big\{z>0:\,\frac{f(z)}{z}>\lambda\Big\}\in[0,+\infty).

We note that, for every
\lambda>0
, assumption
(H)_{f}
-(ii) ensures that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\Big\{z>0:\,\frac{f(z)}{z}>\lambda\Big\}\neq\varnothing,

and therefore
\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda)
is well defined. Moreover, in the prototypical case
f(z)=z^{p}
(with
p>1
), it is straightforward to check that

(3.2)

\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda)=\lambda^{1/(p-1)}.

Definition 3.1

(Kaplan function for
\mathcal{L}
)
.

We say that a function
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
Kaplan function
for the operator
\mathcal{L}
if it satisfies the following properties:

(1)

\kappa\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\kappa>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
;

(2)

\kappa,\,|\nabla\kappa|\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\|\kappa\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=1
;

(3)

there exists
\lambda>0
such that

-\mathcal{L}\kappa+\lambda\kappa\geq 0\quad\text{pointwise in }\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Remark 3.2
.

Arguing as in Remark
2.8
, we observe that Definition
3.1
is well posed in the following sense: if
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies properties (1)–(2), then
\mathcal{L}\kappa
can be computed pointwise in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
This follows directly from Proposition
2.1
, taking into account that

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In what follows, we will need to exhibit a suitable cut-off function built upon a smooth function
\chi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,1]
satisfying the following properties:

i)

\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}([0,+\infty)
;

ii)

\chi\equiv 1
in
[0,1]
and
\chi\equiv 0
in
[2,+\infty)
;

iii)

there exist two positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

|\chi^{\prime}(t)|\leq C_{1}\,\mathbf{1}_{(1,2)}(t),\quad|\chi^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C_{2}\,\mathbf{1}_{(1,2)}(t)\quad\textrm{for all }t\in[0,+\infty).

Once
\chi
is as above, for any
R>0
we define the function
\chi_{R}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,1]
as

(3.3)

\chi_{R}(x):=\chi\left(\dfrac{|x|}{R}\right).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\chi_{R}
be defined as in (
3.3
). Then:

a)

\chi_{R}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, with
\chi_{R}\equiv 1
in
\overline{B}_{R}
and
\chi_{R}\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{2R}
;

b)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\lim_{R\to+\infty}\chi_{R}(x)=1
;

c)

there exists a positive constant
C_{3}>0
such that

(3.4)

|\nabla\chi_{R}(x)|\leq\dfrac{C_{3}}{R}\,\mathbf{1}_{B_{2R}\setminus\overline{B}_{R}}(x)\quad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N};

d)

there exists a positive constant
C_{4}>0
such that

(3.5)

|\Delta\chi_{R}(x)|\leq\dfrac{C_{4}}{R^{2}}\,\mathbf{1}_{B_{2R}\setminus\overline{B}_{R}}(x)\quad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N};

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that there exists a Kaplan function
\kappa
for
\mathcal{L}
, which additionally satisfies the following property: there exists a constant
c>0
such that

(3.6)

0\leq\kappa+|\nabla\kappa|\leq c\,\Psi_{\beta}\quad\text{pointwise in }\mathbb{R}^{N};

If
u_{0}\in C_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
satisfies

(3.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\kappa(x)u_{0}(x)\,dx>\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda)

(
where
\lambda>0
is as in Definition
3.1
-(3), and
\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda)
is as in (
3.1
)
)
, the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

) with initial datum
u_{0}
does
not
admit global classical solutions which satisfy

(3.8)

\kappa u_{t}(\cdot,t)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\quad\forall\,\,t\in(0,T).

Proof.

We will argue by contradiction therefore assuming that

u
is a global solution to (

CP
L

).
Let us now fix
t>0
and
R>0
. Multiplying first equation (

CP
L

) by
\chi_{R}\kappa
and then integrating over the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, gives

(3.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa u_{t}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa\,(-\mathcal{L}u)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa f(u)\,dx

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa\,(\Delta u)\,dx-\mathbf{b}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa\,(-\Delta)^{s}u\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa f(u)\,dx

\displaystyle=:\mathbf{a}\,\mathcal{I}_{1}-\mathbf{b}\,\mathcal{I}_{2}+\mathcal{I}_{3}.

Let us start with
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Recalling Lemma
3.3
-a), there exists a compact set
D
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\chi_{R})\subseteq\overline{B}_{2R}\subset D
and with the additional properties that
\chi_{R}\equiv 0
on
\partial D
and in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus D
. Therefore, integrating by parts we get

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\chi_{R}\kappa\,(\Delta u)\,dx=-\int_{D}\langle\nabla(\chi_{R}k),\nabla u\rangle\,dx=

\displaystyle=-\int_{D}\chi_{R}\langle\nabla k,\nabla u\rangle\,dx-\int_{D}k\langle\nabla\chi_{R},\nabla u\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=:-\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{1}-\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1}.

Now, focusing on
\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{1}
, we notice that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\langle\nabla k,\nabla(\chi_{R}u)\rangle\,dx-\int_{D}u\langle\nabla k,\nabla\chi_{R}\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{D}\chi_{R}u\Delta k\,dx--\int_{D}u\langle\nabla k,\nabla\chi_{R}\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}u\Delta k\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\langle\nabla k,\nabla\chi_{R}\rangle\,dx,

while, as long as
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1}
is concerned

(3.12)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\langle\nabla\chi_{R},\nabla(ku)\rangle\,dx-\int_{D}u\langle\nabla\chi_{R},\nabla k\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{D}ku\nabla\chi_{R}\,dx-\int_{D}u\langle\nabla\chi_{R},\nabla k\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku\nabla\chi_{R}\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\langle\nabla\chi_{R},\nabla k\rangle\,dx.

Combining (
3.10
), (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we finally get that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}u\Delta k\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku\Delta\chi_{R}\,dx+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\langle\nabla k,\nabla\chi_{R}\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{1}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}_{1}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{1}.

Let us now deal with the nonlocal part provided by
\mathcal{I}_{2}
. By exploiting (
2.9
) and (
2.7
) (applied here with
u=u(\cdot,t)
and
v=fg=\chi_{R}\kappa
, see Remark

2.6
), we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u(-\Delta)^{s}(\chi_{R}k)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\chi_{R}(-\Delta)^{s}k\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uk(-\Delta)^{s}\chi_{R}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\mathcal{B}(\chi_{R},k)\,dx

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime}_{2}+\mathcal{I}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{3}.

Now, by
[
6
, Lemma 3.2]
, it follows that

(3.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku\Delta\chi_{R}\,dx+2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\langle\nabla k,\nabla\chi_{R}\rangle\,dx\to 0\quad\textrm{ as }R\to+\infty,

and

(3.14)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uk(-\Delta)^{s}\chi_{R}\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}u\mathcal{B}(\chi_{R},k)\,dx\to 0\quad\textrm{as }R\to+\infty.

Moreover, recalling Lemma
3.3
-b), and using the Dominated Conveergence Theorem, it follows that

(3.15)

\mathcal{I}_{3}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}kf(u)\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}kf(u)\,dx\quad\textrm{ as }R\to+\infty.

We are left with a couple of terms, namely
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{1}
and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}
. Here, we exploit first that
k
is a Kaplan function for
\mathcal{L}
and hence

(3.16)

\mathbf{a}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{1}-\mathbf{b}\mathcal{I}^{\prime}_{2}\geq-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}uk\,dx.

Arguing as for the integral with
f(u)
, it follows that

(3.17)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}uk\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}uk\,dx\quad\textrm{ as }R\to+\infty,

and that

(3.18)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\chi_{R}\kappa u_{t}\,dx\to\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku_{t}\,dx\quad\textrm{ as }R\to+\infty.

Starting from (
3.9
) and using (
3.16
),
we see that, thanks to (
3.18
), (
3.13
), (
3.14
), (
3.15
) and (
3.17
), we can pass to the limit as
R\to+\infty
finding

(3.19)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku_{t}\,dx\geq-\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}kf(u)\,dx\quad\textrm{for all }t>0.

Define now the function
\Phi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)

as

(3.20)

\Phi(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku(t)\,dx.

Recalling that
u(\cdot,0)=u_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and (
3.7
), it follows that

(3.21)

\Phi(0)>\mathbf{s}_{f}(\lambda).

Due to the regularity of both
k
and
u
, we have that
\Phi\in C^{1}((0,+\infty))
and moreover

(3.22)

\Phi^{\prime}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku_{t}\,dx\quad\textrm{ for all }t>0.

Since by Jensen’s inequality

(3.23)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}kf(u)\,dx\geq f\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}ku\,dx\right),

we can write (
3.19
) in terms of
\Phi
(here using (
3.22
) as well) as a first order differential inequality of the form

(3.24)

\Phi^{\prime}(t)+\lambda\Phi(t)\geq f(\Phi(t))\quad\textrm{ for all }t>0,

whose associated initial condition is given by (
3.21
). By a direct qualitative analysis of (
3.24
) and (
3.21
) (see e.g.
[
33
, Lemma 5.3]
), we realize that
\Phi
has to blow up in finite time therefore contradicting our initial assumption on the solution
u
. This closes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.5

(On assumption (
3.6
))
.

As will become clear in the proof of Theorem
3.4
, assumption (
3.6
) is purely technical, and is
related to the nonlocal nature of
\mathcal{L}
: indeed, it allows us to control certain
tail terms
arising from the nonlocal part of
\mathcal{L}
when applied to compactly supported functions.

We also observe that, if
\beta>{N}/{2}
, then
\Psi_{\beta}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and hence (
3.6
) ensures that

\kappa,\,|\nabla\kappa|\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In particular, for such values of
\beta
, the integrability condition in Definition
3.1
-(2) is automatically satisfied.

Remark 3.6

(On assumption (
3.8
))
.

As observed in Remark
3.5
, if

\beta>\frac{N}{2}
, then
\kappa\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore,
if
u_{t}(t)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
t\in(0,T)
, then
(
3.8
) is satisfied.

In this connection, let us note that, for purely local operators,
the above property of
u_{t}(t)
is satisfied by the solutions
constructed via semigroup theory
(see, e.g.,
[
30
, Propositions 7.1.10 and 7.3.1]

and
[
34
, Remark1]
). It is not clear whether an analogous
result remains valid in the present setting, and this issue lies
beyond the scope of the present paper.

The above Theorem
3.4
provides an abstract criterion for the non-existence of global solutions to the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

).
To make this result effective, it is necessary to construct an explicit Kaplan function associated with the operator
\mathcal{L}
.

This is the goal of the next theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\beta>N/2
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
be fixed. Then, the function

\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\varepsilon^{N/2}}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon|x|^{2})^{\beta}}\qquad\big(\text{with $\mathbf{c}_{\beta}=\|\Psi_{\beta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}<+\infty$}\big)

is a Kaplan function for
\mathcal{L}
, further satisfying (
3.6
) in Theorem
3.4
.

More precisely, there exists
\lambda_{0}>0
, independent of
\varepsilon
, such that

(3.25)

\begin{gathered}\text{$-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\kappa_{\varepsilon}\geq 0$ pointwise in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}\\
\text{for every $\lambda\geq\varepsilon^{1/s}\lambda_{0}$}\end{gathered}

Proof.

We first prove the theorem when
\varepsilon=1
. To begin with, we observe that

\kappa_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}=\mathbf{c}_{\beta}^{-1}\Psi_{\beta}(x)

is smooth and strictly positive in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(hence,
property (1) in Definition
3.1
is fulfilled); moreover, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle|\nabla\kappa_{1}|(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\cdot\frac{2\beta|x|}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta+1}}\leq\frac{2\beta}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\cdot\frac{1}{(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}=\frac{2\beta}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\Psi_{\beta}(x),

and thus
\kappa_{1}
satisfies assumption (
3.6
) (with

c=(2\beta+1)/\mathbf{c}_{\beta}
). In particular, since
\beta>N/2
, and by
the definition of
\mathbf{c}_{\beta}
, we deduce that
\kappa_{1}
satisfies also
property (2)
in Definition
3.1
.

Hence, it remains to verify
property (3).
To this end, we proceed by separately estimating the local part
\Delta\kappa_{1}
and the nonlocal part
-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}
of
-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{1}
.

-
Estimate of the local part.
For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

\Delta\kappa_{1}(x)=\frac{2\beta}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta+2}}\big[(2\beta-N+2)|x|^{2}-N\big];

from this, recalling that
\beta>N/2
, we derive the following facts:

(3.26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{i)}\,\,

\displaystyle\Delta\kappa_{1}(x)\geq-\frac{2\beta N}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta+2}}\quad\forall\,\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N};

(3.27)

\displaystyle\mathrm{ii)}\,\,

\displaystyle\text{there exists $\mathcal{A}>0$ such that $|\Delta\kappa_{1}|\leq\mathcal{A}$ pointwise on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

-
Estimate of the nonlocal part.

We begin by recalling that, by the computations carried out in the proof of
[
19
, Corollary 4.1]
, one can derive an explicit expression for
-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}
. More precisely, there exists a constant
\vartheta>0
such that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, it holds

\displaystyle-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\vartheta}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\cdot\mathcal{H}\big(N/2+s,\beta+s,N/2,-|x|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\vartheta}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta+s}}\cdot\mathcal{H}\Big(-s,\beta+s,N/2,\frac{|x|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}}\Big),

where
\mathcal{H}
denotes the hypergeometric function, and we have used the associated Pfaff transformation in the last identity.
Moreover, since
\beta>{N}/{2}
, we have

\lim_{z\to 1^{-}}\frac{\mathcal{H}\big(-s,\beta+s,N/2,z\big)}{(1-z)^{N/2-\beta}}=\gamma<0,

for an explicit constant
\gamma
depending on
N
,
\beta
and
s
(see, e.g.,
[
32
, Chapters 15.2, 15.4]
).
As a consequence, there exist
R_{0}\geq 1
and two positive constants
\eta_{1},\,\eta_{2}
such that

(3.28)

\begin{gathered}0<\frac{\eta_{1}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{N/2+s}}\leq-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}(x)\leq\frac{\eta_{2}}{(1+|x|^{2})^{N/2+s}}\\[2.84544pt]
\text{for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ with $|x|>R_{0}$}.\end{gathered}

In particular, we deduce that
-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to+\infty
, and thus, since
-(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(see Proposition
2.1
), it follows that

(3.29)

\text{there exists $\mathcal{B}>0$ such that $|(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}|\leq\mathcal{B}$ pointwise on $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

Gathering all the above estimates, we can easily complete the verification of property (3).

Indeed, by combining (
3.26
) and (
3.28
),
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|x|>R_{0}
we have

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{1}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}\Delta\kappa_{1}(x)-\mathbf{b}(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2\mathbf{a}\beta N}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta+2}}+\frac{\lambda}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}\big[\lambda-2\mathbf{a}\beta N(1+|x|^{2})^{-2}\big]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}\big[\lambda-2\mathbf{a}\beta N(1+R_{0}^{2})^{-2}\big]\geq 0,

provided that

\lambda\geq\lambda_{1}=2\mathbf{a}\beta N(1+R_{0}^{2})^{-2}>0.

On the other hand, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|x|\leq R_{0}
, by
(
3.27
) and (
3.29
) we have

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{1}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{a}\Delta\kappa_{1}(x)-\mathbf{b}(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{1}(x)

\displaystyle\geq-\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathcal{A}-\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathcal{B}+\frac{\lambda}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+|x|^{2})^{\beta}}

\displaystyle\geq-\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathcal{A}-\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathcal{B}+\frac{\lambda}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+R_{0}^{2})^{\beta}}>0,

provided that

\lambda\geq\lambda_{2}=\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+R_{0}^{2})^{\beta}\cdot\big(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathcal{A}+\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathcal{B}\big)>0.

Summing up, we conclude that
\kappa_{1}
fulfills property (3)

for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, where

(3.30)

\lambda_{0}=\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\}>0.

In other words,
\kappa_{1}
satisfies (
3.25
) (with the above choice of
\lambda_{0}
).

Having completed the proof in the case
\varepsilon=1
, we now consider the general case
0<\varepsilon<1
, exploiting the scaling properties of
\Delta
and
(-\Delta)^{s}
.

To this end, we make the following key observation: by definition,

(3.31)

\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)=\varepsilon^{N/2}\kappa_{1}(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

In view of (
3.31
), it follows immediately that
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
is smooth and strictly positive in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, since the same properties hold for
\kappa_{1}
. Hence,
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
satisfies property (1) in Definition
3.1
.

Moreover, since
0<\varepsilon<1
, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)+|\nabla\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)|

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{N/2}\big(\kappa_{1}(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)+\varepsilon^{1/2}|(\nabla\kappa_{1})(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{N/2}\big(\kappa_{1}(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)+|(\nabla\kappa_{1})(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)|\big)

\displaystyle(\text{since $\kappa_{1}$ fulfills \eqref{eq:ExtraKaplanIntbyParts}})

\displaystyle\leq c\,\varepsilon^{N/2}\Psi_{\beta}(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)\leq c\,\varepsilon^{(N-1)/2}\Psi_{\beta}(x),

As a consequence,
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
satisfies (
3.6
), and therefore also property (2) in Definition
3.1
(see (
3.31
) and recall that
\|\kappa_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}=1
by the choice of
\mathbf{c}_{\beta}
).

In order to verify property (3), we exploit the scaling properties of
\Delta
and
(-\Delta)^{s}
. Indeed, taking into account (
3.31
),
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we have

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)=\mathbf{a}\Delta\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)-\mathbf{b}(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)+\lambda\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad=(\varepsilon\mathbf{a})(\Delta\kappa_{1})(\varepsilon^{1/2}x)-(\varepsilon^{s}\mathbf{b})[(-\Delta)^{s}\kappa_{1}](\varepsilon^{1/2}x)+\lambda\kappa_{1}(\varepsilon^{1/2}x);

from this, since we have already demonstrated that
-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{1}+\lambda\kappa_{1}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
for every
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, and since we have an
explicit
dependence of
\lambda_{0}
with respect to the coefficients

\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, we derive that

-\mathcal{L}\kappa_{\varepsilon}+\lambda\kappa_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
pointwise on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, provided that

\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}(\varepsilon)=\max\big\{2(\varepsilon\mathbf{a})\beta N(1+R_{0}^{2})^{-2},\mathbf{c}_{\beta}(1+R_{0}^{2})^{\beta}\cdot\big((\varepsilon\mathbf{a})\cdot\mathcal{A}+(\varepsilon^{s}\mathbf{b})\cdot\mathcal{B}\big)\big\}.

On the other hand, recalling that
0<\varepsilon<1
(hence,
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{s}
), we have

\lambda_{0}(\varepsilon)\leq\varepsilon^{s}\lambda_{0},

from which we infer that
\kappa_{\varepsilon}
verify property (3) in the stronger form
(
3.25
) (with
\lambda_{0}
as in (
3.30
)). This completes the demonstration.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

We stress that the previous Theorem
3.7
is the only result which does not hold in this form if
\mathbf{a}>0
and
\mathbf{b}=0
(i.e. in the purely local case). It is known indeed (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
16
]
that in this case the right Kaplan function is of the form

\left(\dfrac{k}{\pi}\right)^{N/2}\mathbf{e}^{-k|x|^{2}},

for some
k>0
.

By combining Theorems
3.4
-
3.7
,
we obtain the following ‘more explicit’ result.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
u_{0}\in C_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, and assume that there exist
\beta>N/2,\,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
such that the following
estimate holds

(3.32)

\frac{\varepsilon^{N/2}}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{u_{0}(x)}{(1+\varepsilon|x|^{2})^{\beta}}\,dx>\mathbf{s}_{f}(\varepsilon^{s}\lambda_{0})

(
where
\lambda_{0}>0
as in (
3.30
), and

\mathbf{c}_{\beta}=\|\Psi_{\beta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}
)
. Then, the Cauchy problem (

CP
L

), with initial datum
u_{0}
, does not admit global classical solution.

Proof.

On account of Theorem
3.7
, we know that the function

\kappa_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{\varepsilon^{N/2}}{\mathbf{c}_{\beta}}\frac{1}{(1+\varepsilon|x|^{2})^{\beta}}

is a Kaplan function for
\mathcal{L}
(since
\beta>N/2
and
