Abstract
This document presents the final technical report for Pathways to Improved Representation in
Advanced NucleAr Science (PIRANA), supported by the U.S. Department of Energy Office of
Science (Office of Nuclear Physics) under Award No. DE-SC0024677 through the Reaching a
New Energy Sciences Workforce (RENEW) initiative. The project launched in Fall 2023 at
Skyline College in San Bruno, California, a federally recognized Minority Serving Institution
(MSI) and the only community college participating in the nEXO collaboration. Through this
partnership, Skyline College established an accessible and rigorous research environment for
its students. Over the course of the project, 10 student trainees made meaningful contributions
to nEXO detector R&D and to activities aimed at expanding student engagement in advanced
nuclear science. An additional 24 students completed the Summer Introduction to Research and
Experimentation in Nuclear physics (SIREN), a three-week program developed from the training
materials created for the nEXO trainees. All participating students engaged in opportunities to
present their work locally and at national conferences, take part in public outreach, and
contribute to a range of academic activities across campus. Collectively, these efforts extended
the impact of the project throughout the wider Skyline College community.

 1.​Introduction

1.1 What is nEXO?
nEXO is an international research collaboration of approximately 150 scientists, engineers, and
students from 40 institutions. Its goal is to carry out a next-generation nuclear physics
experiment designed to search for, and potentially observe, neutrinoless double-beta decay in
¹³⁶Xe.

The experiment would employ a 5-tonne liquid-xenon time projection chamber (TPC), operated
deep underground for roughly a decade, to reach a half-life sensitivity for neutrinoless
double-beta decay on the order of 10²⁸ years. This effort is technically demanding and requires
coordinated contributions from numerous institutions and specialists spanning a wide range of
disciplines.

Skyline College, one of the three community colleges in the San Mateo County Community
College District in California, joined the nEXO collaboration in January 2021. As a federally
recognized Minority Serving Institution (MSI), Skyline College is the first community college to
become a member of the nEXO collaboration.

1.2 Project Goals
The primary goal of the project was to support Skyline College student participation in ongoing
research and development activities for nEXO and related efforts. A broader objective was to
expand entry points into research, strengthen student retention in STEM pathways, and build
research capacity at Minority Serving Institutions (MSIs). Because the trainees were community
college students in the early stages of their academic careers, long-term outcomes such as
future specialization in nuclear physics cannot yet be fully assessed. To evaluate project
success, several learning outcomes were established. Upon completion of the program,
trainees were expected to:

 1.​ Gain familiarity with the field of experimental nuclear physics;
 2.​ Develop confidence in their ability to pursue advanced coursework or graduate studies in
 the field;
 3.​ Acquire an understanding of the range of career opportunities in nuclear physics; and
 4.​ Observe and understand the structure and operation of a modern experimental nuclear
 physics project.

In addition, trainees were expected to develop practical skills in:

 1.​ Computer programming;
 2.​ Electronics testing and assembly;
 3.​ Rapid prototyping; and
 4.​ Experimental design.

Student Contributions

Trainee contributions to nEXO and associated activities focused on the following areas:​
 (a) Supporting projects within the nEXO groups at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory and
Stanford University;​
 (b) Assisting with outreach initiatives and hands-on technical projects at Skyline College related
to cosmic-ray detection; and​
 (c) Participating in activities of the nEXO Collaboration Engagement Group (CEG).

To prepare students for these roles, trainees participated in technical workshops designed to
equip them with the skills necessary to complete software and hardware tasks relevant to areas
(a) and (b). Trainees also presented their work locally at the Undergraduate Symposium of
Academic Research (uSOAR), during internship outreach events, and at national conferences.

Program Expansion and the SIREN Summer Program

As student interest increased and returning trainees gained sufficient proficiency to serve as
peer mentors, the program expanded to include a short-term summer research experience. In
2025, the Summer Introduction to Research and Experimentation in Nuclear Physics (SIREN)
program was developed as a three-week experience serving 24 students. Organized into six

teams and supported by peer and faculty mentors, participants completed a one-week technical
training module during which each student constructed a Cosmic Watch detector, a portable
cosmic-ray detection instrument (http://www.cosmicwatch.lns.mit.edu/). Over the following two
weeks, teams conducted group projects using the detectors they had built. The program
concluded with final presentations. Several participants subsequently applied to the 2026
Emerging Researchers National (ERN) Conference; five students had abstracts accepted and
will present their posters in Atlanta, Georgia, in March 2026.

Engagement Through the nEXO CEG

Activities related to area (c) helped students develop a sense of connection to the wider
research community. The nEXO CEG provides structured mentoring opportunities with graduate
students, postdoctoral researchers, and senior scientists, allowing trainees to expand their
professional networks. The Skyline College nEXO group also contributed to the monthly CEG
newsletter, providing students with additional exposure to current developments in xenon-based
experiments.

1.3 Timeline
Skyline College was selected as one of the pilot awardees in the DOE Office of Science’s 2021
Research Traineeships to Broaden and Diversify Nuclear Physics program (FOA 0002456). The
PIRANA program was developed building on the experience and outcomes of that pilot effort,
incorporating several resources created during the earlier award, most notably technical
workshops in soldering, 3D printing, and Python programming.

Trainees entered the program in successive cohorts beginning in Fall 2023. Four students
joined the first cohort in Fall 2023, followed by four students in Summer 2024, and two
additional students in Fall 2025. Across the duration of the project, 4–6 trainees were active at
any given time. All students received technical training and contributed to nEXO-related projects
under the supervision of the Principal Investigator, the laboratory coordinator, and members of
the Stanford University and SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory nEXO groups. Trainees
worked full time during the summer (36.25 hours per week) and part time during the academic
year (7.5 hours per week).

In June 2025, the program expanded with the launch of the SIREN summer experience, in
which an additional 24 students participated. Six trained PIRANA students served as peer
mentors for this program.

Of the ten PIRANA trainees, eight have transferred to four-year universities, including UC Irvine,
UC Berkeley (two students), UC San Diego, Cal Poly San Luis Obispo (two students), and San
José State University. The remaining trainees are expected to transfer in Fall 2026. Most
transferring students have continued their involvement in research at their new institutions.

 2.​Project Activities

2.1 Workshops and Training

Technical Workshops

Each new cohort of trainees completed a structured series of hands-on technical workshops
designed to prepare them for their individual projects. These workshops provided foundational
skills in:

 ●​ Soldering
 ●​ 3D design and printing
 ●​ Arduino programming
 ●​ Circuit design and printed circuit board (PCB) milling
 ●​ Laser cutting
 ●​ Python programming

Through these activities, trainees gained familiarity and confidence in using the tools and
equipment available in the Skyline College Fabrication Lab.

Physics of nEXO and Experiment Context

To ensure a solid understanding of the scientific goals of the experiment, trainees participated in
sessions introducing the physics underlying nEXO and its detector technologies. These
activities enabled students to explain the scientific motivation behind their work when presenting
their results.

Trainees also attended nEXO collaboration meetings, either remotely or in person depending on
meeting location. Participation in these meetings provided exposure to the broader
collaboration, opportunities for professional networking, and a deeper understanding of the
experiment’s technical components.

nEXO Community Engagement Group (CEG)

Students took part in activities organized by the nEXO Community Engagement Group (CEG),
which offers resources to help students navigate challenges commonly encountered early in
research pathways. CEG programming includes seminars and mentoring sessions that support
skill development in communication, professional networking, and strategies for addressing
common barriers to participation. Most trainees engaged with CEG regularly, finding it a
valuable complement to their technical work.

2.2 Individual Projects
Each trainee undertook an individual nEXO-related project aligned with their interests and skill
development goals. Many projects were conducted in collaboration with mentors at Stanford
University or SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory, and several students completed
site-specific training to gain access to SLAC facilities. Additional work was carried out in the
Physics Lab and Fabrication Lab at Skyline College.

Examples of Student Projects
 ●​ UV-LED Driver Development for Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) Testing in Cosmic
 Muon Detection​
 One student completed a project focused on developing a fast ultraviolet (UV) LED
 driver circuit designed to test photomultiplier tubes (PMTs) used in cosmic muon
 detection experiments. Precise measurement is essential in particle-physics
 instrumentation, particularly when detecting faint scintillation light generated as cosmic
 muons interact with a scintillator medium. PMTs convert this weak optical signal into an
 electrical pulse, enabling measurements of muon flux and detector performance.
 Because reliable operation of the PMT is critical, the device must be thoroughly
 characterized prior to use.

 The student designed a UV LED driver capable of producing fast, well-controlled light
 pulses in the 380–430 nm range, representative of typical scintillation wavelengths. This
 controlled light source makes it possible to evaluate key PMT performance metrics,
 including gain, linearity, timing resolution, dark current, and quantum efficiency. When
 the LED emits a pulse, the PMT converts incident photons into electrons via the
 photoelectric effect and then amplifies them through a dynode chain to form a
 measurable electrical signal.

 This project provided hands-on experience with detector calibration techniques,
 electronics development, and the characterization of photodetector components
 essential to particle-physics experiments.

 ●​ Cryogenic Testing of Segmented Sensor Modules with CRYO ASIC Readout:​
 One student conducted a project focused on testing segmented sensor modules

 equipped with custom CRYO ASIC readout chips under cryogenic operating conditions.
 The goal of the project was to evaluate sensor and ASIC performance at temperatures
 relevant to liquid-xenon–based detectors. Testing was carried out using a custom-built
 pulser box and printed circuit board (PCB) designed to inject controlled signals into the
 sensors inside a cryogenic chamber maintained at –110 °C.

 To efficiently route signals to multiple sensor segments, the setup incorporated
 multiplexers (MUXs), enabling systematic testing of each channel without extensive
 rewiring. The project provided experience with cryogenic instrumentation, ASIC-based
 readout systems, and validation methodologies essential to large-scale detector
 development in nuclear and particle physics.

 CRYO ASIC project

●​ High-Speed SiPM Preamplifier Design, Simulation, and Validation​
 One student conducted a project focused on the development and evaluation of a
 high-speed preamplifier circuit for silicon photomultiplier (SiPM) readout, adapted from a
 reference design originating in Jacopo Dalmasson’s dissertation. The front-end system
 integrates four analog stages: a bipolar junction transistor (BJT) differential amplifier, a
 current mirror, a transimpedance amplifier (TIA), and an inverting operational amplifier
 based on the OPA820 model.

 The circuit was designed and modeled in LTSpice, with simulations performed under
 pulse conditions representative of SiPM charge injection. Each analog block was tested
 independently to verify expected gain behavior, transient response, and
 current-to-voltage conversion before assembling the complete signal chain. Frequency
 response studies using Bode analysis quantified stability–bandwidth trade-offs, and
 iterative adjustments to feedback capacitance were explored to suppress ringing and
 improve signal integrity.

 Final validation was carried out using a physical SiPM photosensor and LED-driven
 pulse source. Measured responses closely matched simulation results, demonstrating
 accurate reproduction of gain, timing performance, and waveform shape. The strong
 agreement between simulation and hardware establishes this design as a reliable and
 adaptable front-end option for photon detection applications, with relevance to nuclear

 physics instrumentation, optical sensing, and other time-sensitive photodetection
 systems.

 ●​ Electrical Breakdown Simulation and Fractal Analysis:​
 One student conducted a computational project examining electrical breakdown
 phenomena in dielectric media. Using Python-based simulations, the project compared
 growth patterns arising from two fundamental models:
 ○​ Diffusion-Limited Aggregation (DLA), which produces random dendritic structures
 analogous to snowflake formation, and
 ○​ The Dielectric Breakdown Model (DBM), where growth follows electric-field
 gradients similar to lightning propagation.

 The project focused on quantifying the fractal dimension of the resulting structures and
 visualizing the temporal evolution of electrical tree growth patterns.

Progress Tracking

Trainees maintained individual ePortfolios documenting their projects, skills development, and
reflections. These ePortfolios provided an effective mechanism for communication with mentors
and for students to assess their own progress over time.

 4.​Impact, Lessons Learned and Next Steps

4.1 Impact
The PIRANA program had a significant impact on student training, institutional capacity, and the
broader nEXO collaboration. Over the course of the project, Skyline College successfully
developed a sustainable pathway for community college students to participate in advanced
nuclear physics research, an opportunity not traditionally available at two-year institutions.
Trainees gained hands-on experience with detector R&D, programming, electronics, and
computational modeling, contributing directly to nEXO’s technical efforts.

The program strengthened the research infrastructure at Skyline College by expanding the
capabilities of the Fabrication Lab, formalizing technical workshops, and establishing structured
mentoring relationships with researchers at SLAC and Stanford. The expansion of the program
through the SIREN summer experience further broadened access, enabling dozens of
additional students to develop foundational research and technical skills.

The impact extended beyond direct research participation. Students presented their work at
local and national conferences, contributed to outreach initiatives, and engaged with the nEXO
Community Engagement Group. Many trainees successfully transferred to four-year universities
and continued their involvement in research, an indicator of the program’s effectiveness in
supporting student persistence and preparation for STEM pathways.

4.2 Lessons Learned
Several lessons emerged during the implementation of PIRANA:

Early, structured technical training is essential.

Students with little prior research experience benefited greatly from early workshops in
electronics, programming, and fabrication. This structure increased their confidence and
independence once they began individual projects.

Near-peer mentoring significantly improves outcomes.

As trainees progressed, they became effective peer mentors for new cohorts and SIREN
participants. This model increased student engagement, strengthened technical communication
skills, and reduced barriers typically encountered by first-time researchers.

Clear project scoping and regular mentor communication are critical.

Well-defined tasks, frequent check-ins with SLAC and Stanford mentors, and progress tracking
through ePortfolios helped ensure that projects remained aligned with the students’ skills and
timelines.

Virtual engagement with the collaboration is highly effective.

Remote attendance at nEXO collaboration meetings and CEG activities allowed students to
participate in high-level scientific discussions without travel constraints, increasing their sense of
belonging to the broader research community.

Logistical considerations matter for community college students.

Balancing coursework, work commitments, and research schedules required flexibility. Part-time
academic-year involvement combined with intensive summer work proved to be an effective
model.

4.3 Next Steps
Sustaining the research pathway at Skyline College

Skyline College intends to continue supporting student involvement in research through ongoing
partnerships with SLAC and Stanford. The training materials, workshop modules, and mentoring
structures developed through PIRANA will remain in use for future cohorts.

Expanding the summer SIREN experience

Based on strong participation and positive outcomes, the college plans to continue and expand
the SIREN program, enabling more students to gain early exposure to experimental science and
technical skill development.

Supporting the transfer and research pipeline

As many trainees continue research at their transfer institutions, Skyline College will explore
mechanisms to maintain alumni engagement and track long-term outcomes such as entry into
graduate programs, research internships, or STEM careers.

Exploring additional applications

Several student-developed instruments and software tools, such as Cosmic Watch detectors
and simulation code, may serve as platforms for continued projects or outreach activities.
