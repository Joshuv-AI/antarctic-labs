Intrinsic Riemannian Cross-covariance for Manifold-valued Random Objects
Carlos J. Soto, Cheng Wang, Yujing Huang, Xiaoyu Chen

Abstract
Covariance estimation yields a fundamental second-order statistic underlying representation learning, dimension reduction, and dependence modeling. While covariance has been well understood in Euclidean spaces, it is ill-defined for random objects residing on nonlinear Riemannian manifolds, which increasingly arise in modern machine learning applications involving shapes, symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices, etc. This paper introduces an intrinsic Riemannian cross-covariance for manifold-valued random objects. Our approach defines covariance and correlation by transporting local variations to a common tangent space via parallel transport, yielding a second-order descriptor that is independent of arbitrary coordinate choices. We establish that the proposed covariance inherits desirable properties of its Euclidean counterparts and characterize its asymptotic behavior. Numerical studies on spheres and SPD manifolds, together with real-data experiments on heart valve shapes in Kendall’s shape space, demonstrate the effectiveness of our estimators and verify the stated properties. Our results position the Riemannian covariance as a fundamental tool for second-order learning and analysis in non-Euclidean representation spaces.

Introduction

Understanding the dependence between two random objects is a natural and important task underlying representation learning. This dependence can typically be quantified via the cross-covariance matrix along with subsequent analysis including a scalar covariance and correlation. The foundations for the correlation analysis, however, are grounded in the existence of a vector space structure. For non-Euclidean manifold-valued data, such vector space structures are not well-defined, preventing the natural definition of cross-covariance and correlation.

A Riemannian manifold is a generalization of Euclidean space which need not be flat. Data that naturally lives on Riemannian manifolds is often encountered in statistical analysis. Extending linear, statistical methods to non-linear spaces is a ubiquitous endeavor such as the Fréchet mean, the Riemannian center of mass, which extends the notion of the Euclidean mean to metric spaces
11
. Data such as directional data
22
, meteorological data
12
, protein data
21
, and discrete densities
29
, can all be thought of as spherical data, a Riemannian manifold of constant, positive curvature. Other common examples include covariance matrices, for instance in brain imaging, as elements of the cone of symmetric positive definite matrices
7
, point cloud shape data as elements of complex projective space
15
, and tree structured data
20
. Analyzing such data requires extensions of the standard Euclidean statistical tools.

Due to the prevalence of non-Euclidean data in machine learning analyses and the need to quantifiably analyze such data, generalizations of the notion of covariance have been proposed. Some such generalizations include, to Riemannian manifolds for a single random variable
25
, to Wasserstein space
(
26
)
, to Riemannian functional data

19
;
30
, and to Riemannian manifolds for more than one random variables sharing the same support
1
.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:

1.

we introduce the
intrinsic footpoint-invariant Riemannian cross-covariance
, a notion of covariance between two random variables that live on a Riemannian manifold, and subsequently define the
Riemannian correlation
;

2.

we elucidate the properties of each introduced estimate as well as establish their asymptotic behavior, and;

3.

we demonstrate the effectiveness of our estimates on simulated data on the sphere and the SPD manifold, as well as on experimental shape data via Kendall’s shape space.

2.1
Riemannian Geometry

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a
d-
dimensional smooth topological manifold. At every point
p\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a neighborhood which is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose
\alpha:[-\delta,\delta]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a smooth curve on
\mathcal{M}
such that
\alpha(0)=p
and denote by
\alpha^{\prime}
its derivative. The tangent space of
\mathcal{M}
at
p
, denoted
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
, is the collection of all tangent vectors
\alpha^{\prime}(0)
, that is
T_{p}\mathcal{M}=\{\alpha^{\prime}(0)|\alpha(0)=p,\alpha:[-\delta,\delta]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}\}
. Each tangent space is a vector space with
\dim T_{p}\mathcal{M}=\dim\mathcal{M}
, and the collection of all tangent spaces is referred to as the tangent bundle,
T\mathcal{M}=\{(p,v)|v\in T_{p}\mathcal{M},p\in\mathcal{M}\}
. We equip each tangent space with an inner product
g_{p}(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{p}:T_{p}\mathcal{M}\times T_{p}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which varies smoothly on
\mathcal{M}
. A Riemannian manifold is a smooth manifold equipped with a Riemannian metric,
(\mathcal{M},g)
.

With respect to the metric, we have a unique torsion-free, affine connection
\nabla
, the Levi–Civita connection. This connection allows us to define the notion of parallel vector fields. Given a smooth path
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, the parallel transport of
v\in T_{\alpha(0)}\mathcal{M}
is denoted as
\Gamma_{\alpha(0)}^{\alpha(1)}v
and satisfies
\nabla_{\alpha^{\prime}(t)}V(t)=0
with
V
being a smooth vector field with
V(\alpha(0))=v
. Equivalently the vector field
V
must satisfy
\frac{D}{dt}V=0
with respect to the covariant derivative. Since parallel transport moves tangent vectors in a parallel fashion, it preserves inner products; that is, parallel transport is an isometry, so for
v,w\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
we have
\langle v,w\rangle_{p}=\langle\Gamma_{p}^{q}v,\Gamma_{p}^{q}w\rangle_{q}
. As a consequence, parallel transport preserves vector norms. Given a smooth path
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, with
v\in T_{\alpha(t)}\mathcal{M}
, one can show that there exists a unique parallel vector field
V
such that
V(t)=v

(
18
, Theorem 4.32)
. This, however, is still dependent on the chosen path.

The length
\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
of a smooth path
\alpha
is the cumulative instantaneous rate of change, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}(\alpha)=\int_{0}^{1}\langle\alpha^{\prime}(t),\alpha^{\prime}(t)\rangle^{1/2}_{\alpha(t)}
. A geodesic is a path that minimizes the length between two points. That is, a geodesic is the solution to

\gamma=\argmin_{\alpha:\alpha(0)=p,\alpha(1)=q}\mathcal{L}(\alpha).

This notion of minimal length results in a notion of distance; the distance between
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
is defined as the length of the geodesic between said points. So,
d(p,q)=\mathcal{L}(\alpha)
such that
\alpha
is a geodesic with
\alpha(0)=p
and
\alpha(1)=q
.

The exponential map maps elements from a tangent space to the manifold, that is,
\exp_{p}:T_{p}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
such that
\exp_{p}v:=q
such that there exists a geodesic
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
such that
\alpha(0)=p
and
\alpha(1)=q
and
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=v
. The exponential map is invertible within a neighborhood of its footpoint
p
, this inverse map is referred to as the logarithm map. The logarithm map
\log_{p}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow T_{p}\mathcal{M}
produces a vector in the tangent space with direction and length of the input,
d(p,q)=\|\log_{p}q\|_{p}=\|\log_{q}p\|_{q}
.

As we require the exponential map to be invertible, we need to consider on which domain it is as such. Riemannian manifolds are locally diffeomorphic to a Euclidean space, and the injectivity radius formally quantifies this locality. The injectivity radius at
p\in\mathcal{M}
, denoted as
\inj(p)
, is the largest (supremum) radius of an open ball in
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
centered at the origin such that
\exp_{p}
is defined and
B_{r}\subset T_{p}\mathcal{M}
and
\exp_{p}(B_{r})
are diffeomorphic. The injectivity radius of the manifold,
\inj{\mathcal{M}}
, is the smallest injectivity radius on the manifold,
\inj{\mathcal{M}}=\inf\{\inj(p)|p\in\mathcal{M}\}
.

The Riemannian volume form
\mathrm{d}\omega
is defined as
\mathrm{d}\omega=\sqrt{|g|}\mathrm{d}x^{1}\times\cdots\times\mathrm{d}x^{d}
where
|g|
is the determinant of the matrix representation of the metric in local coordinates. The Riemannian volume form allows us to define densities with respect to this base measure.

The term “footpoint" is not formally defined but generally refers to the point on the manifold at which the object of interest is defined. For instance the footpoint of
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
,
\log_{p}
, and
\exp_{p}
is
p
.
For the remainder of this paper, unless otherwise stated, we assume
\mathcal{M}
to be a complete, compact, finite-dimensional Riemannian manifold. For each manifold we require an exponential map, the logarithm map, and the parallel transport. These are standard tools, and we include them in
A
for completeness.

2.2
Related Literature - Covariance

The Fréchet mean
(
11
)
generalizes the notion of the arithmetic mean to metric spaces and is defined with respect to the Fréchet variance function.
The Fréchet variance of a random variable
X
with probability measure
\lambda
at
x\in\mathcal{M}
is defined as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(d^{2}(x,y))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d^{2}(x,y)\mathrm{d}\lambda(y).

The Fréchet mean is the point on the manifold that minimizes the variance, i.e.,

\mathbb{E}(X)=\mu\coloneq\argmin_{x\in\mathcal{M}}\sigma^{2}_{X}(x).

While the Fréchet variance is defined at any point
x
, if no point is designated, then the variance is assumed to be at the mean, i.e.,
\sigma^{2}_{X}\coloneq\sigma^{2}_{X}(\mu)
. The Fréchet variance may not have a unique minimizer on general manifolds, namely on positively curved manifolds
14
;
2
. To ensure the existence of a unique mean, one is required to bound the density in a geodesic ball centered at
p
with radius
r\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\{\inj\mathcal{M},\pi/\sqrt{\kappa}\}
,
B_{r}(p)
,
(
14
;
16
)
, where
\inj\mathcal{M}
denotes the injectivity radius and
\kappa
is an upper bound on the sectional curvature.

25
first extended the definition of covariance to Riemannian manifolds focusing on a single random variable. Suppose
X
is a random variable as above with a unique mean
\mathbb{E}(X)=\mu
then the covariance of
X
is defined as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{X,X}^{Pennec}=\Cov_{X}(\mu):

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\log_{\mu}X)(\log_{\mu}X)^{T}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{M}}(\log_{\mu}x)(\log_{\mu}x)^{T}d\lambda(x).

Similar to the Euclidean setting, we have that the trace of the covariance is the Fréchet variance, that is,
\tr(\Sigma^{Pennec}_{X,X})=\sigma_{X}^{2}
. Given a dataset
D=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, the empirical estimate of the covariance is
\hat{\Sigma}^{Pennec}_{X,X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\left[(\log_{\hat{\mu}}x_{i})(\log_{\hat{\mu}}x_{i})^{T}\right]
where
\hat{\mu}
is the empirical estimate of the Fréchet mean.

The previous definition, however, is limited to measuring the covariance of a single random variable. Extending the idea of covariance to more than one variable for complex spaces has been of recent interest.
1
propose an extension of the covariance definition to two random variables on Riemannian manifolds. To this end, the authors require that both densities have the same support. That is, given random variables
X
and
Y
defined on
\mathcal{M}
, their respective measures are defined, almost surely, in the same geodesic ball
B_{r}(p)
. They define the covariance as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

[
1
]
The covariance between random variances
X
and
Y
at point
p\in\mathcal{M}
is

\Sigma^{A\&P}_{X,Y}(p)=\mathbb{E}\left[(\log_{p}X)(\log_{p}Y)^{T}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\log_{p}X\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\log_{p}Y\right]^{T}.

The Riemannian covariance is defined as

\text{Rcov}(p)(X,Y)\coloneq\tr(\Sigma^{A\&P}_{X,Y}(p)).

Note that if
Y=X
and
p=\mu
then we have that
\Sigma^{A\&P}_{X,Y}(p)=\Sigma^{Pennec}_{X,X}
and so
\text{Rcov}(\mu)(X,X)=\sigma^{2}_{X}.
The empirical estimate in this case is

\hat{\Sigma}^{A\&P}_{X,Y}(p)=\frac{1}{n}\sum\left[(\log_{p}x_{i})(\log_{p}y_{i})^{T}\right]-\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum\log_{p}x_{i}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum\log_{p}y_{i}\right)^{T}.

Definition
2.1
, by construction, requires both measures to exist in the same geodesic ball; this can be a restrictive limitation. Further, the covariance is dependent on the choice of footpoint
p
.
1
give some guidance on how to choose
p
such as selecting the midpoint of the geodesic connecting the two sample Fréchet means. We aim to address both of these limitations. The following proposition shows that such definition is not footpoint-invariant; the proof can be found in
E
.

Proposition 2.2
.

On a Riemannian manifold with non-zero curvature, the Abuqrais-Pigoli covariance
\text{Rcov}(p)(X,Y)
is not invariant with respect to the choice of footpoint
p
.

27

\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}
denote two random densities with distribution functions
F_{1},F_{2}
, and let
\bar{f}_{i}
(with distribution function
\bar{F}_{i}

\mathcal{F}_{i}

\bar{f}_{1}

\bar{f}_{2}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\langle\widetilde{T}_{1},T_{2}\rangle_{\bar{f}_{2}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\widetilde{T}_{1}(u)-u)(T_{2}(u)-u)\bar{f}_{2}(u)du\right),

(1)

17

\pi_{S}

S\subset\mathcal{M}

\pi_{S}(x)=\argmin_{\tilde{x}\in S}d^{2}(x,\tilde{x})

Let the data be
\{x_{i},y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,
\mathbf{a}=\{a_{i}\}
.
\mathbf{b}=\{b_{i}\}

\bar{a},\bar{b}

\mu,\nu
the Fréchet means of

and
Y

Following this idea, the generalized CCA is expressed as an optimization problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{CCA}_{X,Y}=

\displaystyle\text{corr}(\pi_{W_{x}}(x),\pi_{W_{y}}(y))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{W_{x},W_{y},\mathbf{a,b}}\frac{\sum_{i}(a_{i}-\bar{a})(b_{i}-\bar{b})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}(a_{i}-\bar{a})^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}(b_{i}-\bar{b})^{2}}}

\displaystyle s.t.\quad

\displaystyle a_{i}=\argmin_{a_{i}\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)}\|\log_{\exp_{\mu}(a_{i}W_{x})}(x_{i})\|^{2}

\displaystyle b_{i}=\argmin_{b_{i}\in(-\epsilon,\epsilon)}\|\log_{\exp_{\nu}(b_{i}W_{y})}(y_{i})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

This formulation finds the tangent vectors which determine the geodesic submanifolds such that the correlation of projection coefficients is maximized. Once an appropriate

3.1
Riemannian Covariance

Suppose we have two random variables
X,Y
with unique, respective Fréchet means
\mu,\nu
. To ensure we have unique means we require the following assumption. Proofs for this section can be found in
E
.

Assumption 3.1
.

To ensure we have unique Fréchet means, each dataset
\{Z_{1},Z_{2},\dots,Z_{n}\}
is bounded in an open ball
B_{r}(p)
with
r\leq\frac{1}{2}\min\{\inj\mathcal{M},\pi/\sqrt{\kappa}\}

(
14
;
16
)
, where
\inj\mathcal{M}
denotes the injectivity radius and
\kappa
is an upper bound on the sectional curvature. If
\kappa\leq 0
, then
r
must simply be finite.

Further, our definition
3.3
requires the use of parallel transport. This raises an additional issue of uniqueness. We elaborate on this point in
2.1
, but note that parallel transport is generally not unique unless it is restricted to geodesics. Thus, we have,

Assumption 3.2
.

To ensure a unique parallel transport, we require a unique geodesic between the Fréchet means. Thus, we assume
d(\mu,\nu)<\inj\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 3.3

(Riemannian covariance)
.

Given random variables
X
and
Y
with respective Fréchet means
\mu
and
\nu
, let the Riemannian cross-covariance at
p
be defined as

\Sigma_{X,Y}(p)=\mathbb{E}\left[(\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}X)(\Gamma_{\nu}^{p}\log_{\nu}Y)^{T}\right]

where the parallel transport is with respect to the geodesic
\alpha
,
p\in\alpha
,
\alpha:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
,
\alpha(0)=\mu
, and
\alpha(1)=\nu
.
The Riemannian covariance between
X
and
Y
at point
p
is thus

\Cov_{X,Y}(p)=\tr(\Sigma_{X,Y}(p)).

To motivate our definition, we have the following theorem which shows that our definition is a direct generalization of the Euclidean covariance.

Theorem 3.4
.

When
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the Riemannian covariance reduces to the standard Euclidean covariance.

Remark 3.5
.

In Theorem
3.6
we show that our definition is invariant to choice of
p
. When there is no canonical or reference point choice for
p
, we define the covariance as

\Sigma_{X,Y}=\mathbb{E}\left[(\Gamma_{\mu}^{\nu}\log_{\mu}X)(\log_{\nu}Y)^{T}\right],\qquad\Cov_{X,Y}=\tr(\Sigma_{X,Y}).

In addition, our Riemannian covariance connects to the Wasserstein covariance (Eq.
2.2
) by defining an operator-valued covariance in a common tangent space, whose trace yields a scalar dependence measure. In the one-dimensional Wasserstein setting, the covariance is defined directly as an

L^{2}
inner product, which coincides with the trace of this operator under the flat geometry.

Theorem 3.6

(Footpoint Invariance)
.

We have
\Cov_{X,Y}(p)=\Cov_{X,Y}(q)
for all
p,q\in\alpha
where
\alpha
is the geodesic joining
\mu
and
\nu
.

Our definition is similar to that proposed by
1
, however, we utilize a parallel transport to achieve a common tangent space rather than utilize the tangent space at some midpoint. The use of parallel transport is necessary as
\log_{\mu*}
and
\log_{\nu*}
push the densities forward onto different tangent spaces, so the outer (and inner) product is not well-defined. This issue is not present in the Euclidean setting, as the tangent space at every point is the space itself,
T_{p}\mathbb{R}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Further, note that the vectors
\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}X
and
\Gamma_{\nu}^{p}\log_{\nu}Y
have length equal to the distance between the data and their respective mean:
\|\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}X\|_{p}=\|\log_{\mu}X\|_{\mu}=d(\mu,X)
. So, our definition is a
centered
second moment. Definition
2.1
centers the second moment by subtracting the “mean" e.g.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\log_{p}X\right]
. However,
\mathbb{E}\left[\log_{p}X\right]\neq\log_{p}\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]
, generally, i.e., the mean of the pushforward density onto the tangent space at an arbitrary point is not necessarily the
\log
of the Fréchet mean of the density.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the Hilbert space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators on
T_{\nu}M
, equipped with the inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:=\mathrm{tr}(AB^{\ast})
. Then the Riemannian covariance between
X
and
Y
equals the Hilbert-Schmidt
inner product between the transported intrinsic covariance operator
\widetilde{C}_{X,Y}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\Gamma^{\nu}_{\mu}\log_{\mu}X)\otimes\log_{\nu}Y\bigr]\in\mathcal{H}
and the identity operator
\mathrm{id}_{T_{\nu}M}
.

We characterize the behavior of our estimator in Proposition
3.8
. The proposed Definition
3.3
satisfies some standard results comparable to those of the Euclidean covariance such as generalization of the variance, symmetry, and equaling
0
for independent random variables.

Proposition 3.8
.

The Riemannian Covariance as in
3.3
has the following properties:

1.

\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)=\sigma^{2}_{X}
(Fréchet variance special case);

2.

\Cov_{X,Y}(p)=\Cov_{Y,X}(p)
(Symmetry);

3.

if
\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)=0
, then
X
is degenerate (Degeneracy);

4.

if
X
and
Y
are independent, then
\Cov_{X,Y}(p)=0
(Uncorrelatedness);

5.

for a fixed point
q\in\mathcal{M}
,
\Cov_{X,q}(q)=0
(Variance of a constant);

6.

\Cov_{(a,X),Y}(p)=a\Cov_{X,Y}(p)
where
(a,X)
is
\exp_{\mu}(a\log_{\mu}(X))
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
(Scaling).

Remark 3.9
.

As multiplication is not defined on Riemannian manifolds, item 6 of Proposition
3.8
requires us to define what it means to scale a random variable. We propose a definition and mention the implications with regard to our assumptions in the proof. Further, items 6 and 2 in conjunction with 1 yield
\Cov_{(a,X),(a,X)}(\mu)=a^{2}\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)=a^{2}\sigma^{2}_{X}
.

3.2
Riemannian Correlation

Given the definition of Riemannian covariance, we introduce the notion of Riemannian correlation. This notion generalizes the Pearson correlation from Euclidean spaces to the manifold setting.

Definition 3.10

(Riemannian correlation)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be random variables with respective means
\mu
and
\nu
satisfying Assumption
3.2
. Let
\alpha
be the geodesic with
\alpha(0)=\mu
and
\alpha(1)=\nu
. For some
p\in\alpha
, the Riemannian cross-correlation at point
p
is defined as

\varrho_{X,Y}(p)=\frac{\Sigma_{X,Y}(p)}{\sqrt{\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)}\sqrt{\Cov_{Y,Y}(\nu)}},

and the corresponding Riemannian correlation coefficient is given by

\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)=\tr(\varrho_{X,Y}(p))=\frac{\Cov_{X,Y}(p)}{\sqrt{\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)}\sqrt{\Cov_{Y,Y}(\nu)}}.

Here,
\Cov_{X,X}(\mu)
and
\Cov_{Y,Y}(\nu)
are computed at
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. However, benefiting from invariance under parallel transport, these quantities can be evaluated at any point. The properties of the Riemannian correlation, Proposition
3.12
, parallel those of the Riemannian covariance (i.e., Proposition
3.8
). A fundamental property of Riemannian correlation is its boundedness. Therefore, we have the following result, which is analogous to its Euclidean counterpart.

Proposition 3.11
.

We have that
\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)\in[-1,1]
.

The properties of the Riemannian correlation, Proposition
3.12
, parallel those of the Riemannian covariance (i.e., Proposition
3.8
).

Proposition 3.12
.

The Riemannian correlation coefficient as in
3.10
has the following properties:

1.

\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)=\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(q)
(Invariance);

2.

\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)=\mathcal{R}_{Y,X}(p)
(Symmetry);

3.

If
X
and
Y
are independent, then
\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)=0
(Uncorrelatedness);

4.

\mathcal{R}_{(a,X),Y}(p)=\text{sign}(a)\mathcal{R}_{X,Y}(p)
(Scale invariance);

5.

If X is nondegenerate,
\mathcal{R}_{X,X}(p)=1
(Self-correlation).

3.3
Consistency and Asymptotic Normality

Given i.i.d. samples
\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and sample Fréchet means
\hat{\mu},\hat{\nu}
, we first define the plug-in estimator

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p)\coloneq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{U}_{i}\hat{V}_{i}^{\top},

where
\hat{U}_{i}\coloneq\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}^{p}\log_{\hat{\mu}}(X_{i})
and
\hat{V}_{i}\coloneq\Gamma_{\hat{\nu}}^{p}\log_{\hat{\nu}}(Y_{i})
at point
p
.
We also define the scalar covariance

\Cov_{X,Y}(p)\coloneq\tr(\Sigma_{X,Y}(p))

and its estimator
\widehat{\Cov}_{X,Y}(p)\coloneq\tr(\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p))
.

Assumption 3.13

(Regularity conditions)
.

We impose the following regularity conditions:

(A1)

There exists a geodesically convex open set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{M}
containing
\mu,\nu,p
such that:
(i)
\mathcal{U}
is contained in a normal neighborhood of each point in
\mathcal{U}
,
(ii) sectional curvature on
\mathcal{U}
is bounded, and
(iii) the injectivity radius on
\mathcal{U}
is bounded away from
0
.

(A2)

\mathbb{E}\|\log_{\mu}(X)\|_{\mu}^{2}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\|\log_{\nu}(Y)\|_{\nu}^{2}<\infty
.

(A3)

\mathbb{P}(X\in\mathcal{U},\,Y\in\mathcal{U})\geq 1-\delta
for some
\delta\geq 0
(so the argument applies after truncation).

(A4)

\mu
and
\nu
are unique Fréchet means and the population Fréchet variance functions are twice continuously differentiable on
\mathcal{U}
.
Let

\displaystyle H_{X}

\displaystyle\coloneq\nabla^{2}\Big(x\mapsto\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(X,x)]\Big)\Big|_{x=\mu},

\displaystyle H_{Y}

\displaystyle\coloneq\nabla^{2}\Big(y\mapsto\mathbb{E}[d^{2}(Y,y)]\Big)\Big|_{y=\nu}.

Assume
H_{X}
and
H_{Y}
are positive definite.

(A5)

\mathbb{E}\|\log_{\mu}(X)\|_{\mu}^{4}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\|\log_{\nu}(Y)\|_{\nu}^{4}<\infty
.

(A6)

Define transport maps into
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
:

\phi(x)\coloneq\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}(x),\qquad\psi(y)\coloneq\Gamma_{\nu}^{p}\log_{\nu}(y),

and their plug-in counterparts

\hat{\phi}_{\hat{\mu}}(x)\coloneq\Gamma_{\hat{\mu}}^{p}\log_{\hat{\mu}}(x),\qquad\hat{\psi}_{\hat{\nu}}(y)\coloneq\Gamma_{\hat{\nu}}^{p}\log_{\hat{\nu}}(y).

Assume
\phi,\psi
are
C^{2}
on
\mathcal{U}
and the maps

m\mapsto\Gamma_{m}^{p}\log_{m}(z)
are
C^{2}
jointly in
(m,z)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
with uniformly bounded first derivatives on
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
.
(Under (A1) this holds for the Levi–Civita connection on compact subsets of
\mathcal{U}
.)

Remark 3.14
.

Assumptions (A1)–(A4) are the standard regularity conditions under which sample Fréchet means satisfy strong consistency and
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotics.
Assumptions (A5)–(A6) ensure that the plug-in covariance admits a first-order expansion.

Under these conditions, the following two lemmas will be used to establish the consistency and asymptotic properties of the estimators.

Lemma 3.15

(Strong consistency of sample Fréchet means (
3
, Thm. 2.3))
.

Under (A1)–(A4),
\hat{\mu}\to\mu
almost surely and
\hat{\nu}\to\nu
almost surely.

Lemma 3.16

(
\sqrt{n}
expansion of sample Fréchet means (
4
, Thm. 2.1))
.

Under (A1)–(A5), in
T_{\mu}\mathcal{M}
and
T_{\nu}\mathcal{M}
respectively,

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\log_{\mu}(\hat{\mu})

\displaystyle=H_{X}^{-1}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}+o_{p}(1),

(2)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\log_{\nu}(\hat{\nu})

\displaystyle=H_{Y}^{-1}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}+o_{p}(1),

(3)

where
\xi_{i}\in T_{\mu}\mathcal{M}
and
\zeta_{i}\in T_{\nu}\mathcal{M}
are i.i.d. mean-zero random vectors with finite second moments.
Moreover,
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\xi_{i}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega_{X})
and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum\zeta_{i}\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\Omega_{Y})
.

We then prove the strong consistency of the plug-in covariance estimator
\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p)
for a fixed footpoint
p\in\mathcal{U}
and, hence, of its scalar trace
\widehat{\Cov}_{X,Y}(p)
. This almost-sure convergence justifies treating
\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p)
as a consistent plug-in quantity in the subsequent
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic expansion.

Theorem 3.17

(Strong consistency)
.

Under (A1)–(A4) and (A6), for any fixed
p\in\mathcal{U}
,

\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p)\xrightarrow{a.s.}\Sigma_{X,Y}(p).

Consequently,
\widehat{\Cov}_{X,Y}(p)\to\Cov_{X,Y}(p)
almost surely.

Lemma 3.18

(Asymptotic linear representation of
\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p)
)
.

Assume (A1)–(A6) and fix
p\in\mathcal{U}
.
Let
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
stack columns of a
d\times d
matrix into a
d^{2}
-vector and define

\Delta_{n}\coloneq\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p))-\mathrm{vec}(\Sigma_{X,Y}(p)).

Then

\sqrt{n}\,\Delta_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IF}_{i}+o_{p}(1),

(4)

where

\mathrm{IF}_{i}\coloneq\Big(\mathrm{vec}(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{vec}(Z_{i})\Big)+A_{\mu}H_{X}^{-1}\xi_{i}+A_{\nu}H_{Y}^{-1}\zeta_{i}.

(5)

Here,
Z_{i}\coloneq U_{i}V_{i}^{\top}
with
U_{i}=\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}(X_{i})
and
V_{i}=\Gamma_{\nu}^{p}\log_{\nu}(Y_{i})
,
and
A_{\mu},A_{\nu}
and
\xi_{i},\zeta_{i}
are as defined in (

)–(

) and Lemma

.

Theorem 3.19

(
\sqrt{n}
-asymptotic normality)
.

Under (A1)–(A6), for any fixed

,

\sqrt{n}\Big(\mathrm{vec}(\widehat{\Sigma}_{X,Y}(p))-\mathrm{vec}(\Sigma_{X,Y}(p))\Big)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\Xi),

where

\Xi=\Var(\mathrm{IF}_{1})=\Var\Big(\mathrm{vec}(Z_{1})+A_{\mu}H_{X}^{-1}\xi_{1}+A_{\nu}H_{Y}^{-1}\zeta_{1}\Big).

(6)

Since

is a linear functional of

Corollary 3.20

(CLT for scalar covariance)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem

,

\sqrt{n}\Big(\widehat{\Cov}_{X,Y}(p)-\Cov_{X,Y}(p)\Big)\overset{d}{\to}\mathcal{N}\big(0,\ \sigma^{2}_{\tr}\big),

Remark 3.21

(Plug-in variance estimation and Wald inference)
.

One can estimate
\Xi

\widehat{\Xi}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\mathrm{IF}}_{i}\hat{\mathrm{IF}}_{i}^{\top},

where
\hat{\mathrm{IF}}_{i}
replaces
(\mu,\nu,H_{X},H_{Y},A_{\mu},A_{\nu},\xi_{i},\zeta_{i})
by consistent estimators

For the trace, use

(Correlation)

The consistency and asymptotic normality properties of Riemannian cross-correlation

5
Discussion

We have introduced two novel definitions, the intrinsic footpoint-invariant Riemannian cross-covariance and the subsequent Riemannian correlation. These tools are fundamental for understanding the second-order information present in manifold-valued random objects. Our estimators enjoy many desirable properties (e.g., Proposition
3.8
), which makes them a robust and natural choice for manifold statistical analysis. Lastly, their asymptotic properties are vital for quantifying their behavior.
The experimental results on the sphere, SPD matrices, and Kendall’s shape space show the applicability of our method and validate its properties. Given its effectiveness, the method can further the area of geometric learning on Riemannian manifolds, including regression and principal component analysis.
We explored a few manifolds; however, many others can be considered, such as the Grassmannian, the Stiefel manifold, the spaces of rotation and reflection matrices, hyperbolic space, and product manifolds such as the torus, to name a few.
Further, our method can be extended to Lie groups, where the natural choice for a tangent space is the Lie algebra.

Appendix A
Riemannian Geometry

A.1
Sphere

Let
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
with the metric induced from the ambient space. The exponential map is given by

\exp_{p}(v)=\cos(\|v\|)\,p+\sin(\|v\|)\,\frac{v}{\|v\|}.

The logarithm map is given by

\log_{p}(q)=\frac{\theta}{\sin(\theta)}\,(q-\cos(\theta)p),

with
q\notin\{p,-p\}
and
\theta=\arccos\langle p,q\rangle
. Lastly, the parallel transport of a vector
v
along a geodesic from
p
to
q
is given by

\Gamma_{p}^{q}(v)=v-\frac{\langle\log_{p}q,v\rangle_{p}}{\theta^{2}}\,(\exp^{-1}_{p}q+\exp^{-1}_{q}p),

with
\theta
as above.

A.2
SPDM

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the space of SPD matrices with the affine invariant metric (the Rao-Fisher metric). The exponential map is given by

\exp_{p}(v)=p^{1/2}\,\text{Exp}\!\left(p^{-1/2}vp^{-1/2}\right)p^{1/2},

and the log map is given by

\log_{q}(p)=q^{1/2}\,\text{Log}\!\left(q^{-1/2}p\,q^{-1/2}\right)q^{1/2},

where
Exp
and
Log
are the matrix exponential and logarithm, respectively. The parallel transport of tangent vector
v\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
along a geodesic connecting
p
and
q
is given by

\Gamma_{p}^{q}v=(qp^{-1})^{1/2}v(p^{-1}q)^{1/2}.

A.3
Kendall’s shape space

Define the ordered k-tuple landmark space

\mathcal{L}_{m,k}=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}|\dim(\text{span}(X))=m,k>m\}.

For an object
X\in\mathcal{L}_{m,k}
, we first remove the effect of translation and scaling

\hat{X}=\frac{X-\bar{x}\,\mathbf{1}_{k}^{T}}{\|X-\bar{x}\,\mathbf{1}_{k}^{T}\|_{F}},

where
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the mean across landmarks (i.e., the row-wise mean over the
k
columns) and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. This defines the preshape space

\mathcal{S}_{m}^{k}=\Big\{X\in\mathcal{L}_{m,k}\,\Big|\,X\mathbf{1}_{k}=0,\ \|X\|_{F}=1\Big\},

which lies on a hypersphere
\mathcal{S}^{m(k-1)-1}
. The Kendall’s shape space is further defined by taking quotient space of preshape space under the rotation group

\Sigma_{m}^{k}=\mathcal{S}_{m}^{k}/SO(m).

For each element
\tilde{X}\in\Sigma_{m}^{k}
, there corresponds an orbit in preshape space
\tilde{X}=[\hat{X}]=\{O\hat{X}|O\in SO(m)\}
. Therefore, the exponential map in
\Sigma_{m}^{k}
is defined (on a chosen representative
\hat{X}_{1}
of
\tilde{X}_{1}
) by

\exp_{\tilde{X}_{1}}(v)=\cos(\|v\|_{F})\hat{X}_{1}+\sin(\|v\|_{F})\frac{v}{\|v\|_{F}}.

The logarithm map is given by

\log_{\tilde{X}_{1}}(\tilde{X}_{2})=\frac{\theta}{\sin(\theta)}(O^{*}\hat{X}_{2}-\cos(\theta)\hat{X}_{1}),

where
\theta=\arccos(\langle\hat{X}_{1},O^{*}\hat{X}_{2}\rangle_{F})
and
O^{*}=\argmax_{O\in SO(m)}\langle\hat{X}_{1},O\hat{X}_{2}\rangle_{F}.
The spherical parallel transport cannot be applied on Kendall’s shape space directly, but transported vector can be approximated using a pole ladder algorithm
[
13
]
.

Appendix B
Canonical Correlation

Here we include more details from
17
. The geodesic submanifold
10

S\subset\mathcal{M}
is defined as
S=\exp_{p}(\mathrm{span}(\{b_{i}\})\cap U)
with
U\subset T_{p}\mathcal{M}
and
b_{i}\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
. The geodesic submanifold is the subspace of
\mathcal{M}
generated by pushing the span of some vectors
\{b_{i}\}
onto the manifold. Note that when
|\{b_{i}\}|=1
then
S
is simply a geodesic. Note that once an appropriate
\mathcal{R}^{CCA}_{X,Y}
is obtained, one can determine the covariance by multiplying by the standard deviations of both sets of coefficients.

Notice that projection onto the geodesic submanifold can be expressed in terms of the inner product, thus using Euclidean metric, the Log-Euclidean framework of CCA is actually equivalent to the manifold-valued CCA:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{CCA}_{X,Y}

\displaystyle=\max_{W_{x},W_{y}}\frac{\sum_{i}\langle W_{x},\log_{\mu_{x}}(x_{i})\rangle\langle W_{y},\log_{\mu_{y}}(y_{i})\rangle}{\sqrt{\sum_{i}\langle W_{x},\log_{\mu_{x}}(x_{i})\rangle^{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{i}\langle W_{y},\log_{\mu_{y}}(y_{i})\rangle^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\max_{W_{x},W_{y}}\frac{W^{T}_{x}\Cov(X,Y)\,W_{y}}{\sqrt{W^{T}_{x}\Cov(X,X)\,W_{x}}\sqrt{W^{T}_{y}\Cov(Y,Y)\,W_{y}}},

where
\Cov(X,Y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}[(\log_{\mu_{x}}x_{i})(\log_{\mu_{y}}y_{i})^{T}]
and similar for
\Cov(X,X)
and
\Cov(Y,Y)
. Unlike the general cross-covariance, which accounts for all possible linear combination between datasets, the CCA computes a normalized maximal cross-correlation, thus it tends to overestimate the correlation when datasets share the same coordinates.

Appendix C
Euclidean Covariance and Correlation

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space and let
X
and
Y
be two integrable random variables such that
X,Y\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. The expectation of
X
is defined as

\mathbb{E}(X)=\int_{\Omega}X(\omega)d\mathbb{P}(\omega),

and similary for
\mathbb{E}(Y)
. The covariance of
X
and
Y
is given by

\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}((X-\mathbb{E}(X))(Y-\mathbb{E}(Y))).

Equivalently,

\text{Cov}(X,Y)=\mathbb{E}(XY)-\mathbb{E}(X)\mathbb{E}(Y).

The covariance has the following properties:

1.

Variance:
\Cov(X,X)=\text{Var}(X)
;

2.

Symmetry:
\Cov(X,Y)=\Cov(Y,X)
;

3.

Scaling:
\Cov(aX,Y)=a\Cov(X,Y)
;

4.

Uncorrelatedness: If
X
and
Y
are independent, then
\Cov(X,Y)=0
;

5.

Invariance to constant:
\Cov(X+c,Y)=\Cov(X,Y)
;

6.

Variance of a constant:
\Cov(X,c)=0
;

7.

Additivity:
\Cov(X_{1}+X_{2},Y)=\Cov(X_{1},Y)+\Cov(X_{2},Y)
.
More generally,

\Cov\left(\sum_{i}a_{i}X_{i},\sum_{j}b_{j}Y_{j}\right)=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}a_{i}b_{j}\Cov(X_{i},Y_{j}).

With the variance given by

\text{Var}(X)=\Cov(X,X),

the Pearson correlation is defined as

\rho(X,Y)=\frac{\Cov(X,Y)}{\sqrt{\text{Var}(X)}\sqrt{\text{Var}(Y)}}

Thus we have the following properties for correlation:

1.

Bounded:
\rho(X,Y)\in[-1,1]
;

2.

Symmetry:
\rho(X,Y)=\rho(Y,X)
;

3.

Scale invariance:
\rho(aX+b,cY+d)=\text{sign}(ac)\rho(X,Y)
;

4.

Self-correlation: if X is nondegenerate,
\rho(X,X)=1
.

Appendix D
Covariance Under Basis Rotation

The parallel transport can be interpreted as a rotation of basis when the manifold is viewed as embedded in some ambient space, i.e.
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that we want to move a vector
w
from the tangent space of
p\in\mathcal{M}
to another tangent space of
q\in\mathcal{M}
along the geodesic connecting
p
and
q
. The vector
w\in T_{p}\mathcal{M}
can be written as

w=\sum_{n}a_{i}e_{i}^{p},

where
\{e_{i}^{p}\}
is a basis of
T_{p}\mathcal{M}
, then under the parallel transport, the corresponding basis on
T_{q}\mathcal{M}
is mapped by
e_{i}^{q}=De_{i}^{p}
, where
D\in SO(n)
and the transported vector
w^{\prime}\in T_{q}\mathcal{M}
is

w^{\prime}=\sum_{n}a_{i}e_{i}^{q}=Dw.

Therefore, we identify
D
with parallel transport matrix, denoted
D\simeq\Gamma_{\mu}^{\nu}
.

Under this setting, for two random variables
X,Y\in\mathcal{M}
with respective mean
\mu,\nu
, we have random vectors
u=\log_{\mu}X\in T_{\mu}\mathcal{M}
and
v=\log_{\nu}Y\in T_{\nu}\mathcal{M}
. Let
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{M}
be a geodesic with
\gamma(0)=\mu
,
\gamma(1)=\nu
, and for some

, the corresponding matrices to parallel transport is given by

\displaystyle A\simeq\Gamma_{\gamma(0)}^{\gamma(1)},\quad B(t)\simeq\Gamma_{\gamma(0)}^{\gamma(t)},\quad C(t)\simeq\Gamma_{\gamma(t)}^{\gamma(1)},

where

, and hence we also have
A=C(t)B(t)

\displaystyle A^{T}\simeq\Gamma^{\gamma(0)}_{\gamma(1)},\quad B^{T}(t)\simeq\Gamma^{\gamma(0)}_{\gamma(t)},\quad C^{T}(t)\simeq\Gamma^{\gamma(t)}_{\gamma(1)},

The Riemannian cross-covariance at
p=\alpha(t)
can be defined alternatively as

\displaystyle\Sigma_{X,Y}(p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\Gamma_{\mu}^{p}\log_{\mu}X)(\Gamma_{\nu}^{p}\log_{\nu}Y)^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((\Gamma_{\gamma(0)}^{\gamma(t)}u)(\Gamma_{\gamma(1)}^{\gamma(t)}v)^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}((B(t)u)(C(t)^{T}v)^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(B(t)uv^{T}C(t)).

It is obvious that the covariance matrix is subject to the choice of the p, however for the Riemannian covariance, we have

\displaystyle\Cov_{X,Y}(p)

\displaystyle=\tr(\Sigma_{X,Y}(p))

\displaystyle=\tr(\mathbb{E}(B(t)uv^{T}C(t))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\tr(C(t)B(t)uv^{T}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\tr(Auv^{T}))

\displaystyle=\tr(\mathbb{E}((\Gamma_{\mu}^{\nu}\log_{\mu}X)(\log_{\nu}Y)^{T})

\displaystyle=\Cov_{X,Y}(\nu),

similarly, we also have
\Cov_{X,Y}(p)=\Cov_{X,Y}(\mu)

p=\alpha(t)

\alpha

\displaystyle\coloneq\mathbb{E}\big[U\otimes D_{\nu}\psi_{\nu}(Y)\big].

(17)

Thus

\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{vec}\big(\Delta T_{n}\big)=A_{\mu}\,\sqrt{n}\,\delta_{\mu}+A_{\nu}\,\sqrt{n}\,\delta_{\nu}+o_{p}(1).

(18)

Substituting the
\sqrt{n}
-expansions of
\delta_{\mu},\delta_{\nu}
from Lemma
3.16
yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\mathrm{vec}\big(\Delta T_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=A_{\mu}H_{X}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle+A_{\nu}H_{Y}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\zeta_{i}\right)+o_{p}(1).

Finally, combining (
9
), the vectorized empirical term, and (
19
) gives the asymptotic linear representation (
4
) with influence term (
5
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.19
.

By Lemma
3.18
, we have

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\,\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IF}_{i}+o_{p}(1).

The summands
\mathrm{IF}_{i}
are i.i.d. mean-zero. Under (A5)–(A6), they have finite second moments, hence by the multivariate CLT

\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{IF}_{i}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Var(\mathrm{IF}_{1}))
.
Slutsky’s theorem yields the stated limit with
\Xi=\Var(\mathrm{IF}_{1})
.

We start by generating a random data set

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\cos(2\pi v)

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-z^{2}}\sin(2\pi v)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle=1-w(1-\cos\tau)

where
v,w\sim U(0,1)

\tau

\displaystyle O(\eta)

\displaystyle=I+\sin(\eta)K+(1-\cos(\eta))K^{2}

\displaystyle K

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&-u_{z}&u_{y}\\
u_{z}&0&u_{x}\\
-u_{y}&u_{x}&0\end{bmatrix}

[
6

\eta

(u_{x},u_{y},u_{z})

q

Therefore, the second sample set is generated as

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\exp_{q}\Bigg[\Gamma_{\tilde{\nu}}^{q}\left(\frac{O(\eta)\log_{\hat{\mu}}X_{i}+\epsilon_{i}}{\|O(\eta)\log_{\hat{\mu}}X_{i}+\epsilon_{i}\|}\right)\Bigg],

\epsilon_{i}\sim N_{3}(0,\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}I_{3})

\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\tilde{\nu}
is the Fréchet mean of the set
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
before the transportation. We can expect
q=\hat{\nu}
to be the Fréchet mean of transported data, as there’s no additional noise introduced.

G.2

G.2.1
Data Generation and Experimental Setup

To rigorously assess the proposed estimator, we employ a generative model that decouples intrinsic geometric correlation from ambient curvature effects. The manifold of
d\times d
Symmetric Positive Definite (SPD) matrices,
\mathcal{P}_{d}
, is endowed with the Affine Invariant Riemannian Metric (AIRM). We fix the dimensionality at
d=3
and the sample size at
n=100
for most experiments unless otherwise specified.

The primary dataset
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is generated by perturbing the identity matrix
I_{d}
. Tangent vectors
v_{X,i}\in T_{I_{d}}\mathcal{P}_{d}
are sampled from a symmetric normal distribution with spread parameter
\tau=0.5
and subsequently mapped to the manifold via the Riemannian exponential map, denoted as
X_{i}=\text{Exp}_{I_{d}}(v_{X,i})
.

The dependent dataset
\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is constructed via a two-stage process to ensure that the correlation structure is intrinsic and transport-invariant. First, we generate tangent vectors
v_{Y,i}\in T_{I_{d}}\mathcal{P}_{d}
that are locally correlated with
v_{X,i}
according to the relation
v_{Y,i}=\rho\cdot v_{X,i}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\cdot\epsilon_{i}
. Here,
\rho\in[0,1]
controls the correlation strength, and
\epsilon_{i}
is a random symmetric matrix representing noise, normalized such that
\|\epsilon_{i}\|_{I_{d}}=\|v_{X,i}\|_{I_{d}}
. This normalization ensures that the dispersion of
Y
matches that of
X
. Subsequently, the entire vector cloud
\{v_{Y,i}\}
is parallel transported from
I_{d}
to a target mean
\nu
along the geodesic connecting them. Let
\Gamma_{I_{d}}^{\nu}
denote the parallel transport operator; the final samples are given by
Y_{i}=\text{Exp}_{\nu}\left(\Gamma_{I_{d}}^{\nu}(v_{Y,i})\right)
. By generating correlation locally and then transporting the distribution, we ensure that any observed decorrelation in the estimators is attributable to the estimation method itself rather than artifacts of the data generation process in curved space.
