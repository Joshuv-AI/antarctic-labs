Energy, economic, and environmental (3E) analysis of hybrid water electrolysis through alternative oxidation reactions
Authors: Soyoung Kang, Soomin Lee, Siwon Park, In-Kyoung Ahn, Sanghun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the utilization of hydrogen expands to achieve carbon neutrality, increasing attention has been directed toward technologies capable of reducing energy consumption and greenhouse gas emissions during hydrogen production. Although green hydrogen production through renewable energy-based water electrolysis is regarded as an environmentally friendly pathway, conventional water electrolysis systems still suffer from high levelized cost of hydrogen due to substantial electricity consumption. To address this challenge, this study proposes a Hybrid Water Electrolysis (HWE) system in which the conventional oxygen evolution reaction is replaced by electrochemical oxidation reactions of organic compounds. A 1 MW-scale alkaline water electrolysis system employing Urea, Glycerol, 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), and Glucose as reactants were modeled using Aspen Plus, followed by techno-economic analysis, and life cycle assessment. The analysis results showed that all HWE systems achieved higher hydrogen production rates than conventional alkaline water electrolysis systems (18.66 kg H2 per hr). In particular, the glucose-based system exhibited the highest hydrogen and co-product production rates. The glucose-based system achieved the levelized cost of hydrogen as low as approximately 4.74 $ per kg H2 under high-revenue conditions considering product sales revenue. In addition, the greenhouse gas emission analysis demonstrated that the glucose-based system achieved the lowest emissions when only the HWE system boundary was considered, whereas the urea-based system exhibited the lowest overall emissions when reactant production was included. Through a simulation-based 3-E (Energy, Economy, and Environment) analysis, this study demonstrates the potential of HWE technology to improve the economic feasibility of environmentally sustainable hydrogen production.

Introduction

As the global energy transition toward carbon neutrality accelerates, hydrogen has attracted considerable attention as an environmentally sustainable energy carrier.1,2 Hydrogen does not directly emit carbon dioxide during utilization and is regarded as a next-generation clean energy carrier owing to its high gravimetric energy density.3 In particular, hydrogen can not only replace fossil fuel-based propulsion systems in various transportation sectors such as ships, automobiles, and aircraft, but can also serve as a key feedstock for the production of carbon-free fuels and chemicals, including ammonia.4 Furthermore, hydrogen has strong potential as an energy carrier because it can be produced using renewable electricity and stored for long-term use.5 Because of these characteristics, hydrogen is recognized as a core energy resource linking the power, transportation, and chemical industries and is considered one of the key technologies for achieving a carbon-neutral society.6

The utilization of hydrogen is continuously increasing, leading to growing interest in sustainable hydrogen production technologies. Currently, the most widely used hydrogen production pathway is gray hydrogen production through steam methane reforming based on natural gas.7 Although this process offers high economic feasibility and ease of large-scale production, it generates substantial amounts of carbon dioxide because fossil fuels are used as feedstocks.1 In general, the life cycle Greenhouse Gas (GHG) emissions of gray hydrogen have been reported to exceed approximately 10 kg CO2 per kg H2.8 In contrast, blue hydrogen production combined with carbon capture, utilization, and storage technologies can capture more than 90% of the carbon dioxide generated during the production process, thereby reducing the average GHG emissions to below approximately 2 kg CO2 per kg H2.8 Furthermore, green hydrogen production based on water electrolysis using renewable electricity has been reported to exhibit GHG emissions in the range of approximately 0.88–2.21 kg CO2 per kg H2 depending on the type of renewable energy source employed, and is therefore considered one of the most environmentally sustainable hydrogen production pathways currently available.1,8 Accordingly, improving the efficiency and economic feasibility of renewable energy-based water electrolysis technologies has been recognized as a key challenge for reducing GHG emissions associated with future hydrogen utilization, and various next-generation electrolysis technologies are actively being developed for this purpose.1

Green hydrogen production through renewable energy-based water electrolysis still suffers from higher production costs compared with conventional fossil fuel-based hydrogen production pathways.9 In general, the economic feasibility of hydrogen production is evaluated based on the Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH), which considers both capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operating expenditure (OPEX).10 Conventional fossil fuel-based hydrogen production pathways typically exhibit relatively low LCOH values of approximately 1–3 $ per kg H2, whereas green hydrogen production through renewable energy-based water electrolysis has been reported to require significantly higher costs in the range of approximately 5–15 $ per kg H2 depending on the electricity source and system operating conditions.8 Electricity consumption is known to account for the largest proportion of the operating cost in water electrolysis-based hydrogen production systems.2 Previous studies have reported that electricity costs contribute approximately 46.04% of the total LCOH.11 This is because the electrolysis process requires a substantial amount of electrical energy, indicating that renewable electricity prices directly influence the overall economic feasibility. Therefore, it is essential to develop technologies that can reduce the electricity consumption of water electrolysis systems while improving their economic competitiveness from an operational cost perspective to establish large-scale environmentally friendly hydrogen production systems in the future.

Hybrid Water Electrolysis (HWE) refers to a hydrogen production technology in which the Oxygen Evolution Reaction (OER) occurring at the anode of conventional water electrolysis systems is replaced with alternative oxidation reactions.8 This technology has attracted attention as an effective approach for reducing thermodynamic potential of electrolysis cell and has recently been actively investigated as one of the key strategies for decreasing the energy consumption of electrolysis-based hydrogen production systems.12 The conventional OER requires a high thermodynamic potential, 1.23 V vs. RHE.13 In contrast, when electrochemical oxidation reactions of organic compounds are applied as anodic reactions, the anodic oxidation potential can be reduced.14 Representative alternative oxidation reactions include the urea oxidation reaction (0.37 V vs. RHE), the glycerol oxidation reaction (0.003 V vs. RHE), the HMF oxidation reaction (0.113 V vs. RHE), and the glucose oxidation reaction (0.05 V vs. RHE).15 Therefore, replacing the conventional OER with these alternative oxidation reactions can reduce the electricity consumption required under identical hydrogen production conditions, thereby lowering the operational cost of water electrolysis systems. In addition, hybrid water electrolysis systems offer additional advantages as value-added chemicals can be simultaneously produced during the anodic oxidation reactions.16 For example, formic acid or lactic acid can be produced through the oxidation of glycerol, while 2,5-Furandicarboxylic acid (FDCA) produced from 5-Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) oxidation has attracted attention as a bio-based plastic precursor capable of replacing conventional PET materials.17,18 Furthermore, gluconic acid or glucaric acid produced through glucose oxidation can be utilized in various applications, including food additives, cleaning agents, pharmaceuticals, and chemical industry feedstocks.19 Since these products possess higher utilization potential and economic value compared with the original reactants, HWE technology can be regarded as a next-generation hydrogen production technology.

HWE employing alternative oxidation reactions has recently attracted considerable research attention. However, most previous studies have primarily focused on the valorization of chemicals. Houache et al. (2021) experimentally investigated the electrochemical oxidation of glycerol under various voltage and catalyst conditions and evaluated glycerol conversion rates and product selectivity.17 For glucose-based systems, Medrano-Banda et al. (2024) analyzed glucose conversion, gluconic acid selectivity, and faradaic efficiency under different voltage conditions.19 As such, many previous studies have mainly concentrated on the valorization of chemicals using alternative oxidation reactions, with particular emphasis on improving reaction efficiency and product selectivity. Meanwhile, a few studies have approached the concept of hybrid water electrolysis from the perspective of hydrogen production. Ge et al. (2023) comparatively analyzed the performance of alkaline HWE systems employing various reactants based on literature data, while Roh et al. (2024) evaluated the LCOH and the influence of overpotential on the economic feasibility of urea-, glycerol-, and HMF-based HWE systems.8,15 Wang et al. (2025) conducted analyses of the levelized cost of product and GHG emissions for hydrogen production systems based on methane oxidation reactions.20 However, these studies were generally limited to comparisons of power consumption and levelized costs based on previously reported ununified literature data, while comprehensive process-level analyses including reactant production, electrolysis reactions, and product separation processes were only rarely performed. The key novelty of this study lies in the integration of alternative anodic oxidation reactions with the water electrolysis cell concept to design 1MW-scale water electrolysis processes. This study investigates how electrochemical characteristics, including catalyst degradation, interact with process-level operating conditions and affect overall system performance. The proposed systems are evaluated using an integrated 3-E analysis framework covering the entire process from reactant production and electrochemical hydrogen generation to product separation. This approach enables a comparative assessment of the hydrogen production performance, economic benefits, life-cycle environmental impacts, and the overall technological feasibility of hybrid water electrolysis relative to conventional alkaline water electrolysis.

In this study, process modeling coupled with Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA), and Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) was conducted for HWE systems employing urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose as reactants for hydrogen and chemical production through electrochemical reactions. The electrolysis systems were modeled based on a 1 MW-scale alkaline water electrolysis system. Based on the modeling results, mass and energy balances were calculated for each system, and these results were used to estimate the CAPEX and OPEX required for the TEA. And LCOH was calculated to evaluate the economic feasibility of each system. For the environmental assessment, the system boundary was defined from the reactant production stage to the final product recovery stage, and a life cycle inventory-based LCA was conducted. Life cycle GHG emissions were quantified using the material and energy consumption data obtained from the process modeling, and the environmental impacts of each HWE system were comparatively analyzed.

Methods

System modeling

A 1 MW alkaline water electrolysis system was selected as the reference model to design and compare the performance of HWE systems employing alternative oxidation reactions of urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose.8,15,20–23 The system capacity was set to 1 MW because industrial alkaline water electrolysis systems are commonly designed and operated at the MW scale. The same power capacity was applied to all HWE systems to enable a quantitative comparison of material balances, product yields, and economic benefits according to the type of reactant. All selected reactants can be supplied to the electrochemical reactor in aqueous form and undergo anodic oxidation involving OH− transport under alkaline electrolyte conditions. Accordingly, an alkaline water electrolyzer using an aqueous KOH solution as the electrolyte was adopted for process modeling. In the HWE systems, each reactant was introduced into the anolyte. Because this addition affects the composition and properties of the anodic electrolyte, the anolyte and catholyte circulation loops were modeled separately. Additional separation and purification units were applied following to anode outlet stream to recover the co-products. Hydrogen production was modeled in the same manner as in a conventional alkaline water electrolysis system, with hydrogen recovered from the cathode outlet stream using a gas–liquid separator. Because the equilibrium potential of the hydrogen evolution reaction is 0 V vs. RHE, VHWE was calculated by adding a common voltage loss term, ΔVcommon to EAOR, where ΔVcommon accounts for the cathodic HER activation overpotential and ohmic losses.

From a system-level perspective, the liquid electrolyte is continuously circulated at a sufficiently high flow rate under steady state operating conditions. Aspen Plus, developed by Aspen Technology, is a representative chemical process simulation software that calculates material and energy balances by combining physical-property databases, thermodynamic models, and unit-operation modules. For each process unit, the software solves the governing mass and energy balances together with the specified reaction of phase-equilibrium relationships, thereby determining stream compositions, flow rates, temperatures, pressures, and energy requirements. The ELECNRTL method was employed as the thermodynamic model to account for the behavior of the electrochemical system containing electrolytes.

Glycerol

As shown in Fig. 1, this study designed a process for producing glycerol from by-products generated during biodiesel production. In general, approximately 1 kg of crude glycerol is known to be produced for every 10 kg of biodiesel produced.21 Biodiesel is produced through the transesterification reaction of triglycerides obtained from waste cooking oil or vegetable oil with excess alcohol, during which glycerol is generated as a by-product together with fatty acid methyl esters. The reactant preparation stage of the biodiesel and glycerol production process shown in Fig. 4 was modeled based on the biodiesel production from vegetable oil example provided in Aspen Plus.29 In the transesterification reaction, biodiesel was produced by reacting vegetable oil with methanol, and a methanol recovery process was incorporated to improve methanol recovery efficiency. Subsequently, glycerol was separated through a water washing process, and residual catalysts were removed through a catalyst removal process operated at 50 °C and 1 bar. Techno-economic analysis

To evaluate the economic feasibility of the hybrid water electrolysis systems, a TEA was conducted in this study based on the LCOH. The electrolysis plant was assumed to have an operational lifetime of 20 years and a total alkaline water electrolysis stack lifetime of 80 000 h as shown in Table 7. LCOH represents the total cost required to produce 1 kg of hydrogen and is generally expressed in units of $ per kg H2. It is calculated by considering both the CAPEX required for initial system construction and the OPEX incurred during system operation. The CAPEX included not only the purchase and replacement costs of the alkaline water electrolysis stack but also the costs of balance-of-plant (BOP) equipment essential for electrolyzer operation, including the water purification unit, KOH circulation pumps, gas–liquid separators, and buffer tanks. In addition, because the co-products generated from the alternative anodic oxidation reactions exhibit different physical and chemical properties, the downstream separation and recovery processes were configured individually for each system. Accordingly, the equipment costs of acidification reactors, distillation columns, crystallizers, filters, evaporators, and anion-exchange units required for the recovery of formic acid solution, FDCA, and gluconic acid solution were incorporated into the CAPEX of the corresponding systems. OPEX refers to the annual costs associated with system operation, including maintenance, utilities, water consumption, and chemical purchases. To determine whether the reduction in cell voltage achieved through alternative anodic oxidation reactions translates into an actual improvement in economic performance, the analysis considered not only the operating costs of the electrolyzer but also the costs of reactant purchase and co-product separation and purification. The energy requirements of each separation process were determined from the Aspen Plus simulation results. Thermal energy was assumed to be supplied by methane combustion, and the corresponding methane consumption was included in the OPEX. The costs associated with reactant preparation were estimated based on the market prices of the respective reactants. Life cycle assessment

In this study, a LCA was conducted using SimaPro to compare and analyze the life cycle GHG emissions of four systems. The system boundary was defined as cradle-to-gate, encompassing all stages from reactant production to hybrid water electrolysis and product treatment processes. In addition, the functional unit was defined as the production of 1 kg of hydrogen.

The ecoinvent v3.9.1 database was utilized to quantify the life cycle GHG emissions. Based on this database, the environmental burdens associated with raw material production, process operation, chemical consumption, and product treatment were quantitatively evaluated. Carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O) were considered as the major greenhouse gases in the emission assessment. Since each greenhouse gas exhibits different warming impacts depending on its atmospheric lifetime and radiative forcing, the Global Warming Potential (GWP) was applied to reflect these differences. In this study, GWP values based on the Fifth Assessment Report published by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change were adopted, using GWP(CO2) = 1, GWP(CH4) = 28, and GWP(N2O) = 265.48 Accordingly, the life cycle GHG emissions of the hybrid water electrolysis systems employing the four reactants were calculated based on the following equation.28

Results

Simulation result

As shown in Fig. 10, which compares the product yields based on the simulation results, all hybrid water electrolysis systems exhibited higher hydrogen production rates than the conventional water electrolysis system when applied to a 1 MW-scale alkaline water electrolysis system. The hydrogen production rate of the conventional water electrolysis system was calculated to be 18.66 kg h−1, whereas all systems employing alternative oxidation reactions showed higher hydrogen production rates than this value. In addition, the comparison of co-product production rates revealed that the glucose-based system produced the highest number of co-products among all reactant-based systems under the same electrical energy input conditions. These results demonstrate that hybrid water electrolysis systems employing alternative oxidation reactions can not only improve hydrogen production performance compared with conventional water electrolysis systems, but also simultaneously generate additional economic value through the valorization of reactants into high-value co-products.

Product yields from the simulation results.

The comparison results of the life cycle energy consumption are presented in Fig. 11. In this study, the analysis was conducted by distinguishing between cases that included and excluded the energy consumption associated with the reactant production stage. This distinction was considered because reactants such as urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose are more likely to be supplied through external markets rather than directly produced within the electrolysis plant under actual industrial conditions. Therefore, both scenarios, including and excluding the reactant production pathway within the system boundary, were comparatively analyzed to evaluate the energy consumption characteristics. The most notable finding from the figure is that the heat supplied for product separation is the dominant energy contributor across all system boundaries considered. This is because both processes require substantial thermal energy for the evaporation or distillation steps used to recover the co-products in aqueous form. In particular, the glycerol-based system requires approximately 49 MW of thermal energy, of which distillation accounts for 75% and evaporation for the remaining 25%. In addition, when reactant production is included within the system boundary, electricity consumption for urea production accounts for 44% of the total energy demand. This is because both ammonia and carbon dioxide used in the urea production process require compression to approximately 226 bar through compressors. As a result, the high-pressure synthesis process significantly increased electricity consumption during the urea production stage. For the HMF-based system, approximately 6.7 MW of thermal energy was required during reactant production because the dehydration of fructose is endothermic. In contrast, the separation stage required only a negligible amount of electricity for the separation of H2 and K2SO4. Consequently, the HMF-based system exhibited the lowest overall energy demand among the systems evaluated. All systems were modeled based on the same 1 MW-scale electrolysis unit, the fundamental electricity for water electrolysis was maintained at a similar level across all systems.

Techno-economic analysis

To compare and analyze the economic differences among the systems employing different reactants, the LCOH of each system was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 12, the LCOH breakdown indicates that product separation accounted for the largest cost contribution in the glycerol- and glucose-based systems. This is because additional downstream separation processes, including distillation, evaporation, and anion-exchange separation, are required to recover high-purity products. Value-added products such as formic acid and gluconic acid must satisfy specific purity and concentration requirements to achieve commercial viability. Consequently, the costs associated with multistage separation equipment and thermal energy supply significantly affected the overall economic feasibility of these systems.

LCOH cost breakdown for different HWE systems.

The market prices of value-added chemicals vary depending on product purity, production pathways, and application fields. In this study, market price ranges of 0.64–1 $ per kg for formic acid, 1.38–2.5 $ per kg for FDCA, and 0.15–0.4 $ per kg for gluconic acid were applied to evaluate the LCOH variability according to product selling prices. The analysis results as shown in Fig. 13 indicated that the glucose-based system was the most sensitive to changes in product selling prices. This is because the oxidation reactions of other alternative reactants generally involve 6–8 electrons per reaction, whereas the oxidation reaction of glucose releases only 2 electrons when 1 mol of glucose reacts. Consequently, a larger amount of glucose participates in the reaction under the same electrical power conditions, resulting in a substantially higher production rate of gluconic acid. This high product yield leads to increased revenue from product sales, thereby amplifying the influence of market price fluctuations on the overall system economics. As a result, when product sales revenue was considered, the glucose-based hybrid water electrolysis system achieved an LCOH as low as approximately 4.74 $ per kg H2.

Analysis of LCOH variation with co-product revenue.

Additionally, comprehensive sensitivity and uncertainty analyses were conducted to evaluate system variability beyond a single condition, considering both electrochemical factors, such as catalyst activity and degradation, and changes in economic value arising from market acceptance.

Fig. 14 presents the sensitivity of LCOH to variations in reactant conversion, product selectivity, overpotential, reactant price, product price, and electricity price. Variations in conversion were used to examine the potential economic impact of catalyst stability and deactivation, whereas changes in selectivity and overpotential were evaluated to capture the effects of side reactions and electrochemical losses associated with reaction kinetics, ohmic resistance, and mass transport. The base-case LCOH values followed the order: urea (7.84 $ per kg H2), HMF (22.70 $ per kg H2), glucose (32.42 $ per kg H2), glycerol (48.27 $ per kg H2). The sensitivity results for each system were expressed as percentage changes relative to its corresponding base-case LCOH.

Sensitivity analysis results (a) urea-based HWE system, (b) glycerol-based HWE system, (c) HMF-based HWE system, (d) glucose-based HWE system.

The analysis revealed that variations in reactant prices had the greatest impact on the LCOH across all systems. In particular, the glucose-based system exhibited the largest variation in LCOH with changes in reactant price because a larger amount of glucose participates in the system, resulting in a high proportion of reactant consumption within the overall process. In contrast, the urea-based system showed no change in LCOH with respect to product price variations because no economic benefit from product sales was considered in this system. Since selectivity is directly related to desired product yield, it exhibited a noticeable influence across all systems, particularly in systems with high production rates of value-added chemicals. Overall, these results indicate that the economic feasibility of hybrid water electrolysis systems can be influenced more significantly by reactant price and product market values than by the electrolysis performance itself.

Fig. 15 presents the LCOH uncertainty analysis results derived from the sensitivity analysis-based Monte Carlo simulation using PERT distribution with 100 000 iterations for each reactant. For all four systems, the distributions were right-skewed. The skewness values were 0.51, 0.30, 0.67, and 0.96 for the urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose-based systems. It indicates greater asymmetry for the HMF and glucose pathways because of their larger reactant and co-product price uncertainties.

LCOH uncertainty analysis results (a) urea-based HWE system, (b) glycerol-based HWE system, (c) HMF-based HWE system, (d) glucose-based HWE system.

The coefficients of variation followed the order glycerol (0.07), urea (0.15), HMF (0.38), and glucose (0.53). Thus, the glycerol-based system exhibited the lowest relative variability, whereas the glucose-based system showed the greatest uncertainty in its economic performance. Also, the interquartile range of the urea-based system (7.68–9.53 $ per kg H2) did not overlap with those of the other systems, confirming its economic advantage even under combined parameter uncertainty. Conversely, the glycerol-based system (47.14–52.02 $ per kg H2) remained the least economical. The substantial overlap between the HMF and glucose distributions indicates that their relative economic ranking cannot be determined conclusively under the assumed uncertainties.

To analyze the relationship between the extent of catalyst degradation and LCOH, the time-dependent variation in LCOH was evaluated as a function of the rate of change in current density. Because quantitative catalyst-degradation data for the electrochemical oxidation of urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose under alkaline conditions remain limited, a common degradation profile was applied to all four systems to analysis the impact of catalyst deactivation.56 The profile was derived from an alkaline-electrode stability study reporting a decrease in current density during 180 h of continuous operation. The normalized current-density loss was applied as the degradation factor at each time step, while all other techno-economic parameters, including reactant price, electricity price, overpotential, and CAPEX, were fixed at their base-case values.

As shown in Fig. 16, as catalyst deactivation progressed, LCOH increased in all systems. The glycerol-based system exhibited the smallest relative increase, corresponding to an increase of about 17%. In contrast, the LCOH increased for the glucose-based system and for the HMF-based system, representing increases of approximately 50% and 52%, respectively. These differences are consistent with the cost structures of the individual systems. The glycerol-based system shows relatively low LCOH sensitivity to changes in conversion and selectivity, while its economics are dominated by product-separation costs, which account for approximately 82% of its LCOH. Consequently, changes in catalyst activity exert a comparatively limited influence on its total cost. By contrast, the economic performance of the HMF- and glucose-based systems depends more strongly on co-product recovery and revenue. Therefore, degradation-induced reductions in electrochemical performance and product yield lead to a greater increase in LCOH. After approximately 110–130 h, the LCOH curves of these systems become noticeably steeper, indicating a region in which further catalyst deactivation causes a more pronounced deterioration in economic performance. Overall, the impact of catalyst deactivation on LCOH depends on each system's cost structure. The glycerol-based system is relatively robust, whereas the HMF- and glucose-based systems are more sensitive to losses in catalyst activity and product selectivity.

LCOH variation with catalyst deactivation.

Fig. 17 presents the sensitivity of LCOH to operational penalties associated with catalyst deactivation, including accelerated current-density decline, reduced annual availability due to cleaning and shutdowns, and increased maintenance and replacement costs. The yellow region represents the range from the moderate scenario at the left boundary to the severe scenario at the right boundary. As the degradation conditions became more severe, the LCOH increased in all four systems. The glucose-based systems exhibited the largest variations, indicating greater vulnerability to catalyst deactivation and the associated loss of selectivity and operating availability. In contrast, the glycerol-based system showed a relatively limited response because its LCOH is primarily governed by downstream separation costs. These results demonstrate that catalyst fouling, maintenance-related shutdowns, and premature component replacement can substantially reduce the expected economic benefit of HWE, particularly for systems dependent on stable co-product yields.

Life cycle assessment

The comparison results of the cell voltage reduction effect resulting from the application of alternative oxidation reactions and the corresponding reduction in greenhouse gas emissions per 1 kg of hydrogen production are presented in Fig. 18. The life cycle GHG emissions of conventional renewable energy-based alkaline water electrolysis systems have been reported to be approximately 2.9 kg CO2 eq per kg H2.8 In this study, this value was set as the reference value (100%) to compare the relative greenhouse gas emission reduction effects of each hybrid water electrolysis system. The analysis results showed that the glucose-based system exhibited the highest GHG emission reduction effect. This is mainly attributed to the fact that the glucose-based system achieved the highest hydrogen production rate under identical power input conditions. In other words, producing a larger amount of hydrogen under the same operating conditions reduces the GHG emissions per unit hydrogen production, indicating that improvements in hydrogen production efficiency directly contribute to enhanced environmental performance. These results demonstrate that the reduction in cell voltage and enhancement of hydrogen production achieved through alternative oxidation reactions in hybrid water electrolysis systems can provide not only energy-saving benefits but also positive effects on reducing life cycle GHG emissions.

Scenario analysis

The environmental value of HWE can be further enhanced by sourcing reactants through circular-economy pathways. For example, recovering urea from wastewater can avoid the energy-intensive conventional synthesis process, while simultaneously contributing to wastewater treatment. The use of wastewater-derived urea reduced the LCOH by 35.7% because the recovery pathway involved only extraction, separation, and purification, without the high energy demand of conventional urea synthesis. This substantial reduction is consistent with the cost breakdown in Fig. 12, where the reactant cost accounts for approximately 46.4% of the total LCOH. These combined improvements highlight the importance of resource recovery and reuse in advancing the circular economy and improving the overall sustainability of hybrid water electrolysis.

The scale-dependent viability of the HWE systems was evaluated to assess their potential for industrial application under different renewable-energy capacities. Because the available renewable power can vary considerably depending on topographical conditions, LCOH was compared at system scales of 1 kW, 1 MW, and 10 MW as shown in Fig. 19. In general, LCOH decreased with increasing system scale because of economies of scale. The glycerol-, HMF-, and glucose-based systems exhibited variations of up to approximately 24% across the evaluated scales. This behavior is mainly attributable to the nonlinear scaling of the reactors, tanks, and other equipment used in product separation, for which unit costs change with equipment size and volume. In contrast, the urea-based system showed relatively limited scale dependence because its cost structure is dominated by reactant purchase and the electrolyzer itself. The reactant requirement increases approximately in proportion to system capacity as well as alkaline water electrolysis systems are modular and can be scaled by increasing the number of connected cells and stacks. Consequently, their capital cost increases more linearly with capacity. These results demonstrate how system scale affects the economic viability of each hybrid water electrolysis pathway.

Co-product market analysis

Under the proposed 1 MW hybrid water electrolysis systems, the annual co-product outputs were estimated to be 2 383 582 kg of 85 wt% formic acid solution, 2 892 216 kg of FDCA, and 31,933,465 kg of 50 wt% gluconic acid solution. Future works

Although sulfuric acid-based acidification processes and an ion exchange resin methodology were applied for product recovery in this study, future technological advancements may enable the application of alternative separation and purification technologies such as Bipolar Membrane Electrodialysis, allowing comparative analyses in terms of chemical consumption, process circularity, and energy demand. In addition, the environmental assessment should be expanded beyond global warming potential to include other relevant impact categories, such as acidification potential, eutrophication potential, terrestrial ecotoxicity, and human toxicity, thereby enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of the environmental performance of hybrid water electrolysis systems.

Conclusions

HWE technology employing anodic alternative oxidation reactions has recently attracted significant attention as a next-generation hydrogen production technology capable of simultaneously reducing electricity consumption for water electrolysis while producing value-added chemicals. In this study, the production performance, techno-economic feasibility, and life cycle greenhouse gas emission characteristics of 1 MW-scale alkaline hybrid water electrolysis systems employing urea, glycerol, HMF, and glucose were comparatively analyzed through Aspen Plus-based process modeling. The analysis results showed that all hybrid water electrolysis systems exhibited higher hydrogen production rates than conventional alkaline water electrolysis systems. In particular, the glucose-based system demonstrated the highest hydrogen and co-product production rates. From the techno-economic analysis, the urea-based system exhibited the lowest LCOH under low-revenue conditions without considering product sales revenue, whereas the glucose-based system showed the best economic performance under high-revenue conditions when product sales revenue was included. In addition, the environmental assessment revealed that the glucose-based system achieved the greatest greenhouse gas emission reduction effect. The results of this study demonstrate the potential of hybrid water electrolysis systems as integrated electrochemical platforms capable of simultaneously achieving hydrogen production and the valorization of biomass-derived resources into value-added chemicals.
