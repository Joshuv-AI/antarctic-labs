Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

We will explore the improvement of the electrocatalytic efficiency of CO₂ conversion to ethylene
by coating part of the surface of Cu nanoparticle electrodes with ion-conductng polymers. These
polymers will be produced by depositing monomers onto the electrodes and polymerizing them
via an external stimulus.

Background and Strategy:

The efficiency of electrochemical conversion of CO2 to useful products is a strong function of
ion conduction at the electrode interface. A recent publication by Gewirth et al.1 demonstrated
that polymers could form a useful interface to improve both surface reactivity and selectivity on
co-electrodeposited electrodes consisting of nano copper coated with various polyacrylamides
with amino functionality. The most important conclusion drawn from this study is that the
polymer structure was directly responsible for CO2RR (CO2 Reduction Reaction) selectivity
towards C2 products. This publication reported the highest ever Faradaic efficiency for ethylene
(C2) of about 87% at -0.47 V and a full cell energetic efficiency of about 50%. However, this
approach is not scalable because of both the alkaline conditions employed (which cause a
buildup of carbonates in the cell over time) and the Cu/polymer electrodeposition process
employed.

NREL’s design of electrochemical cells includes the use of mesoporous copper nanoparticles as
the cathode where CO2RR takes place (NREL unpublished results, recently submitted for
publication; Neyerlin et al.). This approach is more scalable than Gewirth’s process. Since
CO2RR occurs on the Cu surface, the surface area of the Cu-nanoparticles represents the quantity
of catalytic sites available for CO2RR. Therefore, etched Cu-nanoparticles designed with high
porosity can be employed to maximize surface area for maximum efficiency. However, CO2RR
also relies on the concentration of protons at the Cu surface. Protons, which are generated from a
variety of anodic processes, must be continuously transported to the Cu -cathode. To this end,
proton-conducting polymers, such as Nafion, have often been employed. The conventional
approach has been to physically mix polymer with Cu-nanoparticles in an ink, hoping the Cu will
be homogenously coated. However, most of the Cu surface area exists inside narrow pores (> 5
nm) and cannot be coated with polymer simply because the polymer is too large to penetrate in.
This results in a very small percentage of the surface actually being coated. To support this
conclusion, we have recently produced results that this typical Nafion/Cu-nanoparticle system
relies more on moisture for ion transport than the Nafion. Thus, our intention is to redesign the
construction of the electrode so that the totality of the Cu surface can be coated with ion-
conducting polymers. To this end, we aim to coat Cu-nanoparticles, with monomer instead of
polymer thoroughly by a vacuum impregnation process. Monomers should be small and mobile
enough to penetrate into the pores to evenly coat the entire surface area. Then, by some external
stimulus, we will trigger polymerization to yield fully coated Cu-nanoparticles. We call this
Polymer Last (PL) as opposed to the conventional process, which we call Polymer First (PF).
See Figure 1 for a schematic view of these PF and PL processes. We started with simple
monomers such as acrylic acid, which is not known to be an exceptional ion conductor but
should be a rather simple candidate to benchmark the coating process. We then evaluate the
polyacrylic acid (PAA)-coated Cu-nanoparticles by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), cyclic
voltammetry, and impedance spectroscopy to determine the efficacy and reliability of our
coating process. If we can claim proof of concept, we will employ more sophisticated monomers
containing functional groups known to be good proton conductors and facilitators of CO2RR,

 2

such as phosphonic acid and sulfonic acid groups (styrene sulfonic acid as one commercially
available example). If successful, we can then easily deploy a combinatorial approach to screen
many ion-conducting polymers to study polymer structure/electrochemical performance
relationships and ultimately improve CO2RR kinetics/selectivity in a scalable and practical
system.

Task 1 – Survey of Ion-Conducting Polymers and Development of Polymer Structure-Property

Task 1.1 – Synthesize and characterize a series of Ion-Conducting Polymers

Task 1.2 – Coat Polymers synthesized in Task 1.1 on NREL’s copper electrodes and assess

Tasks 1.1, 1.2 and 1.3: From the start of this Phase 1 (demonstration) project, our goal was to
establish a synthetic methodology for producing Cu-nanoparticle (CuNP) evenly coated
(including inside the nano-porous structure) with functionalized polymer for applications in
electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR). Standard approaches to CO2RR are generally
characterized by direct mixing of functionalized polymer (ionomers or ion-conducting polymers)
with CuNPs in a solvent to make an ink (we call Polymer First, or PF). The ink is then deposited
onto porous carbon paper gas diffusion electrode (GDE) through one of many methods, such as
spray coating or roll coating. Recent works have led to the understanding that this coating
methodology matters immensely and that the performance of a CO2RR system is dependent on
the electrode fabrication method. Since it is assumed that CO2RR occurs on the CuNP surface
and is governed to some degree by the functionalized polymer (whether that be by ion
conduction, maintenance of local charges, or maintenance of the local pH), we conclude that

 3

different electrode fabrication methods result in different amounts of interaction between CuNPs
and functionalized polymer (coverage) based on how CuNP/polymer phases self-assemble or
arrange themselves during the electrode fabrication process.

To address this issue, we set out to design a process by which CuNPs could be reliably and
uniformly coated with the functionalized polymer. To this end, we envisioned a scenario
where a monomer (instead of a polymer) could be mixed with CuNPs in an ink, followed by
evaporation of solvent to produce a monomer-coated CuNP. The monomer coating the CuNP
could then be triggered by some stimulus (in this case heat) to polymerize over the surface of the
CuNP (PL). For this concept demonstration, we chose free radical polymerization (FRP) of
(meth)acrylamides, as they are diversely functionalized and commercially available, are
electrochemically stable, and have been shown in the past to facilitate CO 2RR.i Additionally,
FRP can be triggered by the thermal decomposition of initiators such as azobisisobutyronitrile
(AIBN), giving us the convenience and control to initiate polymerization only once the monomer
deposition onto the CuNP has been completed.

After screening many monomer choices, we settled on 3-dimethylaminopropyl methacrylamide
(1), shown in Figure 2. This monomer was chosen since it is a viscous liquid at room
temperature (RT) and exhibits low volatility at temperatures above 65°C, making it convenient
for thermal FRP by AIBN. Its corresponding polymer, Poly (3-dimethylaminopropyl
methacrylamide) (P1), is a stable vinyl polymer with a high glass transition temperature
(~150oC) and a pendant amine group, which we thought might provide some electrochemical
functionality. A typical PL synthesis would involve the mixing of CuNP with 1 (6 wt% relative
to CuNP) and AIBN (1 wt% relative to 1) in methanol in a Schlenk flask. Then, a vacuum was
used at RT (for 2 h) to remove methanol, leaving a CuNP presumably coated with 1 and AIBN.
The Schlenk flask was then refilled with N2, and polymerization was induced by heating to 65°C
in a sonicator bath for 12 h. Sonication was used to ensure constant dispersion of monomer in
case of a speculative event where monomer sinks to the bottom or consolidates into its own
phase. The final product was stored in the Schlenk flask under N2 until further use.

 Figure 2. General synthesis for a CuNP-P1-PL sample.

 4

In general, any PL CuNP sample was prepared alongside its PF counterpart (tasks 1.1 and 1.3).
The method for making a PF sample was to prepare a mixture of 1 with AIBN (1 wt% relative to
1) in a known amount of solvent, usually methanol or methanol/H2O. The mixture was prepared
in a Schlenk flask and degassed with several vacuum/N 2 cycles until finally pressurized with N2.
The mixture was then heated to 65oC for 12 h. At this point, NMR was used to confirm full
conversion of monomer. If conversion was complete, a known amount of the solution
corresponding to a desired mass of P1 was added to a known amount of CuNPs. The
CuNP/P1/solvent mixture was sonicated for several hours before finally removing the solvent by
vacuum and finally pressurized with N2 for storage until later use.

Two variations on P1 were synthesized using the hexa-functionalized phosphoric acid molecule
phytic acid (PA) as a dynamic crosslinker and sulfonic acid containing 2-Acrylamido-2-
methylpropane sulfonic acid (AMPS) as a dynamic crosslinking comonomer, as shown in Figure
3 below. Both these variations presumably react with 1 to form a conjugate acid/base pair, which
forms electrostatic bonds. These bonds are dynamic and do not prevent P1/PA polymers or
P1/AMPS copolymers from being soluble in methanol or methanol/H2O in the case of 1/AMPS.
These samples were not extensively characterized electrochemically. However, we did
characterize them by SEM and thus demonstrate that these types of alterations are available to
the synthetic method and could presumably have enhanced ion conduction (and so would be
interesting for this electrochemical application).

  Figure 3. Variations on the PL synthesis using multifunctional acid PA and acidic comonomer
 AMPS. Task 1.4 (stretch target)

 5

A successful PL polymerization was evaluated on the basis of H NMR and scanning electron
microscopy (SEM). In the case of H NMR, 20 mg of CuNP-P1-PL was added to an NMR tube
with 0.5 mL of D2O. Polymer peaks were observed between 1 and 3 ppm. In the event of an
unsuccessful polymerization, monomer peaks would be observed from 5 to 6 ppm. In terms of
qualitative analysis using SEM (Figure 4), polymer could usually be visually observed by
comparison of polymer/CuNP samples to that of blank CuNPs. The CuNPs used in this study
were about 100 nm in diameter; thus, high magnification (50,000 to 100,000x) was generally
used. Energy-dispersive X-ray analysis (EDAX) was attempted but was generally unhelpful.

 to right) CuNP-P1/PA-PL, CuNP-P1/AMPS-PL, CuNP-P1/AMPS-PF. All samples in this figure
 contain 6 wt% polymer relative to CuNPs with the exception of Blank CuNPs. (Task 1.2)

Electrochemical Characterization (tasks 1.2-1.3)

The first samples tested were CuNP-1-PF and CuNP-1-PL at 6 wt% relative to CuNP. These
electrodes were spray coated onto porous carbon GDE with loadings around 0.1-0.2 mg/cm2.
Electrochemical tests were performed in a 25 cm2 custom cell in a zero-gap configuration. In this
configuration, the Cu cathode is in direct contact with the anion exchange membrane (see Figure
5). The anode was a Pt-coated Ti mesh with a layer of IrO2 and 0.1 M KHCO3 was recirculated
at the anode. The gas inlet stream was CO2 at 95% relative humidity, and the cell temperature
was 45 °C.

 6

The electrochemical CO2RR performance of these electrodes was not spectacular but still
provided valuable insight. The CuNP-1-PF sample provided a maximum 10% faradaic efficiency
(FE) for ethylene production at 300 mA/cm2 with lower ethylene production at higher/lower
current densities. Meanwhile, the CuNP-1-PL counterpart produced no ethylene at any tested
current density. One possible explanation for this result is that PL methodology coats the CuNPs
extremely well. So well, in fact, that trace or minimal CO2 can access the Cu. Likewise, because
the CuNP surface is thoroughly coated with an electron-insulative polymer, the CuNPs might not
be electrochemically accessible. While the produced CO is catalyzed by the Cu, the surface
coverage of the CO is likely too low to promote the CO dimerization needed to form C2+
products like ethylene.ii The same samples (CuNP-P1-PL and CuNP-P1-PF, 6 wt%) were then
rod coated onto the same GDE material. This technique allows for much higher loadings of
around 1.1-1.3 mg/cm2. In this case, no ethylene was produced for either PF or PL sample in a
CO2 reduction experiment. Furthermore, no CO was produced in either sample. This is an odd
result that supports the conclusion that P1 limits either the electrochemical accessibility or the
mass transport of the CO2 to the Cu surface. However, since both PF and PL samples gave
similar values, this result is generally inconclusive.

  Figure 5. Electrochemical performance of CuNP-P1-PF and CuNP-P1-PF (6 wt%) electrodes for
 CO2RR in a zero gap cell. (Task 1.2)

Next, we performed coverage diagnostics using electrical impedance spectroscopy. Coverage
metrics are quantified as a ratio of the capacitance at 10% relative humidity (RH) and 100% RH.
In other words, when the CuNPs are totally soaked in water at 100% RH, all of the CuNP surface
area is theoretically available for electrochemical capacitance since water can easily transport
ions to the Cu surface. This total capacitance is measured experimentally with electrochemical
impedance spectroscopy. Then, when the moisture is nearly completely removed (RH = 10%),
the capacitance is measured again. This second measured capacitance at RH = 10 represents the
Cu surface that is capable of transferring charge through the polymer coating. The ratio of these
two capacitances is the normalized coverage of the ionomer as it essentially normalizes the
amount of Cu covered by the polymer by the total Cu surface area. The critical assumption here
is that there is no pathway for ions to transport to the Cu surface except through the polymer
coating. Therefore, the ionic conductivity does somewhat affect the results. In this case, we
compared the CuNP-1-PF and CuNP-1-PL samples (at 6 wt% loading) directly to each other,
somewhat eliminating the ionic-conductivity variable. The result was that the CuNP-1-PF
measured 5% coverage while CuNP-1-PL measured 16% coverage. This 3-fold increase is strong
evidence that the PL method gives a more even and uniform polymer coating over the CuNP
surface when compared to PF.

 Figure 6. Coverage diagnostics for CuNP-P1-PL and CuNP-P1-PF electrodes (6 wt%). (tasks 1.2-1.3)

We hypothesized that 6 wt% relative to CuNP was too much loading for this polymer since it
clearly does not productively facilitate electron transport and possibly blocks CO 2 from reaching
the Cu surface. Thus, we decided to reduce the loading to 0.5 wt% P1 relative to CuNPs. In this
case, the PL electrode was compared to a fluorinated polyethylene/polypropylene copolymer
(FEP) at the same loading of 0.5 wt%. This CuNP/FEP electrode can essentially be thought of as
blank CuNPs since the fluorinated polymer is highly apolar and contributes no appreciable
electrochemical activity. When compared to the CuNP-FEP-PF electrode, the CuNP-1-PL
electrode produces roughly half the amount of CO at all current densities. Since CO2 reduction to
CO is presumed to occur on the surface of the CuNP, and since hydrogen formation is known to
occur on the carbon GDM or the Cu surface, it is fair to conclude that 1 is blocking CO2 from
interacting with CuNPs. While this blocking feature is not ideal for CO2RR, the result speaks to
the effectiveness of the PL method. The fact that CO production is cut nearly in half at even
such a low polymer loading of 0.5 wt%, is evidence of an extremely even and total coating
of the CuNPs by 1 during the PL process.

Figure 7. Electrochemical performance of CuNP-P1-PL and CuNP-FEP-PF (0.5 wt%) electrodes for
                                      CO2RR. (Task 1.3)
Lastly, we performed some electrode diagnostics for the 0.5 wt% loaded CuNP-1-PL sample
compared to CuNP-FEP-PF. For this experiment, we wanted to measure the ability of ions to
transport charge through the CuNPs in the electrode, which we can understand through the

 8

parameter RCL,OH (W-cm2) or the resistance to charge transfer in the catalyst layer. We have
previously correlated low values of RCL,OH to increased production of C2+ products from carbon
monoxide reduction. This parameter can be influenced by the porosity of the electrode and the
ionomer polymer.iii The electrode with 0.5% P1 gave a value of 27.1 W-cm2, compared to a
value of 58.5 W-cm2 from the 0.5% FEP Teflon electrode. While the P1 electrode has increased
charge transport through the catalyst layer, these values are still higher than high- performing
electrodes with values of <8 W-cm2.(ref) The discrepancy in the catalytic capability for the P1
and FEP Teflon electrodes and the RCL,OH values may be due to the hydrophobicity of the FEP,
since hydrophobicity has been shown to promote CO2R.iii

Conclusion: While P1 is not a great polymer for facilitating CO2RR, we are confident that the
PL methodology is highly effective for producing evenly and uniformly coated CuNPs as is
clearly shown in Figure 6. This conclusion is reinforced by SEM qualitative analysis, coverage
diagnostics through impedance spectroscopy, lack of ethylene production for CuNP-P1-PL vs
CuNP-P1-PF (6wt% vs 6 wt%) in a zero gap 25cm2 CO2 reduction cell, and substantial
reduction in CO production for CuNP-P1-PL vs CuNP-FEP-PF (0.5 wt% vs 0.5 wt%). The next
logical step would be to translate this Polymer Last methodology to a polymer with higher
activity for CO2RR.
