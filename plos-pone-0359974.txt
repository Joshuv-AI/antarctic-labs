Environmental, developmental, and genetic factors differentially affect rapid ethylene growth response kinetics in dark-grown Arabidopsis thaliana hypocotyls

Arkadipta Bakshi, Vipsa Patel, Andrew Ward, Mahbuba Ferdous, Brad M Binder

ABSTRACT
Studies on dark-grown wild-type Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings have demonstrated that the hypocotyls respond to the application and removal of the hormone ethylene with stereotypical growth kinetics. This includes a rapid decrease in growth rate when ethylene is applied that stabilizes at a low growth rate approximately 75 min after the addition of saturating levels of ethylene. Hypocotyl growth rate recovers to pre-treatment levels approximately 80 minutes after removal of ethylene. The growth of plants is influenced by environmental conditions that can vary between laboratories as well as genetic backgrounds and developmental factors. To address the importance of these variables on ethylene responses, experiments were conducted to determine the effects of composition of growth medium, seed size, seedling age, and accession on ethylene growth response kinetics. These variables led to only minor alterations in growth inhibition kinetics when ethylene was added with the largest effect being an acceleration in growth inhibition when nutrients were excluded from the medium. By contrast, growth recovery kinetics upon ethylene removal showed more plasticity. Sucrose accelerated growth recovery, whereas other environmental variables delayed recovery compared to controls. Other variables that are often overlooked, such as seed size and seedling age, also affected growth recovery where older seedlings recovered more slowly and plants from larger seeds recovered faster than plants from smaller seeds. Accessions from more northern latitudes tended to recover faster than those from more southern latitudes. Our data indicate that these factors need to be carefully controlled for optimal reproducibility of responses to ensure accurate interpretation of data and make sure important traits are not masked.

Introduction Ethylene is a hormone that affects the development, growth, and stress responses of plants [ 1 ]. One response to ethylene, the triple response, occurs when dark-grown eudicot seedlings are treated with ethylene for several days. In Arabidopsis thaliana this response is characterized by reduced elongation of the hypocotyl and root, increased diameter of the hypocotyl, and increased apical hook curvature [ 2 – 4 ]. Changes in physiological responses from specific mutations are often used to determine the role of a gene. For instance, the decreased growth of the roots and hypocotyls that occurs during the triple response are easily quantified and have been used as an assay for several decades to screen for and characterize ethylene-related mutants leading to much of what we know about ethylene biosynthesis and signal transduction [ 5 , 6 ]. More nuanced information has been obtained by examining the rapid growth responses of hypocotyls of dark-grown A. thaliana seedlings to the application and removal of ethylene. These studies uncovered new details about ethylene receptor function and sub-functionalization [ 7 – 14 ], down-stream signaling components [ 15 – 22 ], apical hook control [ 23 , 24 ], high sensitivity to ethylene [ 25 ], and inhibitors of ethylene perception [ 12 , 26 ]. However, to determine the roles of genes in physiological responses, it is necessary to minimize environmental and developmental variables that affect physiological responses. For instance, various factors such as nutrient availability, seed size, and seedling age affect root growth and gravitropism in A. thaliana [ 27 , 28 ]. Without properly controlling for these variables, the roles of a gene can be masked or misinterpreted. With this in mind, we studied the effects of genetic background, seedling age, seed size, and growth medium on rapid ethylene growth response kinetics to the application and removal of ethylene. Results from this study indicate that growth inhibition kinetics when ethylene is added are less sensitive to changes in these variables than growth recovery kinetics after removal of ethylene. Our data show that these factors need to be carefully controlled to avoid introducing variability in the results that could affect interpretation of the data. Results Effects of growth medium on ethylene growth response kinetics To achieve better experimental reproducibility and understanding about the factors that control ethylene responses, we examined the effects of variations in the growth media on ethylene growth response kinetics. We defined control medium as 0.8% (w/v) plant agar (Sigma, A4800), half-strength Murashige and Skoog basal medium with Gamborg’s vitamins at pH 5.7 with no further additions. For controls, we used 250−300 µm diameter Columbia seeds and analyzed them 2 days after light treatment. Under these conditions, the hypocotyl growth rate prior to application of ethylene was 0.27 ± 0.02 mm h -1 ( Table 1 ). Ethylene response kinetics to a 2 hour exposure to 10 ppm ethylene were similar to prior studies conducted under the same conditions [ 17 ]. This included two phases of growth inhibition when ethylene was added ( Fig 1A ). The first phase was a rapid deceleration in growth followed by a plateau in growth rate. Following this plateau was a second, slower onset deceleration in growth which reached a new steady-state growth rate approximately 75 min after application of ethylene. Growth recovery was first detectable approximately 35 min after removal of ethylene as determined by cumulative sum control (CUSUM) chart analysis to examine deviations in trends of growth. Full recovery to pre-treatment growth rates occurred approximately 80 min after ethylene removal. The growth rate remained higher than pre-treatment rates for several hours. Many studies examining ethylene responses include L-α-(2-amino ethoxyvinyl)-glycine (AVG) to block endogenous ethylene production. Inclusion of 5 µM AVG significantly increased hypocotyl growth rate in the absence of ethylene ( Table 1 ). This likely reflects inhibition of endogenous ethylene production. Despite the higher basal growth rate, the inhibition kinetics were slightly accelerated in the presence of AVG ( Fig 1A ). The latency for growth recovery was slightly delayed from controls where it took 50 min for growth recovery to be detectable by CUSUM. By contrast, the rate of recovery was approximately 50% faster than controls. These differences during growth recovery led to a similar time of growth recovery to pre-treatment growth rates compared to controls. In the presence of 5 µM AVG, there was a growth rate overshoot that then returned to pre-treatment levels approximately 2.75 h after removal of ethylene. In the absence of AVG, the growth rate remained elevated for a longer time. Sucrose is a common additive to plant medium to provide energy and promote growth. Addition of 0.8% (w/v) sucrose did not significantly alter the hypocotyl growth rate under these conditions ( Table 1 ) and growth inhibition kinetics in the presence of sucrose were similar to controls ( Fig 1B ). By contrast, after removal of ethylene, the seedlings grown on sucrose recovered much faster than controls taking approximately 25 min to reach pre-treatment growth rates. These seedlings then had an overshoot in the growth rate followed by an oscillation in growth rate. Growth recovery after removal of ethylene was first detectable approximately 20 min sooner than controls. Because of these results with sucrose and prior observations showing that availability of nutrients affects gravitropism of A. thaliana [ 27 , 28 ], we were curious to know if low nutrient availability would affect ethylene growth response kinetics. When seedlings were grown on 0.8% (w/v) plant agar with no other additions, the growth rate before application of ethylene was not significantly affected ( Table 1 ). However, the seedlings showed faster growth inhibition and an apparent single phase of growth inhibition with no plateau in growth rate prior to full growth inhibition ( Fig 1B ). A similar acceleration in inhibition kinetics was previously described for seedlings lacking the EIN3-Binding F-Box1 gene, which is important in ethylene signaling [ 16 ]. After removal of ethylene, recovery took longer than control conditions. Different types of agar vary in purity and the components present. We therefore also examined responses using 0.8% (w/v) Phytoagar (A20300, Research Products International Corp.) and obtained similar results to those with plant agar. We also examined the effects of pH on ethylene growth inhibition and recovery kinetics by comparing responses at pH 4.5, 5.7, 7.0, and 9.5 ( Fig 1C ). Hypocotyl growth rates in the absence of ethylene were similar for all pHs except at pH 9.5, where the growth rate was significantly reduced ( Table 1 ). Responses to the application of ethylene at pH 4.5 and 7.0 were similar to responses at pH 5.7. Recovery after removal of ethylene took longer at both pH 4.5 and 7.0 with no overshoot in growth rate and at later timepoints, incomplete recovery to pre-treatment growth rates. At pH 9.5, it was difficult to accurately assess growth inhibition kinetics because of noise in the data. With this limitation, it appears that the kinetics of inhibition are similar to what is seen at other pH values. Growth recovery after ethylene removal was strongly impacted at pH 9.5 and did not recover to the pre-treatment rate in the time-course of the experiments. The slow recovery was due to both a longer latency (3 h longer than controls) and a slower rate of recovery (approximately 9-fold slower than controls). Effects of seed size on ethylene growth response kinetics A. thaliana seed size affects various traits such as germination time, root growth, root gravitropism, and flowering time [ 27 , 28 ]. We therefore wished to know whether ethylene response kinetics were affected by seed size. To examine this, we mechanically sieved seeds from a single batch of seeds to give large seeds (> 300 µm in diameter), medium seeds (250–300 µm in diameter) as used for all other experiments described in this study, and small seeds (< 250 µm in diameter). The hypocotyl growth rates prior to treatment with ethylene were similar for seedlings from large and medium seeds but were statistically reduced for small seeds ( Table 1 ). The growth inhibition kinetics were similar for seedlings from all three sized seeds ( Fig 2A ). However, the recovery kinetics after removal of ethylene were different between the three populations. The seedlings from large seeds recovered the fastest and had a large overshoot in growth rate compared to medium seeds. The large seed seedling growth rates plateaued at a higher value than the pre-treatment rate. The plants from small seeds took longer to recovery than the other two seed populations. Effects of seedling age on ethylene growth response kinetics Our prior studies examined ethylene growth kinetics at 2 d after light treatment [ 7 , 25 ] and this was the control timing used for other experiments in the current study. Under the conditions used in this study in darkness, seedlings germinate at approximately 32 h after light treatment [ 29 ]. We wished to determine if seedling age affected ethylene response and recovery kinetics. We therefore examined the ethylene growth response kinetics of seedlings 3 d and 4 d after light treatment ( Fig 2B ). The hypocotyl growth rate of 4 d seedlings in the absence of ethylene was lower for these older seedlings and likely reflects lower seed storage molecules for growth. The growth inhibition kinetics were similar for the three populations. The 3 d seedlings had recovery kinetics similar to 2 d seedlings. By contrast, the 4 d seedlings took longer to recover. Initially, the 4 d seedlings had similar recovery kinetics to the controls. But, at approximately 1 h after ethylene removal, the rate of recovery slowed to approximately 33% of the controls. Ethylene growth response kinetics of different accessions The above results indicate that environmental and developmental factors can affect ethylene growth response kinetics. Since ethylene growth response kinetics vary between species [ 17 ], we also wished to determine if different A. thaliana accessions have different responses to the application and removal of ethylene. We chose accessions from widely disparate geographical regions and used seeds 250−300 µm in diameter. All of the accessions had similar growth rates in air ( Table 1 ) and similar growth inhibition kinetics when ethylene was applied ( Fig 3 ). However, there were differences in growth recovery when ethylene was removed. Wassilewskija (WS-2) and Landsberg erecta (Ler-2) recovered to pre-treatment growth rates slightly faster than Columbia and had an overshoot in growth rate. By contrast, Cape Verde Islands (Cvi-0) and Shahdara (Sha) recovered slower and plateaued at growth rates slightly lower than the pre-treatment rates. When examining these recovery times relative to latitude of origin, accessions from more southern latitudes tended to take longer to recover than those from more northern latitudes ( Fig 4A ). No such correlation occurred relative to the longitudes of origin ( Fig 4B ). Discussion In this study, we demonstrate that environmental, developmental, and genetic factors affect hypocotyl growth and ethylene growth response kinetics of dark-grown A. thaliana hypocotyls. These variables generally cause only minor alterations in growth inhibition kinetics when ethylene is added. By contrast, growth recovery kinetics show more variability. This is consistent with prior studies showing that growth recovery kinetics after ethylene removal are more highly impacted by genetic factors than inhibition kinetics [ 7 , 11 , 13 , 15 – 17 , 19 – 22 , 30 ]. The basis for this higher plasticity in response is not known. With a few exceptions, hypocotyl growth rates are consistent across the variations in environmental conditions used in this study, yet growth recovery after removal is often affected. This indicates that there is not a correlation between basal hypocotyl growth rate prior to application of ethylene and recovery of growth after removal of ethylene. For instance, application of AVG leads to approximately a 50% increase in hypocotyl growth rate which, no doubt, is due to the inhibition of ethylene biosynthesis by this chemical. This is consistent with increased growth in response to application of AVG that was previously measured with end-point analysis [ 31 ]. However, AVG causes only subtle changes in the time for growth recovery which might be due to altered ethylene biosynthesis, but could also be due to other effects of AVG such as reduced auxin biosynthesis [ 32 ]. Exogenous sucrose did not affect the growth rate in the absence of ethylene, but accelerated growth recovery. In contrast, growth recovery of seedlings in the absence of nutrients took longer than controls, even though the basal growth rate in the absence of ethylene was unchanged. A similar lack of correlation between basal growth rate and recovery kinetics after removal of ethylene was previously observed, where loss of specific ethylene receptors or several other components of ethylene signaling, slowed down growth recovery, but had little effect on growth rates in the absence of ethylene [ 7 , 16 ]. Nutrient content of the medium also affects growth recovery. In the absence of nutrients, growth recovery is delayed, even though basal growth before application of ethylene is minimally affected. In contrast, sucrose leads to much faster growth recovery after removal of ethylene. Higher carbon availability for energy is one explanation for faster recovery in the presence of sucrose. However, a confounding factor is that exogenous sucrose leads to increased ethylene biosynthesis by plants [ 33 , 34 ], which is predicted to lead to slower growth and growth recovery. Another complicating factor is that there is a complex interplay between sucrose and ethylene signaling to regulate growth and development of A. thaliana [ 34 – 38 ]. This makes it difficult to model the mechanisms by which exogenous sucrose affects growth recovery. Varying pH between 4.5 and 7.0 does not significantly alter hypocotyl growth rate. It is only at the extremely high pH of 9.5 that the growth rate is significantly reduced. By contrast, variations in these external variables slows down hypocotyl growth recovery after ethylene is removed. Delays in growth recovery are seen when seedlings are grown at pH values that diverge from pH 5.7. Although the most extreme delay is seen at pH 9.5, delays in growth recovery also occur at pH 7.0 and 4.5 compared to pH 5.7. The mechanisms underlying these effects of pH on growth recovery are unknown. Since cell wall loosening is important for growth and is affected by pH [ 39 ], these effects of pH potentially involve alterations in apoplastic pH to impact cell wall loosening. The observation that growth rates in air are unaffected by most changes in pH argues against this idea. Ethylene growth response kinetics are affected by the levels of another plant hormone, gibberellin [ 17 ] and extracellular pH affects gibberellin accumulation [ 40 , 41 ] pointing to a potential role for gibberellin in mediating the differences observed in growth recovery at different pHs. Hypocotyl growth rates are slower in seedlings maintained in darkness for 4 d compared to the 2 d usually used. Smaller seeds also give rise to seedlings with lower growth rates. It is likely that these lower growth rates are due to reduced levels of seed storage oils and proteins for energy since experiments were conducted in darkness with no external source of carbon. Growth recovery after ethylene removal takes longer with older seedlings and seedlings from small seeds. Whether this slower recovery is due to lower seed resources is unclear since the characteristics of the slower growth recovery are different for small seed seedlings compared to older seedlings. For plants from small seeds, the longer time for growth recovery is due to a longer latency time before recovery starts, whereas, for older seedlings it is due to a slower rate of growth recovery. Thus, developmental factors affect ethylene growth recovery kinetics, but may do so via different mechanisms. Natural variation in A. thaliana has led to differences in many traits such as leaf and root architecture, flowering time, disease and stress resistance, and seed dormancy and has been used to explore differences in physiological responses in plants [ 42 – 44 ]. In this study, we show that ethylene growth inhibition kinetics upon application of ethylene are fairly similar between the different accessions examined. By contrast, the time for growth recovery after removal of ethylene differs between these accessions. Given that there is variation in ethylene growth response kinetics between species [ 17 ], this natural variation between accessions of A. thaliana is not surprising. Accessions from southern latitudes took longer to recover than those from farther north. It is unknown if this is a general feature or just applies to the accessions studied. It is also unclear what survival advantages might be conferred by faster growth recovery versus slower recovery. Differences in growth recovery might reflect variations in the optimal growth conditions for each accession. For instance, nutrient availability in the growth medium can alter phenotypic plasticity [ 28 ] and we see clear effects of nutrients in the medium on growth recovery rates of Columbia. This raises the possibility that the different accessions have differing nutrient requirements that affect growth recovery. If true, it is possible that the slower recovery of Cvi-0 and Sha can be accelerated to resemble the other accessions under different nutrient conditions. Another thing to consider is that the time for growth recovery is impacted by alterations in the ethylene receptors and other components of ethylene signal transduction [ 7 , 11 , 13 , 15 , 16 , 19 – 22 ]. Therefore, the differences in growth recovery between these accessions may be due to variations in the level of one or more component of ethylene signaling. This study demonstrates that various factors can measurably alter rapid ethylene growth response kinetics, especially growth recovery kinetics after removal of ethylene. This sensitivity of growth recovery kinetics to various environmental, developmental, and genetic factors adds a level of complexity that needs to be addressed when linking specific genes to alterations in ethylene growth kinetics. For example, a mutation that delays growth recovery could be doing so as a direct result of the mutation. However, if the mutation alters seed size or nutrient requirements, the alterations in ethylene responses might be indirect. The choice of specific conditions for a given study ultimately needs to be determined by the goals of the study. Some factors such as seed accession, pH, and medium composition are obvious factors to control. Other variables, such as seed size and seedling age, are not generally considered, but based on our results should be controlled for. It is also important to note that the composition of the medium has a large effect on ethylene response kinetics. This should be taken into consideration when designing experiments since some effects of ethylene can be partially masked or antagonized by external sugars including sucrose and glucose [ 45 – 47 ], indicating that important alterations in responses could be missed under specific conditions. Overall, our data show the need for carefully controlling various variables to most accurately assess the roles of genes in ethylene growth responses. Materials and methods All experiments were carried out on A. thaliana . Cvi-0 (CS6675), Sha (CS929), and Ler-2 (CS8581) seeds were obtained from the Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC). Accessions were chosen to represent a wide range of regions for the native population (Col and Ler, Germany; Cvi, Cave Verde Islands; Sha, Tajikistan; WS, Russia). In general, formerly described methods were used for time-lapse imaging and data analysis [ 7 ]. Seedling preparation and growth conditions Briefly, A. thaliana seeds were surface sterilized for 30 s with 70% (v/v) ethanol, dried on sterile filter paper, and then placed on agar plates. For controls, 250–300 µm diameter Columbia seeds were plated on 0.8% (w/v) plant agar plates (A4800, Sigma) containing half-strength Murashige and Skoog basal medium with Gamborg’s vitamins, pH 5.7. Seeds were cold-treated for at least 2 d to break dormancy and light treated under white light for at least 2 h to synchronize germination. Unless otherwise noted, time-lapse imaging was carried out 2 d after light treatment. Variations in composition of the medium, seed size, seedling age, and accession are noted in the results section. Measurement of hypocotyl growth rates For all experiments, images of seedlings growing in treatment chambers were acquired every 5 min for 8 h in darkness using infrared illumination. Each treatment chamber consisted of a vertically orientated round agar plate sealed with a lid that allowed for continuous gas flow as previously described [ 48 ]. Gas flow was kept at 100 mL min -1 throughout the experiment resulting in a chamber response time for steady-state equilibration of less than 40 seconds. After a 1 h treatment with ethylene-free air to establish basal growth rates, 10 ppm ethylene was introduced for 2 h, followed by 5 h of ethylene-free air to study growth recovery. Image analysis and growth rate calculations were carried out as previously described [ 7 ]. Briefly, we calculated the growth rate for each 5-minute increment using Microsoft Excel and these data were normalized to the average growth rate in ethylene-free air as determined from measurements made prior to application of ethylene. In some experiments, the growth rates in the absence of added ethylene were determined for 8 h and normalized to the growth rates in the first hour of the measurements. Data were plotted from at least 4 separate experiments from 4 different time-lapse imaging setups and represent the average ± SEM at each time point. Each line drawn in the time-course plots is the two point moving average determined by Microsoft Excel. Statistical analyses Statistical differences in the average growth rate in air were determined with ANOVA using GraphPad Prism ver. 9.3.1. The latency for growth recovery after removal of ethylene was determined using cumulative sum control (CUSUM) chart analysis to evaluate the growth rate trends and calculate the time where the trends diverged between the control and experimental seedlings [ 49 ].
