A tumor microenvironment-responsive calcium overload nanoplatform inducing PANoptosis for enhanced cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Aiyang Tong, Yang Zhou, Yang Ding, Yuelin Zhao, Changna Han, Linlin Wei, Jian Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PANoptosis, a newly characterized form of inflammatory programmed cell death that integrates multiple cell death modalities, offers distinct advantages in both potent tumor cell killing and activation of antitumor immunity. However, strategies that can effectively induce PANoptosis in tumor cells remain scarce. Herein, we constructed a tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoplatform (HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2, HMFC) comprising a CaO2 core and a MnO2 shell, loaded with fingolimod (FTY720) and surface-functionalized with hyaluronic acid (HA) for CD44-mediated targeting. Under the mildly acidic and glutathione (GSH)-rich conditions of the tumor microenvironment (TME), the MnO2 shell degrades, liberating FTY720 and exposing the CaO2 core. The CaO2 subsequently decomposes to release Ca2+ and H2O2. FTY720 inhibits Transient Receptor Potential Melastatin 7 (TRPM7) channels, disrupting Ca2+/Mg2+ homeostasis and thereby provoking severe calcium overload. Simultaneously, MnO2 depletes GSH and, together with CaO2-derived H2O2, promotes a Fenton-like reaction that generates abundant reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby disrupting intracellular redox homeostasis. In addition, Mn2+ released from MnO2 degradation activates the cGAS–STING pathway, further contributing to DC maturation and antitumor immunity. This orchestrated immune response markedly suppresses tumor growth and when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, induces a pronounced abscopal effect. Together, our results indicate that calcium overload, FTY720-mediated TRPM7 inhibition, and MnO2-induced redox imbalance can drive PANoptosis, offering a new concept for enhancing cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

In recent years, immunotherapy has shown considerable promise in cancer treatment by activating the host immune system to recognize and eliminate tumor cells. The intrinsic immunosuppressive TME impedes immune-mediated clearance, resulting in low response rates [1]. Induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) is a potent strategy for enhancing antitumor immunity. When tumor cells undergo ICD, they release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to promote DC maturation, tumor antigen presentation, and subsequent activation of cytotoxic T cells [2]. Yet, tumor cells can evade individual cell death pathways through diverse mechanisms, including cellular repair processes and molecular regulatory networks [3], compromising the immunogenic output of single-pathway ICD inducers, as incomplete cell death can limit DAMPs release [4,5]. The recent research on PANoptosis offers a new theoretical basis for cancer immunotherapy [6]. PANoptosis is a highly coordinated, inflammatory cell death modality that simultaneously incorporates apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis [7]. It exhibits the cardinal features of each of these pathways without being a simple superposition of them [8,9]. And it is driven by a multiprotein complex termed the PANoptosome [10,11]. Through cross-regulation, PANoptosome enables cooperative activation to overcome the resistance of tumor cells to any single death pathway [[12], [13], [14]]. Furthermore, PANoptosis disrupts membrane integrity and provokes massive release of DAMPs, enhancing antigen cross-presentation by DCs, thus initiating adaptive immune responses and eliciting robust antitumor immunity [15,16]. However, PANoptosis remains an emerging concept in cell death biology; the intrinsic molecular regulatory network remains incompletely elucidated. Current induction strategies are largely confined to specific pathogen infections, cytokine storm-associated signals, and a few chemotherapeutics [[17], [18], [19]]. Consequently, developing efficient and controllable PANoptosis-inducing strategies holds great promise for opening new avenues to potentiate cancer immunotherapy.

The role of calcium overload in cancer therapy has attracted considerable attention. Ca2+ is a ubiquitous second messenger to diverse cellular functions, acting as a crucial regulator of processes such as proliferation, metabolism, cell death, and gene expression [20,21], and exerting important immunomodulatory effects on immune cells [[22], [23], [24]]. Sustained elevation of intracellular Ca2+ concentration can provoke calcium overload, leading to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [[25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. However, calcium overload alone typically elicits only limited ICD [30], as tumor cells exhibit a certain tolerance to ionic imbalance and oxidative stress, owing to the presence of multiple Ca2+ transport channels that maintain Ca2+ homeostasis. Herein, tumors can readily adapt to disruption imposed by single-component calcium-based nanomaterials [31], making it difficult for Ca2+ alone to elicit a sustained and effective calcium overload within tumor cells [32], weakening the ICD effect driven by calcium overload. Therefore, we presume that calcium overload functions as an upstream trigger that must be converted into an irreversible, multimodal cell death cascade, exemplified by PANoptosis, to elicit ICD. Hence, it is paramount to construct synergistic strategies that promote intracellular Ca2+ accumulation, disrupt tumor cell ionic balance, and amplify oxidative stress. To achieve robust calcium overload for effective tumor cell killing.

In this study, we constructed a HA-functionalized, TME-responsive calcium overload nanogenerator based on a CaO2 core and MnO2 sheath. Upon TME exposure, the core liberates Ca2+ and H2O2, thereby inducing calcium overload and intensifying oxidative stress. Critically, the surrounding MnO2 shell functions as a TME-responsive redox-regulatory module [33]. It depletes intracellular GSH to disrupt redox homeostasis [34]. At the same time, the released Mn2+ ions subsequently catalyze CaO2-derived H2O2 into highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH) via a Fenton-like reaction under acidic conditions, thereby amplifying the CaO2-mediated oxidative stress insult [35]. Simultaneously, the Mn2+ ions released from MnO2 degradation function as a catalytic cofactor for cGAS, sensitizing its recognition of cytosolic DNA released during PANoptosis and thereby amplifying STING-dependent innate immune activation [36,37], thereby improving tumor antigen presentation and promoting cytotoxic T-cell responses [38,39]. We further loaded the TRPM7 inhibitor FTY720 into the MnO2 shell to cooperatively reinforce the calcium overload effect. TRPM7 plays a pivotal role in maintaining intracellular divalent cation homeostasis; its inhibition lowers intracellular Mg2+ levels, disturbs the Ca2+/Mg2+ balance, and further facilitates Ca2+ accumulation through calcium–magnesium antagonism [40,41]. Leveraging these synergistic actions, the HMFC nanogenerator simultaneously amplifies ionic imbalance, oxidative stress, and immunostimulatory signals to induce PANoptosis—a form of cell death integrating apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis—thereby potentiating antitumor immunity and offering a new strategy for cancer immunotherapy.

Preparation and characterization of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

CaO2 nanoparticles were synthesized according to a previously established protocol with minor modifications [34]. Initially, 100 mg of CaCl2 was solubilized in 30 mL of ethanol under sonication. Upon complete dissolution, 0.5 mL of H2O2 (30 wt%) was introduced, after which 0.5 mL of ammonium hydroxide (4 wt%) was added dropwise. The mixture was reacted under continuous stirring at ambient temperature for 12 h. The precipitated nanoparticles were then isolated via centrifugation and rinsed twice with ethanol.

MnO2 shell deposition was achieved by combining the pre-synthesized CaO2 cores with MnCl2 at a 4:1 M ratio, followed by ambient agitation for 6 h. The core–shell nanoparticles were subsequently harvested via centrifugation, rinsed twice with ethanol, and re-suspended in ethanol for further use. FTY720-loaded MnO2@CaO2 (MnO2-FTY720@CaO2) was obtained by stirring MnO2@CaO2 with FTY720 in ethanol for 24 h. HA was subsequently added to the dispersion to form an outer HA layer, yielding HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2. The morphology of the nanoparticles was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS), and elemental distribution was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS).

The ability of HMFC to consume GSH and produce ·OH was evaluated by the DTNB method and methylene blue (MB) fading method. For the DTNB experiment, different concentrations of HMFC were incubated with 2 mM GSH in PBS (pH 6.5) at 37 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was taken to react with DTNB solution (1 mM) for 5 min and scanned at full wavelength in the range 250-550 nm using a uv-vis photometer. For the MB assay, different samples and various concentrations of HMFC were incubated with 10 μM MB in PBS (pH 6.5) at 37 °C for 30 min, and the supernatant was taken for full-wavelength scanning in the range 450-750 nm after centrifugation.

pH/GSH-responsive release of Ca2+ and FTY720 from HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

The pH/GSH-responsive release behavior of HMFC was assessed by dispersing the nanoparticles in PBS adjusted to different pH values and GSH concentrations. At each time point, aliquots were withdrawn and centrifuged. The resulting supernatant was harvested for quantification, and an equivalent volume of pre-warmed fresh buffer was immediately added to preserve sink conditions. Ca2+ concentrations in the release medium were determined by ICP-MS, and FTY720 was concurrently quantified spectrophotometrically.

Cellular uptake and lysosomal escape of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

CT26 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. We added coumarin-6-labeled HMFC and maintained the cells for 1, 2, 4, or 6 h. The cells were rinsed with PBS, fixed, and subsequently stained with LysoTracker Red to visualize lysosomes and Hoechst 33342 to label nuclei. CLSM imaging was used to track the cellular uptake and lysosomal escape.

Assessment of calcium overload induced by HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

To evaluate the ability of HMFC to provoke calcium overload, CT26 cells were seeded in 24-well or 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After a 4-h exposure to PBS, FTY720, HA-MnO2-FTY720 (HMF), HA@CaO2 (HC), HA-MnO2@CaO2 (HMC), and HMFC for 4 h, the cells were incubated with Fluo-4 AM. Cytosolic Ca2+ levels were visualized by CLSM, and parallel samples were harvested for quantitative flow cytometric analysis.

For mitochondrial Ca2+ assessment, Rhod-2 AM was used as a mitochondrial calcium indicator. CT26 cells were first plated in 24-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well) and allowed to attach overnight. After 4 h of treatment with PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC, the attached cells were loaded with Rhod-2 AM and subsequently visualized by CLSM.

In vitro therapeutic effect of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

CT26 cells were plated in 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The cells were then treated with PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC for 24 h. After incubation, the drug-containing medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with fresh medium. Subsequently, 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 solution (diluted in culture medium) was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader, with a reference wavelength of 600–650 nm.

The tumor-killing capacity of HMFC was further evaluated by live/dead cell staining. CT26 cells were plated in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. Following 12 h exposure to PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC, the cell monolayer was lightly rinsed with PBS. The cells were then stained with Calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) and kept at 37 °C for 30 min. After removing the excess dye with three PBS rinses, live and dead populations were visualized by CLSM.

Determination of intracellular ROS

To quantify intracellular ROS, CT26 cells were plated in 24-well or 6-well plates, maintained overnight, and subsequently treated with PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC for 6 h. Then the cells underwent 30 min exposure to 10 μM DCFH-DA. The excess probe was removed, and fluorescent signals were captured by CLSM. Concurrent samples were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial damage characterization

To monitor mitochondrial membrane potential, CT26 cells were plated in 24-well or 6-well plates and maintained for 24 h. After treatment with PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC for 6 h, the cells were loaded with the JC-1 fluorescent probe. Fluorescence was visualized by CLSM, and parallel samples were collected for flow cytometric analysis.

Induction of PANoptosis by HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

Cell death was assessed using an Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit. CT26 cells were plated in 24-well or 6-well plates and maintained overnight. Following exposure to PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC, the cells were harvested, rinsed with PBS, and resuspended in binding buffer. Thereafter, the cell suspension was incubated with Annexin V-FITC/PI in the dark at ambient temperature. Fluorescence was visualized by CLSM, and parallel samples were subjected to flow cytometric analysis.

CT26 cells were plated in 96-well plates, allowed to attach overnight, and subsequently treated to PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC for 24 h. LDH release in the culture supernatant was measured using an LDH cytotoxicity assay kit. Inhibitor rescue assay. To evaluate whether HMFC concurrently activates multiple cell death pathways, CT26 cells were inoculated into 96-well plates and maintained overnight. Thereafter, the cells underwent 24 h of incubation with HMFC and in combination with belnacasan (VX-765), Ac-DEVD-CHO, or necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), and viability was then determined by CCK-8.

To verify the formation of PANoptosome complexes, immunofluorescence colocalization of ASC with caspase-1, caspase-8, and RIPK1 was performed. CT26 cells were grown on glass coverslips overnight. Following 6 h of exposure to PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC, the cells were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde and rendered permeable using 0.2% Triton X-100 and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Thereafter, the samples were loaded with anti-ASC, anti-caspase-1, anti-caspase-8, and anti-RIPK1 antibodies overnight. Subsequently, secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature under light-shielded conditions. After nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst 33342, coverslips were examined by CLSM.

Detection of immunogenic cell death of CT26 cells in vitro

CT26 cells were plated in 24-well plates and maintained overnight. Upon 6 h exposure to PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, and HMFC, the cells were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde and rendered permeable using 0.2% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h, the samples were loaded with anti-HMGB1 or anti-CRT primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature under light-shielded conditions. Nuclear counterstaining with Hoechst 33342 preceded CLSM imaging. In parallel, intracellular ATP was quantified in identically treated cells via an enhanced ATP assay kit per the supplier's protocol.

Western blotting assay

For immunoblotting, CT26 cells under each treatment condition were harvested, lysed in RIPA buffer, and quantified by BCA. Equivalent protein amounts were then resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF. Blocked membranes were subsequently probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: GSDMD, GSDMD-N, caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1, NLRP3, caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, Bcl-2, Bax, STING, phospho-STING, IRF3, phospho-IRF3, TBK1, phospho-TBK1, MLKL, phospho-MLKL, RIPK1, phospho-RIPK1, ZBP1, AIM2, TRPM7, and GAPDH. The membranes were rinsed and subsequently treated with the respective HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein signals were developed by enhanced chemiluminescence and acquired using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Transcriptomic analysis

CT26 cells were cultured in T75 flasks overnight and then treated with PBS and HMFC for 8 h. Cells were lysed by adding RNA extraction reagent and thoroughly homogenized by repeated pipetting until no visible cell clumps remained and the lysate appeared clear and non-viscous, ensuring complete contact with all growth surfaces. The lysate was immediately transferred to RNase-free tubes and maintained at −80 °C. Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.

Differential expression was evaluated via DESeq2, with significance defined at FDR <0.05 and |log2FC| > 1. Hierarchical clustering (R) and volcano plots (ggplot2) were generated to separate group-wise expression profiles and highlight DEG distribution. For functional annotation, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The GO enrichment results were displayed as faceted bubble plots showing enrichment scores, gene counts, and statistical significance for each term within the BP, CC, and MF. Additionally, KEGG pathways were similarly visualized using bar and bubble charts.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

To further investigate the tumor-suppressive effect of HMFC in vivo, we generated a CT26 allograft model by inoculating 1 × 106 CT26 cells subcutaneously into the right flank of BALB/c mice. When tumors reached ∼100 mm3, the animals were assigned to receive either PBS, FTY720, HMF, HC, HMC, or HMFC via intratumoral injection (15 mg/kg CaO2 eq.) every other day for three doses, with body weight and tumor size recorded before each treatment. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed. Systemic toxicity was evaluated by collecting major organs for H&E analysis. And tumor specimens were procured for H&E and immunofluorescence labeling of p-STING, cleaved caspase-3, N-GSDMD, RIPK1, MLKL, CD4, and CD8.

In vivo immune activation induced by HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

To assess the immune responses triggered by HMFC, TDLNs and tumor tissues were harvested across different treatment groups, processed into single-cell suspensions through mechanical dissociation and filtration, and washed with flow cytometry staining buffer.

For CD8+ T cell analysis, cells were labeled with anti-CD45, anti-CD3, and anti-CD8 antibodies, and CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumors was quantified by flow cytometry. CD4+ T cells were similarly evaluated using anti-CD45, anti-CD3, and anti-CD4 antibodies. Macrophage polarization was examined by first gating on total macrophages with antibodies against CD45, CD11b, and F4/80, and then discriminating M1 and M2 phenotypes with anti-CD80 and anti-CD206 antibodies, respectively. For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were surface-stained with anti-CD45, anti-CD3, and anti-CD8 antibodies and subsequently fixed and permeabilized. Intracellular IFN-γ was then detected with an anti-IFN-γ antibody. To evaluate DC maturation in TDLNs, cells were stained with antibodies against CD11c, CD80, and CD86 and analyzed by flow cytometry. All flow cytometry data were processed using FlowJo software.

In vivo antitumor evaluation of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2 in combination with αPD-L1

Tumor cells evade immune surveillance through multiple mechanisms, among which PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint signaling is among the best-characterized. Anti-PD-L1 antibody (αPD-L1) restores T-cell cytotoxicity by disrupting this inhibitory axis within the TME. Concomitantly, HMFC-driven PANoptosis triggers ICD, eliciting tumor antigen release, augmenting antigen presentation, and priming systemic antitumor immunity. We therefore hypothesized that combining HMFC with αPD-L1 would further potentiate antitumor immunity. To test this hypothesis, we generated a bilateral CT26 allograft model. Animals were subsequently allocated to four treatment cohorts: PBS; HMFC (intratumoral injection, CaO2 at 15 mg/kg); αPD-L1; and HMFC (intratumoral injection, CaO2 at 15 mg/kg) plus αPD-L1 (intravenous injection, 2 mg/kg). Tumor tissues and TDLNs were harvested, and single-cell suspensions were prepared for flow cytometric analysis as described in Section 2. 14. The analysis focused on intratumoral T cell infiltration, DC maturation, macrophage polarization, and CD8+ T cell effector function.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted in GraphPad Prism 9.5.0, with one-way ANOVA followed by Student–Newman–Keuls post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001; ns indicates not significant.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

Following a previously reported method [34], HMFC was fabricated by coating CaO2 nanoparticles with a MnO2 shell, loading FTY720, and subsequently decorating the surface with HA (Fig. 1A). In brief, CaO2 nanoparticles were first synthesized using H2O2 as the peroxygen source. A MnO2 shell was then deposited onto the CaO2 surface via in situ oxidation and electrostatic adsorption, followed by the incorporation of FTY720. And then, HA was introduced to form the outermost layer. The HMFC has an average hydrated particle size of ∼200 nm and a zeta potential of −18 mV (Fig. 1C and D). TEM revealed that the pristine CaO2 nanoparticles were spherical, with a diameter of 108.2 ± 4.6 nm. XPS analysis of MnO2@CaO2 nanoparticles verified characteristic peaks corresponding to Ca 2p, Mn 2p, and O 1s. Specifically, the Mn 2p3/2 peak at 643.5 eV and the Mn 2p1/2 peak at 654.0 eV matched the standard binding energies of MnO2, confirming the presence of Mn4+ (Fig. 1E and F). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy further demonstrated that compared with MFC lacking HA, HMFC displayed a distinct polysaccharide C–O stretch at approximately 1030 cm−1. Characteristic HA absorption features were also observed at ∼3400 cm−1 (O–H stretching), ∼870 cm−1, and ∼710 cm−1 (ring vibrations) [42], verifying successful HA functionalization on the MFC surface (Fig. 1G). These results together confirm the successful construction of HMFC.

The pH-triggered release profiles of HMFC were examined in vitro. At pH 7.4, the release rates of both FTY720 and Ca2+ were relatively low, remaining below 20% within 12 h. In contrast, the 12 h cumulative release of FTY720 was 55.4% at pH 6.5 and 91.2% at pH 5.5 (Fig. 1H), and Ca2+ reached 47.9% and 76.5% (Fig. 1I). These findings indicate HMFC exhibits strong pH dependence and drug release, demonstrating TME-responsive drug release behavior.

The GSH-depleting effect of HMFC was first assessed via DTNB assays across a concentration gradient. GSH levels decreased progressively with increasing HMFC concentrations (Fig. 1J), confirming that HMFC effectively consumes GSH in a concentration-dependent manner. Furthermore, we assessed the ·OH-generating capacity of HMFC via methylene blue (MB) bleaching assays. CaO2 or MnO2 alone induced only minimal MB bleaching, whereas HMFC treatment resulted in a marked decrease in MB absorbance, with the effect becoming progressively more pronounced at higher HMFC concentrations (Fig. 1K and L). These results confirm that HMFC synergistically generates ·OH through the cooperative action of CaO2 and MnO2.

Cellular uptake and lysosomal escape of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

We first examined the cellular uptake of coumarin-6-labeled HMFC. CD44 is predominantly enriched in malignant cells across diverse cancer types; its expression in normal tissues is minimal. Because the nanoparticle surface is coated with HA, HMFC preferentially binds to CD44-overexpressing cells and is subsequently internalized by tumor cells with high efficiency [43]. CLSM images revealed that with the extension of time, the fluorescence intensity in cells gradually increased, indicating effective HMFC internalization by CT26 cells (Fig. 2A). In addition, we examined the cellular uptake efficiency of HMFC under various conditions. Compared with MFC, HMFC exhibited significantly higher intracellular fluorescence. And the fluorescence intensity in the pre-incubation with free HA decreased markedly compared with the HMFC, confirming a CD44-dependent target (Fig. S1). These results demonstrate that HA modification confers CD44-mediated targeting and internalization capability to HMFC. Furthermore, Manders’ colocalization analysis showed a progressive decrease in overlap between the green HMFC signal and the red lysosomal marker over time, with evident separation by 6 h, confirming lysosomal escape (Fig. 2B, Fig. S2). HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2 induced Ca2+ overload capability

Tumor cells are particularly sensitive to Ca2+ concentration change and possess an enhanced capacity for Ca2+ regulation [44]. We therefore examined HMFC's ability to promote calcium overload. We used the Fluo-4 AM as a probe to monitor cytosolic Ca2+ levels. The cells treated with CaO2 or HMC produced only weak fluorescence, whereas HMFC elicited a bright signal, an effect quantitatively confirmed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2C–E), confirming that FTY720-mediated inhibition of TRPM7 channels markedly increased intracellular Ca2+ levels to promote calcium overload. Given mitochondria as critical executioners of calcium overload [45], we then assessed mitochondrial Ca2+ using the Rhod-2 AM probe. Similar to the above findings, HMFC treatment induced pronounced red fluorescence in the mitochondria (Fig. 2C), reflecting massive mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, and ultimately contributes to the tumor-killing effect. To further investigate the role of FTY720 in calcium overload, we knocked down TRPM7 expression in CT26 cells (Fig. S3). In TRPM7-deficient cells, HMFC treatment failed to elicit significant changes in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, and FTY720 treatment failed to elicit significant changes in intracellular Mg2+ concentrations, indicating that the enhanced calcium overload relies on FTY720-mediated TRPM7 inhibition and the subsequent Ca2+–Mg2+ antagonism (Fig. S4).

In vitro therapeutic efficacy and hemocompatibility of HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

Intracellular Ca2+ accumulation is a key event that promotes calcium overload and consequent tumor cell killing. We therefore examined the tumoricidal capacity of HMFC. HMFC treatment resulted in a significantly greater decrease in cell viability compared to all other treatment groups (Fig. 2F), whereas FTY720 alone did not appreciably reduce cell viability (Fig. S5), confirming that 1 μM FTY720 is essentially non-cytotoxic. In contrast, HMC induced marked morphological changes and a significant reduction in cell viability, and the combination of FTY720 with HMC (HMFC) led to even more pronounced effects (Fig. S6). These results indicate that MnO2 and CaO2 act synergistically to kill tumor cells, and that FTY720 further potentiates this synergistic cytotoxicity. Western blot analysis further revealed that FTY720 significantly downregulated TRPM7 expression (Fig. 4I), corroborating a mechanism whereby the drug promotes Ca2+ influx through TRPM7 channel inhibition. To further assess HMFC efficacy, we employed calcein-AM/propidium iodide (PI) double staining to discriminate between live and dead cells. CLSM images showed that HA-MnO2-FTY720 (HMF), HC, and HMC treatments all elicited obvious red fluorescence, signifying cellular injury, whereas HMFC gave the most intense red signal with only sparse green fluorescence, indicative of massive cell death (Fig. 2D). Thus, these results demonstrate that FTY720 facilitates Ca2+ influx to provoke calcium overload, while MnO2 exacerbates oxidative stress by depleting GSH and amplifying ·OH production, culminating in efficient tumor cell eradication. Meanwhile, a hemolysis assay revealed no appreciable hemolysis with HMFC even at 500 μg/mL (Fig. S7), underscoring its favorable biocompatibility.

HMFC-treated cells exhibited pronounced mitochondrial swelling, cristae fragmentation, and aberrant membrane architecture (Fig. 3D, Fig. S8). The calcium overload, mitochondrial injury, and oxidative stress elicited by CaO2 within the TME concurrently activated pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis pathways, leading to a multipathway cell death. We first performed Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining. CLSM revealed that the FTY720 group showed negligible green or red fluorescence, with cells maintaining normal morphology, confirming that FTY720 alone does not compromise cell integrity. CaO2 treatment generated prominent green fluorescence, whereas the HMFC group displayed a substantially elevated proportion of red fluorescence, with cells appearing markedly rounded (Fig. 4A). In addition, flow cytometry was used to quantify. Compared to fewer Annexin V+ or PI+ cells in the control and FTY720 groups, HMFC presents the highest Annexin V+ (19.1%) and PI+ (24.1%) fractions among all groups, with intermediate values for HC (7.80%/6.32%) and HMC (13.9%/16.5%) (Fig. 4C), indicating the activation of apoptosis and pyroptosis. Furthermore, the assembly of PANoptosome is a crucial regulator of the induction of PANoptosis. We evaluated PANoptosome activation by CLSM. HMFC-treated cells displayed visible ASC aggregates that co-localized with caspase-1, caspase-8, and RIPK1 (Fig. 4B), indicating that HMFC drives PANoptosome assembly. Meanwhile, HMFC treatment led to significant LDH release (Fig. 3E). Together, these results demonstrate that HMFC can induce pyroptosis and consequently trigger a potent inflammatory response. Moreover, as shown in Fig. 2G, the cytotoxic effect of HMFC could be partially suppressed by VX-765, Ac-DEVD-CHO, or Nec-1, respectively, implying that the cell death promoted by HMFC involves apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis.

We next investigated the expression of PANoptosis-associated proteins by Western blot to confirm whether HMFC indeed triggers PANoptosis. As shown in Fig. 4F, HMFC treatment markedly elevated NLRP3 protein levels, implying NLRP3 inflammasome activation. The activated inflammasome drove pro-caspase-1 processing, generating cleaved caspase-1. Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed decreased pro-caspase-1 and correspondingly increased cleaved caspase-1. Active caspase-1 in turn cleaved gasdermin D (GSDMD), liberating the N-terminal pore-forming fragment (N-GSDMD). Accordingly, full-length GSDMD decreased while N-GSDMD increased, confirming efficient pyroptotic pathway activation upon HMFC treatment. Concurrently, CaO2 activated caspase-3, with HMFC eliciting the most pronounced effect, whereas MF showed limited caspase-3 activation. HMFC treatment also downregulated Bcl-2 and upregulated Bax (Fig. 4D), indicative of apoptotic pathway engagement. Furthermore, HC, HMC, and HMFC treatments significantly increased the levels of phosphorylated receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (p-RIPK1) and phosphorylated mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (p-MLKL), with the HMFC group showing the most prominent upregulation (Fig. 4H). These data further indicate that the calcium overload and MnO2 synergy also activate necroptotic death. We further examined the expression of upstream sensors and found that HMFC significantly upregulated AIM2 and ZBP1 (Fig. 4G), suggesting their potential involvement in this coordinated cell death response. Together, these findings demonstrate that HMFC induces PANoptosis through synergistic mechanisms to enhance tumor cell killing.

HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2 triggers immunogenic cell death in vitro

PANoptosis is an inherently ICD process that can provoke immune responses through the release of DAMPs. Moreover, calcium overload triggers mitochondrial dysfunction and impairs ATP synthesis in tumor cells, cooperatively promoting tumor cell killing and the liberation of DAMPs. This also enhances antigen presentation, activates DCs, and elevates tumor immunogenicity. Based on this premise, we further explored the capacity of HMFC to induce ICD. As shown in Fig. 4E, HMFC treatment markedly intensified the green fluorescence of CRT, indicative of CRT translocation to the cell surface. Concurrently, the green fluorescence of HMGB1 decreased, suggesting its release. Released HMGB1 can serve as a signaling molecule to foster inflammatory responses and immune activation. We next examined HMFC's ability to activate the cGAS-STING pathway. HMFC significantly increased the levels of phosphorylated STING, IRF3, and TBK1 (Fig. 4J), demonstrating that HMFC can activate the cGAS–STING pathway in tumor cells. These data further support that HMFC not only triggers PANoptosis but also engages STING-dependent innate immune signaling in tumor cells.

In the HMFC-treated group, core pyroptotic genes (NLRP3, GSDMD, Caspase-1, IL-18), executioner apoptotic genes (Bax, Caspase-3/7/8), and necroptotic markers (RIPK3, MLKL) were markedly upregulated, whereas the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was distinctly downregulated, indicating a pro-death shift in the Bcl-2/Bax balance. Additionally, ZBP1 expression was markedly upregulated in HMFC-treated cells (Fig. 5C), consistent with its role in PANoptosome assembly. Compared with the control, HMC inhibited the growth of the tumor to a certain extent, whereas HMFC significantly inhibited tumor growth across all groups (Fig. 6C–E). In vivo immune activation induced by HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2

We next assessed the in vivo antitumor immunity of HMFC. Compared with PBS, HMFC significantly enhanced DC maturation in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs) (Fig. 7D–H). Indicating that HMFC-induced ICD promotes antigen presentation and initiates adaptive immune activation.

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte profiling revealed that HMFC treatment increased tumor-infiltrating CD3+ T cells. Within this population, the proportion of CD3+CD4+ T cells rose from 13.1% to 54.5% (Fig. 7B–I), whereas that of CD3+CD8+ T cells increased from approximately 3.32% to 25.2% (Fig. 7C–J). These results indicate that T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity was amplified. Concomitantly, HMFC significantly increased the frequency of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7E–K), indicating HMFC enhanced effector function to convert ICD signals into functional adaptive immunity, a phenomenon similarly reported in pyroptosis-driven nanotherapeutic systems [46].

We next examined macrophage polarization as a separate indicator of TME remodeling. Notably, HMFC increased the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD80+F4/80+) (Fig. 7F–L) while reducing the proportion of M2 macrophages (CD206+F4/80+) (Fig. 7G–M). Because M1 macrophages exert pro-inflammatory and antitumor functions, whereas M2 macrophages suppress T-cell immunity by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines, this phenotypic shift reflects substantial remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings further corroborate that ICD not only fosters antigen release but also contributes to the establishment of an immune-permissive milieu [47].

HA-MnO2-FTY720@CaO2 potentiates anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy

Tumor cells can evade immune surveillance through multiple mechanisms, among which the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway is one of the most extensively studied. Anti-PD-L1 antibody (αPD-L1) alleviates immunosuppression in the TME and restores T cell activity by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis [48]. Meanwhile, HMFC induces PANoptosis and ICD within tumor tissues, promoting tumor antigen release, enhancing antigen presentation, and activating antitumor immune responses. We therefore hypothesized that combining HMFC with αPD-L1 would further potentiate antitumor immunity.

Tumor growth curves showed that tumors in the saline-treated group grew rapidly. αPD-L1 monotherapy partially suppressed the growth of both proximal and distal tumors, whereas HMFC alone effectively inhibited proximal tumors but exerted only a modest effect on distal tumors. Strikingly, the combination of αPD-L1 and HMFC markedly inhibited the growth of both proximal and distal tumors (Fig. S24), demonstrating a potent tumor-suppressive effect of the combination therapy.

Analysis of DC maturation in TDLNs revealed that, compared with PBS, HMFC or αPD-L1 alone moderately increased the proportion of mature DCs, while the combination group exhibited a substantially higher proportion (Fig. 8B, Fig. S25), indicative of enhanced antigen presentation. Assessment of intratumoral T cell accumulation revealed that the combination therapy elevated the frequencies of both CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells among CD3+ T lymphocytes (Fig. 8C, D, Fig. S26), indicating that the combined treatment could enhance local T cell recruitment and cytotoxic T lymphocyte infiltration, thereby establishing a foundation for antitumor immunity. HMFC + αPD-L1 increases IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 8E, Fig. S27), indicating strengthened CD8+ T cell effector function. Collectively, these results demonstrate that HMFC not only promotes T cell infiltration but also augments their cytotoxic capacity under conditions where αPD-L1 relieves immunosuppression.

We also examined macrophage polarization within the TME. The combination therapy increased the proportion of M1 macrophages (CD80+F4/80+) and decreased that of M2 macrophages (CD206+F4/80+), resulting in an elevated M1/M2 ratio (Fig. 8F, G, Fig. S28), which suggests a transition from an immunosuppressive to a pro-inflammatory TME.

Overall, HMFC combined with αPD-L1 enhances in vivo antitumor immunity by promoting DC maturation, augmenting T cell infiltration and CD8+ T cell function, and modulating macrophage polarization, thereby achieving robust inhibition of both primary and distal tumors.

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully constructed a TME-responsive nanoplatform, HMFC, consisting of a CaO2 core, a MnO2 shell loaded with FTY720, and an outer HA coating, to potentiate cancer immunotherapy through the induction of multiple ICD pathways, including pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, converging into PANoptosis through PANoptosome assembly. In the mildly acidic TME, the MnO2 shell gradually degrades, liberating FTY720 and exposing the CaO2 core while consuming GSH. The exposed CaO2 further decomposes under acidic conditions to generate Ca2+ and H2O2. FTY720 disrupts intracellular calcium–magnesium homeostasis via TRPM7-dependent mechanisms and drives massive Ca2+ influx, provoking calcium overload. This overload triggers mitochondrial damage, impairs ATP synthesis, and elicits a burst of mitochondrial ROS. Meanwhile, H2O2 reacts with Mn2+ ions to produce ·OH, while the depletion of GSH by MnO2 perturbs intracellular redox homeostasis and further amplifies oxidative stress. The synergy between calcium overload and oxidative stress simultaneously activates apoptotic, pyroptotic, and necroptotic pathways, leading to immunogenic cell death. This process is accompanied by the extensive release of DAMPs. Moreover, the Mn2+ ions released from MnO2 degradation activate the cGAS–STING pathway, promoting DC maturation and reshaping the immunosuppressive TME. The DAMPs discharged during PANoptosis cooperate with STING pathway activation to foster DC maturation and immune cell infiltration within the tumor, and this effect can be further amplified by combining with immune checkpoint blockade. Collectively, this study demonstrates that the HMFC nanoplatform achieves TME-triggered drug release, ameliorates hypoxia and immunosuppression to trigger multimodal ICD with features of PANoptosis, offering a new concept for the development of cancer immunotherapy strategies.
