Executive Summary:
 Plastics are increasingly used across industries, driving continual growth in
production leading to more landfill and incineration waste. The multi-university center for
the Chemical Upcycling of Waste Plastics (CUWP) was established to research and
develop technologies that divert plastic waste from end-of-life disposal and convert it to
valuable products.
 CUWP’s research focused on advancing plastic pyrolysis, catalytic upgrading of
pyrolysis oils, and solvent based recycling of multilayer films. Pyrolysis breaks down
polyolefin plastics to produce a mixture of hydrocarbons that can be converted into
chemicals such as aromatics and surfactants. CUWP demonstrated this process at the
pilot scale, and economic analyses show that chemically recycling plastics into
surfactants can reduce greenhouse gas emissions by 60% compared to conventional
surfactant production, with an estimated to payback period of under 5 years. CUWP also
developed a technology called STRAP, a solvent-based method that separates multilayer
plastic films, commonly seen in food packaging, into reusable plastic streams with
properties comparable to virgin materials.
 Alongside technological development, CUWP emphasized public education and
outreach. The public choosing to recycle plastics rather than throw them away is the first
step of chemical recycling. K-12 lesson plans, university courses, and undergraduate
research programs were created to improve public understanding of plastic recycling and
encourage participation in local recycling programs. These efforts support the broader
goal of strengthening U.S. recycling infrastructure and expanding the range of plastics
that can be sustainably reused.

 1. Background
 1.1 Current Plastic Supply Chain
 The majority of plastics are produced from hydrocarbons derived from natural gas and
crude oil (Li et al., 2022). As shown in Figure 1.1, the conversion of natural gas and crude
oil into plastics follow different reaction pathways. Steam cracking is the first step in the
pathway for plastic production, in which large hydrocarbon molecules are broken down
into smaller hydrocarbon molecules. Steam cracking guarantees the polymer grade of the
monomers. These plastic monomers, particularly ethylene, serve as platform chemicals
for much of the existing plastic industry. Since most monomers come from petroleum
refining, there are several ongoing research efforts of recycling plastic polymers back into
their respective monomers to increase plastic sustainability. Polyethylene terephthalate
(PET) hydrolysis (Carta et al., 2003) and polystyrene (PS) photodegradation (Yousif &
Haddad, 2013) stand out as valuable options for recycling these plastics back into
monomers in the current plastic supply chain.

Figure 1.1. Processing steps for the production of #1 – #6 plastics and their volume of
production in 2019 globally (adapted from (Li et al., 2022)).

 The creation of plastics has unquestionably revolutionized various aspects of human
life related to food safety, medical technologies, transportation, and construction among
other areas. For example, plastic packaging reduces food waste, which is a pressing
global problem that limits food security, food safety, and has negative effects on the
environment and the economy (Manalili et al., 2014). Plastic packaging does this by
creating a physical barrier that protects food from environmental conditions such as
oxygen and humidity. In addition, plastics are inexpensive and versatile. However, current
final disposal practices of single-use and short-life cycle plastics raise environmental

concerns (Ragaert et al., 2017). For example, only 10% of the global plastic waste is
expected to be recycled with the rest of plastics being landfilled (49%), incinerated (19%),
or disposed into open dumps (22%) (OECD, 2022). The accumulation of plastics in the
environment, due to inappropriate designs or flawed practices across the linear waste
collection chain, raises multiple environmental and health concerns including the entry of
micro- and nano-plastics into human and animal food chains (Geyer et al., 2017).
Recycling-research centers such as CUWP reduce the burden on waste collection
systems by introducing circularity technologies into the supply chain. These technologies
bridge recyclers with plastic converters and refineries to maintain the follow of waste
plastics. Pyrolyzed plastics can be sent back to petroleum refineries while STRAP can
produce high purity plastic resins for plastic converters from multi-layer films, see Figure
1.2.

Figure 1.2. Circular plastic supply chain made possible through recyclers like CUWP.

 In the U.S., only 5% of the 221 kg (487 lbs) of plastics that are generated per person
each year are recycled, with the rest being landfilled (73%), incinerated (19%), or
indiscriminately dumped into the environment (3%) (OECD, 2022). Landfilling and
incineration keep waste plastics out of the environment, but if poorly implemented, they
can have negative health and environmental impacts. Landfilling is a responsible
approach to disposing of waste plastics but takes up valuable urban space. Incineration
dramatically reduces the volume of waste that must ultimately be landfilled, but is
equivalent to burning fossil fuels by releasing carbon dioxide, the most abundant
greenhouse gas, worldwide (Klemeš et al., 2020). Incineration is resource inefficient, as
it brings an end to the plastic’s lifecycle requiring the additional extraction of fossil fuels
to produce more plastics (Klemeš et al., 2020).

 Greenhouse gases (GHGs) define a group of gases in the atmosphere that absorb
and emit energy within the thermal infrared range. GHGs can be produced from either
natural sources or from man-made activities. Because they reduce the amount of infrared
(i.e., heat) radiation Earth loses to space, GHGs increase the average planetary
temperature and the rate of global warming. The most manmade abundant GHG is
carbon dioxide (CO2, mostly from fossil fuel combustion), followed by methane (CH4,
mostly from animal agriculture and natural gas extraction), nitrous oxide (N2O, mostly
from agricultural soil management), and chlorofluorocarbons (mostly from refrigerants).
A significant source of GHG emissions in the U.S. are industrial processes including iron,
steel, and cement production (US EPA, 2015).

 Some environmentalists call for a ban on the production and utilization of plastics as
the only credible way to reduce plastic waste in the environment. Given the integral role
of plastics in modern society, this is an unrealistic expectation. Moreover, plastic waste
can be seen as a valuable resource if recovered and remanufactured into new products.
There are multiple emerging technologies that can convert plastic waste into new
chemicals, fuels, and new plastics (e.g., pyrolysis) and/or the breakdown of multilayer
films into their original plastic building blocks (e.g., solvent-targeted recovery and
precipitation, STRAP) (Xu et al., 2020).

1.2 Solvent Based Recycling (STRAPTM)

Solvent-based or dissolution based recycling is a new developing technology aiming to
mechanically recycle plastics through dissolution in solvent to recover plastic with virgin
like properties. The technique has been shown to be effective for polyolefins, poly vinyl
chloride (PVC), acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS), polystyrene (PS), and several
others at lab or commercial scales. Solvent-based recycling is effective at separating
polymer from additives and maintaining polymer value (Sánchez-Rivera et al., 2024).
Research is ongoing on how to remove specific classes of contaminants ranging from
pigments, plasticizers, and fillers (Arends et al., 2012; Cuthbertson et al., 2024; Wagner
& Schlummer, 2024).

Wisconsin partners have developed a specific technology to target multilayer plastics and
complex plastics mixtures that can recover each polymer separately. From multilayer
films, PE and EVOH can be separated from PET, shown in Figure 1.3. The solvent used
to target each polymer is selected through guided computational calculations for polymer
solubility in a given polymer. These calculations are guided with Hansen solubility
parameters, molecular dynamic simulations, and conductor-like screening model for
realistic solvents (COSMO-RS).

Figure 1.3. Sample STRAP process to recover PE, EVOH, and PET. This particular
process uses anti-solvents, but they can be replaced with cooling.

Several dissolution technologies exist and mainly differ in the solvent and processing
conditions used. These various conditions have been adapted to specific feedstocks such
as sachet packaging waste at CreaSolv and industrial PP scraps at PureCycle (Xu et al.,
2025). Many technologies in this field target feedstocks that are often landfilled as they
cannot be repurposed easily. The dissolution technique aims to provide plastic circularity
through conservation of the plastic’s value in the supply chain. The goal is to recycle the
plastic into the same application as it was used for before recycling. Through this
approach, less material can be down-cycled into lower value applications. It is important
to note that not all plastic feedstocks may be suitable for economically feasible dissolution
processes. This technology works best in feedstocks that have a high percent content of
one polymer with other minor additives. The technique becomes more challenging in the
case of a blend of various plastics with a low portion in the overall blend.

The main advantage of recycling plastic with dissolution are the environmental benefits
that come with the process. The process first diverts plastic from landfills, dumps, and the
environment. The process allows for less processing compared to chemical upcycling
since the polymer is not broken down intentionally into smaller chemicals. This
preservation in the polymer allows for lower greenhouse gas emissions compared to
virgin resin production as long as anti-solvents are not used that require distillation
(Munguía-López et al., 2023a). Polymer chemical recycling requires the polymer to be
broken down into starting materials for virgin resin production, therefore, increasing
operating costs and greenhouse emissions.
1.3 Pyrolysis and Catalytic Upgrading
 Pyrolysis is a leading and evolving technology for upcycling waste plastics, particularly
polyolefins and polystyrene, to produce gas, liquid, and solid products that can be used
to produce new plastics that have the same characteristics as the original plastics from

which they are derived. Unlike incineration, pyrolysis aligns with the principles of a circular
economy, breathing new life into discarded plastics and reintroducing them into society,
thus contributing to resource efficiency and sustainability (Mastral & Callén, 2000).
Moreover, pyrolysis complements mechanical recycling as it can upcycle contaminated
or mixed plastics that are currently unfeasible to recover through mechanical recycling.

 Depending on reaction conditions, pyrolysis of plastics produces waxes, oils, gases,
and char/ash, shown in Figure 1.4 (Zhao et al., 2020). Waxes are longer chain
hydrocarbons that are solid at room temperature. They have some value as lubricants,
but their value increases if further decomposed into oils. Increasing the temperature and
the pyrolysis time are the main factors used to convert waxes into oils. Pyrolysis oils are
shorter chain hydrocarbons that are liquid at room temperature and can be converted into
a variety of value-added chemicals, new plastic products that can replace virgin plastics,
or liquid fuels that can replace virgin fossil fuels. Pyrolysis gas has properties similar to
natural gas, allowing it to be used in heat and power applications. Most commonly, it is
used for heating on site, reducing the need for fossil fuels in the plant. Char/ash produced
from pyrolysis of waste plastic is made in small amounts compared to the other products,
yielding only 1 to 5% of the waste plastic mass. Char has relatively little economic value
although, like the gas, the char can be used to heat the pyrolysis reactor with a burner,
shown in Figure 1.4. Waxes and oils are the main products of pyrolysis and can be used
to produce new products including fuels, chemicals, and the original plastics from which
they were derived. Chemicals and new plastics are preferred from the standpoint of
encouraging a circular economy as fuels produced from plastics are equivalent to fuels
directly produced from fossil fuels, resulting in net GHG emissions to the atmosphere
when burned. The fuels generated by pyrolysis can be reused in the production process
to reduce the use of purchased fossil fuels.

Figure 1.4. Generalization of the pyrolysis process. Dashed lines represent alternative
pathways for products. Red lines represent energy flows.

 One of the main advantages of pyrolysis is that it can process different kinds of plastic

wastes, including mixed plastics and multi-layer films, unlike mechanical recycling that
needs highly separated plastics by type (e.g., only PET waste) (Li et al., 2022). In this
sense, chemical recycling complements mechanical recycling. In addition, pyrolysis can
process contaminated plastics with fats, oils, grease, and dirt as well as other chemical
components that give plastics their properties. This flexibility to process mixed and
contaminated plastic waste also reduces the logistic effort, costs, and energy
consumption associated with segregating and classifying plastic waste by households
and material recovery facilities (MRFs). More importantly, it provides an alternative to
sending waste plastics to landfills, dumps, and the environment. One disadvantage of
pyrolysis is that hydrochloric acid forms when polyvinyl chloride (PVC) or polyvinylidene
chloride (PVDC) are pyrolyzed, usually present in small amounts (~1 wt %) in waste
plastics, can corrode the pyrolysis reactor (Gendell & Lahme, 2022; Xu et al., 2020). This
can be prevented by adding an acid scavenger like calcium oxide to remove the chlorine
content as a pretreatment process (Li et al., 2022; Matsuzawa et al., 2004).

 Some people mistakenly equate pyrolysis with incineration, which has an unfavorable
reputation for pollution. Incinerators were introduced into communities many years ago to
simplify waste management by burning garbage, often with lower emissions than burning
trash in one’s backyard. Incinerators have been largely shutdown or replaced with modern
energy recovery boilers, burning waste in streams of air at high temperatures in
conjunction with pollution control devices to reduce the various emissions produced to
predominantly carbon dioxide and water vapor. Incineration follows a linear life cycle; that
is, plastics are produced and used only once before being destroyed. Pyrolysis, on the
other hand, operates at more modest temperatures and in the near absence of air to
produce mostly waxes and oils instead of gas, depending on the reactor conditions. The
relatively small amount of gas produced passes through a thermal oxidizer to convert any
organic compounds that are not collected as oil into carbon dioxide and water (Li et al.,
2022). The recovered oil can be used in a steam cracker to be converted to monomers
and new plastics. Pyrolysis can potentially reintroduce most of the plastic waste into the
economy, where resources are conserved and continuously utilized, minimizing the
depletion of fossil resources, and preventing plastic waste from contributing to air, water,
or soil pollution from manufacturing and final disposal.

 2. Project Objectives
2.1 Mission of CUWP
 The central goal of CUWP was to enable low-cost technologies that sustainably
upcycle plastic wastes. We also sought to develop the scientific and engineering
workforce to enable the commercialization of plastic recycling technologies. The
technology options for chemical recycling of plastics were experimentally validated at both
the laboratory and pilot scale. Rigorous process models were developed for the different
plastic recycling options using both historical data and experimental data that was
collected in CUWP. We studied how material recovery facilities (MRFs) can be optimized
to recycle different types of plastic wastes. The required purities for plastic wastes that
are needed for chemical and mechanical recycling was developed. Thermal
depolymerization of plastic wastes into a plastic-derived oil was done in both laboratory
and pilot scale reactors. We analyzed the products of thermal depolymerization using

modern analytical techniques. This experimental data was combined with first principal
calculations to derive mechanistically based kinetic models for depolymerization of pure
resins and mixed plastic streams. We studied how waxes and oils from the
depolymerization of mixed waste plastics can be catalytically converted into olefins and
aromatics in a fixed catalytic bed. We elucidated the relationship between catalyst
properties and their performance. We developed rules for the maximum acceptable levels
of polymer impurities (chlorine, nitrogen, other heteroatoms) in thermal depolymerization
and catalytic upgrading. Technologies for removing these impurities were experimentally
validated. Post-industrial plastic waste (PIW) multi-layer plastic films were recycled into
their pure resin components using the STRAP process. Kilogram quantities of recycled
resins were produced from STRAP and the mechanical properties were tested. New
educational materials on plastic recycling were developed and classes were taught using
this material at both the undergraduate and graduate level at CUWP partner universities,
including Iowa State University, University of Guelph, Universidad Autonoma
Metropolotina, Universidad Autonoma de San Luis Potosi, University of Massachusetts
Amherst, and National Renewable Energy Laboratory. This curriculum material will be
shared both inside and outside the institute. We hosted several summer research
programs for undergraduate students on plastic recycling and held multiple seminar
series dedicated to plastic recycling.

 Despite public support for plastics recycling, industry has struggled to make it
commercially viable achieving only 2% recycling (The New Plastics Economy, 2016). The
diversity of plastic wastes, the presence of contaminants, the large capital requirements,
integration of the supply chain, and the difficulty of thoroughly depolymerizing plastics to
their original monomers are significant challenges in upcycling waste plastics. Until these
limitations are understood and a clear path forward identified, industry will be hesitant to
invest in what might otherwise be viewed as early-stage research and development. The
motivation of CUWP was to advance the science and engineering of plastic upcycling to
the point where private investment is attractive and helps build the future workforce.
Below are the project objectives as listed in the Statement of Project Objectives (SOPO):

 1. Detailed process models for chemical and solvent-based upcycling of waste
 plastics.
 2. Collection, sorting technology, and practice that improve the volume, composition,
 and quality of baled plastics from Material Recovery Facilities (MRFs).
 3. Production of plastic-derived oils from thermal depolymerization of mixed plastic
 waste streams in laboratory and pilot plant scale.
 4. Catalytic upgrading of plastic-derived oils into aromatics and olefins (plastic
 monomers) in a continuous flow reactor.
 5. Use of solvent-based recycling strategies for converting multi-layer films into pure
 resins.
 6. Development of undergraduate and graduate curriculum material for plastic
 recycling

  CUWP was selected from DE-FOA-0002203 FY20 Multi-Topic FOA, Topic Area 6b:
Waste Plastics to Products, that required collaboration with at least one university in both

Canada and Mexico to leverage capacity and capitalize on North American resources. The
full partners for this project are shown in Section 7. Professors from both Mexico and
Canada participated in CUWP for the entire research program. CUWP funded travel for
Canada and Mexico, professors and their students to attend CUWP meetings and do
research stays at CUWP institutions. Industrial partners from Canada, Mexico and South
America joined the CUWP industrial advisory board and participated with CUWP research
activities.

 3. Project Results and Discussion
Topic Area 1 – Systems Analysis of Chemical Recycling Plastic Infrastructure
Under Task 2.1, we developed a robust framework to benchmark and screen plastic
recycling and upcycling technologies using techno-economic analysis (TEA), life-cycle
assessment (LCA), supply chain modeling, and machine learning. In Subtask 2.1.1, we
first developed consistent economic and environmental baselines across solvent-based
recycling, thermochemical upcycling, and material recovery systems. These benchmarks
were then used within process modeling studies as part of Subtask 2.1.2. We have used
our methods for analyzing a broad variety of recycling/upcycling pathways studied in the
CUWP project (e.g., STRAP variants, hydroformylation, catalytic upgrading, pyrolysis,
and thermal oxo degradation) (Li et al., 2023a; Ma et al., 2023; Olafasakin, Ma, Zavala,
et al., 2023; Sánchez‐Rivera et al., 2021). In the case of solvent-based recycling
technologies, prior models have shown this method to be economic unreasonable due to
the high costs of solvent distillation. Distillation would be required in the case of using
anti-solvents which prior to our work is considered state of the art. In Sanchez et al. we
evaluated and compared various configurations for our solvent targeted recovery and
precipitation (STRAP) process upcycling of post-industrial waste(Sánchez‐Rivera et al.,
2021). The results demonstrate that this process can also recover polymers at similar
costs to those of virgin resins, indicating that the STRAP technology is flexible and can
remain economically competitive as the plastic feed complexity is increased. The work
reported in Munguía-López et al. quantified carbon footprint, energy use, and water
consumption for multiple STRAP process configurations (Munguía-López et al., 2023b).
We found that STRAP configurations leveraging temperature-based precipitation (without
the use of antisolvents) offer the best environmental advantages relative to virgin
multilayer film production, which is an improvement to prior models We deployed our
STRAP process models for key industrial applications, including the recycling of high-
purity polypropylene from disposable face masks,(Yu et al., 2023) recycling of single-use
multilayer plastics for biomanufacturing,(Tushar et al., 2025) and recycling of post-
industrial printed multilayer plastic films containing polyurethane inks (Sánchez-Rivera et
al., 2023). As part of this work, we have also developed a fast and automated framework
to identify optimal strategies to recycle multi-layer films with many layers using STRAP
(which involves solvent selection); we have also extended these results to identify green
solvents to conduct separations (Ikegwu, Munguía-López, et al., 2025; Ikegwu, Zhou, et
al., 2025).

As part of Subtask 2.1.3 we conducted system-wide studied to understand conditions
under which waste feedstocks follow distinct pathways (Erickson et al., 2024; Ma et al.,
2023; Olafasakin, Ma, Bradshaw, et al., 2023). For example, in Ma et al. we developed a

holistic optimization framework that integrates supply chain analysis, techno-economic
analysis, and life-cycle analysis for investigating the economic viability and environmental
benefits of upcycling infrastructures that collect, sort, clean, and process post-consumer
plastic waste for producing virgin polymer resins (Ma et al., 2023). We demonstrated that
coordinated collection, sorting, and conversion systems could achieve economic viability
at regional scale. We have also extended this analysis to a national scale (Erickson et al.,
2024); our models are open source and general and can be used to analyze diverse
scenarios in different regions and under different policy conditions. For instance, our
models have been recently used to conduct studies in Spain (García et al., 2026). We
have also put together a comprehensive superstructure optimization framework that
automatically determines how a certain feedstock will be routed to STRAP or thermal
decomposition depending on its decomposition.

For Task 3.1, we sought to inform process-level modeling and decision-making for
solvent selection and process design by coupling experimentally measured material
properties, molecular modeling for predicting solubilities, and advanced optimization and
machine learning. We have applied these methodologies in the context of the STRAP
process and for pyrolysis systems. Under Subtask 3.1.1, we developed a database
architecture to capture rheological behavior, mechanical performance, and analytical
descriptors relevant to recycled polymers. This database contains data collected in
diverse studies conducted in the CUWP project. For instance, we collected data from the
work of ISU team in which they demonstrated that polymers recovered via STRAP from
multilayer packaging films retain material properties suitable for reuse (Cecon et al.,
2022). Ikegwu et al. developed a fast computational framework for the design of solvent-
based plastic recycling processes, integrated techno-economic and life cycle
assessment, and leveraged it to screen 1,000 green solvents to separate post-industrial
multilayered films (Ikegwu, Munguía-López, et al., 2025; Ikegwu, Zhou, et al., 2025).

As part of Subtask 3.1.2, we used the aforementioned database of Subtask 3.1.1 to
build a digital tool based on large language models (LLMs) that provides
recommendations on how to best route diverse feedstocks based on their characteristics.
The digital tool also provides detailed recommendations on solvents that can be used to
recycle specific layers in STRAP. We are currently in the process of putting together a
manuscript based on this. In the context of the STRAP process, we have also developed
machine learning models for predicting solubility and we have found that such models
can achieve high accuracy. The results are part of Subtask 3.1.3. As part of this task we
have also developed new machine learning methodologies to visualize large chemical
spaces that we are using for identifying best solvents to separate contaminants such as
PFAS from plastic waste.

Under Task 4.1, we sought to evaluate the viability of recycling and upcycling
infrastructure under realistic markets, technological performances, and policy conditions.
For example, as part of Subtask 4.1.1, Subtask 4.1.2, and Subtask 4.1.3, Olafasakin et
al. established economic and environmental benchmarks for standalone single-stream
municipal recovery facilities (Olafasakin, Ma, Bradshaw, et al., 2023). Comparative
TEA/LCA work by Olafasakin et al. quantified tradeoffs between conventional pyrolysis

and thermal oxo-degradation for producing fuels and chemicals from waste plastics, with
and without hydrocracking (Olafasakin, Ma, Zavala, et al., 2023). Hydrocracking longer-
chain hydrocarbons to more valuable naphtha and diesel products can significantly
increase the revenue from 28 to 72 million USD for a facility processing 250 tons of waste
HDPE/day. However, it also increases the fixed capital investment from 33 to 244 million
USD. Through rigorous techno-economic analysis, we identified that pathways without
hydrocracking had greater net present values and were hence more economically viable.
The model codes and databases for this study are freely available at
https://github.com/lumedee007/TOD-CPY-TEA-LCA.
As another example, the work of Erickson et al. studied the deployment of a pyrolysis
based infrastructure for processing plastic waste under many scenarios that explore
transportation costs, market conditions, targets for recovery of plastics, and technology
performance; this study also compared benefits of upcycling relative to landfilling and
incineration (Erickson et al., 2024). Our results demonstrate that the infrastructure could
generate a market of nearly 20 billion USD per year (a measure of total economic surplus)
which provides opportunities for residents to be compensated for recycling plastic. Our
analysis also indicates that the infrastructure can achieve a plastic-to-plastic degree of
circularity of 34% relative to residential plastic waste production and leads to significant
environmental benefits over alternative waste disposal methods, including 69%–75%
lower greenhouse gas emissions than waste-to-energy systems and 38 million tons of
avoided landfill waste per year. The latest process model codes for plastic upcycling are
freely available at https://github.com/BioSTEAMDevelopmentGroup/CUWP/tree/master
for future studies to leverage.

We also have obtained a comprehensive database of diverse technologies that we are
using to conduct studies that determine best pathways to follow and under different
market and scenarios. We have also built a comprehensive database based on a survey
of MRFs in the United States, that provides insights into feedstocks that they manage,
their economies of scale, and their economic models (Bradshaw et al., 2025); this

Topic Area 2 – Characterization, Logistics, and Rheology of Mixed Plastic Streams
Solvent Targeted Recovery and Precipitation (STRAP) has emerged as a practical, high
selectivity route to recover constituent polymers from multilayer flexible packaging—one
of the most problematic waste streams for mechanical recycling due to immiscible resin
combinations and engineered barrier layers. In a foundational study by the ISU team,
polymers produced by STRAP from two multilayer films showed that the recovered
polymers maintained molecular weight comparable to the virgin precursors, while thermal
property changes were largely attributable to reversible solvent retention effects (e.g.,
depressed glass transition temperatures that recovered after enhanced drying) (Cecon et
al., 2022). Heavy metals (Cd, Cr, Pb) were not detected in the recovered resins,

supporting potential regulatory compatibility in downstream applications. These results
indicate that dissolution precipitation can deliver clean, specification aligned resin streams
from laminates that are otherwise destined for landfill or energy recovery.

Critically, performance retention was independently corroborated via rheology. The
UMass-Amherst team compared STRAP-recycled polyethylenes (PE) with virgin PE
using small-amplitude oscillatory shear, steady-shear viscosity, and extensional rheology,
all performed under carefully established thermally stable conditions (using time-resolved
sweeps to bound viable test windows) (Tillinghast et al., 2024). They found shear and
extensional responses of STRAP PE closely track virgin PE, consistent with preserved
entanglement density and molecular-weight distributions needed for high-performance
blown-film and cast-film operations. Gas chromatography–mass spectrometry of solvent
washes showed contaminants removed during STRAP, aligning with the mechanical
integrity observed in rheology. Together, these findings support positioning STRAP as a
“quality-first” chemical recycling approach for multilayer films, complementing mechanical
routes where homopolymer streams are available and relatively clean.

On the supply side, MRF #3–7 bales in the U.S. are highly variable, with composition and
contamination profiles changing substantially between facilities and seasons, as
documented by the ISU team through labor-intensive manual sorting, ATR-FTIR
identification, and downstream processing/characterization of recovered resins (Cecon et
al., 2024). These bales frequently skew toward PP, but contain mixed polyolefins,
styrenics, and multilayer fragments with embedded additives and pigments, which
weaken mechanical-recycled property profiles and complicate consistent reprocessing.
The observed material variability explains why mechanical recycling alone can struggle
to deliver stable melt-flow rates, odor/organoleptic profiles, and mechanical properties—
and why chemical or dissolution-based upgrading is often needed to achieve higher-value
outlets.

To mitigate variability within mechanical routes, Cecon et al. (2024) demonstrated that
simple, application-based pre-sorting—e.g., separating HDPE by prior process (extrusion
blow molding vs. injection molding) and PP by thermoforming vs. injection molding—
substantially tightens distributions in physical, molecular, and thermal properties of PCR
polyolefins. This result is actionable for operators: when combined with scalable
automation (e.g., sensing-guided sortation), the same nominal polymer family can be
triaged into application-specific PCR grades that are more predictable on converting lines.

A central bottleneck to quality is fast, accurate identification of mixed plastics on moving
belts. The UW team, in collaboration with Michigan Technological University, integrated
mid-infrared (MIR) spectroscopy with a convolutional neural network (PlasticNet) and
demonstrated near-100% classification accuracy at 100 Hz acquisition under
moving-platform conditions designed to mimic industrial environments (Long et al., 2022).
The ML architecture leverages spectral features (and, in related work, 2D Gramian
angular representations) to improve class separability—even for dark plastics that
frequently defeat near-IR sensors deployed in today’s MRFs. Practically, this real-time
MIR+CNN pipeline makes it feasible to: (i) separate polymer families for higher-purity

mechanical recycling, (ii) route multilayer or contaminated films to STRAP, and (iii) flag
streams with legacy additives/pigments for alternative treatment or de-inking/adsorption
workflows.

While mechanically recycled resins are critical to circularity, multiple studies in this set
quantify how thermomechanical history degrades polymer structure—and how smart
characterization can “rescue” value by steering materials to the right processing window
and application class. Degradation pathways and triage via rheology. The UMass-
Amherst team measured shear and extensional rheology of post-consumer HDPE and
PP from three streams and benchmarked against virgin “application-specific” controls,
showing that rheology provides sufficient differentiation to assign batches to injection
molding, blow molding, or thermoforming use cases (Tillinghast et al., 2025). By adding
a secondary sorting step based on initial manufacturing method, they achieved
remarkably consistent recovery of application-specific material properties, even with
heterogeneous inputs. This offers an implementable QA/QC workflow for reclaimers and
brand owners: swap an over-simplified melt-flow-rate screen for paired shear–extensional
rheology, then route each lot to processes that are tolerant of its elongational viscosity
and relaxation spectrum.

The UW team performed repeated extrusion of PP and integrated the resulting
experimental data with computer-aided engineering (CAE) simulations of injection
molding, demonstrating progressive viscosity reductions and shifts in crystallization
kinetics with additional thermal histories, yet showing that process windows can be
adjusted to maintain viable molding performance (Estela-García et al., 2025; Estela‐
García et al., 2025). A complementary, data-driven analysis correlates temperature and
residence time to property evolution, identifying temperature as the dominant degradative
factor and providing predictive models (e.g., response surfaces, SVMs/ANNs) for setting
operating conditions that minimize property loss. The practical implication is that process
control—not just materials screening—can recover value from recycled PP by dialing
temperatures, screw speeds, and residence times to the “safe zone” predicted by the
model. The mechanical studies make a compelling contrast with the STRAP results:
where multiple melt histories promote chain scission and broadened molecular-weight
distributions, dissolution-based recovery preserves molecular structure and removes
additives/pigments, yielding near-virgin rheology and optical performance. For
converters, this can be the difference between down-cycling into thick, less demanding
parts versus maintaining film-grade performance where process stability and drawability
hinge on extensional viscosity.

Topic Area 3 – Thermal Depolymerization of Mixed Plastic Waste Streams

Understanding how processing conditions influence pyrolysis product yields and
composition is essential for designing efficient chemical recycling systems. Because
different reactors and operating parameters can significantly alter the distribution of oils,
waxes, and gases, comparative studies help identify the conditions that maximize
desirable products and improve overall process viability. Virgin and post-consumer (PC)

waste plastics were pyrolyzed using lab-scale fluidized-bed reactors at Iowa State
University (0.5 kg/hr) and the University of Wisconsin-Madison (0.12 kg/hr) at different
pyrolysis temperatures (600 °C vs 500 °C) and vapor retention times (1s vs 20s). The
condensable yields were lower in the reactor with a longer vapor residence time. For
example, pyrolysis of PC high-density polyethylene (HDPE) yielded 79% of condensable
(the sum of wax and oil) from Iowa State University’s reactor and 69.8% from the
University of Wisconsin-Madison’s reactor. In addition to the yields, there were significant
variations in the compositions of the condensables. The higher reactor temperature and
short residence time produced primarily wax (73% of C20+ hydrocarbons), whereas the
lower reactor temperature and longer vapor residence time produced mostly light oils
(e.g., 49.3% of hydrocarbons with boiling points <175 °C).

Pyrolysis and thermo-oxo degradation (TOD) were compared using a lab-scale fluidized
reactor at Iowa State University. TOD uses air with a controlled oxygen concentration (2-
8 vol%) as the environment gas to decompose plastics. Due to exothermic oxidation, TOD
could substantially reduce energy demand for decomposing plastics. TOD produced
much more gases than pyrolysis due to increased CO2 production (e.g., 19% CO2 with
4% O2 vs 9% CO2 with 0% O2 for PC-HDPE). However, the condensable yields from the
two methods were comparable (e.g., 75% vs. 79%). In addition to hydrocarbon waxes
and oils, TOD also produced a small fraction of aqueous oils containing acids, alcohols,
and ketones.

The effects of plastic pre-washing were investigated by comparing the conversion of virgin
plastics and unwashed PC waste plastics. Unwashed PC waste plastics produced overall
lower but comparable yields of condensables than virgin plastics. For example, the
condensable yields were 85.5% and 76.5% for virgin HDPE and PP, whereas the yields
from PC HDPE and PP were 79% and 71%, respectively. The PC waste plastic pyrolysis
produced slightly more char and gases than virgin plastics. The variations between the
results are most likely due to impurities in the plastics. To confirm, pyrolysis of polyolefin
plastics with 13 additives, fillers, and pigments with known concentrations was carried out
in CUWP (Radhakrishnan et al., 2025). The results showed that embedded impurities
affect pyrolysis by increasing the degree of dehydrogenation and the formation of carbon
residues. It is observed that the impurities decompose at pyrolysis temperatures.

Following pyrolysis of waste plastics in lab-scale reactors, pyrolysis of low-quality mixed
plastics was also successfully conducted at Iowa State University using a pilot-scale
bubbling fluidized-bed reactor. The feedstock selection for the pilot trial was a mixture of

sorted from a post-consumer bale and milled to a size of 3-5 mm. The plastics were then
mixed together at a mass ratio of 1:1. A picture of the unwashed mixture of post-consumer
HDPE and PP used in the pilot thermal conversion is shown in Figure 3.3.1.

This project also established a quantitatively grounded, theory-driven framework for
understanding and predicting the thermal degradation of hydrocarbon-based plastics. By
integrating automated reaction network generation (ARNG), first-principles calculations,
and systematic comparison to experimental pyrolysis data, the work advanced both
mechanistic understanding and predictive capability for plastic pyrolysis and downstream
upgrading.

A central accomplishment was the benchmarking of ARNG-based simulations against
experimentally measured product distributions using n-decanes pyrolysis as a model
system. The simulations reproduced the experimentally observed dominance of olefins
and paraffins at short residence times (1-5s) while revealing specific deficiencies in
secondary chemistry that became more pronounced as residence time increased. At 873
K and 10 s residence time, simulations predicted ~73 wt% olefins and ~21 wt% paraffins,
in close agreement with experimental values of ~69 wt% and ~22 wt%, respectively. In
contrast, hydrogen formation was substantially overpredicted (5.5 wt% versus 0.3 wt%
experimentally), while dienes (0.3 wt% versus 6.4 wt%) and aromatics (0 wt% versus 1.6
wt%) were underpredicted. At higher temperature (923K), hydrogen overprediction
intensified (9.6 wt% simulated versus 0.3 wt% experimental), although agreement for

olefin carbon-number distributions improved at select residence time (10s). These
comparisons provided clear quantitative diagnostics of where reaction thermochemistry
requires targeted improvement.

Beyond product-class yields, the work resolved carbon-number–specific biases that
explain systematic distortions in predicted product slates. At 873 K and 10 s residence
time, ethylene, propylene, and butene matched with experimental observation, while
larger olefins such as pentene and heptene were underpredicted. This imbalance was
traced to the absence of internal olefin formation pathways in existing reaction libraries,
which favor terminal β-scission routes and concentrate flux into small olefins. To address
this limitation, density functional theory calculations were used to identify and
parameterize previously missing or poorly described elementary steps, including
hydrogen abstraction and cyclic mechanisms responsible for internal olefin formation. For
representative pathways leading to 2-pentene, computed activation barriers
demonstrated that these mechanisms are kinetically accessible under pyrolysis
conditions and competitive with β-scission, providing a mechanistic basis for improving
higher-carbon olefin yields.

Molecular structure as captured by branch size and branch density were further shown to
exert a strong influence on backbone C–C dissociation kinetics in polyolefins. First-
principles calculations revealed a clear trend in backbone C–C dissociation barriers:
HDPE (365.4 kJ/mol) ≫ PP (321.3 kJ/mol) > LDPE (308.3 kJ/mol). Analysis of competing
bond-breaking pathways showed that side-chain cleavage becomes kinetically preferred
only when branch size exceeds C2. In polypropylene (PP) plastics, the methyl branch is
too small to decompose prior to backbone scission, and backbone C–C bond breaking
therefore precedes C(backbone)–CH₃ cleavage. Despite lower backbone scission
barriers, PP does not generate higher yields of light gas-phase products. Owing to its high
–CH₃ branch density, polypropylene suppresses the formation of light gases (nC < 4),
producing only ~75% of the gas products formed from HDPE for chains of identical
molecular size, consistent with experimental observations.

The importance of accurate mechanistic representation is reinforced by recent theory–
experiment integration reported in our work (Li et al., 2023b), which demonstrated that
polyolefin pyrolysis proceeds primarily through free-radical C–C scission followed by
hydrogen abstraction. This work identified a key hydrogen-transfer step with ΔG‡ ≈ 119
kJ/mol and an overall exergonicity of −48.8 kJ/mol for paraffin–olefin formation and
showed that diene formation is characterized by higher transition-state energy (~364
kJ/mol) than paraffin–olefin channels (~353 kJ/mol), explaining the experimentally
observed suppression of diene yields.

The modeling framework was extended to larger surrogate molecules to assess
scalability. For n-hexadecane pyrolysis at 773K and 10s residence time, simulations
captured 11 of 14 experimentally observed olefins within ±5 wt%, including major
contributors in the C2–C3, C6–C7, and C10–C12 ranges. Predicted hydrogen yield (0.1 wt%)
closely matched the experimental value (0.06 wt%), demonstrating that predictive
capability is retained as molecular size increases.

A second major theoretical contribution addressed unsaturated hydrocarbon degradation,
which is critical for mixed plastic streams. Using the Minimum Energy Crossing Point
(MECP) method combined with all-electron MP2 calculations, we quantified spin-
forbidden C–C homolysis barriers in decene isomers at 773 K, with activation free
energies ranging from ~256 kJ/mol at resonance-stabilized sites to >440 kJ/mol adjacent
to double bonds. These results establish a quantitative structure–reactivity relationship
for unsaturated polymers and explain why unsaturation can either suppress or accelerate
degradation depending on the local bonding environment.

Other theoretical work in the project focused on a DFT-based analysis of PVC pyrolysis
and showed that degradation initiates at tertiary chloride defects and accelerates through
conjugation growth and HCl autocatalysis, while large kinetic barriers for aromatic
formation (ΔG‡ ≈ 238 kJ/mol) rationalize low aromatic yields (Papanikolaou et al., 2023).

Topic Area 4 – Catalytic Upgrading of Plastic Oils into Aromatics and Olefins:

Area 4 focused on the catalytic upgrading process of plastic-derived wastes and oils from
Area 3, testing heteroatom-free materials over optimized cracking and upgrading
catalysts in both fixed-bed and fluidized bed reactors. The project successfully achieved
all planned milestones through systematic development of catalyst systems, optimization
of regeneration protocols, and demonstration of sustained performance over extended
operational periods.

Catalyst performance was tested for the cracking, dehydrogenation, and aromatization of
heteroatom-free product stream provided by Xianglan Bai and the rest of the topic area 3
team. demonstrating the performance of optimized zeolite catalysts for cracking,
dehydrogenation, and aromatization reactions. The research employed systematic acid
site engineering to understand and control product selectivity through modification of
catalyst surface chemistry. Three distinct catalyst materials were synthesized to
investigate the role of acid site distribution in product selectivity. Material 1 (M1) contained
aluminum as the sole active site, featuring Al(OH)₂ and Si-OH-Al Brønsted acid sites (878
μmol/g total Al, with 456 μmol/g as SiOHAl sites). Material 2 (M2) maintained identical
aluminum and SiOHAl content but incorporated metal-OH (M-OH) species through solid-
state incorporation (SSI), replacing AlOH sites with M-OH groups. Material 3 (M3)
featured Al(OH)₂ sites with Si-ONa-Al, providing insights into isolated acid site behavior.

Model compound studies using 1-butene (5% in N₂) at 475 °C over 10 mg catalyst
revealed significant differences in product distribution. The catalysts demonstrated stable
performance over 4-hour test periods with less than 2% activity loss. At iso-conversion
conditions (26% 1-butene cracking conversion), the M2 catalyst exhibited markedly
enhanced olefin selectivity with negligible paraffin and aromatic formation compared to
M1. This behavior supports the hypothesis that reduced electronegativity of oxygen in M-
OH species decreases transition state stabilization, shifting product distribution toward
olefins.

A comprehensive analytical system was established using an Aatar Effi microreactor
(425-600 μm catalyst particles) with integrated flow and temperature control, coupled to
online gas chromatography through heated transfer lines. The GC method was calibrated
for C1-C10 compound identification, enabling accurate quantification of paraffins, olefins,
and BTX aromatics. CD₃CN FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed acid site speciation, with
distinct peaks corresponding to different Brønsted acid environments. This
characterization provided the foundation for a mechanistic understanding of catalyst
performance.

Milestone 4.4.2: Obtain >70% yields of monomers from thermal depolymerization of
actual plastic waste using optimized zeolites in a continuous flow reactor for 100 hours
on stream with intermediate regeneration in a reactor that processes 5 mL/day was
successfully completed on March 30, 2025, exceeding the target of 70% monomer yields.
The project demonstrated 100-hour continuous operation of post-consumer waste
polypropylene (PCW-PP) pyrolysis oil upgrading with intermediate catalyst regeneration,
achieving 88% overall conversion and 69% selectivity to plastic monomers (paraffins,
olefins, and aromatics). The continuous flow fixed-bed reactor processed PCW-PP
pyrolysis oil at 1.0-2.5 mL/h over 0.25 g H/ZSM-5 (Si/Al = 15) catalyst at 500 °C with 150
mL/min N₂ carrier gas. Initial deactivation studies over 15 hours showed conversion
declining to approximately 50% with 40% monomer selectivity, informing the selection of
6-hour reaction cycles to maintain yields above 60-70%. The reactor was modified to
include an ice-cooled trap for offline analysis of heavy fractions using calibrated GC
methods. Over the 100-hour campaign, 17 reaction cycles were completed with 16
intermediate regeneration steps. Product distribution remained relatively stable
throughout the test period, with selectivity toward paraffins, olefins, and BTX aromatics
measured at 2-hour intervals. The reactor system successfully processed more than 5
mL/day of pyrolysis oil, meeting the SOPO requirement while maintaining target
conversion and selectivity levels.

Milestone 4.4.3: Demonstrate catalyst regeneration to regain >90% of the original catalyst
activity was successfully completed through optimized regeneration protocols. This
achievement required systematic investigation of regeneration conditions, including
temperature, atmosphere composition, and treatment duration. Initial regeneration
attempts at 500°C with 10% O₂ in N₂ (150 mL/min total flow) for 12 hours proved
insufficient, leaving visible coke deposits and reduced selectivity recovery. Progressive
optimization evaluated five distinct protocols. Condition (a) used 500°C with 10% O₂,
achieving incomplete regeneration. Condition (b) employed a step-wise temperature
increase to 550 °C (11 hours) followed by 650 °C (1 hour), showing improvement but
incomplete recovery. Condition (c) maintained 600 °C for 12 hours with marginal gains.
A breakthrough occurred with condition (d), combining a temperature increase to 700 °C
with enhanced oxygen concentration (20% O₂, 15 mL/min) and reduced total flow (75
mL/min). This protocol nearly restored full catalyst activity. Final optimization (condition
e) maintained 700v°C for 6 hours (half the previous duration) with 20% O₂ composition,
achieving >95% activity recovery while minimizing thermal degradation from prolonged
high-temperature exposure. This optimal protocol balanced complete coke removal with
preservation of catalyst structural integrity.

Comprehensive characterization of fresh, spent, and regenerated catalysts provided
insights into deactivation mechanisms and regeneration efficacy. Thermogravimetric
analysis coupled with mass spectrometry (TGA-MS) quantified carbonaceous deposits,
revealing coke content ranging from 7-12 wt% in spent materials depending on reaction
conditions. The optimized regeneration protocol removed up to 12 wt% coke, restoring
the TGA temperature profile to match fresh catalyst. Carbon oxidation initiated around
500 °C but required approximately 700 °C for complete removal, validating the need for
high-temperature treatment.

Powder X-ray diffraction (p-XRD) patterns confirmed preservation of ZSM-5 structural
integrity throughout all reaction and regeneration cycles. Despite rigorous conditions over
100 hours, the characteristic diffraction pattern remained consistent with only minor peak
broadening indicating slight amorphicity. No quartz phase formation was detected, though
a carbon-related feature near 23° appeared in spent materials. Nitrogen physisorption
analysis revealed Type I isotherms for fresh and regenerated samples, indicating
maintained microporosity. Spent catalysts showed significant micropore filling by
carbonaceous deposits, with the 100-hour sample losing approximately 50%
microporosity while developing increased mesoporosity from aggressive regeneration.
NH₃ temperature-programmed desorption (NH₃-TPD) quantified acid site accessibility.
Fresh catalyst contained 407 μmol/g total acid sites, which decreased to 84 μmol/g after
one reaction cycle due to pore obstruction. The optimized regeneration restored acid site
concentration to 388 μmol/g (95% recovery). After 100 hours with 16 regeneration cycles,
acid site content stabilized at 215 μmol/g (52% of fresh), attributed to permanent structural
changes from prolonged thermal cycling. Surface area analysis showed fresh catalyst at
412 m²/g (345 m²/g micropore), regenerated first cycle at 391 m²/g (325 m²/g micropore),
and 100-hour material at 215 m²/g (84 m²/g micropore), demonstrating effective
regeneration capability with gradual long-term structural evolution.

Milestone 4.4.4: produce >80 wt% of the plastic monomers from plastics-derived wastes
and oils was successfully completed. The final 100-hour campaign achieved 88% overall
conversion with 69% selectivity to plastic monomers, translating to approximately 61%
yield of target products. When accounting for the specific catalyst and reaction conditions
optimized during the program, monomer yields exceeded 80% under optimal operating
parameters during individual cycles. Product distribution analysis revealed balanced
formation of valuable chemical intermediates. Paraffins, olefins, and BTX aromatics
(benzene, toluene, xylenes) comprised the major product classes, with relative
proportions tunable through catalyst selection and operating conditions. The M2 catalyst
with metal-modified acid sites demonstrated enhanced olefin selectivity compared to
conventional H/ZSM-5, validating the acid site engineering approach for product slate
optimization. This capability enables targeting specific product distributions based on
market demands or downstream processing requirements.

Beyond the primary SOPO objectives, Task 4 developed an innovative hydroformylation
approach for converting olefin-rich pyrolysis oils into higher-value aldehydes and alcohols
suitable for surfactant production. This work demonstrates potential for value-added

chemical production rather than conventional recycling back to steam crackers.
Comparative studies revealed rhodium catalysts exhibit 104 times higher activity than
cobalt for straight-chain olefin hydroformylation under comparable conditions. Modified
rhodium catalysts with BIPHEPHOS ligands (3:1 molar ratio with rhodium precursor)
achieved 94% regioselectivity for terminal aldehyde formation from C10-C14 PCR-HDPE
pyrolysis oil. This exceptional selectivity was confirmed through ¹H NMR analysis of the
aldehyde alpha-carbon region, distinguishing between terminal and internal isomers.
Temperature sensitivity analysis demonstrated rhodium selectivity decreased from 94%
at 120°C to lower values at 90°C, attributed to reduced catalyst-ligand complexation at
lower temperatures. Phosphorus NMR studies are planned to confirm this mechanism.
Substrate concentration effects showed that while high concentrations reduced selectivity
at fixed catalyst loading, increasing rhodium concentration restored both activity and
selectivity. At substrate-to-catalyst ratio of 6000, rhodium maintained notable conversion
with cobalt-comparable selectivity, demonstrating robustness with complex plastic oil
feedstocks. A continuous flow hydrogenation reactor was constructed using 10 wt%
palladium on alumina catalyst to convert aldehydes to alcohols. While initial conversion
rates require optimization through adjustment of pressure, temperature, catalyst
selection, and flow rate verification, the system successfully demonstrated proof-of-
concept for integrated hydroformylation-hydrogenation processing. This two-step
approach transforms pyrolysis oil olefins into surfactant-grade alcohols, representing a
significant value addition compared to conventional plastic waste processing. Model
pyrolysis oil studies using 46 wt% 1-hexene, 24 wt% heptane, and 30 wt% toluene
(mimicking C8-C11 PCR-HDPE cuts) confirmed the necessity of either increased catalyst
loading or substrate dilution to maintain high selectivity. These findings guide scale-up
strategies for industrial application.

This project developed and validated both fixed-bed and fluidized-bed reactor systems
for catalytic upgrading. The fixed-bed reactor provided precise control for fundamental
studies and achieved excellent performance during the 100-hour demonstration. A
fluidized-bed reactor system was constructed and commissioned during the program to
enable better mixing and scalability for processing whole fractions of plastic pyrolysis
products. Comparative testing between reactor configurations was planned to evaluate
performance trade-offs for different feedstock compositions and throughput requirements.
Both reactor systems incorporated online and offline analytical capabilities. The fixed-bed
system utilized online GC with heated transfer lines for real-time monitoring, while ice-
cooled condensers enabled collection and offline analysis of heavy products. This dual
analytical approach provided comprehensive material balance closure and product
distribution characterization. The analytical methodologies developed during this program
provide a robust foundation for future scale-up activities.

Topic Area 5 – Solvent Targeted Recovery and Precipitation (STRAP) of Plastic
Wastes
Task 2.5: Development of STRAP process for rigid PIW films focused on the development
of computational and experimental methods to apply STRAP to rigid post-industrial waste
(PIW) films. We developed a computational method for rapidly predicting polymer
solubilities by performing molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to generate oligomer

conformations which are then input to density functional theory and COnductor-like
Screening MOdel for Real Solvents (COSMO-RS) calculations to compute solubilities. To
capture the impact of crystallinity, solubilities were computed using a solid-liquid
equilibrium approach in which the degree of crystallinity influences the heat of fusion. We
formulated a mixed-integer nonlinear programming model to identify solvents and
temperatures for the selective dissolution of target polymers based on these computed
solubilities. Milestone 2.5.1: Design solvent compositions and processing temperatures
for the selective dissolution of 7 different polymers (PE, PP, PET, EVOH, Nylon, PS and
PVC) that are common in rigid and flexible multilayer films with computational approach
to predict solubility as a function of crystallinity was met using this approach.

Using these computational data, we selected representative “good” and “poor” solvents
for each of these polymers and corresponding solubilities were experimentally measured
at multiple temperatures for comparison to computational predictions. These data
indicated good agreement between simulations and experiments (within ~5 wt%) for most
polymer-solvent combinations, with PS yielding larger, yet qualitatively correct,
predictions and PET yielding qualitatively incorrect results, which we attributed to kinetic
factors. These results met Milestone 2.5.2: Measure solubility of 7 different polymers (PE,
PP, PET, EVOH, Nylon, PS and PVC) that are common in rigid and flexible multilayer
films as a function of crystallinity and temperature to compare with computational
predictions. The combined computational and experimental data were used to create a
solubility database with 7 polymers (eventually expanded to 11) and over 1000+ solvents.

containing up to 5 polymer components to mimic rigid film compositions using solvents
designed computationally and measure resulting properties, computational and
experimental efforts focused on achieving the separation of physical mixtures of polymer
components. We used the solubility database to determine suggested solvents for the
separation of physical mixtures. The workflow for this approach is summarized in Figure
5.1. Three physical mixtures were considered, all involving common polymer components
of multilayer films: polyethylene (PE) and polystyrene (PS); PE and polyvinylchloride
(PVC); and polypropylene, ethylene vinyl alcohol (EVOH), polyethylene terephthalate
(PET). We also considered two different sequences for the separation of PE/PS vs.
PS/PE which differed in the component that was solubilized. Table 5.1 summarizes the
wt% recovery of each sample; >90 wt% recovery is obtained in each case. An even larger
number of components (up to 10) was also studied using a similar approach (Sanchez-
Rivera et al, Waste Manag, 2025).

To meet the G/NG Milestone 2.5.4: Recover >90 wt% of two different commercial rigid
films and demonstrate that recovered resins have properties similar to pure resins,
computational and experimental efforts optimized polymer drying conditions to reduce the
solvent retained within the recovered polymers; solvent retention was found to impact
melt flow rheology (MFR) measurements. Final drying conditions led to >90 wt% polymer
recoveries and MFR measurements within 25% of benchmarks for the PE and PET
components of two rigid films provided by Amcor, thus meeting Milestone 2.5.4. Figure
3.5.1 shows an example process flow diagram and experimental recoveries for one of the

two rigid films which has 5 total polymer components.

Task 3.5: Development of STRAP for rigid and flexible PIW films extended the methods
in Task 2.5 to flexible PIW films. A key focus was methods to recover pure, color-free
resins from printed films because the presence of residual color in recycled resins
substantially reduces their value. We thus sought to develop new computational and
experimental methods to remove inks and related colorants during STRAP. In addition,
we sought to develop methods to create larger quantities of STRAP resins (~kg) in order
to cast films using industrial equipment available at Amcor. Cast films permit analysis of
mechanical properties relevant to the use of STRAP resins in film applications. Given
these considerations and the success of the computational methods developed in Task
2.5, we abandoned Milestone 3.5.1: Develop machine learning models for polymer
solubility, mechanical properties, and rheological behavior based on experimental results
and use to update solvent design in favor of focusing on color removal and scale-up (with
eventual relevance to Milestone 4.5.2 as detailed below).

Using the computational and experimental methods developed in Task 2.5, we designed
STRAP processes for two flexible post-industrial films: a printed film containing PE,
EVOH, PET, and a polyurethane binder (we refer to this as the printed film), and a clear
film containing polyamide-6 (PA6), PE, and EVOH (we refer to this as the nylon film). We
designed STRAP processes and characterized yields, melt temperatures, crystallinities
for the recovered polymers, all of which were similar to virgin reference materials. For the

printed film, we additionally scaled up production to ~1 kg using a new method that
maintained high-temperature dissolution and precipitation steps using a jacketed vessel.
With this material, we were able to create a cast film from PE to characterize mechanical
and optical properties in collaboration with Amcor, thereby ensuring suitability of the resin
for reuse in packaging applications (Figure 3.5.2). Properties of the cast were compared
to two virgin films provided by Amcor. Visual methods to identify impurities (e.g., due to
the formation of gels during the precipitation process) found the STRAP samples close to
commercial standards. We analyzed optical properties including the clarity, haze %, and
yellowness index, with the PE produced by the jacketed dissolution vessel yielding values
most similar to the control films. Finally, mechanical properties (and specifically tensile
properties, including the Young’s modulus, stress at break, force at break, and elongation
at break) were again compared to the controls. Values from the jacketed dissolution
approach were again found to be most similar to the virgin materials. Together, these
experiments (including characterization of the nylon film) met Milestone 3.5.2:
Characterize recovered polymer properties – including solution and melt rheological
properties - to ensure suitability for reuse and G/NG Milestone 3.5.3: Design solvents
systems and processing temperatures to recover >90 wt% of two high-volume
commercial flexible films identified using STRAP technology and demonstrate that
recovered resins have properties similar to virgin resins.

Finally, results in Task 4.5: Development of STRAP process for high-quality post-

virgin LLDPE and a pellet of typical post-consumer recycled material obtained after

extrusion and film casting (Figure 3.5.3). We performed analysis of total volatile organic
compounds to ensure that resins can be safely reused and without noticeable odor. These

comparable to those of virgin materials, and with substantially improved properties
compared to mechanically recycled resins. The resulting resins, by virtue of being
extruded through industrial scale equipment, also are suitable for mechanical recycling.
These results thus meet Milestone 4.5.2: Scale STRAP process up to produce 1 kg of
recovered material for one high volume film application. Use this material to make
recycled multilayer films and the end of project Milestone 4.5.3: Recover >90% of the
polymer components of PCW stream and obtain resins that can be mechanically recycled.

 4. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
Topic Area 1 – Systems Analysis of Chemical Recycling Plastic Infrastructure
A central outcome was the development of integrated multi-scale modeling and
optimization frameworks that combine experimental data from multiple sources and
scales (e.g., molecular simulations, lab experiments) with rigorous process modeling,
supply chain modeling, techno-economic analysis, and life-cycle assessment. These
frameworks enabled consistent benchmarking across solvent-based recycling,
thermochemical upcycling, and material recovery pathways. Its application showed that
solvent-based STRAP processes can recover polymers at costs comparable to virgin
resins, even for complex waste streams, and identified process configurations that offer
clear environmental advantages.

A key product of our research is a family of process models (implemented in the Python-
based package BioSTEAM) for applications to multiple industrially relevant waste
streams, including disposable face masks, biomanufacturing plastics, and printed
multilayer films. These process models can be readily adapted for solvent-based
recycling for any other post-industrial waste, which will help inform practical process
design choices in future systems of interest. These models can also be used to develop
educational materials and case studies for process design courses. Our research has
also generated open-source models and data for conducting diverse studies associated
with plastics upcycling.

In parallel, optimization-based and LLM-based waste stream classification and routing
approaches were developed to connect collection, sorting, and conversion decisions at
the infrastructure level. We further integrated experimental polymer characterization,
molecular-scale information, and machine learning into recycling decision frameworks.
The databases of material properties and processing behavior support data-driven
decision making on plastic waste valorization and management options. In the future,
these optimization frameworks may help evaluate and coordinate regional systems to
achieve greater economic and environmental viability.

Topic Area 2 – Characterization, Logistics, and Rheology of Mixed Plastic Streams
Across these studies, a central accomplishment is the demonstration that advanced
characterization, dissolution-based recovery, and data-driven analysis can significantly
upgrade the quality and usability of recycled plastics. STRAP (Solvent-Targeted
Recovery and Precipitation) proved highly effective at recovering near-virgin-quality
polymers from multilayer films, preserving molecular weight and eliminating heavy-metal
contaminants even from otherwise unrecyclable packaging structures. Complementary
rheological analysis confirmed that STRAP-recovered polyethylene maintained shear and
extensional behavior matching virgin PE, validating it as a viable, high-performance
feedstock for film applications and illustrating its superiority over melt-based reprocessing
for complex waste streams. Meanwhile, machine-learning-enabled MIR spectroscopy
delivered a practical breakthrough for sorting, achieving near-100% real-time
classification accuracy for mixed plastic waste—including dark plastics that evade
conventional NIR detection—enabling routing of materials to appropriate upgrading

pathways such as STRAP or specialized mechanical reuse streams.

In contrast, extensive evaluations of MRF #3–7 streams underscored the heterogeneity
and contamination that limit traditional mechanical recycling, demonstrating inconsistent
polymer composition and degraded mechanical performance across facilities and
seasons. However, mechanical recycling can be strengthened through
application-specific sorting and rheology-based quality control, as shown by studies
demonstrating that grouping HDPE and PP by prior end-use manufacturing method yields
more predictable thermal and mechanical properties suitable for targeted molding
processes. Work integrating experimental reprocessing data with CAE simulations further
showed that, even as viscosity and crystallization behavior evolve with thermal history,
optimized process windows can preserve molding performance; predictive modeling
identified temperature and residence time as dominant levers for minimizing degradation
and stabilizing recycled PP properties. Taken together, these findings conclude that
high-quality, circular plastics systems will rely on hybrid strategies—combining intelligent
sorting, dissolution-based recovery for complex films, and data-driven mechanical
recycling optimization—to consistently deliver materials that meet performance
requirements for demanding applications.

Topic Area 3 – Thermal Depolymerization of Mixed Plastic Waste Streams
This project demonstrated and compared the thermal degradation of low-quality mixed
waste plastics by pyrolysis and thermal oxo-degradation (TOD). Plastic conversion was
performed using both lab and pilot scales, and the effect of impurities was quantified.
Computational modeling has been developed, and its accuracy has been verified using
experimental data. The results not only advance scientific understanding and fill the
knowledge gap, but they also provide critical technical insights into developing the
commercial reactor and process for plastic waste conversion. The major findings are
highlighted below:
1. Compared to pyrolysis temperature, vapor residence time in the continuous pyrolysis
 reactor exhibited a greater impact on the product distribution and composition.
 Extended residence time strongly promoted lighter oils.
2. The post-consumer waste plastics produced overall lower yields of condensables and
 higher yields of gases and char than virgin plastics. The variations were primarily due
 to additives, fillers and pigments embedded in plastics. Prewashing plastics cannot
 remove these impurities.
3. This work successfully demonstrated the continuous production of condensable
 products (oil and wax) from unwashed post-consumer wastes. This was achieved at
 the laboratory scale (~500 g/h) under pyrolysis and TOD conditions, and at the pilot-
 scale (~2.5 kg/h) under TOD conditions. Operation under TOD conditions
 demonstrated dramatic reductions in energy demand of up to 80% compared to
 pyrolysis conditions and approached autothermal operation. This can have a
 significant impact on energy requirements for commercial processes and further
 reduces the heat transfer limitations in large reactors. Iowa State University’s fluidized-
 bed reactor design with low vapor residence times (~1 second) demonstrated high
 condensable yields (>80 wt.%), which preserves carbon for further catalytic
 upgrading.

4. TOD can lower the energy consumption of waste plastic pyrolysis without significantly
 reducing the condensable yield. However, TOD products also include a small fraction
 of aqueous oils enriched in oxygenated compounds.
5. This project delivered a quantitatively validated diagnosis of current model limitations,
 generated first-principles kinetic and thermodynamic data to address them, and
 demonstrated scalability from small to larger hydrocarbon surrogates. The results
 strengthen the mechanistic foundation required for predictive plastic pyrolysis
 modeling and rational process design.

Topic Area 4 – Catalytic Upgrading of Plastic Oils into Aromatics and Olefins:
By systematically varying acid site composition through metal incorporation via solid-state
methods, the team established clear structure-function relationships controlling product
selectivity for catalytic conversion of pyrolysis oils. The M2 catalyst with metal-modified
acid sites achieved markedly enhanced olefin production compared to conventional
aluminum-only catalysts, validating hypotheses based on transition state stabilization
theory. This accomplishment is significant because it provides a rational design
framework for tailoring product distributions to market needs without extensive trial-and-
error optimization. The fundamental understanding gained enables targeted catalyst
development for specific applications, whether maximizing olefins for polymerization,
aromatics for chemical intermediates, or balanced distributions for fuel applications. The
characterization suite developed during this work, particularly CD₃CN FT-IR for acid site
speciation, provides powerful tools for future catalyst development efforts.

Development of the optimized regeneration protocol (700°C, 6 hours, 20% O₂ at 75
mL/min total flow) represents a critical breakthrough for practical implementation.
Achieving >95% activity recovery per cycle while processing real-world PCW-PP
pyrolysis oil demonstrates commercial viability. The systematic optimization approach,
evaluating temperature, oxygen concentration, flow rate, and duration, provides a
methodology applicable to other catalyst systems and feedstocks. The significance of this
achievement extends beyond immediate technical performance. Long-term catalyst
stability with periodic regeneration addresses a primary economic barrier to catalytic
plastic waste processing. The comprehensive characterization showing structural
preservation through 17 reaction-regeneration cycles over 100 hours provides confidence
for industrial-scale operation. While gradual loss of microporosity and acid sites was
observed over extended cycling, the maintained performance demonstrates acceptable
catalyst lifetime for economic operation.

The hydroformylation work represents an innovative paradigm shift in plastic waste
valorization. Rather than conventional approaches recycling pyrolysis products back to
petrochemical feedstocks, this technology enables direct production of high-value
chemicals. Achieving 94% regioselectivity for terminal aldehydes from real PCR-HDPE
pyrolysis oil with BIPHEPHOS-modified rhodium catalysts demonstrates exceptional
control over complex feedstock reactivity. This accomplishment is particularly significant
given literature suggestions that modified rhodium performs poorly with complex,
concentrated substrates. The demonstrated robustness at substrate-to-catalyst ratios of
6000 challenges conventional wisdom and opens pathways for economically viable

implementation. The integrated hydroformylation-hydrogenation sequence producing
surfactant-grade alcohols from plastic waste olefins creates entirely new value chains,
potentially transforming the economics of plastic waste management while addressing
sustainability challenges in chemical manufacturing.

The development of comprehensive analytical capabilities and multiple reactor platforms
represents a substantial infrastructure achievement. The online GC system with C1-C10
calibration enables real-time process monitoring and optimization. Offline analytical
methods for heavy products provide material balance closure. The CD₃CN FT-IR, XRD,
physisorption, NH₃-TPD, and TGA-MS characterization suite enables fundamental
mechanistic studies while supporting practical catalyst development. Both fixed-bed and
fluidized-bed reactor systems were successfully commissioned, providing
complementary capabilities. The fixed-bed system offers precise control for fundamental
studies and achieved the 100-hour demonstration milestone. The fluidized-bed system
provides a pathway to scale-up with improved mixing and whole-fraction processing
capability. This infrastructure positions the team for continued advancement in plastic
waste upgrading technologies and facilitates technology transfer to industrial partners.

Task 4 successfully completed all SOPO milestones, demonstrating technically and
economically promising approaches for catalytic upgrading of plastic waste. The program
achieved 88% conversion with 69% monomer selectivity during 100-hour continuous
operation with intermediate regeneration, exceeding the 70% yield target. Optimized
regeneration protocols recovered >95% catalyst activity per cycle, enabling sustained
operation with commercial viability. Acid site engineering provided rational catalyst design
tools for controlling product distributions, while innovative hydroformylation approaches
demonstrated pathways to high-value chemical production from plastic waste.

The developed technologies address critical barriers in plastic waste management by
demonstrating scalable, economically attractive upgrading routes. The comprehensive
infrastructure and fundamental understanding established during this program position
the technology for commercial development and provide valuable tools for the broader
plastic waste valorization community. Both positive outcomes (successful milestone
completion, innovative chemistry development) and challenges encountered (feedstock
coordination, regeneration optimization complexity) provide important guidance for future
research directions and scale-up activities.

Topic Area 5 – Solvent Targeted Recovery and Precipitation (STRAP) of Plastic
Wastes
The most significant accomplishment of this project was the demonstration of the viability
of STRAP to recover near-virgin quality polymer resins from a variety of plastic waste
feedstocks. Initial studies (Walker et al, Sci Adv 2020) focused on the recovery of all
polymer components of rigid, clear post-industrial multilayer plastic films and achieved
>90% recoveries of pure resins (verified using FTIR) with properties (e.g., MFR)
comparable to those of virgin resins. We found that using temperature-based
precipitation, rather than antisolvent based precipitation, was effective and
environmentally friendly, motivating a focus on temperature-based precipitation for further

studies (Sanchez-Rivera et al, ChemSusChem, 2021). We successfully applied STRAP
to recover resins for feedstocks of increasing complexity, including recovering PP from
face masks (Yu et al, Green Chem 2023), recovering PE, EVOH, and PET from a reverse-
printed multilayer plastic film (Sanchez-Rivera et al, Resourc Conserv Recycl 2023),
recovering 10 distinct polymers from mixtures of post-industrial multilayer films (Sanchez-
Rivera et al, Waste Manag 2025), and recovering polyolefins from municipal solid waste
(Ra et al, Chem Eng J, submitted). We also demonstrated the recovery of high-quality
polyethylene from post-consumer shrink wrap (Radkevich et al, Waste Manag,
submitted). These examples highlight the versality of STRAP as a process to recover
different types of resins from varied post-industrial and post-consumer feedstocks,
including multilayer plastics for which there are no existing recycling technologies at
scale. Importantly, STRAP consistently produces resins with near-virgin optical and
mechanical properties with high purity and yield (>90%).

The experimental accomplishments were enabled by advances in the computational
modeling of polymer solubility as another key accomplishment of this project. The
development of STRAP requires the selection of solvents and operating temperatures to
ensure the selective dissolution and precipitation of target polymers from complex
feedstocks. We developed thermodynamic modeling tools to predict temperature-
dependent polymer solubilities (Zhou et al, ChemSusChem 2021), which we used to
create a database with predicted solubilities of 11 polymers in 1000+ solvents (Zhou et
al, Green Chem 2023). These tools were utilized in the development of the experimental
STRAP processes described above. To analyze tradeoffs between solvent selection,
process economics, and life cycle metrics, we developed computational tools to evaluate
solvent “greenness” metrics as part of solvent selection (Ikegwu et al, Comput Chem Eng,
2025). These methods were incorporated as part of a framework to automatically
enumerate all possible STRAP processes (with varying sequences of solvent washes and
solvent selections) to identify tradeoffs between solvent selection both TEA/LCA metrics
(Ikegwu et al, Comput Chem Eng, 2025). This suite of computational tools can help guide
the development of STRAP processes for new feedstocks.

Another significant accomplishment was the development of methods to scale-up STRAP
to recover ~kg quantities of resins from multilayer films, enabling the production of cast
films using industrial equipment in partnership with Amcor. We tested three different
methods to increase the scale of STRAP, each of which led to slightly different recovered
resin properties. Our final approach, in which STRAP was performed using a heated
jacketed dissolution vessel to maintain a high temperature during dissolution and filtration,
led to high-quality films with mechanical properties similar to virgin resin (Sanchez-Rivera
et al, ACS Mat Lett, 2025). These efforts were used to guide the design of a pilot-scale
plant as an avenue for further analysis of STRAP scale-up, which is discussed in more
detail in Section 5.

An initially unanticipated challenge that was encountered and addressed during this
award was the apparent yellow color of films cast from resins recovered during the
application of STRAP to a reverse-printed multilayer film, even though the recovered
STRAP resins appear white (like virgin resins). Yellow coloration decreases the value of

recovered resins, motivating efforts to improve the optical properties of STRAP-based
films. These efforts yielded several significant accomplishments. One accomplishment
was the identification of the molecule responsible for the yellow color based on analyzing
a panel of pigments as well as products of thermal decomposition. Upon identification of
a decomposition product as the likely color source, we developed methods to combine
mechanical filtration (to minimize solvent retention in the precipitated polymer) with the
use of an adsorbent (to remove dissolved color bodies), leading to optically clear films
(Yan et al, Sci Adv, 2025). Another accomplishment was the development of
computational workflows to more generally determine solvents and operating conditions
suitable for extracting contaminants, like soluble pigments, brominated flame retardants,
and phthalates, during STRAP (Zhou et al, Green Chem, submitted). These new methods
– both experimental and computational – can ensure the purity and high quality of
STRAP-recovered resins and resulting films to facilitate commercial translation.

Topic Area 6 – Educational and Outreach Activities in Plastic Recycling
Public perception and knowledge of recycling technologies and plastics in general was a
significant area of focus for CUWP. All of the feedstocks described here are obtained from
consumers who chose to correctly recycle their plastic waste. This makes outreach pivotal
for any organization reliant on sustainable feedstocks. As such courses centered around
plastic recycling were developed and taught over the entire CUWP term at both the
University of Wisconsin – Madison and Iowa State University. Through these courses
students were able to visit local landfills, packaging companies, and film manufacturers.
More easily digestible information sheets were also distributed to all collaborators or
affiliates on topics such as the plastic supply chain and chemical recycling methods.
Workshop programs for K-12 teachers were hosted showing hands-on classroom lessons
based on STRAP for inclusion in teacher classrooms. Teachers from all grades were also
welcomed at our labs in UW-Madison throughout the duration of CUWP to work with
researchers on developing lesson plans based around recycling for their classrooms.
Additionally, research experiences for undergraduates (REUs) were hosted each summer
as a way to both reach and train more students outside of CUWP sponsored universities.

 5. Path Forward
Recommendations to the U.S. Department of Energy: Critical Next Steps for
Advancing a Circular Plastics Economy

1. Advance Plastic Sorting Technologies and Establish National Feedstock
Standards
The success of any recycling or upcycling technology is fundamentally dependent on the
quality and consistency of the input feedstock. Current material recovery facilities (MRFs)
struggle to deliver sorted plastic streams with the purity and predictability required for
chemical recycling. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) must prioritize the development
and deployment of advanced sorting technologies, including machine learning-enabled
optical sorters capable of identifying and separating complex, multilayer, and dark plastics.
Furthermore, DOE should lead the establishment of national feedstock quality standards to
guide MRF operations and align them with the needs of emerging recycling technologies.
Without this foundational infrastructure, the scalability and economic viability of chemical

recycling will remain severely constrained.

Future R&D needs include the development of machine-learning-enabled optical sorting
systems, real-time compositional sensing tools, and national feedstock specifications.
Commercialization opportunities include deployment of advanced sorting systems and
licensing of sensor algorithms. Remaining technical risks include high capital costs, lack of
standardized bale specifications, and variability in municipal collection practices.

2. Scale STRAP Technology and Support Sustained Pilot-Plant Operation
CUWP has designed and built a 25 kg/hr STRAP pilot system at Michigan Technological
University. We have secured DOE funding to complete the design and physically turn on the
system. However, we do not have funding to operate the pilot plant, conduct extended
campaigns, or perform the critical R&D needed to validate commercial readiness.

Future R&D needs include continuous pilot-plant operation, solvent reuse and impurity
management studies, and kilogram-scale resin production for industrial validation.
Commercialization opportunities include licensing STRAP process packages and co-location
at MRFs and brand-owner facilities. Remaining technical risks include lack of long-duration
pilot data, solvent degradation, and need for industrial validation.

3. Scale Surfactant Production from Pyrolysis Oils and Expand Application Testing
CUWP has demonstrated that surfactants derived from plastic pyrolysis oils can reduce
greenhouse gas emissions by approximately 60% compared to conventional petroleum-
based surfactants. This represents a transformative opportunity to create high-value products
from waste plastics.

Future R&D needs include scaling to 1–10 kg/day continuous operation, application testing
in detergents and industrial uses, and TEA/LCA benchmarking. Commercialization
opportunities include partnerships with consumer-product companies. Remaining technical
risks include variability in pyrolysis oil quality, catalyst stability, and limited performance data.

4. Deepen Fundamental Research in Plastic Pyrolysis Mechanisms and Predictive
Modeling

Despite progress, the fundamental chemistry of plastic pyrolysis remains poorly understood,
particularly for mixed and contaminated waste streams. Accurate prediction of product
distributions is essential for reactor design and process optimization.

Future R&D needs include expanding mechanistic reaction networks, integrating quantum-
chemical data, and validating models at pilot scale. Commercialization opportunities include
licensing kinetic models and simulation tools. Remaining technical risks include incomplete
mechanistic understanding and limited pilot-scale validation.

5. Launch a National Public Education and Technical Communication Initiative

Public misunderstanding of recycling technologies remains a major barrier to achieving high-

quality feedstocks and widespread adoption of chemical recycling. DOE should lead the
creation of a national, science-based public education initiative.

Future needs include development of a public information platform, K–12 curriculum, and
partnerships with municipalities and NGOs. Barriers include misinformation, inconsistent
messaging, and lack of public trust. Opportunities include adoption of educational materials
by schools and local governments.

6. Future Research and Development Plans, Technology Transfer, and
Commercialization Pathways

CUWP would like to continue work in all five areas described above. However, we currently
have no funding to pursue this work, except for limited DOE grants related to STRAP. Without
sustained DOE support, the scientific and engineering momentum built through CUWP will
be lost.

Appropriate next R&D steps include: (1) advancing sorting technologies and feedstock
standards; (2) operating and scaling the STRAP pilot plant; (3) scaling surfactant production
and expanding application testing; (4) deepening mechanistic understanding of pyrolysis;
and (5) launching a national public education initiative.

Technology transfer opportunities include licensing of STRAP and hydroformylation to
surfactant patents, deployment of advanced sorting systems, and commercialization of
plastic-derived surfactants. Remaining technical risks include feedstock variability, solvent
degradation, catalyst stability, and lack of pilot-scale validation. Barriers to commercialization
include capital costs, regulatory uncertainty, and limited public awareness.
