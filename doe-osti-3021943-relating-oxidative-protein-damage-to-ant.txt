 FULL TECHNICAL REPORT

 Relating Oxidative Protein Damage to Antioxidant Status in Health and Disease
 David Baliu-Rodriguez
 24-LW-026

Abstract
This two-year project evaluated how dietary antioxidants influence oxidative damage in cancer
using complementary analytical and in-vivo approaches. We initially developed a protein-
oxidation labeling workflow and a parallel accelerator and molecular mass spectrometry
(PAMMS) quantification method, but ultimately discontinued the labeling strategy due to
unresolved separation challenges; PAMMS was instead leveraged to quantify radiolabeled
catechol in rat plasma as a methodological benchmark. The biological study used a genetically
engineered murine model (GEMM) for breast cancer (n = 40; four groups of 10: cancer/high-
antioxidant diet, cancer/normal diet, healthy/high-antioxidant diet, healthy/normal diet). In lieu
of the abandoned labeling assay, untargeted metabolomics profiled plasma across groups,
revealing widespread treatment-dependent changes in metabolites. Notably, the lipid mediator
16(R)-HETE was increased in cancer mice fed a high-antioxidant diet versus cancer mice on a
normal diet, consistent with antioxidant-modulated inflammatory/oxidative pathways. Together,
the work delivers a validated analytical foundation, a well-controlled in-vivo dataset, and initial
evidence that antioxidant supplementation can reprogram systemic lipid and redox metabolites
in cancer-relevant contexts.
Background and Research Objectives
A multi-million-dollar industry peddling antioxidant dietary supplements (DAS) has arisen based
on the belief that consumption of these compounds is beneficial to health. Antioxidant
supplements are widely consumed by healthy individuals as well as those suffering from
infectious and chronic diseases. Although the human body naturally produces and maintains its
own antioxidants to protect against oxidative damage, evidence increasingly suggests that taking
dietary antioxidant supplements may be harmful, especially to individuals with chronic diseases
such as cancer (Heaney et al. 2008; Le Gal et al. 2015; Omenn et al. 1996; Sayin et al. 2014). The
question of the presumed benefits of antioxidant supplements is so important that Dr. James
Watson recently stated, “the time has come to seriously ask whether antioxidant use much more
likely causes than prevents cancer (Watson 2013).” While numerous small studies in animals and
humans have claimed benefits from regular consumption of antioxidant supplements, many
others, including large human studies in cancer patients, have in fact shown shocking increases
in mortality rates for individuals taking antioxidant supplements compared to individuals taking
placebos (Ghezzi et al. 2017; Herraiz, Crosas-Molist, and Sanz-Moreno 2016; Omenn et al. 1996;
Watson 2013). Emerging evidence continues to suggest that antioxidant supplements may
exacerbate symptoms and accelerate disease progression in cancer patients by enhancing tumor
survival mechanisms, leading to increased mortality (Herraiz, Crosas-Molist, and Sanz-Moreno
2016; Sayin et al. 2014; Watson 2013). The need to determine the true benefits and risks of
dietary antioxidant supplements is urgent.

Wide acceptance of the oxidative stress hypothesis of disease has lent credibility to the notion
that consumption of DAS is beneficial to health. Oxidative stress, a biological imbalance between
reactive oxygen gen species (ROS) and antioxidants (Curtis et al. 2012; Narasimhan et al. 2010;
Polimeni et al. 2015), has been implicated as an important mechanism of numerous maladies,
including cancer (Bahar et al. 2007; Chang et al. 2008; Crespo-Sanjuán et al. 2015; Jin et al. 2013;
Ma et al. 2013; Santos Bernardes et al. 2016; Zhong and Yin 2015) and metabolic diseases (Curtis
et al. 2012; Narasimhan et al. 2010; Polimeni et al. 2015). In essence, the oxidative stress
hypothesis states that a chronic imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants in the body is a
general mechanism of disease (Ghezzi et al. 2017). ROS can damage biological macromolecules
directly, and can also react with biomolecules such as lipids in chain reactions that generate
additional secondary oxidative products that are themselves reactive, substantially amplifying
the oxidative results. All aerobic organisms are naturally exposed to ROS and possess defense
mechanisms in DNA repair systems and antioxidants. Excessive generation of ROS can overwhelm
the detoxification abilities of natural defense mechanisms, enabling ROS to damage cellular
macromolecules such as protein and DNA. This damage in turn reduces the ability of the tissue
to regenerate and can cause mutagenic lesions on the genetic material, creating a vicious cycle
of tissue damage and leading to disease progression. While it is clear that oxidative stress can be
deleterious, evidence of tissue damage by ROS in different diseases ranges from definitive, as in
heart disease (oxidized cholesterol), to speculative. Measurement of antioxidant levels alone is
insufficient to allow assessment of the biological effects of DAS use.
Building on a field grappling with paradoxical reports that antioxidant supplementation may
exacerbate, not alleviate, oncologic risk, our project set out to quantify oxidative damage
alongside antioxidant status and to test whether DAS measurably shift these endpoints. We
defined two objectives: (Aim 1) develop a 14C adduct post-labeling assay with Parallel
Accelerator and Molecular Mass Spectrometry (PAMMS) (Baliu-Rodriguez, Stewart, and
Ognibene 2023; Ognibene et al. 2015) readout to profile plasma protein oxidation and selected
antioxidants in cancer patients versus healthy individuals; and (Aim 2) run a controlled pilot with
a genetically engineered murine model (GEMM) for breast cancer (Figure 1) to assess how DAS
intake alters oxidative protein damage and antioxidant levels in blood. We achieved robust
radiolabel detection with PAMMS but encountered intractable chromatographic separation of
the labeled protein adducts, prompting a strategic pivot. PAMMS was repurposed to quantify
radiolabeled catechol in rat plasma (methodological validation), and we adopted untargeted
metabolomics in the mouse study to capture redox-sensitive biochemical changes with high
coverage.

Figure 1: Genetically-engineered murine model (GEMM) for studying human breast cancer in mice.
4T1 cells, a highly aggressive, metastasizing mouse mammary cancer cell line used as a model for
stage IV human breast cancer, were grown and injected into C57BL/6 (black 6) mice. Plasma was
collected after 4 weeks.

Because the labeling workflow was not deployable to human specimens, differences in plasma
protein oxidation and antioxidant panels between cancer patients and healthy controls remain
untested in this dataset. In the mouse study (n=40; four groups), metabolomics revealed broad,
diet- and disease-dependent shifts; notably, 16(R)-HETE was elevated in cancer mice on high-
antioxidant diets versus cancer mice on normal diets, consistent with antioxidant-modulated
lipid/redox signaling. These findings indicate that DAS can reprogram systemic metabolism in
cancer contexts, although we did not directly quantify protein adduct burden after
supplementation. We did not directly assay endogenous antioxidant production (e.g., GSH/GSSG,
urate, enzyme activities), so effects of DAS on endogenous antioxidant pools remain an open
question. In sum, Aim 2 was met with a rigorous in vivo dataset and mechanistic leads from
metabolomics; Aim 1 produced a partially validated analytical foundation but did not reach
cohort-level comparisons. The pivot yielded an additional, unplanned objective — system-level
redox metabolomics — which generated actionable hypotheses and a clear path for targeted
oxylipin/protein-oxidation assays and antioxidant panel measurements in follow-on work.
Scientific Approach and Accomplishments
Our initial strategy was a 14C post-labeling assay in which oxidative modifications on plasma
protein side chains were derivatized by covalent attachment of radiolabeled 2-aminopyridine via
a Michael-type addition. Although derivatization was successful, we were unable to achieve
chromatographic removal of unreacted 2-aminopyridine from plasma matrices. Given the
extreme sensitivity of accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS), residual free label would have
dominated the AMS signal and obscured the far lower abundance of radiolabeled amino-acid
adducts. After multiple separation attempts failed to suppress this background to acceptable
levels, we discontinued the post-labeling workflow to avoid false-positive/insensitive readouts.

Figure 2. A representative 14C chromatogram obtained by PAMMS analysis of rat plasma after
14C-labeled catechol administration. All ten analyzed samples had a consistent chromato-graphic
profile characterized by four distinct radiocarbon peaks. The peaks were numbered 1 through 4
by order of elution.
To leverage the strengths of PAMMS while avoiding the separation bottleneck, we implemented
a complementary exposure-tracking assay. 14C-catechol was spiked into smoldering eucalyptus
wood-smoke extract and administered to rats by intranasal instillation. Plasma was collected at
5 min and 2 h post-exposure, purified by solid-phase extraction, and analyzed by PAMMS coupled
to Orbitrap LC-MS. Four distinct elution peaks were detected in the 14C chromatograms of all

samples (Figure 2), indicating that the 14C-labeled catechol had been metabolized into at least
four distinct products.
To identify the radiolabeled ions, extracted ion chromatograms (EICs) from the orbitrap mass
analyzer were overlaid on the 14C chromatogram. The first peak (retention time (RT) = 2.35 min)
corresponded to the [M-H]⁻ ion of C9H17O5N (m/z 218.1034, mass error = 0.0 ppm), consistent
with a catechol-glutamine conjugate. This assignment is supported by the accurate mass,
elemental composition, and the biological context, in which catechol-derived o-quinones are
known to undergo nucleophilic attack by amino acids such as glutamine, forming stable
conjugates via Michael addition. The second peak (RT = 3.56 min) matched the [M-H]⁻ ion of
benzene oxide (C6H6O, m/z 93.0346, mass error = 0.0 ppm), a well-known reactive intermediate
formed during the oxidative metabolism of aromatic compounds. The third peak (RT = 3.90 min)
was identified as the [M-H]⁻ ion of C₈H₇7O₄NS (m/z 212.0023, mass error = 0.94 ppm), consistent
with a cysteine conjugate of catechol. This structure is supported by the presence of sulfur and
nitrogen in the formula, characteristic of conjugates formed via the mercapturic acid pathway in
response to catechol-derived electrophilic species. EIC overlays of the first three peaks are shown
in Figure 3. The fourth peak did not correspond to any detectable ion in the orbitrap data,
suggesting it may represent a radiolabeled compound that is either undetectable by mass
spectrometry under the conditions used or present at levels below the detection limit. The parent
compound was not detected.

Figure 3. 14C chromatogram overlaid with EICs obtained by the orbitrap mass analyzer. [A] Peak
1 aligned with the EIC of 218.1034 m/z; [B] Peak 2 aligned with the EIC of 93.0346 m/z; and [C]
Peak 3 aligned with the EIC of 212.0021 m/z. Band broadening of the 14C peaks are a result of
the combustion process of the moving wire interface and longitudinal diffusion in the capillaries
that transfer the gaseous sample from the interface to the AMS source.
To assess temporal changes in metabolite levels, relative peak intensities were compared
between the 5-minute and 2-hour timepoints using data from five biological replicates per group.
The third peak (C₈H₇O₄NS) exhibited a statistically significant change in abundance over time, as
determined by a paired t-test. The observed decrease in the third peak over time suggests that
the cysteine conjugate is transient, potentially reflecting rapid clearance or further metabolism
following its initial formation. This pattern is consistent with a short-lived intermediate in the
mercapturic acid pathway, formed early after catechol exposure and subsequently processed or
excreted.

This workflow provided both molecular identification and relative quantification of catechol-
derived metabolites at high sensitivity, demonstrating PAMMS’s utility for exposure assessment
and biomarker discovery. Insights from this validated application, together with the limitations
encountered in the post-labeling assay, motivated our subsequent pivot to an untargeted
metabolomics readout for oxidative-stress phenotyping in the mouse diet/tumor model.
We profiled circulating metabolites across four groups (cancer/high-antioxidant diet,
cancer/normal diet, healthy/high-antioxidant diet, and healthy/normal diet) using an untargeted
metabolomics strategy. Untargeted analysis is powerful because it surveys thousands of features
without prespecifying targets, enabling simultaneous detection of central-carbon intermediates,
amino acids, nucleotides, lipids/oxylipins, bile acids, and xenobiotic/adduct species. This breadth
supports discovery of pathway-level shifts, such as redox, lipid signaling, or inflammation, and
yields quantitative signals (peak areas) for statistical testing, differential abundance, and pathway
enrichment. Plasma was extracted using a standard biphasic methyl tertbutyl ether (MTBE)
protocol to partition non-polar (MTBE) and polar fractions (water/methanol), maximizing
coverage and minimizing matrix effects. Data were acquired on the Orbitrap Eclipse (recent
MPIC-supported $1.2M purchase), leveraging high mass accuracy and resolving power for
confident feature detection and tentative annotation, and demonstrating a capability that also
builds toward a lab metabolomics core.

Figure 4. Volcano plot of differential plasma metabolites between cancer/normal-diet and
cancer/high-antioxidant-diet mice. Each point represents a metabolite detected by untargeted
LC-MS on the Orbitrap Eclipse. The x-axis shows log2 fold change and the y-axis shows −log10p
value. Using these cutoffs, 44 metabolites were upregulated in cancer/normal diet (red) and 76
were downregulated (green). n = 10 per group.
Comparing cancer/normal-diet vs. cancer/high-antioxidant-diet mice, we observed a significant
shift in the plasma metabolome (p < 0.05; log2 fold-change > 2): 44 metabolites were higher in
the cancer/normal-diet group (Figure 4), including ophthalmic acid (a recognized oxidative-stress
biomarker), while 76 metabolites were lower, including the oxylipin 16(R)-HETE. These patterns
indicate that dietary antioxidants in the cancer context reprogram systemic metabolism,
dampening specific lipid mediators and altering redox-responsive pathways detectable in blood.

Together, the data validate the Eclipse platform for discovery-scale phenotyping and provide
tractable candidates for targeted follow-up (e.g., oxylipins, γ-glutamyl peptides, and other redox-
linked species).
Mission Impact
This project advances LLNL’s core national security mission, with direct impact on Health Security
and chemical and radiological consequence management, by delivering a dual capability for
chemical-exposure forensics and health-relevant phenotyping: (1) PAMMS-enabled radiocarbon
tracing coupled to high-resolution Orbitrap LC-MS for ultra-sensitive identification and
quantification of xenobiotic metabolites, demonstrated with wood-smoke catechol, leveraging
LLNL’s Biological AMS (BioAMS); and (2) a discovery-scale metabolomics workflow on the new
$1.2M Orbitrap Eclipse that reveals redox and lipid-signaling rewiring in tumor-bearing hosts
under antioxidant diets. Together these establish cornerstone methods for an LLNL
metabolomics core, expand BioAMS integration with molecular MS, and seed cross-directorate
programs spanning Physical and Life Sciences and Global Security in exposure assessment,
biomarker discovery, and precision toxicology. This work directly advances the LLNL Biological
Mission Focus Area, providing mission-relevant analytical platforms and datasets for exposure
attribution, mechanistic toxicology, and precision health assessments, and enabling execution of
bioMFA use cases in wildfire smoke and industrial-release exposure assessment and
consequence management in partnership with BioAMS and the Forensic Science Center. The
results directly support DOE/NNSA priorities in health security, readiness, and consequence
management, enabling rapid attribution of exposures, mechanistic risk assessment, and decision
support during environmental or radiological incidents, and align with DOE Office of Science’s
Biological and Environmental Research on systems-level responses to stressors. They also create
clear on-ramps for external programs at NIH, NCI, NIEHS, EPA, CDC, DOD, DTRA, and DHS, while
attracting collaborations and staff to grow LLNL’s analytical and translational biosciences
portfolio.
Conclusion
We will continue to develop two complementary LLNL capabilities: an untargeted metabolomics
platform on the Orbitrap Eclipse, and PAMMS radiocarbon tracing integrated with high-
resolution LC-MS. For metabolomics, the next steps are to standardize end-to-end SOPs
(extraction, acquisition, QAQC, data processing), stand up curated spectral libraries and
reference materials, and deploy automated pipelines for differential analysis and pathway
reporting, forming the backbone of a Laboratory metabolomics core with decision-quality
outputs. For PAMMS, we will generalize the workflow to diverse radiolabeled stressors, expand
sample-cleanup strategies to ensure ultra-low background, and formalize quantitative
calibration/traceability, enabling sensitive attribution of exposure and metabolism. These
capabilities are immediately transferrable to industry and government via partnerships or a fee-
for-service analytics platform. We envisage multi-agency collaborations (DOE/BER and NNSA for
health security and consequence management; NIH/NCI/NIEHS for translational redox biology;
EPA/CDC for exposure assessment) alongside private-sector support from biotech, pharma, and
instrument vendors. The outcome, metabolomics for system-level phenotyping and PAMMS for

source-aware tracing, strengthens LLNL’s biosurveillance and public-health mission space while
attracting new programs, users, and technical staff.
