 Abstract
 This report summarizes the refractory corrosion data for a matrix of direct-feed high-level waste
 (DFHLW) glasses generated to expand glass property data into the high sulfur composition
 region. The design of the high sulfur glass matrix, HS24, was reported in Reiser et al. (2026).
 Fifty HS24 glasses were tested for the corrosion of Monofrax® K-3 refractory materials.

 The corrosion of K-3 refractory materials in the HS24 glass melts was measured using a static
 crucible method. Two test conditions, 1150 °C for 7 days and 1200 °C for 7 days, were applied.
 The melt-line neck corrosion depth of the test coupons was measured by micro-computed
 tomography (micro-CT). The measured K-3 neck corrosion values were compared to predicted
 values using the EWG3.0 K-3 corrosion model (Lu et al. 2026). Proposed upper limits for K-3
 neck corrosion, 0.04 in. (EWG2.5, Vienna et al. 2024) and 0.025 in. (EWG2.6, Vienna et al.
 2025 and EWG3.0, Lu et al. 2026) were applied to evaluate the neck corrosion data of the HS24
 matrix glasses. Two glasses of this HS24 matrix were designed as replicates of glasses
 previously tested by different laboratories. The inter-laboratory discrepancies of the test results
 were analyzed and are discussed. Future work will continue to investigate the consistency of K-
 3 corrosion data collected by different institutions, and incorporate these factors into future
 model development to better account for experimental variability and support more accurate and
 robust models.

 1.0 Introduction
 Hanford glass melts are contained within their melters by Monofrax® K-3 refractories. K-3
 corrosion – melt composition models are used in glass design to avoid excessive corrosion of
 these refractories (Jin et al. 2023). Initial models were found to be subject to higher than desired
 uncertainties and were valid over limited composition space compared to other models used in
 glass design (Vienna et al. 2025). To improve glass property models, a matrix of glasses was
 designed, fabricated, and tested for various properties (Reiser et al. 2026). Models will be
 developed in the future with these additional K-3 corrosion-glass composition data collected in
 this report to improve prediction performance and expand applicable composition region. This
 will help to improve loading of waste in glass, while reducing risk of unacceptable K-3 corrosion.

 Glass matrix design was described in detail by Reiser et al. (2026) along with results from
 testing other properties of these glasses. The K-3 crucible scale test and characterization
 methodologies were developed and described by Jin et al. (2025). This report summarizes
 these methods in Section 2.0 and documents the testing and characterization results in Section
 3.0. Section 4.0 offers conclusions drawn from the testing.

 2.0 Test Methods
 This section describes how the K-3 corrosion was measured.

 2.1 Glass Fabrication
 The glass fabrication procedure and compositional analysis were reported in Reiser et al.
 (2026); the chemical analysis confirmed that the target glass compositions were met.

 2.2 Refractory Corrosion Test
 The Monofrax® K-3 refractory (Monofrax LLC, Falconer, NY) corrosion test was performed using
 a crucible-scale test method based on ASTM C621 (2022) with minor modifications to the
 crucible size and coupon size. Such modification is common in refractory corrosion research as
 the original ASTM standard is very specific; the data collected by the same modified setup are
 comparable and suitable for modeling. The setup, which has been used to collect K-3 refractory
 corrosion data to support research of Hanford glassed formulation (Russell et al. 2025 and Jin
 et al. 2025), is shown in Figure 2.1. K-3 test coupons were cut from K-3 refractory slabs into
 1 cm × 1 cm × 11 cm coupons. A notch was cut on the top end of the coupons to denote face
 “A–A.” Each coupon was mounted with the crucible lid made by castable alumina (RESCOR™
 CER-CAST CERAMIC 780, Cotronics Corp., Brooklyn, NY) with a 1 cm × 1 cm slot in the
 center. The K-3 test coupon was inserted into a Pt crucible with crushed glass. The loaded
 crucible was heated in a furnace, and the K-3 test coupon was submerged into a static glass
 melt at 1150 °C or 1200 °C for 7 days. Following the 7 days test, the K-3 coupons were
 removed from the glass melt for characterization.

 Figure 2.1. K-3 corrosion test setup in a static glass melt showing general measurements for
 coupon size and positioning, glass depth, and coupon immersion depth. For
 convenience in the following discussion, the test coupon’s dimensions were
 defined by the x-, y-, and z-axes. The x–y plane is the horizontal plane, and the
 z-axis is the vertical axis.

Test Methods 2

 Both pre-test and post-test coupons were scanned using Zeiss Xradia Versa 610 micro-
 computed tomography (micro-CT) (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany). As shown in
 Figure 2.2, the coupons were mounted in 3D-printed plastic sample holders. Up to four coupons
 were bundled to fit in the scanning volume. Each single scan covered a cylindrical volume
 ~4 cm tall and 4 cm in diameter; three to five scans were conducted vertically to cover a 6–
 10 cm length. Single scans must be partially overlapped for image stitching. The voxel size was
 set to 0.04 mm. A certified volume standard was used to verify the voxel size of the micro-CT
 measurement.

 Figure 2.2. Samples for micro-CT scanning.

 The K-3 corrosion was calculated by dimensional changes in the coupons after crucible tests in
 glass melts. As shown in Figure 2.3, the micro-CT scan produces a “stack” of X-ray images,
 which can be reconstructed to a 3D object for each sample. The 3D object can be sliced from
 different angles for analysis. The data from the pre-test and post-test K-3 coupons resulted in
 two stacks of images and were adjusted and aligned for analysis. Each image stack contains a
 50-mm-long section of coupon (1250 slices × 0.04 mm = 50 mm; each slice is one voxel thick,
 0.04 mm), with each slice set to 12 mm × 12 mm (300 × 300 pixels, where the pixel size is the
 same as the voxel size for the 2D images). The pre-test and post-test image stacks of the same
 sample were aligned by matching the slices with visible features (grain sizes, pores, etc.), i.e.,
 the same slice number in the pre- and post-test image stacks should have corresponded to the
 same position on the sample.

 After adjusting and aligning, each slice was converted to a binary outline image and then a
 bounding rectangle of the outline. Adjustment of the contrast of the X-ray images was necessary
 to draw the outline of the post-test coupons to separate the glass and the K-3, which are of
 different brightness in the X-ray images. After generating the stack of outlines from the X-ray
 image stack, a stack of bounding rectangles was generated from the stack of outlines

 3.0 Results and Discussion
 Table 3.1 and Figure 3.1 summarize the measured neck depths of the HS24 glass corroded K-3
 coupons. Each glass was tested under two different conditions: 1150 °C for 7 days and 1200 °C
 for 7 days. For the combined modeling dataset, the corrosion depth values are converted to
 non-SI units of inches in the tables and plots in this section. The original measured values in SI
 units of millimeters can be found in the appendices. Test coupon photos are shown in
 Appendix A, micro-CT scan images are shown in Appendix B, and the original measured
 corrosion data are shown in Appendix C. For each coupon, the neck depth was measured in
 two perpendicular directions, as discussed in Section 2.2. The average values of the two
 measurements are reported with the standard deviation (SD). During the micro-CT
 measurement, setting the threshold to outline the scanned materials can lead to a measurement
      uncertainty of ~1 to 3 voxels (0.0016 to 0.0047 in.).

 In this HS24 matrix, 15 glasses showed low corrosivity on the K-3 coupons without forming a
 distinguishable neck at either 1150 °C or 1200 °C after 7 days. The coupons without a
 distinguishable neck for the corrosion depth measurement are marked in Table 3.1. For another
      nine glasses, HS24-03, HS24-10, HS24-11, HS24-12, HS24-14, HS24-33, HS24-34, HS24-40,
 and HS24-48, the corrosion neck was only observed on the coupons after the 1200 °C for
 7 days test but not after the 1150 °C for 7 days. The other glasses had distinguishable neck
 corrosion on the K-3 test coupons with the necks generally deeper after the 1200 °C for 7 days
 test. Four glasses, HS24-13, HS24-31, HS24-44, and HS24-50, showed equivalent corrosion at
 1150 °C for 7 days and 1200 °C for 7 days test conditions (differences were less than estimated
 uncertainty of ~3 voxels or ~0.0047 in.). There is only one glass, HS24-09, that clearly showed
 a deeper neck on the 1150 °C for 7 days K-3 test coupon compared to the 1200 °C for 7 days
 K-3 test coupon, the cause of which is currently unknown, but could be attributed to factors such
 as reaction layer formation, the presence of pores or pre-existing cracks, or glass viscosity;
 further investigation is warranted.

 Based on data from a previous 1208 °C for 6 days bubbling crucible test (Muller et al. 2018),
 two values were proposed as the upper limits for K-3 neck corrosion, 0.04 in. and 0.025 in. A
 limit of 0.04 in. was used in EWG2.5 (Vienna et al. 2024), based on the EWG-LAW constraint
 (Vienna et al. 2022). A more conservative limit of 0.025 in. was subsequently adopted, which
 has been used since EWG2.6 (Vienna et al. 2025) and retained in EWG3.0 (Lu et al. 2026). An
 offset of 0.3654 ln[in.] was estimated based on previous data from the 1208 °C for 6 days
 bubbling test and 1200 °C for 7 days static test (Lu et al. 2026). By applying the offset to the
 1200 °C for 7 days measured data [Equation (3.1)], the transferred results are comparable with
 the limits of the 1208 °C for 6 days bubbling data. As shown in Figure 3.1(b), only one of the 50
 glasses, HS24-49, had a neck corrosion depth on the K-3 test coupons exceeding the 0.04 in.
 limit, and four, HS24-04, HS24-05, HS24-25, and HS24-49, had neck corrosion deeper than the
 more restrictive limit of 0.025 in.

Results and Discussion 11

 4.0 Conclusions
 This report documents the results of the refractory corrosion tests of a high S matrix (HS24) of
 simulated DFHLW glasses. Each glass was tested using a static crucible setup at two test
 conditions: 1150 °C and 1200 °C for 7 days. The corrosion depth at the melt line, i.e., the neck
 corrosion depth data, was measured by scanning the K-3 coupons before and after testing with
 micro-CT. From the 50 HS-24 glasses, 15 did not show distinguishable neck corrosion after
 testing at either temperature condition. For another nine glasses, the corrosion neck was only
 observed on the coupons after the 1200 °C for 7 days test condition but not after the 1150 °C
 for 7 days. There are six glasses that had deeper neck corrosion on the test coupons after being
 tested at 1150 °C for 7 days than after being tested at 1200 °C for 7 days. The reason for this
 behavior may be attributed to the experimental error related to the defects of the K-3 coupons
 and the limit of the measurement but should be subject to further investigation to rule out other
 causes.

 The measured neck corrosion depth data were compared with model predicted values from the
 latest EWG3.0 K-3 refractory corrosion neck loss model (Lu et al. 2026). The measured and
 predicted values agreed well; however, the prediction is better when the neck corrosion is
 deeper. After applying a 0.3654 ln[in.] offset to the 1200 °C for 7 days data (the offset in the
 model equation was to make the static crucible test data comparable with the limits based on
 bubbling crucible test data), one glass of the 50 glasses, HS24-49, failed the nominal 0.04 in.
 neck corrosion limit, and four glasses, HS24-04, HS24-05, HS24-25, and HS24-49, failed the
 more restrictive 0.025 in. limit.

 Two glasses, HS24-49 and HS24-50, were designed as replicates of previously tested glasses,
 ORPLG27 and APPS1-5. The 1200 °C for 7 days results for HS24-49 (ORPLG27) showed
 consistency between two replicates (static test, measured neck depths of 0.0646 in. and
 0.0614 in. respectively) performed by PNNL; on the other hand, VSL’s results (bubbling crucible
 test, Muller et al. 2018) were lower than PNNL’s (after conversion with an offset for comparison,
      0.029 in. vs. 0.0426-0.0448 in.). For HS24-50 (APPS-05), both SRNL and PNNL tested K-3
 coupons using a static crucible setup, at the same temperatures (1150 °C and 1200 °C), and for
 a 7-day test duration. For the 1150 °C for 7 days test, PNNL’s results were lower than SRNL’s;
 however, for the 1200 °C for 7 days test, the results were consistent between laboratories. The
 inter-laboratory differences of K-3 corrosion data are attributed to variations in test conditions
 and characterization methods. Future work will investigate these factors to better understand
 the observed discrepancies and experimental uncertainty. Quantifying and reducing
 experimental uncertainty can improve data quality, help identify potential model overfitting, and
 ultimately support more accurate and robust model development.
