Safety and efficacy of a feed additive consisting of locust bean gum for cats, dogs, pigs and poultry (FEDIAF)
Authors: EFSA Panel on Additives and Products or Substances used in Animal Feed (FEEDAP), Roberto Edoardo Villa, Giovanna Azimonti, Eleftherios Bonos, Henrik Christensen, Mojca Durjava, Birgit Dusemund, Ronette Gehring, Boet Glandorf, Maryline Kouba, Marta López‐Alonso, Francesca Marcon, Giovanna Martelli, Carlo Nebbia, Alena Pechová, Miguel Prieto‐Maradona, Katerina Theodoridou, Jaume Galobart, Matteo Lorenzo Innocenti, Alberto Navarro‐Villa, Jordi Ortuño, Fabiola Pizzo, Maria Vittoria Vettori, Eleni Gkimprixi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollowing a request from the European Commission, EFSA was asked to deliver a scientific opinion on the assessment of the follow‐up application for re‐evaluation of locust bean gum as a technological additive for cats, dogs, pigs and poultry. In the previous opinion, the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the safety for the target species, for the user and on its efficacy as an emulsifier. In the current assessment, the FEEDAP Panel concludes that locust bean gum is considered safe for cats at 20,000 mg/kg complete feed, dogs at 1320 mg/kg complete feed, pigs at 500–729 mg/kg complete feed and poultry at 278–415 mg/kg complete feed. Regarding user safety, the additive is considered irritant to skin and eyes and is considered a potential skin and respiratory sensitiser. The Panel concludes that the additive alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids may have the potential to act as a gelling agent, thickener and stabiliser, in matrices with high moisture content. However, it cannot conclude on its efficacy, alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids, in complex feed matrices typical of the pet food industry or in feeds with low moisture content.Keywords: gelling agents, locust bean gum, safety, stabilisers, technological additives, thickeners

Data

Methodologies

The approach followed by the FEEDAP Panel to assess the safety and the efficacy of locust bean gum is in line with the principles laid down in Regulation (EC) No 429/2008 6 and the relevant guidance documents: Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the target species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017), Guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the users (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2023) and Guidance on the assessment of the efficacy of feed additives (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2024).

ASSESSMENT

The additive locust bean gum is a natural polysaccharide extracted from the endosperm of the seeds of the carob tree, Ceratonia siliqua. The additive is intended to be used as a technological additive (functional group: gelling agents, stabilisers, thickeners) for use in feedingstuffs for cats, dogs, pigs and poultry with a maximum use level of 25,000 mg/kg complete feed. The additive was fully characterised in a previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022) in which the FEEDAP Panel concluded that the additive is safe for the consumer and the environment under the proposed conditions of use. The Panel also concluded that the additive is efficacious as a gelling agent, thickener and contributes to stabilise canned pet feed when simultaneously used with another hydrocolloid (carrageenan). No conclusions could be reached on the efficacy of the additive as an emulsifier and on the safety for target species and user due to insufficient data.

In the context of the current assessment, the applicant restricted the scope of the application to cats, dogs, pigs and poultry, withdrew the request as an emulsifier and provided additional data to cover the data gaps identified in the previous opinion, which are assessed below.

Safety

Safety for the target species

In 2017, the EFSA ANS Panel delivered an opinion on the ‘Re‐evaluation of locust bean gum (E 410) as a food additive’ (EFSA ANS Panel, 2017) in which it was concluded that locust bean gum is not genotoxic and a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 2500 mg locust bean gum/kg bw in rats, the highest dose tested, was identified in a carcinogenicity study.

Following the methodology described in the guidance on the assessment of the safety of feed additives for the target species (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2017), the maximum safe concentration of the additive in complete feed was calculated, using the NOAEL of 2500 mg/kg bw per day and an uncertainty factor of 100. To support the safety of locust bean gum for cats and dogs at levels higher than the ones calculated above (25,000 mg/kg complete feed), the applicant provided tolerance studies in the respective species.

Tolerance trial in dogs 7

A total of 60 adult (1–11 years old) intact and neutered/spayed male and female (60:40) mixed‐breed 8 dogs ■■■■■ into three experimental groups. A basal wet feed based on ■■■■■ was either not supplemented (control) or supplemented with locust bean gum at 5000 or 10,000 mg/kg feed (as is), which would correspond to 25,000 (1× maximum use level) and 50,000 (2×) mg/kg complete feed, respectively. The content of locust bean gum in feed was indirectly analysed based on the content of total fibre. Based on the results of the analysis, the calculated concentrations of locust bean gum in the experimental diets were well below the intended concentrations (Table 2). ■■■■■. The animals were housed collectively during the experiment and kept individually twice a day for feeding and sampling/monitoring purposes. The experimental feeds were offered for 28 days to maintain body weight and body condition score, 9 according to baseline measurements recorded before the experiment.

Health status was daily monitored, including a more thorough physical examination conducted at the start and the end of the experimental period. Feed consumption was recorded and the faeces 10 scored daily. The body weight and body condition score were measured weekly, and the overall body weight change was calculated for the overall period. Blood was sampled from all animals before the start of the experiment, to establish a baseline, and at the end (day 28), and blood haematology 11 and serum biochemistry 12 were analysed.

The experimental data were analysed using a generalised linear model, with the dog as experimental unit, and the diet and sex as fixed effects. For blood parameters analysis and body weight evaluation, the baseline values were included as covariates. When differences were detected, group means were compared using Tukey test. The significance level was set at 0.10. The results of the health and performance parameters and the significant differences observed in the blood parameters evaluated are shown in Table 3.

The health status of the animals was good throughout the study, and no animal needed to be withdrawn. However, the animals receiving the additive showed a dose‐dependent reduction in the body weight change, statistically different from the control in the overdose group. The animals that received the additive, at either dose, showed a lower body weight at the end of the study. The differences in body weight could be due to lower feed consumption. No differences between groups were observed in the final body condition and faecal scores.

Regarding the blood haematology, a consistent pattern of dose‐dependent decreases was observed in the haemoglobin concentration, mean corpuscular haemoglobin content (MCH) and mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC) with increasing levels of the additive; compared to the control, the effects became statistically different at the use level in the case of haemoglobin, and at the overdose for MCH and MCHC. A similar pattern was observed in other serum biochemistry markers, such as the concentration of proteins, including both albumin and globulin, and some electrolytes (magnesium and calcium). An opposite trend was observed in the serum content of C‐reactive protein. Compared to the control group, the effects on protein concentration were statistically evident at the use level, while for the serum electrolytes and C‐reactive protein at 2×. Although all values remained within physiological ranges, these changes may indicate a general trend towards reduced nutritional status, likely related to the lower intake observed in treated animals. The lower serum cholesterol concentration in supplemented animals was not considered an adverse effect.

Based on the observed differences between the control and the proposed use level in final body weight, haemoglobin concentration and serum proteins, as well as the dose‐dependent effects in other serum biochemistry parameters and the short duration of the tolerance study which will not allow the assessment of extended use of the additive, the Panel cannot conclude on the safety of the additive at the maximum proposed use level of 25,000 mg/kg complete feed for dogs.

The health status of the animals was optimal throughout the study; only one cat belonging to the group receiving the use level was removed from the study due to low feed intake, weight loss and poor stool quality.

The results showed a dose‐dependent reduction in the final body weight and overall body weight change with increasing inclusion of the additive in feed, which became statistically significant in 2× compared to the control. Regarding the blood data, a similar trend was observed in the concentration of basophils, serum potassium, Na:K ratio, cholesterol concentration, alkaline phosphatase activity and prothrombin time. Compared to the control, the values of potassium, alkaline phosphatase and cholesterol were significantly different at 1× and at 2× for the others. The animals receiving the additive at any level showed a significantly higher final faecal score compared to the control.

Overall, the changes observed in the blood parameters were small and all parameters remained within physiological ranges during the experimental period. However, the differences observed in the final body weight and the trends identified in different blood‐related parameters would prevent to conclude at the overdose level. Therefore, based on the data provided, the Panel concludes that locust bean gum is safe when used in the feed for cats at 20,000 mg/kg complete feed. Considering the observed dose‐related trends, monitoring overtime may be considered for locust bean gum.

Safety for the user

In the previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022), the Panel could not conclude on the safety for the user due to the lack of data.

No additional studies were submitted for the current assessment; however, the applicant carried out an extensive literature search. The search covered two databases (FSTA®: the food science resource and CABI: CAB Abstracts®).

Out of 607, nine papers were identified by the applicant as relevant for the assessment of the safety of the additive for the users. The FEEDAP Panel noted that four papers reported effects on the respiratory tract and on the eyes after exposure to locust bean gum alone or in combination with other substances (Hawley et al., 2017; Scoditti et al., 1996; Van der Brempt et al., 1992; Vázquez de la Torre et al., 2020).

Based on the information provided in the SDS, 18 the Panel concludes that the additive should be considered as irritant to skin and eyes, and as a skin and respiratory sensitiser. Exposure of users by any route is considered a risk.

Efficacy

In its previous opinion (EFSA FEEDAP Panel, 2022), the FEEDAP Panel could not conclude on the efficacy of locust bean gum as an emulsifier due to the absence of data. However, the FEEDAP Panel concluded on the efficacy of locust bean gum as a gelling agent, thickener and stabiliser in canned pet feed, when simultaneously used with another hydrocolloid (carrageenan). In the current submission, the applicant withdrew the request for the functional group emulsifier and provided information to support the efficacy of locust bean gum when used alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids.

The applicant submitted a series of published papers in which the effect of locust bean gum, alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids (Copetti et al., 1997; Demirci et al., 2014; Dolz et al., 2007; Mandala et al., 2004; O'Connell et al., 2023; Sittikijyothin et al., 2005; Khoobbakht et al., 2024; Sousa & Goncalves, 2015; Russo Spena et al., 2024; Martins et al., 2012; Higiro et al., 2006; Lurueña‐Martínez et al., 2004; Lin et al., 2024), in the formation of gels and stabilisation of the matrix was studied. Most of the studies were done in aqueous solutions. The Panel acknowledges that locust bean gum, in common with other gums, has the capacity to increase the viscosity of the solutions by the formation of gels, especially after heat treatment. However, it is not possible to directly extrapolate the effect observed in aqueous solutions to complex feed matrices, especially those with low moisture content. Only two of the references provided studied the effect of locust bean gum on complex matrices resembling pet food.

Lurueña‐Martínez et al. (2004) studied the effect of a commercial mixture of a blend of locust bean gum and xanthan gum (proportion of each gum unknown) at 0.5% or 0.6% to low‐fat frankfurters. The addition of the locust bean gum/xanthan gum mixture resulted in lower total expressible fluid and reduced cook loss without an effect on hardness and most textural characteristics. The Panel notes that the effect of the gum mixture is confounded by the use of different fat levels in the different groups.

Dermici et al. (2014) studied the effect of locust bean gum at 0.5%, 1% or 1.5% on the texture, physicochemical and sensory properties of veal meatballs after cooking. The addition of 1.5% locust bean gum reduced cooking losses and increased the firmness of the cooked meatballs, indicating a stabilising effect.

Overall, the Panel acknowledges that the use of locust bean gum alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids may have the potential to act as a gelling agent, thickener and stabiliser, in matrices with high moisture content. However, the evidence provided by the applicant is not adequate to support the efficacy of locust bean gum alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids in complex feed matrices typical of the pet food industry. No evidence has been provided on the efficacy of locust bean gum alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids in feed matrices with high dry matter content.

Post‐market monitoring

The FEEDAP Panel notes that tolerance studies in cats are not intended or capable of detecting any adverse effects that might occur over a long exposure to locust bean gum. For this reason, it is recommended that a post‐market monitoring plan able to recognise any long‐term adverse effects should be introduced.

CONCLUSIONS

Locust bean gum is considered safe for the target species at the respective maximum use levels detailed in the table below:

Regarding user safety, the additive is considered irritant to skin and eyes and is considered a potential skin and respiratory sensitiser. Exposure of users by any route is considered a risk.

The Panel concludes that the additive alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids may have the potential to act as a gelling agent, thickener and stabiliser in matrices with high moisture content. However, it cannot conclude on its efficacy, alone or in combination with other hydrocolloids, in complex feed matrices typical of the pet food industry or in feeds with low moisture content.
