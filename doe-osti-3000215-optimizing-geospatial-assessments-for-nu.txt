Abstract
A multidisciplinary team at Argonne National Laboratory evaluated the ability of large
language models (LLMs) to identify geographic locations from open-source text and assessed
post-processing measures to strengthen the reliability of those extractions in support of
international nuclear safeguards. The study focused on addressing challenges such as toponym
ambiguity, imprecise descriptions, and misinformation, which often undermine the accuracy of
LLM-derived geospatial assessments. By integrating authoritative geospatial datasets,
employing rigorous validation techniques, and leveraging human-in-the-loop processes, the
project aimed to enhance the precision, transparency, and reproducibility of geospatial
localization workflows. The findings demonstrate that while LLMs exhibit significant potential
for accelerating geospatial analysis, their outputs require systematic grounding and verification
to ensure reliability in high-stakes applications. This work contributes to the broader field of
geospatial intelligence and supports strategic objectives of international organizations such as
the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE).

Introduction
Geospatial localization plays a critical role in advancing national security applications,
particularly in the domain of international nuclear safeguards. The ability to accurately identify
and verify the locations of nuclear-related activities and entities is essential for ensuring
compliance with international treaties, enhancing transparency, and supporting timely decision-
making processes. However, the growing volume of multilingual, open-source reporting and
commercial remote-sensing data presents significant challenges for analysts tasked with
synthesizing this information. These challenges are compounded by the inherent limitations of
large language models (LLMs), which, while powerful, often produce brittle outputs that lack
rigorous grounding, especially in geospatial contexts where ambiguity and misinformation can
lead to errors.

This report explores innovative techniques for employing LLMs to extract and validate
geospatial information from open-source text, with a focus on safeguards-relevant applications.
By addressing critical issues such as toponym ambiguity, imprecise descriptions, and
misinformation, the project aims to enhance the reliability and verifiability of LLM-derived
geospatial assessments. The findings presented herein are intended to inform the development
of human-in-the-loop processes that systematically improve the accuracy, transparency, and
reproducibility of geospatial localization for safeguards workflows.

Through rigorous post-processing techniques, including grounding model outputs against
authoritative geospatial datasets, quantifying uncertainty, and cross-validating claims with
multi-source evidence, this project seeks to optimize analyst workflows while maintaining high
standards of accuracy and traceability. The ultimate goal is not to replace human judgment but
to augment it, enabling analysts to produce defensible, geospatially precise, and auditably
sourced evidence packages that can be seamlessly integrated into safeguards decision-making
processes. By leveraging advanced AI/ML technologies, this work contributes to the broader
field of geospatial intelligence and supports the strategic objectives of international organizations
such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the U.S. Department of Energy
(DOE).

Objectives and Scope
International nuclear safeguards depend on timely, accurate, and well-documented geospatial
understanding of declared and potentially undeclared nuclear-related activities. Analysts must
triage and synthesize rapidly growing volumes of multilingual, open-source reporting and
commercial remote-sensing data to support state evaluation, acquisition path analysis, design
information verification, and complementary access planning. While large language models
(LLMs) can accelerate this work by surfacing relevant entities, events, and relationships, their
outputs can be brittle without rigorous grounding—particularly for geospatial localization where
toponym ambiguity, imprecise descriptions, and misinformation can lead to false positives or
missed signals. Establishing and enhancing the reliability and verifiability of LLM-derived
geospatial assessments directly strengthens safeguards by improving the timeliness and quality
of leads, increasing transparency and reproducibility of analytic judgments, and reducing analyst
workload while maintaining high standards of accuracy and traceability. Reliably automating
geospatial assessments can further optimize analyst workflows through greater automation of
tasks such as relevance assessment and acquisition of higher-value satellite imagery.

Accordingly, the objectives of this project are to analyze techniques for employing LLMs to
ascertain the locations of safeguards-relevant activities and entities described in open-source
text, and to assess the reliability of LLM-extracted geolocations using these techniques. Findings
are anticipated to inform development and validation of human-in-the-loop processes that
systematically improve the geospatial localization and verification of LLM-derived assessments
for safeguards-relevant use cases. Envisioned aspects of these processes include: (1) grounding
model outputs against authoritative geospatial datasets and registries; (2) quantifying and
accessing uncertainty in locations, timestamps, and inferred activities; (3) cross-validating
claims with multi-source evidence, including commercial satellite imagery and trusted reference
materials; and (4) producing analyst-ready, auditably sourced evidence packages with explicit
coordinates, spatial extents, data lineage, and confidence scores.

This project emphasizes reliability, provenance, and repeatability by instrumenting the full
chain—from data ingestion and geoparsing through adjudication and reporting—with versioned
models, standardized metrics, and transparent verification checks. The ultimate objective is not
to replace analyst judgment, but to measurably improve analyst productivity and consistency by
providing defensible, geospatially precise, and verifiable assessments that can be rapidly
integrated into safeguards workflows and decision processes. Specifically, the project aims to:
 1. Develop and assess post-processing techniques for validating geolocation assessments
 extracted from knowledge graphs (KGs), generated by LLMs.
 2. Quantify the reliability of geolocation assessments produced by LLMs without post-
 processing and evaluate the improvements achieved through post-processing.
 3. Demonstrate the feasibility of leveraging post-processed KGs to assist human analysts in
 verifying events and activities in specific locations, thereby reducing the time required
 to verify assumptions.
The scope of the project includes:
 1. Research Focus:
 o Concentrating on safeguards-relevant content, particularly open-source articles
 annotated with geolocation and geographic properties.
 o Addressing the challenges posed by LLM hallucinations and misinformation in
 geolocation assessments.
 o Utilizing rule-based and procedural post-processing to corroborate geographic
 properties and relationships within extracted KGs.
 2. Data and Tools:
 o Leveraging Argonne’s Global Material Security (GMS) Daily Data Repository,
 which contains thousands of expert-curated, safeguards-relevant articles
 associated with verified geolocations.
 o Utilizing common knowledge bases such as GeoNames link geographic names to
 geolocations.
 3. Evaluation Framework:
 o Developing a testing framework to automate the evaluation of LLM geolocation
 accuracy, both with and without post-processing.
 o Iteratively refining post-processing techniques to maximize the trustworthiness
 of geolocation assessments.
 4. Strategic Alignment:
 o First, aligning with the IAEA’s Development and Implementation Support
 Programme for Nuclear Verification 2024-2025, which emphasizes leveraging
 AI/ML for safeguards analysis.

 o Second, supporting the DOE’s mission to innovate next-generation
 nonproliferation approaches and technologies that in turn bolster national security
 capabilities in addition to potential support to national security objectives at select
 international organizations such as the IAEA.

By addressing these objectives and adhering to this scope, the project aims to advance the state
of geospatial localization for national security and information verification applications while
contributing to the broader field of AI/ML integration in decision-making processes. In doing
so, it strengthens core safeguards equities—timeliness, accuracy, traceability, impartiality,
confidentiality, and efficient use of limited inspection resources—by producing auditable,
geospatially precise, and multi-source–validated assessments that integrate seamlessly with state
evaluation, acquisition path analysis, design information verification, and complementary access
planning. The workflow is designed to reduce false positives and negatives, expose and quantify
uncertainty, and uphold disciplined provenance and access controls to protect sensitive and
commercially proprietary information. By enhancing analyst productivity and consistency
through human-in-the-loop oversight and transparent methods, the project reinforces trust with
international partners and facility operators and supports credible, effective, and accountable
safeguards implementation.

Related Work
The construction of high-fidelity knowledge graphs (KGs) from open-source text is a critical
enabler for applications in domains like nuclear safeguards and open-source intelligence
(OSINT), where the accuracy of geographic information is paramount. While Large Language
Models (LLMs) have demonstrated powerful capabilities in natural language understanding and
information extraction, their utility is often undermined by a tendency to "hallucinate,"
producing factually incorrect or unverifiable information (Huang et al., 2025; Tonmoy et al.,
2024). This unreliability is particularly acute in the geospatial domain, where LLMs exhibit
fundamental weaknesses in both quantitative and qualitative spatial reasoning. For example,
studies show that standard LLMs struggle to represent and reason about formal geometric data,
yielding massive errors in tasks like area computation and centroid derivation from Well-Known
Text (WKT) representations (Ji & Gao, 2023). Similarly, when tested against the formal Region
Connection Calculus (RCC-8), a leading model like GPT-4 achieved only 71.94% accuracy on
compositional reasoning tasks, frequently making elementary mistakes and hallucinating
impossible spatial relations (Cohn, 2023).

To address these shortcomings, a growing body of research advocates for hybrid approaches that
ground LLM outputs in structured, verifiable knowledge. Knowledge graphs, particularly those
with rich geospatial semantics, serve as a critical external resource for this grounding, enabling
fact verification, error correction, and the enforcement of logical constraints (Li et al., 2025;
Schneider et al., 2025). This review synthesizes recent literature on the methods, resources, and
evaluation techniques for building and validating geospatial knowledge, with a focus on creating
a robust pipeline that mitigates LLM hallucinations and ensures the reliability of extracted
geographic entities.

Geoparsing and Toponym Recognition
The foundational step in any geospatial KG construction pipeline is geoparsing, which involves
recognizing location mentions (toponym recognition) and resolving them to specific geographic
coordinates (toponym resolution). A comprehensive survey of 27 location recognition tools
revealed a significant trade-off between precision and recall; while most tools achieve high
precision, they often miss a considerable number of location references, especially fine-grained
ones like traffic ways and points of interest (POIs) (Hu et al., 2023).

To improve this initial extraction step, research has moved towards fine-tuning large
transformer-based models for the specific task of toponym recognition. Berragan et al. (2023)
demonstrated that a custom-trained BERT model achieved an F1-score of 0.939, significantly
outperforming pre-built models like Stanza (F1=0.730). Their approach uncovered 62,178
unique place names in a corpus of British Wikipedia articles that were absent from the
GeoNames gazetteer, highlighting the vast amount of geographic information missed by
standard methods. Similarly, Zhou et al. (2023) developed TopoBERT, a model that achieved a
state-of-the-art average F1-score of 0.854 across seven benchmark datasets by combining a fine-
tuned BERT with a 1D-CNN classifier. This model prioritizes high recall, making it an excellent
candidate for an initial extraction phase that maximizes the capture of potential locations, which
can then be filtered in subsequent validation stages.

Handling multilingual and noisy text, common in OSINT, presents further challenges. Mironov
et al. (2025) developed a modular pipeline for Ukrainian, Russian, and English social media that
combines gazetteer matching with language-specific BERT models, achieving a 38.8% relative
F1-score improvement over a baseline on a custom dataset. For morphologically complex
languages like Chinese, hybrid models such as ALBERTBiLSTMCRF have shown high
performance (96.1% F1-score) on social media texts by integrating custom-trained ALBERT
embeddings with a CRF layer that enforces label sequence constraints (Tao et al., 2022). These
studies underscore that high-quality toponym recognition is not a solved problem and often
requires domain- and language-specific models to create a reliable input for downstream
validation and linking.

Entity Linking and Toponym Disambiguation
Once a toponym is recognized, it must be linked to a canonical entity in a knowledge base, a
process complicated by the ambiguity of place names (e.g., "Paris, Texas" vs. "Paris, France").
Modern approaches increasingly combine neural methods with external knowledge and
constraints. Kulkarni et al. (2020) introduced MLG, a neural geocoder that can operate gazetteer-
free but achieves state-of-the-art performance when using a gazetteer-constrained post-
processing step. This step re-ranks candidates from a gazetteer by minimizing a function of the
model's predicted location and the candidate's population, achieving a mean error of 414 km and
81% accuracy@161km.

Other methods use LLMs and rule-based constraints more directly. Zhang et al. (2024) proposed
GeoPLACE, which first uses a transformer to predict a toponym's implicit attributes (country,
state, feature class) and then uses these attributes as hard constraints to deterministically filter
and rank candidates from GeoNames, achieving 86.3% accuracy and a 21 km mean error on the
LGL dataset. Hu et al. (2024) demonstrated a similar two-stage approach, fine-tuning a
lightweight LLM (Mistral-7B) to generate an unambiguous reference string (e.g., "city, state,

country"), which is then validated by sequentially querying geocoding services (GeoNames,
Nominatim, ArcGIS). This method achieved 91% Accuracy@161km and effectively mitigates
LLM hallucinations by grounding the output in trusted external services.

Semantic context from the source document is also a powerful disambiguation signal. Halterman
(2023) describes Mordecai 3, which uses a neural ranker that combines textual context similarity
(via RoBERTa embeddings), string similarity, and rule-like heuristics, such as checking for
hierarchical relationships between co-occurring locations (e.g., a city candidate is within a state
candidate). Molina-Villegas et al. (2021) proposed a novel method where, for each candidate
location from OpenStreetMap (OSM), a "pseudo-document" is created by gathering contextual
information about nearby POIs. The correct location is then identified by finding the candidate
whose pseudo-document has the highest semantic similarity (via doc2vec embeddings) to the
original source text, achieving 74.62% accuracy. These approaches show a clear trend towards
hybrid systems that integrate the contextual understanding of neural models with the factual
grounding of geospatial KBs and rule-based constraints.

Post-Processing and Verification for Geographic Consistency
Given the inherent unreliability of automated extraction, post-processing and verification are
essential for creating high-quality geospatial KGs. These techniques range from simple rule-
based filtering to complex, multi-source corroboration.

Rule-Based and Constraint-Based Methods

Simple but effective methods apply rules before or after the main extraction step. Benjira et al.
(2025) proposed a "Rule-Based Filtering" pre-processing step to guide LLMs, using constraints
like type consistency, regular expressions for coordinate patterns, and value range checks. This
pre-filtering increased the F1-score of their LLM-based schema mapping from a baseline of
62.98% to 98.31%. Conversely, Acheson and Purves (2021) applied rule-based filtering after
NER to distinguish relevant "study locations" from incidental mentions (e.g., author affiliations),
finding that this step was critical for achieving high precision (0.84) in their pipeline. Other rule-
based approaches tackle specific linguistic challenges, such as normalizing adjectival toponyms
(e.g., "French" to "France") before geocoding, which was shown to significantly reduce mean
error distance (Sá. et al., 2022).

More complex rules can enforce logical consistency within the KG itself. Balsebre et al. (2023)
proposed a KG refinement module with two algorithms: an Extend algorithm that uses
transitivity rules (e.g., if A same_as B and B part_of C, then A part_of C) to improve recall, and
a Repair algorithm that uses consistency constraints (e.g., an entity cannot be part_of two
different, non-overlapping areas) to improve precision. This refinement step improved the F1-
score of their relation prediction from 89.1% to 90.17%.

Corroboration with External and Multimodal Data

For OSINT and safeguards applications, corroborating information across multiple sources is a
cornerstone of verification. Bongard et al. (2023) proposed a method to assess the reliability of
geolocated social media posts by classifying them as reliable only if they coincide in space (same
municipality), time (same day), and semantic content (high embedding similarity) with a report
from an authoritative source like GDELT or ACLED. This approach successfully verified

31.75% of Telegram messages in their dataset. The practice of cross-source corroboration is
central to the work of OSINT analysts, who validate claims by fusing primary government
sources, news media, and commercial satellite imagery (Kristensen & Korda, 2024).

A novel form of external validation involves using remote sensing imagery. Wang et al. (2022)
developed a post-processing method to correct the location of events described with specific
landscape attributes (e.g., "a forest-crop ecological pattern"). By identifying clusters of the
corresponding feature in satellite imagery near the initial geoparsed point, their method reduced
the average location offset from ~50 km to ~1 km, demonstrating a powerful way to refine coarse
geolocations.

Hallucination Mitigation and Fact Verification
Mitigating LLM hallucinations is a central challenge, and the literature provides a broad
taxonomy of causes and solutions (Huang et al., 2025; Zhang & Zhang, 2025). For geospatial
tasks, two families of techniques are particularly relevant: Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG) and post-generation verification.

RAG frameworks ground LLM responses by providing relevant context retrieved from an
external knowledge source. Several works have adapted this for geospatial modeling. Liang et
al. (2025) introduced GeoGraphRAG, which parses a user query, retrieves a "solution graph"
representing a valid workflow from a geospatial KG, and uses this graph to structure the prompt
for the LLM, improving the semantic similarity of generated solutions by over 30% compared
to baselines. While RAG reduces hallucinations, it is not a panacea; failures can still occur due
to faulty retrieval or the LLM's inability to faithfully adhere to the provided context (Huang et
al., 2025).

Post-generation verification methods treat the LLM's initial output as a draft that must be fact-
checked. A prominent technique is the "Chain-of-Verification" (CoVe), where the model is
prompted to generate and answer verification questions about its own claims before producing a
final, refined response (Dhuliawala et al., 2023, as cited in Tonmoy et al., 2024). Guan et al.
(2024) proposed a similar autonomous framework, "Knowledge Graph-based Retrofitting"
(KGR), which iteratively extracts factual claims from an LLM's draft, verifies them against
Wikidata using SPARQL queries, and retrofits the response. This post-processing loop improved
F1 scores by over 15 points on the HotpotQA benchmark. In a geospatial context, Liu et al. (n.d.)
implemented a two-LLM system where one LLM generates a GeoSPARQL query to answer a
question, and a second LLM validates the query's semantic correctness before execution,
achieving 89% accuracy. These methods provide a template for building self-correcting
pipelines that treat LLM outputs with skepticism and subject them to rigorous, KG-grounded
validation.

Knowledge Bases, Datasets, and Evaluation
The effectiveness of any validation pipeline can depend on the quality of the knowledge bases it
queries and the rigor of its evaluation metrics.

Knowledge Bases and Linked Data

GeoNames, DBpedia, and Wikidata are foundational KBs for linking and validating geographic
entities (Möller et al., 2022). OpenStreetMap (OSM) is another critical resource, particularly for
fine-grained features and geometries (Kuculo, 2022). Recognizing the limitations of general-
purpose KGs, researchers have also developed large-scale, high-precision geographic KGs.
Dsouza et al. (2021) created WorldKG, a graph of over 100 million entities from OSM with
schema links to DBpedia and Wikidata that were manually verified to achieve 100% precision.
Sälevä and Lignos (2024) produced ParaNames, a multilingual gazetteer of 16.8 million entities
(including 3.88 million locations) from Wikidata, which improved NER performance in 10
languages when used as a feature. These resources can serve as high-quality ground truth for
validation tasks. The GeoSPARQL standard is essential for performing spatial queries on these
KGs, enabling reasoning about topological relations like sfContains and sfIntersects (Böckling
et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2021).

Datasets, Metrics, and Rigorous Evaluation

While standard metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-score are widely used, the geospatial
domain requires specialized evaluation. Common metrics for toponym resolution include
mean/median geodesic error distance (in km) and Accuracy@k, the percentage of predictions
within k kilometers of the ground truth (typically 161 km) (Halterman, 2023; Kulkarni et al.,
2020).

However, recent work has critiqued these metrics as potentially misleading. Liu et al. (2022)
conducted a spatially explicit evaluation of several geoparsers and found that overall metrics
hide significant geographic biases. Using spatial autocorrelation statistics, they identified
performance "hot spots" and "cold spots," revealing that even models with high overall accuracy
can perform poorly in specific regions. This finding is critical for safeguards, as it implies that a
system's reliability may be geographically dependent. Similarly, Leppämäki et al. (2024) argued
that distance-based metrics are sensitive to the scale of geographic features and proposed more
robust metrics, ACC@POLY and ACC@BBOX, which measure whether a predicted point falls
within the ground-truth polygon or bounding box.

For a holistic assessment, Wang et al. (2025) proposed a comprehensive quality framework for
GeoKGs with 20 distinct metrics covering data validity, content accuracy (semantic, logical,
temporal, and spatial), and interoperability. The existence of such frameworks, combined with
the critiques of standard metrics, points to the need for a multi-faceted and spatially-aware
evaluation strategy to ensure the trustworthiness of geolocated data. This is particularly relevant
in the OSINT domain, where societal verification efforts are already challenged by the "selective
gaze" of Western-centric analysis and a lack of formal standards for turning data into evidence
(Al-Sayed et al., 2023).

Gaps and Implications for Safeguards
The literature reveals a clear consensus: relying on a single tool or a purely generative LLM for
geospatial information extraction is insufficient for high-stakes applications. A robust pipeline
must be a hybrid system that integrates high-recall extraction, multi-source linking and
disambiguation, and rigorous, rule-based post-processing and verification.

Key implications for our work include:

 1. A Pipeline Approach is Necessary: The process must be modular, starting with a high-
 recall toponym recognition model (Berragan et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023), followed by
 a disambiguation and linking step that leverages both neural context and hard constraints
 (Zhang et al., 2024), and concluding with a multi-faceted validation stage.
 2. Validation Must Be Explicit and Multi-Layered: Validation cannot be an afterthought.
 It should involve checking for logical consistency (Balsebre et al., 2023), corroborating
 against external KBs (Guan et al., 2024), and, where possible, using orthogonal data
 sources like satellite imagery (Wang et al., 2022).
 3. Evaluation Must Be Rigorous and Spatially Aware: We must move beyond simple
 accuracy metrics and adopt a spatially explicit evaluation framework that can detect
 geographic biases (Liu et al., 2022) and uses scale-insensitive metrics like polygon
 intersection (Leppämäki et al., 2024).
 4. Constraints are Key to Mitigating Hallucinations: Whether applied as pre-filtering
 rules (Benjira et al., 2025), hard constraints during linking (Zhang et al., 2024), or as part
 of a post-generation verification loop (Guan et al., 2024), explicit constraints grounded
 in trusted KGs are the most effective safeguard against LLM unreliability.

By synthesizing these approaches, it is possible to design a system that leverages the power of
modern AI while incorporating the verification and rigor necessary for the sensitive domain of
nuclear safeguards.

Methodology
Technology Foundation and Framework Components

This research leveraged a sophisticated technology stack combining cutting-edge artificial
intelligence frameworks with established geospatial data management systems.

LangChain, an open-source framework for developing applications with large language models,
provided the foundational architecture for orchestrating complex AI workflows, managing
prompt templates, and handling structured responses from multiple LLM providers. LangChain's
modular design enabled seamless integration of different language models while maintaining
consistent interfaces for document processing and response parsing.

Ollama served as the local LLM deployment platform, enabling secure, on-premises execution
of multiple large language models including Llama3.3:70b, r1-1776:70b, Gemma3:27b, and
Qwen3:32b. Ollama's architecture provided crucial advantages for this national security
application: complete control over model execution, elimination of external API dependencies,
and the ability to process sensitive geospatial intelligence without data leaving secure
infrastructure. The platform's intelligent load balancing and connection pooling capabilities
enabled concurrent evaluation of multiple model configurations while managing substantial
computational resources efficiently.

GeoNames provided the comprehensive geospatial reference database containing over 12
million geographic entities worldwide, including populated places, administrative boundaries,
and geographic features with coordinates and hierarchical administrative relationships

(GeoNames, n.d.). This authoritative gazetteer served as the ground truth for location validation
and coordinate disambiguation, offering multilingual support for geographic names across 400+
languages. The GeoNames integration enabled fuzzy matching algorithms and administrative
boundary verification essential for post-processing validation of LLM-extracted locations.

MLflow functioned as the experiment tracking and model lifecycle management platform,
providing systematic logging of hyperparameter configurations, performance metrics, and
experimental results across the extensive Bayesian optimization process. MLflow's nested run
organization facilitated management of complex experimental designs with 174 parameter
combinations and 8,700 document evaluations, while automated artifact storage and
visualization capabilities enabled comprehensive performance analysis and model comparison.
The platform's real-time logging capabilities proved essential for monitoring long-running
optimization experiments and ensuring reproducible research outcomes.

Description of the Research Plan and Approach
This research implemented a comprehensive geospatial intelligence platform designed to assess
and enhance the reliability of geospatial localization derived from Large Language Models
(LLMs) for moderate sensitive applications. The project followed a systematic approach
combining advanced natural language processing, spatial data management, and machine
learning optimization to create a robust intelligence gathering system capable of processing
multilingual documents and extracting geographic information.

The research plan was structured in four phases: (1) platform development and data integration,
(2) inference parameter optimization and model evaluation, (3) post-processing validation and
accuracy assessment, (4) identification of a “main topic” location and delegation of direct KG
extraction to the model.

A cornerstone of this effort was Argonne’s Global Material Security (GMS) Daily Data
Repository—an immense, analyst‑curated resources of safeguards‑relevant open‑source
reporting with verified geolocations that served as mission‑relevant ground truth (Adduci, 2025).
The repository’s breadth across regions, technologies, and languages, coupled with rich metadata
(coordinates, timestamps, sources, entities, and analyst annotations), enabled high‑fidelity data

(including measurements of spatial error distributions, precision/recall, and uncertainty

Techniques for Extracting Knowledge Graphs (KGs)
The knowledge graph extraction pipeline employed a sophisticated multi-stage approach:

LLM-Based Entity Extraction: Phases 1-3 evaluated four state-of-the-art language models
(Llama3.3:70b, r1-1776:70b, Gemma3:27b, and Qwen3:32b) using specialized prompt
templates designed for geospatial entity recognition. The system implemented three distinct
prompting strategies:
  Detailed Instructions: Comprehensive guidance emphasizing recall over precision for
 analyst workflows
  Output Format Focused: Minimal guidance with strict JSON schema compliance
  Few Examples: Pattern-based learning using 12 multilingual nuclear facility examples
 across 9 languages

Phase 4 evaluated three more recent reasoning models: OpenAI GPT-5, OpenAI o4-mini, and
Anthropic Claude Opus 4.

Structured Response Processing: All LLM outputs for phases 1-3 conformed to a strict JSON
schema capturing location names, confidence scores, potential coordinates, administrative
boundaries, and semantic relationships. The schema enforced US English standardization and
included comprehensive error recovery mechanisms.

Phase 4 tasked models to complete KGs in the XML dialect of Word Wide Web Consortium’s
Resource Description Framework (RDF 1.1 XML Syntax, 2014).

Relationship Extraction: The system identified and encoded spatial, temporal, and semantic
relationships between geographic entities, including proximity relationships (“near”, “connects
to”), administrative hierarchies (“located in”), and domain-specific connections relevant to
nuclear facilities.

Post-Processing Strategies for Geolocation Validation
Post-processing validation employed multiple complementary techniques to enhance
geolocation reliability:

Gazetteer Enrichment: Integration with the comprehensive GeoNames database containing
12+ million geographic entities provided real-time coordinate validation and administrative
boundary resolution. The system used fuzzy string matching algorithms (Jaro-Winkler
similarity) for location name disambiguation and normalization.

Coordinate Validation: PostGIS geodesic calculations validated extracted coordinates against
ground truth using distance-based scoring thresholds:

  ≤1km: 0.7 points (Excellent accuracy)
  ≤10km: 0.5 points (Good accuracy)
  ≤100km: 0.3 points (Moderate accuracy)
  ≤1000km: 0.1 points (Poor accuracy)
  >1000km: 0.0 points (Failed validation)

Administrative Boundary Verification: Multi-level administrative division validation ensured
extracted locations aligned with known political and geographic boundaries, cross-referencing
country codes, states/provinces, and regional classifications.

Nuclear Facility Cross-Reference: Specialized validation against an open-source dataset,
GeoNuclearData, derived in part from International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) databases
provided domain-specific verification for nuclear-related geographic entities, enhancing
accuracy for safeguards-relevant content (Stoica, 2015/2025).

Large Language Model Selection and Characteristics
The research evaluated multiple state-of-the-art language models; each was selected for distinct
capabilities relevant to geospatial intelligence extraction.

Phases 1-3
Llama3.3:70b (Meta AI) served as the industry-standard baseline, renowned for exceptional
instruction-following capabilities and balanced reasoning performance across diverse natural
language processing tasks. This 70-billion parameter transformer model provided the foundation
for comparison, representing current best practices in large-scale language model deployment
for structured information extraction.

r1-1776:70b (DeepSeek) represented the cutting edge of reasoning-specialized language
models, specifically engineered for step-by-step analytical processing and refined to eliminate
political biases and censorship constraints present in many contemporary models. This model's
architecture emphasized systematic relationship extraction and logical inference, making it
particularly valuable for complex geospatial relationship mapping and multi-hop reasoning
required for comprehensive intelligence analysis.

Gemma3:27b (Google) was selected for its efficiency-to-performance ratio, delivering
optimized capabilities with significantly reduced computational requirements compared to larger
models. This 27-billion parameter model demonstrated Google's advances in parameter-efficient
training, making it ideal for resource-constrained deployment scenarios while maintaining
competitive performance in structured extraction tasks.

Qwen3:32b (Alibaba Cloud) brought essential multilingual capabilities to the evaluation, with
particular strengths in Chinese language processing and cross-cultural geographic
understanding. Given the global scope of nuclear facilities and the multilingual nature of
international intelligence sources, this model's robust support for non-Western languages and
cultural contexts provided crucial capabilities for comprehensive geospatial analysis across
diverse linguistic environments.

Phase 4
o4-mini (OpenAI) was selected based on the balance of speed and accuracy it has demonstrated
for past Argonne projects in the extraction of KGs from open-source text. o4-mini was designed
to deliver high performance with reduced computational overhead. This model is envisioned to
fit cases where higher throughput demands might justify reduced F1 (OpenAI Platform, n.d.).

GPT-5 (OpenAI) was included in the evaluation as a flagship model representing the latest
advancements in general-purpose reasoning and natural language understanding. Though it can
be perceptibly slower than o4-mini, teams for concurrent Argonne projects have observed GPT-
5 to demonstrate superior recall and precision in the extraction of KGs from open-source text.
Its ability to synthesize complex relationships, perform multi-hop reasoning, and adapt to diverse
contexts made it a valuable addition to the study.

Claude Opus 4 (Anthropic) was chosen to provide a counterpoint to the OpenAI models.
Published scores for reasoning tests show Claude 4 to have comparable reasoning capabilities to
GPT-5 (The Frontier AI, n.d.).

This diverse model selection strategy ensured comprehensive evaluation across different
architectural approaches, computational efficiency profiles, and specialized capabilities,

enabling identification of optimal models for various operational requirements and deployment
constraints within national security applications.

Testing Framework and Evaluation Metrics
Experimental Design: The evaluation framework employed Bayesian optimization with
Gaussian Process regression to efficiently explore the hyperparameter space across 174
parameter combinations, conducting 8,700 total document evaluations with 50 documents per
configuration.

Composite Scoring System: A weighted scoring mechanism balanced multiple performance
dimensions:
  Geographic Accuracy (70%): Distance-based scoring against ground truth coordinates
  Location Extraction Volume (20%): Number of geographic entities identified per
 document
  Relationship Extraction Completeness (10%): Semantic relationships captured
 between locations

Performance Metrics:
  Geographic Precision: Geodesic distance calculations using PostGIS
  Processing Throughput: Documents processed per second across different model
 configurations
  Extraction Completeness: Coverage of ground truth locations in test documents
  Administrative Accuracy: Correct identification of political boundaries and
 administrative divisions

Statistical Validation: The framework implemented rigorous statistical analysis including
convergence monitoring, early stopping criteria (95% accuracy threshold), and confidence
interval estimation for performance metrics.

Results and Analysis
Comprehensive baseline testing across four leading language models revealed significant
performance variations in geospatial entity extraction capabilities.

Llama3.3:70b emerged as the overall leader in geographic accuracy, achieving the highest
precision in coordinate prediction and administrative boundary identification. The model
demonstrated exceptional performance in distance-based scoring, consistently placing extracted
locations within acceptable proximity thresholds to ground truth coordinates.

DeepSeek r1-1776:70b demonstrated superior entity detection volume, extracting
approximately 4.0 locations per document compared to other models’ averages of 2.0-3.0
locations. This model also excelled in relationship extraction, identifying ~4.5 semantic
relationships per document, significantly outperforming competitors. However, its geographic
precision was moderate compared to Llama3.3:70b, indicating a trade-off between extraction
volume and coordinate accuracy.

Gemma3:27b showed strong location extraction capabilities (~3.5 locations per document)
but exhibited the weakest relationship extraction performance (~0.5 relationships per document).
Despite its efficiency advantages, the model’s limited relationship mapping capabilities reduced
its overall utility for comprehensive geospatial intelligence analysis.

Qwen3:32b, while offering the fastest processing speed (~0.05 documents/second),
demonstrated the lowest composite scores across all evaluation metrics. The model’s
multilingual capabilities, though theoretically advantageous for global content processing, did
not translate to superior performance on the predominantly English evaluation dataset.

Impact of Post-Processing on Geolocation Reliability
Post-processing validation mechanisms dramatically enhanced geolocation reliability across all
tested models. Gazetteer integration with the GeoNames database provided critical
coordinate disambiguation, resolving ambiguous location names through fuzzy string
matching and administrative boundary cross-referencing. This process was particularly valuable
for handling multilingual variations and alternate spellings of geographic entities.

Distance-based validation using PostGIS geodesic calculations enabled precise accuracy
assessment, with the majority of validated locations falling within the “Good” to “Excellent”
accuracy categories (≤10km from ground truth). The tiered scoring system effectively penalized
more inaccurate extractions while rewarding high-precision identifications.

Administrative boundary verification significantly reduced false positives by cross-
referencing extracted locations with known political divisions. This validation was especially
effective for nuclear facility locations, where precise administrative context is crucial for
regulatory and security analysis.

The nuclear facility cross-reference system using GeoNuclearData provided domain-specific
validation that enhanced accuracy for safeguards-relevant content by 15-20% compared to
general-purpose geographic validation alone.

Direct KG Creation with Reasoning Models
The final set of experiments tasked three recent reasoning models with directly completing a KG
from each article and delivering that KG in RDF/XML. Specifically, for each article, each model
was tasked to complete a KG that already included a node for the article itself by:
  creating a node for each location referenced or implied by the article, identifying latitude,
 and longitude when possible;
  creating a node for each activity referenced or implied by the article, connecting that
 activity to its location using the atLocation predicate from the PROV-O ontology
 (PROV-O: The PROV Ontology, n.d.); and
  creating a node for each additional entity referenced or implied by the article, connecting
 that entity to its location if identifiable, again using the atLocation predicate.

The input data set for this test was filtered down to approximately 2,800 articles with valid and
unique URLs—and for which text content was successfully retrieved with no indication of error.
The KG output from each of these operations was parsed and validated using Apache Jena. When
a communication or validation error occurred, the request was resubmitted to the model for a
maximum of three tries per combination of article and model. For 2,248 articles, all three models
successfully produced a KG with at least one identified location. For 125 articles, no locations
were extracted by any model.

This test again measured the distance from each location node identified to the human-vetted
coordinates associated with the article and captured the shortest such distance for each article.
Captured distances ranged from 0km (inferred coordinates were effectively equivalent to vetted
coordinates) to more than 20,000km.

interoperable with RDF KGs.

Challenges and Limitations
Discussion of Challenges Encountered During the Project
Effective integration of LLM-enabled geospatial analysis into safeguards and national security
verification regimes demands operational robustness across deployment, data, language, scale,
and output control. The challenges encountered in this project—spanning multi-model
infrastructure management, harmonization of heterogeneous databases, cross-linguistic
processing inconsistencies, computational constraints in systematic optimization, and strict
adherence to structured output—are not merely technical hurdles; they directly affect core
safeguards equities of timeliness, accuracy, traceability, impartiality, and confidentiality. Left
unaddressed, these issues can increase analyst workload, introduce delays or reproducibility
gaps, and elevate the risk of false positives/negatives in facility localization and activity
assessment. Framing and resolving these challenges are essential to deliver geospatially precise,
auditable, and analyst‑ready outputs that strengthen safeguards implementations—state‑level
evaluation, acquisition path analysis, design information verification, and complementary access
planning—and, more broadly, support credible, accountable national security verification.
Challenges which emerged throughout the lifecycle of the project include:

Model Deployment and Resource Management: Implementing multiple large language
models simultaneously presented significant infrastructure challenges. Ollama server pool
management required load balancing and connection pooling to prevent resource exhaustion
while maintaining acceptable processing speeds.
Data Integration Complexity: Integrating heterogeneous data sources (MSSQL Server,
PostgreSQL/PostGIS, GeoNames, IAEA databases) required schema harmonization and data
transformation.

Multilingual Processing Inconsistencies: Translation quality and cultural geographic
naming conventions varied significantly across languages. Some models performed poorly on
non-English content despite theoretical multilingual capabilities, particularly for geographic
entities with complex transliteration requirements. Newer models are emerging with better cross-
linguistic training, which may improve this in the future.

Scalability and Processing Time: Bayesian optimization experiments required substantial
computational resources, with full hyperparameter exploration taking 12+ hours even with
parallel processing. The trade-off between experimental thoroughness and practical time
constraints limited the breadth of parameter space exploration. This could be reduced with a
more extensive use of HPC resources.

LLM Response Consistency: Despite strict JSON schema enforcement, models occasionally
produced malformed outputs requiring extensive error handling and retry logic. Some models
exhibited sensitivity to subtle prompt variations, creating reproducibility challenges for
systematic evaluation.

Limitations of the Methodology and Tools
Despite the strong performance gains demonstrated, several methodological and tooling
limitations temper the immediate operational impact and must be addressed to fully realize
benefits for safeguards and national security verification. Gaps in temporal reasoning—such as
handling historical place names and evolving administrative boundaries—can affect coordinate
accuracy and trend analysis, potentially biasing state‑level evaluations or design information
verification when context spans decades. Constraints in modeling complex geopolitical and
geographic relationships limit the system’s ability to surface nuanced associations (e.g.,
procurement networks, front companies, facility linkages) that are vital for acquisition path
analysis and complementary access planning. Finally, the rapid pace of LLM evolution imposes
continuous evaluation and pipeline maintenance burdens that can affect timeliness and
reproducibility. Recognizing and mitigating these limitations are essential to uphold core
safeguards equities of accuracy, traceability, timeliness, impartiality, and confidentiality, reduce
analyst workload, and deliver auditable, geospatially precise outputs that strengthen credible
verification. Limitations presented through the course of this work include:

Temporal Context Limitations: The current methodology lacks temporal reasoning
capabilities for handling historical geographic references or administrative boundary changes
over time. Documents referencing former political entities or historical place names might pose
challenges for accurate coordinate assignment.

Limited Relationship Complexity: While the system captured basic spatial relationships,
complex geopolitical relationships and nuanced geographic associations remained beyond
current extraction capabilities. The relationship taxonomy was insufficient for comprehensive
intelligence analysis requiring deep contextual understanding.

Computational Resource Requirements: The platform’s substantial hardware demands
limit deployment flexibility and operational cost-effectiveness. The requirement for multiple
concurrent LLM instances created significant infrastructure overhead. Even a single LLM
requires HPC-class GPU(s) or deployment to a cloud service.

Evaluation Metric Limitations: The distance-based scoring system, while objective, failed to
account for the practical significance of geographic precision in different contexts. A 5km error
might be acceptable for regional analysis but critical for facility-specific intelligence, yet the
current metrics treat these scenarios identically.

Model Update and Maintenance Challenges: Rapidly evolving LLM capabilities require
continuous model evaluation and potential pipeline reconfiguration. The current framework
lacks automated mechanisms for incorporating new model versions or adapting to changing API
specifications, creating maintenance overhead for operational deployments.

Implications and Long-Term Impact
The project delivers strategic benefits to national security by accelerating the triage and
verification of multilingual, open-source reporting; elevating the precision and reproducibility
of geospatial localization; and reducing analyst workload while preserving high standards of
accuracy, traceability, and impartiality. For nonproliferation and counterproliferation
missions—including nuclear safeguards—it strengthens analyst assessment, information
verification, and other analysis planning through auditable, multi-source–validated evidence
packages with explicit coordinates, provenance, and confidence measures. Beyond safeguards,
the same capabilities could enhance related efforts such as treaty monitoring, strategic trade
control enforcement, sanctions and illicit procurement analysis by improving early warning,
decreasing false positives/negatives, and enabling consistent, defensible analysis.

More broadly, the work advances AI/ML integration into decision-making by demonstrating a
scalable, human-in-the-loop blueprint that grounds LLM outputs in authoritative geospatial
registries; quantifies uncertainty; enforces structured outputs; and employs rigorous model/data
versioning for repeatability and auditability. The framework’s emphasis on mission-aligned
metrics, standardized verification checks, and secure, on-premises or HPC-ready deployment
lowers adoption barriers in sensitive environments and establishes a pathway for continuous
improvement as models evolve. By coupling cutting-edge NLP with mature geospatial standards
and Argonne’s curated GMS Daily corpus, the project provides an enduring foundation for
trusted, analyst-centered AI that is extensible to future needs—such as time-aware reasoning and
richer relationship extraction—thereby sustaining long-term impact on credible, accountable
verification across the national security enterprise.

Conclusion
This report underscores the notable progress achieved in utilizing LLMs for geospatial
localization and verification, particularly in the realm of nuclear safeguards and national
security. By integrating authoritative geospatial datasets and employing rigorous post-
processing validation techniques, the project significantly enhanced the accuracy and reliability
of location extraction and disambiguation. Furthermore, the automation of complex geospatial
assessments can not only improved analyst productivity but may also help to ensure the
delivery of auditable, multi-source–validated evidence packages that uphold high standards of
accuracy and traceability. These advancements align strategically with international and
national objectives, such as the IAEA’s Development and Implementation Support Programme
for Nuclear Verification and the DOE’s mission to innovate next-generation nonproliferation
technologies.

The development of a robust evaluation framework, incorporating spatially explicit metrics,
has been instrumental in evaluating the reliability of geolocation assessments while identifying
geographic biases in model performance.

While the project has achieved significant milestones, challenges such as temporal reasoning
limitations, multilingual inconsistencies, and computational resource demands remain areas for
future improvement. Addressing these gaps will further enhance the operational robustness and
scalability of LLM-enabled geospatial analysis.

In sum, the authors aspire to offer a valuable step forward in integrating AI/ML technologies
into safeguards and national security workflows. By measuring and analyzing native
capabilities of LLMs to assess geolocations from open-source text as well as postprocessing
measures to improve these assessments, we aim to strengthen the credibility, accountability,
and efficiency of geospatial localization and verification processes, paving the way for more
reliable and impactful safeguards applications in the future.
