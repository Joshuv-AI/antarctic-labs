Averages over matrix unitary orbits and spectral order
Jean-Christophe Bourin, Eun-Young Lee

Abstract




Abstract.
We establish matrix versions of the comparisons between the
\ell^{p}
-norms or quasi-norms for sequences of complex numbers.
For instance, given
1\geq q>0
, and a family of
m
normal
d\times d
matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
, we show that

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|A_{k}|^{q}\right\}^{1/q}\!\!\!\!V_{i}^{*}

for some unitary
d\times d
matrices
V_{1},\ldots,V_{d}
. We also give applications to Olson’s spectral order and to the comparison between the symmetric modulus and the quadratic symmetric modulus. In particular we show that the sum
A+B
of two positive matrices submajorizes their Kato supremum
A\vee B
, thereby completing majorization results due to Ando.

Keywords.
Matrix inequalities, unitary orbits, symmetric modulus, Olson’s spectral order.

2020 mathematics subject classification.
15A42, 15A60, 47A30, 47A60.

1
Introduction

A basic inequality for
m
complex numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}
is the comparison, for
0<q\leq 1\leq p
, between the
\ell^{p}
-norm and the
\ell^{q}
-quasi-norm,

\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}|^{p}\right\}^{1/p}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}|\leq\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|a_{k}|^{q}\right\}^{1/q}.

(1.1)

What can be said in the matrix setting? Investigating this question has led us to several elegant matrix inequalities involving unitary orbits and convex or concave functions. These results are established in the next section, where we also discuss an application to the quadratic symmetric modulus arising in Zhang’s triangle inequality. We further explain the significance of Zhang’s 2026 result.

The third section is devoted to applications to the spectral order, including comparisons between sums and parallel sums of positive matrices and their suprema and infima. This completes a line of majorisation results initiated by Ando in the late 1980s. We also include a concise treatment of the spectral order relation
\preceq
, together with a new proof of the fact that, for positive operators,

A\preceq B\iff A^{n}\leq B^{n}\ \ \!{\mathrm{for\ all}}\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

This order, introduced by Olson in 1971, endows the set of Hermitian operators with a lattice structure,
extending the lattice of projections. It allows us to introduce the maximal and minimal symmetric moduli. Adapting Zhang’s inequality to these moduli
appears to be a challenging problem.

Our main tools are powerful matrix analogues of scalar inequalities for convex and concave functions. One of the earliest examples along these lines is von Neumann’s trace inequality (the special case
g(t)=t\log t
is the concavity of von Neumann’s entropy), which states that

{\mathrm{Tr\,}}g\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\leq{\mathrm{Tr\,}}\frac{g(A)+g(B)}{2}

(1.2)

for every convex function
g(t)
defined on an interval containing the spectra of the Hermitian matrices
A
and
B
. If furthermore
g(0)
=0 and
A
and
B
are positive semidefinite, then

{\mathrm{Tr\,}}g(A+B)\geq{\mathrm{Tr\,}}\!\left(g(A)+g(B)\right)

(1.3)

as shown by Rotfel’d in 1969
[
17
]
. We shall use refinements of (
1.2
)-(
1.3
) involving averages over the unitary orbit of a Hermitian matrix (or over the orthogonal orbit in the real symmetric case). In a short final section, we establish a relation between a real symmetric matrix
S
and its diagonal part
\Delta(S)
. In the
3\times 3
case,

\Delta(S)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i=1}^{4}U_{i}SU_{i}^{*}

for some orthogonal matrices
U_{1},\ldots,U_{4}
. We shall employ this observation to derive real analogues of results such as the inequality stated in the Abstract.

Thus, this note lies within the longstanding tradition in matrix analysis of extending scalar inequalities and identities to the noncommutative setting of matrices. Before proceeding,
we fix some notation. We denote by
\mathbb{M}_{d}
the space of complex
d\times d
matrices and
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
its cone of positive semidefinite matrices. For
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, we write
|A|=(A^{*}A)^{1/2}
for its absolute value (or right modulus). If
A\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is Hermitian, we denote by

\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(A)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{d}^{\downarrow}(A)

its eigenvalues arranged in nonincreasing order, and by

A^{\downarrow}
the diagonal matrix in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
having these eigenvalues on the diagonal. For a pair of Hermitian matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, the weak majorization, or submajorization, relation
A\prec_{w}B
means that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(A)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(B)

(1.4)

for all
k=1,\ldots,d
. If,
furthermore, equality holds for
k=d
, that is, if
A
and
B
have the same trace, then we say that
A
is majorized by
B
, and we write
A\prec B
. For each
k
, the sum of the
k
largest eigenvalues occuring in (
1.4
) is a subbaditive functional on the Hermitian part of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
.

2
Unitary orbits

A stronger form of (
1.2
) states that, for Hermitian matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
and a convex function
g(t)
defined on an interval containing the spectra of
A
and
B
,

g\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\{U\frac{g(A)+g(B)}{2}U^{*}+V\frac{g(A)+g(B)}{2}V^{*}\right\}

(2.1)

for some unitary matrices
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
,
[
7
, Corollary 2.2]
. For a concave function, the inequality reverses.
We shall use a multivariable version of this result, together with related inequalities, to prove our main theorems.

2.1
Convex and concave functions

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
g(t)
and
h(t)
be two nondecreasing convex functions on
[0,\infty)
such that
h(0)=0
. If
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
are matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
, then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

g\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}h(A_{k})\right)\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left\{g\circ h\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right)\right\}V_{i}^{*}.

As a special case, taking
g(t)=t^{r}
and
h(t)=t^{p}

with
p,r\geq 1
we obtain a matrix version of the left-hand inequality in (
1.1
).

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
and let
p,r\geq 1
. Then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}^{p}\right)^{r}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right\}^{pr}\!V_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

(Theorem
2.1
) From
[
6
, Corollary 3.2]
we have

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}h(A_{k})\right\|\leq\left\|h\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right)\right\|

(2.2)

for all symmetric (i.e., unitarily invariant) norms
\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Let

A:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}h(A_{k}),\quad B:=h\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right).

By the Fan dominance principle, (
2.2
) is equivalent to

A\prec_{w}B.

Consider the spectral decomposition

B=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\lambda_{k}^{\downarrow}(B)E_{k},

and define

B^{\prime}:=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d-1}\lambda_{k}^{\downarrow}(B)E_{k}\right)+\mu E_{d}

where
\mu:=\lambda_{d}^{\downarrow}(B)-{\mathrm{Tr\,}}B+{\mathrm{Tr\,}}A
is chosen so that

A\prec B^{\prime}.

By the Schur-Horn theorem
(see
[
15
]
or
[
11
]
for elegant proofs),
A
is unitarily equivalent to the diagonal part
of
WB^{\prime}W^{*}
for some unitary
W
; that is,

A=U\Delta(WB^{\prime}W^{*})U^{*}

(2.3)

for some unitary
U\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
.
By an identity due to Bhatia
[
3
, Eq. (2)]
, there exist diagonal
unitary matrices
R_{1},\ldots,R_{d}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\Delta(X)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}XR_{i}^{*}

for every matrix
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Applying this identity to

X=WB^{\prime}W^{*}
yields

\Delta(WB^{\prime}W^{*})=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}R_{i}WB^{\prime}W^{*}R_{i}^{*}.

Hence,

A=U\Delta(WB^{\prime}W^{*})U^{*}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(UR_{i}W)\,B^{\prime}\,(UR_{i}W)^{*}.

Setting
U_{i}:=UR_{i}W
, we obtain

A=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}B^{\prime}U_{i}^{*}.

Since
g(t)
is nondecreasing, we have a unitary
U_{0}
such that

g(A)\leq U_{0}g\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}\right)U_{0}^{*}.

Now, we can take
U=V
in (
2.1
) when the convex function is monotone, and a related inequality for
m
matrices
[
7
, Corollary 2.4]
ensures that

g\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}\right)\leq V_{0}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}U_{i}g(B)U_{i}^{*}\right)V_{0}^{*}.

This combined with the previous inequality yields

g(A)\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}g(B)V_{i}^{*}

for some unitaries
V_{1},\ldots,V_{d}
. Returning to the definitions of
A
and
B
completes the proof.
∎

We record a key point of this proof in the following lemma. The notation
\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)

stands for the Hermitian part of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
with spectra in an interval
\Omega
of the real line.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
and let
g(t)
be a nondecreasing convex function on
\Omega
. If
A\prec_{w}B
, then
there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

g(A)\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}g(B)V_{i}^{*}.

Beyond its role in the proof of Theorem
2.1
, Lemma
2.3
entails a fundamental property of submajorization: convex nondecreasing functions
g(t)
on
\Omega
preserve submajorization
\prec_{w}
on
\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
:

A\prec_{w}B\Rightarrow g(A)\prec_{w}g(B).

(2.4)

Our proof of Lemma
2.3
(and hence of Theorem
2.1
) does not depend on (
2.4
) and provides an alternative derivation of (
2.4
) from a unitary Jensen inequality combined with the Schur-Horn theorem.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
f,g:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be nondecreasing. Assume that
f(t)
is concave and
g(t)
is convex. If
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
are normal matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
, then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

g\circ f\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|\right)\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}g\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(|A_{k}|)\right)V_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

It was shown in
[
4
]
that, for every pair of normal matrices
N,M\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, we have

\|f(|N+M|)\|\leq\|f(|N|)+f(|M|)\|

for all symmetric norms. The corresponding
m
-variables version holds,
as stated in
[
4
, Theorem 3.3]
, or just before
[
4
, Corollary 2.4]
. Thus

\left\|f\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|\right)\right\|\leq\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{m}f(|A_{k}|)\right\|.

This is a submajorization relation
\prec_{w}
between the matrix in the left-hand and that one in the right-hand. So we may apply Lemma
2.3

to conclude
∎

For
1\geq q>0
and the functions
g(t)=t^{1/q}
and
f(t)=t^{q}
, we obtain a matrix version of the right-hand inequality in (
1.1
):

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be normal matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
and let
1\geq q>0
. Then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|A_{k}|^{q}\right\}^{1/q}\!\!\!\!V_{i}^{*}.

Let
{\mathrm{Re\,}}Z=(Z+Z^{*})/2
and
{\mathrm{Im\,}}Z=(Z-Z^{*})/2i
denote the real and imaginary parts of
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Two
further special cases of Theorem
2.4
are the following.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
and let
p\geq 1\geq q>0

\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\left|Z\right|^{pq}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left(|{\mathrm{Re\,}}Z|^{q}+|{\mathrm{Im\,}}Z|^{q}\right)^{p}V_{i}^{*}.

.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
and let
c>0
. Then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}

\mathbb{M}_{d}

\left|Z\right|\wedge cI\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left(|{\mathrm{Re\,}}Z|\wedge cI+|{\mathrm{Im\,}}Z|\wedge cI\right)V_{i}^{*}.

Here, if
T\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}

T\wedge cI=m_{c}(T),\qquad m_{c}(t)=\min\{t,c\},

and
m_{c}(t)

[0,\infty)
. We shall consider the infimum
A\wedge B

A companion result of Corollary

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}

\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}

\mathbb{M}_{d}

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|^{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|A_{k}|\right\}^{\alpha}\!\!V_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

From

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right|^{\alpha}\prec_{w}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}|A_{k}|\right\}^{\alpha}.

2.2
Symmetric moduli

For a matrix
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, its left and right moduli
|Z^{*}|
and
|Z|
are the positive part in the polar decompositions

Z=|Z^{*}|U=|Z^{*}|^{1/2}U|Z|^{1/2}=U|Z|.

These moduli occur in a number of important operator inequalities. There is no reason to privilege the left or the right modulus. Hence, the consideration of symmetrized versions, or means of these two moduli, should bring several interesting results.
The symmetric modulus and the quadratic symmetric modulus of
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
are respectively defined as

|Z|_{\mathrm{sym}}:=\frac{|Z|+|Z^{*}|}{2},\qquad|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}:=\sqrt{\frac{|Z|^{2}+|Z^{*}|^{2}}{2}}.

Note that

|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}=\sqrt{|{\mathrm{Re\,}}Z|^{2}+|{\mathrm{Im\,}}Z|^{2}}

and

|Z|_{\mathrm{sym}}\leq|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}

(2.5)

by operator concavity of
\sqrt{t}
. Very recently Teng Zhang obtained a triangle inequality for the quadratic modulus
[
20
, Theorem 1.9]
:

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Then, for some unitaries
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
,

|A+B|_{\mathrm{qsym}}\leq U|A|_{\mathrm{qsym}}U^{*}+V|B|_{\mathrm{qsym}}V^{*}.

This is a symmetrized version of the famous Thompson’s inequality
[
18
]
. Zhang’s result
was motivated by the following special case
[
9
]
:

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Then, for some unitaries
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
,

|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}\leq U|{\mathrm{Re\,}}Z|U^{*}+V|{\mathrm{Im\,}}Z|V^{*}.

Here (
2.5
) shows that we may replace the left-hand by
|Z|_{\mathrm{sym}}
.
Despite this fact Theorem
2.9
does not hold for the symmetric modulus
|\cdot|_{\mathrm{sym}}
. Two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{3}
are given in
[
21
]
with

\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(|A+B|_{\mathrm{sym}})=\sqrt{2}\left\{\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(|A|_{\mathrm{sym}})+\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(|B|_{\mathrm{sym}})\right\}.

Here the constant
\sqrt{2}
cannot be augmented; a result of
[
10
]
states a weak form of the triangle inequality as follows.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\{X_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Then,

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{m}X_{k}\right|_{\mathrm{sym}}\prec_{w}\sqrt{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left|X_{k}\right|_{\mathrm{sym}}.

We can use Theorem
2.1
to get a further comparison than (
2.5
).
The next result shows that the two symmetric moduli are equivalent up to the optimal constant

\sqrt{2}

in the unitary-orbit order.

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}|Z|_{\mathrm{sym}}V_{i}^{*}.

The constant
\sqrt{2}
cannot be diminished.

Proof.

To see that
\sqrt{2}
is optimal, it suffices to pick

Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

as

|Z|_{\mathrm{qsym}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{2}&0\\
0&\sqrt{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad|Z|_{\mathrm{sym}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

To establish the main conclusion, apply Theorem
2.4
with two matrices
A_{1}=|Z|^{2}
,
A_{2}=|Z^{*}|^{2}
, and the functions
f(t)=\sqrt{t}
and
g(t)=t
. This gives

\sqrt{|Z|^{2}+|Z^{*}|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}\left(|Z|+|Z^{*}|\right)V_{i}^{*}

and dividing both sides by
\sqrt{2}
yields the result.
∎

2.3
Supermajorization

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
. The supermajorization relation
A\prec^{w}B
means that
-A\prec_{w}-B
. In other words,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{\uparrow}(A)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{\uparrow}(B)

for all
k=1,\ldots,d
, where
\lambda_{k}^{\uparrow}(\cdot)
stand for the eigenvalues arranged in nondecreasing order. We then have the following form of Lemma
2.3
.

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
satisfy
A\prec^{w}B
and let
f(t)
be a nondecreasing concave function on
\Omega
. Then
there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

f(A)\geq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}f(B)V_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

Since
-A\prec_{w}-B
, we may apply Lemma
2.3
to
-A
,
-B
, and the nondecreasing convex function
-f(-t)
on
-\Omega
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

3
Spectral order

Until now we have dealt with the usual order
\leq
on the Hermitian part of
\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Another remarkable order is the spectral order
\preceq
, introduced by Olson in 1971
[
16
]
to endow the Hermitian operators on a Hilbert space with a lattice structure extending that of projections.

3.1
Basic properties

We confine ourselves to the setting of
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
and motivate the spectral order
\preceq
as an order on
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
such that
A\preceq B
is equivalent to the natural functional monotonicity property
(ii)
below. Olson proved the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(
i
)

\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A)\leq\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(B)
for all
x>0
;

(
ii
)

f(A)\leq f(B)
for all nondecreasing functions
f:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
.

(
iii
)

A^{n}\leq B^{n}
for all integers
n=1,2,\ldots
.

When these conditions hold,
A
is spectrally dominated by
B
, and we write
A\preceq B
.

Another interesting equivalent condition can be found in
[
13
]
.

The implication

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
is easy as we may assume that
f(t)=c_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\mathbf{1}_{(x_{i},\infty)}
for some positive numbers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
c_{0},\ldots,c_{n}
.
(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
is trivial.

We give a new, short proof of the nontrivial implication
(iii)\Rightarrow(i)
. Suppose
(iii)
holds and
B
is invertible (and so is
A
). Since
1/t
is operator decreasing, (
iii
) ensures

\frac{1}{A^{-1}+\cdots+A^{-n}}\leq\frac{1}{B^{-1}+\cdots+B^{-n}}.

Since
1/(t^{-1}+\cdots+t^{-n})
pointwise converges to
(t-1)_{+}:=\max\{0,t-1\}
as
n\to\infty
, we infer that

(A-1)_{+}\leq(B-1)_{+}.

Therefore, for all
0<q<1
,

(A-1)^{q}_{+}\leq(B-1)^{q}_{+},

and letting
q\searrow 0
we obtain
\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(A)\leq\mathbf{1}_{(1,\infty)}(B)
. Replacing
A
and
B
by
x^{-1}A
and
x^{-1}B
then gives the condition (
i
) for all
x\geq 0
when
A
and
B
are invertible. The general case follows as
A^{n}\leq B^{n}
entails
(A+I)^{n}\leq(B+I)^{n}
because
(x^{1/n}+1)^{n}
is operator monotone. Thus
(iii)
ensures
(i)
for
A+I
and
B+I
, which is equivalent to
(i)
for
A
and
B
since
\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A+I)=\mathbf{1}_{(x-1,\infty)}(A)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
be invertible. Condition
(iii)
in Proposition
3.1
shows that

A\preceq B\iff B^{-1}\preceq A^{-1},

and, in general,
A\preceq A+B
does not hold.

Remark 3.3
.

If
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
, then the family of projections
\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A)\vee\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(B)
is nonincreasing with respect to
x>0
. Hence there exists a unique
C\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
such that

\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(C)=\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A)\vee\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(B).

Condition
(i)
in Proposition
3.1
implies that
A\preceq C
and
B\preceq C
, while any
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
satisfying
A\preceq X
and
B\preceq X
must also satisfy
C\preceq X
.
Consequently,
C=:A\vee B
is the least upper bound of
A
and
B
. One defines in a similar way the greatest lower bound
D=:A\wedge B
with

\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(D)=\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A)\wedge\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(B).

Therefore
(M_{d}^{+},\preceq,\vee,\wedge)
is a lattice.

Example 3.4
.

Consider two matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{2}^{+}

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Then their supremum
A\vee B
is characterized by:

1) If
0<x\leq 1
, then

\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A\vee B)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\vee\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

2) If
1<x<2
, then

\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A\vee B)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\vee\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}.

3) If
2\leq x
, then
\mathbf{1}_{(x,\infty)}(A\vee B)=0
.

Therefore

A\vee B=1I+(2-1)\begin{pmatrix}1/2&1/2\\
1/2&1/2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3/2&1/2\\
1/2&3/2\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, in this example,

{\mathrm{Tr\,}}A\vee B={\mathrm{Tr\,}}(A+B)
,
this seems not surprizing as
A
and
B
are scalar multiples of two noncommuting rank one projections.

Question 3.5
.

In
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
, does the equivalence

{\mathrm{Tr\,}}A\vee B={\mathrm{Tr\,}}(A+B)\iff A\wedge B=0

hold ? Note that
A\wedge B=0
means that the ranges of
A
and
B
have their intersection reduced to
\{0\}
.

3.2
Sum and supremum

For two positive numbers,
a\vee b=\lim_{n\to\infty}(a^{n}+b^{n})^{1/n}
. In 1979, Kato
[
14
]
obtained the following matrix analogue.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. Then

A\vee B=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(A^{n}+B^{n}\right)^{1/n}.

An interesting generalization is
[
1
, Theorem 9]
. For convenience of the reader we prove Kato’s limit theorem.

Proof.

Let
m>n\geq 1
. Operator concavity of
t\mapsto t^{n/m}
ensures

\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\leq\left(\frac{A^{m}+B^{m}}{2}\right)^{n/m}.

As
t\mapsto t^{1/n}

is operator monotone,

\left(\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\right)^{1/n}\leq\left(\frac{A^{m}+B^{m}}{2}\right)^{1/m}.

Hence we have a bounded nondecreasing sequence with respect to the usual order in
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. Since
2^{1/n}\to 1
, its limit is
\lim_{n\to\infty}(A^{n}+B^{n})^{1/n}
. Fix an integer
k>0
. For
n>k
,

A^{k},B^{k}\leq(A^{n}+B^{n})^{k/n}
because
A^{n}\leq A^{n}+B^{n}
and
t^{k/n}
is operator monotone.
Thus

A^{k},B^{k}\leq\left(\lim_{n\to\infty}(A^{n}+B^{n})^{1/n}\right)^{k}

Hence
(iii)
in Proposition
3.1
implies

A,B\preceq\lim_{n\to\infty}(A^{n}+B^{n})^{1/n}.

On the other hand, suppose
A,B\preceq X
and fix
m\in\mathbb{N}
. By
(iii)
in Proposition
3.1
, for every integer
n
,

\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\leq X^{n}.

(3.1)

If further
n\geq m
,

\left(\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\right)^{m/n}\leq X^{m}

as
t^{m/n}
is operator monotone. Letting
n\to\infty
yields

\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\right)^{1/n}\right\}^{m}\leq X^{m}

for every integer
m
. So, again by this crucial condition
(iii)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\right)^{1/n}\preceq X.

Hence
A\vee B=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{A^{n}+B^{n}}{2}\right)^{1/n}
.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. Then there exist unitary matrices
\{V_{i}\}_{i=1}^{d}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

A\vee B\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}(A+B)V_{i}^{*}.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.4
with
g(t)=t
and
f(t)=t^{1/n}
,

(A^{n}+B^{n})^{1/n}\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}V_{i}(A+B)V_{i}^{*}

for some unitaries
V_{i}
.
Letting
n\to\infty
, Kato’s characterization of the supremum and the compactness of the unitary group complete the proof.
∎

To introduce a conjecture to be stated soon, we formulate the following equivalent form of Corollary
3.7
.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. Then, for all symmetric norms,

\|A\vee B\|\leq\|A+B\|,

(3.2)

equivalently,

A\vee B\prec_{w}A+B.

(3.3)

For sake of completness, we note that Ando
[
2
, Lemma 6.15]
observed the infimum counterpart of Kato’s theorem:

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
be invertible. Then

A\wedge B=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(A^{-n}+B^{-n}\right)^{-1/n}.

This is an immediate consequence of Kato’s theorem combined with the fact that the inverse reverses the spectral order. For positive definite matrices,

Y\preceq X\preceq A,B\iff A^{-1},B^{-1}\preceq X^{-1}\preceq Y^{-1}

Consequently
A\wedge B=(A^{-1}\vee B^{-1})^{-1}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(A^{-n}+B^{-n}\right)^{-1/n}
.

3.3
Comments

The submajorization relation (
3.3
) complements the following supermajorization inequality due to Ando
[
2
, Theorem 6.16]
:

A\vee B\prec^{w}A^{\downarrow}\vee B^{\downarrow}.

On the other hand, the supermajorization

A\vee B\prec^{w}A+B
fails in general, already when
A=B
.

It would be highly desirable to extend this results to operators on infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces, possibly unbounded. Readers familiar with noncommutative integration theory in semifinite von Neumann algebras may find the following conjecture plausible.

Conjecture 3.10
.

The submajorization (
3.3
) holds in the positive cone
\mathcal{P}
of the space of all
\tau
-measurable operators
affiliated with a semifinite von Neumann algebra endowed with a faithful normal semifinite trace
\tau
.
Thus (
3.2
) holds for any norm respecting the submajorization in
\mathcal{P}
.

Coming back to the finite dimensional setting, we define for
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
its maximal symmetric modulus and minimal symmetric modulus as, respectively,

|Z|_{\vee}:=|Z|\vee|Z^{*}|,\quad|Z|_{\wedge}:=|Z|\wedge|Z^{*}|.

A few questions then occur.

Question 3.11
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
. Does there exist a constant
c_{\vee}(d)
, depending only on
d
, such that

|A+B|_{\vee}\leq c_{\vee}(d)\left(U|A|_{\vee}U^{*}+V|B|_{\vee}V^{*}\right)

for some unitary matrices
U,V
? Can we choose
c_{\vee}(d)
independent of
d
?
Can we take
c_{\vee}(d)=1
? The latter holds when
A
and
B
are commuting positive matrices.

Conjecture 3.12
.

For every dimension
d
, one may choose
c_{\vee}(d)=1
.

If one replaces the maximal modulus
|\cdot|_{\vee}
by the minimal one
|\cdot|_{\wedge}
, the answer to the corresponding questions is negative. Letting

A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=B^{*}

we have
|A+B|_{\wedge}=I
while
|A|_{\wedge}=|B|_{\wedge}=0
.

We recall a related, strong conjecture proposed by Zhang
[
21
]
:

Conjecture 3.13
.

If
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
, then there exist two unitaries
U,V\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

|A+B|_{\mathrm{qsym}}\leq\sqrt{2}\left(U|A|_{\mathrm{qsym}}U^{*}+V|B|_{\mathrm{qsym}}V^{*}\right)

4
Orthogonal orbits

An orthogonal matrix is a real unitary matrix. To extend our results to real matrices and orthogonal orbits, we first need the following simple observation.
For a positive integer
d
, we denote by
\omega(d)
the smallest dyadic number (
\omega(d)=2^{m}
for some integer
m
) such that
d\leq\omega(d)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

There exist orthogonal, diagonal matrices
\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\Delta(X)=\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}R_{i}XR_{i}^{*}

for every matrix
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
.

Since the matrices
R_{k}

are diagonal with entries
\pm 1
, we have
R_{k}^{*}=R_{k}
. For the proof, it is convenient to denote by
I_{n}
the identity of size
n
.

Proof.

Let
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{2^{m}}
, for an integer
m\geq 1
, be a real matrix partitioned as

Z=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&K\\
L&Z_{2}\end{bmatrix}

where
Z_{1},Z_{2}\in\mathbb{M}_{2^{m-1}}
. Then

\begin{bmatrix}Z_{1}&0\\
0&Z_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\left\{I_{2^{m}}ZI_{2^{m}}+\begin{bmatrix}I_{2^{m-1}}&0\\
0&-I_{2^{m-1}}\end{bmatrix}Z\begin{bmatrix}I_{2^{m-1}}&0\\
0&-I_{2^{m-1}}\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

This proves the lemma when
m=1
. An induction on
m
then yields the result for every dyadic dimension
d=\omega(d)
.

Now, let
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a real matrix with
d<\omega(d)
, and define
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{\omega(d)}
as

Z=\begin{bmatrix}X&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

where the right lower
0
stands for the zero matrix in
\mathbb{M}_{\omega(d)-d}
. By the first step of the proof, the diagonal
\Delta(Z)
of
Z
can be expressed as

\Delta\left(\begin{bmatrix}X&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{k=1}^{\omega(d)}D_{k}\begin{bmatrix}X&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}D_{k}^{*}

for some orthogonal, diagonal matrices
D_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{\omega(d)}
. Let
R_{k}\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
be the diagonal, orthogonal matrix extracted from
D_{k}
by taking its
d\times d
left upper corner. The above equation yields

\Delta(X)=\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{k=1}^{\omega(d)}R_{k}XR_{k}^{*}

and the proof is complete.
∎

Replacing Bhatia’s identity in Section 2 by Lemma
4.1
, we obtain real analogues of our results, with orthogonal orbits replacing unitary ones. For instance, the real version of Lemma
2.3
reads as:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
and let
g(t)
be a nondecreasing convex function on
\Omega
. If
A
and
B
are real and
A\prec_{w}B
, then
there exist ortogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

g(A)\leq\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}U_{i}g(B)U_{i}^{*}.

If
g(t)
is convex nondecreasing,
f(t)=-g(-t)
is concave nondecreasing. Therefore, applying the real version of Lemma
2.13
together with the equivalence
A\prec B\iff-B\prec-A
, we have the following full-majorization counterpart of Lemma
4.2
:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
and let
g(t)
be a monotone convex function on
\Omega
. If
A
and
B
are real and
A\prec B
, then
there exist orthogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

g(A)\leq\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}U_{i}g(B)U_{i}^{*}.

The following corollary follows either from Lemma
4.3
or directly from Lemma
4.2
, since
A
and
B
have the same trace.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{M}_{d}(\Omega)
. If
A
and
B
are real and
A\prec B
, then
there exist orthogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

A=\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}.

The next corollary is a special case:

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{M}_{d}\to\mathbb{M}_{d}
be a positive linear map, unital and trace preserving. If
S\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
is real symmetric, then there exist orthogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\Phi(S)=\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}U_{i}SU_{i}^{*}.

Indeed, it is well known that
\Phi(S)\prec S
. We may assume
S\geq 0
and
the majorization follows readily from the standard variational formula, for
Z\in\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
and
1\leq k\leq d
,

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}^{\downarrow}(Z)=\min\left\{\mathrm{Tr\,}A+k\lambda_{1}^{\downarrow}(B)\right\}

where the minmum is taken over all decompositions
Z=A+B
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. See for instance
[
12
, Theorem 6.20]
. Another proof of
\Phi(S)\prec S
is
[
2
, Theorem 7.1]
.

We now reformulate, in the real setting, Corollary
3.7
. We state its multivariable version.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{m}
be real matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}^{+}
. Then there exist orthogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
such that

\bigvee_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\leq\frac{1}{\omega(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\omega(d)}U_{i}\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\right\}U_{i}^{*}.

The following remark
summarizes the history of the connection between majorization and unitary orbits.

Remark 4.7
.

It has long been known that, for Hermitian (real) matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
, the majorization
A\prec B
is equivalent to the fact that
A
belongs to the convex
hull of the unitary (orthogonal) orbit of
B
,

A=\sum_{i=1}^{N}t_{i}U_{i}BU_{i}^{*}

where the coefficients
t_{j}>0
satisfy
\sum_{j}t_{j}=1
.
See for instance
[
2
, Theorem 7.1]
, where we see that we can take
N=d!
+1, as
d!
is the cardinal of permutation matrices in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
. In fact one may assume that
A
and
B
are diagonal, and Carathéodory’s theorem then shows that we can reduce
N=d+1
. Somewhat surprisingly, this observation seems not to have appeared before Zhan’s 2003 note
[
19
]
. We have seen that we can actually take
N=d
, in case of a unitary orbit, and further obtain an average in the unitary orbit; i.e., equality of all
t_{j}
. For averages over orthogonal orbits, we have shown that one may take
N=\omega(d)
. The final questions below ask whether this bound is optimal.

Question 4.8
.

What is the smallest integer
\varphi(d)
such that there exist orthogonal matrices
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\varphi(d)}
in
\mathbb{M}_{d}
satisfying

\Delta(X)=\frac{1}{\varphi(d)}\sum_{i=1}^{\varphi(d)}U_{i}XU_{i}^{*}.

for every real matrix
X\in\mathbb{M}_{d}
?
Is it
\varphi(d)=d
or
\varphi(d)=\omega(d)
?

Question 4.9
.

The same as Question
4.8
, but with orthogonal matrices
U_{i}
that may depend on
X.
