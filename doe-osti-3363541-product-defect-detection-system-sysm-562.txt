Introduction
Job to Be Done
 Retail sales is a growing market estimated to up to seven percent year over year. [1] With
this growing market there is also a trend in growing rate of retail returns, estimated just last year at
$850 billion. [2] Retail stores must ensure that products available for purchase remain safe,
undamaged, and acceptable to customers throughout their time in the store. This job exists
regardless of the specific solution used because stores are always responsible for preventing
damaged or defective products from reaching customers and when they fail to this is categorized
under operation inefficiencies which accounts for an estimated $12 billion in returns.[3] When
defective items remain on the sales floor, stores may experience increased returns, reduced
customer satisfaction, loss of customer trust, and potential safety concerns depending on the
product type. As a result, the core job to be done is to identify defective products quickly, remove
them from the sales floor before they are purchased, and preserve useful information about the
defect so that the store can improve its handling, stocking, and supplier coordination over time.

 Figure 1: Retail Market Growth and Retail Return Statistics [1][2]

 The need for a more reliable process is especially important in high volume retail
environments where employees manage large numbers of products across many aisles, shelves,
and storage areas. In these settings, manual inspection alone can be inconsistent and difficult to
sustain at the individual item level. At the same time, broader retail trends continue to emphasize
operational efficiency, product visibility, and improved customer experience, creating an
opportunity for more automated and data driven defect detection methods.

Current Situation
Current Systems and Operations
 In many retail environments, defective products are still identified primarily through manual
observation by store employees, customer complaints, routine shelf checks, and product returns

 5

are purchase. Employees may notice visible damage such as dents, leaks, broken packaging, etc.
while stocking shelves or assisting customers, but this process depends heavily on time, attention,
and consistency. Customers may also discover damaged products only after purchase resulting in
returns or complaints that reveal the issue too late to prevent the original sale. [4]

 Figure 2: Reason for Returns [4]

 The current systems used in stores generally focus on inventory quantity, product
movement, and transactions rather than item condition. For example, barcode systems, inventory
management systems, Point of Sale (POS) software systems, are useful for tracking stock levels,
product IDs, and purchases, but they do not typically evaluate whether an individual item has
visible packaging damage or signs of product deterioration. General incident reporting tools may
allow stores to document customer complaints or damaged goods, but these tools are often
reactive and do not provide a continuous or systematic way to detect defects before purchase. As a
result, current practice is more reactive than proactive and does not provide consistent traceability
from the defective item back to a shipment, supplier, or handling process.

Deficiencies/Opportunities
 The current approach creates several deficiencies in store operations. The first being that
defect detection is inconsistent because it relies heavily on employee attention and customer
reporting rather than continuous monitoring. This increases the chance that damaged items remain
available for sale, especially in busy store environments with high product volume. The second
noticeable deficiency is that existing store systems generally do not capture structured item
condition data, making it difficult to analyze defect patterns, identify recurring handling issues, or
trace problems back to specific lots, suppliers, or shipments. A third deficiency is also prevalent
which is that many defects are discovered only after a purchase or complaint which can increase
return related cost, create avoidable customer dissatisfaction, and reduce trust in the store’s
quality practices.

 These deficiencies create a clear opportunity for a more systematic and data driven
solution, as mentioned previously. A future system could improve consistency in identifying

stronger traceability data for store personnel, managers, suppliers, and other stakeholders.

Stakeholder Analysis
Stakeholder Identification and Analysis
 To determine a better system for tracking defective items and preventing them from being
sold, the stakeholders of the system must be identified. There are two different stakeholders, active
stakeholders who will be interacting with the system directly, and passive stakeholders who will still
impact the system but not directly.

Active Stakeholders
 In this situation the active stakeholders who will be participating in the tracking system are
the general store employees, the store managers, corporate quality teams, and suppliers and
manufacturers found in table 1 below. The general store employees will be interacting with the
system daily to remove identified damaged items off the store floor. Store managers will also be
interacting with the system to determine losses and statistics about the product quality of the store
that they will potentially have to report to either higher up if it is a chain or to manufacturers if there
is consistent product damage or large product damage in shipment. In the case of a store being a
part of a larger chain, corporate quality teams would also be accessing the system to review larger
trends in damaged product data to look for ways to better improve the health of the company. The
maintainers of the system will be taking care of all the system’s data tracking equipment and
ensuring that any system patches or updates are made to ensure accuracy.

Acceptance Criteria
 Based on interviews and feedback from the stakeholders including formalizing the
stakeholders’ requirements, the key acceptance criteria of the system were determined as the
following below:

 1. The system shall correctly identify at least 98% of truly defective items.

 2. The system shall make defect flags visible to store employees within 5 minutes of detection.

 3. Store employees shall be able to locate/act on a defect flag under 2 minutes on average.

 4. The system shall retain defect records/associated actions for at least 12 months.

 5. The system shall reduce returned products due to defects to under 10%.

 With this acceptance criteria highlighted, a successful system will be easily identified by
 referencing each throughout the system’s development and lifespan.

Concept for the Proposed System
Concept Generation
 Based on the previously mentioned acceptance criteria as well as stakeholder needs, there
are three potential system concepts.

Desing Concept 1: Digital Image Processing System
 The first concept is a system that uses digital image processing. In this system, you could
utilize the cameras already in place in stores for security purposes as well as including additional
cameras on the shelves, at checkout, and in the back for inspection stations for product that is
being received from the manufacturer and going through processing. Then images will be taken and
analyzed using AI/computer vision and algorithm that will be referencing the product’s expected
images which would be based on manufacturer or brand product pictures as well as baseline
images that would be collected and input from the developers before system launch. The images
will be analyzed for leaks, tears, dents, discoloration, etc. Defective items will be flagged and
logged in the defect database with the time, location, product ID, and defect type.

Design Concept 2: Sensor Suite Tag
 The second concept is an on-product sensor suite. The sensor suite would consist of
environmental sensors including temperature, humidity, shock, capacitive touch, and tilt sensors
along with an optional light exposure sensor and a contact/tamper switch. This suite would be in it’s
own packaging and relevant products would be tagged (high value, sensitive, new, or highly
reported products) by staff. The tag would periodically log the data and send it wirelessly to a
nearby reader. If the set thresholds of the sensors are exceeded the tag would raise a flag or change
its internal state to “inspect”. The store system would reflect this on the specific ID and location,
and staff would be notified to inspect the tagged item and determine whether or not it needed to be
removed from the shelf.

 Figure 4: Concept 2 Sensor Suite [5]

Design Concept 3: Smart Shelf Monitoring
 The third concept is a smart shelf monitoring system. This system is different from concept
two since the sensors would be internal to the shelves that products sit on rather than the products
themselves. These shelves/racks would include weight sensors or load cells under each of the
sections and a RFID reader to detect which items are present. The system would monitor
unexpected changes in weight, which could be due to leaks, broken contents, or missing
components, as well as presence of specific items or batches on the shelf. These anomalies are
logged as potential defects and an alert would be created for an employee to inspect.

Concept Selection
 Now to determine which concept is the best overall to move forward with, a PUGH matrix is
needed to weigh the different needs that are linked back to the stakeholder requirements as well as
the acceptance criteria. For the elements of the Pugh Matrix, the most important criteria were
selected to weigh against each of the three concepts, as seen in Table 6 below. Based on this
criteria Concept #1 for the digital image processing system was chosen as the primary design
concept moving forward. The digital image processing system is the strongest overall solution
because it provides high detection accuracy, good scalability, manageable maintenance, and
strong traceability without requiring modification to individual products. The sensor suite offers
potentially higher detection accuracy, but it performs poorly in cost, integration burden, and
scalability because it requires hardware at the product level. The smart shelf is operationally usable
and scalable, but it is less accurate for direct defect detection and still requires significant
infrastructure integration.

“To be” Operational Scenarios
 The “To Be” operational scenarios describe how the proposed Product Defect Detection
System, using the selected digital image processing system concept, is expected to operate in a
future retail environment. These scenarios are intended to show how the system will support the
store’s overall job to be done by identifying defective products quickly, removing them before
purchase, and preserving useful defect data for reporting and process improvement. This is unlike
the current state which depends heavily on manual observation and inconsistent reporting. This
future system introduces automated image-based monitoring, timely employee alerts, and
structured data capture.

 17

Scenario 1: Defective Item Detected and Removed from Shelf
 In the store a customer accidentally drops a bottled product on the floor, picks it back up,
and puts it back on the shelf. Although not evident immediately, the drop cause a small leak and
dent in the product that is not visible from a distance. An overhead shelf camera and aisle camera
capture the updated images of the product during routine scanning. The digital image processing
System compares the newly scanned image and the expected product appeared stored in its
reference database and detects a likely defect based on the leak pattern and packaging
deformation.

 Within 5 minutes the system generates a defect flag and sends an alert to the nearest store
employee’s handheld device. The alert includes the product type, aisle location, shelf position,
time detected, and suspected defect type. The employee goes to the location, visually confirms the
damage, removes the item from the shelf, and records the action taken I the system. The defect
record is stored with the product ID and location data for future analysis.

 This scenario shows the core job to be done which is identifying defective items quickly
enough that they can be removed before a customer purchases them. It highlights the role of the
general store employee and shows how the system improves speed, consistency, and traceability
over manual inspection alone.

Scenario 2: Store Manager Reviews Trends and Generates a Defect Report
 Over the course of several days, the system detects an unusually high number of dented
cans and crushed packaging for the same product category in one store. Each detection is logged
automatically with image evidence, item identifier, time stamp, shelf location, and defect
classification. The store manager opens the dashboard and views the defect summary by product
category and store area.

 The system shows that the majority of these defects are linked to a recent shipment from
the same supplier lot. The manager exports a standard defect report within ten minutes and uses
the report to notify corporate quality personnel and the supplier about a possible transportation or
packaging problem. The manager is able to utilize the images stored in the system’s database to
corroborate the findings in the report rather than only relying on employee observations of the
products.

 This scenario reflects the manager’s job to be done which is understanding the defect
activity at a high level, tracing issues back to likely causes, and communication action items and
information to higher-level stakeholders. This demonstrates how the system supports proactive
quality management rather than only reacting to customer returns.

Scenario 3: System Maintainer Updates Product Reference Data After Package Chang
 A manufacturer introduces a packaging redesign for a popular product. This redesign
includes changing the label color and slightly altering the shape of the outer container. After this
rollout, the digital image processing system begins generating a higher number of possible defect
alerts than what is normally expected for that product because the newly taken image scans in the
sore no longer closely match the stored baseline images.

 A system maintainer receives an alert through the system health dashboard indicated an
increase in false-positive detections for the product line. The maintainer accesses the
administrative tools, reviews the recent detection logs and images, confirms that the issue is due to
the packaging update rather than actual damage, and uploads the new approved reference images
and model parameters for that product. After validation testing this updates is then released to the
store system with minimal downtime. Following the update, the system resumes normal operation
and correctly distinguishes the new packaging appearance from actual defects.

 This scenario is a support and maintenance scenario because it shows how the system is
sustained over time, how the system maintainers preserve detection accuracy, and how the system
can adapt to real retail changes with requiring major hardware changes.

Use Case Specification
Use Case Specification 1: Identifies Product Defects in Store
 The sequence of interactions involved when the Product Defect Detection System identifies
a product as defect is shown in figure 8 below. The process begins when a camera captures an
image of a product located on the store floor or at an inspection point during routine scans. That
image is then sent to the Product Defect Detection System where the system compares the
observed product appearance against the stored reference images and defect detection criteria
embedded in the models. If the image analysis indicates visible damage, such as a tear, dent, leak,
etc., the system classifies the item as potentially defected and creates a defect record containing
the product identifier, time stamp, location, and suspected defect type. The system then sends a
high-level defect alert to a general store employee, as well as the store manager for reference. The
general store employee will then request access to the detailed product information, this does not
require the system to determine access request since only employees have access to the system

 SYSM – 5620 Final Project 20

and this data is available to all access levels. The system will then redirect them to the product
information and provide the specific product ID and location. The general store employee will then
locate and inspect the item. After confirming the condition of the product, the employee would
remove the defective item from the store floor, or if not defective proceed to the status input step,
and record the defect action taken and what the status of the product is. The defect log would then
be updated in the system and the store manager can then request the product status and the
system would provide back the defect summary on the item. This sequence shows how the system
supports rapid defect identification, timely employee response, accurate record keeping, and high
level visibility among stakeholders to help prevent defective products from being purchased while
also improving traceability.

 A quality function deployment analysis was performed to understand the relationship

digital image processing system, this analysis is shown in the figure 11 below. The WHAT rows were

strong positive correlations ranked highest, followed by medium and weak positive correlations.

strongest design emphasis is placed on secure control access, broad support across store devices,
sufficient storage for records and image data, effective visual coverage of products, and a strong
reference image base for defect comparisons. Lower ranked objects, like image processing latency,
still contribute to system performance but they had less overall influence in the weighted QFD

Reflection
 Beginning the report with the background and problem context was one of the more
challenging parts of the project for me. I tend to view that portion of the paper and process as more
abstract and open ended because it requires establishing the broader need for the system while
also supporting those claims with outside sources whereas I like to focus on the details of the
solutions. I found it less straightforward than the later sections, which to me were more concrete
and structured. Even so I worked through that portion as thoroughly as I could and then moved into
the sections of the project where the system itself began to take shape more clearly.

 One area I particularly struggled with was the stakeholder analysis section. As I was
attempting this the later concepts that I would be addressing were weighing on my mind and began
to feel more like a system of systems because each potential solution relied not only on sensing
hardware, but also software, data processing, and communication function. Due to this nature of
the solutions, many of the requirements I initially identified felt more software oriented than
focused on the sensing differences that would distinguish the future concepts. At that point I
decided to pivot temporarily to concept generation so I could better understand what each
proposed system was actually trying to accomplish. That shift helped me clarify the scope of the
problem and return to the stakeholder analysis with a stronger sense of direction. In that way my
process was not purely linear, but iterative since later design thinking informed and improved
earlier project elements.

 For the remainder of the paper I generally followed the sequence laid out in the rubric and in
class, but I frequently revisited earlier sections to refine them as the system matured. This project
helped me see how the systems engineering process works from beginning to end in a much more
complete way. While the in class exercises and team SRR project were very helpful for building
foundational understanding, working independently through each stage of the process allow me to
engage more deeply with the decisions being made. At the same time I did miss the back and forth
discussion and critique that comes with group work, especially when trying to evaluate whether I
was making the best and most understandable choices and assumptions. Early on I found it
difficult to make decisions without that feedback loop, but as the project progressed I became
more confident in weighing alternatives and moving forward without over focusing on small details
for too long.
