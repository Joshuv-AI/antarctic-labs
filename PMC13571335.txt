Robotic-Assisted Navigational Bronchoscopy Complicated by Systemic Air Embolism
Authors: Paul K. Jain, Abdallatif Dawoud, Mohammad Safi, Madison Leffler, Jacob Gerken, Mohammed Ali, Jalil Qari, Robert Grande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRobotic-assisted bronchoscopy (RAB) is a minimally invasive modality with a highly favorable safety profile. Systemic air embolism (SAE) is an exceedingly rare and catastrophic complication that is only recently emerging in literature surrounding RAB.Case SummaryA 74-year-old woman undergoing RAB developed an immediate ST-elevation myocardial infarction, airway bleeding, and cardiac arrest. Angiography revealed nonobstructive coronary arteries but identified a massive air embolism in the left ventricular apex. Her hospital course was complicated by severe cerebral edema, seizures, and acute limb ischemia.DiscussionWe highlight a catastrophic instance of SAE resulting from RAB. We discuss its pathophysiology, imaging limitations, and the severe contraindications precluding hyperbaric oxygen therapy in patients with underlying structural lung disease.Take-Home MessagesSAE is a life-threatening RAB complication that can mimic acute coronary syndrome. Minimally invasive procedures carry fatal risks, especially in patients lacking the physiologic reserve to tolerate vital rescue treatments.

History of Presentation

A 74-year-old woman presented for outpatient robotic-assisted navigational bronchoscopy with radial endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) to biopsy bilateral pulmonary nodules concerning for recurrent adenocarcinoma. Baseline hemodynamics were stable: blood pressure 127/84 mm Hg, heart rate 79 beats/min, SpO2 94% on room air. Height was 147 cm, weight was 44 kg, and body mass index was 20.3. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated moderate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (forced expiratory volume in 1 second 62% predicted). Her outpatient regimen included 200 μg fluticasone-62.5 μg umeclidinium-25 μg vilanterol/actuation daily; azithromycin 250 mg, 3 x/wk; albuterol HFA 90 μg/actuation, 2 puffs every 6 hours as needed; albuterol nebulizer 2.5 mg every 4 hours as needed; atorvastatin 80 mg nightly; pantoprazole 40 mg daily; cetirizine 5 mg daily; ferrous sulfate 1 tablet daily; and aspirin 81 mg daily. She took no systemic anticoagulants or oral corticosteroids; daily 81 mg aspirin was held 7 days prior.Take-Home Messages•Systemic air embolism is a life-threatening bronchoscopy complication mimicking acute coronary syndrome.•Clinicians must recognize that minimally invasive procedures carry catastrophic risks in patients lacking physiologic reserve.

An interventional pulmonologist with 10 years of institutional experience performed the procedure. General anesthesia involved intravenous propofol, fentanyl, and rocuronium. Inhaled sevoflurane and endotracheal intubation were utilized. The robotic system was attached directly to the endotracheal tube. Mechanical ventilation was maintained at 10 breaths/minute with tidal volumes of 250 to 350 mL (6-8 mL/kg). To minimize barotrauma and pneumothorax risk given underlying COPD, peak inspiratory pressures were maintained at 17 to 22 cm H2O with an initial positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 10 cm H2O. To mitigate vascular injury and bleeding, EBUS confirmed target localization and intraprocedural mobile computed tomography (CT) with fluoroscopy continuously verified needle placement before all biopsies.

The left upper lobe nodule was navigated first. Following EBUS confirmation, mobile CT and fluoroscopy verified needle placement for 3 passes and 8 forceps biopsies. The catheter was then navigated to the right upper lobe anterior segment nodule. EBUS obtained a direct signal; mobile CT confirmed placement for 3 needle passes and 8 forceps biopsies. Touch preparations appeared bloody, and bilateral bronchoalveolar lavage yielded blood-tinged return. The procedure was paused, the robotic system removed, and flexible bronchoscopy with cold saline lavage successfully achieved hemostasis without visible active bleeding.

Immediately thereafter, telemetry revealed acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) followed by severe bradycardia with a rate of 30 to 40. Intravenous atropine (1 mg) was administered. She rapidly lost palpable pulses, transitioning to pulseless electrical activity (PEA). Resuscitative efforts were initiated with 1 mg of intravenous epinephrine; then cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was initiated. She received another dose of intravenous epinephrine at 3 minutes before achieving return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). These efforts lasted approximately 3 to 4 minutes. During the arrest, resuscitative physiological maneuvers included increasing PEEP to 10 cm H2O and escalating the fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) from baseline (25% to 30%) to 100%. Postarrest, she required norepinephrine and phenylephrine infusions for mixed shock and was emergently transferred for cardiac catheterization.

Past Medical History

Recurrent stage IA adenocarcinoma of the right lung (previously treated with stereotactic body radiation therapy), COPD, pulmonary hypertension, tobacco use disorder, and gastroesophageal reflux disease.

Management and Clinical Course

Rationale for withholding antithrombotics

Systemic anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapies for STEMI and atrial fibrillation were withheld. Angiography confirmed nonobstructive coronary arteries, indicating the STEMI was driven by a nonatherothrombotic mechanism (coronary air embolism). Given this mechanism, and the high bleeding risk from 6 transbronchial needle passes and 16 forceps biopsies requiring cold saline lavage, the multidisciplinary team determined the procedural bleeding risks far outweighed any cardiovascular benefits.

Deferment of catheter aspiration and surgical de-airing

On identification of the left ventricular air embolism, mechanical catheter aspiration and open surgical de-airing were actively considered but deferred. Interventional and cardiothoracic teams concluded manipulating a left ventricular catheter carried a prohibitive risk of fragmenting the air mass and precipitating further embolic showers. Furthermore, given her recent cardiac arrest, profound vasopressor-dependent mixed cardiogenic and distributive shock (blood pressure 69/48 mm Hg, heart rate 85-138 beats/min requiring norepinephrine, phenylephrine, vasopressin), severe mixed acidosis (pH 7.22), low body weight, and frailty, she was deemed a prohibitive operative risk for median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, or direct percutaneous left ventricular puncture.

Contraindications to hyperbaric oxygen therapy

Consequently, conservative management was prioritized. She was placed in the Trendelenburg position to trap apical ventricular air and maintained on mechanical ventilation (100% FiO2, PEEP 8 cm H2O) to promote nitrogen washout. While considered, hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) was strictly contraindicated due to her underlying COPD, emphysematous changes, and fresh parenchymal lung biopsy wounds.

Ongoing critical care and outcome

Seizures and epileptiform activity were managed with lorazepam, levetiracetam, and propofol. The acute left lower limb ischemia was managed conservatively (Rooke boot) to allow tissue demarcation. On day 2, she developed supraventricular tachycardia progressing to PEA arrest, requiring an additional CPR cycle for ROSC. Following the day 5 MRI showing irreversible hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, multidisciplinary goals-of-care discussions were held. On hospital day 6, the patient was compassionately extubated and transitioned to comfort measures only.

Discussion

RAB with EBUS has advanced the diagnosis of peripheral pulmonary lesions. Major trials like A Prospective, Multicenter Evaluation of Safety and Diagnostic Outcomes With Robotic-Assisted BronchoscopyResults of the Transbronchial Biopsy Assisted by Robot Guidance in the Evaluation of Tumors of the Lung (TARGET) Trial, Shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy for pulmonary nodules: initial multicenter experience using the Ion Endoluminal System, and meta-analyses show a robust safety profile with major complications (pneumothorax, bleeding) occurring in <5% of cases.1, 2, 3 However, exceedingly rare but catastrophic complications are emerging. Although systemic air embolism (SAE) is a known rare complication of traditional percutaneous biopsies and flexible bronchoscopy, it has only recently been documented with robotic platforms, including reports of cerebral embolization.4 This case highlights the risks inherent to minimally invasive procedures.

SAE pathophysiology during transbronchial procedures involves iatrogenic bronchovenous fistulas. When a biopsy tool traverses a pulmonary vein, a physical conduit forms. If alveolar pressure exceeds pulmonary venous pressure via exacerbation by coughing, Valsalva maneuvers, or positive pressure ventilation, then atmospheric air can enter the pulmonary vein and embolize systemically via the left heart.5,6 Intraprocedural bleeding suggested direct vascular injury resulting in coronary air embolism and STEMI.7 The delayed appearance of right ventricular air likely represents anterograde migration of venous gas initially identified in the innominate vein rather than a new connection.

The differential diagnosis for acute intraprocedural ST-segment elevation is broad. Although acute atherothrombotic plaque rupture must be immediately evaluated, emergent coronary angiography in our patient revealed nonobstructive arteries, ruling this out. The differential thus shifted toward nonatherothrombotic mechanisms. Severe hypoxemia and vasoplegic shock can precipitate demand ischemia; however, the immediate, focal inferior ST elevations observed along with the biopsy were highly suggestive of a discrete embolic event rather than global hypoperfusion. Coronary vasospasm was considered, but the direct temporal relationship to the pulmonary parenchymal trauma and subsequent vascular injury strongly supported coronary air embolism as the primary etiology of the infarction.

Although emergent coronary angiography and fluoroscopy raised high clinical suspicion for intracardiac air embolism, these 2-dimensional modalities cannot definitively differentiate endocardial gas from radiolucent air in overlying extracardiac space. Therefore, an intraprocedural complete TTE was immediately performed, which confirmed the presence of highly echogenic gas within the cardiac chambers. Subsequent contrast-enhanced chest CT provided further 3-dimensional spatial resolution, localizing the air embolism to the left ventricular apex.

Similarly, although cerebral air embolism is a potential mechanism for neurologic injury, the patient's deficits were likely driven by global hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Following cardiac arrest, she required continuous vasopressor support for mixed shock. Subsequent MRI findings showing diffuse restricted diffusion across the cerebral cortex without isolated territorial infarcts represent a classic pattern of severe global hypoperfusion and anoxia secondary to cardiopulmonary collapse rather than isolated embolic phenomena.

SAE management focuses on preventing end-organ ischemia and accelerating gas reabsorption. Initial measures include 100% FiO2 for nitrogen washout and Trendelenburg positioning.8 The gold-standard treatment is HBOT. HBOT reduces bubble volume and restores tissue perfusion while supersaturating plasma with oxygen.9 An absolute contraindication to HBOT is untreated pneumothorax; exposing a patient with or at high risk for a pneumothorax to severe pressure changes can precipitate a fatal tension pneumothorax.9,10 Given our patient's underlying COPD, emphysema, and fresh parenchymal biopsy wounds, her risk was prohibitive. Although prophylactic tube thoracostomies could mitigate this, the multidisciplinary heart team deemed procedural morbidity too high amidst rapid hemodynamic and neurologic decompensation. This underscores a clinical dilemma in which the definitive complication treatment is contraindicated by the precipitating procedure itself.

Conclusions

We report a fatal case of SAE complicated by STEMI, severe cerebral edema, and acute limb ischemia immediately following RAB. Management requires high suspicion, multidisciplinary coordination, and meticulous evaluation of salvage therapies, particularly in patients with severe structural lung disease.
