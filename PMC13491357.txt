Concurrent Jackstone Calculus and Prostate Cancer: Should They Be Treated Concurrently or Sequentially?
Authors: Shuo Tian, Huijuan Li, Yuliang Jiang, Cheng Wang, Shuze Liu, Fei Wu, Hao Ning
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJackstone calculus is a rare urinary stone morphology with a distinctive spiculated appearance, most often reported in the bladder and usually associated with urinary stasis or outlet obstruction. We report a 70-year-old man with 1 year of progressive dysuria and intermittent interruption of urinary stream despite tamsulosin and finasteride. Urinalysis showed pyuria, hematuria, and bacteriuria. Ultrasonography and pelvic computed tomography identified an irregular star-shaped bladder stone and marked prostatic enlargement, with an estimated prostate volume of approximately 125 mL on ultrasonography. Serum total prostate-specific antigen was 16.2 ng/mL, with a free-to-total ratio of 0.138. Systematic transrectal biopsy showed adenocarcinoma in 2 of 12 cores, Gleason score 3 + 3 = 6 (Grade Group 1). After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent laparoscopic radical prostatectomy with concomitant intact stone removal. The 3.1-cm stone showed classic jackstone morphology. Final pathology showed Gleason 3 + 4 = 7 adenocarcinoma (Grade Group 2), upgraded from biopsy, with negative surgical margins. Telephone follow-up at 1, 3, and 12 months showed sustained improvement of lower urinary tract symptoms without recurrent urinary tract infection-related symptoms. Postoperative imaging and prostate-specific antigen data were unavailable. This case is relevant to men’s health because a visually distinctive bladder stone may coexist with clinically relevant prostatic disease. In men with lower urinary tract symptoms and abnormal prostate-specific antigen values, bladder outlet obstruction, infection, or bladder stones should not preclude further oncologic evaluation. In selected patients in whom definitive prostate surgery is chosen, combined stone removal can be a practical single-stage approach.Keywords: jackstone calculus, bladder calculus, prostate cancer, bladder outlet obstruction, case report

Introduction

Jackstone calculus is an uncommon urinary stone morphology characterized by radiating spicules resembling the children’s toy jacks. Published clinical reports remain sparse. A recent scoping review identified only 15 eligible studies and found that jackstone calculi are reported predominantly in the bladder, with fewer cases in the upper urinary tract (Wei et al., 2025). Representative modern reports describe vesical jackstone calculi far more often than renal pelvis or renal calyx lesions (Goonewardena et al., 2021; Lim et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Subasinghe et al., 2017; Symeonidis et al., 2022). Recognition of this morphology is clinically useful because it may suggest the diagnosis on imaging, including sonography (Perlmutter et al., 2002), and because bladder stones in adults commonly arise in the setting of an underlying abnormality, particularly bladder outlet obstruction or urinary stasis (Torricelli et al., 2013).

Recent structural studies suggest that jackstone calculus development is not explained by simple mineral accretion alone. Micro-computed tomography and histologic analysis have shown a protein-rich, radiolucent core within the projecting arms (Canela et al., 2022), and newer morphometric work suggests abrasion-mediated remodeling during distal arm growth (Mashiach et al., 2026). We report a case of concurrent jackstone bladder calculus and prostate cancer in a man with severe lower urinary tract symptoms.

Case Presentation

A 70-year-old man presented with progressive dysuria and frequent interruption of urinary flow during the previous year despite combination treatment with tamsulosin hydrochloride and finasteride. A formal International Prostate Symptom Score was not recorded; symptom severity was assessed clinically based on progressive dysuria and frequent interruption of urinary flow despite combination medical therapy. Digital rectal examination showed an enlarged, non-tender prostate with a rubbery consistency and loss of the palpable median furrow. A brief clinical timeline is shown in Table 1.

Clinical Timeline

Urinalysis and urine sediment analysis demonstrated pyuria, hematuria, and bacteriuria, with increased white blood cells, red blood cells, and bacteria. These findings were considered consistent with urinary tract infection in the setting of bladder stone disease and bladder outlet obstruction. Empirical levofloxacin was administered, after which the patient’s infection-related symptoms resolved. A subsequent urine culture performed after antibiotic treatment showed no bacterial growth; therefore, no specific causative organism was identified. Ultrasonography of the urinary system revealed a 2.9 × 3.4 cm hyperechoic lesion within the bladder with an irregular star-like contour and posterior acoustic shadowing. The prostate measured 6.7 × 6.1 × 5.9 cm, corresponding to an estimated volume of approximately 125 mL using the ellipsoid formula, and showed heterogeneous echogenicity. Pelvic computed tomography confirmed an irregular bladder calculus (Figure 1A). Serum total prostate-specific antigen was 16.2 ng/mL, and the free-to-total prostate-specific antigen ratio was 0.138. Because the degree of prostatic enlargement and the prostate-specific antigen abnormality raised concern for concomitant prostatic pathology, prostate biopsy was performed after treatment of the suspected urinary tract infection. The patient first received empirical levofloxacin, after which infection-related symptoms resolved, and a subsequent urine culture showed no bacterial growth. He then underwent transrectal ultrasound-guided systematic prostate biopsy according to local clinical practice. Two of 12 cores contained adenocarcinoma with a Gleason score of 3 + 3 = 6 (Grade Group 1).

Preoperative pelvic MRI showed marked prostatic enlargement centered on the central gland, with compression of the bladder, but did not show typical imaging features of prostate cancer or enlarged pelvic lymph nodes. Prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PSMA-PET/CT) was not performed. After multidisciplinary assessment incorporating the biopsy findings, prostate-specific antigen level, available imaging findings, severe lower urinary tract symptoms, marked prostatic enlargement, and the need for definitive bladder stone treatment, alternative management strategies were discussed with the patient, including sequential management of the bladder stone and prostate cancer, active surveillance for biopsy Grade Group 1 prostate cancer with separate treatment of the bladder stone, and one-stage radical prostatectomy with concomitant stone removal. After counseling regarding the potential benefits and limitations of concurrent versus sequential management, the patient chose one-stage surgery. He then underwent laparoscopic radical prostatectomy with concomitant intact removal of the bladder stone. No obvious gross intraoperative findings suggestive of chronic bladder inflammation or other relevant bladder abnormalities were observed. The extracted stone measured 3.1 cm in maximal diameter and showed the classic spiculated morphology of a jackstone calculus (Figure 1B). Final histopathological examination of the prostatectomy specimen showed prostate adenocarcinoma with a Gleason score of 3 + 4 = 7 (Grade Group 2), indicating upgrading compared with the biopsy result (Figure 1C and D). The surgical margins were negative. At 1-, 3-, and 12-month telephone follow-up, the patient remained alive, reported good general condition and satisfaction with the surgical outcome, and had sustained improvement of lower urinary tract symptoms. He reported no recurrence of interruption of urinary stream, dysuria, voiding difficulty, or lower abdominal discomfort. He also reported no urinary frequency, urgency, urinary pain, fever, or other symptoms suggestive of urinary tract infection. Because he remained asymptomatic, he did not undergo postoperative computed tomography or ultrasonographic evaluation. Postoperative prostate-specific antigen data were not available.

Discussion

Jackstone calculi are uncommon in humans, but their characteristic spiculated morphology can raise diagnostic suspicion before surgery. Published case reports and review-level evidence indicate that most reported human jackstone calculi occur in the urinary bladder, most often in men with lower urinary tract symptoms, urinary retention, recurrent infection, or other manifestations of urinary stasis or outlet obstruction; however, this morphology is not inherently sex-specific and could presumably occur in women when comparable conditions for urinary stasis are present (Subasinghe et al., 2017; Symeonidis et al., 2022; Wei et al., 2025). In addition, upper-tract cases involving the kidney, renal pelvis, and renal calyx have also been described, indicating that the morphology is distinctive but not anatomically restricted (Goonewardena et al., 2021; Lim et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023).

Existing literature suggests that bladder stones in adults commonly arise in the setting of an underlying abnormality, particularly bladder outlet obstruction or urinary stasis (Torricelli et al., 2013). In addition, prior reports of jackstone calculi have linked this morphology to chronic urinary retention, bladder diverticula, benign prostatic enlargement, or upper-tract obstruction, supporting the view that the characteristic appearance should prompt evaluation of the cause of urinary stasis rather than stone treatment alone (Anderson & Herring, 2022; Lim et al., 2022; Song et al., 2023; Symeonidis et al., 2022). In the present patient, severe lower urinary tract symptoms and marked prostatic enlargement, with an estimated prostate volume of approximately 125 mL, make chronic outlet obstruction the most plausible setting for stone formation. The obstructive symptoms were considered more likely related to marked prostatic enlargement and bladder stone-associated urinary stasis than to the prostate cancer itself. The patient’s pyuria and bacteriuria suggested urinary tract infection, which may have been promoted by urinary stasis related to bladder outlet obstruction and the bladder calculus. However, because the urine culture was performed after antibiotic treatment and showed no bacterial growth, the causative organism could not be identified, and chronic urinary tract infection could not be definitively established.

Beyond diagnostic recognition of a jackstone calculus, the main clinical issue in this case was how to sequence management of suspected urinary infection, bladder stone disease, marked prostatic enlargement with bladder outlet obstruction, and newly diagnosed prostate cancer. The elevated prostate-specific antigen value was interpreted cautiously because infection, inflammation, bladder stone disease, and outlet obstruction may complicate prostate-specific antigen assessment. Therefore, the bladder stone and bacteriuria could have contributed to concern regarding the apparent oncologic risk, but the decision-making was not based on prostate-specific antigen alone. Infection-related findings were treated first with empirical levofloxacin, after which the patient’s symptoms resolved and subsequent urine culture showed no bacterial growth. Prostate cancer was then confirmed by biopsy, and definitive treatment was considered in the context of severe medically refractory lower urinary tract symptoms, marked prostatic enlargement, the need for bladder stone treatment, feasibility of one-stage surgery, and patient counseling.

Management in this setting should be individualized. Reasonable alternatives may include infection control followed by staged bladder stone treatment and reassessment of prostate-specific antigen, active surveillance for selected patients with biopsy Grade Group 1 prostate cancer, or one-stage surgery when definitive prostate treatment and stone removal are both considered appropriate after shared decision-making. Symptomatic bladder stones generally warrant active treatment together with evaluation and treatment of the underlying cause, and systematic review evidence indicates that transurethral cystolithotripsy is appropriate in many routine adult cases (Donaldson et al., 2019; Torricelli et al., 2013). At the same time, contemporary prostate cancer guidance emphasizes individualized management, and active surveillance is commonly considered for many men with Grade Group 1 or otherwise favorable localized disease (Eastham et al., 2022). This case should therefore not be interpreted as a general endorsement of immediate radical treatment for limited-volume Grade Group 1 disease. Rather, in the present patient, severe medically refractory lower urinary tract symptoms, marked prostatic enlargement, the need for definitive bladder stone treatment, biopsy-detected prostate cancer, and the feasibility of one-stage surgery were considered together during multidisciplinary review and patient counseling. Final prostatectomy pathology showed upgrading to Gleason score 3 + 4 = 7 (Grade Group 2), with negative surgical margins. This postoperative finding provides additional oncologic context, although the decision for surgery was primarily based on the preoperative clinical scenario and shared decision-making. Prior literature indicates that concomitant cystolithotomy during radical prostatectomy is technically feasible in selected patients with coexisting bladder stones (Tan et al., 2012).

This report has limitations. Formal stone composition analysis was unavailable. PSMA-PET/CT was not performed; therefore, molecular imaging-based staging information was unavailable. In addition, because urine culture was performed after antibiotic treatment and showed no bacterial growth, the causative organism could not be identified and chronic urinary tract infection could not be definitively established. A formal International Prostate Symptom Score was also not recorded; therefore, symptom severity was described clinically rather than quantified by a validated symptom score. Postoperative follow-up was based on telephone interviews, and postoperative computed tomography, ultrasonography, and prostate-specific antigen data were unavailable. Therefore, radiographic stone recurrence and biochemical oncologic outcomes could not be assessed. Accordingly, this report should be interpreted primarily as a diagnostic and management teaching case rather than an outcome-based report. Nevertheless, the characteristic imaging and gross morphology strongly supported the diagnosis, and the case remains instructive because it emphasizes comprehensive assessment rather than isolated treatment of the bladder stone alone.

Conclusion

This case highlights that the characteristic morphology of a jackstone calculus should prompt evaluation for urinary stasis or outlet pathology, but should not narrow clinical reasoning to the bladder stone alone. In men with lower urinary tract symptoms and abnormal prostate findings, systematic prostate evaluation and individualized shared decision-making remain essential.
