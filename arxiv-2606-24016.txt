Extremal Spanning Trees in Product Grid Graphs
Jiechen Zhang

Abstract
We study how fixed-volume spanning-tree extremality changes when product-grid boundary factors are free, periodic, or mixed. In two dimensions, extremality depends sharply on the boundary type. The free/free and periodic/periodic products both obey a closest-to-square principle: among fixed-area rectangles, P r ​ □ ​ P s P_{r}\square P_{s} and C r ​ □ ​ C s C_{r}\square C_{s} are maximized by the closest-to-square admissible side lengths. The mixed free/periodic cylinder P r ​ □ ​ C s P_{r}\square C_{s} is different: closest-to-square fails, and in the divisor-rich case the optimizing cyclic circumference has scale N 1 / 3 N^{1/3} when the area is N = r ​ s N=rs . In arbitrary dimension we prove pairwise balancing theorems for pure free products and pure periodic products, and then strengthen them by a heat-trace Schur-concavity theorem in logarithmic side lengths. At perfect-power volume this gives the unique maximizers P n □ ​ d P_{n}^{\square d} and, for n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , C n □ ​ d C_{n}^{\square d} . These product-grid comparisons motivate perfect-power conjectures for connected induced lattice subgraphs and periodic analogues.



1
Introduction

Let
\tau(G)
denote the number of spanning trees of a finite connected graph
G
. Throughout, a path factor
P_{r}
is a free boundary factor, while a cycle factor
C_{r}
is a periodic boundary factor. Thus
P_{r}\square P_{s}
is the basic rectangular grid,
C_{r}\square C_{s}
is the torus, and
P_{r}\square C_{s}
is the cylinder. The central question is: among product grids of fixed volume, which side lengths maximize
\tau
, and how does the answer depend on the boundary factors?

The answer is boundary-sensitive even in two dimensions. The free/free case was settled quantitatively by the rectangular-grid balancing theorem of Zhang
[
17
]
: among equal-area rectangular grids, moving the side lengths toward a square gives a sharp linear lower bound for the logarithmic spanning-tree gain, with optimal constant
\arsinh(1)
, and the closest-to-square divisor rectangle uniquely maximizes
\tau
up to rotation. We study what survives under periodic and mixed boundary conditions, and extend the theory to higher-dimensional pure products.

For periodic/periodic products, square-balancing survives: fixed-area tori
C_{r}\square C_{s}
are also maximized by the closest-to-square admissible divisor pair. For mixed free/periodic products, it fails. At area
36
,
\tau(P_{9}\square C_{4})>\tau(P_{6}\square C_{6})
, and at area
120
,
\tau(P_{20}\square C_{6})>\tau(P_{12}\square C_{10})
. The cylinder is governed instead by a competition between a free-boundary penalty and a periodic finite-size correction, and the long-cylinder scale developed below is not
N^{1/2}
but
N^{1/3}
for the periodic circumference.

This gives a finite-size classification for the symmetric boundary cases and an exact/asymptotic theory for the mixed cylinder case. The sharp constants distinguish the boundary conditions. Rectangles have the best positive linear side-sum constant
\alpha=\arsinh(1)
. Tori balance strictly but have no positive universal linear side-sum constant: the best such constant is
0
. Cylinders have an exact free-boundary penalty
-\log\sinh(\alpha s)
in the cyclic circumference
s
; the same
\alpha
is sharp, but it competes with a periodic correction of order
N/s^{2}
rather than with another side length.

In higher dimensions the main balancing results concern the two pure products

P_{\mathbf{n}}=P_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square P_{n_{d}}\quad\text{and}\quad C_{\mathbf{n}}=C_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{n_{d}},

We prove that every nontrivial product-preserving balancing of two coordinates strictly increases the spanning-tree count. Thus fixed-volume maximizers are pairwise balanced. For general
N
and
d\geq 3
, terminal pairwise-balanced divisor tuples need not be unique, so this is a finite reduction rather than a universal closest-to-hypercube uniqueness theorem. This arithmetic obstruction can occur even at a perfect power: the tuple
(50,54,64,75)
has product
60^{4}
and is already pairwise balanced.
The heat-trace method gives a stronger global comparison. After extending the one-dimensional heat traces from integer side lengths to positive real side lengths, they become strictly log-convex in logarithmic side length. Karamata’s inequality then gives a Schur-concavity theorem for
\log\tau
in logarithmic side lengths: among pure path products, and among pure cycle products with all lengths at least
3
, a tuple whose logarithmic side lengths are more balanced has more spanning trees. The perfect-power theorem is the uniform special case.

Preliminaries

For
r\geq 1
define
\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)=2-2\cos(\pi i/r)
for
0\leq i\leq r-1
, and for
r\geq 3
define
\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)=2-2\cos(2\pi i/r)
for
0\leq i\leq r-1
. These are the Laplacian spectra of
P_{r}
and
C_{r}
, respectively. The Laplacian spectrum of a Cartesian product is the multiset of all sums of eigenvalues from the factors. Kirchhoff’s Matrix–Tree Theorem
[
7
]
gives

|V(G)|\,\tau(G)=\prod_{\lambda\in\Spec^{+}(G)}\lambda,

where
\Spec^{+}(G)
denotes the positive Laplacian eigenvalues counted with multiplicity.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
r\geq 1
,
\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)=r
, with the empty product convention when
r=1
. For
r\geq 3
,
\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)=r^{2}
.

Proof.

The path identity follows from
\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}2\sin(\pi i/(2r))=\sqrt{r}
, and the cycle identity from
\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}2\sin(\pi i/r)=r
. Since the path and cycle Laplacian eigenvalues are the squares of the corresponding sine factors, these identities give the displayed products.
∎

We shall use two one-dimensional characteristic polynomial identities.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
z>0
and
x=2\cosh z-2
. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\bigl(x+\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)\bigr)=\frac{\sinh(rz)}{\sinh z},\qquad r\geq 1,

and

\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\bigl(x+\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)\bigr)=\left[\frac{\sinh(rz/2)}{\sinh(z/2)}\right]^{2},\qquad r\geq 3.

Moreover,

\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\bigl(x+\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)\bigr)=4\sinh^{2}(rz/2).

Proof.

For paths, the monic polynomial
q_{r}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}(x+\lambda_{i}^{P}(r))
satisfies the Chebyshev recurrence
q_{r+1}(x)=(x+2)q_{r}(x)-q_{r-1}(x)
, with
q_{1}(x)=1
and
q_{2}(x)=x+2
. Substituting
x=2\cosh z-2
gives
q_{r}(x)=\sinh(rz)/\sinh z
.

For cycles, the standard identity

\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(2\cosh z-2\cos\frac{2\pi i}{r}\right)=2\cosh(rz)-2=4\sinh^{2}(rz/2)

gives the product including
i=0
. Dividing by the
i=0
factor
x=4\sinh^{2}(z/2)
gives the positive-mode formula.
∎

Set

c(x)=\arcosh(2-\cos\pi x)=2\arsinh\left(\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}\right),\qquad d(x)=\arcosh(2-\cos 2\pi x)=2\arsinh|\sin\pi x|,

for
0\leq x\leq 1
.
We write
\alpha=\arsinh(1)
. Thus
c(1)=2\alpha
,
d(1/2)=2\alpha
,
d(x)=d(1-x)
, and
d
is increasing on
[0,1/2]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
t>1
and

G_{t}(y)=\log\frac{1-e^{-2ty}}{1-e^{-2y}},\qquad y>0.

Then
G_{t}(y)>0
and
G_{t}
is strictly decreasing.

Proof.

Positivity is immediate from
t>1
. Differentiating gives
G_{t}^{\prime}(y)=2t/(e^{2ty}-1)-2/(e^{2y}-1)
, equivalently

G_{t}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{1}{y}\left(\frac{2ty}{e^{2ty}-1}-\frac{2y}{e^{2y}-1}\right).

The function
(e^{u}-1)/u
is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
, so its reciprocal
u/(e^{u}-1)
is strictly decreasing. Since
2ty>2y
, this gives
G_{t}^{\prime}(y)<0
.
∎

3
Two-Dimensional Product Formulas

The following formulas are the unweighted two-dimensional specializations with
two free factors, one free and one periodic factor, and two periodic factors
of the hypercubic boundary-condition products of Tzeng and Wu
[
14
]
. We record
them in a form adapted to the later balancing arguments, after the one-
dimensional zero-mode factors have been separated and evaluated.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
r,s\geq 1
,

\tau(P_{r}\square P_{s})=\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\prod_{j=1}^{s-1}\bigl(\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)+\lambda_{j}^{P}(s)\bigr).

For
r\geq 1
and
s\geq 3
,

\tau(P_{r}\square C_{s})=s\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}\frac{\sinh(rd(k/s))}{\sinh d(k/s)}.

For
r,s\geq 3
,

\tau(C_{r}\square C_{s})=rs\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}\left[\frac{\sinh\left(\frac{r}{2}d(k/s)\right)}{\sinh\left(\frac{1}{2}d(k/s)\right)}\right]^{2}.

Proof.

The spectra of the products are the pairwise sums of the one-dimensional spectra. For
P_{r}\square P_{s}
, Kirchhoff’s theorem gives a prefactor
(rs)^{-1}
. The factors with one zero coordinate contribute

\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)\prod_{j=1}^{s-1}\lambda_{j}^{P}(s)=rs

by
Section

2
, canceling the Kirchhoff prefactor.

For
P_{r}\square C_{s}
, the one-dimensional factors contribute

\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}\lambda_{k}^{C}(s)=rs^{2}.

After division by
rs
, a factor
s
remains. For each
1\leq k\leq s-1
,

\lambda_{k}^{C}(s)=2\cosh d(k/s)-2,

and the path polynomial identity in
Section

2
gives the displayed product.

For
C_{r}\square C_{s}
, the one-dimensional factors contribute
r^{2}s^{2}
, and Kirchhoff leaves the prefactor
rs
. The cycle polynomial identity with
z=d(k/s)
gives the mixed product.
∎

4
Periodic Trapezoidal Sums

Let

\gamma(\theta)=\arcosh(2-\cos\theta),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R},

which is even and
2\pi
-periodic. On
0\leq\theta\leq\pi
it agrees with
d(\theta/2\pi)
.
Also let

I=\int_{0}^{1}d(x)\,dx=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\gamma(\theta)\,d\theta.

For
s\geq 1
put

S_{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}d(k/s).

Lemma 4.1
.

For every
s\geq 1
,

\frac{S_{s}}{s}=I+\frac{2}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(1-e^{-s\gamma(\phi)})\,d\phi.

Consequently
S_{s}/s
is increasing in
s
, and

S_{s}=sI-\frac{\pi}{3s}+O(s^{-3}).

Proof.

For
a\geq 1
,

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(a-\cos\phi)\,d\phi=\log\frac{a+\sqrt{a^{2}-1}}{2}.

With
a=2-\cos\theta
this gives

\gamma(\theta)=\log 2+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(2-\cos\theta-\cos\phi)\,d\phi.

Fix
\phi>0
and write
q=e^{-\gamma(\phi)}
. Then

2-\cos\theta-\cos\phi=\frac{1-2q\cos\theta+q^{2}}{2q}.

The Fourier expansion

\log(1-2q\cos\theta+q^{2})=-2\sum_{m\geq 1}\frac{q^{m}}{m}\cos(m\theta)

is filtered by the periodic grid
\theta=2\pi k/s
: only multiples of
s
survive. Hence

\frac{1}{s}\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\gamma(2\pi k/s)=I+\frac{2}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(1-e^{-s\gamma(\phi)})\,d\phi.

The
k=0
summand is zero, so the left side is
S_{s}/s
.

For fixed
z>0
, the function
p^{-1}\log(1-e^{-pz})
is increasing in
p>0
. The identity therefore implies that
S_{s}/s
increases with
s
.

It remains to expand the integral. Choose
\delta>0
small. On
[\delta,\pi]
the function
\gamma
is bounded below by a positive constant, so the contribution is
O(e^{-cs})
. On
[0,\delta]
we have
\gamma(\phi)=\phi+O(\phi^{3})
and
\gamma(\phi)\asymp\phi
. With
u=s\phi
this gives

s\gamma(u/s)=u+O(u^{3}/s^{2}),\qquad 0\leq u\leq\delta s.

Moreover

\left|\log(1-e^{-s\gamma(u/s)})-\log(1-e^{-u})\right|\leq\frac{Cu^{3}}{s^{2}(e^{cu}-1)},

which is integrable in
u
on
[0,\infty)
. After the change of variables
d\phi=du/s
, this majorant gives an
O(s^{-3})
error, and replacing the truncated interval
[0,\delta s]
by
[0,\infty)
changes the integral only by an exponentially small tail. Therefore

\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(1-e^{-s\gamma(\phi)})\,d\phi=\frac{1}{s}\int_{0}^{\infty}\log(1-e^{-u})\,du+O(s^{-3}).

The standard integral equals
-\pi^{2}/6
, which gives the expansion. The constants in this estimate are absolute; this is the uniform input used later in the fixed-area cylinder expansion.
∎

We shall also need massive variants. For
m>0
define

\gamma_{m}(\theta)=\arcosh\left(2+\frac{m}{2}-\cos\theta\right),\qquad\theta\in\mathbb{R}.

Set

U_{s}^{C}(m)=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\gamma_{m}(2\pi k/s),\qquad U_{s}^{P}(m)=\sum_{k=0}^{s-1}\gamma_{m}(\pi k/s).

Lemma 4.2
.

For each
m>0
, both normalized sums
U_{s}^{C}(m)/s
for
s\geq 1
and
U_{s}^{P}(m)/s
for
s\geq 1
are increasing in
s
.

Proof.

The cycle identity is exactly the proof of
Section

4
with
\gamma
replaced by
\gamma_{m}
. Indeed,

2+\frac{m}{2}-\cos\theta-\cos\phi=\cosh\gamma_{m}(\phi)-\cos\theta,

so, with
q=e^{-\gamma_{m}(\phi)}
, the same Fourier expansion and periodic-grid filtering give

\frac{U_{s}^{C}(m)}{s}=I_{m}+\frac{2}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(1-e^{-s\gamma_{m}(\phi)})\,d\phi,

where
I_{m}=\pi^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\gamma_{m}(\phi)\,d\phi
. Since
\gamma_{m}(\phi)>0
, monotonicity follows from the same pointwise monotonicity in
s
.

For paths, let

T_{s}(m)=\frac{1}{s}\left(\frac{\gamma_{m}(0)+\gamma_{m}(\pi)}{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\gamma_{m}(\pi k/s)\right).

Equivalently, by the symmetry
\gamma_{m}(2\pi-\phi)=\gamma_{m}(\phi)
, this half-weighted sum is the normalized cycle sum on the
2s
-point grid:
T_{s}(m)=U_{2s}^{C}(m)/(2s)
.
Substituting
2s
into the cycle identity therefore gives

T_{s}(m)=I_{m}+\frac{1}{\pi s}\int_{0}^{\pi}\log(1-e^{-2s\gamma_{m}(\phi)})\,d\phi.

Also

\frac{U_{s}^{P}(m)}{s}=T_{s}(m)+\frac{\gamma_{m}(0)-\gamma_{m}(\pi)}{2s}.

The integral term is increasing in
s
, and the final term is increasing because
\gamma_{m}(0)<\gamma_{m}(\pi)
. Hence
U_{s}^{P}(m)/s
is increasing.
∎

5
Rectangles and Tori

5.1
Rectangles with Two Free Factors

We use the rectangular-grid theorem in the following form.

Theorem 5.1

(
[
17
, Theorem 1.2]
)
.

Let
A,B,a,b
be positive integers satisfying

AB=ab,\qquad A\leq a\leq b\leq B.

Then

\log\tau(P_{a}\square P_{b})-\log\tau(P_{A}\square P_{B})\geq\arsinh(1)\bigl((A+B)-(a+b)\bigr).

If the rectangles are distinct, the inequality is strict. The universal constant
\arsinh(1)
is optimal.

Corollary 5.2
.

Among divisor rectangles
P_{r}\square P_{s}
with
rs=N
, the closest-to-square divisor pair uniquely maximizes
\tau
, up to rotation.

5.2
Tori with Two Periodic Factors

Exact gain identities

Theorem 5.3
.

Let

AB=ab,\qquad 3\leq A\leq a\leq b\leq B.

Set
t=a/A=B/b
. If
t=1
, the two tori are identical. If
t>1
, then

\log\frac{\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b})}{\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B})}=(t-1)(AS_{b}-bS_{A})+2\Gamma_{T},

where

\Gamma_{T}=\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}G_{t}\left(\frac{A}{2}d(k/b)\right)-\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}G_{t}\left(\frac{b}{2}d(j/A)\right).

Proof.

The case
t=1
is immediate. Assume
t>1
. The torus product formula gives, after comparing both tori to
C_{A}\square C_{b}
,

\log\frac{\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b})}{\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B})}=2\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}H_{t}\left(\frac{A}{2}d(k/b)\right)-2\sum_{j=1}^{A-1}H_{t}\left(\frac{b}{2}d(j/A)\right),

because the vertex prefactors contribute
\log(a/A)-\log(B/b)
in the two comparisons, which is zero since
a/A=B/b=t
. Here

H_{t}(y)=\log\frac{\sinh(ty)}{\sinh y}=(t-1)y+G_{t}(y).

Separating the linear and residual parts gives the claimed identity.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

5.3
, if
t>1
, then
\Gamma_{T}>0
.

Proof.

Since
t>1
and
a\leq b
, we have
b>A
. For
1\leq j\leq A-1
, define

k_{j}=\begin{cases}\lfloor bj/A\rfloor,&j\leq A/2,\\
b-\lfloor b(A-j)/A\rfloor,&j>A/2.\end{cases}

These are distinct elements of
\{1,\ldots,b-1\}
. On the first half they are strictly increasing and lie at or below
b/2
; on the second half they are strictly increasing and lie above
b/2
. By construction,
\dist(k_{j}/b,\mathbb{Z})\leq\dist(j/A,\mathbb{Z})
. Using
d(x)=d(1-x)
and the monotonicity of
d
on
[0,1/2]
, we get
d(k_{j}/b)\leq d(j/A)
, and therefore
(A/2)d(k_{j}/b)\leq(b/2)d(j/A)
. Since
G_{t}
is decreasing, each matched term from the first sum in
\Gamma_{T}
is at least the corresponding term from the second sum. After these
A-1
matches, the first sum still has
b-A
unmatched terms, all positive by
Section

2
. Hence
\Gamma_{T}>0
.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Let

AB=ab,\qquad 3\leq A\leq a\leq b\leq B.

Then

\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b})>\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B})

unless the pairs are identical.

Proof.

Let
t=a/A=B/b
. If
t=1
, the pairs are identical. If
t>1
,
Theorem

5.3
gives

\log\frac{\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b})}{\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B})}=(t-1)(AS_{b}-bS_{A})+2\Gamma_{T}.

The periodic trapezoidal monotonicity in
Section

4
gives
AS_{b}-bS_{A}\geq 0
, and
Section

5.2
gives
\Gamma_{T}>0
.
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Among admissible divisor pairs
rs=N
with
r,s\geq 3
, the closest-to-square torus
C_{r}\square C_{s}
uniquely maximizes
\tau
, up to rotation.

Proof.

If
d_{1}<d_{2}\leq\sqrt{N}
are admissible divisors, set

,
B=N/d_{1}
,

, and

. Then

, so

5.5
applies.

Torus asymptotics and the absence of a linear boundary constant

For
r,s\geq 3
put
R_{r,s}=\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\log(1-e^{-rd(k/s)})
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
r,s\geq 3
,

\log\tau(C_{r}\square C_{s})=rS_{s}+\log(r/s)+2R_{r,s}.

Proof.

Starting from the torus product formula,

\log\tau(C_{r}\square C_{s})=\log(rs)+2\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\log\frac{\sinh(rd(k/s)/2)}{\sinh(d(k/s)/2)}.

For
d=d(k/s)
,

\log\frac{\sinh(rd/2)}{\sinh(d/2)}=\frac{r-1}{2}d+\log(1-e^{-rd})-\log(1-e^{-d}).

The denominator product is

\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}(1-e^{-d(k/s)})=e^{-S_{s}/2}\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}2\sinh\frac{d(k/s)}{2}=e^{-S_{s}/2}s,

using
Section

2
. Substitution gives

\log(rs)+(r-1)S_{s}+2R_{r,s}+S_{s}-2\log s=rS_{s}+\log(r/s)+2R_{r,s}.

∎

For
\rho>0
, define

\eta(i\rho)=e^{-\pi\rho/12}\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1-e^{-2\pi\rho\ell})

and

\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\log\rho+4\log\eta(i\rho)=\log\rho-\frac{\pi\rho}{3}+4\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\log(1-e^{-2\pi\rho\ell}).

Proposition 5.8
.

Uniformly when
\rho=r/s
remains in a compact subset of
(0,\infty)
and
r,s\to\infty
,

\log\tau(C_{r}\square C_{s})=Irs+\mathcal{T}(\rho)+o(1).

Moreover, the eta modular identity
\eta(i/\rho)=\sqrt{\rho}\,\eta(i\rho)
gives

\mathcal{T}(\rho)=\mathcal{T}(1/\rho).

Proof.

By
Section

4
,

rS_{s}=Irs-\frac{\pi r}{3s}+o(1)=Irs-\frac{\pi\rho}{3}+o(1).

It remains to identify the limit of
R_{r,s}
. Since
d(x)=2\pi x+O(x^{3})
at
0
and
d(x)=d(1-x)
, for each fixed
\ell\geq 1
,

rd(\ell/s)\to 2\pi\rho\ell,\qquad rd((s-\ell)/s)\to 2\pi\rho\ell.

On compact
\rho
-ranges, the bound
d(k/s)\geq c\min(k,s-k)/s
gives a summable majorant for the logarithmic residual terms, while the middle range is exponentially small uniformly in
\rho
. Dominated convergence therefore gives

R_{r,s}\to 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\log(1-e^{-2\pi\rho\ell}).

Combining this with
Section

5.2
proves the expansion. The symmetry follows immediately from the displayed modular identity.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

There is no constant
\beta>0
such that every nontrivial torus balancing move satisfies

\log\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b})-\log\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B})\geq\beta\bigl((A+B)-(a+b)\bigr).

Equivalently, the best universal linear side-sum constant for tori is
0
.

Proof.

Let

(A_{n},B_{n})=\bigl(n^{2},(n+1)^{2}\bigr),\qquad(a_{n},b_{n})=\bigl(n(n+1),n(n+1)\bigr).

Then
A_{n}B_{n}=a_{n}b_{n}
,
3\leq A_{n}<a_{n}=b_{n}<B_{n}
for large
n
, and

(A_{n}+B_{n})-(a_{n}+b_{n})=1.

The areas are equal. Applying
Section

5.2
to the two tori, the leading area terms cancel and the remaining shape terms both tend to
\mathcal{T}(1)
. Hence

\log\tau(C_{a_{n}}\square C_{b_{n}})-\log\tau(C_{A_{n}}\square C_{B_{n}})\to 0.

Thus no positive
\beta
can work uniformly. The exact balancing theorem still gives strict positivity for each fixed nontrivial move.
∎

6
Cylinders

A cylinder has one free factor and one periodic factor, and its extremal scale is different from the two symmetric cases.

6.1
Cylinder Normal Form

For fixed
N=rs
and integer
s\geq 3
, allow the path length
r=N/s
to be real and define the fixed-area relaxation

F_{N}(s)=\log\left[s\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}\frac{\sinh((N/s)d(k/s))}{\sinh d(k/s)}\right],\qquad r=N/s.

Let
A_{r,s}=\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\log(1-e^{-2rd(k/s)})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
r=N/s
,

F_{N}(s)=rS_{s}-\log\sinh(\alpha s)+A_{r,s}.

Proof.

Using

\frac{\sinh(rd)}{\sinh d}=e^{(r-1)d}\frac{1-e^{-2rd}}{1-e^{-2d}},

we get

F_{N}(s)=\log s+(r-1)S_{s}+A_{r,s}+B_{s},\qquad B_{s}=-\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}\log(1-e^{-2d(k/s)}).

Here
B_{s}>0
; it is the contribution of the denominator factors in the cylinder product formula.
It remains to show

B_{s}=S_{s}-\log s-\log\sinh(\alpha s).

By
Section

2
,

\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}2\sinh\frac{d(k/s)}{2}=s.

Also, applying the cycle determinant identity at
x=4
gives

\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}2\cosh\frac{d(k/s)}{2}=\sinh(\alpha s),

because
4=2\cosh(2\alpha)-2
. Hence

\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}(1-e^{-2d(k/s)})=e^{-S_{s}}\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}2\sinh d(k/s)=e^{-S_{s}}s\sinh(\alpha s).

Taking negative logarithms proves the claim.
∎

6.2
Exact Cylinder Circumference Gain

Proposition 6.2
.

For
3\leq s_{1},s_{2}\leq N
and
r_{i}=N/s_{i}
,

F_{N}(s_{2})-F_{N}(s_{1})=N\left(\frac{S_{s_{2}}}{s_{2}}-\frac{S_{s_{1}}}{s_{1}}\right)-\log\frac{\sinh(\alpha s_{2})}{\sinh(\alpha s_{1})}+A_{r_{2},s_{2}}-A_{r_{1},s_{1}}.

Proof.

This is the difference of the exact normal forms in
Section

6.1
, using
r_{i}=N/s_{i}
.
∎

Section

6.2
isolates the two competing mechanisms in the free–periodic case. Increasing the cyclic circumference increases the normalized periodic trapezoidal term
S_{s}/s
, but it also pays the exact free-boundary penalty
-\log\sinh(\alpha s)
.

6.3
Failure of Square Balancing

Proposition 6.3
.

Closest-to-square does not maximize
\tau(P_{r}\square C_{s})
at fixed area. In fact

\tau(P_{9}\square C_{4})=9022397309950500>6009209192448000=\tau(P_{6}\square C_{6}),

and

\tau(P_{20}\square C_{6})=1788611877285241916171285961489325853733676245188126625000

is greater than

\tau(P_{12}\square C_{10})=506827984454602606473153918154960615493126180174922465000.

Proof.

These are exact evaluations of the product formula in Proposition
3
, computed and verified by a computer program. The arithmetic can be reproduced without numerical approximation as follows. Let

q_{1}(x)=1,\qquad q_{2}(x)=x+2,\qquad q_{r+1}(x)=(x+2)q_{r}(x)-q_{r-1}(x),

so that
q_{r}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{r-1}(x+\lambda_{i}^{P}(r))
. If
T_{s}^{\mathrm{Ch}}
denotes the Chebyshev polynomial, then

p_{s}(x)=(-1)^{s-1}\frac{2-2T_{s}^{\mathrm{Ch}}(1-x/2)}{x}=\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}(x-\lambda_{k}^{C}(s)).

Thus

\tau(P_{r}\square C_{s})=s\prod_{k=1}^{s-1}q_{r}(\lambda_{k}^{C}(s)).

We evaluate this symmetric product exactly using the recurrence for
q_{r}
together with the defining polynomial for the nonzero cycle eigenvalues. Evaluating these recurrences gives the displayed integers.
∎

6.4
Bounded-Aspect Cylinder Asymptotics

Lemma 6.4
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that, uniformly when
r/s\geq 1
,

A_{r,s}=O(e^{-cr/s}).

Proof.

Since
d(x)=2\arsinh|\sin\pi x|
, there is
\eta>0
such that

d(k/s)\geq\eta\,\frac{\min(k,s-k)}{s}\qquad(1\leq k\leq s-1).

Since
r/s\geq 1
, this gives
e^{-2rd(k/s)}\leq e^{-2\eta}<1
. Hence

-\log(1-u)\leq Cu
uniformly for all values of
u
that occur below. Pairing

k
with
\min(k,s-k)
gives

|A_{r,s}|\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{s-1}e^{-2rd(k/s)}\leq C\sum_{\ell\geq 1}e^{-2\eta(r/s)\ell}=O(e^{-cr/s}).

∎

Proposition 6.5
.

Let
\rho=r/s
. Uniformly when
\rho
remains in a compact subset of
(0,\infty)
and
r,s\to\infty
,

\log\tau(P_{r}\square C_{s})=Irs-\alpha s+\mathcal{C}(\rho)+o(1),

where

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\log 2-\frac{\pi\rho}{3}+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\log(1-e^{-4\pi\rho\ell}).

Thus the bounded-aspect cylinder has the square-lattice area term, one free-boundary term
-\alpha s
, and no logarithmic corner term.

Proof.

Use the exact normal form with
N=rs
. The trapezoidal expansion gives

rS_{s}=Irs-\frac{\pi r}{3s}+o(1)=Irs-\frac{\pi\rho}{3}+o(1).

Also

-\log\sinh(\alpha s)=-\alpha s+\log 2+o(1).

It remains to identify
A_{r,s}
. For fixed
\ell\geq 1
,

2rd(\ell/s)\to 4\pi\rho\ell,\qquad 2rd((s-\ell)/s)\to 4\pi\rho\ell.

As in the torus proof, the lower bound
d(k/s)\geq c\min(k,s-k)/s
gives a summable majorant on compact
\rho
-ranges, and the middle range is exponentially small uniformly in
\rho
. Hence

A_{r,s}\to 2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\log(1-e^{-4\pi\rho\ell}),

which proves the expansion.
∎

Classical finite-size normalizations.

The bounded-aspect torus has no boundary-length term: after the area term, the aspect dependence is only the bounded modular shape term
\mathcal{T}(\rho)
. This is the square-lattice rectangular-torus specialization of the classical discrete-torus complexity asymptotic, where the eta term is the real-torus determinant contribution
[
1
,
2
]
. In the free-energy convention
F=-\log Z
used in the physics literature,
Section

5.2
reads

F=-Irs-\log\rho-4\log\eta(i\rho)+o(1),

which is the isotropic toroidal correction

f_{0}(\xi)=-\log\xi-4\log\eta(i\xi)
of Izmailian and Kenna
[
6
]
, with
\xi=\rho
.

For the cylinder,

\mathcal{C}(\rho)=\log 2+2\log\eta(2i\rho).

Taking the periodic length in Izmailian–Kenna notation to be
M=s
, the free length to be
N=r
, and the anisotropy parameter to be
z=1
, their cylinder identity and expansion of
Z_{0,0}(1,M,2N)
give

F=-\log Z=-Irs+\alpha s-\log 2-2\log\eta(2i\rho)+o(1),

the negative of
Section

6.4
. Thus the cylinder expansion recovers the mixed free and periodic finite-size correction: one surface term and no logarithmic corner term
[
6
]
.

Proposition 6.6
.

The linear coefficient in the cylinder free-boundary term is exactly

\alpha=\arsinh(1).

More precisely,

-\log\sinh(\alpha s)=-\alpha s+\log 2+o(1),

so the coefficient
\alpha
cannot be replaced in the cylinder normal form or in the bounded-aspect expansion.

Proof.

The identity follows from

\sinh(\alpha s)=\frac{1}{2}e^{\alpha s}(1-e^{-2\alpha s}).

Substituting this into the exact normal form in
Section

6.1
shows that any different linear coefficient would leave an error of order
s
in bounded-aspect cylinders.
∎

6.5
The
N^{1/3}
Circumference

Proposition 6.7
.

Uniformly when
s\to\infty
and
r/s=N/s^{2}\to\infty
,

F_{N}(s)=IN-\alpha s-\frac{\pi N}{3s^{2}}+\log 2+O\left(\frac{N}{s^{4}}\right)+O(e^{-2\alpha s})+O(e^{-cN/s^{2}}).

Proof.

Combine the normal form with
Section

4
:

rS_{s}=IN-\frac{\pi N}{3s^{2}}+O(N/s^{4}).

Also

-\log\sinh(\alpha s)=-\alpha s+\log 2+O(e^{-2\alpha s}),

and
Section

6.4
gives the final error term.
∎

The model correction in
Section

6.5
is

\Phi_{N}(s)=-\alpha s-\frac{\pi N}{3s^{2}}.

It has the unique real maximum at

s_{0}=\left(\frac{2\pi N}{3\alpha}\right)^{1/3}.

Theorem 6.8
.

Let
N\to\infty
, and let
s_{N}^{*}
be an integer maximizer of the real-relaxed objective
F_{N}(s)
, with
r=N/s
allowed to be real, over
3\leq s\leq\sqrt{N}
. Then

s_{N}^{*}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\arsinh(1)}\right)^{1/3}N^{1/3}+O(1).

Proof.

The expansion in
Section

6.5
is uniform on windows
s\asymp N^{1/3}
. In that range the error is
O(N^{-1/3})
. Taylor expansion of
\Phi_{N}
at
s_{0}
gives

\Phi_{N}(s_{0})-\Phi_{N}(s)=\frac{3\alpha}{2s_{0}}(s-s_{0})^{2}+O\left(\frac{|s-s_{0}|^{3}}{N^{2/3}}\right)

for
|s-s_{0}|=o(N^{2/3})
.
Indeed,

\Phi_{N}^{\prime\prime}(s)=-\frac{2\pi N}{s^{4}},\qquad\Phi_{N}^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=\frac{8\pi N}{s^{5}},

and
s_{0}\asymp N^{1/3}
, so
\Phi_{N}^{\prime\prime\prime}(s)=O(N^{-2/3})
throughout any fixed multiple window around
s_{0}
.

It remains to localize globally. Let
\bar{s}
be an integer nearest to
s_{0}
. For large
N
this is an admissible point, and
Section

6.5
gives

F_{N}(\bar{s})\geq IN-C_{1}N^{1/3}

for some constant
C_{1}
. On the other hand, the exact normal form gives
A_{r,s}\leq 0
, and the strictly negative integral term in
Section

4
gives
S_{s}/s<I
; hence

F_{N}(s)\leq IN-\log\sinh(\alpha s)\leq IN-\alpha s+\log 2.

Choosing
M
so large that
\alpha M>C_{1}+1
excludes all
s\geq MN^{1/3}
from being maximizers.

For small
s
, the identity in
Section

4
implies
S_{s}/s<I
, and the expansion of
S_{s}
together with finitely many remaining values gives a constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\frac{S_{s}}{s}\leq I-\frac{c_{0}}{s^{2}}\qquad(s\geq 3).

Since
-\log\sinh(\alpha s)\leq 0
for
s\geq 3
, we get

F_{N}(s)\leq IN-\frac{c_{0}N}{s^{2}}.

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
so small that
c_{0}\varepsilon^{-2}>C_{1}+1
excludes all
s\leq\varepsilon N^{1/3}
. Thus every maximizer lies in a fixed window
\varepsilon N^{1/3}\leq s\leq MN^{1/3}
.

On this window
Section

6.5
is uniform and gives
F_{N}(s)=IN+\Phi_{N}(s)+O(N^{-1/3})
. The scaled function
x\mapsto-\alpha x-\pi/(3x^{2})
has a unique maximum at
x=s_{0}/N^{1/3}
, so the preceding comparison first confines every maximizer to
|s-s_{0}|\leq\eta N^{1/3}
for any fixed small
\eta>0
, once
N
is large. Taking
\eta
small enough, the Taylor formula above gives

\Phi_{N}(s_{0})-\Phi_{N}(s)\geq c_{2}\frac{(s-s_{0})^{2}}{N^{1/3}}

throughout this window. Comparing again with the
O(N^{-1/3})
error in
Section

6.5
shows that
|s_{N}^{*}-s_{0}|\leq K
for a fixed
K
, which is the asserted
O(1)
bound.
∎

6.6
Divisor-Rich Cylinders

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
N\to\infty
through integers admitting a divisor
u_{N}\mid N
with
u_{N}\geq 3
and

u_{N}\sim\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\arsinh(1)}\right)^{1/3}N^{1/3}.

Then every maximizing cylinder circumference
s_{\mathrm{opt}}
among all divisors
s\mid N
with
s\geq 3
satisfies

s_{\mathrm{opt}}\sim\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\arsinh(1)}\right)^{1/3}N^{1/3}.

If in addition

u_{N}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\arsinh(1)}\right)^{1/3}N^{1/3}+O(1),

then every maximizing circumference satisfies

s_{\mathrm{opt}}=\left(\frac{2\pi}{3\arsinh(1)}\right)^{1/3}N^{1/3}+O(1).

Proof.

The divisor
u_{N}
supplies an admissible cylinder with
u_{N}\asymp N^{1/3}
, hence
u_{N}\leq\sqrt{N}
for all large
N
. Since
u_{N}/s_{0}\to 1
,
Section

6.5
gives

F_{N}(u_{N})=IN+\Phi_{N}(s_{0})+o(N^{1/3})=IN-O(N^{1/3}).

We first rule out the complementary strip
s>\sqrt{N}
. From the exact normal form,
A_{r,s}\leq 0
and, again by
Section

4
,
S_{s}/s<I
, so

F_{N}(s)\leq IN-\log\sinh(\alpha s)\leq IN-\alpha s+O(1).

Thus if
s>\sqrt{N}
, then
F_{N}(s)\leq IN-\alpha\sqrt{N}+O(1)
, which is strictly smaller than
F_{N}(u_{N})
for all large
N
. Therefore every maximizer among all admissible divisors
s\mid N
,
s\geq 3
, lies in the range
3\leq s\leq\sqrt{N}
.

On this remaining range, the large- and small-
s
bounds used in the proof of
Theorem

6.8
confine every maximizing divisor to a fixed window
\varepsilon N^{1/3}\leq s\leq MN^{1/3}
: the competitor
u_{N}
has value
IN-O(N^{1/3})
, while
F_{N}(s)\leq IN-\alpha s+O(1)
excludes
s\geq MN^{1/3}
for large
M
, and
F_{N}(s)\leq IN-c_{0}N/s^{2}
excludes
s\leq\varepsilon N^{1/3}
for small
\varepsilon
.

On this fixed window,
Section

6.5
is uniform; equivalently, for fixed
0<c<C<\infty
and
cN^{1/3}\leq s\leq CN^{1/3}
,

F_{N}(s)=IN+N^{1/3}\varphi(s/N^{1/3})+o(N^{1/3}),\qquad\varphi(x)=-\alpha x-\frac{\pi}{3x^{2}}.

The function
\varphi
has a strict unique maximum at
x_{0}=s_{0}/N^{1/3}
, so for each fixed
\delta>0
there is
c_{\delta}>0
such that
|s/s_{0}-1|\geq\delta
implies
\Phi_{N}(s)\leq\Phi_{N}(s_{0})-c_{\delta}N^{1/3}
. Such an
s
cannot maximize against the admissible competitor
u_{N}
, whose value is
IN+\Phi_{N}(s_{0})+o(N^{1/3})
. This proves
s_{\mathrm{opt}}\sim s_{0}
.

If
u_{N}=s_{0}+O(1)
, then
F_{N}(u_{N})=IN+\Phi_{N}(s_{0})+O(N^{-1/3})
. Once
s_{\mathrm{opt}}\sim s_{0}
, the Taylor expansion in the proof of
Theorem

6.8
gives
\Phi_{N}(s_{0})-\Phi_{N}(s)\geq c(s-s_{0})^{2}/N^{1/3}
throughout a fixed small multiple window around
s_{0}
. Comparing with the uniform
O(N^{-1/3})
error in
Section

6.5
gives
s_{\mathrm{opt}}=s_{0}+O(1)
.
∎

Remark 6.10
.

There is no unconditional theorem of this form for all
N
. The graph problem only permits cyclic circumferences
s
that divide
N
, and such divisors need not exist near the real optimum
s_{0}\asymp N^{1/3}
. The theorem above optimizes over all admissible cyclic divisors under the stated divisor-rich hypothesis; without that hypothesis, divisor availability can force the best admissible circumference away from the
N^{1/3}
scale.

7
Massive Determinant Inequalities

For
m\geq 0
define

D_{m}(r,s)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\left(m+\lambda_{i}^{P}(r)+\lambda_{j}^{P}(s)\right),

with the zero factor omitted when
m=0
. Similarly, for
r,s\geq 3
define

E_{m}(r,s)=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\prod_{j=0}^{s-1}\left(m+\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)+\lambda_{j}^{C}(s)\right),

with the zero factor omitted when
m=0
.

Theorem 7.1
.

If

AB=ab,\qquad A\leq a\leq b\leq B,

then

D_{m}(a,b)>D_{m}(A,B)

for every
m\geq 0
, unless the pairs are identical.

Proof.

For
m=0
,

D_{0}(r,s)=rs\,\tau(P_{r}\square P_{s}),

so the result follows from
Theorem

5.1
because
ab=AB
.

Assume
m>0
and put

f_{m}(x)=\arcosh\left(2+\frac{m}{2}-\cos\pi x\right),\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

For fixed
j
, set
z=f_{m}(j/s)
. Then
m+\lambda_{j}^{P}(s)=2\cosh z-2
, and the path polynomial identity shows that the ratio from side length
A
to side length
a
in the first coordinate is
\sinh(az)/\sinh(Az)
.
Let
t=a/A=B/b>1
. Comparing through
(A,b)
yields

\log\frac{D_{m}(a,b)}{D_{m}(A,B)}=\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}H_{t}\bigl(Af_{m}(k/b)\bigr)-\sum_{j=0}^{A-1}H_{t}\bigl(bf_{m}(j/A)\bigr),

where
H_{t}(y)=(t-1)y+G_{t}(y)
. Therefore

\log\frac{D_{m}(a,b)}{D_{m}(A,B)}=(t-1)(AU_{b}^{P}(m)-bU_{A}^{P}(m))+\Gamma.

Here

\Gamma=\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}G_{t}\bigl(Af_{m}(k/b)\bigr)-\sum_{j=0}^{A-1}G_{t}\bigl(bf_{m}(j/A)\bigr).

The main term is nonnegative by
Section

4
. For the residual, match
j
to
k_{j}=\lfloor jb/A\rfloor
. Since
b>A
, these indices are distinct and satisfy
k_{j}/b\leq j/A
. The function
f_{m}
is increasing on
[0,1]
, so
Af_{m}(k_{j}/b)\leq bf_{m}(j/A)
. By
Section

2
, each matched residual term is at least its partner, and the first sum has
b-A
unmatched positive terms. Hence
\Gamma>0
.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

If

AB=ab,\qquad 3\leq A\leq a\leq b\leq B,

then

E_{m}(a,b)>E_{m}(A,B)

for every
m\geq 0
, unless the pairs are identical.

Proof.

For
m=0
,

E_{0}(r,s)=rs\,\tau(C_{r}\square C_{s}),

so
Theorem

5.5
applies.

Assume
m>0
and set

f_{m}(x)=\arcosh\left(2+\frac{m}{2}-\cos 2\pi x\right).

For fixed
k
, the cycle product including the zero mode is

\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\left(m+\lambda_{i}^{C}(r)+\lambda_{k}^{C}(s)\right)=4\sinh^{2}\left(\frac{r}{2}f_{m}(k/s)\right).

With
t=a/A=B/b>1
, comparison through
(A,b)
gives

\log\frac{E_{m}(a,b)}{E_{m}(A,B)}=2\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}H_{t}\left(\frac{A}{2}f_{m}(k/b)\right)-2\sum_{j=0}^{A-1}H_{t}\left(\frac{b}{2}f_{m}(j/A)\right).

Hence

\log\frac{E_{m}(a,b)}{E_{m}(A,B)}=(t-1)(AU_{b}^{C}(m)-bU_{A}^{C}(m))+2\Gamma.

Here

\Gamma=\sum_{k=0}^{b-1}G_{t}\left(\frac{A}{2}f_{m}(k/b)\right)-\sum_{j=0}^{A-1}G_{t}\left(\frac{b}{2}f_{m}(j/A)\right).

The main term is nonnegative by
Section

4
.

For the residual, match
j=0
to
k_{0}=0
. For
1\leq j\leq A-1
, use the folded matching from
Section

5.2
. Since
f_{m}(x)=f_{m}(1-x)
and
f_{m}
is increasing on
[0,1/2]
, the matched indices satisfy
f_{m}(k_{j}/b)\leq f_{m}(j/A)
. Thus
(A/2)f_{m}(k_{j}/b)\leq(b/2)f_{m}(j/A)
, and the monotonicity of
G_{t}
gives a nonnegative matched residual. The first sum has
b-A
unmatched positive terms, so
\Gamma>0
.
∎

8
Higher-Dimensional Pure Products

8.1
Pure Path Products

For
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
write

P_{\mathbf{n}}=P_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square P_{n_{d}}.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
H=P_{n_{3}}\square\cdots\square P_{n_{d}}
, with
H
interpreted as the one-vertex graph when
d=2
. If

AB=ab,\qquad A\leq a\leq b\leq B,

then

\tau(P_{a}\square P_{b}\square H)>\tau(P_{A}\square P_{B}\square H)

unless the pairs are identical.

Proof.

Let the positive Laplacian eigenvalues of
H
be
\mu\in\Spec^{+}(H)
. Product spectra and Kirchhoff’s theorem give

ab|V(H)|\,\tau(P_{a}\square P_{b}\square H)=D_{0}(a,b)\prod_{\mu\in\Spec^{+}(H)}D_{\mu}(a,b).

Indeed, the zero mode of
H
contributes exactly the two-dimensional determinant
D_{0}(a,b)
, and each positive eigenvalue
\mu
contributes the massive determinant
D_{\mu}(a,b)
. The same formula holds for
(A,B)
. Since
ab=AB
, the normalizing factors are equal, and every determinant factor strictly increases by
Theorem

7.1
unless the pair is unchanged.
∎

8.2
Pure Cycle Products

For
n_{i}\geq 3
write

C_{\mathbf{n}}=C_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{n_{d}}.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
H=C_{n_{3}}\square\cdots\square C_{n_{d}}
, with
H
interpreted as the one-vertex graph when
d=2
. If

AB=ab,\qquad 3\leq A\leq a\leq b\leq B,

then

\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b}\square H)>\tau(C_{A}\square C_{B}\square H)

unless the pairs are identical.

Proof.

The same slicing argument gives

ab|V(H)|\,\tau(C_{a}\square C_{b}\square H)=E_{0}(a,b)\prod_{\mu\in\Spec^{+}(H)}E_{\mu}(a,b).

The zero mode of
H
contributes
E_{0}(a,b)
, and each positive mode contributes
E_{\mu}(a,b)
. Since
ab=AB
, the normalizing factors agree, and
Theorem

7.2
increases every factor unless the pair is unchanged.
∎

Definition 8.3
.

A divisor tuple
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
of fixed product is
pairwise balanced
if no pair of coordinates can be replaced by a strictly more balanced divisor pair with the same product; that is, for no
i<j
with
\min(n_{i},n_{j})=A
and
\max(n_{i},n_{j})=B
is there a factorization
AB=ab
with
A<a\leq b<B
.

Corollary 8.4
.

Every fixed-volume maximizer in the pure path class is pairwise balanced. Every fixed-volume maximizer in the pure cycle class, with all side lengths at least
3
, is pairwise balanced. If a fixed product has a unique terminal pairwise-balanced tuple up to permutation, then that tuple is the unique maximizer up to permutation.

Proof.

If a maximizing tuple admitted a nontrivial balancing of two coordinates,
Theorem

8.1
or
Theorem

8.2
would strictly increase
\tau
without changing the volume. Let
\bar{u}=d^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log n_{i}=d^{-1}\log(n_{1}\cdots n_{d})
, which is unchanged under product-preserving moves. Repeated strict balancing moves terminate because the quantity

\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\log n_{i}-\bar{u})^{2}

strictly decreases under each move while the product is fixed, and there are only finitely many divisor tuples of a fixed product. Indeed, for the two changed logarithmic side lengths
x\leq y
with fixed sum, their contribution to the global log-variance is

(x-\bar{u})^{2}+(y-\bar{u})^{2}=2\left(\frac{x+y}{2}-\bar{u}\right)^{2}+\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2},

so replacing the pair by a closer pair strictly decreases this contribution. If the terminal tuple is unique, every starting tuple has a strictly increasing path to it unless it is already terminal.
∎

8.3
Obstruction to Pairwise Methods

Proposition 8.5
.

The tuple
(50,54,64,75)
has product
60^{4}
and is pairwise balanced. Consequently, perfect-power volume alone does not imply that
(n,\ldots,n)
is the unique terminal tuple under pairwise balancing.

Proof.

First,
50\cdot 54\cdot 64\cdot 75=12960000=60^{4}
. For a pair
x<y
, a nontrivial balancing move exists exactly when
xy
has a divisor
a
with
x<a\leq\sqrt{xy}
, in which case the replacement is
(a,xy/a)
. The six pairs in the displayed tuple have no such divisor:

\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}(x,y)&xy&\{a:a\mid xy,\ x<a\leq\sqrt{xy}\}\\
\hline\cr(50,54)&2700&\varnothing\\
(50,64)&3200&\varnothing\\
(50,75)&3750&\varnothing\\
(54,64)&3456&\varnothing\\
(54,75)&4050&\varnothing\\
(64,75)&4800&\varnothing\end{array}

Thus no pair of coordinates can be replaced by a strictly more balanced divisor pair.
∎

Remark 8.6
.

Section

8.3
is an obstruction to the pairwise-divisor method, not to the perfect-power maximizer statement itself. The obstruction is purely number-theoretic: the relevant real balancing moves exist, but no admissible intermediate integer divisor is available for any pair in the tuple. The finite pairwise reduction may stop at a nonuniform terminal tuple even when the total volume is a perfect power, so a proof of perfect-power uniqueness has to use additional information.

8.4
Heat-Trace Majorization and Perfect Powers

We now prove a logarithmic majorization theorem by a method that does not
depend on pairwise divisor moves. The obstruction in

Section

8.3
shows that pairwise balancing can stop at
nonuniform terminal tuples even when the total volume is a perfect power. To
compare whole fixed-product families at once, we use the heat-trace
representation of the Laplacian determinant. The required input is a
one-dimensional convexity statement after extending path and cycle heat traces
from integer side lengths to positive real side lengths.

The probabilistic notation below is only a compact way to encode positivity and
variance identities for heat kernels and theta-type sums. The extremal
comparison remains deterministic; random variables are used as bookkeeping for
log-convexity.

For a connected graph
G
with Laplacian eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N-1}
, write

Q(G)=\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}\lambda_{j}.

Thus
\tau(G)=Q(G)/N
.

Lemma 8.7
.

If
G
and
H
are connected graphs with the same number of vertices, then

\log Q(H)-\log Q(G)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\Tr(e^{-tL_{G}})-\Tr(e^{-tL_{H}})\right]\frac{\,dt}{t}.

Proof.

Apply
\log a-\log b=\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{-bt}-e^{-at})\,\,dt/t
to the positive Laplacian eigenvalues of
H
and
G
and sum. The two zero modes cancel because both graphs are connected, and the equal vertex counts cancel the leading small-
t
term of the traces. The integral is therefore convergent at
0
, and convergence at
\infty
is exponential on the positive eigenspaces.
∎

Let
h_{X}(m,t)=\Tr(e^{-tL(X_{m})})
for
X=P
or
X=C
. Product heat traces factor:

\Tr(e^{-tL(X_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{n_{d}})})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{X}(n_{i},t).

Thus it is enough to prove a one-dimensional strict convexity statement. If,
for each fixed
t>0
, the real-side-length extension satisfies that

u\mapsto\log\mathcal{H}_{X}(e^{u},t)
is strictly convex and agrees with
h_{X}(m,t)

at integer
m
, then Karamata’s inequality compares the full product heat traces
under logarithmic majorization. The preceding lemma then converts that
comparison into a determinant comparison.

Definition 8.8
.

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

x_{1}^{\downarrow}\geq\cdots\geq x_{d}^{\downarrow}
be its decreasing rearrangement.
For positive integer tuples
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
and

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})
with equal product, say that

\mathbf{n}

log-majorizes

\mathbf{m}
if

(\log n_{1},\ldots,\log n_{d})
majorizes

(\log m_{1},\ldots,\log m_{d})
, that is, if

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\log n_{i})^{\downarrow}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\log m_{i})^{\downarrow}\qquad 1\leq k<d,

and equality holds for
k=d
.

Lemma 8.9
.

For
t>0
and
x>0
, define

\mathcal{H}_{C}(x,t)=xe^{-2t}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\lvert q\rvert x}(2t),

where
I_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind. If
m\geq 3
is an integer, then
\mathcal{H}_{C}(m,t)=h_{C}(m,t)
. Moreover

u\longmapsto\log\mathcal{H}_{C}(e^{u},t)

is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We first check agreement with the ordinary cycle heat trace at integer side
lengths. The Fourier–Bessel expansion

e^{r\cos\theta}=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\ell}(r)e^{i\ell\theta}

and the roots-of-unity filter give, for integer
m\geq 3
,

h_{C}(m,t)=e^{-2t}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}e^{2t\cos(2\pi k/m)}=me^{-2t}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\lvert q\rvert m}(2t).

This is exactly
\mathcal{H}_{C}(m,t)
.

We next represent the fractional trace as a positive mixture of Gaussian theta
factors. We use the Hartman–Watson representation in the following probability-measure form
[
4
,
16
]
:
for each
R>0
there is a probability measure
\mu_{R}
supported on

(0,\infty)
such that, for
\nu\geq 0
,

\frac{I_{\nu}(R)}{I_{0}(R)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\nu^{2}\sigma/2}\,\mu_{R}(\,d\sigma).

The Bessel series is absolutely convergent, and all terms in the representation
are nonnegative, so Tonelli’s theorem permits applying this formula term by
term. Therefore

\mathcal{H}_{C}(x,t)=e^{-2t}I_{0}(2t)\int_{0}^{\infty}x\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-\sigma q^{2}x^{2}/2}\,\mu_{2t}(\,d\sigma).

It remains to prove logarithmic convexity for the Gaussian theta factors and
then pass to this positive mixture.

For
a>0
put

\Theta_{a}(x)=x\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-aq^{2}x^{2}}.

Let

\theta(c)=\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-cq^{2}},\qquad A(c)=-c\frac{\theta^{\prime}(c)}{\theta(c)}.

Then
\Theta_{a}(e^{u})=e^{u}\theta(ae^{2u})
. If
K
is the discrete Gaussian
random variable with

\mathbb{P}_{c}(K=k)=\frac{e^{-ck^{2}}}{\theta(c)}

and
Y=K^{2}
, then
A(c)=c\,\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y]
and

A^{\prime}(c)=\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y]-c\,\operatorname{Var}_{c}(Y).

Differentiating
\log\Theta_{a}(e^{u})
gives

\frac{\,d^{2}}{\,du^{2}}\log\Theta_{a}(e^{u})=-4cA^{\prime}(c),\qquad c=ae^{2u}.

We claim that
A^{\prime}(c)<0
for every
c>0
.

For
c\geq\pi
, since
Y^{2}\geq Y
,

\operatorname{Var}_{c}(Y)\geq\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y](1-\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y]).

Also

\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y]\leq 2\sum_{k\geq 1}k^{2}e^{-\pi k}=\frac{2e^{-\pi}(1+e^{-\pi})}{(1-e^{-\pi})^{3}}<1-\frac{1}{\pi}\leq 1-\frac{1}{c}.

Hence

A^{\prime}(c)\leq\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y]\bigl(1-c(1-\mathbb{E}_{c}[Y])\bigr)<0.

For
0<c<\pi
, Poisson summation gives

\theta(c)=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{c}}\,\theta\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{c}\right),

and therefore

A(c)+A\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{c}\right)=\frac{1}{2}.

Differentiating,

A^{\prime}(c)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{c^{2}}A^{\prime}\left(\frac{\pi^{2}}{c}\right)<0,

because
\pi^{2}/c>\pi
. Thus
\log\Theta_{a}(e^{u})
is strictly convex.

Finally, positive mixtures of log-convex functions are log-convex by Hölder’s
inequality, and here strictness is preserved by the nondegenerate support of the
Hartman–Watson measure. Indeed, let
u\neq v
,
0<\lambda<1
, and

w=(1-\lambda)u+\lambda v
. For every
\sigma>0
, strict log-convexity of

\Theta_{\sigma/2}
gives

\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{w})<\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{u})^{1-\lambda}\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{v})^{\lambda}.

Since
\mu_{2t}
is supported on
(0,\infty)
, this strict inequality holds on
a set of full
\mu_{2t}
-measure. Integrating it and then applying Hölder gives

\int\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{w})\,\mu_{2t}(\,d\sigma)<\left(\int\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{u})\,\mu_{2t}(\,d\sigma)\right)^{1-\lambda}\left(\int\Theta_{\sigma/2}(e^{v})\,\mu_{2t}(\,d\sigma)\right)^{\lambda}.

Multiplication by the positive constant
e^{-2t}I_{0}(2t)
does not affect
log-convexity. This proves the asserted strict convexity of

\log\mathcal{H}_{C}(e^{u},t)
.
∎

Lemma 8.10
.

For
t>0
and
x>0
, define

\mathcal{H}_{P}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}_{C}(2x,t)+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-4t}).

If
m
is a positive integer, then
\mathcal{H}_{P}(m,t)=h_{P}(m,t)
. Moreover

u\longmapsto\log\mathcal{H}_{P}(e^{u},t)

is strictly convex on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

We first verify the integer specialization. The same Bessel expansion, now
with the path angles
\pi k/m
, gives

h_{P}(m,t)=e^{-2t}\left[m\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\lvert 2qm\rvert}(2t)+\sinh(2t)\right].

Since, by definition,

\mathcal{H}_{C}(2m,t)=2me^{-2t}\sum_{q\in\mathbb{Z}}I_{\lvert 2qm\rvert}(2t),

we have

h_{P}(m,t)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}_{C}(2m,t)+\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-4t}).

Thus
\mathcal{H}_{P}
agrees with
h_{P}
at integer side lengths.

The convexity reduces to the cycle case plus a positive constant. The function

u\mapsto\log\mathcal{H}_{C}(2e^{u},t)
is strictly convex by

Section

8.4
. Put

G(u)=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}_{C}(2e^{u},t)
and

c_{t}=\frac{1}{2}(1-e^{-4t})>0
. If
u\neq v
,
0<\lambda<1
, and

w=(1-\lambda)u+\lambda v
, then

G(w)<G(u)^{1-\lambda}G(v)^{\lambda}.

The two-term Hölder inequality gives

c_{t}+G(u)^{1-\lambda}G(v)^{\lambda}\leq(c_{t}+G(u))^{1-\lambda}(c_{t}+G(v))^{\lambda}.

The first inequality is strict, and the second preserves the comparison after
adding the same positive constant to both endpoint values. Combining them
proves strict log-convexity of
c_{t}+G(u)=\mathcal{H}_{P}(e^{u},t)
.
∎

Theorem 8.11
.

Let
\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})
and
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})
be positive
integer tuples with equal product. If
\mathbf{n}
log-majorizes
\mathbf{m}
,
then

\tau(P_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square P_{n_{d}})\leq\tau(P_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square P_{m_{d}}),

with equality only when the tuples agree up to permutation. If all
n_{i},m_{i}\geq 3
,
then also

\tau(C_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{n_{d}})\leq\tau(C_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{m_{d}}),

again with equality only when the tuples agree up to permutation.

Proof.

We give the proof simultaneously for
X=P
and
X=C
, using the cycle
hypotheses in the periodic case. Fix
t>0
. By

Sections

8.4
and
8.4
, the function

f_{t}(u)=\log\mathcal{H}_{X}(e^{u},t)

is strictly convex and agrees with
\log h_{X}(m,t)
at admissible integer side
lengths. Since
(\log n_{i})
majorizes
(\log m_{i})
, Karamata’s inequality gives

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log h_{X}(n_{i},t)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{t}(\log n_{i})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{t}(\log m_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log h_{X}(m_{i},t).

The inequality is strict unless the logarithmic side-length tuples agree up to
permutation. Therefore

\Tr(e^{-tL(X_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{n_{d}})})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{X}(n_{i},t)\geq\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{X}(m_{i},t)=\Tr(e^{-tL(X_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{m_{d}})}),

strictly for every
t>0
unless the tuples agree up to permutation.

Apply
Section

8.4
with

G=X_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{n_{d}}
and

H=X_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{m_{d}}
. The equal-product assumption gives
equal vertex counts. If the tuples are not permutations of one another, then

\log Q(X_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{m_{d}})-\log Q(X_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square X_{n_{d}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{X}(n_{i},t)-\prod_{i=1}^{d}h_{X}(m_{i},t)\right]\frac{\,dt}{t}>0.

Dividing by the common Matrix–Tree normalization preserves the strict
inequality for
\tau
. If the tuples agree up to permutation, the product
graphs are isomorphic. Thus
\log\tau
is Schur-concave in the logarithmic side
lengths on the admissible pure product tuples.
∎

Corollary 8.12
.

Let
n_{1},\ldots,n_{d},n
be positive integers with
n_{1}\cdots n_{d}=n^{d}
.
Then

\tau(P_{n}^{\square d})\geq\tau(P_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square P_{n_{d}}),

with equality only when
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})=(n,\ldots,n)
.
If
n\geq 3
and all
n_{i}\geq 3
, then

\tau(C_{n}^{\square d})\geq\tau(C_{n_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{n_{d}}),

again with equality only when
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})=(n,\ldots,n)
.

Proof.

The logarithmic side-length vector has average
\log n
, so it majorizes
(\log n,\ldots,\log n)
. Apply
Theorem

8.11
. Equality forces agreement up to permutation with the constant tuple, hence all side lengths are equal to
n
.
∎

9
Discussion

The three two-dimensional free and periodic products split into two behaviors. Rectangles with two free factors and tori with two periodic factors are balanced by moving the two side lengths toward each other at fixed product. The cylinder is asymmetric: the cyclic circumference is a boundary-sensitive variable, and in the divisor-rich case the optimizer has scale
N^{1/3}
rather than
N^{1/2}
when divisors are available near that scale.

The arbitrary-dimensional theorems are intentionally pure free and pure periodic statements. For products of paths, and separately for products of cycles with lengths at least
3
, every nontrivial balancing of a pair of coordinates increases the spanning-tree count. This proves pairwise balancing of fixed-volume maximizers and gives uniqueness whenever the terminal pairwise-balanced tuple is unique. The heat-trace argument gives a stronger comparison: within each pure product class,
\log\tau
is Schur-concave in logarithmic side lengths. Thus a tuple that is less spread out in the logarithmic majorization order has at least as many spanning trees. This proves the uniform hypercube and hypertorus are the unique maximizers in the pure free and pure periodic classes at volume
n^{d}
. For general
N
in dimension
d\geq 3
, we still do not assert a unique closest-to-hypercube maximizer, because the logarithmic majorization order is only a partial order on divisor tuples.

These product results suggest a higher-dimensional perfect-power form of the square-maximality conjecture for induced grid subgraphs. Let
\mathcal{L}_{d}
be the nearest-neighbor graph on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Conjecture 9.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 1
. If
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
has
|S|=n^{d}
and the induced graph
\mathcal{L}_{d}[S]
is connected, then

\tau(\mathcal{L}_{d}[S])\leq\tau(P_{n}^{\square d}).

Equality should occur only for the
d
-dimensional box
P_{n}^{\square d}
, up to lattice translation and coordinate permutation.

For
d=2
this specializes to the square-maximality conjecture recorded by Procaccia and Tucker-Foltz
[
10
]
. For non-perfect-power
N
, one still expects a maximizing induced grid subgraph to be a compact near-box shape, but the correct finite shape may be non-product. Tapp’s boundary-sensitive estimates support this compactness principle, but the product-grid comparisons here show that exact spanning-tree maximality requires more than edge-count or boundary-size control.

There is also a natural periodic analogue, although the correct ambient class is less canonical than connected induced subgraphs of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. One finite-quotient formulation is the following. Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be a full-rank sublattice, let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be
\Lambda
-periodic, and let
\mathcal{L}_{d}[S]/\Lambda
be the corresponding finite simple quotient graph. This includes rectangular tori
C_{m_{1}}\square\cdots\square C_{m_{d}}
by taking
S=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\Lambda=m_{1}\mathbb{Z}e_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus m_{d}\mathbb{Z}e_{d}
.

Conjecture 9.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 3
. Among connected quotient graphs
\mathcal{L}_{d}[S]/\Lambda
with
n^{d}
vertex orbits, the spanning-tree count is at most

\tau(C_{n}^{\square d}).

Equality should occur only for the standard product torus
C_{n}^{\square d}
, up to the natural quotient symmetries.

For general periodic volumes, the right finite benchmark is left open. Product tori remain the natural comparison class, and inside that subclass
Theorem

8.11
identifies the most balanced admissible tori whenever the relevant logarithmic majorization comparison decides the divisor tuples. The broader periodic conjecture should be viewed more cautiously than
Section

9
: different choices of the ambient periodic class may lead to slightly different formulations, but the pure cycle products provide the natural benchmark.

Mixed products with both free and periodic factors in higher dimension are not classified here for structural reasons. Pairwise balancing would have to compare a free factor with a periodic factor, and the corresponding two-dimensional slice is a massive cylinder; the cylinder analysis shows that this slice is asymmetric rather than closest-to-square monotone. The heat-trace proof also uses the pure structure. For pure products, Karamata’s inequality acts on a sum of identical functions, either all
\log\mathcal{H}_{P}(e^{u},t)
or all
\log\mathcal{H}_{C}(e^{u},t)
. In a mixed product the constrained real-side-length heat-trace objective is instead a sum of distinct strictly convex functions. For each fixed
t
this still gives a unique derivative-matching minimizer in logarithmic side lengths, but that minimizer is not forced by symmetry and need not be independent of
t
. Thus both methods stop at the same boundary: pure products are governed by symmetry, while mixed products require isolating competing analytic penalties.
