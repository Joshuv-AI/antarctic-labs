Introduction

The National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) was commissioned in 2014. As the facility entered
its second decade of operations it had experienced several long, unscheduled downtime periods (longer
than 12 hours) due to equipment failures in both storage ring and booster dipole magnet power supplies,
klystron transmitters, and cryogenic plant. With the likelihood of these events becoming more frequent as
the facility aged, a program to improve reliability and achieve the full performance of the machine was
undertaken. The backup storage ring dipole power supply project was developed under this program.

The 60 dipole magnets in the NSLS-II Storage Ring are connected in a series circuit and powered by a
single 400 kW power supply [1]. Dipole magnet current needed to achieve 3 GeV beam energy is
363.96 A. The dipole power supply is a large system that represents a single-point failure mode for
NSLS-II that can require many hours to recover from should a failure occur. The backup storage ring
dipole power supply is part of an accelerator improvement project with the purpose of reducing
cumulative beam downtime resulting from failures of the main dipole power supply.

This Note reports on the approach taken to develop and successfully commission the backup dipole power
supply. The backup dipole power supply is a semi-custom technical solution, combining commercial-off-
the-shelf (COTS) power converters with in-house designed components to augment the performance of
the commercial equipment. A transfer switch, developed in-house, selects between primary and backup
power supply systems. We describe in detail the design and construction of the power supply including
supplemental reference material that is not part of the electrical and mechanical build documentation
package released on the NSLS-II SharePoint website.

Design Considerations for the Backup System

Several high-level factors had to be considered before deciding on an overall design concept for the
backup dipole power supply.

1. Single vs Dual Backup Power Supplies

The main dipole power supply powers the storage ring dipole magnet circuit with a DC voltage of
approximately 820 V and is built from two identical series-connected power supplies, PS1 and PS2, each
operating at 410 V. This topology establishes a grounded reference point at the midpoint where the two
power supplies connect together. Creating a halfway connection point that can be tied to ground reduces
the maximum voltage from ground seen by the dipole magnets to ±410 V compared to 820 V. This

 2

reduces stress on the magnets, magnet cabling, and internal power supply components, and enhances
personnel safety.

Early design concepts for the backup power supply considered building only a single power supply which
could replace either the positive (PS1) or negative (PS2) dipole power supplies. However, it became
evident that the logistics of switching a single supply into either of the two existing power supply circuit
positions were complicated compared to a single, complete power supply that could substitute for both
PS1 and PS2 power supplies together. The latter approach leaves the Personnel Protection System (PPS)
and Top-Off Safety System (TOSS) unaffected and simplifies the cabling needed to transfer between
primary and backup power supplies. In addition, a suitable commercial off the shelf power supply
providing a cost-efficient solution was identified allowing the full backup power supply approach to be
achieved within the original budget, and so this is the approach that was taken.

2. Energy Storage

Each PS1 and PS2 power supply in the primary dipole power supply includes an energy storage capacitor
bank which holds approximately 100 kJ of potential energy during operation. This stored energy is
needed for maintaining current regulation during routine AC power line dips which can last for 0.1 s or
longer.

It was decided at the outset that since the backup dipole power supply was not expected to be operated for
extended periods of time, the requirement for hold-up during AC power line dips was not a design goal.
Therefore, high capacitive energy storage was not included in the backup dipole power supply design
concept.

3. Traditional vs Modular Design

Large power supplies (>100 kW) have traditionally been built either as full custom systems or low
volume standard products in equipment cabinets with specialized component layouts. Traditional
constructions generally have fewer components and less overall complexity but are more difficult to
service and lack redundancy.

With the understanding that mean time to restore (MTTR) was to be prioritized over mean time between
failures (MTBF), and that an MTTR of less than two hours was a goal, the backup dipole power supply
construction concept placed a strong emphasis on modularity and maintainability. A modular design offers
several advantages over traditional construction when MTTR is the priority. In particular, a modular
design:

 a) Makes possible N+1 Redundancy
 b) Offers easier service and quicker repair – failed components can be swapped out and serviced off-
 line.
 c) Eschews large 60 Hz 3/6-phase transformers and instead uses direct rectification of the 3-phase
 480 VAC service. Output isolation is achieved with small high-frequency transformers in each
 power module.
 d) Can be used as building blocks for other large power supply applications, e.g. charger for
 ramping booster power supplies.

4. Transfer Switch

The mechanism for switching between primary and backup power supplies needs to satisfy two
requirements. It must allow for rapid switchover (15-30 minutes) between primary and backup systems,
and it must maintain secure electrical connections of the 535 MCM magnet cables over many switch
cycles.

Friction type contacts like those found on a knife switch were ruled out since their reliability could
degrade over time.

Electro-mechanical contactors were ruled out since an inadvertent cutoff of power to the contactor coils
could result in a break in the connection between the power supply and the magnet load while in
operation. This would likely produce a significant arc event from the collapsing magnetic field in the
dipole magnets across the open circuit.

Even absent a dropout of the contactor, the quality of connection with switch contacts was not expected to
be as good as bolted connections for a high precision, high stability magnet circuit.

Instead, a custom switch design was developed utilizing slotted links that could be easily slid between
primary and backup positions in a single motion and then bolted into place.

5. Controls and Regulation

NSLS-II power supplies interface to the EPICS controls network though custom hardware components,
namely Power Supply Controllers (PSCs), Power Supply Interfaces (PSIs), and Regulator-Controllers.
Next generation controls developed for the LBNL ALS-U project integrate these three components into a
single chassis called a zPSC. Dozens of user interface pages developed in Control System Studio (CSS)
and Phoebus allow operators, engineers, and technicians to control magnet power supplies through the
power supply controls equipment.

As with the decision to build a complete backup system for both PS1 and PS2, it was decided that a
separate and redundant controls system for the backup dipole power supply would be easier to implement
than an approach relying on modifications to existing controls that would have to work with both primary
and backup power supplies. Since it is likely that both systems would need to be operated at the same
time if the off-line system is to be worked on during beam operations, it seemed appropriate to have an
independent control system for the backup power supply, and it made sense to use a new, next generation
zPSC for this purpose. In this Note, the new zPSC is hereafter referred to only as PSC.

A. Liquablade AC/DC Power Converters

Liquablade power converters manufactured by Astrodyne/TDI are a family of water-cooled, 16.5 kW
power converters packaged in standard 19 inch rack mountable chassis requiring 1U of vertical rack
space. The model of Liquablade power converter used in the backup dipole power supply is rated for
500V/33A operation. The power density (power per unit volume) offered by these power converters is
state of the art at the time of this writing, and their isolation specification of 1500 V is higher than any
other COTS AC/DC power converter in this power class.

Liquablade power converters can be combined in parallel (output terminals of multiple units connected
together, plus to plus, minus to minus) into larger groups to deliver hundreds of kilowatts of power. For
each plus and minus power section in the backup dipole power supply, 12 Liquablade power converters
are used to provide half the power needed by the magnet circuit.

Setpoint control is digital over Controller Area Network (CAN) bus. Liquablade CAN bus setpoint
control resolution is 14 bits. Figure 2 is a picture of one Liquablade power converter chassis.

B. Low Pass Filter

Liquablade power converters are switch-mode type power converters intended primarily for industrial
markets where output noise from the power converters is not a primary concern. Since each Liquablade
power converter itself is comprised of three internally paralleled, asynchronous switch-mode power
modules, switch-mode voltage ripple for the composite set of 12 parallelled chassis is seen in narrow
spectral bands around the nominal power converter switching frequency and its harmonics. The nominal
switching frequency in the power converters is 250 kHz, and the noise amplitude seen at these
frequencies is on the order of 1 V.

The dipole magnet circuit is routed all the way around the NSLS-II storage ring, so to limit
electromagnetic interference (EMI) in cable trays, offset conduits, the storage ring tunnel, and elsewhere
in the facility, a low pass filter was developed in-house to attenuate unwanted high frequency noise from
the Liquablade power converters. Figure 3(a) shows a schematic of the filter, and Figure 3(b)-(d) show
what the filter chassis looks like. The central conductor passing through the toroidal magnetic cores and
protruding out from the chassis rear panel is a laminated bus product from nVent. The laminated bus is

bus can carry the 400 A rated current of the power supply with a temperature rise of only a few degrees.

C. Linear Amplifier

While the Liquablade power converters provide the power to energize the magnets, they lack the
precision, stability, and reproducibility required by accelerator physics for control of the beam. The linear
amplifier is an in-house designed power stage that adds low noise (<1 mVrms), wide bandwidth (1 kHz),
and high precision (20-bit or 1 part in 106) performance to the backup dipole power supply.

Although not strictly needed for the primary dipole power supply, the linear amplifier was included in the
original design as part of a conservative approach to minimize 60 Hz AC line noise and its harmonics for
the IR beamlines at NSLS-II. The IR beamlines are particularly sensitive to such noise in the beam. For
the backup dipole power supply, the linear amplifier was a necessity since the 14-bit control resolution of
the Liquablades did not meet the control reference resolution requirement for the dipole power supply.

The linear amplifier is a class A amplifier comprised of parallelled MOSFET transistors each receiving a
setpoint signal from the PSC PID regulator output. The linear amplifier is a control element placed in
series with the Liquablade power converters to significantly increase the precision of the output current in
the magnet load. Acting as a variable resistor, the linear amplifier adjusts its resistance to maintain a
steady, regulated output voltage, absorbing fluctuations in its input supply. The linear amplifier has an
operating bandwidth of 1 kHz.

Each linear amplifier printed circuit board (PCB) assembly has ten paralleled MOSFET stages. The linear
amplifier chassis parallels four linear amplifier PCB assemblies for a total of 40 paralleled MOSFET
stages. The MOSFETs are specifically engineered for safe operation in their linear region and are not
optimized for on/off switching. Since mismatches in MOSFET characteristics would lead to unequal
current sharing among MOSFET stages, each MOSFET is controlled by a local current feedback loop to

 9

force current in the MOSFET to match the setpoint within an acceptable tolerance. A resettable circuit
breaker is placed in series with each MOSFET stage to prevent a MOSFET that has failed short from
hogging current from the other stages. Figure 5 illustrates the current feedback circuit for one MOSFET
amplifier stage.

The MOSFET current feedback control loop is the innermost control loop in the overall regulation
scheme for the power supply. The MOSFET current loop gets its control signal from the output of the
linear amplifier voltage feedback controller (PSC CH2 or CH3) which in turn receives its control signal
from the output of the PSC current regulation loop controller (PSC CH1) which represents the outermost
feedback control loop in the system.

Figure 5. Current feedback loop for maintaining equal current sharing among paralleled linear amplifier
MOSFET power stages.

The linear amplifier is a next generation linear power stage based on the design used in the primary dipole
power supply. Refinements include:

 1. Differential sensing of current shunts for current sharing feedback control loops. This was done
 to prevent high frequency (50 kHz) instability observed in the development of the original linear
 amplifier for the primary dipole power supply.
 2. Hall sensor current readback for each of the ten MOSFET stages. This is a very useful diagnostic
 readback that tells how well the 40 MOSFET amplifier stages are sharing current.

 10

 3. 1-wire temperature monitoring system accommodates unlimited number of sensors with only
 three wires required. Four temperature sensors are used for each PCB assembly, compared to a
 single sensor in the primary dipole power supply.
 4. Diagnostic housekeeping data packet delivered over a serial data connection that includes
 current readback for the 40 MOSFET amplifier stages, voltage drop across the linear amplifier,
 current in the diode clamp, temperature sensor readbacks, and digital status which includes open
 circuit breaker faults.
 5. An Atmega 2560-16AU microcontroller for detecting over-voltage (OVV), over-temperature
 (OVT), over-current (OVC), and open circuit breaker faults. The microcontroller sums detected
 fault conditions into a single sumfault signal to the PLC. The specific interlocking fault
 condition can be determined from the diagnostic housekeeping data packet.
 6. More compact design using water-cooled chill plate assemblies rather than air-cooled heatsink
 assemblies. The four linear amplifier pcb assemblies, diode clamp, and data communications
 board are all integrated into a single linear amplifier chassis.

Over-Voltage Protection

The linear amplifier is controlled by voltage feedback from its output to facilitate tracking with the
Liquablade power converters during ramping. The linear amplifier’s voltage control loop gets its setpoint
from the magnet current regulation loop. The Liquablade power converters are controlled in such a way
as to always maintain a nominal voltage drop across the linear amplifier of 7 V to 8 V, both during steady
state operation and when the power supply is ramping up and down. The MOSFETs used in the linear
amplifier have a voltage rating of 100 V. However, the Liquablade power converters are capable of
producing voltages greater than the voltage rating of the MOSFETs which could damage or destroy the
transistors under a fault or anomalous condition.

Overvoltage protection for the linear amplifier is provided in the form of a diode string which acts as a
passive voltage clamp across the linear amplifier elements until an active interlock can turn off the
Liquablade power converters. The diode string contains 14 series-connected diodes which begin to
conduct current when the voltage across the string is approximately 12 V. The active interlock is handled
by both sensing the voltage across the linear amplifier and by measuring the current through the diode
clamp. If the measured voltage across the linear amplifier exceeds 12 V, or if the current in the diode
clamp exceeds 1 A, the Atmega microcontroller on the linear amplifier PCB signals a fault to the PLC
controller which turns off the Liquablade power converters.

Snubber

Since the inductance of the magnet load is approximately 1 H, an RC snubber circuit is added to flatten
the linear amplifier’s gain versus frequency response. This is necessary because the current feedback
control loops used for MOSFET current sharing will have a markedly peaky frequency response if loaded
only by magnet inductance. The snubber adds an AC coupled resistance to the load that presents as purely
resistive at frequencies above 20 Hz. Figure 7 shows a schematic representation of the snubber circuit and
a picture of the snubber assembly mounted between AC line filters in the power supply rack.

E. CAN Bus Controller

Setpoint control and diagnostic readbacks from the Liquablade power converters are handled via CAN
bus. Since NSLS-II power supply control is analog PID, a suitable analog to CAN bus converter was
needed. The vendor product offering for this function was not available at the time the backup dipole
power supply was under development, and additional capability was needed anyway, so we designed our
own in-house CAN bus controller. Figure 9 is a picture of the CAN bus controller chassis.

 Figure 9. CAN bus controller chassis.

The CAN bus controller circuit’s key components are three Atmega 328 microcontrollers, three MCP2515
CAN controllers, and a 16-bit analog-to-digital- converter (ADC). Microcontroller 1 and CAN controller
1 are used for setpoint control of the Liquablade power converters. The ADC samples and digitizes the
analog input control from the PSC. An offset to the digitized control voltage is numerically added to make
the Liquablade output approximately 7.5 V greater than the output of the linear amplifier. A fine gain
adjustment is also applied to match the DC transfer function of the Liquablade power converters to the
transfer function of the linear amplifier for maintaining the 7.5 V difference near zero and full scale
output. The processed command voltage is then converted into a CAN bus setpoint command.

Microcontroller 2 and CAN controller 2 monitor the PS1 CAN bus and extract diagnostic readback data
from the PS1 Liquablade power converters. Microcontroller 3 and CAN controller 3 monitor the PS2
CAN bus and extract diagnostic readback data from the PS2 Liquablade power converters. Each
Liquablade power converter returns diagnostic data for each of its three internal power modules.
Diagnostic data include eight digital status bits, DC output voltage, DC output current, and module
temperature.

In steady state operation, the CAN bus setpoint is constant, and CAN controllers 2 and 3 continually
receive diagnostic data from the PS1 and PS2 Liquablade power converters through individual addressing
of each Liquablade internal power module. However, the CAN bus update rate while receiving diagnostic
data is not fast enough for ramping the power supply up and down. During up and down-ramp periods,
the CAN1 controller switches to broadcast mode which allows setpoint commands to be sent to all
Liquablade power converters at a faster rate (approximately 50 Hz). During broadcast mode, CAN bus
diagnostic data are not available. Since the NSLS-II controls system has no signal to indicate start of

 15

ramp, the CAN controller circuit must detect that a ramp is underway by examining changes in the
digitized ADC data while discriminating against sample to sample variations due to noise. This detection
must happen quickly (< 0.2 s) on downramps to ensure that the linear amplifiers do not see an over-
voltage condition near the start of the ramp.

It was observed during testing that the linear amplifier voltage drop would decrease during up-ramps and
increase during down-ramps by approximately 1.5 V even with the gain properly adjusted for 7.5 V drop
at 0 V and full scale. It was determined that the change in voltage drop during ramping was due to a
lagging response of the Liquablade power converters to the CAN bus ramp setpoint commands when the
ramp rate was 10 A/s or higher. To correct for the lag, derivative feed-forward compensation was added to
the digitized CAN setpoint data to advance the Liquablade setpoints for better tracking with the linear
amplifier output to maintain the 7.5 V drop across the linear amplifier during ramping.

Installation

The mechanical design of the power supply was strongly influenced by the length of both the Liquablade
power converters and the linear amplifier water-cooled chill plate. Once a strategy for designing the series
pass enclosure was formulated the rack could be ordered. An off the shelf equipment rack from Amco
with optional side compartments for the AC and water was chosen. The decision for all high-power AC
distribution to be mounted outside the rack also played into the decision. The power supply rack
dimensions:48”w X 47”d X 97”h, AC and Water are 8”wide each inclusive. The single rack solution was
accomplished by carefully designing each subsystem to be as small as practically possibly.

Figure 10 shows the rack installations of the primary and backup dipole power supplies. The eight
equipment cabinets of the primary dipole power supply are reduced to one cabinet for the backup dipole
power supply. Each power supply still requires a separate equipment rack for controls. The backup dipole
power supply’s considerably smaller footprint on the floor is due to several factors:

 1. The water-cooled design of the Liquablade power converters and linear amplifiers allows for
 more compact sub-assemblies than the air-cooled components in the primary dipole power
 supply.
 2. The backup power supply has no significant energy storage, i.e., no capacitor bank. The capacitor
 banks occupy two full equipment cabinets in the primary dipole power supply.
 3. AC/DC isolation is achieved without large 60 Hz mains transformers in favor of many small
 high-frequency transformers that offer higher power density than iron core 60 Hz transformers.
 Without the 3-phase transformers, another two cabinets can be removed from the system.
 4. The Liquablade power converters and linear amplifiers are highly integrated chassis enclosures,
 and they fill a significant volume of the power supply rack. Overall packing density in the power
 supply rack is therefore significantly higher than in the primary dipole power supply cabinets.

AC Power Distribution

AC power for the backup dipole power supply originates in the Service Building 3 (SB3) electrical
equipment room through an 800 A circuit breaker whose service was previously unused. From the
equipment room, power is routed via 500 MCM cables through the SB3 mezzanine and through overhead
conduit above the storage ring mezzanine to where the backup power supply is installed in C05. AC
switchgear in C05 mounted on Unistrut behind the power supply and controls racks includes two
remotely controlled motorized circuit breakers (MCBs), a PPS contactor enclosure, and distribution
breaker panels which fan out 3-phase 480 VAC 30 A service to each of the 24 Liquablade power
converters in the power supply rack.

For safety, the motorized circuit breakers are remotely actuated during application and removal of
complex Lockout/Tagout (LOTO). Remotely controllable breakers reduce the risk of personal injury from
arc flash and reduce the LOTO PPE requirements. Absence of voltage tester devices (AVTs) provide the
means for verifying a safe working condition (zero energy state) from both the AC voltage going into the
power supply rack and the DC output voltage of the power supply. The PPS contactors are placed before
the 30 A AC circuit breaker panels and are used as a safety rated shutdown for the power supply system.

Personnel Protection System (PPS)

A personnel safety system for the storage ring and beamline front ends is required for dipole power
supply operation. The dipole power supply serves as a critical device for beam shutdown from an access
control interlock for both the beam lines and the storage ring. The backup dipole power supply required
new interfaces be constructed for each of these PPS systems. To allow repair and testing of the offline
power supply the PPS provides automatic bypassing of the offline power supply based on transfer switch
position. The logic and hardware of the PPS system needed to be modified to accommodate the hardware
and new features and to provide full integration of safety into the backup power supply.

Transfer Switch

The transfer switch is an in-house designed mechanism for selecting which dipole power supply –
primary or backup – is connected to the dipole magnet circuit. The switch is comprised of four 11 inch x 2
inch x 0.4 inch slotted copper bars attached to a Glastic carrier plate that allows all four links to be moved
at the same time. The links bolt down onto the cable lug terminations of the 535 MCM DLO magnet
cable.

Standard hardware and engineered Beville washers provide optimum compression of the connections with
under 15 µΩ of electrical resistance. The insulating support is commercially available standard 4500 VAC
design.

Changing the position of the transfer switch requires unbolting the links, sliding the mechanism into the
opposite position, and retightening the bolted connections. This process can be completed in less than 15
minutes.

Position switches attached to the Glastic carrier plate connect to the PPS system, allowing PPS logic to be
bypassed for the system not connected to the dipole magnets.

An inner Lexan cover door behind the cabinet exterior door allows the outer door to be opened for safe
observation of transfer switch position. The Lexan cover is interlocked through the REER Safety Relay.

PSC Regulator Tuning and Calibration

In its intended application, a 4CH PSC would normally be used for precision current regulation for up to
four magnet power supplies. For the backup dipole power supply, the multi-channel resources of a 4CH
PSC were repurposed such that CH1 is still used for current regulation, but channels 2 and 3 are now used
for voltage regulation of the PS1 and PS2 linear amplifiers respectively, while CH4 provides setpoint
control for the Liquablade power converters.

 26

The digital to analog converters (DACs) in channels 2-4 that would normally provide magnet current
setpoints are not used. Instead, a wire connection is added inside the PSC chassis between the PID
regulator output of CH1 and the PID error amplifier inputs of channels 2-4. The CH1 PID regulator
output thus becomes the voltage setpoint for channels 2-4.

For CH1, current feedback comes in the usual way from the DCCT current transducer at the power supply
output to the input of channel 1. But now, PS1 output voltage feedback from the 100:1 attenuator chassis
goes to the input of CH2, and PS2 output voltage feedback from the 100:1 attenuator chassis goes to the
input of CH3. CH1 is tuned for a current regulation loop bandwidth of approximately 2 Hz, while
channels 2 and 3 are each tuned for a voltage regulation loop bandwidth of approximately 1 kHz.

CH4 is configured as a gain of one channel that passes the voltage control signal unchanged to the CH4
PID output from where it connects to the CAN bus controller analog input. Figure 17 illustrates the
scheme for utilizing the 4CH PSC hardware resources to meet the backup dipole power supply’s
regulation loop requirements.

 Figure 17. Block diagram showing modifications to standard 4CH PSC for backup dipole power supply
 regulation feedback loops and Liquablade power converter control.

The PID amplifier section in each channel consists of three identical amplifier stages each of which is
configurable on its own as a specialized compensator including but not limited to PID type control. The
PSC circuit sums the three stages into a composite control output making possible high customization of
pole-zero placement for fine tailoring of the controller’s gain/phase transfer function. Figure 18 is an
excerpt from the PSC circuit schematic drawing that shows the control section, and Figure 19 shows what
a single amplifier stage looks like.

 Figure 18. PSC regulator circuit.

 Figure 19. PSC regulator circuit single stage.

Since current regulation in magnet power supplies is typically dominated by the single pole of the magnet
(L/R time constant), PID control is usually sufficient for delivering high precision, high stability current
regulation. With the PSCs in NSLS-II, standard practice has been to assign one amplifier stage strictly for
proportional control and one amplifier stage strictly for integral control. In special cases, e.g. fast
corrector or ramping power converters, derivative or additional lag-lead compensation might also be used
to improve phase margin of the control loop.

Tuning of the control loops is done using plug-in daughterboards that set the compensation for the
amplifier stage the daughterboard connects to. Circuit board jumpers in the PSC allow for selecting which
of the three amplifier stages in each channel contributes to feedback control.

Figure 20 is a schematic of the compensation daughterboard. A daughterboard used for setting the value
of integral control will usually only have the C3 component installed, and a daughterboard used for
setting the value of proportional control will usually only have the R1 component installed. For the
backup dipole power supply, the current feedback controller on CH1 was set up this way. The CH2 and
CH3 voltage controllers also required some lead-lag compensation, i.e., Type III compensation in the
parlance of power converter control, so it was easier to put all the compensation components onto a single
daughterboard. Figure 21 is a picture of the daughterboards plugged into the PSC regulator board.

Prior to the commissioning of NSLS-II, all magnet power supplies underwent an initial calibration
operation [4]. At that time, an effort was undertaken to build a modest capability in DC current metrology
at NSLS-II, so a primary standard for DC current and an automated calibration system were developed.
The primary dipole power supply was calibrated using this system. Since the backup dipole power supply
was brought online twelve years after commissioning of NSLS-II with all of the machine’s fine tuning
already established, a decision was made not to calibrate the backup dipole power supply against the
primary standard but rather to cross calibrate it against the primary dipole power supply. This was
accomplished by adjusting PSC CH1 gain and offset calibration constants such that at operating current,
the measured B field in the C03G3 90 mm dipole magnet when running with the backup dipole power
supply would match the reading obtained when running with the primary dipole power supply. The B
field in this magnet is continuously monitored by a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) Teslameter
system with a remote probe head placed inside the magnet between the beam chamber and magnet pole
face. Figure 22 is a picture of the NMR Teslameter instrument.

 Figure 22. NMR Teslameter for ultra-precise measurement of uniform DC magnetic field.

The motivation behind cross calibrating the backup dipole power supply to the primary dipole power
supply was to make switching from primary to backup as transparent as possible to machine operators,
enabling injection of beam into the storage ring with good efficiency without requiring adjustment to the
dipole power supply setpoint or other machine parameters.

DC Current Transformer (DCCT)

High precision current feedback to the current regulator circuit comes from a DCCT fluxgate-type current
transducer. The DCCT used in the backup dipole power supply has a current rating of 400 A and a transfer
ratio of 2000:1. Because the bore hole diameter of the DCCT is smaller than the 535 MCM magnet cable,
a transition bar was needed. The assembly was designed as small as possible and positioned in an area of
the rack near the top that was underutilized. The frame was constructed of fiberglass U shaped strut for
insulation purposes. Flexible bus connections from the front of the rack interfaced with the DCCT and
the rear fed the output cables feeding the transfer switch. Figure 23(a) shows a mechanical drawing of
DCCT buswork and framing, and Figure 23(b) shows a picture of the DCCTs mounted at the top of the
power supply rack. The large heatsink in the picture is for the freewheeling diode.

Water Cooling

Each Liquablade power converter chassis and linear amplifier chassis dissipates approximately 800 W
and 3 kW respectively during operation. Water cooling is required to remove the heat from these devices
in the power supply equipment rack. Condensation of water on internal chassis components must be
avoided to prevent damage to the power supply. De Ionized water is not recommended for the Liquablade
power converters, so that leaves us with process chilled water which at NSLS-II is maintained at a
temperature of 55 °F. At this temperature condensation is a real possibility especially on humid days. A
pumping station that controls the water temperature above the dew point is required. After flow and
temperature tests a specification for a pump station was developed. A semi off the shelf unit with a heat
exchanger was purchased. The excess heat from the power supply is transferred to the process chilled
water. The pumping station’s specifications are:

Pump: 3 HP at 3500 RPM
Flow: 35 gpm at 52 PSI
Reservoir capacity: 68 gallon

Testing and Commissioning

The overall strategy for integration and testing of the backup dipole power supply was as follows:

 1. Test and validate in-house developed electronic PCBs and chassis assemblies at the bench.
 2. Move components into backup dipole power supply test stand rack for subsystem electrical
 testing, i.e., electrical testing of partially integrated subassemblies. The test stand rack was
 located on the experimental floor and was built with four pre-production Liquablade power
 converters for development and test early in the project.
 3. Move tested components into power converter and controls racks for build-out on the
 experimental floor in building 740.
 4. Perform system level electrical testing after power converter and controls racks are installed on
 mezzanine and tied into AC power and cooling water system.
 5. Complete development of PLC software, and test protection interlocks on mezzanine.
 6. Integrate PSC current regulation loop around fully integrated PS1 plus PS2 system.

Subsystem electrical testing in the test stand rack brought together four Liquablade power converters, the
100:1 attenuator chassis, one low pass filter, one linear amplifier, and the CAN bus controller into a
functional power supply. The goal of subsystem electrical testing was to gain confidence in the operation
of these components as an integrated system, albeit with lower full power capability. An external voltage
controller test box, built specifically as test support equipment for this project, was used for voltage
feedback control, providing the same functionality as the PSC PID voltage loop controllers without
requiring the complexity of an EPICS controls system. PS1 and PS2 power supplies were tested
individually in the test stand, each requiring its own unique hookup since the PS1 linear amplifier is
positioned on the low side of PS1 and the PS2 linear amplifier is positioned on the high side of PS2.
Figure 26 illustrates the electrical hookups for PS1 and PS2 electrical subsystem testing.

 36

The testing philosophy was based on NSLS-II EE Group experience that if voltage-mode power supplies
could be individually demonstrated to meet all relevant performance requirements, i.e., precision, noise,
bandwidth, and isolation, then 1) connecting two power supplies in series would also work as expected,
and 2) applying the PSC current regulation loop around the whole system would work as predicted by
analysis and simulation models. Both of these expectations did indeed turn out to be correct, and full
system integration and testing were accomplished successfully.

Performance

Initial tests with beam in the NSLS-II storage ring showed that short and long term stability of beam are
comparable to stability seen when operating with the primary dipole power supply. In terms of electrical
performance, short and long term stability of dipole magnet current while operating with beam are shown
in Figure 27. Figure 27(a) shows DCCT current readbacks over an interval of 10 s. Figure 27(b) shows
DCCT current readbacks over a period of 120 hours and a subset of same data over a period of 1000 s.
Long term stability is at the level of 1.9 ppm or 1.9 parts in 106.

 (a)

 (b)

 Figure 27. Magnet current stability. (a) Short-term. (b) Long-term.

It should be noted that the apparent lower standard deviation of long term data is due to the fact that the
data in Figure 27(b) have been processed through a digital filter in the PSC that integrates acquired data
samples over one 60 Hz AC line cycle. This filtering significantly reduces the bandwidth of the
measurement compared to the short term data. In the short term data plot the 1-sigma value of 5.36 ppm
includes much of the digitizer’s intrinsic noise. The 1-sigma standard deviation in the long term data more
closely represents real variations in magnet current, evident from the visually observable correlation
between the DCCT1 and DCCT2 waveforms over periods of tens of seconds.

Figure 28 compares short term stability of the linear amplifier output voltage with the output voltage of
the Liquablade power converters through the proxy of voltage drop across the linear amplifier. Since the
voltage drop plot equates to Liquablade output minus linear amplifier output, and since the linear
amplifier output is essentially constant throughout the data record, variations seen in the voltage drop
waveform are the same variations present in the ~420 V DC output of the Liquablade power converters.

Here it should be noted that the stepwise jumps in the Liquablade output are due to quantization noise in
the ADC in the CAN bus controller and are not inherent to the Liquablde power converters. If the
Liquablade power converters receive a constant valued setpoint over the CAN bus, they will not display
these stepwise jumps in their outputs. The jumps are due to the fact that quantization noise peaks in the
CAN controller’s ADC occasionally cross code boundaries between Liquablade digital setpoints.

 Figure 28. Voltage measurements – PS2 linear amplifier and linear amplifier voltage drop.

Figure 29 shows the dynamic response of the backup dipole power supply to a transient dip in 480 VAC
power in the NSLS-II facility. The transient is nearly an 80 V drop in AC line voltage for a duration of
approximately 0.1 s. This input transient is not detectable in the Liquablade power converter output
voltage to within the resolution of the PSC’s 16-bit ADC digitizer measuring the voltage. However a

 39

small transient just before t=0 with an amplitude of approximately 0.05 V can be observed in the voltage
drop waveform in Figure 28. The significant attenuation of the input AC power line drop disturbance
through the Liquablade power converters results in no detectable transient propagating through the linear
amplifier or into the dipole magnet current. It is reasonable to conclude that the Liquablade power
converter topology which combines an active power factor correction boost regulator input stage with a
buck regulator output stage may provide ride-through performance to short duration AC line dips
comparable to that of the primary dipole power supply which relies on an energy storage capacitor bank
to perform the same function.

Conclusion

A combination of commercial off-the-shelf water-cooled power converters with custom designed passive
and active filter stages and custom controls has been demonstrated to be a successful model for a high
precision 400 kW magnet power supply. Until now, the storage ring dipole power supply has been a single
point failure mode for NSLS-II. The new power supply is expected to improve NSLS-II’s operational
reliability with the availability of a redundant main dipole power supply for the storage ring. The power
supply and controls racks together occupy a small physical footprint along with external wall-mounted
AC switchgear and a pumping station for cooling water. The power supply’s regulated current is stable
over 120 hours with a measured standard deviation of 1.9 parts in 106.
