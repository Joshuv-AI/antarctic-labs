Transdiagnostic child mental health trajectories in Japan: protocol for a nationwide web‑based open cohort study
Authors: Takashi Yamada, Kohei Kurita, Tomoko Nishimura, Makoto Fujii, Arika Yoshizaki, Tomoka Yamamoto, Yoshiko Iwatani, Ikuko Hirata, Eiko Honaga, Ryo Kimura, Masaya Tachibana, Ikuko Mohri, Ryo Kawasaki, Masayuki Endo, Nagahide Takahashi, Taiichi Katayama, Kenji J Tsuchiya, Kuriko Kagitani-Shimono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreventive psychiatry requires longitudinal, transdiagnostic data from before adolescence, yet such data are scarce. We propose a nationwide, digital, open cohort in Japan to track mental health trajectories from birth to late adolescence using multi-informant assessments (caregivers and adolescents) within a transdiagnostic, biopsychosocial framework. Diagnosis-open recruitment will combine community-facing dissemination with clinically linked neurodevelopmental enrichment (e.g. autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), addressing underrepresentation of neurodivergent children and adolescents alongside neurotypical peers. An optional saliva sub-cohort will support genetic and epigenetic analyses. This design will characterize risk and protective factors and their developmental timing to inform stage-specific prevention and early intervention.MethodsFrom February 2026, approximately 7,000 children and caregivers (up to ~14,000 individuals) will be recruited nationwide from community and clinical settings into a longitudinal online cohort. Eligible participants are caregivers aged ≥ 18 years of children and adolescents aged 0–18 years; adolescents aged 13–18 years will also complete self-reports. Enrollment and annual follow-ups will use a secure web platform integrated with LINE, a widely used communication app in Japan. Validated questionnaires will annually assess mental health and development: caregivers report on all children, and adolescents provide self-reports. The principal repeated transdiagnostic outcome is the caregiver-reported Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) Total Difficulties score across ages 2–18 years. Adolescent self-reported scores will be analyzed as complementary informant-specific secondary outcomes. Longitudinal mixed-effects models will examine developmental changes and associations with risk and protective factors, with exploratory genetic and epigenetic analyses.DiscussionThis large longitudinal cohort will generate insights into child and adolescent mental health trajectories in Japan. Following participants from infancy through adolescence will help identify early risk and resilience indicators across traditional diagnoses. Integrating multi-informant surveys with genetic and epigenetic data will clarify how biological, psychological, and social factors interact over time. Nationwide digital recruitment aims to broaden geographic reach and reduce burden; representativeness will be empirically evaluated and findings interpreted cautiously. Results will inform the timing and targets of stage-specific preventive and early interventions in community and clinical settings.Trial registrationJapan Registry of Clinical Trials (jRCT), jRCT1050250205. Background

Mental disorders are among the leading causes of global disability and disease burden and are characterized by chronic and recurrent symptoms [1, 2]. They substantially reduce quality of life, lower productivity, and increase healthcare costs, posing a major challenge to public health systems in high-, middle-, and low-income countries [3]. Despite remarkable advances in etiological research, including neuroscience and genetics, the causal mechanisms underlying most psychiatric syndromes remain poorly understood [4]. Consequently, available treatments, including pharmacotherapy and cognitive behavioral therapy, show only modest efficacy, with low remission rates and frequent recurrence across the lifespan [5–7].

Epidemiological evidence consistently shows that most mental disorders have their onset early in life; consequently, preventive psychiatry has attracted growing attention [8–10]. Approximately three-quarters of all lifetime cases emerge before age 25 years [8, 9], and mental disorders account for 45% of years lived with disability (YLDs) among individuals aged 10–24 years [11]. Furthermore, early-onset cases are often associated with more severe, complex, and persistent clinical trajectories than later-onset cases [12, 13]. These findings underscore an urgent need to reorient psychiatry from its traditional focus on established adult mental illness to a preventive framework centered on early detection and intervention in childhood and adolescence [12, 14]. The aim of preventive psychiatry is to identify risk and protective factors and to intervene early enough to reshape developmental trajectories before chronic illness becomes entrenched [12].

Within this preventive framework, early transdiagnostic detection has attracted growing attention [15–18]. In diagnosis‑specific frameworks—for example, the psychosis at‑risk mental state (ARMS), where attenuated psychotic‑like experiences are used to designate risk for schizophrenia spectrum disorders—risk is tied to a single disorder’s symptom set [19, 20]. However, youth presentations are often transdiagnostic and heterogeneous: subthreshold symptoms and functional changes (e.g., mixtures of anxiety, depressive affect, sleep disturbance, and psychotic‑like experiences) cut across traditional categories and often evolve heterotypically over time, rendering single‑disorder risk ill-suited for early intervention [17, 21]. Large population‑based data likewise show that comorbidity is pervasive and bidirectional across mental disorders, and that cross‑disorder risks are sustained [22]. Accordingly, a transdiagnostic, staging‑based approach offers distinct advantages: it organizes emerging syndromes, targets cross‑cutting risk factors and early features, and supports earlier, broader intervention before full‑blown disorders develop [16–18]. By focusing on shared mechanisms and dimensional indicators rather than on diagnosis-specific approaches, it may better identify at‑risk youth, reduce missed cases arising from heterotypic or subthreshold presentations, and shift trajectories across diverse conditions [16–18].

However, evidence for transdiagnostic preventive psychiatry remains incomplete, particularly regarding protective factors. Most studies have reported on adolescents and young adults, leaving the preadolescent period (0–12 years), a critical window of neurobiological and psychosocial development, understudied [23, 24]. During this early period, rapid changes in brain maturation, social environments, and emotional regulation shape vulnerability or resilience to later mental disorders [18]. Focusing on this preadolescent period can reveal modifiable protective processes, such as self-regulation, secure caregiver-child relationships, and supportive school environments, thereby facilitating the identification of transdiagnostic protective factors and guiding upstream prevention [25, 26].

Furthermore, recent studies highlight that this transdiagnostic model still faces challenges in clinical implementation [16]. It struggles to predict clinical outcomes with high precision because it often lacks detailed assessments of specific symptom dimensions and fails to incorporate the broader biological, psychological, and social factors influencing illness onset and progression [16]. Notably, few longitudinal studies have reported how biological, psychological, and social factors interact across early life to shape mental health trajectories [27, 28]. This evidence gap may limit our ability to identify early transdiagnostic risk and protective factors, and to design effective prevention strategies before the emergence of clinically relevant psychiatric manifestations.

This early-life focus is further warranted because many neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), typically emerge in early childhood and can substantially shape later mental health trajectories [29, 30]. In recent years, the prevalence of NDDs has increased, drawing public health attention [31, 32]. Focusing on ages 0–12 may allow researchers to characterize diverse mental health trajectories among children with NDDs and their neurotypical peers [27, 33]. In this developmental context, internalizing conditions such as anxiety and depressive disorders frequently co-occur with ASD and ADHD, further complicating clinical presentation and potentially influencing long-term outcomes [29, 30]. Further, the timing of symptom emergence or diagnosis can delineate subgroups within a disorder, with childhood-identified cases differing from those identified during adolescence [34, 35]. Together, these observations highlight the need for longitudinal, population-based cohort studies that start in early childhood.

Given the lack of longitudinal early childhood research adopting a transdiagnostic approach, we will launch a large-scale, open, online cohort study that characterizes how mental health risk and protective factors develop from early childhood through adolescence. This cohort will enable systematic collection of data on transdiagnostic symptom dimensions and biological, psychological, and social factors that may influence the onset and course of mental disorders. Recruitment will be diagnosis-open: community-based recruitment will be complemented by clinically linked dissemination to enrich representation of childhood-onset neurodevelopmental conditions (e.g., ASD or ADHD), without constituting a separate disorder-specific clinical arm. By integrating these multilevel data, we will build a developmental model of mental health risk and protective factors that captures how social and biological processes interact over time to shape vulnerability and resilience.

An online, continuously expanding open cohort allows flexible participation across a broad age range and geographic regions, thereby facilitating population-based analyses that reflect real-world diversity. Compared with single-site cohorts, nationwide online recruitment reduces infrastructural burden and per-participant costs while increasing geographic coverage [36, 37]. Although open-cohort designs present methodological challenges, such as potential age imbalance, cohort effects, and challenges in estimating incidence, accelerated longitudinal modeling approaches can mitigate these limitations [38] and efficiently estimate age-related trajectories despite staggered entry times. Taken together, this study design provides a foundation for preventive and early-intervention psychiatry grounded in developmental and population neuroscience.

Methods/design

Study design and setting

This study is designed as a prospective longitudinal open cohort with entirely online recruitment and data collection. It will be conducted nationwide in Japan among individuals from diverse regions and backgrounds. The cohort will be initiated in February 2026, with recruitment proceeding on a rolling basis until the prespecified target sample size is achieved. Participants may enter the cohort at any age between 0 and 18 years and will be followed annually within a birthday-anchored assessment window until age 18 years under the current protocol. Accordingly, the study will be analyzed using an accelerated longitudinal design, in which repeated observations from participants entering at different ages contribute to overlapping age intervals. Chronological age at assessment will serve as the primary developmental timescale. Follow-up beyond age 18 years is not included in the current protocol, although future extension could be considered subject to separate approval.

Participants will be recruited from both community-facing and clinically linked settings using printed and electronic materials (e.g., flyers and online announcements). Community-based dissemination will include channels such as community networks, schools, web- and app-based platforms, and, where feasible, municipality-linked child-rearing information channels and routine municipal touchpoints (e.g., maternal and child health handbook issuance and child-support services). Clinically linked dissemination at study initiation will be centered primarily on child and adolescent mental health outpatient services at the University of Osaka, with possible expansion to additional collaborating institutions.

Clinically linked dissemination is intended to enrich representation of childhood-onset neurodevelopmental conditions—often underrepresented in voluntary cohort studies [39–41]—rather than to constitute a separate clinical arm or comprise the majority of the cohort. Given the voluntary, fully online enrollment and the potential for redistribution of study information across overlapping networks, fixed proportions of recruitment sources are not prespecified. Participant-level recruitment pathway will not be reliably assigned for all participants; therefore, cohort composition will be characterized empirically using baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including caregiver-reported clinician-diagnosed conditions.

Recruitment materials will include a QR code or a link directing caregivers to a secure online registration portal integrated with LINE; however, formal recruitment and consent will not be conducted via LINE itself. Enrollment and electronic informed consent will be completed through the LINE-integrated online platform; detailed recruitment and consent procedures are provided in the Supplementary File.

Reporting is multi‑informant and age‑specific: caregivers (≥18 years) will complete child‑specific questionnaires for each enrolled child aged 0–18 years, and adolescents aged 13–18 years will additionally complete self‑report questionnaires via a secure web-based digital platform accessible across multiple devices (smartphones, tablets, and computers). This approach will allow us to reach participants nationwide while minimizing burdens related to in-person study visits, scheduling, and paper-based questionnaires.

Eligibility criteria

Caregiver–child dyads will be enrolled if they meet the following criteria: caregivers must be adults (≥18 years) residing in Japan, serving as the child’s parent or legal guardian, able to read Japanese, and able to complete online surveys using a compatible digital device (e.g., smartphone, tablet, or computer). Children must be aged 0–18 years. Caregivers will enroll one or more age-eligible children; when multiple children are enrolled, a separate dyad and corresponding survey set will be created for each child. Survey participation is age-specific: adolescents aged 13–18 years will be invited to complete self-report modules in addition to caregiver reports, whereas children aged < 13 years will participate only via caregiver reports (i.e., no direct child survey).

The cohort is diagnosis-open and does not exclude children with psychiatric or neurodevelopmental conditions. The clinically linked pathway at study initiation serves as a targeted neurodevelopmental enrichment pathway, reflecting the case mix of the initial collaborating specialty service and focusing on childhood-onset neurodevelopmental conditions such as ASD, ADHD, and related conditions. Children with other psychiatric disorders remain eligible through broader community-facing and healthcare-linked recruitment pathways and will be characterized longitudinally using caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses and dimensional symptom measures.

Individuals will be excluded if they are unable to provide informed consent or adequate study data, including (a) caregivers (or adolescent participants) who cannot understand study information provided in Japanese, (b) those without access to a compatible digital device (smartphone, tablet, or computer) or who are unable to operate basic online survey functions, and (c) cases deemed unsafe or impracticable by the investigators (e.g., severe illness in the child or caregiver that would preclude participation). No exclusions are made based on a child’s specific diagnoses or mental health status.

Primary objectives

The study’s primary objectives are as follows:

1. Longitudinal trajectories of child mental health

To characterize longitudinal trajectories of cross-cutting dimensional symptoms and emotional/behavioral well-being from infancy and early childhood through age 18 years in a nationwide Japanese cohort, using a transdiagnostic framework [16–18]. In this protocol, “transdiagnostic” refers to the assessment of symptom dimensions, functioning, and contextual factors across diagnostic categories, rather than to systematic diagnostic ascertainment or linkage to electronic health records within the cohort.

2. Integration of biological and environmental data for prevention

To integrate genetic and epigenetic data from the saliva sub‑cohort with phenotypic data to assess biological contributions (e.g., polygenic risk) and potential gene–environment interactions relevant to early, targeted interventions and preventive strategies.

3. Identification of risk and protective factors

To identify demographic, environmental (family, school, and community), and psychosocial risk and protective factors associated with child and adolescent mental health outcomes, and to pinpoint the critical developmental periods when these factors exert the strongest influence.

Data collection and assessments

Data will be collected via secure, web-based surveys integrated with the LINE messaging platform (a widely used communication app in Japan). LINE will function as a notification and routing channel to the study-specific secure web application; all responses will be entered into and stored within the study database, not within LINE itself. The baseline survey may be completed at any time of the year. Annual follow-up assessments will be scheduled within a one-month window before or after each child’s birthday until age 18 years, with automated reminders delivered via LINE. Caregivers will complete age-appropriate measures for all enrolled children (aged 0–18 years). Adolescents aged 13–18 years will additionally complete self-report mental health questionnaires via a secure, adolescent-specific workflow within the web platform.

The minimum age of 13 years for self-report was selected to support private and independent participation, consistent with national data indicating high rates of personal smartphone ownership among Japanese junior high school students. According to a 2024 survey by Japan’s Children and Families Agency, smartphone ownership increases from 43% in late primary school to approximately 80% by junior high school [42]. The adolescent assent and self-report workflow will be distinct from the caregiver consent process and designed to support private and independent completion. Caregiver consent will not provide caregivers with access to adolescent responses through the study platform. Nevertheless, because surveys are completed remotely in participants’ home environments, privacy at the point of completion cannot be fully controlled by the study team, particularly when devices are shared or others are nearby.

To support sustained participation over the long duration of the cohort study, non-responders will receive automated follow-ups and, when needed, personalized messages. Brief newsletters or infographics will be distributed periodically via LINE to inform participants about study milestones or general findings without sharing personal data. Participant contact and background information, including updates to residential details where applicable, will be updated at each assessment to reduce loss to follow-up due to relocation or changes in contact details. These updates will also enable secondary analyses incorporating residential or area-based context when sufficiently detailed data are available. Together, these measures aim to promote retention and minimize missing data attributable to dropout.

To minimize respondent burden, assessments will comprise a core annual battery supplemented by age- or need-based modules. Caregiver-reported SDQ Total Difficulties will serve as the principal repeated transdiagnostic anchor across the broadest developmental span (ages 2–18 years), reflecting its consistent administration across childhood and adolescence. As no single instrument spans the full 0–18-year range, infancy and other developmentally specific periods will be characterized using age-appropriate measures and will not be assumed to be directly comparable with later SDQ scores. Adolescent self-reported measures, including the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) self-report, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 modified for Adolescents (PHQ-9A) [43, 44], and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) [45], will be analyzed as complementary, informant-specific secondary outcomes rather than combined with caregiver reports into a single composite metric.

Additional caregiver- and adolescent-reported modules will assess domain-specific symptoms, functioning, and contextual factors, including attention, social communication, sleep, school functioning, media use, parenting stress, caregiver mental health, family context, life events, and residential changes. Adaptive survey routing presents modules based on participant age and prior responses. The complete instrument catalog, including informant type (caregiver vs adolescent), age-by-wave schedule, and analytic role of each measure, is provided in Tables 1, 2, 3 and aligned with the birthday-anchored schedule. In addition to continuous scale scores, clinically relevant events will be captured annually via caregiver report (e.g., new clinician-diagnosed mental health or neurodevelopmental conditions, school attendance/absenteeism, and service utilization).

All instruments will either have Japanese validated versions or will be translated following standard procedures. To reduce respondent burden, each annual survey is kept concise, focusing on core mental health outcomes and key predictors. The online survey platform allows adaptive questioning; thus, certain modules are presented based on the child’s age or previous responses (e.g., questions about school experiences are shown only if the child is school-aged).

Up to 2,000 children (ages 0–18) will be invited to an optional saliva sub‑study at baseline or a designated follow‑up. Participants who opt in will receive a home collection kit (Oragene Discover OGR-675; DNA Genotek, Ottawa, Canada) with pre‑paid return materials. Caregivers will collect the sample using age‑appropriate methods (e.g., assisted collection using the absorbent sponges supplied with the OGR-675 kit) and mail it back to the laboratory. DNA will be extracted and stored in a biobank for genetic analyses, with a subset to be processed for DNA methylation profiling. Samples will be labeled only with a unique study ID (no personal identifiers). Consent and assent procedures for biospecimen collection are described under “Enrollment and consent procedure.” Because participation in the saliva sub-study is optional, the final number of available biospecimens will depend on caregiver consent and, where applicable, adolescent assent may be lower than the planned 2,000 samples. We will consider these constraints when interpreting the results of genetic and epigenetic analyses.

All study data will be handled using a secure, access-controlled, cloud-based database operated under approved data-management procedures. Data will be protected by appropriate safeguards, including encryption or equivalent technical measures during transmission and storage, separation of identifying information from research data using unique study identifiers, restricted access for authorized study personnel, and prespecified retention and disposal procedures. Detailed operational procedures are provided in the Supplementary File.

Outcomes and measures

The primary outcome hierarchy is defined to support interpretability across age groups and informants. The principal repeated outcome is caregiver-reported SDQ Total Difficulties score across ages 2–18 years. Other caregiver-reported standardized measures, including age-appropriate developmental and symptom measures, will be treated as secondary or modular outcomes. For infancy and other periods requiring developmentally specific instruments, interpretation will focus on construct- and instrument-specific trajectories rather than direct raw-score equivalence with later childhood or adolescent questionnaires. Adolescent self-reported SDQ, PHQ-9A/SMFQ, GAD-7, and other sensitive-content modules will be analyzed as complementary informant-specific secondary outcomes. In the overlapping age range (13–18 years), caregiver-adolescent agreement and discrepancy will be described in secondary analyses and, where appropriate, examined using parallel models. Clinically relevant events, including caregiver-reported new clinician diagnoses, school attendance problems (e.g., chronic absenteeism), and service utilization, will be analyzed as secondary outcomes or clinical-context variables. The current protocol does not include structured diagnostic interviews or routine longitudinal electronic health record linkage; therefore, diagnostic variables will be interpreted as caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses rather than independently validated diagnostic outcomes.

Key exposures, predictors, and covariates are summarized in Table 3. These include baseline characteristics/covariates (e.g., child sex, age, socioeconomic indicators, developmental history, and baseline neurodevelopmental status) and time-varying environmental and contextual exposures measured longitudinally (e.g., family structure, caregiver mental health, parenting stress, parenting and home environment, school context, sleep, media use, life events, service utilization, and residential/contact changes). Clinical-context variables, including caregiver-reported clinician-diagnosed conditions, will also be incorporated. Where sufficiently detailed residential data are available, area-level context may be examined in secondary analyses (e.g., neighborhood-level factors), although such linkage analyses are not a primary aim of the present protocol. Of special interest are predictors related to neurodevelopmental status and whether risk or protective factors such as media use, sleep, parenting stress, caregiver mental health, and school context have differential associations in neurodivergent youth.

From the genetic and epigenetic data, additional exploratory analytic variables will be derived. In the subset of participants with available genetic data, polygenic risk scores (PRS) will be computed for relevant psychiatric conditions, including, but not limited to, schizophrenia, depression, anxiety disorders, ADHD, and ASD, using standard methods (e.g., clumping and thresholding or LDpred, as appropriate) with reference summary statistics from large-scale genome-wide association studies [86, 87]. Epigenetic measures, such as DNA methylation at stress-related gene loci or epigenome-wide methylation patterns, may serve as exploratory biological outcomes or predictors [88]. These genetic and epigenetic measures are considered exploratory components of the sub-study and will be analyzed alongside survey data to investigate biopsychosocial mechanisms.

Sample size and power considerations

The target sample size of 7,000 caregiver–child dyads (up to approximately 14,000 individuals) is based on feasibility considerations and illustrative power and precision considerations for the planned longitudinal analyses. As an open longitudinal cohort with rolling enrollment and annual, birthday-anchored follow-up, effective power and precision will depend on realized recruitment and retention, subgroup prevalence, the average number of assessment waves contributed per participant, outcome variance, and the within-person correlation structure. Therefore, the numerical examples below should be interpreted as approximate benchmarks rather than definitive power estimates.

As a cross-sectional benchmark, if the smaller subgroup comprises approximately 7–10% of the cohort, a total sample of 7,000 dyads would yield high statistical power (approximately 90% or greater, two-sided α = 0.05) to detect a small standardized mean difference of approximately Cohen’s d = 0.15 between groups, assuming complete data and conventional independent-group approximations. In the planned mixed-effects trajectory models, repeated observations may improve precision relative to a single cross-sectional comparison; however, the magnitude of this gain will depend on the number of follow-up waves, retention, and within-person correlations. Conversely, attrition, sparse data in some age intervals, or lower-than-expected subgroup prevalence may reduce effective power. Thus, subgroup analyses, including analyses of children with caregiver-reported clinician-diagnosed neurodevelopmental conditions, will be interpreted in light of realized subgroup sizes and follow-up density. For example, if approximately 5% of the enrolled cohort meets this criterion, approximately 350 children would be available for subgroup trajectory analyses prior to accounting for attrition and incomplete follow-up.

For the optional saliva sub-study, a planned maximum of 2,000 participants provides a basis for exploratory genetic and epigenetic analyses. Under simplified assumptions, this sample size may be informative for detecting modest polygenic score associations, for example those explaining approximately 1–2% of outcome variance. However, effective power will depend on the number of available biospecimens, genotype quality control, ancestry structure, outcome completeness, multiple-testing burden, and the availability of longitudinal phenotypic data. Accordingly, genetic and epigenetic analyses will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Overall, no single definitive sample size calculation is appropriate for the broad aims of this open observational cohort. Instead, the target sample is intended to support robust descriptive estimation, longitudinal trajectory modeling, and sensitivity analyses, while acknowledging that precision for specific subgroup, interaction, and biomarker analyses will depend on the observed cohort composition and follow-up patterns.

Statistical analysis plan

Analyses will proceed in phases corresponding to baseline characterization and longitudinal objectives. All analyses will be conducted using appropriate statistical software (e.g., R or SPSS), with a detailed statistical analysis plan finalized before locking data for each wave. Baseline characteristics of the cohort will be summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean scores and prevalence estimates for mental health indicators, as well as distributions of key demographic and clinical characteristics. As the cohort is voluntary and recruited online, it will not be assumed to be nationally representative a priori. Representativeness will be evaluated empirically by comparing baseline distributions of key characteristics (e.g., age, sex, region/prefecture, and available socioeconomic indicators) with relevant Japanese national benchmarks where available. For analyses intended to approximate population-level prevalence or trajectories, calibration or post-stratification weighting and inverse-probability weighting will be considered as sensitivity analyses. Baseline comparisons across subgroups (e.g., by age group, sex, or caregiver-reported neurodevelopmental status) will be conducted using appropriate descriptive and inferential methods to assess potential sampling differences.

Longitudinal trajectories of child mental health will be estimated using mixed-effects regression models (also referred to as multilevel models or growth curve models) [89, 90]. Chronological age at assessment will be the primary timescale for developmental analyses. Given rolling enrollment and birthday-anchored annual follow-up, participants entering at different ages will contribute repeated observations to overlapping age intervals in an accelerated longitudinal design. For each continuous outcome (e.g., caregiver-reported SDQ Total Difficulties or adolescent self-reported PHQ-9A/GAD-7 scores), age at assessment will be included as a fixed effect, with a random intercept for each child and random slopes where supported by the data. Both linear and nonlinear age effects will be examined using categorical age terms, spline functions, or polynomial terms, as appropriate. Calendar year of assessment will be included as a covariate to address potential period effects. As a sensitivity analysis, an alternative parameterization incorporating age at entry and time since enrollment will be fitted to distinguish between-participant differences at cohort entry from within-participant longitudinal change.

Analyses will adhere to the predefined measurement hierarchy. Caregiver-reported SDQ Total Difficulties will be modeled as the principal repeated transdiagnostic outcome across ages 2–18 years. For measures repeated over time, longitudinal measurement comparability will be assessed where feasible using measurement invariance analyses or related sensitivity analyses. When different age-appropriate instruments are required across developmental stages, interpretation will focus on construct- and instrument-specific trajectories rather than direct raw-score equivalence across questionnaires.

To investigate gene–environment interplay underlying child and adolescent mental health trajectories, genetic and epigenetic measures will be incorporated into longitudinal statistical models. PRS will be standardized and included as predictors in regression models to examine their associations with baseline mental health and developmental trajectories, including PRS × time interaction terms [91]. For example, we will test whether a higher PRS for ADHD is associated with higher initial hyperactivity scores and a slower decline in these scores with age, consistent with the persistence of ADHD-related traits. Genetic analyses will account for population stratification as appropriate (e.g., by including principal components derived from genetic data), although the cohort is expected to be predominantly of Japanese ancestry. Epigenetic analyses will be exploratory and hypothesis-generating: epigenome-wide association analyses will be conducted to identify baseline DNA methylation sites associated with stress exposure or mental health outcomes, and, where repeated samples are available, to examine longitudinal changes in methylation patterns within individuals. Given the sample size and the burden of multiple testing, results from these analyses will be interpreted cautiously.

To identify risk and protective factors, exposures and covariates summarized in Table 3 will be incorporated into mixed-effects models. Key a priori factors include child sex, socioeconomic indicators, baseline neurodevelopmental status, baseline mental health status, caregiver mental health, parenting stress, sleep, media use, school context, life events, and other family or environmental variables. Interactions with age will be used to examine whether associations vary across developmental stages and to identify potential sensitive periods. Moderation analyses will evaluate whether selected risk or protective factors have differential associations among neurodivergent youth. Analyses comparing community-facing and clinically linked recruitment sources will be avoided when source cannot be reliably assigned at the participant level. Instead, the degree of clinical enrichment will be characterized using baseline clinical information such as caregiver-reported clinician-diagnosed conditions and dimensional symptom measures.

Longitudinal changes in diagnostic profile and comorbidity burden will be examined as secondary analyses. Annual incidence of new caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses will be summarized, along with the emergence of additional diagnoses over follow-up as an index of comorbidity burden. Where repeated diagnostic information is sufficient, changes in broad diagnostic profile across waves will be described. These outcomes will be analyzed, as appropriate, using generalized mixed-effects models for repeated binary or count data. Where feasible, exploratory state-transition analyses will be conducted to characterize movement between no-diagnosis, single-diagnosis, and multimorbidity states. All diagnostic analyses will be interpreted as changes in caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses rather than formally validated diagnostic transitions.

Missing data are anticipated at the item level (e.g., skipped individual questionnaire items) and the wave level (participants lost to follow-up or skipping an entire year’s survey). For item-level missingness in validated questionnaires, we will use instrument-specific rules specified in scoring manuals or prespecified study scoring procedures. For example, SDQ subscales will be scored only when at least 3 of 5 items are completed, and the Total Difficulties Score will be computed only when all four relevant subscales are computable. For PHQ-9/PHQ-9A, GAD-7, SMFQ, and other scales without prespecified prorating rules in this protocol, incomplete scale scores will not be prorated and will instead be treated as missing. Detailed missing-item handling rules are provided in Supplementary Table S1. For wave-level missingness, our primary mixed-effects analyses can accommodate missing outcome data under a missing-at-random (MAR) assumption by using all available data points for each individual [92]. To support the plausibility of this assumption, models will include variables associated with dropout (e.g., age or baseline scores), and additional sensitivity analyses will be conducted. We will perform sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation for key outcomes and covariates in each wave [92]. Multiple imputations by chained equations will be used to generate several complete datasets, which will then be analyzed and pooled to ensure that conclusions align with the mixed-model approach. We will compare baseline characteristics of participants who remain in the study with those lost to follow-up to assess attrition bias, and, if differences are found, we may apply inverse probability weighting as another sensitivity check.

Discussion

In this protocol, we describe a large and inclusive cohort study with several notable strengths. Below, we highlight five major strengths of the study design.

First, approximately 7,000 children and their caregivers (up to ~14,000 individuals) across Japan will be enrolled, making this one of the largest longitudinal studies of child and adolescent mental health in the country. The broad age range (0–18 years) and targeted effort to enrich representation of neurodivergent children (e.g., those with ASD or ADHD) alongside neurotypical peers will improve the public health relevance of the findings. By combining community-facing recruitment with clinically linked neurodevelopmental enrichment, the cohort captures a wide spectrum of developmental and mental health profiles, from typical development to high-risk conditions. This diverse, transdiagnostic sampling is crucial for identifying both shared and unique trajectories of mental well-being in youth.

Second, a major strength of the study is the fully digital, low-burden data collection platform for enrollment and annual follow-up. The LINE-integrated workflow enables reminders and routing to the secure study web application while keeping research responses in the study database rather than in LINE. This approach enables nationwide reach and convenient participation at low operational cost, while minimizing data entry errors through automated capture. Electronically administered questionnaires yield results equivalent to traditional paper forms [93]. Web-based surveys also offer efficiency and broad coverage, although researchers must address potential issues such as selective nonresponse, digital access bias, and data quality [94]. Our remote design reduces geographical barriers and ensures continuity of data collection even if participants relocate or in-person sessions become impractical. Overall, the use of a secure online system makes longitudinal participation more feasible for busy families and enhances consistency in follow-up data collection.

Third, this study places particular emphasis on the preadolescent developmental window (0–12 years), which is a critical period often underrepresented in mental health research. By tracking children from infancy through childhood, we can detect the early emergence of behavioral and emotional difficulties and can observe how risk and protective factors evolve before adolescence. Many influences on later mental health (e.g., self-regulation skills, family environment, school support) originate in early childhood [95–97]; capturing these in our cohort will clarify their timing and impact while interventions may still modify their course. In addition, following participants from the first signs of any neurodevelopmental condition allows for nuanced comparisons between neurodivergent and neurotypical pathways. We will be able to distinguish disorder-specific patterns from broader developmental trends, and to examine whether the timing of diagnosis or symptom onset delineates subgroups with distinct trajectories. This longitudinal breadth, spanning from the earliest developmental stages into the teenage years, is expected to yield insights into how early-life experiences and neurodevelopment shape mental health outcomes across childhood and adolescence.

Fourth, the study’s transdiagnostic, biopsychosocial framework is another key advantage. Rather than focusing on single diagnoses, we will measure dimensional symptoms and cross-cutting risk/protective factors across biological, psychological, and social domains. This approach aligns with emerging youth mental health paradigms that favor clinical staging and interventions based on level of need over traditional diagnostic boundaries [16]. Early presentations in children and adolescents often involve mixed or subthreshold symptoms spanning multiple domains, which a transdiagnostic design can capture more effectively. By integrating assessments of common mechanisms (for example, sleep problems, stress reactivity, parenting behaviors) and resilience factors across conditions, our cohort can highlight shared pathways of risk and targets for prevention that might be overlooked in a disorder-specific study. This orientation is supported by recent research suggesting that overreliance on rigid diagnostic categories has impeded progress in understanding and supporting youth with neurodevelopmental and mental health difficulties [98]. In contrast, a transdiagnostic longitudinal dataset allows us to explore how diverse factors interact over time to influence a range of mental health outcomes, potentially informing more flexible, personalized intervention strategies.

Fifth, a further strength of our design is the multi-informant data collection. Caregivers will provide reports for all participating children, and adolescents (13–18 years) will complete self-report questionnaires about their own mental health. This dual-informant strategy enhances the richness and reliability of the data by capturing different perspectives on the child’s well-being [99]. For instance, teenagers can report internalized feelings or thoughts that may not be evident to their parents, whereas caregivers can report on observable behaviors, school performance, or other contextual factors that adolescents might not fully disclose. Using multiple informants thus helps to overcome the well-known discrepancies that often occur between youth self-reports and parent reports, which can otherwise impact assessment and treatment planning [100]. The adolescent-specific workflow is designed to support private completion and to prevent caregiver access to adolescent responses within the platform. Together, the multi-informant approach will provide a more comprehensive picture of each participant’s mental health than reliance on a single informant alone [99], thereby strengthening outcome measures.

Limitations: Despite its strengths, this study has some potential limitations. First, as a voluntary online cohort, it is subject to selection bias: families who participate via the internet may not represent the general population and may be more health-conscious, technologically comfortable, or of higher socioeconomic status, whereas those with limited internet access may be underrepresented [94]. In addition, no regional quotas are prespecified, and clinically linked dissemination may further enrich the sample for neurodevelopmental or other clinical characteristics. Therefore, the cohort should not be assumed to be nationally representative a priori, and prevalence estimates will be interpreted cautiously. Representativeness will be evaluated empirically by comparing baseline demographic and regional distributions with national benchmarks where possible. For analyses intended to approximate population-level estimates, calibration or post-stratification weighting and inverse-probability weighting will be considered as sensitivity analyses. Although clinically linked recruitment may introduce imbalance relative to the general population, it also allows the cohort to capture a wider spectrum of mental health and neurodevelopmental profiles than would be possible with a purely community-based sample.

Second, because participation is fully online and self-administered, there is a limited opportunity to verify responses or to ensure uniform understanding of questions across diverse respondents [100]. Differences in interpretation, particularly for psychological items translated from validated scales, could introduce measurement variability across age groups or cultural contexts. In addition, although adolescent self-report is completed through a separate workflow with restricted caregiver access within the platform, privacy at the point of survey completion cannot be fully controlled in home environments, particularly when devices are shared or others are present. Measurement variability can be partially mitigated through analytic approaches such as measurement invariance testing, latent variable modeling, and sensitivity analyses that examine the robustness of findings across subgroups.

Third, all mental health measures are obtained through questionnaires rather than structured clinical interviews, which may introduce reporting bias and limit diagnostic precision. Information on prior and incident conditions is based on caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses, and routine longitudinal electronic health record linkage is not included in the current protocol. Although this approach is pragmatic and scalable, findings involving diagnostic profile, diagnostic shifts, or comorbidity burden should be interpreted as changes in caregiver-reported clinician diagnoses rather than independently validated diagnostic transitions.

Finally, another challenge is related to long-term retention. Under the current protocol, follow-up is limited to age 18 years and does not extend into young adulthood. With annual follow-ups extending over many years, participant attrition may lead to missing data or bias, although several retention strategies have been implemented to mitigate this risk (see "Data collection and assessments" in the Methods section).

In conclusion, using this nationwide open cohort will provide a unique platform to advance the understanding of developmental mental health across childhood and adolescence. By adopting a transdiagnostic framework from early childhood and integrating multi-informant and biological data, we address key gaps in preventive psychiatry. Despite anticipated challenges, the resulting longitudinal dataset is expected to inform early detection, prevention, and stage-specific intervention, contributing to developmentally informed preventive psychiatry in children and adolescents.
