 SECTION 1.0
 INTRODUCTION

 A number of Hanford high level waste (HLW) streams contain sufficiently high
concentrations of chromium for it to become the waste loading limiting component in the waste.
Chromium has relatively low solubility in borosilicate glasses and leads to the formation of
various crystal phases including spinels. The extent of crystal formation must be managed during
melter processing to prevent operational issues and potential premature melter failure that can
result from excessive accumulation of crystals in the melter. In previous work for the
Department of Energy (DOE) Office of River Protection (ORP), high-waste loading glass
formulations were developed for representative HLW streams that are high in chromium [1].
Subsequently, melter tests conducted with different forms of chromium, either Cr2O3 or
Cr(NO3)3.9H2O, resulted in differing levels of crystallization in the product glass [2].
Furthermore, this study showed that the addition of nitrate to the feed as an oxidizer reduced the
amount of crystals otherwise observed in the discharged glass [2], demonstrating that controlling
the redox conditions in the melter feed and therefore the cold cap can limit the formation of
crystalline phases. In another set of studies for ORP using Hanford HLW with high
concentrations of aluminum, iron, and manganese it was shown that the form of these
constituents in the HLW strongly influenced the processing characteristics of the respective feeds
[3-6]. This report describes the results from work to elucidate the effects of the form of
chromium on glass crystallization, feed properties, and feed processing characteristics on
Hanford HLW feeds with high concentrations of Cr. The sources of chromium that were
investigated include commercially available Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4), which are two
forms of chromium that are reported to be present in Hanford HLW. The effects of added
oxidants while processing Cr(OH)3 was also be investigated. As described in the Test Plan for
this work [7], the objectives were accomplished through a combination of crucible-scale tests,
small-scale tests with additives to influence redox state, and melter tests. This work builds on
previous work performed at the Vitreous State Laboratory (VSL) of The Catholic University of
America (CUA) for ORP to increase the waste loadings and processing rates of HLW glass
formulations [1, 3, 4, 6, 8-14].

 The baseline HLW glass formulations for the Hanford Tank Waste Treatment and
Immobilization Plant (WTP) have been specified to ensure that the glasses are homogenous,
contain essentially no crystalline phases, are processable in joule-heated, ceramic-lined melters
and meet WTP Contract terms. Thus, they are relatively conservative in terms of achievable
waste loadings. The overall WTP mission will require the immobilization of tank waste
compositions that are dominated by mixtures of aluminum, chromium, bismuth, iron,
phosphorous, zirconium, and sulfur compounds as waste loading limiting components. Glass
compositions for several of these waste mixtures have been developed based upon previous
experience and current glass property models [15-18]. In order to improve waste loadings, ORP
has initiated a testing program to develop and characterize HLW glasses for wastes that are
limited by Al, Al plus Na, Bi, Cr, Sr, Zr, P, S, etc. [19-21]. Results of this work have
demonstrated the feasibility of increases in waste loadings from about 25 wt% to 33-50 wt%

 10

(based on oxide loading) in the glass, depending on the waste stream. It is expected that these
higher waste loading glasses will reduce the HLW canister production requirement by about 25%
or more [10].

 Under a separate contract with Bechtel National, Incorporated (BNI) to support the WTP,
VSL has developed and tested glass formulations for WTP HLW waste compositions to provide
data to meet the WTP contract requirements and to support system design activities [22-27]. That
work was based upon small­scale batch melts (“crucible melts”) using waste simulants. Selected
formulations were also tested in small-scale, continuously fed, joule-heated melters (DuraMelter
(DM)100 and DM1200) [28-33]. That testing was directed towards waste streams from the then-
planned early feed tanks for the WTP (i.e., AZ-101, AZ-102, C-106/AY-102, and
C-104/AY-101). These wastes are high in iron (AZ-101, AZ-102 and C-106/AY-102) or thorium
(C-104/AY-101) and are significantly different from those used in enhancement tests performed
by VSL for ORP (i.e., wastes limited by Al, Al/Na, Bi, and Cr). Baseline glass formulations to
treat these high-Fe wastes were developed under the BNI contract. During that time, the glass
throughput requirement for the HLW melter was initially 400 kg/(m2·day), which was
subsequently increased to 800 kg/(m2·day). As a result, the baseline high-Fe HLW glass
formulations for WTP perform only slightly better than the 800 kg/(m2·day) processing rate
requirement. Furthermore, the baseline waste loadings for the Fe-limited HLW compositions are
only slightly higher than the BNI contract minimum. Since that time, in work performed for ORP
on other HLW compositions, VSL has developed small-scale test methods to assess processing
rates of melter feeds and included them as an integral part of glass formulation development.
This methodology was used successfully to develop glass formulations for high-Al Hanford
HLW that showed processing rates in excess of 2000 kg/(m2·day) while achieving high waste
loadings. The same methodology can be applied to the development of improved glass
formulations for other Hanford HLW in order to provide ORP with a significantly more robust
operating envelope with reduced risk of throughput shortfalls.

 A number of Hanford HLW streams contain high concentrations of chromium. The
current WTP contract minimum for chromium in chromium-limited HLW glasses is 0.5 wt%
Cr2O3. Oxidative leaching has been included as part of the WTP pretreatment process to reduce
the concentration of chromium in the feed to the HLW vitrification facility. However, if higher
concentrations of chromium can be tolerated in the HLW glass, oxidative leaching can
potentially be eliminated from the pretreatment process or the number of HLW streams that
undergo oxidative leaching can be minimized without substantial increase in the number of
canisters produced from Cr-limited HLW streams. Such work would also support the
consideration of scenarios in which some or all of the steps in the pretreatment facility are
bypassed (e.g., direct feed HLW).

 Chromium is frequently identified in Hanford tank waste as mixed phases with other
elements such as aluminum, iron, and nickel. Leach tests on REDOX sludge showed that a large
fraction (up to 60%) of the chromium was entrained in boehmite [34]. The “Hanford Waste
Mineralogy Reference Report” [35] summarizes known mineralogical phases for solids found in
Hanford tank waste and lists the following chromium-containing species: donathite
[Fe(Fe,Cr)2O4], chromite (FeCr2O4), hydrogarnet-uvarovite [(Ca,Sr)3(Cr,Al)2(OH)12],
Fe-Cr-Ni-O-C crystals, and Al-Cr rich amorphous phases. Sodium chromate (Na2CrO4) was

 11

found in trace amounts; this is consistent with the high solubility of chromate such that much of
it would be expected to report to the supernate rather than the sludge. Oxides and hydroxides of
chromium were identified in waste samples in all degrees of hydration: Cr(OH)3, CrOOH, and
Cr2O3 [36].

 Previous work has shown that the form of chromium in the waste simulant appears to
affect Cr-containing spinel crystallization in the glass [1, 2]. The present report describes work
performed to further investigate the effects of Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4) on spinel
crystallization and the use of nitrate as an additive to feeds containing Cr(OH)3 in order to
influence its behavior during feed processing in the melter. The sources of chromium
investigated in this work include commercially available Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4),
which are two forms of chromium that are reported to be abundant in Hanford HLW. The use of
commercially available forms of chromium hydroxide and chromite is necessary in view of the
quantities required for melter testing. Prior to use, these materials were characterized with
respect to the chemical compositions and the amounts and types of phases that are present. The
work included preparation and characterization of crucible melts to assess the tendency towards
crystal formation for feeds with Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4) at variable nitrate contents.
The results from crucible-scale testing supported DM100 melter tests designed to further assess
the tendency towards spinel formation as a function of chromium form and feed nitrate content.
The DM100-BL, which is one of two DM100 melter systems installed at VSL, was selected as
the platform for these tests due to its use in the first phase of testing with this high chromium
waste and extensive previous use in processing rate determination for various HLW streams and
glass compositions [1-6, 9-14, 29-33, 37-45].

1.1 Test Objectives

 The primary objective of the work described herein was to determine the effect of two
forms of chromium reported to be abundant in the waste (Cr(OH)3 and chromite) on spinel
formation in the glass, feed properties, and feed processing characteristics during the vitrification
process. A secondary objective of the work was to evaluate oxidants that influence the redox
state of chromium in the glass, which has the potential to reduce spinel crystallization thereby
allowing potential increases in waste loadings in HLW glasses with high chromium content. This
was accomplished through a combination of crucible-scale and DM100 melter system tests.

1.2 Quality Assurance

 This work was conducted under a quality assurance program that is in place at the VSL.
The program is compliant with applicable criteria of 10 CFR 830.120; Office of Civilian Waste

1.3 DM100 Melter System

        1.3.1    DM100 Feed System

 A schematic diagram of the DM100 vitrification system is shown in Figure 1.1. The
melter feed is introduced in batches into a feed container that is mounted on a load cell for
weight monitoring. The feed is stirred with a variable speed mixer and constantly recirculated
except for periodic, momentary interruptions during which the weight is recorded. A peristaltic
pump is used to provide a uniform delivery of feed to the melt surface. Feed is directed from the
recirculation loop that extends to the top of the melter and then diverted to the peristaltic pump,
which regulates the flow of feed through a Teflon-lined feed line and water-cooled feed tube into
the melter.

 1.3.2 Melter System

 Cross-sectional diagrams of the DM100-BL melter are shown in Figures 1.2.a-c. The
DM100-BL unit is a ceramic refractory-lined melter fitted with five electrodes: two pairs of
opposing Inconel 690 plate electrodes and a bottom electrode. Power can be supplied in either
three-phase or single-phase configurations. All of the tests in the present work were performed
with the upper and lower electrodes on each side connected together and powered by a
single-phase supply; the bottom electrode was not powered. Melt pool agitation is achieved by
either a removable lance entering from the top of the melter or a permanent bubbler installed
through the bottom electrode. In these tests, the lance bubbler was used. The glass product is
removed from the melter by means of an airlift discharge system. The melter has a melt surface
area of 0.108 m 2 and a variable glass inventory of between 110 kg, when only the bottom pair of
 P
 P

electrodes is used, and about 170 kg when both pairs of electrodes are used, which was the case
in the present tests.

 1.3.3 Off-Gas System

 For operational simplicity, the DM100-BL is equipped with a dry off-gas treatment
system involving gas filtration operations only. Exhaust gases leave the melter plenum through a
film cooler device that minimizes the formation of solid deposits. The film-cooler air has
constant flow rate and its temperature is thermostatically controlled. Consequently, under
steady-state operating conditions, the exhaust gases passing through the transition line (between
the melter and the first filtration device) can be sampled at constant temperature and airflow rate.
The geometry of the transition line conforms to the requirements of the 40-CFR-60 air sampling
techniques. Immediately downstream of the transition line are cyclonic filters followed by
conventional pre-filters and high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters. The temperature of the
cyclonic filters is maintained above 150°C while the temperatures in the HEPAs are kept

 13

sufficiently high to prevent moisture condensation. The entire train of gas filtration operations is
duplicated and each train is used alternately. An induced draft fan completes the system.

1.4 Experimental Procedures and Methods

 1.4.1 Crystallinity in Glass Samples after Isothermal Heat Treatment

 Melter feed samples were heat-treated in covered crucibles. For each test, the dried
melter feed (to yield 20 grams of glass) was added to a platinum crucible (200 ml), which was
then heated at a rate of about 20°C per minute from room temperature to the heat treatment
temperature and held isothermally for the prescribed time interval. For these samples, the glass
depth at the center of the crucible was about 15 mm. At the end of the heat treatment period, the
sample was quenched by contacting the outer surface of the crucible with cold water.

 The samples after the heat treatment were prepared for both Scanning Electron
Microscopy/Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM/EDS) examination and X-ray
diffraction (XRD) analysis by grinding and sieving. Microscopic and spectroscopic examination
and quantitative XRD (QXRD) were used to determine the volume fraction and identity of
crystalline phases, and SEM/EDS was used to determine the chemical composition of the glass
matrix and spinel phases.

 1.4.2 Feed Samples from Melter Tests

 Feed samples were taken directly from as-received drums and the melter feed
recirculation line during each test. Feed samples were poured into a platinum/gold crucible and
placed into a programmed furnace for drying and fusion to form a glass. The glass produced
from this fusion was ground to less than 200 mesh and sealed in 20-ml vials for subsequent
analysis by X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy (XRF), or by acid digestion followed by
direct-current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (DCP-AES) on the resulting solution. The
feed samples were also characterized for their density, pH, water content, and glass yield.

 1.4.3 Glass Product

 The glass product is discharged from the melter into 5-gallon steel pails periodically
using an air-lift system. The discharged product glass is sampled at the end of each test by
removing sufficient glass from the top of the pails for compositional analysis and secondary
phase determination. Sample preparation for chemical analysis begins with size reduction and
sieving. All samples are then subjected to XRF analysis to determine the concentrations of all
elements except boron and lithium. A series of National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST) reference materials are used for confirmation of the XRF data. Boron and lithium are
determined by total acid dissolution of ground glass samples in HF/HNO3 and subjecting the
resulting solutions to DCP-AES analysis.

 14

 1.4.3.1 Secondary Phases

 Secondary phases in the glass samples were determined by optical microscopy and
SEM/EDS. Secondary phases due to crystallization and phase separation can be identified using
these methods. Quantitative determination of the amount of crystals in glass samples was
performed by SEM in conjunction with image analysis and by QXRD.

 1.4.3.2 Mineral Phases by Quantitative X-Ray Diffraction

 Powder diffraction data were gathered using a Thermo-Fisher X-tra theta-theta
diffractometer fitted with a Cu X-ray tube. Each sample is ground and sieved to 325 mesh
particles. Five weight percent of a corundum internal standard is mixed with the powdered
sample for quantitative analysis of the crystalline phases. Each powder pattern is scanned from 5
to 80 degrees in 2-theta using a collection time of 2 seconds per data point. JADE9 Software is
used to perform the search-match identification of the crystalline phases as well as whole pattern
fitting to determine the weight percent of each crystalline phase in the sample.

 1.4.3.3 Mössbauer Spectroscopy

 Mössbauer spectroscopy is an analytical technique that utilizes the recoil-free emission
and resonant absorption of gamma rays by nuclei bound in solids. The energies of the gamma ray
emitter (source) and absorber (sample) have to be closely matched and, therefore, the number of
elements that can be studied using this technique is limited. One of the elements that has been
studied extensively using Mössbauer spectroscopy is iron. In this work, Mössbauer spectroscopy
was used to measure the fraction of iron in the Fe2+ and Fe3+ states, which can be used as a
measure of the redox state of the glass sample.

 Mössbauer spectra were collected using an American Magnetic/Ranger Scientific
MS-1200 system equipped with a 57Co source in a rhodium matrix with glass powder as the
sample. Both divalent and trivalent iron show doublet peaks in the Mössbauer spectra and the
peak areas are proportional to the concentrations of the respective species in the glass. Even
though the peaks overlap partially, software can be used to deconvolute the peaks and calculate
the peak areas. The ratio of the areas of the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks is equal to the ratio of their
concentrations in the glass. The redox measurements are calibrated using a set of six standard
glass samples ranging in Fe2+ to Fetotal values from 7 to 90%. The standards include a NIST
traceable Obsidian Rock (SRM 278), five standard glasses analyzed by Corning, Inc. for their
redox state, and the SRL-EA glass. The NIST Standard iron foil (SRM 1541) was used to
calibrate the instrument and determine the zero-velocity channel. Since Doppler shifts in energy
are measured in Mössbauer spectroscopy, the velocity is a measure of the shift in energy;
knowing the zero-velocity channel therefore helps in identifying the Fe2+ and Fe3+ peaks.

2.1 Waste Simulant

 The composition of the HLW simulant selected for testing is based on the blended wastes
from double shell tanks SY-101 and SY-102, both of which have high chromium contents.
Formulation of the simulant composition started with the inventory data on insoluble
components for these two tanks from the Best Basis Inventory (BBI) Hanford Tank Waste
Operations Simulator (HTWOS) model run (April 17, 2012). To simulate the effects of
pretreatments, the appropriate wash, leach, and oxidative leach factors were applied to the tank
solids [48, 49]. The calculated total oxide masses after pretreatment were 68.8 MT and 87.7 MT,
respectively, for SY-101 and SY-102. The blended waste consists of about 50 components,
including radioactive oxides such as UO3. In order to maintain a manageable number of
components and to eliminate the use of radioactivity in testing, all minor components (i.e.,
< 0.1 wt%), radioactive oxides, and noble metals were omitted in the definition of the HLW
simulant. The resulting HLW composition on an oxide basis, which is given in Table 2.1,
contains 98.72 wt% of the original oxides. The HLW simulant composition is obtained by
normalization of the oxide composition, which is also given in Table 2.1.

 The composition of the HLW simulant listed in Table 2.1 has a Cr2O3 concentration of
4.04 wt%, which can be compared with 3.32 wt% found in the Cr-limited waste previously
provided by ORP and tested at VSL [10]. The Cr2O3 content in the present simulant is also
higher than the maximum Cr2O3 concentration (2.45 wt%) found in more than 4000 pretreated
batches of HLW given in the River Protection Project System Plan [50]. The most abundant
oxide in this HLW simulant is Al2O3 (44.07 wt%), which is considerably higher than that found
in the previously studied Cr-limited waste (27.64 wt%). The glass waste loading for the previous
Cr-limited waste stream was limited by the formation of a sulfate-chromate salt phase as a result
of the high concentrations of chromium and sulfur in that stream. In contrast, the presently
selected waste stream is high in chromium but has very low sulfur content. As a result, glass
waste loading for the new stream was expected to be limited by chromium-containing crystal
phases instead of salt phases. The calculated SO3 concentration in the blended waste before
omission of minor components is 0.07 wt% but after removal of the minor components, SO3 is
not present in the simulant. To complete the formulation of the HLW simulant for melter testing,
the volatile components were included. Table 2.2 provides a recipe to produce the HLW simulant
(for 100 kg of waste oxides). The volatiles and their respective concentrations calculated for the
blended SY-101 and SY-102 waste are: carbonate (0.094 g/100 g oxide), nitrate (1.572), nitrite
(0.543), and organic carbon (6.228).

 SECTION 3.0
 SPINEL FORMATION IN CRUCIBLE TESTS

3.1 General Approach and Experimental Design

 As one of the waste loading limiting components in HLW glass formulations, chromium
dissolves sparingly in borosilicate glasses and is prone to precipitate various crystalline phases,
most commonly iron-chrome-spinel. The extent of crystal formation must be managed during
HLW processing to prevent operational issues and potential premature melter failure that can
result from excessive accumulation of spinel crystals in the melter.

 As a transition metal element, chromium tends to assume multiple valence states under
typical waste melter operating conditions. Although the redox equilibrium determines the
ultimate concentration of each species, kinetic factors and the limited residence time in the
melter can prevent the reactions from reaching completion, resulting in a range of concentrations
of redox species depending on the starting materials and operating conditions. It is possible,
therefore, that the crystallization of Cr-rich spinel can also vary for the same final glass product
if the concentrations of Cr3+ and Cr6+ (for constant total Cr) vary in response to changes in
starting materials, processing duration, or local redox conditions. As demonstrated in a
previous study with the HLW-HCr-16 glass formulation [2], the volume % of Cr-based spinel is
sensitive to the form of chromium chemicals used in batching the glass or melter slurry feed. The
amount of spinel phase present in a high-Cr HLW glass varied considerably depending on dwell
time at a given temperature and more strongly on the chemical form of chromium in the batch
[2]. The data acquired from the previous study motivated the present investigation of two
additional forms of chromium in the melter the feed regarding their impact on spinel formation.

 The crucible-scale experiments were designed to collect data to address the following:

 • The evolution of vol% spinel in the glass with different chemical forms of Cr as a
 function of time at temperatures typical for waste glass melter operation.

 • The evolution of vol% spinel in the glass with redox state and the effects of oxidizing
 additives.

 • The evolution of the size and chemical composition of spinel in the glass with time
 for different chemical forms of chromium and redox conditions.

 The experimental work was designed to monitor the evolution of the spinel phase with
regard to its size, chemical composition, and vol% with time for HLW-HCr-16 glass formulation
prepared from two different chromium sources: chromite flour (Al-Fe-Cr spinel, Table 2.5) and
reagent grade trivalent chromium hydroxide (Cr(OH)3). In addition, the combination of chromite
or chromium hydroxide and oxidizing nitric acid (HNO3) was also included to determine the
effect of oxidizing feed additives. Feed samples taken during melter testing were used for these

 19

tests; results from characterization of these feed samples are given in Section 5.1. Feeds with the
two chromium source materials were dried as the starting materials and tested at 1100oC at
different prescribed heat treatment durations (Table 3.1). The choices of heat treatment times
were intended to capture the changes in spinel vol% under the experimental conditions.

 The interpretation of the experimental data collected in this work is based on the
following assumptions, as restated from our previous report [2]:

 • Cr-rich spinel crystallization or dissolution in the HLW glass melt (HLW-HCr-16) is
 not the rate limiting process that determines the spinel vol% at the typical melter
 operating temperature. The concentrations of trivalent and hexavalent chromium
 species therefore play an important role in influencing spinel vol% present in the
 HLW glass melt. These concentrations are influenced by the reaction time and local
 redox conditions.

 • The crystallization or dissolution of Cr-rich spinel depends strongly on the
 concentration of trivalent chromium species in the molten glass. Consequently, for a
 glass with fixed total chromium content, more spinel phase will form as the reaction
 shifts toward increasing trivalent chromium because only trivalent chromium can be
 accommodated in spinel. Conversely, less spinel phase will form (or part of the prior-
 existing spinel phase will dissolve) as the reaction shifts toward decreasing trivalent
 chromium.

 • The growth or dissolution of Cr-rich spinel will inevitably change the total chromium
 concentration in the glass matrix (regardless of valence state). Therefore,
 measurement of Cr2O3 in the glass matrix can provide a direct and independent
 indication of the progress of spinel crystallization or dissolution.

 The experimental data collected in this work are presented in the following sections,
organized by chromium source material. The emphasis of the study is on the impact of the
chromium source material on the crystallization characteristics of HLW-HCr16 glass melts.

3.2 Characteristics of Spinel Phase in Melter Feed with Chromite Flour

 Two feed samples with identical oxide composition to formulation HLW-HCr-16 were
prepared using commercially available chromite (Table 2.5). Feed sample ZBL-F-52A used only
chromite flour as the chromium source; however, a small amount of nitric acid was added to
ZBL-F-52A to make ZBL-F-121A (Table 3.1). Overall, the percentage of spinel phase measured
by SEM and QXRD varied considerably, as indicated by the large standard deviations in Figure
3.1 and Table 3.1. However, the average vol% spinel value by SEM and QXRD show a more or
less downward trend with heat treatment time for both feed samples (Figure 3.1 and Table 3.1),
and appear to converge towards a value of 0.7-0.8 vol% spinel, which is reasonably close to the
value reported from previous work on HLW-HCr-16 (around 0.9 vol%, [2]). The dissolution of
spinel phase as indicated by the downward trend in vol% spinel for ZBL-F-52A (chromite flour)
did not raise the Cr2O3 content in the coexisting glass matrix with time; rather, the Cr2O3 content

in the glass decreased with time (Figure 3.2). On the other hand, the Al2O3 concentration
increased slightly with decreasing spinel content with heat treatment time (Tables 3.2 and 3.3,
Figure 3.2). Considering the relatively higher Al and lower Cr concentrations in the spinel phase
present in the glass after short heat treatment times (Figures 3.3 and 3.4), dissolution of such a

 SEM images of spinel phases in two feed samples after 8 hours to 7 days heat treatment
are characterized by a wide range of crystal sizes, varying from around 50 microns down to

 1) Generally, larger crystals tended to have high Al content at a given heat treatment

 2) The size dependence of the Al content in spinel becomes weaker with longer heat

 prepared from feed did not crystallize from the melt, but started as the chromium

3.4 Relationship Between Spinel Formation and Chromium Source Chemicals

 Our previous work demonstrated that the spinel contents of HLW-HCr-16 glass melts
depend strongly on whether Cr2O3 or Cr(NO3)3-9H2O is used as the chromium source material
[2]. Two additional chemical forms of chromium source materials were tested in this work to
evaluate formation of spinel in glass melts. Overall, the chemical forms of chromium source
materials were found to strongly affect spinel formation in HLW-HCr-16. However, the
characteristics of the impact of chromium source on spinel crystallization vary depending on the
nature of the source materials. Chromium can assume two major valence states as dissolved Cr
species in borosilicate glass melts. The redox equilibrium between trivalent Cr3+ and hexavalent
Cr6+ can drive reactions involving the starting chromium source materials. The kinetics of these
reactions largely control the formation and evolution of Cr-bearing spinel crystal in the shorter
term. For example, Cr2O3 as the source material would promote the formation of spinel upon
dissolving into the borosilicate glass melt more than that indicated by the equilibrium condition
because of the slow process of oxidizing trivalent Cr3+. With time, part of this high chromium
spinel would dissolve back into the glass melt as part of the trivalent Cr3+ converts to hexavalent
Cr6+ to attain redox equilibrium with the ambient air condition (or melter atmosphere). On the
other hand, nitrate introduced together with the trivalent chromium chemical (e.g.,
Cr(NO3)3·9H2O) would create a locally more oxidizing environment than ambient air, which
would temporarily suppress the formation of Cr-bearing spinel. Consequently, the evolution of
spinel content in the glass would take the opposite route with time, with more spinel crystals
forming as some hexavalent Cr6+ converts to trivalent Cr3+ to attain redox equilibrium [2]. On the
surface this analysis seems applicable to the present work. Introduced as trivalent Cr3+ (as
chromite flour, largely Fe2+Cr3+2O4, in ZBL-F-52A, or as Cr(OH)3 in ZBL-F-75A), the spinel
content decreases with dwell time for both feed series and appears to stabilize at around 0.8 vol%
spinel for ZBL-F-52A and 0.6 – 0.7 vol% spinel for ZBL-F-75A. However, detailed analysis
suggests that more complicated processes are involved in the evolution of spinel crystallization.

 If the redox reactions between two major chromium species in the glass control spinel
crystallization, the Cr3+concentration in the spinel phase would change in response to the Cr3+
concentration change in glass. As shown in Tables 3.5 to 3.7, the chromium content in spinel was
relatively low at the early stage of evolution for ZBL-F-52A, increasing with time towards an
average value of around 20.5 atom%, which does not support the working model of spinel
formation controlled by Cr redox reaction in the molten glass. Since chromite flour with higher
Al content was the Cr source, the spinel composition would change in order to approach
equilibrium with the host glass melt. Apparently, this change resulted in the gradual lowering of
Al content in the final spinel phase. In other words, with Al-bearing chromite (a form of spinel)
as the chromium source, the kinetics of chromium redox reactions in the glass melt becomes
secondary to transformation of the spinel via reaction with the coexisting molten glass. As shown
in Figures 3.3 and 3.4, the range of Al and Cr concentrations in the spinel become smaller with
time and the average Al concentration goes down while the Cr concentration goes up. Therefore,
the time evolution of spinel concentration in the ZBL-F-52A series is the joint result of spinel

 23

dissolution/transformation (from relatively high Al to Cr-based spinel) and chromium redox.
This explains the overall trends of increasing Al2O3 and decreasing Cr2O3 concentrations with
time in the coexisting glass (Figure 3.2). Adding HNO3 to the ZBL-F-52A feed did not alter the
evolution path of spinel. Compared to ZBL-F-52A, ZBL-F-121A (with chromite + HNO3) has
similar spinel content, Al and Cr concentrations (atom% vs. time for spinel phase, Figures 3.1,
3.3, 3.4), and Al2O3 and Cr2O3 concentrations in the coexisting glass (Figure 3.5). Moreover,
examining the Al concentration in spinel as a function of crystal size (Figures 3.8, 3.9), the spinel
evolution path of these two feed series look virtually identical. Considering that the evolution of
the spinel phase in these two chromite feeds are likely dominated by exchange reaction involving
Al and Cr with molten glass, it is probably not surprising that addition of an oxidizing agent did
not have an impact on spinel crystallization.

 The evolution of spinel in two feed samples with Cr(OH)3 show strong impact of the
addition of small amount of HNO3. Although the spinel content decreases with time for ZBL-F-
75A (Cr(OH)3), the feed-turned-to-glass contained considerably less spinel at the early stage than
was the case for ZBL-F-52A (chromite flour). With addition of HNO3, ZBL-F-91B
(Cr(OH)3+HNO3) showed only trace amounts of spinel at heat treatment times of up to 72 hours.
The addition of nitric acid not only suppressed crystallization of spinel phase from the glass melt,
but also promoted formation, at trace amounts, of higher Cr/lower Al spinel in the early stage of
spinel evolution (Figures 3.14 and 3.15). The locally highly oxidizing condition created by nitric
acid during feed conversion would shift redox reaction towards more Cr6+. As a result, more Cr
dissolves in the glass matrix (Figure 3.17) and less spinel forms in the feed-turned-to-glass ZBL-
F-91B (Figure 3.12). The different evolution paths of spinel between ZBL-F-75A (Cr(OH)3) and
ZBL-F-91B (Cr(OH)3+HNO3) are reflected clearly in the differing concentrations of Al in
spinels with different crystal sizes (Figures 3.16 and 3.18). The Al concentration in ZBL-F-75A
varied widely in samples after 8 hours at 1100oC. With time, larger populations of spinel crystals
contained lower Al concentrations, eventually converging to around 2 atom% Al, similar to the
Al concentration in spinels after long heat treatment durations with other Cr sources. In clear
contrast, the Al concentration in ZBL-F-91B remained almost constant at around 2 atom % Al
for all four samples heat treated between 8 hours and 7 days at 1100oC.

 Overall, the data and analysis of high Cr HLW feeds with two Cr source materials can be
summarized as below:

 1. Spinel crystallization is sensitive to chromium source materials during feed conversion.
 Chromite flour, a complex iron-chrome spinel with a considerable amount of aluminum,
 resulted in a glass melt with higher than usual spinel content at the early stage of
 evolution. Spinel crystallization is considerably less in glass with Cr(OH)3 as the Cr
 source. However, given time, the spinel crystal content converges to an equilibrium value
 of around 0.8 vol%.

 2. Spinel content in high Cr glass is strongly influenced by redox conditions and particularly
 the local redox environment during feed conversion. ZBL-F-91B with Cr(OH)3 and nitric
 acid was largely crystal free up to 3 days at 1100oC with almost all chromium dissolved
 in the glass matrix, while ZBL-F-75A with Cr(OH)3 contained about 1 vol% spinel and
 considerably lower Cr2O3 concentration in the glass. However, the Cr redox equilibrium

 24

 did not appear to be as important for spinel content in melter feed with chromite flour as
 the Cr source. The spinel content and key spinel crystallization characteristics are
 virtually identical between ZBL-F-52A and ZBL-F-121A, without and with nitric acid,
 respectively. Analysis of the morphology and composition of spinel in those two feed-
 turned-to-glass samples indicated that spinel crystals predominantly originated from the
 chromite flour. Consequently, the effect of Cr redox reaction was masked by the
 dissolution/modification of the existing spinel phase.

3.5 Summary and Conclusions from Crucible-Scale Tests

 The data on the evolution of spinel crystal as a function of time collected for the
HLW-HCr-16 formulation demonstrate that the starting chromium source material and the local
redox environment can strongly influence the crystal content in the high-Cr HLW glass melt as it
approaches equilibrium with respect to spinel characteristics. This is a result of the relatively
slow kinetics of the underlying reactions under conditions representative of waste vitrification.
Spinel formation can start and evolve depending on the origin of the spinel phase in question. As
shown in ZBL-F-75A with Cr(OH)3 as the chromium source, spinel crystallization from molten
glass was controlled by the local redox conditions during the early stages of glass melting or feed
conversion at temperatures typical for melter operation. Introduction of oxidizing nitric acid
suppressed spinel formation from otherwise identical ZBL-F-91B feed-turned-to-glass for as
long as 7 days. The higher Cr6+/Cr3+ ratio caused by addition of oxidant maintained a
considerably higher chromium concentration in the glass matrix. In contrast, starting from
chromite (Al-bearing Fe-Cr spinel) as the Cr source, spinel crystals in feed-turned-to-glass of
ZBL-F-52A resulted mostly from dissolution of chromite flour and modification of the spinel
composition through exchange between the spinel and the glass matrix. In that case, introduction
of oxidizing nitric acid had little impact on both the volume% and composition of spinel phase in
ZBL-F-121A. The data from crucible-scale tests show the importance of chromium source
materials and the kinetics of spinel formation (or dissolution) on the resulting spinel
concentration and composition in the HLW glass melt under the test conditions.

about seven percent when nitric acid was added to the feed creating more oxidizing conditions.
The amount of divalent iron in glass discharged while processing feed with chromite is higher, at
twenty two to forty six percent. The high concentration of divalent iron in the glass sample
discharged after processing feed containing chromite is attributable in part to the presence of
chromite, which has eighty four percent reduced iron in the sample. The proportion of reduced
iron in the samples of glass taken from the melter floor was twenty one to twenty five percent,
depending on the amount of chromite incorporated into the sample. In some of the samples taken
from the melter floor and the glass discharged after processing feed containing chromium
hydroxide, the amounts of reduced iron was about the same in the crystalline and glass phases, in

6.1 Particulate Sampling

 The melter exhaust was sampled for metals/particles according to 40-CFR-60 Methods 3,
5, and 29 at steady-state operating conditions during each test segment. The concentrations of
off-gas species that are present as particulates and gaseous species that are collected in impinger
solutions were derived from laboratory data on solutions extracted from air samples (filters and
various solutions) together with measurements of the volume of air sampled. Particulate
collection required isokinetic sampling, which entails removing gas from the exhaust at the same
velocity that the air is flowing in the duct (40-CFR-60, Methods 1-5). Typically, a sample size of
30 dscf was taken at a rate of between 0.5 and 0.75 dscfm. Total particulate loading was
determined by combining gravimetric analysis of the standard particle filter and chemical
analysis of probe rinse solutions. An additional impinger containing 2 N NaOH was added to the
sampling train to ensure complete scrubbing of all acid gases. The collected materials were
analyzed using direct current plasma atomic emission spectroscopy for the majority of the
constituents and ion chromatography (IC) for anions. Melter emission fluxes are compared to
feed fluxes and emission samples taken during the tests in Table 6.1. Notice the distinction that is
made between constituents sampled as particles and as "gas". The "gaseous" constituents are
operationally defined as those species that are scrubbed in the impinger solutions after the air
stream has passed through a 0.3 µm heated filter. Both samples were well within the 90 – 110%
limits for isokinetic sampling.

 Particulate emissions constituted from 0.45 to 0.65 percent of feed solids during the
present tests while processing feeds containing chromite and chromium hydroxide. This level of
carryover is comparable to that measured while processing the same high chromium HLW
composition with Cr2O3 and low nitrate concentrations [1] and twice that with chromium nitrate
or Cr2O3 and sodium nitrate [2] for feeds on the same melter at the same temperature of 1150ºC
and fixed bubbling [1]. In tests without the added nitrate, bismuth was the most abundant emitted
particulate increasing the overall particulate carryover the same as in previous tests with Cr2O3
and low nitrate concentrations [1]. During the tests processing feeds with chromium hydroxide,
the bismuth emission rate decreased from 115 mg/min to 11.7 mg/min with the addition of nitric
acid to the feed, similar to observations while processing chromium oxide with and without
added nitrate [2]. The lower level of carryover in the present tests is similar to that measured
while processing various feeds containing HLW simulants with significant amounts of bismuth
in chromium- (0.24 percent carryover), aluminum-plus-sodium- (0.06 percent carryover), and
bismuth-limited HLW wastes (0.23 percent carryover) [10]. The factor that distinguishes the
higher emission rates for the previous test with Cr2O3 [1] and the present test with chromium
hydroxide and chromite from the present and past tests with nitrates [2] and the previous test
with bismuth-limited waste [10] is the higher molar ratio of organic carbon to nitrate of 12 in the
tests without added nitrate as compared to 1.4 to 2.6 in tests with added nitrate and 0.1 in the
earlier tests [10]. High levels of bismuth volatility in response to reducing conditions in the

 35

melter feed are also inferred from bismuth concentrations in the product glass not increasing
towards the target concentration until nitric acid was added to the feed. This effect was also seen
in a DM10 test with bismuth-limited waste in which bismuth concentrations in the glass product
decreased 100% in response to additions of sugar to the melter feed [10]. Conversely, chromium
carryover increased by a factor of five under more oxidizing conditions from half a percent to
two and half percent when nitric acid was added to feed containing chromium hydroxide. This
observation is consistent with testing of feeds containing Cr2O3 with and without added nitrate
[1, 2] as well as chromate being more volatile than other chromium species.

 As expected, the feed element emitted at the lowest melter decontamination factor (DF)
was fluorine. This was expected based on previous tests with this composition [1, 2] and the lack
of measureable fluorine in the glass product. Other elements exhibiting some volatile behavior
were chromium, boron, bismuth, sulfur, and potassium. It is difficult to fully assess the carryover
of sulfur since it was present in the feed only as a contaminant and some of the sulfur in the feed
is volatilized during the crucible melting process. The expected increasing volatility of alkali
metals with increasing atomic mass was observed: potassium carryover being the highest
followed by sodium then lithium. The relative carryover of the feed constituents was unaffected
by the form of chromium used (Cr2O3, Cr(OH)3, chromite) whereas the ratio of organic carbon to
nitrate did affect the carryover of reducible species, particularly bismuth and chromium. Boron
and fluorine were the only elements detected in the impinger solutions collected downstream of
the heated particle filter in the sampling train, which constitutes the “gas” fraction of the melter
emissions.

6.2 Particulate Sampled from Off-Gas System

 Material accumulated in the cyclones downstream of the film cooler was sampled and
analyzed after each testing sequence to further characterize particulate carryover during these
tests. Analysis of material accumulated in the cyclones from the present and previous tests [2]
with this high chromium composition is provided in Table 6.2 for mineral content and chemical
composition. Also included is an analysis of some particulate removed from the film cooler at
the end of testing. Cyclone solids contain all of the feed components in similar proportions to the
feed for many of the elements, enrichments over feed for volatile elements such as boron,
bismuth, chromium, and sodium, and depletions of the non-volatile elements aluminum and
silicon. These proportions of volatile and non-volatile elements in the cyclone particulate
indicate loss of volatile components from glass pool and cold cap as well as moderate bulk
carryover of feed from the melter. Small amounts of chlorine, sulfur, magnesium, and zirconium
were detected in the cyclone deposits despite not being included in the target composition as a
result of residue from preceding tests or being a feed contaminant. Particulate from the present
tests contains a greater proportion of boron with lesser amounts of sodium and iron than previous
tests with chromium oxide due in part to the present feed being over target with respect to boron
and the differences in the feeds processed prior to each of the test series. The results from XRD
analysis support this mechanism for the generation of the particulate with the most abundant feed
mineral, quartz being identified in the particulate along with volatile borates and chromates.
Spinels (detected as magnetite) were present in all cyclone particulate samples, although they
were up to twice as abundant in the present tests with chromium hydroxide. Chromate minerals

 36

were detected only following tests with chromium hydroxide and nitric acid, supporting the
notion that chromium is oxidized with the addition of nitric acid to the feed. Unlike the earlier
tests that used Cr2O3 as the chromium source, the chromium mineral eskolite was not detected in
the cyclone particulate after the present tests using chromium hydroxide and chromite as the
chromium source.

 The deposits taken from the film cooler at the end of testing were mostly alkali sulfates
and borates, contained high concentrations of bismuth, and lesser amounts of feed additives,
particularly silica. Bismuth, chlorine, and sulfur are five to eight times more concentrated in the
film cooler deposits than in the contemporaneously sampled cyclone with a half to a third as
much aluminum, calcium, iron, and silicon. This suggests that volatile constituents condensed on
the film cooler and entrained mineral additives are not prone to adherence to the film cooler. The
presence of significant concentrations of bismuth and sulfur in the deposits is particularly
noteworthy given the potential corrosion of Inconel. It is important to note that although enough
of the deposit was available for analysis, the deposit was not very extensive and did not occlude
flow through the film cooler, as shown in Figure 6.1. Also, film cooler deposits developed over
several test sequences while processing a variety of HLW feed compositions as expected given
the low concentration of bismuth in the feed and the lack of sulfur and chlorine in the target
waste composition processed in the present tests.

6.3 Gases Monitored by FTIR

 Melter emissions were monitored in each test for a variety of gaseous components, most
notably CO and nitrogen species, by Fourier Transform Infra Red Spectroscopy (FTIR). The
off-gas system temperature is maintained well above 100°C beyond the sampling port
downstream of the DM100 HEPA filter to prevent analyte loss due to condensation prior to
monitoring. The data, therefore, represent the relative concentrations of volatile gaseous species
in the melter exhaust. A summary of the range and average concentrations of gaseous species
monitored during each test is provided in Table 6.3. The analytes listed in Table 6.3 are those
that were thought likely to be observed during the tests based on previous work; no other species
were detected in the off-gas stream by FTIR. The concentrations of five monitored species,
nitrogen monoxide, nitrogen dioxide, water, carbon dioxide, and carbon monoxide, are plotted in
Figures 6.2.a - 6.5.b. The relative amounts of gaseous species monitored depended on the
amount of nitrate added to the feed and the feed processing rate. The most abundant nitrogen
species monitored was NO, which is in keeping with previous melter tests with both HLW and
low activity waste (LAW) feeds. The concentrations of other nitrogen species were 20 and 2
times lower than NO during tests with and without nitric acid additions to the feed, respectively.
The concentrations of most species decreased from the turnover to the test with chromite in
response to the decrease in feed rate. The concentrations of NO, NO2, and N2O were much
higher while processing feed with added nitric acid, as expected, and relatively constant in the

monoxide, and ammonia were highest in tests with feed without nitric acid. The concentrations

the turnover. Low concentrations of HF were measured throughout the tests, consistent with the
lack of fluorine retention in the glass product over the course of the tests and the gaseous fluorine

 SECTION 7.0
 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

 Previous work has shown that the form of chromium in the waste simulant affects
Cr-containing spinel crystallization in the glass [1, 2]. This report describes work performed to
further investigate the effects and, in particular, the effects of Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4)
on spinel crystallization and the use of nitrate as an additive to feeds containing Cr(OH)3 in order
to influence its behavior during feed processing in the melter. The sources of chromium
investigated in this work include commercially available Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4),
which are two forms of chromium that are reported to be abundant in Hanford HLW. The use of
commercially available forms of chromium hydroxide and chromite is necessary in view of the
quantities required for melter testing. Prior to use, these materials were characterized with
respect to the chemical compositions and the amounts and types of phases that are present. The
work included preparation and characterization of crucible melts to assess the tendency towards
crystal formation for feeds with Cr(OH)3 and chromite (FeCr2O4) with nitrate additions. The
results from crucible-scale testing supported DM100 melter tests designed to further assess the
tendency towards spinel formation as a function of chromium form and feed nitrate content.

 The data on the evolution of spinel crystal as a function of time collected for the
HLW-HCr-16 formulation demonstrate that the starting chromium source material and the local
redox environment can strongly influence the crystal content in the high-Cr HLW glass melt as it
approaches equilibrium with respect to spinel characteristics. This is a result of the relatively
slow kinetics of the underlying reactions under conditions representative of waste vitrification.
Spinel formation can start and evolve depending on the origin of the spinel phase in question. As
shown in ZBL-F-75A with Cr(OH)3 as the chromium source, spinel crystallization from molten
glass was controlled by the local redox conditions during the early stages of glass melting or feed
conversion at temperatures typical for melter operation. Introduction of oxidizing nitric acid
suppressed spinel formation from otherwise identical ZBL-F-91B feed-turned-to-glass for as
long as 7 days. Higher Cr6+/Cr3+ ratios caused by addition of oxidant maintained a considerably
higher chromium concentration in the glass matrix. In contrast, starting from chromite (Al-
bearing Fe-Cr spinel) as the Cr source, spinel crystals in feed-turned-to-glass of ZBL-F-52A
resulted mostly from dissolution of chromite flour and modification of the spinel composition
through exchange between the spinel and the glass matrix. Introduction of oxidizing nitric acid
had little impact on both the volume% and composition of spinel phase in ZBL-F-121A. The
data from crucible-scale tests show the importance of chromium source and the kinetics of spinel
formation (or dissolution) on the resulting spinel concentration and composition in the HLW
glass melt under the test conditions.

 A series of melter tests were conducted on the DM100 using the same SY-101/ SY-102
high chromium HLW simulant with the HLW-HCr-16 glass formulation. Tests on the DM100
were conducted at 1150oC at the nominal bubbling rate of 9 lpm to allow comparison to results
obtained previously with high chromium HLW and glass composition containing chromium
oxide and chromium nitrate as the chromium source [1, 2]. The feed rate was adjusted to provide

 39

the desired complete cold cap. The achieved glass production rates of about 1200 kg/m 2 /day P
 P

were comparable to those in earlier tests with this composition. Tests were preceded by a
turnover with the HLW-HCr-16 glass composition using chromite as the chromium source. The
three following tests were distinguished by using chromite as the chromium source in the first
test; using chromium hydroxide as the chromium source in the second test; and using chromium
hydroxide as the chromium source with the addition of nitric acid during the third test. Glasses
produced during these tests were analyzed for crystal content by two different methods to assess
the effects of these test variables on the tendency to form chromium containing spinels and
Mössbauer spectroscopy as an indication of the reducing conditions in the melt pool. Samples
were also taken from the floor of the melter to determine the type and amount of spinels settled
out of the melt pool.

 Analysis of the crystals in glasses from melter testing showed two distinct types of
spinels: the iron, chromium, nickel variety observed in previous tests, which form in the glass
melt similar to previous tests [1, 2], and one very similar to the chromite used in the feed during
the turnover and initial test, which did not incorporate into the molten glass. The most significant
factor influencing the amount of crystalline phases in melt pool and discharged glasses was the
presence of nitrate in the melter feed. Glass samples from the end of tests with chromium oxide,
chromium hydroxide, and chromite as the chromium source with no added nitrate all have crystal
contents between 1.4 and 2.1 volume percent, whereas glass samples from the end of tests with
chromium nitrate as the chromium source or added nitrate have crystal contents between 0.4 and
1.1 volume percent. If only melt pool or “dip” samples are considered, the amount of crystals in
the glass pool after processing feed with higher concentrations of nitrates was only 0.4 to 0.9
volume percent. Of the samples from tests with added nitrate, the highest crystal content was in
the samples taken while processing chromium nitrate as a result of the lower nitrate
concentration, whereas the lowest crystal content was measured in samples taken after
processing chromium hydroxide with nitric acid. Feed from tests with chromium hydroxide with
nitric acid and chromium oxide with sodium nitrate contained the same amount of nitrate and
therefore the differences between the measured crystal content in the discharged glass (0.6 vs.
0.9 vol. %) is attributable to the form of chromium, the settling out of crystalline phases from the
glass pool while feeding chromium hydroxide, or variability in sampling and analysis. However,
tests clearly show that nitrate added to feed with either chromium oxide or hydroxide as the
chromium source significantly reduces the amount of spinel formation. Of the samples from tests
without added nitrate, the average melt pool and discharge glass sample analysis had a crystal
content of 1.5 vol% suggesting that the form of chromium does not have an effect on the crystal
content of the melt pool or discharge glasses. This may be misleading since spinels need to form
in the glass melt while feeding chromium oxide or chromium hydroxide, whereas spinels already
exist in the feed with chromite. Mössbauer analysis also shows the same correlation between
more reducing conditions and higher crystal content as well as the crystals containing more
divalent iron when processing feed containing chromite as the chromium source. The highest
amounts of crystalline phases in glass taken from the melt pool floor was up to 3.7 volume %
spinels detected by SEM analysis. The amounts of crystals in the glass sampled from the melter
floor did not correlate with the form of chromium used in the preceding test but appeared to be
determined by sampling variation inherent from sampling from the melt floor. The highest
concentration of spinels were in glass sampled from the melter floor one week after testing
indicating that spinels persist on the melt floor after testing and that some the spinels may have

 40

originated from earlier tests with other HLW compositions. Conversely, the melt pool sample
taken after a week of idling contained no measureable spinels, indicating that they either settled
to the melt floor during idling or were incorporated into the glass.

 Particulate emissions constituted from 0.45 to 0.65 percent of feed solids during the
present tests while processing feeds containing chromite and chromium hydroxide. This level of
carryover is comparable to that measured while processing the same high chromium HLW
composition with Cr2O3 and low nitrate concentrations [1] and twice that with chromium nitrate
or Cr2O3 and sodium nitrate [2] for feeds on the same melter at the same temperature of 1150ºC
and fixed bubbling [1]. In tests without the added nitrate, bismuth was the most abundant emitted

and low nitrate concentrations [1]. During the tests processing feeds with chromium hydroxide,
the bismuth emission rate decreased from 115 mg/min to 11.7 mg/min with the addition of nitric
acid to the feed, similar to observations while processing chromium oxide with and without
added nitrate [2]. Conversely, chromium carryover increased by a factor of five under more

feed containing chromium hydroxide. This observation is consistent with testing of feeds
containing Cr2O3 with and without added nitrate [1, 2] as well as chromate being more volatile

 Melter DFs were determined for most elements in the feed. The feed element emitted at
the lowest melter DF was fluorine. This was expected based on previous tests with this
composition [1, 2] and the lack of measureable fluorine in the glass product. Other elements
exhibiting some volatile behavior were chromium, boron, bismuth, sulfur, and potassium. The
relative carryover of the feed constituents was unaffected by the form of chromium used (Cr2O3,
Cr(OH)3, chromite) whereas the ratio of organic carbon to nitrate did affect the carryover of
reducible species, particularly bismuth and chromium. The relative amounts of gaseous nitrogen
oxide species monitored increased with nitrate additions to the feed and the feed processing rate,
as expected. Conversely, carbon monoxide and ammonia concentrations were highest in tests
with feed without any added nitrate.

7.1 Recommendations for Future Work

 The results of the testing presented herein and previous tests with this high chromium
HLW composition [2] demonstrate the suppression of spinel crystallization with the
incorporation of nitrate in melter feed. This was observed for two different forms of chromium,
chromium oxide and chromium hydroxide likely to be present in HLW tanks. More generally the
testing illustrated the sensitivity of crystal formation and elemental volatility to the balance of
oxidants and reductants in the melter feed. This balance is actively managed in LAW
vitrification with the addition of sugar to prevent melt pool foaming, reduce nitrogen oxide
emissions, and deter the formation of secondary phases on the melt pool surface [51-77]. For
HLW vitrification, the active management of the oxidation state within the cold cap and glass
pool by the addition of oxidants or reductants to the melter feed is not currently in the baseline.
This situation is a consequence of baseline HLW feed projections that show very low levels of
anions and organic carbon as a result of initially projected high washing efficiency. However, the

 41

evolution of the WTP flow-sheet appears to be towards greater fractions of supernate species
remaining in the HLW feed. Excessive variations in waste oxidants and reductants can lead to
overly oxidizing or overly reducing conditions in the melt pool, respectively. Overly oxidizing
conditions can lead to foaming in the melt pool and significant decreases in the glass production
rate. Overly reducing conditions in the melt pool can lead to formation of reduced phases such as
metals, metalloids (such as sulfides and selenides) and minerals such as spinels; these phases are
typically much denser than the glass, and can be highly electrically conductive, low melting,
corrosive, and very fluid.

 Previous testing with high iron HLW feeds has shown the reduction of iron with minor
addition of reductants (sugar) to the melter feed [78]. Other tests with high iron HLW feed were
conducted to determine the relative amounts of oxalate and nitrate that can be tolerated and to
develop a preliminary strategy for mitigating such risks [79]. These tests provided guidelines for
a single HLW composition that contained little chromium and no bismuth, both of which are
sensitive to the redox conditions in the cold cap and molten glass. Assumptions for volatile
contents (waste organics, nitrogen oxides, and carbonates) for most of the HLW optimization
studies for ORP [3-6, 9-14, 80] have been based on one actual C-106/AY-102 waste [81], which
has very low concentrations of these oxidants and reductants; thus, the effects of various
oxidants and reductants on several waste components has yet to be determined.

 The results of the testing presented herein demonstrate that chromium in the form of
spinels in the waste feed is not fully incorporated into the glass melt and has the propensity for
settling to the floor of the melter. Crucible scale testing indicated that the chromite in the melter
feed is unaffected by the addition of oxidants such as nitrate to the melter feed. Spinel type
minerals containing iron, manganese, and chromium have been measured in various HLW tanks
at WTP [35, 36]. The likelihood that these spinel type minerals in other HLW compositions
would also not be incorporated into the molten glass has yet to be evaluated.

 Further work that is recommended for optimization of processing of WTP high
chromium, manganese, and iron HLW feeds is outlined below.

• Suppression of Spinel Crystallization in WTP HLW Streams: The present testing
 demonstrated the successful suppression of spinel crystallization by addition of nitrates in
 feeds with chromium oxide and chromium hydroxide as the chromium source. However, the
 results are based on a single high chromium HLW composition from the Hanford tanks.
 Subsequent work should extend these results to address the full range of HLW feeds with a
 tendency towards formation of Cr, Mn, and Fe spinels, and various forms of Cr, Mn, and Fe
 that are expected to be processed at the WTP.

• Cr, Fe, Mn Spinels in WTP HLW Streams: The present testing demonstrated the lack of
 complete incorporation of chromite as a chromium source into the molten glass and settling
 of the crystalline phase to the melter floor. However, the results are based on a single high
 chromium HLW composition from the Hanford tanks with a single spinel mineral.
 Subsequent work should extend these results to address the full range of HLW feeds and Cr,
 Mn, and Fe spinels expected to be processed at the WTP.

 42

• Effect of Nitrates and Waste Organics on Other WTP HLW Feed Types: Present and past
 testing was based on a single high chromium HLW composition and a single high iron [79]
 HLW composition. Subsequent work should extend these results to address the full range of
 HLW feeds expected to be processed at the WTP. In particular, a high chromium HLW
 composition with a low waste organic content in contrast to the high concentration evaluated
 in the present and earlier tests [1, 2].

• Nitrate Concentrations: Testing should be performed to define the maximum nitrate
 concentration beyond which sugar additions are required, as well as the minimum amount to
 be added to prevent the reduction of metals and suppress spinel formation.

• Oxalate as a Reductant: Testing should be performed to better define the relationship
 between organic carbon from oxalate as compared to organic carbon from sugar. This is one
 of the factors that is required to determine the amounts of nitrate or sugar that needs to be
 added for redox control. Although oxalate is expected to be the principal source of organic
 carbon in the HLW feeds, similar testing should be performed for other organic species if
 they are expected to be present in significant concentrations.

• Integrated System Testing: Testing on the DM1200 WTP HLW Pilot Melter system provides
 data from a one-third scale system with a prototypical feed delivery system and off-gas
 treatment train. The DM10 is also equipped with scaled primary off-gas system components
 (Submerged Bed Scrubber and Wet Electrostatic Precipitator) as well as the capability to
 recycle off-gas effluent solutions back to the melter feed. Melter emissions are profoundly
 impacted by the amount of volatiles in the melter feed, thus creating different demands on
 off-gas system components and changing the chemical composition of off-gas effluent
 solutions. Testing is necessary to evaluate potential interactive effects on system operation
 arising from the incorporation of various levels of volatiles in HLW streams to provide data
 on the performance of each unit operation, input for flow-sheet models and regulatory
 requirements, and information on recycle streams.
