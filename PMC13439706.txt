A systematic review and critical analysis of the evidence for transmission ratio distortion in humans
Authors: Ziyi Dai, Gregory Costain, M Jakobsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMendel's law of equal segregation states that during gamete formation, the 2 alleles at a gene locus segregate such that each gamete has an equal probability of containing either allele. Transmission ratio distortion (TRD) occurs when 1 of the 2 alleles from either parent is preferentially transmitted to the offspring, resulting in a deviation from the expected 1:1 ratio. Although TRD has been observed and studied in nonhuman species, the full extent and underlying biology of TRD in humans remains poorly summarized. Here we present a systematic review to assess evidence of TRD in the human genome, tracing reports from the 1970s through 2025. Overall, 96 studies including 42 different human variants/genes/loci met inclusion criteria. These studies provided only preliminary and/or conflicting evidence of TRD. Study methods were limited by multiple recurrent biases. Experimental validation of the biological mechanism(s) underlying the putative distortion was rarely performed or possible. TRD warrants renewed attention in the field of human genetics, especially with the growing availability of very large, family-based genome-wide sequencing datasets.Keywords: selfish selection, transmission ratio distortion, non-Mendelian inheritance

Background

Mendel's first law (law of equal segregation) states that during gamete formation, the 2 alleles at a gene locus segregate such that each gamete has an equal probability of containing either allele. Transmission ratio distortion (TRD; sometimes referred to as transmission distortion or TD) refers to a departure from this expected 1:1 Mendelian inheritance ratio (Friocourt et al. 2023). TRD is widely recognized and well studied in nonhuman species (Friocourt et al. 2023). For example, meiotic drivers are classic genetic elements that lead to TRD (Lindholm et al. 2016; Bravo Núñez et al. 2018; Fishman and McIntosh 2019). These “selfish” genetic variants manipulate the process of meiosis to affect their own preferential transmission regardless of the impact on organismal fitness (Lindholm et al. 2016). The archetypal example of meiotic drivers is the t-haplotype in mice, a nonrecombinant region located on mouse chromosome 17 that in heterozygous males can be transmitted to up to 99% of offspring (Herrmann and Bauer 2012). Mechanisms causing TRD are known at each mammalian reproductive stage, from gametogenesis, to gametic competition during fertilization, to postimplantation selection (Huang et al. 2013; Friocourt et al. 2023). Observations of TRD across the eukaryotic kingdoms have motivated searches for TRD in humans dating back decades (Taylor and Ingvarsson 2003; Huang et al. 2013; Nadeau 2017; Fishman and McIntosh 2019; Hurst 2019; Price et al. 2019; Xia and Ouyang 2020; Friocourt et al. 2023). However, the evidence for human TRD has not been systematically collected and assessed through a contemporary lens.

In this review, we summarized the modest, and sometimes conflicting, evidence for TRD of specific human coding sequence variants, structural variants, and tandem repeat expansions. The best studied examples of TRD in nonhuman animals (eg mouse t-haplotypes (Herrmann and Bauer 2012), segregation distorter gene complex in Drosophila melanogaster (Sandler et al. 1959; Ganetzky 1999; Larracuente and Presgraves 2012)) did not have human equivalents. For most putative TRD in humans, the mechanism was not established. A renewed effort to understand the extent of TRD in the human genome could provide new insights into genome biology and human gametogenesis.

Methods

We searched the OVID MEDLINE database for reports of human TRD on 9 April 2024 and again on 6 September 2025, using the string: ((transmi* or segregat* or allel*) adj3 distort*).tw,kf, (preferential adj3 transmission).tw,kf. The results from the 2 searches were combined with an OR statement. No other limitations were added to the search. Two reviewers (authors Z.D. and G.C.) independently performed title and abstract screening, and then full-text review, using the Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia; available at www.covidence.org). We excluded (i) nonhuman (ie plants, fungi, and nonhuman animals) studies; (ii) mathematical theory/statistical modeling/computer simulation papers; (iii) reviews and commentaries; (iv) studies using transmission disequilibrium testing specifically to identify disease-associated variants/loci (ie those studies looking for apparent TRD caused by overtransmission of disease risk alleles to affected individuals, rather than true biological TRD); and (v) human studies not reporting on specific genes/loci/chromosome rearrangements or otherwise with insufficient data to calculate transmission ratios. Discrepancies were resolved by face-to-face discussion until consensus was reached. Author Z.D. manually extracted data from all remaining papers and cross-indexed each paper's reference list with the primary search to identify missed papers.

The primary outcomes for each variant/gene/locus were observed transmission ratio, magnitude of deviation from expected transmission ratio, and statistical significance. Stratified outcomes, eg by sex of transmitting parent, were available for some papers. Additional extracted data included study population, ascertainment strategy, and proposed biological mechanism of TRD. We defined positive TRD as a higher rate of transmission of a nonwild-type (often pathogenic for disease) variant than was expected by Mendel's first law and negative TRD as a lower rate of transmission.

Results

Overview of included studies

Overall, 96 of 2,234 screened papers met inclusion criteria for this study (Fig. 1). Findings related to a total of 42 variants/genes/loci and various chromosome rearrangements. Data from each study are summarized in Table 1. The distribution by year of publication is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Half the studies (47; 49%) focused on individually rare, submicroscopic variants, primarily in genes associated with monogenic disorders (Table 1). Only 4 genes had both 2 or more studies and consistent findings: ASS1, GNAS, KCNQ1, and SMN1. Experimental methods included pedigree studies (ie studying allele transmission in families over 1 or more generations), counting outcomes from prenatal testing, counting outcomes from preimplantation genetic testing, and single sperm analysis. The remainder of the included studies focused on structural chromosome anomalies or genome-wide polymorphisms. Several studies also investigated maternal versus paternal transmission, identifying instances of suspected parent sex-specific TRD.

TRD of pathogenic variants causing autosomal recessive diseases

Many TRD studies analyzed data from human pedigrees wherein individuals had undergone targeted testing for a specific monogenic disorder. One recurring hypothesis was that positive TRD of a variant causing a severe autosomal recessive condition might contribute to allele retention in the population. For example, 2 studies reported positive TRD for single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in the gene ASS1 causing citrullinemia (Kleijer et al. 2006; Larovere et al. 2012). Kleijer et al. (2006) studied prenatal testing data where both parents were known from family history to be heterozygous “carriers” for citrullinemia and found 36/91 (39.6%) fetuses inherited both parental variants (Kleijer et al. 2006). Larovere et al. (2012) observed a biallelic inheritance rate of 12/21 (57.1%), compared again with the expected 25% risk. A similar study design was used in an as-yet-unreplicated study of PMM2 pathogenic variants causing a congenital disorder of glycosylation (Schollen 2004). The authors noted that 2 specific recurrent missense variants in PMM2 were major contributors to their finding of positive TRD in fetuses at 25% risk of biparental inheritance, though they were underpowered to demonstrate statistically significant overtransmission of either variant in isolation.

More common recessive diseases with “founder mutations” leading to major geographic or ancestral differences in incidence were also investigated for TRD (Table 1). TRD could be an explanation for the persistence of these founder mutations at a relatively high frequency in a population. Kitzis and colleagues first reported “unusual segregation” of CFTR alleles causing cystic fibrosis, with distortion in the sex ratio of unaffected siblings of probands with cystic fibrosis (Kitzis 1988). Later studies analyzing (i) restriction fragment length polymorphisms used to determine haplotype around the CFTR gene (Mornet et al. 1989), (ii) single sperm analysis (Williams et al. 1993), and (iii) transmission in families with carrier parents (European Working Group on Cystic Fibrosis Genetics 1995) all found no evidence supporting TRD. Similarly, TRD was investigated as a potential mechanism by which β-thalassemia-causing HBB variants were maintained in the local population in Ferrara, Italy (Papi et al. 1977). The researchers found no evidence of distortion in families with 1 or 2 heterozygous parents (Papi et al. 1977). Over a 7-year period, a later group published 3 papers proposing maternal TRD of alleles associated with sickle cell anemia and β-thalassemia in Brazilian families where the parents were ascertained as probands (Silva and Ramalho 1996a, 1996b; Duchovni-Silva and Ramalho 2003). However, Fleming and colleagues found these HBB allele frequencies in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium in a cohort of newborn babies (Fleming 1996).

TRD of pathogenic sequence variants causing autosomal dominant diseases

TRD has been observed in the tumor suppressor genes RB1, SDHB, and TP53 and the proto-oncogene PTPN11 (Table 1). An initial study reported positive patrilineal TRD of RB1 disease-causing alleles (Munier et al. 1992), but follow-up pedigree-based studies had conflicting results (Munier et al. 1994; Naumova and Sapienza 1994; Seminara and Dryja 1994). Single sperm typing found allele bias in only 1 of 6 donors (Girardet et al. 2000). More recent studies reported that pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in SDHB and TP53 were overtransmitted to offspring in families with SDHB-related cancer predisposition and Li-Fraumeni syndrome, respectively (Davidoff et al. 2023; Halpern et al. 2025). TRD remained significant after adjustments such as excluding probands from transmission counts. As for other disease genes, these findings could have significant implications for genetic counseling. Whether germline variation in cancer-driving genes may demonstrate TRD as a group, secondary to proliferation or cell growth advantages, has not been studied. Outside of repeat expansion disorders (see below), there are limited data to suggest other rare autosomal dominant conditions demonstrate TRD (Table 1).

TRD of short tandem repeats associated with repeat expansion disorders

Large pedigrees facilitated the discovery of short tandem repeat expansions as the explanation for the segregation patterns (including anticipation) of several genetic diseases. Many of these repeat expansion disorder loci—such as ATXN1, ATXN2, and ATXN3, associated with spinocerebellar ataxias—were then investigated for TRD (Table 1). Studies of these 3 spinocerebellar ataxia genes were performed across 3 decades (1996–2019), with different genetic ancestries, different modes of ascertainment, and different definitions of “expanded” (nonwild-type, by our definition). The usual hypothesis was positive TRD for expanded alleles, which was difficult to distinguish from ascertainment bias in clinically ascertained pedigrees. Overall, there was no conclusive empirical data for TRD. For ATXN1, larger CAG repeats within the normal (not disease-causing) range showed no TRD (MacMillan et al. 1999), and disease-causing CAG repeats in German families with spinocerebellar ataxia showed positive TRD (Riess et al. 1997). For ATXN2, negative TRD was observed for CAG expansion in sibships of Brazilian families with spinocerebellar ataxia type 2 (Sena et al. 2019). For ATXN3, larger CAG repeats within the normal (not disease-causing) range showed no TRD or negative TRD (Rubinsztein and Leggo 1997; MacMillan et al. 1999; Bettencourt, Fialho, et al. 2008a) and disease-causing CAG repeats families with spinocerebellar ataxia showed no TRD or positive TRD (Ikeuchi et al. 1996; Riess et al. 1997; Bettencourt, Santos, et al. 2008b; Souza et al. 2016). Two sperm analysis studies yielded conflicting results with respect to sperm allele fraction in males with ATXN3 expansions (Takiyama et al. 1997; Grewal et al. 1999).

Whether there is TRD of tandem repeat expansions in DMPK, the gene associated with myotonic dystrophy 1, has also been debated. Positive TRD was first reported by Carey et al. (1994) in families without a known history of myotonic dystrophy but with at least 1 heterozygous parent with DMPK alleles of varied sizes (ie larger allele with equal to or greater than 19 CTG repeats and smaller allele with less than 19 repeats). The larger allele was significantly overtransmitted to offspring in 266 total meiosis events, driven primarily by paternal overtransmission (Carey et al. 1994). Additional pedigree studies found (i) positive TRD of disease-causing expansions from parents of either sex (Gennarelli et al. 1994); (ii) overtransmission of the larger sized allele (in the normal range of 5 to 30 repeats), regardless of absolute size, from parents of either sex (Chakraborty et al. 1996; Shaw et al. 1996); (iii) positive TRD in maternal but not paternal meiotic events (Chakraborty et al. 1996); and, (iv) no statistically significant TRD after excluding probands from counts (Zatz et al. 1997; Magee and Hughes 1998). Additional studies involved gamete, embryo, and prenatal timepoints, again with conflicting results. In 3 men with 1 DMPK allele ≥19 repeats and the other <19 repeats, single sperm analysis found no significant difference in allele fraction (Leeflang et al. 1996). There was preferential transmission of repeats in the size range of 19 to 37 in n = 335 embryos undergoing preimplantation genetic testing (Dean et al. 2006). There was no statistically significant TRD seen in 83 fetuses who underwent prenatal genetic testing because of a parent with a known disease-causing DMPK expansion (Zunz et al. 2004).

Findings in other genes had either no published follow-up studies attempting replication [ARX (Shoubridge et al. 2012); CTLA4 (Tsai et al. 1998: 1); INS (Eaves et al. 1999)] or failed to replicate [ATN1 (Ikeuchi et al. 1996; MacMillan et al. 1999; Takiyama et al. 1999); FMR1 (Webb et al. 1986; Teague et al. 1998)].

TRD of human polymorphisms, including genome-wide assessments

Advances in genotyping and sequencing technologies ultimately enabled analyses of TRD beyond single candidate loci (Naumova et al. 1995, 1998, 2001; Paterson et al. 2003, 2009; Zollner et al. 2004; Yang et al. 2008; Deng et al. 2009; Santos et al. 2009; Meyer et al. 2012; DeGiorgio et al. 2014; Lucotte et al. 2022) (also reviewed in Huang et al. 2013). Early studies by Naumova et al. (1995) and Naumova et al. (1998) demonstrated distortion linked to X-chromosome inactivation patterns and the sex-of-offspring-specific overtransmission of haplotypes from maternal grandfathers to male offsprings only. They also reported TRD signals in imprinted regions essential for embryonic growth and development, consistent with the hypothesis that TRD of grandparental origin is because of imprinting and embryo loss (Naumova et al. 2001).

More recently, Lucotte et al. (2022) proposed a framework to detect sex-specific TRD of SNPs and identified candidate sex-antagonistic regions with overtransmission of alleles to one sex and undertransmission to the other sex.

One early concern prompting TRD studies was that TRD could be a confounding factor for the complex disease loci mapping studies of that era (Paterson et al. 2003). The Framingham Heart Study provided one of the first large and accessible, well-genotyped “general population” genetic datasets. An early study of 1,700 individuals from these Framingham Heart Study families did not have TRD findings reaching a genome-wide significance threshold (Paterson et al. 2003). A follow-up study using an expanded Framingham Heart Study dataset attributed most of the apparent distortion findings to genotyping error (Paterson et al. 2009). Similar studies, using either the Framingham and other datasets, consistently had difficultly overcoming the confounding effects of genotyping error and/or had findings that did not replicate in other datasets (Yang et al. 2008; Deng et al. 2009; Santos et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2012, 2013; Meyer et al. 2012). More recently, (DeGiorgio et al. 2014) developed a model for detecting balancing selection and identified a single gene candidate FANK1 with strong evidence for balancing selection and marginal TRD signal. If TRD is population specific (Liu et al. 2013) or resulting from combinatorial effects of multiple different variants/loci (Zollner et al. 2004), this could explain some of the challenges in replicating findings. Single sperm sequencing has been used to investigate specific alleles (see above), but there has also been 1 genome-wide search that did not identify evidence of transmission distortion at this developmental stage (Carioscia et al. 2022).

TRD of human chromosomes with balanced structural rearrangements

Boué and Gallano (1984) identified an overtransmission of the 13q14q Robertsonian translocation from parents to offsprings and an overtransmission of chromosomal inversions from father carrier only in families who underwent prenatal diagnosis. Similar patterns of TRD were not detected in a subsequent study investigating pedigrees with balanced pericentric chromosome inversions (Sherman et al. 1986). In a 2001 study where the authors compiled published segregation data in 677 offsprings of parents with Robertsonian translocations (not including data from the above-referenced 1984 study), they also identified significant overtransmission (56.3% to 58.7%) of the translocation from mothers to offspring, but not from fathers (De Villena and Sapienza 2001).

Human correlates of nonhuman animal TRD findings

Nonhuman animal results were identified during our systematic review but not included in our (human-focused) primary analyses. Beyond mouse t-haplotypes and segregation distorter, there have been reports of TRD in mouse (Arends et al. 2022), chicken (Ren et al. 2021), swine (Casellas et al. 2014; Godia et al. 2020), turkey (Abdalla et al. 2020), lesser kestrel (Aparicio et al. 2010), bovine (Khatib et al. 2008; Murphy et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2009; Casellas et al. 2017; Id-Lahoucine et al. 2022, 2023; Martin et al. 2022; Rodrigues et al. 2025), horse (Bailey 1986; Weitkamp et al. 1988; Laseca et al. 2023), nonhuman primate (Smith 1982), oyster (Foltz 1986), fire ant (Ross and Shoemaker 2018), and worm (Seidel et al. 2011). A subset of these putative TRD loci had human orthologs: ATXN2, CAPN3, CDKN1A, CDKN1B, DMPK, FGF2, MMACHC, MST1, PRCD, SHB, and SPAM1. There was overlap with the genes included in Table 1 (Pardo-Manual de Villena et al. 1996, 1997, 2000; Monckton et al. 1997; Zheng et al. 2001; Taveau et al. 2004; Kiehl et al. 2006; Kriz et al. 2007; Mbikay et al. 2007; Anguera et al. 2008; Calounova et al. 2010; Kanatsu-Shinohara et al. 2010; Nordstrand et al. 2010; Moreno-Garcia et al. 2014; Clark et al. 2024). For example, the mouse Atxn2 is >90% identical to the human ATXN2 protein at the amino acid level but lacks the polyQ tract (CAG repeat region). Monckton et al. (1997) generated transgenic mouse lines with expanded CTG/CAG repeats from the human locus and observed overtransmission of the transgene to 66% (55/83) of the offspring [and also reduced litter size compared to other transgene lines {P < 0.05}] (Monckton et al. 1997). Another human–mouse comparative analysis (for the ARX gene) is reviewed elsewhere (Friocourt et al. 2023). The cross-species identification of the same TRD genes highlights 2 possibilities: (i) targeted searching for TRD of variants in unstudied human orthologs of genes impacted by TRD in another species and (ii) using nonhuman model systems to investigate TRD signals from human studies.

Discussion

We systematically reviewed the evidence for TRD in the human genome. Many human TRD studies focused on rare variants associated with disease phenotypes and on analyzing data from pedigrees. When families are ascertained because of affected individuals, the resulting datasets are (i) limited in size and thus statistical power and (ii) subject to ascertainment bias that defies simple correction (see below). Although some studies proposed positive or negative TRD for specific variants, there remain no empirically robust human examples comparable to, eg the classic mouse and fly examples. Single sperm typing has generally not provided major replication evidence nor unique mechanistic insights. Similarly, genome-wide scans have highlighted the technical difficulty of detecting TRD at scale. Most TRD signals have been confounded by genotyping artifacts and limited statistical power. Studies to date suggest pervasive TRD across the genome is unlikely, but have not ruled out that specific loci may demonstrate measurable and biologically significant TRD.

Most research in human TRD is subject to a myriad of biases resulting from pedigree analysis. Families are typically ascertained because of specific phenotypes, with more affected individuals in the family making it more likely that the family will come to clinical attention (Table 1). Ascertainment bias and testing bias cannot be corrected simply by removing probands from counts. Stoppage rules, for example, can cause families to alter their plans for future children after having 1 or more children impacted by a rare condition, thereby distorting sibship analyses (Jones and Szatmari 1988). Selection bias is a major limitation of single site cohort studies, where the starting point of a data series is chosen arbitrarily after noticing a recent pattern (eg that the last several families who underwent prenatal genetic testing happened to have a fetus that inherited the risk variant(s)). Untangling negative TRD from reduced fitness associated with phenotypic effects of the nonwild-type variant is similarly challenging.

The prevalence of TRD across the entire human genome remains an open question. Several studies conducted genome-wide analyses of TRD using single nucleotide polymorphisms. However, most TRD signals either failed to reach significance or were likely false positives due to genotyping errors. Meyer et al. (2012) also found that significant TRD signals in 1 dataset did not replicate across others. Although rare variants account for most previously reported cases of TRD in humans, they could not be considered in these initial genome-wide scans. Evans et al. (2006) calculated in 2006 that tens of thousands of parent–child trios would be needed to detect TRD at a genome-wide scale. Similarly, Huang et al. (2013) noted the need for “access to very large and error-free genotype databases with unselected phenotypes.” A renewed interest in TRD studies of rare variants is expected now that there are a growing number of accessible, large, trio-based genome-wide sequencing datasets (Firth et al. 2011; 100,000 Genomes Project Pilot Investigators et al. 2021; Costain et al. 2021; Trost et al. 2022). Studying trios will also facilitate assessments for parent sex-specific TRD.

There were several limitations of our systematic review. We apologize to the authors of any studies missed by our search strategy. Negative results were likely underreported in the literature. The extracted data were heterogeneous, which complicated our attempts to identify larger trends in the literature and prevented meta-analyses. We limited our scope and purposefully did not capture instances of viability selection (eg recessive lethality (Zollner et al. 2004)) in humans, which can create the appearance of TRD in pedigrees. Historical literature related to, eg possible distorted human sex ratios in sibships and across large pedigrees, and seminal treatises such as Bell's The Treasury of Human Inheritance, may contain additional insights related to human TRD but fell outside our search window (Harper 2005). We also did not review (i) data for other mammals, crops, plants, or microorganisms; (ii) underlying mechanisms and biology of selfish selection and TRD that could potentially apply to humans; and (iii) efforts to consider TRD within the framework of existing theories, models, and observations from population genetics and evolutionary biology. Fortunately, these topics have been reviewed by other authors (Lindholm et al. 2016; Bravo Núñez et al. 2018; Fishman and McIntosh 2019; Hurst 2019; Price et al. 2019; Xia and Ouyang 2020; Friocourt et al. 2023).

In summary, early studies proposed TRD at specific human loci, often using data from pedigrees and techniques such as linkage analysis or the transmission disequilibrium test. However, these studies were limited in size, in scale, and by ascertainment bias. There are relatively few contemporary studies of human TRD. TRD continues to be a possible mechanism that maintains deleterious, disease-associated variants in the population despite the negative selection resulting from reduced fitness (Leeflang et al. 1996; Magee and Hughes 1998). TRD therefore warrants renewed attention in the field of human genetics, especially with the growing availability of genome-wide sequencing datasets.
