Appendix S1
Title:

Abstract
Habitat selection and space use are fundamental to understanding animal distribution and informing conservation strategies. Traditional methods for quantifying habitat preferences and deriving utilization distributions from location data obtained from telemetry devices assume regular sampling and negligible measurement error. These assumptions are routinely violated in marine systems and practitioners typically regularize and filter data before fitting models. These two-step procedures fail to propagate uncertainty from the filtering stage into subsequent analyses and can yield biased estimates. Habitat-driven Langevin diffusion models offer an elegant alternative by representing movement as a stochastic process driven by habitat-selection dynamics in continuous time, naturally accommodating irregular sampling. However, a state-space formulation that incorporates measurement error by treating true locations as latent variables remains challenging because habitat data depending on these locations are no longer observed. We facilitate the use of such state-space formulation by using the Laplace approximation to simultaneously integrate over the true locations and account for the habitat covariates encountered along those latent paths, yielding a single-stage framework that can be efficiently implemented with Template Model Builder (TMB). By doing so, we provide the first TMB implementation capable of handling covariates that depend on latent variables, allowing inference via fast and efficient maximum likelihood estimation. Simulation studies demonstrated our approach outperforms the two-step method, recovering the sign and overall magnitude of habitat-selection parameters even under substantial measurement error and missing data (both common in marine studies) and yielding more accurate utilization distribution and trajectory reconstructions. An application to narwhal telemetry data demonstrates that the two-step method masks the habitat selection coefficient substantially towards zero selection, while our unified approach recovers a much stronger selection signal. By providing a solution for both measurement error and temporal irregularity when inferring habitat selection and utilization distributions, our approach addresses several long-standing challenges and offers a computationally efficient, flexible framework for movement ecologists that is applicable across a wide range of taxa and environments.

Introduction

Animals do not move uniformly across their habitat, and mapping the probability of species presence across a landscape is fundamental in ecology, and for informing conservation and ecosystem management (
15
;
62
;
49
). Habitat selection analyses aim to uncover these dynamics by quantifying which environmental features animals select relative to what is available to them, thereby indicating relative preference (or avoidance) for particular habitats (
46
;
49
). This idea underlies the concept of the utilization distribution (UD;
2
), which provides a spatial map of relative intensity of space use, showing where the animal is most likely to be. The UD thus helps identifying home ranges (
79
), key habitats (
36
), migration corridors (
71
), and zones of potential overlap with human activities (
34
). Such insights are vital for better understanding animal space use and guiding conservation and management efforts (
54
).
Advances in tracking technology have allowed ecologists to collect extensive fine-scale movement data for many species across diverse environments (
65
;
59
). Movement data are rich multivariate time series that can offer insights into how animals respond to environmental conditions (
36
), disturbance (e.g., presence of predator;
30
), and social factors (
80
), thereby helping us understand patterns of space use. Many methods have been developed to estimate habitat selection and UDs from telemetry data. Standard approaches fit a resource selection function (RSF) to quantify habitat selection (
10
;
11
;
46
;
39
;
37
;
35
;
49
), and the UD is then derived as the normalized product of the RSF and an availability distribution, which describes the distribution of habitat covariates over locations accessible to the animal (
37
;
49
). Although multiple methods exist for fitting RSFs to telemetry data, the most common are use–available logistic regression (
31
;
27
) and point process models (
84
;
39
;
37
). Despite their widespread use, RSFs rely on the assumption of spatio-temporal independence among locations, which is increasingly violated by modern high-resolution movement data (
65
). Statistical methods have therefore been developed to model how animals respond to environmental conditions while accounting for the inherent spatio-temporal structure of movement data.

Although several approaches have been proposed to account for autocorrelation in RSFs, such as data thinning (
61
), weighted likelihood (
1
), generalized estimating equations (
68
), and variance inflation (
60
), step-selection functions (SSFs) have emerged as a prominent solution by conditioning habitat selection on the animal’s current location and movement capacity (
29
;
81
;
7
;
26
;
28
). Specifically, at each step, habitat availability is constrained by the animal’s movement, such that selection is evaluated only among locations that are reachable given the preceding position and movement characteristics. Step-selection functions therefore describe habitat selection at a local scale, and the parameter estimates are inherently tied to the temporal resolution of the sampled observations (
73
). While some ecological questions are precisely focused on such local, short-term selection, for example, how the immediate presence of a predator affects prey habitat selection (
48
), this dependence is limiting in the many ecological applications that focus on characterizing the species’ broad scale space use, which is more naturally described by a spatial UD (
55
). Although step-scale selection can be translated into long-term space use, both theoretical and empirical work have shown that SSFs do not recover the UD implied by an RSF (
76
;
55
).
Moreover, since parameter estimates depend directly on the sampling interval, SSF-derived inferences are not directly comparable across datasets collected at different temporal resolutions, nor easily related to broader-scale species distribution models.

Several approaches have been proposed to upscale inference from SSFs to the UD (see
67
for a review), though the most commonly used rely on simulation-based procedures (
78
) rather than explicit theoretical formulations. This reliance on simulation makes it difficult to obtain a simple parametric representation of the UD and complicates the quantification of uncertainty, which is typically ignored (
58
). Continuous-time stochastic processes have recently provided an elegant solution to these issues, naturally accommodating irregularly sampled data while yielding a global UD from local movement decisions via their limiting distribution
(
16
;
72
;
88
;
55
, e.g.,)
. Among them, the habitat-driven Langevin diffusion
(
57
;
58
)
has emerged as a particularly promising approach for habitat selection and UD inference across temporal scales. At the fine scale, the Langevin stochastic differential equation models continuous movement dynamics, with a drift driven by local gradients in the habitat potential surface. This potential surface is defined by a standard RSF, ensuring that the stationary distribution of the location process (i.e., the UD) is strictly proportional to the RSF. This broad-scale UD emerges from continuous, fine-scale movement responses to these gradients over time. In recent years, growing interest in the Langevin model has led to multiple extensions (
50
;
20
).

Standard habitat selection models, including the Langevin diffusion, do not account for location measurement error. However, such error is pervasive in animal telemetry studies, particularly in marine systems (
19
;
74
;
87
). Commonly used tracking data that are subject to measurement error include the Advanced Research and Global Observation Satellite (Argos;
74
) and Fastloc GPS systems (
14
;
19
). Inferring habitat selection from noisy locations can result in bias and, ultimately, misleading conclusions. To address this issue, state-space models (SSMs) are routinely used as a preliminary filtering step. Implemented via
R
packages such as
aniMotum
(
41
),
ctmm
(
16
) and
crawl
(
40
;
41
), these models incorporate measurement error and aim to predict the true movement track, typically modeled as a random walk. This two-step approach, which first corrects for measurement error and then performs ecological inference using the corrected locations, has become a standard analysis pipeline in habitat selection analyses of error-prone data
(
48
;
47
;
28
, e.g.,)
. However, most users do not propagate the uncertainty from the filtering stage into subsequent analyses, which may therefore introduce bias, the extent of which has not been thoroughly investigated (
57
). Such bias may arise, for example, when the movement model used for filtering is overly simplistic.

Building on the Langevin diffusion framework, we explicitly express the stationary distribution as an RSF and incorporate measurement error into the model via an SSM. We propose a highly efficient likelihood-based fitting method implemented within the Template Model Builder (TMB;
44
) framework. By combining automatic differentiation with the Laplace approximation, TMB enables efficient and scalable maximum likelihood inference in complex SSMs, while simultaneously recovering unobserved true trajectories. Crucially, our setting differs from standard state-space TMB implementations
(
3
, e.g.,)
because the habitat covariates must be evaluated at the unobserved true locations. Specifically, we account for the unobserved covariate values while integrating over the true locations during the optimization process within TMB. To our knowledge, this is the first TMB implementation of SSMs to handle covariates that depend on latent variables, resolving a fundamental challenge in ecology. The proposed approach is therefore fast, intuitive, and powerful, making it well-suited for estimating habitat-selection parameters and UDs from complex tracking data.

We evaluate our method’s ability to recover the UD and habitat selection parameters in a simulation study spanning a wide range of measurement-error and missing-data scenarios. We also compare its performance with that of a standard two-step approach. We then illustrate our approach with tracking data from narwhal (
Monodon monoceros
), a species vulnerable to the effects of climate change such as declines in sea ice and rising shipping traffic (
66
;
33
). Previous studies have shown that bathymetry plays a significant role in narwhal habitat selection (
45
;
42
;
38
). In this context, our objective is to assess our method’s ability to identify this relationship and to compare it with the results from a two-step approach.

Methods

Langevin diffusion model for animal movement

Movement, represented as the joint velocity and location process, is modeled as a underdamped Langevin diffusion process
(
57
;
58
)
, which generalizes correlated random walks by allowing movement to be directed towards regions of higher values of a potential function denoted as
\log\pi
. The stationary distribution
\pi
represents the (time-invariant) steady-state distribution of the location process. In habitat selection analyses,
\pi
is typically written as a function of an RSF and corresponds to a UD
(
46
;
57
)
. Local movement decisions in the Langevin framework reflect habitat selection preferences (drift towards higher
\log\pi
), with resource availability dynamically constrained by the movement process itself. Since
\pi
is the limiting distribution of the location process, the animal’s long-term pattern of space use stabilizes to
\pi
, creating a direct link between local habitat selection and broad-scale space use. This model is referred to as the Langevin movement model throughout.

Let
\bm{\mu}_{t}=(\mu_{t}^{x},\mu_{t}^{y})^{\top}
and
\bm{v}_{t}=(v_{t}^{x},v_{t}^{y})^{\top}
denote the location and velocity in the
x

and
y
directions at time
t\geq 0
. Given initial conditions
\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{v}_{0}
, their joint dynamics are governed by the following system of stochastic differential equations (SDEs;
58
):

\begin{cases}d\bm{\mu}_{t}=\bm{v}_{t}\,dt,\\
d\bm{v}_{t}=-\gamma\bm{v}_{t}\,dt+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\gamma}\sigma\,d\bm{B}_{t},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\nabla(\cdot)
refers to the gradient operator. Within the framework defined in (
1
), the location process
\bm{\mu}_{t}
follows the standard position-velocity relationship. In contrast, the equation that drives the velocity process describes more complex patterns that result from three driving forces. First, a friction force
-\gamma\bm{v}_{t}
, with
\gamma>0
, damps the velocity and governs its autocorrelation structure, with large
\gamma
leading to a rapid decrease of autocorrelation, and low
\gamma
yielding more persistent movement. Second, a standard Brownian noise
\bm{B}_{t}
captures unexplained variation in the system (e.g., variability in movement and behavior). Third, an environmental drift
\sigma^{2}\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{t})
pulls the animal along environmental gradients and towards regions of higher
\pi
, with speed parameter
\sigma
. Since all components of (
1
) are isotropic (with scalar
\gamma
and
\sigma
and an isotropic gradient), the joint process is isotropic (see
20
for an anisotropic formulation).

Under mild regularity conditions
(
18
)
, the joint process (
1
) has a unique stationary distribution
\pi^{\star}(\bm{\mu},\bm{v})\propto\pi(\bm{\mu})e^{-||\bm{v}||_{2}^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}

(
58
)
, where the location marginal is
\pi
, linking local movement decisions to the long-term UD, while the velocity marginal is a zero-mean Gaussian independent of location and carries no information about habitat selection. Consequently, throughout this manuscript,
\pi
is referred to simply as the stationary distribution of the (location) process. The function
\pi(\bm{\mu})
captures how the environment influences animal movement. Following
58
, we specify
\pi(\bm{\mu})\in\mathbb{R},
as a function of an RSF:

\pi(\bm{\mu})=\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}c_{k}(\bm{\mu})\right)}{\int_{\Omega}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}c_{k}(\bm{\mu^{\star}})\right)d\bm{\mu^{\star}}},

(2)

where each
c_{k}
is a differentiable spatial covariate and
\beta_{k}
is its associated selection coefficient. This formulation assumes that, at a broad scale, all locations in the study area are equally accessible, and thus the selection coefficients are interpreted as in a standard RSF
(
46
;
77
)
: positive values of
\beta_{k}
indicate preference for higher values of
c_{k}
, negative values indicate avoidance of higher values of
c_{k}
and
\beta_{k}=0
indicates no response. The exponential term in the numerator in equation (
2
) is what is commonly referred to as an RSF
(
46
;
77
)
. The gradient driving the movement process is then

\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{k}\nabla c_{k}(\bm{\mu}).

(3)

The long-term distribution emerging from these local decisions is captured by the stationary distribution
\pi
, which reflects space use at a broad scale. The stationary distribution
\pi
is therefore a UD proportional to the RSF (equation (
2
)). Specifically,
\pi
corresponds to the UD derived from an RSF when the availability distribution is uniform across the study area (
37
;
49
).

We use the location data to infer behavior and estimate the dynamics of the unobserved velocity process. Specifically, we are interested in estimating the movement parameters
\sigma
and
\gamma
, along with the habitat selection parameters
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K}
. Let
(\bm{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\mu_{t}^{x},v_{t}^{x},\mu_{t}^{y},v_{t}^{y})^{\top}_{t\geq 0}
denote the joint process of location and velocity.
Suppose
(\bm{Z}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is observed at discrete times
t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}
. We write
\bm{z}_{i}=(\mu_{i}^{x},v_{i}^{x},\mu_{i}^{y},v_{i}^{y})^{\top}
for the realization of the process at time
t_{i}
, where
\mu_{i}^{x}:=\mu_{t_{i}}^{x}
and similarly for the remaining components. The time elapsed between consecutive observations is denoted
\Delta_{i}=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
for
i=0,\ldots,n-1
. We approximate the joint process of location and velocity by assuming that
\nabla\log{\pi}(\bm{\mu})
is constant over the movement path connecting an observation at time
t_{i}
with the subsequent observation at
t_{i+1}
.
With this approximation,
\bm{Z}_{t}
is a Markov process with Gaussian transition densities. Specifically, we have (see
58
for more details):

\bm{Z}_{t_{i+1}}\mid\{\bm{Z}_{t_{i}}=\bm{z}_{i}\}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{\eta}_{i},\bm{Q}_{i}),\quad 0\leq i\leq n-1

(4)

with mean vector

\bm{\eta}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{i}^{x}+\dfrac{v_{i}^{x}(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}})}{\gamma}+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}\,\partial_{x}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i})}{\gamma}\left(\Delta_{i}-\dfrac{1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}}{\gamma}\right)\\[34.14322pt]
v_{i}^{x}e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}\,\partial_{x}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i})}{\gamma}\left(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}\right)\\[34.14322pt]
\mu_{i}^{y}+\dfrac{v_{i}^{y}(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}})}{\gamma}+\dfrac{\sigma^{2}\,\partial_{y}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i})}{\gamma}\left(\Delta_{i}-\dfrac{1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}}{\gamma}\right)\\[34.14322pt]
v_{i}^{y}e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}-\dfrac{\sigma^{2}\,\partial_{y}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i})}{\gamma}\left(1-e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}\right),\end{pmatrix}

(5)

where
\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i})=(\partial_{x}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i}),\,\partial_{y}\log\pi(\bm{\mu}_{i}))^{\top}
.
Since the
x
and
y
dimensions evolve independently, the covariance matrix is block diagonal and can be written as follows:

\bm{Q}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{Q}_{i}^{\text{1D}}&\bm{0}\\[11.38092pt]
\bm{0}&\bm{Q}_{i}^{\text{1D}}\end{pmatrix},

(6)

where each
2\times 2
block is the covariance matrix
\bm{Q}_{i}^{\text{1D}}
written as follows:

\bm{Q}_{i}^{\text{1D}}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^{2}\left(\dfrac{2\Delta_{i}}{\gamma}-\dfrac{e^{-2\gamma\Delta_{i}}}{\gamma^{2}}-\dfrac{3}{\gamma^{2}}+\dfrac{4e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}}{\gamma^{2}}\right)&\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\left(1-2e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}+e^{-2\gamma\Delta_{i}}\right)\\[34.14322pt]
\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}\left(1-2e^{-\gamma\Delta_{i}}+e^{-2\gamma\Delta_{i}}\right)&\sigma^{2}\left(1-e^{-2\gamma\Delta_{i}}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(7)

In reality, since the underlying movement process operates in continuous time,
\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu})
varies continuously along the true path between observations. Discretizing this process therefore introduces a bias, which grows as the time interval between observations increases (
9
). Thus, in our case study, we choose to mitigate this bias by splitting large gaps into independent track segments.

Measurement model for error-prone observed location data

The Langevin movement model traditionally assumes that locations are observed without error. However, most tracking data are recorded with some degree of uncertainty. For example, Argos data are typically accompanied by error estimates derived from least-squares or Kalman filtering, while Fastloc GPS data are characterized by circular error (
52
;
74
;
87
). To address this, we couple the Langevin movement model with an observation model that accounts for measurement error, specifically for Kalman-filtered Argos and Fastloc GPS data (
41
). The core idea is that the observed locations correspond to the true locations with additive noise, the magnitude and distribution of which depends on the type of data and the associated uncertainty estimates. Following standard practice in the animal movement literature, we use a Gaussian observation model (
51
;
41
). Therefore, we consider the observed location
\bm{y}_{i}
as a realization of the observation process
\bm{Y}_{i}
with the following conditional distribution:

\bm{Y}_{i}\mid\bm{\mu}_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(\bm{\mu}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}_{i}\right).

(8)

For Argos locations, we define
\bm{\Sigma}_{i}
using the error ellipses associated with the location recorded at time
t_{i}
. Therefore, for a two-dimensional observation process
\bm{Y}_{i}
, we use:

\bm{\Sigma}_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1,{i}}^{2}&\sigma_{12,{i}}\\
\sigma_{21,{i}}&\sigma_{2,{i}}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

(9)

with

\sigma_{1,{i}}^{2}=\left(\frac{M_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}r_{i}+\left(\frac{m_{i}\psi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}r_{i},

(10)

\sigma_{2,i}^{2}=\left(\frac{M_{i}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}r_{i}+\left(\frac{m_{i}\psi}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}r_{i}

(11)

and

\sigma_{12,i}=\sigma_{21,i}=\frac{(M_{i}^{2}-m_{i}^{2}\psi^{2})}{2}\cos(r_{i})\sin(r_{i}),

(12)

where
M_{i}
and
m_{i}
correspond to the semi-major and semi-minor axis lengths, respectively,
r_{i}
corresponds to the orientation of the semi-major axis associated with the observation recorded at time
t_{i}
and
\psi
scales the semi-minor axis of the error ellipse (
51
;
74
;
41
). Thus, model (
8
) requires that the measurement error associated with each location point (i
.
e
.
,
M_{i}
and
m_{i}
for
t_{i}\in[t_{0},t_{n}]
) is known and accurate. For Argos data, these are provided directly with each observation (
74
;
41
). For Fastloc GPS data, we assume a circular error of
50
meters which translates to
m_{i}=M_{i}=50
with
\psi=1
(
87
). However, the parameter
\psi
is generally recommended to be estimated rather than fixed, as this corrects for potential inaccuracies in the reported location uncertainties, such as systematic underestimation from the Kalman filter and the circular error assumption
(
41
)
. Henceforth, we will refer to the Langevin movement model with measurement error simply as the Langevin SSM.

2.1
Inference

As both the true positions
\bm{\mu}_{i}
and the velocities
\bm{v}_{i}
are not observed, they need to be integrated out of the joint likelihood function. Crucially, this also means the spatial covariates, which depend on the latent positions, are not observed. We account for this covariate uncertainty by extracting the covariate values
c_{k}(\bm{\mu})
and calculating the gradients
\nabla c_{k}(\bm{\mu})
from raster data during the optimization within TMB (
44
), a technique that, to our knowledge, has not been previously used in this framework. Because the spatial covariates are a function of the latent locations, they are integrated over all possible true paths via the Laplace approximation.

We conduct inference by maximizing the marginal likelihood function of the observed locations under the Langevin movement model. Specifically the exact marginal likelihood function for the vector of model parameters
\bm{\theta}=\left(\psi,\,\bm{\beta},\,\sigma,\,\gamma\right)
is

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=\int f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})\;d\bm{z}=\int g_{\psi}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{z})h_{\bm{\beta},\,\sigma,\,\gamma}(\bm{z})\;d\bm{z},

(13)

where
\bm{z}=(\bm{z}_{1},\dots,\bm{z}_{n})
denotes the stacked latent variables (i.e., true location and velocity process) in the multidimensional case,
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K})^{\top}
,

g_{\psi}(\cdot\mid\bm{z})
is the product of Gaussian observation densities defined in equation (
8
),
and
h_{\bm{\beta},\,\sigma,\,\gamma}(\cdot)
is the product of transition densities given in equation (
4
). Equation (
13
) is written for a single track. For
M
independent tracks sharing model parameter
\bm{\theta}
,
the full likelihood is
\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathcal{L}_{m}(\bm{\theta})
,
where each
\mathcal{L}_{m}
takes the same form as equation (
13
)
applied to the
m
-th individual track.

Due to the inherent nonlinearities, the high-dimensional integral is analytically intractable.
An attractive candidate for numerically efficient approximate frequentist inference is given by the
Laplace approximation
(
24
;
83
). The Laplace method approximates the log-integrand by a second-order Taylor expansion (with respect to
\bm{z}
) centered at its mode, for a given parameter vector
\bm{\theta}
. Plugging this approximation back into equation (
13
) results in a Gaussian integral with an explicit analytical solution. Conveniently, the approach is fully automated and readily available in TMB (
44
), provided the user defines the joint negative log likelihood (see Appendix S1: Theoretical background on the Laplace approximation but also see Additional implementation details on the Laplace approximation). Once the model is fitted, TMB provides model parameter estimates and, conveniently, predictions of the random effects (true locations and velocity in our case) as well as uncertainties for both, respectively.
The package is numerically highly efficient by using automatic differentiation to compute first and second derivatives required for the Laplace approximation, as well as by automatically detecting sparsity in the second derivative function.

The Laplace approximation is exact when the state-space model is linear and Gaussian, such that the joint distribution of all random effects is multivariate Gaussian
(
82
)
.
Here, the nonlinearity introduced by
\pi_{{}_{\text{UD}}}(\cdot)
in equation (
5
) violates this condition, hence in our case, the procedure is approximate. Nevertheless, the Laplace approximation often performs remarkably well in practice, particularly when the integrand
f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
is dominated by a single mode and the sample size provides sufficient information to concentrate the distribution (
82
; further theoretical details on the Laplace approximation, including potential sources of bias and implementation details, are provided in Appendix Appendix S1: Theoretical background on the Laplace approximation and Additional implementation details on the Laplace approximation). We assess any potential bias introduced by the approximation in our simulation study.

After fitting the model, we obtain the estimated UD from the estimated selection coefficients as follows:

\hat{\pi}(\bm{\mu})\approx\frac{\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}_{k}c_{k}(\bm{\mu})\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\beta}_{k}c_{k}({\bm{\mu}_{j}^{\star}})\right)},

(14)

where
\{{\bm{\mu}_{1}^{\star}},\ldots,{\bm{\mu}_{M}^{\star}}\}
is a set of locations spanning the study domain
\Omega
. In practice, the
{\bm{\mu}_{j}^{\star}}^{\prime}s
correspond to the center of each grid cell of the covariate rasters. As the UD is a simple function of the habitat selection coefficients and the covariates, uncertainty in
\pi(\bm{\mu})
can be quantified from the estimated covariance matrix using the Delta method
(
64
, e.g.,)
or Monte Carlo methods
(
58
)
.
We provide an open-source package with all the necessary functions for fitting the Langevin SSM model, available on GitHub (
https://github.com/bmcclintock/langevinSSM
).

To compare the estimated UD with the true simulated UD, we use the Bhattacharyya’s affinity (BA;
8

25

BA=\iint_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sqrt{\vphantom{\widehat{UD}(x,y)}{UD}_{true}(x,y)}\,\sqrt{\widehat{UD}(x,y)}\,dx\,dy,

(15)

such that the statistic equals
1
when the two UDs are identical and is
0
when there is no overlap. While intermediate values can be difficult to interpret in absolute terms,
we use the BA as a relative measure of similarity between the estimated and true UDs
(
25
)
: values closer to
1
indicate better recovery of the true UD, and differences in BA across scenarios reflect differences in estimation accuracy. Intuitively, a higher BA indicates that the two UDs assign similar probabilities of use to the same locations, meaning that an animal simulated from the true UD and one simulated from the estimated UD would be likely to be found in the same areas.

The procedure requires initial values for both the model parameters
\bm{\theta}
and the latent process
\bm{z}
(true locations and velocities), as estimation is carried out by jointly maximizing the likelihood over parameters and the latent process. In particular, since observed locations are treated as noisy measurements of the true positions, the true location trajectory is latent. Since complex models can be sensitive to initialization in likelihood-based optimization
(
5
)
, we adopt a strategy informed by preliminary simulations. Latent locations are initialized at the observed positions, with missing values filled by linear interpolation separately for each track, and velocities are initialized at zero. For the model parameters, we consider two approaches: a neutral initialization, where
\gamma
and
\sigma
are estimated from the data and all selection coefficients are set to zero, and an initialization at the true parameter values. To improve numerical stability, an initial optimization over the latent states is performed conditional on fixed model parameters before proceeding to joint optimization over both states and parameters.

For the two-step method, we first apply a pre-filtering step, fitting a correlated random walk with
aniMotum
as is standard in ecological studies
(
28
, e.g.,)
. In the second step, we then fit the Langevin movement model, treating the predicted locations from the correlated random walk as the “true” locations. When data are missing,
aniMotum
provides predicted locations at both observed and missing time points, effectively imputing the missing data such that the two-step method is fitted on a complete sequence of predicted locations. Model fitting is performed using TMB.

Narwhal movement data

4
Discussion

We proposed a Langevin state-space model to infer animal habitat selection and utilization distributions from irregular, error-prone, and complex movement data. Maximum likelihood inference is carried out efficiently using the Laplace approximation and automatic differentiation as implemented in TMB, with an R package for simulating and fitting the model available on GitHub (
https://github.com/bmcclintock/langevinSSM
).
The proposed method greatly improves upon the traditional two-step approach, which consists of first fitting a simpler model to account for measurement error and/or temporal irregularity and then fitting a more complex model to the predicted locations from the first stage. Our simulations showed that the Langevin SSM better preserves the UD, minimizes bias in habitat selection parameters, and improves the recovery of individual trajectories. When applied to narwhal data, our method preserves more of the information contained in the raw observations than the two-step approach, enabling the recovery of a stronger effect of bathymetry. Narwhal tracking data are highly irregular, combine multiple data sources (Fastloc GPS and Argos), and are collected in complex landscapes such as fjord systems. The strong performance of our method reflects its suitability for accommodating these data complexities.

As measurement error increases, we found that both methods tend to underestimate the strength of habitat selection, with parameter estimates increasingly biased towards zero selection. This bias reflects a loss of information arising from greater uncertainty, leading to a weaker habitat-selection signal. However, this bias is consistently larger and more substantial for the two-step method, even with low measurement error, whereas it grows only gradually with the Langevin SSM. This discrepancy in bias suggests that two-step approaches are not well suited to accommodate substantial measurement error. By remaining robust to such uncertainty, our method provides a more reliable framework for estimating utilization distributions and associated habitat-selection parameters. Both methods appeared robust to missing data, likely because they are formulated in continuous time and evaluated on a fine temporal grid, such that sporadic removals of observations have only a negligible impact on inference. Although we examined a broad range of scenarios to represent realistic animal movement patterns, our analysis was not exhaustive and future research should investigate the effect of introducing missing data in blocks. Additionally, the effect of sampling rate and covariate correlation on the performance of the proposed method should be explored. Coarser sampling intervals may introduce bias (e.g.,
57
;
50
;
58
, but see
9
for an approach to mitigating such attenuation bias), while correlated covariates can reduce inferential power. Fortunately, the speed with which our Langevin SSM can be fitted with TMB facilitates further simulation studies investigating the properties of the model. For example, simulations with
n=25{,}000
observations on average required only 14 sec per model fit on an Apple M2 Max with 64GB RAM. Extensive simulation studies for the Langevin SSM simply are not practical using sampling-based approaches (e.g., Markov chain Monte Carlo, particle filtering).

The relatively poor performance of the two-step method was not surprising because the true data-generating model was a Langevin diffusion, whereas the movement model fitted in the first step of the two-step approach was a correlated random walk that ignored habitat selection. However, it is common practice in movement ecology to use a simpler model in the first step to filter and regularize raw tracking data, and subsequently fit a more complex model to the processed tracks for habitat selection analysis. The two-step approach could in principle be improved by using a more flexible movement model in the first step, such as a move persistence model (
3
;
41
), which extends the correlated random walk by allowing behavioral persistence to vary over time. However, we found that even the more modest upgrade from a simple random walk to a correlated random walk did not greatly improve model performance, with comparable levels of bias in the selection coefficients and inflation in
\gamma
and
\sigma
(see Appendix S1: Additional results). Besides, more complex models are often harder to fit. Together, these results suggest that the core limitation of the two-step approach lies not in the choice of movement model but in the decoupling of movement from habitat selection. Since habitat selection inherently influences movement, estimating movement independently in a first step necessarily introduces bias. Jointly estimating both, as in the Langevin SSM, is therefore inherently better suited to account for measurement error and temporal irregularity in the data.

Our findings from the narwhal movement data align with previous works identifying bathymetry as an important covariate in narwhal habitat selection (
45
;
42
;
38
). The relatively low persistence velocity estimate of approximately 15 minutes is likely due to the complex geography of the area. The intricate fjord system requires frequent directional changes, which prevents narwhal from maintaining straight trajectories and limits the model’s ability to detect prolonged periods of persistence in movement. Despite these frequent directional changes, our results revealed a clear pattern in depth preference and suggest an increased use of deep-water habitats. These findings are consistent with previous studies (
69
;
63
;
85
), and align with
85
who estimated a summer kernel utilization distribution focused along the deep central section of Eclipse Sound. As deep-diving marine mammals, narwhal likely use these areas primarily for foraging (
86
;
85
). However, deep diving may also be associated with other behaviors. For example, bottom contact during dives could help molting
(
85
)
, or alternatively deep diving may reflect a behavioral response to disturbance (
23
). Furthermore, the association between deep-water habitat use and deep-diving should be interpreted with caution, as narwhal may also spend considerable time at shallow depths within deep-water areas, such as during social interactions (
85
;
38
). It is also important to note that our model assumes a linear relationship between bathymetry and habitat selection, which may not fully capture the complexity of narwhal space use. The true relationship could be non-linear, with narwhal selecting for a particular depth range rather than responding uniformly across the bathymetric gradient. Moreover, additional environmental covariates such as distance to shore and seafloor slope are expected to influence habitat selection (
38
).

After developing our approach, we became aware of the related work by
20
, who independently incorporate measurement error into the Langevin framework via an SSM. However, their formulation does not express the stationary distribution in terms of habitat covariates, and their focus and methods differ considerably from ours. Specifically,
20
propose a Langevin movement model with a Gaussian mixture potential surface that explicitly incorporates measurement error to estimate true tracks from noisy data. Their approach models observations as Student’s
t
deviations around the true locations, using a mixture of
t
-distributions to accommodate both heavy-tailed and
X
-shaped error structures. Particle filtering is then used to recover the underlying trajectories given the model parameters. While their method effectively reconstructs true trajectories from noisy observations, it assumes model parameters are known and does not use the Langevin movement model for habitat selection inference, leaving both parameter estimation and habitat selection inference unexplored. Building on the same modeling framework, we explicitly express the stationary distribution as a resource selection function and simultaneously estimate all model parameters and unobserved processes via maximum likelihood. To reduce the computational costs of particle filtering
(
22
)
, we opt for a TMB Laplace approximation, offering a faster and more scalable solution.

Adding a penalty term to the negative log-likelihood discouraged locations from being estimated on land and substantially improved location estimation compared with the two-step approach. The method proposed by
20
, which enforces spatial constraints directly within the stochastic differential equation, is closely related to our approach. Specifically, their (exact) formulation can be approximated by including
d_{\text{water}}^{2}
as a covariate in
\pi(\bm{\mu})
, where
\beta_{\text{water}}=1/{2\lambda}
and
\lambda
is the penalty term of
20
. Differences in performance between the two approaches are likely attributable to different modeling choices. In particular,
20
use a potential surface with attraction towards activity centers, which further pulls locations towards the center of the feasible domain and may help keep them away from land. However, such a hotspot may not be ecologically appropriate for narwhal in Qikiqtaaluk. Additionally, their simulation study is conducted with known model parameters, thereby removing an additional source of variability. The rotational component in their SDE, however, is a particularly promising extension for species moving through spatially constrained environments such as narwhal in narrow fjords
20
.

We did not explicitly address the potential bias that can be introduced by the discretization in equation (
4
), which assumes that
\nabla\log\pi(\bm{\mu})
remains constant along the movement path between consecutive observations at times
t_{i}
and
t_{i+1}
. This bias should not be an issue in our simulation study, since we use a very fine temporal grid to minimize it, but it may be problematic in applications to real data with irregular and sometimes large observation gaps.
In practice, the magnitude of this bias is difficult to assess since it depends jointly on the speed of the animal’s movement, the strength of the drift term, and the rate at which environmental covariates change across space. As such, we recommend that practitioners working with coarser temporal resolutions (e.g., a couple of observations a day) consider the method proposed by
9
to reduce this discretization bias, which can be readily incorporated within our framework, though it comes at an increased computational cost. Future work could explore whether the trade-off is worthwhile for improving parameter estimates in practical applications. An alternative solution is provided by
20
, who directly tackle this limitation using splitting schemes, which split the non-linear SDE into a linear part with an exact Gaussian solution and a non-linear ordinary differential equation solved separately. Their approach elegantly handles non-linearity, although it would also likely increase computational cost.

The Langevin movement model provides a natural and powerful framework for quantifying habitat selection and deriving utilization distributions from spatial covariates. We focused on bathymetry in our narwhal example, although numerous other spatial features are relevant in marine systems, such as foraging “hotspots”, distance to colony, or distance to shore
(
4
, e.g.,)
. Habitat selection analyses using the Langevin movement model typically do not account for the temporal dynamics inherent in many environmental covariates (e.g., sea-ice concentration, temperature). Allowing covariates to vary over time violates the stationarity assumption of the Langevin movement model defined in equation (
1
). Consequently, the resulting potential surface represents instantaneous habitat selection rather than a true long-term utilization distribution, but this can still provide valuable insights into animal movement patterns (
58
). One simple approach to address this limitation is to incorporate average covariate values over the course of a study, as is frequently done in habitat selection studies (e.g., sea surface temperature in
39
, shipping traffic density in
89
). Exploring formulations with time-varying covariates while preserving some form of stationarity (e.g., periodically varying covariates as shown by
43
in hidden Markov models) would be a promising direction for future work. Time-varying habitat-selection coefficients that allow space use to change over time
(
17
;
58
, e.g., by season)
is another promising avenue for future research.

In this section, we provide further details on the theoretical foundations of the Laplace approximation and discuss potential sources of bias.

The Laplace approximation assumes that the integrand

f_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})

function of
\bm{z}

at its mode
(
82
)

by

in the integrand is the density

(
82
)
. However, the nonlinearity introduced by

\pi(\cdot)

4

\bm{z}
, which can introduce bias in
the approximated marginal likelihood. While the approximation generally
fails when the integrand has multiple modes, a Gaussian approximation
can remain appropriate even when the integrand is non-Gaussian, provided
it is unimodal and well-concentrated around its mode

(
13
;
82
)
.

An additional theoretical consideration is that standard asymptotic justifications for the Laplace approximation do not apply when the number of random effects grows with the sample size
(
70
)
, as is the case here since both locations and velocities are treated as latent variables. However, the Markov structure implies that each latent state
\bm{z}_{i}
depends only on its neighbors and its associated observation
\bm{y}_{i}
, such that the approximation relies on the local rather than global behavior of the integrand. Thus the Laplace approximation is expected to perform well when time intervals
\Delta_{i}
are short, such that
\nabla\log\pi
varies little between consecutive observations and the transition density between consecutive latent states is close to Gaussian. We therefore recommend limiting large time gaps in the data and performing track segmentation when necessary (e.g., as described in
Section
2.3
).

Appendix C
Additional implementation details on the Laplace approximation

Conveniently, the Laplace approximation is fully automated in the TMB
R
package (
44
), requiring only that the user provides the negative log-integrand from equation (
13
) as a
C++
script. The package then returns the (negative) logarithm of the Laplace approximation to equation (
13
) along with its gradient as a standard
R
function, which can be directly used for numerical optimization with quasi-Newton methods. During each optimization step—i.e., each call to the function or its gradient—TMB automatically performs an
inner
optimization over
\bm{z}
, required for the Taylor expansion in the Laplace approximation.
For users familiar with
C++
, TMB provides a flexible framework to implement fast, automatic Laplace approximations for a wide range of models, requiring only the specification of the negative log-likelihood (
44
;
3
;
6
;
53
;
12
).

Appendix D
Additional details on the Laplace approximation in the narwhal case study

In this section, we provide details on the Laplace approximation used
to evaluate the penalized marginal likelihood

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\bm{\theta})
in the narwhal case study.

The model parameters

\bm{\theta}
are estimated by minimizing the Laplace
approximation to
-\log\mathcal{L}_{p}(\bm{\theta})=-\log\int f_{p,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})\,d\bm{z}
, given by:

-\log\mathcal{L}_{p}(\bm{\theta})\approx-n\log\sqrt{2\pi}+\frac{1}{2}\log\det\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})-\log f_{p,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\hat{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})),

(S2)

where
\hat{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})
is the mode of

-\log f_{p,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
with
respect to
\bm{z}
for fixed
\bm{\theta}
, and

\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})
is the Hessian of
-\log f_{p,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
with respect
to
\bm{z}
, evaluated at
\hat{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})
.

In practice, this approximation is fully automated by TMB

(
44
)
and the user supplies the negative
log-integrand
-\log f_{p,\bm{\theta}}(\bm{y},\bm{z})
as a
C++
function, and TMB
internally computes the mode
\hat{\bm{z}}(\bm{\theta})
,
the Hessian
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})
, and returns

-\log\mathcal{L}_{p}(\bm{\theta})
along with its gradient
with respect to
\bm{\theta}
.

When the error ellipses do not overlap with any water, the penalty cannot push the associated locations toward water (Figure
S4

S4

\psi

10
). Since
\hat{\psi}\approx 5
, the Langevin SSM effectively assumed larger uncertainty ellipses for the observation equation.

In addition, the squared distance-to-water penalty produces a steep gradient for locations far from the shore, but this gradient becomes nearly null close to complex shorelines, such as within narrow fjords. As a result, some positions may be insufficiently corrected. This mechanism also likely explains why certain locations that were originally on water but very close to land were pushed onto land, as the penalty in these areas was not strong enough to counteract spurious movement toward land. While a linear distance penalty could improve adjustments near the coast, its gradient is too weak to correct distant terrestrial points, often pushing them beyond the study area rather than into viable aquatic habitat.

Appendix F
Additional simulation

Spatial constraint in the Langevin SSM

We conducted a simulation study to assess whether habitat selection patterns are preserved and accurately recovered when a spatial constraint is incorporated into the model as in equation (
16
). We use the same
framework as in the main simulation study, with an additional covariate consisting of fourteen polygons of varying sizes representing landmasses, together with the boundary of the study area buffered by 20 units to represent the surrounding coastline, to which a strongly negative selection coefficient (
-100
) was assigned to enforce strict avoidance. Movement was then simulated under high measurement error (
50\%
and
60\%
) to ensure that a proportion of observed locations fell within the constrained regions. During estimation, a negative selection coefficient was included for the constraint covariate in
\pi
in addition to the penalty written as a function of
d^{2}_{\text{water}}
in equation (
16
), with
\psi
fixed at its true value. We additionally examined the effect of placing a prior on
\sigma
. However, the UD was computed without the constraint covariate, as it represents a hard spatial constraint rather than a genuine avoidance process, yielding the same UD as in the previous simulation study. Figure
S5
illustrates examples of the constrained UD and simulated tracks under
60\%
measurement error (note that the figure displays the UD and observed locations only), where the dark patches represent constrained regions. On average, the proportion of land-based data in the
100
simulated datasets is
37\%
, compared to
10\%
in the narwhal dataset.

and

\geq 100

\sigma

for

0.39
,
-0.36
, and
-0.30

The main objective of this simulation study is to evaluate how the penalty influences the Langevin SSM estimates. Even when compared with the unconstrained two-step method, the constrained Langevin SSM consistently outperforms the two-step approach.
