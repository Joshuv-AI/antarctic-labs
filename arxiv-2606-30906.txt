Abstract
. Artificial Intelligence is increasingly applied to the field of law, and has the potential to increase access to justice. One particular movement that is gaining traction is that of agentic AI, wherein AI agents, based on Large Language Models (LLMs) can take autonomous actions. In particular, multi-agent approaches in the legal domain remain largely unexplored. In this paper, we investigate multi-agent deliberation methods for legal reasoning tasks using LLMs. We explore multi-agent deliberation (MAD) and introduce two novel multi-agent frameworks inspired by courtroom procedures and legal argumentation. Our experiments on both legal and non-legal benchmarks reveal that multi-agent frameworks achieve comparable overall performance to baseline large language models, but produce significantly distinct answers. Notably, these approaches can successfully solve cases that the baseline fails to address, and vice versa. We conduct a qualitative evaluation and highlight scenarios where multi-agent frameworks outperform monolithic approaches. For example, multi-agent approaches appear better suited for answering questions that require critical thinking from multiple perspectives. Our work positions multi-agent systems as a promising direction for AI in the legal domain, while demonstrating the potential of law-inspired multi-agent approaches for deliberation.

Method

In our experiment, we compare three multi-agent frameworks to a baselines large language model: a standard
Multi-Agent-Deliberation (MAD)

We explore the different approaches by comparing their performance and answers on a set of five datasets, consisting of four legal reasoning tasks and one logical reasoning task.
The latter was chosen to demonstrate the novel legal-inspired approaches are domain-agnostic and can thus also solve non-legal tasks.
All tasks are binary in nature and require the model to answer either ‘yes’ or ‘no’.

3.1.
Deliberation Frameworks

Baseline

The Baseline classifier is a simple framework used to compare with the three multi-agent frameworks. In this setup, a question is given to a large language model that should give an answer and a short explanation. This Baseline classifier acts as a reference point to better grasp the performance and behaviors of the multi-agent frameworks. Questions are answered using a single model call.

Multi-Agent Deliberation (MAD)

Our Multi-Agent Deliberation (MAD) classifier employs three large language models, referred to as agents, each responsible for producing a prediction. The framework is grounded in prior work inspired by the Society of Mind
(
12
)
. Over a fixed number of deliberation rounds, each agent is shown the responses of the other two and asked to revise its own accordingly. In our implementation, the agents make an initial prediction and then deliberate for two rounds. The final answer is then determined by majority vote. Given three initial predictions and two rounds of three refinements each, the MAD setup requires a total of nine model calls.

3-Ply

We introduce the 3-Ply classifier, based on a simulated courtroom with three LLM agents representing the plaintiff, defendant, and judge. The plaintiff aims to convince the judge that the answer to the question is ‘yes’, whereas the defendant will claim that the answer is ‘no’. The impartial judge will then decide, based on the merits of the arguments of the plaintiff and defendant, what the answer to the question should be and why.
The reasoning behind this 3-Ply approach is that both perspectives are always considered explicitly.
The 3-Ply classifier process consists of three parts: an initial argument by the plaintiff, a counterargument by the defendant, and finally a rebuttal by the plaintiff. The judge uses the arguments put forth across all parts to determine the final answer. This leads to a total of four model calls to answer questions using the 3-Ply framework.

Parrots

The Parrot classifier is a novel framework that we introduce, based on a recent notion in the field of argumentation that large language models should argue with us by design
(
29
)
. Instead of an agreeable stochastic parrot, proponents of this notion suggest a set of four critical parrots, each with a different perspective. We apply this notion to multi-agent deliberation, where an artificial agent named ‘Alex’, represented by an LLM, argues back and forth with a set of four parrots, represented by a single LLM. Following the literature, parrots challenge assumptions or definitions used by Alex or other parrots (Socratic), rebut or undermine the arguments of Alex and other parrots’, testing their robustness (Cynical), offer alternative perspectives or possible interpretations that might otherwise be overlooked (Eclectic), and critically evaluate the logical strength of the arguments, highlighting weaknesses, inconsistencies or fallacies (Aristotelian). In our framework, the Alex agent makes an initial prediction in the same fashion as the Baseline classifier. The parrots react to this prediction, each from their own perspective. Subsequently, Alex responds to the parrots and is asked whether they want to continue the conversation or not. In our experiment, Alex may continue the conversation for a maximum of three rounds. The number of model calls per question is, therefore, varied between three and seven.

3.3.
Experimental setup

In our experiment, we randomly sample 250 questions from each of the datasets, yielding a total of 1250 questions for evaluating our deliberation frameworks. We ensure a balanced label distribution in these questions. The four frameworks are tasked with solving these questions. To illustrate, we show an example question from the Privacy dataset in Figure
2
. Here we see a clause from a privacy policy, alongside a description. The question asks whether that description is correct with regard to the clause. In this example question, the answer is ‘yes’ because under this particular privacy policy geolocation data includes WiFi location data. Hence, even though the WiFi location data is not directly mentioned, it is deemed to be included under the broader clause, which illustrates typical interpretative reasoning in which lawyers engage.

To improve performance, we make use of 2-shot prompting, where we provide the frameworks with two similar examples from a separate training dataset in addition to the main question. We use BM25 ranking to identify the two most similar questions, a standard information retrieval method to estimate document–query relevance
(
3
)
.

We used OpenAI’s GPT-5-mini model, as their models are among the most used. For financial reasons, we did not explore larger and more expensive models, which we address in the discussion. The prompts used by the frameworks were created based on the literature wherever possible and empirical results based on light prompt engineering using a smaller model (gpt-5-nano). For instance, the system prompt that describes the perspectives of the parrots agent was taken verbatim from the paper that introduced the concept
(
29
)
.

5.
Discussion

Based on the performance of the four frameworks, as shown in Figure
3
, no single framework consistently outperforms the others. In two of the datasets, the 3-Ply framework achieves the highest F1 score, while the MAD framework performs best on the remaining three. Across all datasets, the average F1 scores are highly similar, differing by no more than 1.5 points.

The absence of a significant performance difference does not imply that the frameworks made similar predictions. A significant difference was observed between the predictions of each multi-agent framework and those of the baseline classifier. As illustrated in Figure
4
, approximately 7.5–10% of the questions received different answers from the multi-agent frameworks compared to the baseline. In roughly half of these cases, the baseline’s answer was incorrect, whereas the multi-agent framework provided the correct response.
Furthermore, we observe an interesting asymmetry in the Privacy dataset: many questions that the baseline fails to solve are correctly resolved by at least one of the multi-agent frameworks, whereas the reverse does not occur; every case correctly solved by the baseline is also solved by at least one multi-agent framework. Although this comparison reflects the combined behavior of multiple frameworks rather than a one-to-one evaluation, it suggests that multi-agent approaches can capture forms of reasoning that remain inaccessible to the baseline classifier, even though they also introduce their own errors.

We illustrate this phenomenon in Figure
5
, which presents the outputs of the four frameworks for the example question shown in Figure
2
.
In this example, the Baseline produces an incorrect answer by failing to focus on the fact that the clause does not explicitly mention WiFi location data.
This highlights a recurring pattern in the results, where frameworks need to balance between literal and pragmatic interpretations.
Although MAD ultimately provides the correct answer, it first needs to reconcile conflicting agent responses that include hallucinatory justifications unrelated to the question (for instance, references to cell phone number data).
By contrast, both 3-Ply and Parrots clearly articulate the central issue, favouring a pragmatic interpretation over the literal one adopted by the Baseline.
Because of the structured nature of their deliberation, these two multi-agent frameworks arrive at a more nuanced, legally informed, and ultimately correct stance.

Our general qualitative observations suggest that multi-agent frameworks are effective in cases where legal clauses are ambiguous or allow multiple interpretations, such as the literal and pragmatic interpretation in Figure
2
.
In such scenarios, MAD and 3-Ply often achieve higher accuracy than the baseline by reconciling diverse perspectives, while Parrots can identify task-specific patterns in the data that the baseline overlooks.
Additionally, multi-agent outputs provide richer justifications, which can make hybrid human-AI collaboration more effective.
Hallucinations remain a key limitation in all frameworks, as justifications sometimes include entities or conclusions not present in the source text.
Recent work argues that these hallucinations are structurally inevitable, arising from the fundamental design of large language models
(
6
)
.
We should note that these findings are exploratory, and they highlight the need for standardized evaluation methods for multi-interpretation reasoning in legal AI as a direction for future work.

One of the major downsides of multi-agent approaches to LLMs is that they require more calls and are thus more expensive in terms of resources. While the baseline only requires a single call, 3-Ply uses 4 per question, and MAD uses 9. The Parrot framework used an average of 3.48 calls per question, as it can decide to end the deliberation dynamically. In terms of pure performance, the extra calls used by the multi-agent approaches do not seem to improve the reasoning of the models.

In Table
1
, we more closely examined the MAD framework using different numbers of agents and rounds of reflection.
We see that performance remains rather stable across the different parameters. For instance, a setup with only 3 agents and no reflection rounds yields an F1 score of 89.4, only 0.9 lower than those with two reflection rounds. Note that in the setups without reflection rounds, the MAD classifier effectively acts as an ensemble with majority voting. This suggests that the reflection itself therefore does contribute significantly to the performance. More reflections do seem to increase the number of disagreements with the baseline classifier, including the number of correct disagreements. So while the performance does not seem to change, the reasoning behavior does change slightly.

We evaluate the effect of the number of deliberation rounds in the Parrots framework in Table
2
. Similar to the MAD framework, changing the number of rounds or making it dynamic, does not seem to impact performance significantly. The number of disagreements appears to decrease slightly with more static rounds, and appears higher with a dynamic number of deliberation rounds.

Comparing results across datasets shows that some benchmarks are inherently easier than others. However, with respect to the frameworks, there is no clear distinction between performance on legal versus non-legal benchmarks, as the observed differences are comparable.
A closer analysis of errors reveals that most mistakes across all frameworks were false negatives, with rates of 82.02% for the Baseline, 82.18% for MAD, 65.59% for 3-Ply, and 85.42% for Parrots. The 3-Ply framework is the exception, showing a notably lower false-negative rate, which may reflect its inherently more polarized setup.

We should note that our frameworks were tested using a smaller commercially available language model due to financial constraints. Larger models, with larger context windows, could yield different reasoning behaviour as it can effectively work with more dialogue and information. The behavior of LLM is non-deterministic, and therefore, results may be influenced by noise.
Furthermore, since all of benchmarks are publicly available on the internet, there is chance of data contamination, and that the LLM may have been exposed to the questions during training, which could yield inflated results.
We hypothesize that such contamination may have a larger positive impact on monolithic systems than on multi-agent approaches, as the latter benefit from deliberation between agents; however, this remains an open question for future research.

One advantage of a multi-agent approach is that inter-agent reasoning is made explicit through argumentation. However, these arguments are not assessed for soundness or validity, and LLMs are known to produce illogical conclusions even from seemingly coherent arguments
(
39
)
. In particular, there is no guarantee that agents act rationally with respect to previously stated arguments, whether produced by other agents or by themselves. At the same time, AI & Law offers a well-established body of work on formal argumentation. Future research could therefore investigate neuro-symbolic approaches that integrate these formal argumentation methods into multi-agent systems.

Another potential advantage of a multi-agent approach is that it may help mitigate sycophancy, i.e., the tendency of a single model to uncritically agree with the user or with earlier outputs. This effect can arise when agents are encouraged to adopt differing perspectives, as in the MAD approach, and more explicitly in systems such as 3-Ply and Parrots, where agents are assigned specialized roles with predefined viewpoints. Future work should empirically assess under which design choices such role- and perspective-based agent architectures meaningfully reduce sycophantic behavior, and when they merely reproduce it at the group level.

6.
Conclusion

In this study, we explore the potential of multi-agent deliberation in legal reasoning tasks. We find that the multi-agent approaches do not provide a higher overall performance. By providing various perspectives, however, the multi-agent frameworks can solve questions that monolithic approaches cannot, while failing on other. Additionally, we introduce two new multi-agent deliberation frameworks inspired by courtroom procedures and argumentation theory, showing that explicitly incorporating diverse viewpoints can lead to alternative conclusions.

Prompts used in the Multi-Agend Deliberation (MAD) system.

You are an impartial judge. Your primary task is to answer the question as accurately as possible.
The plaintiff always argues "yes" and the defendant always argues "no," but treat these as positions, not facts.
Base your decision primarily on the question itself.
Use the arguments as supplemental reasoning to refine your judgment, but consider their logical validity and relevance carefully.
Do not invent new facts. Decide clearly between "yes" and "no."

(a)

System prompt for Judge agent.

You are the plaintiff. Always argue "yes" for the question.
Support your position with reasoning that directly addresses the question, highlighting logical connections, implications, and relevant evidence.
Ensure your argument is clear, structured, and concise. Do not fabricate facts.

(b)

System prompt for Plaintiff agent.

You are the defendant. Always argue "no" for the question.
Critically examine the question and anticipate potential flaws or assumptions in a "yes" argument.
Provide a clear, structured critique that is grounded in the question. Do not fabricate facts.

(c)

System prompt for Defendant agent.

The court is considering the following question:

\{question\}

As the plaintiff, argue "yes" and provide a structured, concise reasoning chain that directly supports this position in relation to the question.

(d)

Prompt for intial argument of the plaintiff (first ply).

The court is considering the following question:

\{question\}

The plaintiff has argued "yes" as follows:

\{plaintiff\_argument\}

As the defendant, argue "no" and provide a critical, structured counterargument.
Focus on weaknesses, assumptions, or gaps in the plaintiff’s reasoning and relate them to the question.

(e)

Prompt for the counterargument of the defendant (second ply).

The court is considering the following question:

\{question\}

The plaintiff’s argument:

\{plaintiff\_argument\}

The defendant’s argument:

\{defendant\_argument\}

As the plaintiff, respond to the defendant’s strongest points.
Reinforce your "yes" position and ensure your reasoning is clearly grounded in the question.

(f)

Prompt for rebuttal of the plaintiff (third ply).

A legal dispute has been presented regarding the following question:
\{question\}

The following arguments have been presented:

Plaintiff (yes):

\{plaintiff\_argument\}

Defendant (no):

\{defendant\_argument\}

Plaintiff rebuttal:

\{plaintiff\_rebuttal\}

Current instructions:

As judge, first evaluate the question independently.

Then consider the plaintiff’s and defendant’s arguments as reasoning chains. Compare them systematically:

1. Identify which points are logically sound and relevant to the question.

2. Identify which points are weak, irrelevant, or flawed.

3. Determine whether the arguments reinforce or contradict each other.

4. Use this analysis to refine your final decision.

Treat the arguments as supplemental reasoning, not as new facts. Base your verdict primarily on the question.

Explicitly reason step by step and conclude strictly with:

Answer: yes or Answer: no

(g)

Prompt for the final decision by the judge.

Prompts used in the 3-Ply system.

You are Alex, a legal expert who must reach a well-reasoned conclusion to a difficult question.

(a)

System prompts for the User agent.

You are four parrots who deliberate with Alex (a legal expert) and with each other:

- The Socratic Parrot: Challenges the assumptions or definitions used by Alex or other parrots. Are the terms and concepts well defined? Does Alex interpret the question correctly?

- The Cynical Parrot: Rebuts or undermines Alex’s and other parrots’ arguments, testing their robustness.

- The Eclectic Parrot: Offers alternative perspectives or possible interpretations that might otherwise be overlooked.

- The Aristotelian Parrot: Critically evaluates the logical strength of arguments, highlighting weaknesses, inconsistencies, or fallacies.

Your goal is to improve Alex’s reasoning so that the final answer to the question is as accurate and well-founded as possible.

This is a structured deliberation, not casual dialogue. The parrots ask targeted questions, raise objections, or provide alternative framings, but they do not simply restate Alex’s conclusions.
Parrots may speak in sequence if each has a distinct and meaningful contribution, but avoid redundancy.
The conversation should stay coherent and concise, always focused on solving the question.
If a parrot has nothing useful to add, they should explicitly say so.

(b)

System prompts for the Parrots agent.

Parrots, it is your turn to respond to Alex. Choose the most suitable parrots to speak in the most suitable order. Give priority to parrots that have not spoken yet. Make sure to only add useful commentary and do not repeat yourselves. You do not have to answer the question, only help Alex reason. Parrots that agree with Alex should not speak. Keep your comments short and concise.

(c)

Prompt for the parrots agent.

Alex, please respond only to the parrots who spoke in the last round.

Use the format ’Response to [Parrot Name]: …’.

Do not repeat questions or what was said before.
Keep your responses short and concise.
You may ask follow-up questions.

Then, after your response and questions, refine your argument if needed. At the end of your response:

1. Give a summary of your reasoning.

2. Restate your current stance clearly in the form:

Answer: Yes or Answer: No

3. On a new line, decide if you want to continue the conversation:

Continue: yes (default — if there are still open questions or doubts OR if not all parrots have spoken yet.)

Continue: no (if you are confident in your stance OR the parrots are only repeating points you have already addressed).

If you choose "Continue: no", briefly explain why further discussion is unnecessary. Write the explanation before answer and continue.
