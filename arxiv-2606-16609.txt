Abstract
. This work investigates the stability of outliers from the circular law, via the convergence of their associated diagonal overlaps between eigenvectors – also known as the squared eigenvalue condition numbers. We consider and compare two paradigmatic cases, namely: (1) The Complex Ginibre Ensemble conditioned on the existence of an outlier; (2) The outlier induced by a rank-one Hermitian perturbation of a complex Ginibre matrix. In both cases, we prove almost sure convergence towards a specific constant that only depends on the radius of the outlier and its status – either conditioned or induced. These results can be generalized to other complex integrable ensembles with the same techniques, and complement our understanding of eigenvalue stability in non-Hermitian ensembles.

1.
Introduction

A key question in random matrix theory is to quantify the stability of an eigenvalue under a perturbation of the matrix; in non-Hermitian ensembles especially, eigenvalues are expected to be less stable – in various ways – than in Hermitian settings. Since the seminal works of Chalker and Mehlig
[

15

\mathscr{O}_{ij}:=\langle L_{i}|L_{j}\rangle\langle R_{j}|R_{i}\rangle,

where
(L_{i})

\langle L_{i}|R_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}

The way in which the matrix of overlaps quantifies eigenvalue stability is for instance made clear by the following fact

Proposition 1

Let

In particular, the stability of the eigenvalue

This paper investigates the stability – and thus, the distribution of the diagonal overlap – of an eigenvalue that lies outside the unit disk: such eigenvalues are usually called outliers. We consider two paradigmatic cases and compare them. The first case is that of the complex Ginibre ensemble conditioned on
\{\lambda_{1}=z\}

|z|>1

z=t

t>1

2.
Stability of a conditioned outlier

In this section, we consider the Complex Ginibre Ensemble
conditioned
on having an outlier – that is to say that we simply assume that the outlier happens to be there, despite the relative rareness of such an event. Without loss of generality, we will assume that the outlier is the eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
, and study its diagonal overlap
\mathscr{O}_{11}
, for which we prove convergence almost surely (Theorem
7
), and in expectation (Proposition
8
), towards the order one constant
\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{2}-1}
.

Figure 3.
The spectrum of a Ginibre matrix conditioned on
\lambda_{1}=3
, with the relative size of diagonal overlaps indicated on a color scale.

We will rely on the following lemmata, which can all be proved by classical techniques.

Lemma 4

(Gaussian tail estimate)
.

Let
(X_{k}^{(N)})_{1\leq k\leq N}
be an array of i.i.d. complex standard Gaussian variables. Then, for every
\gamma>2
, almost surely, there exists an integer
N_{0}
such that

\forall N\geq N_{0},\ \forall k\in[\![1,N]\!],\ \left|X_{k}^{(N)}\right|^{2}<\gamma\log N.

Lemma 5

(Second largest eigenvalue
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\{\lambda_{k}^{(N)}\}_{1\leq k\leq N}
be an array of random complex numbers such that for every
N
,
\{\lambda_{1}^{(N)},\dots,\lambda_{N}^{(N)}\}
is distributed like the spectrum of a complex Ginibre matrix conditioned on
\{\lambda_{1}=z\}
with fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
; then almost surely, for any fixed
\epsilon>1
there exists an integer
N_{0}>0
such that

\forall N\geq N_{0},\ \forall k\in[\![2,N]\!]\ |\lambda_{k}|<1+\epsilon.

Lemma 6

(Conditional Circular Law
[
19
]
)
.

Let
\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}\}
the spectrum of a complex Ginibre matrix conditioned on
\{\lambda_{1}=z\}
with fixed
z\in\mathbb{C}
; almost surely, the empirical spectral distribution

\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}

converges weakly to the circular law. That is to say that, for any continuous bounded function
\phi
, we have

\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\phi(\lambda_{k})\xrightarrow{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\phi(z)\mathrm{d}z.

Note that the conditioned Ginibre process with joint density (
4
) is a determinantal process that resembles the unconditioned Ginibre process, with a slightly modified kernel. The bound on the second largest eigenvalue was readily justified in
[
19
]
with a variation of Kostlan’s argument
[
14
]
, and the expected circular law was obtained through the classical kernel estimates from Ameur, Hedenmalm and Makarov
[
2
]
.

With the above facts in mind, we can provide a proof of our main theorem.

Theorem 7

(Almost-sure convergence of diagonal overlaps)
.

Let
G
be a complex Ginibre matrix conditioned on
\{\lambda_{1}=z\}
with
|z|>1
. Then, the diagonal overlap associated to
\lambda_{1}
converges almost surely to a constant, namely:

\mathscr{O}_{11}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{a.s.}\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{2}-1}.

Proof.

The distribution of the diagonal overlap
\mathscr{O}_{11}
was investigated in
[
6
]
, and in particular it was found (Theorem 2.2) that

(6)

\mathscr{O}_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\prod_{k=2}^{N}\left(1+\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}\right),

where
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N}
are the eigenvalues of
G
, and
X_{1},\dots,X_{k}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian variables. The probability measures associated to these families of variables are denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X}
respectively.
Taking the logarithm of (
6
) yields

(7)

\ln\mathscr{O}_{11}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\ln\left(1+\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}\right).

We then use the following bound on the logarithm:

\forall c>\frac{1}{2},\quad\exists\eta>0,\quad\forall x\in[-\eta,\eta]\quad\left|\ln\left(1+x\right)-x\right|<c|x|^{2}.

For our current purpose, any choice of
c
and
\eta
will do; for instance one may take
c=1
and
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
. Indeed, thanks to lemmata
4
and
5
, we know that there are constants
\gamma>2
and
\delta>0
such that with probability
1
, for
N
large enough,

\forall k=1,\dots,N\qquad|X_{k}|^{2}<\gamma\log N\ \quad\text{and}\quad\quad|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|>\delta

in particular, with probability
1
, for large enough
N
, the term
\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}
in (
7
) is smaller than any prescribed
\eta
, so that we can write:

\left|\ln\mathscr{O}_{11}-\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}\right|<c\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{|X_{k}|^{4}}{N^{2}|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{4}}<\frac{c\gamma^{2}(\log N)^{2}}{N\delta^{4}},

a deterministic bound which tends to
0
.
We then consider the random variables

Y_{k}=\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{X}(|X_{k}|^{2})}{|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}=\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}-\frac{1}{|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}

which (with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{X}
) are independent and centered with uniformly bounded fourth moment; thus their average
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}Y_{k}
converges almost surely to
0
,
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-almost surely. This gives us:

\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{|X_{k}|^{2}}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}=\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{N|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}+o_{\mathbb{P}_{X}-a.s.}(1)

which, by Lemma
6
, converges
\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}
-almost surely to an integral that one can explicitly compute:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=2}^{N}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}}\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{\mathbb{P}_{\lambda}-a.s.}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\frac{1}{|\lambda_{1}-z|^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}z=\ln\left(\frac{|\lambda_{1}|^{2}}{|\lambda_{1}|^{2}-1}\right).

This concludes the proof, as with overall probability
1
,
\mathscr{O}_{11}
is arbitrarily close to
\frac{|\lambda_{1}|^{2}}{|\lambda_{1}|^{2}-1}
for large enough
N
.
∎

Alternatively, this result for a conditioned outlier can be obtained via the approach used by Fyodorov
[
12
]
, where an exact finite-
N
expression of the joint density of
\lambda_{1}
and
\mathscr{O}_{11}
is obtained via partial Schur form and supersymmetry; indeed, the asymptotics of the joint density (2.13) yield convergence to a point mass at the value
\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{2}-1}
in the case of an outlier.
One reason to present the above description of the distribution of
\mathscr{O}_{11}
is that the same method can be applied to eigenvalues of the complex elliptic Ginibre ensemble (as was done in
[
19
]
) and also in principle to other non-Gaussian but integrable ensembles such as the spherical ensemble or the truncated unitary ensembles
[
11
]
.

Note that the diagonal overlap of an outlier eigenvalue is still an unstable object and that, for instance, it does not converge in
L^{2}
; indeed, a quick verification based on formula (
6
) and the joint density (
4
) shows that
\mathscr{O}_{11}
has infinite variance even when conditioned on
\lambda_{1}
outside the bulk. However, its expectation is finite, and converges to the same value, as we now prove.

Proposition 8

(Convergence of the conditional expectation of diagonal overlaps)
.

In the regime where
|z|>1
,

(8)

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathscr{O}_{11}\ |\ \lambda_{1}=z\right)=\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{2}-1}+O(N^{-1}).

Proof.

For a complex Ginibre matrix, we know from
[
1
,
6
,
9
]

that the conditional expectation of the diagonal overlap is given by

(9)

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathscr{O}_{11}\ |\ \lambda_{1}=z\right)=N\left(\frac{e^{(N)}(N|z|^{2})}{e^{(N-1)}(N|z|^{2})}-|z|^{2}\right),

for any
z\in\mathbb{C}
. This expression was studied in the bulk (
|z|<1
) where it is asymptotically equivalent to
N(1-|z|^{2})
.
In the regime where
|z|>1
, we rely on the relevant asymptotic from Lemma
17
(proved in the appendix) and find, with
t=|z|^{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\mathscr{O}_{11}\ |\ \lambda_{1}=z\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(\frac{e^{(N-1)}(N|z|^{2})+\frac{N|z|^{2}}{N!}}{e^{(N-1)}(N|z|^{2})}-|z|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(1-|z|^{2}+\frac{N^{N}|z|^{2N}}{N!e^{(N-1)}(N|z|^{2})}\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(1-|z|^{2}+\frac{1}{\frac{1}{|z|^{2}-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{|z|^{2}}{(|z|^{2}-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})}\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(1-|z|^{2}+(|z|^{2}-1)\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{N}\frac{|z|^{2}}{(|z|^{2}-1)^{2}}+O(N^{-2})}\right)

\displaystyle=N\left(1-|z|^{2}+(|z|^{2}-1)\left(1+\frac{1}{N}\frac{|z|^{2}}{(|z|^{2}-1)^{2}}+O(N^{-2})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{|z|^{2}}{|z|^{2}-1}+O(N^{-1})

which yields the claim.
∎

3.
Stability of the outlier of a rank-one perturbation

In this section, we consider the rank-one perturbation

G(t):=G+tvv^{*}.

where
G=(G_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq N}
is a complex Ginibre matrix with i.i.d entries
G_{ij}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1/N)
, and
t>1
is a fixed perturbation parameter.
By general property of unitary invariance of Ginibre matrices
3
3

3

Indeed, for any given unitary matrix
U
,
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}UGU^{*}
and so with the relevant choice of
U
, one obtains
G(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}G+te_{1}e_{1}^{*}
.
, we may assume that
v=e_{1}
, the first vector of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, so that only the coefficient
G_{11}
is affected by the perturbation. It is well known
[
8
,
18
]
that the empirical spectral measure of
G
converges almost surely to the circular law
\mu_{\mathbb{D}}:=\frac{1}{\pi}1_{\lvert z\lvert\leq 1}\mathrm{d}^{2}z
and that the spectral radius satisfies
\rho(G):=\max_{\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(G)}\lvert\lambda\lvert\rightarrow 1
almost surely. In particular, the unperturbed Ginibre ensemble has no outlier at the macroscopic scale; the rank-one perturbation
te_{1}e_{1}^{*}
does not affect the global spectral distribution : the empirical spectral measure
\mu_{G(t)}
of
G(t)
still converges almost surely to
\mu_{\mathbb{D}}
. However, for
t>1
, an outlier eigenvalue appears
[
18
]
. More precisely, almost surely, for
N
large enough, the spectrum of
G(t)
consists of exactly one eigenvalue located in a neighborhood of
z=t
, while all the remaining eigenvalues lie inside an arbitrary small neighborhood of the unit disk, as represented on Figure
4
. We denote the outlier eigenvalue by
\lambda_{1}
.

Figure 4.
The spectrum of a perturbed Ginibre matrix
G(t)=G+tvv^{*}
with
t=3
, with the relative size of diagonal overlaps indicated on a color scale. Note that the outlier eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is located in a small neighborhood of its expected value, but not exactly at

.

Our goal in this section is to determine the limiting behavior of
\mathscr{O}_{11}
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.
By definition we have

G=\begin{pmatrix}a_{N}&b_{N}^{*}\\
c_{N}&D_{N}\end{pmatrix}

with
a_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(0,\frac{1}{N}\right),\ b_{N},c_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(0,\frac{1}{N}I_{N-1}\right),\ D_{N}\sim\mathrm{Gin}\left(N-1,\frac{1}{N}\right)
all independent from each other.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\lambda\notin\mathrm{Sp}(D_{N})
, then

(10)

\lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(G(t))\iff F(\lambda):=\lambda-(a_{N}+t)-b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)c_{N}=0

where
\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda):=(\lambda-D_{N})^{-1}

and the overlap associated to an eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
is

\mathscr{O}_{1}1=\frac{(1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})c_{N})(1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}b_{N})}{\lvert 1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{2}c_{N}\lvert^{2}}.

Proof.

The first part of the proposition is a direct application of Schur’s complement formula, which yields

\mathrm{det}(\lambda-G)=\mathrm{det}(\lambda-D)F(\lambda).

This proves the first part of the proposition.

Let us look for a right eigenvector of
G
for the eigenvalue
\lambda
, of the form

r=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}

with
x\in\mathbb{C},\ y\in\mathbb{C}^{N-1}
.
Then

Gr=\lambda r\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}(a_{N}+t)x+b_{N}^{*}y=\lambda x\\
c_{N}x+D_{N}y=\lambda y\end{cases}\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}F(\lambda)=0\\
y=\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)c_{N}x\end{cases}\Longleftrightarrow y=\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)c_{N}x.

Taking
x=1
we get that
r=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)c_{N}\end{pmatrix}

is a right eigenvector of
G
. Likewise one can see that
l^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}1&b^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)\end{pmatrix}

is a left eigenvector. Note that
l^{*}r=1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda)^{2}c_{N}:=s
.
Therefore if we define
l_{0}^{*}:=\frac{l^{*}}{s}
and
r_{0}:=r
we have that

\mathscr{O}_{11}=\lvert\lvert L_{1}\lvert\lvert^{2}\lvert\lvert R_{1}\lvert\lvert^{2}=\frac{(1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})c_{N})(1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}b_{N})}{\lvert 1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{2}c_{N}\lvert^{2}}

which concludes the proof.
∎

The proof now reduces to the analysis of the three resolvent quantities

1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}b_{N},\qquad 1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)c_{N},\qquad b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}^{2}(z)c_{N}.

We first establish almost sure pointwise convergence of the quadratic terms towards

q(z):=\frac{\lvert z\lvert^{2}}{\lvert z\lvert^{2}-1},

using a multiplicative decomposition obtain from the Schur form together with the circular law. The bilinear term is shown to converge pointwise to
0
using classical probabilistic arguments.

These pointwise convergences are then upgraded to uniform convergence on compact subsets of
\{\lvert z\lvert>1\}.
The key ingredients are a resolvent bound from the literature, yielding uniform control of
\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)
on compact sets away from the unit disk, and standard resolvent identities, which imply equicontinuity of the quantities under consideration. We can then promote these pointwise convergences to uniform convergence using a finite-net argument.

Finally, since the outlier eigenvalue converges almost surely to
t
with
t>1
, it eventually belongs to a fixed compact neighborhood of
t
,
K\subset\{\lvert z\lvert>1\}
, for instance a closed ball with appropriate radius. The uniform convergence results may therefore be evaluated at
z=\lambda_{1}
, yielding the claimed asymptotics for the overlap.

We begin with the first technical ingredient of the proof, namely a uniform bound on the resolvent outside the unit disk.

Lemma 10
.

Let
K\subset\{\lvert z\lvert>1\}
a compact. Then there exists
C_{K}>0
such that almost surely

\sup_{z\in K}\lvert\lvert\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\lvert\lvert\leq C_{K}

for all sufficiently large
N
.

Proof.

We will prove that there exist
C_{K}>0
such that

\inf_{z\in K}s_{\min}(z-D_{N})\geq C_{K}

which is equivalent to our statement. For
z\in\mathbb{C}
consider the Hermitian matrix
Y_{N}^{z}:=(z-D_{N})(z-D_{N})^{*}
. It follows from Proposition 2.2 and equation (
18
a) of
[
10
]
that the empirical spectral distribution of
Y_{N}^{z}
converges to a deterministic measure supported on an interval
[e_{-}(z),e_{+}(z)]
such that
e_{-}(z)>0
whenever
\lvert z\lvert>1.
Since
K
is a compact subset of
\{\lvert z\lvert>1\}
, continuity of
e_{-}
implies that
m_{K}:=\inf_{z\in K}e_{-}(z)>0
. Fix
z\in K
. Since the interval
[0,m_{K}/2]
lies strictly outside the limiting support of the spectrum of
Y_{N}^{z}
, the absence of outliers for
Y_{N}^{z}
(see e.g Theorem 3 of
[
20
]
or Theorem 1.1 of
[
3
]
) implies that almost surely, for all
N
large enough,
\lambda_{\min}(Y_{N}^{z})\geq m_{K}/2
. Equivalently,
s_{\min}(z-D_{N})\geq\sqrt{m_{K}/2}
. To obtain a uniform estimate let
\delta:=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{m_{K}/2}
and choose a finite
\delta
-net
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{M}\}\subset K
. By the previous pointwise estimate, almost surely, for all
N
large enough,

s_{\min}(z_{j}-D_{N})\geq\sqrt{m_{K}/2}

for every
j=1,\ldots,M.
Now using the fact that
s_{\min}
is
1

[
5

)

s_{\min}(z-D_{N})\geq s_{\min}(z_{j}-D_{N})-\lvert z-z_{j}\lvert\geq\delta.

Therefore, almost surely, for all sufficiently large
N
,

\inf_{z\in K}s_{\min}(z-D_{N})\geq\delta

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now prove the key probabilistic representation underlying the proof of the pointwise convergence.

Lemma 11
.

Set
Q_{N}(z):=1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}b_{N}
for
z\notin Sp(D_{N})
and denote by
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}
the eigenvalues of
D_{N}
. Then

Q_{N}(z)\overset{}{=}\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(1+\frac{\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}}{N\lvert z-\mu_{k}\lvert^{2}}\right)

where
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussian random variables, independent of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}.

Proof.

Let
D_{N}=UTU^{*}
be a Schur decomposition of
D_{N}
, where
T
is upper triangular and has diagonal entries
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}
. Since
b_{N}
is independent of
D_{N}
and has a unitary invariant Gaussian
\beta:=U^{*}b
has law
\mathcal{N}_{C}(0,N^{-1}I)
and is independent of
T
and moreover

b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}b_{N}=\beta^{*}(z-T)^{-1}(z-T)^{-*}\beta.

Thus it is enough to prove the identity for
T
and
\beta
.

Let
T_{k}
be the
k\times k
upper-left principal block of
T
, and let
\beta_{k}
be the vector made of the first
k
coordinates of
\beta.
Define
R_{k}(z):=(z-T_{k})^{-1}
and
Q_{k}(z):=1+\beta_{k}^{*}R_{k}(z)R_{k}(z)^{*}\beta_{k}.
Then
Q_{0}(z)=1
and
Q_{N-1}(z)
is the quantity of interest. Write

T_{k+1}=\begin{pmatrix}T_{k}&\tau_{k}\\
0&\mu_{k+1}\end{pmatrix},\ \beta_{k+1}=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{k}\\
\eta_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Conditionally on the spectrum, the vectors
\tau_{k}
have independent complex Gaussian entries of variance
1/N
and are independent of the previous columns. Also, the variables
\eta_{k}
are independent complex Gaussians of variance
1/N
, independent of
T
. Set
y_{k}:=R_{k}(z)^{*}\beta_{k}.
Then
Q_{k}(z)=1+\lvert\lvert y_{k}\lvert\lvert^{2}.
Write

y_{k+1}=\begin{pmatrix}y_{k}\\
\gamma_{k+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
(z-T_{k+1})^{*}y_{k+1}=\beta_{k+1}
and

(z-T_{k+1})^{*}=\begin{pmatrix}(z-T_{k})^{*}&0\\
-\tau_{k}^{*}&\overline{z}-\overline{\mu_{k+1}}\end{pmatrix}

we obtain that

\gamma_{k+1}=\frac{\eta_{k+1}+\tau_{k}^{*}y_{k}}{\overline{z}-\overline{\mu_{k+1}}}.

Therefore,

Q_{k+1}(z)=1+\lvert\lvert y_{k+1}\lvert\lvert^{2}=Q_{k}(z)+\frac{\lvert\eta_{k+1}+\tau_{k}^{*}y_{k}\lvert^{2}}{\lvert z-\mu_{k+1}\lvert^{2}}

Now define
X_{k+1}:=\sqrt{\frac{N}{Q_{k}(z)}}(\eta_{k+1}+\tau_{k}^{*}y_{k}).
Then

Q_{k+1}(z)=Q_{k}(z)\left(1+\frac{\lvert X_{k+1}\lvert^{2}}{N\lvert z-\mu_{k+1}\lvert^{2}}\right).

We now identify the law of the
X_{k}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1},T_{k},\beta_{k}).
Then
y_{k}
and
Q_{k}(z)
are
\mathcal{F}_{k}
-measurable. Moreover,
\eta_{k+1}
and
\tau_{k}
are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and

\eta_{k+1}+\tau_{k}^{*}y_{k}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(0,\frac{1+\lvert\lvert y_{k}\lvert\lvert^{2}}{1}\right)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}\left(0,\frac{Q_{k}(z)}{N}\right).

Hence
X_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}_{C}(0,1).
The conditional law does not depend on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, therefore
X_{k+1}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
. In particular,
X_{k+1}
is independent of the spectrum and of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
. By induction
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N-1}
are i.i.d. standard complex Gaussians independent of
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{N-1}
. Iterating the recurrence from
Q_{0}(z)=1
, we obtain

Q_{N-1}(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\left(1+\frac{\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}}{N\lvert z-\mu_{k}\lvert^{2}}\right)

which concludes the proof.
∎

Note that an identical argument applies to
1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)c_{N}
, yielding the same product representation. We now prove the pointwise convergence of
Q_{N}(z)
towards
q(z)
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}
be such that
\lvert z\lvert>1
. Then

Q_{N}(z)\xrightarrow[N\rightarrow\infty]{a.s}q(z).

Proof.

Define
a_{k}(z):=\frac{1}{\lvert z-\mu_{k}\lvert^{2}}
and
S_{N}(z):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)
. We first prove that
\log Q_{N}(z)-S_{N}(z)\rightarrow 0
a.s, then we use the almost sure circular law to get the desired convergence. By Lemma 12 the following equality in law holds true

\log Q_{N}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\log\left(1+\frac{a_{k}(z)\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}}{N}\right).

Since
\lvert z\lvert>1
we may choose
\varepsilon>0
such that
\lvert z\lvert>1+\varepsilon
and by the almost sure convergence of the spectral radius of
D_{N}
to
1
, almost surely, for all
N
large enough,
\rho(D_{N})\leq 1+\varepsilon/2.
Therefore for all
k=1,\ldots,N-1
we have
a_{k}(z)\leq 4/\varepsilon^{2}
almost surely for all
N
large enough. Using the fact that
0\leq x-\log(1+x)\leq x^{2}
for all
x\geq 0
we get

\left\lvert\log Q_{N}(z)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}\right\lvert\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)^{2}\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{4}.

Since
a_{k}(z)
is eventually uniformly bounded and

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{4}<\infty,\ \textit{almost surely,}

we obtain that

\log Q_{N}(z)-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}\rightarrow 0,\ \textit{almost surely.}

Then,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}=S_{N}(z)+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)(\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}-1).

Since the weights
a_{k}(z)
are eventually uniformly bounded, and the variables
\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}-1
are independent, centered, and have finite fourth moment we can use Lemma 4 and deduce that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}a_{k}(z)(\lvert X_{k}\lvert^{2}-1)\rightarrow 0,\ \textit{almost surely}

and thus
\log Q_{N}(z)-S_{N}(z)\rightarrow 0
almost surely. Now by the almost sure circular law applied to the eigenvalues of
D_{N}
,

S_{N}(z)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{1}{\lvert z-\mu_{k}\lvert^{2}}\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{D}}\frac{1}{\lvert z-u\lvert^{2}}\frac{d^{2}u}{\pi}=\log q(z)

which concludes the proof.
∎

We now upgrade this pointwise convergence to a uniform one on a compact outside the unit disk.

Proposition 13
.

Let
K
be a compact such
K\subset\{\lvert z\lvert>1\}
. Then, a.s,

\sup_{z\in K}\lvert Q_{N}(z)-q(z)\lvert\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset K
dense countable. Then, a.s,

\forall z\in\mathcal{D},\ Q_{N}(z)\rightarrow q(z).

Now we work on the almost sure event

\Omega_{0}:=\{\forall z\in\mathcal{D},\ Q_{N}(z)\rightarrow q(z)\}\cap\{\lvert\lvert b\lvert\lvert\rightarrow 1\}\cap\{\textit{for all $N$ large enough}\ \sup_{z\in K}\lvert\lvert\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\lvert\lvert\leq C_{K}\}.

Using the resolvent identity
\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)-\mathbf{R}_{N}(w)=(w-z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(w)
we get that for all
N
large enough

\lvert\lvert\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)-\mathbf{R}_{N}(w)\lvert\lvert\leq C_{K}^{2}\lvert z-w\lvert.

We denote
A(z):=\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}
. Then noting that

A(z)-A(w)=(\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)-\mathbf{R}_{N}(w))\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}+\mathbf{R}_{N}(w)(\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}-\mathbf{R}_{N}(w)^{*})

we obtain that

\lvert\lvert A(z)-A(w)\lvert\lvert\leq 2C_{K}^{3}\lvert z-w\lvert.

Now for all
N
large enough we have
\lvert\lvert b\lvert\lvert\leq 2
. Hence

\displaystyle\lvert Q_{N}(z)-Q_{N}(w)\lvert

\displaystyle=\lvert b^{*}(A(z)-A(w))b\lvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert\lvert b\lvert\lvert^{2}\lvert\lvert A(z)-A(w)\lvert\lvert

\displaystyle\leq 8C_{K}^{3}\lvert z-w\lvert.

Therefore the
(Q_{N})
are equi-Lipschitz on
K
, a.s, for all
N
large enough. The limit function
q
is continuous, so uniformly continuous on
K
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
\eta>0
such that
8C_{K}^{3}\eta\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
and
\lvert q(z)-q(w)\lvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
whenever
\lvert z-w\lvert\leq\eta
.
Since
\mathcal{D}
is dense and
K
is a compact, there exists a finite
\eta
-net meaning
z_{1},\ldots,z_{M}\in\mathcal{D}
such that for all
z\in K
there exists a
j
such that
\lvert z-z_{j}\lvert\leq\eta.
For all
N
large enough we have

\max_{1\leq j\leq M}\lvert Q_{N}(z_{j})-q(z_{j})\lvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Let
z\in K
and
z_{j}
such that
\lvert z-z_{j}\lvert\leq\eta
, we then have

\displaystyle\lvert Q_{N}(z)-q(z)\lvert

\displaystyle\leq\lvert Q_{N}(z)-Q_{N}(z_{j})\lvert+\lvert Q_{N}(z_{j})-q(z_{j})\lvert+\lvert q(z_{j})-q(z)\lvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Therefore, a.s, for all
N
large enough

\sup_{z\in K}\lvert Q_{N}(z)-q(z)\lvert\leq\varepsilon

which concludes the proof.
∎

By the same argument one also obtains
\sup_{K}\lvert 1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(z)c_{N}-q(z)\lvert\rightarrow 0
almost surely.

We now prove the convergence towards

of the bilinear term

Proposition 14
.

Let
K
be a compact such

. Then, a.s,

\sup_{z\in K}\lvert b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}^{2}_{N}(z)c_{N}\lvert\rightarrow 0.

Proof.

Let
z\in K

Y_{N}(z)\rightarrow 0
a.s. Define
\mathcal{F}:=\sigma((D_{N},b_{N})_{N})

Theorem 15

Let

and

is a complex Ginibre matrix with entries of variance

Let
K:=D(t,\varepsilon)

. Now almost surely, for all

\lambda_{1}\in K\ \textit{and}\ \mathrm{Sp}(D_{N})\cap K=\emptyset.

By Proposition

\mathscr{O}_{11}=\frac{(1+c_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})c_{N})(1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{*}b_{N})}{\lvert 1+b_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}_{N}(\lambda_{1})^{2}c_{N}\lvert^{2}}.

Now by the previous uniform convergence results, almost surely, for all

Since

∎

4.
Conclusion: some comments on methods and phenomena

We have studied the distribution of diagonal overlaps for outliers with two slightly different techniques. In the first case, we have used the decomposition of the law coming from the Schur form, and in the second case we have used Schur complements to obtain a characterization of the outlier, and uniform bounds that hold with high probability outside the bulk. Each method is taylored to the parameters of the problem: indeed, the first technique cannot easily be applied to the second case, because the distribution of the Schur form of a perturbed matrix is not so simple to characterize; and the second technique cannot be used for the first problem, because conditioning on an event of measure zero tilts the distributions and jeopardizes the uniform bounds (which only hold on an event of high probability – but then, presumably not under the conditioning). This is also the reason why there is no contradiction between the two results, which describe two related but distinct regimes.

All recent works on overlaps and their distribution in integrable ensembles suggest an interpretation of the dynamical version of the ‘freezing at the edge’ phenomenon
[
7
]
, and an answer to this natural question: why are eigenvalues more stable at the edge than inside the bulk? The key insight seems to be that the diagonal overlaps (which always encapsulates the stability of the associated eigenvalues, as shown by Proposition
1
as well as other related results in the literature
[
6
,
13
,
9
]
) depend multiplicatively on the distance between eigenvalues, with the most relevant contributions coming from the closest neighbors. This is exactly true for the complex Ginibre ensemble, and can be expected to still be the case at leading order for a variety of reasonable distributions. As a consequence, eigenvalue instability is roughly a multiplicative quantity of neighboring eigenvalues, whose overall scale depends chiefly on the behavior of nearest neighbors. The difference between the bulk and the edge, at microscopic level, being essentially the fact that the nearest neighbors tend to occupy a half-space rather than the whole neighborhood, it is to be expected as a rule of thumb that the diagonal overlaps at the edge scale like the square root of those in the bulk. In the context of the complex Ginibre ensemble, this heuristic is exactly true, with diagonal overlaps having order
N
in the bulk,
\sqrt{N}
at the edge, and order
1
for outliers as established in this paper. These scales reflect the fact that an edge eigenvalue has roughly half as much close neighbors compared to a bulk eigenvalue, and that an outlier eigenvalue typically has no close neighbor at all.

Appendix: asymptotics of the incomplete Gamma function

5.1.
Estimates on the partial sums of the exponential

We recall the definition of the incomplete Gamma function:

\Gamma(N,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}s^{N-1}e^{-s}ds.

This function appears naturally when dealing with statistics of the complex Ginibre ensemble; in Section
2
, we rely on a precise asymptotic expansion of the incomplete Gamma function, specifically in the range
x=Nt
, with
t>1
, corresponding to the squared radius of an outlier.

Lemma 16

(Asymptotic expansion of
\Gamma(N,Nt)
)
.

For any fixed
t>1
, the following estimate holds

\Gamma(N,Nt)=(Nt)^{N-1}e^{-Nt}\left(\frac{t}{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{t^{2}}{(t-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})\right).

Proof.

A change of variable
Nu=s
gives

\Gamma(N,Nt)=N^{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}u^{N-1}e^{-Nu}du=N^{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}u^{-1}e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}du.

Let us denote
\varphi(u)=\ln(u)-u
,
\varphi^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1-u}{u}
and perform an integration by parts:

N^{-N}\Gamma(N,Nt)=\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1-u}\frac{1}{N}\partial\left(e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}\right)du=\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{t-1}e^{N\varphi(t)}-\frac{1}{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-u)^{2}}e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}du,

which isolates in fact the first term of the asymptotic expansion; one more integration by part yields:

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1-u)^{2}}e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{u}{(1-u)^{3}}\partial\left(e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}\right)du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}e^{N(\ln(t)-t)}-\frac{1}{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}\partial\left(\frac{u}{(1-u)^{3}}\right)e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}du,

which gives us the second term. To bound the last term, we notice first that

\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{u}{(1-u)^{3}}\right)=\frac{1+2u}{(1-u)^{4}}=\frac{2}{(u-1)^{3}}+\frac{3}{(u-1)^{4}},

which is bounded by

C(t)=\frac{2}{(t-1)^{3}}+\frac{3}{(t-1)^{4}}

as
u\geq t>1
. We then study the remaining integral term.

\displaystyle\int_{t}^{\infty}u^{N}e^{-Nu}du

\displaystyle=e^{-Nt}\int_{0}^{\infty}(v+t)^{N}e^{-Nv}dv

\displaystyle=N^{-1}e^{-Nt}\int_{0}^{\infty}(t+\frac{w}{N})^{N}e^{-w}dw

\displaystyle=N^{-1}e^{N(ln(t)-t)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+\frac{w}{Nt})^{N}e^{-w}dw

\displaystyle\leq N^{-1}e^{N(ln(t)-t)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{w(\frac{1}{t}-1)}dw

Since
t>1
in our regime, the last term is the integral of a negative exponential : it is a positive constant
K(t)
. We have

\left|-\frac{1}{N}\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{u}{(1-u)^{3}}\right)e^{N(\ln(u)-u)}du\right|\leq C(t)K(t)\frac{1}{N^{2}}e^{N(ln(t)-t)}

Hence,

\displaystyle\Gamma(N,Nt)

\displaystyle=N^{N}\left(\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{t-1}e^{N(ln(t)-t)}-\frac{1}{N}(\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}e^{N(\ln(t)-t)}-e^{N(ln(t)-t)}O(\frac{1}{N^{2}}))\right)

\displaystyle=N^{N-1}t^{N}e^{-Nt}\left(\frac{1}{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})\right)

which is the claim.
∎

Lemma 17

(Asymptotic behavior of partial sums of the exponential)
.

In the regime
t>1
:

\frac{N!}{(Nt)^{N}}e^{(N-1)}(Nt)=\frac{1}{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})

Proof.

We first write classically

e^{(N-1)}(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{(N-1)!}\int_{x}^{\infty}s^{N-1}e^{-s}ds=\frac{e^{x}}{(N-1)!}\Gamma(N,x).

So in the regime
x=Nt
with
t>1
, we find

e^{(N-1)}(Nt)=\frac{e^{Nt}}{(N-1)!}\Gamma(N,Nt)=\frac{N^{N-1}t^{N}}{(N-1)!}\left(\frac{1}{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2})\right)

and finally

\frac{N!}{(Nt)^{N}}e^{(N-1)}(Nt)=\frac{1}{t-1}-\frac{1}{N}\frac{t}{(t-1)^{3}}+O(N^{-2}).

∎
