Dynamical tidal Love numbers of black holes under generic perturbations: Connecting black hole perturbation theory with effective field theory
Sumanta Chakraborty, M.V.S Saketh, Tanja Hinderer, Jan Steinhoff

Abstract
The foundation for modeling the coupling of the internal structure of compact objects in binary systems to their dynamics and emitted gravitational waves is a systematic effective field theory (EFT) framework, where each compact object is replaced by a worldline endowed with a set of internal degrees of freedom. These degrees of freedom encode finite-size effects and thereby distinguish between different classes of compact objects. Among finite-size effects, tidal interactions play a central role, as they are associated to various kinds of deformations of a body under the influence of external tidal fields. In this work, we analyze the dynamical tidal response of Kerr black holes to generic-spin perturbations, focusing primarily on the scalar and gravitational cases, and working to linear order in frequency. We establish an EFT description of the perturbed black hole that accounts for the couplings between the spin, gravitoelectric and -magnetic tidal fields. We match this to wave-like solutions to the full black hole perturbation equations in order to determine the tidal response coefficients. In particular, we obtain the dynamical Love number, which appears at linear order in frequency for spinning black holes, and derive an approximate expression for the dynamical tidal response, including both dissipative and conservative pieces. We also examine several technical subtleties that arise in the matching procedure, with special emphasis on the mixing of multipolar modes induced by the spin of the compact object, which proves to be essential for a consistent treatment.

II
The tidal response in the effective worldline theory

A coarse-grained effective description of a tidally perturbed compact object such as a BH can be obtained in an (worldline) effective field theory (EFT), where the object is modelled as a point-entity described by a worldline augmented with certain additional multipolar degrees of freedom to account for finite size effects. For linear tidal effects, the multipolar degrees of freedom are chosen to be induced by external tidal fields via a linear functional, which in turn is characterized by tidal response functions. These functions are a priori undetermined, but can be fixed by matching the solutions to the perturbation equations in EFT to the solutions of the perturbation theory in the far-zone (see
1
).

In this section, we briefly outline such an EFT description, and the solutions to the perturbation equations in the EFT for scalar and gravitational fields which are later used to fix the tidal response in the low-frequency, large-wavelength regime via matching. Some crucial but navigable subtleties arise since the equations for BH perturbation theory (BHPT) are most simply solved when the angular basis is spanned by spheroidal harmonics, while the tidal degrees of freedom in the EFT given by the multipole moments, and the fields they couple to are more naturally described using STF tensors which are closely related to spherical harmonics as both are representations of the rotation group in 3-dimensions (SO(3)).

We will work with perturbations of different spin weights corresponding to scalar, vector, and tensor fields, coupled to the worldline. Although the detailed structure of the action varies for different fields, the resulting actions can all be written schematically as the sum of three components as

\mathcal{A}_{\rm EFT}=\mathcal{A}_{\rm p.p}+\mathcal{A}_{\rm field}+\mathcal{A}_{\rm tidal}.

(1)

Here,
\mathcal{A}_{\rm p.p}
contains the terms describing the dynamics of a “point particle”. More precisely, it includes only the couplings between the spin and four-velocity of the representative worldline
z^{\mu}(\tau)
and the external field, along with any terms describing the internal dynamics of the compact object without involving fields
steinhoff2016dynamical-df7
;
Vines:2018gqi
;
Vines:2016unv
. The term
\mathcal{A}_{\rm field}
contains the action of the external field itself, and is therefore not localized on the worldline. Finally,
\mathcal{A}_{\rm tidal}
encodes the interaction between the induced tidal moments of the compact object and the external field.

(23)

Thus, we can rewrite

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}&=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell^{\prime}}^{\ell-\ell^{\prime}}\mathcal{N}_{\ell^{\prime}}\chi^{|\ell-\ell^{\prime}|}\epsilon^{\ell+\ell^{\prime}+1}F^{(\ell^{\prime}-\ell)}_{\ell m}C^{\ell^{\prime}m}_{\rm reg}\,.\end{split}

Now, the right hand side only has applied tidal field coefficients, which we can freely tune in principle. Let us now manually choose to set

\displaystyle C_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}=\mathcal{M}_{\ell}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell_{0}}^{\ell-\ell_{0}}\mathcal{N}_{\ell_{0}}\chi^{|\ell-\ell_{0}|}\epsilon^{\ell+\ell_{0}+1}F^{(\ell_{0}-\ell)}_{\ell m}C^{\ell_{0}m}_{\rm reg}\,.

\displaystyle=\chi^{|\ell-\ell_{0}|}\epsilon^{\ell+\ell_{0}+1}f_{\ell_{0}m}^{(\ell-\ell_{0})}C^{\ell_{0}m}_{\rm reg}\,.

In this work, we will be primarily interested in computing the diagonal tidal response, i.e.,
f_{\ell_{0}m}=f^{(0)}_{\ell_{0}m}

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\ell m}&=C^{\ell m}+\sum_{k=1}^{[\ell/2]}(\chi\epsilon)^{2k}r^{\ell m}_{-2k}C^{\ell-2k,m}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\chi\epsilon)^{2k}r^{\ell m}_{2k}C^{\ell+2k,m}\,.\end{split}

(31)

The relationship between

II.1.4
Iterative fixing of EFT response coefficients

For say

\displaystyle f_{2m}^{(-2)}=\lambda_{0m}r^{0m}_{2}\,.

Now, keeping

Using the result that

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{2m}=\lambda_{2m}-\chi^{4}r_{-2}^{2m}r_{2}^{0m}\lambda_{0m}\,.\end{split}

Thus, we could fix the EFT response coefficient
f_{2m}
. We can similarly fix

\displaystyle f_{3m}=\lambda_{3m}-\chi^{4}r_{-2}^{3m}\lambda_{1m}r_{2}^{1m}\,.

Now, consider

\chi^{4}f^{(-4)}_{4m}&=\lambda_{0m}\chi^{4}r_{4}^{0m},\text{ at}~\ell=0\,.\end{split}

(39)

We can solve them to get

\displaystyle f_{4m}^{(-4)}=\lambda_{0m}r_{4}^{0m}\,,

(40)

\displaystyle f_{4m}^{(-2)}=\lambda_{2m}r_{2}^{2m}-\chi^{4}\lambda_{0m}r_{4}^{0m}r_{-2}^{2m}\,.

(41)

Now, for
\ell_{0}=\ell=4

\displaystyle\begin{split}f_{4m}+\chi^{4}r^{4m}_{-2}f^{(-2)}_{4m}+\chi^{8}r^{4m}_{-4}f^{(-4)}_{4m}=\lambda_{4m}\,,\end{split}

(42)

which using the previous results connecting

&-\chi^{8}\lambda_{0m}r_{-4}^{4m}r_{4}^{0m}\,.\end{split}

(43)

We can similarly proceed to obtain relation between EFT response

II.1.5
Asymptotic form of the EFT scalar field

Before turning to Gravitational case, it is useful to use the relations between tensor and spherical harmonic basis to rewrite the asymptotic field, obtained by taking the limit
\omega r\gg 1
of

as,

\begin{split}\phi&=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{\ell}}\left[C_{\rm reg}^{I_{\ell}}\partial_{I_{\ell}}\sin(\omega r)+C_{\rm irreg}^{I_{\ell}}\partial_{I_{\ell}}\cos(\omega r)\right]\\
&=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{\ell}\Big[N_{I_{\ell}}\Big(C_{\rm reg}^{I_{\ell}}\sin\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\\
&+C_{\rm irreg}^{I_{\ell}}\cos\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\Big)\Big]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{r^{2}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(45)

Now, we use

C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg/irreg}N_{I_{\ell}}=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}C^{\ell m}_{\rm reg/irreg}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\,,

(46)

to rewrite the asymptotic form of the scalar field as

\begin{split}\phi&=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)(-1)^{\ell}\Big\{C_{\rm reg}^{\ell m}\sin\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\\
&+C_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}\cos\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\Big\}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}r^{2}}\right)\,.\\
&=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}R_{\ell m}^{\rm EFT}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}r^{2}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(47)

This is the asymptotic results for the scalar field obtained from EFT. Finally, using
30
, we can rewrite this in spheroidal basis as

\begin{split}\phi&\approx\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell,m}S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\Big\{R^{\rm EFT}_{\ell m}+\sum_{k=1}^{[l/2]}(\chi\epsilon)^{2k}\tilde{r}^{\ell-2k,m}_{2k}R^{\rm EFT}_{\ell-2k,m}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(\chi\epsilon)^{2k}\tilde{r}^{\ell+2k,m}_{-2k}R^{\rm EFT}_{\ell+2k,m}\Big\}\,.\\
&=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)(-1)^{\ell}\Big\{D_{\rm reg}^{\ell m}\sin\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\\
&+C_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}\cos\left(\omega r-\ell\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\Big\}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\omega^{2}r^{2}}\right)\,.\end{split}

(48)

This yields

\begin{split}D^{\ell m}&=C^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}+\sum_{k=1}^{[l/2]}(i\chi\epsilon)^{2k}\tilde{r}^{\ell-2k,m}_{2k}C^{\ell-2k,m}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(i\chi\epsilon)^{2k}\tilde{r}^{\ell+2k,m}_{-2k}C^{\ell+2k,m}\,.\end{split}

(49)

Comparing with
31
, we obtain the relations
\tilde{r}^{\ell-2k,m}_{2k}=(-1)^{k}r^{\ell m}_{-2k}
,
\tilde{r}^{\ell+2k,m}_{-2k}=(-1)^{k}r^{\ell m}_{2k}
. We can then explicitly write the relationship between the tidal response coefficients in spheroidal and spherical basis. For instance, we can rewrite
37

\begin{split}f_{2m}&=\lambda_{2m}-\chi^{4}r_{-2}^{2m}r_{2}^{0m}\lambda_{0m}\,,\\
&=\lambda_{2m}-\chi^{4}\tilde{r}_{2}^{0m}\tilde{r}_{-2}^{2m}\lambda_{0m}\,,\\
&=\lambda_{2m}-\chi^{4}\frac{(1-m^{2})(4-m^{2})}{1620}\lambda_{0m}\,,\end{split}

(50)

Similarly, we can fix

as

\begin{split}f_{3m}&=\lambda_{3m}-\chi^{4}r_{-2}^{3m}r_{2}^{1m}\lambda_{1m}\,,\\
&=\lambda_{3m}-\chi^{4}\tilde{r}_{2}^{1m}\tilde{r}_{-2}^{3m}\lambda_{1m}\,,\\
&=\lambda_{3m}-\chi^{4}\frac{\left(4-m^{2}\right)\left(9-m^{2}\right)}{52500}\lambda_{1m}\,,\end{split}

(51)

and similarly proceed further for higher values of
\ell
.

The above shows how to obtain the EFT tidal response coefficients given the ratio of
D^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}/D^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}

.

II.2
Gravitational case

In the previous subsection, we showed how scalar tidal responses can be matched between EFT and perturbation theory. We now review how that procedure extends to generic (
s\neq 0
) perturbations. The extension is relatively straightforward, up to two main differences. Firstly, there are now couplings between neighbouring modes as well since there are two parities (electric and magnetic) for a single
\ell m
mode. Additionally, the tidal responses of electric and magnetic parts of the perturbation need to be related in some way. In particular, we will need to set them equal to each other. This is because there is only a single second order perturbation equation governing a complex curvature scalar in the perturbation theory, with no explicit parity dependence. Below, we discuss the procedure in detail for
s=-2
, i.e., gravitational perturbations. The same steps apply for
s=-1
but we do not give them explicitly.

II.2.1
EFT tidal fields and induced multipole moments

The tidal/finite size action in
52
can be generalized for the case of gravitational perturbation as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\rm tidal}&=-\int d\tau\sqrt{-u_{\mu}u^{\mu}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}Q_{E}^{I_{\ell}}E_{I_{\ell}}\\
&-\int d\tau\sqrt{-u_{\mu}u^{\mu}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell!}Q_{B}^{I_{\ell}}B_{I_{\ell}}\,.\end{split}

(52)

where the gravito-electric and -magnetic tidal fields are related to the Riemann curvature tensor by

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{\mu_{L}}&=\nabla_{\langle\mu_{L-2}}R_{\mu_{\ell-1}|\alpha|\mu_{\ell}\rangle\beta}u^{\alpha}u^{\beta}\,,\\
B_{\mu_{L}}&=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\langle\mu_{L-2}}\epsilon_{|\gamma|\mu_{\ell-1}}{}^{\alpha\beta}R_{|\alpha\beta|\mu_{\ell}\rangle\delta}u^{\gamma}u^{\delta},~\ell\geq 2\,,\end{split}

(53)

are the electric and magnetic tidal fields. As before, the tidal fields induce the multipole moments, and the induction relation for the scalar case, see
7
, is modified for the gravitational case as,

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{E}^{I_{\ell}}(\omega)&=M^{2\ell+1}F^{I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}E^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\omega)\\
&+\chi\Big[M^{2\ell}F^{(-1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}B^{\langle I_{\ell-1}}(\omega)\hat{s}^{i^{\prime}_{\ell}\rangle}\\
&+M^{2\ell+2}F^{(1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}B^{I^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}(\omega)\hat{s}_{i^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}\Big]\\
&+\chi^{2}\left[\cdots\right]+\cdots\,,\\
Q_{B}^{I_{\ell}}(\omega)&=M^{2\ell+1}F^{I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}B^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\omega)\\
&-\chi\Big[M^{2\ell}F^{(-1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}E^{\langle I_{\ell-1}}(\omega)\hat{s}^{i^{\prime}_{\ell}\rangle}\\
&+M^{2\ell+2}F^{(1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}E^{I^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}(\omega)\hat{s}_{i^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}\Big]\\
&+\chi^{2}\left[\cdots\right]+\cdots\,.\end{split}

(54)

We note that coupling between multipolar orders starts at linear order in spin, between neighbouring modes. This is allowed since the gravito-electric (-magnetic) fields transform as
(-1)^{\ell}
(
(-1)^{\ell+1}
) under parity. It is also worth noting that the tidal response tensors are not labelled as
E
or
B
. This is because the linear tidal response is expected to be identical (with proper normalizations and signs) for both parities for Kerr BHs
Goldberger:2005cd
;
Saketh:2022xjb
;
saketh2023dynamical-b30
;
Bhatt:2024rpx
. This can be understood as a symmetry under
E^{I_{\ell}}\rightarrow iB^{I_{\ell}}
,
B^{I_{\ell}}\rightarrow-iE^{I_{\ell}}
.

II.2.2
Relating response functions to asymptotic wave amplitudes

Now, the metric perturbations asymptotically far away from the object are given (for monochromatic perturbations) in terms of
h^{\mu\nu}\equiv\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}-\eta^{\mu\nu}
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}h_{ij}&=\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{\ell-2}}\Bigg(C^{K_{\ell}}_{\mathcal{E},\rm reg}\Pi^{k_{\ell-1}k_{\ell}}_{ij}\partial_{K_{\ell-2}}\\
&+\omega^{-1}C_{\mathcal{B}}^{K_{\ell}}\Pi^{k_{\ell}m}_{ij}\epsilon_{k_{\ell-1}mn}\partial_{K_{\ell-2}n}\Bigg)\psi_{\rm reg}+(\rm reg\leftrightarrow\rm irreg)\,,\end{split}

(55)

where, the regular part is
\psi_{\rm reg}=e^{-i\omega t}\sin(\omega r)/\omega r
, and the irregular part is
\psi_{\rm irreg}=e^{-i\omega t}\cos(\omega r)/\omega r
. The projector is given by
\Pi^{ij}_{kl}=(1/2)(P^{i}{}_{k}P^{j}{}_{l}+P^{j}{}_{k}P^{i}{}_{l}-P^{ij}P_{kl})

P^{ij}=\delta^{ij}+\partial^{i}\partial^{j}/\omega^{2}

The tidal fields at the position of the BH, which we choose to be the origin (
x_{i}=0
), are obtained by evaluating
53
at the origin. This yields,

\displaystyle E^{I_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell+1}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\omega^{\ell}C_{\mathcal{E},\rm reg}^{I_{\ell}}e^{-i\omega t}\,,

(56)

\displaystyle B^{I_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=i(-1)^{\ell}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\omega^{\ell}C_{\mathcal{B},\rm reg}^{I_{\ell}}e^{-i\omega t}\,,

(57)

where
\mathcal{N}_{\ell}=2^{-\ell-3}(1+\ell)(2+\ell)\sqrt{\pi}/\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell)
, as shown in
B
.

The tidal fields induce multipole moments via
54
, which in turn connects to the irregular coefficients
C^{L}_{\rm\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{B},\rm irreg}
via the equations of motion similar to the scalar case. Working with linearized gravity, the equations of motion are given by
Saketh:2022xjb

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu}h_{ij}=16\pi G|g|\Pi^{kl}_{ij}T_{kl}.

(58)

The stress energy tensor can be obtained from the action by varying the metric perturbations as shown in Ref.
Saketh:2022xjb
. This yields relations between the multipole moments and the coefficients parametrizing the irregular part of the metric perturbation as

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega t}C_{\mathcal{E},\rm irreg}^{L}

\displaystyle=4(\ell!)^{-1}(-1)^{\ell}\omega^{\ell+1}Q^{L}_{E}\,,

(59)

\displaystyle e^{-i\omega t}C_{\mathcal{B},\rm irreg}^{L}

\displaystyle=i4(\ell!)^{-1}(-1)^{\ell}\omega^{\ell+1}Q^{L}_{B}\,.

(60)

Thus, the relation

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{\mathcal{E},\rm irreg}^{I_{\ell}}&=\frac{-4}{l!}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\epsilon^{2\ell+1}F^{I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}_{\rm\mathcal{E},reg}\\
&-\frac{4}{l!}i\chi\Big[\mathcal{N}_{\ell-1}\epsilon^{2\ell}F^{(-1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{\langle I^{\prime}_{\ell-1}}_{\mathcal{B},\rm reg}\hat{s}^{i^{\prime}_{\ell}\rangle}\\
&+\mathcal{N}_{\ell+1}\epsilon^{2\ell+2}F^{(1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{I^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}_{\mathcal{B},\rm reg}\hat{s}_{i^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2})\,,\end{split}

(61)

and an identical relation for
\mathcal{E}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{B}
.
Now, it would be convenient to combine the above relations into a single one, suitable for comparison against the relevant quantity governed by the Teukolsky equation. The Teukolsky equation governs the Teukolsky scalar
\psi_{4}
, which admits a similar decomposition into regular and irregular parts. The corresponding coefficients

that enters

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{\rm irreg}^{I_{\ell}}&=\frac{-4}{l!}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\epsilon^{2\ell+1}F^{I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\\
&-\frac{4}{l!}i\chi\Big[\mathcal{N}_{\ell-1}\epsilon^{2\ell}F^{(-1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{\langle I^{\prime}_{\ell-1}}_{\rm reg}\hat{s}^{i^{\prime}_{\ell}\rangle}\\
&+\mathcal{N}_{\ell+1}\epsilon^{2\ell+2}F^{(1)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}C^{I^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}_{\rm reg}\hat{s}_{i^{\prime}_{\ell+1}}\Big]+\mathcal{O}(\chi^{2})\,,\end{split}

(62)

Now, we can just repeat the steps as in the scalar case, with the only difference being that we should switch to the

II.2.3
Structure of the response functions

To proceed further, we define

C^{\ell m}=\int d\Omega C^{I_{\ell}}\hat{N}_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}\bar{m}_{i_{\ell-1}}\bar{m}_{i_{\ell}\rangle}{}_{-2}\bar{Y}_{\ell m}\,,

(63)

where

, where
\hat{\theta}.\hat{\theta}=\hat{\phi}.\hat{\phi}=m.\bar{m}=1
. Meanwhile,

. Now, we can define, in a similar manner as before, a tensor
{}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I_{\ell}}_{\ell m}

{}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)={}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I_{\ell}}_{\ell m}\hat{N}_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}\bar{m}_{i_{\ell-1}}\bar{m}_{i_{\ell}\rangle}\,.

(64)

Note that we have the relation (derived in

)

\begin{split}&\int d\Omega\hat{N}_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}m_{i_{\ell-1}}m_{i_{\ell}\rangle}\hat{N}^{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}-2}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell^{\prime}-1}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell}^{\prime}\rangle}\\
&=\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{J_{\ell}}_{I_{\ell}}\frac{8\pi(\ell-2)!}{\ell(\ell-1)(2\ell+1)(2\ell-3)!!}\,.\end{split}

(65)

Substituting

, and using

C^{\ell m}=C^{I_{\ell}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{I_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\frac{8\pi(\ell-2)!}{\ell(\ell-1)(2\ell+1)(2\ell-3)!!}\,,

(66)

and the inverse as

C^{I_{\ell}}=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}C^{\ell m}\,_{-2}\mathcal{Y}_{\ell m}^{I_{\ell}}\,.

(67)

Going forward, for convenience, we define

\zeta_{\ell}=\frac{8\pi(\ell-2)!}{\ell(\ell-1)(2\ell+1)(2\ell-3)!!}.

(68)

Now, similar to the scalar case, we can define the following quantities:

\begin{split}&\zeta_{\ell}~F^{(n)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}E^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{I_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\\
&=\zeta_{\ell}~F^{(n)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\ell}^{\ell}E^{\ell m^{\prime}}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}_{\ell m^{\prime}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{I_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\\
&=\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\ell}^{\ell}F^{(n)\ell m}{}_{\ell m^{\prime}}E^{\ell m^{\prime}}\,,\end{split}

(69)

where

\displaystyle F^{(n)\ell m}{}_{\ell m^{\prime}}=\zeta_{\ell}~F^{(n)I_{\ell}}{}_{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I^{\prime}_{\ell}}_{\ell m^{\prime}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}_{I_{\ell}}^{\ell m}\,.

(70)

Just as in the scalar case, axisymmetry ensures that different

\displaystyle F^{(n)\ell m}{}_{\ell m^{\prime}}=F^{(n)}_{\ell m}\delta^{m}_{m^{\prime}}\,.

(71)

As before, we need to treat STF tensors involving direct products or contractions with the spin-vector which also feature in
7

\ell,m

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\zeta_{\ell}C^{\langle I_{\ell-k}}\hat{s}^{i_{\ell-k+1}}\dots\hat{s}^{i_{\ell}\rangle}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\\
&=\zeta_{\ell}C^{I_{\ell-k}}\hat{s}^{i_{\ell-k+1}}\dots\hat{s}^{i_{\ell}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\\
&=\zeta_{\ell}\sum_{m^{\prime}}C^{\ell-k,m^{\prime}}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I_{\ell-k}}_{\ell-k,m^{\prime}}\hat{s}^{i_{\ell-k+1}}\dots\hat{s}^{i_{\ell}}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\,.\end{split}

(72)

Since we have oriented the spin-axis to be the
\hat{z}
direction, the spin-vector has
\ell=1,~m=0
, and we can thus write

\displaystyle{}_{-2}Z_{\ell}^{k}\delta^{m}_{m^{\prime}}=\zeta_{\ell}\,{}_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I_{\ell-k}}_{\ell-k,m^{\prime}}\hat{s}^{i_{\ell-k+1}}\dots\hat{s}^{i_{\ell}}\,_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\,.

Using this, we can write

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\zeta_{\ell}C^{\langle I_{\ell-k}}\hat{s}^{i_{\ell-k+1}}\dots\hat{s}^{i_{\ell}\rangle}{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}=C^{\ell-k,m}{}_{-2}Z_{\ell}^{k}\,.\end{split}

(74)

Now, consider the contraction

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\zeta_{\ell}C^{I_{\ell+k}}\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+k}}\dots\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+1}}~{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\\
&=\zeta_{\ell}\sum_{m^{\prime}=-\ell-k}^{\ell+k}C^{\ell+k,m}\,_{-2}\mathcal{Y}^{I_{\ell+k}}_{\ell+k,m^{\prime}}\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+k}}\dots\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+1}}~{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}\,.\end{split}

Once again, the accompanying product must vanish when

\displaystyle\begin{split}\zeta_{\ell}C^{I_{\ell+k}}\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+k}}\dots\hat{s}_{i_{\ell+1}}\,{}_{-2}\bar{\mathcal{Y}}^{\ell m}_{I_{\ell}}=C^{\ell+k,m}~{}_{-2}\bar{Z}^{k}_{\ell+k}\,.\end{split}

Now, we can finally rewrite

\displaystyle\begin{split}C^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}&=\frac{-4}{\ell!}\mathcal{N}_{\ell}\epsilon^{2\ell+1}F_{\ell m}C^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}\\
&-\frac{4}{\ell!}(i\chi)\Big[\mathcal{N}_{\ell-1}\epsilon^{2\ell}F^{(-1)}_{\ell m}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell-1}^{1}C^{\ell-1,m}_{\rm reg}\\
&+\mathcal{N}_{\ell+1}\epsilon^{2\ell+2}F^{(1)}_{\ell m}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}_{\ell+1}^{-1}C^{\ell+1,m}_{\rm reg}\Big]\\
&+\chi^{2}\left[\cdots\right]+\cdots\,.\end{split}

(77)

Here we have defined

{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}^{0}_{\ell}=1,\;\;\;{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}^{k>0}_{\ell}={}_{-2}Z^{k}_{\ell+k},\;\;\;{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}^{k<0}_{\ell}={}_{-2}\bar{Z}^{|k|}_{\ell}\,,

(78)

where

where

As before, switching on just one mode of regular field at a time, at say

\begin{split}C^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}=\frac{-4}{\ell!}i^{\beta}\chi^{|\ell-\ell_{0}|}{}_{-2}\mathcal{Z}^{\ell-\ell_{0}}_{\ell_{0}}\mathcal{N}_{\ell_{0}}\epsilon^{\ell+\ell_{0}+1}F^{(\ell_{0}-\ell)}_{\ell m}C^{\ell_{0}m}_{\rm reg}\,,\end{split}

This can be considered a convenient choice for the normalization of the tidal response for the EFT. Thus, we have the relation between irregular and regular part using the new response function as,

\displaystyle C_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}

\displaystyle=(i\chi)^{|\ell-\ell_{0}|}\epsilon^{\ell+\ell_{0}+1}f_{\ell_{0}m}^{(\ell-\ell_{0})}C^{\ell_{0}m}_{\rm reg}\,.

(82)

This is similar to the scalar case, except that in the gravitational case

In this work, we will be primarily interested in computing the diagonal tidal response, i.e.,
f_{\ell_{0}m}=f^{(0)}_{\ell_{0}m}

\displaystyle C^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}=\epsilon^{2\ell+1}f_{\ell m}C^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}\,.

(83)

Once again, in perturbation theory, we obtain a relationship in spheroidal harmonic basis of spin weight ‘-2’ as

\displaystyle D^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}=\epsilon^{2\ell+1}\,_{-2}\lambda_{\ell m}D^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}\,,

(84)

where

\ell,m

\displaystyle\begin{split}D^{\ell m}&=C^{\ell m}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}(i\chi\epsilon)^{k}{}_{-2}r^{\ell m}_{-k}C^{\ell-k,m}\\

The relation in

\displaystyle\begin{split}D_{\rm reg}^{\ell m}=(i\chi\epsilon)^{|l-l_{0}|}\,_{-2}r^{\ell m}_{l_{0}-l}\,C_{\rm reg}^{\ell_{0}m}\,,\end{split}

(87)

where we have defined

(88)

This provides the connection between EFT tidal response

II.2.4
Iterative fixing of the tidal coefficients

Here we will solve the connection formula,

\ell=\ell_{0}=2
(since for gravitational perturbation the lowest mode is
\ell=2

\displaystyle f_{\ell m}=\,_{-2}\lambda_{\ell m}\,,~\ell=2\,.

(89)

We can solve it similar to the scalar case for

f_{3m}^{(-1)}=\,_{-2}\lambda_{2m}\,{}_{-2}r^{2m}_{1}\,.

(90)

Subsequently, setting

\begin{split}&f_{3m}-\chi^{2}f_{3m}^{(-1)}\,{}_{-2}r^{3m}_{-1}=\,_{-2}\lambda_{3m}\,.\\

The above explicitly depicts the connection between EFT and BH perturbation theory responses for the two lowest lying modes,

Once the coeffcients

II.2.5
Asymptotic form of gravitational field in EFT

We showed earlier how the asymptotic structure of the scalar field can be used to match between EFT coefficients

\begin{split}\psi_{4}&=\omega^{2}\frac{e^{-i\omega(t-r)}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}i^{\ell-2}(C^{K_{\ell-2}ij}_{\mathcal{E},\rm out}+C^{K_{\ell-2}ij}_{\mathcal{B},\rm out})N_{K_{\ell-2}}\bar{m}_{i}\bar{m}_{j}\\
&+\mathcal{O}(r^{-2})\,,\end{split}

(93)

where
C_{\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{B},\rm out}^{K_{\ell-2}ij}=(1/2)(C_{\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{B},\rm irr}^{K_{\ell-2}ij}-iC_{\mathcal{E}/\mathcal{B},\rm reg}^{K_{\ell-2}ij})

\begin{split}&\int d\Omega~C^{K_{\ell-2}ij}\bar{m}_{i}\bar{m}_{j}N_{K_{\ell-2}}~{}_{-2}\bar{Y}^{\ell m}=C^{\ell m}\,,\end{split}

(94)

Moreover, using the normalization of

\begin{split}&\int{}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi){}_{-2}\bar{Y}_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)d\Omega=1\,,\\
&\sum_{\ell,m}{}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi){}_{-2}\bar{Y}_{\ell m}(\theta^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sin^{2}\theta}\delta(\theta-\theta^{\prime})\delta(\phi-\phi^{\prime})\,,\end{split}

\begin{split}\psi_{4}&=\omega^{2}\frac{e^{-i\omega(t-r)}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}i^{\ell-2}C^{\ell m}_{\rm out}{}_{-2}Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\,,\\

where in the second line we have used the result from BH perturbation theory, and in each of the lines we have ignored terms

\begin{split}\psi_{4}=\omega^{2}&\frac{e^{-i\omega(t-r)}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}i^{\ell-2}[C^{\ell m}_{\rm out}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}(-i\chi\epsilon)^{k}\,_{-2}\tilde{r}^{\ell-k,m}_{k}C^{\ell-k,m}_{\rm out}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(i\chi\epsilon)^{k}\,_{-2}\tilde{r}^{\ell+k,m}_{-k}C^{\ell+k,m}_{\rm out}]{}_{-2}S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\,.\end{split}

(97)

Comparing the above expression with the last equality in

\begin{split}D^{\ell m}&=C^{\ell m}+\sum_{k=1}^{\ell-2}(-i\chi\epsilon)^{k}\,_{-2}\tilde{r}^{\ell-k,m}_{k}C^{\ell-k,m}\\
&+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(i\chi\epsilon)^{k}\,_{-2}\tilde{r}^{\ell+k,m}_{-k}C^{\ell+k,m}\,.\end{split}

Comparing with

,
\,{}_{-2}\tilde{r}^{\ell+k,m}_{-k}=\,_{-2}r^{\ell m}_{k}
, where

\begin{split}f_{3m}&=\,_{-2}\lambda_{3m}+\chi^{2}\,_{-2}\lambda_{2m}\,{}_{-2}r^{2m}_{1}\,{}_{-2}r^{3m}_{-1}\\

&=\,_{-2}\lambda_{3m}-\chi^{2}\,_{-2}\lambda_{2m}\frac{4(9-m^{2})}{567}\,.\end{split}

III
Scalar field on a Kerr background: Amplitude of scattering states

In this section, we focus on external scalar perturbations, playing the role of an external tidal field, on the Kerr background. We know that such a perturbation, in the static limit, does not lead to conservative deformations of the Kerr multipole moments, which translates to vanishing tidal Love numbers
Damour:2009va
;
Kol:2011vg
;
Ivanov:2022qqt
;
LeTiec:2020bos
;
Chia:2020yla
;
Poisson:2021yau
;
Berens:2025okm
;
Sharma:2025xii
;
Parra-Martinez:2025bcu
;
Hui:2021vcv
;
Sharma:2024hlz
;
Barbosa:2025uau
;
BenAchour:2022uqo
;
Hui:2022vbh
;
Charalambous:2022rre
;
Katagiri:2022vyz
. Here we focus on dynamical scalar perturbations, in the frequency domain, and investigate the dynamical (frequency-dependent) tidal response. Typically, the computations performed using BH perturbation theory are not gauge invariant. This makes it essential to include the additional step of matching the BH perturbation theory with the EFT discussed in the previous section, which provides a coarse-grained description of the perturbed BH and is the essential baseline for computing dynamics and GWs in a binary system. We closely follow the methods of
creci2021tidal-42e
, and perform the computation of the DTRF in the following steps (also see
1
) — (a) solving the scalar perturbation in the near-zone as well as in the far-zone regime, (b) imposing the ingoing boundary conditions at the horizon, (c) matching the near-zone and the far-zone solutions in the intermediate-zone, and finally (d) taking the asymptotic limit of the far-zone solution for the scalar field and matching with the EFT computation.

Following the above prescription, we first present the behaviour of the scalar field in the near-zone regime, obtained with the approximation
\omega(r-r_{+})\ll 1
, where
r_{+}
is the horizon radius of the BH. Subsequently, we solve the perturbation equations in the far-zone are using the approximation
(M/r)\ll 1
, where
M
is the mass of the BH. The matching of the near-zone with the asymptotic solution determines how information on the near-horizon behavior is imprinted in far-field amplitudes, which we in turn match with the EFT. Throughout this computation, we use a small frequency approximation,
M\omega\ll 1
and work only up to linear order in this quantity.

III.1
The basic equations

We start with BH perturbation theory for scalar perturbations. Given the stationary and axi-symmetric nature of the background Kerr geometry, we decompose the scalar field
\Phi
as,

\displaystyle\Phi=\sum_{\ell,m}\int d\omega e^{-i\omega t}R_{\ell m}(r)S_{\ell m}(\theta)e^{im\phi}\,,

(100)

where
R_{\ell m}(r)
is the radial function and
S_{\ell m}(\theta)
the angular function. The BH perturbation equations in this case are equivalent to the Klein-Gordon equation for a massless scalar field,
\square\Phi=0
. This leads to the following decoupled radial and angular equations,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\dfrac{dS_{\ell m}}{d\theta}\right)-\left(a^{2}\omega^{2}\sin^{2}\theta+\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\widetilde{A}_{\ell m}\right)S_{\ell m}=0~,

(101)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dr}\left(\Delta\dfrac{dR_{\ell m}}{dr}\right)+\left[\frac{\omega^{2}(r^{2}+a^{2})^{2}-4arM\omega m+a^{2}m^{2}}{\Delta}-\widetilde{A}_{\ell m}\right]R_{\ell m}=0~.

(102)

Here,
\Delta=r^{2}-2Mr+a^{2}
, and
a=(J/M)
is the rotation parameter, where
J
is the angular momentum and
M
is the mass of the BH. The quantity
\widetilde{A}_{\ell m}
is the separation constant between radial and angular equations. The above angular equation can be converted to the one obtained by Teukolsky
1973ApJ…185..635T
with the substitution
\widetilde{A}_{\ell m}=E_{\ell m}+a^{2}\omega^{2}
, and the radial equation also converts to the corresponding Teukolsky equation with
\lambda=\widetilde{A}_{\ell m}-2am\omega=E_{\ell m}-2am\omega+a^{2}\omega^{2}
, where
E_{\ell m}
is the separation constant between the radial and the angular sectors in Teukolsky’s formalism. The angular equation determines that
S_{\ell m}(\theta)e^{im\phi}
are the spheroidal harmonics. In what follows, we will use the parametrization used by Teukolsky
1973ApJ…185..635T
for the radial equation.

III.2
Near-zone solution for the radial equation

In this section, we will determine the near-zone solution to
102
. We introduce the dimensionless radial coordinate

z=\frac{(r-r_{+})}{(r_{+}-r_{-})}\,,

(103)

where
r_{\pm}=M\pm\sqrt{M^{2}-a^{2}}
are the event and the Cauchy horizons. Thus it follows that
\Delta=(r-r_{+})(r-r_{-})=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2}z(1+z)
. Therefore we obtain the following structure for the radial equation describing scalar perturbation of the Kerr background:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d}{dz}\left[z(1+z)\dfrac{dR_{\ell m}}{dz}\right]+\frac{\left[P_{+}^{2}-\lambda z(1+z)\right]}{z(1+z)}R_{\ell m}=0~,

(104)

where we have assumed
\omega(r-r_{+})\ll 1
and have defined

P_{+}\equiv(2Mr_{+}\omega-am)/(r_{+}-r_{-})=2Mr_{+}\bar{\omega}/(r_{+}-r_{-}),

(105)

with
\bar{\omega}\equiv\omega-m\Omega_{+}
, and
\Omega_{+}\equiv(a/2Mr_{+})
being the angular velocity of the event horizon, located at
r=r_{+}
.

The general solution of the above second order differential equation, describing the radial part of perturbations of the Kerr background due to an external scalar field in the near-zone regime, becomes,

\displaystyle R^{\rm(near)}_{\ell m}=(1+z)^{iP_{+}}\Big[Az^{-iP_{+}}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(-\hat{\ell},\hat{\ell}+1;1-2iP_{+};-z\right)

\displaystyle+Bz^{iP_{+}}\,_{2}F_{1}\left(-\hat{\ell}+2iP_{+},\hat{\ell}+1+2iP_{+};1+2iP_{+};-z\right)\Big]~,

(106)

where we have expressed the separation constant as
\lambda=\hat{\ell}(\hat{\ell}+1)
, such that the quantity
\hat{\ell}
has the following expansion in powers of
M\omega
,

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}(\hat{\ell}+1)=E_{\ell m}-2am\omega+a^{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+1)-2am\omega+a^{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2a^{2}\omega^{2}\left[\frac{m^{2}+\ell(\ell+1)-1}{(2\ell-1)(2\ell+3)}\right]+\mathcal{O}(M^{3}\omega^{3})\,.

(107)

This can be solved to yield,

\hat{\ell}=\ell-\{2am\omega/(2\ell+1)\}+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2}).

(108)

The fact that
\hat{l}-l=\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
, will be useful in our later analysis. The above near-zone radial solution for scalar tidal perturbations, presented in
III.2
, has two unknown constants
A
and
B
, one of which is fixed by the relevant boundary condition condition at the BH horizon, namely purely ingoing modes.

III.2.1
Ingoing boundary condition at the horizon

To fix one of the two arbitrary constants appearing in
III.2
, we consider the limit to the BH horizon (
z\to 0
). Using the fact that the Hypergeometric function becomes unity as their argument vanishes, the near-zone radial perturbation function becomes,

\displaystyle R^{\rm(near)}_{\ell m}(r\to r_{+})

\displaystyle\simeq Az^{-iP_{+}}+Bz^{iP_{+}}

\displaystyle=Ae^{-iP_{+}r_{*}}+Be^{iP_{+}r_{*}}~.

(109)

Here,
r_{*}
is the Tortoise coordinate, defined through the differential equation:
(dr/dr_{*})=(\Delta/r^{2})
, which in the near-zone region can be approximated as
r_{*}\sim r_{+}\ln z
. As the term
e^{iP_{+}r_{*}}
in the decomposition of
100
belongs to outgoing waves at the horizon, it follows that the coefficient
B
must be set to zero, since for BHs we expect only ingoing modes to be present. Hence the radial part of the scalar perturbation associated with the Kerr BH takes the form,

\displaystyle R^{\rm(near)}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=A_{\rm in}^{\rm H}(1+z)^{iP_{+}}z^{-iP_{+}}

\displaystyle\times\,_{2}F_{1}\left(-\hat{\ell},\hat{\ell}+1;1-2iP_{+};-z\right)~.

(110)

This provides the near-zone solution for the radial perturbation function due to a scalar field on the Kerr background, with
A_{\rm in}^{\rm H}
being an arbitrary constant. To verify the above result, consider Ref.
Chia:2020yla
, which yields an identical expression for the radial function with
\hat{\ell}\to\ell
. This is because Ref.
Chia:2020yla
ignores quite a few terms of
\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
. In this work, however, we have kept all such terms but ignored terms of
\mathcal{O}(M\omega z)
. This leads to
III.2.1
, with
\hat{\ell}=\ell+\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
. In contrast, Ref.
sasaki2003analytic-827
retains all possible terms and expresses the solution as an infinite series of hypergeometric functions, which does not match with the above result. We plan to revisit this infinite series expansion of the hypergeometric function to determine the TLNs in future work.

III.2.2
Limit to the intermediate zone

The same solution in the intermediate region, i.e., in the region satisfying
(r_{+}/r)\ll 1
can be determined by computing the above radial function in the large
z
regime. Given the asymptotic expansion of the hypergeometric function, the near-zone radial function in
III.2.1
, in the corresponding limit becomes

\displaystyle R_{\ell m\,\textrm{(int)}}^{\rm(near)}

\displaystyle=A_{\rm in}^{\rm H}\left[\frac{\Gamma(1-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell})}{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}\frac{r^{\hat{\ell}}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{\hat{\ell}}}\left(1+\cdots\right)+\frac{\Gamma(1-2iP_{+})\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell})\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}\frac{r^{-\hat{\ell}-1}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-\hat{\ell}-1}}\left(1+\cdots\right)\right]\,.

(111)

Thus, asymptotically the radial function consists of two independent power law branches, namely
r^{\hat{\ell}}
and
r^{-1-\hat{\ell}}
. We have only kept the leading order pieces in each branch. The “
\cdots
” denote sub-leading terms, decaying as a power law in the radial coordinate
r
, denoting fractional corrections to the leading order contribution. Since
M\omega\ll 1
, it follows that
\hat{\ell}>0
, and hence the
r^{\hat{\ell}}
branch describes a growing mode related to the tidal field, while the
r^{-1-\hat{\ell}}
branch describes a decaying mode and yields the response of the body. For static perturbations, we have
\hat{\ell}=\ell
and hence the usual static tidal and response fields of the body follow. But note that the tidal field has sub-leading terms, which might raise concerns about ambiguities in the definition of the response function
gralla2017ambiguity-aad
;
Kol:2011vg
;
ivanov2022revisiting-a24
. For rotating BHs,
\hat{\ell}
is given by
108
, and it involves
\ell
corrected by a frequency-dependent quantity. As the frequency can take arbitrary values, it follows that
\hat{\ell}
is analytically continued and hence those sub-leading terms cannot mimic the response function, thus avoiding ambiguities. For the static case and for the case
a=0
, where
\hat{\ell}=\ell
, the ambiguity problem needs to be avoided by an explicit analytic continuation of the angular number
\ell
.

It is worth mentioning that the large
z
limit of the near-zone solution seems a bit odd, but effectively, we need to satisfy the following condition:
\omega r\ll 1
. Thus in the dynamical scenario, the growing and decaying behaviour of the perturbing field holds true within a sphere of radius
(\omega)^{-1}
. Thus we have three characteristic scales in the problem: (a) the horizon at
r_{+}
, (b) the frequency scale
\omega^{-1}
and (c) the asymptotic region. The near-zone solution is valid in a region
r_{+}<r\ll\omega^{-1}
, while the intermediate zone is centered about
\omega^{-1}
, connecting the near-zone and the far-zone, whereas the far-zone corresponds to
r\gg M
(see
1
). Therefore,
111
describes the extension of the near-zone solution to the intermediate zone.

In the next section, we will determine the far-zone solution and shall match its small distance behaviour with the near-zone solution in the intermediate region, as obtained above.

III.3
Far-zone Solution

The far-zone solution is obtained by taking the asymptotic limit, i.e.,
(r/M)\gg 1
, of the radial equation
102
. In the asymptotic limit, we keep the leading order terms in front of the derivatives of
R_{\ell m}
, while in the coefficient of
R_{\ell m}
we keep the sub-leading terms as well. This is necessary because the leading order term
\omega^{2}r^{2}
is not that large in the small frequency approximation. Thus, in the far-zone, the radial part of the scalar perturbation in the Kerr background satisfies the following differential equation,

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{2}R^{\rm(far)}_{\ell m}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{2}{r}\dfrac{dR^{\rm(far)}_{\ell m}}{dr}

\displaystyle+\left[\omega^{2}-\frac{\lambda+2ma\omega}{r^{2}}\right]R^{\rm(far)}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2}r^{-2},r^{-3})~.

(112)

Here, we assume
(r/M)\gg 1
. Since we are keeping terms linear in
M\omega
, it follows that
\lambda+2am\omega=E_{\ell m}+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})=\ell(\ell+1)+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
. Hence, the above differential equation has the following general solution,

\displaystyle R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}=\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}}{\sqrt{r}}J_{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}(\omega r)+\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}}{\sqrt{r}}Y_{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}(\omega r)~.

(113)

Note that the far-zone solution depends on
\ell
, rather than
\hat{\ell}
. This will lead to additional Logarithmic terms in the response function due to improper matching at the intermediate zone. We will discuss this issue in detail later. Thus the far-zone solution is given by the Bessel functions with two arbitrary constants
A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}
(since the Bessel function
J_{\alpha}(x)
is regular as
x\to 0
) and
A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}
(as the associated Bessel function
Y_{\alpha}(x)
is irregular as
x\to 0
). We will now consider two limits,
\omega r\ll 1
, which defines the intermediate zone, and
r\to\infty
, describing the asymptotic limit. We will first discuss the limit to the intermediate zone and then shall move on to discuss the asymptotic limit, which will be used to match with the EFT.

III.3.1
Limit to the intermediate zone

The behaviour of the far-zone solution in the intermediate zone is obtained by taking the
\omega r\ll 1
limit of
113
. As is well known, Bessel functions for small arguments scale as power law for both first and second kind. Thus in the intermediate region, the radial solution of the far-zone behaves as,

\displaystyle R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}(\omega r\ll 1)

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}}{\sqrt{r}\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega r}{2}\right)^{\ell+\frac{1}{2}}-\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}}{\sqrt{r}}\left(\frac{\omega r}{2}\right)^{-\ell-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})}{\pi}

\displaystyle=\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}}{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})}\left(\frac{\omega r}{2}\right)^{\ell}\left[1-\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}}{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}}\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{2}{\omega}\right)^{2\ell+1}\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)r^{-2\ell-1}\right]~.

(114)

As evident, the far-zone solution in the intermediate regime also has a growing mode as well as a decaying mode, identical to the behaviour observed in the asymptotic limit of the near-zone solution, as presented in
111
. There is one important difference though, the power of the growing and the decaying modes differ by
\hat{\ell}-\ell
. We will discuss the implications of this in the subsequent section. This facilitates matching between the near-zone and the far-zone solution, which we will discuss in
III.4
below. Before discussing this matching, we also consider the asymptotic limit of
III.3.1
needed to connect with the EFT.

III.3.2
Limit to the asymptotic region

Consider now the asymptotic limit of the far-zone solution
113
, which is obtained by taking
\omega r\gg 1
. In this limit, the Bessel functions can be expressed in terms of sinusoidal functions, such that,

\displaystyle R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}(\omega r\gg 1)

\displaystyle\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi\omega}}\Big[\frac{A_{\ell(\textrm{reg})}^{\infty}}{r}\sin\left(\omega r-\frac{\ell\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{A_{\ell(\textrm{irreg})}^{\infty}}{r}\cos\left(\omega r-\frac{\ell\pi}{2}\right)\Big]~.

(115)

Note that the above represents only the radial part of the perturbation, which will be multiplied by the angular part
S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)e^{im\phi}
, along with
e^{-i\omega t}
, and then summed over
\ell
and
m
in order to determine the total scalar perturbation
\Phi
. This result will be useful for the matching to the EFT, which we will present below.

III.4
Matching in the intermediate zone

In this section we will discuss one of the most important aspect of this computation, namely matching of the near-zone and the far-zone solutions in the intermediate zone. The matching is possible only if both the solutions share an identical behaviour in the intermediate zone. In our case, it turns out that the far-zone solution in the small
\omega r
limit, see
III.3.1
, and the near zone solution in the large
(r/M)
limit, see
111
, have identical behaviour, i.e., they both include a growing piece
\sim r^{\ell}
, as well as a decaying piece
\sim r^{-\ell-1}
, modulo the factor involving
(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\ln r
. Notice that we are computing the ratio between arbitrary constants and hence they cannot depend on
\ln r
, which suggests to express
\ln r\sim\ln(1/\omega)
, as
r\sim\omega^{-1}
is the region where the intermediate zone ends. Therefore, the coefficient of
r^{\ell}
as well as the coefficient of
r^{-\ell-1}
coming from far and near-zone must be matched. This yields both
A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}
and
A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}
in terms of
A_{\rm H}^{\rm in}
. Thus we obtain the ratio
(A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}/A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)})
to be,

\displaystyle\frac{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}}{A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)}}

\displaystyle=-\pi\left(\frac{\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}\frac{\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}

\displaystyle\times\left\{1+2(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\ln\left[\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})\right]+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})\right\}~.

(116)

Due to
\hat{\ell}
being non-integer, c.f.
108
, it follows that
\Gamma(-\hat{\ell})
is finite and hence the above ratio is non-zero. The same argument holds for
\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell})
. As we will show later, the ratio of these two quantities will turn out to be finite and important for determining the dynamical response function.

At this stage, a few comments on the logarithmic term in the above expression are in order. Such a logarithmic term often arises in the analysis involving dynamical Love numbers of BHs, in particular, to describe the running of the tidal couplings with energy scale
saketh2023dynamical-b30
. These terms are common in the scattering approaches, as well as in BH perturbation theory
saketh2023dynamical-b30
. However, in the present context the logarithmic term scales as
\omega\ln\omega
, since
(\hat{\ell}-\ell)\sim M\omega
, and (a) has the form of a tail term, (b) is higher order in
M\omega
than considered here. The tail effect, on the other hand, comes from scattering of GW from the Newtonian potential and hence has no contribution to tides. Thus we ignore this logarithmic term in this analysis, while we expect that a better understanding of this term may arise from a proper matching procedure, e.g., the MST method
mino1997black-3ab
;
sasaki2003analytic-827
.

III.5
Matching with EFT

In this section we match the results obtained from BH perturbation theory to EFT, as detailed in
II
. We note that the tidal response of BH perturbation theory is denoted by
\lambda_{\ell m}
, see
84
and is related to the ratio
(A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}/A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)})
. To determine the exact relation we compare
49
with
III.3.2
.
The BHPT scalar field for a given mode
\omega
in
III.3.2
can be rewritten as

\begin{split}&\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\omega}}\Phi_{\omega}=\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell,m}S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)R_{\ell m}\end{split}

(117)

Comparison of this expression with the EFT one in
49
, along with the expression for
R_{\ell,m}
from
III.3.2
then yields the relations

D_{\rm reg}^{\ell m}=(-1)^{\ell}A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)},~D_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}=(-1)^{\ell+1}A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}\,,

(118)

Thus, the ratio
D_{\rm irreg}/D_{\rm reg}
is identical to that of
A
’s upto a minus sign. In particular, we have,
(D_{\rm irreg}^{\ell m}/D_{\rm reg}^{\ell m})=-(A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm irreg)}/A^{\infty}_{\ell(\rm reg)})
, such that the BH perturbation theory response function reads,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\pi\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{2M}\right)^{2\ell+1}\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})}+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})~.

(119)

Note that this result is in the spheroidal basis, which need to be converted to spherical basis in order to obtain the EFT response function. Even though the spheroidal to spherical transformation for the scalar perturbation appears at
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
, it follows that there are off-diagonal terms in the response function which actually contributes at those orders. As a consequence, since for scalar perturbation,
\ell
th mode couples to
(\ell\pm 2)
th modes, it follows that the EFT response function is equal to BH response function for
\ell=0,1
modes. While for
\ell\geq 2
, the EFT response function is given by certain linear combination of
\ell=2
and
\ell=0
BH perturbation theory response function. The details of the computation follows from
II
. In what follows we will massage the BH perturbation theory response function, which, in turn can be related to EFT response function through appropriate transformations.

III.6
The dynamical scalar tidal response of black holes

Through matching with the point-particle EFT, we have been able to determine the EFT response function in terms of
\lambda_{\ell m}
. In this section, we will simplify the corresponding expression for
\lambda_{\ell m}
and hence will determine the scalar Love number and dissipation number, which are defined as the real part and the imaginary part of the response function, respectively. For that purpose, we may first rewrite the dynamical response function, keeping only terms which are linear in
M\omega
, as follows (for a derivation see
C
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}(\omega)=\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm stat}+\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm dyn}\times M\omega\,,

(120)

where the rescaled response function
\widetilde{\lambda}
is defined by

\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)^{2\ell+1}\Gamma[\ell+(1/2)]\Gamma[\ell+(3/2)]\lambda_{\ell m}\,.

(121)

Note that the above decomposition
120
neatly separates out the stationary and the dynamical rescaled response functions, each of which is given by,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm stat}=iP^{a}_{+}\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell)^{2}}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(k^{2}+4\{P_{+}^{a}\}^{2}\right)\,,

(122)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm dyn}=\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm stat}\left\{-i\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})\left(-\frac{2m\pi a}{M(2\ell+1)}+\frac{4i\pi r_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\left(-\frac{4ma}{M(2\ell+1)}\right)\Big[\Psi(1+\ell)-\Psi(2+2\ell)-\Psi(1+2\ell)+\frac{1}{2}\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})+\frac{1}{2}\Psi(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\Big]

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\quad+\left(\frac{4ir_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\Big[\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\Big]\right\}\,,

(123)

where
P_{+}^{a}=-\{am/(r_{+}-r_{-})\}
and
P_{+}^{\omega}=\{2Mr_{+}\omega/(r_{+}-r_{-})\}
. It is evident that the above expression has both real and imaginary parts for generic values of the rotation parameter
a
, the angular number
\ell
and the frequency
\omega
, and hence will provide non-trivial scalar tidal responses. Before jumping onto the most general case, let us consider a few specific cases, which we summarize below.

•

Static scalar response of Schwarzschild BHs
obtained by setting
a\to 0
,
P_{+}=2M\omega\to 0
,
r_{-}=0
,
\hat{\ell}=\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. This leads to
\lambda_{\ell}^{\rm Sch}(0)=0
. For analytically continued
\ell
, and using
C
from
C
we can also express the rescaled response function as,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\rm Sch}|_{\rm stat}=\frac{1}{2^{4\ell+2}}\frac{\tan(\pi\ell)\left\{\Gamma(1+\ell)\right\}^{2}}{\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)}~.

(124)

If we now consider
\ell
to be an integer, the above response function identically vanishes, which is consistent with previous findings in the literature
Chia:2020yla
;
LeTiec:2020bos
;
Bhatt:2024rpx
. Further, the above result matches with Eq.(3.122) of Ref.
creci2021tidal-42e
modulo our rescaling of the response function. Thus static TLNs as well as static dissipation numbers of a Schwarzschild BH vanish identically.

•

Dynamical scalar response of Schwarzschild BHs
obtained by setting,
a\to 0
,
P_{+}=2M\omega
,
r_{-}=0
,
\hat{\ell}=\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. In that case, there is a non-zero dynamical piece given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\rm Sch}|_{\rm dyn}=\frac{2iM\omega\,\Gamma(1+\ell)^{2}}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(k^{2}\right)\,.

(125)

This result, using
C
from
C
, can also be expressed as,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell}^{\rm Sch}|_{\rm dyn}=\frac{2\pi iM\omega}{2^{4\ell+1}}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+1)^{2}}{\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)}\,,

(126)

This result matches with Eq. (3.125) of
creci2021tidal-42e
provided we rescale the result of
126
by using the factor introduced in
121
. Further, as the response function is purely imaginary, it corresponds entirely to absorption effects and implies that Schwarzschild BHs have vanishing induced non-dissipative tidal deformations at linear order in the frequency. This is consistent with other results as well
chakrabarti2013new-fa4
;
saketh2023dynamical-b30
. In the non-spinning limit, the dissipation function reduces to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell}=\frac{2M\omega\,\Gamma(1+\ell)^{2}}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(k^{2}\right)\,.

(127)

For
\ell=2
this becomes:
\nu_{2}=M\omega/90
, which is consistent with other results in the literature, e.g., those from the scattering-amplitude
Saketh:2023bul
and from the perturbative
Chakraborty:2025wvs
approaches.

•

Stationary scalar responses of Kerr BHs
are obtained by keeping
a\neq 0
, while setting
\omega=0
. Thus we have
P_{+}=P_{+}^{a}=-\{am/(r_{+}-r_{-})\}
, and
\hat{\ell}=\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
. This yields a non-zero result, given by
122
. Using
C
from
C
we re-express the rescaled response function as,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}^{\rm Kerr}|_{\rm stat}=-\frac{iam\pi}{2^{4\ell+1}\left(r_{+}-r_{-}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left[k^{2}+4\left(P_{+}^{a}\right)^{2}\right]}{\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)}\,.

(128)

As the response function is imaginary, the stationary TLNs of Kerr BHs vanish identically, while the dissipation function becomes non-zero. From the imaginary part of the response function, we obtain,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{am\pi}{2^{4\ell+1}\left(r_{+}-r_{-}\right)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left[k^{2}+4\left(P_{+}^{a}\right)^{2}\right]}{\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\ell+\frac{3}{2}\right)}\,.

(129)

Therefore, Kerr BHs have a non-zero dissipation number at zeroth order in frequency. This arises from the dragging of space due to rotation near the horizon, where for Kerr BHs one replaces
\omega
by the co-rotating frequency
\bar{\omega}=\omega-m\Omega_{\rm H}
.

•

Dynamical scalar response of Kerr BHs
at linear order in the frequency. The coefficients of the linear-in-frequency piece of the conservative tidal response are given by half of the real part of the response function. Given the decomposition of the response function from
120
, a similar decomposition applies for the conservative piece, with the leading order static piece vanishing. Thus from the dynamical response, we obtain the following non-dissipative tidal response for the scalar case,
\widetilde{k}_{\ell m}=M\omega\widetilde{k}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}
, while the dissipative part reads,
\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}=\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}+M\omega\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}
where, each of these coefficients are given by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=P^{a}_{+}\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell)^{2}}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}\left(k^{2}+4\{P_{+}^{a}\}^{2}\right)\,,

(130)

\displaystyle\widetilde{k}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-m\chi\frac{\pi}{(2\ell+1)}\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}

(131)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\nu}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}\left\{\frac{4r_{+}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})}\left[\pi\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})-\textrm{Im}\Big\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\Big\}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\qquad-\left.\frac{4ma}{M(2\ell+1)}\Big[\{\Psi(1+\ell)-\Psi(2+2\ell)-\Psi(1+2\ell)+\frac{1}{2}\textrm{Re}\left\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})+\Psi(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\right\}\Big]\right\}\,,

(132)

Here,
\chi\equiv(a/M)
is the dimensionless spin parameter of the Kerr BH. It is evident that the above expression for the scalar tidal response depends on the frequency linearly through the term
ma\omega
. Thus in the case of axi-symmetric perturbations (
m=0
), the scalar response vanishes identically. This dependence on
a
is further boasted by the result that
P_{+}^{a}\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})\sim 1
, for small rotation.

It is evident that the above expression is not well behaved in the extremal limit. This is because of the explicit
(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1}
terms in the response functions. The inverse powers of
(r_{+}-r_{-})
terms in
P_{+}^{a}
, on the other hand are cancelled by the scaling factor in
121
, which involves
(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2\ell+1}
. This ill-behaved nature of the above dissipation and tidal Love numbers in the extremal limit arises due to the assumption that
P_{+}^{\omega}=(2Mr_{+}\omega/r_{+}-r_{-})\ll 1
, which does not hold in the extremal limit, even if
M\omega\ll 1
. Thus the above results are valid only in the non-extremal case. To rectify this issue we present another alternative form of the response function:

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell m}^{\rm extremal}

\displaystyle=i\widetilde{P}_{+}\left(\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left[j^{2}\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{2M}\right)^{2}+4\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}\right]\frac{(\ell!)^{2}}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\left\{1+i\frac{2\pi am\omega}{2\ell+1}\coth(2\pi P_{+})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[1-\frac{2am\omega}{2\ell+1}\left\{2\Psi(1+\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)\right\}\right]\,,

(133)

where
\widetilde{P}_{+}=r_{+}\omega-(am/2M)
. In arriving at the above expression, we have expressed
\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}\pm 2iP_{+})
as
\Gamma(1+\ell\pm 2iP_{+})
, since in the extremal limit
P_{+}\gg 1
, hence the
\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
terms inside the Gamma functions can be ignored. Now in the extremal limit,
r_{+}\to r_{-}
,
\widetilde{P}_{+}
is finite and appears in the expression for the response function as
\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2\ell+1}
, which can be expressed as,
-(am/2M)^{2\ell+1}\{1-(2\ell+1)(2M^{2}\omega/am)\}
. Similarly, in the extremal limit
P_{+}\to\infty
, leading to
\coth(2\pi P_{+})\to 1
. Thus the response function in the extremal limit becomes,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\ell m}^{\rm extremal}=-im^{2\ell+1}\left(\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma(\ell+\frac{1}{2})\Gamma(\ell+\frac{3}{2})}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(1-(2\ell+1)\frac{2M\omega}{m}\right)\left(1+i\frac{2\pi am\omega}{2\ell+1}\right)

\displaystyle\Big[1-\frac{2am\omega}{2\ell+1}\big\{2\Psi(1+\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)\big\}\Big]\,.

(134)

In what follows we will show that this expression is identical to the one obtained from a separate calculation for extremal spins.

To summarize, the scalar Love number vanishes for both Schwarzschild and Kerr BH geometries in the stationary configuration. In the dynamical case, the conservative scalar Love number vanishes for Schwarzschild BHs, but is non-zero for Kerr BHs and scales as
am\omega
. On the other hand, the dissipation numbers are non-zero for Kerr BHs under stationary perturbations and for both Schwarzschild and Kerr BHs under dynamical perturbations. These results are consistent with previous findings in the literature
tiec2020tidal-db0
;
chia2020tidal-b97
;
charalambous2021vanishing-deb
. Note that the same holds for the EFT response functions as well.

IV
Generic spin perturbation of the non-extremal Kerr background

In this section, we discuss the spin-
s
perturbations of a Kerr background due to external tidal fields and the associated dynamical response functions. Owing to the gauge invariance of scalar functions, the gravitational perturbations are best described by the Weyl scalars
\psi_{0}
and
\psi_{4}
, in terms of which the perturbations are separable in the radial and the angular parts. The same approach will work for scalar and electromagnetic perturbations as well and all of them can be expressed in the following manner
Teukolsky:1974yv
;
1973ApJ…185..635T
:

\displaystyle\rho^{s-|s|}\Psi_{-s+|s|}=\sum_{\ell m}\int d\omega\underbrace{e^{-i\omega t}e^{im\phi}\,_{s}S_{\ell m}(\theta)\,_{s}R_{\ell m}(r)}_{\psi_{-s+|s|}}\,,

(135)

where
s=\pm 2
denotes gravitational perturbation,
s=\pm 1
denotes electromagnetic perturbation and
s=0
denotes scalar perturbations. We have introduced the quantity
\psi_{-s+|s|}
, which is the frequency-space Weyl scalar. The radial part
{}_{s}R_{\ell m}(r)
satisfies the following differential equation
Teukolsky:1974yv
;
1973ApJ…185..635T
:

\displaystyle\Delta\dfrac{d^{2}_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dr^{2}}

\displaystyle+(s+1)\Delta^{\prime}\dfrac{d_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dr}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{K^{2}-is\Delta^{\prime}K}{\Delta}+4irs\omega-\lambda\right]\,_{s}R_{\ell m}=0~.

(136)

Here,
\lambda=E_{\ell m}-2am\omega-s(s+1)+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
is the separation constant between radial and angular equations, and
K\equiv(r^{2}+a^{2})\omega-am
. The angular equation, on the other hand, can be expressed as
Teukolsky:1974yv
;
1973ApJ…185..635T
;
Press:1973zz
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\left(\sin\theta\dfrac{d_{s}S_{\ell m}}{d\theta}\right)+\Big(a^{2}\omega^{2}\cos^{2}\theta-\frac{m^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}

\displaystyle-2a\omega s\cos\theta-\frac{2ms\cos\theta}{\sin^{2}\theta}-\frac{s^{2}}{\sin^{2}\theta}+E_{\ell m}\Big)\,_{s}S_{\ell m}=0~,

(137)

where
E_{\ell m}=\ell(\ell+1)-2ma\omega\{s^{2}/\ell(\ell+1)\}+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
and the solutions of the above differential equation for the angular function are the spin-weighted spheroidal harmonics. These spheroidal harmonics can be expressed in terms of spherical harmonics, which we have already discussed in
II
.

IV.1
The near-zone solution

In this section, we derive the solution of the radial equation in the near-zone and apply the appropriate boundary condition at the horizon. Introducing the radial coordinate
z
defined in
103

it follows that,
\Delta=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2}z(1+z)
, and
\Delta^{\prime}=(d\Delta/dr)=(r_{+}-r_{-})(1+2z)
. On substituting the above results in
IV
, and ignoring terms
\mathcal{O}(M\omega z)
, we obtain the following radial differential equation,

\displaystyle z(1+z)\dfrac{d^{2}_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dz^{2}}+(s+1)(1+2z)\dfrac{d_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dz}

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{P_{+}^{2}}{z(1+z)}-\frac{isP_{+}(1+2z)}{z(1+z)}-\lambda+4i\omega sr_{+}\right]\,_{s}R_{\ell m}=0\,.

(138)

Use of the near-zone approximation
M\omega z\ll 1
, allows us to write
K\approx(r_{+}^{2}+a^{2})\omega-am=2M\omega r_{+}-am
. Further, the quantity
P_{+}
appearing in the radial equation is defined as
P_{+}\equiv(2Mr_{+}\omega-am)/(r_{+}-r_{-})
. We further introduce a quantity
\hat{\ell}_{s}
, which is defined as
\lambda-4i\omega sr_{+}\equiv\hat{\ell}_{s}(\hat{\ell}_{s}+1)-s(s+1)
, such that
E_{\ell m}=\lambda+2am\omega+s(s+1)+\mathcal{O}(a^{2}\omega^{2})=\hat{\ell}_{s}(\hat{\ell}_{s}+1)+2am\omega+4i\omega sr_{+}+\mathcal{O}(a^{2}\omega^{2})
. From the above expression, note that
\hat{\ell}_{s=0}=\hat{\ell}
, the quantity that we have used to describe the near-zone physics in the scalar case. Therefore, it follows that,

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}_{s}(\hat{\ell}_{s}+1)=E_{\ell m}-2am\omega-4i\omega sr_{+}

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+1)+M\omega\left(-\frac{4isr_{+}}{M}-\frac{2am}{M}-\frac{2ams^{2}}{M\ell(\ell+1)}\right)~,

(139)

implying,
\hat{\ell}_{s}=\ell+\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
. With this parametrization, the above equation can be solved exactly, yielding,

\,{}_{s}R^{\rm(near)}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=C_{1}\left(\frac{z}{1+z}\right)^{iP_{+}}\,_{2}F_{1}(-\hat{\ell}_{s}+s,1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+s,1+s+2iP_{+},-z)

\displaystyle+C_{2}\,z^{-iP_{+}-s}(1+z)^{-iP_{+}}\,_{2}F_{1}(-\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+},1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+};1-s-2iP_{+};-z)~.

(140)

The above solution involves two arbitrary constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, which need to be fixed by an appropriate boundary condition at the horizon. This is discussed in the next section.

IV.1.1
Boundary condition at the horizon

As we are studying perturbations of a BH spacetime, whose distinguishing feature is the presence of an event horizon, the appropriate boundary conditions are that there are only purely ingoing modes at the BH horizon. Considering the
z\to 0
limit of
IV.1
, we obtain,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(near)}

\displaystyle=C_{1}z^{iP_{+}}+C_{2}z^{-iP_{+}-s}

\displaystyle\simeq C_{1}e^{i\bar{\omega}r_{*}}+\frac{C_{2}}{\Delta^{s}}e^{-i\bar{\omega}r_{*}}\,.

(141)

When combining this near-horizon radial behavior with the time-dependence in the decomposition of the Weyl scalars
135
, we see that the term
C_{1}
describes purely outgoing waves near the horizon, while
C_{2}
corresponds to purely ingoing waves
Teukolsky:1974yv
. Thus, it follows that
C_{1}=0
and the near-zone solution becomes,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(near)}=C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}z^{-iP_{+}-s}(1+z)^{-iP_{+}}

\displaystyle\times\,_{2}F_{1}(-\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+},1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+};1-s-2iP_{+};-z)~,

(142)

where
C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}
is an overall arbitrary constant. This provides the near-zone radial perturbation, consistent with the ingoing boundary condition at the horizon.

IV.1.2
Solution in the intermediate zone

Having derived the near-zone solution, with appropriate boundary condition at the horizon, let us consider its asymptotic limit, in order to determine the solution to the radial equation in the intermediate zone. In the
z\to\infty
limit, i.e., in the intermediate zone, the above solution, presented in
IV.1.1
, yields,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m\,\textrm{(int)}}^{\rm(near)}=\frac{C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}\,\Gamma(1-s-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{s})}{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-s)}\frac{r^{\hat{\ell}_{s}-s}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{\hat{\ell}_{s}-s}}

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}\,\Gamma(1-s-2iP_{+})\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell}_{s})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(-s-\hat{\ell}_{s})}\frac{r^{-1-\hat{\ell}_{s}-s}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1-\hat{\ell}_{s}-s}}\,.

(143)

Note that the above solution in the limit of
s\to 0
, exactly matches with
111
, derived in the context of scalar perturbations. Thus, the near-zone solution has a growing mode
r^{\hat{\ell}_{s}-s}
as well as a decaying mode
r^{-\hat{\ell}_{s}-1-s}
, and the coefficients of these modes must be matched with the corresponding far-zone solution. Thus, we next consider the far-zone radial equation.

IV.2
The solution in the far-zone

Consider now the radial Teukolsky equation for generic spin in the far-zone

\displaystyle r^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dr^{2}}+2(s+1)r\dfrac{d_{s}R_{\ell m}}{dr}

\displaystyle+\left[\omega^{2}r^{2}+2is\omega r-2am\omega-2isM\omega-\lambda\right]\,_{s}R_{\ell m}=0~,

(144)

where
\lambda
is defined below
IV
. At this stage, we introduce
\tilde{\ell}
, which is defined as,
\tilde{\ell}(\tilde{\ell}+1)=\lambda+2am\omega+2isM\omega+s(s+1)
, such that we obtain,

\displaystyle\tilde{\ell}

\displaystyle(\tilde{\ell}+1)=E_{\ell m}+2isM\omega+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+1)+M\omega\left(2is-\frac{2ams^{2}}{M\ell(\ell+1)}\right)+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})~,

(145)

such that
\tilde{\ell}=\ell+\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
, as well as for
s=0
,
\tilde{\ell}=\ell+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
, which is consistent with our results derived in
III
. Given the above definition of
\tilde{\ell}
, the solution of the radial Teukolsky equation in the far-zone becomes,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega r}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}\Big[B_{1}U(1+\tilde{\ell}-s,2+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{B}_{2}L(-1+s-\tilde{\ell},1+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)\Big]~.

(146)

Here
U(a,b;x)
is the confluent hypergeometric function,
L(a,b;x)
is the Laguerre polynomial, and
B_{1},\,\widetilde{B}_{2}
are integration constants. It is possible to express the Laguerre polynomial in terms of the other confluent hypergeometric function
M(a,b;x)
, yielding the following expression for the radial function in the far-zone,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega r}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}\Big[B_{1}U(1+\tilde{\ell}-s,2+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)

\displaystyle+B_{2}M(1-s+\tilde{\ell},2+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)\Big]~.

(147)

The confluent hypergeometric function
U(a,b;x)
can be expressed in terms of
M(a,b;x)
using
239
in
D
, and hence we obtain the above far-zone radial function solely in terms of
M(a,b;z)
as,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}=e^{-i\omega r}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[\Big\{B_{2}+B_{1}\frac{\Gamma(-1-2\tilde{\ell})}{\Gamma(-\tilde{\ell}-s)}\Big\}M(1+\tilde{\ell}-s,2+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)

\displaystyle+B_{1}\frac{\Gamma(1+2\tilde{\ell})}{\Gamma(1+\tilde{\ell}-s)}(2i\omega r)^{-1-2\tilde{\ell}}M(-s-\tilde{\ell},-2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)\Bigg]\,.

(148)

Anticipating that we will ultimately need to identify the regular and irregular pieces of the radial solution, we change the integration constants to two new constants
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}
and
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}
defined as linear combinations of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
:

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle+B_{1}\frac{\Gamma(-1-2\tilde{\ell})}{\Gamma(-\tilde{\ell}-s)}\equiv\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}~,

(149)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle(2i\omega)^{-1-2\tilde{\ell}}\frac{\Gamma(1+2\tilde{\ell})}{\Gamma(1+\tilde{\ell}-s)}\equiv-\left(\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\right)\Gamma\left(\bar{\mu}\right)\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{-\bar{\mu}}~,

(150)

where we have introduced the two constants
\bar{\nu}=\tilde{\ell}-|s|+(1/2)
and
\bar{\mu}=\tilde{\ell}+|s|+(1/2)
, such that
\bar{\nu}+\bar{\mu}=2\tilde{\ell}+1
.

In terms of these newly defined constants,
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}
and
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}
, the radial solution for generic spin perturbation in the far-zone reads,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}=e^{-i\omega r}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}M(1+\tilde{\ell}-s,2+2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(\bar{\mu})}{r^{1+2\tilde{\ell}}}\left(\frac{2}{\omega}\right)^{\bar{\mu}}M(-s-\tilde{\ell},-2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)\Bigg]\,,

(151)

To determine the integration constants explicitly in terms of properties of the perturbed BH, we first take the limit of the far-zone solution to the intermediate zone, where we match it with the near-zone solution. We also need the limit to the asymptotic region to match with the EFT.

Note that in the
s\to 0
limit,
\bar{\nu}=\ell+(1/2)=\bar{\mu}
. Further, we can use
242
in
D
in order to express the far-zone solution in terms of Bessel functions of order
\ell+(1/2)
. One can show that such a computation directly produces
113
, the far-zone solution for scalar perturbations. Thus our results are consistent with the scalar limit.

IV.2.1
Solution in the intermediate zone

Having derived the general solution to the radial equation in the far-zone, let us consider the
\omega r\ll 1
limit and determine the solution in the intermediate zone. For this purpose, we can use the results presented in
240
of
D
, such that
IV.2
reduces to,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m\,\textrm{(int)}}^{\rm(far)}

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}

\displaystyle-\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{-\bar{\mu}}r^{-1-\tilde{\ell}-s}\,.

(152)

Note that, alike the near-zone solution, the above far-zone solution also has a growing mode
(\sim r^{\tilde{\ell}-s})
and a decaying mode
(\sim r^{-1-\tilde{\ell}-s})
in the intermediate zone. Expressing,
\tilde{\ell}=\hat{\ell}_{s}+\epsilon_{\rm s}M\omega
, with

\epsilon_{s}=\left(\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\right)\left[2is\left(1-\frac{2r_{+}}{M}\right)+\frac{2am}{M}\right]\,,

(153)

the above intermediate zone solution can be expressed as,

\displaystyle R_{\ell m\,\textrm{(int)}}^{\rm(far)}

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}r^{\hat{\ell}-s}(r_{+}-r_{-})^{\tilde{\ell}-\hat{\ell}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[1+\epsilon_{\rm s}M\omega\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\left(\frac{2}{\omega}\right)^{\bar{\mu}}r^{-1-\hat{\ell}-s}(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-\tilde{\ell}+\hat{\ell}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[1-\epsilon_{\rm s}M\omega\ln\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\right]~.

(154)

Note that the power of the growing mode and the decaying mode in the intermediate zone is identical for both far-zone and near-zone solutions and hence the respective coefficients can be properly matched to order
O(M\omega)
. The appearance of the
\ln r
term is not new, as it had appeared in the case of scalar perturbations as well, which can be seen by noticing that
\epsilon_{s=0}\neq 0
. As usual, after matching between far and near-zone the arbitrary constants cannot be a function of radius, and hence it seems appropriate to set
r\sim\omega^{-1}
. Thus, in the general spin case also there is a correction of
\mathcal{O}(\omega\ln\omega)
. As we have already emphasised earlier this term can be a tail effect, with no connection to the tidal field, and this is a term of higher order than what we have considered here. Hence, we will ignore the logarithmic piece and, hopefully, this can be better understood in the future using the MST method
sasaki2003analytic-827
.

IV.2.2
Solution in the asymptotic region

In order to find the matching of the perturbation theory approach with the EFT techniques we need to consider the asymptotic limit of the far-zone solution. For this purpose, we will first re-express the far-zone solution in terms of Bessel functions and then we will take the asymptotic limit. Therefore, to obtain the far-zone solution in terms of Bessel functions one can use the identities within
D
, presented in
244
and
245
. Using these relations, we obtain, for generic spin,

\displaystyle M(1+\tilde{\ell}-s,2+2\tilde{\ell},2i\omega r)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)e^{i\omega r}\left(\frac{i\omega r}{2}\right)^{-\bar{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}g_{k}\,I_{\bar{\nu}+k}(i\omega r)\,,

(155)

where,
I_{\alpha}(x)
is the modified Bessel function,
\bar{\nu}=\tilde{\ell}-|s|+(1/2)
has already been introduced earlier and the coefficient
g_{k}
has the following expression:

\displaystyle g_{k}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\frac{s}{|s|}\right)^{k}\frac{\left(\bar{\nu}+k\right)}{\bar{\nu}}\frac{\Gamma(2\bar{\nu}+k)}{\Gamma(2\bar{\nu})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Gamma(2\bar{\nu}+1+2|s|)}{\Gamma(2\bar{\nu}+1+2|s|+k)}\frac{(-2|s|)_{k}}{k!}~,

(156)

where
(a)_{k}
is the Pochhammer symbol. Along identical lines, the other confluent hypergeometric function appearing in
IV.2
can also be written as,

\displaystyle M(-s-\tilde{\ell},-2\tilde{\ell};2i\omega r)

\displaystyle=\Gamma(1-\bar{\mu})e^{i\omega r}\left(\frac{i\omega r}{2}\right)^{\bar{\mu}}

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}\widetilde{g}_{k}I_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(i\omega r)\,,

(157)

where
\bar{\mu}=\tilde{\ell}+|s|+(1/2)
has also been defined earlier, and the coefficient
\widetilde{g}_{k}
reads,

\displaystyle\widetilde{g}_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s}{|s|}\right)^{k}\frac{\left(-\bar{\mu}+k\right)}{-\bar{\mu}}\frac{\Gamma(-2\bar{\mu}+k)}{\Gamma(-2\bar{\mu})}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\Gamma(-2\bar{\mu}+1+2|s|)}{\Gamma(-2\bar{\mu}+1+2|s|+k)}\frac{(-2|s|)_{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{s}{|s|}\right)^{k}\frac{\left(\bar{\mu}-k\right)}{\bar{\mu}}\frac{\Gamma(1+2\bar{\mu})}{\Gamma(1+2\bar{\mu}-k)}\frac{\Gamma(2\bar{\mu}-2|s|-k)}{\Gamma(2\bar{\mu}-2|s|)}~.

(158)

Therefore, using
IV.2.2
and
IV.2.2
in
IV.2
, the far zone solution in the asymptotic regime takes the form,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m\,(\infty)}^{\rm(far)}=i^{-\bar{\nu}}B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}r^{|s|-s-\frac{1}{2}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}g_{k}\,I_{\bar{\nu}+k}(i\omega r)

\displaystyle-i^{\bar{\mu}}\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(1-\bar{\mu})\,r^{|s|-s-\frac{1}{2}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}\widetilde{g}_{k}I_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(i\omega r)\,.

(159)

We next use the following relations between the modified Bessel functions and the Bessel functions of the first and second kinds given by
I_{\bar{\nu}+k}(i\omega r)=i^{k+\bar{\nu}}J_{\bar{\nu}+k}(\omega r)
and
I_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(i\omega r)=i^{-\bar{\mu}+k}J_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(\omega r)
, and the identity
\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(1-\bar{\mu})=\{\pi/\sin(\pi\bar{\mu})\}
. Upon using these identities, the far-zone solution can be expressed in terms of Bessel functions as,

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m}^{\rm(far)}

\displaystyle=\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{r^{-|s|+s+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}i^{k}g_{k}\,J_{\bar{\nu}+k}(\omega r)

\displaystyle-\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{r^{-|s|+s+\frac{1}{2}}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}i^{k}\widetilde{g}_{k}\frac{J_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(\omega r)}{\sin(\pi\bar{\mu})}\,.

(160)

This explains the labelling of the constants
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}
and
B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}
, as these are the coefficients of
J_{\bar{\nu}+k}(\omega r)
(regular in the
\omega r\to 0
limit), and
J_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(\omega r)
(irregular in the
\omega r\to 0
limit), respectively. In the
s\to 0
limit, only
k=0
contributes to the sum, and
g_{0}=1=\widetilde{g}_{0}
. Moreover, in the case of
s\to 0
,
J_{\bar{\nu}+k}(\omega r)\to J_{\ell+1/2}(\omega r)
, as well as
J_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(\omega r)/\sin(\pi\bar{\mu})\to Y_{\ell+1/2}(\omega r)
. Thus it follows that we get back the results of
113
and our results are consistent with the scalar perturbation derived earlier.

Having expressed the far-zone solution in terms of Bessel function, we will now consider the large
r
limit. Expressing
\tilde{\ell}=\ell+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega
, where,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\ell}=\left(\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\right)\left(2is-\frac{2ams^{2}}{M\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\,,

(161)

we obtain the following asymptotic expansion of the Bessel functions:

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle J_{\bar{\nu}+k}(\omega r)=\sqrt{\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{\pi\omega r}}\Big\{\sin\left[\omega r-\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]\cos\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]$}}

\displaystyle-\cos\left[\omega r-\frac{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]\sin\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]\Big\}\,,

(162)

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle J_{-\bar{\mu}+k}(\omega r)=\sqrt{\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{\pi\omega r}}\Big\{\cos\left[\omega r-\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]\cos\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]$}}

\displaystyle-\sin\left[\omega r-\frac{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]\sin\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]\Big\}\times(-1)^{\ell+k}\,.

(163)

Therefore, the asymptotic behaviour of the far-zone radial function can also be expressed in terms of sinusoidal functions. This is useful in the context of EFT in order to deal with the issue of mode mixing, as discussed below. Substituting the asymptotic expansions
IV.2.2
and
IV.2.2
into
IV.2.2
leads to the following asymptotic behaviour of the radial function,

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m\,(\infty)}^{\rm(far)}$}}

\displaystyle=\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\sqrt{\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{\pi\omega}}\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\sin\left[\omega r-\frac{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]}{r^{-|s|+s+1}}\,\Bigg\{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}i^{k}g_{k}\cos\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]+(-1)^{|s|}B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}(-i)^{k}\widetilde{g}_{k}\sin\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k-\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]\Bigg\}$}}

\displaystyle\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle-\sqrt{\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{2}{\pi\omega}}\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\cos\left[\omega r-\frac{\pi}{2}(\ell-|s|)\right]}{r^{-|s|+s+1}}\,\Bigg\{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}i^{k}g_{k}\sin\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k+\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]+(-1)^{|s|}B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}\,\sum_{k=0}^{2|s|}(-i)^{k}\widetilde{g}_{k}\cos\left[\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{\pi}{2}\left(k-\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\right)\right]\Bigg\}\,.$}}

(164)

In the limit
s\to 0
, the summations in the above expression no longer exists and
g_{k}(s=0)=g_{0}=1=\widetilde{g}_{k}(s=0)=\widetilde{g}_{0}
. Further, as
\epsilon_{\ell}\to 0
for scalar perturbation, as well as
\cos[\pi(\tilde{\ell}+|s|)]\to(-1)^{\ell}
, it follows that the asymptotic radial function reduces to the one presented in
III.3.2
, thus verifying that the
s\to 0
limit correctly recovers the results of the previous section for scalar perturbation of Kerr BHs.

IV.3
Matching in the intermediate zone

The limit of the far-zone solution in the intermediate zone, given by
IV.2.1
, can be matched with the limit of the near-zone solution in the intermediate zone, presented in
IV.1.2
. This yields the following relations between the coefficients of the growing and decaying modes in the near and the far-zone solutions:

\displaystyle\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}=\frac{C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{\tilde{\ell}-s}}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1-s-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{s})}{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-s)}\right)\,,

(165)

\displaystyle\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\left(\frac{2}{\omega}\right)^{\bar{\mu}}=-\frac{C_{\rm H}^{\rm in}}{(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1-\tilde{\ell}-s}}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1-s-2iP_{+})\Gamma(-1-2\hat{\ell}_{s})}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(-s-\hat{\ell}_{s})}\right)~.

(166)

From the far-zone solution
IV.2.1
, we note that
D^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}
is the coefficient of the decaying mode in
r
and
D^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}
is the coefficient of the growing mode in
r
, and hence the ratio of the decaying to the growing mode must be related to the response function. Thus we explicitly state the corresponding ratio between the growing and the decaying mode:

\displaystyle\frac{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm irreg}}{B^{\infty}_{\ell m,\,\rm reg}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{s+1}\left[\frac{\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})}{2}\right]^{2\tilde{\ell}+1}\frac{\sin[\pi\hat{\ell}_{s}+2i\pi P_{+}]}{2\cos(\pi\hat{\ell}_{s})}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+2iP_{+})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu})}\right)\,.

(167)

This provides the desired relation between the regular and the irregular piece for generic spin perturbations arising from BH perturbation theory alone. In the
s\to 0
limit, we obtain
\tilde{\ell}\to\ell
,
\hat{\ell}_{s}\to\hat{\ell}
and
\bar{\nu}=\bar{\mu}=\ell+(1/2)
, such that the right hand side of
IV.3
reproduces
III.5
. We now proceed to discuss the connection of the asymptotic limit of the far-zone solution to the EFT.

IV.4
Matching with EFT

We now want to match the EFT radial function in
96
with the asymptotic expression of the BH perturbation theory radial function in the far-zone in
IV.2.2
, for the gravitational case (
s=-2
). The electromagnetic case follows in a similar fashion and is thus not discussed in detail here. In order to match BH perturbation theory and EFT we note that
IV.2.2
can also be expressed as,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}R^{\rm(far)}_{\ell m(\infty)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i^{-\ell}\omega^{2}\frac{e^{-i\omega(t-r)}}{\omega r}\left(B_{\ell m,\rm reg}^{\infty}\frac{32i\ell(\ell-1)}{(3+8\ell+4\ell^{2})\omega}\right.

(168)

\displaystyle\left.+B_{\ell m,\rm irreg}^{\infty}\frac{32\ell(\ell+1)}{(3-8\ell+4\ell^{2})\omega}\right)\,.

The regular and irregular pieces match directly with those in
96
after expanding spin weighted (
s=-2
) spheroidal harmonics into spin weighted spherical harmonics.

To proceed further, it is convenient to redefine the integration constants in
168
as,

\begin{split}\bar{B}^{\infty}_{\ell m,\rm reg}&=B_{\ell m,\rm reg}^{\infty}\frac{64\ell(\ell-1)}{(3+8\ell+4\ell^{2})\omega}\,,\\
\bar{B}^{\infty}_{\ell m,\rm irreg}&=-B_{\ell m,\rm irreg}^{\infty}\frac{64\ell(\ell+1)}{(3-8\ell+4\ell^{2})\omega}\,.\end{split}

(169)

Then, we can write

\begin{split}\psi_{4}=(-1)^{\ell}\omega^{2}\frac{e^{-i\omega(t-r)}}{\omega r}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}i^{\ell-2}\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm out}{}_{-2}S_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi)\,,\end{split}

(170)

where
\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm out}=(1/2)(\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}-i\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm reg})
. The coefficients can now be directly identified by comparing with
96
. This yields

\begin{split}D_{\ell m}&=(-1)^{\ell}\bar{B}_{\ell m}\,,\end{split}

(171)

which is valid for both regular and irregular coefficients. One can then follow the analysis in
II.2
to compute the tidal-response coefficients in EFT which are a priori defined in spherical harmonic basis. Here, we will restrict to computing the tidal response coefficients in the spheroidal harmonic basis.

IV.5
Tidal response function

The determination of the dynamical tidal response function of a Kerr BH under generic spin perturbation follows an identical route to that adopted in the scalar case. The regular solution encodes the tidal field, while the irregular solution, which is sourced by a delta function at the origin, corresponds to the quadrupole moment. Using the results obtained by matching with the EFT, provided in the previous section, BH perturbation theory tidal response function reads,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\mathcal{\lambda}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=(M\omega)^{-2\ell-1}\frac{D^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}}{D^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}}=(M\omega)^{-2\ell-1}\frac{\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}}{\bar{B}^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}}=-(M\omega)^{-2\ell-1}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{B^{\ell m}_{\rm irreg}}{B^{\ell m}_{\rm reg}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{2M}\right)^{2\ell+1}\left[\frac{\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})}{2}\right]^{2(\tilde{\ell}-\ell)}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{\sin[\pi\hat{\ell}_{s}+2i\pi P_{+}]}{2\cos(\pi\hat{\ell}_{s})}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+2iP_{+})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu})}\right)\,.

(172)

Note that
\{\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})\}^{2(\tilde{\ell}-\ell)}=1+2\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\ln\{\omega(r_{+}-r_{-})\}\simeq 1
, as we are ignoring terms of
\mathcal{O}(\omega\ln\omega)
. Given the above, we first consider a rescaled response function
{}_{s}\widetilde{\mathcal{F}}_{\ell m}
, related to the original response function as:

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{2M}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)^{2\ell+1}\frac{(\ell-1)}{(\ell+1)}\left(\frac{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)

(173)

\displaystyle\times\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2){}_{-2}\mathcal{\lambda}_{\ell m}\,.

Using the results from
E
, and ignoring terms of
\mathcal{O}(\omega\ln\omega)
and
\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})
we obtain upto linear order in
M\omega
,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}={}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}+{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}M\omega\,,

(174)

where the stationary response function yields (see
E
),

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\rm stat}_{\ell m}=iP_{+}^{a}\frac{(\ell-s)!(\ell+s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\{n^{2}+4(P_{+}^{a})^{2}\}\,,

(175)

with
P_{+}^{a}
defined in
248
. This result matches with earlier results in the literature
LeTiec:2020bos
;
chia2020tidal-b97
;
Bhatt:2024rpx
. As evident, the above expression is purely imaginary and hence the tidal Love numbers of a Kerr BH under general perturbations identically vanish. The dynamical response function, as in
174
, reads (see
E
):

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}=P_{+}^{a}\frac{(\ell-s)!(\ell+s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\{n^{2}+4(P_{+}^{a})^{2}\}\Big[\left(\frac{\pi\Delta\ell+2i\pi P_{+}^{\omega}}{M\omega}\right)\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{2P_{+}^{\omega}}{M\omega}\right)\left\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+}^{a})\right\}-i\epsilon_{\ell}\left\{\Psi(\bar{\mu}_{e})+\Psi(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})\right\}

\displaystyle+i\left(\frac{\Delta\ell}{M\omega}\right)\left\{\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+}^{a})+\Psi(1+\ell-s)+\Psi(1+\ell+s)+\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}\Big]\,.

(176)

Given the above response functions, the conservative and dissipative tidal responses are the real and imaginary parts of
174
. We substitute the expressions for the quantities
\epsilon_{\ell}
,
\Delta\ell
,
P_{+}^{\omega}
from
161
,
249
, and
248
respectively, and define the dissipative coefficients to linear order in frequency by

{}_{-2}\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}+M\omega{}_{-2}\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}^{\rm dyn}=\textrm{Im}[{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}].

(177)

We find that the stationary part of the response function is purely imaginary and

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}=P_{+}^{a}\frac{(\ell-s)!(\ell+s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\{n^{2}+4(P_{+}^{a})^{2}\}\,.

(178)

The dynamical part of the dissipation function from
IV.5
is,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}^{\rm dyn}={}_{-2}\widetilde{\nu}_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}\Bigg[\left(\frac{4\pi sr_{+}}{M(2\ell+1)}+\frac{4\pi r_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})-\left(\frac{4r_{+}}{r_{+}-r_{-}}\right)\textrm{Im}\left\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+}^{a})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{2ams^{2}}{M\ell(\ell+1)(2\ell+1)}\left\{\Psi(\bar{\mu}_{e})+\Psi(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})\right\}-\frac{2am}{M(2\ell+1)}\left(1+\frac{s^{2}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\left\{\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+}^{a})+\Psi(1+\ell-s)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\Psi(1+\ell+s)+\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}\Bigg]\,.

(179)

Similarly, the conservative part of the response has the low-frequency expansion:

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{k}_{\ell m}=\frac{1}{2}\,{\rm Re}[_{-2}{\widetilde{\lambda}}_{\ell m}]={}_{-2}\widetilde{k}_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}+M\omega{}_{-2}\widetilde{k}_{\ell m}^{\rm dyn}\,.

(180)

As discussed above,
{}_{-2}\widetilde{k}_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}=0
for a Kerr background, while the dynamical coefficient reads:

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{k}^{\rm dyn}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=_{-2}\nu_{\ell m}^{\rm stat}\Big[-\frac{m\chi}{(2\ell+1)}\left(1+\frac{s^{2}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\pi\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})

\displaystyle+\frac{s}{2\ell+1}\left\{\Psi(\bar{\mu}_{e})+\Psi(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})\right\}+\frac{2sr_{+}}{M(2\ell+1)}\Big\{\Psi(1+\ell-s)+\Psi(1+\ell+s)

\displaystyle+\textrm{Re}[\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+}^{a})+\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})]-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\Big\}\Big]\,.

(181)

Here
\Psi
denotes the digamma function. One can straightforwardly check that the above expression reduces to
130
in the limit
s\to 0
. Moreover, note that in the limit of
a\to 0
, the dynamical coefficient at linear order in
M\omega
vanishes, which is also consistent with the previous results in the literature
Chakraborty:2025wvs
;
Katagiri:2024wbg
;
saketh2023dynamical-b30
. In addition, for axisymmetric perturbations, i.e., for
m=0
, we obtain
P_{+}^{a}=0
, and hence the dynamic tidal response function vanishes identically up to linear order in
M\omega
.
Note that unlike the case of the dynamical tidal Love number, the dynamical dissipation number depends explicitly on
(r_{+}-r_{-})^{-1}
and hence is ill-behaved in the extremal limit. We will discuss these issues in a subsequent paragraph. All in all, the above provides the complete expressions for the stationary as well as dynamic Love numbers and dissipation numbers for a Kerr BH under arbitrary perturbations.

We would like to point out one interesting feature of the above dynamical response function in this regard. First of all, note that the term involving sinusoidal functions in
IV.5
can be expressed as
(1/2)\sinh(2\pi P_{+})[1+\mathcal{O}(M\omega)]
. Similarly, the following combination of Gamma functions becomes
\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})=\{2\pi P_{+}/\sinh(2\pi P_{+})\}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}(k^{2}+4P_{+}^{2})\left[1+\mathcal{O}(M\omega)\right]
. Hence the overall scaling of the dynamical response function and of the dynamical TLNs becomes
iP_{+}\prod_{k=1}^{\ell}(k^{2}+4P_{+}^{2})
. Recalling the definition of
P_{+}
from
105
, this shows that the response function changes sign as the frequency crosses the superradiant bound
m\Omega_{\rm H}
.

Finally note that the expression for the dynamical response function as in
IV.5
is ill-behaved in the extremal limit. This is a consequence of the fact that in the extremal limit,
\{P_{+}^{a},P_{+}^{\omega}\}\to\infty
, and
r_{+}\to r_{-}
. The divergent behaviour of the response function in
IV.5
in the extremal limit arises from the fact that in deriving
IV.5
we had assumed
P_{+}^{\omega}\ll 1
, which is not true in this limit. However, the physical response is finite for extremal spins, as can be seen from an alternative expression for the dynamical response function we calculated:

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda_{\ell m}=i\widetilde{P}_{+}\left(\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}\right)\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{(\ell-s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\left[j^{2}\left(\frac{r_{+}-r_{-}}{2M}\right)^{2}+4\widetilde{P}_{+}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\times\left[1-i\pi\Delta\ell_{s}\coth(2\pi P_{+})\right]\Big[1+\Delta\ell_{s}\left\{2\Psi(1+\ell-s)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\epsilon_{\ell}\left\{\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}\Big]\,.

(182)

The well-behaved-ness can be first apprehended from the definition of the quantity
\widetilde{P}_{+}=r_{+}\omega-(am/2M)
, which approaches finite values in the extremal limit. In fact, in the extremal limit,
r_{+}\to r_{-}\to M
, along with
a=M
, the above response function for gravitational perturbation becomes,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda_{\ell m}=-im^{2\ell+1}\left\{1-(2\ell+1)\frac{2M\omega}{m}\right\}\left(\frac{\pi}{2\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}\right)\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{(\ell-s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}

\displaystyle\times\left[1-i\pi\Delta\ell_{s}\right]\Big[1+\Delta\ell_{s}\left\{2\Psi(1+\ell-s)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}-\epsilon_{\ell}\left\{\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}\Big]\,,

(183)

where we have used the result that in the
P_{+}\to\infty
limit,
\coth(2\pi P_{+})\to 1
, and at the same time we have replaced
\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}\pm 2iP_{+})
by
\Gamma(1+\ell\pm 2iP_{+})
, where we have ignored the
\mathcal{O}(M\omega)
term as
P_{+}\gg 1
. We will compute now the extremal case in an explicit manner and shall match the respective results with the expression of the dynamical response function as presented above.

V
Generic spin perturbation of an extremal Kerr black hole

In this section we would like to briefly touch upon the dynamical tides for an extremal Kerr BH. In this case, the spin length
a
is the same as the mass
a=M
and the two horizons coincide. Thus, we have
\Delta=(r-r_{+})^{2}
, with
r_{+}=M=a
. Note that, since for an extremal Kerr BH
r_{+}=r_{-}
, the coordinate
z
introduced in
103
and used in all previous calculations vanishes and thus cannot be used for studying the extremal case. Instead, we introduce a new radial coordinate
y\equiv(r/r_{+})-1
and express the Teukolsky equation in the Boyer-Lindquist coordinate as,

\displaystyle y^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}{}_{s}R^{(\rm BL)}_{\ell m}}{dy^{2}}+2(s+1)y\dfrac{d{}_{s}R^{(\rm BL)}_{\ell m}}{dy}+\left[\frac{K^{2}-2isKr_{+}y}{r_{+}^{2}y^{2}}+4is\omega r_{+}(1+y)-\lambda\right]{}_{s}R^{(\rm BL)}_{\ell m}=0~.

(184)

Here, the superscript to the radial coordinate denotes that the radial function is expressed in the Boyer-Lindquist coordinate system.
It turns out that in the near-zone region, the above equation does not admit an analytic solution. However, when switching to advanced null coordinates
(v,r,\theta,\widetilde{\phi})
, where
dv=dt+\{(r^{2}+a^{2})/\Delta\}dr
and
d\widetilde{\phi}=d\phi+(a/\Delta)dr
, the Teukolsky equation can be solved in the near-zone region. On the other hand, the solution of the Teukolsky equation in the far-zone is best solved in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, rather than in advanced null coordinates. Thus we will solve the near-zone Teukolsky equation in null coordinates, while the far-zone Teukolsky equation is considered in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates.

V.1
Near-zone Solution

Under the near-zone approximation (
M\omega\ll 1
and
M\omega y\ll 1
), we have,
K\approx(r_{+}^{2}+a^{2})\omega-am
, and
\omega r\approx\omega r_{+}
, such that the radial Teukolsky equation
184
in the ingoing null coordinates becomes,

\displaystyle y^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}}{dy^{2}}+\left[2(s+1)y-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}\right]\dfrac{d{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}}{dy}+\left[-\frac{4is\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}-\lambda\right]{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}=0~.

(185)

Here, the superscript implies the use of Eddington-Finkelstein like coordinates. In addition, we have introduced
\widetilde{P}_{+}=2M\bar{\omega}
, where
\bar{\omega}=\omega-(ma/2M^{2})
is the frequency in the co-rotating frame of reference. Moreover, the radial function
{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}
in ingoing null coordinates is related to the radial function
{}_{s}R^{(\rm BL)}_{\ell m}
in the Boyer-Lindquist coordinate system through the following transformation:
{}_{s}R^{(\rm BL)}_{\ell m}=e^{-i\omega r_{*}}e^{im\widetilde{r}}{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}
, where
r_{*}
is the Tortoise coordinate and
d\widetilde{r}\equiv(a/\Delta)dr
. Further note that, the separation constant
\lambda
is given by
\lambda=\hat{\ell}_{e}(\hat{\ell}_{e}+1)-s(s+1)
.
Therefore, the radial Teukolsky equation in the near zone, in ingoing null coordinates, takes the following final form:

\displaystyle y^{2}\dfrac{d^{2}{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}}{dy^{2}}+\left[2(s+1)y-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}\right]\dfrac{d{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}}{dy}+\left[-\frac{4is\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}-\hat{\ell}_{e}(\hat{\ell}_{e}+1)+s(s+1)\right]{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}=0~,

(186)

which has the following solution in terms of confluent hypergeometric functions:

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=Ay^{\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}M\left(-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s,-2\hat{\ell}_{e};-\frac{2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}\right)+By^{-1-s-\hat{\ell}_{e}}M\left(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s,2+2\hat{\ell}_{e};-\frac{2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}\right)~.

(187)

Here,
M(a,b;z)
is the confluent hypergeometric function and
\hat{\ell}_{e}
is related to the angular momentum such that,

\displaystyle\hat{\ell}_{e}=\ell-\frac{2M\omega}{2\ell+1}\left[m\chi\left(1+\frac{s^{2}}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\right]+\mathcal{O}(M^{2}\omega^{2})\,.

(188)

Note that,
\hat{\ell}_{e}-\ell=\hat{\ell}_{s}-\ell
, which for
s=0
reduces to
\hat{\ell}-\ell
. Thus the renormalized angular momentum in the extremal case behaves identically to that in the non-extremal case.
Given the near-zone solution we will now take the near-horizon limit and hence impose appropriate boundary conditions at the horizon, and then we take the asymptotic limit for matching with the far-zone solution at the intermediate zone.

V.1.1
The limit to the horizon

We first consider the near-horizon limit, which corresponds to
y\to 0
. In this case, using
241
in
D
, the radial function in the limit towards the horizon becomes,

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}|_{\rm hor}=y^{-2s}\left[A\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(s-\hat{\ell}_{e})}e^{\pm i\pi(\hat{\ell}_{e}+s)}(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+})^{\hat{\ell}_{e}+s}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+B\frac{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}+s)}e^{\mp i\pi(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)}(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+})^{-1-\hat{\ell}_{e}+s}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+e^{-\frac{2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}}\left[A\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(-s-\hat{\ell}_{e})}(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+})^{\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+B\frac{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)}(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+})^{-1-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}\right]\,.

(189)

For an extremal BH, the ingoing solution of the radial Teukolsky function behaves as
\Delta^{-s}\approx y^{-2s}
, and the outgoing solution takes the form
e^{-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}/y}
. Since for BHs there are no outgoing solutions at the horizon, it follows that the coefficient of
\exp(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}/y)
must vanish. Hence the ratio
(B/A)
takes the following form,

\displaystyle\frac{B}{A}=-\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}~.

(190)

Therefore, by substituting the ratio
(B/A)
in
187
, the radial function
{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}
, purely ingoing at the BH horizon, becomes,

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{(\rm EF)}_{\ell m}=Ay^{\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}\Bigg[M\left(-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s,-2\hat{\ell}_{e};-\frac{2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(\frac{-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\times M\left(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s,2+2\hat{\ell}_{e};-\frac{2i\widetilde{P}_{+}}{y}\right)\Bigg]~.

(191)

This yields the solution of the radial Teukolsky equation in the near-zone, which is purely ingoing at the BH horizon. The next step is to take the asymptotic limit and hence determine the behaviour of the radial solution in the intermediate-zone, to be matched with the far-zone solution.

V.1.2
Extension to the intermediate zone

In order to arrive at the intermediate zone, we take the limit
y\to\infty
of
V.1.1
, which implies that the argument of the confluent hypergeometric functions must vanish, and since
\lim_{z\to 0}M(a,b;z)=1
, we obtain,

\displaystyle{}_{s}R^{\textrm{(EF)}\,\textrm{(int)}}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=A\left(\frac{r}{r_{+}}\right)^{\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}\Bigg[1+\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)}{\Gamma(-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s)\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\left(\frac{r_{+}}{r}\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}\left(2i\widetilde{P}_{+}\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}\Bigg]~.

(192)

Here, we have expressed
y\approx(r/r_{+})
, leading to the standard behaviour of the radial function, with one part growing as
r^{\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}
, and another part is decaying as
r^{-1-\hat{\ell}_{e}-s}
. Since
\hat{\ell}_{e}
is a complex quantity, the analytical continuation is automatically satisfied, and hence the above neatly separates the tidal part from the response.

V.2
Far-zone Solution and matching at intermediate zone

The far-zone solution, on the other hand, is identical for non-extremal and extremal BHs, as the asymptotic behaviour is identical with the rotation parameter
a
set to be equal to the mass
M
everywhere. Therefore, the far-zone solution is still given by
IV.2
, but in Boyer-Lindquist coordinates. The small
r
limit of the far-zone solution is then explicitly given by

\,{}_{s}R_{\ell m\,\textrm{(int)}}^{\textrm{(BL)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}r^{\tilde{\ell}-s}

\displaystyle-\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{-\bar{\mu}}r^{-1-\tilde{\ell}-s}\,,

(193)

where
\bar{\mu}=\widetilde{\ell}+|s|+(1/2)
and
\bar{\nu}=\widetilde{\ell}-|s|+(1/2)
, with
\widetilde{\ell}
being defined by
IV.2
. The above small
r
expansion of the far-zone solution can now be matched with
V.1.2
, yielding,

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm reg}}{\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{\bar{\nu}}=AM^{s-\tilde{\ell}}\,,

(194)

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm irreg}}{\pi}\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{-\bar{\mu}}M^{-1-\tilde{\ell}-s}=A\frac{\Gamma(1-s+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(-s-\hat{\ell}_{e})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad\times\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(-2i\widetilde{P}_{+}\right)^{2\hat{\ell}_{e}+1}\,.

(195)

We note that there are extra factors of
\exp(-i\omega r_{*})
and
\exp(im\tilde{r})
in the connection formulae, due to converting the radial function in Boyer-Lindquist to advanced null coordinates, where
(dr_{*}/dr)=\{(r^{2}+a^{2})/\Delta\}
and
(d\tilde{r}/dr)=(a/\Delta)
. However, in the far-zone, neither of these contributes. From the above connection formulae, we finally obtain,

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm irreg}}{C^{\infty}_{\rm reg}}=\frac{\Gamma(1-s+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(-s-\hat{\ell}_{e})}\frac{\Gamma(-2\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(-4iM\bar{\omega}\right)^{2\hat{\ell}_{e}+1}\frac{\pi}{\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{M\omega}{2}\right)^{2\tilde{\ell}+1}\,.

(196)

Using the identities involving Gamma functions, we can re-express the above expression as,

\displaystyle\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm irreg}}{C^{\infty}_{\rm reg}}=\frac{\Gamma(1-s+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\frac{\Gamma(1+s+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(-4iM\bar{\omega}\right)^{2\hat{\ell}_{e}+1}\frac{\pi(-1)^{s+\ell}}{2\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{M\omega}{2}\right)^{2\tilde{\ell}+1}\,.

(197)

This provides the matching between the two zones in BH perturbation theory. We now consider matching of the far-zone in BH perturbation theory with the EFT solution and hence obtain the gauge invariant dynamical response function.

V.3
EFT and asymptotic matching: The response functions

The EFT action and the subsequent computations performed in
II
can be directly applied here, or, in other words, the EFT of an extremal BH is identical to that in the context of non-extremal BHs. Hence all the results from the EFT calculations also hold in the extremal case. Below we discuss the case for scalar, as well as for gravitational perturbation.

V.3.1
Scalar Perturbation

For scalar perturbations, using the EFT results from
II
, the BH perturbation theory tidal response function turns out to be,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{M\omega}\right)^{2\ell+1}\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\left(-4iM\bar{\omega}\right)^{2\hat{\ell}_{e}+1}\frac{\pi(-1)^{\ell+1}}{2\Gamma(\ell+1/2)\Gamma(\ell+3/2)}\left(\frac{M\omega}{2}\right)^{2\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{\ell+1}\pi}{2^{2\ell+2}}\times\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{e})}{\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\right)\times\frac{\left(2im-4iM\omega\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}}{\Gamma(\ell+1/2)\Gamma(\ell+3/2)}\,.

(198)

Expanding
\hat{\ell}_{e}
in powers of
M\omega
, we can rewrite the above response function upto linear orders in
M\omega
as,

\displaystyle\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-im^{2\ell+1}\times\frac{\pi}{2}\times\left(\frac{(\ell!)^{2}}{(2\ell)!(2\ell+1)!}\right)\times\frac{\left\{1-(2\ell+1)\frac{2M\omega}{m}\right\}}{\Gamma(\ell+1/2)\Gamma(\ell+3/2)}

Further, since

, a comparison between

and

V.3.2
Gravitational Perturbation

The response function associated with the gravitational perturbation of an extremal Kerr BH, using the EFT matching used in
IV.4
, takes the following form,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=-(M\omega)^{-2\ell-1}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\frac{C^{\infty}_{\rm irreg}}{C^{\infty}_{\rm reg}}

\displaystyle=-(M\omega)^{-2\ell-1}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(3+\hat{\ell}_{e})\Gamma(\hat{\ell}_{e}-1)}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{e})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{e})}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(-4iM\bar{\omega}\right)^{2\hat{\ell}_{e}+1}\frac{\pi(-1)^{\ell}}{2\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(\bar{\nu}+1)}\left(\frac{M\omega}{2}\right)^{2\tilde{\ell}+1}\,.

(200)

This can be further simplified to yield,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi(-1)^{\ell+1}}{2^{2\ell+2}}\frac{\left(2im-4iM\omega\right)^{1+2\hat{\ell}_{e}}}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{(\ell+2)!(\ell-2)!}{(1+2\ell)!(2\ell)!}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left[1+\Delta\ell_{e}\left\{\Psi(3+\ell)+\Psi(\ell-1)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)\right\}\right]\left[1-\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\left\{\Psi(\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=-i\frac{\pi m^{2\ell+1}}{2}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{(\ell+2)!(\ell-2)!}{(1+2\ell)!(2\ell)!}\right)\times\frac{1}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}

(201)

\displaystyle\times\left[1-i\pi\Delta\ell_{e}\right]\left[1-\frac{2M\omega(1+2\ell)}{m}\right]\,.

(202)

Note that the above expression exactly matches with the result obtained in

, and hence for gravitational perturbation as well the response function for extremal BH coincides with the response function of non-extremal BH in the extremal limit.

The above response function can also be expressed as
{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}={}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext\,(stat)}_{\ell m}+M\omega{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext\,(dyn)}_{\ell m}
. Substituting
\omega=0

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext\,(stat)}_{\ell m}=-i\frac{\pi m^{2\ell+1}}{2}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{(\ell+2)!(\ell-2)!}{(1+2\ell)!(2\ell)!}\right)\times\frac{1}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}\,.

(203)

As evident, this response function is purely imaginary, thereby leading to vanishing stationary TLNs for extremal Kerr BH.
The dynamical response function, on the other hand, is given by,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext\,(dyn)}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle={}_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext\,(stat)}_{\ell m}\Big[-\frac{2(2\ell+1)}{m}-\left(\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\right)\left(-4i-\frac{8m}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\left\{\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{2m}{(2\ell+1)}\left(1+\frac{4}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\left\{\Psi(3+\ell)+\Psi(\ell-1)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-i\pi\right\}\Big]\,.

(204)

Since the static response function is purely imagniary, it follows that the dynamical response function has both real and imaginary parts, i.e., at
\mathcal{O}(M\omega)

\displaystyle{}_{-2}k_{\ell m}^{\rm ext}={}_{-2}k_{\ell m}^{\rm ext\,(stat)}+M\omega{}_{-2}k_{\ell m}^{\rm ext\,(dyn)}\,,

(205)

where,
{}_{-2}k^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}=(1/2)\textrm{Re}\,_{-2}\lambda^{\rm ext}_{\ell m}
. As already discussed, the static part of the response function is purely imaginary, and hence the static TLNs vanish identically. The dynamical part is given by,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}k_{\ell m}^{\rm ext\,(dyn)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi m^{2\ell+1}}{2}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{(\ell+2)!(\ell-2)!}{(1+2\ell)!(2\ell)!}\right)\times\frac{1}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}

\displaystyle\times\left[\left(\frac{8}{2\ell+1}\right)\left\{\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}+\frac{2m\pi}{(2\ell+1)}\right]\,.

(206)

The dissipation numbers, on the other hand, are defined as,
{}_{-2}\nu_{\ell m}^{\rm ext}=\textrm{Im}\,_{-2}\lambda_{\ell m}^{\rm ext}

\,{}_{-2}\nu_{\ell m}^{\rm ext\,(stat)}=-\frac{\pi m^{2\ell+1}}{2}\frac{(\ell+1)}{(\ell-1)}\left(\frac{3+8\ell+4\ell^{2}}{3-8\ell+4\ell^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{(\ell+2)!(\ell-2)!}{(1+2\ell)!(2\ell)!}\right)\times\frac{1}{\Gamma(\ell+5/2)\Gamma(\ell-1/2)}\,,

(207)

\,{}_{-2}\nu_{\ell m}^{\rm ext\,(dyn)}={}_{-2}\nu^{\rm ext\,(stat)}_{\ell m}\Big[-\frac{2(2\ell+1)}{m}+\left(\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\right)\frac{8m}{\ell(\ell+1)}\left\{\Psi(\ell+5/2)+\Psi(\ell-1/2)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{2ma}{M(2\ell+1)}\left(1+\frac{4}{\ell(\ell+1)}\right)\left\{\Psi(3+\ell)+\Psi(\ell-1)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)-2\Psi(2+2\ell)\right\}\Big]\,.

(208)

Note that the vanishing of static TLNs, and existence of non-zero dynamical TLNs hold for Kerr BHs in general, irrespective of the spin of the BH. The above analysis is for gravitational perturbation, but also holds for
s=0
. Furthermore, the dynamical TLNs vanish for either zero rotation (
a=0
) or for axisymmetric perturbations (
m=0
). Otherwise, the dynamical TLNs of an extremal/non-extremal Kerr BH under generic tidal perturbations are non-zero.

VI
Summary of key results and discussion

In summary, we have calculated the dynamical tidal response function characterizing the ratio of a BH’s induced multipoles to the strength of the tidal perturbation to linear order in frequency. Our method is based on defining the response via a worldline EFT and then linking it to BH perturbation theory for fields with generic spin
s
. The worldline EFT describes a (perturbed) compact object on large scales and is the essential foundation for further calculations of GWs from binary systems. The matching between EFT and strong-field BH perturbation is an important step towards computing GW signatures on a binary system due to tidal effects. In particular, this determines the tidal response function in terms of the ratio of wave amplitudes that are regular and irregular at the location of the BH, taken to be the origin, where the regular parts correspond to modes of the external tidal field, and the irregular parts are related to the induced multipole moments. While similar calculations had already been performed in previous literature, our work filled in gaps in the methodology of linking the worldline EFT with the BH perturbation theory for generic spins, both in term of angular momentum of the BH and the spin weights of the tidal perturbation fields, and providing explicit, ready-to-use results for the relevant response coefficients.

A complication in relating BH perturbation theory results to those of the EFT arose from the fact that the EFT formulation is based on symmetric-trace-free tensors, corresponding to a spherical-harmonic basis, while BH perturbation theory calculations are naturally decomposed into spheroidal harmonics. Connecting the different bases generally leads to mode mixing. In addition, the off-diagonal components of the EFT tidal response couples with these mode mixing coefficients in such a manner, that even after keeping only linear order terms in
M\omega
, the BH perturbation theory response function gets related to the diagonal and off-diagonal EFT response function through mixing coefficients.

As far as BH perturbation theory is considered, one can relate the regular and irregular coefficients of BH perturbation theory in the far-zone (
\omega r\gg 1
) to the regular and irregular coefficients in the EFT. Keeping in mind the contribution of both diagonal and off diagonal response coefficients of EFT to the BH perturbation theory response coefficient, one can invert the relation and hence each EFT response coefficient can be determined in terms of diagonal and off-diagonal BH response coefficients. Following this which we computed the BH response coefficients for scalar and gravitational perturbations, both for non-extremal and extremal Kerr BH.

Throughout the text, we have discussed several checks of our results in the limiting cases, such as against the zero-frequency computations by Refs.

VI.2
Outlook

Our work made several simplifying assumptions that we plan to relax in future work. On the side of BH perturbation calculations, we took the approach to solve the radial Teukolsky equation both in the near-horizon regime and the far-zone to determine the relation between integration constants in the far-zone to imprints of near-horizon properties on asymptotic fields. We informally verified that this agrees with the leading-order results using the Mano-Suzuki-Takasugi (MST) method, which provides much more refined solutions with wider validity. More detailed full calculations using the MST solutions are an important next step left to future work, and will also elucidate logarithmic frequency dependencies and tail effects, all of which could be neglected at the leading-order to which we were working.

On the side of the matching with the worldine EFT, we have fully established the leading-order link to the BH perturbation results based on wave amplitudes for scalar as well as gravitational perturbations. Our matching involves diagonal as well as non-diagonal and mixing coefficients from spherical to spheroidal transformation. This leaves next-to-leading order matching in
\mathcal{O}(M^{4}\omega^{4})
as an important aspect of future work.

Our aim in this work was not only to obtain explicit results for the tidal response of spinning black holes but also to flesh out the methodology and address issues such as the mode mixing. The methodology has broad applicability and several immediate extensions, for instance, to higher order in frequency and more generally, to describe full tidal resonance effects, and to go beyond the linear response. Applications to other kinds of compact objects, effects of dark matter, and scenarios beyond four-dimensional General Relativity are also left for future work.

Finally, a main objective of our work is to provide inputs for use in GW measurements of BH binary inspirals. The results developed here can already be used in existing parameterized waveform models, and will serve as a basis for future refinements and inclusion of more realistic tidal effects in full inspiral-merger-ringdown waveform models.

Appendix A
Computation of scalar tidal fields using worldline EFT

The part of scalar perturbations which are regular at origin are given by

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm reg}=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{\ell}}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\partial_{I_{\ell}}\psi_{\rm reg},\quad\psi_{\rm reg}=\frac{\sin(\omega r)}{\omega r},

(211)

where
C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}
are symmetric trace-free (STF) tensors. Now, we use the formula given in
creci2021tidal-42e
, which is

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}f(r)=x_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{\ell}f(r).

(212)

This yields,

\displaystyle\phi=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\omega^{\ell}}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}x_{I_{\ell}}\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{\ell}\psi_{\rm reg}.

(213)

Now, we use the taylor expansion
\sin(\rho)/\rho=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\rho^{2k}/(2k+1)!
, which leads to

\displaystyle\phi=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}x_{I_{\ell}}\frac{(-1)^{k}2^{\ell}k!}{(k-l)!(2k+1)!}(\omega r)^{2k-2\ell}

(214)

Now, the tidal fields are given by

\displaystyle E_{J_{\ell^{\prime}}}=\partial_{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle}\phi_{\rm reg}|_{\vec{x}=0}=e^{-i\omega t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{(-1)^{k}2^{\ell}k!}{(k-l)!(2k+1)!}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle}x_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}(\omega r)^{2k-2\ell}.

(215)

It is clear that only those terms which satisfy
\ell^{\prime}+\ell=2k
can contribute by counting powers of
x
and derivatives. Moreover, in the limit where we take it to origin, the result can only depend on invariant tensorial combinations or that of
\hat{n}^{i}=x^{i}/r
, but the latter would be ill-defined at origin, and thus eliminated (in favour of invariant tensorial combinations) upon a suitable averaging procedure on a sphere. Thus, only those terms with
\ell^{\prime}=\ell
can contribute, as otherwise, the quantity
\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}}\rangle}x_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}(\omega r)^{2k-2\ell}
, would need to depend on terms involving the unit vector
\hat{n}_{i}
. Now, if
\ell^{\prime}=\ell=k
, then we simply have

\displaystyle E_{J_{\ell}}=\partial_{\langle J_{\ell}\rangle}\phi_{\rm reg}|_{\vec{x}=0}=e^{-i\omega t}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}2^{\ell}\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\langle J_{\ell}\rangle}x_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}.

(216)

We can proceed as follows: In
216
, we use the formula

\displaystyle\partial_{\langle I_{\ell}\rangle}=\partial_{I_{\ell}}-\partial^{2}\partial_{(I_{\ell-2}}\delta_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell})}+\dots,

(217)

where
(...)
represents symmetrization. Now, note that these derivatives act upon
x_{I_{\ell}}
(where we drop the
\langle...\rangle
because it is contracted with an STF tensor
C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}
). Thus, when any term with fewer than
\ell
derivatives acts on
x_{I_{\ell}}
, it will leave behind some powers of
x
and thus such terms vanish at the origin. Thus, we can simply write

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{J_{\ell}}&=\partial_{\langle J_{\ell}\rangle}\phi_{\rm reg}|_{\vec{x}=0}=e^{-i\omega t}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{2^{\ell}(-1)^{\ell}\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\langle J_{\ell}\rangle}x_{I_{\ell}}=e^{-i\omega t}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{2^{\ell}(-1)^{\ell}\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{J_{\ell}}x_{I_{\ell}}\\
&=e^{-i\omega t}\omega^{\ell}C^{I_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{2^{\ell}(-1)^{\ell}\ell!\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}\delta_{J_{\ell},(I_{\ell})}=e^{-i\omega t}\omega^{\ell}C^{J_{\ell}}_{\rm reg}\frac{2^{\ell}(-1)^{\ell}\ell!\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!}.\end{split}

(218)

This concludes the computation. We now show the analogous computation for gravitational case.

(220)

(...)

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{K_{\ell-1}(i}\hat{\partial}_{j)}\hat{\partial}_{\langle K_{\ell-1}\rangle}\psi_{\rm reg}&=C_{K_{\ell-1}(i}\hat{\partial}_{j)}\hat{x}_{\langle K_{\ell-1}\rangle}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell-1}\psi_{\rm reg}\\

(221)

Similarly, the last (fourth) term can be simplified as

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{\partial}_{i}\hat{\partial}_{j}\hat{\partial}_{\langle K_{\ell}\rangle}\psi_{\rm reg}&=C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{\partial}_{i}\hat{\partial}_{j}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell}\psi_{\rm reg}\\
&=2\ell C_{K_{\ell-1}(i}\hat{x}_{j)}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-1}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell+1}\psi_{\rm reg}+(\ell)(\ell-1)C_{K_{\ell-2}ij}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-2}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell}\psi_{\rm reg}\\
&+\delta_{ij}C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell+1}\psi_{\rm reg}+C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell+2}\psi_{\rm reg}.\end{split}

(222)

We can now rewrite the last line of
220
as

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{K_{\ell}}\Pi_{k_{\ell-1}k_{\ell},ij}\hat{\partial}_{K_{\ell-2}}&=C_{K_{\ell-2}ij}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-2}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell-2}\left[1+2(l-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)\right]\\
&+2C_{K_{\ell-1}(i}\hat{x}_{j)}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-1}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell}\left[1+\frac{\ell}{2}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)\right]-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell}\left[1-\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{2}C_{K_{\ell}}\hat{x}_{i}\hat{x}_{j}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell+2}\,.\end{split}

(223)

Now, we want to argue that the tidal fields only receive contributions from the first term. The definition of electric tidal field is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}E_{I_{\ell}}=\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\partial_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}R_{i_{\ell-1}|0|i_{\ell}\rangle|0}=-\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\partial_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}h_{i_{\ell-1}i_{\ell}\rangle}.\end{split}

(224)

Now, consider how the derivatives will act on the expression in
223
. Just as in the scalar case, non-zero contributions in the limit
\vec{x}\rightarrow 0
can only arise if the number of derivatives (
\ell-2
) match the number of position vectors
\hat{x}_{i}
. However, when the position vectors contain
\hat{x}_{i}
or
\hat{x}_{j}
, then the derivative acting on them will lead to delta functions of the derivative-index with
i
or
j
, which will be eliminated when we symmetrize and remove traces. Similarly, the term with
\delta_{ij}
can also be dropped as it is eliminated upon trace-removal. The magnetic part of the metric perturbations will not contribute due to parity considerations. Thus, only the first term in
223
will contribute and we can write

\displaystyle E_{I_{\ell_{0}}}=-\frac{\omega^{\ell_{0}}}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}C_{K_{\ell-2}\langle i_{\ell_{0}-1}i_{\ell_{0}}}\hat{\partial}_{I_{\ell_{0}-2}\rangle}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-2}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell-2}\left[1+2(l-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)\right]\psi_{\rm reg}.

(225)

We can evaluate this similar to the scalar case. Once again, let us first consider evaluating the above expression without symmetrization and trace-removal, and take it at the final stage. We have

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\frac{\omega^{\ell_{0}}}{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}C_{K_{\ell_{0}-2}i_{\ell_{0}-1}i_{\ell_{0}}}\hat{\partial}_{I_{\ell_{0}-2}}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell-2}}\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)^{\ell-2}\left[1+2(l-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell-1)\left(\frac{\hat{\partial}_{r}}{\hat{r}}\right)\right]\psi_{\rm reg}.\end{split}

(226)

Now, as reasoned before in the scalar case, only terms where
\ell_{0}=\ell
can contribute at the origin, as any other terms will produce terms that will be eliminated upon symmetrization and trace-removal later, and thus we can drop the sum. Further more, using the Taylor expansion of
\sin(x)/x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\rho^{2k}/(2k+1)!
as before, we have

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{\ell_{0}}}{2}\lim_{\vec{x}\rightarrow 0}C_{K_{\ell_{0}-2}i_{\ell_{0}-1}i_{\ell_{0}}}(\hat{\partial}_{I_{\ell_{0}-2}}\hat{x}_{K_{\ell_{0}-2}})\sum_{k=\ell_{0}-2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}k!}{(2k+1)!}\hat{r}^{2k-2\ell_{0}}\left(\frac{2^{\ell_{0}-2}}{(k-\ell_{0}+2)!}\hat{r}^{4}+\frac{(\ell_{0}-1)2^{\ell_{0}}}{(k-\ell_{0}+1)!}\hat{r}^{2}+\frac{\ell_{0}(\ell_{0}-1)2^{\ell_{0}-1}}{(k-\ell_{0})!}\right).

(227)

Now, again, the relevant terms at origin are obtained when
k=\ell_{0}-2
in first term,
k=\ell_{0}-1
in second, and
k=\ell_{0}
in the last term, yielding

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega^{\ell_{0}}}{2}C_{K_{\ell_{0}-2}i_{\ell_{0}-1}i_{\ell_{0}}}(\hat{\partial}_{I_{\ell_{0}-2}}\hat{x}_{K_{{\ell_{0}}-2}})(-1)^{\ell_{0}}2^{-\ell_{0}-2}\frac{(1+\ell_{0})(2+{\ell_{0}})\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell_{0})}.

(228)

Finally, evaluating the derivatives (and removing traces and symmetrization which is trivial) we get

\displaystyle E_{I_{\ell_{0}}}=(-1)^{\ell_{0}+1}\omega^{\ell_{0}}C_{I_{\ell_{0}}}2^{-\ell_{0}-3}\frac{(1+\ell_{0})(2+\ell_{0})\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(\frac{3}{2}+\ell_{0})}.

(229)

The magnetic field can be evaluated similarly.

(232)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin(\pi\hat{\ell}+2i\pi P_{+})}{2\pi\cos(\pi\hat{\ell})}\times\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}+2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell})^{2}}{\Gamma\left(2\hat{\ell}+1\right)\Gamma\left(2\hat{\ell}+2\right)}~.

Dynamical response function: Generic Spin

In this appendix, we will present all the results for the simplification of the dynamical response function of a Kerr BH under generic spin perturbation. The starting point is
IV.5
in the main text, and here we will simplify parts of that result. First of all, the collection of the Gamma functions yield,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-2iP_{+})\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}-s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+s)\Gamma(1+\hat{\ell}_{s}+2iP_{+})}{\Gamma(2+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(1+2\hat{\ell}_{s})\Gamma(\bar{\mu})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu})}=\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\Gamma(1+\ell-s)\Gamma(1+\ell+s)\Gamma(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)\Gamma(\bar{\mu}_{e})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Big[1+\Delta\ell\left\{\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+})+\Psi(1+\ell-s)+\Psi(1+\ell+s)+\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+2iP_{+}^{\omega}\left\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+})\right\}-\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\left\{\Psi(\bar{\mu}_{e})+\Psi(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})\right\}\Big]\,,

(247)

where,

P^{a}_{+}=-am/(r_{+}-r_{-}),\qquad P_{+}^{\omega}=2Mr_{+}\omega/(r_{+}-r_{-}),

(248)

and
\Delta\ell_{s}
has the following expression:

\displaystyle\Delta\ell_{s}=\omega\left[\frac{4isr_{+}}{2\ell+1}-\frac{2am}{2\ell+1}-\frac{2ams^{2}}{\ell(\ell+1)(2\ell+1)}\right]\,.

(249)

The leading order contribution from the Gamma function turns out to be,

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(1+\ell-2iP^{a}_{+})\Gamma(1+\ell-s)\Gamma(1+\ell+s)\Gamma(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})}{\Gamma(2+2\ell)\Gamma(1+2\ell)\Gamma(\bar{\mu}_{e})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})}=\frac{2\pi P_{+}^{a}}{\sinh(2\pi P_{+}^{a})}\frac{(\ell-s)!(\ell+s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\{n^{2}+4(P_{+}^{a})^{2}\}}{\Gamma(\bar{\mu}_{e})\Gamma(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})}\,.

(250)

Similarly, the combination of the sinusoidal functions appearing in the response function, given by
IV.5
, can be re-expressed as,

\displaystyle\frac{\sin[\pi\hat{\ell}_{s}+2i\pi P_{+}]}{2\cos(\pi\hat{\ell}_{s})}=\frac{i}{2}\sinh(2\pi P_{+}^{a})+\left(\frac{\pi\Delta\ell+2i\pi P_{+}^{\omega}}{2}\right)\cosh(2\pi P_{+}^{a})\,.

(251)

Combining all of these, the dynamical response function of a Kerr BH under generic spin perturbation reduces to,

\displaystyle{}_{-2}\widetilde{\lambda}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell-s)!(\ell+s)!}{(2\ell+1)!(2\ell)!}\prod_{n=1}^{\ell}\{n^{2}+4(P_{+}^{a})^{2}\}\left[iP_{+}^{a}+\left(\pi\Delta\ell+2i\pi P_{+}^{\omega}\right)P_{+}^{a}\coth(2\pi P_{+}^{a})\right]

\displaystyle\times\Big[1+\Delta\ell\left\{\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+})+\Psi(1+\ell-s)+\Psi(1+\ell+s)+\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-2\Psi(2+2\ell)-2\Psi(1+2\ell)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+2iP_{+}^{\omega}\left\{\Psi(1+\ell+2iP^{a}_{+})-\Psi(1+\ell-2iP_{+})\right\}-\epsilon_{\ell}M\omega\left\{\Psi(\bar{\mu}_{e})+\Psi(1+\bar{\nu}_{e})\right\}\Big]\,.

(252)

This is the result we have used in the main text.

Appendix F
Normalization relevant for spheroidal harmonic decomposition

Here, we show how to derive
65
, restated below.

\begin{split}&\int d\Omega N_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}m_{i_{\ell-1}}m_{i_{\ell}\rangle}N^{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}-2}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell^{\prime}-1}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell}^{\prime}\rangle}\\

where

\begin{split}N^{i}N_{i}=1,~N^{i}m_{i}=0,~m^{i}m_{i}=0,~m^{i}\bar{m}_{i}=1.\end{split}

\begin{split}&\int d\Omega N_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}m_{i_{\ell-1}}m_{i_{\ell}\rangle}N^{\langle J_{\ell^{\prime}-2}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell^{\prime}-1}}\bar{m}^{j_{\ell}^{\prime}\rangle}=\alpha\delta^{\ell^{\prime}}_{\ell}\delta^{J_{\ell}}_{I_{\ell}}.\end{split}

I_{\ell}
and
J_{\ell}^{\prime}
separately. It must comprise of invariant tensors
\delta_{ij}
and
\epsilon^{ijk}
with coefficients determined by the dot products between the various vectors. These two statements, along with the orthogonality of
N^{i}
and

are sufficient to estalish this result. Now, let us contract both sides for

\begin{split}\int d\Omega N_{\langle I_{\ell-2}}m_{i_{\ell-1}}m_{i_{\ell}\rangle}N^{\langle I_{\ell-2}}\bar{m}^{i_{\ell-1}}\bar{m}^{i_{\ell}\rangle}&=\alpha\delta^{I_{\ell}}_{I_{\ell}}=\alpha(2\ell+1),\\

(256)

where in the third line we used the fact that

254
to replace the STF brackets with symmetrization. In the last line, we once again used the orthogonality of
\vec{N}

\vec{m}
, along with the last relation in
254

\int d\Omega N_{I_{\ell}}N^{I_{\ell}}=\frac{4\pi\ell!}{(2\ell+1)!!},

(257)

to further evaluate

\begin{split}\frac{(\ell-2)!2!}{\ell!}\int d\Omega N_{I_{\ell-2}}N^{I_{\ell-2}}&=\alpha(2\ell+1),\\

(258)
