Executive Summary
This report summarizes the framework developed through the Interregional Transmission
Operational Coordination project to study market-to-market congestion management across
day-ahead and real-time markets. Although U.S. nodal wholesale electricity markets have
delivered significant regional benefits, coordination across regions remains limited and uneven
across operational stages.

The project advances both methodology and software to enable rigorous and systematic
comparisons of alternative coordination designs:

 • Sienna Decomposition, an extension to the open-source Sienna platform for large scale
 grid scheduling simulations using production cost modeling (National Laboratory of the
 Rockies n.d.), enables users to define areas, coordinated subsystems, and synchronous
 regions; configure coordination structures by market stage; and flexibly enforce
 monitored transmission constraints, including energy flows and post-reserve-deployment
 flows under defined generation outage contingencies.

 • A multistage evaluation framework links day-ahead and real-time market outcomes
 and includes an emulation stage to systematically calculate pre- and post-contingency
 transmission flows. This framework quantifies transmission limit violations and
 aggregates production and violation costs to provide consistent performance metrics.

 • A loop flow representation and data passing scheme that captures key interactions
 across regions during market-clearing processes.

Beyond baseline coordination, the study incorporates two operational features that affect
reliability and efficiency:

 • Intra- and interregional reserve deliverability constraints, which ensure procured
 reserves can be deployed without violating monitored transmission limits following
 generation contingencies. The framework allows contingency events to be modeled by
 area and by market stage.

 • High-voltage direct current (HVDC) optimization for alternating current
 transmission congestion management, enabling HVDC schedules—either within or
 across areas under fixed net interchange—to relieve alternating current transmission
 constraints.

For real-time distributed coordination, three algorithms are designed and implemented:

 • Method 1 (single shadow price exchange): Uses the most recent state estimator flows
 and prior-period shadow prices from both regions to determine relief or relaxation targets
 within each market-clearing optimization. This approach minimizes information
 exchange and implementation changes.

 v

 • Method 2 (relief cost curve exchange): Extends Method 1 by exchanging piecewise-
 linear relief cost curves that better capture marginal congestion relief costs across
 multiple flow levels. This approach improves convergence and scalability to multiple
 lines and more than two regions.

 • Method 3 (alternating direction method of multipliers): Provides convergence
 through updating dual variables but can exhibit larger interim flow violations. It is
 included primarily for comparison to highlight operational implications.

Case studies on a three-area IEEE Reliability Test System (RTS) modified by the Grid
Modernization Lab Consortium and a large-scale Eastern Interconnection model demonstrate the
framework can systematically evaluate different market coordination structures. The analysis
emphasizes methodological development rather than monetized benefit estimates;
comprehensive benefit quantification will require expanded datasets and additional scenarios.

The study demonstrates that the Sienna Decomposition framework can be used to evaluate
operational strategies for market coordination across different stages, assess new coordination
methods, and analyze future intra- and interregional HVDC integration, as well as reserve
product and deliverability design.

Future work will focus on:

 • Extending datasets and contingency definitions to incorporate forecast errors and
 uncertainties between day-ahead and real-time operations, including intra-hour
 variability

 • Exploring interchange optimization and reserve sharing across regions

 • Advancing interregional HVDC optimization for both interchange scheduling and
 congestion management.

1 Multiregional Market-to-Market Coordination
  Overview
1.1 Market-to-Market Coordination on System-Wide Constraints
In North America, wholesale electricity markets have delivered significant benefits to their
regions. Midcontinent Independent System Operator (MISO) and PJM—the two largest
independent system operators (ISOs)—have reported annual regional benefits of approximately
$4 billion to $5 billion from integrated planning and market operations (MISO n.d.; PJM
n.d.).The nodal market design explicitly accounts for generation and transmission physical
constraints through security-constrained unit commitment (SCUC) and security-constrained
economic dispatch (SCED) market-clearing processes.

Within these market-clearing formulations, four categories of system-wide constraints are
typically enforced: (1) power balance constraints, (2) reserve requirement constraints, (3)
transmission flow constraints under energy dispatch, and (4) transmission flow constraints
accounting for post-contingency events and reserve deployment. Incorporating these constraints
into the market-clearing co-optimization enables regional transmission organizations (RTOs) to
achieve welfare maximization, price transparency, and the efficient and reliable utilization of
transmission capacity.

However, significant opportunities remain to improve coordination across seams between
different operational entities (Simeone and Rose 2024). In control room operations, power
balance and reserve procurement fall under the balancing authority function, whereas
transmission congestion management is handled through the reliability coordination function.
These functions are governed by separate coordination agreements involving different entities.
For example:

 • New York Independent System Operator (NYISO) and Independent System Operator–
 New England (ISO-NE) have implemented coordinated transaction scheduling for
 interchange to support power balance (ISO-NE and NYISO 2011).
 • MISO and PJM have implemented market-to-market joint operating agreements (M2M-
 JOAs) for coordinated congestion management (PJM 2008; “Joint Operating Agreement”
 2008).
 • In many cases, North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) transmission
 loading relief (TLR) procedures are used to manage congestion through transaction
 curtailments (NERC n.d.).
Negotiating and implementing coordination agreements typically require substantial effort. It is
therefore important to evaluate the benefits of coordination across different constraint categories
and to study methods for achieving convergence under various coordination mechanisms.

The examples of seams coordination agreements in the Eastern Interconnection are summarized
in (Staff Whitepaper 2025).

1.2 Coordination Across Multiple Operational Stages
Market operations are conducted through a multistage process spanning multiday, day-ahead,
intraday, and real-time dispatch. In North America, coordination during the operational planning
stages—from multiday through intraday—remains limited; coordinated transaction scheduling
andM2M-JOA are implemented primarily in real time.

In the United States, nodal markets have demonstrated high efficiency in power balance,
congestion management, and reserve procurement. Among U.S. ISOs, MISO has the largest
installed generation capacity, totaling 203 GW (MISO 2025).

In principle, forming a fully coupled nodal market across the continental United States could
achieve a globally optimal solution. However, doing so would require addressing substantial
computational challenges and overcoming institutional and business-structure barriers. Prior to
such an evolution, it is essential to develop a systematic framework to evaluate coordination
methods across different operational stages. Without effective coordination across these stages,
the industry may be unable to realize the full benefits identified in the planning studies such as
the National Transmission Planning Study (U.S. Department of Energy n.d.).

1.3 Study Framework
For four categories of system-wide constraints, there are 12 possible coordination configurations
(Table 1).

Coordination may vary across operating stages—for example, there is, in general, limited
coordination (C0) in the day-ahead stage, and in some ISO seams M2M-JOA is used for energy
flow coordination (C2). This report focuses on congestion-management configurations C2 and
C8.

Real-time coordination, such as M2M-JOA, is implemented in a distributed fashion along seams
among several Eastern ISOs. Paired RTOs exchange shadow prices and adjust relief requests to
achieve flow and shadow price convergence. As demonstrated by Potomac Economics (2020),
this method may exhibit convergence issues or converge to suboptimal solutions.

Through the Interregional Transmission Operational Coordination (IRTOC) project, the team at
the National Laboratory of the Rockies has developed methodologies and software tools to
systematically study multistage market-to-market (M2M) coordination. Under the IRTOC
project, the team developed Sienna Decomposition under the open-source Sienna modeling
framework (National Laboratory of the Rockies n.d.) to evaluate the impacts of different
coordination configurations at different operational stages. Its functions include:

 • Areas, subsystems, and islands to model balancing authorities, coordinated areas, and the
 synchronized grid, with flexibility to define coordination entities and coordinated
 transmission constraints
 • Benchmark mathematical optimization formulations for each coordination configuration
 • Multistage simulation with flexibility to study different configurations at different stages
 • Reserve deliverability constraints for predefined generation contingency events and
 response areas in the market-clearing formulation

 • High-voltage direct current (HVDC) optimization and its impact on alternating current
 (AC) transmission-constraint coordination
 • An emulator stage that mimics state estimation and evaluates pre- and post-contingency
 flows under generation outage events with reserve deployment
 • Consistent economic and reliability metrics; production costs from multiarea simulations
 and flow violation costs from the emulator enable consistent evaluation of different
 coordination structures

 In this report, the term area is used interchangeably with region, representing the entity
 responsible for balancing authority functions.

2 Multistage Market Coordination Study Framework
The market-clearing processes differ across the multiday, day-ahead, intraday, and real-time time
frames.

 • In the multiday and day-ahead markets, ISOs solve a single optimization problem
 spanning multiple intervals. In the multiday time frame, operators typically commit only
 units with startup and notification times longer than 1 day (i.e., long-lead units), deferring
 commitment decisions for shorter-lead units to the day-ahead market or intraday
 commitment. In the day-ahead market, clearing results for all intervals are determined
 and published simultaneously.
 • In the intraday time frame, unit commitment is performed on a rolling basis (e.g., every
 15 minutes) by solving a multi-interval SCUC problem. Operators make ongoing
 commitment decisions based on the updated SCUC results.
 • In real time, some ISOs solve a single-interval real-time security-constrained economic
 dispatch (RT-SCED), whereas others solve a multi-interval SCED, both on a rolling basis
 (e.g., every 5 minutes). In either case, only the dispatch results for the first interval are
 typically published.
Given the complexity associated with intraday commitment and multi-interval RT-SCED, this
report focuses on the day-ahead market and single-interval RT-SCED in the context of
interregional transmission operational coordination.

2.1 Day-Ahead Forward Process
In the day-ahead time frame, each RTO solves a multi-interval SCUC, some followed by more
granular SCED, and the process may take several hours (Chen et al. 2018). Two potential
approaches—centralized and distributed—can be used to coordinate AC transmission congestion
management across regions. In this report, we use a system with three regions designated here as
𝑎𝑎, 𝑏𝑏, and 𝑐𝑐 to illustrate the coordination models.

Even though the centralized model can provide the best solution on managing congestion on
coordinated regions (𝑎𝑎 and 𝑏𝑏 in this example), it has two main challenges:

 1. It will require overcoming business-structure barriers to allow the coordinated regions to
 share market inputs and models for centralized coordination.
 2. It may be computationally expensive to optimize two or more markets together.
The European Union has established processes for day-ahead and intraday market coupling.
These processes rely on simplified, zonal aggregated models (Estermann et al., 2025), which
may require less computational time but are generally less efficient in managing congestion than
nodal approaches.

2.1.2 Distributed Day-Ahead Coordination
For distributed day-ahead coordination, each RTO clears its own day-ahead market. Then the
coordination partners exchange clearing results, such as the binding transmission constraints and
shadow prices. The coordination partners then revise their SCUC/SCED model to incorporate the
information from the partners and re-solve their own SCUC/SCED. This distributed solution
approach can be challenging and unrealistic for the following reasons:

 • The day-ahead market-clearing problem is large and subject to strict time constraints,
 making iterative approaches computationally challenging.
 • The SCUC problem is nonconvex and may not warm-start effectively. As a result,
 solving subsequent iterations may not be significantly faster than the initial SCUC,
 potentially increasing the total solution time.
 • In the day-ahead time frame, the number of binding transmission constraints can be large,
 making convergence difficult to achieve.
For these reasons, we do not study distributed day-ahead coordination approach under this
project.

2.2 Real-Time Market
Even if RTOs can commit units to adequately manage congestion in day-ahead market, real-time
operations may encounter unexpected transmission violations because of uncertainty. However,
effective coordination in forward operational processes, such as the day-ahead market, can
reduce the likelihood and severity of real-time flow violations. Real-time coordination can
similarly be implemented in either a centralized or a distributed manner. Although it is unlikely
that multiple markets would be jointly cleared for congestion management in real time, a
centralized real-time model is developed in this study to serve as a benchmark for evaluating the
performance of distributed coordination algorithms.

Real-time markets are solved on a rolling basis. Generation and load from all regions inject into
and withdraw from the grid, and their actual impacts on transmission flows are observed in state
estimation results in control rooms. In North America, grid operators generally have good
visibility of neighboring transmission systems. In this study, we assume the coordinating regions
can observe similar state estimation flows on the M2M coordinated transmission constraints.

3 Intra- and Interregional Reserve Deliverability
Reserves are defined for two main purposes: (1) managing uncertainties under normal operating
conditions, such as regulation and flexible ramping products and (2) responding to post-
contingency events, such as contingency reserves.

The purpose of reserve deliverability constraints is to ensure transmission limits are not violated
after reserve deployment. Reserves can be deployed in many ways, and it is generally impractical
to formulate all possible deployment scenarios. For contingency reserves, the most severe
events—such as the loss of the largest generating unit—are typically considered (Chen et al.
2014).

In this project, we focus on contingency reserves to illustrate constraints that ensure reserve
deliverability across both intraregional and interregional transmission constraints. We assume
reserve sharing is not allowed; therefore, a generation contingency in one region must be covered
by contingency reserves within the same region.

In the market-clearing model, each region monitors only its own contingency events and the
associated reserve deployment impacts on transmission constraints monitored by that region.
Post-reserve-deployment transmission flow constraints and post-reserve-power-balance
constraints are enforced similar to Wang and Chen (2021).

4 Intraregional and Interregional HVDC Optimization
  for Alternating Current Congestion Management
In this report, we assume lossless HVDC. Intraregional lossless HVDC does not affect the
regional power balance. The AC line flow sensitivities at the “from” and “to” ends of an HVDC
line can differ; therefore, HVDC can be scheduled to relieve AC transmission congestion,
including M2M AC line constraints. Although interregional HVDC may have its “from” and “to”
buses located in different regions, its schedule must maintain consistent megawatt injection and
withdrawal at the two ends. As a result, it needs to be scheduled by a single entity. In this study,
we assume the receiving region is responsible for optimizing HVDC.

The focus of this work is on congestion management for M2M AC transmission constraints. We
assume the net interchange, which equals the sum of HVDC and AC tie-line flows, is fixed.
HVDC can be scheduled by the scheduling entity to relieve M2M AC congestion as well as other
internal AC transmission congestion.

The model can be extended in the future to represent HVDC as a dynamic schedule, where the
net interchange can be adjusted based on the HVDC schedule. This extension would allow
HVDC to be scheduled to simultaneously optimize the interchange and AC transmission flows.

5 Sienna Decomposition Development
Through the IRTOC project, the team expanded the National Laboratory of the Rockies’ open-
source Sienna software (National Laboratory of the Rockies n.d.) by introducing a new Sienna
Decomposition functionality.

5.1 Operational Structure
To represent operational entities and their collective impacts on the power system, the model
incorporates combinations of balancing areas, synchronous regions, and coordinated subsystems
as illustrated in Figure 2.

Area represents a balancing authority responsible for maintaining scheduled interchange and
reserve procurement. In this report, the term area is used interchangeably with region.

In Sienna, a subsystem consists of a subset of the full system components. For this project,
subsystems are used to represent coordinated operational entities— several areas that coordinate
for interchange, transmission congestion management, and/or ancillary services. All areas within
a subsystem have some level of visibility in each other’s operations.

The IRTOC project focuses on the coordination of transmission congestion management.
Therefore, interchange schedules and reserve requirements are maintained within each area.
However, multiple areas within a subsystem can jointly optimize their operations to manage
M2M transmission constraints. Each subsystem defines a set of coordinated transmission
constraints, which may or may not coincide with physical tie-lines.

Areas may span multiple asynchronous regions that are connected via HVDC links. Properly
representing synchronous regions is important to ensure power transfer distribution factors
(PTDFs) are calculated properly. PTDFs must be computed separately within each synchronous
region to ensure correct line flow calculation in the optimization.

This structure increases the complexity of model construction and simulation. With the new
framework, Sienna supports different operational configurations across multiple simulation
stages. The emulator can also mimic state estimation and generation contingency analysis to
simulate the resulting impacts on the interconnected power system.

The Sienna Decomposition multistage simulation framework allows users to configure
AreaPTDF or SplitAreaPTDF models, define subsystems, and specify monitored transmission
constraints and generation outages at different stages.

This subsection describes how data is passed between stages and how this affects area
interchanges and loop flows.

• Area interchanges
 The day-ahead and real-time models in Section 1 assume fixed interchange schedules. In
 practice, interchanges are largely driven by physical transactions scheduled by scheduling
 entities.
 In this study, a network model without any enforced transmission constraints (i.e. a
 copperplate model) is used in Stage 1 to determine economically efficient interchanges.
 Interchange flow limits are set sufficiently wide, representing unconstrained economic
 interchange across all areas.
 The resulting interchange schedules from Stage 1 are passed forward and fixed in
 subsequent stages.
• External injections and withdrawals

6 Real-Time Coordination Algorithm Development
Several real-time distributed coordination algorithms are developed and implemented in Sienna
Decomposition and summarized in Chen et al. (2025). Chen et al. identifies shortcomings in
existing M2M approaches, where coordinated regions exchange shadow prices and relief
requests to optimize congestion relief allocations across interconnected regions. Two methods
are proposed to enhance flow and price convergence.

The first method proposes both regions use state estimator flows directly to determine relief
requirements, eliminating delays and potential oscillations caused by using market flows
calculated from the prior period under the existing M2M approach.

The second method involves exchanging transmission relief cost curves, enabling each region to
integrate relief cost curves from other regions into its RT-SCED. This method can effectively
extend the coordination to multiple transmission lines and across more than two regions.

The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) is also applied to the M2M
coordination problem and compared with the proposed methods.

Case studies on small- and large-scale systems demonstrate the effectiveness of these
approaches.

6.1 Method 1: Single Shadow Price Exchange
Under the currently implemented M2M coordination processes (Luo et al. 2015), each
transmission line has a monitoring RTO (MRTO) responsible for managing and controlling its
flow through market-clearing processes. Typically, transmission congestion is managed solely by
the MRTO. If the MRTO cannot relieve the constraint independently, it may request TLR under
NERC procedures (NERC n.d.). For RTOs with M2M operational agreements, the MRTO can
request relief from the non-monitoring RTO (NMRTO).

Under the current M2M implementation (Chen et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2021), the MRTO
calculates relief requests based on the solution from the previous period and exchanges shadow
prices with the NMRTO. However, delays in these calculations and the use of piecewise-linear
offer curves can lead to discontinuous dispatch adjustments, resulting in oscillations in shadow
prices and transmission flows (Chen et al. 2017; Baldick and Chatterjee 2014).

For large RTOs in the United States, coordinated transmission constraints are typically
observable in the state estimation of both RTOs. For simplicity, we assume both RTOs observe
the same state estimation flow for the coordinated transmission lines at time 𝑡𝑡 − 1. The Sienna
Emulator is used to calculate the equivalent state estimation flow based on generation dispatch
results and load levels from all regions.

In Method 1, the M2M process is improved by eliminating the need for the MRTO to calculate
and send relief requests based on previous solutions. Instead, the two RTOs use the latest
observed state estimation flows and the previous-period shadow prices from both regions to
determine the amount of relief or relaxation through SCED. Each RTO uses shadow prices as

 22

indicators to estimate the cost of providing or receiving congestion relief from the neighboring
region.

This method requires minimal information exchange and only minor updates to the SCED
formulation. However, it assumes shadow prices remain constant and do not change with
variations in flow contributions. As a result, it may require more iterations to converge and may
be more difficult to extend to multiple transmission constraints or coordination among more than
two regions.

6.2 Method 2: Relief Cost Curve Exchange Method
The relief cost curve method extends the single shadow price exchange approach by exchanging
a piecewise-linear cost curve that quantifies the marginal cost at each level of relief. This cost
curve better represents the cost variation across different relief levels, allowing each region to
optimize its relief actions based on more detailed cost information provided by neighboring
regions.

After solving SCED, each RTO generates relief cost curves by adjusting the right-hand side of
the coordinated transmission constraints (d2) and (d7) and re-solving SCED to obtain the
corresponding shadow prices (i.e., relief costs) for the adjusted flow contributions.

The generated relief cost curves are then exchanged and incorporated into the SCED run for the
next interval. Additional computation time is required to generate these curves. In the Eastern
Interconnection case study in Chen et al. (2025), generating the curves requires approximately
10% of the SCED dispatch solution time, and this overhead can be further reduced through
parallel computing.

The case study in Chen et al. (2025) demonstrates this method can coordinate multiple
transmission constraints with coordination across two or three regions. It also converges faster
than Method 1.

6.3 Method 3: Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers
In Ren et al. (2025), an ADMM-based coordination method is proposed to address the M2M
congestion management problem with convergence guarantees, in which relief requests are
obtained through iterative updates of dual prices. Because ADMM adjusts dual variables
iteratively to guide convergence, transmission flow violations can remain large before
convergence is achieved. To compare the operational implications of different approaches, the
ADMM-based method is also implemented and evaluated on a large-scale, real-world Eastern
Interconnection model.

By contrast, the two new proposed methods explicitly enforce flow limit constraints, enabling
faster reduction of flow violations during the coordination process. These methods exchange
either shadow prices or relief cost curves. Each RTO incorporates the received shadow prices or
relief cost curves directly into its market-clearing model to determine the economically efficient
relief contribution from the coordinating RTOs.

Operational implications are critical when developing M2M coordination methods. In practice,
when a transmission constraint violation exceeds a specified threshold (e.g., 125% of the

 23

emergency rating at MISO), it is classified as a violation of interconnection reliability operating
limits (Midcontinent Independent System Operator 2024). Failure to operate within
interconnection reliability operating limits—or to mitigate violations within the required time
frame—may constitute a violation of NERC reliability standards and may result in substantial
penalties (“Top–001-5–Transmission Operations” n.d.).

Case studies on a large-scale Eastern Interconnection system demonstrate the two proposed new
methods more effectively address operational flow violation risks while maintaining
coordination efficiency.

The 12 configurations are simulated on the EI model for the following dates: January 15, 2023,
July 15, 2023, and October 15, 2023. The simulation results are shown in Figure 5. For each day,
the graph presents the differences in production cost, violation cost, and total cost relative to the
corresponding values under Sim_1_RvD0_hff0.

Key takeaways from the EI simulation:

 • Sim_3, with day-ahead coordination, provides the largest reduction in violation cost and
 total cost, especially when the violation cost is high on January 15, 2023.
 • Sim_2, which models M2M constraints in the day-ahead market using approximated
 external loop flows, also helps reduce violation costs. The total cost reduction is smaller
 than that in Sim_3 but is significantly larger than the reduction achieved by coordinating
 only in real time when the violation cost is high on January 15, 2023.
 • Sim_4, with M2M coordination only in real time, may reduce violation and total costs;
 however, the reductions are smaller than those achieved in Sim_2 and Sim_3 in most
 cases.
 • Feeding forward HVDC schedules (hff1) and enforcing reserve deliverability (RvD1) in
 Sim_2 and Sim_3 may further reduce total costs.
 • Under Sim_1 and Sim_4, where M2M constraints are not considered in the day-ahead
 market, feeding forward HVDC schedules from the day-ahead to the real-time stage may

 35

 not be beneficial (hvff1 versus hvff0 on Sim_1 and Sim_4). Not feeding forward HVDC
 allows the monitoring region to optimize HVDC in real time and may help relieve M2M
 constraints.

There is no interregional HVDC between MISO and SPP in existing EI model. Therefore, the
impact of HVDC scheduling on interregional AC congestion is relatively small. However,
preliminary results suggest that, when AC transmission constraints are adequately represented in
the market-clearing process, HVDC can be dispatched to help alleviate AC congestion. The
study also demonstrates that the Sienna Decomposition framework can be used to evaluate
operational strategies for future intra- and interregional HVDC integration.

In summary, without accounting for uncertainties between day-ahead and real-time operations or
intra-hour variability, this study shows that day-ahead coordination may deliver significant
value. Real-time coordination alone may be insufficient; effective day ahead and real time
coordination—incorporating HVDC optimization and reserve deliverability modeling—is
essential for managing interregional transmission congestion.

7.2 Real-Time Distributed Algorithms
Real-time distributed algorithm case studies are presented in Chen et al. (2025).

First, a small system with three regions and nine generators is used to compare coordination
based on single shadow price exchange (Method 1), relief cost curve exchange (Method 2), and a
benchmark centralized congestion management approach. The comparison focuses on key
metrics such as transmission line flows and congestion shadow prices. The results show the cost
curve exchange method enables generator outputs to converge more rapidly toward the
benchmark solution compared to the single shadow price exchange method. This analysis
demonstrates the effectiveness of both methods for M2M congestion management, with the cost-
curve-based method exhibiting faster convergence.

The relief cost curve exchange method is further extended to scenarios involving three-region
coordination on a single transmission line and two-region coordination on two transmission
lines, both of which demonstrate fast convergence.

Second, a large-scale EI study is performed. In this case, Method 1 and Method 2 are compared
with an ADMM-based coordination approach (Method 3) for two-region coordination on a single
transmission line. The results show the monitored line flow under ADMM initially deviates
significantly from the optimal solution and converges only after multiple iterations. In contrast,
both the shadow price exchange and the relief cost curve exchange methods achieve flow
convergence within a single interval. In addition, the shadow prices under ADMM converge
more slowly than those obtained using the relief-cost-curve-based approach.

Finally, the effectiveness of the relief-cost-curve-based method is further evaluated on the EI
model for three-region coordination and for coordination across two monitored transmission
lines.

 37

In summary, Method 1 is a practical enhancement to existing real-time M2M coordination in
Eastern ISOs, requiring relatively modest software changes. Method 2 is more effective for
coordinating across multiple regions with more transmission constraints, but involves greater
implementation complexity. Both methods outperform ADMM in achieving flow convergence.

8 Conclusion and Future Work
The IRTOC project develops a modeling and evaluation framework to study IRTOC and assess
alternative M2M congestion management designs across day-ahead and real-time markets. The
framework extends the Sienna platform with Sienna Decomposition, a multistage evaluation
framework to enable flexible representation and systematic assessment of market coordination
design.

Additional modeling capabilities developed through this project include reserve deliverability
constraints and HVDC optimization for AC congestion management, and several real-time
distributed coordination algorithms.

Case studies on both the RTS-GMLC test system and a large-scale EI model demonstrate the
framework can systematically evaluate alternative coordination structures and analyze their
economic and operational impacts.

Without accounting for uncertainties between day-ahead and real-time operations or intra-hour
variability, this study shows that day-ahead coordination can deliver significant value, while real-
time coordination alone is insufficient. For real-time coordination, the framework evaluates
different distributed algorithms and demonstrates that the proposed methods are effective in
achieving flow and price convergence.

Future work will focus on expanding datasets and scenarios to better capture forecast uncertainty
and operational variability between day-ahead and real-time markets. Additional research will
explore interregional interchange optimization, reserve sharing mechanisms, and enhanced
interregional HVDC coordination strategies. Further development of scalable coordination
algorithms for large multiregion systems will also be pursued to support practical
implementation.
