Causes of death across the life course in the Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN): a systematic analysis for the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023

GBD 2023 ASEAN Causes of Death Collaborators

Abstract

SummaryBackgroundThe drivers of mortality and their associated risk factors evolve throughout the course of life. Understanding these transitions is essential for effective, equitable, and age-appropriate health interventions.MethodsAs part of the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2023, we examined life expectancy and the probability of death from birth to age 70 years (70q0) from 1990 to 2023 for each Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) member state. In addition, we assessed causes of death and risk factor by age and sex across different life stages. Introduction

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) region, established in 1967, constitutes 8·6% of the global population and is one of the fastest-growing economies in the world. However, the region is characterised by stark socioeconomic asymmetries. The Human Development Index ranged from 0·946 in Singapore to 0·606 in Cambodia in 2025, whereas the projected gross domestic product per capita in 2026 ranges from more than US$107 760 in Singapore to US$1520 in Myanmar. The socioeconomic diversity fundamentally defines the region's health landscape.

Since 1990, ASEAN has undergone an unevenly paced epidemiological transition, with persistent infectious and maternal and child health burdens in several states alongside rising non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in all., Disparities in health system capacity have yielded discrepancies in health outcomes., , Although health inequities related to specific diseases have been explored within the region,, , , no research has systematically examined comparative all-cause mortality profiles and assessed age-sex-specific patterns from birth to adulthood across the entire ASEAN.

A life-course approach addresses this gap by linking early-life exposures and structural determinants to later-life health outcomes and disparities. Earlier regional applications were confined to childhood and adolescence or individual countries, without adulthood or cross-country comparison. Because mortality drivers and their risk factors evolve across the life course,, we apply this framework using the latest findings from the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2023 to examine causes of death across all 11 ASEAN member states. This granular, stage-specific analysis identifies age-sex-specific diseases and risk factors driving mortality to inform targeted interventions, promote longevity, and improve health equity across the region.

Methods

Overview

Leveraging the best available data sources and a systematic analytical modelling approach, GBD 2023 produced geographically and temporally comparable estimates for various demographic and epidemiological metrics, including life expectancy and mortality for 292 causes of death, by age and sex, across 204 countries and territories, and from 1990 to 2023.,

In this study, we examined life expectancy and probability of death from birth to age 70 years (70q0) across ASEAN and its member states from 1990 to 2023, and evaluated mortality patterns specific to cause and risk factor by life stages, sex, and location in 2023. Although Timor-Leste acceded to ASEAN only in October, 2025, we included it to provide the most current evidence base for the bloc as presently constituted, supporting forward-looking regional health planning. We defined life stages as: infancy and childhood (birth to age 9 years); adolescence (ages 10–24 years), following the expanded definition proposed by Sawyer and colleagues, which more closely reflects the biological, neurocognitive, and social transitions of this life stage; and adulthood (age 25 years and older).

GBD cause and risk factor hierarchy

GBD classifies causes and risk factors into four hierarchical levels. Level 1 comprises three categories: communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases; NCDs; and injuries, partitioned into 22 Level 2 clusters and disaggregated further at Levels 3 and 4. Risk factors are similarly organised, from three Level 1 groups (environmental and occupational, behavioural, and metabolic) into 20 Level 2 clusters with further subdivisions., For the present analysis, we focused on causes of death at Level 3 and risk factors at Level 2.

Data sources and processing

Data sources are detailed in the ; mortality data were gathered from vital registrations, surveys, literature, and census data across ASEAN. Standard GBD processing corrections were applied, including age and sex splitting of deaths with insufficient information and redistribution of non-specific or implausible causes (garbage codes) to valid underlying causes. Sources were excluded if more than 50% of deaths were garbage codes or if completeness fell below 50%. Further details can be found elsewhere. Owing to gaps in primary data, estimates for certain causes and countries rely on statistical modelling (described in the Estimation methods section). The modelling process enables the production of internally consistent estimates, facilitating comparison across causes, age, sex, location, and time.

Risk factor exposure data were gathered from systematic reviews, household and health surveys, administrative records, and ground-sensing or remote-sensing data. Relative risks (RRs) were derived primarily from systematic reviews of studies reporting health outcomes associated with risk factor exposure.,

Estimation methods

Life expectancy

Life expectancy is the mean number of years for which an individual is expected to live at birth, assuming that age-specific mortality rates observed in a given year remain constant over the life course. Age-specific all-cause mortality rates were estimated using the OneMod framework, with country-specific all-cause mortality as the outcome and the Socio-demographic Index, HIV and COVID-19 mortality, and a summary measure of risk factor impact as covariates. These resulting rates were used to construct location-sex-specific life tables from which life expectancy was derived; further details are provided in the GBD 2023 Demography capstone.

Probability of death

The probability of death metric reflects the likelihood of premature death within a specific age interval. Using age-specific all-cause mortality estimates and the life tables derived, we computed all-cause 70q0 by sex for each ASEAN member state from 1990 to 2023, which serves as a complementary measure of premature mortality risk.

Cause-specific mortality

Cause-specific mortality was estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble model. Estimates were rescaled to the all-cause mortality envelope using the Cause of Death Correction procedure so that cause-specific estimates sum to all-cause totals; full details are provided in the GBD 2023 Cause of Death capstone.

Risk factor estimation

Risk-attributable burden was estimated using the comparative risk assessment framework, integrating population exposure, RR, the theoretical minimum risk exposure level, and underlying mortality burden. Exposures were modelled using techniques including spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and DisMod-MR. Most risk–outcome relationships were evaluated using the Burden of Proof framework. Population-attributable fractions were calculated for each risk–outcome pair and multiplied by the corresponding cause-specific mortality to yield risk-attributable mortality by age, sex, location, and year.

Presentation of estimates

Cause-specific mortality estimates are presented in counts and age-specific rates per 100 000 population. For changes over time, we present percentage changes over the 1990–2023 period, calculated as the change from 1990 to 2023 relative to the 1990 baseline. Reported estimates are the mean across a 250-draw distribution, with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) defined as the 2·5th and 97·5th percentiles across the draws.

Research and reporting practices

GBD 2023 complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting recommendations (). The statistical code used for GBD 2023 estimation is available upon request.

Role of the funding source

The funders of this study had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

Life expectancy and probability of death

In 2023, life expectancy in the ASEAN region was estimated at 70·1 years (95% UI 69·6–70·7) for males and 76·2 years (75·7–76·7) for females, a regional increase since 1990 of 12·2% for males and 10·3% for females. Singapore had the highest estimated life expectancy for both sexes (83·1 years [82·9–83·4] for males and 87·5 years [87·3–87·8] for females), whereas the lowest life expectancy was in Myanmar for males (61·6 years [60·1–63·0]) and Laos for females (67·2 years [66·0–68·4]). Since 1990, Laos saw the largest relative increase in life expectancy (33·3% for males and 27·5% for females), and the Philippines saw the smallest relative increase (5·0% for males and 4·3% for females; , ).

Across all ASEAN member states, the 70q0 from all causes combined was higher for males than for females (). In 2023, the regional 70q0 was estimated at 40·3% for males and 26·9% for females, and was lowest in Singapore (13·4% for males and 7·5% for females). Nine of 11 countries had male 70q0 exceeding 30%, with four surpassing 40% and the peak being in Myanmar (56·9%). Among females, four countries exceeded 30%, with the highest 70q0 observed in Laos (42·6%). Between 1990 and 2023, 70q0 decreased by 22·6% for males and 25·7% for females, most steeply in Singapore (–61·7% in males and –65·1% in females) and least in the Philippines (–7·6% in males and –8·4% in females; ).

Causes of death by life stages

Neonatal disorders were the leading cause of death among infants younger than 1 year, accounting for 42·9% (95% UI 36·7–49·1) of infant mortality in males (51 200 [42 700–60 300]) and 39·8% (34·1–45·8) in females (34 300 [28 900–42 200]; ; ). Congenital birth defects followed as the second leading cause and lower respiratory infections as the third. Among children aged 2–4 years and 5–9 years, divergence in the primary cause of death was observed between sexes (, ).

Underlying mortality rates differed substantially across ASEAN (). For instance, neonatal disorder mortality among male infants younger than 1 year ranged from 55·3 deaths per 100 000 population (95% UI 40·6–64·5) in Singapore to 2261·2 deaths per 100 000 population (1740·6–2892·4) in Laos (). Lower respiratory infection mortality among female children aged 2–4 years ranged from 0·7 per 100 000 population (0·3–1·0) in Singapore to 56·2 per 100 000 population (32·3–91·8) in Timor-Leste ().

Among males aged 10–14 years, road injuries were the leading cause of death, accounting for 14·9% (95% UI 8·5–19·9) of mortality burden and a total of 2260 (1270–3040) deaths, followed by drowning. Among females in this age group, drowning predominated, resulting in 970 (672–1280) deaths, narrowly exceeding road injuries (960 [518–1350] deaths; , ). At ages 15–19 years, injury-related deaths became more prominent for males; road injuries accounted for 25·0% (17·0–31·0) of mortality burden, corresponding to 8230 (5740–10 800) deaths, followed by conflict and terrorism, drowning, and self-harm. Among females in this age group, road injuries also emerged as the top cause of death, accounting for 14·4% (8·2–19·9) of total mortality burden (3040 [1630–4270] deaths), followed by maternal disorders and tuberculosis. These patterns largely persisted into the age group of 20–24 years (, ).

Some divergence was observed in the leading causes of death among adolescents across ASEAN. For example, drowning was the leading cause of death among females aged 10–14 years in most of the region but ranked 19th in Singapore, where self-harm was the leading cause instead. Diabetes was the second (females) and fourth (males) leading cause of death for those aged 20–24 years in Brunei (), yet diabetes did not rank within the top five in any other country (). Cause-specific mortality rates also differed by country. Road injury mortality among males aged 15–19 years ranged from 49·0 per 100 000 population (25·0–67·1) in Thailand to 3·0 per 100 000 population (2·3–3·7) in Singapore (). For maternal disorders, a leading cause of death among females aged 20–24 years, mortality rates ranged from 43·0 per 100 000 population (22·7–65·3) in Timor-Leste to 0·1 per 100 000 population (0·1–0·1) in Singapore ().

Road injuries remained the prevailing cause of death among young adult males aged 25–34 years (, ). Ischaemic heart disease rose to become the leading cause of death by ages 35–39 years before being replaced by stroke from age 50 years onward. Male cirrhosis and other chronic liver disease mortality ranked fourth at ages 25–29 years and peaked at ages 40–44 years (10·4% [8·4–12·9], 11 800 [8860–15 300] deaths; , ), remaining a top three cause during ages 30–59 years. Stroke was the leading cause of death through most of mid to late adulthood, peaking at ages 70–74 years (59 100 [47 900–73 000] deaths; , ).

Among females, maternal disorders were the leading cause of death at ages 25–34 years, accounting for nearly 10% of total mortality. From age 35 years, stroke became the leading cause, followed by ischaemic heart disease; this ranking was maintained up to ages 85–89 years. Diabetes rose to third by ages 65–69 years (, ). By ages 60–64 years, the top three causes of death were stroke, ischaemic heart disease, and diabetes. Towards late adulthood the sexes converged: stroke, ischaemic heart disease, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and lower respiratory infections were the top five at ages 70–79 years. Alzheimer's disease rose to become a major cause in the oldest age groups (, ).

Leading causes of mortality diverged across countries in early adulthood, particularly among females. At ages 25–29 years, road injuries dominated in Malaysia, Thailand, and Viet Nam; maternal disorders in Indonesia, Myanmar, the Philippines, and Timor-Leste; lower respiratory infections in Laos; HIV/AIDS in Cambodia; diabetes in Brunei; and self-harm in Singapore (). Causes converged by mid to late adulthood but outcomes stayed disparate. Stroke showed the greatest disparity: at ages 55–59 years, female stroke mortality ranged from 7·1 deaths per 100 000 population (95% UI 4·4–9·5) in Singapore to 248·3 per 100 000 population (158·0–369·0) in Myanmar, with a similar gradient among males ().

Mortality burden attributable to risk factors

In 2023, malnutrition was the leading risk factor in infancy and childhood in ASEAN, accounting for 48·4% (95% UI 42·1–55·4) of the mortality burden in male infants younger than 1 year (57 800 [48 900–66 600] deaths) and 45·2% (39·4–50·8) in females (38 800 [33 400–45 000] deaths; , ). Air pollution was the second leading risk factor in infancy, whereas unsafe water, sanitation, and hygiene grew in relative importance throughout childhood. During adolescence, occupational risks emerged as a leading factor among males aged 15–19 years, accounting for 4·8% (3·3–6·5) of mortality burden and 1570 (1080–2230) deaths, followed by high alcohol use; for females aged 15–19 years, kidney dysfunction and occupational risks were the two leading factors (, ). Across adulthood, tobacco was the leading risk factor among males (13–27% of mortality burden at ages 30–64 years). From age 65 years onwards, high blood pressure replaced tobacco as the leading risk, contributing to more than one-quarter of mortality burden during late adulthood. Dietary risks and air pollution also contributed consistently (, ). For females, unsafe sex and dietary risks were the top two risk factors for mortality among young adults aged 25–34 years. From age 35 years onwards, dietary risks and high blood pressure emerged as the leading risk factors and remained among the top five throughout adulthood (, ). High BMI was another key risk factor, particularly in mid-adulthood, contributing to 11·0% (5·6–16·3) of mortality among females aged 50–54 years (13 300 [6560–20 000] deaths). Air pollution contributed to more than 15% of mortality among both males and females aged 70 years and older (, ).

Discussion

This study provides a life-course perspective on the drivers of premature mortality across the ASEAN region. Our findings reveal substantial disparities in life expectancy and 70q0, with a life expectancy gap as wide as 20 years between the lowest-performing and highest-performing member states. Throughout the life course, leading mortality drivers showed similar age patterns: neonatal disorders dominate infancy; lower respiratory infections prevail in early childhood and drowning in later childhood to early adolescence; road injuries impose a substantial burden in adolescence and young adulthood, particularly among males; maternal disorders account for a notable share among young females in many ASEAN states; and NCDs, including ischaemic heart disease, stroke, and diabetes, dominate from mid-adulthood onwards. Yet, outcomes diverge markedly; neonatal mortality rates, for instance, differ 40-fold between the highest-performing and lowest-performing countries. These patterns underscore substantial health inequities and differences in health-system capacity, suggesting opportunities for joint regional action.

The increase in life expectancy and reduction in 70q0 between 1990 and 2023 reflect remarkable achievements in population health. The COVID-19 pandemic was associated with a temporary decline in 2020–21, before life expectancy resumed its upward trajectory. These advancements were accompanied by socioeconomic development, including gross domestic product growth, rising Human Development Index, and expanded universal health coverage., However, progress remains uneven. In 2024, Singapore's gross domestic product per capita was 65 times that of Myanmar, with government health spending at 21% of public expenditure versus 4% in Myanmar. The divergence is mirrored in health outcomes across the region.

The leading causes of death echo the 15 priority conditions in the Global Health 2050 report by the Lancet Commission on Investing in Health, which targets a 50% reduction in probability of premature death before age 70 years by 2050. These conditions—spanning eight neonatal, childhood, maternal, and infectious causes and seven NCDs—align closely with the causes of death identified here. By applying a life-course perspective, our analysis highlights how the leading causes of death vary across life stages and identifies when burdens are most salient.

In the early years of life, neonatal disorders constitute the leading cause of death among infants younger than 1 year across ASEAN. This category includes, in order of magnitude: neonatal preterm birth, neonatal encephalopathy, neonatal sepsis, neonatal haemolytic disease, and other neonatal disorders, with the first two accounting for more than 30% of neonatal deaths. Although the predominance of neonatal disorders among infants younger than 1 year is expected, the magnitude of the disparity in mortality rates is concerning. High mortality from neonatal disorders is likely to reflect inadequacies in obstetric services. Several conditions are strongly associated with modifiable maternal risk factors, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, haemorrhagic conditions, anaemia, and maternal obesity. Managing these risks requires robust antenatal care, yet access remains restricted in resource-constrained countries; in Laos, only 68·9% of pregnant women attended four or more antenatal care visits in 2023, falling far short of the 90% WHO, UNICEF, and UN Population Fund target. Restricted access to facility-based delivery further compounds neonatal risks. In Timor-Leste and Myanmar, the proportions of facility births are among the lowest in the region, at 58·1% and 67·6% respectively. Beyond delivery services, many facilities have insufficient neonatal intensive care and diagnostic capacity. Furthermore, national newborn screening programmes remain unevenly implemented. Whereas Singapore, Thailand, and the Philippines have long-established screening programmes spanning birth defects, hearing and vision impairment, and metabolic and endocrine disorders, Cambodia, Laos, and Myanmar face persistent coverage gaps, probably widening inequities in early detection and intervention.,

During late childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood, various types of injuries emerge as key drivers of premature mortality. Drowning was the leading cause of mortality among male children aged 2–9 years, and remained prominent into early adolescence. This burden is preventable through layered strategies, from community measures such as water-access barriers and swimming skills training to water-transport and disaster-preparedness policies, yet these remain unevenly implemented across ASEAN., Feasible approaches include adaptation of effective regional programmes, such as Viet Nam's survival swimming curricula and Thailand's Child Drowning Prevention committee, alongside other WHO-recommended interventions.

During adolescence and young adulthood, road injuries became the leading cause of death among males aged 15–34 years, with substantial regional differences. Potential contributors include high motorcycle use, low helmet and seatbelt use compliance, gaps in traffic law enforcement, and insufficient post-crash emergency care in parts of the region. Although ASEAN introduced a regional road-safety strategy in 2016 addressing five pillars of road safety, reducing road-injury mortality remains a public health priority for the region., Deviating from regional patterns, self-harm was the leading driver of mortality among adolescents and young adults in Singapore. Increasing self-harm rates have also been documented in Malaysia and Brunei. These patterns reflect multiple interacting determinants, including underlying mental health conditions, psychological and interpersonal stressors, and structural barriers to care., Although prevention approaches vary across ASEAN, two priorities recur: destigmatising and decriminalising suicide attempts, which are still punishable in Brunei and Myanmar; and strengthening self-harm surveillance, which remains sparse across the region.,

The prevailing injury types at each life stage are broadly consistent with each country's socioeconomic development, following the pattern of the epidemiological transition. Some rates nonetheless stand out against global GBD 2023 results: drowning among children aged 5–9 years in Cambodia, Timor-Leste, and Viet Nam ranked within the 20 highest globally, and road injuries among males aged 15–19 years in Thailand ranked second among upper-middle-income countries. Reducing these preventable deaths calls for a concerted multisectoral response.,

Throughout mid to late adulthood, ischaemic heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases were the leading causes of death across ASEAN, reflecting the region's epidemiological transition. Although disease rankings were similar, outcomes diverged substantially, probably reflecting unequal access to care and differences in modifiable behavioural risk exposure. Among these risks, tobacco was the leading risk factor among those aged 30–64 years, with more than one-quarter of deaths in males aged 55–69 years attributable to this risk. Yet, tobacco control remains unevenly implemented under the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC). Singapore has one of the region's lowest male smoking prevalences following comprehensive measures, whereas Indonesia, where male smoking prevalence exceeds 50%, remains the only member state not a party to the FCTC. Strengthening FCTC implementation, drawing on models such as Singapore's, could reduce tobacco-attributable mortality in ASEAN. More broadly, primary prevention offers a cost-effective approach to avert long-term NCD burden and systemic strain.

Distinctive risk factors shape mortality at different life stages. In early childhood, malnutrition predominated, accounting for more than 40% of under-5 deaths. During adolescence, occupational risks predominated among males, and kidney dysfunction and occupational risks affected females, the former partly reflecting regional vulnerabilities associated with unequal access to education, early workforce entry, and inadequate social protection. In adulthood, metabolic and dietary risk factors became increasingly salient. Air pollution weighed most heavily in childhood and late adulthood, although its dominant source, whether household air pollution from solid fuels or ambient particulate matter, varied by age and geography. Addressing these sources requires accelerating clean cooking in countries with high household air pollution burden and strengthening regional governance to reduce transboundary haze, both demanding investments and coordination across national and regional levels., Unlike most risk factors concentrated within specific life stages, air pollution acts continuously across the life course, from early childhood to late adulthood, making air-quality policy uniquely high leverage.

This study has certain limitations. First, the precision of cause-specific mortality estimates depends on the quality of available data. Many ASEAN countries do not have complete vital registration, surveillance systems, and medically verified cause-of-death data; estimates for these settings therefore rely on statistical models using covariates and spatiotemporal patterns, and carry wider 95% UIs. Cross-country comparisons should be interpreted with caution, and these gaps highlight the need to strengthen national health information systems to improve future estimates and policy relevance. Second, the region has experienced major mortality shocks between 1990 and 2023, including natural disasters, civil conflicts, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Extensive efforts have been made to correct for these events; for instance, COVID-19 deaths misclassified as other causes were identified and corrected in the vital registration data through a dedicated machine-learning procedure. Nonetheless, the variable quality and inherent stochasticity of data from these periods hamper the precision of estimates. Third, although 70q0 provides a complementary summary of premature mortality, it does not have the granularity to identify the specific ages at which mortality risks are most concentrated. Fourth, because this analysis applies a life-course perspective using age-stratified estimates rather than longitudinal cohort data, it does not capture individual-level trajectories or distinguish age, period, and cohort effects. Our life-stage comparisons should be interpreted in this light. Finally, risk factors examined are not exhaustive. GBD inclusion requires sufficient evidence of causation as defined under the Burden of Proof methodology, so risk factors with insufficient evidence were excluded, potentially omitting emerging environmental and behavioural drivers of disease burden.

In conclusion, by examining causes of death across the life course, our findings illustrate how mortality drivers shift with age, underscoring the need for interventions tailored to each life stage. Between 1990 and 2023, mortality profiles across ASEAN have become more similar in composition, although the associated burden still differs substantially. Achieving equitable progress will require robust information systems and sustained multilateral cooperation, anchored in ASEAN's health-governance architecture, including the ASEAN Community Vision 2045 and its ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community Strategic Plan (2026–35), to address shared regional determinants and elevate health as a collective priority.
