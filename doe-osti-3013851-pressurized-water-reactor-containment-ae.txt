ABSTRACT
This report documents results from MELCOR calculations performed to determine the aerosol
settling rate in pressurized water reactor containments in support of the Nuclear Regulatory
Commission (NRC) Regulatory Guide (RG) 1.183, “Alternative Radiological Source Terms for
Evaluating Design Basis Accidents at Nuclear Power Reactors,” which is the methodology for
determining alternate source terms in design basis accidents at nuclear power reactors. RG 1.183
allows for aerosol settling due to natural deposition processes in the containment. The historical
guidance for aerosol settling rates was established in the 1994 report, NUREG/CR-6189, “A
Simplified Model of Aerosol Removal by Natural Processes in Reactor Containments”. This work
provides new aerosol settling rate recommendations based on the same sequences and representative
containment designs considered in SAND2023-01313, “High Burnup Fuel Source Term Accident
Sequence Analysis”. Many containment response calculations were performed using high-
performance computing resources that evaluated the uncertainty in the aerosol settling rate versus
time. The uncertainty evaluations included parameters from the historical guidance in
NUREG/CR-6189, uncertainty parameters from the Surry State of-the Art Consequence Analyses
(SOARCA) uncertainty assessment study, and the sequences and plant designs used in
SAND2023-01313. These results show higher aerosol settling rates during the in-vessel and long-
term phases than the historical guidance. The higher settling rates are attributed to integrated
methods not possible in 1994, including the in-vessel accident progression coupled to the
containment response, time-dependent and location specific releases into representative and
sequence specific containment compartments, the inclusion of the associated steam, water, fog
droplets, and hydrogen sources, and the features of the detailed containment models from the
SOARCA program. There was a high dependence on the sequence and plant design attributes that
strongly supported using the median settling response, which included time dependent influences
from all sequences and both plant designs.

Sensitivity studies were also performed against the historical guidance for aerosol removal due to
containment spray operation, which is documented in NUREG/CR-5966. A MELCOR spray
sensitivity calculation was compared to an example calculation from the report. The MELCOR
aerosol removal rate due to spray operation tracked closely to the median response of the historical
approach. uncertainty calculations were performed for the two plant designs using the previously
described uncertainty variables. Due to differences in the spray coverage, the effectiveness of the
sprays differed between the two plant designs and were presented separately. However, both
designs showed negligible benefit of the sprays at very low flow rates, which was consistent with
median values from NUREG/CR-5966. At a 10X higher flowrate (i.e., more typical of the rated
spray flowrate), both plants showed high effectiveness of the sprays to remove airborne aerosols.
The median result from NUREG/CR-5966 bounded nearly all the results from the ice condenser
calculations. Similarly, the 10th, median, and 90th percentile MELCOR large dry containment results
showed a higher aerosol removal rate than the NUREG/CR-5966 median results and less
uncertainty.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is in the process of updating Regulatory Guide 1.183
(RG 1.183) to Revision 2. As part of this process, the NRC hosted several public workshops to
share changes being considered in the newest revision (i.e., see Reference [1]). Stakeholders at the
public workshops identified several areas for the NRC to consider, which were subsequently
prioritized. The focus of this report is on one such priority, namely, the development of updated
containment decontamination coefficients using the new alternate source term phase durations for
pressurized water reactors (PWRs) from SAND2023-01313.
Methodology
This report documents results from MELCOR calculations performed to determine the aerosol
settling rate in PWR containments. New aerosol settling rate recommendations are developed for
RG 1.183, Revision 2 based on the same sequences and representative containment designs
considered in SAND2023-01313, “High Burnup Fuel Source Term Accident Sequence Analysis”.
Many containment response calculations were performed using MELCOR to evaluate the
uncertainty in the aerosol settling rate versus time. The results of the MELCOR uncertainty
evaluations included parameters from historical guidance for aerosol settling rates
(NUREG/CR-6189, “A Simplified Model of Aerosol Removal by Natural Processes in Reactor
Containments”), uncertainty parameters from the Surry State-of-the-Art-Consequence Analyses
(SOARCA) uncertainty assessment study, and the sequences and two plant designs used in
SAND2023-01313.
Uncertainty Quantification Summarized Results
The present results of these MELCOR uncertainty evaluations show higher aerosol settling rates
during the in-vessel and long-term phases than past studies (e.g., NUREG/CR-6189). Figure ES-1
compares the aggregated median MELCOR uncertainty quantification aerosol settling response to
historical guidelines, illustrating the increased settling rates during the in-vessel and long-term phases
compared to past studies.
The higher settling rates are attributed to advanced integrated methods not available in past studies
including the in-vessel accident progression coupled to the containment response, time-dependent
and location specific releases into representative and sequence-specific-containment compartments,
the inclusion of the associated steam, water, fog droplet, and hydrogen sources, and the features of
the detailed containment models from the SOARCA program. There was a high dependence on the
sequence and plant design attributes that strongly supports the use of a median settling response.

Key Recommendations
There are several key recommendations from this study. These recommendations stem from the
objective to best represent the results of the mechanistic MELCOR calculations for regulatory
applications that lack models for thermal-hydraulics and aerosol physics present in a severe accident.
For example, the source term to the containment from the mechanistic MELCOR calculations does
not arrive uniformly and is instead informed by models for thermal-hydraulics and aerosol physics.
This contrasts to a simplified source term used in regulatory applications (e.g., Technical
Information Document (TID)-14844, NUREG-1465, and more recent SAND2023-01313). The
sequences comprising the new alternate source term (AST) (RG 1.183) include diverse events and
timings. These variations in events and timings are statistically sampled into a representative median
response. Those sampled integral releases and timings do not match any single sequence, which was
a requirement of RG 1.183. The regulatory applications of the AST also do not represent the diverse
thermal-hydraulic conditions, location of the source to the containment, and discrete events such as
hot leg creep rupture failure, pressurizer relief tank (PRT) rupture disk opening, and the accumulator
discharge. However, all these effects are present in the mechanistic MELCOR calculations and
embedded into the various sequence results.
The results presented in this report encompass various accident progressions that have diverse
results and timings, whereas the RG 1.183 AST is an amalgamation of representative effects that
must be distilled into recommendations for applications that require prescribed inputs

 xi

(e.g., prescribed timings and magnitudes of the source term phases1, prescribed containment
pressure responses, prescribed aerosol settling rates, prescribed spray aerosol removal effectiveness,
and plant-specific leakages).
The pertinent recommendations from this study are as follows:
Recommendation #1: The gap and in-vessel aerosol settling rate should be specified, consistent with
this study, to provide the correct boundary conditions for the long-term response. It is
recommended to form a single aerosol settling coefficient, λe,in-vessel, that is consistent with the timing
from SAND2023-01313. The λe, in-vessel will calculate the same non-dimensional ratio of airborne
mass to total mass in the containment at the end of the in-vessel phase that was predicted in the
current MELCOR calculations and provide the correct boundary conditions for the long-term
response.
The current results of the MELCOR in-vessel phase accident progression show the impact of
location-specific sources to the containment with co-dispersed steam, water, fog droplets, and
hydrogen. All the sequences include the accumulator discharge into core with conditions ranging
from hot intact fuel to a highly degraded configuration during the in-vessel phase. The results from
the uncertainty study show large bursts of steam and water to the containment, which led to
significant settling prior to the long-term phase relative to the historical guidance in
NUREG/CR-6189.
While the long-term phase results show increased settling from this study, the greatest change
occurred during the in-vessel phase with the simultaneous dispersion of steam, water, fog droplets,
and hydrogen with released radionuclides into the sequence specific compartment locations. The
calculated in-vessel phase settling coefficients from the accident sequences required post processing
to match the timings in SAND2023-01313, which will be described next.
The effective aerosol settling coefficient, or λe, implies a rate process. However, the timings of the
various sequences are diverse, and no single sequence aligns with statistically processed results and
timings in SAND2023-01313. The in-vessel phase is further complicated by simultaneous aerosol
additions to the containment with concurrent settling. Due to these complications, a single aerosol
settling coefficient, λe,in-vessel, is recommended for the entire in-vessel phase using the in-vessel phase
timing in SAND2023-01313.
Recommendation #2: The median values of the settling rate should be used to best represent the
diverse accident sequences and plant designs considered in this study. While the 10th percentile
settling rate (i.e., lower settling, more conservative regarding unsettled aerosols within the
containment) have been used in previous studies and recommendations, the current MELCOR
results show strong dependencies on both the sequence specifications and the plant design. The
effect is so significant that the 10% and 90% MELCOR uncertainty results are dominated by one
plant type and one or two similar sequences. Using the 10% and 90% values would neither meet the
RG 1.183 AST requirements nor represent the spectrum of results considered in this study. For this
reason and consistent with RG 1.183 AST requirements, the median value is recommended. Because

Recommendation #3: The recommended aerosol settling guidance does not include variations due
to rated thermal power. Several parameters in NUREG/CR-6189 accounted for variations in the
containment size and the settling surface area. The technology at the time of the study meant that
only one or two control volumes and associated heat structures model were varied. The analysis
developed empirical expressions that characterized these parameters. It was concluded that the
accurate depictions of the two representative containments, the range of diverse sequences, and the
wide variations in the magnitude of the source term via a multiplier addressed or replaced the need
for these parameters. This is also consistent with the new SAND2023-01313 source term, which
does not vary due to PWR containment type or thermal power rating.
Recommendation #4: The median values of the spray decontamination from NUREG/CR-5966
should be used. The present MELCOR results and those from NUREG/CR-5966, “A Simplified
Model of Aerosol Removal by Containment Sprays,” show that the 10% values from the
NUREG/CR-5966 study lie significantly outside the median values. Although a comprehensive
spray uncertain study was not performed, the other uncertain parameters of aerosol size and source
term multiplier spanned a wide range of conditions in the spray sensitivity studies. The 10% values
in the NUREG/CR-5966 study are also significantly different from the reported median response in
NUREG/CR-5966.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) is in the process of updating Regulatory Guide 1.183
(RG 1.183), Revision 1, “Alternative Radiological Source Terms for Evaluating Design Basis
Accidents at Nuclear Power Reactors” to Revision 2. As part of this process, the NRC hosted
several public workshops to share changes being considered in the newest revision (i.e., see
Reference [1]). Stakeholders at the public workshops identified several areas for the NRC to
consider, which were subsequently prioritized. The stakeholder priorities and the NRC responses
are summarized in Figure 1-1. The focus of this report is Priority Number 3 (see Figure 1-1), which
is the development of updated decontamination coefficients using the new phase durations for both
pressurized water reactors (PWRs) and boiling water reactors (BWRs).2 The scope of the present
work is limited to PWR containment decontamination factors (i.e., aerosol settling rates) for use in
AST RG 1.183 regulatory applications. The objective of the present work is to provide
recommendations for new aerosol settling coefficients following the methodology in
SAND2023-013133 by using MELCOR with an uncertainty assessment.
The new revision to RG 1.183 is motivated to support new fuels for operating reactors to enhance
their operational efficiency and economics. In the imminent deployment timeframe, industry is
focused on employing new fuels that can achieve a higher burnup, which are commonly referred to
as high burnup (HBU) fuel. In support of the NRC’s new guidance for RG 1.183, SNL recently
completed a number of studies to update the technical basis for the source term to the containment
that encompassed a range of new fuels [4][5][6] (e.g., SAND2023-01313). The outputs from the
previous source term work are tables for the magnitude and timing of the radionuclide release to the
containment. The source term in RG 1.183 is described as the Alternative Source Term (AST), as it
provides an alternative to the original source term described in RGs 1.3 [7] and 1.4 [8] that used
values prescribed in TID-14844 [9], “Calculation of Distance Factors for Power and Test Reactor
Sites. Technical Information Document,” [9]. A history of regulatory source terms and the new high
burnup source is described in SAND2023-01313, “High Burnup Fuel Source Term Accident
Sequence Analysis” [4].
The new high burnup source term in Reference [4] did not address the natural deposition of

The overall technical approach is summarized in this section. Some relevant background leading up

2.1. Background
NUREG-1465, “Accident Source Terms for Light-Water Nuclear Power Plants,” [12] describes the
original technical basis for the RG 1.183 AST. NUREG-1465 established the new source term, its
technical basis, and the in-containment removal mechanisms. The process to evaluate the
consequences from the AST is shown in Figure 2-1. In the context of the present study, the
RG 1.183 AST is established in “High Burnup Fuel Source Term Accident Sequence Analysis,”
SAND2023-01313. The source term and the timings of the release from the new AST are specified
in Figure 2-1 in the source term (ST) [4] (i.e., the upper left-hand side of the Figure 2-1). The ST is
released into the containment according to the timing and release magnitude specified in
SAND2023-01313 [4].
Consistent with requirements developed in RG 1.183, the AST has the following attributes:
 “The AST must not be based upon a single accident scenario but instead must
 represent a spectrum of credible severe accident events. Risk insights may be used,
 not to select a single risk-significant accident, but rather to establish the range of
 events to be considered. Relevant insights from applicable severe accident research
 on the phenomenology of fission product release and transport behavior may be
 considered.” [31]

Using this requirement and other requirements specified in RG 1.183, NUREG-1465 and
SAND2023-01313 established the results and an approach that would be acceptable for application
of the AST. It is noted, which will be highlighted in the recommendations, the AST must not be
based on a single scenario.
The focus of the present work is the evaluation of the aerosol natural deposition rate in the
containment, or λ, in the middle of Figure 2-1. The NUREG-1465 guidance was established in
1995 along with technical guidance on aerosol natural deposition (NUREG/CR-6189) and technical
guidance on aerosol decontamination by containment sprays (NUREG/CR-5966). NUREG-1465
leveraged Source Term Code Package (STCP) results [17] performed in support of NUREG-1150,
“Severe Accident Risks: An Assessment for Five U.S. Nuclear Power Plants” [18]. The NRC judged
that the guidance in NUREG/CR-6189 and NUREG/CR-5966 is acceptable for regulatory
applications as defined in RG 1.183, Rev. 0.
Starting in 2007, NRC directed a number studies at SNL to perform MELCOR calculations in
support of issues related to higher fuel burnups and mixed oxide fuels [13][14][15][16]. The
approach used new sequences determined from the individual plant examinations (IPEs) that
followed the NUREG/CR-1150 study (Reference [18]). The PWR sequences were first documented
in SAND2011-0128, "Accident Source Terms for Light Water Nuclear Power Plants Using High

 4

Burnup or MOX Fuel” [14]. The technical approach using MELCOR culminated in the work
documented in SAND2023-01313 (Reference [4]). However, the newer MELCOR studies in
SAND2023-01313 and the previous studies [13]-[16] only focused on the cumulative release to the
containment and did not report natural deposition rates in the containment.
The NRC directed a large study documenting the state-of-the-art in reactor consequence analyses,
SOARCA. While not directly focused on RG 1.183 applications, the SOARCA study and the
associated uncertainty assessments developed high fidelity plant models and methods to document
the state-of-the-art in reactor severe accident consequence analyses (References [19]-[24]). The
SOARCA program received an international expert panel review, multiple Advisory Committee on
Reactor Safeguards (ACRS) reviews, and had cooperation from the representative plants for accurate
geometric and modeling data. The same methods and plant models were used in SAND2023-01313

2.2. MELCOR Plant Models
The SOARCA and source term and accident progression calculations were performed using the
MELCOR code [25][14][15][16]. Figure 2-2 shows some of the major NRC MELCOR code

 5

applications, including SAND2023-01313 and SOARCA. The two PWR input models used in the
current study have been developed and updated over the long timeframe shown in Figure 2-2. The
research and regulatory usage of these two models are characteristic of the two predominant types
of PWR containments deployed in the United States (US); that is, large dry and ice condenser
containments. Both models benefitted from further utility interaction during the SOARCA study to
confirm and update their geometry, system performance, and operator actions during postulated
severe accidents. The large dry containment model (i.e., Surry) benefitted from multiple interactions
with Dominion Power during the original SOARCA study [21]. Similarly, the ice condenser
containment model (i.e., Sequoyah) benefitted from the work with Tennessee Valley Authority
related to the Sequoyah Uncertainty Assessment [23], including a walkdown of the yet unfinished
Watts Bar Unit 2 containment (which has a similar design to Sequoyah nuclear power plant). The
containment portions of both models evolved from earlier applications for NUREG-1150 using the
CONTAIN code [26][27].
The two MELCOR models subdivide the containment into compartments (Figure 2-3). The
nodalization includes additional details for areas where thermal-hydraulic and radionuclide sources
from the primary system enter the containment and other regions important to deposition (e.g., the
pressurizer relief tank and the ice chests in the Sequoyah model). The large dry containment and ice
condenser primary systems for the in-vessel accident progression are described in more detail in
References [4] and in the SOARCA documentation (References [19]-[24]).
During the various accident progressions considered in the current study and in SAND2023-01313,
the releases to the containment occur at the appropriate locations in the modeled containment.
Furthermore, the thermal-hydraulic and radionuclide sources are co-dispersed together. For
example, the pressurizer relief tank (PRT) initially condenses steam released from the pressurizer
safety valves. After a few safety valve cycles, the water in the PRT heats toward saturation
conditions and pressurizes the PRT. The PRT rupture disk opens and the subsequent releases to the
containment go through this pathway. The hydraulic and radionuclide effluent pass through the
remaining PRT water and tank atmosphere before exiting through the open rupture disk into the
containment. Radionuclides retained in the PRT are not available for leakage from the containment.
Both models include leakage through the reactor coolant system pump seals when the seal cooling is
not available. The pump seal water leakage can change to steam, hydrogen, and radionuclides if the
pump seals uncover. The pipe breaks for the loss-of-coolant accidents (LOCAs) occur in the
compartment where the piping resides. The high-pressure accident sequences include heating of the
hot leg and its eventual creep rupture, which results in the rapid discharge of the accumulator water
into an overheated core. The rapid vaporization of the accumulator water and blowdown of the
primary system are discrete events in the accident progression that have a large impact on the
containment steam and radionuclide concentrations. The hot leg pipe break occurs at the interface
between the steam generator compartment and the reactor cavity.
The important phenomena in all these sources are the localized, co-dispersed release and mixing of
water, steam, hydrogen, inert aerosol materials (e.g., control rod and oxidized zircaloy), and
radionuclides before their transport throughout the containment. The releases occur in confined
compartments representative of the containment design. For the ice condenser design, all releases
occur in the lower portion of the containment, which is effectively isolated from the dome region.
The effluents are directed into the ice chests, which enhance the natural deposition of aerosols.
The aerosol settling results in the present study show strong dependencies on plant design and
sequence that are impacted by the rate and timing from the various release points that overwhelm all

 6

other uncertainties. The station blackout (SBO) sequences start with pump seal leaks that progress
to releases from the PRT. Eventually, a discrete change in the aerosol concentration occurs
following the hot leg creep rupture and primary system blowdown into the containment. The
subsequent releases occur more slowly at low pressure with potential for some in-vessel retention.
The reactor coolant pump seal failure, the small LOCA, and the large LOCA sequences have a much
different accident progression. The large LOCA includes release of a rapid steam and water source
to the containment with a simultaneous full accumulator discharge before the release of any
radionuclides. The seal LOCA sequence is a much smaller leak that includes many of the attributes
of the SBO sequence including a high-pressure hot leg creep rupture. Some of the sequences include
auxiliary feed water injection that delays the timing to the core damage. The large LOCAs in both
plants also include emergency core cooling system (ECCS) water injection to the primary system
until the supply tanks empty.
These sequence and plant variations have large impacts on the timing of the aerosol release into the
containment and their settling characteristics. Consequently, the plant models and sequence
selection provide a wide range of results that meets the requirements of the RG 1.183, i.e., “the AST
must not be based upon a single accident scenario but instead must represent a spectrum of credible
severe accident events.” The MELCOR models and accident progression results capture these
variations.

2.3. Evaluation of the Aerosol Settling Rate
The large dry containment large dry containment (i.e., Surry) and ice condenser plant (i.e., Sequoyah)
MELCOR models used in SAND2023-01313 [4] were slightly modified to determine the aerosol
settling rate. First, the sequences were reviewed and all interface locations for radionuclide releases
between the primary system and the containment were identified. At each location, additional
integral tracking was added to capture all thermal-hydraulic sources (i.e., steam, fog droplets, water,
and hydrogen) and all radioactive and non-radioactive sources. Relative to the latter, the noble gas
and gaseous iodine discharge to the containment was tracked due to its impact on the airborne decay
heating. The non-radioactive sources include any vaporized silver, indium, and cadmium from the
control rods and structural aerosols created during the rapid oxidation of the zircaloy fuel cladding.
The integral mass for each radionuclide class released to the containment was captured separately.
The collected information constituted all data necessary to simulate all 12 accident sequences
considered in SAND2023-013134.
The MELCOR accident progression calculations require about 72 hours to complete a 72-hour
simulation. However, most of the computational time occurs during the core degradation phase. To
facilitate more calculations for the uncertainty quantification (see Section 3), the full containment
models were separated from the integrated plant model. The containment models were adapted
accordingly, to read the sequence-specific and location-specific sources. The modified containment
models read the integral mass and energy sources and created rate additions that replicated the
various sequences. The resulting models could replicate both the in-vessel portion of the accident
and subsequent long-term response.
It is assumed the RHR system arrests the accident and prevents vessel failure. Several other
variations of the long-term response were considered. The variations ranged from modeling
post-Fukushima Flexible and Diverse Coping Strategies (FLEX) water addition prior to vessel
failure, post-Fukushima FLEX water addition after vessel failure, and recovery of the emergency
diesels or onsite power. Due to modeling and phenomenological uncertainties related to arresting an
accident in-vessel or ex-vessel, and the complex physics of fuel quenching and the associated steam,
water and hydrogen sources, the quench phase was not modeled. These approaches would be a
significant deviation from previous RG 1.183 applications and prone to timing and injection
magnitude issues that would impact the long-term phase source terms to the containment. The
selected approach was to model the entire in-vessel accident progression up to the point of vessel
failure (i.e., encompassing the complete gap and in-vessel source term from SAND2023-01313). At
the end of the in-vessel phase, the high flow residual heat removal (RHR) system is recovered.
The decay heat from the in-vessel debris is removed without any new heat or material sources to the
containment for the long-term phase of the accident. The long-term approach focuses on the natural
aerosol removal process rather than an accident management study. Several other variations of the
RHR recovery were also considered. If the RHR initially operates in injection mode, then the
simulation of the debris quenching and water spills over into the containment would create a large
new source of steam, water, hydrogen, and waterborne radionuclides. A second related approach
was simulated where the decay heat from the debris in the vessel boiled away injected water.
However, this created a large wet steam source to the containment that enhanced aerosol settling

It was also recognized that long-term containment decay heat removal is also needed. The decay

removal following the long-term phase recovery of the in-vessel injection created an inconsistency.
However, the slow pressurization of the containment during the long-term phase also created a
steam source and an increase in the containment steam partial pressure. This led to enhanced

accident management guidelines direct the use of containment sprays to manage the containment
pressurization. The issue of separating spray performance from natural deposition processes was
addressed by omitting the containment radionuclide decay heat power during long-term phase of the
accident. In summary, the radionuclide decay heat and its potential impact on the aerosol removal
rate was neglected in the long-term phase “in-vessel” and “ex-vessel” responses. (The impact of
sprays on the aerosol removal rate is discussed in Section 5.)
The containment models also included a new set of aerosol tracking algorithms. The tracking
algorithms provided fundamental parameters such as the total and airborne aerosol mass in every
control volume of the containment. It also separated water soluble or hygroscopic aerosols from
non-hygroscopic aerosols. There were also separate calculations for short interval and integral
aerosol settling rates for the in-vessel phase and long-term phase. These time-step by time-step
quantities provided the necessary information for the post-processing described in Section 3.

3. UNCERTAINTY QUANTIFICATION METHODOLOGY
Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis techniques are useful for identifying which parameters have the
largest effect on model outputs [29]. In the context of the current work, the primary quantities of
interest (QoI) for uncertainty quantification (UQ) are the average effective and integral aerosol
settling rate terms, λe and λintegral, and the related ratio of the airborne aerosol mass to the total
injected aerosol mass in containment, Mairborne/Mtotal.
A limited uncertainty and sensitivity analysis was run for the two PWR plants, the 11 sequences (see
footnote 4 on page 10), and design parameters. Section 3.1 describes the overall process and
workflow of the analysis. Section 3.2 briefly describes the selected sampling method, Latin
hypercube sampling (LHS). Section 3.3 lists the design parameters, ranges, and distributions
sampled. Section 3.4 describes in greater depth the quantities of interest for the study.

3.1. Process and Workflow
The UQ was performed using the following workflow:
 1. Computational toolkit (Dakota):
 a. Generate list of samples from selected sampling method (incremental LHS) and
 parameter distributions using selected UQ computational tool (Dakota).
 2. Select single sample:
 a. Create MELCOR input from template files and sampled parameters.
 b. Verify MELCOR run will start without input or early execution errors.
 c. Iterate through remaining samples until all MELCOR inputs generated.
 3. SNL high performance computing (HPC) resources:
 a. Submit all generated MELCOR inputs to the HPC queue.
 b. All cases run on HPC.
 4. Python
 a. Post process all aerosol settling QoIs from the MELCOR results.

3.1.1.     Dakota
Dakota is a computational toolkit for large-scale iterative analyses that is developed at SNL [29].
Dakota was selected for the UQ analysis as it supports the selected method (i.e., incremental LHS),
is supported on the SNL computing environments, and is easy to use with text-based-inputs such as
the ones used with MELCOR. Dakota operates in a similar fashion to other computational toolkits
that are used for iterative analysis. The first part of a sampling analysis is to select the list of sampled
parameters, their distributions, and ranges. This information is given to Dakota using a specially
formatted text input. A driver is then used to run the analysis. The driver is a user-supplied script
with special keywords used to access the Dakota parameters and results files. In the case of this
workflow (Figure 3-1), the driver reads in a single sample and then populates a templated MELCOR
input with perturbed parameters. The template-to-input generation step is performed with a
user-specific Python script written for this work. The driver then checks that the generated input file
will successfully launch MELGEN and MELCOR without error. A status is recorded to the Dakota
output and failed instances are flagged for easier debugging of the individual files.
In a “typical” Dakota use case, Dakota would also be used to run the generated inputs and
postprocess the results. However, the selected workflow decouples these steps; Dakota is used solely
for the sample generation and generating the MELCOR input files. The actual MELCOR inputs
were run using the SNL HPC environment, which allowed many samples to be run in a relatively
short period of time.

3.2. Sampling Method
The method selected for the sampling of parameters is incremental LHS. LHS is a statistical method
for generating a near-random sampling of a multidimensional distribution. It is an established
statistical method that creates a more uniform and representative samples of variables for a
multidimensional space compared to pure random sampling [29]. LHS is a useful sampling
technique when the behavior of the output may be dominated by only a few variables as the
sampling method ensures that each variable is sampled across the full range [30].
 16

An LHS is generated for N variables and M samples by dividing each of the N variables into M
equally probable intervals or subspaces. Each sample is then drawn from one of these subspaces. In
the case of multiple variables, the component subspaces across the different variables are drawn at
random. This is represented in Figure 3-2, which shows an illustration of M=4 samples over N=2
parameters, parameters a and b. Each red dot represents a sample located within an interval of M.
The equally probable intervals are shown with black lines and there is one sample drawn from each
{a,b} subspace.
Dakota conveniently includes an option to run an “incremental LHS” [29]. An incremental LHS is
shown in Figure 3-3, where each interval of M is subdivided into two subintervals of M. The original
four red samples are still part of the LHS; however, an additional four yellow samples populate
sub-intervals in the original matrix. The original intervals are shown in black, with the subdivided
intervals shown in blue. This illustrates the advantage of incremental LHS; each increment
(i.e., doubling of samples) of the LHS is also a complete LHS, allowing additional samples to be
taken while also retaining older samples [29]. It is usually unknown beforehand how many samples
are needed to complete the LHS and produce sufficiently converged results; adding additional
sample points does not require restarting sampling which can translate into major time and resource
savings. The utilized incremental LHS interval included 1600 total samples, equaling intervals of
{25,50,100,200,400,800,1600}.

3.3. Uncertainty Parameters for Aerosol Settling Rate
Ten parameters were selected to be part of the current LHS UQ analysis. The parameters were
selected from three sources.
The first source was the historical work done in NUREG/CR-6189 [10]. The NUREG/CR-6189
study performed a comprehensive examination of parameters impacting the in-vessel source term

 17

generation, the in-vessel behavior, the ex-vessel behavior including core debris concrete interactions,
and many fundamental aerosol physics parameters. The study dismissed some of the parameters
with a rationale and settled on approximately 50 parameters for the uncertainty evaluations.
Each parameter from NUREG/CR-6189 was reviewed with accident progression and aerosol
specialists at SNL and the NRC. Nearly all in-vessel and ex-vessel accident progression parameters
were dismissed. The in-vessel parameters were largely dismissed because the source term
methodology and source term were established in SAND2023-01313, which received an expert
panel review [4]. These parameters included physical parameters inherent to the radionuclide release
models. It was concluded that the range of sequences and the modern fission product release
models adequately addressed most of these uncertainties. This conclusion was strengthened by
retaining three key parameters from the in-vessel progression list in NUREG/CR-5966 [10]. These
included an overall scaling factor for the source term magnitude to the containment; variation of the
mass median diameter of the aerosols released to the containment; and variation of the aerosol size
distribution (i.e., the geometric standard deviation).
All ex-vessel accident progression parameters were dismissed because the current applications of
RG 1.183 no longer include ex-vessel radionuclide sources from core debris-concrete interactions or
late in-vessel fission product re-vaporization source to the containment. The accident is assumed to
be arrested, and the containment remains intact (except for the nominal leakages) for the purpose of
calculating the health consequences.
There were also several parameters in historical study that accounted for variations in the
containment size and the settling surface area. The technology at the time of the study meant that
only one or two control volumes and associated heat structures model were varied. The analysis
developed empirical expressions that characterized these parameters. It was concluded that the
accurate depictions of the two representative containments, the range of diverse sequences, and the
wide variations in the magnitude of the source term via a multiplier addressed or replaced the need
for these parameters.
Next, the aerosol physics parameters were reviewed. Some were discarded as being adequately
addressed through the range of diverse sequences, and the wide variations in the magnitude of the
source term via a multiplier addressed these parameters. Specifically, the current study retained an
overall multiplier on the radioactive source term to the containment, an overall multiplier on the
non-radiative structural aerosols to the containment, a multiplier to the wall heat transfer, a steam
solubility multiplier for the hygroscopicity aerosols (chemical forms of cesium), and an aerosol
conductivity multiplier.
Variation in the aerosol density was strongly considered but discarded for several reasons. First, the
high steam content in the containment overwhelmed the volume fraction of the airborne aerosols
towards the density of water. Second, the code benchmarking to light water reactor containment
aerosol experiments also assumed a water density was representative in a wet containment response.
Finally, it was not possible to correctly address multiple densities within the framework of the
current aerosol physics model. Consequently, it was decided that using the aerosol settling shape
factor as a surrogate for variable density was adequate to address the actual variations in radionuclide
densities. In all cases where the historical uncertainty parameters were selected, the same range and
distributions were used.
The second source for uncertainty parameters was the Surry SOARCA Uncertainty Analyses [24].
Dr. Dana Powers, the author of the historical aerosol settling study, also advised and reviewed the
SOARCA study except in limited circumstances where he recused himself as an ACRS member with

 18

a conflict of interest. He directed the SOARCA study to aerosol shape factor data and the associated
distribution, which was also used in this study. The SOARCA study included a multiplier to the
containment heat transfer, which was also one of the parameters in the historical study. However,
the range and distribution from the SOARCA study were used here, which overlapped the historical
study variations.
Finally, the plant designs and sequences used in SAND2023-01313 were sampled parameters. As will
be discussed in Section 4, the plant design and sequence had the greatest impact on the aerosol
settling rate. The new methodology of diverse plant designs and diverse sequences generated the
most significant variations in the aerosol settling rate.

4.3.1. Effect of Sequence Type
The accident sequence and plant design dominated the response of the UQ results. This can be
observed from the figures below, which shows that for each accident sequence, the end of the
in-vessel and the end of long-term phase6 airborne mass ratio and integral lambda values are overall
tightly grouped on a per-sequence basis. The airborne mass ratio is the more useful parameter of the
two because the lambda includes the time duration of the accident, which is highly variable.
Consequently, the following discussion focuses on the state at the common point of the end of the
in-vessel phase.

of magnitude. This time interval for the long-term phase also matches the range of guidance provided in
NUREG/CR-6189 [10].

Figure 4-10 and Figure 4-11 show the airborne aerosol mass ratio at the end of the in-vessel phase
and long-term phase (i.e., at the end of the calculation, which is 32 hr after the end of the in-vessel
phase). Similarly, Figure 4-12 and Figure 4-13 have the same time points but show the integral
settling coefficient. The two parameters and timeframes present sequence-specific views of the plant
and scenario impacts on the aerosol settling rate. In this context, a low airborne mass ratio or a high
settling coefficient at the end of the in-vessel phase (Figure 4-10 and Figure 4-12, respectively), are
characteristic of high aerosol settling during the in-vessel phase. These attributes are dominated by
the large dry containment large LOCA sequence (1c), which not only included the full pipe break of
the reactor coolant system (RCS) piping but also the coincident discharge of all four accumulators
and ECCS injection. The large amount of water, steam, and direct pathway for continued steaming
led to this sequence having the largest settling rate during the in-vessel phase. The similar ice
condenser large LOCA sequence (4f) is evident in the in-vessel settling coefficient results (see
Figure 4-12) but, because of the lack of ECCS injection during the in-vessel phase, the early aerosol
settling mass in the containment is lower (see Figure 4-10).7
The other end of the in-vessel spectrum of results is dominated by Sequences 1d and 4g, with the
highest airborne mass ratios at the end of the in-vessel phase. These sequences progress as a
high-pressure transient until the hot leg creep rupture failure. The only radionuclide releases to the
containment occur through the PRT prior to the hot leg failure in Sequence 1d and the PRT and the
small LOCA in Sequence 4g. The large dry containment small LOCA (Sequence 1b) and the large
dry containment SBO with RCP seal leakage (1a) also have high airborne mass ratios. The common
attributes of Sequences 1a, 4g, and 1d are a high-pressure accident progression with releases
primarily through the PRT, a rapid transition to vessel failure within 7 hr, and no RCS or steam
generator water injection. Although Sequence 1b had AFW, the loss of RCS inventory in the 1-inch
break limited its effectiveness, and the accident proceeded to vessel failure by 12 hr. The
combination of rapid timelines to vessel failure with higher retention of released radionuclides
in-vessel contributed to the high mass fractions in these sequences.
The other key observation from Figure 4-10 is the effectiveness of the ice condenser design to limit
the airborne mass during the in-vessel phase through the combination of decontamination in the ice
chests and the isolation of the lower containment from the large upper dome. Sequences 4a to 4f
resulted in a factor of 10 to 100 reduction in the aerosol mass ratio by the end of the in-vessel phase.
Note, Sequences 4a and 4b included containment sprays in injection mode until the water storage
tank emptied, which did not show a significant impact on the airborne aerosol mass ratio due to the
isolation of the lower containment from the upper containment. Consequently, any airborne
aerosols in the lower containment are not directly impacted by the sprays.

4.3.2. Effect of Aerosol Shape Factor
The airborne mass ratio and settling coefficients versus the aerosol shape factor magnitude are
shown in Figure 4-14 through Figure 4-17. The aerosol shape factor impacts both the aerodynamic
drag and the agglomeration. The two effects move in unison with longer chains of aerosols. A larger
aerosol shape factor increases the aerodynamic drag, which decreases the Stokes settling velocity.
However, the larger aerosol shape factor also promotes more agglomeration, which leads to larger
aerosols that settle faster. These are competing effects depending on the potential for agglomeration.
The impact is very clear in the in-vessel phase of the accident where all sequences show more
settling with a larger shape factor. The enhanced agglomeration from large releases into the lower
containment compartments was more important than the slower settling velocity. This evident with
the decreasing airborne mass ratio with an increasing shape factor (see Figure 4-14) and a decreasing
airborne settling coefficient (see Figure 4-16). In comparison to the long-term results, the short-term
results show a smaller spread of results and more consistent trends.
The long-term behavior has mixed results with some sequences showing more settling
(i.e., agglomeration is more important) and some showing less settling (i.e., the slower deposition
velocity is more important). The overall trend during the long-term phase across all sequences is
consistent with the Surry SOARCA Uncertainty Assessment results [24], which is a slower long-term
deposition rate with larger shape factors. In low aerosol density regions of the containment with low
agglomeration potential (e.g., the containment dome), the higher shape factor leads to significantly
slower settling rates. Unlike the in-vessel results, the airborne mass ratios and settling coefficients
span many orders of magnitude as a function of the specific sequence and have some mixed trends.
Nevertheless, the overall trend considering all cases resulted in a net decreasing settling rate that
characterized the importance of this uncertainty parameter.

4.4. Curve Fitting Analysis
SAND2023-01313 established the phase timings and source terms from a statistical process called
bootstrapping [4]. The study and the methodology were reviewed by an expert committee. The
durations of the gap and in-vessel phases were established in SAND2023-01313 (Table 4-6). In
contrast, the 1600 calculated results in this study had unique timings and results. Consequently, there
is a requirement to reconcile the UQ results from the current study with the results established in
SAND2023-01313. This study must provide (a) statistically informed settling rates that replicate the
calculated results from the mechanistic MELCOR accident progression calculations and (b) be
consistent with the phase timings from SAND2023-01313 for regulatory applications.
As the current study progressed, it became clear that the predicted accident progressions included
diverse results and extreme discrete events (e.g., PRT rupture disk opening, hot leg creep rupture,
accumulator discharge) that resulted in very complicated and discontinuous instantaneous settling
rates. The in-vessel phase was particularly complicated with simultaneous radionuclide additions to
the containment and aerosol settling. In contrast, the regulatory applications assume constant
radionuclide mass additions during each phase and lack the complex and diverse containment
thermal-hydraulics present in the various sequences. For these reasons, post-processing was
necessary to achieve the same statistically-calculated airborne aerosol mass fractions at the end of
each phase.
To achieve the objective of specified airborne mass fractions at the end of each phase, three key
post-processing approaches were used. The gap phase is a somewhat artificial distinction with a
long-standing historical precedence for the AST, so it is maintained in SAND2023-01313 and
accommodated here. The recommended approach is discussed in Section 4.4.1. The in-vessel phase,
which includes almost all the radionuclide release to the containment, preserved the aerosol airborne
mass fraction endstate from the statistical median of all 1600 cases at the transition to the long-term
phase. The in-vessel aerosol settling coefficient is derived to be consistent with the phase endstate
results from this study and the phase duration from SAND2023-01313, which is explained in
Section 0.
The long-term phase starts at the conclusion of the in-vessel phase and the recovery of emergency
core cooling systems. The long-term phase results required some special care to address diverse
results from the 1600 UQ calculation. Each calculation had a unique endstate at the transition from
the in-vessel phase to the long-term phase. Consequently, the airborne results were renormalized by
the amount airborne mass at the start of the long-term phase (i.e., an airborne ratio of 1 at the start
of the long-term phase) to calculate the settling rate during this phase. The approach to the
long-term phase settling coefficient evaluation is presented in Section 4.4.34.4.3.

Finally, the long-term phase approach used a different post-processing method to achieve a
re-normalized airborne settling rate that matched the median of the calculated results. The long-term
phase is from hours 5.3 and beyond and immediately follows the conclusion of the in-vessel phase
(see Table 4-6). The long-term phase results used curve fits from the median long-term phase
aerosol settling coefficient rate. However, this required some renormalization to remove the starting
point bias on the long-term phase results from the 1600 in-vessel phase endstates. The following
specifications were used to achieve the desired results.
 1. The mass scaling of each long-term phase result was renormalized to the airborne aerosol
 mass at the start of the long-term phase. Consequently, every long-term phase result started
 with,
 Mairborne/Mlong-term phase = 1
 2. The long-term phase time scale was also renormalized to be time after the end of the
 in-vessel phase. Therefore, the starting point for every long-term phase result was,
 Mairborne/Mlong-term phase (tlong-term phase= 0 sec)= 1
 3. Using the renormalized, time-dependent history of each result, new long-term phase

 phase airborne mass ratio histories, the integral long-term phase settling coefficients can be

 long-term phase (i.e., 32 hours after the end of the in-vessel phase). Specifically, the integral

 54

 value contains the history of the airborne aerosol mass ratio to any time (i.e., not possible

 4. The time-dependent median result from the renormalized long-term phase integral settling

5. EFFECT OF SPRAYS
The historical technical basis for aerosol removal by the containment sprays was established in
NUREG/CR-5966 near the publication of NUREG-1465 and the initial RG 1.183 AST
guidance [32]. A limited scope sensitivity study was performed to benchmark the MELCOR
containment response model to the historical guidance in NUREG/CR-5966. Section 5.1 shows a
comparison of the MELCOR spray model to an example calculation provided in the study. Due
differences in the containment designs that impact spray effectiveness, the ice condenser and large
dry containment spray results are presented separately. Section 5.2 presents a limited uncertainty
quantification using the ice condenser model with spray operation throughout the long-term phase.
Similarly, Section 5.3 presents a limited uncertainty quantification using the large dry containment
model with spray operation throughout the long-term phase.
The spray sensitivity study in Sections 5.2 and 5.3 are reported separately for the two plant designs
for two reasons. First, the ice condenser model already benefits from aerosol decontamination in the
ice chests. Second, the upper dome portion of the ice condenser containment where the sprays are
located is isolated lower containment. This is a design feature to promote flow through the ice
chests. Consequently, the sprays droplets cannot reach the ice chests or the lower containment. In
contrast, there are pass-through regions in the large dry containment where droplets can fall into
lower containment compartments.

5.1. Sensitivity Study on Effect of Sprays Using Example 1 from
          NUREG/CR-5966
NUREG/CR-5966 provided a comprehensive evaluation of parameters impacting the effectiveness
of the containment sprays. A mechanistic model based on current knowledge of the physical
phenomena was developed. The study used a Monte Carlo method to sample 20 uncertain quantities
related to the physics associated with the spray droplet behavior as well as the initial and boundary
conditions expected to be associated with severe reactor accidents. The results of the uncertainty
analysis were used to construct simplified expressions for the spray decontamination coefficients.
The resulting spray removal rate is a parametric expression that varies with the spray flowrate, the
spray fall height, and the airborne concentration. The parametric expression fits alternate
coefficients to capture the 10%, median, and 90% spray removal rate effectiveness. Tabular values
and two examples are provided in the report.
The first step was to compare the spray removal rate from MELCOR for an example provided in
the report. Example 1 simulated a containment with spray headers located 3000 cm above the
operating floor. It has an atmospheric loading of aerosols of 10 g/m3 at the end of significant
radionuclide release. The sprays provide a water flux of 0.10 cm3-H2O/cm2-s. The spray droplets
pass through half the containment volume. The example calculates the time to reduce the aerosol
concentration to 0.1 g/m3, or a decontamination factor of 100.
A MELCOR test model was constructed with the same specifications. After 4 pages of equations
and evaluations, the calculated results are presented in NUERG/CR-5966 (Table 32). The
comparison of the calculated results from NUTRG/CR-5966 to the MELCOR calculation is shown
in Figure 5-1. The NUREG/CR-5966 results include the 10%, median, and 90% results to reach
decontamination factors of 10, 100, 1000, and 10,000. The MELCOR results closely follow the
median results through a decontamination factor of 1000 before showing a slightly slower removal
rate to a decontamination factor of 10,000. Note, the airborne aerosol concentration is extremely
low at 0.01 g/m3 and below (i.e., a decontamination factor of 1000 and greater). The sprays are less

 66

effective at the various removal mechanisms of impaction, interception, and diffusion with very
small aerosol concentrations.
Overall, the MELCOR results agree with the median results from NUREG/CR-5966. This single
comparison suggests the MELCOR best-estimate results are well represented by the median result
from NUREG/CR-5966. The 10% value from NUREG/CR-5966 shows very low effectiveness of
the sprays, which is from the bias of limiting uncertainty variables that significantly degrade the spray
effectiveness. The other insight is the effectiveness of the sprays under the specified conditions. In
just 30 minutes, the aerosol concentration is a factor 1000 lower.

5.2. Limited Uncertainty Quantification Using the Ice Condenser Sequences
Next a limited uncertainty quantification was performed using the ice condenser model. The scope
of the study was limited to the uncertainty parameters presented in Section 5.2. Of the 20 spray
uncertainty parameters identified in the NUREG/CR-5966, two parameters were common with the
current study (i.e., aerosol size and aerosol shape factor).
The sprays were assumed to start after the in-vessel phase and run for the remainder of the
long-term phase. In the comparisons in this section, the other natural mechanisms of aerosol settling
are also active (i.e., gravitational settling, thermophoresis, diffusiophoresis, and Brownian motion).

 67

Consequently, the spray aerosol removal mechanisms of impaction, interception, and diffusion are
also supplemented by various other natural deposition processes.
The spray results showed almost no impact for the lower flowrate (i.e., compare Figure 5-2 without
sprays to Figure 5-3 with a 0.001 cm3-H2O/cm2-s spray flowrate). However, there is an observable
impact at the higher flow rate (i.e., compare Figure 5-2 without sprays to Figure 5-4 with a
0.01 cm3-H2O/cm2-s spray flowrate).9 The impact is more significant for the LOCA sequences than
the SBO sequences. The spray region in the ice condenser dome typically only contains very small
aerosols that passed from the lower containment through the ice chests to the upper containment.
The small aerosols stay airborne longer and can impact the overall containment aerosol settling rate.
The sprays are only in the dome region, so sequence-specific effects that transmit more aerosols to
the dome show more benefit from spray operation.
The calculated median decontamination coefficient for the 0.01 cm3/cm2-s spray flowrate in Table 9
from NUREG/CR-5966 to reach a decontamination factor of 1000 is 4.5e-4 s-1. The 4.5e-4 s-1
settling rate corresponds to 4.5 hr to reach a decontamination level of 1000. In comparison of the
natural settling rate from Figure 5-2 with the 0.01 cm3/cm2-s spray flowrate in Figure 5-4, the
NUREG/CR-5966 spray decontamination rate is approximately consistent to the lower grouping of
SBO sequences but low relative to the LOCA sequences. The cumulative benefit of the sprays and
the natural deposition mechanisms (esp., the ice chests) was especially impactful for the LOCA
sequences but somewhat less impactful for the SBO sequences.
The corresponding value from NUREG/CR-5966 for a 0.001 cm3/cm2-s spray flowrate from
Table 15 in NUREG/CR-5966 is a 6.6x10-5 s-1 settling rate that corresponds to 29 hr to reach a
decontamination level of 100 (see the NUREG/CR-5966 result on Figure 5-3). This value lies below
the natural deposition processes already present in the ice condenser results and illustrates the low
impact of the spray at a flow rate of 0.001 cm3/cm2-s.
In the higher flowrate case comparison, the effective decontamination rate (λe) is ~1.e-3 s-1 at 3 hr in
the LOCA sequences with a 0.01 cm3/cm2-s spray flowrate versus only ~2.e-4 s-1 at 3 hr without
sprays. The comparable impact on the SBO sequences is ~6.e-4 s-1 with sprays and 3.e-4 s-1 without
sprays. The effective decontamination rate (λe) includes the integral effects over time to reach a
particular decontamination level. The ratio of the effective decontamination rates with and without
sprays at 3 hr provides an indication of the net spray benefit versus the two sequences. A more
quantitative disccusion is provided with the large dry containment results in Section 5.3, which does
not include ice chsests or isolation of the lower containment from the upper containment.

6. RECOMMENDATIONS
There are several key recommendations from this study. These recommendations stem from the
objective to best represent the results of the mechanistic MELCOR calculations for regulatory
applications that lack models for thermal-hydraulics and aerosol physics present in a severe accident.
For example, the source term to the containment from the mechanistic MELCOR calculations does
not arrive uniformly, as the simplified source term used in regulatory applications (e.g., TID-14844,
NUREG-1465, and the more recent SAND2023-01313 [4]). The sequences comprising the new
alternate source term (AST) (RG 1.183) include diverse events and timings that are statistically
sampled into a representative median response. The sampled integral releases and timings do not
match any single sequence, which was a requirement of RG 1.183. The regulatory applications of the
AST also do not represent the diverse thermal-hydraulic conditions, location of the source to the
containment, and discrete events such as hot leg creep rupture failure, pressurizer relief tank rupture
disk opening, and the accumulator discharge. However, all these effects are present in the
mechanistic MELCOR calculations and embedded into the various sequence results.
The results presented in this report encompass various accident progressions that have diverse
results and timings whereas the AST is an amalgamation of representative effects that must be
distilled down into recommendations for applications that require prescribed results (i.e., prescribed
timings and magnitudes of the source term phases, prescribed containment pressure responses,
prescribed aerosol settling rates, prescribed spray removal effectiveness, and plant-specific leakages).
Recommendation #1: The gap and in-vessel aerosol settling rate should be specified, consistent with
this study, to provide the correct boundary conditions for the long-term response. It is
recommended to form a single aerosol settling coefficient, λe,in-vessel, that is consistent with the timing
from SAND2023-01313 (see Table 6-1). The λe, in-vessel will calculate the same non-dimensional ratio
of airborne mass to total mass in the containment at the end of the in-vessel phase that was
predicted in the current MELCOR calculations and provide the correct boundary conditions for the
long-term response.
The current results of the MELCOR in-vessel phase accident progression show the impact of
location-specific sources to the containment with co-dispersed steam, water, fog droplets, and
hydrogen. All the sequences include the accumulator discharge into core conditions ranging from
hot intact fuel to a highly degraded configuration during the in-vessel phase. The results from the
uncertainty study show large bursts of steam and water to the containment, which led to significant
settling prior to the long-term phase relative to the historical guidance in NUREG/CR-6189.
While the long-term phase results show increased settling from this study, the greatest change
occurred during the in-vessel phase with the simultaneous dispersion of steam, water, fog droplets,
and hydrogen with released radionuclides into the sequence specific compartment locations. The
calculated in-vessel phase settling coefficients from the accident sequences required post processing
to match the timings in SAND2023-01313, which will be described next.
The effective aerosol settling coefficient, or λe, implies a rate process. However, the timings of the
various sequences are diverse, and no single sequence aligns with statistically processed results and
timings in SAND2023-01313. The in-vessel phase is further complicated by simultaneous aerosol
additions to the containment with concurrent settling. Due to these complications, a single aerosol
settling coefficient, λe,in-vessel, is recommended for the entire in-vessel phase using the in-vessel phase
timing in SAND2023-01313. Since the amount of aerosol released to the containment during the
gap phase is <1% of the total aerosol mass, it is further recommended that λgap release is 0 s-1 and only

settling during the 4-hr in-vessel phase is specified. Consequently, the calculation of λearly in-vessel is
based on a 4-hr interval and results in the statistically evaluated Mairborne/Mtotal predicted in the current
MELCOR calculations at the end of the in-vessel phase.

Recommendation #2: The median values of the settling rate should be used to best represent the
diverse accident sequences and plant designs considered in this study. While the 10th percentile
settling rate (i.e., lower settling, more conservative regarding unsettled aerosols within the
containment) have been used in previous studies and recommendations, the current MELCOR
results show strong dependencies on both the sequence specifications and the plant design. The
effect is so significant that the 10% and 90% MELCOR uncertainty results are dominated by one
plant type and one or two similar sequences. Using the 10% and 90% values would neither meet the
RG 1.183 AST requirements nor represent the spectrum of results considered in this study. For this
reason and consistent with RG 1.183 AST requirements, the median value is recommended. Because
the median result is derived from a diverse set of sequences and plant features, the median is
well-considered to include the effects of all sequences.

only one or two control volumes and associated heat structures model were varied. The analysis

wide variations in the magnitude of the source term via a multiplier addressed or replaced the need

does not vary due to PWR containment type or thermal power rating.
