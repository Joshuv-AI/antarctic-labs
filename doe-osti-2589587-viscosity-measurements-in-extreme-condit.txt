ABSTRACT
Radiation-hydrodynamics simulations are critical to the NNSA complex for understanding high
energy density physics and for addressing problems in national security science. However, current
rad-hydro codes typically do not account for viscosity, which generally leads to large uncertainties
in problems involving mixing of materials under shock loading. This report details progress made
towards experimental measurements of fluid viscosity at high pressures and temperatures achieved
by shock wave compression techniques, as well as complementary material model development
and hydrodynamic simulations.

1. INTRODUCTION

The problem of mixing occurs throughout high energy density science and is especially relevant
for understanding inertial confinement fusion and planetary science, among many others. The
Reynolds number, Re, is a dimensionless parameter that describes the character of fluid flow:

where 𝜌, 𝑢, 𝐿, 𝜇 are the density, flow speed, length scale, and viscosity of the fluid, respectively. Flu-
ids with small Re exhibit laminar flow, while larger values correspond to turbulent flow. Viscosity,
then, sets the mixing timescale of fluids because turbulent flow results in fast mixing. Simulations
examining these problems typically ignore viscosity or include it parametrically in a “mix-model”
because viscosity is poorly constrained by a lack of high fidelity data for thermodynamic regimes
of interest. Consequently, simulations typically do not, in general, accurately capture the physics of

where 𝜇 (also commonly denoted 𝜂) is the shear viscosity and 𝜁 is the bulk viscosity. Due to
extraordinary computational complexity, many hydrocodes do not solve the full Navier-Stokes
(NS) equations, but instead the so-called Euler equations, which are obtained from NS by assuming
zero viscosity and thermal conductivity. Importantly, these codes typically employ a “numerical”
or “artificial” viscosity, which is unphysical but is required to accurately model the propagation
of shock waves on a discrete grid of points with finite spacing [5, 6, 7]. This numerical viscosity
is distinct from the physical viscosity, which is an intrinsic material property that depends on the
thermodynamic state of the material. The difference is perhaps most clearly seen by noting that
in the limit of an infinitely dense mesh, the artificial viscosity required to propagate a shock wave
smoothly approaches zero, but the physical viscosity remains unchanged.
Although a proper viscous treatment is important for accurately predicting phenomena like insta-
bility growth rates, material mixing rates, and dissipation length scales, practical calculations are
stymied by a lack of knowledge of viscosity for most materials under high pressure and temperature
conditions. Transport coefficients have been a focus of atomistic simulations from the earliest
days of the field. Yet when it comes to viscosity, more attention has been paid to model sys-
tems such as the hard-sphere liquid and one-component plasma than any particular “real” material
[8, 2, 9, 3, 10, 11, 12, 4].
Previous experimental efforts to measure viscosity at pressures above several gigapascals (GPa)
have focused on various schemes of measuring the decay of a non-planar shock wave or interface

 13

[13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. But because these measurements are very difficult to reproduce and have
lacked verification via other techniques their reliability is difficult to ascertain. This is perhaps
most clearly seen in the case of water, for which the shockwave estimates of the viscosity near 10
GPa are a factor of about 106 larger than those from other methods [19, 16, 20, 21]. On the other
hand, similar experiments to determine the strength of solid metals seem to have enjoyed much
more success [22, 23].
This report outlines progress in the development of a new experimental technique to measure the
attenuation of a non-planar shock front in time and space using photon-doppler velocimetry (PDV).
This technique demonstrates an important first step towards measuring viscosity of fluids at high
pressures and temperatures. The experiments were carried out on fused silica (SiO2 ), which is a
transparent oxide commonly used both as a high pressure standard and window material in many
dynamic compression experiments [24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. A principal goal of the project was to
do more than obtain data in regimes where accuracy may be difficult to judge. In addition to the
experimental developments we also carried out complementary ab-initio calculations of viscosity
along the principal Hugoniot states of fused silica in order to provide a baseline estimate for planned
experiments and beyond. We also implemented a physical viscosity model in the magnar hydrocode
to better understand how viscous effects modify the shock front and attenuation rate, as well as
inviscid ALEGRA simulations designed to guide the experimental design. Finally, through the use
of a university partnership LDRD, an independent set of viscometry experiments were carried out.
These experiments included the first-ever high-pressure measurements of the viscosity of pure SiO2
and provide a crucial constraint on the pressure dependence of the viscosity of the fluid.

2. MATERIAL MODEL DEVELOPMENT

2.1. Density Functional Theory based molecular dynamics calculations

A series of Density Functional Theory based molecular dynamics (DFTMD) calculations were
carried out in order to compute the shear viscosity of liquid SiO2 and beryllium at high pressures
and temperatures. Generally speaking, our overall approach follows a well-established workflow
outlined in our previous work [30, 29, 31, 32, 33]. All calculations were carried out using the VASP
(version 6.3.2) code, an implementation of Kohn-Sham DFT using periodic boundary conditions
and a plane wave basis [34, 35, 36]. In order to increase the efficiency of the calculations projector-
augmented pseudopotentials were used with valence configurations of 3𝑠2 3𝑝 2 for Si, 2𝑠2 2𝑝 4 for
O, and 1𝑠2 2𝑠2 for Be. The plane wave basis used an energy cutoff of 800 eV for both SiO2 and Be
calculations. Beryllium calculations were carried out in a 128 atom cubic supercell and sampled
the Brillouin zone at a single point, 𝑘 = (0.25, 0.25, 0.25). SiO2 calculations were carried out in a
120 atom cubic supercell and sampled the Brillouin zone at the gamma point, 𝑘 = 0.
The viscosity calculation workflow consisted of a set of MD calculations in the canonical ensemble
to obtain a well equilibrated liquid structure which was then sampled to select uncorrelated snapshots
from which independent microcanonical calculations were carried out and from which viscosity was
estimated [37]. For the microcanonical calculations the energy is not exactly conserved. Therefore
these calculations required a much smaller timestep in order to obtain accurate results. In the case
of SiO2 the timestep for these simulations were 1.0 and 0.05 femtoseconds, respectively, while for
beryllium, timesteps of 0.2 and 0.05 femtoseconds were used.

2.3. Significance of the shear viscosity in nuclear fusion applications

Beryllium is a crucial material for the Magnetized Liner Inertial Fusion (MagLIF) platform that is
actively being studied at Sandia National Laboratories. In the MagLIF platform, a hollow cylindrical
of beryllium—called a liner—compresses fusion fuel through a Z-pinch produced by Sandia’s Z
machine. The material properties of the beryllium liner control the development of hydrodynamic
instabilities that disrupt neutron production and reduce the quality of the experiment. Experiments
are simulated using magnetohydrodynamic codes that require material models as input. Two thrusts
of this LDRD were 1) assessing the quality of our approaches for generating material properties
quickly and accurately, and 2) estimating the values of the viscosity of the beryllium liner throughout
the implosion.
In Sec. 2.3.1, we detail the phase space that a beryllium liner traverses throughout the MagLIF
implosion. As the liner spans orders of magnitude in both temperature and density, our models
must be accurate across this wide space. Then, in Sec. 2.3.2, we use a variety of approaches for
estimating the shear viscosity of beryllium at the conditions relevant to the MagLIF experiment. We

 23

find that the viscosity increases during the implosion implying that the viscosity is more significant
later in time.

morphology of the imploding beryllium liner that compresses and confines the fuel. An enormous

or thermal conductivity, these differences can be much larger. [49] The two MD approaches should
be used complementary—DFT-MD are accurate and more easily produced at lower temperatures.
In contrast, the AA-MD data are more accurate at higher temperatures and densities.
We note that visualizing these data are difficult because at each position in time, the temperature
and density changes for the beryllium liner. As a result, the density and temperature at a given
location on the liner is parameterized by time. The viscosity as a function of time for the different
approaches is displayed in Fig. 2-10. First consider the data produced via MD. We note that at
early time, the viscosity from DFT-MD is much higher than the data produced from AA-MD. The
results between the two MD approaches improves as time increases. This is mainly due to the fact
that the average-atom model becomes more accurate at high density and high temperature.
Using a variety of analytic models, that generate data rapidly across a large density and temperature
range, we see that the models are in modest agreement with the MD results. Namely, the SMT
model agrees with the AA-MD data across nearly the entire time space. However, the YGBI model
appears to well-approx the DFT-MD data at early time. These agreements are due in part to the
approximations used in developing them. From this comparison, we have confidence in their use for
producing look-up tables for implementation in hydrodynamic codes—a topic that will be discussed
in Sec. 3.

3. HYDRODYNAMIC SIMULATIONS

3.1. Viscous Shock Modeling in Magnar

In this section we consider single material, inviscid and viscous evolution of sinusoidally perturbed
shock waves. This work can divided into two pieces. In the first part we study perturbed shock wave
dynamics in an ideal gas with a goal of qualitatively describing the post-shock hydrodynamics, the
dependence on the amplitude of the shock perturbation and the role of shear and bulk viscosities.
In the second part we consider the dynamics in silica using the SESAME equation of state table
7361 and a range of shear viscosities.

3.1.1. Numerical Method Overview

The numerical simulations presented in this section are calculated in a simulation code called
Magnar. At a high level, Magnar is a second order Godunov, finite volume method. The calculations
here use a piecewise linear version of the PPM method [50] and include transverse flux corrections
following the CTU method [51]. Simulations presented below with an ideal gas are performed
with an exact Riemann solver and those with silica use a multi-state, numerical Riemann solver
in preparation for publication. The viscous simulations are calculated using Godunov splitting,
alternating between an evolution of Euler’s equations and a viscous stress update. The shear and
bulk viscosities are treated as constant and the integration method is a simple, two-step predictor /
corrector method where the velocity gradient tensor is calculated using finite differences on node
centers.
The viscous stress update equation is described by a parabolic system of equations and because the
integration method is explicit, it has a linear stability constraint that scales like

3.1.2. Simulation Overview, Initial and Boundary Conditions

The problem setup in this section follows the same basic configuration as the linear theory presented
in [15]. We consider a two-dimensional domain with initially piece-wise constant states separated
by a sinusoidal boundary 𝑥 𝑏 = 𝐴 sin(𝑘 𝑦), where A is the initial amplitude of the perturbation.
Consistent with a small amplitude shock perturbation, we choose these two states to be connected
by the Rankine-Hugoniot relations for a one-dimensional shock wave with a given shock velocity
𝑈𝑠 and particle velocity 𝑈 𝑝 . We consider a shock wave that is propagating in the +𝑥 direction
and a reference frame in which the shock wave is at rest. Thus, the pre-shock state, denoted by
subscript 0 and located to the right of the boundary (𝑥 > 𝑥 𝑏 ) has an initial velocity 𝑢 𝑥0 = −𝑈𝑠 and
the post-shock state, denoted by subscript 1 and located to the left of the boundary (𝑥 < 𝑥 𝑏 ) has an
initial velocity 𝑢 𝑥1 = 𝑈 𝑝 − 𝑈𝑠 . The simulation domain is (−2𝜆 ≤ 𝑥 ≤ 2𝜆) and (−𝜆/2 ≤ 𝑦 ≤ 𝜆/2)
with standard inflow / outflow boundary conditions on the 𝑥-boundaries and periodic boundary
conditions on the 𝑦-boundaries. This simulation domain is chosen to be sufficiently large that the
viscous stress has minimal impact on the inflow 𝑥-boundary.

and neglecting viscosity. To simplify the problem setup, we choose a length scale such that the
wavelength 𝜆 = 1, a mass scale such that the pre-shock mass density 𝜌0 = 1, and a time-scale such

4. ALEGRA SIMULATIONS

In this section we discuss inviscid modeling and simulation results. Preliminary modeling helped
assess prior work and choose experimental parameters for this work. Predictive modeling of the
present configuration was used to evaluate diagnostic configurations. Finally, postdictive modeling
was used to evaluate the conditions attained in the present experiments.

4.1. Experimental Configuration

The basis for this work is a planar shock impact on a corrugated sample, as described from
experimental work in 1960’s USSR and reported in 1990’s USA [15]. The common features
include a system to generate a planar shock, samples with a sinusoidal “corrugation” on the impact
surface, and a diagnostic system to measure the spatially and temporally varying profile of the
shock as it interacts with the corrugation. The basic approach and analysis continue to be used on
more materials and diagnostic configurations [16][52][53].

The experimental schematic for this LDRD is illustrated in Fig. 4-1. A two-stage gas gun (2)
accelerates a driver plate with an attached tantalum flyer disk (2). The ballistic flyer impacts on
a thin tantalum foil target (3), launching symmetric shocks into both flyer and target. The shock
transmits to a corrugated surface (4), launching a sinusoidal shock in the SiO2 sample. A set of
PDV or VISAR probes (5) is used to measure the shock velocity over an array of points in the
sample. As the shock propagates into the sample, the sinusoidal perturbations eventually decay to
a planar shock.

4.2. Baseline Simulation

The Alegra finite element program is used to model flyer impact and shock propagation [54].
Alegra is designed and validated to model shock hydrodynamics and has capabilities to model
interacting materials with extendable equations of state. An acknowledged limitation is that Alegra
“excludes any notion of physical fluid viscosity” [54]. Therefore, these simulations represent the
inviscid limit of real physical processes. Comparison of the model predictions with viscid model
predictions and experimental measurements is expected to identify differences due to viscosity.
The simulations described here use the Eulerian formulation, where materials move through a
static grid. This allows arbitrary initial material placement and allows voids between materials to
close freely. The desired overall size of the corrugated sample is approximately 3.5 mm along the
direction of shock propagation and 10 mm in each perpendicular direction. For a desired resolution
of 10 µm, this leads to impractically large 3D simulations (∼350M elements or 35k cores), but
routine 2D simulations (∼350k elements or 35 cores).
In practice, the geometry can be trimmed closer to the corrugated region and the resolution can be
relaxed away from the interface. Both changes reduce the computational cost without adversely
affecting the accuracy. Presently available High Performance Computing resources have higher
processor counts, such as the Flight cluster with 112 cores per node. Running these simulations on
additional cores decreases the wall time, at a negligible efficiency cost.
The initial conditions are set by using Alegra “diatom insert” commands to place materials in the
domain. The corrugated sample is modeled in SolidWorks and exported as an STL file, which is
then loaded into the simulation with zero velocity. The flyer and foil are inserted as box objects
in the Alegra input deck, with impact velocity for the flyer and zero velocity for the foil. For
convenience, the flyer is placed directly adjacent to the foil, so that time zero in the simulation is
the instant of flyer impact on the foil.
The free parameters for the simulations are the flyer impact velocity, the corrugation wavelength
and amplitude, and material models.

4.3. Reference Simulations

Two reference simulations are performed to establish the validity of the Alegra models for the
desired experimental applications. This provides a point of comparison with prior work, and helps
characterize the length and time scales of the experiments.

The simulations diverge quickly even at early times, as shown in Fig. 4-7. The flyer arrives at
trough points at progressively later times for higher amplitude corrugations. Although each profile
of shock front and flyer surface resembles the previous reference simulation (c.f. Fig. 4-2), there
are already visible differences. In particular, note the concave curvature of the flyer face near the
troughs for amplitudes ≥50 µm.
An initially surprising observation is that simulations with the same amplitude to wavelength ratio
do not follow self-similar scaling. For example, the 40 µm amplitude at 100 µm wavelength and
the 80 µm amplitude at 200 µm wavelength are easily distinguished. This can be explained by
remembering that a single flyer velocity is used across all simulations. A self-similar solution
would require the flyer velocity to be scaled according to the amplitude and characteristic time.
However, due to non-linearity of the Rankine-Hugoniot relations, the shock speed would still not
scale linearly, so no self-similar solution should be expected.
At the intermediate times shown in Fig. 4-8, the simulations have separated into two groups. The
simulations with amplitudes ≤30 µm maintain a sinusoidal shock front. The simulations with
amplitudes ≥40 µm develop jets at the trough points and have non-sinusoidal shock fronts.
This occurs because the maximum angle between the flyer and sample becomes steeper at higher
amplitudes. At a critical angle, the oblique shock launched into the flyer causes material to deflect
toward the center of the trough points. As that material converges, it accelerates like a shape charge
jet. For amplitudes ≥60 µm, this jet reaches the trough point before the flyer and even before the
shock from the peak points. The resulting shock front is not sinusoidal, and is therefore not suitable
for the desired viscosity measurement.
At the late times shown in Fig. 4-9, the transient effects of the corrugation have passed and the
shock front in each simulation appears as a simple planar shock. However, at high amplitudes the
flyer and sample materials are mixed in patterns reminiscent of a Rayleigh-Taylor instability.

4.5. Synthetic Diagnostic Postprocessing

A challenge in evaluating these models for verification and validation is that experimental mea-
surements do not resolve all of the internal features with the same accessibility and resolution as a
simulation. One way to approach this problem is to take synthetic measurements of the simulation
in the same way that a diagnostic measures the real experiment.
Two main diagnostics are used for the experiments. Photon Doppler Velocimetry (PDV) and Visual
Interferometer System for Any Reflector (VISAR) both interpret light signals interacting with a
surface to determine the surface velocity. The specific details of the experimental methods are not
necessary for constructing analogous probes of the simulations. Instead, the relevant observable
feature (surface velocity) is processed according to features of the real diagnostic.

4.5.1. Singe Fiber PDV/VISAR Probe

Standard techniques for PDV and VISAR use individual fiber optic lines (“fibers”) pointed toward
the surface, placed close enough that the field of view is limited to a desired point. This configuration
is easily reproduced in the simulation by taking a single lineout through the sample at the desired
point. The lineout is then postprocessed using a standard shock-finding routine to determine the
apparent velocity of the interface as a function of time. This approach can be used to probe either
a witness surface or a reflective shock front, consistent with the physical diagnostics.
The output from this synthetic probe is a time history of velocity at the point.

4.5.2. Multi Fiber Array “Frankenfurter” Probe

The “Frankenfurter Probe” consists of 24 fibers in a hexagonal close packed array, with three rows
of 8 fibers as shown in Fig. 4-10. This configuration allows an array of points to be measured
simultaneously. The equivalent configuration is achieved in a 2D simulation by taking lineouts at
the array of desired points.
For simplicity, the center row of fibers is assigned to lie in the plane of the 2D domain. The
vertical offset of the other rows is handled differently depending on whether the observed surface
is a reflective shock or a witness material. For a reflective shock, the vertical separation can be
neglected since light freely traverses the sample. For a witness surface, the vertical position of the
probe is related to a shock arrival location according to the inclination angle of the wedge. The
lineout is postprocessed to mask any signal prior to shock arrival at the witness material, which is
equivalent to applying a minimum time filter on the lineout.
The Frankenfurter probe is also subject to small alignment errors, on the order of 0.2 mm and 1°
relative to the position and orientation of the sample corrugation. This introduces a systematic
error in the relative positions (and time filters) of the lineouts in the simulation. Since the Alegra
simulation has properties over the full 2D domain, the synthetic probe alignment can be set and
adjusted after the simulation. In practice, a large number of postprocessing measurements at
different alignment positions and angles can be made on a single simulation. Then one optimal

This synthetic probe outputs up to 25 time histories of velocity at each of the fiber points.

4.5.3. Diffuse Spot “Pigtail” Probe

Since the corrugation wavelength is comparable to the fiber spacing in the Frankenfurter probe,
an alternative single fiber technique was used. In this mode, a single fiber is pointed toward the
surface, but placed farther away so that the view angle encompasses an area on the surface. The
received signal blends all velocities over the field of view. The experimental analysis processes the
optical signal through a Fourier transform to obtain a power spectrum, and then maps frequency
bands to velocities.
This configuration is achieved in a 2D simulation by taking a number of lineouts across the diameter
of the desired field of view. The lineouts are assigned relative weights, computed as the product
of an effective area and a view angle factor. The effective area is found by dividing the projected
circular area into strips centered on each lineout point. If the lineouts are evenly spaced, the center
lineout has the largest area and the effective area decreases for lineouts on either side. The view
angle factor is computed from Lambert’s cosine law, using the relative angle between the shock
front normal and the lineout.
Since velocities are already available in the simulation lineouts, the additional steps of integrating
the weighted lineouts and performing a Fourier analysis of the average signal are not necessary.
Given the large uncertainties in choosing the weights and view angle factor, it is more straightforward
to use the envelope of velocities computed over the set of lineouts.
This synthetic probe outputs two time histories representing the envelope of minimum and maximum
velocities over the field of view.

5.3. Summary of results and other lessons learned

The experimental campaigns at the STAR facility and the ISP (summarized in Table 5-1) were
extremely helpful in identifying a suitable experimental configuration capable of providing data
for comparison with hydrocode simulations. The best results were obtained for pigtail style PDV
probes which utilize multiple, tightly packed bare fibers. The central-most fiber is used to deliver
laser light to the probed region (send fiber), while adjacent fibers are used to collect the reflected
laser light (receive fibers). Care must be taken to ensure that the probe is as close to the rear of the
fused silica sample as possible to minimize back reflections from the rear of the sample.
The ISP campaign also provided valuable insight into target fabrication. The initial targets were
assembled by pooling epoxy on the tantalum base plate and bringing the corrugated fused silica
sample down onto the base plate. This procedure was found to result in numerous bubbles in the
corrugation region (see Fig. 5-14), presumably caused by air that was trapped in the corrugations
as the sample was brought down onto the epoxy from above. This procedure was changed for
later experiments. First, the corrugated sample was placed down first such that the corrugated
surface was facing up. Second, the epoxy (Angstrombond) was degassed after mixing using a
vacuum pump and bell jar. A needle syringe was then used to over fill the corrugation with the
degassed epoxy (i.e., the epoxy was puddle well over the corrugated region. Finally, the tantalum
(or sapphire) base plate was carefully lowered onto the corrugated sample and was clamped during
the curing process. This procedure minimized the possibility for air to be trapped in the corrugation
region and substantially reduced the number and size of bubbles.

6. IN-SITU HIGH PRESSURE FALLING SPHERE VISCOMETRY

This chapter summarizes results from a University Partnership LDRD awarded to Prof. Alisha
Clark at the University of Colorado Boulder and are currently being prepared for publication in
S. Rosenfeld et al., "In-situ high pressure viscometry of SiO2 melts", High Pressure Research, (in
preparation).

6.1. Introduction

The physical properties of silica (SiO2 ) liquids are of fundamental importance in condensed matter
physics as well as materials and planetary sciences. As an important end-member of the magnesium-
iron-silicate system, the transport properties of liquid silica are of special interest due to their
influence on heat and mass transfer in terrestrial planets. At one-atmosphere, the viscosity of
amorphous silica displays Arrhenius behavior where there is a linear relationship between viscosity
and the temperature above the glass transition temperature (𝑇𝑔 ), which is defined as the temperature
at which the viscosity is 1012 Pas [44].

Despite this, the viscosity at high pressure has been unmeasured to date due to several experimental
challenges. Silica has a high melting temperature above 2000 K at one-atmosphere, which makes
it difficult to contain and therefore investigate. The most common and reliable experimental
technique for high pressure viscosity measurements is in-situ multi-anvil falling sphere viscometry
[45, 46, 47]. This technique employs the large-volume press to confine a sample (approximately
1 cubic millimeter volume) at pressures up to several gigapascals for hours at a time. While
pressurized, the sample is heated by a resistive heating technique and the sample is interrogating
by X-ray imaging. Here we describe the first ever high pressure measurements of the viscosity of
liquid SiO2 obtained from falling sphere viscometry experiments carried out in the large volume
press. The results indicate that the pressure dependence of the viscosity is larger than that of more
chemically complex silicates. The results obtained here will be used in combination with results
discussed elsewhere in this report to develop a wide-ranging viscosity model for liquid SiO2 .

6.2. Results

Viscosity measurements of liquid SiO2 were carried out under atmospheric pressures (105 Pa) as
well as at high pressure (109 Pa). Both sets of experiments were performed with the same sample
material: high-purity fused silica manufactured by Corning Incorporated with lower than detectible
(<1 ppm) OH content (Corning Glass Code 8655).

6.2.2. Falling sphere experiments at high pressure

Falling sphere viscometry involves tracking the downward fall of a dense, inert sphere in the sample
melt to obtain the sphere’s terminal velocity, which yields a measurement of the melt’s viscosity,
𝜇, via a simplified version of Stokes Law:

 2𝑔𝑟 2 Δ𝜌
 𝜇= (6.2)
 9𝑣
where 𝑔 is the gravitational constant, Δ𝜌 is the difference in densities between the melt and the
sphere, 𝑟 is the radius of the sphere, and 𝑣 is the terminal velocity of the sphere [56]. While
previous studies have often used the Faxén correction, which is added to account for wall effects
on the settling velocity of the sphere it has been shown to be unnecessary for high-pressure silicate
melt falling sphere viscosity experiments, where the size of the sphere relative to the size of the
capsule is small enough such that it does not experience any significant effects from the capsule
walls during its descent ([57, 58, 59, 60]).

To perform high pressure in situ falling sphere viscometry experiments, we used the multianvil
apparatus installed on the 28-ID-2 and 13-ID-D beamlines at NSLS-II and APS, respectively.
The mulitanvil press generates hydrostatic pressure by compressing a nest of 8 truncated tungsten

whole beampath. Modifications include a straight-walled furnace, rather than the original stepped

determined by X-ray diffraction of Au, MgO and BN. High temperatures are reached by resistively
heating the graphite heater and are measured by a type-C thermocouple. The sample capsule is
loaded with a 1mm tall 1mm diameter solid SiO2 glass cylinder with gaps filled in with the starting

7. CONCLUSIONS

As a result of this LDRD we are now able to calculate the viscosity of fluids and plasmas at high
pressures and temperatures with high-accuracy and precision. We have developed an experimental
platform that to measure the viscosity at conditions relevant to high-energy-density physics. And
we have implemented approaches for incorporating these data into design codes that support the
mission needs of the NNSA.
Prior to the work completed in this LDRD, the ability to systematically calculate the shear viscosity
from ab-initio simulations did not exist. This is a necessary step in developing models that
produce tables of these data across the wide temperature and density range that are required by
hydrodynamic and magnetohydrodynamic codes. With this new capability we are exploring the
potential applications to MagLIF experiments.
It is important that calculations of the viscosity from numerical approaches, e.g. MD or analytic
models, are validated against experimental data. To this end, we have developed a platform
aimed at measuring the viscosity to validate the approaches that are used to generate the datasets
that hydrocodes will utilize. The experiments outlined in this report lay a foundation for future
experimental efforts on the Z machine and other HED facilities. Moreover, data from the Z machine
will supplement the gas gun data in a physical regime that is not accessed by gas guns—providing
a dataset that will be used to validate models over a larger parameter space.
Finally, utilizing these data in magnetohydrodynamic simulations is non-trivial. Oftentimes, doing
so requires substantial changes to existing codes to account for the additional terms that appear in
the hydrodynamic equations of motion. A main difficulty we overcame was the limited capability to
simulate shock propagation through a viscous fluid, which we overcame by implementing physical
viscosity in a smaller hydrodynamics code.
Future work includes the implementation of a density- and temperature-dependent model of the
shear viscosity into hydrocodes that simulate nuclear fusion experiments. In particular, this model
and capability will support the ongoing efforts at Sandia National Laboratories.
