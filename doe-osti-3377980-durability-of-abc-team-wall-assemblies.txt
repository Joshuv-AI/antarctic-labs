 ABSTRACT

This study evaluates the long-term hygrothermal durability of four advanced retrofit wall systems using a
field test facility located in Hollywood, South Carolina, representative of a mixed-humid coastal climate
(Zone 3). The research focuses on assessing the thermal and moisture performance of a baseline wall
assembly retrofitted with (1) Tremco/Dryvit prefabricated panel systems (Revitalite and Fedderlite), (2)
Reinforced Fiberglass Plastic (RFP) panels developed by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), and (3)
Vacuum Insulated Panels (VIP) integrated with EPS by Home Innovation Research Labs (HIRL) and
ORNL. A baseline wall representing a typical uninsulated wood-frame construction was used for
comparison.

Over 24 months (starting from December 2023 to December 2025) of continuous field monitoring,
temperature, relative humidity, and heat flux data were collected at multiple locations in each wall system
using embedded sensors. In addition, outdoor environmental parameters, including temperature, relative
humidity, rainfall intensity, and solar radiation, were continuously monitored, while indoor temperature
and relative humidity were also recorded throughout the two-year period. Results demonstrated that all
retrofitted assemblies outperformed the baseline wall in terms of thermal performance and moisture
management. Because both the primary insulation materials and panel thicknesses differ, the resulting R-
values of the panels also vary. As expected, all configurations demonstrate substantially higher thermal
resistance compared to the baseline wall.

Hygrothermal performance was further evaluated using WUFI simulation to characterize the moisture
transport through the wall assemblies in ASHRAE’s Climate Zones 4 to 6. The simulation result shows the
retrofitted walls perform well under varying cavity insulation and vapor barrier scenarios.

 1. BACKGROUND

Improving the energy efficiency and moisture resilience of existing buildings is a growing priority,
especially in hot and high humidity locations. Retrofitting exterior wall systems with insulated cladding
panels offers a practical and scalable solution to reduce energy consumption and enhance indoor comfort
(Biega & Krarti, 2024, Dalkowski, 2024). However, concerns remain regarding the long-term moisture
durability and thermal effectiveness of these retrofits when exposed to real-world environmental conditions
(Maref et al. 2010, Conley et al. 2021). Although this study focuses on existing building retrofits, the results
can also support the design and evaluation of new construction using continuous exterior cladding systems.

To address this challenge, a collaborative field study was conducted at the Natural Exposure Testing (NET)
test facility in Hollywood, South Carolina, using full-scale wall assemblies exposed to natural weathering.
The project tested a baseline wall and four retrofit systems, each representing different material innovations
and construction techniques, installed side by side for direct performance comparison. This comprehensive
setup enabled accurate evaluation of their thermal regulation and moisture control capabilities against a
conventional baseline wall.

2.2 TYPES OF WALL SYSTEMS AND CONSTRUCTION

At the NET test facility, four different types of retrofitted wall assemblies were constructed and installed
side by side. These retrofitted walls were developed by different stakeholders to come up with retrofit
cladding solutions to enhance energy efficiency, moisture management, and long-term durability. To
provide a point of reference for evaluating the retrofit systems, an additional wall, referred to as the baseline
wall, was also constructed.

The baseline wall represents a typical uninsulated wood frame construction and consists of five layers: vinyl
siding as exterior cladding, spun bonded polyolefin membrane (SBP) house wrap, oriented strand board
(OSB) sheathing, an uninsulated 2x4 stud cavity, and a painted interior gypsum board.

The four retrofitted wall assemblies were developed and supplied by three different organizations: Tremco,
Home Innovation Research Labs, and Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). Each organization
contributed a different retrofit approach, reflecting distinct choices in material selection and assembly
design. These variations allow for a comprehensive comparison of different retrofit methods under identical
environmental conditions. Each retrofit panel system will be described in detail below.

2.2.1 Tremco/Dryvit Panel Systems

Tremco/Dryvit has developed a panelized retrofit system aimed at improving the building’s thermal
performance/energy efficiency. For this study, two types of their panel systems were used: the Fedderlite
and Revitalite panel systems. While both systems share similar core components, such as insulation, base
coat, reinforcing mesh, stucco finish, and channel guides, they differ primarily in their internal structural
support. Revitalite panels include a fiberglass internal framing member designed to support window

 3

installations, whereas Fedderlite panels rely solely on expanded polystyrene (EPS) insulation without
additional framing. Both systems were evaluated for their moisture management capabilities and overall
contribution to building envelope performance improvements. Both panels include paired rows of metal
channels attached to the back of the retrofit panels and anchored to the existing wall sheathing to facilitate
panel overhang and support. The metal channels are spaced at 24 in. on center in the intermediate sections
and positioned 6 in. on center from the top and bottom edges of the panels.

The Fedderlite panel system is a prefabricated, lightweight insulated façade solution designed for
retrofitting or recladding above-grade exterior walls. Lacking any internal structural framing, Fedderlite
panels consist solely of an EPS insulation core, making them extremely lightweight (approximately 2.5
lbs/ft²) and easy to handle. As the manufacturer’s list states, these panels are particularly suited for
applications where window integration is not required. The lightweight nature and factory-controlled
prefabrication process expedite installation [Dryvit product sheet]. Table 1 and Figure 3 show a typical

2.3 SENSOR LAYOUT AND INSTRUMENTATION

Temperature, relative humidity (RH), and heat flux transducer sensors were installed at various locations
within the wall assemblies to monitor hygrothermal and thermal performances. Combination
RH/temperature (RH/T) sensors were used throughout the wall layers, except for the exterior cladding and
interior side of the drywall. The exterior cladding and the interior face of the drywall were equipped with
thermistors to measure temperature only. RH sensors were placed on the exterior face of OSB sheathing
and the interior face of the drywall. At each of these locations, sensors were installed at three heights: 16
inches below the top of the wall, 16 inches above the bottom, and at the vertical midpoint. To avoid
interference from wall studs, all three sensor positions were horizontally offset by 1ft from the vertical
geometric centerline of the wall. Heat flux transducers were installed on the interior side of the drywall
across all wall systems, and on the exterior side of the sheathing for walls incorporating the ABC retrofit
panels. For the wall system with the VIP retrofit panel, an additional RH and temperature sensor was
installed on the OSB layer that covers the VIP panel, as shown in Figure 10(b). The sensor layouts for the
three retrofit wall systems—Revitalite, Fedderlite, and RFP panels—are similar and depicted in Figure
10(a). The sensor layout for the VIP wall system, which includes the additional instrumented layer, is shown
in Figure 10(b). The Baseline wall configuration and sensor locations are shown in Figure 10 (c).

2.5 THERMAL RESISTANCE ESTIMATION FROM FIELD MEASUREMENTS

The thermal estimation method is developed in accordance with ASTM C 1155 (Standard Practice for
Determining Thermal Resistance of Building Envelope Components from the In-Situ Data). A data-driven
approach was developed to estimate the apparent thermal resistance (R-value) of the wall assembly using
long-term field measurements of temperature and heat flux. The analysis was conducted on a two-year
dataset of hourly averaged indoor air temperature (𝑇𝑖𝑛), outdoor surface temperature (𝑇𝑜𝑢𝑡), the sheathing
board temperature (𝑇𝑜𝑠𝑏), and inward heat flux (𝑞𝑖𝑛) measured at the interior surface.

The thermal driving potential across the wall was defined as the temperature difference, Δ𝑇 = 𝑇𝑖𝑛 ― 𝑇𝑜𝑢𝑡,
calculated at each time step. To identify periods suitable for resistance estimation, a moving-window
filtering approach was applied to the full dataset. Specifically, consecutive 6 to 24-hour windows
(corresponding to 6 to 24 hourly data points) were extracted and evaluated against predefined steadiness
criteria. For each window, the mean temperature difference (Δ𝑇) and mean heat flux (𝑞) were computed.
Data with insufficient thermal driving force (i.e., ∣Δ𝑇∣ > 3.6°𝐹 ) were excluded to avoid instability in the
resistance calculation. ASTM C1155 requires sufficient thermal driving force to obtain stable heat flux
measurements but does not specify a minimum temperature difference threshold. Therefore, a conservative
threshold of 3.6°F (2°C) was adopted to minimize measurement uncertainty and improve the reliability of
the calculated thermal resistance.

To further ensure quasi-steady conditions, the temporal variability of both temperature difference and heat
flux within each window was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV). The CV was defined as the
ratio of the standard deviation to the absolute mean value for each variable. CV of certain variation were

temperature difference satisfied CVΔ𝑇 < 0.5. These thresholds were selected to filter out transient periods
characterized by rapid fluctuations in boundary conditions or measurement noise.

2.6 HYGROTHERMAL MODELING OF THE WALL SYSTEMS WITH RETROFIT PANELS

Hygrothermal simulations were conducted to evaluate the impact of exterior retrofit panels on wall system
performance. The analysis considered four representative climate zones and corresponding cities: Zone 3 –
Charleston, SC; Zone 4 – Baltimore, MD; Zone 5 – Chicago, IL; and Zone 6 – Minneapolis, MN.

Three retrofit panel configurations—RFP, VIP+EPS, and Fedderlite—were evaluated against baseline wall
assemblies. The baseline wall configurations varied in terms of cavity insulation and vapor control strategy,
including: (i) 2×4 stud cavities with cellulose insulation and (ii) uninsulated air cavities, combined with
either kraft-faced vapor retarders or no vapor barrier.

Simulations were performed over a three-year period using WUFI Software (WUFI Pro) with cold-year
climate data. Indoor boundary conditions were defined in accordance with ASHRAE’s Criteria for
Moisture-control Design Analysis in Buildings (ASHRAE Standard 160).

The baseline wall assembly consisted of vinyl siding, spun-bonded polyolefin (SBP), plywood sheathing,
a 2×4 wall cavity (with or without cellulose insulation), optional kraft paper vapor retarder, and interior
painted gypsum board.

Retrofit panels were installed on the exterior side of the existing sheathing system (SBP + plywood). The
performance of the retrofitted assemblies was compared to the baseline walls, with primary emphasis on
moisture durability. Specifically, the key evaluation metric across all scenarios was the mold index at the
sheathing board, assessing whether the addition of retrofit panels increased or mitigated mold growth risk
relative to the baseline condition.

The weather station located at the NET facility recorded outdoor relative humidity, temperature, solar

3.1 OUTDOOR AND INDOOR CLIMATE

Figure 12 shows the outdoor daily average temperature and relative humidity values during the testing
period. The outdoor RH over the two-year monitoring period reveals a seasonal pattern, with consistently
higher RH levels observed during the summer and early fall months, and lower values during winter and
early spring. Peak humidity conditions occurred in late summer, with August 2025 RH peak exhibiting the
highest monthly mean RH of 88.4%, followed closely by August 2024 (85.7%) and September 2024
(85.3%). These months are characterized not only by high mean values but also relatively low standard
deviations (typically <10%), indicating persistently humid conditions rather than short-term spikes. In
contrast, the lowest RH conditions were recorded during winter months, particularly in January 2025 RH
minimum with a mean RH of 65.6%, followed by March 2025 (65.8%) and February 2024 (71.7%).

Table 4 highlights that the monthly mean lowest RH conditions predominantly occur during winter and

The long-term field hygrothermal performance of the retrofitted panel systems was assessed and compared
with the baseline wall system. The results are presented below. Relative humidity (RH) was measured at

 4. CONCLUSION

Field data collected over a 24-month monitoring period demonstrates the benefits of using insulated
cladding systems for retrofitting existing wall assemblies in humid climates. All four tested retrofit systems
significantly improved the thermal performance of the walls compared to the baseline uninsulated wall and
no issue of moisture was observed.

These results underscore the importance of careful material selection and integrated panel design in retrofit
applications. The findings support the use of advanced panelized retrofits to improve building envelope
durability, enhance energy efficiency, and maintain occupant comfort.

Overall, the retrofit interventions resulted in a substantial improvement in the thermal performance of the
wall assemblies when compared to the baseline wall condition. The baseline wall exhibited a relatively low

 31

thermal resistance of approximately 2.8 °F·ft²·h/Btu, indicating limited insulation capacity. Following the
application of the retrofit panels, the measured whole-wall R-values increased significantly across all cases,
reaching approximately 19.4–20.2 °F·ft²·h/Btu for the Revitalite, Fedderlite, and RFP systems, and up to
35.1 °F·ft²·h/Btu for the VIP system.

Through modeling, the sheathing board moisture content, together with the corresponding mold index
assessment, indicates a clear and consistent improvement in hygrothermal performance for all retrofitted
wall systems relative to the baseline. While the baseline assemblies frequently exhibited moisture levels
exceeding critical thresholds and elevated mold index values, the retrofitted walls (with Fedderlite, RFP,
and VIP) maintained moisture contents largely below risk levels, resulting in low mold index.
