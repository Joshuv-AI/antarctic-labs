Executive Summary
An internal preliminary evaluation of Critical Technology Elements (CTEs) for the LANSCE
Modernization Project (LAMP) was completed in 2023. This included determining corresponding
Technical Readiness Levels (TRLs) for all subsystems using the criteria of DOE G 413.3-4A, Technical
Readiness Assessment Guide.
This revised report includes a summary of the recent design modifications required to meet the project Key
Performance Requirements (KPPs), some of which may reduce technical risks to the project. These recent
design modifications are based on the LAMP Conceptual Design which is the design evaluated in this
document and include:
 • Further optimization of the low-energy and medium-energy beam transport regions (LEBT and
 MEBT, respectively), including relocation of various functional elements (i.e. choppers, kickers,
 and bunchers).
 • An additional 100-keV H- ion source to separate ion-source function based on beam delivery
 requirements.
 • A high-repetition-rate pulsed kicker magnet to select/merge the two H- ion beams into a common
 low-energy beam transport.
 • Modification and further optimization to a more conventional 3-MeV Radio Frequency Quadrupole
 (RFQ) design. Performance of the RFQ has been optimized to deliver the required three types of
 beams while meeting the project Key Performance Parameters (KPPs).
 • The addition of a second chopper in the medium-energy beam transport (MEBT) line to reduce the
 required pulser voltages.

The scope of the evaluation was limited to the project Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) elements as
defined for the RFQ Injector and Drift Tube Linac (DTL) systems only. Integration of Instrumentation and
Controls (I&C) and Safety Systems was not considered, although specific technologies as related to the
RFQ and DTL systems were included. Other elements of the project such as Shielding, System Design,
Technical Management, and additional facility integration needed to enable off-line testing and pre-
installation commissioning were not evaluated.
Each technical subsystem element was evaluated for technical readiness; however, not all were found to
meet the criteria for a CTE.
Critical Technology Elements are defined as:
 • Technology that directly impacts a functional requirement of the process or facility.
 • Limitations in the understanding of technology results in a potential schedule or cost risk.
 • Technology that is new or novel.
 • The technology is modified.
 • The technology is expected to operate in an environment and/or achieve a performance beyond its
 original design intention or demonstrated capability.
Three subsystem elements were determined to meet the CTE criteria. Their associated Technical Readiness
Levels (TRLs) are summarized in the table below. These subsystem elements have the lowest technical
readiness due to either being new, novel or modified, requiring additional Research and Development
(R&D) to meet the project KPPs and subsystem requirements, or present technologies have not been
demonstrated in a relevant environment. All other subsystems were determined to have a TRL of 8,
indicating that actual operating systems exist having similar performance requirements as needed for
LAMP. Details of the technical readiness evaluation for each subsystem is given in the following sections
of this report.

The beam choppers were assessed to be low TRL because no presently operating choppers exist that meet
the threshold performance requirements as determined by the preconceptual and conceptual design studies.
Detailed parameter studies will be required to develop appropriate designs for the two choppers, as well as
performance testing to reduce technical and potential schedule risks. However, recent design improvements
that include modifications to include a two-chopper design in the MEBT may allow a significant reduction
of required pulser voltages, and therefore, may reduce technical risk.
Two viable options exist for the RFQ Structure: 4-rod or 4-vane. A down-select decision will need to be
made at some point based on engineering design considerations related to structure cooling, rigidity, ease
of access and maintenance, etc. From a beam performance perspective, either a 4-rod or 4-vane RFQ
structure should be able to meet single-beam performance requirements based on operating facilities
worldwide. Single beam operation is assessed low technical risk. However, no operating multi-beam RFQ
accelerator has been demonstrated or exists in operation today. Therefore, comprehensive simulations to
verify the design approach, followed by system validation in the laboratory, will be required to reduce
significant technical risk. Recent improvements in the RFQ design during conceptual design, including
moving to a more conventional design followed by parameter optimization for each beam species and pulse
structure requirements to meet KPPs, may reduce the technical risk prior to validation in the laboratory.
Although the individual components that comprise the MEBT are low-risk and commercially available, the
MEBT must be capable of efficiently steering and matching multiple beams into the DTL. This will require
a special beam-dynamics design since these two beams exit the RFQ accelerator with the same transverse
phase-space projections but should have differing phase-space projections to be properly focused and
matched into the acceptance of the DTL. Such a MEBT design has not been done before, however the
methods and tools to do so exist. Comprehensive simulations are ongoing to develop a MEBT design,
followed by system validation in the laboratory, to reduce significant technical risk. The recent addition of
a second H- ion source and a high-repetition-rate kicker magnet further complicate the MEBT design but
should not further increase technical risk. Operating examples of kickers that can meet the performance
requirements for LAMP exist.

1 Introduction
The goal of LAMP is to replace obsolete and near-end-of-life systems of the LANSCE linear accelerator
with modern systems. The project scope replaces the Cockcroft-Walton injectors with a modern radio-
frequency quadrupole (RFQ) injector and the drift-tube linac (DTL). This modernization will ensure the
long-term viability of LANSCE and enhance its capability to respond to current and future NNSA mission
needs through increased operational reliability and performance.
1.1 Project Background
The Los Alamos Neutron Science Center (LANSCE) is located at Technical Area 53 (TA-53) at Los
Alamos National Laboratory in Los Alamos, New Mexico. LANSCE is driven by an 800 mega-electron
volt (MeV) proton accelerator that delivered first beam in 1972. The LANSCE accelerator is unique in that
it accelerates both H- (to full energy of 800 MeV) and H+ ions (up to 100 MeV currently but has accelerated
high-power H+ beam to 800 MeV in the past) and supports five separate experimental areas that operate
simultaneously with each having different timing and beam current requirements. Many of the LANSCE
accelerator front end components date back to its original commissioning in 1972, including the ion sources,
Cockcroft-Walton (CW) generators, and the Drift Tube LINAC (DTL) which accelerates the beam up to
100 MeV.
The purpose of the LANSCE Accelerator Modernization Project (LAMP) is to replace and modernize
multiple accelerator systems and components to improve beam delivery, reliability and availability. LAMP
modernization will improve system maintainability and prepare the facility for operation through 2050. The
scope of modernization currently includes the LANSCE front end (up to 100 MeV). This scope includes
modernizing the two ion sources, replacing the two CW generators with a modern Radio Frequency
Quadrupole (RFQ), and replacing the DTL which is exhibiting severe degradation and has significantly
exceeded its expected lifespan. Modernization of accelerator systems beyond the 100 MeV beam energy
are not within the currently approved scope of LAMP, however, end-to-end modeling through the entire
accelerator including the Proton Storage Ring (PSR) will need to be performed to verify the project’s
performance requirements for all five experimental areas.
LAMP will be executed as a congressional line-item project under DOE Order 413.3b [1] for the National
Nuclear Security Agency (NNSA). The design will follow the Program Requirements Document (PRD) for
LAMP [2] and other requirements that flow down from the PRD.

2 Technology Reviewed
The technology reviewed is based on the LANSCE Front-End Upgrade Conceptual Design Study. 1 Figure
1 shows the approved conceptual design layout of the Front-End Upgrade. It also shows an enlarged view
of the RFQ-based 3-MeV injector section. The conceptual design beamline layouts shown in Fig. 1 reflect
recent modifications to the preliminary design made to optimize system performance and ensure
compliance with project KPPs.
The injector consists of two independent, nearly identical beamlines for H+ and H- beams, merged into a
common beamline upstream of the RFQ. Each beamline contains a deflector, a chopper, a pre-buncher, a
low-frequency buncher (in the H- beamline only), and solenoids and quadrupoles. Emittance measurement
stations are included to match the beam to key beamline locations. The first two solenoids provide transverse
matching of the beam from each source to each deflector. The beam has a waist in the middle of the
deflector. The requisite time structure for each of the 120 Hz cycles is created by deflecting the beam
between cycles. A second solenoid is used to create the beam waist in the low-frequency buncher (H- only)

and the pre-buncher. The subsequent combination of four quadrupoles (quadruplet) matches the beam to
the chopper, creating a beam waist in the center of the chopper. A second magnetic quadruplet matches the
beam to the main buncher, creating another beam waist. The last pair of solenoids matches the beam to the
RFQ, resulting in the required Twiss parameters at the entrance to RFQ. Recent modifications to the Low-
Energy Beam Transport (LEBT) include the addition of a second H- ion source and a high-repetition-rate
pulsed kicker magnet, enabling selection between the 16-mA or 35-mA H- beam for delivery to the
appropriate experimental area.
The Medium Energy Beam Transport (MEBT) follows the RFQ. It includes quadrupole magnets and three
Radio Frequency (RF) cavities to match the output beam from the RFQ into the DTL structure. The MEBT
design now includes two choppers used to reduce the side lobes in the compressed H- beam being sent to
the Weapons Neutron Research (WNR) facility. A two-chopper design may allow a significant reduction
of required pulser voltages, and therefore, may reduce technical risk. Emittance measurement stations are
also included in the MEBT.
The DTL consists of six individual tanks, each progressively accelerating the beams from 3 MeV to 100
MeV. The DTL uses permanent magnet quadrupole magnets for transverse beam focusing. Additional
magnets for beam matching and beam diagnostics are located in a space between each DTL tank.

3 Technology Readiness Evaluation Process

determined using the processes and guidelines outlined in DOE G 413.3-4A, Technology Readiness
Assessment Guide. Identification of CTEs used the preliminary project WBS (Fig. 2 below). Critical
Technology Elements are defined as:
 • Technology that directly impacts a functional requirement of the process or facility.
 • Limitations in the understanding of technology results in a potential schedule or cost risk.
 • Technology that is new or novel.
 • The technology is modified.
 • The technology is expected to operate in an environment and/or achieve a performance beyond its
 original design intention or demonstrated capability.
Technical readiness levels were determined for each subsystem using the standard DOE Technical
Readiness Level Scale shown below in Figure 3.

The ability of technology to meet the minimum threshold requirements was considered in determining a
TRL. The ability of each subsystem to meet objective requirements was also evaluated but not included in
the TRL value determination. Table 1 below summarizes the high-level LAMP KPPs. 2

4 Results – RFQ Injector System
The RFQ Injector System contains several subsystem elements. These include ion sources, the low-energy
beam transport (LEBT), beam choppers, the RFQ accelerator, the medium energy beam transport (MEBT)
system that transports the beam from the RFQ to the entrance of the DTL, and associated RF, beam
diagnostics, and instrumentation and controls. Results of the technical readiness evaluation for subsystem
elements of the RFQ Injector System are summarized in the subsections below.

4.1 Ion sources – H+ and H-
Subsystem Description: The Ion Source subsystem is the first major element of the beamline. The current
layout assumes two nearly identical low-energy beam transport (LEBT) lines to allow for simultaneous
multiple beam injection into the RFQ accelerator. Each LEBT requires separate ion sources, one for protons
(H+) and two for negative hydrogen ions (H-). Each ion source subsystem produces the appropriate charged
Hydrogen atoms so that the particles can be accelerated and focused for injection into the beam line.
Separate high-voltage systems for each ion source, operating at 100 kV, are required to produce the initial
accelerating voltage for each beam.
Performance Requirements: The threshold and objective performance requirements for the Ion Sources
are listed in the table below. They are based on the estimated RFQ and DTL transmissions and the 100
MeV KPPs shown in Table 1.

Brookhaven, and other accelerator facilities.

H+ Ion Source
High current, high-duty factor proton ion sources (H+) meeting the LAMP performance requirements are
in operation at several existing linear accelerator facilities. Several new proton sources are also under
development that could meet the LAMP requirements. Typical types of high-current, high-duty-factor H+
ion sources include duoplasmatron sources and microwave-driven Electron Cyclotron Resonance (ECR)
sources. These ion sources routinely operate in pulsed or continues wave (CW) mode with high reliability
and long operating lifetimes. Most ECR ion sources operate at 2.45 GHz and can deliver over 40 mA in
peak current. The existing LANSCE duoplasmatron H+ ion source was originally developed for production
of a 50-mA proton beam but was later modified for higher brightness and now has a maximum output of
30 mA. Several ion source options exist from commercial vendors or can be built by a national laboratory.
Several representative examples of operating H+ ion sources are summarized below and were used in the
technical readiness evaluation of this subsystem. These include the duoplasmatron source at LANSCE, the
(Source d'Ions Légers à Haute Intensité) SILHI ECR source at (Atomic Energy Commission) CEA-Saclay,
France and the (Facility for Antiproton and Ion Research) FAIR ECR source at Gesellschaft für
Schwerionenforschung (GSI), Germany. All these sources exceed the LAMP requirements. Therefore, the
H+ ion source is evaluated to be TRL 8.

H- Ion Source
The existing LANSCE multi-cusp converter H- ion source, operating at 80 kV, has demonstrated
performance consistent with meeting the LAMP threshold performance requirements, but would require
modification for 100-kV operation. Past development results indicate that some performance improvements
may be possible but are unlikely to lead to meeting the objective performance requirements for beam current
and source recovery from maintenance. Other operating high-TRL ion sources will also require
modifications for higher extraction voltage operation.
Based on recent operational experience, the RF-driven type of H- ion source injector that is used at the Oak
Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL) Spallation Neutron Source (SNS) and the Japan Proton Accelerator
Complex (JPARC) is the most applicable to meeting the LAMP project requirements for high H- beam
current. Operational data for this type of ion source has shown ion-source lifetimes exceeding 90 days
before requiring servicing while delivering high peak beam current at high duty factor. The tables below
list the demonstrated performance parameters for these two operational ion sources. This technology has
been proven to work in its final form and under expected conditions. Therefore, the H- ion source is
evaluated to be TRL 8.

Ion Source High-Voltage System

4.2 Low-Energy Beam Transport
Subsystem Description: The low-energy beam transport (LEBT) line is located at the front of the
accelerator system and contains the necessary elements used to transport the beam from the output of the
ion source to the input of the subsequent RFQ accelerator. The LEBT typically performs the following
functions:
 • matching the beam from the ion source to the transport line,
 • matching the beam into the RFQ accelerating structure,
 • providing the capability to measure beam properties, and
 • providing fast switching (chopping) before the RFQ to introduce time structure to the beam.

Typical LEBT designs are based on magnetic focusing. The use of magnetic focusing allows the beam to
be nearly fully neutralized by residual gas present in the transport line. The magnetic fields of the solenoids
and quadrupoles do not affect the cloud of ion-electron plasma resulting from beam ionization of the
residual gas. The residual gas in the LEBT comes mainly from the ion source, however, better neutralization
can be achieved by introducing heavier gases such as Kr or Xe to the H2 gas in the LEBT. Introduction of
additional gas to improve beam neutralization is a common practice for all magnetic LEBTs. The beam
neutralization minimizes emittance growth due to nonlinear space-charge forces in the beam. Major
subsystem components of the LEBT include beam pipes, vacuum valves and pumping ports, RF deflectors,
RF bunching cavities, solenoid and quadrupole focusing magnets, dipole steering magnets, beam choppers,
and various beam measurement devices. LAMP requires two LEBTs, one for the H+ beam and one for the
H- beam.
Performance Requirements: N/A. Performance requirements for the beam choppers and beam diagnostics
are evaluated as separate subsystems and are not included as part of the LEBT evaluation.

4.3 Choppers – 100-keV LEBT Chopper and 3-MeV MEBT Chopper
Subsystem Description: Beam chopping is used to generate a specific time structure in the beam. This is
achieved through use of some type of deflecting structure, usually located in the front end of the accelerator.
However, to generate the beam timing structure for the multiple beams at LANSCE, a combination of beam
chopping upstream of the RFQ in the LEBT and additional chopping after the RFQ in the MEBT are
required. Beam chopping in the MEBT is used to eliminate any low-intensity satellites in the beam structure
delivered to the Weapons Neutron Research (WNR) facility that would create an undesirable background
(dark current) for experiments. Each chopper subsystem consists of the beam deflecting structure and the
associated fast pulser or RF system to drive the deflector. A performance parameter for most chopper
systems is the chopping rise/fall time. The chopper rise/fall time determines how well the beam is chopped,
generally a shorter rise/fall time is preferred, however the exact requirement is determined by the specific
application. It should be noted that rise/fall times of a few nano seconds (ns) or less are very challenging to
achieve at high voltage, limited by current pulser technology.
The type of deflecting structure used can be a set of simple Direct Current (DC) deflecting plates, an
electrostatic meander-line (traveling wave), a beam transformer using an induction cavity, an RF cavity
operating in a deflecting mode, or a single or combination of Einzel lenses. The existing LANSCE 750 keV
H- chopper consists of two traveling-wave helix electrodes which apply a vertical kick to the beam using
an applied interelectrode voltage of 1.3 kV. An advantage of using a traveling-wave structure is that it
eliminates unnecessary transverse beam emittance growth and beam losses typically seen in traditional sine-
wave choppers for poorly bunched beams.

4.4 RFQ Structure
Subsystem Description: The Radio-Frequency Quadrupole (RFQ) accelerator is the first major RF
accelerator element of the beamline. The 100-keV beams produced by the ion sources are matched into the
RFQ using each LEBT (H+ and H-) and are accelerated by the RFQ to final beam energies of 3 MeV. The
RFQ must also provide beams that have other specific characteristics, such as emittance, that allow the
beam to be further captured and accelerated by the downstream DTL.
To meet LANSCE operational requirements, the RFQ must accelerate both H+ and H- simultaneously over
a wide range of beam currents, pulse lengths, and chopping patterns:
 • H+ (IPF) - 100 Hz rep. rate, no chopping pattern.
 • H- (WNR) - 100 Hz rep. rate, concurrent with the H+ to IPF, chopping with individual
 micropulses 1.8 microseconds apart.
 • H- (Lujan) - 20 Hz rep. rate, chopped 290 ns beam on - 70 ns beam off during the RF
 macropulse for injection into the PSR: 20 Hz dedicated for ring injection.
Multi beam operation requires both beam species to be captured and accelerated simultaneously in
alternating RF buckets in the RFQ. This has not yet been demonstrated. Typical multi-beam RFQ operation
such as at the Facility for Rare Isotope Beams (FRIB), and other similar heavy-ion facilities, accelerate
multiple charge states of the same element in the same RF bucket and then separate these into individual
beams for delivery to experiments, not beams of opposite charge.

Basis of Evaluation (Bottom Line Upfront):
Proven but Application-Specific Technology (TRL 8 for single-beam): RFQ accelerators are mature,
widely used, and well-understood technologies, serving as the standard front-end accelerator at major
facilities worldwide due to their efficiency, compactness, and well-benchmarked design tools. Both 4-vane

mechanical solutions. For LAMP, either configuration is viable at 201.25 MHz, with tradeoffs in cooling,
rigidity, cost, and maintainability, introducing manageable design complexity within established

beams with different pulse structures and currents. This type of operation—multi-species, opposite charge
beams in alternating RF buckets—has not been demonstrated in an operational RFQ. Although simulations
using validated tools show feasibility, experimental validation is still required.
Application-Specific Technology
RFQ accelerators have been under continuous development and improvement for many decades for a
variety of accelerator operation requirements. They have become accepted world-wide (CERN, LANSCE,
Fermilab, Brookhaven, ORNL/SNS, ISIS/Rutherford, etc.) as the standard of initial/first stage acceleration
for ion linear accelerators. This is due to their ability to efficiently bunch and accelerate a low-velocity ion
beam, their high RF efficiency, their compactness, and the ability to tune the output parameters using well-
defined design principles and well-benchmarked design and simulation codes. The RFQ accelerator is used
as the first stage of RF acceleration in every major ion accelerator facility worldwide for a broad range of
applications. However, each RFQ design is unique based on the specific application and performance
requirements. Given well-defined specifications, several vendors worldwide can design and fabricate an
RFQ structure for LAMP.
The RFQ accelerator essentially comes in two configurations – 4-vane or 4-rod. The 4-vane RFQ has
historically been used for light-ion acceleration when RF frequencies in the range of 200 MHz – 400 MHz
are used, with compact designs demonstrated at operating RF frequencies as high as 750 MHz at CERN for
medical applications. The 4-rod RFQ has historically been used to accelerate heavy ions which typically
requires lower RF frequencies (<200 MHz). The cross-sectional area of the RFQ is inversely proportional
to the RF frequency, making a high-frequency RFQ more compact than a low-frequency RFQ. The two
types of RFQ accelerators also operate in distinctly different electromagnetic modes. For the 4-vane RFQ,
the vane geometry and other dimensions of the RFQ structure define the correct electromagnetic operating
mode. As a result, the design of the 4-vane RFQ structure must accommodate the physics requirements as
well as vacuum and thermomechanical requirements for operation, cooling, and stabilization of the
structure. In contrast, the 4-rod RFQ operates in an electromagnetic mode that is independent of the outer
structure housing the vanes of the RFQ. In this type of RFQ the fields are confined near the beam axis, on
the vanes and near the vanes. As a result, the outer vessel used for a 4-rod RFQ can have nearly any shape
(cylindrical, rectangular, etc.; several operating examples with these geometries exist). Additionally, only
cooling of the vanes is generally required. These characteristics of the 4-rod RFQ make this type of RFQ
accelerator inherently simpler and potentially lighter in overall weight. Significant advancements have
occurred in the last two decades in the application of 4-rod RFQ accelerators. Several operating examples
outside of the US now exist in the higher frequency range (201 MHz - 325 MHz) to accelerate hydrogen
ions. This advancement was primarily driven by the reduced cost and lower complexity of the 4-rod RFQ.

manner that allows access to the internal vanes, if necessary for repair or replacement. An RFQ of either

Novel Requirement
The present conceptual design approach is conventional but features a shortened shaper section, optimized
for preserving the WNR beam “core” bunch charge and minimizing the formation of leading and trailing
“satellite” bunches. By using a more conventional design approach and well-benchmarked codes, the risk
of the RFQ design is significantly reduced. However, because multiple-beam acceleration has not been
demonstrated in an RFQ, the overall technical readiness of the RFQ subsystem is TRL 5. Continuing
comprehensive simulations are required to develop an RFQ design and to verify the design approach,
followed by system validation in the laboratory.

Development History and Status:
An RFQ Test Stand exists in AOT Division that can be used to demonstrate multi-beam acceleration and
potentially also the beam chopping requirements for LAMP. The present test stand configuration consists
of a single ion source capable of producing a 35-keV, 35-mA peak current, H+ beam, a 2-solenoid LEBT,
and a 750-keV, 4-rod RFQ. Simultaneous dual-species beam operation would require several test stand
modifications including the installation of choppers, an H- ion source and an additional new LEBT. A

4.5 Medium Energy Beam Transport MEBT)
Subsystem Description: The medium-energy beam transport (MEBT) is generally a relatively simple
beamline configuration used to transport the beams from the output of the RFQ accelerator to the entrance
of the DTL. The MEBT typically performs the following functions:
 • provides transverse and longitudinal beam matching from the RFQ accelerating structure into the
 DTL,
 • provides the capability to measure beam properties, and
 • provides additional fast switching (chopping) before the DTL to eliminate low-intensity satellites in
 the beam pulses delivered to the WNR experimental facility.
Major subsystem components of the MEBT include beam pipes, vacuum valves and pumping ports, an RF
bunching cavity for longitudinal beam matching, quadrupole focusing magnets, dipole steering magnets,
fast beam choppers, and various beam measurement devices.
Performance Requirements: N/A. Performance requirements for the MEBT are being developed during
the project design process. Performance requirements for the beam choppers and beam diagnostics are
evaluated separately and are not included in the MEBT subsystem evaluation.

 Evaluation of TRL: TRL 5

Basis of Evaluation: The individual components that comprise the MEBT, except for the beam choppers,
are of technical low-risk and commercially available and are considered TRL 8.
However, the MEBT must be capable of efficiently matching beams with two different charge states (e.g.
H+ and H-), and significantly differing bunch charges into the DTL. To meet LANSCE operational
requirements, the MEBT must be capable of matching and steering both H+ and H- simultaneously over a
wide range of beam currents, pulse lengths, and chopping patterns at 3 MeV:
 • H+ (IPF) - 100 Hz rep. rate, generally no chopping pattern, 250 µA, 12 mA, 59 pC;

LAMP-ENG-RPT-003; LAMP Technical Readiness Evaluation Report Rev.02
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 29

 • H- (WNR) - 100 Hz rep. rate, concurrent with the H+ to IPF, chopping with individual
 micropulses 1.8 microseconds apart, 4 µA, 35 mA, 224 pC;
 • H- (Lujan) - 20 Hz rep. rate, chopped 290 ns beam on/70 ns beam off during the RF
 macropulse for injection into the PSR; 20 Hz dedicated for ring injection, 100 µA, 16
 mA, 80 pC.
This will require a special beam-dynamics design for the MEBT since these beams of differing charge exit
the RFQ accelerator with the same transverse phase-space projections but should have differing phase-
space projections at the DTL entrance to be properly matched. Transverse and longitudinal matching are
further complicated by the approximate factor of three differences in bunch charge between the WNR and
Lujan H- beams. This will require special beam matching considerations or compromised beam matching
into the DTL. Furthermore, setting beam injection angle and position into the DTL is complicated by the
presence of oppositely charged beams. The LAMP conceptual design for the MEBT indicates there is a
solution for the matching (focusing) problem, but a solution for steering and beam alignment / injection has
not yet been developed. Comprehensive simulations are needed to validate a MEBT design with appropriate
focusing and steering strategies, to be followed by system validation in a physical beamline, to reduce
significant technical risk. Therefore, because of the multi-beam matching and opposite-sign steering
requirements, the MEBT subsystem is evaluated to be TRL 5.

5.4 DTL Instrumentation and Controls (I&C)
Subsystem Description: The instrumentation and controls (I&C) system for the drift-tube linac (DTL)
accelerator must provide the interface between the accelerator equipment and the accelerator operators. At
a lower performance level, the system function allows tuning and verification of operational beam
parameters at the DTL output. At a higher performance level, the system should be capable of automatic
closed-loop tuning of the accelerator. In addition to accommodating standard conventional beam
measurements and industrial control interfaces, the system must offer flexible applications, communication
protocols, data archivers, and high-level system architecture to build a distributed control system for
maintaining and monitoring beam quality to meet specific performance and LANSCE beam requirements.
Performance Requirements: TBD. Performance requirements for the LAMP DTL I&C subsystem will
need to be developed during the project.

Assessment of TRL for Phase I: TRL 8

Basis of Assessment: As a generic system, the instrumentation and control system must be capable of
supporting multiple types of input/output (I/O) points and six different beam property measurements
(current, emittance, etc.). For decades, I/O requirements for accelerators have been met with commercial-
off-the-shelf electronics. These electronics are coupled with readily available high-level software
applications to form a distributed control system. These systems integrate industrial controls with high

LAMP-ENG-RPT-003; LAMP Technical Readiness Evaluation Report Rev.02
Los Alamos National Laboratory Page 40

speed, data-intensive instrumentation while providing archiving and other control system central services.
This is the case with all major accelerator facilities, including CERN, IAC, Jefferson Lab, KEK, and
LANSCE. The electronics take the form of Programmable Automation Controllers, and standard computer
bus systems, which often include Field Programmable Gate Arrays, while high-level applications include
real-time operating systems (RTEMS and VxWorks), National Instrument’s LabVIEW and the
Experimental Physics and Industrial Control System known as EPICS. The implementation of this type of
technology is very mature. The application of adaptive control and other modern control techniques such
as extremum seeking algorithms have been successfully demonstrated in an R&D environment on both
small subsystems and on large operating accelerators. Accelerator auto-tuning techniques have also been
successfully applied for medical accelerator applications.
A functioning modern control system exists at LANSCE. Modifications to the existing system and
integration required for LAMP should be straightforward for the proposed DTL system. Additionally, The
AOT Division Instrumentation and Controls group has a broad range of experience and key capabilities
including the development of both interceptive and non-interceptive beam diagnostics, design and
development of control, data acquisition, and timing hardware systems, and design and development of
software used in large, distributed control system environments. Therefore, the technical readiness of the
DTL I&C subsystem is TRL 8.

5.5 DTL Vacuum
Subsystem Description: The DTL Vacuum subsystem provides and maintains the low vacuum required in
the six DTL tanks. Components of the system include bellows, fittings, flanges, vacuum pumps, vacuum
gauges, vent valves, and isolation valves to monitor, maintain, and control the vacuum. This subsystem
requires integration with other mechanical systems, beam diagnostics, and controls related to the DTL.
Performance Requirements: TBD. Performance requirements for the LAMP DTL Vacuum subsystem will
need to be developed during the project. The present LANSCE DTL and remainder of the accelerator
complex operate at vacuum levels in the low 10–8 Torr range. The LAMP DTL is expected to have similar
vacuum requirements.

Basis of Evaluation: The vacuum requirements for LAMP are expected to be standard for this application,
with similar system configurations used at many similar accelerator facilities worldwide. All major
subsystem components of the DTL Vacuum subsystem are available through US and international
commercial vendors, either as standard components or as low-risk build-to-specification components. AOT
Division has significant experience in designing, operating, and maintaining high-vacuum systems.
Therefore, the technical readiness of the DTL Vacuum subsystem is TRL 8.
