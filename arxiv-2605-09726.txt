On the Impossibility of Specification Testing of Interference Models Based on Exposure Mappings
Chao Gao, Christopher Harshaw, Fredrik Sävje, Yitan Wang

Abstract
Researchers use interference models based on exposure mappings to facilitate estimation of causal effects in randomized experiments with interference. To test the veracity of such models, researchers can use specification tests that aim to detect departures from the stipulated model. However, existing tests suffer from poor power and are often unable to detect important model violations. The main result in this paper is to show that the specification testing problem for exposure mapping models is inherently difficult, and the poor power of existing tests is inescapable. In particular, the worst-case Type I and Type II error rates must sum to one for any specification test of such models, ruling out the existence of a uniformly consistent test. This is the worst-case overall error rate achieved by a naive test that discards all data and arbitrarily rejects the null at random; the testing problem is in this sense impossible. This negative result holds true for all exposure mappings, all sample sizes, for uniformly bounded outcomes, and for alternatives that are maximally separated from the null. While some tests can detect some type of departures from the null model, there will always be relevant departures from the null that are undetectable. Informative specification tests must therefore restrict the alternative model against which they seek to attain power for, beyond the restrictions imposed by the exposure mappings alone. We illustrate this by providing a uniformly consistent test for differentiating no-interference from a network-linear-in-means model.

Introduction

Interference occurs when the treatment assigned to one unit causally affects other units in an experiment.
When interference is present, researchers typically stipulate a model of the structure of the interference, and estimate causal effects under the assumption that the specified model is correct.
The predominate way to specify an interference model in empirical practice is to use exposure mappings based on a network of historical interactions between units
(
1
)
.

It can be challenging to specify appropriate interference models, because the pattern of interference is often complex and subtle, and researchers sometimes worry that their models do not capture all interference.
A way to alleviate this worry is to conduct a specification test, examining whether the data collected from the experiment provides evidence that the stipulated model is misspecified.
Testing of interference models can also be of independent scientific interest.

In this paper, we examine testing of interference models.
Our main result is that uniformly informative specification testing of models based on exposure mappings is impossible, in the sense that there exists no uniformly consistent test of the correctness of any such models.
In fact, we show that the sum of the minimax optimal Type I and Type II error rates always sum to one, meaning that there exists no specification test that is better in the worst case than a test that discards all data and arbitrarily rejects the null at random.
This impossibility result holds for all exposure mappings, for all sample sizes, for uniformly bounded outcomes, and for alternatives that are maximally separated from the null.

Progress can be made only by making a priori restrictions on the structure of the interference beyond the restrictions imposed by the exposure mappings themselves.
That is, we must rule out some potential outcome functions by assumption without support from the data.
The problem is related to the fact that exact Fisher randomization tests have poor power against certain deptures from a sharp null hypothesis.
Our main result can be seen as a formalization and extension of this insight to all possible testing procedures and all possible interference models based on exposure mappings.
One consequence is that interference models based on exposure mappings cannot be validated using the experimentally collected data alone.

A conclusion of our main result is, however, not that tests of interference models are not without practical value.
Researchers who understand their proper scope can use specification tests of interference models to learn about the structure of interference in settings where it is reasonble to impose additional a priori restrictions on the interference structure beyond the restrictions imposed by the exposure mappings alone.
To illustrate this perspective, we provide a consistent specification test of the null hypothesis of no interference versus the alternative hypothesis that the interference is structured according to a network linear-in-means model
(
14
;
6
)
.
The network linear-in-means model is a restriction of a more general exposure mapping model, which is what allows us to construct an uniformly informative test.

1.1
Related Works

The main strand of the literature on testing for interference builds on Fisher-style randomization tests
(
8
;
19
)
.
Such tests require sharp null hypotheses, allowing all potential outcomes to be imputed for all (counterfactual) treatment assignments.
A common sharp null hypothesis is that treatment is completely inefficacious, including no interference effects, meaning that a test using such a null also implicitly tests for the presence of interference.

5
extends this logic to richer interference models, provided that the models imply sharp null hypotheses.

The main limitation of unconditional randomization tests is that the conventional sharp null restricts both the primary effects and interference, so it is not a test exclusively of interference.
A null hypothesis of no interference alone is not globally sharp, so some potential outcomes cannot be imputed and the full unconditional distribution of common test statistics cannot be calculated.

2
addresses this with a conditional test using a test statistic that depends only on the outcomes of a subset of the units, often called the focal units.
The distribution of the statistic is calculated conditional on the realized treatment assignments of the focal units, ensuring that all relevant potential outcomes can be imputed under the null.

Several papers have extended the conditional randomization test approach.

3
study a broader class of null hypotheses related to interference and the choice of focal units.

4
provide a general framework for conditional randomization tests that allows more flexible conditioning on the observed treatments with the aim of improving power.

17
describe a graph-theoretic approach for finding a large set of focal units again with the aim of improving power.

18
use random graph models as a source of randomness, yielding better power in settings with highly dependent treatments.
To our knowledge, there are no formal analyses of power for conditional randomization tests, and power properties of these tests have primarily been investigated heuristically using simulations.

Specification tests for interference not based on Fisher-style randomization tests have also been developed.

16
describe a testing procedure using a hierarchical design, under which treatments are assigned using either complete or cluster-based randomization.
Without interference, the expected values of common treatment effect estimators are the same under both designs, so differences between such estimators can serve as a test of a no-interference assumption.

7
describes a method to estimate a lower bound on the number of units affected by other units’ treatments.
When there is no interference, there are no such units, so the lower bound can similarly act as a basis for a specification test.

The predominant method to specify interference models in the recent interference literature is to use exposure mappings, as introduced by
1
.
The related concept of effective treatments was introduced by
15
.
An exposure mapping is a low-dimensional summary of the full treatment vector.
An exposure mapping is said to be correctly specified when the summary it produces preserves all treatment information relevant for outcomes.
Most of this literature focuses on estimation and inference for average exposure effects under a correctly specified interference model rather than testing the correctness of the model itself.
However,
11
adapt the conditional-randomization approach to test interference models based on exposure mappings, but the power properties of such specification tests have not been formally characterized.

Throughout the paper, we use the overall testing error to evaluate the performance of a specification test.
The overall testing error takes into account both Type I and Type II error rates of a test.
The minimax testing error of a hypothesis testing problem is commonly used to characterize its difficulty
(
9
)
.

2
Design-Based Specification Tests

2.1
Preliminaries

We consider a randomized experiment with
n
units.
The researcher implements an intervention
\boldsymbol{z}
chosen at random from a set
\Omega
, containing all interventions under consideration.
The experimental design is the (random) mechanism by which an intervention is chosen from
\Omega
.
Formally, the interventions form a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
and the experimental design
\mathcal{D}
is a probability measure
\mathcal{D}:\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
.
In conventional experiments with binary treatments, an intervention is a binary vector
\boldsymbol{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots z_{n})\in\Omega=\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Our results extend beyond this conventional setting, but we focus on binary treatments for clarity.

Each unit
i\in[n]
has an associated
potential outcome function

y_{i}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
that captures the unit’s response under each intervention in
\Omega
.
The potential outcome functions depend on the entire intervention
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
, allowing for interference.
For notational convenience, we collect the
n
individual functions into a vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{y}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that captures the responses for all units under each intervention:

\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})=\lparen y_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),y_{2}(\boldsymbol{z}),\dots y_{n}(\boldsymbol{z})\rparen\kern 5.0pt.

Researchers impose structural restrictions on the potential outcome functions to facilitate estimation and inference
(
10
)
.
We can encode the restrictions as subsets
\mathcal{M}
of the set of all measurable functions with signature
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We refer to such subsets as interference models.
A model
\mathcal{M}
is correctly specified if it contains the true potential outcome function:
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}
.
A common type of model in the recent interference literature is based on exposure mappings, which we describe in Section
3.1
.

2.2
Specification Tests and Minimax Testing Error

We refer to the interference model under examination as the
null model
and denote it with
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
The null model is tested against an
alternative model
, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
Intuitively, the null model consists of all potential outcome functions that the researcher deems plausible a priori in the empirical setting at hand.
The alternative model is more permissive, consisting of all functions that the researcher is not comfortable definitively ruling out a priori.
The null is thus nested in the alternative:
\mathcal{M}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

In addition to structural interference restrictions, researchers often impose regularity conditions to ensure that the potential outcome functions are sufficiently well-behaved.
This could, for example, be moment conditions that prevent extreme outliers.
In this paper, we consider models that are restricted using a bound on the uniform moment:
\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{i\in[n]}\sup_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega}\lvert y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\rvert
.
We use this moment condition because of its simplicity and because it is the strongest among commonly used moment bounds.
Proving impossibility under a strong moment condition implies impossibility also under weaker moment conditions.
To be precise, if
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{\prime}
denote the null and alternative models only encoding structural interference restrictions, we consider the following restricted null and alternative models:

\mathcal{M}_{0}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}^{\prime}:\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}_{1}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{\prime}:\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\big\}\kern 5.0pt.

The choice of
1
for the upper bound is purely to simplify the notation; it can be replaced by
\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C
for any constant
C>0
without any meaningful changes to our results.

A specification test seeks evidence against the null hypothesis
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
If sufficient evidence is found, the null model is rejected in favor of the alternative.
Formally, a
statistical test
is a measurable function
\phi:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
that takes as input the realized intervention
\boldsymbol{z}
and observed outcomes
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})
and returns
1
when the null is rejected.
The test can implicitly depend on other aspects of the units, such as covariates and observed historical patterns of interactions.
In the design-based framework, the researcher can select the experimental design in addition to the statistical test.
A
testing procedure
is a pair
(\mathcal{D},\phi)
for the specification testing problem at hand, consisting of an experimental design
\mathcal{D}
and a statistical test
\phi
.

Definition 1

(Type I error control)
.

A testing procedure
(\mathcal{D},\phi)
controls the Type I error at rate
\alpha\in[0,1]
if
\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}\Esym_{#1}[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))]\leq\alpha
.

Existing tests of interference models have predominately focused on controlling the Type I error.
However, Type I error control alone is not difficult to achieve nor particularly helpful.
Indeed, it is trivially achieved by a test that never rejects the null.
More subtly, it can be achieved by testing procedures that largely ignore the realized outcomes
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})
, and it might not be obvious from a cursorily inspection of a procedure that it achieves error control by effectively discarding most of the outcome data.
The concern with such procedures is that they lack power: the rejection rate
\Esym_{#1}[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))]
is low also in the alternative model
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
If the rejection rate is
\alpha
in both
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}
, the test provides no information about whether the null model is correctly specified even if it successfully controls the Type I error rate.

For a testing procedure to be useful, it must have power against meaningful departures from the null model.
That is, the rejection rates must be notably larger than
\alpha
for relevant functions in the alternative.
It is typically not possible to achieve large rejection rates for all functions in
\mathcal{M}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{0}
, because functions in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
that are close to the boundary of
\mathcal{M}_{0}
will be practically indistinguishable from some functions in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
Instead, we consider rejection rates for functions in
\mathcal{M}_{1}
that are well-separated from the null, in the sense that the functions are non-negligibly different from all functions in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
We formalize separation using a functional.

Definition 2
.

A
separation functional

g:\mathcal{M}_{1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
has two properties:
(i) (distinguishing)
g(\boldsymbol{y})=0
if and only if
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}

(ii) (homogeneity)
g(\gamma\cdot\boldsymbol{y})=\gamma^{2}\cdot g(\boldsymbol{y})
for all
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

The first property states that the functional distinguishes functions inside and outside the null model by assigning positive values only to functions that are outside the null.
The second property states that scaling a potential outcome function results in quadratically growing separation.
This ensures that the separation
g(\boldsymbol{y})
grows as
\boldsymbol{y}
goes further away from the null.
It is possible to impose a degree of homogeneity other than two, but all separation functionals we consider will be quadratic.

A
\delta
-separated alternative model

\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
is the subset of functions
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
that are sufficiently different from the null according to the separation functional:
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}:g(\boldsymbol{y})\geq\delta\big\}
.
When evaluating a testing procedure, we consider alternatives that are
\delta
-separated from the null.
We refer to
\delta
as the
separation value
.
As
\delta
increases, the corresponding alternative
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
becomes smaller, making the testing problem easier:
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta^{\prime})
for
\delta\geq\delta^{\prime}
.

Definition 3
.

Consider a testing procedure
(\mathcal{D},\phi)
.
For a given separation value
\delta\geq 0
, the
overall testing error

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)
is the sum of the worst-case Type I and Type II errors:

\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}\Esym_{#1}\Big[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\Big]+\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)}\Esym_{#1}\Big[1-\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\Big].

The overall testing error
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)
captures how well a testing procedure can distinguish the null from well-separated functions in the alternative.
A testing procedure that is able to perfectly distinguish between the null model and a well-separated alternative has testing error
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=0
.
Testing procedures that discard the data and arbitrary reject the null at random, and procedures that never reject the null, have testing error
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=1
.
This is because any decrease in the Type I error rate is perfectly offset by a corresponding increase in the Type II error rate for such tests, indicating that they are uninformative about whether the null model is correctly specified.
In principle, the overall testing error can be greater than one, but practically,
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=1
marks the greatest relevant error rate, as it can be achieved by naive procedures that ignore all data from the experiment.

Definition 4
.

A testing procedure
(\mathcal{D},\phi)
is
uniformly consistent
if the overall testing error approaches zero for all separation values:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)\to 0
for all fixed
\delta>0
.

A uniformly consistent testing procedure can distinguish between the null and any meaningful departures from the null given enough data.
Consistency is defined with respect to a sequence of null and alternative models, using triangular array asymptotics that is standard in the design-based literature.
In this asymptotic regime, each experiment in the sequence has its own potential outcome function
\boldsymbol{y}^{(n)}
, null model
\mathcal{M}_{0}^{(n)}
, and alternative model
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{(n)}
, although we suppress the dependence on
n
for notational simplicity.

We consider uniform consistency to be the minimal requirement for a testing procedure to be useful as a specification test in practice.
An inconsistent procedure would not be able to distinguish some meaningful departures from the null even if we had infinite data.
To use an inconsistent procedure for specification testing, we would be required to rule out a priori all meaningful departures from the null that the procedure is unable to detect.
But this defeats the purpose of the specification test, because the alternative model contains exactly the potential outcome functions that we were not comfortable to rule out a priori.

When we fail to construct a testing procedure that is uniformly consistent, or otherwise fail to achieve acceptable error rates, we might ask if this is due to our own inability or if the specification testing problem at hand is inherently difficult.
To understand the fundamental difficulty of the specification testing problem, we turn to the minimax testing error
\mathcal{R}^{*}
which represents the best achievable testing error of any testing procedure.

Definition 5
.

The
minimax testing error
for separation
\delta
is
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)=\inf_{\mathcal{D},\phi}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)
.

Definition 6
.

A specification testing problem is
impossible
at separation
\delta
if the corresponding minimax testing error is one:
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)=1
.

If a testing problem has
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)=1
, there exists no testing procedure that is more informative about the correctness of the null, as judged by the overall testing error, than a naive procedure that ignores all information provided by the experiment.
In this sense, a testing problem is impossible when
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)=1
.
There might exist testing procedures for impossible testing problems that can detect some types of departures from the null, and such a procedure would be informative for that particular type of misspecification of the null model.
However, if a testing problem is impossible, there will always be meaningful departures from the null that the researcher was not comfortable ruling out a priori but which are empirically undetectable.
Progress can then be made only by imposing additional restrictions on the null or alternative models that are neither substantiated by prior knowledge about the empirical setting nor supported by the data.

The main result of this paper, as described in the next section, is to show that specification tests of null and alternative models based on discrete exposure mappings are impossible in the sense formalized by Definition
6
.

3
Impossibility of Specification Testing of Exposure Mappings

3.1
Exposure Models

Following
1
, an exposure mapping is a low-dimensional summary of the entire high-dimensional intervention
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
For each unit
i\in[n]
, the researcher specifies a finite set of exposures
\Delta_{i}
, and the exposure mapping
h_{i}:\Omega\to\Delta_{i}
maps each intervention
\boldsymbol{z}
to an exposure in
\Delta_{i}
.
It is convenient to collect all
n
exposure mappings into a single vector-valued function
\boldsymbol{h}(\boldsymbol{z})=(h_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),\dotsc,h_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}))
.

An exposure model stipulates that the structure of interference is captured by some specified exposure mappings, in the sense that the dimension reduction from
\Omega
to
\Delta_{i}
is without loss of information for each unit.
Formally, the interference model based on a collection of exposure mappings
\boldsymbol{h}
is

\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{h}}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{y}:y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\text{ for all }i\in[n]\text{ and }\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\text{ satisfying }h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert{\infty}\leq 1\Big\}\kern 5.0pt.

If
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
is correctly specified, then there exist functions
\tilde{y}_{i}:\Delta_{i}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\tilde{y}_{i}(h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}))
for all
i\in[n]
and all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
This formalizes the understanding that the dimension reduction imposed by correctly specified exposure mappings is without loss of information.
A consequence is that an interference model
\mathcal{M}_{\boldsymbol{h}}
based on exposure mappings can be parameterized by the collections of functions
\tilde{y}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{y}_{n}
, specifying the value of the true potential outcome function for all units and exposures.

Most interference models considered in the literature are explicitly or implicitly based exposure mapping models.
We here provide some common examples.

•

No Treatment Effect.

Consider the model stipulating that the intervention has no causal effect whatsoever.
In this case, each unit has a single exposure
\Delta_{i}=\{1\}
and the exposure mapping is a constant function:
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=1
.
This model is correctly specified when the potential outcome function is constant, meaning that exists a
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
for each unit such that
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\alpha_{i}
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
This model is commonly used as the null hypothesis in Fisher-style randomization tests.

•

No Interference.

The most common interference model in causal inference is one that allows for arbitrary effects of the units’ own treatments but no interference between units, commonly called the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA).
Each unit has here two exposures
\Delta_{i}=\{0,1\}
and the mapping captures the unit’s own treatment:
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=z_{i}
.
If correctly specified, we can write
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\alpha_{i,0}\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=0]+\alpha_{i,1}\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=1]
for some
\alpha_{i,0},\alpha_{i,1}\in\mathbb{R}
.

•

Stratified Network Interference.

A common interference model that allows for interference restricts it to be anonymous, in the sense that the number of treated neighboring units in some network matters for the outcome but not their identity
(
12
)
.
To formalize this model, let
\mathcal{N}(i)
be the set of neighbors of unit
i
in the network at hand.
The exposure set of unit
i
is then
\Delta_{i}=\{0,1\}\times\{0,1,\dotsc,\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert\}
, where
\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert
is the network degree of the unit.
The corresponding exposure mapping captures the unit’s own treatment and the number of treated neighboring units:
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\textstyle\big\lparen z_{i},\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}z_{j}\big\rparen
.
If correctly specified, the potential outcome function can take a most
2(\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert+1)
unique values.

•

Arbitrary Network Interference.

A more complex interference model allows for non-anonymous interference, in the sense that the outcome might differ depending which neighbors are treated even if the number of treated neighbors is the same
(
13
)
.
Here, the exposure set of unit
i
is the power set of its own index and the indices of its neighbors:
\Delta_{i}=\mathcal{P}(\{i\}\cup\mathcal{N}(i))
.
The exposure mapping captures the exact combination of treatments assigned to the unit and its neighbors:

h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\{j\in\{i\}\cup\mathcal{N}(i):z_{j}=1\}
.
If correctly specified, the potential outcome function can take a most
2^{\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert+1}
unique values, demonstrating that it allows for considerably more complex interference than the stratified interference model.

3.2
Separation of Exposure Models

We consider specification tests of a null model
\mathcal{M}_{0}
based on a collection of exposure mappings
\boldsymbol{h}_{0}(\boldsymbol{z})=(h_{1}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),h_{2}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),\dotsc,h_{n}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z}))
against an alternative model
\mathcal{M}_{1}
based on some other exposure mappings
\boldsymbol{h}_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})=(h_{1}^{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),h_{2}^{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),\dotsc,h_{n}^{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))
.
As in the general setting in Section
2
, the alternative is more permissive or expressive than the null, meaning that all functions in the null are also in the alternative:
\mathcal{M}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{1}
.
The examples in the previous subsection have such nested structure, where the each model is nested in all subsequent models.
For example, the third model, stratified interference, is a superset of the two preceding models and a subset of the fourth model.

Our results are not sensitive to the exact choice of the separation functional
g
, and we can accommodate essentially any reasonable choice.
For concreteness, we consider a functional based on the sum of maximum square deviations of
y_{i}
in the alternative model among all interventions that map to the same exposure in the null model.
That is, if under the null
h_{i}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z})=h_{i}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})
, then the null stipulates that
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})
.
When the alternative departs from the null, there exists some
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
such that
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\neq y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})
.
Our separation functional captures the magnitude of this departure:
\{y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})-y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\}^{2}
.

Formally, the separation functional we use is

g(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{e\in\Delta_{i}^{0}}\sup\Big\{\big\{y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})-y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\big\}^{2}:\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\in\Omega\;\text{ s.t. }\;h_{i}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z})=h_{i}^{0}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})=e\Big\}.

As intended, the functional is zero
g(\boldsymbol{y})=0
only when
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
The uniform moment bound
\lvert y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\rvert\leq 1
restricts how much functions in the alternative can depart from the null.
Among
\boldsymbol{y}
satisfying
\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
, the separation can be at most
g(\boldsymbol{y})\leq(4/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\Delta_{i}^{0}\rvert
which corresponds to potential outcome function that differ from the null with the maximum amount
\{y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})-y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\}^{2}=4
for all units and exposures.

Because the moment bound restricts the maximum separation, we have
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)=\emptyset
for sufficiently large
\delta
.
If
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)=\emptyset
, the researcher has effectively ruled out all meaningful departures from the null already before running the experiment, making it pointless to test the null.
Thus, a non-trivial specification test necessitates a non-empty
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
.
We refer to all
\delta
that yield
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)=\emptyset
as
trivial separation values
.

3.3
Impossibility of Specification Testing

For a null model
\mathcal{M}_{0}
based on exposure mappings and a well-separated alternative model
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
also based on exposure mappings, let
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)
denote the minimax testing error according to Definition
5
of the corresponding testing problem.

Theorem 3.1
.

The specification testing problem corresponding to a null exposure model
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and the alternative exposure model
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
is impossible for all non-trivial separation values
\delta
, i.e.
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)=1
.

Theorem
3.1
says that specification testing of exposure mappings is an extremely challenging testing problem.
The theorem holds for all possible nested exposure models no matter how large the separation is between them, provided that the separation value
\delta
remains non-trivial, and it holds no matter the number of units
n
there are in the experiment.
A consequence of the theorem is that a uniformly consistent testing procedure can never be constructed for specification testing of exposure models.
Relaxing the uniform moment bound in
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
only makes the testing problem more difficult.

3.4
Proof Sketch

The proof of Theorem
3.1
constructs a lower bound on the minimax testing error using mixture distributions
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
over the null model
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and separated alternative model
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
, respectively.
The intuition is that the testing problem is hard if we can construct
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
that make the observed data distributions similar under both mixtures.
The following lemma formalizes this intuition.

Lemma 3.2
.

Given mixture
\Pi_{0}
supported on the null
\mathcal{M}_{0}
and mixture
\Pi_{1}
supported on a non-empty
\delta
-separated alternative
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
, the minimax testing error is lower bounded as

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)\geq 1-\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\Esym_{#1}\big[d_{\textrm{TV}}(Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big],

where
Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{z})
is the distribution of
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})=(y_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}),y_{2}(\boldsymbol{z}),\dots y_{n}(\boldsymbol{z}))
under mixture
\Pi_{k}
for a fixed
\boldsymbol{z}
, and
d_{\textrm{TV}}
is the total variation distance.

Proof.

The lemma follows from two key insights, following an approach reminiscent of Le Cam’s method for constructing lower bounds.
The first insight is that a maximum is lower bounded by an average.
In particular, for any mixture
\Pi_{0}
supported on the null
\mathcal{M}_{0}
, we have

\sup_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}}\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]\geq\Esym_{#1}\Big[\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]\big].

(1)

Applying this insight also to the alternative model and rearranging the order of the expectations,

\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)\geq\inf_{\mathcal{D}}\inf_{\phi}\bigg\{\Esym_{#1}\Big[\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]+\Esym_{#1}\big[1-\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]\big]\bigg\}.

(2)

The second insight is that the infimum over tests
\phi
is separable with respect to the interventions
\boldsymbol{z}
, in the sense that we can interpret
\phi
as set of
\lvert\Omega\rvert
unrelated functions indexed by
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
For a fixed intervention
\boldsymbol{z}
, let
\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}
denote the function
\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto\phi(\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{u})
.
After applying separability and rearranging, the right-hand side above equals

\displaystyle=\inf_{\mathcal{D}}\Esym_{#1}\Bigg[\inf_{\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}}\Big\lparen\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]+\Esym_{#1}\big[1-\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]\Big\rparen\big]

\displaystyle=1-\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\Esym_{#1}\Bigg[\sup_{\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}}\Big\lparen\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]-\Esym_{#1}\big[\phi_{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]\Big\rparen\big].

The lemma now follows by applying the definition of the total variation distance as the supremum over all measurable 0/1-valued functions of their expected difference under the two measures.
∎

The proof of Theorem
3.1
proceeds by constructing explicit mixtures
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
for the null and alternative models with the property
d_{\textrm{TV}}(Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))=0
for all interventions
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
This means that
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
are indistinguishable no matter the intervention, so no design can tell the two distributions apart.
The consequence is that
\sup_{\mathcal{D}}\Esym_{#1}\big[d_{\textrm{TV}}(Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))\big]=0
, which in turn implies that
\mathcal{R}^{*}(\delta)\geq 1
by Lemma
3.2
, completing the proof.

The construction of the mixtures
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
for the general case is conceptually simple but notationaly cumbersome, so we have opted to present the general construction in the
appendix. However, it is possible to illustrate the central aspects of the construction in a simpler setting, focusing on testing the sharp null of no treatment effect whatsoever against the alternative of no interference, corresponding to the first two examples described at the end of Section
3.1
.
We state this setting as a corollary of Theorem
3.1
for clarity.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let the null exposure model be no treatment effects, using exposure mappings
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=1
.
Let the alternative exposure model be no interference, using exposure mappings
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=z_{i}
.
The specification testing problem of distinguishing between these two models is impossible for all non-trivial separation values.

Potential outcome functions in the null model
\mathcal{M}_{0}
are all of the form
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\alpha_{i}
, with
\alpha_{i}\in[-1,1]
by the moment restriction.
The null model can thus be parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\lparen\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n}\rparen\in[-1,1]^{n}
.
Similarly, all functions in the alternative
\mathcal{M}_{1}
are of the form
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\beta_{i,0}\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=0]+\beta_{i,1}\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=1]
, so the alternative can be parametrized by
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}=\lparen\beta_{1,0},\dotsc,\beta_{n,0}\rparen
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}=\lparen\beta_{1,1},\dotsc,\beta_{n,1}\rparen
, both in
[-1,1]^{n}
.
The separation functional takes a simple form:
g(\boldsymbol{y})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\lparen\beta_{i,1}-\beta_{i,0}\big\rparen^{2}
.

The mixture
\Pi_{0}
we use for the null model is such that
\alpha_{i}
is set to either
-1
or
1
with equal probability independently between units, meaning that that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is uniform over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
The mixture
\Pi_{1}
for the alternative model is such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
is also uniform over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
, but
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}=-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
.
This means that
\lparen\beta_{i,1}-\beta_{i,0}\rparen^{2}=4
with probability one under
\Pi_{1}
, so all units always have the largest treatment effect allowed by the moment restriction.
Thus, we achieve the largest possible separation, and these mixtures can be used for any non-trivial separation value.

Lemma 3.4
.

The mixtures
\Pi_{0}
and
\Pi_{1}
are empirically indistinguishable:
d_{\textrm{TV}}(Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))=0
for all
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.

Proof.

Fix an intervention
\boldsymbol{z}\in\Omega
.
Under the null mixture
\Pi_{0}
, the distribution of
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})=\lparen\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{n}\rparen
is uniform over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
.
Under the alternative mixture
\Pi_{1}
, we have
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})=\lparen\beta_{1,z_{1}},\dotsc,\beta_{n,z_{n}}\rparen
.
Marginally, each coordinate is uniform over
\{-1,1\}
, and because the parameters in
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}
are independent between units, the coordinates of
\lparen\beta_{1,z_{1}},\dotsc,\beta_{n,z_{n}}\rparen
are independent.
It follows that
\boldsymbol{y}(\boldsymbol{z})
is uniform over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
also under
\Pi_{1}
.
Thus,
Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z})=Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z})
, so
d_{\textrm{TV}}(Q_{0}(\boldsymbol{z}),Q_{1}(\boldsymbol{z}))=0
.
∎

4
Consistent Test Against Linear-in-Means Model

The impossibility result in the previous section applies to specification tests based on exposure mappings.
Uniformly informative specification tests are potentially possible for interference models that impose restrictions beyond those imposed by the exposure mappings alone.
To illustrate this, we consider testing a null exposure model against an alternative model that impose additional restrictions, effectively ruling out some of the potential outcome functions a priori.

The null
\mathcal{M}_{0}
under consideration here is the exposure model based on mappings
h_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=z_{i}
, capturing a no interference assumption.
The alternative is the so-called network linear-in-means model, which stipulates that each potential outcome function is linear in the fraction of treated neighbors in some observed network.
As above, let
\mathcal{N}(i)
be the set of unit
i
’s neighbors in an undirected network.
The linear-in-means model is then

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}:y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\beta_{i,1}+\beta_{i,2}z_{i}+\beta_{i,3}T_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\big\}\kern 5.0pt,

where
T_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert^{-1}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}z_{j}
is the fraction of unit
i
’s neighbors who are treated.
The degree of each unit is
d_{i}=\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert
and the maximum degree is
d_{\textrm{max}}=\max_{i\in[n]}d_{i}
.
In order to avoid minor technical details, we assume that each subject in the network has at least two neighbors:
d_{i}\geq 2
.
We relax this assumption in the
appendix. The separation functional is
g(\boldsymbol{y})=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i,3}^{2}
, mirroring that
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
if and only if
\beta_{i,3}=0
for all
i\in[n]
.

The linear-in-means model is reminiscent of the stratified network interference model, presented as the third example in Section
3.1
.
However, because the linear-in-means model stipulates that the potential outcome functions are linear in
T_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})
, it restricts the functions beyond the restrictions imposed by the exposures in the stratified model.
The linear-in-means model is therefore not an exposure model as understood in this paper.

We construct a uniformly consistent testing procedure by constructing a consistent estimator of the separation functional
g(\boldsymbol{y})
evaluated at the true potential outcome function.
The design
\mathcal{D}
is the Bernoulli design under which each treatment
z_{i}
is drawn independently with
\Pr\lparen z_{i}=1\rparen=p
.
We write
y_{i}
and
T_{i}
as shorthand for
y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})
and
T_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})
to avoid notational clutter.
Our estimator of the separation value
g(\boldsymbol{y})
is then
\widehat{g}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}Y_{i}^{2}
, where
Y_{i}=y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})
is the observed outcome and

W_{i}=\frac{\Varsym\lparen T_{i}\rparen(T_{i}^{2}-\Esym[T_{i}^{2}])-\big\lparen\Esym[T_{i}^{3}]-\Esym[T_{i}^{2}]\Esym[T_{i}]\big\rparen\big\lparen T_{i}-\Esym[T_{i}]\big\rparen}{\Varsym\lparen T_{i}\rparen(\Esym[T_{i}^{4}]-\Esym[T_{i}^{2}]^{2})-\big\lparen\Esym[T_{i}^{3}]-\Esym[T_{i}^{2}]\Esym[T_{i}]\big\rparen^{2}}.

The weighting term
W_{i}
depends only on
T_{i}
, whose distribution is known.
The separation estimator is based on the estimation approach for quadratic functionals described by
10
, which was there used for variance estimation of linear point estimators.
The underlying idea of the approach is to construct
W_{i}
so that
\Esym_{#1}[W_{i}Y_{i}^{2}]=\beta_{i,3}^{2}
for all
i\in[n]
.
We describe the construction of the estimator in detail in the
appendix.

Proposition 4.1
.

There is a
C>0
such that
\Esym[\lparen\widehat{g}-g(\boldsymbol{y})\rparen^{2}]\leq C\cdot d_{\textrm{max}}^{5}/n
for all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
.

The proposition bounds the mean square error of the separation estimator using the maximum degree of the network
d_{\textrm{max}}
and is therefore consistent in mean square if
d_{\textrm{max}}=o\lparen n^{1/5}\rparen
.
To test the null
\mathcal{M}_{0}
against a
\delta
-separated alternative
\mathcal{M}_{1}(\delta)
, we consider a threshold test
\phi=\mathbf{1}[\widehat{g}\geq\tau]
where the threshold is chosen as
\tau=(d_{\textrm{max}}^{5}/n)^{1/4}
.
This choice ensures that the threshold will be converging to zero while also asymptotically dominating the statistical error of
\widehat{g}
.
The following theorem shows that this procedure is uniformly consistent.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the specification test of the no-interference model against the linear-in-means model.
If
d_{\textrm{max}}=o\lparen n^{1/5}\rparen
, the Bernoulli design together and threshold test
\phi=\mathbf{1}[\widehat{g}\geq\tau]
with
\tau=(d_{\textrm{max}}^{5}/n)^{1/4}
are uniformly consistent:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=0
for all
\delta>0
.

The proofs of Proposition
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
are presented in the
appendix.

5
Concluding remarks

An implication of our impossibility result is that specification testing is less useful than what we believe many researchers expect.
The most pessimistic perspective is that the scope of a specification testing is so narrow that it should fill essentially no role in empirical practice.
While we find the use of specification tests in current empirical practice too optimistic, we still see a role for specification tests, provided that researchers understand their limitations.
In particular, researchers should be mindful that there exists no test that can detect all types of departures from the null for specification testing problems based on exposure models.
Researchers must therefore rule out some departures from the null using prior knowledge and use testing procedures that target the departures that they are not comfortable ruling out a priori.
If a researcher is not comfortable ruling out any of the departures a priori, the only option is to entertain the possibility that the null is misspecified.

An important strand of the literature on interference has focused on testing of interference models using Fisher-style randomization tests and sharp null hypotheses.
It is acknowledged that this type of test has poor power against certain departures from the null.
Our impossibility result shows that poor power is an inescapable aspect of this type of testing problem.
The upshot is that the pursuit of uniformly informative specification tests of exposure mapping models is not productive, and focus should be directed to constructing tests that can detect certain types of departures.
For many existing tests of interference models, the relevant departures for which the test has power against are implicit.
It is therefore difficult for empirical researchers to judge whether the test is useful for their purposes.
We believe it would be useful to explicitly state what departures are of interest, and construct tests that target those departures.
The uniformly consistent test we describe in Section
4
is one such example.

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{e_{0}\in\Delta_{1}^{0}}\sup\Big\{(\beta_{i,e_{1}}-\beta_{i,e_{1}^{\prime}})^{2}:e_{1},e_{1}^{\prime}\in s_{i}(e_{0})\Big\}

∎

Recall that

.

Lemma

Appendix B
Consistent Test Against Linear-in-Means Model

In this section, we provide a detailed analysis for the specification test for SUTVA against a network linear-in-means model.
We begin by reviewing the setting.
The null model is given as

\mathcal{M}_{0}=\big\{\boldsymbol{y}:y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\alpha_{i,1}\cdot\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=1]+\alpha_{i,0}\cdot\mathbf{1}[z_{i}=0]\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\big\}\kern 5.0pt

and the alternative is given as

\mathcal{M}_{1}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{y}:y_{i}(\boldsymbol{z})=\beta_{i,1}+\beta_{i,2}z_{i}+\beta_{i,3}\Big\lparen\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}(i)}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}z_{j}\Big\rparen\text{ and }\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1\Big\}\kern 5.0pt,

where
\mathcal{N}(i)
is the neighborhood of subject

in the underlying network
G
.
Recall that the degree of each node is denoted
d_{i}=\lvert\mathcal{N}(i)\rvert
and the maximum degree is
d_{\textrm{max}}=\max_{i\in[n]}d_{i}

In the main body, we made the simplifying assumption that all subjects in the network have degree at least
d_{i}\geq 2
, but we consider the general case here.
We still assume that

for all

, as the parameterization
(\beta_{i,1},\beta_{i,2},\beta_{i,3})
of

is not injective when

.
The restriction

is merely a technicality, because the null and alternative models trivially coincide for units with
d_{i}=0
, so the null is known to be true a priori for units with no neighbors.

Recall that the separation functional is defined as

g(\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i,3}^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Our goal is to construct a consistent estimator of the separation value

for any
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
with uniformly bounded outcomes
\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}\leq 1
.
We will do so by constructing individual estimators for the

terms.

To this end, we propose the following weighted estimator.
We designed this weighting using the general Riesz representation principle for quadratic functionals provided in
10

We omit the tedious calculations required to derive the estimator using this technique; instead, the following proposition demonstrates the feasibility of the general approach:

Proposition

\displaystyle\Esym\Big[\{\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}Z+\beta_{3}X\}^{2}\cdot W\Big]

\Esym[XW]=0

To this end, let us calculate each of these quantities:

\displaystyle\Esym[W]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Varsym\lparen X\rparen\big\{\Esym[X^{2}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\big\}+\big\lparen\Esym[X^{3}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\Esym[X]\big\rparen\big\{\Esym[X]-\Esym[X]\big\}}{\Varsym\lparen X\rparen\lparen\Esym[X^{4}]-\Esym[X^{2}]^{2}\rparen-\lparen\Esym[X^{3}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\Esym[X]\rparen^{2}}=0

\displaystyle=\frac{\Varsym\lparen X\rparen\big\{\Esym[X^{3}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\Esym[X]\big\}+\big\lparen\Esym[X^{3}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\Esym[X]\big\rparen\big\{\Esym[X^{2}]-\Esym[X]^{2}\big\}}{\Varsym\lparen X\rparen\lparen\Esym[X^{4}]-\Esym[X^{2}]^{2}\rparen-\lparen\Esym[X^{3}]-\Esym[X^{2}]\Esym[X]\rparen^{2}}=0

We apply this general weighting estimator to the setting of the linear-in-means model.
For notational simplicity, we define the (random) fraction of treated neighbors as
x_{i}=1/d_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}z_{j}
.
Throughout this section, we will focus on the Bernoulli design where binary treatments
z_{1}\dots z_{n}
are chosen independently with
\Pr\lparen z_{i}=1\rparen=p
.

Our next goal will to be calculate the first four moments of the random fraction of treated neighbors

.
In these calculations, the following fact will be very useful: the
k

\Esym[X^{k}]=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}{n\brack k}n^{\underline{k}}p^{k}\kern 5.0pt,

where

is the Stirling number of of the second kind and

is the

.
This will be useful because under the Bernoulli design, the number of treated neighbors

is Binomial with parameters

and

Using this, we can calculate the first four moments of

1.

d_{i}\cdot\Esym[x_{i}]=d_{i}p

2.

d_{i}^{2}\cdot\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]=d_{i}p+d_{i}(d_{i}-1)p^{2}

3.

d_{i}^{3}\cdot\Esym[x_{i}^{3}]=d_{i}p+3d_{i}(d_{i}-1)p^{2}+d_{i}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)p^{3}

d_{i}^{4}\cdot\Esym[x_{i}^{4}]=d_{i}p+7d_{i}(d_{i}-1)p^{2}+6d_{i}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)p^{3}+d_{i}(d_{i}-1)(d_{i}-2)(d_{i}-3)p^{4}

Now that the first four moments of

\displaystyle W_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Varsym\lparen x_{i}\rparen\big\lparen x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\big\rparen-\big\lparen\Esym[x_{i}^{3}]-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\Esym[x_{i}]\big\rparen\Big\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\Big\rparen}{\Varsym\lparen x_{i}\rparen\lparen\Esym[x_{i}^{4}]-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]^{2}\rparen-\lparen\Esym[x_{i}^{3}]-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\Esym[x_{i}]\rparen^{2}}

For the remainder of the appendix, we restrict our attention to an equal treatment probability

.
This choice greatly simplifies the weighting term:

W_{i}=8d_{i}^{2}\big\lparen 1-\frac{1}{d_{i}}\big\rparen^{-1}\Big\{\big\lparen x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\big\rparen-\big\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\big\rparen\Big\}\kern 5.0pt.

Note that this weighting term is well-defined only when the subject has degree at least

.
In other words, a subject is required to have at least 2 neighbors in order to ensure sufficient variation in

so that our weighting technique can estimate
\alpha_{i,3}^{2}
in an unbiased manner.
Let
T=\{i:d_{i}=1\}
denote the set of subjects with exactly one neighbor.

We define the separation estimator as

\widehat{g}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\notin T}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}\cdot W_{i}\kern 5.0pt.

The following proposition characterizes the bias of the separation estimator.

Proposition
S
2
.

For all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
, the bias of the separation estimator is bounded as

g(\boldsymbol{y})-\Esym[\widehat{g}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in T}\beta_{i,3}^{2}\leq 4\cdot\frac{\lvert T\rvert}{n}\kern 5.0pt.

Proof.

By Proposition
S1
, we have that
\Esym[\widehat{g}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\notin T}\beta_{i,3}^{2}

Thus,

g(\boldsymbol{y})-\Esym[\widehat{g}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in T}\beta_{i,3}^{2}
.
To finish the proof, it suffices to show that
\lvert\beta_{i,3}\rvert\leq 2
.
To this end, observe that
\beta_{i,3}=y_{i}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{e}_{i})-y_{i}(\boldsymbol{0})

Using the triangle inequality together with the uniform bound

\lvert\beta_{i,3}\rvert=\lvert y_{i}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{e}_{i})-y_{i}(\boldsymbol{0})\rvert\leq\lvert y_{i}(\boldsymbol{1}-\boldsymbol{e}_{i})\rvert+\lvert y_{i}(\boldsymbol{0})\rvert\leq 2\kern 5.0pt,

We now seek to bound the variance of our estimator.
We will use the dependency graph method where first we calculate the variance of the individual estimators, and then we bound the number of individual estimators that can be correlated.
The technique itself is somewhat crude as the covariance between individual estimators is bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
Although it is possible to refine this method further, this analysis suffices for our present goal.

Lemma

3

Consider the Bernoulli design with

For all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}

\Varsym\lparen Y_{i}^{2}W_{i}\rparen\leq\Esym\Big[Y_{i}^{4}W_{i}^{2}\Big]\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{y}\rVert_{\infty}^{4}\cdot\Esym[W_{i}^{2}]\kern 5.0pt.

Using the formula for the weight

\displaystyle\Esym[W_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=2^{6}d_{i}^{4}\Big\lparen 1-\frac{1}{d_{i}}\Big\rparen^{-2}\Esym\Big[\Big\{\lparen x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\rparen-\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\rparen\Big\}^{2}]

(3)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{7}d_{i}^{4}\Big\lparen 1-\frac{1}{d_{i}}\Big\rparen^{-2}\Big\{\Esym\big[\lparen x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\rparen^{2}]+\Esym\big[\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\rparen^{2}]\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{9}d_{i}^{4}\Big\{\Esym\big[\lparen x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\rparen^{2}]+\Esym\big[\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\rparen^{2}]\Big\}\kern 5.0pt,

(5)

We now seek to bound the two expectations.

\Esym\big[\lparen x_{i}-\Esym[x_{i}]\rparen^{2}]=\Varsym\lparen x_{i}\rparen=\Varsym\Big\lparen\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}z_{j}\Big\rparen=\frac{1}{d_{i}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}\Varsym\lparen z_{j}\rparen=\frac{p(1-p)}{d_{i}}=\frac{1}{4d_{i}}\kern 5.0pt.

Next, using the expressions for

\displaystyle d_{i}^{4}\cdot\Esym[(x_{i}^{2}-\Esym[x_{i}^{2}])^{2}]

\displaystyle=d_{i}^{4}\cdot\Esym[x_{i}^{4}]-\Big\lparen d_{i}\Esym[x_{i}^{2}]\Big\rparen^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{i}\big\lparen p-7p^{2}+12p^{3}-6p^{4}\big\rparen+d_{i}^{2}\big\lparen 6p^{2}-16p^{3}+10p^{4}\big\rparen+d_{i}^{3}\big\lparen 4p^{3}-4p^{4}\big\rparen

where in the final two inequalities used that

The following lemma keeps track of which individual estimators are independent.

If

Proposition

For all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}

\Varsym\lparen\widehat{g}\rparen\leq 2^{9}\cdot\frac{d_{\textrm{max}}^{5}}{n}\kern 5.0pt.

\displaystyle\Varsym\lparen\widehat{g}\rparen

\displaystyle=\Varsym\Big\lparen\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{2}W_{i}\Big\rparen

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in M_{i}}\Covsym\lparen Y_{i}^{2}W_{i},Y_{j}^{2}W_{j}\rparen

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in M_{i}}\sqrt{\Varsym\lparen Y_{i}^{2}W_{i}\rparen\Varsym\lparen Y_{j}^{2}WIj\rparen}

Combining the analysis of the bias (Proposition
S2

Proposition
S
6
.

For all
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}
,
the mean square error of the separation estimator is bounded as

\Esym\big[\lparen g(\boldsymbol{y})-\widehat{g}\rparen^{2}\big]^{1/2}\leq 2^{5}\cdot\Big\lparen\frac{\lvert T\rvert}{n}\vee\frac{d_{\textrm{max}}^{5/2}}{n^{1/2}}\Big\rparen\kern 5.0pt.

The first is that

The second is that

Recall that our statistical test is based on evaluating whether the estimated separation

In order to ensure that the threshold goes to zero but is also asymptotically dominating the mean square error, we set it as

The following theorem uses Markov’s inequality to show that this is a consistent test.

Theorem

.

Suppose that the network

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=0

Proof.

Let us being by considering a fixed separation value

We seek to show that the testing error converges to zero:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{D},\phi,\delta)=0

Consider a function in the null model
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{M}_{0}
.
