A note on the Ratio and Inertia Bounds for the k k -Independence Number
Jun Gao, Jie Ma, Oleg Pikhurko

Abstract
The k k -th power G k G^{k} of a graph G G is the graph on the same vertex set where the edge set consists of those pairs of distinct vertices of G G that are at distance at most k k from each other. A. Abiad, G. Coutinho, and M. A. Fiol [ On the k k -independence number of graphs , Discrete Mathematics 342 (2019), 2875–2885] proposed extensions of the classical ratio (for regular graphs) and inertia bounds to the independence number of G k G^{k} for k ⩾ 2 k\geqslant 2 . Continuing a line of work comparing these two parameters with other known bounds, we show that the ϑ \vartheta -function of L. Lovász and the weighted inertia bound of A. R. Calderbank and P. Frankl, when applied directly to G k G^{k} , perform at least as well as the ratio and inertia bounds of Abiad–Coutinho–Fiol, respectively. In particular, ϑ ⁡ ( G k ) \vartheta(G^{k}) provides a polynomial-time computable upper bound on the independence number of G k G^{k} that is at least as strong as the ratio bound when the latter applies (i.e., when the graph G G is regular).



1
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with vertex set
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
and
k
be a positive integer. The
k
-independence number
of
G
, written
\alpha_{k}(G)
, is the size of a largest subset
S\subseteq V
such that any two distinct vertices of
S
are at distance greater than
k
in
G
. Equivalently,
\alpha_{k}(G)=\alpha(G^{k})
is the standard independence number of the
k
-th power

G^{k}
of
G
, which is the graph on the same vertex set where two distinct vertices
u
and
v
are joined if they are at distance at most
k
in
G
. The parameter
\alpha_{k}
naturally arises in coding theory: in many natural ambient discrete metric spaces
({\mathcal{X}},d)
one can build a graph
G
on
{\mathcal{X}}
such that an error correcting/detecting code of minimum distance
k+1
corresponds exactly to a
k
-independent set in
G
.

Abiad, Coutinho and Fiol
[
1
]
introduced two upper bounds on
\alpha_{k}(G)
, which are defined as follows. For a symmetric matrix
X\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
, let
\lambda_{1}(X)\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}(X)
denote its eigenvalues. Let
A\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the adjacency matrix of
G
. For
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
(that is,
p
is a real polynomial of degree at most
k
), define

W(p):=\max_{u\in V}(p(A))_{u,u},\qquad w(p):=\min_{u\in V}(p(A))_{u,u},\qquad\lambda(p):=\min_{i\in[2,n]}p(\lambda_{i}(A)).

(1)

Define
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
as the minimum over all
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
of

{\mathcal{I}}^{p}_{k}(G):=\min\bigl\{\,|\{i:p(\lambda_{i}(A))\geqslant w(p)\}|,\ |\{i:p(\lambda_{i}(A))\leqslant W(p)\}|\,\bigr\}.

Furthermore, if
G
is regular then define
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
as the infimum of

{\mathcal{R}}^{p}_{k}(G):=n\cdot\frac{W(p)-\lambda(p)}{p(\lambda_{1}(A))-\lambda(p)},

(2)

over all
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
with
p(\lambda_{1}(A))>\lambda(p)
.

Theorem 1
(Inertia Bound, Abiad, Coutinho and Fiol
[
1
, Theorem 3.1]
)

For every graph
G
and every integer
k\geqslant 1
, it holds that
\alpha_{k}(G)\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
.

Theorem 2
(Ratio Bound, Abiad, Coutinho and Fiol
[
1
, Theorem 3.2]
)

For every regular graph
G
and every integer
k\geqslant 1
, it holds that
\alpha_{k}(G)\leqslant{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
.

For
k=1
, we get the well-known bounds on
\alpha(G)
due to Cvetković
[
5
]
and Hoffman
[
9
]
, see
[
1
]
.
The functions
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
and
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
have been actively studied, including their comparisons with other known upper bounds on
\alpha(G^{k})
for various families of graphs
G
. We refer the reader to the recent survey by Abiad, Peters and Ravagnani
[
2
]
.

Both of these bounds require choosing a polynomial
p
. As discussed in
[
2
]
, this task (for either of the bounds) can be expressed as a mixed integer linear program (MILP). The number of Boolean variables in the provided MILPs is equal to the number of distinct eigenvalues of
G
which can potentially be as large as the number of vertices in
G
. So this MILP is not well suited for practical calculations.

Very recently, Abiad, van Hoesel and Michaux
[
3
]
presented a polynomial-time (in
|V(G)|
) algorithm for determining the value of the Inertia Bound
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
for every fixed
k
. For the Ratio Bound
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
, the original paper of Abiad, Coutinho and Fiol
[
1
]
provided a closed-form expression for
k=2
. An analogous closed-form expression for
k=3
was obtained by Kavi and Newman
[
12
]
.
Fiol
[
7
]
showed that if the number of closed walks in
G
of any given length
\ell\leqslant k
and starting at a vertex
u
does not depend on
u
then
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
can be computed by a linear program.
A polynomial-time algorithm that computes
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
in general is not known.

A well-known parameter that upper bounds
\alpha(G)
is the
\vartheta
-function
of Lovász
[
14
]
(who used it to determine the Shannon capacity of the 5-cycle). One of its equivalent definitions (
[
14
, Theorem 3]
) which is convenient in this note is

\vartheta(G):=\min\{\lambda_{1}(X):X\in\mathcal{L}_{G}\},

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}_{G}
is the set of
n\times n
symmetric matrices
X
such that

X_{i,j}=1
for every
i,j\in[n]
that are equal or non-adjacent in
G
.

Here we show that the value of the
\vartheta
-function on
G^{k}
is at most the Ratio Bound
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
.

Theorem 3

For every regular graph
G
and every integer
k\geqslant 1
, it holds that
\vartheta(G^{k})\leqslant{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
.

As we already remarked,
\vartheta(G)\geqslant\alpha(G)
for any graph
G
.
Also,
\vartheta(G^{k})
can be computed in polynomial time in
n
on input
(G,k)
as a semi-definite program with one
n\times n
-matrix variable. Thus,
\vartheta(G^{k})
provides a polynomially computable upper bound on
\alpha_{k}(G)
for an arbitrary graph
G
that, by Theorem
3
, performs at least as well as the Ratio Bound
{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
of Abiad, Coutinho and Fiol
[
1
]
for regular graphs.

Next, we compare the Inertia Bound
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
with the strengthening of Cvetković’ bound
[
5
]
that was introduced by Calderbank and Frankl
[
4
]
and is defined as follows.
A
weighted adjacency matrix
of
G
is a symmetric matrix
X\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
such that
X_{i,j}=0
whenever
i,j\in[n]
are equal or non-adjacent in
G
. Note that we allow arbitrary real values for adjacent indices
i
and
j
.
The parameter
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
, which we call the
Weighted Inertia Bound
here, is defined as the minimum number of non-negative eigenvalues attained by any such matrix:

n_{\geqslant 0}(G):=\min_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{G}}\left|\{i\in[n]:\lambda_{i}(X)\geqslant 0\}\right|,

where
\mathcal{H}_{G}
denotes the set of weighted adjacency matrices of
G
. It can be derived by eigenvalue interlacing that
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)\geqslant\alpha(G)
; see
[
4
]
. Also, the classical bound by Cvetković
[
5
]
is obtained by allowing
X
to be
A
or
-A
only.

It was verified that
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)=\alpha(G)
for all graphs with at most
10
vertices (see the discussion in
[
6
, Section 6]
), and Godsil
[
8
]
asked if equality always holds. However, Sinkovic
[
15
]
showed that there are graphs (namely the 17-vertex Paley graph or this graph minus a vertex) with
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
strictly larger than
\alpha(G)
. Examples of graphs where these two parameters are strikingly far apart were constructed by Kwan and Wigderson
[
13
]
and Tang, Zhang and Elphick
[
16
]
.

The computational complexity of
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
is open but sometimes the choice of weights can be guided by symmetries of the problem, as it was done in the original paper of Calderbank and Frankl
[
4
]
for the intersection graph on
k
-sets and Huang, Klurman and Pohoata
[
11
]
for the hypercube graph.

Our second result states that
n_{\geqslant 0}(G^{k})
is always at most
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
.

Theorem 4

For every graph
G
and every integer
k\geqslant 1
, it holds that

n_{\geqslant 0}(G^{k})\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G).

Remark.

We would like to thank Jiang Zhou for pointing out, after the first version of this manuscript was posted on arXiv, that Theorem
3
can also be derived from Theorem 4.1 in
[
17
]
(by setting
M=p(A)-\lambda(p)I
and taking
x
to be the all-ones vector in the formula
\vartheta(\cdot)
therein).

2
Preliminaries and Proofs

Let
X\in\mathbbm{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric matrix. Recall that
\lambda_{1}(X)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(X)\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}(X)
denote the eigenvalues of
X
.
We say that
X
is
positive semidefinite
if
\lambda_{n}(X)\geqslant 0
(that is, all of its eigenvalues are non-negative). For symmetric matrices
X,Y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
, we say that

X

interlaces

Y
if they satisfy the following chain of inequalities:

\lambda_{1}(X)\geqslant\lambda_{1}(Y)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(X)\geqslant\lambda_{2}(Y)\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}(X)\geqslant\lambda_{n}(Y).

We will need the following two results that can be directly derived from the more general inequalities of Weyl on the eigenvalues of the sum of two Hermitian matrices (see e.g.
[
10
, Theorem 4.3.1]
).

Corollary 5
(See
[
10
, Corollary 4.3.12]
)

Let
X
and
Y
be
n\times n
real symmetric matrices, and suppose that
Y
is positive semidefinite. Then

\lambda_{i}(X)\leqslant\lambda_{i}(X+Y),\quad\mbox{for all $i=1,\dots,n$.}

Corollary 6
(See
[
10
, Corollary 4.3.9]
)

Let
X
and
Y
be
n\times n
real symmetric matrices. Suppose that
Y
is positive semidefinite and has rank 1. Then
X+Y
interlaces
X
.

The
\vartheta
-function of Lovász has many other equivalent reformulations. Here we need the following minor variation where we allow diagonal entries to assume values at least
1
(instead of being exactly
1
). Namely, we claim that

\vartheta(G)=\min\bigl\{\lambda_{1}(X)\ :\ X\in\mathcal{L}^{*}_{G}\bigr\},

(4)

where
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{G}
consists of all symmetric matrices
X\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
such that
X_{i,j}=1
for all non-adjacent distinct
i,j\in[n]
and
X_{i,i}\geqslant 1
for all
i\in[n]
. (Recall that the vertex set of the graph
G
is assumed to be
[n]
.) Note that the difference between
\mathcal{L}^{*}_{G}
and the set
\mathcal{L}_{G}
from the definition of
\vartheta(G)
in (
3
) is that the former set allows diagonal entries to be arbitrary reals at least 1 (instead of each forced to be 1 as in the latter set). Thus
\vartheta(G)
is at least the right-hand side of (
4
). On the other hand, take any matrix
X\in\mathcal{L}^{*}_{G}
and let
X^{\prime}
be obtained from
X
by changing all diagonal entries to
1
. Then
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{G}
. Also,
\lambda_{1}(X^{\prime})\leqslant\lambda_{1}(X)
by Corollary
5
, since
X-X^{\prime}
is a diagonal matrix with non-negative diagonal entries
X_{i,i}-1
for
i\in[n]
. Thus (
4
) holds.

Our proof of Theorem
3
is an adaptation of the proof by Lovász
[
14
]
of the special case
k=1
of it (namely that, for any regular graph
G
,
\vartheta(G)
is always at most the Hoffman Bound).

Proof of Theorem
3
.
Let
G
be any regular graph on
[n]
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A)\geqslant\dots\geqslant\lambda_{n}(A)
. Take any
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
with
p(\lambda_{1}(A))>\lambda(p)
and
define

\beta:=\frac{n}{p(\lambda_{1}(A))-\lambda(p)}>0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\gamma:=W(p).

(5)

(Recall that
\lambda(p)
and
W(p)
are defined in (
1
).)
Let
X:=J-\beta(p(A)-\gamma I)
, where
J
denotes the
n\times n
matrix with all entries equal to
1
and
I
is the identity matrix.

Let us check that
X
satisfies all assumptions in (
4
), our minor reformulation of the Lovász
\vartheta
-function. Clearly,
X
is a symmetric matrix. Take any distinct
i,j\in[n]
which are non-adjacent in
G^{k}
.
For every integer
m\in[0,k]
, the
(i,j)
-th entry of
A^{m}
is 0 since it counts the number of
m
-step walks from
i
to
j
in
G
while the distance between
i
and
j
is strictly larger than
k
. Thus
(p(A))_{i,j}=0
and
X_{i,j}=1
, as desired. Also, the
i
-th diagonal entry of
X
equals
1-\beta((p(A))_{i,i}-\gamma)
, which is at least
1
since
\beta>0
and
\gamma=W(p)\geqslant(p(A))_{i,i}
. (This consideration dictated our choice of
\gamma
.)

The matrices
J
,
I
and
A
share the all-ones vector
{\mathbbm{1}}
as an eigenvector with the corresponding eigenvalue being
n
,
1
and
\lambda_{1}(A)
respectively (where we use the regularity of
G
in the statement involving
A
). Furthermore, on the orthogonal complement of
{\mathbbm{1}}
, the matrices
J
and
I
act by scalar multiplication (by
0
and
1
respectively). Hence the eigenvalues of
X
are
n-\beta(p(\lambda_{1}(A))-\gamma)
(with eigenvector
{\mathbbm{1}}
) and
-\beta(p(\lambda_{i}(A))-\gamma)
for
i\in[2,n]
. Thus, by
\beta
being positive, the maximum eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}(X)
of
X
is
\max\{\,n-\beta(p(\lambda_{1}(A))-W(p)),\ -\beta(\lambda(p)-W(p))\,\}
. Our choice of
\beta
makes these two terms equal, giving that
\lambda_{1}(X)
equals
{\mathcal{R}}^{p}_{k}(G)
, the quantity in (
2
). Since
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
was arbitrary with
p(\lambda_{1}(A))>\lambda(p)
, we conclude that
\vartheta(G^{k})\leqslant{\mathcal{R}}_{k}(G)
.

Let us turn to Theorem
4
. For a symmetric matrix
X\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
and
t\in{\mathbbm{R}}
define

f(X,t):=|\{i\in[n]:\lambda_{i}(X)\geqslant t\}|+|\{j\in[n]:X_{j,j}<t\}|.

Let
n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)
be the minimum value of
f(X,t)
over all
X\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{G}
and
t\in{\mathbbm{R}}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}
denotes the set of symmetric matrices
X\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n\times n}
with
X_{i,j}=0
for distinct non-adjacent
i,j\in[n]
. Note that the difference between
\mathcal{H}_{G}^{*}
and
\mathcal{H}_{G}
is that the former set allows arbitrary diagonal entries (while the latter requires that all diagonal entries are 0).
Our next result shows that this extra freedom does not help and the parameters
n_{\geqslant 0}^{*}
and
n_{\geqslant 0}
coincide.

Proposition 7

It holds that
n_{\geqslant 0}^{*}(G)=n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
for any graph
G
.

Proof of Proposition
7
.

Suppose that
V=[n]
.
Then we have

n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)=\min_{t,\,X\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{G}}f(X,t)\quad\text{and}\quad n_{\geqslant 0}(G)=\min_{X\in\mathcal{H}_{G}}f(X,0).

If we take any optimal choice of
X
for
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
then by considering the same matrix
X
and
t=0
in the definition of
n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)
we conclude that
n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)\leqslant n_{\geqslant 0}(G)
.

Let us prove the converse inequality.
Let
t\in\mathbbm{R}
and
X_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{*}_{G}
be such that

n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)=f(X_{1},t).

Let
X
be the matrix obtained from
X_{1}
by setting all diagonal entries to
t
.

It follows from
X-tI\in\mathcal{H}_{G}
and
f(X-tI,0)=f(X,t)
that
n_{\geqslant 0}(G)\leqslant f(X,t)
.
So it is enough to show
f(X,t)\leqslant n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)
.
Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1}
be a sequence of matrices, where for each
i\in[n]
,
X_{i+1}
is obtained from
X_{i}
by setting the
i
-th diagonal entry
(X_{i})_{i,i}
to
t
. Clearly
X=X_{n+1}
. Fix any
r\in[n]
.

If
(X_{r})_{r,r}\geqslant t
then, by Corollary
6
, we have

|\{i:\lambda_{i}(X_{r+1})\geqslant t\}|\leqslant|\{i:\lambda_{i}(X_{r})\geqslant t\}|

while clearly
|\{j:(X_{r+1})_{j,j}<t\}|=|\{j:(X_{r})_{j,j}<t\}|
, which implies that
f(X_{r+1},t)\leqslant f(X_{r},t)
.

If
(X_{r})_{r,r}<t
, then we have
|\{j:(X_{r})_{j,j}<t\}|=|\{j:(X_{r+1})_{j,j}<t\}|+1
.
Let
j
be the maximum integer in
[n]
such that
\lambda_{j}(X_{r})\geqslant t
(and set
j=0
if no such integer exists), so that
|\{i:\lambda_{i}(X_{r})\geqslant t\}|=j
.
By Corollary
6
,
\lambda_{i+1}(X_{r+1})\leqslant\lambda_{i}(X_{r})
for every
i\in[n-1]
. In particular, if
j\leqslant n-2
then
\lambda_{j+2}(X_{r+1})\leqslant\lambda_{j+1}(X_{r})<t
, so all eigenvalues of
X_{r+1}
of index at least
j+2
are smaller than
t
and

|\{i:\lambda_{i}(X_{r+1})\geqslant t\}|\leqslant j+1=|\{i:\lambda_{i}(X_{r})\geqslant t\}|+1.

If
j\geqslant n-1
then the above bound is trivial.
So we also have
f(X_{r+1},t)\leqslant f(X_{r},t)
.

Thus for any
r\in[n]
we have

f(X_{r+1},t)\leqslant f(X_{r},t),

which implies that
f(X,t)=f(X_{n+1},t)\leqslant f(X_{1},t)=n^{*}_{\geqslant 0}(G)
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.
Take any
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
.
Then both
p(A)
and
-p(A)
are in
\mathcal{H}_{G^{k}}^{*}
since
p(A)_{i,j}=0
for any
i,j\in[n]
at distance more than
k
in
G
. Observe that

\displaystyle f(p(A),w(p))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{i:p(\lambda_{i}(A))\geqslant w(p)\}|,

\displaystyle f(-p(A),-W(p))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\{i:p(\lambda_{i}(A))\leqslant W(p)\}|.

(Indeed,
w(p)=\min_{u}(p(A))_{u,u}
and
W(p)=\max_{u}(p(A))_{u,u}
, so the second summand in the definition of
f
vanishes in each case.) Thus

n_{\geqslant 0}^{*}(G^{k})\leqslant\min\left\{f(p(A),w(p)),\,f(-p(A),-W(p))\right\}={\mathcal{I}}^{p}_{k}(G).

Since
p\in{\mathbbm{R}}_{k}[x]
was arbitrary, we conclude that
n_{\geqslant 0}^{*}(G^{k})\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
. By Proposition
7
,
n_{\geqslant 0}(G^{k})=n_{\geqslant 0}^{*}(G^{k})\leqslant{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(G)
, as desired.
