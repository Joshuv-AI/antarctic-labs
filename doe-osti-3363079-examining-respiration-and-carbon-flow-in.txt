Project Title: Examining respiration and carbon flow in intermittent, urban rivers using novel
chamber methodologies

We acknowledge federal support through DOE Award Number SC0023086 to UTSA project
team members.

Abstract
Intermittent rivers and ephemeral streams (IRES) comprise up to 60% of the total length of all
river networks on the globe. Due to water abstraction, stream intermittent flow is likely to become
more widespread in the future. IRES have been shown to have high microbial activity due to the
drying and then wetting of streambeds after rainfall. It is important to understand post-flood
microbial activity in these ecosystems as we may be underestimating their contribution togas
emissions. This work’s research goals are to understand how perennial (always flowing) and
intermittent (flow stops and then resumes once rainfall occurs) reaches of an urban river in San
Antonio, Texas, differ in their microbial population identities and if these populations change due
to the type of organic matter present. These efforts were done in concert with the Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory (PNNL) consortia. We found that intermittent streams that are dry have lower
microbial activity relative to flowing streams and this is likely due to less easy-to-degrade carbon.
Intermittent dry sediments, in turn, had lower diversity of bacteria/archaea. These data show that
droughts which cause streams to stop flowing have cascading impacts on both ecosystem
microbiota and their contribution to nutrient biodegradation; such a finding indicates reductions in
important ecosystem services.

Project Activities Summary
Original project objectives were:

 (1) Measure state changes in stream microbial respiration among intermittent and perennial
 reaches in a drought-prone urban river
 (2) Dissect contribution of water-column and hyporheic sediments to ecosystem-scale
 respiration estimates by using respiration chambers designed by PNNL scientists (M.
 Kauffman)
 (3) Relate respiration rates to dissolved organic matter quantity, composition, and other major
 ion frequencies after flooding events

Hypotheses were based upon the functioning of field respiration chambers originally designed by
PNNL staff scientists meant to be deployed short-term in rivers. However, we learned significant
electronic malfunctions with chambers resulting in us discontinuing that specific path. Instead,
we chose to adopt the Worldwide Hydrobiogeochemistry Observation Network for Dynamic
River Systems (WHONDRS; https://www.pnnl.gov/projects/WHONDRS) field protocols used in
their 2019 sampling campaigns (see Borton et al., 2022). This allowed us the opportunity to
continue collaboration with PNNL and still address our Objective 3 to some degree. Our revised
objectives include:

 (1) Measure state changes in stream microbial respiration among intermittent and perennial
 reaches in a drought-prone urban river using an in vitro method
 (2) Connect bacterial diversity in hyporheic sediments to ecosystem-scale respiration estimates
 by using in-vitro respiration assays designed by PNNL scientists
 (3) Understand differences in dissolved organic matter quantity, composition, and other major
 ion frequencies after flooding events
First, the majority of data presented, unless otherwise stated, is available through the M.Sc.
student, Mariana Vergara, graduate thesis via ProQuest (Vergara, 2025). This funding award
supported her thesis work.

To test our new project objectives, we selected 7 intermittent and 7 perennial stream reaches in
the San Antonio River Watershed in and near metropolitan San Antonio (Figure 1). Sites were
sampled during a dry period, August-September 2023, and a wet period, January-February 2024.
Sites with intermittent flow, and dry upon sampling, are categorized as “intermittent non-
flowing” which were most intermittent sites during the dry season. Sites with intermittent flow
but flowing upon sampling are categorized as “intermittent flowing.” Sites with flow never
ceasing are “perennial”, most of which have discharge from industrial or wastewater sources.
Throughout the results, these categories are referred to as “flow type” and the time of sampling
(wet versus dry period) is “season.” The WHONDRS sampling approach was used to obtain
several parameters, including water-column nutrients and organic matter, sediment nutrient
pools, grain size, and organic matter, respiration performed in-vitro, and 16S rRNA gene-
sequencing for both water and sediments (Table 1).

Major Results
Additional results not presented are provided in Vergara (2025).

Objective 1: Stream microbial respiration rate changes

Microbial respiration, as measured via in-vitro chambers incubations, differed between flow
conditions (dry versus wet periods; Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.06). During the dry season, respiration
was ~71% lower in intermittent, non-flowing sites relative to intermittent flowing and perennial.
During the wet season, no differences among flow types were detected (Kruskal-Wallis p =
0.48). However, mean respiration rates were higher in perennial sites relative to intermittent
flowing sites during the wet season (Figure 2). In summary, intermittent sites non-flowing upon
had lower respiration rates after water addition and oxygen consumption measurements in the
laboratory relative to other flow types. This indicates that drought in these study systems without
permanent flow reduces the capacity for the microbial communities to respire therefore
potentially altering carbon flow dynamics.

Figure 2. Mean respiration rate of collected sediment samples during the (A) dry and (B) wet season with ±1
standard error bar. Post-hoc statistical differences within seasons are denoted with * ≤ 0.05, ** ≤ 0.01, and *** ≤
0.001.

Objective 2: Stream microbial diversity

Bacterial/archaeal Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASV), a mathematical proxy for bacterial
species, richness differed across flow types in both the dry (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.06) and wet
season (p = 0.04). Richness was higher in perennial sites relative to intermittent flowing but did
not differ from intermittent non-flowing sites. Similarly, Shannon’s Diversity was higher in
perennial sites relative to intermittent flowing sites during the wet season (p = 0.05) only. Faith’s

phylogenetic diversity (PD), which calculates the sum of a phylogenetic tree’s branch lengths
within a community, also was greatest in perennial sites relative to intermittent non-flowing, not
intermittent flowing (p ≤ 0.04; Figure 3).

When evaluating how community composition changes, flow type, season, and their interaction
accounted for only ~18% of the variation in composition across the experiment. Biogeochemical
indicators also measured in this study did correlate with composition. Notably, sediment nitrogen
and DOC as well as respiration rate and several classes of organic matter compounds (see
Objective 3).

panels). Post-hoc statistical differences within seasons are denoted with * ≤ 0.05, ** ≤ 0.01, and *** ≤ 0.001.

Objective 3: Organic matter dynamics

Data generated for organic matter composition was derived from Fourier transform ion cyclotron
resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR/MS) which allows identification of individual OM
molecules and elemental composition, to some degree. This data generation was led by Vanessa
Garayburu-Caruso at PNNL. Here, we show our results for sediment-based OM molecular
composition. However, we also have water column OM data as well.

Significant differences in water extractable organic matter compound classes from sediments
were detected among flow types. During the dry season, carbohydrate-like (Kruskal-Wallis p =

 7

0.01) and condensed-hydrocarbon like (p = 0.04) molecules had higher relative abundances in
intermittent non-flowing sites but lower lipid-like molecules (p = 0.03) relative to perennial sites.
Further, carbohydrate-like molecules were lower in intermittent flowing relative to intermittent
non-flowing sites (Figure 5). During the wet season, similar to the dry season, carbohydrate-like
molecules were in higher abundance in both intermittent flow types relative to perennial whereas
protein-like molecules were greater in perennial reaches (p ≤ 0.007; Figure 5).

Figure 5. Boxplots of organic matter (OM) molecules classified into classes based on their elemental composition.
Post-hoc statistical differences within seasons are denoted with * ≤ 0.05, ** ≤ 0.01, and *** ≤ 0.001.

In addition to compound classes, FT-ICR data can provide a thermodynamic estimate of Gibbs
Free Energy of carbon oxidation (∆G°cox) based on the elemental composition of organic matter
molecules. In this work, during the dry period, ∆G°cox did not differ across flow types.
However, during the wet period, ∆G°cox was lower in perennial streams relative to intermittent

flowing streams indicating more thermodynamic favorable OM that is bioavailable in perennial
rivers. The more negative ∆G°cox, the more thermodynamically favorable (Figure 6).

Figure 6. Boxplots of ∆G°cox calculated across flow types. Intermittent flowing reaches had higher ∆G°cox than
perennial (p < 0.001).
