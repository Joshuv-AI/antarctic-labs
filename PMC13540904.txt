Longitudinal inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden and incident heart failure across cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic stages: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Fangyi Dai, Yong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure remains a major health burden among older adults. Although systemic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and central adiposity are individually recognized as heart failure risk factors, composite biomarkers capturing their longitudinal convergence and interaction with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) staging remain unexplored.ObjectivesTo investigate the association of longitudinal CTGW composite index (C-reactive protein × triglyceride-glucose index × waist-to-height ratio) trajectories and cumulative burden with incident heart failure, and to examine effect modification by baseline CKM stage.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 1946 participants aged 50 years and older from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, free of heart failure at Wave 2 (2004–2005), followed through Wave 8 (2016–2017). Group-based multi-trajectory modeling jointly classified longitudinal CRP, triglycerides, glucose, and WHtR across three waves. Cumulative CTGW burden was quantified using the trapezoidal rule. Cox proportional hazards regression with sequential adjustment and restricted cubic spline analyses were employed.ResultsOver a median follow-up spanning 12 years, 113 participants (5.8%) developed incident heart failure. Three distinct trajectories were identified: low-stable (n = 697, 35.8%), moderate-metabolic (n = 943, 48.5%), and high-inflammatory (n = 306, 15.7%). In fully adjusted models, the high-inflammatory trajectory conferred the greatest risk (HR = 2.69, 95% CI 1.46–4.93, P = 0.001), followed by the moderate-metabolic trajectory (HR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.17–3.29, P = 0.011). For cumulative burden, the highest quartile was significantly associated with incident heart failure (HR = 2.35, 95% CI 1.31–4.24, P = 0.004; P for trend < 0.001), with each standard deviation increase corresponding to 19% higher risk (HR = 1.19, 95% CI 1.04–1.36, P = 0.014). A nonlinear dose-response relationship was confirmed (P for nonlinearity = 0.032), with the risk threshold identified at approximately 8.2. Stratified analyses revealed significant effect modification by CKM stage (P for interaction = 0.031), with the strongest association observed in Stage 3–4 (HR = 2.37, 95% CI 1.24–4.53, P = 0.009). Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent effect directions across age, sex, BMI, and comorbidity strata without significant interactions.ConclusionsLongitudinal CTGW trajectories and cumulative burden are independently associated with incident heart failure in a nonlinear dose-response manner, with the association amplified across advancing CKM stages, supporting the CTGW composite index as a potential integrative biomarker for heart failure risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03310-0.

Introduction

Heart failure is a major and growing clinical challenge in ageing populations, arising from complex interactions among cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, inflammatory, and adiposity-related abnormalities [1]. Rather than developing from a single pathway, heart failure often reflects the cumulative impact of multiple upstream disturbances, including insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, central adiposity, and kidney dysfunction [2, 3]. This multidimensional pathophysiology is increasingly captured by the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic framework, which emphasizes the continuum from apparently healthy status to overt cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. However, risk stratification within this framework still relies largely on baseline clinical categories and may not fully reflect the dynamic accumulation of inflammatory, metabolic, and adiposity burden over time [6]. Longitudinal composite biomarkers that integrate these interconnected domains may therefore provide complementary information for identifying individuals at elevated risk for heart failure.

Systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and central adiposity represent three closely related but biologically distinct domains implicated in the development of heart failure [7, 8]. C-reactive protein is commonly regarded as a marker of chronic low-grade inflammatory activity, which may contribute to endothelial dysfunction, myocardial remodeling, and vascular injury [8]. The triglyceride-glucose index has been widely used as a simple surrogate marker of insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation [9, 10], whereas waist-to-height ratio provides an anthropometric measure of central adiposity that may better capture cardiometabolic risk than body size alone [11, 12]. Importantly, these abnormalities rarely occur in isolation. In older adults, inflammatory activation, impaired glucose-lipid metabolism, and abdominal adiposity may reinforce one another, forming an interconnected risk milieu that is not fully represented by any single biomarker. A composite index integrating CRP, TyG, and WHtR may therefore offer a practical approach to approximating the joint inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden relevant to heart failure risk [13].

Although single-time-point biomarkers are useful for risk assessment, they may not adequately capture the chronicity, fluctuation, or cumulative nature of inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden. This limitation is particularly relevant in older adults, whose cardiometabolic profiles may evolve gradually over years before the clinical onset of heart failure. Longitudinal trajectory analysis allows the characterization of heterogeneous patterns of biomarker evolution, while cumulative exposure metrics may better reflect the sustained biological burden experienced over time [14]. Therefore, evaluating both CTGW trajectories and cumulative CTGW exposure may help clarify whether persistent or progressive inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity abnormalities are associated with future heart failure risk.

Despite growing interest in TyG-related adiposity indices and CKM-based cardiovascular risk assessment, several important gaps remain. First, most existing studies have focused on baseline or single-domain indicators, whereas the longitudinal convergence of inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and central adiposity has been less well characterized [15]. Second, whether cumulative inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden is associated with incident heart failure in older adults remains insufficiently understood [16]. Third, it is unclear whether the association between such longitudinal burden and heart failure risk differs across CKM stages, which may represent different levels of underlying cardiometabolic risk. To address these gaps, we used data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing to examine the associations of CTGW trajectories and cumulative CTGW exposure with incident heart failure, and to assess potential effect modification by baseline CKM stage.

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This prospective cohort study utilized data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), a nationally representative longitudinal panel study of community-dwelling adults aged 50 years and older residing in England (https://www.elsa-project.ac.uk). ELSA was initiated in 2002 and conducts biennial assessments of health, economic, and social circumstances through face-to-face interviews, self-completion questionnaires, and nurse-led clinical examinations [17]. The present analysis drew on data from Wave 2 (2004–2005) as the baseline, with longitudinal biomarker measurements extending through Wave 4 (2008–2009) and Wave 6 (2012–2013), and heart failure ascertainment at Wave 8 (2016–2017). ELSA received ethical approval from the South Central—Berkshire Research Ethics Committee (approval number: 17/SC/0588), and all participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting of this study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study population

From 9432 ELSA participants enrolled at Wave 2, we excluded individuals aged below 50 years (n = 89), those with missing heart failure or cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) staging data (n = 3581), missing triglyceride-glucose index data (n = 2220), missing C-reactive protein data (n = 627), missing glucose data (n = 475), and those with prevalent heart failure at baseline (n = 494). The final analytic sample comprised 1946 participants followed through Wave 8.

Exposure variables

The primary exposure was the CTGW composite index, defined as the multiplicative product of serum C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/L), the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). The TyG index was calculated as ln(triglycerides [mg/dL] × glucose [mg/dL]/2), with unit conversions applied using the factor 18.01559 to convert mmol/L to mg/dL. WHtR was computed as waist circumference (cm) divided by height (cm). All biochemical measures were obtained from fasting blood samples collected during nurse visits at Waves 2, 4, and 6. Cumulative CTGW exposure (cumCTGW) was computed using the trapezoidal rule to estimate the area under the curve across the three measurement waves spanning 8 years, then divided by total follow-up duration to yield a time-weighted average.

Outcome variable

The primary outcome was incident heart failure, ascertained through self-reported physician diagnosis at Wave 8. Participants reporting heart failure at baseline were excluded to ensure incident case identification. Follow-up time was calculated in months from the baseline interview to the date of heart failure diagnosis or censoring at Wave 8.

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on established risk factors for heart failure and potential confounders of the exposure-outcome relationship. Demographic variables included age (continuous, years), sex, education level, and marital status. Lifestyle factors comprised smoking status and alcohol consumption. Clinical variables included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), hypertension (yes/no), diabetes mellitus (yes/no), and history of stroke (yes/no). Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 8), with depression defined as a score of 3 or higher. Baseline CKM staging (Stages 0–4) was determined according to the American Heart Association framework, incorporating excess adiposity, metabolic risk factors, chronic kidney disease severity, and prevalent cardiovascular disease.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by CTGW and cumCTGW quartile groups using means with standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, medians with interquartile ranges for skewed variables (identified by Shapiro-Wilk test, P < 0.05), and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables.

Longitudinal trajectories of CTGW component biomarkers were characterized using group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM), implemented via finite mixture models to jointly model CRP, triglycerides, glucose, and WHtR trajectories across three waves. All biomarkers were standardized to Z-scores prior to modeling to ensure comparable scaling. Quadratic polynomial terms for time were included, with a linear specification employed as fallback when quadratic models failed to converge. The optimal number of trajectory groups was determined by comparing Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) across models with 2 to 5 groups, with a minimum prior probability constraint of 0.05 to prevent degenerate solutions. Model adequacy was assessed through average posterior probabilities of assignment, with values exceeding 0.70 considered acceptable.

The association between CTGW trajectory groups and incident heart failure was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression across three sequential adjustment models: Model 1 adjusted for age and sex; Model 2 additionally adjusted for education, marital status, smoking, and alcohol consumption; Model 3 further adjusted for BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, and CES-D 8 score. The low-stable trajectory served as the reference group. For cumCTGW analyses, hazard ratios were estimated per quartile group (with the lowest quartile as reference) and per one standard deviation increase. Linear trend was assessed by entering quartile-specific median values as a continuous variable. The dose-response relationship between cumCTGW and heart failure risk was examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) within Cox regression, with knot number (3–7) selected by minimizing AIC. The median cumCTGW value served as the reference, and nonlinearity was tested using the Wald statistic for the nonlinear spline components.

Stratified analyses were performed across baseline CKM stages (0, 1, 2, and 3–4 combined) to evaluate effect modification, with multiplicative interaction tested by including cross-product terms. Subgroup analyses assessed consistency of trajectory-outcome associations across strata defined by age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, BMI (< 25 vs. ≥ 25 kg/m2), hypertension, diabetes, and CKM risk category, with the stratification variable removed from adjustment in corresponding models. Interaction P-values were derived from likelihood ratio tests comparing models with and without cross-product terms.

Missing biomarker data across waves were addressed through longitudinal imputation prior to analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided with significance set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3 with key packages including survival (v3.5), rms (v6.7), flexmix (v2.3), tableone (v0.13), forestploter (v1.1), ggridges (v0.5), and patchwork (v1.2).

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Of 9432 ELSA participants enrolled at Wave 2, 89 were excluded for age below 50 years, 3581 for missing heart failure and CKM data, 2220 for missing TyG data, 627 for missing CRP data, 475 for missing glucose data, and 494 for baseline prevalent heart failure, yielding a final analytic cohort of 1946 participants followed through Wave 8 (Fig. 1). During follow-up, 113 (5.8%) developed incident heart failure and 1833 remained event-free.

Longitudinal distributions of CTGW components and heart failure incidence

Beyond baseline differences, longitudinal biomarker trajectories across Waves 2, 4, and 6 revealed distinct temporal patterns among CTGW components. Mean CRP remained relatively stable from Wave 2 (3.28 ± 5.18 mg/L) to Wave 4 (3.30 ± 5.43 mg/L) before declining modestly at Wave 6 (2.97 ± 4.78 mg/L), while triglycerides showed a gradual decrease from 1.69 ± 1.06 to 1.45 ± 0.76 mmol/L (Fig. 2A and B). Fasting glucose increased from 4.98 ± 0.77 at Wave 2 to 5.45 ± 0.94 mmol/L at Wave 6, and WHtR rose slightly from 0.568 ± 0.072 to 0.582 ± 0.078 (Fig. 2C and D). The CTGW composite index remained stable across waves, with mean values of 13.9 ± 22.0, 14.2 ± 23.7, and 12.7 ± 20.6 at Waves 2, 4, and 6, respectively (Fig. 2E). Heart failure incidence varied substantially across baseline CKM stages, rising from 2.9% in Stage 1 (16/555) and 3.1% in Stage 0 (8/261) to 7.1% in Stage 2 (75/1050) and 17.5% in Stage 4 (14/80) (Fig. 2F). Spearman correlation analyses demonstrated that CTGW was predominantly driven by CRP (r = 0.989–0.991 across waves), with moderate correlations with WHtR (r = 0.458–0.517) and weaker associations with triglycerides and glucose.

CTGW trajectory identification

Building on the observed longitudinal patterns, GBMTM was applied to jointly model CRP, triglycerides, glucose, and WHtR trajectories. Model selection based on AIC and BIC identified K = 3 as optimal (AIC = 50164.9, BIC = 50498.5) (Fig. 3A). Three distinct trajectory groups were identified: low-stable CTGW (n = 697, 35.8%), moderate-metabolic CTGW (n = 943, 48.5%), and high-inflammatory CTGW (n = 306, 15.7%) (Fig. 3D). Average posterior probabilities exceeded the 0.70 acceptability threshold in all groups, with values of 0.949, 0.947, and 0.971 for the low-stable, moderate-metabolic, and high-inflammatory groups, respectively (Fig. 3B). The low-stable group maintained consistently low CRP (1.04–1.11 mg/L) and WHtR (0.519–0.530) across waves, while the high-inflammatory group exhibited persistently elevated CRP (7.76–8.74 mg/L), triglycerides (1.73–2.22 mmol/L), and WHtR (0.619–0.634). Mean CTGW composite index values across waves were 3.7–4.1, 12.2–14.0, and 34.5–38.6 for the three groups, respectively (Fig. 3C).

Association between CTGW trajectories and incident heart failure

Using the three identified trajectory groups, Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to evaluate their association with incident heart failure. In Model 1, adjusted for age and sex, the moderate-metabolic group had a significantly elevated risk compared with the low-stable reference (HR = 2.11, 95% CI 1.30–3.44, P = 0.003), and the high-inflammatory group demonstrated a further increased risk (HR = 2.92, 95% CI 1.67–5.12, P < 0.001). After additional adjustment for education, marital status, smoking, and alcohol consumption in Model 2, the associations remained significant for both the moderate-metabolic (HR = 1.94, 95% CI 1.16–3.25, P = 0.012) and high-inflammatory groups (HR = 2.58, 95% CI 1.41–4.72, P = 0.002). In the fully adjusted Model 3, which further incorporated BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, stroke, and CES-D 8 score, the moderate-metabolic group retained a significantly higher risk (HR = 1.96, 95% CI 1.17–3.29, P = 0.011), and the high-inflammatory group showed the highest risk (HR = 2.69, 95% CI 1.46–4.93, P = 0.001) (Fig. 4).

To complement the trajectory-based analysis, cumulative CTGW exposure was quantified using the trapezoidal rule across Waves 2, 4, and 6. Higher cumCTGW quartiles were associated with greater BMI, CRP, triglycerides, waist circumference, and prevalence of hypertension and depression (all P < 0.05). Stratified analysis across baseline CKM stages

To examine whether the CTGW–heart failure association varied by baseline cardiometabolic risk, stratified analyses were performed across CKM stages. For cumCTGW as a continuous exposure (per 1 SD increase), no significant associations were observed in Stage 0 (HR = 1.32, 95% CI 0.58–3.01, P = 0.504) or Stage 1 (HR = 1.45, 95% CI 0.91–2.31, P = 0.118), whereas significant associations emerged in Stage 2 (HR = 1.53, 95% CI 1.22–1.92, P < 0.001) and Stage 3–4 (HR = 2.37, 95% CI 1.24–4.53, P = 0.009), with a significant interaction (P for interaction = 0.031).

In the trajectory-based stratified analysis, the high-inflammatory group demonstrated a significantly elevated risk relative to the low-stable reference within Stage 2 (HR = 2.24, 95% CI 1.16–4.33, P = 0.014). In Stage 3–4, the high-inflammatory group showed a numerically higher but non-significant risk (HR = 4.18, 95% CI 0.68–25.68, P = 0.114), likely constrained by limited sample size (n = 14, events = 2). Similarly, in Stage 0 and Stage 1, point estimates for the moderate-metabolic and high-inflammatory groups were elevated but did not reach statistical significance. The trajectory-by-CKM interaction was also significant (P for interaction = 0.038) (Fig. 7).

Subgroup analyses

To further assess the robustness of the trajectory–heart failure association, subgroup analyses were conducted across nine predefined strata. The direction of effect for the high-inflammatory trajectory relative to the low-stable reference was generally consistent across subgroups, with notably elevated point estimates observed among participants without hypertension (HR = 3.38, 95% CI 1.43–8.00, P = 0.006), those aged below 65 years for the moderate-metabolic trajectory (HR = 2.78, 95% CI 1.23–6.30, P = 0.014), and those without stroke for both the moderate-metabolic (HR = 1.79, 95% CI 1.01–3.17, P = 0.048) and high-inflammatory groups (HR = 2.21, 95% CI 1.07–4.57, P = 0.032). Estimates within the diabetes and stroke subgroups with events were unstable due to small sample sizes. No statistically significant interactions were detected for any subgroup variable, including age (P for interaction = 0.125), sex (P = 0.848), BMI (P = 0.771), hypertension (P = 0.064), diabetes (P = 0.179), and depression (P = 0.969) (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study of older adults from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, we found that longitudinal inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden, captured by CTGW trajectories and cumulative CTGW exposure, was associated with incident heart failure. Three distinct CTGW trajectory patterns were identified, and participants with moderate-metabolic or high-inflammatory trajectories had a higher risk of developing heart failure than those with a low-stable trajectory. In parallel, greater cumulative CTGW exposure showed a graded and nonlinear association with heart failure risk, with the excess risk becoming more apparent at higher burden levels. Importantly, these associations appeared to vary across baseline CKM stages, with stronger associations observed among participants with more advanced cardiometabolic risk profiles. Together, these findings suggest that integrating inflammatory, metabolic, and adiposity-related information over time may provide complementary information for heart failure risk assessment beyond selected conventional baseline risk factors [2, 3].

Our findings extend the growing body of evidence on TyG-related adiposity indices and cardiovascular risk. Previous studies have suggested that indices combining insulin resistance surrogates with anthropometric measures, such as TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, may capture cardiometabolic risk more comprehensively than either metabolic or adiposity markers alone [11, 15, 18, 19]. However, these indices primarily reflect metabolic dysfunction and body fat distribution, whereas chronic low-grade inflammation is commonly considered another relevant biological domain linking cardiometabolic abnormalities to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [8, 20]. By incorporating CRP together with TyG and WHtR, the CTGW composite index may approximate a broader inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity phenotype [21]. The observed associations of both moderate-metabolic and high-inflammatory CTGW trajectories with incident heart failure suggest that heart failure risk may be shaped not only by metabolic-adiposity burden but also by the inflammatory context in which this burden develops.

Several biological pathways may help explain the observed association between elevated CTGW burden and incident heart failure. Insulin resistance and glucose-lipid dysregulation may promote endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neurohormonal activation, and myocardial energy metabolic impairment Individuals [22], all of which have been implicated in the progression from cardiometabolic risk to cardiac dysfunction [13, 23, 24]. Central adiposity may further contribute through adipose tissue inflammation, altered adipokine secretion, ectopic fat deposition, and increased haemodynamic load [25–27]. In this context, CRP may not only reflect systemic inflammatory activation but also capture part of the inflammatory milieu accompanying metabolic and adiposity-related abnormalities [28, 29]. The stronger association observed in the high-inflammatory trajectory group suggests that persistent inflammatory activation superimposed on metabolic-adiposity burden may mark a subgroup with greater susceptibility to heart failure [30, 31]. Nevertheless, because CRP is a nonspecific inflammatory marker and this study was observational, these mechanistic interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than causal evidence [32].

An important feature of our study is the longitudinal assessment of CTGW burden. Cardiometabolic and inflammatory abnormalities may persist, fluctuate, or progress over time, and a single baseline measurement may therefore misclassify long-term exposure status [14]. By jointly modelling repeated CRP, triglyceride, glucose, and WHtR measurements, the trajectory-based approach captured heterogeneous patterns of inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity evolution that were not apparent from baseline values alone [33]. Similarly, cumulative CTGW exposure provided a time-weighted estimate of sustained burden across multiple assessment waves outcomes [34]. The nonlinear dose-response relationship observed in our analysis further suggests that the association between CTGW burden and heart failure risk may not increase uniformly across the entire exposure range, but may become more evident once a certain burden level is reached [35, 36]. These findings highlight the potential value of considering both the pattern and accumulation of cardiometabolic-inflammatory abnormalities when evaluating heart failure risk in older adults.

The stratified analyses by baseline CKM stage provide additional insight into the clinical context in which CTGW burden may be most informative. The association between cumulative CTGW exposure and incident heart failure was more evident among participants with CKM Stage 2 and Stage 3–4, whereas associations were weaker and statistically non-significant in Stage 0 and Stage 1. This pattern is broadly consistent with the CKM framework, which conceptualizes cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic abnormalities as an interconnected continuum rather than isolated disease categories [2, 3]. Individuals with more advanced CKM stages may already have a substrate of metabolic dysfunction, renal impairment, vascular injury, or subclinical cardiovascular disease, which could increase their susceptibility to additional inflammatory and adiposity-related stress [3, 37]. Therefore, CTGW may have potential as a complementary marker within CKM-based risk stratification and may help generate hypotheses about residual heterogeneity in heart failure risk among individuals within similar baseline CKM categories. However, given the limited number of events in some CKM strata, especially Stage 3–4, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger cohorts.

This study has several strengths, including the use of a prospective population-based cohort, repeated biomarker measurements across multiple waves, trajectory-based modelling of inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity patterns, and assessment of cumulative exposure over time [17]. The integration of CTGW with CKM staging also provides a clinically relevant framework for exploring heterogeneity in heart failure risk among older adults. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, incident heart failure was ascertained by self-reported physician diagnosis, which may be subject to recall error or misclassification, and information on heart failure phenotype, cardiac imaging, natriuretic peptides, and medication use was unavailable [1, 38, 39]. Second, although multiple covariates were adjusted for, residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors cannot be excluded. Third, CRP is a nonspecific inflammatory marker, and the CTGW index should be interpreted as an approximate composite measure rather than a direct measure of underlying pathophysiological processes. Fourth, the number of heart failure events was modest, particularly in some CKM strata, which may have limited statistical power for interaction and subgroup analyses. Finally, because the study was conducted among older adults in England, the generalizability of these findings to younger populations or other ethnic and geographic settings requires further investigation [17]. Future studies with external validation, more detailed cardiovascular phenotyping, and repeated assessment of CKM status are warranted to clarify whether CTGW provides incremental value for heart failure risk assessment Central adiposity [40].

Conclusion

In this prospective cohort study of older adults, longitudinal inflammatory-metabolic-adiposity burden, reflected by CTGW trajectories and cumulative CTGW exposure, was associated with incident heart failure. Participants with moderate-metabolic and high-inflammatory CTGW trajectories showed higher heart failure risk, and cumulative CTGW demonstrated a nonlinear dose-response association, with stronger associations observed among individuals with more advanced CKM stages. These findings suggest that integrating inflammation, insulin resistance-related metabolic dysregulation, and central adiposity over time may provide complementary information for heart failure risk assessment within the CKM framework. Further studies with external validation, detailed heart failure phenotyping, and repeated CKM assessment are warranted to determine whether CTGW offers incremental value beyond established clinical and cardiometabolic risk markers.
