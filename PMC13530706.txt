Poster 11. Blood Flow Restriction Therapy After ACL Reconstruction Does Not Improve Functional Strength or Increase Thigh Muscle Volume at 12 Weeks Post-Operative: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Andrew Sheean, Keith A Knurr, Daniel J Cognetti, Ivan J Antosh, Joshua Shaw, Marc Blaylock, Matthew R Schmitz, Rachel Cuenca, Joshua McKinley, Joshua Tadlock, David J Tennent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Persistent quadriceps and hamstring muscle weakness negatively affects the outcomes of ACL reconstruction (ACLR.) Blood flow restriction (BFR) therapy has been proposed as a means to improve strength and mitigate muscle deconditioning post-operatively. If this technique is successful in improving thigh muscle function in the immediate post-operative period, it may be worthwhile to expanding its use to improve the outcomes of ACLR. Prior comparative trials among heterogenous cohorts have observed mixed results and largely inconclusive findings. As such, the efficacy of BFR in the immediate post-ACLR period has not been demonstrated in a group of patients undergoing ACLR performed using a standardized approach (i.e., autograft type and post-operative activity limitations.) Therefore, the purposes of this study were to determine if BFR incorporated into 12 weeks of post-ACLR rehabilitation increases 1. Functional thigh muscle strength and, 2. Thigh muscle volume when compared to standard of care rehabilitation. We hypothesized that the use of BFR for post-operative rehabilitation after quadriceps tendon autograft ACLR would not result in increased functional strength or thigh muscle volume at 12-weeks post-ACLR.Methods:Patients undergoing primary ACLR with quadriceps tendon autograft without post-operative weight bearing or activity restrictions were randomized post-operatively to either a BFR or non-BFR rehabilitation for 12 weeks post-operative (NCT04519801.) The RCT was conducted during the early-phase (within 12 weeks of ACLR) rehabilitation and compared interventional (BFR) and non-interventional (non-BFR) groups. Once randomized, subjects performed the same work-matched selected exercises, ranging from one to three exercise sets per session, at targeted (BFR target of 30% one repetition maximum at 60-80% arterial occlusion of the operative limb; non-BFR target of 40-60% repetition maximum or 6-8/10 rate of perceived exertion) intensities. Functional thigh muscle strength (isometric an isokinetic) was measured pre-operatively and at 12 weeks post-operative. Thigh muscle volume was measured using a 3-T Signa LX MRI (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI.) A previously validated 3D computer-learning artificial intelligence (AI) algorithm for automatic muscle segmentation platform (Springbok Analytics, Charlottesville, VA.) was used to derive bilateral thigh muscle volumes (Figure 1) within 7 days of ACLR and 12 weeks post-operative. Linear mixed effects models, with fixed effects of group, time, and a group-by-time interaction and a random effect for patient, were used to compare difference in muscle volume and strength of both the surgical limb (Nm/kg) and limb symmetry indices (LSI) between groups and overtime. Tukey adjusted pairwise p-values are reported with α = 0.05. An a priori power analysis was based on data published by Ohta et al. In that trial, investigators measured and reported concentric contraction strength during isokinetic contraction at angular velocities of 60 and 180 degrees per second. Isometric contraction strength was also measured 60 degrees. Results were reported as a percentage (injured/uninjured). Using one of these (concentric contraction at a rate of 180 degrees per second) a sample size was calculated. Investigators reported a mean injured/healthy ratio of 77% (13) for the BFR group and 65% (13) for the non-BFR rehabilitation group. Using these data with an alpha of 0.05 and desired power of 80%, it was determined that 38 subjects (19 subjects per group) would be required to reach 80% power for measurements of thigh muscle strength.Results:Forty-five (16 females, 25 males) were randomized and completed 12 weeks of either non-BFR or BFR rehabilitation regimens. Table 1 provides a summary of baseline demographics of the two cohorts. Seven patients (13.5%) across the BFR and non-BFR cohorts did not complete the full 12 weeks of rehabilitation interventions and were subsequently excluded from the final analysis. There were no differences between groups in terms of thigh muscle isometric and isokinetic strength (Figure 2.) There were no significant differences between groups in quadriceps muscle volume or hamstring muscle volume at 12 weeks post-ACLR (Figure 3.)Conclusions:The addition of BFR in the first 12 weeks of postoperative rehabilitation after quadriceps tendon autograft ACLR did not improve thigh muscle strength or thigh muscle volume. At present, this trial represents the largest RCT comparing the effects of BFR in a population of patients undergoing ACLR with a standardized approach (i.e., autograft type, post-operative activity limitations.)
