 1.0 Introduction
A multitude of groundwater remediation techniques for chlorinated volatile organic compounds (cVOCs)
have been conducted at the A/M-Areas of the Savannah River Site (SRS). Historical in-situ chemical
oxidation (ISCO) injections of potassium permanganate and sodium persulfate conducted in 2018 and 2020,
have left residual oxidant concentrations in the A/M-Area groundwater system. ISCO events targeted dense
non-aqueous phase liquids (DNAPLs) through the injection of strong oxidants within plume zones
upgradient of current groundwater recovery wells RWM-008 and RWM-018. These residual oxidants could
significantly impede the operation of the mercury removal system needed to meet National Pollutant
Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) permit requirements for the ongoing pump-and-treat system that
hydraulically controls potions of the A/M-Area plume. Effluent treated groundwater from this system is
discharged to surface water at the receiving outfall of the M-1 Air Stripper. To prevent interference of the
M-1 Air Stripper system by residual oxidants, groundwater recovery has been ceased at well RWM-018.
Modeling of oxidant transport in A/M-Area from ISCO estimated that the potential oxidant load contributed
by RWM-018 could be as high as 120 mg/L. It is also possible that oxidants may be contributed via pumping
at well RWM-008 in the near future as residual oxidants within the aquifer migrate toward its zone of
influence. Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) has conducted research to investigate potential pre-
treatment chemicals to neutralize these residual oxidants, before reaching the M-1 Air Stripper, to allow for
resumed groundwater recovery at RWM-018 and continued pumping at RWM-008. Deployment of
ascorbic acid for neutralization of residual oxidants at RWM-018 (and possibly RWM-008) is
recommended based on results from previous reductant testing (SRNL, 2021). For a maximum estimated
oxidant load at RWM-0018, a well-head treatment system would need to dose about 400 gallons of ascorbic
acid each month to ensure complete neutralization. A second system with additional reagent should also be
considered for deployment at RWM-008.

 2.0 Objectives
The primary objective of this work was to build upon previously conducted research by investigating the
practical field use of ascorbic acid reductant in a pre-treatment system. To do this, SRNL evaluated the
stability of 2 molar (M) ascorbic acid solution under various storage conditions, including the possible
impacts from photodegradation and temperature changes, to simulate the long-term storage of ascorbic acid
solutions in various outdoor conditions. The efficacy of this solution for neutralization of oxidant, after
simulated storage conditions, was then tested. Additionally, the impact of reductant introduction on total
recovered groundwater pH was also simulated to ensure that this additive treatment would not lower the
pH of groundwater entering the M-1 Air Stripper, causing it to fall outside of the defined waste acceptance
criteria (WAC). This report contains results from both stability experiments and the pH assessment.

 3.0 Method
The testing approach was designed to simulate real-world conditions of ascorbic acid reductant storage and
its introduction to the groundwater recovery system of the M-1 Air Stripper. Reductant solutions were
prepared to concentrations recommended for field use in proposed treatment systems. All testing conducted
for pH assessments utilized recovered groundwater from the M-1 Air Stripper system to match field
conditions as closely as possible.

3.1 Part 1: Stability testing
Stability testing assessed the impact of storage conditions on ascorbic acid stability in solution. Temperature
and natural light exposure were varied to isolate variables that could contribute to ascorbic acid reductant
degradation, if any. Solutions were exposed continuously to conditions for up to four weeks during testing
to determine the efficacy of ascorbic acid reductant solutions as a function of storage time under varied
temperature and light exposure conditions. The 2 M ascorbic acid solution used for testing was selected
based on previous reductant saturation testing (SRNL, 2021) and represents the maximum concentration of

 1

reductant that can be put into solution without precipitation from oversaturation. Utilization of concentrated
reductant is necessary to reduce additive volumes and subsequently the equipment and materials needed to
deploy the proposed reductant additive system.

All reductant solutions consisted of the reducing agent (ascorbic acid), deionized (DI) water, and dilute
sulfuric acid. Each acidified reductant solution was comprised of equal volumes of ascorbic acid (in DI
water) and 1 M sulfuric acid in DI water. Reductant solutions were acidified with sulfuric acid to allow for
the complete reduction of Mn7+ (from permanganate) to Mn2+ and prevent the formation of intermediate
manganese oxidation states, such as Mn4+, from forming.

3.1.1 Photodegradation Testing
Reductant solutions used in wellhead treatment systems may be exposed to natural light for prolonged
periods of time. Degradation of reductant solutions resulting from exposure to ultra-violet (UV) light could
possibly occur from prolonged solution storage under these conditions. To determine if light has an impact
on solution stability or efficacy after storage, a subset of solutions were exposed to natural UV light.
Periodically, these solutions were tested to determine the capability of reductant to neutralize known
quantities of oxidant. Aliquots of each solution were stored separately; one in clear plastic (HDPE) to allow
for exposure to natural light and one in an opaque plastic (HDPE) container to obscure light (Figure 3-1).
Other container materials were not investigated because of their potential incompatibility with the solution
(e.g. some metals) or lack of practicality in field systems (e.g. glass). All testing was performed in duplicate.

 Figure 3-1. Ascorbic Acid Sample Storage Container Types - Clear and Opaque HDPE Plastic

All containers were stored outdoors under ambient conditions for approximately 30 days to assess any
changes to solution stability and/or efficacy as a result of UV light exposure. During photodegradation
testing, each solution from each storage condition was assessed for a) qualitative signs of change (e.g. color
change) and b) visual precipitation (if any) semiweekly. Additionally, an aliquot of each solution was
removed and used to neutralize a sample of known oxidant concentration once a week to identify any
reduction in efficiency resulting from photodegradation.

3.1.2 Thermal Degradation Testing
Storage temperature was also varied for each container type to assess its impact on the stability of reductant
solutions. Three experiments were performed using a Binder KMF 240 Environmental Chamber to control

 2

ambient conditions for each test. Aliquots of ascorbic acid reductant solution were divided and placed into
containers. Each experiment consisted of duplicate opaque HDPE containers and clear HDPE containers of
ascorbic acid solution. Samples were stored in three ambient conditions: a) at room temperature, b) in a
cold environment (~ 40 °F), and c) in a hot environment (~ 90 °F). A constant climate was maintained in
each of these experiments for four weeks. The environmental chamber was kept void of light to remove
any possible interference from photodegradation.

Semiweekly during this study, each solution from each storage condition was assessed for a) qualitative
signs of change (e.g. color change) and b) visual precipitation (if any). Once per week, an aliquot of ascorbic
acid solution from each storage condition was collected and used to neutralize a sample of known oxidant
concentration to identify any change in reductant solution efficacy.

A freshly made sample was used to confirm neutralization dosing quantities prior to storage. Results from
this trial, prior to any thermal or photodegradation testing, are referred to as ‘baseline’ in testing results.
Results from all three temperature trials (cold, hot, and room temperature) were compared to the baseline
sample results at the completion of testing.

3.1.3 Reductant Efficacy Testing
Once per week, each ascorbic acid reductant solution was tested for any measurable loss in efficacy.
Experimentation was performed by dosing an oxidant solution of known concentration with each reductant
solution. Concentrations of oxidant were identical between tests. If the volume of reductant needed to reach
complete reduction increased over time, it would be concluded that the reductant solutions had been
degraded under those storage conditions. The efficacy testing approach is as follows:
 1. Small, approximately 5 mL, aliquots of ascorbic acid reductant solution were retrieved from each
 parent container undergoing either thermal or photodegradation testing each week. These aliquots
 were obtained individually from each replicate sample following visual inspection.
 2. Under constant agitation at 200 rpm, oxidant solutions (1500 ppm potassium permanganate in DI
 water) were placed into beakers monitored by a YSI water quality meter to measure pH and
 oxidation-reduction potential (ORP).
 3. The pH and ORP measurements of oxidant stock solutions were allowed to stabilize and then
 recorded before any reductant addition.
 4. Measured volumes of ascorbic acid reductant were added to each oxidant stock solution until
 complete neutralization was achieved.
 I. Following each addition of ascorbic acid reductant, pH and ORP values were allowed to
 stabilize and were recorded.
 II. Notation of any intermediate precipitation or color changes were also recorded following
 each ascorbic acid reductant addition.
 5. Complete neutralization was determined after ORP readings were noted to have reached an
 asymptote following subsequent additions of ascorbic acid reductant. At the conclusion of each
 titration, final values of pH, ORP, visual inspections, and total ascorbic acid reductant volumes
 were recorded.
 6. Volumes of ascorbic acid reductant necessary for complete neutralization were then compared to
 baseline values to determine any potential degradation in reductant efficacy.

3.2 Part 2: Combined Network pH assessment
To ensure the pH of M-1 Air Stripper influent is within the WAC-required range of 2 to 12.5 after reductant
is introduced, the pH assessment experimentation was designed to estimate the pH of ascorbic acid-treated
M-Area groundwater once it reaches the M-1 Air Stripper inlet. Groundwater at the M-1 Air Stripper inlet
represents water from multiple individual recovery wells. A batch study was used to simulate dosage at the

 3

RWM-018 wellhead and then dilution from other recovery wells in the network, simulating groundwater
conditions prior to entering the M-1 Air Stripper inlet for treatment.

For the combined network pH assessment, a 140 mL solution of known oxidant concentration was
generated to simulate recovered groundwater from well RWM-018. This solution contained 1500 ppm total
oxidant (potassium permanganate), which is the highest oxidant concentration previously measured in
M-Area wells (SRNL, 2021). This sample was then dosed with 2 M ascorbic acid solution based on
recommendations from previous testing (SRNL, 2021). Once treated with the ascorbic acid solution,
M-Area groundwater collected from the M-1 Air Stripper inlet was used to dilute the sample by
approximately 8.2 times. This dilution factor was determined based on the combined average recovery
water flow rate from each operating well in the recovery well network (Table 3-1). The RWM-018 median
flow rate when operated was 54.8 gpm, while the total flow from all the recovery wells (based on median
flow for each) is 454.8 gpm. Therefore, the averaged total flow rate of the M-1 Air Stripper recovery well
network, when operating, was found to be approximately 8.2 times the flow rate of RWM-018.

The pH of each solution was measured periodically during this experiment. Measurements of pH were
recorded prior to ascorbic acid addition, after the reductant had been added, and then following dilution
with M-1 Air Stripper inlet groundwater. For these experiments, it was assumed that the stannous chloride
system does not significantly impact the pH of the M-1 Air Stripper influent based on recent monitoring
data collected from the Comprehensive Environmental Data Management System (CEDMS) database.
Historical measurements show that M-Area groundwater has an average pH of approximately 4-5 prior to
treatment (source: CEDMS data for WSM001 and WSM003 clusters 1/1/2022-present) and the pH at the
M-1 Air Stripper inlet has varied between 4.5 and 6.9 since 2023. The pH of the M-1 Air Stripper effluent
has historically varied between 4.7 to 6.5 since 2023, indicating the stannous chloride additions have
minimal impact. This supports the assumption that pH is consistent within this range with the addition of
stannous chloride to M-1 Air Stripper influent groundwater. Any measurable deviation from this range
during combined network pH assessment would then likely be attributable to the addition of the simulated
groundwater from well RWM-018 or the ascorbic acid reductant used to neutralize its oxidant content.

 4.0 Results and Discussion

4.1 Part 1: Stability testing

4.1.1 Photodegradation Testing
Both clear and opaque bottles containing ascorbic acid were stored outside under ambient conditions to
assess the potential impact of photodegradation on the efficacy of the solution for oxidant neutralization.
Replicates contained in opaque containers were subject to identical conditions as samples contained within
clear containers to isolate reductant efficacy variations caused by photodegradation. Each solution from
each storage condition was assessed for a) qualitative signs of change and b) visual precipitation (if any).
A visual change in ascorbic acid solution color (darkening of yellow color; example shown in Figure 4-1)
was observed for all photodegradation samples, but no precipitation was observed in any sample throughout
the duration of testing.

Figure 4-1. Qualitative (color) change of ascorbic acid solution during storage. Image (a) was taken
 prior to storage and image (b) was taken after storage for four weeks.

These ascorbic acid solutions were also used to neutralize lab-generated oxidant standards containing 1500
ppm potassium permanganate a) immediately following preparation (prior to any storage) and then b) once
weekly for four weeks following identical procedures detailed in section 3.1.3. Reaction endpoints of the
redox titrations were determined through both color change, as well as continuous ORP (mV) measurement
using a YSI probe. Complete neutralization is considered achieved in each sample when ORP values drop
rapidly and significantly after reductant addition and then plateau at a reduced value once the reaction is
complete.

Initial scoping trials were completed to determine the minimum amount of 2 M ascorbic acid needed to
neutralize 140 mL of a solution with 1500 ppm potassium permanganate. This was done by slowly adding
the higher strength ascorbic acid solution while monitoring ORP until a baseline was established after the
initial drop (Figure 4-2). From this test, it was determined that a minimum 2.1 mL volume of reductant
solution was needed to completely neutralize the 1500 ppm oxidant solution. This dosing volume was used
in all the subsequent neutralization testing completed in this study.

Figure 4-3 displays these redox titration curves for photodegradation testing conducted in clear containers
that were stored outside, i.e., exposed to natural UV light. The ‘baseline’ curve represents results from
neutralization of oxidant solution with the 2 M ascorbic acid solution prior to any storage. No noticeable
differences in efficacy were observed for the clear bottles as time progressed. For all solution storage
durations (1-4 weeks), during ascorbic acid dosing, the initial ORP of the solutions were relatively
consistent (between 673.1 and 752.3 mV) and then dropped significantly following reductant addition.
Stabilization occurred after the addition of 2.1 mL of solution in all cases. These trends mirror that seen for
the ‘baseline’ sample (tested immediately following preparation), supporting that UV light did not result in
degradation of the ascorbic acid solutions within four weeks of sample exposure.

Though solution color did darken slightly with storage in opaque containers, similar to the solutions in clear
containers, replicate samples contained in opaque containers showed no noticeable differences in titration
efficacy seen as time progressed for outside storage (Figure 4-4). The trends seen in opaque bottle data
mirrors that of the clear bottles stored in the same manner under identical conditions. The lack of significant
difference between the clear and opaque bottles under the same conditions supports that UV light exposure
does not significantly decrease the efficacy of ascorbic acid reductant following four weeks of exposure.

The lack of ascorbic acid degradation in response to UV exposure is likely a function of pH. Under acidic
conditions, ascorbic acid is more resistant to photodegradation than under alkaline conditions (Yin et al.,
2022). The 2 M reductant solution has a pH of about 4.25 when in solution with sulfuric acid, which likely
contributed to its stability under UV-irradiation.

Though a decrease in efficacy was not seen as a result of photodegradation from outside storage in clear or
opaque containment, gas generation within containers was first noted following one week of testing. An
audible release of pressurized gas was noted during opening all containers kept in outdoor conditions;
however, no physical deformation to any of the sample bottles was observed. The gas being produced as a
result of outside storage of the ascorbic acid and sulfuric acid mixture is likely carbon dioxide (CO2). CO2
is a biproduct from ascorbic acid degradation, which can be accelerated by heat and the acidic environment
created from the addition of sulfuric acid (Yin et al., 2022). It is recommended that a well-head treatment

 6

system design incorporate a venting mechanism for gas release. Proper venting of the storage tank could be
accomplished with automated check valves, for example, to allow pressure to be released and prevent gas
buildup.

4.1.2 Thermal Degradation Testing
Ascorbic acid reductant solutions were stored under varying temperature conditions to assess the impact of
thermal degradation on efficacy and stability over time. Three tests were conducted using ascorbic acid
contained in clear and opaque containers stored in cold (40 °F), room temperature (~ 70 °F), and hot (90 °F)
conditions. An environmental chamber was used to maintain both hot and cold environments, and room
temperature samples were stored on the lab bench away from windows for the duration of testing. Both
clear and opaque bottles were included in this testing for each condition to identify any variability caused
by exposure to light.

Each solution from each storage condition was assessed for a) qualitative signs of change and b) visual
precipitation (if any). A visual change in ascorbic acid solution color (darkening of yellow color) was,
again, observed for all samples in this trial, but no precipitation was observed in any sample throughout the
duration of testing.

No change in efficacy as a result of cold storage was seen over the four-week testing period, regardless of
container color (clear vs opaque; Figure 4-5). There was also no change in efficacy seen to occur from room
temperature storage (Figure 4-6). Results of these tests are consistent with data found during a literature
review of related laboratory studies conducted with ascorbic acid. The concentration of ascorbic acid within
a solution has been shown to affect its stability. Lower concentration solutions more readily degrade than
those with elevated concentrations, especially in the presence of light (Yin et al., 2022). The lack of efficacy
reduction seen in photodegradation and thermal degradation testing (cold and room temperature) results
can likely be attributed to the use of high concentration solutions.

 8

Ascorbic acid reductant stored under hot conditions, however, show more varied results than those for the
cooler temperatures. Results of high temperature condition testing are shown in Figure 4-7. A significant
decrease in ORP is still seen to occur with the initial 2.1 mL addition of ascorbic acid stored under hot
conditions. However, these results show ORP values that do not reach an asymptotic plateau for either
bottle type (clear or opaque) after this initial sharp decrease in contrast to other trials. Instead, the ORP
values begin to increase, or rebound, sharply as more ascorbic acid is added. In addition, data collected
during neutralizations with the samples stored under high temperature (90 F°) shows that ORP values do
not consistently reach the -400 mV range seen in lower temperature trials before rebounding to higher
readings. These results indicate that storage at higher temperatures may result in degradation of the
reductants and a resulting decrease in efficacy. Ascorbic acid can be degraded by heat. Previous studies
have found that a storage temperature below about 25 °C (~ 77 °F) is typically preferable for maintaining
the ascorbic acid content of a liquid (Yin et al., 2022). This reductant can be significantly degraded when
stored around 90 °F, even when stored in the dark (Yin et al., 2022). Degradation at elevated temperatures
above ~90 °F is supported by the reduced efficacy that was observed with the solutions stored under hot
conditions in this study. Storage tank temperature regulation should be considered when designing a
chemical dosing system to prevent potential loss in reductant efficacy, particularly in summer months.

Audible pressure/gas release was also heard when opening all hot-stored bottles. Visible bottle deformation
(Figure 4-8) and the occurrence of gas trapped in solution (Figure 4-9) was observed as a result of hot
conditions for both bottle types. Significant physical deformation is seen for the opaque bottles stored in
hot conditions only, but some mild deformation (rounding of bottle base) was also noted for clear
containers. Deformation of opaque containers was observed following two weeks of storage, while the less
severe deformation was not noted in the clear containers until week four. The gas produced as a result of
heating a mixture of ascorbic acid and sulfuric acid is likely CO2, generated as a biproduct of ascorbic acid
degradation in the acidic environment created from the addition of sulfuric acid. Sulfuric acid is not
expected to lead to significant gas formation at 90 °F, and it is unlikely that a direct reaction is occurring
between ascorbic acid and sulfuric acid that would result in gas generation under these conditions (Yin et
al., 2022).

Best Practices for a long term ascorbic additive storage and additive system are informed by stability testing
results. A chemical additive system is proposed to be located at the RWM-0018 wellhead to continuously
inject ascorbic acid reductant into recovered groundwater for oxidant neutralization. Efficient design of
such an additive system would likely seek to minimize maintenance and chemical handling while
maximizing reliability and efficiency. Best practices for consideration in an additive system design as a
result of SRNL testing include:
 • Thermal protection from temperature extremes
 o The use of a double lined insulated storage tank to hold ascorbic acid solutions would help
 with managing temperature increases in the warmer months. Insulation of any transfer
 lines/connections between the tank and injection point may also be employed to ensure that
 complete thermal protection is achieved for lines that contain additive beyond a larger
 reservoir.
 o Use of a covering or (vented/open) enclosure to keep the tank shaded may also help with
 reducing maximum temperatures of an additive system enclosure.
 o Implementation of a climate controlled, weatherproof enclosure could also be used for
 temperature regulation and should be considered.
 o Constant mixing or agitation of stored ascorbic acid reductant solutions should also be
 considered to ensure more consistent temperature distribution throughout a storage vessel.
 • Storage vessel ventilation
 o The buildup of CO2 gas within some sample containers during testing could also potentially
 occur in storage vessels within an additive system. Though temperature regulation may
 minimize this gas buildup, proper venting of the storage tank through passive or active

 9

 means should be considered. Simple check valves to allow pressure to be released and
 prevent gas buildup could be sufficient for this purpose.
 o Pressure gauges or CO2 monitors external to any additive system enclosure may also be
 beneficial to monitor any potential gaseous buildup.

4.2 Part 2: pH assessment
To ensure the pH of the M-1 Air Stripper influent is still within the acceptable permit-required range of 2
to 12.5 after addition of ascorbic acid solution, a batch test was executed to estimate the pH of ascorbic
acid-treated M-Area groundwater once it reaches the M-1 Air Stripper inlet. Dosage at the RWM-018
wellhead was simulated by neutralizing a sample of high oxidant concentration (1500 ppm potassium
permanganate) with the 2 M ascorbic acid solution (based on previous dosing trials). Following
neutralization, the sample was diluted with M-Area groundwater to mimic its combination with
groundwater from other recovery wells in the network during transit to the M-1 Air Stripper. pH values
were recorded at all steps of this batch testing (Table 4-1). This test was performed in duplicate, and good
agreement was found between replicates at all stages of testing.

acidic environment (like M-Area groundwater) generates dehydroascorbic acid from the oxidation of

increases as the reaction consumes hydrogen ions provided by the sulfuric acid, causing the pH to rise as
the reaction proceeds towards completion.

schemes from additional wells would also be unlikely to increase the pH of a combined groundwater

 5.0 Conclusions
Storability and pH testing show that ascorbic acid reductant solutions may be practically employed in field
scale treatment systems for the neutralization of residual oxidants without compromising the pH limits of
M-1 effluent discharge water. By simulating the conditions of longer term storage of the proposed ascorbic
acid reductant solution, under varied conditions representative of outdoor storage, recommendations for
best practices and design considerations have been identified to optimize field scale additive system design
using ascorbic acid solutions. Several key factors were identified during stability testing that indicate

 13

temperature regulation and venting are of primary importance for long term storage of ascorbic acid
reductant solutions.

The simulation of ascorbic acid amended groundwater from well RWM-018 proves that there is little risk
to the overall M-1 Air Stripper groundwater recovery and treatment system by the addition of oxidant pre-
treatment systems using this reductant. Previous testing has shown that the use of ascorbic acid to neutralize
residual ISCO oxidants is no threat to the efficiency of stannous chloride treatment at the inlet of the M-1
Air Stripper (SRNL, 2024). Further, testing conducted by SRNL to mimic the combination of ascorbic
treated groundwater recovered from well RWM-018 into the overall recovery system of the M-1 Air
Stripper will not jeopardize effluent fluid quality objectives related to set pH ranges.

Using the appropriate additive system design choices and ascorbic acid reductant solution formulations, the
treatment of residual oxidant in recovery water from wells RWM-018, and potentially RWM-008, is
possible and recommended without threatening the ongoing groundwater recovery system of A/M-Area.
SRNL recommends the use of such a treatment system with the objective of resuming groundwater
extraction at well RWM-018.

 6.0 Recommendations
Recommendations based upon observed characteristics of ascorbic acid during storability and pH testing
include:
 • It is recommended that the ascorbic acid reductant containers be shielded or otherwise protected
 from maximum ambient temperatures during summer months should these solutions be stored for
 multiple weeks within a treatment system.
 • It is also recommended to incorporate a venting mechanism into the chemical additive system to
 prevent possible pressurization of storage tanks or CO2 buildup.
 • Other recommended design considerations include direct sunlight shielding by canopy or
 enclosure, double walled or insulated storage vessels for ascorbic solutions, and/or stirring
 mechanisms to evenly distribute the temperature during storage.
 • Testing supported that dosing of ascorbic acid at the recommended rates based on maximum
 expected oxidant concentrations expected in M-Area groundwater did not result in M-1 Air Stripper
 influent pH exceeding the permitted range, and that maximally concentrated ascorbic solutions (2
 M) be used for additive systems.
