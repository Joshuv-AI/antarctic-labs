Cost-effectiveness of iron deficiency screening in pregnancy across ferritin-based thresholds∗
Authors: Daniel Wang, Manraj Sra, Ding Quan Ng, Aniketh Tadepalli, Kristina Rinaldi, Satoko Ito, Adam Cuker, George Goshua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Second- and third-trimester ferritin-based screening for ID is a cost-effective intervention compared with no screening.•A higher ferritin screening threshold (30 μg/L) is more cost-effective than a lower threshold (15 μg/L).

Introduction

Iron deficiency (ID) is the most common micronutrient deficiency worldwide and affects ∼85% of all pregnancies by the third trimester across high-, middle-, and low-income countries.1, 2, 3, 4 Per the 2019 Global Burden of Disease study, ID was identified as the primary risk factor contributing to the burden of maternal disorders around the globe and is the most common cause of anemia in pregnancy.5,6 The likelihood of developing ID and its untreated sequela, ID anemia (IDA), increases greatly in pregnancy because of higher physiologic iron requirements to support fetal and placental growth as well as maternal blood volume expansion.2,7,8 An average pregnancy will require an additional 1 g elemental iron beyond baseline needs from conception to delivery.2 However, observational data from low-, middle-, and high-income countries show that inadequate dietary iron intake and poor compliance with dietary guidelines are widespread among pregnant women.9, 10, 11 Left unaddressed, ID in pregnancy is associated with significantly worsened maternofetal outcomes, including low birth weight, prematurity, postpartum hemorrhage, intrauterine growth restriction, and overall maternal morbidity.2,8,12, 13, 14 Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that low neonatal iron status is associated with significantly poorer memory, attention, and cognitive speed, even despite repletion of iron stores later in infancy.15, 16, 17, 18 These deficits, particularly in recognition-based memory, attention, planning, and mental well-being, have been shown to persist into late childhood, school age, and even adolescence, underscoring the crucial role of iron in early brain development.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25 Clinical consensus holds that the benefits of iron supplementation with the goal of maintaining iron sufficiency and proper fetal iron loading through pregnancy outweigh the risks of oversupplementation and iron overload.26,27

Despite the prevalence of ID and its well-recognized association with poor gestational outcomes for both mother and child, there are currently no US guidelines for ID (with or without anemia) screening in pregnancy, including by the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, which recommends routine screening with complete blood count (CBC) only.28,29 This stands in contrast to international guidance from the European Hematology Association and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics, which recommend regular assessment of both hemoglobin and iron studies as early as possible in pregnancy, with subsequent treatment if ID is identified.30,31 Furthermore, existing ferritin-based reference ranges set forth by the World Health Organization (WHO) for the diagnosis of ID in pregnancy (≤15 μg/L in first-trimester individuals, with no recommendations for the second or third trimester) fall well below established physiologic thresholds (<20-25 μg/L).32, 33, 34 In recognition of this critical gap, the American Society of Hematology Guideline Panel on the Diagnosis of Iron Deficiency presented a conditional recommendation to use a serum ferritin threshold of ≤30 μg/L and recommended against a ferritin threshold of <15 μ/L for the diagnosis of ID in pregnancy.35 Importantly, the effects of population-wide ID screening in pregnant individuals in the United States have not been established. To address this gap, we conducted, to our knowledge, the first cost-effectiveness analysis of screening ferritin thresholds for the identification and treatment of ID in pregnancy.

Methods

Model overview

We built a Markov cohort model of reproductive-age women in the United States to examine the cost-effectiveness of screening for ID during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy using a ferritin threshold of (1) ≤30 μg/L vs (2) ≤15 μg/L (in line with current WHO diagnostic thresholds for first-trimester pregnancies) vs (3) no screening (ie, the status quo). A threshold of 30 μg/L was chosen as a more physiologically appropriate threshold than the current WHO diagnostic standards while remaining specific for ID in pregnancy, based on previously published work.33,36 Individuals in our model begin in a nonpregnant state and may become pregnant, after which they progress to states of (1) healthy pregnancy without ID, (2) pregnancy with ID properly identified and treated with 1 g IV iron dextran, or (3) pregnancy with undiagnosed and untreated ID. Ferritin screening is performed once during each of the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. After pregnancy, patients return to the nonpregnant health state. Background mortality based on US life tables is applied throughout.37 We chose to model supplementation with IV rather than oral iron, given evidence that IV iron facilitates more rapid and effective replenishment of both iron stores and erythropoiesis in the second and third trimesters based on ferritin and hemoglobin measurements.38, 39, 40 IV iron dextran was chosen specifically because of its ability to rapidly deliver 1 g elemental iron in a single administration.41 Patients who are treated with IV iron may experience infusion-related anaphylactic reactions, which may result in death. All individuals who are diagnosed with ID were assumed to establish longitudinal care (consisting of annual visits until the end of reproductive life span at menopause) with a hematologist after childbirth. We assumed a starting aged 15 years to reflect the best available age-specific data across all input parameters, recognizing that the median age at menarche in the United States is 12 years and may be decreasing over time.42,43 Model transition-state cycles were 1 year in length, and analyses were conducted over a lifetime horizon. The primary outcome of interest was the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio and its reformulation, the incremental net monetary benefit. We performed this analysis from a modified societal perspective, taking into account patient wages lost to iron-related infusion time and doing so across a range of conventionally accepted willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds in the United States ($50 000-$150 000/quality-adjusted life year [QALY]) while anchoring our base-case results at a WTP threshold of $120 000/QALY per recommendations from the latest American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology statement on cost/value methodology in clinical practice.44,45 Costs and utilities were discounted at a 3% annual rate, in line with recommendations from the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine.46 We constructed our model using TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2025 (TreeAge Software, Williamstown, MA). An additional analysis comparing empiric daily oral iron supplementation without screening and ferritin-based ID screening in pregnancy is reported in our supplement (supplemental Table 1). Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards guidelines were implemented where applicable.

Costs

All costs were adjusted to $2025 using the personal consumption expenditures price index per the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, unless otherwise indicated.50,51 Age- and sex-specific average annual health care costs for women were incorporated from the US Medical Expenditures Panel Survey.52 The costs of ferritin and CBC laboratory testing were sourced from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) 2026 Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule.53 The cost of IV supplementation with iron dextran was sourced from CMS 2026 Medicare Part B Average Sales Price data.54 The cost of IV iron administration was also taken into account using median hourly nursing wages per the US Bureau of Labor Statistics and supply costs per the CMS Durable Medical Equipment, Prosthetics, Orthotics, and Supplies Fee Schedule.55,56 CMS Physician Fee Schedule data were used to inform the cost of annual hematology follow-up visits for ID.57

QALYs

Health outcomes estimated by our model were expressed in QALY, a measure that accounts for both health-related quality of life and length of life. Health-related quality of life was quantified by utility values ranging from 0 (death) to 1 (perfect health). The baseline utility of perfect health for US women was sourced in an age-dependent manner from EQ-5D-5L (EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level) data for the general population of the United States.58 Disease- and sex-specific QALYs for patients with ID were informed by EQ-5D index values drawn from the largest (n = 236) prospective study of the effects of ID and anemia on health-related quality of life in women of reproductive age.59 These utilities were then adjusted to account for potential geographic and temporal differences to the modern-day United States, derived using concurrent country-specific general population EQ-5D reference norms.58,60 Trimester-specific health-related quality-of-life measurements were incorporated multiplicatively based on a robust (n = 5250) sample of EQ-5D-5L data from currently or recently pregnant individuals in the United States.61 Finally, the utility decrement of anaphylaxis related to IV iron supplementation was incorporated.62

Sensitivity analysis, threshold analysis, and model validation

To quantify uncertainty within our model, we examined the effect of each individual parameter on model results in deterministic sensitivity analyses and propagated uncertainty in all parameters simultaneously in probabilistic sensitivity analyses. In deterministic sensitivity analyses, when available, all parameters were varied by the greater of their established 95% confidence intervals or ±20% of their base-case point estimate. In probabilistic sensitivity analyses, we used β-program evaluation and review technique distributions for utilities and transition probabilities and γ distributions for costs (Table 1) while simultaneously ensuring random draws from each distribution via 10 000 second-order Monte Carlo simulations. An additional threshold analysis was conducted to identify the maximum utility of a pregnant individual with ID at which screening was cost-effective. Model internal validation was conducted by comparing the per-person birth rate generated across 10 000 iterations of our model with expected birth rate data from the US National Vital Statistics Report.

Results

Model validation

Across 10 000 iterations and averaged across all screening arms, the average fertility rate per patient was 1.596 births per woman (95% confidence interval, 1.582-1.610) compared with the average US fertility rate of 1.62 in 2023.

Base case

In the base case, screening for ID in pregnancy using a ferritin threshold of 30 μg/L was the cost-effective strategy, generating 25.14 QALYs at a cost of $204 300 compared with 25.09 QALYs at a cost of $202 500 for no screening (Table 2). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was $34 000/QALY (95% credible interval, $32 000-$36 200/QALY). Screening using a ferritin threshold of 15 μg/L was dominated, generating 25.12 QALYs at a cost of $203 700.

Deterministic, probabilistic, and threshold sensitivity analyses

Deterministic sensitivity analysis revealed no parameter variation that changed our conclusion that screening with a ferritin cutoff of 30 μg/L was the cost-effective strategy (Figure 2). The parameters to which our model was most sensitive were, in order, the age-specific annual birth probability, the prevalence of ID at a ferritin threshold of 30 μg/L in the third trimester of pregnancy, the cost of annual hematology follow-up, and the cohort starting age; however, no change in these parameters caused our results to favor screening with a ferritin threshold of 15 μg/L or no screening. Threshold analysis revealed that, at a WTP threshold of $120 000/QALY, screening with a ferritin threshold of 30 μg/L remained the favored strategy up to a utility difference between pregnancy without ID and pregnancy with ID <0.033 QALYs. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis demonstrated that screening using a ferritin threshold of 30 μg/L was favored in 100% of 10 000 Monte Carlo iterations (Figure 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to both (1) assess the cost-effectiveness of second- and third-trimester screening for the diagnosis and treatment of ID in pregnancy (vs no screening) and (2) compare a more physiologic ferritin-based screening threshold than the WHO-recommended diagnostic threshold (commonly used in US practice settings) from a societal perspective.8,27,35 Our results across base-case and sensitivity analyses demonstrate that ID screening in pregnancy using a ferritin threshold of ≤30 μg/L is a cost-effective intervention that improves quality-adjusted life expectancy in women of reproductive age in the United States compared with screening at ≤15 μg/L (WHO diagnostic standard in the first trimester) or no screening. In lay terms, our population-level model of costs, quality-of-life benefit, and adverse events related to ID screening in US pregnant women across the life span finds that screening for ID with a higher threshold yields the greatest value per health care dollar spent. These findings remain consistent across a range of conventionally accepted WTP thresholds. These results are driven by both the relatively low cost of screening and the effectiveness of IV iron-based treatment for ID, the specificity of physiologically derived ferritin testing for ID, and the overall benefit to health-related quality of life conferred to pregnant women. Underpinning this benefit is the greater percentage of women accurately diagnosed and appropriately treated for ID at a higher ferritin threshold, which remains robust with a marginal increase in utility from ID treatment in pregnancy of as little as 0.033 QALYs.

ID is systematically underdiagnosed and undertreated as a consequence of clinical, societal, and structural causes, representing an enormous burden to women’s quality of life around the globe.4,63, 64, 65, 66, 67 This remains true in pregnancy even in high-income settings.6,68 Simultaneously, the deleterious effects of untreated ID across the life span, particularly with respect to both maternofetal health and childhood development in the antepartum and postpartum periods, are well documented.2,5,17,19 Clinicians across specialties, including hematology, gynecology, gastroenterology, and general internal medicine, have called attention to the importance of proper identification and assessment of ID.35,69, 70, 71 Despite these calls, the US Preventive Services Task Force concluded that current evidence is “insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms” of screening for ID and IDA in pregnancy to prevent adverse maternal and fetal outcomes.28 Our findings address this evidence gap by illustrating the clear benefit to maternal quality-adjusted life expectancy and overall health-economic value of population-level second- and third-trimester ferritin-based screening of ID across all comers. We build upon previous work highlighting the benefits of ID screening in nonpregnant women of reproductive age in the United States, extending to the uniquely vulnerable period of pregnancy when iron status is crucially important, and iron balance is precarious in the face of increased maternal and fetal demands.13,72 Furthermore, our results demonstrate the cost-effectiveness of establishing a standardized, physiologically sound threshold for the detection of ID in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy that is well above any WHO-recommended lower limits of normal. This ≤30 μg/L ferritin threshold is based on rigorous clinical and translational evidence delineating appropriate ferritin ranges for ID in pregnant individuals, which provides strong grounds against using lower historic ranges based on flawed sampling and incorrect assumptions regarding the low prevalence of ID.14,32,33,73 Our model also represents an important proof of concept for the implementation of population-wide ID screening in pregnancy, which may be adapted beyond a US perspective into the context of low- and middle-income countries, where ID and IDA continue to pose a disproportionately large challenge to women’s health.5,63 If our model is adopted, the downstream benefits of addressing ID propagate not only to maternal outcomes at delivery but also to childhood growth, cognitive and behavioral development, and overall well-being. In short, given that routine prenatal care already requires numerous blood-based infectious, genetic, and immunologic screenings, ID screening in pregnancy may represent a high-value, low-barrier health intervention with immense intergenerational impact.

Our model has several strengths. First, we use best-available prevalence estimates of ID at each ferritin threshold examined based on the largest (n = 629) longitudinal prospective study of iron status across pregnancy to date, conducted in a high-resource health care setting similar to the United States.36 Second, the transition probability of pregnancy and childbirth was informed directly by the latest available US fertility data, and model internal validation demonstrated high fidelity between simulated birth rates and real-world data.47 Our model is also intentionally structured to compare 3 separate strategies of screening using ferritin levels ≤30 μg/L, ≤15 μg/L, and no screening, the latter capturing the current US clinical reality in which many pregnant women are not screened for ID.6,28,68 Furthermore, our analysis is conducted from a modified societal perspective, taking into account wages lost because of IV iron infusion time (inclusive of travel to infusion sites and postinfusion monitoring time), the cost of nursing-driven infusion time, and infusion-related supplies for the administration of IV iron dextran. Beyond this, we conservatively incorporate the cost of annual follow-up visits to a hematologist after identification of ID upon screening, assuming that 100% of patients are referred to a specialist, while recognizing that in real-world practice, many cases of uncomplicated ID can be followed up in the primary care setting.69,74 Finally, key parameters are varied extensively in our deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses, throughout which our finding that screening with a ferritin benchmark of ≤30 μg/L is cost-effective remains robust. The strength of our findings is further corroborated by an exploratory threshold analysis that demonstrates that ID screening using a ferritin threshold of ≤30 μg/L remains the preferred strategy up to a small utility difference between ID and non-ID pregnancy states.

We also recognize several limitations. First, we assume no identification of ID without anemia from CBC values alone, which are routinely collected as part of the obstetric standard of care in the United States.29 Second, we do not explicitly examine alternative IV iron formulations beyond iron dextran, noting that multiple possible options are available in the United States (eg, iron sucrose, ferumoxytol, and ferric carboxymaltose) that differ in cost, administration, and availability depending on practice setting and patient insurance.75 Third, because no on-treatment–specific utility data have been reported for pregnant women with ID undergoing IV iron supplementation using generic preference-based measures of health-related quality of life suitable for health economic analyses, we estimated the utility of the nonpregnant ID, nonpregnant non-ID (ie, perfect health), pregnant non-ID, and pregnant with ID health states from a published EQ-5D study of Finnish women with ID without anemia. These data are further standardized to account for time period and geography, alongside validated EQ-5D population norms from US women and multiplicatively account for trimester-specific utility norms in pregnancy.58,59,61 Fourth, all economic models are specific to their country of interest, and this model is specific to the US context; analysis of ferritin screening outside of the United States cannot be assumed to reach the same conclusion. In addition, our model does not explicitly account for any utility benefit conferred to children of mothers treated for ID during pregnancy or any decrement to children of mothers with undiagnosed and untreated ID through delivery. This is a result of the notable lack of any studies evaluating measures of health-related quality of life in children of mothers who were iron deficient during pregnancy. However, we believe this limitation represents a strength of our conclusions, namely, that we find screening for ID at a physiologically appropriate ferritin threshold cost-effective even when assuming that there is no health benefit to children when their mothers are treated for ID during the gestational period. Available evidence, as previously discussed, points convincingly toward the opposite.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 This includes a recently published prospective study investigating the relationship between maternal ID in early pregnancy, neonatal iron status, and neurodevelopment, illustrating that children born to iron-deficient mothers (ferritin <30 μg/L) display lower cord ferritin concentrations at the age of 15 weeks and lower language and motor capabilities at the age of 2 years than their iron-sufficient peers.2,22 We eagerly anticipate forthcoming pediatric quality-of-life work that may be readily incorporated into future health-economic evaluations of ID screening in pregnancy.

In summary, to the best of our knowledge, we conducted the first cost-effectiveness analysis of ferritin-based screening for ID during pregnancy in the United States. We found that second- and third-trimester screening at a threshold of ≤30 μg/L ferritin is the cost-effective option and improves quality-adjusted life expectancy for mothers when compared with screening at ≤15 μg/L ferritin or no screening (the status quo), even without accounting for any fetal benefit. These results are driven by the proper identification of ID and treatment with IV iron supplementation during pregnancy as well as by the superiority of a physiologically informed ferritin threshold in addressing the additional proportion of women who may be missed with a lower cutoff (eg, WHO first-trimester recommendation). Taken together, our findings provide a strong economic rationale for screening for ID during pregnancy in the United States.
