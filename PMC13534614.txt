Classification of bacterial biological warfare agent simulants through 2D Py-GC/MS data coupled with deep learning
Authors: Georgios Kirtsanis, Alejandra Vargas-Valderrama, Joeri Vercammen, Georgios Dolias, Spyridon Kintzios, Konstantinos Ioannidis, Stefanos Vrochidis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIdentification and detection of biological warfare agents (BWAs) constitute a critical task for both biodefence applications and public health protection. Gas Chromatography coupled with Mass Spectrometry (GC/MS) represents a robust analytical platform due to its sensitivity to Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) and Fatty Acid Methyl Esters (FAMEs). However, the high chemical complexity of microorganisms necessitates more comprehensive molecular profiling. In this context, Pyrolysis-GC/MS (Py-GC/MS) is employed to thermally decompose intact bacterial cells, generating high-dimensional chromatographic signatures comprising a broad spectrum of volatile degradation products. In this study, eight bacterial species, including BWA simulants, Bacillus atrophaeus, Francisella philomiragia, Escherichia coli, Streptococcus mitis, Yersinia enterocolitica subsp. enterocolitica, Acinetobacter baumannii, Agromyces mediolanus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis, were analyzed at three concentration levels using a Py-GC/MS device. Four complementary data representations were derived from each measurement: 2D GC×MS chromatograms, 1D Total Ion Count (TIC) signals, FAME feature profiles, and Principal Component (PC) projections. The resulting dataset is publicly released to support reproducibility and further research. Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models are subsequently employed to extract discriminative features and perform classification. For Gram type classification, each data representation–model pair is trained and validated on four bacterial classes and evaluated on four unseen classes, enabling a direct assessment of out-of-distribution generalization. For species-level identification, models are trained and evaluated on a subset of four bacterial classes, including two BWA simulants. The experimental results demonstrate that 2D chromatographic representations, when coupled with advanced DL architectures, consistently achieve superior performance in terms of accuracy and F1-score. Furthermore, the Limit of Detection (LOD) is systematically evaluated across all experimental settings, providing additional insight into the sensitivity and robustness of each data representation-model pair.Keywords: Biological warfare agents (BWAs), Simulants, Bacteria, Deep learning (DL), Machine learning (ML), Pyrolysis – Gas Chromatography – Mass Spectrometry (Py-GC/MS)Subject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Microbiology

Introduction

Rapid and accurate identification of high-consequence biological agents, including Biological Warfare Agents (BWAs), is crucial for outbreak management, public health protection, and biodefence, irrespective of whether the event is natural, accidental, or deliberate. Early detection allows for timely containment, protection of exposed populations, and coordinated medical and logistical interventions. Current identification approaches, including bio-molecular techniques such as PCR1 and LAMP2, and protein-based identification methods like MALDI-TOF MS3, are highly sensitive and fast but limited to known biological targets. In addition, these tools are ineffective for broader chemical, radiological, and nuclear threat detection, restricting their applicability in comprehensive field assessments.

Gas Chromatography coupled with Mass Spectrometry (GC/MS) has been widely adopted for the detection of narcotics4, volatile chemical warfare agents5,6, and explosives7, with portable systems already commercially available for field use8. Since the late 20th century, the potential for extending GC/MS to the identification of biological agents has been explored9–12.In microbial analysis, including bacteria and fungi, GC/MS typically targets two classes of volatile analytes: volatile organic compounds (VOCs)13–16 and fatty acid methyl esters (FAMEs)17–20. VOCs reflect the metabolic fingerprint of microorganisms and can provide information about species-specific metabolism and physiological state. In contrast, FAMEs, derived from fatty acids, a structural component of the cell envelope, provide a fingerprint that not only enables consistent genus- or species-level identification but also offers valuable structural and taxonomic information19. Combined analysis of FAMEs, carbohydrates, and dipicolinic acid has also proven useful for identifying spores from fungi and bacteria21,22. Virus detection remains challenging, as most GC/MS signals originate from residual host cell material rather than virions, underscoring the need for further research20.

While VOCs and FAMEs analyses are effective, they often require extensive sample preparation and focus on a narrow set of biomarkers, thereby discarding much of the chemical complexity present in microorganisms that could be valuable for discriminating among very closely related species. Pyrolysis–GC/MS (Py-GC/MS) addresses this limitation by thermally decomposing intact bacterial cells and releasing a broad range of volatile compounds. As a result, this technique generates chemically rich profiles inherently high-dimensional, spanning retention times, mass-to-charge ratios (m/z), and ion intensities. Thus, advanced computational approaches are needed to extract reproducible and biologically meaningful patterns20.

Advanced pre-processing techniques systematically automate the labor-intensive processes of chromatographic alignment, baseline correction, and peak deconvolution, which prove particularly challenging when analyzing biological agent pyrolysates comprising hundreds of distinct components. These methods yield cleaner datasets characterized by improved signal-to-noise ratios and substantially reduced dimensionality. Consequently, these enhanced data representations significantly enhance the efficacy of automated simulant detection through the implementation of state-of-the-art Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) models. Recent advances in ML and DL enable the effective exploitation of complex analytical techniques, such as Py-GC-IMS23–26, MALDI-TOF MS27–29, and Py-GC/MS19,30,31, for capturing high-dimensional spectral information. In particular, microorganisms generate a diverse and complex range of chemical compounds, necessitating advanced computational approaches to effectively leverage this rich information for accurate analysis and classification.

In this study, an open-source Py-GC/MS dataset was generated from eight bacterial species, including BWA simulants, Bacillus atrophaeus (a surrogate for Bacillus anthracis), Francisella philomiragia (a surrogate for Francisella tularensis), Escherichia coli, Streptococcus mitis, Yersinia enterocolitica subsp. enterocolitica (a surrogate for Yersinia pestis), Acinetobacter baumannii, Agromyces mediolanus, and Staphylococcus epidermidis. Based on this dataset, We explored different data representations, including targeted (bacterial FAMEs) and non-targeted representations, such as 1D Total Ion Counts (TICs), 2D GCxMS Chromatograms contour plots, and Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-derived features. These representations were used to train and evaluate eight ML/DL models. Furthermore, we investigated the Limit Of Detection (LOD) of the bacteria with respect to concentration and examined how detection limits influence the classification performance of the models.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, culture conditions, and sample preparation

Four Gram-negative strains, E. coli (DSM 500), F. philomiragia (DSM 7535), Y. enterocolitica (DSM 4780) and A. baumannii (DSM 25645), and four Gram-positive strain, B. atrophaeus (DSM 675), S. mitis (DSM 12643), A. mediolanus (DSM 40) and S. epidermidis (DSM 30905), were obtained from the Deutsche Sammlung von Mikroorganismen und Zellkulturen (DSMZ). Colonies were recovered from frozen glycerol stocks by streaking on tryptic soy agar plates (TSA; VWR Chemicals, Belgium) and incubating overnight either at 37 C (E. coli, F. philomiragia and A. baumannii, S. mitis), or 30 C (B. atrophaeus, Y. enterolitica, A. mediolanus and S. epidermis). Individual colonies of each strain were inoculated in 30 ml of tryptic soy broth (TSB; VWR Chemicals, Belgium) and cultured under agitation at 180 rpm for 16 h at 37 C (30 C for B. atrophaeus). For enumeration, 500 μL of each overnight culture was serially diluted (-) in TSB, and 100 μL of each dilution was plated on TSA to estimate Colony-Forming Units (CFU). The remaining 29.5 mL of culture was harvested by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 20 min at 4 C. The bacterial pellet was washed once with 10 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Cytiva, USA). Cells were inactivated by heating at 95 C for 1 h in a thermoshaker at 500 rpm. To confirm complete inactivation, 100 μL of the treated suspension was seeded on TSA and incubated under the appropriate growth conditions; colonies were not observed after 72 h. Inactivated cells were collected by centrifugation (4,000 × g, 20 min, 4 C), washed twice with 10 ml of ultrapure water and the resulting pellet was stored at 4 C until further analysis.

Bacterial analysis with Py-GC/MS

Bacterial cultures were prepared in ultrapure water at 5 mg/mL (1e11 CFU/ml, High level) and subsequently diluted to 0.5 mg/mL (1e10 CFU/ml, Medium level) and 0.05 mg/mL (1e9 CFU/ml, Low level) after vigorous vortexing. Pyrolysis analyses were carried out using a Frontier Laboratories EGA/PY-3030D multi-shot pyrolyzer (Interscience, Netherlands), equipped with an autosampler and installed on a split/splitless i-connect injector module of a Thermo Fisher Scientific TRACE 1600 GC (Interscience), which was hyphenated to a Thermo Fisher Scientific ISQ 7610 single quadrupole MS (Interscience). Samples were prepared by carefully placing an Auto-Rx disc (Frontier Laboratories) in a metal pyrolysis cup with tweezers. Next, 2 μL of the appropriate bacterial dilution was added, 2 μL tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH, 25 wt. percent in methanol, Merck) and, finally, a small plug of fiberglass wool (Frontier Laboratories). Before use, tweezers, pyrolysis cups and fiber glass wool were decontaminated in a flaming torch. Pyrolysis was carried out at 550 C (single shot) with a split ratio of 50 mL/min. The GC oven was programmed from 40 C (1.0 min) to 325 C at 15 C/min. Helium was used as carrier gas at 1.0 mL/min. The MS was operated in full scan mode (45–450 amu) at a scan rate of 0.2 scans/sec. MS transferline and ion source temperatures were set at 325 C and 300 C, respectively. A solvent delay of 2.0 min was applied during TMAH. The capillary column was a 30 m x 0.25 mm I.D. x 0.25 μm df Rxi-5Sil MS (Restek, Interscience). FAMEs were identified using a Bacterial Acid Methyl Ester (BAME) mix (10 mg/mL in methyl caproate, Supelco). The standard was injected directly into the pyrolysis cup without adding TMAH.

Open-Sourced dataset, pre-processing, and data representations

The four bacterial cultures (B. atrophaeus, S. mitis, E. coli, and F. philomiragia) were analyzed using Py-GC/MS at three different concentration levels, as described above. For the low and medium concentrations, five replicate analyses were performed for each level, while for the high concentration, eight analyses were conducted. Consequently, each bacterium yielded a total of 18 analyses, resulting in an initial dataset of 72 measurements for training and validation. In addition, four bacteria (A. baumannii, Y. enterocolitica, A. mediolanus, and S. epidermidis) were analyzed only at high concentration. Each of these produced eight measurements, contributing an additional 32 chromatograms. These were used exclusively for testing the Gram type classification model. Overall, the complete dataset comprises 104 measurements. This dataset is released as open-source to enable the research community to conduct experiments and support further development in this area.

As presented in Fig. 1(a), three pre-processing steps were applied to standardize the data, remove background noise, emphasize the regions of interest for each chromatogram and significantly reduce data dimensionality. Each raw 2D GC×MS chromatogram had an uneven and large size of approximately 450 mass values and 5,300 retention time points. First, to standardize the data representation across samples and obtain consistent dimensions, two consecutive one-dimensional linear interpolation steps were applied. 1D linear interpolation was performed along the mass (m/z) axis of each scan, resampling the chromatogram to integer mass values in the range [45, 450] (m/z). Subsequently, 1D linear interpolation was applied along the retention time axis to obtain a common retention time grid of 1,081 points spanning the range [120, 1200] (s). This procedure produced chromatograms of identical dimensions of 406 × 1,081, suitable for subsequent ML and DL analyses. Second, a white top-hat filter of size seven was applied to each sample as a standard baseline correction method in both image processing and GC/MS processing tasks32. This step removes background noise while preserving the characteristic peaks of the important compounds of the sample. Finally, we extracted the Region Of Interest (ROI) to retain the most informative part of the spectrum and remove noise caused by analytes, contamination, and column effects. Specifically, we kept retention times between [451, 900] (s) and mass/charge values between [61, 360] (m/z). Values below 61 (m/z) and before 451 (s) are primarily analyte artifacts, while values beyond 360 (m/z) and after 900 (s) mainly represent contamination and column fingerprints. After these steps, each chromatogram was represented as a 450 × 300 matrix, suitable for subsequent comparison and classification tasks. This significantly reduced the two-dimensional data representation to approximately 5% of its original size.

Overview of the 2D GCxMS chromatograms processing workflow of a high concentration E. coli sample. (a) Pre-processing pipeline applied to raw 2D GCxMS chromatograms. (b) Resulting data representations (TICs, FAMEs, PCs) used for machine learning and deep learning analysis.

Later, as discussed in the literature, various data representations have been proposed to analyze data from Py-GC/MS devices. As shown in Fig. 1(b), in addition to the introduced 2D GCxMS chromatogram, we extracted three more compact representations, each with different applicability. One of these is the Total Ion Count (TIC) chromatogram. Researchers often collect data from the GC column with respect to retention times to avoid the complexity of the 2D GCxMS space. This results in TICs for each distinct retention time, yielding a 1D representation. As illustrated in the example, characteristic peaks appear in the sample, corresponding to important compounds present in the culture, but without separation of the MS dimension. Consequently, compounds with the similar retention time but different m/z ratios overlap in the same peak. Another common technique is to extract FAME features. In our case, we obtained a compact representation of 26 FAMEs (see Table 1 for additional information), which are highly representative for distinguishing between bacterial classes. These FAME features are generated by analyzing specific FAMEs in controlled environments and then correlating the results with sample curves at defined retention time and m/z coordinates. Two specific FAMEs (Methyl hexadecanoate and Methyl octadecanoate) have been left out of the representation (NA), since their relative huge values compared to the rest, are a result of environment’s contamination. To complement these compact features, the unsupervised dimensionality reduction technique PCA was applied to the 2D GC×MS chromatograms of the training sets and subsequently used to transform the corresponding validation sets in each fold separately. This approach enabled the reduction of the original data to 30 principal components while retaining approximately 95% of the cumulative explained variance. While ensuring information leakage from the validation set is prevented, thereby an unbiased evaluation of classification performance is reported. PCA reduces data dimensionality by transforming correlated variables into a smaller set of uncorrelated components that captures the maximum variance of each individual chromatogram. Finally, each representation of each unique sample was independently normalized to the range [0, 1] before being used in supervised classification models. This ensures that results are not biased by differences in bacterial concentration and intensities, while also improving model performance, as normalization is a standard practice in ML/DL research.

ML/DL analysis

Our main contributions are twofold: i) the development of a unified experimental framework for Gram type classification, where models are trained and validated on four bacterial classes and evaluated on four previously unseen bacterial classes, and ii) the specific identification of four bacterial species, including critical BWA simulants.

Furthermore, we evaluated the constructed dataset under two distinct settings: one incorporating all available concentration levels, and another restricted to medium and high concentrations. This design enables the estimation of the limit of detection (LOD) and facilitates the assessment of how bacterial concentration influences classification performance when using ML and DL models.

To achieve this, a five-fold stratified cross-validation with a fixed random seed number of 42 was applied. In this setup, each fold was used once as a validation set while the remaining folds formed the training set. Stratified cross-validation is a standard technique in tasks with limited data, as it prevents the biases and poor generalization that may arise from a single validation/test split. For each experiment, we extracted four different data representations of FAMEs, PCs, TIC, and 2D GCxMS. Later, we trained a range of state-of-the-art (SOTA) models for supervised classification. For ML, Support Vector Machine (SVM), Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), XGBoost, and Partial Least Square - Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) were employed. For DL, we employed a Fully Connected Neural Network (FCNN) consisting of three hidden layers with 512 neurons each, ReLU activation functions, and a dropout rate of 0.5 after each hidden layer, followed by an output layer with (N) neurons corresponding to the number of output classes. For 2D data representations, we additionally evaluated more advanced computer vision architectures, namely ResNet1833, VGG11BN34, and InceptionV335, which are widely used for image classification tasks.

Performance was evaluated using Accuracy as presented in equation (1) and F1-Score in equations (2), metrics that are particularly informative in this context. For each feature–model pair, we reported the mean and standard deviation across folds. The best-performing configurations are indicated as follows: the best model within a specific feature–concentration pair is shown in bold, the best model within a specific data representation and any concentration setting is underlined, and the best model within a concentration setting and any data representation is *starred*. Consequently, the highest overall results appear in the form of both bold and underlined (). Finally, the summed confusion matrix of the best-performing model across all validation folds is reported, highlighting class-wise performance.

Results

Gram type classification

Binary classification into Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria is often a valuable first step in bacterial diagnostic workflows. This distinction primarily reflects differences in cell-envelope architecture and is associated with differences in envelope-associated lipid and fatty-acid composition, which are captured in the FAME profiles generated by our Py-GC/MS workflow. Gram type therefore provides a biologically meaningful and chemically grounded target for initial classification in our dataset.

Therefore, bacteria datasets were first classified according to their Gram type. B. atrophaeus and S. mitis were grouped as Gram-positive, while E. coli and F. philomiragia were classified as Gram-negative. When we include all concentration levels, we observe that across most data representations, the FCNN achieves the highest performance, highlighted in bold. An exception arises with the 2D GCxMS representation, where ResNet18, a more complex model, outperforms the rest. We also investigated the ability of the models to classify the Gram type of previously unseen bacterial cultures and evaluate their generalization performance. To achieve this, two gram-negative bacteria, A. baumannii and Y. enterocolitica; and two gram-positive bacteria, A. mediolanus and S. epidermidis, were analyzed eight times each by Py-GC/MS device at high concentration. The SOTA model InceptionV3 was trained on the entire initial dataset and then tested on this newly created dataset. The results are notable, with an Accuracy of 84.38% and an F1-Score of 84.36%. The recall and precision of the model reach 84.36% and 84.51%, respectively, while the corresponding confusion matrix is reported in Fig. 2(b).

These results indicate promising generalization behavior under the examined conditions. However, the species diversity in the training set is limited, with only two species per class, and the Gram-negative samples are largely confined to closely related lineages. Furthermore, certain taxa exhibit distinctive fatty-acid chemotypes, which may bias the Py-GC/MS-based classification toward species- or lineage-specific signatures rather than Gram status alone. Therefore, the current evaluation should be considered a proof-of-concept, and broader phylogenetic and strain-level sampling will be necessary to establish robust Gram-level generalization.

BWA simulants species identification

While Gram type classification provides a useful first step in bacterial identification, the ultimate goal is the accurate identification of bacterial species and, when possible, strains. This is especially critical when dealing with potential BWAs, where species-level resolution can determine the difference between a benign organism and a high-risk pathogen.

To this end, we evaluated the ability of our models to classify each of the four bacterial species individually, including the two BWA simulants: Bacillus atrophaeus and Francisella philomiragia. Discussion

The field of CBRN (Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear) defense is increasingly moving toward the development of multipurpose technologies capable of detecting a wide range of threat agents with a single platform. Among these, GC/MS has emerged as a leading analytical tool due to its versatility and sensitivity. However, its application to biological agents has been limited by two major challenges: the relatively low abundance of volatile compounds produced by microorganisms, and the high complexity of the resulting spectra.

To address these limitations, we explored the integration of Py-GC/MS with advanced computational models. Our proof-of-concept shows that Py-GC/MS can be used not only for Gram type classification but also for species-level identification, including the detection of BWA simulants, thereby expanding the potential of GC/MS-based systems for comprehensive CBRN threat detection.

A key novelty of this work is the systematic comparison of multiple data representations of Py-GC/MS measurements, ranging from two-dimensional GCxMS chromatograms and one-dimensional TIC chromatograms to derived FAME fingerprint profiles and PCA-generated features. By evaluating these representations with both ML methods and more complex DL architectures, this study establishes a comprehensive baseline for supervised classification tasks. To our knowledge, such a side-by-side evaluation of ML and DL pipelines across different data representations for Py-GC/MS data has not been previously reported, highlighting the contribution of this work to the field. Additionally, we release an open-source dataset comprising a total of 104 measurements, including: i) 72 Py-GC/MS chromatograms obtained from four bacterial cultures (B. atrophaeus, F. philomiragia, E. coli, and S. mitis, with the former two considered BWA simulants), and ii) 32 additional chromatograms from four bacterial species analyzed at high concentration (A. baumannii, Y. enterocolitica, A. mediolanus, and S. epidermidis, which are used exclusively for testing).

MS-based methods often require relatively high bacterial concentrations to achieve accurate identification, which can limit their applicability. Although our study still relied on moderate to high bacterial loads, the results herein presented suggest that ML/DL approaches may help improve the effective LOD. Specifically, for Gram type classification, our models performed well even at lower concentrations, indicating that we were operating above the LOD threshold for this task. For species-level identification, the best classification performance was achieved with medium to high bacterial concentrations; however, training with low concentrations yielded promising results. Interestingly, performance varied depending on the algorithm used. Remarkably, the highest Accuracy and F1-Scores were obtained when models were applied to the full spectra and the 2D GCxMS representation. These findings confirm that the LOD is influenced not only by the hardware (Py-GC/MS) but also by the analytical model and the richness of the dataset, with more comprehensive datasets enabling better predictions.

Historically, microbial identification efforts focused on detecting a limited set of well-characterized biomarkers, such as specific volatile compounds or genetic sequences. However, the emergence of artificial intelligence has enabled a paradigm shift toward analyzing the full spectrum of available data. In this study, we demonstrated that combining the complete spectral information, using the 2D GCxMS representation, with ML/DL models significantly improves classification Accuracy and F1-Score compared to using only the reduced FAMEs subset. This was true for both Gram type and species-level classification, highlighting the value of exploiting the broader chemical fingerprint generated during pyrolysis for more robust and informative bacterial identification. Future work should focus on characterizing these additional compounds to better understand their biological relevance.

An important bottleneck in the use of microbial detection systems is the reliance on prior knowledge of the target organisms. The absence of comprehensive datasets that include potential biothreat agents, especially rare, emerging, or engineered strains, limits the effectiveness of conventional identification methods. Therefore, there is a critical need for tools capable of generalizing beyond the training data and identifying organisms not previously encountered. Our results show that some of the tested models are robust enough to correctly classify previously unseen bacterial species according to their Gram type, demonstrating their potential for generalization in real-world scenarios. Further work with a larger and more diverse dataset could enable exploration of this generalization capability at the species level.

The FCNN model outperforms the other models on FAME, PC, and 1D TIC representations, while SOTA performance is reported from the combination of 2D GCxMS data with complex computer vision architectures such as InceptionV3 with 90.55% Accuracy and 90.23% F1-Score. Additionally, we analyzed four unseen bacterial cultures in high concentration and inferenced the best performing model InceptionV3, which accurately distinguished the Gram type of the unknown samples with an Accuracy and F1-Score of 84.38% and 84.36% respectively. In contrast, BWA simulant identification can be defined as more challenging, requiring discrimination among closely related species and showing a clear concentration effect, with models trained and tested on higher-concentration samples consistently performing better in 96.29% of the cases. This concentration dependency underscores the importance of signal quality for both Gram-level and BWA-level classification. Once again, the FCNN model achieves the best results on the three one-dimensional representations, while InceptionV3 and ResNet18 coupled with 2D GCxMS chromatograms deliver the top performance for this task reporting 84.73% Accuracy and 82.69% F1-Score respectively.

Finally, the superior performance of two-dimensional representations in these experiments suggests a promising direction for future research. Since 2D data structures generate richer spatial and chemical information, methods optimized for such outputs may achieve even higher levels of classification performance. In particular, extending the approach to complementary two-dimensional techniques such as Py-GC-IMS could provide new opportunities for the detection of biological agents of concern, including relevant BWA simulants. Future studies should also explore the integration of multi-modal datasets, where Py-GC/MS and Py-GC-IMS data are combined to enhance robustness, as well as the generalization of the models across instruments and laboratories. Such directions could accelerate the translation of ML/DL-based pipelines from controlled research settings into reliable field-deployable systems for bio-defense and public health applications.
