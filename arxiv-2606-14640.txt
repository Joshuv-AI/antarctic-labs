Online Convex Optimization with Sublinear Noisy Probes
Simone Di Gregorio, Anupam Gupta, Stefano Leonardi, Matteo Russo

Abstract
We study Online Convex Optimization (OCO) over a convex set K ⊆ ℝ d K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d} , where in each round t t the learner selects x t ∈ K x_{t}\in K and then observes a convex loss f t : K → [ 0 , 1 ] f_{t}:K\to[0,1] , with the goal of minimizing regret to the best fixed decision in hindsight. We introduce a unified probing model that generalizes two recent lines of work: sublinear best-expert queries in the experts setting ( 29 ) , and pairwise (comparison-based) feedback available every round in OCO ( 9 ) . In our framework, the learner has a budget of k ≤ T k\leq T pairwise probes ; on a probed round it may query two points and learn which one has smaller loss. Our main result shows that even a sublinear and noisy probe budget can provably improve worst-case regret in the full feedback OCO regime. With k k δ \delta -noisy pairwise probes, we obtain Reg T ≤ O ⁡ ( min ⁡ { d ​ T ​ ln ⁡ T , d ​ T ​ ln ⁡ T k ​ | 1 − 2 ​ δ | } ) , \textup{{Reg}}_{T}\leq O\!\left(\min\left\{\sqrt{dT\ln T},\;\frac{dT\ln T}{k|1-2\delta|}\right\}\right), which is tight (up to logarithmic factors in T T ) across T T , k k and δ \delta . Specifically regarding the noise parameter δ ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \delta\in[0,1] , the regret guarantee smoothly degrades as the oracle response approaches a coin flip, i.e., δ \delta is close to 1 / 2 \nicefrac{{1}}{{2}} . When applying the same techniques to a finite K K for the prediction with d d experts setting, the resulting rates are instead completely tight in all parameters, including d d . Our analysis gives a streamlined treatment of pairwise probing in OCO by quantifying the benefit of probing via a variance reduction effect, combined with a second-order (variance-based) analysis of Continuous Exponential Weights ( 10 ; 13 ) .

Introduction

Online convex optimization (OCO) is a general framework for sequential
decision-making in which, over a horizon of
T
rounds, a learner
repeatedly selects a decision
x_{t}
from a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
incurs an adversarially chosen convex loss
f_{t}(x_{t})
, and receives
feedback at the end of each round
(
31
;
20
;
26
)
. The
learner’s performance is measured by
regret
, defined as the gap
between its cumulative loss and that of the best fixed comparator

x^{\star}\in K
in hindsight:

\textup{{Reg}}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x_{t})\;-\;\inf_{x\in K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x).

In the
full-information
variant, the entire loss function
f_{t}

(or equivalently a subgradient oracle at every point) is revealed after
each round; classical methods such as Online Gradient Descent and
Follow-the-Regularized-Leader achieve
\Theta(\sqrt{T})
regret (up to
problem-dependent geometry factors). In the bandit version, only
f_{t}(x_{t})
is revealed for the played point
x_{t}
.

The OCO framework subsumes Online linear optimization (OLO), where losses are linear and expressed as
f_{t}(x)=f_{t}^{\top}x
for vector
f_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. As a further special case, we have the classical online learning with finitely many
actions/experts: taking
\Delta^{d-1}
(the
d-1
-dimensional
simplex) and linear losses, the regret
against the best vertex recovers the usual notion of regret in
the experts problem. In this discrete specialization, full
feedback reveals all coordinates of
f_{t}
, while bandit feedback
reveals only
f_{t}(x_{t})
for the played expert
x_{t}
.

Motivated by the recent surge of algorithms augmented by external
predictions, hints, or side-information
(
24
)
, we
study a model in which the learner may occasionally obtain limited

advance
information about the losses
before
committing
to its decision on that round. Most relevant to our setting are

9
and
29
, who show that even very
restricted query access can dramatically reduce regret. In

9
, the learner is allowed
pairwise
comparison probes
: at the beginning of each round
t
, before
choosing the decision
x_{t}
, the learner may choose two candidate
decisions and learn which one will incur smaller instantaneous loss in
this round, without revealing the loss values themselves. They obtain
time-independent regret bounds for online linear optimization and
improved rates for OCO under additional curvature assumptions. In the
discrete bandits setting, they show that pairwise comparisons can
yield regret of order
O(d\ln T)
in the stochastic
case. Complementarily,
29
allow
best-expert
probes
(which they call queries) in the experts special case only,
which reveal the identity of the minimizing expert in the current
round
t
(before the decision
x_{t}
is played); however, only

k\leq T
such queries can be made. In this setting, they show a
regret bound of
O\!\left(\min\left\{\sqrt{T\ln d},\nicefrac{{T\ln d}}{{k}}\right\}\right)
.

These results raise the following natural question:

If the learner is able to issue only a sublinear number
k\ll T
of (possibly noisy)
pairwise
probes, how much can the OCO regret be improved with respect to the base
\sqrt{T}
rate?

To address this question, we first formalize an OCO-with-probing model
that unifies both discrete and continuous settings, and then state our
results.

1.1
Our Model

We present a probing-augmented online optimization protocol. The OCO and prediction with expert advice
settings arise as two specializations.

1.1.1
Online convex optimization with noisy probing

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be either a compact convex set or a finite set. The interaction proceeds for
T
rounds against an

oblivious adversary
who pre-commits to a sequence of loss
functions bounded in
[0,1]
:
\{f_{t}:K\to[0,1]\}_{t=1}^{T}
.
At each round
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
, the learner picks

x_{t}\in K
, incurs loss
f_{t}(x_{t})
, and then receives feedback
f_{t}(x)
for all
x\in K
. Moreover, for appropriate functions
f
and
g
we define
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle=\int_{K}f(x)g(x)\mu(dx)
, where the base measure
\mu
is explicit or clear from the context.

Probing budget.

In addition to the feedback, the learner may issue
probes

in at most
k
rounds. Let
Q\subseteq\{1,\dots,T\}
denote the set of probed rounds, decided (possibly randomly and adaptively) by the learner, with
|Q|\leq k
. If
t\in Q
, before selecting
x_{t}
, the learner may
specify a (possibly randomized) finite set of
C
candidate points

P_{t}=\{y_{t,1},\dots,y_{t,C}\}\subseteq K
,
and the probe returns the identity of the candidate with minimal
instantaneous loss in
P_{t}
, possibly corrupted by noise. In
this paper we focus on the least powerful nontrivial probes, namely
pairwise comparisons with
C=2
.

To model noise, we assume that the
probe returns the candidate with the
maximal
(instead of
minimal) loss with probability
\delta
, but our results clearly apply to less challenging
noise: for example, the oracle could return, with probability
\delta
, any of the two probed points, which is better than returning the
\arg\max
, and our regret bounds still hold.
Moreover, any probe model with general
C>2
can be reduced to the pairwise case by constructing
P_{t}
as multiset made up of
y_{t,1}
repeated
C-1
times and
y_{t,2}
once: our upper bounds thus extend for general
C>2
.

Definition 1.1

(Noisy comparison probe)
.

Fix
\delta\in[0,1]
and an integer
C\geq 1
.
A
\delta
-noisy (comparison) probe
at round
t
is a (possibly randomized)
set
P_{t}=\{y_{t,1},\dots,y_{t,C}\}\subseteq K
together with an outcome:

\hat{y}_{t}\;=\;\begin{cases}y_{t}^{\star}\in\arg\min_{y\in P_{t}}f_{t}(y),&\text{with probability }1-\delta,\\
y_{t}^{-}\in\argmax_{y\in P_{t}}f_{t}(y),&\text{with probability }\delta.\end{cases}

Whenever the minimizer or maximizer is not unique, ties are broken uniformly at random. When
\delta=0
, we call the probe
noiseless
. Moreover, a probe is
global
when
P_{t}=K
and
\delta=0
, that is the probe outcome is
y^{\star}_{t}\in\arg\min_{y\in K}f_{t}(y)
with probability
1
, assuming the minimum is achieved.

Protocol.

Formally, first an
oblivious adversary pre-commits to a sequence of convex loss
functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{T}
bounded in
[0,1]
, and then, in each
round
t=1,\dots,T
:

1.

The learner decides if
t\in Q
: if so, it chooses
P_{t}\subseteq K

and observes
\hat{y}_{t}\in P_{t}
.

2.

The learner chooses
x_{t}\in K
(possibly as a randomized function of
\hat{y}_{t}
and
the past).

3.

The learner incurs loss
f_{t}(x_{t})
and observes full feedback
\{f_{t}(x)\}_{x\in K}
.

Regret.

For any point
x\in K
, define the cumulative loss

L_{T}(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x)
. Similarly, let
L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}})=\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x_{t})
be the cumulative loss of a learner
Alg
whose decisions over rounds are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{T}
, each belonging to
K
. In turn, these points are sampled from the probability measures induced by the densities
p_{1},\ldots,p_{T}
set by the learner
Alg
across rounds. In the following, for any time
t>0
, we abuse notation and write
x\sim p_{t}
for
x
being sampled from the probability measure induced by
p_{t}
and the base measure
\mu
. Moreover, let
v_{t}={\mathbb{V}}_{x\sim p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x)\right]
be the variance of the function at time
t
according to
p_{t}
, and let
V_{t}=\sum_{\tau\leq t}v_{\tau}
be the cumulative variance up until time
t
.
The (worst-case) expected regret
of an algorithm
Alg
is

\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}})\right]=\sup_{\boldsymbol{f}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x_{t})\right]\;-\;\inf_{x\in K}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x)\right\},

where the expectation is with respect to the learner’s internal
randomness and the probe noise, and the supremum ranges over all
obliviously chosen sequences
\boldsymbol{f}=\{f_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
of admissible
losses bounded in
[0,1]
; for online convex optimization, we restrict to convex losses. Our goal is sublinear regret, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}})\right]=o(T)
, while leveraging at most

k
noisy comparison probes.

1.2
Our Results and Technical Challenges

Our main contribution is to show that even a
sublinear
number of pairwise comparison probes can substantially reduce regret. We unify the two probing paradigms of
9
;
29
into a single framework in which the learner is granted a budget of
k\leq T

pairwise
(possibly noisy) probes over a horizon of
T
rounds, and we analyze the resulting regret tradeoffs for full feedback OCO and prediction with experts.

Regret guarantees.

With
k
pairwise
\delta
-noisy probes, we prove that one can achieve

\textup{{Reg}}_{T}\leq O\!\left(\min\left\{\sqrt{dT\ln T},\;\frac{dT\ln T}{k\,|1-2\delta|}\right\}\right),

(1)

for general convex losses bounded in
[0,1]
(see

Theorem

3.1
). In the special case of experts (i.e., linear
losses over the vertices of the simplex
\Delta^{d-1}
), we can
replace a
factor of
d\ln T
by
\ln d
for both terms of the
\min
in (
1
) to get an improved
bound of

\textup{{Reg}}_{T}\leq O\!\left(\min\left\{\sqrt{T\ln d},\;\frac{T\ln d}{k\,|1-2\delta|}\right\}\right).

(2)

A key takeaway is that the regret bounds of (
2
) match those
obtained in
29
under
best-expert
probes, where
in each queried round the learner effectively “looks ahead” and
learns the globally optimal action
x_{t}^{\star}
. In the special case of
experts
only
, they leverage a crucial property of best-expert
probes: on a probed round, the learner can guarantee the minimum
possible loss, since it knows the expert with lowest loss at that
round. This yields a non-positive (and potentially negative)
contribution to regret during probing rounds. Combined with a standard
Hedge bound on the remaining rounds, this leads to worst-case regret

O\left(\tfrac{T\ln d}{k}\right)
once
k\geq\Omega(\sqrt{T\ln d})
.

Making this argument with pairwise probes runs into a basic
obstacle even just for the special case of experts: to benefit, one
must compare against (or identify) a near-best expert with
sufficiently large probability. One approach to doing this is adapting the arguments from
29
and mix
a uniformly random probe with probes from the distribution of the
Hedge algorithm, to avoid hurting the baseline performance. In
Appendix

B
, we carry over this analysis, attaining suboptimal rates even in the noiseless case. In contrast, our algorithms manage to get the (optimal) bounds in
Equation

2
using only (noisy) pairwise probes, moreover getting the rate in
Equation

1
for general bounded convex losses over an
arbitrary convex body
K
!

Our approach and the main technical insight.

Our main theorem (
Theorem

3.1
)
simultaneously handles (i) general convex losses over general convex bodies, (ii) sublinear probe budgets, and (iii) noisy probes, recovering essentially tight (in terms of
T,k
and
\delta
) bounds on the regret rate. The algorithm is a direct and adaptive probe-augmented version of
continuous
exponential weights: it maintains a density
p_{t}
over
K
and samples points accordingly (e.g.,
10
). This route avoids differential privacy machinery and dispenses with curvature/gradient assumptions
(
9
)
.

For noiseless probing, the key insight is that a pairwise comparison drawn from
p_{t}
yields an advantage that can be measured by the
variance
of the losses under
p_{t}
. Concretely, on a probing round
t
, comparing two i.i.d. samples from
p_{t}
and playing the better one decreases the expected loss by
{\mathbb{V}}_{x\sim p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x)\right]
, the variance of the function according to the density
p_{t}
:

\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\mid t\in Q\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\right]-{\mathbb{V}}_{p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\right].

We then couple this with a variance-based analysis of continuous exponential weights (following the less standard “second-order” style guarantees of
13
), which relates the baseline regret to the same variance term. This alignment is what enables the
\nicefrac{{T}}{{k}}
-type improvement without an additional factor in
d
, nor making assumptions about gradient norms or curvature.

Noisy probes and learning when to trust them.

Noisy probes introduce a further challenge: the noise level
\delta
is unknown, so the learner must also infer whether to trust the probe outcome. We cast this as a lightweight meta-learning layer that, on probed rounds, decides whether to
follow
the probe’s suggestion or
invert
it. Instantiating this meta-problem with an adaptive two-action learner allows us to recover the bound in (
1
) with the correct dependence on
|1-2\delta|
.

Lower bounds.

Finally, we show that the rates in (
2
) are tight in all parameters for prediction with expert advice, and those
in (
1
) are tight in
T,k
and
\delta
(up to logarithmic factors in
T
) for
general convex losses over arbitrary convex sets; we give these
results in
Appendix

A
.

1.3
On the Power of the Adversary and the Definition of Noisy Probes

The protocol above requires the adversary to fix the loss
f_{t}
before the learner decides whether to probe at time
t
. It is crucial that the adversarial loss construction is not coupled with the probing decision. If the adversary could anticipate a probe, she could set
f_{t}\equiv 0
for that round, rendering the probe useless. This would degrade the optimal regret to
\Theta(\sqrt{T-k})
, preventing the faster
\tilde{\Theta}(\nicefrac{{T}}{{k}})
rates we achieve. This constraint distinguishes our work from the “adversarially noisy” probes considered in the literature
(
5
;
7
;
8
;
6
;
9
)
. In those settings, the adversary effectively knows when probing occurs.
For instance,
9
derive a lower bound of
\Omega(\sqrt{T-k})
by constructing an instance where the adversary sets losses to
(0,0)
specifically during noiseless probes (and
(0,1)
vs.
(1,0)
each with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
otherwise). This contradicts our results because their model assumes the adversary has access to the learner’s internal randomization (knowing
when
a probe happens), whereas our model does not.

Related Work

The model of learning-augmented algorithms
(
24
)
has been most recently considered in the context of Online Linear Optimization (OLO) over a convex polytope
(
31
)
. Here, the learner has access to a query mechanism that returns vectors correlated with the true loss vectors. When the optimization domain is the
d
-dimensional unit sphere and the algorithm has full feedback, this additional information enables a reduction in regret from
O(\sqrt{T})
to
O(\ln T)
, even if the learner only receives such hints on
O(\sqrt{T})
rounds
(
5
;
7
;
6
)
. Interestingly, this is not possible with bandit feedback, and a
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
regret is unavoidable even if the hint vectors are well correlated with the true ones
(
8
)
. The same work shows that active queries to specific points at which to evaluate the loss function are enough to guarantee regret rates of the form
O(d^{3/2}\ln T)
, where
d
is the dimension of the ambient space. Their techniques do not directly apply to our setting, since their probes are defined differently from ours and those from
(
9
)
.
Moreover, in subsequent work,
2
show that under bandit feedback, even in the experts setting and with access to best-arm probes, the worst-case regret remains
\Omega(\sqrt{T-k})
.

Regarding pairwise probes for OCO in particular, the closest work to ours is

9

\Theta(Hd+Gd^{3/2})
regret
bound in the full feedback setting when probing is available every

k=T
), under uniform bounds
G,H
on gradient norms and
curvature. Their analysis relies on a differential-privacy-based
reduction to Be-The-Regularized-Leader (BTRL),
and the
dependence on curvature and gradient bounds appears intrinsic to
their arguments, which we avoid using our techniques.

Techniques developed in this work, as well as in prior ones, aim to adapt to structure in the loss sequence during non-probing rounds, leading to regret guarantees that depend on the "easiness" of the instance. These forms of easiness include stochastic losses or small effective loss ranges. In full-information settings, such structure can be leveraged by algorithms like Hedge to achieve regret depending on the easiness of the instance, without degrading worst-case performance
(
11
;
15
;
21
;
23
)
. Another relevant direction is the predictable sequences framework, which focuses on scenarios where the loss sequence follows a regular pattern or is correlated with past observations
(
28
)
. Algorithms in this line adjust their learning dynamics to exploit such predictability and achieve improved regret when it exists
(
35
;
37
)
. Limited supervision has also been studied through the abstention learning framework, where learners may choose not to predict and instead defer to an oracle, thereby trading off prediction effort with access to external feedback
(
22
;
30
;
39
;
12
;
25
;
16
)
.

Our work is also connected to stochastic probing, where the learner acts under uncertainty and can selectively probe the environment to obtain partial feedback
(

. This model has applications in problems like Pandora’s Box
(

(

, it holds that:

\displaystyle\ln\mathbb{E}\left[{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta X\right)\right]\leq-\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2(1-|\eta|)}\cdot{\mathbb{V}}\left[X\right]\qquad\forall\,\eta:|\eta|<1.

To use the above, we define an intermediate normalizer, which uses the same learning rate
\eta_{t}
but after observing
f_{t}
; conventionally, in what follows, we let
\eta_{1}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}=\int_{K}p_{1}(x)\cdot{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}L_{t}(x)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=Z_{t}\cdot\int_{K}p_{t}(x)\cdot{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}f_{t}(x)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=Z_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}f_{t}(x_{t})\right)\right].

Applying Proposition
3.3
to the bounded random variable
f_{t}(x_{t})
with density
p_{t}
, we then obtain:

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}}{Z_{t}}\right)=\ln\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}f_{t}(x_{t})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\eta_{t}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{x_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\right]+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot{\mathbb{V}}_{x_{t}\sim p_{t}}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=-\eta_{t}\cdot\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t}.

Hence, using that
\eta_{t}\leq 1
and rearranging:

\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t}}\ln\left(\frac{Z_{t}}{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}}\right)+\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t}.

Let us now write

\displaystyle Z_{t+1}=\int_{K}p_{1}(x)\cdot{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t+1}L_{t}(x)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{K}p_{1}(x)\cdot{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}L_{t}(x)\right)^{\nicefrac{{\eta_{t+1}}}{{\eta_{t}}}}\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{K}p_{1}(x)\cdot{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{t}L_{t}(x)\right)\mu(dx)\right)^{\nicefrac{{\eta_{t+1}}}{{\eta_{t}}}}

\displaystyle=(\widetilde{Z}_{t+1})^{\nicefrac{{\eta_{t+1}}}{{\eta_{t}}}},

where the inequality follows by Jensen’s inequality since the function
y\mapsto y^{\nicefrac{{\eta_{t+1}}}{{\eta_{t}}}}
is concave in
y
as
\eta_{t+1}\leq\eta_{t}
. Combining the above two displays, we have:

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t}}\ln\left(\frac{Z_{t}}{\widetilde{Z}_{t+1}}\right)+\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t}\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{t}}\ln Z_{t}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t+1}}\ln Z_{t+1}+\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t},

as desired.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
q
be an absolutely continuous density with respect to
p_{1}
. If
\Lambda\geq\max(1,\textup{KL}(q||p_{1}))
, it holds that
Algorithm

1
without probes, referred to as
\textup{{Alg}}_{0}
, satisfies:

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-q,f_{t}\right\rangle\leq 2\Lambda+5\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})}.

Proof.

First, we use
Lemma

3.2
and obtain via a telescopic sum throughout
t
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{1}}\ln Z_{1}-\frac{1}{\eta_{T+1}}\ln Z_{T+1}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t}=-\frac{1}{\eta_{T+1}}\ln Z_{T+1}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t},

where we have observed that
Z_{1}=\int_{K}p_{1}(x)\mu(dx)=1
by definition, so that
\ln Z_{1}=0
. We next bound the two terms separately. Now, for all
q\ll p_{1}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle Z_{T+1}=\int_{x\in K}p_{1}(x){\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{T+1}L_{T}(x)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x\in K:\,q(x)>0}q(x)\frac{p_{1}(x)}{q(x)}{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{T+1}L_{T}(x)\right)\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[{\textup{exp}}\left(-\eta_{T+1}L_{T}(x)-\ln\left(\frac{q(x)}{p_{1}(x)}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq{\textup{exp}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[-\eta_{T+1}L_{T}(x)-\ln\left(\frac{q(x)}{p_{1}(x)}\right)\right]\right),

where the first and third inequalities hold because the integrand is positive and because of Jensen’s inequality, respectively. The expectation is well-defined since
q\ll p_{1}
. We thus have:

-\frac{1}{\eta_{T+1}}\ln Z_{T+1}\leq\frac{1}{\eta_{T+1}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[\eta_{T+1}L_{T}(x)\right]+\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle q,f_{t}\right\rangle+\frac{\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})}{\eta_{T+1}}.

Second, choosing
\eta_{t}=\min\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\sqrt{\tfrac{\Lambda}{1+V_{t-1}}}\right)
, we have that
\tfrac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\leq\eta_{t}
and also (e.g., Lemma 14 in
15
):

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\eta_{t}}{2(1-\eta_{t})}\cdot v_{t}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\eta_{t}v_{t}\leq 1+\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{v_{t}}{\sqrt{1+V_{t-1}}}

\displaystyle=\,1+\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{v_{t}}{\sqrt{1+V_{t}}}+\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{T}v_{t}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+V_{t-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+V_{t}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{v_{t}}{\sqrt{1+V_{t}}}+\sqrt{\Lambda}\cdot\sum_{t=2}^{T}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+V_{t-1}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+V_{t}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sqrt{\Lambda}\left(1+\sum_{t=2}^{T}\frac{v_{t}}{\sqrt{1+V_{t}}}\right)\leq 1+\sqrt{\Lambda}\left(1+\int_{V_{1}}^{V_{T}}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+3\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})}.

Combining the derivations above, we obtain, using our assumption on
\Lambda\geq\max(1,\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1}))
:

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t}-q,f_{t}\right\rangle\leq\frac{\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})}{\eta_{T+1}}+1+3\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})}\leq 2\Lambda+5\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})},

as desired.
∎

3.2
Loss during probing rounds

We decompose the loss for probing rounds in two components: (i) the loss assuming
\delta
is known (
Lemma

3.5
)—corresponding to the loss suffered by the superior action between
F
and
I
; and (ii) the excess regret incurred relative to that superior action (
Lemma

3.6
).

In the subsequent lemmas, we denote the instantaneous losses of actions
F
and
I
by
\ell_{t}(F)
and
\ell_{t}(I)
, and their cumulative losses over the rounds in
Q
by
L_{Q}(F)
and
L_{Q}(I)
, respectively. These losses are random variables contingent on the sampling of the outer algorithm and the Bernoulli noise variables governing the probe responses (
Definition

1.1
). To avoid conditioning on
Q
before it is needed, we extend the definition of
\ell_{t}(F)
and
\ell_{t}(I)
to rounds
t\notin Q
by considering the necessary Bernoulli noise and pairs
y_{t,1},y_{t,2}
, which are completely excluded from the learning protocol.

Lemma 3.5
.

The following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\left[\ell_{t}(F)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-(1-2\delta)v_{t}\quad\text{if}\ \delta\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\left[\ell_{t}(I)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle+(1-2\delta)v_{t}\quad\text{if}\ \delta>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

Therefore, accounting for the choice of
Q\subseteq[T]
, the expected cumulative loss accumulated by the better of the two actions
F
and
I
is:

\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}\leq\frac{k}{T}\cdot\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-|1-2\delta|\cdot V_{T}\right).

Proof.

First, assume
\delta\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. By definition, we have that the action
“follow the oracle”
incurs loss:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p_{t}}\left[\ell_{t}(F)\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{K\times K}p_{t}(u)p_{t}(y)\cdot(1-\delta)\cdot\min(f_{t}(u),f_{t}(y))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\delta\cdot\max(f_{t}(u),f_{t}(y))\,\mu(du)\,\mu(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{K\times K}p_{t}(u)p_{t}(y)\cdot\frac{f_{t}(u)+f_{t}(y)-(1-2\delta)\cdot|f_{t}(u)-f_{t}(y)|}{2}\,\mu(du)\,\mu(dy)

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-(1-2\delta)\cdot\int_{K\times K}p_{t}(u)p_{t}(y)\cdot\frac{(f_{t}(u)-f_{t}(y))^{2}}{2}\,\mu(du)\,\mu(dy)

\displaystyle=\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-(1-2\delta)\cdot\left(\int_{K}p_{t}(u)f^{2}_{t}(u)\,\mu(du)-\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-(1-2\delta)v_{t}.

Summing over
t\in Q
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\,|\,Q\right]\leq\sum_{t\in Q}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-(1-2\delta)\cdot\sum_{t\in Q}v_{t}
. Similarly, if
\delta>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, we have, via a derivation almost identical to the one above:

\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\,|\,Q\right]\leq\sum_{t\in Q}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle+(1-2\delta)\sum_{t\in Q}v_{t}
, thus getting a bound for the action
“invert the oracle”
.
Hence, taking another expectation over
Q
and combining the two cases depending on the sign of
1-2\delta
, we get:

\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}\leq\frac{k}{T}\cdot\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-|1-2\delta|\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T}v_{t}\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

The
Meta-Learner
has expected cumulative loss bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Meta-Learner}})\right]\leq\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}+3.

Proof.

First, recall that the
Meta-Learner
runs Hedge with adaptive learning rate (i.e., AdaHedge from
13
run on the two actions
F,I
and for rounds
Q\subseteq[T]
only). For any
t\in Q
, let
\tilde{p}_{t,F},\tilde{p}_{t,I}
be the probabilities maintained by the
Meta-Learner
over
F,I
. Similarly, let
\tilde{v}_{t}
be the instantaneous variance induced by
\tilde{p}_{t,F},\tilde{p}_{t,I}
of the
Meta-Learner
, i.e.,
\tilde{v}_{t}=\tilde{p}_{t,F}\tilde{p}_{t,I}(\ell_{t}(F)-\ell_{t}(I))^{2}
, and let
\tilde{V}_{Q}=\sum_{t\in Q}\tilde{v}_{t}
. Observe that

\tilde{v}_{t}=\tilde{p}_{t,F}\tilde{p}_{t,I}(\ell_{t}(F)-\ell_{t}(I))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}(\ell_{t}(F)-\ell_{t}(I))^{2}.

Also note that
\lvert\ell_{t}(F)-\ell_{t}(I)\rvert=\lvert f_{t}(y_{t,1})-f_{t}(y_{t,2})\rvert=\Delta_{t}
, which means that
\tilde{V}_{Q}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{t\in Q}\Delta^{2}_{t}
. In particular, since
y_{t,1}
and
y_{t,2}
are i.i.d. and taking the difference outputs a centered random variable,
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in Q}\Delta_{t}^{2}\,|\,Q\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}2v_{t}\mathbbm{I}\left\{t\in Q\right\}
, where
v_{t}
is the variance at
t
of the original learning task.

In addition, Theorem 6 in
(
13
)
gives a regret of the
Meta-Learner
(AdaHedge) with respect to the better of
F,I
of at most, letting
\mathcal{G}
be the sigma-algebra generated by
Q
, the noise variables and the sampling from
p_{t}
:

\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Meta-Learner}})-\min\{L_{Q}(F),L_{Q}(I)\}\,|\,\mathcal{G}\right]\leq 2\sqrt{\tilde{V}_{Q}\ln 2}+\frac{4\ln 2}{3}+2\leq 2\sqrt{\tilde{V}_{Q}}+3.

Combining the above and taking expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Meta-Learner}})\,|\,\mathcal{G}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\min\{L_{Q}(F),L_{Q}(I)\}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{\tilde{V}_{Q}}\right]+3

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{V}_{Q}\right]}+3

\displaystyle\leq\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in Q}\Delta_{t}^{2}\,\Bigg|\,Q\right]\right]}+3

\displaystyle=\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+\sqrt{\sum_{t=1}^{T}2v_{t}{\mathbb{P}}\left[t\in Q\right]}+3

\displaystyle=\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}+3,

where the second inequality holds by using Jensen’s inequality twice, and the second-to-last equality by using standard properties of conditional expectations.
∎

3.3
Proving the regret bound

We can finally prove the claimed regret bound from
Theorem

3.1
, and we start by bounding term (A) in
Equation

3
using the results from the previous two subsections.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
q
be a probability density function on the domain
K
, such that
q\ll p_{1}
.
Then, for an unknown noise parameter
\delta\geq 0
and for any sequence of measurable functions
f_{t}
bounded in
[0,1]
,
Algorithm

1
guarantees, provided
\Lambda\geq\max(1,\textup{KL}(q||p_{1}))
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle q,f_{t}\right\rangle\leq\min\left(12\sqrt{\Lambda T},\frac{25\Lambda T}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)+4.

Proof.

Let us decompose the loss suffered by
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
into probing and non-probing rounds:

\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T\setminus Q}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right].

In particular, note that the loss suffered by
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
during the probing rounds is equal to that of the
Meta-Learner
. Since we have the same type of update to
p_{t}
in both probing and non-probing rounds, we have that
\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T\setminus Q}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\,|\,Q\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbbm{I}\left\{t\notin Q\right\}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}(x_{t})\right]
, and since
Q
is uniform:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T\setminus Q}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{k}{T}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle.

By
Lemma

3.5
and
Lemma

3.6
, it also holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(\textup{{Meta-Learner}})\right]\leq\min\{\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(F)\right],\mathbb{E}\left[L_{Q}(I)\right]\}+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}+3

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k}{T}\cdot\left(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle-|1-2\delta|\cdot V_{T}\right)+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}+3.

Therefore, combining the two above displays, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle p_{t},f_{t}\right\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}+3-\frac{k|1-2\delta|}{T}\cdot V_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle q,f_{t}\right\rangle+2\Lambda+\underbrace{5\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})}+\sqrt{\frac{2k}{T}V_{T}}}_{\leq 7\sqrt{\Lambda(1+V_{T})}}+3-\frac{k|1-2\delta|}{T}\cdot V_{T}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle q,f_{t}\right\rangle+\min\left(10\sqrt{\Lambda T},\frac{23\Lambda T}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)+4+2\Lambda,

where the second inequality holds by
Lemma

3.4
, while the third because
V_{T}\leq T
and the maximizing
V_{T}
is
V_{T}=\tfrac{49T^{2}\Lambda}{4k^{2}(1-2\delta)^{2}}-1
. To get the final bound for the first argument of the minimum, notice that
\Lambda\leq\sqrt{\Lambda T}
if
\Lambda\leq T
, while if
\Lambda>T
, then the loss is trivially bounded by
T<\sqrt{\Lambda T}
. The bound for the second argument follows since
\nicefrac{{T}}{{k\lvert 1-2\delta\rvert}}\geq 1
, with the convention of setting it to
\infty
when
\delta=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
∎

We now prove
Theorem

3.1
by removing the dependency of the previous analysis on
q
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.1
.

First, define
S(x^{\star},r)=(1-r)x^{\star}+rK
, for a generic
x^{\star}\in K
. Now fix any
t\in[T]
and let
q
be the uniform density over
S(x^{\star},r)
, i.e.
q(x)=\frac{1}{\text{Vol}(S(x^{\star}\!,r))}\mathbbm{I}\left\{x\in S(x^{\star},r)\right\}
: the base measure in
Algorithm

1
is thus in this case the Lebesgue measure. By definition of Minkowski sum, for any
y\in S(x^{\star},r)
there exists
x\in K
such that
y=(1-r)x^{\star}+rx
.
By convexity of
f_{t}
,

f_{t}(y)\leq(1-r)f_{t}(x^{\star})+rf_{t}(x)\leq(1-r)f_{t}(x^{\star})+r,

since
f_{t}(x)\leq 1
. Rearranging then gives
f_{t}(y)-f_{t}(x^{\star})\leq r
for all
y\in S(x^{\star},r)
. This addresses the (B) term in
Equation

3
.

To address the (A) term, notice that
q\ll p_{1}
and
\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})=d\ln(\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})=\Lambda
, since:

\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})=\ln\left(\frac{\text{vol}(K)}{\text{vol}(S(x^{\star},r))}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{\text{vol}(K)}{r^{d}\text{vol}(K)}\right)=d\ln\left(\frac{1}{r}\right).

Plugging the equality in the bound from
Lemma

3.7
, then yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\min\left(12\sqrt{dT\ln(\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})},\frac{25dT\ln(\nicefrac{{1}}{{r}})}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)+4+rT,

and setting
r=\nicefrac{{1}}{{T}}
gives the claimed bound.
∎

3.4
Prediction with Expert Advice

In the prediction with expert advice setting,

Equation

3
and
Lemma

3.7
imply a regret bound,
with the proof relying on the fact that the convexity of domains and functions was only used when proving
Theorem

3.1
. We thus have to appropriately switch to the discrete setting: the domain is finite, where the experts can be seen as the
d
vertices of
\Delta^{d-1}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid x_{i}\geq 0\,\forall\,i,\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}=1\}
, the
(d-1)
-dimensional simplex. Recall that its set of vertices is
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
.
2
2

2

We stress that we are using this notation for finite sets of points to match the notation used in the lower bounds in
Appendix

A
.

Theorem 3.8
.

Consider the problem of prediction with expert advice with full feedback and
k
pairwise comparison probes, with the domain
K=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
.
Then, for an unknown noise parameter
\delta\geq 0
and for any sequence of functions
\{f_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
in
[0,1]
, when instantiated with
p_{1}
being the mass function of the uniform distribution over
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
and
\Lambda=\ln d
,
Algorithm

1
has regret

\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]\leq O\left(\min\left(\sqrt{T\ln d},\frac{T\ln d}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)\right).

Proof.

For this result, the base measure of
Algorithm

1
is the counting measure over
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
, while
p_{1}
is the probability mass function of the uniform distribution over the same set. Consider the mass function
q(x)=\mathbbm{I}\left\{x=x^{\star}\right\}
, thus corresponding to a Dirac on
x^{\star}
, here the minimizing expert, so that
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[f_{t}(x)\right]=f_{t}(x^{\star})
, and term (B) in
Equation

3
is null. Clearly,
q\ll p_{1}
. For term (A), we have:

\textsc{KL}(q||p_{1})=\sum_{x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}}q(x)\cdot\ln\left(\frac{q(x)}{p_{1}(x)}\right)=\ln d.

Therefore, since we set
\Lambda=\ln d
,

Lemma

3.7
gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]-\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(x^{\star})\leq\min\left(12\sqrt{T\ln d},\frac{25T\ln d}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)+4,

which was to be shown.
∎

4
Concluding Remarks

In this work, we analyze Online Convex Optimization (OCO) with a sublinear budget of pairwise probes. This generalizes
9
, which requires pairwise probes at
every
step, as well as
29
, which focuses on the
stronger
best-expert probes. Our main finding is that pairwise probes are as informative as best-expert probes and that our algorithms are robust to noise. In
Theorem

3.1
, we establish the following regret rates, which are tight in
T,k
and
\delta
up to logarithmic factors in
T
, and for experts completely tight also in
d
:

\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]\leq O\left(\min\left(\sqrt{dT\ln T},\frac{dT\ln T}{k|1-2\delta|}\right)\right).

Among the remaining open questions, one concerns understanding the benefit of probes in the special case of exp-concave functions, as opposed to general convex ones. Another relates to the trade-off between computational efficiency and improved regret: our main CEW algorithm requires computing an integral over a convex set, which is hard in general. It would therefore be interesting to develop efficient algorithms that achieve comparable guarantees.

Lemma A.3

\frac{0.4690}{\sqrt{T\xi(1-\xi)}}\ \leq\ \frac{\ln d}{8\sqrt{\pi}\,d}.

(5)

Then

(6)

The proof proceeds in steps:

Step 1.

Hedge keeps over experts is not affected by earlier probes or earlier decisions taken by the algorithm. Let
z_{t}
denote the point/expert played by the algorithm in (probing or non-probing) round
t
. During non-probing rounds
t\in\overline{Q}=\{1,\dots,T\}\setminus Q
, the expected loss
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
suffers is

\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\mid t\in\overline{Q}\right]=\sum_{x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}}p_{t}(x)\cdot f_{t}^{\top}x.

Moreover, in rounds
t\in Q
,
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
probes the expert of minimum loss with probability
\nicefrac{{1}}{{d}}
. In the event that the expert of minimum loss is not probed, we have that, in expectation, the algorithm
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
suffers at most the loss
\textup{{Alg}}_{0}
(Hedge) would have suffered. Hence, the expected instantaneous loss of
\textup{{Alg}}_{k}
at round
t\in Q
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\mid t\in Q\right]\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\sum_{x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}}p_{t}(x)\cdot f_{t}^{\top}x+\frac{f_{t}^{\top}x^{\star}_{t}}{d}.

Summing over all
t
, by linearity of expectation, we have
that the expected loss of the algorithm is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\right]=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\cdot\mathbbm{I}\left\{t\in\overline{Q}\right\}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\cdot\mathbbm{I}\left\{t\in Q\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\mid t\in\overline{Q}\right]\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left[t\in\overline{Q}\right]+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[f_{t}^{\top}z_{t}\mid t\in Q\right]\cdot{\mathbb{P}}\left[t\in Q\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{T}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}}p_{t}(x)\cdot f_{t}^{\top}x

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{k}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\right)\sum_{x\in\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}}p_{t}(x)\cdot f_{t}^{\top}x+\frac{f_{t}^{\top}x^{\star}_{t}}{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{k}{dT}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[L_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{0})\right]+\frac{k}{dT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}^{\top}x^{\star}_{t}.

Combining the above with
Equation

17
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{dT}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\textup{{Reg}}_{T}(\textup{{Alg}}_{0})\right]-\frac{k}{dT}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}^{\top}(x^{\star}-x^{\star}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{dT}\right)\frac{\ln d}{\eta(1-\eta)}+\left(\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}-\frac{k}{dT}\right)\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}^{\top}(x^{\star}-x^{\star}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{4\sqrt{T\ln d},\frac{2Td\ln d}{k}\right\},

by assuming
T\geq 4\ln d
and choosing
\eta=\max\left(\sqrt{\frac{\ln d}{T}},\frac{k}{dT+k}\right)
, since
\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}^{\top}(x^{\star}-x^{\star}_{t})\leq T
. If
T<4\ln d
, the regret guarantee is satisfied since
\eta=\frac{k}{dT+k}
and the regret is bounded by
T
.
∎
