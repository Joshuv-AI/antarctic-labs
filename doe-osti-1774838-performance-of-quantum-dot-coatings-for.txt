 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 The two major outcomes from this DOE supported collaboration between UbiQD and NREL were:
 1) an expert analysis/modeling of the expected performance and cost of luminescent solar
 concentrating windows with quantum dot coatings, and 2) critical R&D characterization of
 materials and device performance analysis using equipment and processes that are too expensive
 for UbiQD to perform in-house. The latter also included third-party validation of the device
 performance with an NREL-certified conversion efficiency that was ultimately published. (see
 ACS Energy Lett. 2018 and recently ACS Appl. Energy Mater. 2020).

 Summary Introduction:

 UbiQD, Inc. is an advanced materials company based in Los Alamos, NM that is powering product
 innovation with quantum dots. The company licenses and holds core intellectual property related
 to large scale synthesis of non-toxic quantum dots (QDs), glass-based luminescent solar
 concentrators (LSCs), and electricity-generating solar windows (among others). Integration of
 photovoltaic (PV) energy generation into fenestration is currently a very active area of research.
 Multiple approaches are being taken to develop potential products in this area.

 Figure 1 Above: Published Schematic of UbiQD’s proprietary QD- LSC technology. Below: photo of
                                           ~1m2 QD-LSC.

 The majority of approaches to integration of PV within window technologies require construction
 of the full PV device actually on the window glass similar to current dynamic (electrochromic)
 window products. This can create technical as well as building integration challenges including
 the need for substantial R&D investments in the development of new materials. UbiQD’s approach
 is differentiated from other approaches in that it does not require the entire PV device to be
 integrated into the window glass. UbiQD uses a layer of QD materials embedded in a polymer
 matrix and laminated between two glass lites to absorb and re-direct solar energy to PV devices
 integrated into the window frame (see Figure 1). This can greatly simplify integration at the
 product level by allowing UbiQD to produce units through known present manufacturing channels
 and processes. This effort included three tasks that NREL conducted on UbiQD’s behalf under the
 EERE Small Business Voucher Program. Those tasks are listed, and results are summarized below.
 The results of this study were also summarized in a peer reviewed ACS Energy Letters paper. This
 publication is cited in the reference section below.

 Task 1: Assist UbiQD in Modeling of System
 Performance

 The performance potential of the LSC technology in actual building models was simulated using
 NREL’s OpenStudio building energy modeling software, which leverages EnergyPlus for physics-
 based, climatic building energy simulation. An integrated photovoltaic calculator was used to
 estimate power production, and the OpenStudio Server programmatically generated the climate
 zone-specific, code-compliant building models, and reported the results. The baseline solar/visible
 transmittance was climate zone and code dependent, which was then modified by addition of the
 LSC device. The 50% VLT LSC technology was modeled on windows by covering 50% of the
 glazed area with the PVs. The baseline solar heat gain coefficients were not modified in this
 simulation, and therefore no thermal benefit/hindrance was introduced by the LSCs in this model.
 The model included North, South, East, West facing sides, various building sizes, various window-
 to wall ratios, and other parameters. It was possible to predict how the production varied in
 different locations and times of day or year (Figure 2, top). The weather inputs were from actual
 weather in 2009, showing the effect of cloudy days as well (Figure 2, bottom).In order to determine
 if a target power production of 50 W/m2 in direct sunlight (~5%) is a viable benchmark to enable
 a short payback for tall buildings, we modeled the power generation of various heights of tall
 buildings equipped with LSCs for 28 different cities globally. The 50W/m2 dropped to about 20
 W/m2 when considering the movement of the sun (on average). To model the annual electricity
 production of these buildings, we assumed that the windows were equipped with 5% efficient solar
 cells that transmitted 50% of the solar spectrum. For each building, we varied the number of stories
 and the percentage of the glass on the building façade and calculated the annual electricity
 production using the “typical meteorological year” weather data (2009) for each city’s
 representative climate zone (Figure 3). The results from this simulation show that at a target output
 of 50 W/m2 an 80-story building could produce over 1 GWh of electricity annually, which would
 offset ∼$200K in annual electricity for a building located in New York, for example, enough to
 power roughly 350 apartments.

 Task 2: Estimation of System Costs and Energy Payback Time

 NREL has been modeling U.S. PV system costs since 2009. For this project, NREL created a cost
 model for UbiQDs system based on similar rooftop systems (including the balance of systems and
 indirect costs, see Figure 4). Overall, the additional costs beyond the window added cost (above a
 traditional window) were close to 50% of the total capital cost. Our methodology includes bottom-
 up accounting for all system and project- development costs incurred when installing the systems.
 We attempt to model best-in-class installation techniques and business operations from an
 installed-cost perspective. Critically, our quantitative models also provide a rigorous analytical
 basis for estimating the techno-economic impacts and cost- performance tradeoffs of different
 system installation technologies and techniques. Costs are represented from the perspective of the
 developer/installer; thus, all hardware costs represent the price at which components are purchased
 by the developer/installer. Also, the benchmark represents the sales price paid to the installer;
 therefore, it includes profit in the cost of the hardware and the profit the installer/developer
 receives, as a separate cost category. It does not include any additional net profit, such as a
 developer fee or price gross-up, which are common in the marketplace, however, it did include a
 4% contingency cost. Figure 5 details modification of existing PV-based economic models to the
 UbiQD photovoltaic window technology. A financed project was modeled for a 40-50 story
 building that would produce roughly 1.25MW. A 30% Federal ITC tax credit and 5-yr accelerated
 MACRS depreciation was included. The ultimate payback time was estimated at roughly 4.5 years
 as seen in Figure 6. This compares to a typical payback time of about 4 years for a utility-scale
 solar farm and about 7-10 years for a residential rooftop system.

 Task 3 Augment Company’s Materials and Device Characterization Capabilities

 This task had three major focus areas. The first was to provide access to analytical instruments
 such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM) that is available at NREL in order to perform
 characterization of UbiQD’s QD materials. The second was an identification of the fastest non-
 radiative and radiative processes measured in the near infrared (NIR) for CuInS 2 QDs that emit
 around 850 nm. The third was general characterization of LSC prototypes. Of these efforts, the
 second one (spectroscopy) was not pursued based on UbiQD’s request. This spectroscopy portion
 of the task was replaced by durability analysis of LSC devices based on industry recognized testing
 parameters.

 Much of the work conducted under this task focused on analysis of the UbiQD QD materials via
 transmission electron microscopy (TEM) as this equipment is not currently possessed by UbiQD.
 An example TEM image of UbiQD materials is shown in Figure 7. TEM was employed to
 characterize the size, shape and crystallinity of UbiQD’s QD materials. The image in Figure 7
 shows various QD structures with particles sizes below 10 nm as well as exhibiting crystalline
 ordering. Additional analytical support was provided as a part of the efforts described below and
 further data is presented there including optical characterization and energy conversion
 performance characterization.

 To characterize the electrical photoconversion efficiency (PCE) of UbiQD devices, polycrystalline
 Si solar cells manufactured by Aoshike, having an estimated PCE of 11% (for Air Mass 1.5 solar
 illumination), were attached to the perimeter of the QD-LSC glass laminate using an optical epoxy
 and wired in series. The completed device, which had active area of 10 cm Å~ 10 cm (99.73 cm2

 7

 as defined by a metal mask) was then sent to the NREL in order to measure a certified PCE as well
 as the device’s external quantum efficiency (EQE). The current vs voltage response and EQE were
 measured at 25 °C under an ASTM G173 global spectrum, which had an irradiance of 1000 W/m2.
 The PCE and EQE were then measured for the sample in two different configurations: first, a
 nonreflective black absorbing substrate was placed beneath the LSC to make sure the light only
 made a single pass through the partially transparent LSC. This configuration would mimic an
 insulating glass unit that does not have any other coatings on the inside of a window, and the QDs
 would only absorb the incident sunlight. The second configuration used a mirror beneath the LSC
 in order to simulate a low-e coating that most commercial IGUs have on an inner surface of a
 double-pane window. Low-e coatings are regularly used to control the heat gain through the
 window by reflecting NIR light (typically wavelengths > 750nm) incident on the window, but
 because most low-e coatings are thin films of metal, some of the visible light is reflected as well.
 This light can then be absorbed by the LSC and boost its performance. The I−V curves and
 respective EQE curves are shown in Figure 5a and 5b, respectively. A measured certified PCE for
 the nonreflective substrate is 2.18% and increases to 2.94% when the reflective substrate is used.
 These PCE values indicate that the device could produce ∼22−30 W/m2 (with a VLT of 44%),
 depending on what additional coatings were on the IGU.

 Figure 8. Current vs voltage curves for certified UbiQD laminated LSC devices with and without a
 reflective substrate present for the original certification.

 The current vs. voltage measurements were made using NREL’s Abet solar simulator and represent
 the first-ever PCE-certified solar windows. The spectrum of the solar simulator and intensity are
 set using a spectroradiometer, primary reference cells and spectral mismatch correction factors to
 give the electrical performance under the Global Reference Spectrum (IEC 60904). The sample
 temperature was held at ~25°C during the measurements. Quantum efficiency (QE) was measured
 at four locations on the device and then averaged. QE was largely independent of light bias level,
 but some calculated spectral mismatch values varied from 0.3% to 1.5%. Consequently, a 1.5%
 relative error was added to the error calculations for ISC (short circuit current), JSC (short circuit
 current density), and fill factor derived from the I-V curves.

 Figure 9. Current vs voltage curves for the final set of certified UbiQD laminated LSC devices.

 Before the conclusion of the CRADA project the company got a second set of samples that were
 roughly 2x larger certified at NREL. The champion device had a performance of 3.6% (Figure 9),
 showing an improvement of more than 20% relative over the first batch of smaller test devices.
 The results were only just published in August of 2020 in ACS Applied Energy Materials. As far
 as we know this is the highest PCE for an LSC device ever reported at this size scale, and one of
 the few certified solar windows ever reported. Progress on performance was largely due to the
 DOE’s support because material improvements translated into device performance.

 Durability performance of example UbiQD LSC devices was assessed based on a modification of
 the ASTM E-2141/E-2953 method developed by NREL to assess durability of dynamic windows.
 While this test was not specifically designed for performance assessment of PV windows, it often
 serves as an initial testing condition for a variety of emerging fenestration technologies and, hence,
 was applied in this situation. UbiQD provided multiple samples of QD embedded interlayer
 materials laminated between two sheets of glass for testing. Optical properties of these samples
 were characterized upon receipt and samples were placed in an Atlas Ci5000 testing chamber and
 exposed to simulated 1 sun illuminance at 70°C and ambient relative humidity. Samples were
 removed from exposure at varying time intervals and performance was again tested for comparison
 to initial results. Testing was conducted up to a total of 3000 hours of exposure. Potential device
 refinement points were identified in this study and in combination with guidance from industry
 partners have helped guide UbiQD in improved materials choices moving forward.

 Summary Conclusion:

 In conclusion, the analysis conducted under this project indicates that laminated glass QD LSCs
 for electricity generation on building façades are an attractive means to realize net-zero power
 consumption for modern cities. Energy modeling indicated that application of proposed UbiQD

 9

 LSC devices at a target output of 50 W/m2 an 80-story building could produce over 1 GWh of
 electricity annually, which would offset ∼$200K in annual electricity for a building located in
 New York, for example. The PV balance-of-systems and indirect costs were found to be near 50%
 of the total costs. We modeled financing the project with a 30 year loan, and based on asset
 financing assumptions, a reasonable 4.5 year payback time was estimated. However, the then-
 current 30% solar ITC tax benefit was assumed in this estimation, but it is now at 26% and
 dropping to 22% in 2021. The QD-LSC prototypes enabled by high-quantum yield, near infrared-
 emitting CuInS2/ZnS QDs were originally demonstrated to have a record high optical efficiency
 of 8.1% and certified champion PCE of 2.9%. Near the end of the project, the company certified
 new and improved devices with a champion PCE of 3.6% showing a ~20% improvement, and a
 new record performance. Both results were published, the first in 2018 and the second more
 recently in 2020. Materials studies verified the properties of UbiQD QD materials as well as
 provided critical insight toward the delivery of a durable product solution. With further
 optimization, laminated glass LSCs have the potential to disrupt the urban energy landscape by
 offering seamless low-cost integration of energy production into the façades of buildings.
