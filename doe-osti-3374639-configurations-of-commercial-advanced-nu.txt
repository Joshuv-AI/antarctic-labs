 Executive Summary
 This report is the second deliverable 1 of a series of reports set forth by the Department of Energy
 (DOE), led by the American Bureau of Shipping (ABS), for the research award, “Accelerating
 Commercial Maritime Demonstration Projects for Advanced Nuclear Reactor Technologies.” This
 report highlights the technical, regulatory, and economic feasibility of nuclear-maritime
 configurations.

 This report is based on the expertise of the American Bureau of Shipping, the National Reactor
 Innovation Center, and all supporting parties. The configurations that drive the integration of
 modern reactor technologies and the commercial maritime sectors have been a long-standing
 question in both communities. This report attempts to lay out frameworks by which this can be
 attempted. The scope of the report is laid out in three separate sections. The first section establishes
 some identified applicable nuclear systems suitable for use in a maritime environment and the
 criteria for maritime use of nuclear energy. The second section deep dives into the regulatory
 landscape for reactor technology in the US and how it could integrate maritime considerations into
 the legislation. Finally, the third section considers the business case for commercial nuclear maritime
 and presents key economic evaluations for this use case. Additionally, this section presents
 configuration options for nuclear-maritime applications and the technoeconomic recommendations
 that stem from this.

 This report looked at the nuclear-maritime framework from a broad perspective. Future research
 should focus on narrowing the scope of the nuclear-maritime configuration into multiple output
 deliverables. Work of this sort can expand from a high level (e.g. section by section) or look at
 individualized aspects from the report at a deeper level (e.g. reviewing applicability of specific
 reactor technology to existing US legislation). Additionally, further work should be undertaken in
 regard to technoeconomic evaluation of maritime-nuclear feasibility as the reactor technology
 matures and more applications are identified as candidates for nuclear integration.

 Continuing work of this sort will address the ongoing questions in the maritime and nuclear
 communities regarding vessel and unit costs, navigation through the U.S. regulatory landscape, and
 overall feasibility of nuclear power as a possible alternative energy to achieve energy independence.
 Getting ahead of planning should include further collaboration with U.S. regulators and the
 American Bureau of Shipping to facilitate a more streamlined approach for new designs and for long
 term fleet considerations. Overall, there is momentum to continue discovery of the implications of
 installation of nuclear power into maritime systems, at this point in time, and capitalization is more
 possible now than it has been in decades.

 In addition, this work supports the recently issued nuclear Executive Orders (EOs). Specifically, EO
 14299, “Deploying Advanced Nuclear Reactor Technologies for National Security”, and EO 14300,
 “Ordering the Reform of the Nuclear Regulatory Commission,” by ensuring the rapid development,
 deployment, and use of advanced nuclear technologies; and increasing the deployment of new
 nuclear reactor technologies, such as Generation III+ and IV reactors, modular reactors, and
 microreactors to support America leading the commercialization of affordable and abundant nuclear
 energy.

 1.3 Background
 In recent years, industrial, social, financial, and political interests
 have shifted to reconsider nuclear power as a solution for
 meeting various energy demands. Advanced nuclear
 technologies show promise for enabling global energy
 innovation efforts and national energy independence, with the
 expected decline of oil reserves driving the adoption of resilient,
 reliable, and secure forms of energy.

 As discussed in the Road Map Report and shown in Figure 1, there
 are many potential nuclear-maritime applications. This report
 elaborates on these by considering their technical feasibility,
 regulatory complexity, and market value.

 1.3.1 The Goal of Demonstration: Feasibility
 The primary goal of a demonstration is to showcase the feasibility of
 a proposed plan, project, or idea. A feasible demonstration results
 in a successful and fully adoptable/implementable solution
 composed of technical, regulatory, and economic aspects. However,
 many complexities are related to feasibility, including aspects of
 public perception, as seen in Figure 2, which are further defined
 below for the purpose of this report.

 Technical feasibility focuses on implementing a technical or
 engineering solution. This includes the ability to meet functional
 criteria and engineering constraints. In this report, technical
 feasibility considerations are discussed in Part 2.

 Regulatory feasibility concerns the meeting of criteria and
 constraints established by social acceptance, with risk-mitigation
 measures being implemented through required or optional technical
 standards and policies. Regulations primarily create a framework for
 technologies and related processes to show that safety expectations

2.1 Criteria for Operating in Marine
Environments
Land-based nuclear power plant operators have many
years’ experience operating and troubleshooting nuclear
power applications. However, in marine environments,
additional functional criteria may be required in reactor
engineering to meet international standards. First, nuclear
reactors and support systems must adhere to size and
weight requirements to fit the unit without interfering with
primary operations. Floating units such as ships and
offshore structures are highly sensitive to weight and
volume displacement when it comes to maintaining
stability. There may be other size limitations because of
shipyard capacity, towage, or delivery arrangements, and
marine route restrictions such as lock and canal dimensions
or clearance under bridges.

 code only applies to nuclear propulsion plants using pressurized
 LWRs [7], its safety objectives and principles may be adopted or
 interpreted for other reactor types. Other rules may exist; for
 example, rules and guides set up independently by classification
 societies or the International Association of Classification Societies
 (IACS).

 In addition to designing safe, technologically feasible solutions,
 considerations and decisions made in the design phase have major
 implications for economic feasibility. The commercial nuclear vessel
 concepts from the 1950s struggled to gain traction against the
 economic challenges related to the size of the emergency planning
 zone (EPZ) (and related preparation costs at port), unauthorized port
 access without appropriate safety preparations and plans, and the
 competition from oil and gas. Understanding such historic economic
 challenges, modern reactor designs for marine applications must
 incorporate low-cost features and design solutions in order to
 remain economical over the application’s entire lifetime. This will be
 essential to developing competitive applications in the era of
 innovative energy solutions.

 To communicate the technical suitability of a design, risk and safety
 assessments must be performed, including using modern
 probabilistic safety analysis (PSA) techniques. A technology-agnostic
 standard is available from the American Society of Mechanical
 Engineers and the American Nuclear Society (ASME/ANS RA-S-1.4-
 2021) to guide such analyses [8]. PSAs consider all credible initiating
 events that may disrupt normal operations, from mechanical failures
 to events created by external forces such as tsunamis or hurricanes.
 The response of the plant systems, structures, and components (and
 operators, as appropriate) will be traced to one or more end states
 of interest.

 6.3 Nuclear Maritime Technoeconomic Recommendations
 The previous sections provided contextual technoeconomic
 considerations on nuclear for maritime systems. Several tradeoffs
 between technology types, sizes, and configurations were discussed
 qualitatively. Detailed recommendations will likely require more in-
 depth quantitative analyses; however, several observations can
 already be made about the future potential for leveraging nuclear
 technology for maritime applications.

 In the case of fixed systems, nuclear-based synthetic fuel generation
 is gaining traction for various other industrial applications. Offshore
 FNPPs might represent an ideal configuration that provides multiple
 streams of revenue for the nuclear power plant (ability to perform
 electricity arbitrage that can subsidize synthetic fuel) and still
 generate a pathway for energy independence the maritime industry.
 For these applications, SMRs are more ideal (compared to
 microreactors) due to their larger scale. Promising technology
 includes LWRs (due to their maturity) and HTGRs (due to their ability
 to avoid/minimize heat augmentation).

 In the case of mobile systems, microreactor concepts appear more
 applicable due to greater versatility and applicability to a broader
 range of vessel classes. Promising technologies include HTGR and
 HPR microreactors. Deploying more than one of these per vessel can
 improve reliability but minimize costs by pooling common
 infrastructure (e.g., several microreactors with one single control
 room, propeller, etc.).

 Further testing and increased technological maturity may make
 MSRs suitable for various marine applications.

A2/1. Introduction to Nuclear Energy
Modern physics recognizes that the universe is composed of matter.
The building blocks of matter are atoms, which are comprised of
subatomic particles: protons (positively charged), neutrons (carrying
no charge), and electrons (negatively charged). The nucleus at the
atom’s center binds the protons and neutrons within itself via the
“strong nuclear force.” This is the strong interaction that confines Figure 27: Atomic model (©zizou7/Shutterstock)
the protons and neutrons within the nucleus, and which overcomes
the repulsive behavior of like charges between the protons, as
shown in Figure 27.

Fundamentally, nuclear engineering endeavors to harness the
tremendous energy stored in the nucleus, specifically the energy
released when the strong nuclear force is manipulated. This can be
done through nuclear reactions: either fission or fusion, as shown in
Figure 28. Fission occurs when the nucleus of an atom splits into two
or more smaller nuclei. This type of reaction accounts for all nuclear
energy applications at present. Fusion is the opposite process, with
two or more atomic nuclei being combined to form one or more
different atomic nuclei. Fusion still requires extensive R&D, thus it is
not discussed further.

  The findings mirror the ones in the previous subsection but provide
  useful target values for nuclear developers. In general, Scenario M-
  1, without accounting for any tax credits or substantial learning,
  shows that nuclear technology may only struggle to compete if fossil
  fuel prices are lower than the current market averages. This holds
  true for both engine sizes, but is more challenging for smaller
  engines. However, the required breakeven costs in current market
  conditions for high fossil fuel cost conditions exceed the expected
  viable nuclear build costs, highlighting nuclear as a promising
  competitor. Scenario M-2 shows that the impact of an ITC can
  improve the competitiveness of nuclear for both engine sizes
  considered. This improvement pushes the required nuclear costs well
  above what is already realistic to be competitive with current or high
  fossil fuel prices. The presence of an ITC even makes nuclear
  competitive with low fossil fuel costs. Scenario M-3 shows that
  learning rates improve competitiveness even further—in fact, so
  much so that both engine sizes became competitive even at low
  fossil fuel prices. If learning rates of 10% can be achieved, the costs
  for the early demonstration could easily fall in comparison to that of
  plant Vogtle by several factors [143] (the only new nuclear
  construction in the U.S.), and the subsequent 10th unit would be
  competitive even under low fuel costs.
