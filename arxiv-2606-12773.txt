Near-Trinions: Complete Classification of Unital Three-Dimensional Real Associative Algebras
Joel A. Shelton

Abstract
. Frobenius’ theorem settles, definitively, the question of three-dimensional real division algebras: there are none. What it leaves open — and what this paper addresses — is the question of which three-dimensional real associative algebras actually exist. We call these near-trinions and classify them completely, up to ℝ \mathbb{R} -algebra isomorphism. The answer is exactly six isomorphism classes; we provide canonical representatives, explicit multiplication tables, and a collection of module-theoretic invariants sufficient to distinguish every pair. The classification proceeds by stratifying on the radical dimension d d : Wedderburn–Artin resolves the semisimple stratum; in the d = 1 d=1 stratum, the Peirce decomposition locates the radical generator in either a diagonal or off-diagonal corner, producing the commutative and non-commutative branches respectively and ruling out the residue field ℂ \mathbb{C} by a characteristic sign obstruction; and the d = 2 d=2 stratum splits on whether rad ​ ( A ) 2 \mathrm{rad}(A)^{2} vanishes. The argument not only recovers the six classes but explains why the radical dimension takes values in { 0 , 1 , 2 } \{0,1,2\} , why the d = 1 d=1 case splits on commutativity, and why no near-trinion with residue field ℂ \mathbb{C} can exist. One direction for subsequent work is proposed.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The problem and its origin

The sequence of real normed division algebras — the real numbers
\mathbb{R}
,
the complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
, the quaternions
\mathbb{H}
, and the octonions
\mathbb{O}

— occupies dimensions one, two, four, and eight, a pattern that admits
a structural explanation.
Each algebra in the sequence arises from its predecessor by the

Cayley–Dickson construction

[
1
]
, which doubles the dimension at every
step while sacrificing one algebraic property:
\mathbb{R}
is an ordered field;

\mathbb{C}
loses ordering but retains commutativity;
\mathbb{H}
loses commutativity
but retains associativity;
\mathbb{O}
loses associativity but retains the
weaker alternative law.
What this construction cannot produce is an algebra of dimension three.
The doubling mechanism jumps from two to four, and the gap between them
is not a missing algebra in the sequence but a structural impossibility —
one that Frobenius made precise.

Theorem 1.1

(Frobenius, 1878
[
2
]
)
.

The only finite-dimensional associative real division algebras are

\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, and
\mathbb{H}
.

A three-dimensional real division algebra — an object one might
naturally call a
trinion
, by analogy with the Latin sequence

singuli, bini, trini, quaterni
— does not exist.
The proof is short: any such candidate must contain
\mathbb{C}
as a
subalgebra, after which any additional generator
j
must satisfy

j^{2}=-\lambda^{2}
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{*}
, making

(j/\lambda)^{2}=-1
and producing a second copy of
\mathbb{C}
independent
of the first.
The three elements
1
,
i
, and
j/\lambda
are then linearly
independent over
\mathbb{R}
and their product
ij/\lambda
contributes a
fourth, driving the dimension to at least four and leaving no room for
a trinion.

A self-contained presentation of this argument appears in
[
8
]
;
the present paper is, in a sense, the natural sequel.

The question is simply this: Frobenius’ theorem tells us that no
three-dimensional
division
algebra over
\mathbb{R}
exists, but it says
nothing about three-dimensional associative algebras in general.
The class of all such algebras — the
near-trinions
— has exactly
six members, as Theorem
1.2
establishes.

1.2.
What is and is not in the literature

The classification problem for low-dimensional associative algebras has
since accumulated a substantial literature.
The three-dimensional case over
\mathbb{R}
was first addressed by Scheffers

[
7
]
in 1891.
The modern treatment — complete, rigorous, and explicitly over both
\mathbb{R}

and
\mathbb{C}
— appears in Kobayashi, Shirayanagi, Takahasi, and Tsukada

[
3
]
, whose six isomorphism classes agree with
Theorem
1.2
; the correspondence is made explicit in
Proposition
2.5
.

Given this prior work, the contributions of the present paper are
the following.

Framework.

The near-trinion framing gives the classification a narrative coherence
and a connection to the long history of the problem that the earlier
treatments, which approach the question as a routine enumeration, do not provide.

Proof method.

The argument in
[
3
]
is coordinate-based: structure
constants are varied until all isomorphism classes are enumerated.
It produces the correct list but does not explain
why
the list
has the structure it does.
The present argument proceeds through the Jacobson radical stratification
and Peirce decomposition (Sections
2
and

3
).
This approach answers a different question: not only
which
algebras
exist, but
why
the radical dimension
d
takes values in
\{0,1,2\}
,
why the
d=1
case splits on commutativity, and why no near-trinion with
residue field
\mathbb{C}
can exist.
The last point merits proof here, since it drives the entire
d=1
analysis. If
A/\mathrm{rad}(A)\cong\mathbb{C}
and
\mathrm{rad}(A)=\mathbb{R}\eta
for some nonzero
\eta\in\mathrm{rad}(A)
, then
i\cdot\eta=c\eta
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
,
so applying
i
again gives
i^{2}\cdot\eta=c^{2}\eta
. Since
i^{2}=-1\in\mathbb{C}
, we obtain

(-1)\eta=c^{2}\eta
, hence
c^{2}=-1
, impossible over
\mathbb{R}
.
The
d=1
stratum therefore splits into exactly two near-trinions, determined
entirely by whether the radical generator occupies a diagonal or off-diagonal
Peirce corner subalgebra.

2.1.
The Jacobson radical: three characterizations

For finite-dimensional algebras over a field, the Jacobson radical admits
three descriptions that are, in general, distinct objects; the content of
the following proposition is that they coincide.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
A
be a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-algebra.
Define the following subsets of
A
:

(a)

J_{1}(A)=\bigcap\{\mathfrak{m}:\mathfrak{m}\text{ a maximal left ideal of }A\}
;

(b)

J_{2}(A)=
the sum of all nilpotent left ideals of
A
;

(c)

J_{3}(A)=\{a\in A:1-ba\text{ is invertible for all }b\in A\}
.

Proposition 2.2

(Equivalence of characterizations
[
5
]
)
.

For any finite-dimensional
\mathbb{R}
-algebra
A
,

J_{1}(A)=J_{2}(A)=J_{3}(A)
.
This common object is the
Jacobson radical

\mathrm{rad}(A)
,
and it is the unique largest nilpotent two-sided ideal of
A
.

Remark 2.3
.

The three characterizations illuminate different aspects of the radical.

J_{1}
is computed directly in Section
2.4
.

J_{2}
is used in the classification: the radical is nilpotent and
bounded in degree.

J_{3}
provides a practical test:
a\in\mathrm{rad}(A)
if and only if

1-a
is invertible.

Recall that the Loewy length
\ell\ell(A)
is defined in Section 1.4
(Conventions); for
\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{3})
it takes the value
3
, the maximum
possible in dimension three.

Proposition 2.7
.

The maximal left ideals of the six near-trinions, with

to some corner

, meaning
e_{i}\cdot j=j

There are two essentially distinct cases.

Case (a): diagonal placement
(
i=k

Without loss of generality
j\in e_{1}Ae_{1}
, so
e_{1}j=je_{1}=j

and
e_{2}j=je_{2}=0

Here

.
Since
e_{1}Ae_{1}\cong\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})
and

e_{2}Ae_{2}\cong\mathbb{R}
, we obtain
A\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2})
.
This algebra is commutative.

\boxed{A\;\cong\;\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^{2}).}

Hence, Case (a) yields exactly (NT3).

Case (b): off-diagonal placement
(
i\neq k
).
Without loss of generality
j\in e_{1}Ae_{2}
, so

e_{1}j=j

je_{2}=j

The multiplication table on

e_{1}^{2}=e_{1},\quad e_{2}^{2}=e_{2},\quad e_{1}e_{2}=0,\quad e_{1}j=j,\quad je_{2}=j,\quad e_{2}j=je_{1}=j^{2}=0.

Under the identification

,
e_{2}=e_{22}
,

The algebra is

\boxed{A\;\cong\;T_{2}(\mathbb{R}).}

The two cases produce non-isomorphic algebras: (NT3) is commutative and
(NT6) is not.
Thus the subcase
A/\mathrm{rad}(A)\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
yields exactly the two near-trinions
(NT3) and (NT6).

Subcase:

A/\mathrm{rad}(A)\cong\mathbb{C}
:
impossible in dimension three.

If
A/\mathrm{rad}(A)\cong\mathbb{C}
and
\mathrm{rad}(A)=\mathbb{R}\eta

\mathrm{rad}(A)

But then

Hence, the subcase
A/\mathrm{rad}(A)\cong\mathbb{C}
is impossible, and the

3.3.

Here

The product

Subcase:

J^{2}=0

Taking a basis
\{1,\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}\}

\varepsilon_{a}\varepsilon_{b}=0

\boxed{A\;\cong\;\mathbb{R}[\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}]\big/(\varepsilon_{1}^{2},\,\varepsilon_{2}^{2},\,\varepsilon_{1}\varepsilon_{2}).}

The isomorphism class is independent of the choice of basis for
J

any two bases are related by an invertible linear map
\phi\colon J\to J
.
Since
J^{2}=0
, every product of two elements of
J

linear change of basis
J\to J
fixes all products and hence extends (with

1\mapsto 1

\mathbb{R}
-algebra automorphism of
A

Hence, the subcase
J^{2}=0
yields exactly (NT4).

Subcase:

J^{2}\neq 0
, so
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}J^{2}=1
.
Let

Since
x\notin J^{2}=\mathbb{R}x^{2}
and
\dim_{\mathbb{R}}J=2

Since

and nilpotency of

and hence
x^{3}=0

Thus

\boxed{A\;\cong\;\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{3}).}

Any other generator

extends uniquely to an
\mathbb{R}
-algebra

\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^{3})

(x^{\prime})^{3}=0

x^{3}=0
.
Hence, the subcase
J^{2}\neq 0

5.
Directions for Further Work

The classification is specific to finite-dimensional associative unital

\mathbb{R}
-algebras of dimension three, and three features of this setting
are essential. Frobenius’ theorem restricts the Wedderburn–Artin
summands over
\mathbb{R}
to matrix rings over
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{C}
, or
\mathbb{H}
; over

\mathbb{C}
or a finite field the decomposition differs. The effect is already
visible in dimension three:
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{C}
has no analogue over
\mathbb{C}

since
\mathbb{C}
does not split as a non-trivial product of
\mathbb{C}
-algebras,
and accordingly
[
3
]
lists five unital algebras over

\mathbb{C}
versus six over
\mathbb{R}
. Finite-dimensionality underlies both the
Loewy-series bound
d\leq 2
and Nakayama’s Lemma; neither holds in
infinite dimensions. The Jacobson radical and Wedderburn–Artin theorem
presuppose associativity; Lie, Jordan, and octonion algebras require
different methods
[
6
,
4
,
1
]
.

The next problem is the classification over finite fields.
Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, Wedderburn’s little theorem replaces Frobenius’ theorem
in the semisimple stratum, and the cubic extension
\mathbb{F}_{q^{3}}
enters
as a semisimple algebra with no real counterpart.
The sign obstruction used in the
d=1
stratum — the equation

c^{2}=-1
having no real solution — is genuinely specific
to
\mathbb{R}
: over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q\equiv 1\pmod{4}
the equation is
solvable, and the obstruction, if there is one, must be found elsewhere.
