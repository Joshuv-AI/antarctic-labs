4 A Central Plant Retrofit Assessment Guide for Owners

This document offers support to owners considering
optimization upgrades, retrofits, or complete
replacement of a central plant, with a particular
focus on improving energy efficiency and reliability
of the central plant, ultimately leading to reduced
energy costs. It outlines recommended steps to
prepare for and facilitate a thorough central plant
assessment, empowering owners to move forward
with the appropriate next steps. While the primary
focus is on central plants serving commercial
and institutional buildings, the principles and
recommendations presented can also be adapted
for use in other building types, such as industrial or
manufacturing facilities.

How to Use This Resource References
This resource may be used by an energy manager, owner’s representative, facility
manager, building engineer, and/or building operator. Throughout the document, the
user of this resource will be referred to as “the owner.” The following should be kept in
mind when utilizing this resource:
y This document organizes the central plant assessment into four phases, with each
  phase resulting in a discrete outcome to support decision making.
y The activities described in each of the phases may be executed partially or fully by
  internal staff and/or shared with a third-party auditor or consultant for execution.

This resource is organized as follows: the Central Plant Assessment Phases section
describes the four phases of the central plant assessment; the Outputs section
summarizes the outputs for each assessment phase; and the Case Studies section
provides information on published case studies that owners can review for examples
of successful projects.

Overview of a Central Plant
A central plant is a system that provides hot and/or chilled water to a single building
or several buildings. Central plants can have multiple end uses such as space
conditioning, domestic hot water, or process loads. Central plants may also be
referred to as central utility plants (CUP) or central heating and cooling plants and
may be part of a district energy system (DES). Common types of heating and cooling
equipment found in central plants are boilers, chillers, cooling towers, and heat
pumps. Energy storage and backup power systems are commonly used to improve
reliability and for emergency backup needs. Heating and cooling are distributed to
buildings through piping infrastructure, typically using water or steam, and connected
within the buildings to the end-use equipment or process loads.

1.5 Preliminary Data Trending Assessment
To conduct a comprehensive central plant assessment and identify feasible solutions,
it is critical for the consultant to understand all existing loads (e.g., electricity, natural
gas, water, chilled water, hot water, steam, etc.). The owner’s team should prepare for
this by collecting thermal and electrical load metering data at monthly, hourly, or sub-
hourly resolution for a predetermined period (usually one typical year) to capture multiple
seasons. While monthly consumption is easiest to extract from monthly utility bills, hourly
or sub hourly resolution will provide the consultant with a clearer picture of the load
profiles, especially during periods with significantly different operational schedules.

Metering data should be gathered for all major equipment at the central plant and for all
buildings served by the central plant, if available. Hourly thermal load data can often be
sourced from the building management system (BMS) trend data. For electrical loads, data
should be obtained at the highest frequency available, ideally 15-minute intervals, allowing
for more granularity to assess electrical trends at the central plant and utility service level
(if the central plant does not have a dedicated service), and across the buildings served by
the central plant.

2.3 Initial Electrical Load Assessment References
From the electrical trend data collected in Phase 1, the historical peak electrical demands,
typically in kilowatts (kW), should be determined to understand the maximum electrical
use at the central plant and utility service level. Similarly, reviewing coincident peak
electrical demand loads across buildings will help determine which loads are driving the
peak electrical demand at the utility service level. Additionally, the available electrical
capacity can now be determined as the difference between the peak electrical demand
and the incoming service size at the main plant(s).

In Phase 1, the owner may have identified potential increases in demand due to growth
in buildings or building layouts, change in use type and equipment, or from additional
equipment required for reliability and redundancy needs. In these cases, the central
plant electrical demand will likely increase to serve new loads and an electrical service
upgrade may be required. This can be determined by conducting an electrical load study
that follows the NFPA 70 National Electric Code Section 220.87 (National Fire Protection
Association, 2023).

The electrical load study may be completed at this stage or once thermal equipment
solutions have been determined in Phase 3. Conducting the electrical load study at
this stage can provide an electrical capacity budget to work within to avoid increases in
electrical service size or other major electrical upgrades. This can be helpful for prioritizing
certain building efficiency measures that can recapture electrical capacity (if on the same
utility service as the central plant) and for informing central plant solutions.

Thermal Stress Test Outputs
Traditionally, heating systems have been designed to supply high-temperature (≥180°F)
hot water for heating. However, lowering the hot water supply temperature (HWST) allows Case Studies
for more efficient gas systems (e.g., with condensing boilers) and electric heating systems,
along with the use of low-grade waste heat. Even if your system already operates with References
a HWST below 180°F, further reducing the supply temperature can provide additional
efficiency benefits and expand the range of equipment replacement options that can be
considered in later phases.

In order to achieve a lower HWST, it is important to confirm both the capability of the
central plant to achieve lower temperatures and the ability of the buildings being served
by the central plant to provide adequate heating with lower HWST. Optimizing the HWST
before evaluating potential solutions will increase confidence that efficient solutions with
HWST limitations will be feasible without major investments in building equipment and
distribution upgrades. Thermal stress testing is a low-cost method that can either be
completed on all building heating systems or just a sample of them to inform the feasibility
of proposed solutions. Often, buildings are oversized by design and may therefore be able
to meet their heating demands at lower water supply temperatures without needing to
install new coils or expand existing equipment layouts.

2.5 Building-Level Efficiency and Reliability Measures
Prior to evaluating in-depth solutions for the central plant, it is important to assess
reliability measures to reduce operational risk and assess efficiency measures to reduce
the heating, cooling, and electrical loads at the building level. This can help to reduce the
central plant’s thermal loads, avoid upgrades to the incoming electrical service size, and
lower utility costs associated with the central plant. Doing so not only reduces energy
consumption but also enables the purchase of smaller equipment, lowers the required
hot water supply temperatures, and decreases both capital costs and maintenance
needs. This is particularly important in cases with limited building-level energy efficiency
initiatives, a history of deferred maintenance, or high utility rates. Furthermore, building-
level measures can provide critical near-term progress toward portfolio-level efficiency
goals.

The consultant will develop a baseline energy model to represent the existing central

on the building envelope, HVAC systems, ventilation requirements, space use types, space
occupancies, lighting and plug load values, and setpoint and operational schedules.

 Central Plant
