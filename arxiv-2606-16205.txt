Integrability and transformations in the bilinear method: An introduction
Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
This is a partial review of the bilinear method, focusing on the integrability based on the 3-soliton-solution condition and the transformations between τ \tau functions. Key words: bilinear method, Bäcklund transformation, τ \tau function, vexter operator



1
Introduction

In 1971, Ryogo Hirota invented the celebrated bilinear method
[
25
]
.
According his recollection in
[
32
]
and the report by Daisuke Takahashi
[
67
]
,
he was motivated by the research of Morikazu Toda
who found 2-soliton solution (2SS) for the Toda lattice in 1968.
He tried to construct a 3-soliton solution (3SS) for this equation but he failed.
The breakthrough came up in April of 1971: He got a 3SS for the sine-Gordon equation
using the superposition formula (known as the Bianchi identity) of its Bäcklund transformation.
Later, he transformed the nonlinear self-dual network equation into a bilinear form
and from which he obtained a 3SS.
Then, he successively obtained the bilinear forms and 3SSs for the Toda lattice,
the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation and the sine-Gordon equation.
In his celebrated paper
[
25
]
in 1971, the
N
-soliton solution (NSS)
of the bilinear KdV equation is presented in the determinant of a generalized Cauchy matrix.
Soon after, this form was re-derived by Wadati and Toda
[
71
]

from the Inverse Scattering Transform
[
13
]
.
Without the time dependence, this form has been obtained by Kay and Moses
[
34
]
in 1956,
from the solution of the Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko (GLM) equation
[
14
,
50
]
in the reflectionless case.
Hirota’s solution is nowadays known as the
\tau
function of the KdV equation,
while, as a notion,
\tau
function has played a more general and more important role in the theory of integrable, systems, far beyond the solutions of bilinear equations.

In 1974, Hirota introduced the bilinear operator “
D
” in
[
27
]

and started to study bilinear Bäcklund transformations (BTs).
In the same paper he also explored the relation between bilinear BTs
and Lax pairs, which connects the BTs and integrability from the bilinear perspective.
In 1980, Hirota reported the fact that any KdV-type bilinear equation always has a 1SS and a 2SS,
no matter whether it is integrable or not
[
29
]
.
It is natural to realize that having a 3SS should mean “something” for a KdV-type bilinear equation.
Hirota came up with a question in
[
29
]
: Under what conditions a bilinear equation may have a NSS
in certain form (see Sec.
3.2
), which is called the bilinear equation being integrable in Hirota’s sense.
In 1987, Jarmo Hietarinta searched for possible KdV-type bilinear equations that admit 3SSs
[
16
]
.
Later, he extended the search to the bilinear equations of the modified KdV (mKdV) type and
sine-Gordon type under the conditions of having 3SSs
[
17
,
18
]
,
and to the complex bilinear equations (e.g. the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) type
and the Benjamin-Ono (BO) type)
[
19
]
, for which the existence of a 2SS may imply integrability.
Hietarinta summarized his search in
[
20
]
.
In this paper, we will employ the KdV-type bilinear equations to try understanding the relations between
having a 3SS and being integrable.

The bilinear method drew the attention of Mikio Sato, who invited Hirota, Satsuma,
together with Jimbo and Miwa, to his seminars
[
38
]
.
This leads to Sato’s observation on the connection between the bilinear Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation
and the Plüker relations on the Grassmannian
[
64
]
.
Soon after, Sato’s KP theory was developed by the Kyoto group to become a universal theory for integrable systems.
In this theory, vertex operator plays a remarkable role, which reveals the algebraic structures of

\tau
functions as well as integrable equations.
The name “vertex operator” comes from its geometric interpretation in string theory:
it is an operator inserted at a point (a vertex) in spacetime where a particle is created or annihilated.
In 1978, Lepowsky and Wilson
[
45
]
presented a vertex operator
that is isomorphic to the affine Lie algebra
A^{(1)}_{1}
.
In 1981, Date, Kashiwara and Miwa
[
11
]

deformed the operator and connected it with the
\tau
function of the KdV equation,
which led to a series of beautiful work on transformation groups and integrable systems
[
56
]
.
In this paper, we will illustrate how vertex operators play roles in generating
\tau
functions
for the KdV and KP hierarchies.

This paper presents a partial review of the bilinear method,
focusing on the integrability based on the 3SS condition
and the transformation between
\tau
functions by the BTs and vertex operators.
We mainly referred to two monographs: one is
[
31
]
by Hirota,
and the other is
[
56
]
by Miwa, Jimbo and Data.
We will also include some recent progress of the bilinear method.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce basic notations, notions and properties of Hirota’s bilinear derivatives.
Then we employ the KdV equation and KP equation as examples to show the procedure of deriving
of multi-soliton solutions from their bilinear forms.
We also illustrate the asymptotic analysis of interactions of solitons.
In Section
3
,
first, we show the existence of the 1SS and 2SS for a general KdV-type bilinear equation.
Then we explain the property of elastic scattering of multi-solitons and its connection with the 3SS condition.
This may help us to understand why the 3SS condition implies integrability for the KdV-type bilinear equations.
In Section
4
we introduce bilinear BTs and their connections with Lax pairs and superposition formulae.
In Section
5
we show how vertex operators generate
\tau
functions
for the KdV and KP hierarchies.
Finally, some remarks are given in Section
6
.

2
Introduction to the bilinear method: The KdV and KP(II)

In this section, we introduce basic notations, notions and properties of Hirota’s bilinear derivatives.
The KdV equation and KP equation will serve as examples to illustrate the procedure of deriving multi-soliton solutions
and the asymptotic analysis of interaction of solitons.

2.1
Bilinear operator

Let us start from Hirota’s bilinear operator that he introduced in
[
27
]
in 1974.

Definition 2.1
.

For
C^{\infty}
differential functions
f(x,y)
and
g(x,y)
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, their
bilinear derivatives
are defined as:

D^{m}_{x}D^{n}_{y}f(x,y)\cdot g(x,y)=(\partial_{x}-\partial_{x^{\prime}})^{m}(\partial_{y}-\partial_{y^{\prime}})^{n}f(x,y)g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|_{x^{\prime}=x,y^{\prime}=y}.

(2.1)

where
D
is called
Hirota’s bilinear operator
. Here
\partial_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
.

The definition (
2.1
) can also be introduced as follows
[
28
]
:

e^{\epsilon D_{x}+\kappa D_{y}}f(x,y)\cdot g(x,y)=f(x+\epsilon,y+\kappa)g(x-\epsilon,y-\kappa).

(2.2)

In fact, expanding both sides at
(\epsilon,\kappa)=(0,0)
and comparing coefficients of
the power
\epsilon^{m}\kappa^{n}
, one can get definition (
2.1
).
Here are simple examples of bilinear derivatives:

\displaystyle D_{x}f\cdot g=f_{x}g-fg_{x},

\displaystyle D^{2}_{x}f\cdot g=f_{xx}g-2f_{x}g_{x}+fg_{xx},

\displaystyle D^{3}_{x}f\cdot g=f_{xxx}g-3f_{xx}g_{x}+3f_{x}g_{xx}-fg_{xxx},

\displaystyle D_{x}D_{y}f\cdot g=f_{xy}g-f_{x}g_{y}-f_{y}g_{x}+fg_{xy},

\displaystyle D^{m}_{x}f\cdot g=\sum^{m}_{j=0}(-1)^{j}\mathrm{C}^{j}_{m}f^{(m-j)}(x)g^{(j)}(x),

where
\mathrm{C}^{j}_{m}=\frac{m!}{(m-j)!j!}
,
f^{(j)}=\partial^{j}_{x}f(x)
.
From these examples one can see the difference between the bilinear derivatives of
f
and
g

and Leibniz’s rule of the
m
-th order
derivative
\partial^{m}_{x}(fg)
.

Bilinear derivatives admit symmetric and bilinear properties:

\displaystyle D^{m}_{x}f\cdot g=(-1)^{m}D^{m}_{x}g\cdot f,

\displaystyle(aD^{m}_{x}+bD^{n}_{y})f\cdot g=aD^{m}_{x}f\cdot g+bD^{n}_{y}f\cdot g,

\displaystyle D^{m}_{x}D^{n}_{y}(af+bg)\cdot h=aD^{m}_{x}D^{n}_{y}f\cdot h+bD^{m}_{x}D^{n}_{y}g\cdot h,

where
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
, and particularly,

D^{m}_{x}f\cdot 1=\partial^{m}_{x}f(x).

In addition, for linear exponential functions, e.g.
e^{kx+\omega t}

(k,\omega\in\mathbb{C})
,
there is a simple formula for their bilinear derivatives:

\displaystyle D^{m}_{x}D^{n}_{y}e^{\eta_{1}}\cdot e^{\eta_{2}}=(k_{1}-k_{2})^{m}(\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{n}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(2.3)

where

\eta_{i}=k_{i}x+\omega_{i}y+\eta^{(0)}_{i},~~k_{i},\omega_{i},\eta^{(0)}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}.

(2.4)

The above definition and properties can be extended into arbitrary dimensions.
Suppose that

\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{s}),~\mathbf{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{s}),~\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\cdots,q_{s})

are vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
.
Then, denoting
\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{s}p_{i}t_{i}
,
and
D_{\mathbf{t}}=(D_{t_{1}},D_{t_{2}},\cdots,D_{t_{s}})
,
we can define

e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot D_{\mathbf{t}}}f(\mathbf{t})\cdot g(\mathbf{t})=f(\mathbf{t}+\mathbf{p})g(\mathbf{t}-\mathbf{p}).

(2.5)

Suppose
P(\mathbf{t})
is a polynomial of
\mathbf{t}
, and introduce
P(D_{\mathbf{t}})
.
For example, if
P(\mathbf{t})=3t_{1}^{2}t_{2}+2t_{2}t_{3}
,
then
P(D_{\mathbf{t}})=3D_{t_{1}}^{2}D_{t_{2}}+2D_{t_{2}}D_{t_{3}}
.
For a general
P(D_{\mathbf{t}})
, it is not difficult to find

\displaystyle P(D_{\mathbf{t}})e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}\cdot e^{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{t}}=P(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})e^{(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{t}},

(2.6)

\displaystyle P(D_{\mathbf{t}})e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}\cdot 1=P(\mathbf{p})e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}=P(\partial_{\mathbf{t}})e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}},

(2.7)

where
\partial_{\mathbf{t}}=(\partial_{t_{1}},\partial_{t_{2}},\cdots,\partial_{t_{s}})
.
Besides, for the linear function
\eta_{i}
defined in (
2.4
), one can prove that

\displaystyle D^{r}_{x}D^{s}_{y}(e^{\eta_{1}}f(x,y))\cdot(e^{\eta_{2}}g(x,y))

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}(D_{x}+k_{1}-k_{2})^{r}(D_{y}+\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})^{s}f(x,y)\cdot g(x,y),

(2.8)

and particularly, when
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}
, one has

D^{r}_{x}D^{s}_{y}(e^{\eta_{1}}f)\cdot(e^{\eta_{1}}g)=e^{2\eta_{1}}D_{x}^{r}D_{y}^{s}f\cdot g,

(2.9)

which is known as the gauge property of bilinear derivatives. A more general case of this property is

\displaystyle P(D_{\mathbf{t}})(e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}f(\mathbf{t}))\cdot(e^{\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{t}}g(\mathbf{t}))=e^{(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{t}}P(D_{\mathbf{t}}+\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q})f(\mathbf{t})\cdot g(\mathbf{t}),

(2.10)

and

P(D_{\mathbf{t}})(e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}f)\cdot(e^{\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}g)=e^{2\mathbf{p}\cdot\mathbf{t}}P(D_{\mathbf{t}})f\cdot g\,.

(2.11)

2.2
N soliton solutions

In this part we show how Hirota’s method works in finding NSS.
The KdV equation and KP equation will serve as examples.

2.2.1
NSS of the KdV equation

The KdV equation reads

u_{t}+6uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=0.

(2.12)

Note that its coefficients can be arbitrary constants without changing integrability.
Usually we consider its potential form

w_{t}+3(w_{x})^{2}+w_{xxx}=0,~~(u=w_{x}).

(2.13)

Under the transformation

u=2(\ln f)_{xx},~~\hbox{i.e.}~~w=2(\ln f)_{x},

(2.14)

Eq.(
2.13
) can be written as
[
25
]

f_{xt}f-f_{x}f_{t}+f_{xxxx}f-4f_{xxx}f_{x}+3(f_{xx})^{2}=0,

(2.15)

which is

(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})f\cdot f=0

(2.16)

in terms of the bilinear operator
D
given in (
2.1
).
The above equation is called the bilinear form of the KdV equation (
2.12
), or the bilinear KdV equation;
the solution
f
is known as the
\tau
function of the KdV equation; once we have
f
,
we can get back a solution of the KdV equation through the transformation (
2.14
).

Note: In 1971 Ryogo Hirota first introduced the bilinear method to derive NSS of
the KdV equation
[
25
]
.
At that time the bilinear form of the KdV equation was written as (
2.15
);
the bilinear operator
D
as defined in Definition
2.1
was introduced later by Hirota
in
[
27
]
in 1974.

It is easy to check

f=1+e^{\eta},~~\eta=kx-k^{3}t+\eta^{(0)},~~(k,\eta^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R})

(2.17)

satisfies the bilinear KdV equation (
2.15
).
To achieve more solutions, one can (perturbatively) expand

f=1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}f^{(i)}\varepsilon^{i},

(2.18)

where subscript
(i)
is for numbering coefficients.
Substituting the above into (
2.16
) and comparing coefficients of each power of
\varepsilon
,
we reach to an equation system

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1}:~~~(\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x})f^{(1)}=0,

(2.19a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}:~~~(\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x})f^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{2}(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})f^{(1)}\cdot f^{(1)},

(2.19b)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{3}:~~~(\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x})f^{(3)}=-(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})f^{(1)}\cdot f^{(2)},

(2.19c)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}:~~~(\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x})f^{(4)}=-(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})(f^{(1)}\cdot f^{(3)}+\frac{1}{2}f^{(2)}\cdot f^{(2)}),

(2.19d)

\displaystyle~~~\cdots\cdots.

For (
2.19a
) we can take
f^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{i}}
, where

\eta_{i}=k_{i}x-k_{i}^{3}t+\eta_{i}^{(0)},~~k_{i},\eta_{i}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.20)

Since (
2.19a
) is a homogeneous linear equation, for any positive integer
N

f^{(1)}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}e^{\eta_{i}}

(2.21)

gives a solution to (
2.19a
) as well,
where
\{\eta_{i}\}
are defined by (
2.20
).

Now, let us look at in more details the equation system (
2.19
) with
f^{(1)}
given as (
2.21
).
When
N=1
,
f^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{1}}
satisfies (
2.19a
);
meanwhile, the rest equations in (
2.19
) hold when taking
f^{(2)}=f^{(3)}=\cdots=0
.
Thus,

f=1+\varepsilon e^{\eta_{1}}

(2.22)

provides a solution to the bilinear KdV equation (
2.16
).
This means the “perturbation” formula (
2.18
) can be truncated as shown in (
2.22
).
As a consequence,
\varepsilon
can be arbitrary in (
2.22
).
Taking
\varepsilon=1
in (
2.22
) and substituting it into (
2.14
) we have

u=2(\ln f)_{xx}=2[\ln(1+e^{\eta_{1}})]_{xx},

(2.23)

which is a 1SS of the KdV equation.

When
N=2
, from (
2.21
) we have
f^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}
, and then
solving equation (
2.19b
) we find

f^{(2)}=A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},~~A_{12}=\Bigl(\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\Bigr)^{2}.

(2.24)

Meanwhile,
f^{(j)}=0~(j=3,4,\cdots)
solve the rest equations in (
2.19
).
Thus,

f=1+\varepsilon(e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}})+\varepsilon^{2}A_{12}\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}

(2.25)

provides a second solution to the bilinear KdV equation (
2.16
), which
will lead to a 2SS for the KdV equation via (
2.14
).

When
N=3
, from (
2.21
) we can find a solution for (
2.19
):

\displaystyle f=

\displaystyle 1+\varepsilon(e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+e^{\eta_{3}})

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}(A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}+A_{13}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{3}}+A_{23}e^{\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}})

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{3}A_{12}A_{13}A_{23}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}},~~~A_{ij}=\Bigl(\frac{k_{i}-k_{j}}{k_{i}+k_{j}}\Bigr)^{2}.

(2.26)

Again, through (
2.14
) it gives a 3SS to the KdV equation.
Note that Hirota got the above 3SS in May of 1971
[
32
]
.

For a general number
N
, Hirota presented the following compact form:

f=\sum_{\mu=0,1}\mathrm{exp}\left(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\mu_{j}\eta_{j}+\sum^{N}_{1\leq i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}a_{ij}\right),

(2.27)

where
\eta_{j}
is defined as in (
2.20
),
e^{a_{ij}}=A_{ij}
, and the summation of
\mu
means to take
all possible
\mu_{j}=\{0,1\}

(j=1,2,\cdots,N)
.
NSS of the KdV equation is given by (
2.14
).
Eq.(
2.27
) is a truncated form of (
2.18
) (we have taken
\varepsilon=1
since
\varepsilon

is actually arbitrary).
A proof of (
2.27
) satisfying (
2.16
) can be found in
[
25
]
or
[
2
]
.

2.2.2
NSS of the KP(II) equation

The KP equation reads

(4u_{t}+6uu_{x}+u_{xxx})_{x}+3\sigma u_{yy}=0,~~(\sigma=\pm 1),

(2.28)

where when
\sigma=1
it is known as the KP(II) equation and when
\sigma=-1
it is the KP(I) equation.
We solve the KP(II) equation. By the transformation

u=2(\ln f)_{xx},

(2.29)

the KP(II) equation is bilinearized as
[
65
]

(4D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x}+3D_{y}^{2})f\cdot f=0.

(2.30)

With the expension

f=1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}f^{(i)}\varepsilon^{i},

(2.31)

the bilinear KP(II) equation (
2.30
) yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{1}:~~~(4\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x}+3\partial_{y}^{3})f^{(1)}=0,

(2.32a)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}:~~~(4\partial_{xt}+\partial^{4}_{x}+3\partial_{y}^{3})f^{(2)}=-\frac{1}{2}(4D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x}+3D_{y}^{2})f^{(1)}\cdot f^{(1)},

(2.32b)

\displaystyle~~~\cdots\cdots.

For (
2.32a
) we can take
f^{(1)}=e^{kx+hy+\omega t}
, and
k,h,\omega
satisfy
the dispersion relation

4k\omega+k^{4}+3h^{2}=0.

(2.33)

Compared with the KdV equation, here we have two free parameters.
For a better parametrisation, we introduce
h=ak
, by which we have
\omega=-k(k^{2}+3a^{2})/4
.
Here
a
is an arbitrary parameter, as free as
k
.
In practice we take
k=p-q,~a=p+q
, and it follows that

k=p-q,~~h=p^{2}-q^{2},~~\omega=-(p^{3}-q^{3}).

(2.34)

Thus, we have

f^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{1}},

(2.35)

where

\eta_{i}=(p_{i}-q_{i})x+(p_{i}^{2}-q_{i}^{2})y-(p_{i}^{3}-q_{i}^{3})t+\eta_{i}^{(0)},~~(p_{i},q_{i},\eta_{i}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}).

(2.36)

Then 1SS of the KP(II) equation can be obtained as (taking
\varepsilon=1
)

u=2(\ln f)_{xx}=\frac{(p_{1}-q_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathrm{sech}^{2}\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}.

(2.37)

To get more solutions, similar to the KdV equation, we take

f^{(1)}=\sum^{N}_{i=1}e^{\eta_{i}},

(2.38)

where
\eta_{i}
is defined by (
2.36
).
When
N=2
, corresponding to 2SS, we find (taking
\varepsilon=1
)

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(2.39a)

which solves (
2.30
), where

A_{ij}=\frac{(p_{i}-p_{j})(q_{i}-q_{j})}{(p_{i}-q_{j})(q_{i}-p_{j})}.

(2.39b)

Continuing this procedure, for 3SS,
f
has the same structure as (
2.26
). For NSS, there is

f=\sum_{\mu=0,1}\mathrm{exp}\left(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\mu_{j}\eta_{j}+\sum^{N}_{1\leq i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}a_{ij}\right),

(2.40)

where
\eta_{j}
is defined as in (
2.36
),
e^{a_{ij}}=A_{ij}
which is defined in (
2.39b
),
and the summation of
\mu
means to take
all possible
\mu_{j}=\{0,1\}

(j=1,2,\cdots,N)
.
Then, NSS of the KP(II) equation is obtained via (
2.29
).

2.3
Asymptotic analysis of 2SS

Classical solitons are characterized by their particle behavior, namely,
the elastic scattering property: each soliton keeps its own amplitude and velocity after interaction.
In the following, for the KdV equation and the KP equation,
we illustrate such scattering properties with mathematical explanations.

2.3.1
The KdV equation

First, let us look at 1SS (
2.23
) of the KdV equation, i.e.

u=\frac{k_{1}^{2}}{2}\mathrm{sech}^{2}\eta_{1},~~\eta_{1}=k_{1}x-k_{1}^{3}t+\eta^{(0)}_{1},

(2.41)

which describes a solitary wave as depicted in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
(a) 1SS of the KdV equation.
(b) trajectory of the vortex of 1SS:
\eta_{1}=0
, where
k_{1}=1,~\eta_{1}^{(0)}=0
.

The maximum of the wave, i.e. the amplitude, which is
\frac{k^{2}_{1}}{2}
, occurs when
\eta_{1}=0
.
When

\eta_{1}=0
, i.e.

x(t)=k^{2}_{1}t-\frac{\eta^{(0)}_{1}}{k_{1}},

(2.42)

we have a straight line as depicted in Figure
1
, denoting the trajectory of the vertex of the wave.
From (
2.42
) we have

x^{\prime}(t)=k^{2}_{1}
, which stands for the velocity of the wave.
One can see that the velocity is always positive, which means the solitary wave described by the KdV equation is of single direction.

2SS of the KdV equation exhibits elastic scattering behavior, as depicted in Figure
2
and Figure
3
.
The 2SS is

\displaystyle u=2(\ln f)_{xx},

(2.43a)

\displaystyle f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(2.43b)

where

\eta_{i}=k_{i}x-k_{i}^{3}t+\eta_{i}^{(0)},~~A_{12}=\Bigl(\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\Bigr)^{2}.

(2.43c)

Figure 2:
2SS (
2.43
) of the KdV equation (
k_{1}=0.6,~k_{2}=1.1,~\eta^{(0)}_{1}=\eta^{(0)}_{2}=0
).

(a)
t=-12
, (b)
t=-5
, (c)
t=1
, (d)
t=10
.

Figure 3:
2SS (
2.43
) of the KdV equation (
k_{1}=0.6,~k_{2}=1.1,~\eta^{(0)}_{1}=\eta^{(0)}_{2}=0
).

Such a scattering behavior can be understood mathematically using asymptotic analysis,
of which the generic procedure is the following.

We consider
f
in (
2.43b
) and assume
k_{1}>k_{2}>0
without loss of generality.
Suppose
\eta_{1}=c
(

\eta_{1}=c

To do that, we rewrite (

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(2.44b)

Because of

+\infty,&t\to+\infty.\end{array}\right.

Therefore in

f\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+e^{\eta_{1}},&t\to-\infty,\\
e^{\eta_{2}}(1+A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}}),&t\to+\infty.\end{array}\right.

(2.45)

Due to the gauge property of bilinear derivatives,
the factor

in the above does not change solutions of the bilinear KdV equation
(

), and does not change the value of

either.
Thus, if we observe 2SS along the straight line

u=2[\ln(1+e^{\eta_{1}})]_{xx};

(2.46a)

and when

(2.46b)

This is still the original 1SS (

) (after interaction it has the amplitude and velocity as same as before)
but there is a phase shift
-\frac{2}{k_{1}}\ln\bigl(\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\bigr)
.

Similarly, in the coordinate system
(\eta_{2},t)
we can see

e^{\eta_{1}}\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}+\infty,&t\to-\infty,\\
0,&t\to+\infty.\end{array}\right.

Then we have

f\sim\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{\eta_{1}}(1+A_{12}e^{\eta_{2}}),&t\to-\infty,\\
1+e^{\eta_{2}},&t\to+\infty.\end{array}\right.

(2.47)

Thus we can see that the soliton determined by
k_{2}
keeps its amplitude and velocity before and after the interaction
but gains a phase shift
\frac{2}{k_{2}}\ln\bigl(\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\bigr)

Such phase shifts due to the interaction can be seen in Figure

3
Hirota’s integrability, elastic scattering and 3SS condition

In this section we will focus on the KdV-type bilinear equations
which always admit 1SS and 2SS, no matter whether they are integrable or not.
We will introduce integrability of this type bilinear equations in Hirota’s sense.
Then we will explain the property of elastic scattering of multi-solitons and its connection with the 3SS condition.
This may help us to understand why the 3SS condition implies integrability for the KdV-type bilinear equations.

3.1
2SS of the KdV-type bilinear equations

It is amazing that many bilinear equations of the KdV type automatically admit 1SS and 2SS.

3.1.1
Bilinear equations of the KdV type

Consider the following bilinear equation

P(D_{\mathbf{t}})f\cdot f=0,

(3.1)

where
P
is an even polynomial, i.e.
P(\mathbf{t})=P(-\mathbf{t})
and satisfying
P(\mathbf{0})=0
.
Such a bilinear equation (
3.1
) is called a bilinear equation of the KdV type
[
29
,
16
]
.
Assume that

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}},

(3.2a)

where

\eta_{1}=\mathbf{p_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{1}.

(3.2b)

It follows that

f\cdot f=1\cdot 1+1\cdot e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{1}}\cdot 1+e^{\eta_{1}}\cdot e^{\eta_{1}}.

Since the terms
1\cdot 1
and
e^{\eta_{1}}\cdot e^{\eta_{1}}
varnish under the action of
P(D_{\mathbf{t}})
,
we have

P(D_{\mathbf{t}})f\cdot f=2P(D_{\mathbf{t}})e^{\eta_{1}}\cdot 1=2P(\partial_{\mathbf{t}})e^{\eta_{1}}=2P(\mathbf{p_{1}})e^{\eta_{1}}.

Thus, once
P(\mathbf{p_{1}})=0
, (
3.2
) gives a solution to (
3.1
).
We call
P(\mathbf{p_{1}})=0
to be the dispersion relation of the bilinear equation (
3.1
).

Consider

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(3.3a)

where
A_{12}
is a constant to be determined,

\eta_{i}=\mathbf{p_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{i}

(3.3b)

satisfying the dispersion relation

P(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0.

(3.3c)

Substituting (
3.3a
) into equation (
3.1
) and making use of the
dispersion relation (
3.3c
),
it is easy to see that (
3.3a
) satisfies equation (
3.1
) provided

A_{12}=-\frac{P(\mathbf{p_{1}}-\mathbf{p_{2}})}{P(\mathbf{p_{1}}+\mathbf{p_{2}})}.

(3.3d)

It is Hirota who first found this fact
[
29
]
.
Here we note that “automatically” existing 2SS means there is no extra condition on
\mathbf{p_{i}}
beyond
the dispersion relation (
3.3c
).

3.1.2
Other cases

As an example let us look at the following bilinear system (
[
18
]
, Sec.6):

\displaystyle B(D_{\mathbf{t}})G\cdot F=0,

(3.4a)

\displaystyle A(D_{\mathbf{t}})(F\cdot F+\epsilon G\cdot G)=0,

(3.4b)

where
A
is an even polynomial,
B
is a polynomial
and
\epsilon=\pm 1
. The above equation system admits a 1SS;

F=1,~~G=e^{\eta_{1}},~~\eta_{1}=\mathbf{p_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{1},

where
\eta_{1}
satisfies dispersion relation:
B(\mathbf{p_{1}})=0
.
One type of 2SS of (
3.4
) is:

\displaystyle F=1-A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},~~G=e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}},

(3.5a)

where
\eta_{i}=\mathbf{p_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{i}
satisfies the dispersion relation
B(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0
, and

A_{12}=-\epsilon\,\frac{A(\mathbf{p_{1}}-\mathbf{p_{2}})}{A(\mathbf{p_{1}}+\mathbf{p_{2}})}.

(3.5b)

Not any bilinear equation (system) will automatically admit a 2SS.
For example, the following bilinear system (the NLS-type)
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle B(D_{\mathbf{t}})G\cdot F=0,

(3.6a)

\displaystyle A(D_{\mathbf{t}})F\cdot F=2\epsilon|G|^{2},

(3.6b)

where
A
is an even polynomial,
B
is a polynomial,
F\in\mathbb{R}(\mathbf{t})
,

G\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbf{t})
,
|G|^{2}=GG^{*}
and
*
stands for complex conjugate. It has a 1SS:

F=1+a\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{1}^{*}},~~G=e^{\eta_{1}},

where
\eta_{1}=\mathbf{p_{1}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{1}
,
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}
,
\eta_{1}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{C}
,
B(\mathbf{p_{1}})=0
and

a=\frac{-\epsilon}{A(\mathbf{p_{1}}+\mathbf{p_{1}}^{*})}
.
However, its 2SS does not exist automatically
[
19
]
.

3.2
Hirota’s integrability and 3SS condition

Take the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
) as an example. For such an equation, i.e.

P(D_{\mathbf{t}})f\cdot f=0,

(3.7)

it is said to be Hirota-integrable if for all positive integers
N
, it has a NSS of the form

f=1+\varepsilon\sum^{N}_{i=1}e^{\eta_{i}}+\{\hbox{finite number of higher-order terms in $\varepsilon$}\},

(3.8)

without any further conditions on the parameters
\mathbf{p_{i}}
beyond the dispersion relation

P(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0.

(3.9)

In general, for a bilinear equation (system), when it has a 1SS which is described by
e^{\eta_{i}}

(\eta_{i}=\mathbf{p_{i}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{i})
,
there is a condition on
e^{\eta_{i}}
, for example, the dispersion relation, which we call 1SS-condition.
If the bilinear equation (system) allows a solution which is described by a polynomial of
arbitrarily many
\{e^{\eta_{i}}\}
(where each
e^{\eta_{i}}
describes one single soliton),
and there is no extra condition on each
e^{\eta_{i}}
besides the 1SS-condition,
we say the bilinear equation (system) is
Hirota integrable
.

Hirota presented the following form for the NSS of the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.7
)
[
29
]
:

f=\sum_{\mu=0,1}\mathrm{exp}\left(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\mu_{j}\eta_{j}+\sum^{N}_{1\leq i<j}\mu_{i}\mu_{j}a_{ij}\right),

(3.10a)

where
\eta_{j}=\mathbf{p_{j}}\cdot\mathbf{t}+\eta^{(0)}_{j}
are defined as (
3.3b
),

P(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0,~~~e^{a_{ij}}=A_{ij}=-\frac{P(\mathbf{p_{i}}-\mathbf{p_{j}})}{P(\mathbf{p_{i}}+\mathbf{p_{j}})},

(3.10b)

the summation of
\mu
means to take
all possible
\mu_{j}=\{0,1\}

(j=1,2,\cdots,N)
;
and further than that,
the following condition is needed:

\sum_{\sigma=\pm 1}\left[P(\sum^{N}_{j=1}\sigma_{j}\mathbf{p_{j}})\times\Bigl(\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}P(\sigma_{i}\mathbf{p_{i}}-\sigma_{j}\mathbf{p_{j}})\Bigr)\right]=0,

(3.11)

the summation of
\sigma
means to take
all possible
\sigma_{j}=\{1,-1\}

(j=1,2,\cdots,N)
.
This condition holds automatically for the
N=2
case.

Those bilinear equations (systems) that automatically have 2SS may not have a 3SS;
even when they have a 3SS, they might not be Hirota integrable (there may be extra condition on
\mathbf{p_{i}}
).
One famous example is the
(2+1)-dimensional sG equation (see
[
26
]
)

\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy}-\varphi_{tt}=\sin\varphi,

(3.12)

which, by the transformation

\varphi=4\arctan\frac{g}{f},

(3.13)

is transformed into bilinear form

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{2}+D_{y}^{2}-D_{t}^{2})g\cdot f=gf,

(3.14a)

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{2}+D_{y}^{2}-D_{t}^{2})(f\cdot f-g\cdot g)=0.

(3.14b)

The above system is a special case of (
3.4
)
and it has a 2SS automatically (cf. (
3.5b
)). Its 3SS reads
[
26
]

\displaystyle f=1+A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}+A_{13}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{3}}+A_{23}e^{\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}},

(3.15a)

\displaystyle g=e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+e^{\eta_{3}}+A_{12}A_{13}A_{23}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}},

(3.15b)

where

\displaystyle\eta_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y-c_{i}t+\eta^{(0)}_{i},

(3.15c)

\displaystyle\hbox{dispersion~relation:}~a^{2}_{i}+b_{i}^{2}-c_{i}^{2}=1,

(3.15d)

\displaystyle A_{ij}=\frac{(a_{i}-a_{j})^{2}+(b_{i}-b_{j})^{2}-(c_{i}-c_{j})^{2}}{(a_{i}+a_{j})^{2}+(b_{i}+b_{j})^{2}-(c_{i}+c_{j})^{2}},

(3.15e)

and an extra condition is needed:

\left|\begin{array}[]{ccc}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}\\
b_{1}&b_{2}&b_{3}\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&c_{3}\end{array}\right|=0.

(3.15f)

In the above results, for the 2SS which exists automatically, the only condition on
\{a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}\}
is the
dispersion relation (
3.15d
);
however, if 3SS exists, in addition to the dispersion relation, the extra condition (
3.15f
) is required.
Thus, (
3.14
) is an bilinear system that possesses a 3SS
but is not integrable in Hirota’s sense.
Hirota once proposed such a question in
[
29
]
for the KdV-type bilinear equations:
“Under what conditions does
P
satisfy the identity (
3.11
)?”

For the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
),

3SS-condition
is specially referred to the following:
the bilinear equation (
3.1
) has a 3SS,
and the condition on each
\eta_{i}

is nothing beyond the 1SS-condition.
In general, it is conjectured that for KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
)
the 3SS-condition is equivalent to Hirota’s integrability.

In the following, let us take the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
) as an example
to see how the form of 3SS is determined by the elastic scattering behavior of multi-solitons.

The property of elastic scattering of multi-solitons requires the following:
“removing a soliton from NSS, the left (N-1) solitons keep the elastic scattering structure of (N-1)SS”.
Removing a soliton means the soliton is far from others  —— which, mathematically, can be done through two ways:
either
e^{\eta_{k}}\to 0
or
e^{\eta_{k}}\to\infty
(refer to the asymptotic analysis of 2SS in Sec.
2.3
).
For the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
), it automatically has a 2SS (see (
3.3
)):

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},

(3.16a)

where

P(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0,

(3.16b)

A_{ij}=-\frac{P(\mathbf{p_{i}}-\mathbf{p_{j}})}{P(\mathbf{p_{i}}+\mathbf{p_{j}})}.

(3.16c)

With the requirement of elastic scattering property, if there is no further condition on
\mathbf{p_{i}}
beyond (
3.16b
),
after analysis
(considering a general form for 3SS and assuming the 2SS (
3.16a
)
is a result of a 3SS after removing one soliton under the elastic scattering property)
we can find the 3SS of equation (
3.16
)
can only be the following form

\displaystyle f=

\displaystyle 1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+e^{\eta_{3}}

\displaystyle+A_{12}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}+A_{13}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{3}}+A_{23}e^{\eta_{3}+\eta_{3}}

\displaystyle+A_{12}A_{13}A_{23}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}+\eta_{3}},

(3.17)

and
A_{ij}
must be defined as (
3.16c
).
If we start from the 3SS (
3.17
) and
assuming the above 3SS is the result of a 4SS after removing one soliton under the elastic scattering property,
we can reach to a form for 4SS.
Continuing such a procedure one can obtain 5SS, 6SS,
\cdots
.

Thus, for the KdV-type bilinear equation (
3.1
),
if we only require the dispersion relation (
3.16b
) and the elastic scattering property,
its NSS (if it has) can only be the form (
3.10
),
(cf. (
2.27
) for the KdV equation).

Now, if (
3.10
) provides a solution to (
3.1
), then (
3.1
) is Hirota’s integrable.
However, not all the bilinear equations have their 3SSs which are only built on
the dispersion relation:
P(\mathbf{p_{i}})=0
.
In 1987, Jarmo Hietarinta found that the KdV-type bilinear equations that satisfy the 3SS-condition are:

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{4}-4D_{x}D_{t}+3D_{y}^{2})f\cdot f=0,

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{3}D_{t}+aD_{x}^{2}+D_{t}D_{y})f\cdot f=0,

\displaystyle[D_{x}D_{t}(D_{x}^{2}+\sqrt{3}D_{x}D_{t}+D_{t}^{2})+aD_{x}^{2}+bD_{x}D_{t}+cD_{t}^{2}]f\cdot f=0,

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{6}+5D^{3}_{x}D_{t}-5D_{t}^{2}+D_{x}D_{y})f\cdot f=0,

etc., where,
a,b,c
are arbitrary constants. For more results for other types of bilinear equations,
one can refer to
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
and
[
22
]
.
For the discrete bilinear equations of the KdV-type on
3\times 3
stencil,
the equations allowing 3SSs are listed in
[
24
]
.

Note: In
[
20
]
Hietarinta proposed a question
on the algorithmic way to nonlinaerizing bilinear equations.
Recently, such a way has been developed in
[
77
,
49
]
.
The algorithm is designed based on the relations between Hirota’s bilinear derivatives and
the Bell polynomials, which are revealed by Gilson, Lambert and their collaborators

[
15
,
43
]
.

4
Bäcklund transformations

Bäcklund transformation (BT) requires compatibility, which connects it with integrability.
Hirota developed a technique to construct bilinear BTs
[
27
]
.
In this section, we will introduce his technique and
explain the connections between BTs and Lax pairs.
We will also illustrate superposition formulae of BTs,
which led Hirota to finding 3SS for the sG equation (cf.
[
32
]
).

4.1
Bilinear identities

To construct bilinear BTs, first, we introduce a way to generate a large class of bilinear identities.

Property 4.1
.

The following equality holds (see
[
28
]
, Appendix I):

\displaystyle e^{D_{1}}(e^{D_{2}}a\cdot b)\cdot(e^{D_{3}}c\cdot d)

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle e^{\frac{1}{2}(D_{2}-D_{3})}(e^{\frac{1}{2}(D_{2}+D_{3})+D_{1}}a\cdot d)\cdot(e^{\frac{1}{2}(D_{2}+D_{3})-D_{1}}c\cdot b),

(4.1)

where
D_{i}=\varepsilon_{i}D_{x}+\delta_{i}D_{t}
,
\varepsilon_{i},\delta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
a,b,c,d
are
C^{\infty}
functions of
(x,t)
.

It can be verified directly.

In the following we give some examples to show how the formula (
4.1
) works in generating bilinear identities.

Example
2.1.1: Taking
D_{2}=D_{3}
in (
4.1
) yields

e^{D_{1}}(e^{D_{2}}a\cdot b)\cdot(e^{D_{2}}c\cdot d)=(e^{D_{2}+D_{1}}a\cdot d)\cdot(e^{D_{2}-D_{1}}c\cdot b).

(4.2)

Next, taking
D_{1}=\delta D_{x},~D_{2}=\varepsilon D_{x}
in (
4.2
) and expanding the exponential functions of both sides, we have

\displaystyle(1+\delta D_{x}+\cdots)[(1+\varepsilon D_{x}+\cdots)a\cdot b)]\cdot[(1+\varepsilon D_{x}+\cdots)c\cdot d)]

\displaystyle=~

\displaystyle[(1+(\varepsilon+\delta)D_{x}+\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon+\delta)^{2}D^{2}_{x}+\cdots)a\cdot d)]

\displaystyle~~~~~\times[(1+(\varepsilon-\delta)D_{x}+\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon-\delta)^{2}D^{2}_{x}+\cdots)c\cdot b)].

The coefficient of the term
\varepsilon\delta
leads to a bilinear identity

D_{x}[(D_{x}a\cdot b)\cdot(cd)-(D_{x}c\cdot d)\cdot(ab)]=(D^{2}_{x}a\cdot d)bc-(D^{2}_{x}c\cdot b)ad.

(4.3)

Example
2.1.2: Taking
D_{2}=D_{3},b=c,d=a
in (
4.1
) yields

e^{D_{1}}(e^{D_{2}}a\cdot c)\cdot(e^{D_{2}}c\cdot a)=(e^{D_{2}+D_{1}}a\cdot a)\cdot(e^{D_{2}-D_{1}}c\cdot c).

(4.4)

Then we take
D_{1}=\varepsilon D_{x},~D_{2}=\delta D_{t}
, and from the coefficient of
\varepsilon\delta
term in the
expansion we get

2D_{x}(D_{t}a\cdot c)\cdot(ac)=(D_{x}D_{t}a\cdot a)c^{2}-(D_{x}D_{t}c\cdot c)a^{2};

(4.5)

from
\varepsilon^{4}
term we get

2D_{x}[(D_{x}^{3}a\cdot c)\cdot(ac)-3(D_{x}^{2}a\cdot c)\cdot(D_{x}a\cdot c)]=(D_{x}^{4}a\cdot a)c^{2}-(D_{x}^{4}c\cdot c)a^{2}.

(4.6)

In general, by specially taking
D_{i}
in the formula (
4.1
) and
comparing coefficients of
\{\varepsilon,\delta\}
, one can obtain various bilinear identities.
They play important roles in constructing bilinear BTs
and nonlinear superposition formulae based on the bilinear BTs.

4.2
Bilinear Bäcklund transformations

In this section we take the bilinear KdV equation (
2.16
), i.e.

(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})f\cdot f=0

(4.7)

as an example, to illustrate how a bilinear BT is constructed and how it works in finding solutions.

Suppose that
g
is also a solution of (
4.7
), i.e.

(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})g\cdot g=0.

(4.8)

Then the following relation holds

g^{2}(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})f\cdot f-f^{2}(D_{x}D_{t}+D^{4}_{x})g\cdot g=0,

(4.9)

i.e.

[(D_{x}D_{t}f\cdot f)g^{2}-(D_{x}D_{t}g\cdot g)f^{2}]+[(D^{4}_{x}f\cdot f)g^{2}-(D^{4}_{x}g\cdot g)f^{2}]=0.

(4.10)

Now, employing the identities (
4.5
) and (
4.6
), (taking
a=f,c=g
), one can rewrite (
4.10
) as

2D_{x}[(D_{x}^{3}+D_{t})f\cdot g]\cdot(fg)+6D_{x}[(D_{x}f\cdot g)\cdot(D^{2}_{x}f\cdot g)]=0.

(4.11)

Next, introduce

D^{2}_{x}f\cdot g=\lambda fg,

(4.12a)

where
\lambda
is a constant independent of
x
.
Substituting it into (
4.11
) yields

2D_{x}[(D_{x}^{3}+D_{t}+3\lambda D_{x})f\cdot g]\cdot(fg)=0.

Then we can take

(D_{x}^{3}+D_{t}+3\lambda D_{x})f\cdot g=0.

(4.12b)

The two equations, (
4.12a
) and (
4.12b
),
compose a coupled system which provides a bilinear BT of the KdV equation (
2.12
).
In fact, from the above procedure we can see that when (
4.12a
) and (
4.12b
) hold, equation (
4.9
) holds.
In other words,
if
f
is a solution to (
4.7
), so is
g
to (
4.8
), due to (
4.9
).
The above idea is due to Hirota
[
27
]
.

In many cases, using a bilinear BT to calculate soliton solutions
is not as convenient as directly using the original bilinear equation.
Now we show some details. First, taking
\lambda=\frac{k^{2}_{1}}{4}
and
f=1
(noticing that
f=1
is a solution of (
4.7
)),
and substituting them into (
4.12
), one can find that
g
needs to satisfy the following
linear equations:

\displaystyle g_{xx}=\frac{k_{1}^{2}}{4}g,

\displaystyle g_{t}+g_{xxx}+\frac{3}{4}k_{1}^{2}g_{x}=0.

As a solution we can take

g=g_{1}=e^{\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}},

where

\eta_{i}=k_{i}x-k_{i}^{3}t+\eta_{i}^{(0)},~~(k_{i},\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}).

(4.13)

Thus, 1SS of the KdV equation (
2.12
) can be expressed by
u=2(\ln g_{1})_{xx}
.

Next, taking
f=g_{1},~\lambda=\frac{k^{2}_{2}}{4}
and substituting them into (
4.12
), we have

\displaystyle(D^{2}_{x}-\frac{k^{2}_{2}}{4})g\cdot(e^{\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}})=0,

(4.14a)

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{3}+D_{t}+\frac{3}{4}k^{2}_{2}D_{x})g\cdot(e^{\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\eta_{1}}{2}})=0.

(4.14b)

To get a solution for
g
,
we assume
g
to be the following form:

g=g_{2}=\alpha(e^{\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}{2}})+\beta(e^{\frac{\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}}{2}}+e^{-\frac{\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}}{2}}),

where
\eta_{i}
is defined as (
4.13
) and
\alpha,\beta
are undetermined constants.
Substituting the above
g
into (
4.14
) we find: when
\alpha=k_{1}-k_{2}
and
\beta=-(k_{1}+k_{2})
,
g
satisfies (
4.14
).

Next, we can take
f=g_{2},~\lambda=\frac{k^{2}_{3}}{4}
, and from (
4.12
) we solve out solution
g=g_{3}
.
However, it is apparent that the above procedure is not as “mechanized” as the one we used in Sec.
2.2.1
to
calculate NSS from the bilinear KdV equation (
2.16
).
In general we can successively take
\lambda=\frac{k^{2}_{j}}{4},~j=1,2,\cdots,N
to calculate higher order solutions;
the general formula of
g
for a generic
N
has the following form:

g_{N}=\sum_{\varepsilon=\pm 1}\left[\,\prod_{1\leq j<l}^{N}\varepsilon_{l}(\varepsilon_{j}k_{j}-\varepsilon_{l}k_{l})e^{\frac{1}{2}\sum^{N}_{j=1}\varepsilon_{j}\eta_{j}}\right],

(4.15)

where
\eta_{j}
is defined as (
4.13
), the summation over
\varepsilon
means to take all possible
\varepsilon_{j}=\{1,-1\}

(j=1,2,\cdots,N)
.

A proof that (
4.15
) satisfies the bilinear KdV equation (
2.16
)
can be found in Chapter 5 of
[
5
]
by making use of Wronskians.

4.3
Deformations of bilinear BTs

We have already seen that using bilinear BT (
4.12
)
to calculate soliton solutions is not as convenient as using the original bilinear equation (
2.16
).
The reason is
f=1,~g=1
are not a solution pair to (
4.12
).
To change such a situation, we try deforming bilinear BTs.

In (
4.12
) we replace
f
and
g
with
e^{\xi_{1}}f
and
e^{\xi_{2}}g
, respectively, i.e.

f\to e^{\xi_{1}}f,~~~g\to e^{\xi_{2}}g,

(4.16)

where
\xi_{i}=p_{i}x+q_{i}t+\xi^{(0)}_{i},~p_{i},q_{i},\xi^{(0)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Noticing that solutions of the KdV equation can be expressed through
u=2(\ln f)_{xx}
or
u=2(\ln g)_{xx}
,
such a replacement does not change solutions; of course, due to gauge property (
2.11
) of bilinear equations,
such a replacement does not change bilinear equations (
4.7
) and (
4.8
), either.
Using formula (
2.8
), we have

\displaystyle D_{x}(e^{\xi_{1}}f)\cdot(e^{\xi_{2}}g)=e^{\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}}[(p_{1}-p_{2})fg+D_{x}f\cdot g],

\displaystyle D_{x}^{2}(e^{\xi_{1}}f)\cdot(e^{\xi_{2}}g)=e^{\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}}[(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}fg+2(p_{1}-p_{2})D_{x}f\cdot g+D_{x}^{2}f\cdot g],

\displaystyle D_{x}^{3}(e^{\xi_{1}}f)\cdot(e^{\xi_{2}}g)=e^{\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}}[(p_{1}-p_{2})^{3}fg+3(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}D_{x}f\cdot g

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+3(p_{1}-p_{2})D_{x}^{2}f\cdot g+D_{x}^{3}f\cdot g],

\displaystyle D_{t}(e^{\xi_{1}}f)\cdot(e^{\xi_{2}}g)=e^{\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}}[(q_{1}-q_{2})fg+D_{x}f\cdot g].

By means of them we rewrite (
4.12
) into

\displaystyle[D_{x}^{2}+2(p_{1}-p_{2})D_{x}]f\cdot g=[\lambda-(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}]fg,

(4.17a)

\displaystyle\{D_{t}+D_{x}^{3}+3(p_{1}-p_{2})D_{x}^{2}+3[\lambda+(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}]D_{x}\}f\cdot g

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~=-[(q_{1}-q_{2})+(p_{1}-p_{2})^{3}+3\lambda(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2}]fg.

(4.17b)

Introducing

2(p_{1}-p_{2})=\lambda^{\prime},~~\lambda=(p_{1}-p_{2})^{2},~~(q_{1}-q_{2})+4(p_{1}-p_{2})^{3}=0,

so that we can simplify (
4.17a
) to

\displaystyle(D_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}D_{x})f\cdot g=0,

(4.18a)

and further, eliminating
D_{x}^{2}
term in (
4.17b
) yields

\displaystyle(D_{t}+D_{x}^{3})f\cdot g=0.

(4.18b)

Equations (
4.18a
) and (
4.18b
)
compose a deformed bilinear BT of the KdV equation (
2.12
).
Note that this deformed bilinear BT connects with a new discrete integrable equation
(cf.
[
9
]
, equation (5.20)).

Compared with (
4.12
),
the deformed BT (
4.18
) admits solutions
f=g=1
,
which facilitates the calculation: it allows to calculate
f
and
g
by perturbation expansions.
Assuming

f=1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}f^{(i)}\varepsilon^{i},~~~g=1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}g^{(i)}\varepsilon^{i}

(4.19)

and substituting them into (
4.18
) yield

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}\partial_{x})(f^{(1)}-g^{(1)})=0,

(4.20a)

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}\partial_{x})(f^{(2)}-g^{(2)})=-(D_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}D_{x})f^{(1)}\cdot g^{(1)},

(4.20b)

\displaystyle(\partial_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}\partial_{x})(f^{(3)}-g^{(3)})=-(D_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{\prime}D_{x})(f^{(1)}\cdot g^{(2)}+f^{(2)}\cdot g^{(1)}),

(4.20c)

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots;

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{3})(f^{(1)}-g^{(1)})=0,

(4.20d)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{3})(f^{(2)}-g^{(2)})=-(D_{t}+D_{x}^{3})f^{(1)}\cdot g^{(1)},

(4.20e)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{3})(f^{(3)}-g^{(3)})=-(D_{t}+D_{x}^{3})(f^{(1)}\cdot g^{(2)}+f^{(2)}\cdot g^{(1)}),

(4.20f)

\displaystyle\cdots\cdots.

To get solutions, first, we can take
g^{(j)}=0,~(j\geq 1)
, which corresponds to a zero solution
to the KdV equation (
2.12
).
Taking
\lambda^{\prime}=-k_{1}
, from (
4.20a
) and (
4.20b
) we find a solution

f^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{1}},~~\eta_{i}=k_{i}x-k_{i}^{3}t+\eta^{(0)}_{i},~~(k_{i},\eta^{(0)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}),

(4.21)

where note that
\eta_{i}
is as same as (
2.20
).
For those
f^{(j)},~(j\geq 2)
we can trivially take them to be 0.
Thus, a 1SS is obtained by
u=2[\ln(1+f^{(1)})]_{xx}
.

Next, still considering
g
as a new seed solution and taking
g^{(1)}=e^{\eta_{1}}
,
g^{(j)}=0,~(j\geq 2)
,
in the following we will see that the deformed bilinear BT does bring some new aspects.

Case 1: Taking
\lambda^{\prime}=-k_{2}
and assuming

f^{(1)}=ae^{\eta_{1}}+be^{\eta_{2}},

where
\eta_{i}
is defined in (
4.21
),
from (
4.20a
) and (
4.20b
)
we find
a=-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}-k_{2}}
and
b
can be an arbitrary constant.
Next, from (
4.20b
) and (
4.20e
) we find

f^{(2)}=-\frac{b}{a}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}.

For
f^{(j)},~(j\geq 3)
, we can take them to be 0.
Thus, a 2SS is obtained by
u=2(\ln f)_{xx}
, where

f=1+ae^{\eta_{1}}+be^{\eta_{2}}-\frac{b}{a}e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},~~a=-\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{k_{1}-k_{2}}.

(4.22)

Case 2: Still take
\lambda^{\prime}=-k_{1}
(as in getting 1SS). In this case we have

f^{(1)}=\zeta_{1}e^{\eta_{1}},~~~f^{(2)}=e^{2\eta_{1}},

where
\eta_{1}
is defined in (
4.21
),
\zeta_{1}=2k_{1}(x-3k_{1}^{2}t)+\zeta^{(0)},~\zeta^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
f^{(j)}=0,~(j\geq 3)
.
The obtained solution is

u=2(\ln f)_{xx}
, where

f=1-\zeta_{1}e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{2\eta_{1}}.

(4.23)

The solution is known as a double-pole solution (corresponding to the case
k_{2}\to k_{1}
).

The above are two examples. If in (
4.22
) we scale
a,b
to be 1 by redefining the constants
\eta^{(0)}_{i}
,
f
can be written as

f=1+e^{\eta_{1}}+e^{\eta_{2}}+A_{12}\,e^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}},~~A_{12}=\Bigl(\frac{k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}+k_{2}}\Bigr)^{2},

which is the standard Hirota’s form for 2SS of the KdV equation.
If in (
4.22
) we take
b=-a
and the limit
k_{2}\to k_{1}
, we can obtain (
4.23
).
Double-pole solutions correspond to the case that the transmission coefficient
T(k)

in the Inverse Scattering Transform (IST) has
a double pole (note that simple poles leads to solitons).
Double-pole solutions can be obtained from many different ways,
for example, from superposition formula
[
41
]
,
IST
[
74
,
69
]
Darboux transformation
[
52
,
52
]
,
Wronskian technique
[
66
,
75
]

bilinear method
[
6
,
12
]
,
Cauchy matrix approach
[
73
,
76
]
, etc.

The deformed bilinear BTs have advantages in allowing more freedom in calculations and
obtaining more kinds of solutions.
If we keep taking
\lambda^{\prime}=0
in each step of the transformations, we may obtain high order rational solutions.
Besides, rational solutions can also be derived directly from bilinear equations
[
29
]
,
or from superposition formulae
[
1
]
,
or from determinantal approach (e.g.
[
75
]
).
For more examples on deformed bilinear BTs, one can refer to

[
8
,
3
]
, etc.

4.4
BTs and Lax pairs

A bilinear BT appears as an system of equations and actually requires compatibility among these equations.
This fact brings BTs and Lax pairs together.

For the KdV equation (
2.12
), i.e.

u_{t}+6uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=0,

(4.24)

its Lax pair reads (e.g.
[
2
]
)

\displaystyle\phi_{xx}+u\phi=\lambda\phi,

(4.25a)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}=\phi_{xxx}+3(\lambda+u)\phi_{x},

(4.25b)

where
\phi
serves as the eigenfunction and
\lambda
as the spectral parameter.
Noticing that in the bilinear BT (
4.12
),
f
and
g
correspond to two solutions of the KdV equation:

u=2(\ln f)_{xx}
and
\widetilde{u}=2(\ln g)_{xx}
,
we put them together and we have

\widetilde{u}=u+2(\ln\phi)_{xx},~~~\phi=\frac{g}{f},

(4.26)

which is as same as the relation of two solutions of the KdV equation obtained from the Darboux transformation
[
53
]
.

In the bilinear BT (
4.12
), taking

u=2(\ln f)_{xx},~~~\phi=\frac{g}{f},

(4.27)

and rewriting (
4.12
) in terms of
u
and
\phi
directly yield the Lax pair (
4.25
).
In the reverse direction, substituting (
4.27
) into the Lax pair (
4.25
), one can find
the bilinear BT (
4.12
).

In general, once one has a bilinear equation, one can construct its bilinear BT,
and with suitable substitutions from the bilinear BT one can obtained a Lax pair for
the related nonlinear equation.
Hirota extended this idea to the discrete case and found new discrete integrable equations,
e.g.
[
28
]
.

There is a nonlinear BT which was presented by Wahlquist and Estabrook in 1973
[
72
]
:

\displaystyle(\widetilde{w}+w)_{x}=2\lambda-\frac{1}{2}(\widetilde{w}-w)^{2},

(4.28a)

\displaystyle(\widetilde{w}-w)_{t}=\frac{1}{2}[(\widetilde{w}-w)^{3}]_{x}-6\lambda(\widetilde{w}-w)_{x}-(\widetilde{w}-w)_{xxx},

(4.28b)

where
w
satisfies the potential KdV equation (
2.13
), i.e.
u=w_{x}
satisfying the KdV equation (
2.12
).
Once
\widetilde{w}
is solved out from (
4.28
),
\widetilde{u}=\widetilde{w}_{x}
provides a new solution to the KdV equation.

The BT (
4.28
) can be bilinearized
[
29
]
.
Taking

w=2(\ln f)_{x},~~\widetilde{w}=2(\ln g)_{x},

(4.29)

and substituting them into (
4.28
), one can obtain the bilinear BT (
4.12
).

With (
4.29
) and
\phi=g/f
, which yields
\widetilde{w}-w=2(\ln\phi)_{x}
, from (
4.28
)
one can directly obtain the Lax pair (
4.25
) of the KdV equation.

Note: Lax pair, bilinear BT and nonlinear BT
provide different forms for the same thing.
Around the year 1974,
many researchers considered relations between BTs and Lax pairs,
see, e.g.
[
7
,
27
,
42
,
70
,
55
]
, etc.

4.5.1

Note: Here are some remarks on the Bäcklund transformation and Bianchi identity.
For more details one can refer to the book

Historically, Bianchi (in his habilitation thesis in 1879)
introduced a purely geometric construction for pseudospherical surfaces.
He reformulated the construction
in mathematical terms as a transformation

(without any parameter).
Later, Bäcklund (1883) published his celebrated transformation

(
\sigma
is a parameter):

\displaystyle\phi_{u}+\theta_{v}=(\sin\phi\cos\theta+\sin\sigma\cos\phi\sin\theta)/\cos\sigma,

\displaystyle\phi_{v}+\theta_{u}=(-\cos\phi\sin\theta+\sin\sigma\sin\phi\cos\theta)/\cos\sigma,

(4.37b)

and

are independent variables.
It
extends Bianchi’s construction and allows
the iterative construction of pseudospherical surfaces.
Bianchi (1885) showed the above transformation to be associated with an elegant invariance of the sG equation:

\theta_{uu}-\theta_{vv}=\sin\theta\cos\theta,

if
\theta
is a solution and
\phi
satisfies (
4.37
),
then
\phi
is a solution of the sG equation

\phi_{uu}-\phi_{vv}=\sin\phi\cos\phi.

Before Bianchi, Darboux (1883) already found the above invariance for a special case
\sigma=0
.
Later,
Bianchi (1892) demonstrated that Bäcklund’s transformation
\mathbb{B}_{\sigma}

admits a commutativity property

\mathbb{B}_{\sigma_{1}}\mathbb{B}_{\sigma_{2}}=\mathbb{B}_{\sigma_{2}}\mathbb{B}_{\sigma_{1}}
,
which is termed as the “Permutability Theorem”.
Based on the permutability, he was able to obtained a nonlinear superposition formula (Bianchi identity)
of Bäcklund’s transformation.

\displaystyle X(p_{s},q_{s})\cdots X(p_{2},q_{2})X(p_{1},q_{1}){\hbox{\small{$\circ$}}}1=\Biggl(\prod_{1\leq i<j}^{s}A_{ij}\Biggr)\mathrm{exp}\Biggl(\sum_{j=1}^{s}(\xi(\mathbf{t},p_{j})-\xi(\mathbf{t},q_{j}))\Biggr).

(5.30)

Then it is not difficult to get the results in Theorem

.

6
Concluding remarks

In this partial review of the bilinear method, we focused on two topics of the KdV-type bilinear equations:
the integrability based on the 3SS condition
and the transformations between
\tau
functions via BTs and vertex operators.
The existence of BTs also indicates integrability,
while the vertex operators reveal algebraic aspects of
\tau
functions and the related integrable equations.

Physically, the elastic scattering property for arbitrary number of solitons
implies integrability.
For a KdV-type bilinear equation, which always admits a 1SS and a 2SS,
if it is integrable, then we can consider the 2SS as a result of a 3SS after removing one soliton
under the elastic scattering property.
It turns out that such a 3SS must take Hirota’s form (
3.10
) for
N=3
.
Repeating this, one can increase the number of solitons step by step, and finally recover the NSS (
3.10
).
Along the above narration, it can be understood the existence of a 3SS
does mean something to the integrability of a KdV-type bilinear equation.
In Hietarinta’s search for integrable bilinear equations,
except for the KdV-type
[
16
]
,
the mKdV-type and sG-type bilinear equations in his consideration
also admit 1SSs and 2SSs
[
17
,
18
]
.
So, the existence of a 3SS might imply integrability for these types of bilinear equations.
For the NLS-type and BO-type bilinear equations considered in
[
19
]
,
only 1SSs automatically exist, which means the existence of a 2SS
may imply integrability.
Note that recently the author and collaborators developed a procedure to
nonlinearize bilinear equations by using the connections between the Bell polynomials and
Hirota’s bilinear derivatives
[
77
,
49
]
.
This may provide a solution to the question raised by Hietarinta in
[
20
]
:
finding an algorithmic way to nonlinearize bilinear equations.

The BTs, on one side,
indicate a way to generate new solutions and Lax pairs
as well as integrability.
To construct a bilinear BT, except for by means of Hirota’s technique as what we have shown in Sec.
4
,
one can also employ the connections between the Bell polynomials and
Hirota’s bilinear derivatives. Examples can be found in
[
44
]
.
On the other side, BTs provide a way to integrable descretization, which was observed
by Levi and Benguria in 1980
[
46
]
.
For the KdV equation, the nonlinear BT (
4.32a
)
is nothing but the differential-difference potential KdV equation, cf.
[
23
]
,
and its bilinear form (
4.38
) is considered as a
differential-difference potential KdV equation.

For the vertex operators,
we only focus on the way they provide transformations for
\tau
functions.
It is well known that once a vertex operator and its corresponding
\tau
function are secured,
one can have a bilinear identity and then a hierarchy of bilinear equations
that have the
\tau
function as their solution.
A recent progress is a building of the bilinear framework for elliptic solitons
[
47
,
48
]
.
These solutions are obtained by replacing the classical exponential function by the Lamé function,
which describe solitons on an elliptic background.
Researchers are trying to understand more about elliptic solutions from Sato’s theory for integrable systems,
e.g.
[
33
,
57
,
58
]
.
