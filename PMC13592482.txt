Components of generalized onset seizures with focal evolution progressing to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures using quantitative electroencephalography in children: Focal or generalized?
Authors: Juan Toro‐Perez, Erick Sell, Asif Doja, Ana Suller‐Marti, Katherine Muir, Sarah Healy, Srinivas Bulusu, Sharon Whiting, Roberto Caraballo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbsence seizures evolving to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures may have focal or asymmetric clinical features. Using quantitative electroencephalography (EEG) may evaluate this phenomenon and the involved neuronal network. We conducted a retrospective chart review of pediatric patients with generalized epilepsy and normal brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) admitted to an epilepsy monitoring unit. Our results show that seven of 66 patients with generalized epilepsy, all females, had generalized onset seizures with focal evolution (GOFE) to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures. Age at seizure onset was 7.5 years, at epilepsy syndrome diagnosis 8.14 years, and at GOFE diagnosis 13.04 years. Three had juvenile absence epilepsy, two had juvenile myoclonic epilepsy, and two had generalized genetic epilepsy. Eight seizures were recorded. Six patients had normal EEG background and one mild slowing. Quantitative EEG (QEEG) spectrogram with spike detection analysis showed 2.5–3.5‐Hz generalized spike–wave activity followed by focal buildup over the lateral frontal, occipital, and frontal–central regions (right more than left) before bilateral tonic–clonic progression. In conclusion, in our cohort of patients with GOFE, QEEG analysis accurately showed that after absence seizure there is focal activation of the frontal (mainly right) and occipital cortices with evolution to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure. We hypothesize that GOFE could involve the activation of the thalamus and the cortex via the thalamocortical network, based on the QEEG analysis and the literature functional MRI evidence in absence seizures. Additional study of more patients to confirm findings is required.

INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic generalized epilepsy (IGE) is an epileptic syndrome representing 15%–20% of people with epilepsy (a subtype of genetic generalized epilepsy [GGE]), encompassing childhood absence epilepsy, juvenile absence epilepsy (JAE), juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME), and generalized tonic–clonic seizures alone. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 GGE accounts for 23%–43% of new onset epilepsy in children and adolescents. 5 , 6 , 7 The mean age at onset varies depending of combination of absence, myoclonic, myoclonic–tonic–clonic and generalized tonic–clonic (GTC). 7 These types of seizures may present forced head and eyes deviation or version. 3 , 7 , 8 Eighty percent of them respond to the appropriate treatment. 9 The electroencephalogram (EEG) typically shows 2.5–5.5‐Hz generalized spike–wave (GSW)/generalized polyspike–wave (GPSW) elicited by hyperventilation, sleep, or awakening with or without photoparoxysmal response and may have fragmentation. 8 , 10 , 11 , 12

Absence seizures progressing to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures were reported as “the double generalization phenomenon in juvenile absence epilepsy” 13 or “absence‐to‐bilateral‐tonic‐clonic seizure as a generalized seizure type.” 14 Some of these patients may have focal features during the progression to a GTC seizure, a phenomenon referred to as generalized onset seizures with focal evolution (GOFE), 15 which was first described in 2009 by Williamson. 16 The incidence and prevalence of this seizure type is unknown. Continuous video‐EEG monitoring is the gold standard diagnostic tool. Quantitative EEG (QEEG) processes, transforms, and analyzes the digital EEG signals using complex mathematical algorithms, giving information about specific frequency bands, signal complexity, simplistic analysis of connectivity, and network analysis. 17 , 18

Following the International League Against Epilepsy seizure classification and QEEG analysis, we hypothesize that absence seizures may lead to focal evolution before progressing to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures. The primary objective of this study was to characterize the electrographic abnormalities and possible network underlying this transition from an absence to a bilateral tonic–clonic seizure in patients with generalized epilepsy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We conducted a retrospective chart review of all patients with GGE admitted to the epilepsy monitoring unit (EMU) of a provincial pediatric epilepsy center during May 2014 to May 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Children's Hospital of Eastern Ontario Research Ethics Board (REB protocol no: 22/56X, ROMEO file no: 20221223).

We analyzed clinical history, EMU EEG recordings, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and genetic tests to identify patients with both absence and generalized tonic–clonic seizures. Inclusion criteria included the following: absence seizures progressing to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures, normal brain MRI, and 48–72 h of video‐EEG recording. Exclusion criteria included the following: patients with severe developmental epileptic encephalopathy.

EMU EEGs were recorded with XLTEK EMU‐40 with a sampling rate of 1024 Hz and a bandpass filter of .3 and 70 Hz. EEG data were reviewed with NeuroWorks software version 9.1. Selected EMU EEGs underwent QEEG analysis using Persyst software (version P13). The specific algorithms used for analysis were as follows: EEG view in longitudinal bipolar montage and EEG view in Laplacian montage with artifact reduction on and both EEG and trends in spike/seizure detection view for morphology, voltage, and quantification.

Three certified epileptologists (J.T.‐P., S.W., R.C.) reviewed the EEGs using the following criteria. To identify GOFE progressing to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure, a regular 10–20 system EEG view in longitudinal bipolar montage was used. The parameters utilized in this study were as follows: 1‐Hz low‐frequency filter (LFF), 70‐Hz high‐frequency filter (HFF), 60‐Hz notch, 10‐μV/mm sensitivity, and 30‐mm/s time base (TB). To corroborate the seizure and its focal evolution (defined by a generalized to focal transition), recording was reviewed in EEG view in Laplacian montage using Persyst analysis with artifact reduction on, 1‐Hz LFF, 70‐Hz HFF, 60‐Hz notch, 10‐μV/mm sensitivity, and 30‐mm/s TB. To evaluate the neuronal network involved in GOFE to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure, a split screen containing both the EEG view (same to corroborate seizure and 15‐s duration) and the trend view windows showing spike/seizure detection allowed us to adjust settings. The types of discharge were displayed using a color‐coded scheme: green bar for generalized spike detection (0–3 per second), dark red bar for right hemisphere spike detection (0–3 per second), dark blue bar for left hemisphere spike detection (0–3 per second), dark red bar for right hemisphere spike rate threshold (≥3 per 10 s), dark blue bar for left hemisphere spike rate threshold (≥3 per 10 s), and light red bar for seizure probability.

RESULTS

Six hundred twenty‐seven patients were admitted to the EMU during May 2014 to May 2024; 66 of them had a diagnosis of GGE. All of them had absence and GTC seizures. Seven patients, all females, had GOFE and were included. Eight seizures were recorded (two seizures from Patient #2), and the interrater reliability was .81–1. Three patients had JAE, two had JME, and two had GGE. One of the patients with JAE had a 15q11.2 interstitial loss, whereas the patients with GGE had pathogenic variants in SLC13A5 and CFTR (ΔF508 and L206W). We included the patient with SLC13A5 due to the absence of severe psychomotor delay as well as because her EEG did not support an ongoing epileptic encephalopathy, which was in keeping with a milder phenotype. The mean age at seizure onset was 7.5 years (range = 1–12, SD ±5.0139), at epilepsy syndrome diagnosis was 8.14 years (range = 1–13, SD ±5.1455), and at GOFE diagnosis was 13.04 years (range = 4–16.5, SD ±4.2338; see Table S1).

With respect to the seizure types, all had absence and GTC seizures; four reported myoclonic seizures and one focal motor seizures (SLC13A5). Perinatal history was unremarkable. Six of them had normal neurodevelopment (one had mild motor delay). Comorbidities reported were depression (2/7), suicidal ideation (2/7), anxiety (1/7), functional neurological disorder (1/7), migraine (1/7), cystic fibrosis (1), and asthma (1/7).

The EMU video‐EEG recording showed a normal background with appropriate posterior dominant rhythm in six of seven and runs of generalized 5–6‐Hz theta activity with normal posterior dominant rhythm in one (SLC13A5). Intermittent photic stimulation elicited photo paroxysmal response in four patients and triggered a GOFE in one at 12 Hz. Hyperventilation triggered absence seizures in six patients, absence status epilepticus in one, and myoclonic seizures in one. DISCUSSION

GOFE is an electroclinical pattern in which generalized onset epileptic activity, manifesting as an absence seizure, evolves into focal discharges during the same ictal event, often followed by bilateral tonic–clonic activity. This electroclinical pattern has been described in patients with GGE. In a group of patients with GGE, the distinction between generalized and focal epileptiform activity can be blurred, with features that fall in between these two extremes, and in some cases, there may be an overlap. 15 , 16 , 19 These seizure patterns pose a diagnostic dilemma and are a challenge for even the most experienced epileptologists.

The cohort of patients with GOFE studied in our institution were all females; this is a meaningful finding. We have no explanation for this, however, including our cases, 60% are females, 28% males, and 12% unknown (see Table S2). More work is needed to determine why females may be more at risk of this pattern. Clinically and electrographically, our patients showed features similar to those reported in the first description of this seizure type (2009), which included six patients with IGE showing generalized onset seizures with focal EEG and clinical evolution. 16 Another study in Nashville (2016) reported nine patients with generalized epilepsy who showed semiology concerning for focal seizures, with video‐EEG monitoring confirming GOFE. 19 Recently, GOFE was reported in three patients in Strasbourg (2022), highlighting that GOFE shares many features with GGE and that its recognition is clinically important to avoid misdiagnosis of focal epilepsy and consequently the inappropriate use of narrow spectrum antiseizure medications. 20 Recently, another study reported that focal electroclinical features are commonly seen in generalized seizures, whereas GOFE represents a largely unknown ictal variation in GGE, bearing a risk of misclassification (see Table S2). 21 In some cases, the prominent focal signs could lead to intensive video‐EEG monitoring as part of a presurgical evaluation even including stereo‐EEG. 15 , 19 , 22 Recognizing this seizure type implies treatment reassessment including the use of broad spectrum antiseizure medication such as valproic acid alone or in association with lamotrigine. 22 Additionally, some specific single gene genetic conditions as SLC13A5 have different seizure types, including absence seizures with the main electrographic patterns including 3‐Hz generalized spike and wave showing features of IGE. 23

Three theories were proposed to explain this seizure type. 24 The first is focal cerebral dysfunction or a focal/lateralized weakness of seizure initiation–termination mechanisms. 16 The second is a focal ictal transformation of an IGE seizure variant due to a coexistent focal epilepsy (not seizures), which may require more complex management. 24 The third proposes that existing hyperexcitable cortical areas or systems may continue firing independently after the diffuse/generalized corticothalamic oscillations are inhibited. 25

Furthermore, a review of generalized absence seizures described the presence of different motor clinical manifestations, even with the same electrographic pattern, which could be related to the broad neuronal network involved in the seizure generation and seizure propagation, implying thalamocortical fibers. 26 This statement is reinforced by functional brain MRI studies, which reported the involvement of the thalamus, caudate nuclei, and default mode areas during generalized spike–wave paroxysms and absence seizures. Moreover, they confirm high synchronization activity in the thalamocortical network by demonstration of blood oxygen level‐dependent (BOLD) activation in the thalamus, the frontomesial cortex, and the cerebellum, and BOLD deactivation in default mode areas. 27 , 28

In our cohort of patient, we found a considerable right‐lateralized frontal predominance. Woermann et al. analyzed a voxel‐based morphometry of 35 patients with IGE, reporting subtle changes in the frontal lobe by MRI. 29 Seneviratne et al. reported volume reduction or increase in the frontal and thalamic cortical areas in a voxel‐based morphometry study. 28 Kim et al. reported small volume of cortical gray matter in the right frontal lobe as well as regional smaller volume in the rostral and middle frontal, right lateral orbitofrontal, right precentral, right superior and middle temporal regions. Also, small volume was noticed in the left middle and inferior temporal regions. 30 The reason the right frontal lobe has more predisposition or impairment in patients with IGE remains unclear. Is there an anatomical predisposition or is this a consequence of the continuous thalamocortical network activation?

Deep evaluation of the seizures with QEEG analysis, specifically using the spectrogram, increases the accuracy of the diagnosis in different seizure types due to sensitivity of 72% to identify seizures and sensitivity of 94% to determine focality of the seizures. 18 QEEG analysis with Persyst software uses deep learning and artificial neuronal networks to identify and classify interictal and ictal epileptiform discharges. Spike detection and seizure detection views are the most accurate tools to monitor background activity, screen for artifacts, analyze waveform morphology, and map voltage field spread. 18 One study investigated under QEEG structured data segmentation including frequency bands, cortical regions, and hemispheric localization for the accurate diagnosis of pediatric epilepsy. 31 In our cohort, the analysis described was applied and confirmed our diagnosis of GOFE. The presence of the green bar (spike detection generalized) time lock with absence seizure suddenly stopped and led to focal evolution in the red bar (right hemisphere) or blue bar (left hemisphere) to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure (red bar in seizure probability and black bars in spike detection in all foci; Figure 1). Subsequently, we confidently halted any further evaluation.

Conclusions

In our cohort of patients with GOFE, QEEG analysis accurately showed that after absence seizure there is focal activation of the frontal (mainly right) and occipital cortices with evolution to bilateral tonic–clonic seizure. We hypothesize that GOFE could involve the activation of the thalamus and the cortex via the thalamocortical network based in the QEEG analysis and the literature evidence of functional MRI in absence seizures. A detailed history, appropriate diagnostic tools such as video‐EEG recording of seizures, and awareness of all diagnostic possibilities can help avoid misdiagnosing GOFE as focal epilepsy, defining appropriate treatment, and may prevent unnecessary presurgical evaluations. Additional study of more patients to confirm findings is required.

Limitations

Although findings from this study suggest that GOFE may be a seizure type in patients with GGE, this cannot be definitively concluded due to the small number of patients (all female) included in a single‐center retrospective study, which limits generalizability. In addition, the variability in seizures recorded across patients, the lack of a GGE control group, and QEEG software restrictions (such as the use of Laplacian montage in some seizures with first focal activations at occipital electrodes [O1, O2]) could contribute to the limitations of this study. Further studies are needed to validate and expand on these findings. In the future, researchers could consider high‐density EEG with standardized or exact low‐resolution electromagnetic tomography source reconstruction, which could help to address the spatial resolution problem. Also, effective connectivity measures such as Granger causality or directed transfer function would add propagation directionality beyond spike density asymmetry. In addition, EEG functional MRI would allow direct testing of the thalamocortical hypothesis. Is well known that patients with GGE do not undergo to stereo‐EEG; however, the use of this invasive study could be individualized in specific types of patients with GGE.

Significance

This is the first reported series of GOFE cases in which all patients were female and in which all EEG recordings were systematically analyzed using QEEG. QEEG applies specific mathematical algorithms, including spectral analysis focused on power density, automated spike detection and localization, and neuronal network analysis, enabling objective assessment of the temporal relationships within EEG recordings and providing an unbiased characterization of neuronal activity. In addition, this series represents the second largest collection of recorded seizures documenting this uncommon and underrecognized seizure type. The systematic application of QEEG in this context may improve diagnostic accuracy for GOFE and help reduce misclassification as focal epilepsy.
