Trends and disparities in ischemic heart disease and cardiogenic shock mortality in the United States: A 25-year national analysis (1999–2024)
Authors: Syed Tawassul Hassan, Anushah Faheem Ilyas, Syeda Lyba Onaiz, Naveed Ahmad, Ali Jan Yaqubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiogenic shock (CS) is a lethal complication of ischemic heart disease (IHD) leading to increased mortality, yet we lack information on long-term mortality patterns and disparities that exist among CS patients with IHD. Our study assesses temporal trends and demographic variations in mortality associated with IHD and CS in the United States.MethodsWe analyzed the CDC WONDER database for adults aged ≥45 years from 1999 to 2024. Deaths listing both IHD and CS as underlying or contributing causes were extracted. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population were calculated, and temporal trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression to estimate the annual percent change (APC).ResultsOverall, 304,230 mortalities were recorded from 1999 to 2024. The overall AAMR decreased from 14 to 10.9 over the study period. However, after initial declines, a noticeable trend reversal occurred, with rates rising sharply from 2011 to 2021 (APC: 4.39). Males exhibited a twofold higher average AAMR than females (13.1 vs. 6.8). Non-Hispanic (NH) American Indians averaged the highest AAMR (11.2) and exhibited a sustained mortality increase spanning nearly the entire study period. Regionally, the highest rates were observed in the South (9.8) and in non-metropolitan areas (10.7).ConclusionDespite an overall 25-year decline, mortality from IHD and CS surged significantly between 2011 and 2021. Persistent disparities among males, NH American Indians, older individuals, and rural populations highlight the need for targeted interventions.Keywords: Ischemic heart disease, Cardiogenic shock, Mortality trends, Healthcare disparities, Epidemiology

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is known to be the leading cause of death globally, affecting 197.2 million individuals and causing 9.1 million deaths and 182.0 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2019 [1]. In the United States (U.S.), it accounts for 30% of all deaths among people over 35 years old, representing a significant impact on public health [2]. Despite the medical advances in treatments and interventional procedures and public health progress over the last few decades that have had a pronounced effect in reducing IHD mortality, the increasing prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors, including obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, poses the threat of reversing these gains [3]. Cardiogenic shock (CS), however, is another critical condition that continues to be a primary cause of mortality and morbidity in the U.S., with data indicating a threefold rise in hospitalizations for CS between 2004 and 2018 pointing towards an emerging healthcare problem [4], [5]. IHD and CS are both associated with increased healthcare service utilization, disability, and mortality among older populations.

With reference to the occurrence of IHD, CS occurs as a result of extensive myocardial ischemia and necrosis, which results in severe left ventricular dysfunction. This results in a vicious cycle of myocardial ischemia, compensatory vasoconstriction with increased afterload, neurohormonal activation, and systemic inflammation because of the release of cytokines and nitric oxide [6]. This results in the failure of other vital organs. The involvement of the right ventricle can further compromise the patient's survival because of septal displacement and impaired left ventricular filling [7].

Significant research has already focused on the mortality rate associated with IHD and CS. However, the mortality rate specifically associated with CS among patients with IHD needs to be better understood. CS is considered one of the most critical complications that can arise from IHD and is associated with a significant increase in mortality rates despite the advancements in modern cardiovascular medicine. This study utilized the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) database to assess the temporal trends and demographic variations in the mortality rate associated with IHD and CS in the U.S. between 1999 and 2024.

Methods

Study setting and population

The CDC WONDER database was used to obtain mortality data relating to IHD and CS in the U.S [8]. Information from death certificates from the Multiple Cause-of-Death Public Use dataset was analyzed to determine cases listing both IHD and CS as either underlying or contributing cause of death. The International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes I20-I25 were used to define IHD, and the diagnosis of CS was made with the ICD 10 code R57.0 [9]. These code selections are supported by previous epidemiological studies [5], [10]. The study population includes individuals over the age of 45, and analysis was conducted at the national, state, and regional levels for all U.S. states and the District of Columbia. The study adhered to STROBE guidelines and did not require review board authorization because the utilized datasets were publicly available and contained deidentified information [11].

Data extraction and variables

We organized the data based on demographic and geographic factors such as age, sex, race and ethnicity, census region, urbanization level, and place of death. Racial/ethnic groups were classified as NH American Indian, NH Asian, NH African American, NH White, and Hispanic. We restricted the age range to 45 years or older because younger age groups had small absolute mortality counts leading to data suppression and unreliable rate estimates. Further, the population was grouped into middle-aged (45–64 years) and older adults (65–85+ years) for the age-stratified analysis. Geographical characteristics encompassed urbanization levels (metropolitan vs. non-metropolitan), census regions (based on U.S. Census Bureau definitions as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West), and states [12]. Place of death data were also analyzed, including medical facilities, decedent's home, hospice facility, nursing home, and other facilities.

Statistical analysis

A direct standardization method was used to calculate age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) as per 100,000 population against the 2000 U.S. standard population [13]. AAMRs were also stratified by sex, race and ethnicity, age groups, census regions, urbanization status, and place of death to evaluate subgroup-specific trends. Temporal trends in AAMRs were analyzed using the Joinpoint Regression Program software (V5.2) [14]. A grid search approach was utilized for the identification of the optimal number of inflection points (joinpoints). By minimizing the Bayesian information criterion, this method selects the best-fitting model and evaluates statistical significance with P < .05 permutation testing. Based on the observed temporal trends, log-linear regression models were used to estimate the annual percent change (APC) at 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Trends were considered statistically significant if the APC differed from zero with P < .05. To ensure accuracy, deaths with counts between one and nine were suppressed according to CDC WONDER guidelines and excluded from analysis. Mortality rates based on fewer than 20 deaths were deemed statistically unstable and excluded to prevent bias from random variation.

Results

Proportional mortality trends across variables

Overall, 304,230 mortalities were recorded in the database from 1999 to 2024, listing both IHD and CS. Males (59.68%) formed the highest proportion of deaths compared to females (40.32%). Deaths were reported the highest in older populations (79.71%) and among individuals with an NH White ethno-racial background (78.27%). The South region (37.96%) and the residents of metropolitan areas (79.49%) experienced the highest mortality among their geographical counterparts. Analysis of place of death data revealed that the highest proportion was recorded in medical facilities (94.19%), followed by nursing home/long-term care facilities (2.18%) and decedent's home (2.07) (Supplemental Table 1) (Supplemental Fig. 1).

Annual overall mortality trends stratification

For individuals with IHD and CS, the overall AAMR substantially decreased from 14 in 1999 to 10.9 in 2024. However, a considerable decline was observed during the initial years (1999–2024), which persisted at a significantly slower rate through 2011, after which a noticeable trend reversal was observed with rates rising sharply from 2011 to 2021 (APC: 4.39*; 95% CI: 3.91 to 5.22), followed by stable modest decline through 2024 (Supplementary Tables 2,3) (Fig. 1).

Gender based trend stratification

Sex stratified analysis revealed that males exhibited twofold higher average AAMR than females (13.1 vs. 6.8). Both genders showed significant decreases in the early and mid-2000s, with an APC of −9.61* (95% CI: −11.24 to −8.57) in females and an APC of −7.79* (95% CI: −8.85 to −7.03) in males. This downward trend continued until 2012 in females and non-significantly through 2011 in males, followed by sharp rises through 2021 with APCs of 4.06* (95% CI:3.37 to 5.69) in females and 4.68* (95% CI: 4.22 to 5.31) in males. However, the trend concluded with non-significant slight decreases among both genders through 2024 (Supplementary Tables 2,3) (Fig. 2).

Ethno-racial trend stratification

Across the study period, NH American Indians averaged the highest AAMR (11.2), followed by Hispanics (9.6), NH Whites (9.5), NH African Americans (9.0), and lastly, NH Asians (8.6). It is important to note that while NH American Indians exhibited the highest AAMR, this group accounted for the smallest absolute number of deaths (n = 1982), which can produce more volatile rate estimates relative to larger population subgroups (Table 1). Significant decreases were observed across all races in the early 2000s, followed by a brief stabilization in rates and a subsequent trend reversal with significant surges observed across most cohorts. NH African Americans experienced a sharp increase from 2011 to 2018 (APC: 6.91*; 95% CI: 5.74 to 11.53), NH Whites from 2012 to 2021 (APC: 4.53*; 95% CI: 3.90 to 5.84), and Hispanics from 2010 to 2021 (APC: 4.72*; 95% CI: 3.96 to 6.75). Notably, NH American Indians exhibited a sustained, significant mortality increase spanning nearly the entire study period (2001–2024; APC: 1.62*; 95% CI: 0.67 to 4.25). However, a sensitivity analysis was conducted to ensure this finding was not an artifact of the 2021 transition from bridged-race to single-race categorizations in the CDC WONDER database. For this NH American Indian datasets were truncated at 2020 and analysis confirmed a robust, significant increase in mortality prior to the coding shift (2001–2020 APC: 2.14*; 95% CI: 1.17 to 4.77). However, in the most recent years (2021–2024), trends plateaued for most groups, but NH African Americans continued to face a significant upward trajectory from 2018 to 2024 (APC: 2.78*; 95% CI: 1.05 to 3.85) (Supplementary Tables 3,4) (Fig. 3).

Age group specific trends

Age stratified analysis identified the older adults (≥65 years; AAMR: 21.2) carry substantially higher burden of mortality compared to middle aged individuals averaging approximately tenfold higher than that of those in their middle ages (45–64 years; AAMR: 2.8). Significant reductions were observed in rates in both cohorts from 1999 to 2005 followed by a brief stability in middle age until 2011 and a slower significant decrease in older adults through 2012 (APC: −1.95*; 95% CI: −3.38 to −0.46). Following that, middle-aged adults experienced sharp increases across two consecutive segments, 2011 to 2017 (APC: 8.23*; 95% CI: 6.99 to 11.38) and 2017 to 2021 (APC: 4.40*; 95% CI: 2.16 to 6.30). Similarly, older adults demonstrated a significant increase from 2012 to 2021 (APC: 4.26*; 95% CI: 3.69 to 5.55). However, these rising trends ceased in the final years of the study (2021–2024), with middle-aged individuals showing a significant decline (APC: −2.77*; 95% CI: −5.28 to −0.90) while older adults experienced a non-significant plateau (Supplementary Tables 3,5) (Fig. 4).

Geographic and regional trends

AAMR varied greatly across different regional subgroups, with documented mortality rates in the upper 90th percentile states (Tennessee, Indiana, Arkansas, Rhode Island, North Dakota, and West Virginia) averaged twice as high as those in the lower 10th percentile group (Minnesota, Colorado, Wisconsin, Alaska, Montana, and Illinois). Regionally, the highest rates were observed in the South (9.8), followed by the West (9.8), then the Northeast (9.2), and lastly, the Midwest (9.0). Geographic trend analysis for the available urban-rural data spanning from 1999 to 2020 further revealed comparable but distinct temporal mortality patterns, with non-metropolitan areas (10.7) having a higher average AAMR compared to metropolitan regions (8.8). Both groups experienced early periods of significant declining trends followed by a brief period of stabilization. Following that a significant trend reversal occurred in both groups with non-metropolitan areas demonstrating a sustained rise in mortality from 2011 to 2020 (APC: 4.16*; 95% CI: 3.19 to 6.87), while metropolitan areas experiencing a sharp surge from 2012 to 2018 (APC: 5.20*; 95% CI: 4.62 to 7.12) before stabilizing with a non-significant rise through 2020 (Supplemental Tables 3, 6–8) (Fig. 5) (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Using the CDC WONDER database, we looked at mortality trends in the U.S. for individuals with both IHD and CS during a 25-year period (1999–2024). To begin, the data reveal an overall drop in AAMRs from 1999 to the early 2010s, followed by a notable flip with significantly increasing mortality trends from 2011 to 2021, before stabilizing in the most recent years. Secondly, substantial variations were detected across demographic groupings, with men and older adults (≥65 years) incurring greater mortality burden than females and middle-aged individuals. Thirdly, substantial variations were found among ethno-racial and geographic groups, with greater mortality rates among NH American Indians, inhabitants of Southern states, and those living in non-metropolitan regions than their counterparts.

The overall AAMRs showed a 22% decrease during a 25-year period. This general decrease, however, covers up critical turning points in the pattern, with the most significant drops noted in the early 2000s when evidence-based methods of cardiovascular therapy gained widespread adoption, including early revascularization techniques, guideline-directed medical therapy, and primary prevention programs [15], [16]. The slowing of mortality declines after 2011 and subsequent reversal with sharp increases from 2011 to 2021 correlates with broader national trends indicating notable increases in CS-related mortality and a decrease in cardiovascular disease mortality improvements [17], [18]. A mechanistic explanation for this inflection point likely involves a convergence of escalating comorbidity burdens, paradoxical shifts in acute care survival, and evolving diagnostic awareness. First, the cumulative population-level burden of the obesity and diabetes epidemics likely reached a critical threshold during this decade, driving more complex, multi-vessel IHD that is increasingly susceptible to cardiogenic shock [3], [19].

Second, national quality-improvement programs successfully and significantly reduced door-to-balloon time for primary percutaneous coronary intervention, but this might have resulted in the survival benefit in a paradoxical way [20]. Further, patients who have previously died due to sudden cardiac issues in the pre-hospital setting are now able to live long enough to reach the intensive care unit and later develop CS [20]. At the same time, the increasingly widespread use of recent mechanical circulatory support devices like Impella and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation has expanded the elderly population with more severe complications, which may not always result in overall improvements in mortality [5]. Lastly, the increased mortality cannot be solely linked to the changes in coding practices in 2015, but the introduction of dedicated multidisciplinary shock teams and standardized shock treatment algorithms significantly increased clinical awareness and likely incentivized explicit recognition and documentation of CS [5]. The dramatic increase in AAMRs leading to 2021 coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, a period characterized by both direct COVID-19 cardiovascular toxicity and indirect healthcare disruptions that occurred at large scale throughout the pandemic. The direct SARS-CoV-2 infection has been shown to cause endothelial dysfunction, myocardial injury, and heighten the risk of thrombosis, exacerbating pre-existing IHD [21]. However, in an indirect manner, the pandemic also resulted in an unprecedented drop in health-care utilization, with many patients avoiding emergency departments during the lockdown, resulting in delayed presentations of AMI and a higher risk of developing irreversible CS upon arrival [21]. In addition, caution is needed when interpreting data from 2020 and 2021 due to coding artifacts associated with the pandemic, as during this period COVID-19 was seen as a prominent cause of death, and may have influenced IHD and CS coding, possibly overestimating these mortality rates as underlying or contributing causes. This is because the relatively small decrease in 2021–2024 is probably a return to pre-pandemic levels of health service use, rather than a clear and sustained improvement in cardiovascular health.

Males had consistently greater AAMRs than females throughout the research period, with average rates around twice as high. Females showed a higher relative decline throughout the early period, which is consistent with previous reports that women have shown quicker improvements in IHD mortality than men [15]. These findings contradict current data on sex differences in CS outcomes. A comprehensive review and meta-analysis of 81 studies, including over 1.6 million patients, discovered that women with CS had 35% higher unadjusted odds of mortality and 10% higher adjusted odds of mortality than men, with a pooled mortality of 40.8% in women against 35.9% in men [22]. Numerous studies have found that women in CS are less likely to undergo invasive cardiac treatments such as coronary angiography, percutaneous coronary intervention, and mechanical circulatory support [22], [23]. When men and women received early treatment within 24 h, in-hospital mortality did not differ considerably, which could point to the possibility that gender variations in outcomes may be due to differences in treatment patterns [24]. It is critical to note that our findings represent population-level mortality rates and should not be interpreted as measures of clinical CS lethality or case fatality. The observed higher rates in males likely reflect a higher population incidence of IHD and CS, rather than inherently worse clinical outcomes once shock has developed. However, these associations should be interpreted cautiously, as our study design doesn't allow causal inference. The greater population-level mortality rates found in males in our study could be due to men having a significantly higher incidence of IHD and CS, although case fatality rates in women are higher once CS starts [25].

Substantial racial and ethnic disparities in mortality were maintained throughout the research period, with NH American Indians having the highest average AAMRs. These categories follow CDC WONDER classifications based on death certificate data and may be misclassified, particularly for Hispanic and American Indian populations [26]. During the early 2000s, all racial and ethnic groups had large decreases in mortality, followed by trend reversals. NH American Indians displayed a marked and consistent significant increase throughout almost the entire time period, while NH African Americans experienced a sharp rise from 2011 to 2018 and continued on this increasing trend through 2024, while all other groups displayed plateauing patterns in recent years. According to the Strong Heart Study, American Indians have at least twice the prevalence of coronary heart disease as all other ethnic groups. Additionally, NH American Indians develop heart problems at an earlier age than NH Whites, with a third of cardiovascular deaths occurring before age 65 [27]. A study of premature myocardial infarction mortality from 1999 to 2020 discovered that NH American Indians had a proportionate mortality rate of 22.8% compared to 14.8% in other racial groups, with no improvement over time despite declining MI mortality rates [28]. These discrepancies might be attributed to the intricate interplay between several social determinants of health, such as financial status and access to medical care [29], [30]. NH African American and Hispanic patients are less likely than NH White patients to undergo invasive cardiac surgery, according to prior research on CS outcomes [31]. The need for focused therapies that address the underlying social and economic problems that affect cardiovascular fitness across racial and ethnic groups is highlighted by the persistent and, in some cases, widening disparities [32].

Though our results showed the greatest differences among NH American Indian populations, these findings should be interpreted in the context of changing epidemiological definitions and methodological limitations. The definition of race and ethnicity in National Vital Statistics System (NVSS) mortality data changed during the study period, where bridged-race categories were used up to 2020, and then single-race categories were implemented in 2021 [33]. This change is an inherent aspect that may affect the comparability of race-stratified trends over the entire 25 years. In addition, the size of the NH American Indian subgroup has historically been underestimated or overestimated on death certificates, leading to what might be termed “false density” of subgroups over time [34], [35]. However, our sensitivity analysis restricted the data to 2020, as we were concerned that 2021 coding might have influenced the observed increase in NH American Indian mortality, but a significant mortality increase (2001–2020 APC: 2.14*) before categorization change indicates a real underlying public health disparity; however, the size of the AAMRs could be affected by these systemic reporting nuances.

An age-stratified study found significant differences in mortality burdens between older adults (≥65 years) and middle-aged adults (45–64 years), with older adults incurring tenfold higher average AAMRs. From 1999 until the early 2010s, both age groups had significant decreases, followed by a trend reversal. The substantial decline in middle-aged persons from 2021 to 2024 contrasts with the non-significant plateau in older individuals. The much larger absolute burden among older persons could be due to both age-related physiological changes and accumulated lifetime exposure to cardiovascular risk factors [36]. This is consistent with current research on CS. The American Heart Association's scientific statement on CS in older people emphasizes that age is a non-modifiable risk factor for death and is related with greater in-hospital mortality at all stages of shock severity, however, they emphasize that age alone should not be used to guide individual patient care decisions in CS, and that a complete multidisciplinary approach is critical to creating best practices for this high-risk group [37]. CS has been reported in more than 10% of people over the age of 75 with acute myocardial infarction [37]. European cohort data found that patients 75 years and older had a considerably greater frequency of CS than younger patients in both STEMI and non-STEMI [38]. According to the FRENSHOCK registry, about one-third of patients with CS in critical care units were over the age of 75, with adjusted hazard ratios for 1-month mortality of 2.5 and 1-year mortality of 2.01 compared to younger patients [39]. The disproportionate increases among younger adults observed in our study are consistent with prior CDC WONDER analyses, which show that young and middle-aged adults (25–69 years) have increasing AAMRs in recent years, with cardiovascular disease and diabetes mellitus-related mortality increasing from 2014 to 2019 [19].

Geographic differences in mortality were significant, with the top 90th percentile rate states averaging almost double the rates of the bottom 10th percentile states. Regionally, the South and West had the greatest AAMRs, while non-metropolitan areas regularly surpassed metropolitan areas. Following early dips, both urban and rural regions underwent trend reversals, with steady gains in rural areas (2011−2020) and rapid spikes in urban areas (2012–2018). WONDER analysis revealed that increases in CS-related death from 2009 onwards were concentrated in the Southern U.S. [40]. Significant regional variation in adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality was found in the analysis of NIS data pertaining to the management and outcomes of acute MI with CS from 2000 to 2016. The Midwest and West had lower adjusted odds of in-hospital mortality, while the South had higher odds than the Northeast [41]. According to an AHA policy statement, total cardiovascular disease, heart disease, and stroke mortality are 18%, 21%, and 12% higher in non-metropolitan areas than in metropolitan areas, which is consistent with the higher death rate in non-metropolitan areas [42]. Counties with age-standardized IHD mortality rates above the 90th percentile were found to be concentrated in central Oklahoma, eastern Kentucky, and the Mississippi River Valley, according to a JAMA analysis of county-level cardiovascular mortality from 1980 to 2014 [26]. A number of factors contribute to the difference in cardiovascular events between urban and rural communities. A new study in JAMA Cardiology found that rural people had a much greater likelihood of coronary heart disease than urban populations, with the greatest disparities among young adults. Furthermore, when controlling for social risk factors such as poverty, educational attainment, lack of nutrition, and housing status, rural-urban disparities were almost completely abolished, showing that socioeconomic dynamics rather than healthcare availability alone may be driving these spatial patterns [43]. Moreover, a longitudinal county-level investigation showed rural-urban status as one of 17 significant socioeconomic characteristics connected with cardiovascular disease mortality, with the rural-urban split remaining stable over the previous decade [44]. These findings underline the importance of concentrated public health measures that target both healthcare facilities and socioeconomic drivers of healthcare in order to minimize geographical inequities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, mortality data derived from death certificates may be subject to inaccuracies in the reporting of underlying causes of death by certifying physicians. Although ICD-10 coding is performed using standardized automated systems and trained coders, misclassification of the cause of death may still occur. Second, the classification of race and ethnicity in NVSS mortality data altered across the research period. Bridged race categories were utilized until 2020, then single-race categories were established beginning in 2021 once race bridging was discontinued. Although consistent aggregated categories were used in this analysis, minor differences in classification may limit the direct comparability of race-specific trends across the entire study period [33]. Third, race/ethnicity misclassification on death certificates has been reported in prior studies, particularly among American Indian and Alaska Native populations, which may affect subgroup estimates [34], [35]. Fourth, urban-rural studies were limited to 1999–2020 due to the lack of county-level age-specific population data necessary to compute age-adjusted death rates in CDC WONDER in the following years. Finally, the ecological retrospective design limits causal inference; thus, relationships discovered between demographic or geographic groupings cannot be applied to individual risk.

Conclusions

We found that overall, there has been an observed decline in AAMRs among individuals with IHD and CS, though a significant sharp rise was noted between 2011 and 2021, which gained stability afterwards. Individuals who were NH American Indians, males, older individuals, from the Southern region, and from non-metropolitan areas were found to have higher mortality rates. While our findings underscore the need for targeted public health initiatives, these recommendations are based on population-level mortality patterns and should be complemented by clinical registry data that can adjust for shock severity and individual treatment patterns. Therefore, it is vital to develop targeted interventions aimed at reducing cardiovascular risk, improving early detection and treatment of CS, and advanced cardiac care. Further research is warranted to identify the determinants and treatment options that might help reduce the burden of mortality associated with IHD complicated by CS.
