 1. INTRODUCTION

Postirradiation examination of tristructural-isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles from the US Department of
Energy Office of Nuclear Energy Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR)
Program’s final irradiation experiment (AGR-5/6/7) is currently underway at both Oak Ridge National
Laboratory (ORNL) and Idaho National Laboratory [1]. The Furnace for Irradiated TRISO Testing
(FITT) was deployed at ORNL under the AGR Program to explore the fission product release from
individual TRISO particles under inert environments [2] as well as the response of individual TRISO
particles in oxidizing environments [3]. The FITT was designed to support controlled heat treatment of
irradiated TRISO particles under oxidizing conditions to study the potential response of the fuel to rare
off-normal air ingress events considered for high temperature reactors. In such a hypothetical accident,
introduction of air to the primary coolant loop while the reactor is at elevated temperatures could oxidize
the fuel. Because the graphitic matrix and outer pyrolytic carbon (OPyC) layer would oxidize in air under
these conditions, the silicon carbide (SiC) layer of the TRISO particles could plausibly experience
oxidation [4]. Because the SiC layer of TRISO particles is the primary structural layer and the primary
barrier to the release of fission products not contained within the kernel, understanding the response of
irradiated TRISO particles to such oxidizing conditions is relevant to establishing TRISO-based reactor
design and operation.

Based on the limitations of destructive SEM imaging methods applied in prior assessments of SiC
oxidation and recession in TRISO particles [3, 5], a new method was developed at ORNL using
nondestructive x-ray computed tomography (XCT) coupled with the FITT. XCT has two significant
advantages for this use. First, because it is nondestructive, it can be used to image the same particles
before and after oxidation, removing the impact of statistical variation in layer thicknesses between
particles. Second, because it is fully three-dimensional (3D), it can capture the full particle response to
oxidation instead of a singular, limited two-dimensional (2D) slice. Previous work at ORNL developed
automated algorithms for segmentation of XCT images of TRISO particles into the kernel and various
coating layers [6]. These algorithms have been successfully applied for geometric measurement of both
as-fabricated and irradiated TRISO particles [7]. Further development of these algorithms was needed for
application of quantitative XCT segmentation to measurement of SiC oxidation and recession. First, a
method to geospatially identify and align XCT images of the same particle in different orientations was
needed to correlate pre- and post-oxidation XCT images of the same particle. Second, an additional
segmentation sub-algorithm was needed to segment the oxidation layer on the outside of the SiC
following heat treatment. Both modifications were successfully completed and demonstrated as part of
this work and are detailed in Section 2.2.

 2. BACKGROUND

2.1 SILICON CARBIDE AND TRISO OXIDATION

Idaho National Laboratory is establishing the Air Moisture Ingress Experiment (AMIX) to assess fuel-
form level oxidation response and radionuclide transport under oxidizing conditions to support
establishing the fuel performance safety basis under rare off-normal air or moisture ingress events [8].
Prior to commissioning, an understanding of the expected response to oxidizing environments was sought
to support development of a targeted test matrix. Historic irradiated fuel sphere testing was conducted on
the KORA system at Forschungszentrum Jülich (FZJ) in Germany and provided initial insights. The
testing in KORA showed a difference in behavior between testing fuel spheres with matrix graphite and
batches of 10 coated particles. The testing of the 10 coated particles showed indication of failure at
1400ºC at ~400 h and rapid failure at temperatures above 1500 ºC [9]. The FITT was deployed to explore

 1

the oxidation test space, with the historic KORA tests as a guide, to help establish the particle level
response prior to AMIX commissioning.

Initial efforts at ORNL explored the oxidation response of individual TRISO particles from the second
AGR irradiation campaign (AGR-2). Details of this effort are found in Skitt et al. [3]. Here multiphase
uranium oxide, uranium carbide (UCO) TRISO particles from AGR-2 Compact 5-4-2 were exposed to
variable oxidizing environments. AGR-2 Compact 5-4-2 was selected as it represented a relatively
average compact across the range in irradiation test conditions, where AGR-2 Compact 5-4-2 experienced
a time-averaged, volume-averaged (TAVA) temperature of 1071ºC, average burnup of 12.03% fissions
per initial metal atom (FIMA), and a fast fluence of 3.14×1025 n/m2. Sets of particles were exposed to
temperatures ranging from 1200–1400ºC in environments ranging from 2–21% O2 (balance N2) for up to
400 h. As-fabricated particles were included in the same tests to serve as control samples. After testing
the integrity of the particles was assessed and the thickness of the oxide layer measured by cross-sectional
analysis using a focused ion beam coupled with scanning electron microscopy evaluation. The measured
oxide thicknesses were similar for irradiated and unirradiated particle. However, after longer exposures
the irradiated particles exhibited greater failure fractions, reaching 60–70% compared with 0–10% for the
unirradiated particles after 400h at 1400ºC, similar to the KORA test [10]. The relationship between
particle failure and oxide thickness is shown in Figure 1.

A general trend of increased thickness coupled with failure fraction was observed. The oxide layer
observed in cross-section of the AGR-2 Compact 5-4-2 particles increased in thickness at more aggressive

test conditions but also complexity as noted by the large uncertainty in Figure 1. A parabolic rate constant
was calculated from the oxidation analysis which can be used to inform oxidation response, however,
measuring only the oxide thickness leaves a key gap as the relative thickness of the oxide does not
directly translate to recession of the SiC layer. The oxide layer was non-uniform around the outer surface
of the particle and the oxide layer structure varied across the thickness of the oxide with variable porosity
and in some instances the observation of a secondary oxide layer [5]. SiC recession was identified as the
critical performance variable as Multiphysics models predict increased particle failure probability with
decreasing SiC layer thickness [11]. Measuring SiC recession directly for particles subjected to oxidation
was identified as a priority metric to interpret particle failure as a function of oxidation response. A
systematic XCT approach was targeted to analyze particle 3D structure before and after oxidation to
quantify SiC layer thickness (recession) and interpret potential failure modes to comprehensively interpret
oxidation response and SiC recession building off oxide thickness analysis.

2.2 XCT QUANTITATIVE SEGMENTATION METHODS

After reconstruction, XCT images consist of 3D matrices of intensity values, in which the intensity of
each voxel represents the relative x-ray attenuation within that volume based on the cumulative input of
the individual underlying radiographs. The raw radiography images are darker in areas of high attenuation
and brighter in areas of low attenuation, however, in this work the reconstructed XCT image sections are
shown inverted with bright areas representing high x-ray attenuation (correlated with high density and
high atomic number) and dark areas conversely representing low x-ray attenuation (correlated with low
density and low atomic number). In XCT images of TRISO particles, the uranium-bearing kernel is the
brightest, while the SiC layer is substantially brighter than the graphitic layers. The previously developed
XCT segmentation algorithm uses these strongly contrasting regions as its starting point. The algorithm
segments the kernel and the SiC layers from the remainder of the particle based on their relatively high
intensity and on geometric rules derived from the known physical structure of the particle (i.e., the kernel
is solid and the SiC is a solid shell). The initialized segmentations of each layer are then refined using a
coupled web process. Each apparent boundary point is assessed based on the anticipated layer thickness
and standard deviation, the local image intensity gradient and texture, and the average position of the
adjoining network of boundary points. A weighted Bayesian process is used to update each point to the
most likely true boundary and then repeated for several iterations until the process converges at the actual
layer boundary [6].

Once the kernel and the SiC layer are confidently segmented, other layer interfaces are initialized based
on known average layer thicknesses. The outer edge of the OPyC layer and the inner edge of the inner
pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) layer are each initialized based on an average thickness for those layers from the
SiC boundary. In unirradiated TRISO, the inner IPyC boundary and the outer buffer boundary are the
same but in irradiated particles with buffer densification, an initial outer buffer boundary is set based on
an estimated thickness for the layer moving out from the kernel. This approach works best because during
buffer densification the kernel and buffer typically remain concentric with each other, while the SiC and
pyrolytic carbon layers remain concentric with each other. Once these layer boundaries are initialized,
they are refined using the same coupled web process used previously, and the iterations continue until
convergence is reached for each layer. Once this process is complete, the final output is a network of
boundary points defining the limits of each layer within the TRISO particle. An arbitrary number of
points may be used, but typically 5,000 unique combinations of spherical angles are used with
approximately even angular spacing. These boundaries may then be used to compute layer volumes or
local parameters such as layer thicknesses or curvatures [6].

The previously developed XCT segmentation algorithm uses these strongly contrasting layers as its
starting point. The algorithm segments the kernel and the SiC layers from the remainder of the particle
based on their relatively high intensity and on some geometric rules derived from the known physical

 3

structure of the particle (i.e., the kernel is solid and the SiC is a solid shell). Once the kernel and the SiC
layer have been segmented, the boundaries between the other layers are initialized based on the known
geometry of the particle. The outer edge of the OPyC and the inner edge of the IPyC are initialized based
on their known approximate thicknesses and the segmented outer and inner edges of the SiC layer. In
irradiated TRISO particles in which the buffer layer may have largely detached from the IPyC, the outer
edge of the buffer layer is initialized based on an approximate known layer thickness and the segmented
outer edge of the kernel. Once all layers are initialized, an iterative refinement algorithm is employed to
find the best fit for each layer boundary. In each iteration, the local best fit for the boundary at each point
is determined based on a weighted combination of image intensity, texture, expected thickness, and the
boundary position at adjacent points in 3D space. This iterative process is completed at least 10 times for
each boundary until the segmentation converges. Then, the final boundaries are converted into a
segmented voxel map of the particle.

 3. PRE-OXIDATION CHARACTERIZATION

Two sets of 30 TRISO particles each were characterized using XCT before oxidation in the FITT. One set
consisted of as-fabricated particles electrolytically deconsolidated from Compact SN1269, representing
typical unirradiated AGR-5/6/7 particles. These particles were picked randomly from the full set of
deconsolidated particles from that compact.

The second set of 30 particles was selected from those electrolytically deconsolidated from irradiated
AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3. This compact was irradiated to 14.77% FIMA and a fast fluence of 5.47×1025
n/m2 at a TAVA temperature of 1,363°C. This represents a relatively high burnup for the AGR-5/6/7
experiment and an upper bounding irradiation temperature for a reference high-temperature gas-cooled
reactor design. After deconsolidation, inventories for key fission products were measured for each particle
using the Irradiated Microsphere Gamma Analyzer (IMGA) system. The 30 irradiated particles for FITT
testing were randomly selected from those with fission product inventories in the Gaussian distributions
that naturally form near the calculated nominal fission product inventory due to variations in as-fabricated
kernel size and irradiation conditions across the compact.

Characterization of the unirradiated and irradiated particle sets was performed in the same system using
the same parameters and sample loading methodology to ensure consistency. Samples were loaded in
Lucite capsules with V-notches designed to hold particles in place during imaging. The walls of the
Lucite in the V-notched area were left deliberately thin to minimize attenuation of low-energy x-rays,
which are crucial for segmentation of low-Z materials such as the buffer and pyrolytic carbon layers in
TRISO. Slip-fit caps were developed for the tops of the Lucite capsules to enable secure containment of
particles for imaging without permanently securing them in place using epoxy, as is commonly done for
XCT of TRISO particles intended for subsequent destructive materialographic preparation. In addition,
smaller Lucite capsules consisting of a V-notch section on the top and a slip-fit cap on the bottom were
developed to enable multiple particles to be loaded into the XCT system at a time, which greatly
enhanced throughput.

Particles were imaged by XCT in a ZEISS Versa 620 system using a source-to-sample distance of 86 mm,
a sample-to-detector distance of 30 mm, and a 20× magnifying objective. These settings combined to
produce a 0.5 μm voxel size. The x-ray source was set to an accelerating voltage of 100 kV and a power
of 14 W. In prior work at ORNL, this voltage provided the best compromise of low-Z and high-Z image
quality for TRISO particles, and 14 W represents the optimal power for the x-ray source at this voltage in
terms of maximizing the number of x-rays generated. A total of 1,601 radiographs distributed evenly over
360° were collected for each particle imaged, and the individual exposure time was 10 s. This yielded a
total imaging time per particle of approximately 6 h, which enabled a stack of three particles to be imaged
each working day while allowing time for sample transfer to and from the hot cells. Radiographs were

 4

reconstructed into 3D intensity images using the integrated ZEISS Reconstructor software with automated
center shift identification and no beam hardening correction.

After reconstruction, the kernel and surrounding layers for each particle were segmented using the
algorithms described in Section 2.2.

The ability to identify particles imaged more than once at random orientations was developed and
demonstrated on surrogate particles prior to this work, removing the need for full particle tracking
between pre- and post-oxidation imaging. Demonstration was done by imaging seven surrogate TRISO
particles by XCT, then having other staff select one at random to be imaged a second time at a random
orientation for matching to the original set. Identification of the selected particle was performed in two
steps. First, the outer radii of the kernel, buffer, IPyC, and SiC for the blind test particle were each
compared to the values for the set of seven original particles. The OPyC layer was not included, as it will
be burned off during oxidation testing. Data for each particle are given in Table 1, with radii from the
original set of particles which are close to those of the blind test particle bolded. Based on this initial
analysis, Particle 1 was the most likely match, but a second confirmatory step was used after this initial
analysis. As shown in Figure 2, the local curvature topography for Particle 1 and the blind test particle
were compared and rotated to show alignment of the unique topographical features created by
asphericities present in each TRISO particle, confirming the match.

 4. HEAT TREATMENT IN FITT

4.1 OXIDATION TESTING IMPROVEMENTS

The initial installation of the FITT was previously described by D. Skitt et al. in a journal article on
oxidation results from the previous FITT experiment [5]. At the beginning of FY26, efforts were made to
improve the holder design to overcome particle handling challenges and improve oxygen concentration
monitoring. When operations staff who perform the unloading and loading of the FITT system were
consulted, they reported that the largest challenge with the previous holder design was handling it. Long

 8

tongs were used to handle the holder, and this method risked dropping, losing, or crushing particles. The
new holder, which is shown in Figure 5, secures the particles between two screens with slit sizes of
510 μm. The next challenge with the previous design was the gas flow delivered to the open-faced holder.
The concern was that the gas flowed over and contacted only the topside of the particles. The new holder
overcame this concern by forcing gas down through the holder and up through the screen, thus
surrounding the particles with the gas flow. The goal was a more uniform distribution of gas flow around
the particles. The new holder was fabricated by binder jet 3D printing SiC and performing chemical vapor
infiltration of SiC to provide dense parts. No post-machining was performed. The outer tube and the O2
delivery tube were alumina. A Rapidox 3100 OEM-3 by Cambridge Sensotec was installed to improve
the monitoring of oxygen concentration. This three-channel analyzer enabled oxygen monitoring to be
performed on both FITT furnaces. The third channel was installed outside the contamination area near the
gas supply bottles. This placement allowed checks to be performed on the gas supply prior to the gas
entering the laboratory.

the dwell time.

4.3 POSTTEST PARTICLE HANDLING

Post-test XCT analysis was performed following the oxygen exposure. Following each test, the outer
alumina tube was containerized inside radiological bags and prepped for transport to a glovebox to unload
the SiC holder. Two types of XCT holder were used for this analysis. The type of holder does not impact
XCT results but rather determines how many times the particles are handled by operators and the
efficiency of analysis. Figure 7 shows the triple-stack modular XCT holder and the single-particle epoxy
XCT holder. It also shows the process flow for getting particles out of the FITT and into a holder ready
for XCT analysis. The process flow is conceptual and shows the additional handling of the triple-stack
holder compared to the single-particle holder. FX01 and FX02 used the triple-stack holder because of
efficiency concerns for data collection in the environment of the Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory.
This means that the particles were not secured in a holder until they reached the fume hood location.
During the unloading of the FX01 outer alumina tube in a glovebox, operators found only eight particles.
FX02 originally had 10 particles at IMGA, but by the time they reached the fume hood for loading, only
eight particles remained. The particles were carefully handled, but they were brittle enough that even
small forces could cause fracture. Following this determination, the single-particle holder was used for
FX03 and FX04. This holder secures the particles in epoxy at IMGA following the glovebox unloading.

 5. POST-OXIDATION CHARACTERIZATION

After oxidation in the FITT, particles were transferred back to the same XCT system described in
Section 3 for pre-oxidation characterization. Not all particles survived removal from the FITT sample
holder, and as noted in Section 4.3, the triple-stack holders may have contributed to the loss of some
particles in the FX01 and FX02 tests. XCT imaging of the particles that survived was used to identify
each as either being an as-fabricated or an irradiated particle. Table 3 provides a summary of the particles
recovered from each test.

All five as-fabricated particles were recovered from the 100h FX03 test, with XCT images of each given
in Figure 8. These particles all had intact and relatively thick oxide layers, with a mean oxide layer
thickness across all five particles of 8.99μm and an interparticle standard deviation of 0.87μm. Only two

 11

whole as-fabricated particles and one fragment of an as-fabricated particle were recovered from the 200h
FX01 test, with XCT images of each given in Figure 9. The lack of buffer densification and the buffer’s
full attachment to the IPyC layer in the fragment indicate that it came from an unirradiated particle, and
the lack of oxidation of the inner layers indicates that particle failure occurred during sample handling
after oxidation testing. Interestingly, the oxidation layers on these particles are visibly thinner and rougher
surfaced than those from the shorter FX03 test. This indicated the possibility of spalling and loss of the
oxide layer during testing and handling. The FX01 particles were the first ones tested, and developing
techniques for post-test handling may have contributed to particle loss as this was the only test in which
all five as-fabricated particles were not recovered. Finally, all five as-fabricated particles were recovered
from the 300h FX02 test, with XCT images of each given in Figure 10. Here there is further direct
evidence of oxide layer loss, as three of the particles have relatively thin oxide layers, while two show
varying level of oxide layer delamination. It is possible that the gentler post-test particle handling
procedures implemented after the FX01 test were responsible for these fragile delaminated layers being
partially preserved.

While the as-fabricated particles were generally similar in uniform oxidation response and were
universally recovered after the initial FX01 test, the irradiated particles were more varied. One or two
irradiated particles did not survive recovery in each case and based on the state of the recovered particles
this may have been due to increased fragility following particle failure. Careful analysis of XCT imaging
of the irradiated particles showed that every irradiated particle recovered experienced localized SiC
failure along with general SiC recession and oxidation.

Three irradiated particles were recovered from the 100h FX03 test. One particle shown in Figure 11 failed
fully, with a large section of SiC removed and the kernel and internal layers oxidized and removed. There
was significant oxidation on both the inner and outer surfaces of the SiC layer. The other two particles
shown in Figure 12 and Figure 13 were intact, but close examination identified through-holes in the SiC
layer in both which had apparently been plugged with a thick layer of oxide, preventing full particle
burnout as was seen in Figure 11. The particle in Figure 12 had single hole in the SiC layer with a locally
degraded region around it, while the particle in Figure 13 had three holes in the SiC layer and appears to
have undergone kernel migration into the buffer layer, moving away from the holes.

Four irradiated particles were recovered from the 200h FX01 test. XCT images of three of these particles
with full SiC failure are shown in Figure 14. In each particle there was broad oxidation of the SiC layer,
localized failure of the SiC, and internal layers and a substantial fraction of the kernel have been burned
away. The fourth irradiated particle recovered from this test, shown in Figure 15, had a morphology
similar to the two intact particles from FX01. In this case the localized attack in the SiC layer appears to
not have fully penetrated the layer, and the local oxide layer in the vicinity was relatively thicker than
around the rest of the particle.

Finally, three irradiated particles were recovered from the 300h FX02 test. XCT images of these particles
are shown in Figure 16. All three failed, with significantly more kernel oxidation than was observed in the
comparable failed particles from the 200h FX01 test in Figure 14.

 7. DISCUSSION OF RESULTS

Several interesting results are evident from a qualitative review of the tomographic images obtained. First,
the particle fragment from the FX01 test shown in Figure 9 demonstrates that post-test handling can cause
particle damage and supports the concern raised in prior FITT experiments that some observed damage
may be handling-related. This fragment was clearly from a particle damaged after oxidation in the FITT
because the OPyC layer has been burned away but the other graphitic layers remain untouched. However,
the directly observed SiC degradation and through-layer failures in recovered irradiated particles
demonstrate that handling damage alone cannot account for the observed response.

The second qualitative observation that can be made from the particle XCT images was confirmation that
local oxide layer thickness measurement was insufficient to adequately capture the mechanisms at play in
the oxidation of TRISO particles. In several cases, the as-fabricated particles demonstrated signs of
delamination and loss of part of the oxide layer, whereas in the irradiated particles, failure of the SiC was
observed in every case to be due to localized attack rather than generalized thinning and recession.
Because the as-fabricated and irradiated particles were tested together, the results suggest that as-
fabricated geometric heterogeneity or sample holder interaction alone does not explain the differing
responses. The origin of this heterogenous response remains under investigation and may be associated
with irradiation-induced changes in the particles.

This leads into the third qualitative observation: the oxidation behavior in the as-fabricated and irradiated
particles fundamentally differed in each of the FITT tests completed. Particles of both types were mixed
together in the same sample holder and were nominally subjected to the same oxidizing conditions.
However, the as-fabricated particles consistently presented relatively uniform oxide layers and associated
uniform SiC thinning, whereas the irradiated particles had scalloped, uneven oxide layers with increased
delamination and localized degradation of the SiC layer, leading to particle failure. This differing
behavior of unirradiated and irradiated particles contrasts with what was previously observed [5] and may
be a result of the relatively high burnup and irradiation temperature of these particles, possibly in
combination with the finer grain size of the AGR-5/6/7 SiC layer compared with prior AGR fuels,
although this dataset does not isolate these effects [15].

The three recovered irradiated particles recovered each showed varying levels of localized SiC attack
failure. FX01-02 appears to have had a small portion of intact SiC remaining in the degraded region, and
had no significant internal changes compared to the pre-FITT XCT of the same particle as can be seen in
Figure 18 and Figure 20. FX03-01 had a full through-hole in the SiC layer and had substantially more
high-Z dispersion than its pre-FITT XCT image showed, as well as incipient kernel migration into the
buffer layer as can be seen in Figure 12 and Figure 20. Finally, FX03-04 had three through-holes in the
SiC layer, and experienced significant kernel migration and high-Z dispersion as shown in Figure 19 and
Figure 21. The presence of dense oxide phase in the local SiC though-layer failure may influence overall
release of radionuclides in oxidizing environments as an open pathway to release (e.g., fracture) was not
immediately established. However, determination of any effect requires further examination.

The quantitative results given in Table 4 represent the successful demonstration of a new capability to
measure not just oxide layer growth but actual SiC layer recession on a per-particle basis. While the
number of particles which could be analyzed was limited due to the high particle failure rates for
irradiated particles in this experiment, the demonstration of this method represents a step forward in the
scientific understanding of irradiated particle oxidation response. Equally important, the full-particle

 25

imaging enabled by XCT successfully identified SiC layer failures in ostensibly intact particles with small
SiC through-holes. It is likely that less comprehensive post-test analysis would have missed these
localized failure points entirely. This result also points to a broader issue that TRISO failure under
oxidizing conditions may be less related to general SiC layer recession and more related to localized
failure.

 8. CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE WORK

To address the characterization limitations of prior TRISO oxidation experiments, XCT imaging with
quantitative segmentation was applied to sets of 30 as-fabricated and irradiated particles from the AGR-
5/6/7 experiment. These particles were then oxidized at 1,400°C under a 20% O2 atmosphere for periods
of 100–400 h in the FITT at ORNL. After oxidation, particles were again imaged using XCT with
quantitative segmentation. The ability to quantify the degree of SiC recession and oxidation was
successfully demonstrated for the set of three irradiated particles which survived intact from these
experiments. As discussed herein, several relevant results were also identified from a qualitative
assessment of the post-FITT as-fabricated and irradiated particle XCT images. Most notably, the observed
delamination of the outer oxidation layer demonstrated the necessity of direct measurement of SiC
recession, and in the irradiated particles, localized SiC failure modes were identified indicating that
unirradiated particles may not adequately represent the response of irradiated fuel under postulated air-
ingress accident conditions.
