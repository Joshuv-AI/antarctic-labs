Hilbert’s 16th problem for arrangements of curves on a surface
Giacomo Maletto

Abstract
. We introduce a combinatorial structure ( n , W , T ) (n,W,T) encoding the topological type of a curve transverse to a fixed cellular arrangement of curves on a compact real surface, in terms of intersection numbers, Dyck words and rooted trees. We apply this formalism to analyze a natural generalization of Hilbert’s 16th problem to arrangements of curves. We obtain a complete classification of arrangements of three lines and a cubic, and a partial classification of arrangements of three lines and a quartic. This is achieved using Bézout-type obstructions, Viro’s patchworking and translations, and by developing the Julia library NWT to handle large databases of curves.

An example

Let us look at any curve, for example

1.2.
Motivation and history

The problem we pose has already been studied successfully in many cases:

1.2.1.
d=(d)
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
: Hilbert’s original 16th problem

In its English translation published
[
10
]
in 1902, the first part of Hilbert’s 16th problem is stated as follows:

16. PROBLEM OF THE TOPOLOGY OF ALGEBRAIC CURVES AND SURFACES.

The maximum number of closed and separate branches which a plane algebraic curve of the
d
-th order can have has been determined by Harnack. There arises the further question as to the relative position of the branches in the plane. […]
A thorough investigation of the relative position of the separate branches when their number is the maximum seems to me to be of very great interest, and not less so the corresponding investigation as to the number, form, and position of the sheets of an algebraic surface in space.

In our terms, the problem can be framed as follows: find out which classes of
1
-arrangements are
(d)
-realizable.

A nonsingular algebraic curve of degree
d
(or more generally, a complete 1-dimensional topological submanifold)
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
admits a particularly easy description
[
27
]
. Its connected components are all homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Since the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
has two elements, we have two cases:

•

those connected components that are non-trivial as elements of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
are called
pseudolines
, and there are none if
d
is even and one if
d
is odd;

•

the connected components trivial in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
are called
ovals
, and each of them separates
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
into two connected components, one of which is orientable and is called the interior of the oval.

A
region
is a connected component of the complement of
\mathcal{C}
. Based on the observation that there is always a unique region whose closure is non-orientable (which is the complement of all interiors of ovals), we can associate to the curve
\mathcal{C}
a rooted tree, whose vertices are the regions, which are connected by an edge if the closures of the two corresponding regions intersect, and with root the unique non-orientable region. This tree, together with the binary information of whether the curve has a pseudoline or not, completely determines the topological type of the curve, and is commonly called its
real scheme

[
19
]
. Our construction of combinatorial curves is a generalization of this phenomenon to larger arrangements.

Figure 4
.

Figure 5
.
Topological types of quintic curves.

For example, for
d=4
there are 6 topological types, namely the quartics consisting of 0,1,2,3 or 4 non-nested ovals, or 2 nested ovals
[
18
]
(shown in Figure
4
).

Hilbert himself worked on the case
d=6
of sextic curves on the plane, arriving “by a complicated process” to a classification of the possible topological types – however, his arguments were incomplete, and only in 1969 the classification was completed by Gudkov, by fixing a mistake he himself made in 1954
[
26
]
.

The classification for
d=7
was completed by Viro
[
24
]
in 1979, for
d=8
it is still open but much is known
[
17
]
while for
d\geq 9
our picture is very incomplete.

1.2.2.
d=(d)
in other surfaces

There are many works on Hilbert’s 16th problem dealing with real curves on surfaces other than
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
29
]
, for instance on quadrics
[
3
,
15
]
, Hirzebruch and ruled surfaces
[
28
,
2
]
and real del Pezzo surfaces
[
16
]
. Additionally, Viro’s Patchworking Construction
[
25
]
works for any toric surface.

We note that the techniques described in this article also work for any other nonsingular surface, but a similar classification for e.g. curves of a given bidegree
(d,e)
on
\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{R})
or other toric varieties is relegated to future works.

1.2.3.
d=(1,1,\dots,1)
: Folkman-Lawrence topological representation theorem

The Folkman-Lawrence topological representation theorem
[
5
]
gives a correspondence between arrangements of pseudolines and rank 3 oriented matroids. The question of which of these classes are realizable from arrangements of lines becomes the question of which oriented matroids are stretchable, which is a NP-hard problem
[
23
]
[§ 5.3].

Our article suggests another way to compute the possible pseudoline arrangements
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k+1})
given a fixed pseudoline arrangement
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k})
: these correspond exactly to the floatless combinatorial curves on
(\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{k})
whose topological type is that of a pseudoline, and respecting
(1,\dots,1)
-Bézout at order 0. The stretchability question is thus equivalent to the question of
(1,\dots,1)
-realizability of these configurations.

1.2.4.
Toric varieties: GKZ’s refined Hilbert problem

In
[
6
]
[§ 11.5] the “refined Hilbert problem” is introduced. Given a finite subset
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{k-1}
and
Q=\conv(A)
its convex hull, we denote by
\mathbb{R}^{A}
the space of polynomials of the form
\sum_{\omega\in A}a_{\omega}x^{\omega}
where
x^{\omega}=x_{1}^{\omega_{1}}\dots x_{k-1}^{\omega_{k-1}}
. Any non-zero
f\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
naturally defines a hypersurface
Z_{f}
inside the toric variety
X_{A}
. Suppose
X_{A}
is smooth. The subset

\nabla_{A}(\mathbb{R})=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{A}\mid\text{$Z_{f}\subset X_{A}$ is singular}\}

forms an algebraic hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{A}
, called the
A
-discriminantal variety
, and the
natural Hilbert problem
is the study of the connected components of the complement
\mathbb{R}^{A}\setminus\nabla_{A}(\mathbb{R})
, corresponding to the study of the hypersurfaces
Z_{f}(\mathbb{R})
up to rigid isotopy in
X_{A}(\mathbb{R})
. Letting

\mathbb{R}^{A}_{\text{gen}}=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{A}\mid Z_{f}\pitchfork X^{0}(\Gamma)\text{ for all faces $\Gamma\subseteq Q$}\}

where
\pitchfork
denotes transverse intersection (i.e.
X\pitchfork Y
if
\codim(X\cap Y)=\codim(X)+\codim(Y)
and
X\cap Y
is smooth) and
X^{0}(\Gamma)
is the orbit in
X_{A}
associated to a face
\Gamma
, the
refined Hilbert problem
is the study of the connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{A}_{\text{gen}}
and corresponds to the study of arrangements formed by
Z_{f}(\mathbb{R})
and the orbits
X^{0}(\Gamma)
intersecting transversely up to rigid isotopy.

For an integer
d>0
, if
A=\conv\{(0,0),(d,0),(0,d)\}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
then
X_{A}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and the orbits
X^{0}(\Gamma)
correspond to the points
(1:0:0),(0:1:0),(0:0:1)
, the axes
\{x=0\},\{y=0\},\{z=0\}
and
\mathbb{P}^{2}
itself. The refined Hilbert problem thus becomes the classification up to rigid isotopy of nonsingular projective algebraic curves of degree
d
intersecting
\{xyz=0\}
transversely. Thus, our analysis of
(1,1,1,d)
-realizability is a weakening of this problem to up to topological equivalence (which in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is equivalent to ambient isotopy) instead of rigid isotopy.

1.3.
Notation

The open and closed unit balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are denoted respectively
\mathbb{B}^{n},\mathbb{D}^{n}
. The real plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
is parametrized by coordinates
(x,y)
, while the projective real plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
has homogeneous coordinates
(x:y:z)
.

The set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is denoted
[n]
.

A tuple/list of elements of a set
A
is an element
a\in A^{<\infty}=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}A^{i}
. The only difference between a tuple and a list is that to denote its components, the notation
a_{i}
is used for the former and
a[i]
for the latter. The concatenation of two tuples/lists
a,a^{\prime}\in A^{<\infty}
is denoted
a^{\frown}a^{\prime}
.

A Dyck word is a list of elements of
\{\text{``(''},\text{``)''}\}
in which the same number of “(” and “)” is used, and at no point of the list there are more preceding “)” than “(”.

Borrowing dependent pairs from type theory, for a set
A
and an indexed family of sets
\{B_{a}\}_{a\in A}
we use the following notation:

\sum_{a\in A}B_{a}:=\{(a,b)\mid a\in A,b\in B_{a}\}.

2.
Cellular decompositions of a surface

Our treatment of surfaces and curves is purely topological - smoothness is never mentioned - although we require submanifolds to be “neat” in the sense of
[
11
]
[1, §4]. The given topological definition of transverse intersection reflects the definition of neat submanifold, and is weaker than the standard
C^{1}
definition involving tangent spaces, but it is sufficient for our needs.

2.1 Definition
.

A
surface

\mathcal{S}
is a compact 2-dimensional topological manifold, with boundary
\partial\mathcal{S}
and interior
\interior\mathcal{S}
. A
curve on
\mathcal{S}
is a closed set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathcal{S}
which is locally flat, i.e. for all
p\in\mathcal{C}
there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\ni p
and a homeomorphism

(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{U})\simeq\begin{cases}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\{y=0\})&\quad\text{if $p\in\interior\mathcal{S}$}\\
(\{x\geq 0\},\{y=0\})&\quad\text{if $p\in\partial\mathcal{S}$}.\end{cases}

A curve is not required to be connected, i.e. it can be empty or can have multiple components, which are necessarily homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{1}
if disjoint with
\partial\mathcal{S}
(in which case they are called
ovals
), or to
\mathbb{D}^{1}
with the two distinct boundary points in
\partial\mathcal{S}
.

Two curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{S}
have
transverse intersection
(in symbols
\mathcal{C}\pitchfork\mathcal{D}
) if

•

the intersection
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}
is finite;

•

for every
p\in\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}
, there exists an open neighborhood
\mathcal{U}\ni p
and a homeomorphism
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{U},\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{U})\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{2},\{x=0\},\{y=0\})
.

A collection of curves
\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}
on
\mathcal{S}
have
transverse intersection
if they have pairwise transverse intersection and no three of them have a common point.

A
k
-arrangement
on
\mathcal{S}
is a
k
-tuple
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
of curves on
\mathcal{S}
that intersect transversely. The
regions
determined by the arrangement are the connected components of
\interior\mathcal{S}\setminus\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

The following definition is a slight strengthening of the usual definition of CW complex
[
9
]
[Appendix] of dimension 2.

2.2 Definition
.

A
cellular decomposition
of
\mathcal{S}
is a tuple
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
coming from a filtration

\varnothing=\mathcal{X}_{-1}\subset\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}_{1}\subset\mathcal{X}_{2}=\mathcal{S}

of closed sets, such that

•

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{X}_{0}
is the set of
vertices
,

•

\mathcal{E}
is the set of connected components of
\mathcal{X}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{0}
, which are called
edges
,

•

\mathcal{F}
is the set of connected components of
\mathcal{X}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{1}
, which are called
faces
,

together with fixed continuous maps
\varphi_{e}\colon\mathbb{D}^{1}\to\overline{e}
and
\varphi_{f}\colon\mathbb{D}^{2}\to\overline{f}
called
characteristic maps
such that

•

\varphi_{e}|_{\mathbb{B}^{1}}\colon\mathbb{B}^{1}\to e
is a homeomorphism,
\varphi_{e}(\mathbb{D}^{1})=\overline{e}
and
\varphi_{e}(\partial\mathbb{D}^{1})\subseteq\mathcal{V}
for all edges
e\in\mathcal{E}
;

•

\varphi_{f}|_{\mathbb{B}^{2}}\colon\mathbb{B}^{2}\to f
is a homeomorphism,
\varphi_{f}(\mathbb{D}^{2})=\overline{f}
and
\varphi_{f}(\partial\mathbb{D}^{2})
is a union of elements of
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{V}
for all faces
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

We assume
\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}
to be finite (which implies the “weak topology” part of the definition of CW complexes
[
9
]
[Page 521]) and that
\partial\mathcal{S}
is the union of elements of
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{E}
.

Furthermore, given a characteristic map
\varphi_{f}
, consider the boundary
\partial f=\varphi_{f}(\partial\mathbb{D}^{2})
of a face
f\in\mathcal{F}
. Let

\displaystyle\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}\colon[0,1]

\displaystyle\to\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto(\cos(2\pi t),\sin(2\pi t)).

and

\displaystyle\partial\varphi_{f}:=\varphi_{f}\circ\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}\colon[0,1]

\displaystyle\to\partial f

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto\varphi_{f}((\cos(2\pi t),\sin(2\pi t))).

We do not require the CW complex to be regular; however, we further assume the following:

•

\partial\varphi_{f}(0)\in\mathcal{V}
is a vertex for all faces
f\in\mathcal{F}
;

•

\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
is a finite set of points;

•

through
\varphi_{f}
, each component of
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}\setminus\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
maps homeomorphically onto an edge.

A curve
\mathcal{D}
on
\mathcal{S}
is
transverse to
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
if
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{V}=\varnothing
and
\mathcal{D}
has transverse intersection with
\bigcup\mathcal{E}
inside
\interior\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{V}
(where
\bigcup\mathcal{E}
forms a curve). The
regions
determined by
\mathcal{D}\pitchfork(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
are the connected components of
\interior\mathcal{S}\setminus(\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{D})
.

2.3 Remark
.

As for general CW complexes, each characteristic map
\varphi_{f}
is a closed quotient map, and
\varphi_{f}(\partial\mathbb{D}^{2})=\partial f
.

Given a
k
-arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
, let
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\partial\mathcal{S}
and consider the tuple
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
where

•

the vertices
\mathcal{V}
are the points of

\bigcup_{0\leq i<j\leq k}\mathcal{C}_{i}\cap\mathcal{C}_{j};

•

the edges
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}\cup\dots\cup\mathcal{E}_{k}
are labeled from 0 to
k
, where the elements of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{C}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{V}
;

•

the faces
\mathcal{F}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{S}\setminus(\bigcup_{i=0}^{k}\mathcal{E}_{i}\cup\mathcal{V})
.

If
e\in\mathcal{E}_{i}
we say that
e
has
index

i
.

2.4 Definition
.

An arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
is
cellular
if the induced tuple
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is a cellular decomposition.

2.5 Example
.

On
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
with homogeneous coordinates

consider the lines

\mathcal{L}_{x}=\{x=0\},\quad\mathcal{L}_{y}=\{y=0\},\quad\mathcal{L}_{z}=\{z=0\}.

Then the arrangement of three lines
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z})
and of two lines
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{z})
are cellular. They form the cellular decompositions resp.
(\mathcal{V}^{(3)},\mathcal{E}^{(3)},\mathcal{F}^{(3)})
,
(\mathcal{V}^{(2)},\mathcal{E}^{(2)},\mathcal{F}^{(2)})
shown in Figure

, where the orientations of the edges and the faces are also fixed. The arrangement of one line

2.6 Definition
.

A homeomorphism
\eta\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
is
compatible with
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
if it restricts to permutations of these sets, and furthermore it
fixes
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
if these permutations are the identity and for all edges
e\in\mathcal{E}
the characteristic map
\varphi_{e}
has the same orientation as
\eta\circ\varphi_{e}
. Two
k
-arrangements
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k}),(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{k})
are
topologically equivalent
if there exists a homeomorphism
\eta\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
such that
\eta(\mathcal{C}_{i})=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
.

2.7.
Adding a curve to a cellular decomposition

Let
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{S}
be a curve transverse to a cellular decomposition
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
on
\mathcal{S}
. Let
\gr(\mathcal{D})
be the union of the components of
\mathcal{D}
meeting
\bigcup\mathcal{E}
, and let
\fl(\mathcal{D})=\mathcal{D}\setminus\gr(\mathcal{D})
. Consider the tuple
(\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})},\mathcal{E}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})},\mathcal{F}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})})
of
\mathcal{S}
where

•

\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}=\mathcal{V}\cup\big(\gr(\mathcal{D})\cap\bigcup\mathcal{E}\big)
;

•

\mathcal{E}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}
are the connected components of
\bigcup\mathcal{E}\cup\gr(\mathcal{D})\setminus\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}
;

•

\mathcal{F}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{S}\setminus(\bigcup\mathcal{E}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}\cup\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})})
.

2.8 Theorem
.

(\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})},\mathcal{E}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})},\mathcal{F}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})})
forms a cellular decomposition of
\mathcal{S}
(up to a choice of characteristic maps).

We say that
\gr(\mathcal{D})
is the
ground part of
\mathcal{D}
and that
\fl(\mathcal{D})
is the
floating part of
\mathcal{D}
. Similarly, the connected components of
\mathcal{S}\setminus(\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{D})
are divided into
ground regions
if their closure meet
\bigcup\mathcal{E}
, and
floating regions
otherwise. We call
ground faces
the elements of
\mathcal{F}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}
: each ground face contains exactly one ground region and some floating regions, whose arrangement will be encoded in the floating trees in Definition
3.4
. If
\fl(\mathcal{D})
is empty, we say that
\mathcal{D}
is
floatless
.

We first need some definitions and lemmas which will also be useful for the following.

2.9 Definition
.

Given a cellular decomposition
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{S}
, for any face
f\in\mathcal{F}
let
l(f)
be the number of points in
\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{V}\cap\partial f)\subset\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
. We temporarily set the numbers
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{l(f)}=1
such that

\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{V}\cap\partial f)\subset\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}=\{\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}(t_{0}),\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}(t_{1}),\dots,\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}(t_{l(f)})\}.

Then,
\partial\varphi_{f}((t_{i-1},t_{i}))=e\in\mathcal{E}
is an edge, and since
\partial\varphi_{f}|_{(t_{i-1},t_{i})}
is a homeomorphism by our assumptions, the composition

\varphi_{e}^{-1}\circ\partial\varphi_{(t_{i-1},t_{i})}\colon(t_{i-1},t_{i})\to\mathbb{B}^{1}=(-1,1)

is strictly monotone. So for any
i=1,\dots,l(f)
we define

\{+,-\}\times\mathcal{E}\ni\partial_{i}(f):=\begin{cases}+e&\text{if $\varphi_{e}^{-1}\circ\partial\varphi_{(t_{i-1},t_{i})}$ is increasing,}\\
-e&\text{if $\varphi_{e}^{-1}\circ\partial\varphi_{(t_{i-1},t_{i})}$ is decreasing.}\end{cases}

The value
\partial_{i}(f)
represents what is the
i
-th edge crossed while traveling along
\partial\varphi_{f}
and whether this happens with the same orientation as
\varphi_{e}
or the opposite. We also define the open triangle

\Delta_{i}(f):=\varphi_{f}\big(\{(r\cos(2\pi t),r\sin(2\pi t))\mid r\in(0,1),t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i})\}\big)

to be the image under
\varphi_{f}
of the
i
-th “slice” of the disk.

An important consequence of our definitions is that
\overline{\Delta_{i}(f)}\supset\partial_{i}(f)
.

.

2.10 Lemma
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{D}^{2}
be a connected curve such that
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}\neq\varnothing
(so
\mathcal{C}\simeq\mathbb{D}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}=\{p,q\}
is made up of two points). Then
\mathcal{C}
splits
\mathbb{B}^{2}
into two connected components
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2}
, each homeomorphic to
\mathbb{B}^{2}
and whose closure is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Moreover, up to homeomorphism of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
fixing
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
pointwise, the curve
\mathcal{C}
is determined by
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{L}_{2}
be the two connected components of
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}\setminus\{p,q\}
. Then
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{L}_{2}
are two closed curves which by the Jordan-Schönflies curve theorem
[
22
]
enclose two subsets resp.
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2}
both homeomorphic to
\mathbb{B}^{2}
and whose closure is homeomorphic to
\mathbb{D}^{2}
; this proves the first part of the assertion.

For uniqueness, consider another curve
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with the same properties as
\mathcal{C}
and such that
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}=\{p,q\}
. In the same way as before, the closed curves
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{L}_{1},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{L}_{2}
enclose resp.
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{1},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{2}
. Since
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
by definition of curves on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
are images of maps
\mathbb{D}^{1}\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
, it is immediate to find a homeomorphism
\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{L}_{1}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{L}_{1}
which fixes
\mathcal{L}_{1}
pointwise, which can be extended to a homeomorphism
\eta_{1}\colon\overline{\mathcal{U}_{1}}\to\overline{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{1}}
fixing
\mathcal{L}_{1}
(as any homeomorphism
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}\to\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
can be radially extended to a homeomorphism
\mathbb{D}^{2}\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
). In the same way we can obtain a homeomorphism
\eta_{2}\colon\overline{\mathcal{U}_{2}}\to\overline{\mathcal{U}^{\prime}_{2}}
fixing
\mathcal{L}_{2}
and which agrees with
\eta_{1}
on
\mathcal{C}
, so by gluing
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
we finally obtain a homeomorphism
\eta\colon\mathbb{D}^{2}\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
fixing
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
pointwise and such that
\eta(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

∎

2.11 Lemma
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is transversal to a cellular decomposition
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
then for each face
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\varphi^{-1}_{f}(\mathcal{C})
is a curve on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Proof.

To verify that
\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{C})
is a curve, we need to distinguish between points on
\interior(\mathbb{D}^{2})=\mathbb{B}^{2}
and on
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
. If
p\in\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{C})\cap\mathbb{B}^{2}
then there exists an open neighborhood
p\in\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{S}
such that
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{C})\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{2},\{y=0\})
, and up to restriction we can suppose
\mathcal{U}\subseteq f
.
Then, by setting
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}=\varphi^{-1}_{f}(\mathcal{U})
we have that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
is an open neighborhood of
p
and

(\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\cap\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))\simeq(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{C})\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{2},\{y=0\})

as desired.

If instead
p\in\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{C})\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
, then
\varphi_{f}(p)\in\partial f\setminus\mathcal{V}
and in particular
\varphi_{f}(p)\in e
for some edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
. Let
i
be such that

p\in\mathcal{U}:=\{(r\cos(2\pi t),r\sin(2\pi t)\mid r\in(0,1],t\in(t_{i-1},t_{i})\},

and notice that
\partial_{i}(f)=\pm e
, that
\varphi_{f}(\mathcal{U})=\Delta_{i}(f)\cup e
and that
\mathcal{U}
is an open neighborhood of
p
in
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. There are now two possibilities.

(1)

There exists another
i^{\prime}
such that
\partial_{i^{\prime}}(f)=\pm e
. Then,
\Delta_{i}(f)\cup\Delta_{i^{\prime}}(f)\cup e
is an open neighborhood of
p
, inside which (by the fact that
\mathcal{C}\pitchfork e
) we can find an open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}
such that

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}\cap e,\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{C})\simeq(\mathbb{R}^{2},\{y=0\},\{x=0\}).

Now, through this homeomorphism,
\mathcal{W}\setminus e\simeq\{y\neq 0\}
and

\mathcal{W}\setminus e=(\mathcal{W}\cap\Delta_{i}(f))\sqcup(\mathcal{W}\cap\Delta_{i^{\prime}}(f))

so without loss of generality,
\mathcal{W}\cap\Delta_{i}(f)\simeq\{y\geq 0\}
. Let
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}=\mathcal{W}\cap(\Delta_{i}(f)\cup e)
. Then,

(2.11.1)

(\mathcal{W}^{\prime},\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\cap e,\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{C})\simeq(\{y\geq 0\},\{y=0\},\{x=0\}).

Finally,
\varphi_{f}
is injective when restricted to
\mathcal{U}
. Additionally,

\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{W})\cap\mathcal{U}=\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{W}^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{U}

is an open neighborhood of
p
, and by taking
\mathcal{W}
small enough we can suppose that
\overline{\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{W})\cap\mathcal{U}}\subset\mathcal{U}
. Then the restriction

\psi:=\varphi_{f}|_{\overline{\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{W})\cap\mathcal{U}}}

is closed map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space, so a homeomorphism with its image, which lets us conclude that

(2.11.2)

(\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{W}),\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2},\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{W})\cap\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{C}))\simeq(\mathcal{W}^{\prime},\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\cap e,\mathcal{W}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{C}).

By composing the homeomorphisms (
2.11.1
) and (
2.11.2
) we get what we wanted.

(2)

There is no other
i^{\prime}
such that
\partial_{i^{\prime}}(f)=\pm e
. We proceed similarly to before, the only difference being that this time we take the open neighborhood
\mathcal{W}\subset\overline{f}
so that

(\mathcal{W},\mathcal{W}\cap e,\mathcal{W}\cap\mathcal{C})\simeq(\{y\geq 0\},\{y=0\},\{x=0\}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

We need to show that for all faces
f\in\mathcal{F}
and for all connected components
g
of
f\setminus\gr(\mathcal{D})
, there exists a characteristic map
\varphi_{g}\colon\mathbb{D}^{2}\to\overline{g}
, where in particular
\varphi_{g}(\partial\mathbb{D}^{2})
is a union of elements of
\mathcal{V}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})}
and
\mathcal{E}_{\gr}
. Call arcs the connected components of
\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\gr(\mathcal{D}))
. We proceed by induction on the number
n
of arcs. If
n=0
, then
f=g
and the proposition is obvious.

For
n>0
, consider any arc
\alpha
. By Lemma
2.10
, it splits
\mathbb{B}^{2}
into two open subsets
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2}
such that
(\overline{\mathcal{U}_{i}},\mathcal{U}_{i})\simeq(\mathbb{D}^{2},\mathbb{B}^{2})
. We show that
\psi:=\varphi_{f}|_{\overline{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}
is, after the identification of its source with
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, a characteristic map for the face
\varphi_{f}(\mathcal{U}_{1})
of the decomposition
(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
of
\overline{f}
where

•

\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=(\mathcal{V}\cap\overline{f})\cup(\varphi_{f}(\overline{\alpha})\cap\partial f)
;

•

\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is the set of connected components of
(\partial f\cup\varphi_{f}(\alpha))\setminus\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, or equivalently the element
\varphi_{f}(\alpha)
together with set of connected components of
\partial f\setminus\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
;

•

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is the set of connected components of
\overline{f}\setminus(\bigcup\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{V}^{\prime})=f\setminus\varphi_{f}(\alpha)
, that is,

\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\{\varphi_{f}(\mathcal{U}_{1}),\varphi_{f}(\mathcal{U}_{2})\}.

Similarly,
\varphi_{f}|_{\overline{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}
is a characteristic map for
\varphi_{f}(\mathcal{U}_{2})
. Hence,
(\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime})
is a cellular decomposition of
\overline{f}
. Now, there are yet
<n
arcs to consider on separately
\varphi_{f}|_{\overline{\mathcal{U}_{1}}}
and
\varphi_{f}|_{\overline{\mathcal{U}_{2}}}
, but by inductive hypothesis after adding those arcs we get a cellular decomposition.

We now prove that
\psi
has the required properties.

(1)

\psi
is a homeomorphism restricted to
\mathcal{U}_{1}
: obvious from being a restriction of
\varphi_{f}
, and
\mathcal{U}_{1}\subset f
.

(2)

\psi
is surjective: since
\varphi_{f}
is a closed map, we conclude
\psi(\overline{\mathcal{U}_{1}})=\overline{\psi(\mathcal{U}_{1})}
.

(3)

\psi(\partial\mathcal{U}_{1})
is a union of elements of
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
: using notation from the proof of Lemma
2.10
,
\partial\mathcal{U}_{1}=\mathcal{L}_{1}\cup\{p,q\}\cup\alpha
. We have that
\psi(\alpha)\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
, that
\psi(p),\psi(q)\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, and
\psi(\mathcal{L}_{1})
is a union of connected components of
\partial f\setminus\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
together with elements of
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, as desired.

Additionally,
\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
is a finite set (in fact, a subset of
\varphi^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
together with
\{p,q\}
), and
\psi
sends components of
\partial\mathcal{U}_{1}\setminus\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
homeomorphically onto edges: for the elements of
\mathcal{L}_{1}\setminus\psi^{-1}(\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
this follows from
\varphi_{f}
being a characteristic map, and
\psi|_{\alpha}
is a homeomorphism since
\alpha\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
.
∎

3.
From geometry to combinatorics

This section develops the central idea behind this work, namely the translation in combinatorial terms of the geometric problem of describing topological types of curves transverse to cellular decompositions. The basic idea is not new - the resulting combinatorial objects are usually called
dessin d’enfant
. There are also some similarities with the constructions presented in
[
4
]
. The main contributions of this work are the application of this line of reasoning to arrangements of curves, the description of multiple criteria for the realizability of such dessin, and the implementation of each of these constructions in
Julia
.

3.1 Definition
.

Consider a tuple
(V,E,F)
where
V,E,F
are finite sets, together with functions
\partial_{0},\partial_{1}\colon E\to V
and a function
\partial_{*}\colon F\to(\{+,-\}\times E)^{<\infty}
.

We say that
(V,E,F)
is a
combinatorial cell complex on
\mathcal{S}
if it can be obtained from a cellular decomposition
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
of
\mathcal{S}
in the following way:

•

as sets,
V=\mathcal{V}
,
E=\mathcal{E}
and
F=\mathcal{F}
;

•

for all
e\in\mathcal{E}
,

\partial_{0}(e)=\varphi_{e}(-1),\quad\partial_{1}(e)=\varphi_{e}(1);

•

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\partial_{*}(f)=\big(\partial_{1}(f),\dots,\partial_{l(f)}(f)\big).

A homeomorphism
\eta\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{S}
compatible with
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
induces functions

•

\eta_{V}\colon V\to V
where
\eta_{V}(v)=\eta(v)
;

•

\eta_{E}\colon E\to\{+,-\}\times E
where
\eta_{E}(e)=+\eta(e)
if
\varphi_{\eta(e)}
and
\eta\circ\varphi_{e}
have the same orientation, and
\eta_{E}(e)=-\eta(e)
otherwise;

•

\eta_{F}\colon F\to\{+,-\}\times F
where
\eta_{F}(f)=+\eta(f)
if
\partial\varphi_{\eta(f)}
and
\eta\circ\partial\varphi_{f}
have the same orientation, and
\eta_{F}(f)=-\eta(f)
otherwise.

The set of functions
(\eta_{V},\eta_{E},\eta_{F})
obtainable in this way are the
automorphisms
of
(V,E,F)
. The homeomorphism
\eta
fixes
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
precisely when
\eta_{V}=\mathrm{id}_{V}
,
\eta_{E}=+\mathrm{id}_{E}
and
\eta_{F}=+\mathrm{id}_{F}
.

If
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is the cellular decomposition constructed from a cellular arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
, we additionally consider the
index function

i\colon E\to\mathbb{N}
defined as
i(e)=i
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}_{i}
(recall that
\mathcal{E}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{E}
are the connected components of
\mathcal{C}_{i}\setminus\mathcal{V}
).

The
i
-automorphisms
of
(V,E,F)
are the automorphisms of
(V,E,F)
fixing the function
i
, that is,
i\circ\eta_{E}=i
. These describe the effect that a homeomorphism inducing a topological equivalence of
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
with itself has on the combinatorial structure of its decomposition.

3.2 Example
.

The cellular decomposition
(\mathcal{V}^{(3)},\mathcal{E}^{(3)},\mathcal{F}^{(3)})
coming from the arrangement
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z})
induces the combinatorial cell complex
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
with functions
\partial
and
i
defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{0}(x)=\partial_{0}(X)=(0:1:0),\quad\partial_{1}(x)=\partial_{1}(X)=(0:0:1),\quad i(x)=i(X)=1,

\displaystyle\partial_{0}(y)=\partial_{0}(Y)=(0:0:1),\quad\partial_{1}(y)=\partial_{1}(Y)=(1:0:0),\quad i(y)=i(Y)=2,

\displaystyle\partial_{0}(z)=\partial_{0}(Z)=(1:0:0),\quad\partial_{1}(z)=\partial_{1}(Z)=(0:1:0);\quad i(z)=i(Z)=3,

\displaystyle\partial_{*}(xyz)=(+x,+y,+z),

\displaystyle\partial_{*}(xYZ)=(+x,+Y,+Z),

\displaystyle\partial_{*}(XyZ)=(+X,+y,+Z),

\displaystyle\partial_{*}(XYz)=(+X,+Y,+z).

The group of automorphisms of
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
has 24 elements and can be identified with the group of the rotational symmetries of the octahedron. We can name each element
\eta
of the group after the preimage of the edges
x,y,z\in E^{(3)}
through
\eta_{E}
, obtaining the classification in Figure
9
. Each of these automorphisms comes from a projective transformation of the plane of the form

(x:y:z)\mapsto(\pm T_{1}:\pm T_{2}:T_{3})

where
\{T_{1},T_{2},T_{3}\}
is a permutation of
\{x,y,z\}
. The subgroup of
i
-automorphisms has 4 elements.

The combinatorial cell complex
(V^{(2)},E^{(2)},F^{(2)})
coming from the cellular decomposition induced by the arrangement of two lines
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{z})
has functions
\partial,i
defined as

\displaystyle\partial_{0}(xX)=\partial_{1}(xX)=\partial_{0}(Zz)=\partial_{1}(Zz)=(0:1:0),\quad i(xX)=1,\;i(Zz)=3;

\displaystyle\partial_{*}(xXZz)=(+xX,+Zz),\quad\partial_{*}(xXzZ)=(+xX,-Zz).

The group of automorphisms is of order 8, with the subgroup of
i
-automorphisms being of order 4.

.

(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
and

, illustrated through their action on a combinatorial curve. In both cases the first column is the subgroup of

-automorphisms.

3.3 Definition
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a curve on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. The
Dyck word
W(\mathcal{C})
associated to
\mathcal{C}
is the Dyck word constructed by starting with the empty word and while traveling along
\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}
, adding a “(” or a “)” to the end of the word whenever a component of
\mathcal{C}
is met resp. for the first or second time.

3.4 Definition
.

Let
(V,E,F)
be a combinatorial cell complex on
\mathcal{S}
. A
combinatorial curve
transverse to
(V,E,F)
is given by the following data
(n,W,T)
:

•

for every edge
e\in E
, a non-negative integer
n(e)
such that
n(f):=\sum_{i=1}^{l(f)}n(e_{i})
is even for all faces
f\in F
;

•

for every face
f\in F
, a Dyck word
W_{f}
of length
n(f)
;

•

for every
g\in\sum_{f\in F}[n(f)/2+1]
, a rooted tree
T_{g}
, called the
floating tree
on
g
.

From a curve
\mathcal{D}
transverse to
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
we construct the following combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)
transverse to
(V,E,F)
:

•

for every edge
e\in\mathcal{E}
, let
n(e):=\#(\mathcal{D}\cap e)
be the number of points of intersection of
e
with the curve
\mathcal{D}
;

•

for every face
f\in\mathcal{F}
, let
W_{f}=W(\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{D}))
be the Dyck word associated to the curve
\varphi_{f}^{-1}(\mathcal{D})
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
;

•

for every face
f\in\mathcal{F}
, consider the ground faces
g\in\mathcal{F}_{\gr(\mathcal{D})},g\subseteq f
in the order in which each
\overline{\varphi_{f}^{-1}(g)}
is first met by traveling along
\partial\varphi_{\mathbb{D}^{2}}
. If

is the

be the rooted tree formed by the regions of

contained inside

For an example see Figure

.

3.5 Remark
.

In examples and in the library
NWT
, an ordering of the set
F
of faces is necessarily chosen. This lets us index the rooted trees
T_{(f,i)}
using a single index
g
, which represents the lexicographic ordering of the pairs
(f,i)
. Furthermore, in the library, each rooted tree
T_{g}
is encoded as a pair
(g,W)
where
W
is the Dyck word encoded by a depth-first traversal of the rooted tree, where backtracking is recorded. For such a traversal, an ordering of siblings in each vertex of the tree is required: we use a canonical order, given by recursively sorting children. In this way, two combinatorial curves
(n,W,T),(n^{\prime},W^{\prime},T^{\prime})
transversal to the same combinatorial cell complex are “mathematically” equal if and only if, as
Julia
objects,
n=n^{\prime}
,
W=W^{\prime}
and
T=T^{\prime}
.

3.6 Remark
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a curve intersecting
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
transversely, and consider the induced combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)
. Given a homeomorphism
\eta
, the curve
\eta(\mathcal{D})
will have an induced combinatorial curve
(n^{\prime},W^{\prime},T^{\prime})
which depends uniquely on
(n,W,T)
and the functions
\eta_{V}
,
\eta_{E}
and
\eta_{F}
. In this way, the group of automorphisms of
(V,E,F)
acts on the combinatorial curves transverse to
(V,E,F)
. Clearly, a homeomorphism
\eta
fixes
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
if and only if it induces the trivial action.

3.7 Lemma
.

Up to homeomorphism fixing
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
pointwise, a curve
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
whose every component meets the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
is determined by the set
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
and the Dyck word
W(\mathcal{C})
.

Proof.

A Dyck word is the same as an ordered tree, i.e. a rooted tree in which an order is specified for every set of children of a vertex. In our case, each non-root vertex of this tree corresponds to a component of
\mathcal{C}
, which we can see as an arc
\alpha\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{D}^{2}
where the endpoints
\alpha(0),\alpha(1)
are determined by
W(\mathcal{C})
and the set
\mathcal{C}\cap\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
, and where
\alpha(t)\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
for all
0<t<1
. Then, by traversing the tree depth-first, we show that up to homeomorphism there is only one way to place each arc
\alpha
at each step. Indeed, by Lemma
2.10
, this arc is unique up to homeomorphism and splits the region containing it in two regions over which the same lemma applies.
∎

3.8 Lemma
.

Up to homeomorphism fixing
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
pointwise, a curve
\mathcal{C}
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
disjoint from
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
is determined by the rooted tree formed by the connected components of
\mathbb{B}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{C}
, where the root is the unique component whose closure contains
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of
3.7
, we traverse the tree depth-first and show that at each step there is only one way up to homeomorphism to place each oval. By choosing such a traversal, the oval is always contractible inside the open region it is to be placed, so it is easy to see that the placement is unique up to homeomorphism of that region fixing its boundary.
∎

3.9 Theorem
.

The construction in Definition
3.4
defines a bijection

\frac{\{\mathcal{D}\mid\mathcal{D}\pitchfork(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})\}}{\text{homeomorphism that fixes $(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})$}}\to\{(n,W,T)\mid(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V,E,F)\}

3.12 Remark
.

We say that two arrangements

(\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{k})
are

(n^{\prime},W^{\prime},T^{\prime})\pitchfork(V^{\prime},E^{\prime},F^{\prime})

Before describing this procedure, consider the families of triangles

\{\Delta^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})\}_{(f^{\prime},i^{\prime})}

\overline{\Delta_{i}(f)}\supset\partial_{i}(f)
for all

as follows: for any
e\in E

\gr(e)\in E_{\gr}^{<\infty}
as in §
4.1
. If
\partial_{i}(f)=se

s\gr(e)=(se^{\prime}_{1},\dots,se^{\prime}_{n(e)+1})
(remember that
s=-
reverses the tuple), we let

where
g_{j}\in F_{\gr}

is the sequence of ground faces

Define

\overline{\Delta_{i^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})\cap\Delta_{i}(f)}\supset\partial_{i^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})

\partial_{i^{\prime}}(f^{\prime})

Notice that the data

Unlike

We begin our construction by considering the quotient
\pi\colon F_{\gr}\to\pi(F_{\gr})

\gr(G)_{0}

g^{\prime}(f^{\prime},i^{\prime})=\pi(g(\psi(f^{\prime},i^{\prime})))

To finally obtain the floating forest of

Notice that after the removal, some vertices in

5.
Obstructions to realizability

5.1.
The curve type of an arrangement

It is well-known
[
19
]
that the topological type of a curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is determined by two pieces of information:

•

whether the curve has a
pseudoline
(that is, a connected component which does not disconnect
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
) or not;

•

the rooted tree formed by the regions determined by the curve, where the root is the unique region whose closure is non-orientable, which we call the
external region
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a curve of degree
d
transverse to a cellular arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{L},\mathcal{C}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
where
\mathcal{L}
is a line. Let
i\colon\mathcal{E}_{\gr}\to[k+1]
be the index function where
i(e)=i
for every
e\subset\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
i(e)=k+1
for
e\subset\mathcal{D}
. We want to determine the topological type of
\mathcal{D}
, forgetting the cellular arrangement, from just the combinatorial data
(n,W,T)
. This is called the
curve type
of
(n,W,T)
.

First suppose
\mathcal{D}
has a pseudoline. Take the region graph
G_{k+1}
whose edges represent regions separated by
\mathcal{D}
, and merge any two regions separated by any other curve
\mathcal{C}_{i}
(that is, any two vertices joined by any edge in
G_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,k
) obtaining a graph
G^{\prime}_{k+1}
. Then, the vertices of
G^{\prime}_{k+1}
correspond to the regions determined by
\mathcal{D}
, and the presence of the pseudoline ensures that the external region has a self-loop. Thus in this case we have determined
\mathcal{D}
up to topological equivalence.

Suppose now on the contrary that
\mathcal{D}
has no pseudoline. Consider the region graphs
G_{1},G_{k+1}
and merge any two regions separated by a curve
\mathcal{C}_{i}
for just
i=2,\dots,k
, obtaining two graph
G^{\prime}_{1},G^{\prime}_{k+1}
. Since
\mathcal{D}
has no pseudoline, it is easy to see that the arrangement
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})
admits an external region, separated from itself by the line
\mathcal{L}
, which determines a unique vertex
v^{\prime}_{\mathrm{ext}}\in G^{\prime}_{1}
with a self-loop. Now starting from
G^{\prime}_{k+1}
merge any two vertices separated by an edge in
G^{\prime}_{1}
, obtaining the quotient graph
\pi\colon G^{\prime}_{k+1}\to G^{\prime\prime}_{k+1}
whose points represent the regions determined by
\mathcal{D}
. Then, the external region is the image
\pi(v_{\textrm{ext}})
of the external region of
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})
under the quotient formed by forgetting the line
\mathcal{L}
.

Of course, a priori we do not know whether
\mathcal{D}
has a pseudoline or not, so we first check whether
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{D})
has an external region, i.e. whether
G_{1}^{\prime}
has a self-loop or not, and then follow the first or the second procedure accordingly.

5.2.
Bézout’s criterion of order 0

The real version of Bézout’s theorem states that two nonsingular algebraic curves
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
on
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
of degree resp.
d,d^{\prime}
transverse with each other intersect in
\#(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\leq dd^{\prime}
points, and

dd^{\prime}\equiv\#(\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{C}^{\prime})\pmod{2}.

Suppose we are given a
(1,d_{2},\dots,d_{k})
-arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{L},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
inducing the combinatorial cell structure
(V,E,F)
, where
\mathcal{L}
is a line. The Bézout’s theorem gives us multiple criteria for the
d_{k+1}
-realizability of a combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V,E,F)
.

In the following, let
d:=(d_{1},\dots,d_{k+1})
and if
(n,W,T)
is
d_{k+1}
-realizable let
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
be one realization.

5.3 Definition
.

We say that
(n,W,T)

respects basic
d
-Bézout
if for any
i=1,\dots,k
the sum
\sum_{i(e)=i}n_{e}
is a number
\leq d_{i}d_{k+1}
with the same parity as
d_{i}d_{k+1}
.

Respecting basic Bézout is a necessary criterion for the
d
-realizability of
(n,W,T)
which puts a bound on the numbers
n_{e}
. These in turn determine the length of the Dyck words
W_{f}
. A simple way to bound the size of the floating trees
T_{g}
is Harnack’s inequality, which states that a curve of degree
d
can have at most
(d-1)(d-2)/2+1
ovals; using the known results on Hilbert’s 16th problem for curves of degree
1\leq d\leq 8
together with our algorithm to determine the topological type of a curve, we get a stronger criterion. As a consequence, fixed
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
, we can effectively list all possible candidate
d
-realizable combinatorial curves
(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V,E,F)
, and in particular the set of
d
-realizable combinatorial curves transverse to
(V,E,F)
is finite.

We can find stronger consequences of Bézout’s theorem. Let
G
be the region graph of
(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V,E,F)
. A
combinatorial curve

l
with intersection numbers
(l_{1},\dots,l_{k+1})
is a closed walk on
G
passing through exactly
l_{i}
edges of
G_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,k+1
, such that for all
i\in[k+1]
,
l_{i}\leq d_{i}
and
l_{i}
has the same parity as
d_{i}
. Since
d_{1}=1
, necessarily
l_{1}=1
: this guarantees that, if realized by an actual connected curve,
l
would be a pseudoline.

5.4 Definition
.

A combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)

respects
d
-Bézout at order 0
if for any two regions
r,r^{\prime}\in G
there exists a combinatorial line passing through them.

Figure 13
.

. Note that there is a closed walk through regions 11 and 12 crossing the curve 4 times, but no such walk which also crosses each axis exactly once.

Respecting
d
-Bézout at order 0 is a necessary condition for
d
-realizability, since for any two regions determined by
\mathcal{D}\pitchfork(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
we can obviously find an actual line
\mathcal{L}
transverse to
(\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{D})
going through those regions, which translates to the existence of a combinatorial line satisfying the above requirements on its intersection numbers, by Bézout’s theorem.

We now discuss an effective algorithm to check whether a combinatorial curve respects Bézout at order 0 or not. Similar techniques are standard in computer science and graph theory
[
13
,
7
]
. Let
G
be the region graph and identify the set of its vertices with
[\rho]
. Letting

P:=\{0,\dots,d_{1}\}\times\cdots\times\{0,\dots,d_{k+1}\},

we can give its powerset
\mathcal{P}:=2^{P}
the structure of a semiring by letting

\displaystyle 0_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle:=\varnothing,

\displaystyle 1_{\mathcal{P}}

\displaystyle:=\{(0,\dots,0)\},

\displaystyle s+_{\mathcal{P}}s^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=s\cup s^{\prime},

\displaystyle s\cdot_{\mathcal{P}}s^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\{(p_{1}+p^{\prime}_{1},\dots,p_{k+1}+p^{\prime}_{k+1})\mid p\in s,p^{\prime}\in s^{\prime},p_{i}+p^{\prime}_{i}\leq d_{i}\;\forall i\in[k+1]\}.

Construct the symmetric matrix
A\in\mathcal{P}^{\rho\times\rho}
where for all
r,r^{\prime}\in[\rho]
,

A_{r,r^{\prime}}=\{e_{i}\mid\text{$(r,r^{\prime})$ is an edge of $G_{i}$}\}

where
e_{i}
are the unit vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{k+1}
. Let
A^{i}=A\cdot_{\mathcal{P}}\ldots\cdot_{\mathcal{P}}A
be the matrix multiplication using our semiring structure. If
\delta:=d_{1}+\dots+d_{k+1}
then

B:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}A^{i}=I_{\mathcal{P}}+A+A^{2}+\dots+A^{\delta}\in\mathcal{P}^{\rho\times\rho}

is a symmetric matrix where each entry
B_{r,r^{\prime}}
is the set of all possible tuples
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{k+1})\in P
such that there exists a walk in
G
from
r
to
r^{\prime}
passing through
p_{1}
edges of index 1,
p_{2}
edges of index 2 etc.

5.5 Proposition
.

Suppose that
(n,W,T)
respects basic
d
-Bézout. Then it also respects
d
-Bézout at order 0 if and only if for all
r,r^{\prime}\in[\rho]
there exists a tuple
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k+1})\in B_{r,r^{\prime}}\cdot_{\mathcal{P}}B_{r,r^{\prime}}
such that
p_{i}
has the same parity as
d_{i}
for all
i\in[k+1]
.

Proof.

There exists a tuple
(p_{1},\dots,p_{k+1})\in B_{r,r^{\prime}}\cdot_{\mathcal{P}}B_{r,r^{\prime}}
if and only if there exist tuples
p^{\prime},p^{\prime\prime}\in B_{r,r^{\prime}}
with
p^{\prime}+p^{\prime\prime}=p
, which means that there is a walk in
G
from
r
to
r^{\prime}
passing through
p^{\prime}_{i}
edges of
G_{i}
and a walk from
r^{\prime}
to
r
passing through
p^{\prime\prime}_{i}
edges of
G_{i}
: by joining them we get the required combinatorial line.
∎

5.6.
Bézout’s criterion of order
n

The following is a family of Bézout criteria defined for floatless curves. Consider as before a
(1,d_{2},\dots,d_{k})
-arrangement
(\mathcal{C}_{1}=\mathcal{L},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{k})
inducing
(V,E,F)
, and a combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V,E,F)
which respects
(d_{1},\dots,d_{k+1})
-Bézout at order 0, and for which
T_{g}
is the trivial tree for all
g
, i.e.
(n,W,T)
is floatless. Then
(n,W,T)
is fully characterized by the combinatorial cell complex
(V_{\gr},E_{\gr},F_{\gr})
. Let
l
be a combinatorial line on
(n,W,T)
. A
realization
of
l
is a floatless combinatorial curve
(n^{(l)},W^{(l)},T^{(l)})
transverse to
(V_{\gr},E_{\gr},F_{\gr})
which has the curve type of a line (so, has a pseudoline and only the external region) and that agrees with
l
when seen as a walk on the graph of
(V_{\gr},E_{\gr},F_{\gr})
. Concretely, we can find all such realizations by determining from
l
the values
n^{(l)}
, generating all possible Dyck words and filtering out those that have the wrong curve type or do not agree with
l
.

5.7 Definition
.

For any
n
positive integer, we say that
(n,W,T)

respects
d
-Bézout at order
n
if for all pairs of regions
r,r^{\prime}\in F_{\gr}
there exists a
d
-combinatorial line
l
through
r,r^{\prime}
and a realization
(n^{(l)},W^{(l)},T^{(l)})\pitchfork(V_{\gr},E_{\gr},F_{\gr})
that respects
(d_{1},\dots,d_{k+1},1)
-Bézout at order
n-1
.

.

5.9 Definition
.

The combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)

admires
d
-Bézout
if it respects
d
-Bézout at order
n
for all
n
positive integers.

Clearly, Bézout at order
n
and admiration are necessary conditions for realizability. There are some natural questions related to admiration:

(1)

are there non-
d
-realizable
d
-admirers of Bézout?

(2)

is there a finite procedure to check
d
-admiration?

We implemented an algorithm that checks Bézout at order
n
for floatless arrangements. In practice, we used Bézout at order 1 in our analysis, but even checking Bézout at order 2 is too slow to be of any use. This makes it hard to study higher-order Bézout criteria and admiration at the moment.

Also, an interesting problem is to find an effective way to check Bézout at orders
>0
for non-floatless arrangements: the most problematic step is finding all the realizations of a combinatorial line, since the presence of floating ovals increases the complexity considerably.

6.
Construction of realizable cases

6.1.
Viro’s patchworking construction

The main tool we employ to generate realizable cases is Viro’s Patchworking Construction
[
25
]
. What is striking is that even by taking into consideration the interactions of the curve with the three lines
\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z}
, no adjustments have to be made to the original construction by Viro.

In this section we follow the treatment given in
[
6
]
[11, §5] (in particular, no unimodularity assumptions are needed). We notice that through the theory of combinatorial cell complexes and arrangements developed in the previous sections, Viro’s construction is particularly natural to formulate: consider the polytope

Q_{d}=\conv\{(0,0),(d,0),(0,d)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}

and let
A=Q_{d}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by its integer points. A
triangulation

(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
of
(Q_{d},A)
is a cellular decomposition of
Q_{d}
into triangles (with straight segments as edges, as opposed to “topological” triangles) such that
\mathcal{V}\subseteq A
, i.e. every vertex belongs to
A
. A
regular triangulation
is a triangulation such that there exists a height function
h\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
with the property that by taking the convex hull of the graph
\Gamma_{h}=\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\mid(x,y)\in\mathcal{V},z=h(x,y)\}
and projecting down through
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y)
the faces of the hull, we recover exactly the original triangles in
\mathcal{F}
.

Suppose we are given a regular triangulation
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
of
(Q_{d},A)
, with vertices
\mathcal{V}
and height function
h\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
. Choose an arbitrary sign function
\varepsilon\colon\mathcal{V}\to\{+,-\}
and let

n_{e}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\varepsilon(\partial_{0}(e))\neq\varepsilon(\partial_{1}(e))$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since each triangle
f\in\mathcal{F}
has either 0 or 2 edges
e
with
n_{e}=1
, the only possible choice of Dyck word
W_{f}
is resp. the empty word or “()”. If we now choose the trivial rooted tree for every ground face
g\in\mathcal{F}_{\gr}
we get a combinatorial curve
(n,W,T)
transverse to the combinatorial cell complex
(V,E,F)
induced by
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
. Now let
i\colon E\to\{0,1\}
be the index function such that
i(e)=1
if
e\subset\partial Q_{d}
is on the boundary of the triangle and
i(e)=0
otherwise. By removing the edges of index 0 as in §
4.4
we obtain a combinatorial curve
(n_{xyz},W_{xyz},T_{xyz})
transverse to the triangle
Q_{d}
.

Finally, let
\varepsilon_{xYZ}(x,y)=(-1)^{x}\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon_{XyZ}(x,y)=(-1)^{y}\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon_{XYz}(x,y)=(-1)^{x+y}\varepsilon
, and construct resp.
(n_{xYZ},W_{xYZ},T_{xYZ})
,
(n_{XyZ},W_{XyZ},T_{XyZ})
and
(n_{XYz},W_{XYz},T_{XYz})
in the same way. Then by interpreting these as combinatorial curves on the faces
xyz,xYZ,XyZ,XYz
of
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
we obtain a combinatorial curve which we name
C(\mathcal{T},\varepsilon)
.

6.2 Theorem

(Viro)
.

The combinatorial curve
C(\mathcal{T},\varepsilon)
is
(1,1,1,d)
-realizable from the polynomial

f_{t}(x,y,z)=\sum_{(a,b)\in\mathcal{V}}\varepsilon(a,b)t^{h(a,b)}x^{a}y^{b}z^{d-a-b}

for any
t>0
sufficiently small.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{t}=\{f_{t}=0\}
. As stated in
[
6
]
[Page 382], for
t>0
sufficiently small, the curve
\mathcal{C}_{t}
is nonsingular, intersects the axes
xyz=0
transversely, and its isotopy type in the space of nonsingular curves of degree
d
transverse to the axes only depends on
(\mathcal{T},\varepsilon)
. By
[
6
]
[Theorem 11.5.6], the intersection
\mathcal{C}_{t}\cap\{x\geq 0,y\geq 0,z\geq 0\}
has topological type
(n_{xyz},W_{xyz},T_{xyz})
, and similarly for the other faces. Thus, the arrangement
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z},\mathcal{C}_{t})
has topological type
C(\mathcal{T},\varepsilon)
.
∎

6.3.
Translations

Suppose we are given a degree
d
curve
\mathcal{D}\pitchfork(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z})
with affine equation
f(x,y)=0
. Then a generic vertical translation
f(x,y-y_{0})=0
of
\mathcal{D}
gives us a new degree
d
curve
\mathcal{D}_{y_{0}}\pitchfork(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z})
. In order to generate new realizable combinatorial curves from old ones, it would be useful to have a procedure to turn the combinatorial data
D\pitchfork(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
induced by
\mathcal{D}
into the combinatorial data
D_{y_{0}}
induced by
\mathcal{D}_{y_{0}}
. Unfortunately, knowing
D
is not enough to be able to recover
D_{y_{0}}
for any
y_{0}
: the topological type of the arrangement might change for any critical point of the linear functional
\alpha(x,y)=y
restricted to
\mathcal{D}
, and at the intersections
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{L}_{x}
.

However, by the algebraicity of
\mathcal{D}
there is only a finite number of such points, so for
y_{0}\gg 0
large enough the topological type of the upward translation
\mathcal{D}_{y_{0}}
depends uniquely on the topological type of
\mathcal{D}
. Let
D=(n,W,T)
. Then, to construct
D_{y_{0}}
(Figure
16
),

(1)

remove
L_{y}
from the configuration, obtaining
(n^{\prime},W^{\prime},T^{\prime})\pitchfork(V^{(2)},E^{(2)},F^{(2)})
;

(2)

insert a line
L_{y}^{\prime}
going through the same ground faces that
L_{z}
meet: concretely, the two faces above
L_{y}^{\prime}

W^{\prime\prime}_{xyz}=W^{\prime}_{xXZz},\quad W^{\prime\prime}_{xYZ}=W^{\prime}_{xXzZ}

while
W^{\prime\prime}_{XyZ}
is the word comprised of
n_{Z}
consecutive “(” followed by
n_{Z}
“)”, and similarly
W^{\prime\prime}_{XYz}
is made of
n_{z}
consecutive “(” followed by the same number of “)”. The values
n^{\prime\prime}
are set from
n^{\prime}
as follows,

n^{\prime\prime}_{x}=n_{x}+n_{X},\quad n^{\prime\prime}_{y}=n_{Z},\quad n_{z}^{\prime\prime}=n_{z},\quad n^{\prime\prime}_{X}=0,\quad n^{\prime\prime}_{Y}=n_{z},\quad n^{\prime\prime}_{Z}=n_{Z},

while the floating trees
T^{\prime\prime}
are inherited from
T^{\prime}
on the faces
xyz,xYZ
and are trivial on
XyZ,XYz
.

The result
(n^{\prime\prime},W^{\prime\prime},T^{\prime\prime})
is the combinatorial curve
D_{y_{0}}
we were looking for. Other translations can be obtained from this one by applying the automorphisms of
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
, which do not affect the algebraicity or the degree of the curve since every such automorphism comes from a projective transformation. This two-step process of using the upward translation and other automorphisms can be repeated multiple times: in practice, we apply it to arrangements generated by Viro’s patchworking, repeating it until no new cases are obtained.

7.
Classification

In the repository associated to this work
[
14
]
, a full database of the combinatorial curves discussed here is available. In particular, to reproduce the results in this section it is sufficient to run the notebooks
cubic.ipynb
and
quartic.ipynb
. All computations use exact arithmetic throughout.

7.1.
Description of the enumeration

The classification procedure has many steps, which we now describe in the case of arrangements of three lines and a quartic. The case of cubics is similar but simpler.

Step 1 - Enumeration of all regular triangulations

Viro’s construction (§
6.1
) takes as one of its inputs any regular triangulation of
\conv\{(0,0),(4,0),(0,4)\}
. To generate as many cases as possible, we start by generating all possible such triangulations. This is carried out in the the
Macaulay2

[
8
]
script
quartic_triangulations.m2
and uses the
Topcom

[
20
]
package. The symmetry group of the triangle acts on the set of regular triangulations, and in order to reduce the number of cases, only one triangulation per orbit is kept in the database. The output is the database
quartic_triangulations.csv
and consists of 97460 triangulations, together with the values of the height functions which certify that the triangulations are regular.

Step 2 - Viro’s patchworking

Viro’s patchworking (§
6.1
) is applied to every triangulation and every possible sign choice. Symmetries arising from permutations of the faces is leveraged in order to again reduce the number of sign choices considered. In particular, once a sign
\varepsilon
has been used, the signs relative to the other 4 faces such as
\varepsilon(x,y)=(-1)^{x}\varepsilon(x,y)
are not considered.

Step 3 - Symmetries and translations

To the resulting combinatorial curves, we first apply the action of the automorphism group
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
(Example
3.2
) in order to recover those cases not considered by removing the symmetries in Step 1 and 2. Then we add those obtained by upward translation (§
6.3
) and again the action of the automorphism group of
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
; this process is repeated until no new curves emerge, which is after 2 times. These make up the set of realized curves
viro
, saved in the compressed database
quartic_viro.jsonl.gz
together with information on the triangulation they were generated from.

Step 4 - Generation of all floatless cases

To get the admissible curves, which are our candidates for what can be realizable, we first generate all combinatorial curves transverse to
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
which

•

are floatless;

•

have
quartic curve type
, meaning they have curve type (§
5.1
) equal to one of the 6 possible for quartic curves (between 0 and 4 non-nested ovals, or two nested ovals);

•

respect Bézout at order 1 (§
5.6
).

We end up with 40270 curves. This is done by first generating the possible intersection numbers
n
which satisfy basic Bézout and the rule that each
n_{f}
must be even. Then, for each face
f
, we generate all possible Dyck words
W_{f}
with lengths compatible with
n
. Finally, from every combination of Dyck words, the combinatorial curves
(n,W,T_{0})
with all trivial floating trees are formed: for each of these, we check whether the curve type is that of a quartic and whether it satisfies Bézout at order 1, removing it if the criteria are not satisfied.

Step 5 - Generation of all admissible cases

We first need a lemma.

7.2 Lemma
.

Suppose that
(n,W,T)\pitchfork(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
is a
(1,1,1,4)
-realizable combinatorial curve. Let
(n,W,T_{0})
be the floatless curve obtained by removing all floating ovals. Then
(n,W,T_{0})
has quartic curve type and admires
(1,1,1,4)
-Bézout, so it is among those generated in Step 4.

Proof.

The condition of having between 0 and 4 non-nested ovals or 2 nested ovals is obviously still met after removing ovals. Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be a curve realizing
(n,W,T)
. Then its ground part
\gr(\mathcal{C})
realizes
(n,W,T_{0})
. Any line transverse to
\mathcal{C}
is also transverse to
\gr(\mathcal{C})
and intersects it in
\leq 4
points, so
(n,W,T_{0})
respects Bézout at order 0, and by induction on
n
respects Bézout at any order
n
.
∎

Generate all combinatorial curves
(n,W,T)
transverse to
(V^{(3)},E^{(3)},F^{(3)})
which

•

have quartic curve type;

•

respect Bézout at order 0; and for which

•

after removing all floating ovals, the floatless curve
(n,W,T_{0})
is among those generated in Step 4.

By the lemma, this is a necessary condition for
(1,1,1,4)
-realizability. This the set of admissible curves
bezout
, stored in the compressed database
quartic_bezout.jsonl.gz
. To generate them, start from the set of floatless curves obtained in Step 4, and insert floating ovals in all possible ways that do not make the resulting arrangement have non-quartic type or violate Bézout at order 0.

This is done as follows: from a floatless curve
(n,W,T_{0})
, the
line distance matrix
is calculated, which is a matrix
L_{r,r^{\prime}}
such that for every pair of ground regions
g,g^{\prime}
, the entry
L_{g,g^{\prime}}
records the minimum number of intersections between the curve and a combinatorial line going through both
g,g^{\prime}
. The line distance matrix is calculated using the matrix
B\in\mathcal{P}^{\rho\times\rho}
defined in §
5.2
. A few remarks are that:

•

the fact that Bézout at order 0 is respected is equivalent to the fact that
L_{g,g^{\prime}}\leq 4
for all entries, and we want each entry to be
\leq 4
also after adding the floating ovals.

•

If we insert a floating region
f
inside a ground region
g
, then
L_{f,g^{\prime}}=L_{g,g^{\prime}}+2
for all ground regions
g^{\prime}
.

•

If we insert two floating regions
f,f^{\prime}
inside the ground regions resp.
g,g^{\prime}
(which can coincide), then
L_{f,f^{\prime}}=4+L_{g,g^{\prime}}
.

Therefore, to find all possible ways to add ovals to
(n,W,T_{0})
, let

R=\{g\in F_{\gr}\mid L_{g,g^{\prime}}\leq 2\text{ for all $g^{\prime}\in F_{\gr}$}\}

and let
m
be the maximum number of floating ovals that could be added to the curve, which is 0 if
(n,W,T_{0})
is made up of 2 nested ovals, and
4-c
if it has
c
non-nested ovals. Consider any multiset
M
of size
\leq m
of elements of
R
: in light of the previous remarks, we can insert one floating oval in each element of the multiset and obtain a curve
(n,W,T)
respecting Bézout at order 0, if and only if for each pair of elements
g,g^{\prime}\in M
(which can be equal if
g
appears twice or more in
M
) we have
L_{g,g^{\prime}}=0
. We assume that the added floating ovals do not nest with each other: we do not need to systematically consider the insertion of nested floating ovals, since for quartic curves the only way to have two floating ovals nested with each other is for them to be the whole curve, so there are only 4 such cases (one for each face of
(n,W,T_{0})
).

Step 6 - Final check

Verify that
viro
is a subset of
bezout
as expected, and find out the difference
rest
.

The correctness of this algorithm proves that the “realized” elements of
viro
are indeed realizable, and that all realizable cases are among the “admissible” elements
bezout
.

For the following results, recall (Example
3.11
) that the combinatorial curves up to
i
-automorphisms are the topological types of labeled arrangements
(\mathcal{L}_{x},\mathcal{L}_{y},\mathcal{L}_{z},\mathcal{C})
, while the combinatorial curves up to automorphisms are the topological types of unlabeled arrangements allowing for permutations of the three lines.
