Post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis of the shoulder: a case report
Authors: Jinxin Zhang, Chengjun Hao, Houzhi Huang, Shenghui Shi, Shuang Yang, Zhenzhong Bao, Ying Guo, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

Introduction

Chronic hemorrhagic synovitis of the shoulder following trauma is rare. Recurrent intra-articular bleeding may lead to synovial hypertrophy and hemosiderin deposition, producing imaging findings that resemble proliferative synovial disorders.2,4,9 Accurate diagnosis is essential because management strategies and prognosis differ substantially between reactive and neoplastic synovial diseases. Pigmented villonodular synovitis (PVNS), currently classified as diffuse-type tenosynovial giant cell tumor (TGCT), is the most recognized pigmented synovial disorder and is characterized by locally aggressive behavior and a risk of recurrence.7 Distinguishing post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis from PVNS and other pigmented conditions of the shoulder can therefore be challenging. We report a rare case of post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis presenting with diffuse black discoloration of the glenohumeral joint, with the aim of illustrating that correlation of trauma history, serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, arthroscopic appearance, metabolic evaluation, and histopathological examination may help distinguish this reactive condition from PVNS and other pigmented synovial disorders.

Case report

A 54-year-old man presented with progressive swelling, pain, and restricted active motion of the left shoulder for 12 months following trauma. The patient was right-hand dominant and sustained a left shoulder injury in a motor vehicle accident with a direct impact mechanism, without associated shoulder dislocation or subluxation. Initial MRI obtained shortly after the injury demonstrated a massive rotator cuff tear involving the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, and subscapularis tendons, accompanied by superior migration of the humeral head (Fig. 1), and the patient was managed conservatively at that time. Before presentation, he had not undergone any surgical intervention, aspiration, or injection, and had only received conservative treatment with oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. His medical and family history was unremarkable. Physical examination revealed visible swelling and mild local tenderness without erythema or warmth. Active range of motion was severely limited (abduction 45°, forward flexion 50°, external rotation 15°), whereas passive motion was preserved, consistent with pseudoparalysis. Mild atrophy of the shoulder girdle musculature was noted. Pre-operative American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons and Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand scores were 71.4 and 61.7, respectively. Pre-operative MRI at presentation demonstrated increased glenohumeral joint effusion and diffuse synovial thickening with multiple low-signal foci on both T1- and T2-weighted images, suggestive of hemosiderin deposition. Arthroscopy revealed diffuse synovial hyperplasia with extensive brown-to-black pigmentation and a burn-like appearance throughout the glenohumeral joint. Finely granular hemosiderin-like deposits were observed on the synovial surface. The synovium was hypertrophic and hypervascular but lacked villous projections or nodular tumor-like lesions. Arthroscopic findings demonstrated a massive rotator cuff tear with retraction to the level of the glenoid, consistent with Patte grade 3. The supraspinatus tendon and most of the infraspinatus tendon were irreparable because of severe chronic attritional degeneration associated with extensive pigmented synovial changes (Supplementary Video 1). Therefore, complete rotator cuff repair was not possible. A limited partial repair was performed, including repair of the subscapularis tendon and the residual repairable infraspinatus tissue to restore the anterior-posterior force couple. The long head of the biceps tendon was not treated with standard isolated tenodesis; instead, it was transferred posteriorly and fixed to the greater tuberosity using the “Chinese Way” technique as an augmentation procedure for the irreparable posterosuperior rotator cuff tear.

Given the unusual pigmentation, further investigations were undertaken post-operatively to exclude systemic metabolic disorders. Liver and renal function tests were normal. Rheumatoid factor was negative. Serum ceruloplasmin levels and 24-hour urinary copper excretion were within normal limits, and ophthalmologic examination revealed no Kayser–Fleischer rings, effectively excluding Wilson disease.

Histopathological examination confirmed post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis. Microscopic examination demonstrated synovial hyperplasia with numerous hemosiderin-laden macrophages. Prussian blue staining showed iron-positive particles, supporting hemosiderin deposition. No villous or nodular architecture, multinucleated giant cell proliferation, or tumor-like features were observed (Fig. 4).Figure 4Histopathological examination of the synovial lesion. Discussion

Post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis is an uncommon cause of chronic joint swelling and dysfunction following trauma.6 Repeated intra-articular bleeding leads to synovial hypertrophy and hemosiderin deposition, which may produce imaging findings that closely resemble PVNS. Because both entities demonstrate low-signal intensity on MRI secondary to hemosiderin accumulation, radiologic differentiation can be challenging.

The availability of initial postinjury MRI in this case provides important insight into the chronological evolution of the disease. The presence of a chronic massive rotator cuff tear suggests long-standing biomechanical dysfunction, which may contribute to persistent joint stress and recurrent microhemorrhage. Over time, this could result in synovial hypertrophy and hemosiderin deposition, ultimately manifesting as diffuse intra-articular pigmentation resembling PVNS and Wilson disease. Although post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis mimicking PVNS has been previously described, shoulder involvement with such diffuse black pigmentation is exceptionally uncommon. Therefore, the value of this case lies not in establishing a new disease entity but in providing comprehensive clinicopathological documentation of a rare shoulder presentation with serial imaging, arthroscopic visualization, metabolic work-up, histopathological confirmation, and post-operative follow-up.

PVNS, currently classified as diffuse-type TGCT, is characterized by villous or nodular synovial proliferation, multinucleated giant cells, lipid-laden macrophages, and tumor-like stromal growth.5 In contrast, post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis represents a reactive process without neoplastic proliferation. In the present case, arthroscopy revealed diffuse pigmented synovial hypertrophy with finely granular hemosiderin-like deposits but no villous projections or nodular masses. Histopathological examination confirmed post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis, demonstrating synovial hyperplasia with hemosiderin-laden macrophages and absence of PVNS-type villonodular architecture.

A distinctive feature of this case was the diffuse dark discoloration of the glenohumeral joint observed intraoperatively. Such a macroscopic appearance is unusual and may raise suspicion for diagnoses beyond PVNS, including systemic metabolic or pigment-related disorders.

Wilson disease, a disorder of copper metabolism characterized by abnormal copper deposition in multiple tissues, may be associated with pigmentary changes and should therefore be considered when atypical intra-articular discoloration is encountered.8 In the present case, diffuse black pigmentation was observed throughout the glenohumeral joint during arthroscopy, and such extensive pigmentation is rarely described in reactive synovial disorders. Therefore, additional investigations were undertaken to exclude systemic metabolic causes of abnormal pigment deposition, particularly Wilson disease. Serum ceruloplasmin levels, 24-hour urinary copper excretion, and ophthalmologic examination for Kayser–Fleischer rings were all normal. Furthermore, there was no biochemical, ophthalmologic, or hepatic evidence of copper deposition, effectively ruling out Wilson disease in this patient.

Similar diagnostic challenges have been described in other joints. Zampeli et al10 reported a post-traumatic intra-articular ankle hematoma that mimicked localized PVNS on MRI, in which ankle arthroscopy played a crucial role in both diagnosis and definitive treatment. Likewise, Khasawneh et al6 reported a pediatric case of post-traumatic hemosiderotic synovitis of the knee that closely mimicked diffuse PVNS on MRI. The diagnosis was ultimately established by histopathological examination, highlighting the difficulty of distinguishing these entities based on imaging findings alone. These reports across different joints suggest that post-traumatic hemorrhagic or hemosiderotic synovitis may consistently present with PVNS-like imaging and arthroscopic features, potentially leading to misdiagnosis.

This rarity and the overlap in imaging characteristics may result in unnecessary concern for neoplastic synovial disease. In our patient, the absence of villous or nodular synovial architecture on arthroscopy and histology was critical in excluding PVNS and confirming a reactive process.

From a clinical standpoint, distinguishing post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis from PVNS is essential because management strategies and prognosis differ substantially. Diffuse-type TGCT/PVNS may require more extensive synovectomy and carries a recognized risk of recurrence.7 In contrast, available case-based evidence suggests that recurrence after arthroscopic synovectomy or débridement for post-traumatic hemorrhagic or hemosiderotic synovitis is uncommon, although the current literature is largely limited to case reports and small series with variable follow-up.1,6,10 Given that our follow-up was limited to 6 months, longer observation is warranted to confirm the durability of symptom relief and exclude delayed recurrence.

In this case, arthroscopic management, including synovectomy and treatment of the associated rotator cuff and biceps pathology, was associated with meaningful early functional improvement, which is generally consistent with previous reports of arthroscopic treatment for shoulder synovial disorders.3 However, because multiple procedures were performed simultaneously, the individual contribution of synovectomy to the clinical outcome cannot be determined. This case highlights the importance of correlating clinical history, imaging findings, intraoperative appearance, laboratory evaluation, and histopathology when evaluating pigmented synovial lesions of the shoulder. Considering post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis as a potential diagnosis may help guide appropriate clinicopathological evaluation when diffuse intra-articular pigmentation is encountered after trauma. However, given the single-case nature of this report, broader diagnostic or therapeutic recommendations cannot be made, and further cases with longer follow-up are needed to better define its clinical course, recurrence risk, and optimal management. Therefore, the functional scores are presented as descriptive outcome data only and should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment efficacy.

Conclusion

We report a rare case of post-traumatic hemorrhagic synovitis of the shoulder characterized by diffuse black pigmentation of the glenohumeral joint observed arthroscopically. This case suggests that reactive hemorrhagic synovitis may be considered in the differential diagnosis of pigmented synovial lesions after trauma, particularly when MRI findings suggest hemosiderin deposition. Correlation of clinical history, serial imaging, arthroscopic appearance, metabolic evaluation, and histopathological findings may help avoid misdiagnosis as neoplastic or systemic pigmentary disease. Although arthroscopic management was associated with symptomatic and functional improvement at the 6-month follow-up, the short-term follow-up, combined surgical procedures and single-case design preclude definitive conclusions regarding the individual therapeutic effect of synovectomy, treatment efficacy, recurrence risk, or generalizability.
