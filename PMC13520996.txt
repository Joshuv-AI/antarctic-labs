Effects of dietary solid and liquid lysophospholipid application methods on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, enzyme activity, nutrient-transporter mRNA abundance, intestinal morphology, and meat quality in broiler chickens fed energy-reduced diets
Authors: Song Zhang, Xunpeng Jiang, Songlin Wang, Ye Wei, Jian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The biological efficacy of dietary lysophospholipids (LPLs) in broiler diets may be significantly modulated by their technical delivery methods. This study evaluated the effects of direct solid addition and liquid applications (oil-water pre-emulsification or pure oil pre-mixing) of LPLs on growth performance, nutrient digestibility, intestinal morphology, and gene expression in broilers fed energy-reduced diets. A total of 480 one-day-old male Ross 308 broilers were assigned to five treatments, with eight replicate pens of 12 birds. The treatments were a basal diet (T1), an energy-reduced diet (T2; 100 kcal/kg less metabolizable energy [ME]), and T2 supplemented with LPLs. The supplemented treatments received solid LPLs at 250 g/t (T3), oil-water pre-emulsified liquid LPLs at 500 g/t (T4), or oil-premixed liquid LPLs at 500 g/t (T5).Reducing dietary energy significantly deteriorated the overall feed conversion ratio (FCR) (P < 0.05). Broilers in liquid LPL groups exhibited higher final body weights and lower d21-42 FCR compared to both T2 and T3 groups (P < 0.05), with no differences between T4 and T5. All LPL treatments improved the digestibility of crude fat, gross energy and dry matter on days 21 and 42 (P < 0.05), while liquid methods further maximized dry matter digestibility at day 42 (P < 0.05). Morphologically, LPL supplementation restored the jejunal villus height to crypt depth ratio compromised by the low-energy diet (P < 0.05). Compared with solid addition, liquid LPLs increased the relative mRNA abundance of L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1), cationic amino acid transporter 1 (CAT1), fatty acid-binding protein 1 (FABP1). In conclusion, liquid LPL applications are superior to traditional solid addition in enhancing broiler finisher growth performance, nutrient digestibility, and transporter gene expression in reduced-energy diet. Notably, direct pre-mixing of LPLs in dietary oil is as highly effective as external oil-water pre-emulsification, offering an optimized strategy for reduced-energy dietary utilization.

Introduction

The poultry industry is a rapidly growing livestock sector that supports global food security by providing economical, high-quality animal protein (Abbas et al., 2025b). Global poultry meat production continues to increase with consumer demand, economic development, and urbanization. Advances in genetics, precision nutrition, and management have enabled modern broilers to achieve rapid growth, improved feed efficiency, and high breast meat yield (Abbas et al., 2022, 2024, 2025a).

Commercial diets commonly contain high energy and crude protein concentrations to support the growth potential of modern broilers (Zuidhof et al., 2014). However, lipid digestion in young broilers can be limited by gastrointestinal immaturity and low endogenous lipase and bile salt secretion. Exogenous emulsifiers, particularly lysophospholipids (LPLs), are therefore used to improve lipid emulsification, nutrient absorption, and broiler performance. Previous evidence indicates that dietary LPL supplementation can improve feed efficiency and nutrient utilization by supporting lipid digestion and intestinal nutrient transport (Priyatno et al., 2025).

The efficacy of LPLs may vary with dietary formulation and the physiological stage of the bird. Our previous work showed that LPL supplementation improved performance in both basal and energy-reduced diets (Zhao and Kim, 2017). Evidence also suggests that dietary fat source and inclusion level may influence the response to LPLs (Wealleans et al., 2020). In young broilers, LPL supplementation has improved growth and feed efficiency together with growth-related and nutrient-transporter gene expression (Zhang et al., 2022).

Recent advances in poultry feed manufacturing emphasize accurate additive delivery, mixing uniformity, and process control (Rubio et al., 2023; You et al., 2024). The physical form and application method of functional additives may therefore affect their distribution within the feed matrix. Although emulsifiers are commonly added directly to feed, alternative liquid application strategies remain poorly studied. These strategies include pre-mixing an emulsifier with dietary oil and preparing an oil-water pre-emulsion before feed incorporation. Only a study In Pekin ducks, fat pre-emulsification improved growth performance, nutrient utilization, and digestive enzyme activity (Zeng et al., 2022). However, it remains unclear whether direct solid addition, oil-phase pre-mixing, and oil-water pre-emulsification alter LPL efficacy in broilers.

Therefore, this study evaluated direct solid addition, oil-phase pre-mixing, and oil-water pre-emulsification of LPLs in broilers fed energy-reduced diets. We assessed growth performance, nutrient digestibility, intestinal morphology, and relative mRNA abundance of nutrient-transporter genes.

Materials and methods

Institutional approval

The experimental protocols describing the management and care of animals were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Ethic Approval Code: SICAUAC202512-1; Chengdu, China). All procedures were conducted within the Institute of Animal Nutrition, Key Laboratory for Animal Disease-Resistance Nutrition of China Ministry of Education.

Animals, diets, and housing

All LPL products were incorporated into the complete feed. The solid and liquid products were prepared from the same crude oil containing 16.4 % total LPLs. For the solid product, the crude LPL oil was diluted with silicon dioxide to obtain a product containing 6 % LPLs. For the liquid product, the crude oil was diluted mainly with oil to obtain a product containing 3 % LPLs. The measured LPL fractions were lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC; 7.9 %), lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI; 2.7 %), lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE; 4.1 %), and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA; 1.7 %). The products were supplied by Adisseo Life Science Co., Ltd. The LPL composition was quantified using quantitative phosphorus-31 nuclear magnetic resonance (31P NMR) spectroscopy based on the integrated phosphorus resonance signals assigned to the individual LPL classes.

A total of 480 one-day-old male Ross 308 broiler chicks, with an initial body weight of 48.4 ± 0.2 g, were used. Birds were assigned to five treatments, with eight replicate pens of 12 birds. The treatments were a basal control diet (T1), an energy-reduced diet (T2; basal metabolizable energy [ME] minus 100 kcal/kg), and three LPL-supplemented T2 diets. Treatment T3 contained the 6 % solid LPL product at 250 g/t. Treatments T4 and T5 contained the 3 % liquid LPL product at 500 g/t. Thus, each supplemented diet provided 15 g/t of active LPLs. Before feed incorporation, the solid product used in T3 was pre-diluted one-fold with soybean meal taken from the corresponding diet formula. This step improved distribution without introducing an additional dietary ingredient. The pre-diluted product was then added to the dry feed mixer. For T4 (pre-emulsification), dietary fat and liquid LPLs were mixed first, followed by the addition of 10 L of water. The mixture was then homogenized at 3,000 r/min for 20 s using a high-shear homogenizer to form a stable emulsion before being sprayed onto the feed. For T5, the liquid product was mixed directly with dietary oil at 35 r/min for 20 s without added water. The resulting oil-LPL mixture was then sprayed into the feed mixer. Accordingly, T4 represented oil-water pre-emulsification, whereas T5 represented direct oil-phase pre-mixing.

Broilers were housed in three-tier battery cages in an environmentally controlled facility. Birds had ad libitum access to feed and water throughout the experiment. Room temperature was maintained at 33°C for the first 3 d and then reduced by 2°C weekly until it reached 24°C. All diets met or exceeded the nutrient requirements recommended by the National Research Council (1994).

Growth performance and meat quality

Body weight (BW) and feed intake (FI) were recorded by replicate pen on d 1, 21, and 42. These measurements were used to calculate average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR). Mortality and the body weight of dead birds were recorded daily. Feed conversion ratio was corrected for mortality using the weight of dead birds and the corresponding pen feed intake. On d 42, one bird per replicate pen was randomly selected for carcass evaluation, providing eight birds per treatment. Breast and thigh muscle measurements included pH at 45 min and 24 h postmortem, drip loss, color (L*, a*, and b*), and cooking loss. Muscle pH was measured with a portable pH meter (pH-Star, Matthäus, Pöttmes, Germany). Meat color was measured with a Minolta Chromameter CR-300 (Minolta Camera, Osaka, Japan).

Nutrient digestibility

Apparent total tract digestibility (ATTD) was determined using 0.4 % titanium dioxide (TiO2) as an inert marker. Marker-containing diets were fed from d 18 to 21 and from d 39 to 42. Clean excreta were collected during the final 3 d of each phase, pooled by replicate pen, and dried at 65°C. Diets and excreta were analyzed for dry matter (DM), crude protein (CP), and ether extract (EE) according to AOAC (2007). Gross energy (GE) was measured using an adiabatic bomb calorimeter.

Digestive enzyme activity and intestinal morphology

Ileal digesta were collected on d 42, homogenized, and centrifuged for enzyme analysis. Lipase, α-amylase, and trypsin activities were measured with commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Jejunal segments of approximately 2 cm were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured using an Olympus image-processing system (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The villus height-to-crypt depth ratio (VH:CD) was then calculated.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver and jejunal mucosa using RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). RNA concentration and purity were determined with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). RNA integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Approximately 1 µg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed with a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Reactions used TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). Each 10-µL reaction contained 5 µL of TB Green Premix, 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µmol/L), 1 µL of cDNA, and 3.2 µL of RNase-free water. Thermal cycling began at 95°C for 30 s. This was followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 5 s and 60°C for 30 s. A melting-curve analysis confirmed amplification specificity.

The nutrient transporter genes were glucose transporter 5 (GLUT5), peptide transporter 1 (PEPT1), neutral amino acid transporter B0AT1 (B0AT1), L-type amino acid transporter 1 (LAT1), related to b0,+ amino acid transporter (rBAT), and cationic amino acid transporter 1 (CAT1). They also included excitatory amino acid transporter 3 (EAAT3), fatty acid-binding protein 1 (FABP1), and sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporter 1 (SMCT1). The collagen-related genes were collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1), transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), and matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2). Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed as a completely randomized design using the general linear model procedure of SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Normality and homogeneity of variance were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. The pen was the experimental unit. When the treatment effect was significant, means were separated using Tukey's multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Discussion

Growth performance, nutrient digestibility, and ileal digestive enzymes

Dietary energy is a central determinant of maintenance and production in modern broilers (Hu et al., 2021). Broilers were historically considered able to adjust FI when offered diets with different energy densities (Leeson et al., 1996). In this study, however, T2 birds did not increase FI enough to compensate for the energy reduction, and their overall FCR deteriorated. Nikbakhtzade et al. (2024) similarly suggested that gastrointestinal capacity may limit feed intake in modern, high-performance broilers. Contemporary broilers often consume feed near gut capacity (Ebeid et al., 2022). Physical fill and diet bulk density may therefore constrain compensation for reduced dietary energy.

LPL supplementation mitigated the adverse effect of the energy-reduced diet on overall FCR and final BW. This response agrees with a meta-analysis showing that exogenous emulsifiers improve broiler weight gain and feed conversion (Fernandes et al., 2023). The principal novel finding was that LPL efficacy depended on application method. The liquid treatments produced greater D-42 BW and similar overall feed efficiency than direct solid addition. Zeng et al. (2022) proposed that oil-water pre-emulsification may promote the formation of smaller, more stable lipid droplets before ingestion. However, T4 and T5 did not differ in the present study. This observation suggests that the prior addition of water to form an external emulsion may be not strictly requisite for maximizing LPL efficacy on growth performance.

Instead, we hypothesize two primary mechanisms driving the superiority of liquid delivery systems. First, pre-mixing LPLs directly into the liquid lipid phase may allow for a vastly superior and more homogeneous dispersion of the emulsifier throughout the feed matrix compared to dry solid addition. This intimate physical contact ensures that the LPLs are perfectly positioned at the lipid boundary, enabling a more rapid and uniform emulsification process immediately upon entering the aqueous environment of the gastrointestinal tract. Second, an "in situ pre-emulsification" phenomenon may occur during the feed manufacturing process itself. The intrinsic moisture of the feed ingredients, combined with steam applied during conditioning, may interact with the LPL-enriched oil coating to spontaneously generate an emulsion before the feed is even consumed. Therefore, dissolving LPLs into the dietary lipid phase is likely to offers a highly efficient and practically viable strategy to optimize lipid utilization.

During the grower phase, T4 and T5 increased crude fat, DM, digestibility relative to T3. This result is consistent with the benefits of fat pre-emulsification reported in Pekin ducks (Zeng et al., 2022). The advantage of liquid application was not observed during the starter phase. This stage-dependent response may reflect differences in dietary oil inclusion or gastrointestinal development. Lysophospholipids reduce lipid-droplet size and increase the oil-water interfacial area available to lipase (Siyal et al., 2017). The increased lipase activity and nutrient digestibility observed here may therefore contribute to the improved growth performance.

Intestinal morphology and relative mRNA abundance of jejunal collagen-related genes

LPLs may also support growth by improving intestinal integrity. Their incorporation into enterocyte membranes may alter membrane fluidity and permeability (Eid et al., 2025; Maingret et al., 2000). Previous studies have reported favorable effects of LPL supplementation on intestinal morphology (Boontiam et al., 2017; Papadopoulos et al., 2018). However, Cloft et al. (2021) observed altered jejunal gene expression without corresponding changes in villus height or crypt depth. Brautigan et al. (2017) found that a complete lysolecithin product increased collagen expression and villus length, whereas purified LPC did not reproduce these responses. This finding supports the concept that multiple lysophospholipid classes—such as LPE, LPA, LPI, and LPC (Scholfield, 1981)—must act synergistically within the lysolecithin complex to effectively translate molecular up-regulation into tangible macroscopic improvements in intestinal morphology. Together, these findings suggest that intestinal responses may depend on the LPL profile and the dietary context.

Mechanistically, LPLs may support jejunal integrity by modulating extracellular matrix (ECM)-receptor interaction and focal adhesion signaling. Li et al. (2022) reported that LPLs up-regulated REG4 and SERPINE2, which are involved in epithelial regeneration and ECM remodeling. Consistent with this mechanism, the present study showed increased relative mRNA abundance of COL1A1 and MMP2, particularly with liquid LPL application, suggesting enhanced ECM formation and turnover. The comparable responses to oil-phase pre-mixing and oil-water pre-emulsification further indicate that both liquid application methods may provide sufficient LPL dispersion to support intestinal remodeling. This study did not measure intestinal digesta viscosity, in vivo lipid-droplet size, micelle formation, transporter protein abundance, or transporter activity. Therefore, the proposed luminal and molecular mechanisms should be interpreted as possible explanations rather than direct functional evidence.

Nutrient transporters expression

In addition to their physical effects, LPLs may influence nutrient transport through receptor-mediated signaling. Zhang et al. (2022) reported that LPL supplementation increased fatty acid, amino acid, and growth-related gene expression. Van Hoeck et al. (2025) also observed increased expression of amino acid transporters and lipid-metabolism regulators after LPL exposure. These authors proposed that amphiphilic LPL fractions may activate G protein-coupled receptor signaling in enterocytes.

In the present study, LPL supplementation increased the relative mRNA abundance of several lipid, amino acid, and carbohydrate transporter genes. The liquid treatments increased FABP1 and LAT1 mRNA abundance more strongly than solid addition. In contrast, all LPL treatments increased GLUT5 mRNA abundance to a similar extent. This pattern suggests that nutrient transporters may not respond uniformly to LPL application method. The greater FABP1 and LAT1 responses may reflect improved LPL dispersion and lipid-associated signaling in the liquid treatments. The common GLUT5 response may instead reflect a broader improvement in intestinal absorptive status. Increased digestive enzyme activity and nutrient-transporter mRNA abundance have been associated with improved nutrient utilization in broilers (Toschi et al., 2024). Accordingly, the transporter responses observed here may partly explain the improvements in nutrient digestibility and production performance. However, transporter protein abundance and activity were not measured, so this proposed link requires further investigation.

Meat quality

Modern broiler selection and high-energy diets frequently compromise meat quality by driving rapid muscle hypertrophy and excessive glycogen storage. This fuels intense postmortem anaerobic glycolysis, leading to rapid pH decline and impaired water-holding capacity (WHC) (Petracci et al., 2015; Trocino et al., 2015). Accordingly, dietary energy in our study significantly altered breast meat pH and drip loss, while thigh meat remained unaffected. This resilience in thigh meat is intrinsically linked to its higher proportion of oxidative fibers (Types I and IIa), which offer superior buffering capacity against lactic acid compared to the highly susceptible fast-twitch glycolytic fibers (Type IIb) of breast muscle (Baéza et al., 2012).

Interestingly, LPL supplementation in our study successfully stabilized breast meat pH but increased cooking loss in solid addition and pre-emulsification of LPLs. The pH stabilization aligns with Eid et al. (2025), who attributed this to improved intramuscular water and fat distribution. Conversely, the elevated cooking loss corroborates An et al. (2020) and is likely a physiological trade-off of the accelerated muscle fiber expansion induced by LPLs, which can loosen the protein matrix and facilitate moisture exudation during thermal processing.

Crucially, these phenotypic responses highlight that the meat quality benefits of LPLs are highly context-dependent. For instance, Ahmad et al. (2023) found no pH alterations using LPLs in low-energy diets, while Eid et al. (2025) noted reduced cooking loss under specific stress conditions. Collectively, this suggests that under optimal conditions with metabolic challenges (e.g., high-energy feeding), LPLs prioritize rapid tissue deposition, which stabilizes pH but increases susceptibility to thermal moisture loss. In contrast, under high-energy or stressful environments, LPLs may shift toward acting primarily as structural membrane stabilizers. Ultimately, irrespective of the delivery method—whether solid or liquid—the specific application technique of LPLs exerts no further significant impact on the overall meat quality profile.

Practical implications, study limitations, and future directions

Industrial adoption of LPL application methods requires balancing biological efficacy with feed-mill feasibility. Solid addition (T3) is operationally simple but produced generally weaker responses under the tested conditions. Oil-phase pre-mixing (T5) produced responses comparable to oil-water pre-emulsification (T4) while requiring a simpler preparation process. In contrast, T4 requires high-shear equipment and an additional water phase. However, processing costs, energy consumption, starch gelatinization, and feed-mill throughput were not measured. Therefore, the relative industrial feasibility of these methods requires commercial-scale validation.

A strength of this study was the controlled comparison of three application methods using the same crude LPL profiles source and an equivalent active dose. However, all LPL-supplemented treatments were based on the energy-reduced diet, RT-qPCR measured only mRNA abundance, and intestinal viscosity and in vivo lipid dispersion were not evaluated. Future studies should compare these application methods across dietary energy levels and include functional, intestinal physicochemical, feed-processing, and economic measurements.

Conclusion

Dietary LPL supplementation mitigated the adverse effects of an energy-reduced diet on broiler performance, nutrient digestibility, and intestinal morphology. Deliver method influenced LPL efficacy, with liquid treatments producing stronger responses than direct solid addition. Direct oil-phase pre-mixing and oil-water pre-emulsification produced comparable biological effects. Thus, oil-phase pre-mixing may provide a simpler industrial strategy for applying liquid LPLs. This practical interpretation should be confirmed through commercial-scale processing and economic evaluation.
