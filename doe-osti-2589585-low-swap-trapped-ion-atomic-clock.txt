ABSTRACT: (250-word limit)
For this project we attempted to show signature traits of excellent optical clock performance in a
trapped ion system which had an integrated photonic delivery system for all clock algorithm
beams. These traits were 1) Contrast for the clock transition, 2) Coherence of the clock
transition, and 3) ion lifetime. We were partially successful and were able to demonstrate the
clock laser portion of an optical clock with ~ 4.4𝑥10―14 𝜏 fractional frequency instability.
Additionally, we made progress towards a background-free detection method.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
Precision clocks, based on atomic references, are the key technology for maintaining global
timing impacting global communications, fundamental research, infrastructure, positioning, and
more [1-3]. The highest performance clocks have high Size, Weight, and Power (SWaP), and are
not suitable for field deployment. Portable clocks are a high priority for US government research
agencies as witnessed by the high number of recent calls for this technology [4-9].

The R&D question this project was trying to answer was: “Can signature traits of excellent clock
performance be shown in a trapped ion system using integrated photonic components?” These
traits are
 1) clock transition contrast of >= 90%
 2) clock coherence time of >= 3 ms
 3) Ion lifetime of ~ hour
Additionally, if substantial progress toward these traits was obtained, an optical clock was to be
produced and benchmarked in frequency stability and operation time. The integrated photonic
components used were optical waveguides, which guided the light once on chip (through edge
coupling on the side of the trap, see slide two of the addendum) to the trapping region where the
light would be diffracted out of the waveguide and onto various trapped ions. The ions, +Yb171,
would be trapped in individual wells.
Of the three main traits we sought in the system we attained varying degrees of success, see
addendum slide three. We achieved a clock contrast of 86%, which is close enough to the desired
contrast as to count as a success and a system with this contrast would not be limited by the
contrast. For clock coherence, we achieved a time of 1.73 ms. This is relatively close to the goal
of 3 ms but is different enough that it would be a moderate obstacle to achieving great clock
performance. Finally, the desired ion lifetime of roughly an hour was not successful, and we

were only able to show an average lifetime of roughly 1 minute. This was largely due to sub-par
vacuum pressure, likely due to existence of non-UHV materials in the chamber such as the fibers
and fiber v-groove arrays, and charge noise on the diffraction gratings induced by the UV light
throughput.

We were able to demonstrate an the laser portion of an optical clock with a frequency instability
of 4.4𝑥10―14 𝜏 for over two hours, see addendum slide four. In addition to this result, we
potentially created a new way of detecting ion fluorescence that would be “background free”.
More experimentation is necessary to determine if this is true, see addendum slide five.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
To create an optical clock, and to study the aforementioned primary traits necessary to achieve
good clock performance, trapped ions were used as the frequency reference, specifically, ion
species +Yb171. See reference [10] for details of a similar ion trap system. See addendum slide
two. Importantly, the trap contained monolithically integrated photonic components such as
optical waveguides and diffraction gratings that were used to deliver all necessary beams to the
ions. The light was input in the waveguides via edge coupling, with a fiber v-groove array. Two
holes were drilled in a blank conflat that was attached to the chamber and the fibers were input
through these whole. The holes were then filled with low out-gassing epoxy and sealed to form a
vacuum-tight seal. All experiments were carried out at room temperature while the chamber
pressure was in the 10-11 to 10-10 torr regime.

Once the light was coupled onto the chip and into the waveguides it was guided to the trapping
region, where the diffractive gratings steered the light unto numerous ions. The quadrupole
transition of +Yb171, with m=0 for both ground and excited state, was used as the clock
transition, having a wavelength of approximately 435.5 nm. The clock algorithm consisted of
state preparation, where the ions were prepared in the |2S1/2,F=0,m=0> state, followed by
probing by the quadrupole beam, created by a Toptica TA-SHG pro system, to promote the ions
into the |2D3/2,F=2,m=0> state. The state of the ions was then detected with a resonant Doppler
beam at roughly 369.5 nm. The information about each ion’s state was then related to an FPGA
based system, which also controlled the laser sequence, for analysis and to determine the
necessary frequency shift as controlled by free space AOMs. The cycle was then repeated. Ion
lifetime when the algorithm was running was roughly 1 minute. Lost ions were replaced through
an automatic loading and shuttling sequence.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:

The results are detailed in addendum slides three and four. Concerning the three traits and the
target values, as described in the introduction and executive summary, we were partially
successful. For the contrast, which in this case is for the quadrupole clock transition, a result of
86% was achieved. While the goal was 90% or above, this was close enough to consider that this
level of contrast would not significantly negatively impact the overall clock performance. It is
likely that the contrast was limited by the initial quanta of heat. Due to relatively poor optical
coupling, it was not possible to sideband cool the ions during the clock cycle, so the initial
quanta # was likely in the 10-20 region with a thermal distribution. This could be improved by
improving the optical coupling, which would allow for sideband cooling.

For the coherence time, a value of ~ 1.73 ms was achieved whereas a value of 3 ms or greater
was sought. This coherence time likely did reduce clock performance, although at only a
moderate level. This was likely limited both by the initial quanta number present and the heating
rate the ions suffered. Since the device was at room temperature the heating rate was in the ~1
quanta/ms region, limiting long coherence times. This could be improved by either going to
lower temperatures, which would require significant infrastructure changes, using high
axial/radial frequences, which could offset the heating rate reduction by producing more heat on
the trap, or by cleaning the trap surface with such techniques as Argon-ion surface milling.

Lastly, the observed ion lifetimes of roughly 1 minute were far from the goal of an hour. This
had a significant impact on clock performance in that it required constant reloading of the lost
ions, which not only interrupted the algorithm but also likely caused electric field noise from
charge build up on the trap surface due the UV nature of the ionizing beams. The short lifetimes
were likely caused by the non-UHV material in the vacuum chamber, namely the fibers with
acrylate coating and the various dielectric components of the fiber v-groove array.

Although the goals were not reached for the various traits, we were able to produce the laser
portion of an optical clock that achieved an Allan deviation trend line of 4.4𝑥10―14 𝜏 and that
ran for over two hours on multiple occasions. The limiting factor of the performance was likely
the relatively high ratio of state preparation and measurement time to the probe time, which was
over 1. This was due to the low amount of ion fluorescence collected, causing the measurement
time to be on the order of a few ms. This is due to the fact that it was not possible to collect ion
fluorescence from the “top” view, the standard way to collect, because the resonant Doppler
beam to excite the ion would create too much noise when exiting the grating. We had to collect
light from the side of the trap. In the future it would be advantageous to implement a background
free detection method, as detailed in addendum slide five.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:

We anticipate at least one publication from this effort. As we were able to demonstrate an optical
atomic clock which showed reasonable performance (far from the best optical clocks but
significantly above that of any microwave clock) using only waveguide-delivery light for the
clock algorithm, this publication would detail that work. We will target the journal Phys. Rev.
Lett., but if unsuccessful, we will submit to Nat. Phot. . While we have not produced enough data
for a publication on the topic of background free measurement, there is strong evidence that this
technique will be successful. Upon further development, this work would be appropriate for a
journal such as Phys. Rev. A.

We have registered for the 2025 Southwest Quantum Information and Technology (SQuInT) to
present our optical clock work, and will attend potentially more conferences on this topic, or on
the background free topic, if sufficient funding can be obtained. Concerning IP, Technical
advance #17076 has been filed, covering the background free detection method. This method is
not limited to +Yb171 but could be use in almost any ion species/isotope that has hyperfine
structure, and possibility even those that do not, although implementation would likely be more
difficult in that scenario.

Next step for this research will likely require more funding than was available and a longer time
horizon. While the principle of the topic has been proven, to make this system practical and
useful in the field, significant effort must be spent on the engineering side to shrink the physics
package and control electronics. Another important step is the inclusion of a transparent but
conductive coating, such as ITO, on the trap diffraction gratings to protect the ion from charge
noise created when UV light exists the grating.

For further details on impact, see slide six and seven of the addendum.

CONCLUSION:

Over the course of this project, we created the laser portion of an optical atomic clock using
multiple +Yb171 ions as frequency references and studied its clock properties. What is unique
about this system is that all laser light used for the clock algorithm, that is for cooling, preparing,
probing, and detection the ions, was delivered through monolithically integrated photonic
components such as waveguides and diffraction gratings. The achieved frequency instability was
on the order of 4.4𝑥10―14 𝜏 and ran for over two hours on multiple occasions. We studied key
traits of the system including transition contrast and coherence, and ion lifetime. For transition
contrast, we came close to our goal of 90%, achieving 86%. For coherence time, we achieved ~
1.73 ms where our original goal was 3 ms. These were likely limited by initial phonon count and
high heating rate. These could be improved with Argon-ion milling the trap surface in situ and
covering the diffraction gratings with ITO. For ion lifetime, our goal of an hour average lifetime

was not close to the actual lifetime achieved (while running the clock algorithm), which was
around one minute. This was likely due to non-UHV compatible materials present in the
chamber, namely the optical fiber coating and materials associated with the fiber v-groove array,
and charge noise on the diffraction gratings. Suitable alternative materials need to be found in the
future for better performance. In addition to the creation of this photonically enabled clock, we
studied a new technique for background free detection. This work is preliminary but hold much
promise for advancing traps using all waveguide-delivered light.

We plan to submit our work concerning the clock system for publication in Phys. Rev. Lett. Or
Nat. Phot. The background free method is not yet ready for publication, and we will look for
suitable funding to continue the effort.

The creation of a portable optical atomic clock is of great significance to inertial navigation,
especially in GPS denied environments. With further development, the technology developed
under this project could lead to a fully deployable system.
