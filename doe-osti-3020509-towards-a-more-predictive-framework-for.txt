Project Overview
Laser-driven particle acceleration (LDPA) has emerged as a critical technology for high-energy-
density physics applications since its discovery at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory
twenty years ago. However, realizing the full potential of these particle sources requires
understanding the fundamental acceleration mechanisms and developing enhanced target designs
for improved performance. This research addressed the need for controllable, high-performance
laser-driven proton sources through two complementary approaches: experimentally
investigating sheath field dynamics in multi-picosecond laser regimes and developing novel
three-dimensional printed microstructured targets to achieve enhanced particle acceleration.

Our approach combined experimental measurements of accelerating fields using proton
radiography techniques with systematic scaling studies of additively manufactured targets with
controlled density gradients. The research achieved significant milestones including the first
direct measurements of Target Normal Sheath Acceleration (TNSA) sheath fields in the multi-
picosecond regime for pulse durations from 0.6 to 10 picoseconds, demonstration that longer
pulse durations sustain stronger electric fields for extended periods enabling enhanced proton
acceleration, and validation of microstructured targets achieving record proton energies of 105.5
MeV with conversion efficiencies up to 8 percent compared to ~1-2% percent for conventional
targets. These results provide fundamental understanding of acceleration mechanisms in
unexplored parameter regimes and demonstrate practical pathways to high-flux applications in
fusion energy research, medical physics, and national security applications.

Mission Impact
This research directly supports LLNL missions in high-energy-density science, inertial
confinement fusion, and advanced diagnostic capabilities. The experimental measurements of
sheath field dynamics provide fundamental understanding needed to optimize laser-driven
particle sources for proton radiography applications critical to stockpile stewardship missions,
while enhanced target designs provide pathways to high-flux sources needed for proton fast
ignition concepts in inertial fusion energy. The work establishes new experimental capabilities
for measuring electromagnetic field evolution in laser-plasma interactions, advancing objectives
in plasma physics and high-energy-density science. The research supports NNSA missions
through improved diagnostic capabilities for high-energy-density experiments and provides
foundational understanding for optimizing advanced high-intensity short-pulse laser systems
including the National Ignition Facility Advanced Radiographic Capability. The demonstrated

performance improvements in microstructured targets offer practical benefits for applications
requiring high-energy, high-flux particle sources while reaffirming LLNL leadership in novel
target design and additive manufacturing for laser-plasma applications.

Abstract
Laser-driven particle acceleration has progressed from fundamental research to a promising
approach for diverse applications in fusion energy, medical physics, and national security. This
project addressed critical gaps in understanding fundamental acceleration mechanisms and
performance limitations through integrated experimental and computational approaches. We
conducted the first experimental measurements of Target Normal Sheath Acceleration field
dynamics in multi-picosecond regimes and demonstrated enhanced performance using three-
dimensional printed microstructured targets. The research revealed that multi-picosecond laser
pulses sustain stronger accelerating electric fields for extended periods compared to sub-
picosecond pulses, directly explaining enhanced proton energies observed on multi-kilojoule
laser systems. As a mechanism to tune and optimize of the accelerating sheath fields, novel 3D-
printed microstructured targets were explored in depth in this project and achieved proton
energies exceeding 100 MeV and six to eight times improved conversion efficiency compared to
conventional targets. These advances provide fundamental understanding of acceleration physics
in unexplored parameter regimes and demonstrate pathways to high-flux applications required
for fusion energy research and advanced diagnostic capabilities.

Background and Research Objectives
The field of laser-driven particle acceleration
began with the discovery at Lawrence
Livermore National Laboratory that high-
intensity laser beams could produce beam-like
sources of high-energy protons from thin
solid targets [1]. Since this breakthrough,
experiments have demonstrated production of
diverse particle species including protons,
light and heavy ions, electrons, positrons, and
neutrons [2]. The most widely studied
mechanism, Target Normal Sheath
Acceleration, occurs when high-intensity
lasers interact with thin foils, creating

Despite significant progress over twenty years
of research, gaps remained in understanding acceleration mechanisms, particularly regarding
field dynamics and their relationship to particle characteristics. Early theoretical work provided
models for expected proton energies and field profiles [5], but uncertainties around key
parameters such as acceleration time scales required reliance on semi-empirical relationships [4].
While empirical scaling laws provided useful guidance, they had limitations when applied to
unexplored parameter regimes and did not fully account for observed deviations from established
predictions. Figure 1 shows a compilation of TNSA proton experiments using flat foils from the

sub-picosecond (green points) regime and the multi-picosecond regime (red points). The banded
regions show established scaling laws like the Fuchs model [4] the relate laser intensity to
maximum proton energy. However, recent experiments on multi-ps facilities including the
National Ignition Facility-Advanced Radiographic Capability (NIF-ARC) and OMEGA
Extended Performance (EP) laser have shown enhanced energies when compared to these scaling
laws as shown by the red points on Figure 1 [6]. These systems operate with unique parameters
including multi-kilojoule energies, large focal spots, and relatively long pulse durations, resulting
in quasi-relativistic intensities that unexpectedly produce proton energies two to four times
higher than predicted by conventional models. Multi-kJ, high intensity laser systems are
particularly important for the field of high-energy-density physics, a core mission of the
laboratory, since they allow for the generation of high fluxes of ion or photon sources for
probing of dense materials or materials in extreme conditions [6]. However, the enhancement
mechanisms driving this improved performance were not understood, limiting optimization
efforts and application development.

Furthermore, conventional flat foil targets often using in TNSA experiments had also plateaued
in performance, with maximum proton energies around 70 MeV and conversion efficiencies
typically of a few percent [7]. In this project we explored novel target approaches using
microstructured arrays in the highly relativistic regimes and also the largely unexplored in the
multi-picosecond, quasi-relativistic parameter space of multi-kilojoule laser systems.

The primary research objectives were to understand enhancement mechanisms in multi-
picosecond laser-driven particle acceleration and demonstrate improved target designs for high-
flux applications. Specific objectives included measuring sheath field dynamics experimentally
to understand how field evolution differs between pulse duration regimes, scaling novel
microstructured targets across relevant parameter ranges to optimize performance and validating
experimental observations through advanced simulations to establish predictive understanding.

Advanced Target Design Using
Microstructured Arrays: During the
course of this project, fundamental research
in the sheath field dynamics demonstrate
that laser parameters such as pulse duration
are one way to tune and optimize the sheath
and thus the accelerating particle
parameters. Another way to tune this
sheath is through advanced targets. In this Figure 8: Experimental setup at the OMEGA-EP laser facility
pursuit, we conducted comprehensive studying the use of 3D printed microstructured targets
scaling studies of three-dimensional printed
microstructured targets manufactured using two-photon polymerization techniques. These targets
consist of micron-scale wires arranged in log-pile arrays with controlled density gradients
achieved through variable wire spacing along the target depth (shown in Figure 8). The
manufacturing process enables precise control of target characteristics including wire diameter,
target thickness, wire pattern, and density profile.

Over the course of four experimental campaigns at the OMEGA Extended Performance facility,
rigorous optimization was performed studying different total thicknesses from 5 to 50
micrometers and various printing patterns to understand effects on ion acceleration. Two primary
microstructured target types were investigated: Stochastic Microstructured Array (SMA) targets
with randomized but well-characterized wire patterns, and Log-Pile Microstructured Array
(LPA) targets with ordered grid arrangements, both featuring defined density gradients.

The microstructured targets demonstrated exceptional performance improvements over
conventional targets. Stochastic Microstructured Array targets achieved a record proton
maximum energy of 105.5 MeV at 500 Joules laser energy and 0.6 picosecond pulse duration,
while 10-micrometer thick Log-Pile Microstructured Array targets reached 79 MeV with 8
percent conversion efficiency [9]. In direct comparison, a 5-micrometer thick plastic solid
density target under identical laser parameters yielded only 50 MeV maximum energy with 1.4
percent conversion efficiency, demonstrating the substantial performance advantage of
microstructured designs.

Performance improvements were attributed to enhanced laser-electron coupling enabled by
relativistic transparency effects. The targets' controlled low density allows laser breakthrough,
enabling extended electron heating of the sheath behind the target and sustaining the accelerating
electric field for longer durations compared to solid density targets. OSIRIS particle-in-cell
simulations validated this mechanism (Figure 10), showing that the extended electron heating
results in significantly higher ion flux and energy. The two-photon polymerization printing
technique enables precise control over average target density, allowing optimization for the
relativistic transparency regime to boost coupling efficiency.

Figure 10: Electron phase space for (a) solid foil and (b) microstructured target from particle-in-cell simulations in OSIRIS. Laser
flux is traced in blue and the original target position is shown by vertical lines at x = 20λo to 30λo. The microstructure turns
transparent due to relativistic effects, allowing a significant portion of the laser to heat the electron sheath behind the target
region. This sheath heating results in a much stronger electric potential for accelerating ions.

kJ regime since lasers in this parameter space as previously described have increased relevance

was the foundation of an awarded Discovery Science campaign on the NIF-ARC facility led by

isochoric heating of material for studies of materials at extreme conditions. Given the flexibility

Mission Impact

This research directly advances multiple LLNL mission areas through new understanding of
fundamental acceleration physics and demonstration of enhanced target performance. The
experimental measurements of sheath field dynamics provide foundational understanding needed
to optimize laser-driven particle sources for proton radiography applications critical to stockpile
stewardship missions. The ability to predict and control field evolution enables tailored source
characteristics for specific diagnostic requirements, improving efficiency and effectiveness of
high-energy-density experiments.

Enhanced understanding of acceleration mechanisms supports development of advanced
diagnostic capabilities for high-energy-density experiments relevant to stockpile stewardship and
inertial confinement fusion research. The demonstrated relationship between pulse duration and
field sustainability provides practical guidance for optimizing laser systems and experimental
configurations to maximize diagnostic performance. As an example, this work has been
foundational for the creation of a new proton radiography platform on the NIF-ARC which can
be used by users in need of probing of dense plasmas and measurement of the electric and
magnetic fields contained in plasmas [10].

The microstructured target performance provides pathways to high-flux proton sources needed
for proton fast ignition concepts in inertial fusion energy research, proton radiography, isochoric
heating for warm dense matter studies and other laser-driven sources such as neutrons for non-
destructive evaluation.

The work establishes new experimental capabilities at LLNL for measuring electromagnetic field
evolution in laser-plasma interactions, providing tools for validating advanced simulation codes
and understanding complex plasma physics phenomena. These capabilities support broader
Laboratory objectives in computational physics validation and multi-physics modeling
development. The research also provides foundational understanding supporting optimization of
advanced laser systems including the National Ignition Facility Advanced Radiographic
Capability. The insights into pulse duration effects and target optimization inform operational
strategies for maximizing diagnostic capability and experimental efficiency.

Conclusion

The research establishes fundamental understanding of enhanced laser-driven particle
acceleration in multi-picosecond regimes and demonstrates practical pathways to improved
target performance through advanced manufacturing. The experimental validation of sustained
field dynamics with longer pulse durations provides clear explanation for enhanced performance
observed on multi-kilojoule laser systems and establishes principles for optimization.

Immediate next steps include implementing optimized target designs in operational laser
systems, extending measurements to additional pulse duration and intensity regimes, and
developing predictive models based on experimental validation. The demonstrated
microstructured target performance enables immediate applications in high-energy-density
experiments requiring enhanced particle sources.

Technology transfer opportunities exist for medical physics applications including proton
therapy and radioisotope production, where enhanced conversion efficiency could enable more
compact and cost-effective systems. Future research directions include extending measurements
to additional acceleration mechanisms beyond Target Normal Sheath Acceleration, investigating
novel target architectures enabled by advanced manufacturing techniques, and developing hybrid
approaches combining optimized pulse shaping with microstructured targets. The integration of
enhanced targets with high-repetition-rate laser systems represents significant opportunity for
practical applications requiring high-average-power particle sources. The work positions LLNL
at the forefront of laser-driven particle acceleration research and provides foundations for next-

generation applications in fusion energy, national security, and advanced manufacturing using
laser-accelerated particles.
