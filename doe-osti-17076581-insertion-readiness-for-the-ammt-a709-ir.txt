 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The growing nuclear industry is developing technology concepts around nonlight-water reactor
technologies, such as molten salt cooled, sodium cooled, or gas cooled. All of these concepts will use
higher temperatures as a means to extract energy at a higher efficiency than current plants and therefore
put a larger demand on the structural materials. Thus, material performance must increase for greater
safety margins, longer lifetimes, and reduced maintenance costs. Alloy 709 (A709) is championed as the
next generation of austenitic alloys for advanced nuclear reactors. MX, M23C6, and Nb-rich precipitates
within the hardened alloy pin dislocations and lead to better creep properties than the widely used
traditional type 304 and 316 stainless steels. Although this alloy is undergoing ASME Boiler and Pressure
Vessel Code qualification for high-temperature reactors, the effects of radiation are not included in this
dataset. In order to use a material in nuclear construction, both as-fabricated and irradiated properties
must be sufficient for the targeted use case within an acceptable safety margin.

 This report provides a high-level status update on capsule preparations for the High Flux Isotope
Reactor (HFIR) portion of the A709 irradiation campaign to generate engineering data supported by
scientific understanding. This document refines the previously proposed irradiation plans from 2024 and
2025 toward insertion of multiple types of capsules into the HFIR to generate specimens for
postirradiation performance measurements of time-independent and time-dependent properties. A high-
level, year-by-year plan is presented with a focus on first-of-a-kind (FOAK) postirradiation examination
(PIE) creep performance on the wrought A709 heat 539900 and welded A709, with limited work
comparing heat-to-heat variations on time-independent properties by including heat 58776 and solution
annealed A709. In the near term, FY27 will position the program to produce FOAK engineering data by
inserting both general tensile (GENTEN) and mini buttonhead creep (MBHCRP) capsules for the priority
heat of A709. The campaign will produce FOAK creep property measurements for wrought A709 in
FY28 at multiple combinations of stress and/or temperature. FY29 will focus on high-temperature time-
independent tensile properties up to 800°C and produce FOAK PIE creep data on welded A709. FY30
will pursue microstructure analysis to understand the creep and tensile performance at the more
mechanistic level. Throughout the campaign, creep tests will be performed to gather data to support the
generation of knockdown factors in comparison to the ASME code case dataset.

 Multiple HFIR standardized GENTEN irradiation capsules to measure time-independent
properties are ready for insertion. The GENTEN capsules are designed for temperatures in the range of
400°C–800°C with damage accumulation up to 10 dpa. The thermal behavior was simulated for capsules
located in a HFIR target rod rabbit holder (TRRH) under nominal operating conditions. The results of the
thermal analyses were used to determine the design parameters of capsules of A709 for the gas gap
thickness and gas composition appropriate for each irradiation temperature design. A sensitivity analysis
using the thermal conductivities of multiple alloys showed that temperature deviations from A709 are
under 5%. This outcome provided confidence that mixing alloys within a capsule has a minimal effect on
the capsule design temperature. Procurement based on the design calculations led to the assembly and
readiness of multiple standardized GENTEN capsules.

 The mini buttonhead (MBH) capsule design centered around a single subsize MBH specimen
matured from concept to full design. The full capsule design contains the MBH specimen, six SSJ2
subsize tensile specimens, and associated passive SiC thermometry. Thermal analysis demonstrated the
feasibility of achieving target temperatures of 700°C and 800°C in the MBH gauge region. The MBH
specimen was approved for HFIR insertion. Fabrication efforts are underway, with readiness for insertion
anticipated in FY27. Concurrently, a size-effects study was initiated to compare standard full-size ASTM
specimens with subsize MBH and SSJ geometries. At the two higher temperatures of 700°C and 800°C,
the MBH and full-size ASTM geometry tests exhibited overlapping creep behavior with comparable

 1

creep-rupture lifetimes. The overall creep strain curves for subsize SSJ specimens fell below those of the
MBH and full-size ASTM specimens. In anticipation of the results of the size effect study, Oak Ridge
National Laboratory initiated a scoping study to determine the feasibility of deploying a creep frame
within the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing (IMET) hot-cell facility to perform PIE creep
tests on the MBH specimens. The study identified space within the IMET to locate a frame and created an
action plan to adapt existing mini creep-frame designs to account for remote operation in a hot-cell
environment. For ease of deployment, a single frame is planned, with future frames incorporating
impending operational experience. Efforts in FY27 will support the fabrication and verification of this
new in-cell creep capability, the transfer of irradiation capsules to and from HFIR, and the preparation
work to produce FOAK data on the creep performance of A709 in FY28.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Nuclear power holds a promise to provide a cheap, reliable energy source for the ever-increasing demand
in the United States. Although the current fleet of light-water reactors (LWRs) provides gigawatt-scale
baseload power, numerous applications can be served by smaller power units. The growing industry is
developing nuclear technology concepts around nonlight-water reactor technologies, such as molten salt
cooled, sodium cooled, or gas cooled. All of these concepts will attempt to use higher temperatures as a
means to extract energy at a higher efficiency than current plants and therefore put a larger demand on the
structural materials. Improved performance of structural materials could enable greater safety margins,
longer plant lifetimes, and reduce maintenance costs.

Alloy 709 (A709), also known as UNS S31025, is championed by the Advanced Reactor Technologies
program to be qualified as the next generation of austenitic alloys for Class A components construction
for advanced nuclear reactors. A709 has a nominal composition of Fe-20Cr-25Ni, with Mn, Mo, and Nb
added to form precipitating phases. The program downselected a heat treatment of 1,150°C minimum
solution anneal, with a subsequent additional precipitation treatment at 775°C heat treatment for 10 h to
form MX, M23C6, and most importantly, a fine density of Nb-rich precipitates on dislocations [1]. The
Nb-rich precipitates pin the dislocations and lead the alloy to better creep properties than the traditional
widely used type 304 and 316 stainless steels. A substantial mechanical-property data package has been
generated for plate products conforming to ASTM A240 and A480 through a multinational laboratory
collaboration among the US Department of Energy’s Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL), Idaho
National Laboratory (INL), and Argonne National Laboratory (Argonne) [2]. The data package enabled
completion of the draft 100,000 h Alloy 709 Code Case, which is currently under ballot for approval in
the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (ASME) Boiler and Pressure Vessel Code (BPVC)
Section III, “Rules for Construction of Nuclear Facility Components-Division 5-High Temperature
Reactors” [3]. However, the required dataset for inclusion in the BPVC does not include environmental
effects, such as corrosion or radiation, which are needed to support the use of A709 in the nuclear
industry.

The Advanced Materials and Manufacturing Technologies (AMMT) program proposed a neutron
irradiation campaign in 2024 to capture the evolution of properties for the plate form of A709, with
additional details clarified in 2025 [4]. To use a material in nuclear construction, both as-fabricated and
irradiated properties must be sufficient for the targeted use case within an acceptable safety margin. The
irradiation campaign needs to provide engineering data to establish operational design parameters and
determine how environmental factors alter mechanical response. Because the fabrication of engineering-
scale components will require welds to join pieces of this alloy, a variation of A709 used for welding
must also be included and evaluated. The neutron irradiation campaign will advance the scientific
understanding of the effects of irradiation on A709 and, more importantly, provide the critical design data
needed to enable engineering solutions that account for environmental effects. This work is critical to
extending the use case for A709 to a broader range of facilities with distinct environments, such as
neutron spectra, neutron fluence rates, temperatures, and service lifetimes, and to establishing a pathway
for evaluating future material environmental effects.

This report provides a high-level status update on capsule preparations for the High Flux Isotope Reactor
(HFIR) portion of the A709 irradiation campaign. This document refines the previously proposed
irradiation plans from 2024 and 2025 [4, 5] toward insertion of multiple types of capsules into HFIR to
generate specimens for postirradiation performance measurements in Section 2. The activity covers both
use of established capsule designs and the advancement of a new capsule designed around a mini
buttonhead (MBH) creep specimen. Multiple HFIR standardized general tensile (GENTEN) irradiation
capsules are ready for insertion, and the MBH capsule fabrication is underway, with readiness for

 3

insertion anticipated in FY27. This report provides the calculations necessary to support time-independent
property measurements using standardized capsules and those ready for insertion in Section 3. The
detailed design and analysis necessary to establish a new standard capsule for irradiation of miniature
buttonhead creep (MBHCRP) specimens is presented in Section 4. A scoping study, which is presented in
Section 5, was conducted concurrently with the new capsule analysis to determine the feasibility of
deploying a miniature creep test frame in the Irradiated Materials Examination and Testing (IMET)
facility at ORNL. Finally, Section 6 details the plan for FY27 to insert capsules and prepare for
postirradiation examination (PIE) in FY28.

 2. A709 IRRADIATION CAMPAIGN MATRIX AND OVERVIEW

The overall goal of the A709 irradiation campaign is to develop an operational envelope for the plate and
weld forms of A709 by providing data of engineering importance supported by scientific understanding.
The planned operational window ranges from 300°C to 650°C, with the upper application temperature
bound to 800°C. The expected displacement damage from the neutron environment for the structural
applications of this alloy are up to 10 dpa. Although intended for fast neutron spectra, dedicated test
reactors for fast neutron spectra are not available in the United States. Therefore, the neutron spectra for
this campaign are planned to be the mixed spectrum of HFIR and the use of a cadmium shroud to increase
the fast to thermal neutron ratio in the Advanced Test Reactor (ATR). Both time-independent properties
and time-dependent properties will be assessed through PIE. Time-independent properties include fracture
toughness, hardness, and tensile properties. The time-dependent properties focus on the creep rupture and
limited creep crack growth performance of A709 following irradiation. In situ collection of time-
dependent properties is planned for future phases of the program. Microstructural characterization
throughout the program will help determine the origins of the observed mechanical properties. Related
information on the composition of the A709 heats to be examined in the campaign are listed in Table 1
with additional processing details contained in previous reports [6-8].

Both facilities will perform irradiation experiments on a combination of samples for time-independent
and time-dependent properties, targeting insertion dates in fiscal years 2027 through 2030. A range of
target temperatures from 300°C to 800°C is included to gather properties. Because creep is primarily a
concern at high temperatures, the irradiation campaign limits the investigation of time-dependent
properties to a range similar to that of the creep tests already performed on the as-prepared material from
600°C to 800°C. Time-independent properties at lower temperatures are driven by microstructure
evolution inducing embrittlement and swelling, nominally 500°C and below, and thus are captured in this
plan. Specimens to evaluate time-independent properties are also included at the higher temperatures to
provide a comparison to the as-prepared properties.

This plan makes use of the strengths of each facility. The high neutron fluence rate of HFIR allows higher
displacement levels of 2 and 10 dpa to be obtained, with a crossover point for HFIR and ATR at 2 dpa. In
addition, INL will target 1 dpa to provide data on the progression and evolution of mechanical properties
over a range of relevant damage levels when coupled with the higher-dose irradiations performed at
HFIR. The larger-diameter flux trap positions in ATR allow for larger sample geometries than in the flux
trap of HFIR, removing some of the anticipated specimen size effects when comparing with the
nonirradiated properties. Details of the intended capsules and sample geometries are detailed in later
sections of this document for the HFIR portion of this A709 irradiation campaign. Through the
measurement of properties in a thermal neutron-rich environment in HFIR and a thermal neutron-shielded
environment in ATR, the distinction between the environments and, thus, between the properties will
provide conservative estimates of how transmutation-induced helium affects mechanical performance.
This first-of-a-kind (FOAK) data at relatively low displacements per atom (dpa) will provide insight to
help interpret the properties of A709 in an upcoming irradiation campaign at the Jōyō sodium-cooled fast
reactor test facility [9], up to 160 dpa at temperatures up to 550°C.

Baseline engineering data for the design of components starts with time-independent properties. Tensile
properties such as yield stress, uniform elongation, ultimate tensile strength, and total elongation provide
valuable design parameters. Bombardment with neutrons changes each of these properties, and thus each

 5

must be measured. Irradiation at both HFIR and ATR will generate time-independent properties using
traditional tensile tests and slow strain rate tests. The HFIR irradiations will be accomplished using the
GENTEN design described further in Section 3. Tensile properties will be measured both at room
temperature and at the target irradiation temperature to assess radiation-induced changes in performance.
Key design parameters for structural materials are the time-dependent properties of creep, fatigue, and
creep-fatigue. Obtaining these properties during in-core experiments requires sophisticated equipment and
dedicated programs for each set of measurements. The AMMT program decided to prioritize
postirradiation creep properties as the initial data to support the alloy’s use in nuclear environments.
Because of the irradiation times and long duration of creep tests, priority is given to providing FOAK data
within the time frame of the program using a limited number of irradiation and test temperatures at a
single damage level of 2 dpa to produce at least four points on a Larson–Miller plot.

To achieve the goals of the program, a high-level year-by-year plan for the HFIR irradiations is presented
in Table 2, stretching over four years from FY27 to FY30. The campaign is structured to focus on PIE
creep performance on wrought A709 heat 539900 and welded A709, with limited work comparing heat-
to-heat variations by including the 58776 heat and solution annealed A709. By stretching the insertion
schedule over multiple fiscal years, the mixture of insertions and PIE allows FOAK data each year and
provides additional statistics by the end of FY30. Specimens were fabricated from A709 and their variants
to fill capsules for FY27. Additional sources within the AMMT program provided specimens of laser
powder bed fusion (LPBF) A709, directed energy deposition (DED) A709, LPBF 625, LPBF 244, and
wrought 316H. Additionally, specimens of alloy 625, both forged and produced by powder metallurgy
with hot isostatic pressing (PM-HIP), originally provided by the Electric Power Research Institute
(EPRI), were included for comparison to a previous irradiation campaign conducted in ATR [10] and
associated characterization and ion irradiation data [11-15].

In the near term, FY27 will position the program to produce FOAK engineering data by inserting both
GENTEN and MBHCRP capsules for the priority heat 539900 of A709. Additional variants of A709 will
be included in the GENTEN capsules up to 10 dpa such as solution annealed A709, a secondary wrought
heat, 58776, and A709 produced using either LPBF or DED advanced manufacturing (AM) methods.
Forward-looking high-temperature alloys like alloy 625 in both wrought and AM forms and alloy 244
will be included as space allows in both capsule types.

Looking to future years, the groundwork laid in FY27 will lead to regular data acquisition of key
performance properties. Assuming a miniature creep frame is successfully deployed at the IMET in FY27,
the campaign will produce FOAK creep property measurements for wrought A709 in FY28 at multiple
combinations of stress and/or temperature. Additional MBHCRP capsules will be inserted with either
wrought A709 or welded A709 to generate PIE creep data in future years. Preliminary PIE tensile tests
will gather time-independent properties up to 600°C across a range of A709 variants, whereas the final set
of GENTEN capsules will be inserted to round out this study. FY29 will focus on high-temperature time-
independent tensile properties up to 800°C and produce FOAK PIE creep data on A709 weld specimens.
Additional MBHCRP capsules will be inserted to generate increased statistics on wrought and welded
A709 and can be guided by PIE creep-modeling needs elsewhere in the AMMT program. FY30 will
pursue microstructure analysis to understand the creep and tensile performance at a more mechanistic
level and insert additional MBHCRP capsules to fill data gaps for a comparison of the PIE property data
with ASME BPVC code case data. At this point, the campaign will have generated sufficient data for the
539900 heat of wrought A709. This year-by-year plan only examines the PIE creep performance of the
539900 heat and welded A709. If the irradiation campaign continues, an additional heat of A709, heat
58776, would undergo a dedicated campaign to provide heat-to-heat variation in PIE creep performance.

 3. GENTEN DESIGN, FABRICATION, AND ASSEMBLY

The GENTEN irradiation capsule design [16] will be used to irradiate miniature tensile specimens for this
campaign to generate time-independent properties. This design is a commonly used standardized vehicle
that allows for the insertion of various specimen geometries within a standard HFIR flux trap capsule,
known as a “rabbit.” The internal assembly is flexible in that that allows for the inclusion of various
specimen geometries and a range of material types, all covered by existing safety basis calculations. This
flexibility makes it a cost-effective means for irradiated material investigation, with a relatively short
inception-to-insertion timeline for most campaigns. A rendering of the GENTEN capsule and its
components is shown in Figure 2.

The GENTEN design used for this campaign includes three holder subassemblies stacked within a
standard rabbit. Each holder contains four specimen stacks, held in place by a central spring pin. Each
specimen stack will include either three SSJ2 specimens (0.5 mm thick) or one each of SSJ2 and SSJ4
specimens (1 mm thick) to meet the PIE needs. The space around the specimens’ gauge sections will be

 7

filled with chevrons fabricated from A709 material. Additionally, each specimen stack contains a
rectangular SiC passive thermometer for postirradiation temperature assessment [17, 18].

The coolant flow through the HFIR flux trap around the outside surface of the rabbit provides the constant
thermal boundary condition to which all the neutronic heat generated in the capsule internals flows. This
creates a temperature distribution in which the temperature is highest at the center of the assembly and
decreases radially toward the outside of the assembly. The holder stack is offset from contact with the
housing end cap and housing bottom of the capsule on either end by a steel wave spring. This ensures that
the primary thermal path for generated heat is the radial gap between the outside faces of the holders and
the internal surface of the capsule housing. Therefore, the desired temperature distribution of the
assembly is ultimately reached by adjusting two primary parameters: the size of the radial gap and the
conductivity of the interstitial noble gas mixture with which this space is filled.

 Figure 2. Rendering of a GENTEN rabbit capsule assembly showing each component.

The analyses were performed using Ansys Mechanical thermal finite element models, with a rendering of
the mesh for the GENTEN model shown in Figure 3. A configuration of the assembly with three SSJ2
specimens in every stack was used. The effect of including an SSJ4 specimen in place of two SSJ2
specimens in any given stack will be considered negligible. The full geometry of the assembly was
meshed with solid elements, primarily consisting of 10-node tetrahedral and 20-node hexahedral
elements, as well as a small quantity of 15-node wedge elements. Mesh controls were used to ensure that
the primary items of interest (specimens, chevrons, and SiC passive thermometers [TMs]) included at
least two elements through the thickness of each body. The appropriate material models were assigned to
each part mesh, with all the specimens and chevrons characterized as A709 and all holders as Mo.

 8

 Figure 3. Rendering of the finite element mesh of the GENTEN model.

The thermal behavior was simulated for capsules located in a HFIR target rod rabbit holder (TRRH)
under nominal operating conditions. Within the TRRH, axial position 2 was chosen as the targeted
irradiation location. These results will also apply to position 6, which is symmetric about the reactor axial
centerline and functionally equivalent. Because the power profile decreases exponentially towards the
axial periphery of the reactor, a spatially varying heating profile was applied on a per-element basis, with
FEA elements representing volume closest to the reactor centerline having the highest internal heat
generation rate. A convective boundary condition was applied to the external radial surface of the capsule
wall, with parameters calculated based on the flow of coolant around the capsule. Thermal conductance
between all bodies in the assembly was calculated by a suite of user-defined macros that apply the
thermal conductivity of the interstitial gas and account for microscopic aspects of conductance between
surfaces.

The results of the thermal analyses were used to determine the design parameters appropriate for each
irradiation temperature design. The two variable parameters used to reach the target temperature were fill
gas composition (He or Ne) and the outer diameter of the Mo holders. These values and their respective
temperature results are shown in Table 3. The results are presented for each axial holder location, with
holder position 1 being the farthest from the reactor centerline and holder position 3 being the closest. An
example of a temperature result contour plot for the specimens is shown in Figure 4. The overall design
temperature for each capsule is met within the constraints, albeit with deviations from the nominal
temperature for each axial holder.

 4. MBHCRP DESIGN AND THERMAL CONSIDERATIONS

The MBHCRP capsule is primarily designed around a single subsize MBH specimen. The MBH
specimen, shown in Figure 7, has a gauge diameter and length of 3 mm and 28.6 mm, respectively. The
total length of the specimen is 45 mm, and the head diameter is 5.722 mm. The rabbit capsule housing
inner diameter of 9.5 mm and internal length of 61.7 mm limit the assembly to a single MBH specimen
per rabbit. In an effort to maximize the internal volume of the rabbit, a design goal of incorporating
additional SSJ2 specimens was established. Similar to the GENTEN rabbit described previously, the
MBH design needs to incorporate a specimen holder to establish an insulating gas gap. However, unlike
GENTEN, the holders cannot simply be through-holes for the specimen to slide into because of the large
gap that would form around the specimen gauge region. Therefore, a part had to be designed to fill this
gap to allow for a heat transfer flow path from the specimen to the HFIR primary coolant. A coffin was
designed to clamshell around the MBH specimen before insertion into the holder. Owing to the profile of
the MBH specimen, the internal shape of the coffin leads to complicated machining. In order to prioritize
the contact around the specimen gauge region for heat transfer, intentionally relieving the contact around
the MBH heads was investigated in the subsequent thermal analysis. Because of the flat faces on the
MBH specimen, this left room in the rabbit for a sleeve containing SSJ2 specimens to be inserted on each
side of the MBH specimen, along with a row of SiC passive thermometers for postirradiation specimen
verification.

 13

 Figure 7. MBHCRP capsule design (a) internal exploded view, (b) radial cross section, and
 (c) final assembled capsule.

Applying the same process used for the GENTEN capsule, the analyses were performed using Ansys
Mechanical thermal finite element models, with a rendering of the mesh for the MBH capsule model
shown in Figure 8. The full geometry of the assembly was meshed with solid elements, primarily
consisting of 10-node tetrahedral and 20-node hexahedral elements, as well as a small number of 15-node
wedge and 13-node pyramid elements. Mesh controls were used to ensure that the primary items of
interest (specimens andTMs) included at least two elements through the thickness of each body. The
appropriate material models were assigned to each part mesh, with all the specimens and the coffin
characterized as A709 and the holder as Mo.

 14

 Figure 8. Rendering of the finite element mesh of the MBH model.

The complex fabrication of the coffin led to the need for additional analyses. During the initial design of
the MBH capsules, the MBH specimen heads were cooler than the gauge region (~100°C). However, the
coffin was modeled in direct contact with the specimen in the gauge and head regions. The coffin was
loosened around the head region of the specimen, accomplishing several goals: reducing temperature
range across the specimens, ensuring contact between the specimen and coffin in the gauge region, and
reducing part complexity to decrease part costs. Doing so increased the insulating gas region around the
specimen heads, driving up the temperatures in the head regions. The temperature results shown in this
report are given with the nominal gap conditions, reducing temperature distribution across the specimen
to about 60°C in both temperature cases.

The MBH capsules are modeled in TRRH axial position 4, at the reactor centerline. Neutronic and
convective boundary conditions are applied in a manner similar to the GENTEN design. The thermal
results for the 700°C case are given in Figure 9 and Figure 10, and those for the 800°C case are given in
Figure 11 and Figure 12. The MBH specimens are represented in several views to show the temperature
distribution through the thickness and along the gauge and head regions. The SSJ2 specimens are shown
as complete specimens with the gauge regions isolated. During postirradiation examination, the TMs will
give significant input into the irradiation conditions, and because the outer TMs show an increased
temperature range and skewed distribution compared with the center TMs, these components are also
given separately. The center TMs have a significantly more even distribution and should be prioritized
over the outer TMs if limited differential scanning calorimetry samples are taken for post-irradiation
temperature verification Finally, the coffin temperatures distributions are also given because the A709
material is of interest for further study using this component. Beyond the temperature distribution figures,
Table 6 gives the average, maximum, and minimum temperatures expected in select components.

 5. SCOPING STUDY OF MINI CREEP-FRAME DEPLOYMENT IN ORNL HOT CELLS

As stated previously in Section 2, high-temperature creep performance is one of the priority time-
dependent properties to establish a safety envelope for the use of A709 as a structural alloy. Standard
creep-testing frames are traditionally large, floor-standing systems designed to apply sustained
mechanical loads at elevated temperatures over long durations using specimens conforming to ASTM E8
specifications [23]. However, induced radioactivity and space constraints within HFIR and ATR limit the
use of ASTM full-size or subsize specimens and led to the development of the MBHCRP capsule to
accommodate MBH creep specimens (Section 4). Given that both ORNL and INL will produce a
significant number of creep specimens to test over the campaign’s lifetime, creep-frame availability is
paramount to the success of the program. Thus, ORNL initiated a scoping study to determine the
feasibility of deploying a creep frame within the IMET hot-cell facility. The challenge that arose when
opting to deploy a frame into the hot cell was the limited space available in the work area. The solution is
a proposed hot-cell mini creep-frame design based on an existing prototype currently in use at ORNL to
test subsize specimens.

The existing prototype uses a mechanically simple, dead-weight load application system in conjunction
with a split-tube furnace, as shown in Figure 13. The frame is constructed using off-the-shelf strut channel
components, reducing weight, cost, procurement, and assembly time. The system is modular in nature,
and its current configuration boasts three frames tied together into a single footprint. The hot-cell version
of this mini creep-frame design will incorporate these concepts for the construction of the system but will
require specific modifications to accommodate remote operation in a hot-cell environment. For ease of
deployment, a single frame is planned, with future frames incorporating impending operational
experience.

 18

 Figure 13. ORNL mini creep-frame prototype with three units.

The mini creep frame will be deployed into Cell 2 of the IMET 3025E hot-cell facility at ORNL. Space
within this work area is highly limited, and therefore adapting the assembly’s footprint must be optimized
for a designated area within Cell 2. The aim will be to keep the frame compact enough to ensure that the
cell access paths for technicians and operators remain unobstructed while preserving vital workspace for
additional in-cell activities, such as specimen handling, identification, and sorting. The footprint of the
proposed design is 24 × 26 in., accounting for the frame layout and lift table integration, which will fit in
the proposed usable area of the hot cell (Figure 14).

 19

Figure 14. (left) Schematic detailing the footprint available for the mini creep frame within cell 2 at the IMET
 and (right) a photo of the area within cell 2 to be used for creep-frame installation.

Access to the cell interior is extremely limited, and mechanical manipulators must be used for nearly all
activities. To ensure manipulator-friendly operation, the system will implement a remotely operated
hydraulic lift table to accommodate single-step load application, with a mock-up shown in Figure 15. The
lift table will be controlled from outside of the cell to allow for seamless control. The weight application
system will be a simple, pin-select weight stack to allow the operator to select a predetermined weight by
inserting a single pin. Once the weight is selected, the lift table will be raised into position to allow the
operator to hook the weight to the system load train. Once hooked, the lift table will be remotely lowered
to perform a single-step load application to the sample. The weights will be weighed through ORNL’s
metrology procedures and certified individually before hot-cell installation and regularly checked to
ensure proper stress application.

 20

  Figure 15. Proposed hot-cell mini creep-frame assembly showing the pin-select weight stack and remotely
 operated lift table.

Custom fixturing is currently under consideration, with attention being given to accounting for load train
compliance, manipulator handling, off-normal events, and workflow optimization in the hot cell. These
optimizations will be made to facilitate ease of loading samples into the fixture and handling the fixture
itself via a remote manipulator. The focus will be on maintaining system simplicity for use in the hot-cell
environment and streamlining operations by reducing end-user complexity.

An additional concern specific to the hot-cell environment is the degradation of components caused by
radiation. High beta and gamma dose rates within the cell require that sensitive instrumentation
electronics, temperature controllers, and automated data acquisition hardware remain outside of the cell
environment. Cable networks, signal lines, and thermocouple connections will be routed through
standard, shielded, cell-face feedthroughs to prevent equipment degradation. The effects of signal loss
through extended transmission will be investigated by repeating out-of-cell creep experiments before use
with irradiated materials to ensure functionality.

When a creep test fails, the dead weights will drop suddenly and impact the floor, causing vibrational
transfer to nearby systems. In a hot-cell environment, this disrupts nearby equipment and operations and
has the potential to scatter tools or radioactive materials throughout the cell. To mitigate the transfer of
sudden, large vibrations, a “catch table” prototype has been proposed. The catch table will consist of four
hydraulic piston shock absorbers mounted to a plate that rests on top of the lift table and directly under
the weight stack. When the dead weights fall, the catch table will absorb the shock and provide a soft
landing, mitigating vibrational transfer. The catch table and hydraulic lift table can then be lowered to
remove the two creep specimen halves and proceed with post-test processes.

differences in specimen geometry. The ASME code case data were gathered using standard full-size

because the subsize specimens fall outside the normal permissible dimensions. Two thermocouples were

 Preliminary creep strain curves are presented in Figure 16 for three creep conditions overlapping
with the ASME code case data: 600°C at 330 MPa, 700°C at 160 MPa, and 800°C at 82 MPa. These tests
were designed for short creep-rupture times to produce an assessment that can inform the irradiation and
PIE test matrices and assess the specimen size effect. Several important observations were made from the
data:

• Significant differences emerged based on the specimen geometry. The creep strain curves of the
 subsize SSJ specimens exhibited evident differences in creep behavior, creep rupture strain, and creep
 life relative to the MBH and full-size ASTM specimens.
• At the two higher temperatures, 700°C and 800°C, the MBH and full-size ASTM geometry tests
 exhibited overlapping creep behavior with comparable creep-rupture lifetimes.

The uncertainty in creep behavior resulting from the small sample size in this preliminary study warrants
the research team’s attention. A limited number of additional creep tests on subsize specimens are
ongoing and will be reported in a future study. Based on the results obtained to date, the MBH specimen
appears to be the safest choice for the key PIE creep data because it shows the closest agreement with the
standard test results. To enable a comparison between the MBH and SSJ4 geometries, SSJ4 specimens are
included in the GENTEN capsules of the HFIR campaign and provide a transitive association between
PIE creep data produced from irradiation in HFIR and ATR.

 7. SUMMARY

In summary, the A709 irradiation campaign for the AMMT program is proceeding well. The needs of the
program were defined to support the deployment of wrought A709 by generating data of engineering
importance supported by scientific understanding. The overall goals of the campaign are to gather

 24

mechanical performance data to determine conservative knockdown factors for creep and tensile
performance of both plate A709 and its weld. The damage levels and temperature conditions are set to
examine life-limiting behaviors, including swelling, embrittlement, and creep rupture. To accomplish
these goals, a multi-laboratory coordinated campaign will expose A709 to the nation’s strongest neutron
sources, HFIR and ATR. Within the HFIR campaign, specimens for time-independent and time-
dependent properties will be inserted using the GENTEN and MBHCRP capsules respectively. The
GENTEN capsules are standardized capsules targeting damage levels up to 10 dpa in a temperature range
of 400°C–800°C. Thermal analysis confirmed the viability of the test matrix and led to the fabrication and
assembly of multiple ready-to-insert capsules containing variants of A709 and several future-looking
alloys, including alloy 625. Probing time-dependent properties required a new capsule to be developed
around a miniature creep specimen. Detailed physical and thermal design work provided confidence in
the ability of the capsule to irradiate the miniature creep specimens at high temperatures. Additional space
within the capsule was allocated to the inclusion of miniature tensile specimens to further understand the
high-temperature evolution of materials of interest. With the large number of creep specimens being
generated through irradiations in HFIR and ATR, the limitation of testing throughput was addressed by
determining a type of creep-testing frame and location within the IMET complex at ORNL where the
frame can be installed. Efforts in FY27 will support the deployment of this new in-cell creep capability,
the transfer of irradiation capsules to and from HFIR, and the preparation work to produce FOAK data on
the creep performance of A709 in FY28.
