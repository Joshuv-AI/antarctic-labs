Background/IntroducJon Electronic excita-ons called quasipar-cles are a source of
decoherence in superconduc-ng quantum circuits. The temperature-dependent equilibrium
quasipar-cle density is predicted by the theory of Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieﬀer (BCS), but
experimental measurements show larger than expected quasipar-cle popula-ons at
temperatures below 100 mK where most quantum circuits operate. The nature of the excess
quasipar-cle popula-on requires further study—both characteriza-on and mi-ga-on—to
advance the performance of superconduc-ng devices for applica-ons in Nuclear Physics and
Quantum Informa-on Science. Environmental radia-on of both terrestrial and cosmic origins is
a source of quasipar-cles, so it is important to quan-ta-vely assess the eﬃciency with which
this radia-on is converted to quasipar-cles. The primary conversion path is somewhat indirect.
Environmental radia-on interacts with the insula-ng substrate of a quantum circuit, and the
resul-ng phonons spread within the substrate un-l they ul-mately interact with the circuit’s
superconduc-ng thin ﬁlms (paQerned on the surface of the substrate). We note that this
conversion path is also how the signal of interest is generated and collected in various rare
event search experiments such as searches for neutrinoless double-beta decay, coherent
neutrino scaQering, and dark maQer interac-ons.

Technical Report Project DE-SC0021415 was a collabora-ve eﬀort between students and staﬀ at
the University of Colorado Boulder, Paciﬁc Northwest Na-onal Laboratory, and the Na-onal
Ins-tute of Standards and Technology. Collec-vely, we had three signiﬁcant accomplishments.
First, we debuted the use of Thermal Kine-c Inductance Detectors (TKIDs) to study the eﬀects
of environmental radia-on. TKIDs are superconduc-ng microresonators whose electrical
proper-es respond to the local temperature. We designed, fabricated, and operated TKID
sensors on micromachined silicon islands. Environmental radia-on absorbed in the island
produces a per-event spectroscopic signal and the island geometry provides a well-deﬁned
interac-on volume. While there are opportuni-es to further improve their performance, we
demonstrated TKID devices with wide dynamic range, good linearity, and good energy
sensi-vity. Much like qubits, TKIDs are read out using a microwave tone on a common feedline.
Indeed, it is possible for TKIDs and qubits to share readout electronics, cabling, and the feedline
itself so that sensors and processing elements may be readily combined in the future. We
published a manuscript in the Journal of Low Temperature Physics [1] and another in the IEEE
Transac9ons on Applied Superconduc9vity [5] describing the physics of TKID devices.

Second, we used TKIDs to measure the spectrum of events caused by environmental radia-on
in substrates of two thicknesses (0.5 and 1.5 mm) located inside a 50 mK cryostat at UCB. The
spectra cover a wide energy range from a few keV up to several MeV. Interes-ngly, there is a
tail of very high energy events due to cosmic protons and neutrons that was not considered in
previous work. We compared the measured spectra to predic-ons based on independent
informa-on about the radia-on source terms and simula-ons of the propaga-on of those
sources into the cryostat and their subsequent interac-on with the substrates. Measured and
predicted spectra show an excellent level of agreement with no free parameters in the
predic-ons. Our results provide researchers with quan-ta-ve informa-on about the expected
background spectra in the substrates of their quantum circuits. Our results also provide
guidance on ways to protect quantum circuits from environmental radia-on including substrate
thinning and micromachined mechanical isola-on. Our primary manuscript on this topic was
published in the pres-gious journal Physical Review X Quantum [3] and we published a
secondary manuscript in IEEE Transac9ons on Applied Superconduc9vity [4] that allows readers
to compute the radia-on background under a variety of diﬀerent condi-ons that may beQer
capture their own circumstances.

Third, we simulated phonon transport in our TKID devices using the Geant4 Condensed MaQer
Physics (G4CMP) package. We simulated device geometries with micromachined mechanical
isola-on and thin-ﬁlm phonon traps to demonstrate the poten-al of these methods to protect
quantum circuits and to demonstrate the u-lity of G4CMP for modeling quantum devices. Our
work contributed to the ﬁrst deﬁni-ve G4CMP publica-on in Nuclear Instruments and Methods
A [2], which features a TKID device example.
