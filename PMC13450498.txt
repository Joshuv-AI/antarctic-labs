Development and internal validation of models to predict postoperative troponin and myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery by incorporating hypotension as a predictor: single-centre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Ryan Best, Sihaoyu Sherry Gao, Su-Yin MacDonell, PJ Devereaux, Paula Branco, Matthias Görges, Alana M Flexman, Lang Wu, Ron Ree, Charles Lo, Janny Xue Chen Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS) is associated with poor outcomes. Current prediction models have limitations and rarely incorporate postoperative hypotension. Our primary objective was to develop and validate a prediction model incorporating postoperative hypotension to predict subsequent postoperative high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T concentrations and MINS.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre retrospective cohort study of patients aged ≥45 yr who underwent inpatient noncardiac surgery between 2020 and 2021 at St. Paul’s Hospital, Canada. We excluded patients with missing vital signs or postoperative troponin concentrations, those who underwent repeat surgery <72 h after surgery, and those who were readmitted <72h after surgery. We developed multivariable linear mixed-effects models using percentage of time with postoperative hypotension (mean arterial pressure <75 mm Hg) on postoperative days (PODs) 0–2, age, sex, emergency surgery, and revised cardiac risk index to predict maximum troponin concentration on PODs 1–3 (primary model) and MINS (secondary model). Internal validation was performed using repeated patient-based five-fold cross-validation with 100 repetitions, using the root mean square error (RMSE), marginal R-squared (R2), and conditional R2 as metrics.ResultsWe included 846 patients (mean age 72 yr [standard deviation 11]; 57.4% male). Notably, 35% of patients (299 of 846) developed MINS between PODs 0 and 3. The primary linear mixed-effects model for log-transformed troponin showed a conditional R2 of 0.94, a marginal R2 of 0.20, and a cross-validated RMSE of 0.90 during internal validation. The internally validated MINS model area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.69 (95% CI 0.63–0.75). The inclusion of intraoperative hypotension did not improve the prediction of troponin or MINS.ConclusionsA model including postoperative hypotension to predict postoperative troponin concentrations fits our data well, but did not show sufficient accuracy and is not ready for clinical practice. Better-quality data and additional clinical variables may enhance model development.Trial registration. Clinicaltrials.gov NCT05188001, registered 7 January 2022.Keywords: intraoperative hypotension, myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery, perioperative, postoperative hypotension, risk prediction, troponin

Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS) has an incidence of 12%–15% and is associated with an increased risk of 30-day1,2 and 1-yr mortality.3 MINS is defined by the American Heart Association (AHA) as elevated troponin concentrations above the 99th percentile of the upper reference limit with an increase and decrease within 30 days after surgery, caused by ischaemia without a precipitating non-ischaemic cause.4 Postoperative days (PODs) 0, 1, and 2 account for approximately 40%, 40%, and 10% of measured MINS, respectively,5 although this estimate is limited by the lack of routine monitoring of MINS beyond POD 3. As >90% of patients with MINS are asymptomatic, routine cardiac troponin monitoring is essential for diagnosis.5 Current guidelines recommend risk stratification and postoperative troponin monitoring to detect MINS in high-risk patients.4 The Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) recommends that high-risk patients identified using the revised cardiac risk index (RCRI) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-pro-BNP) concentrations undergo daily troponin monitoring for 3 days after surgery.6 The current MINS screening strategy has multiple limitations. Well-established cardiac risk stratification models, such as the RCRI,7 perform poorly when predicting MINS. In an external validation study of 35 815 patients, the RCRI achieved a low C-statistic of 0.65 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.62–0.68) for a composite outcome that included MINS, myocardial infarction, nonfatal cardiac arrest, or cardiac death.8 Alarmingly, patients with no RCRI risk factors developed cardiac complications at a rate of 8%,8 and amongst patients with MINS, a low preoperative risk predicted by RCRI was associated with increased 1-yr mortality.9 Accurate identification of patients at high risk of MINS can facilitate targeted monitoring10, 11, 12, 13, 14 and reduce unnecessary bloodwork in patients at low risk of MINS.

There has been increasing research interest in predicting postoperative MINS.10, 11, 12, 13, 14 However, these models differ in their definitions of MINS, which makes cross-comparison challenging. Importantly, many prediction models do not update as patients’ perioperative courses evolve, including their vital signs. Perioperative blood pressure is an objective and routinely collected variable associated with MINS15,16 that may contribute to risk prediction. Postoperative mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) of <75 mm Hg lasting >635 min was associated with an increased risk of myocardial injury up to POD 3, although intraoperative hypotension was not associated with MINS.16

Our primary objective was to develop and internally validate a model that incorporates the duration of postoperative hypotension (MAP <75 mm Hg) to predict the daily maximum high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-TnT) concentrations on PODs 1, 2, and 3. We chose troponin because it is the primary method used to monitor for and diagnose MINS.4 Moreover, using troponin rather than MINS as the outcome allows the model to remain useful as MINS definitions evolve, recognising that troponin is not a substitute for MINS, as troponin encompasses both ischaemic and nonischaemic causes. Our secondary objective was to develop and internally validate models to predict MINS on PODs 1, 2, and 3.

Methods

This was a single-centre retrospective cohort study at a tertiary academic hospital (St. Paul’s, Canada). We obtained institutional ethics approval (H20-03995, 10 May 2021) and pre-registered the protocol (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05188001, 7 January 2022). The study was conducted and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement17 and the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guideline.18

Study population

We included patients aged 45 yr and older who underwent inpatient noncardiac surgery between 1 January 2020 and 30 June 2021 and received our institutional MINS protocol for postoperative troponin monitoring, as per CCS guidelines.6 This protocol included daily troponin concentrations on PODs 0–3, an ECG in the post-anaesthesia care unit, and contacting the perioperative care team when troponin or ECG results were available, or the troponin concentration was more than 5 ng L−1 above a baseline. As all patients undergoing surgery have their vital signs automatically captured in the electronic health record (EHR), we excluded patients without any vital signs to avoid including patients whose surgeries were cancelled. Patients without postoperative troponin were excluded, as MINS can be asymptomatic and requires routine troponin monitoring. We also excluded patients who underwent repeat surgery <72 h after surgery, as this would complicate the operationalisation of intraoperative and postoperative hypotension predictors. We also excluded patients who were readmitted <72 h postoperatively. If patients had multiple eligible admissions, only the first inpatient admission was included.

Data extraction

Data were extracted from hospital electronic medical records (Cerner SurgiNet Anaesthesia and Powerchart), with pre-linked data from the Canadian Institute for Health Information Discharge Abstract Database. The dataset included vital signs from 24 h before surgery to POD 3 and laboratory results from 1 month before surgery to POD 3. Manual chart review was performed for each patient to extract clinical data not automatically retrieved.

Outcomes

In our primary model, the outcome was the maximum daily hs-TnT on PODs 1, 2, and 3, as reported by the hospital laboratory (Elecsys® Troponin T Gen 5 STAT).19 We selected troponin concentration as the primary outcome so that the model stays relevant even if the troponin thresholds used to define MINS evolve with new studies. We chose the absolute value of troponin as the outcome, rather than the relative change, owing to the variability in troponin change thresholds across guidelines. By modelling the troponin directly, the clinician can transparently interpret troponin results in the clinical context, including pre-existing elevation. This may allow for an early blood draw (rather than waiting for the next daily troponin) for earlier detection of MINS or reduce unnecessary bloodwork if the troponin is predicted to be low. If a patient has multiple troponin measurements on a given POD, to capture the more clinically important peak. Owing to the limitations of retrospective data and daily troponin monitoring, we used the day rather than the hour as a unit of time to avoid overestimating precision.

In our secondary model, we defined MINS using troponin and clinical criteria as per the AHA definitions.4 Briefly, MINS was defined with postoperative troponin of 20 to <65 ng L−1 with an absolute increase of ≥5 ng L−1 amongst any postoperative troponins or ≥65 ng L−1, ‘attributable to a presumed ischaemic mechanism (i.e. supply-demand mismatch or atherothrombosis) in the absence of an overt precipitating nonischaemic cause (e.g. pulmonary embolism).’ The timestamp for the MINS diagnosis was set at the time the elevated troponin sample was drawn. Nonischaemic causes that may be temporally associated (around the time before troponin elevation), including venous thromboembolism, sepsis, rapid atrial fibrillation, and cardioversion within 24 h as per the VISION trial,20 were individually chart reviewed. Rapid atrial fibrillation was established as the presence of atrial fibrillation on ECG with a ventricular rate of >100 beats min−1 with a need for intervention (chemical/electrical cardioversion). Sepsis was defined as the presence of infection and a systemic inflammatory response, per the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock.21 In patients with abnormal baseline troponin values (>14 ng L−1) who were considered chronic and stable, postoperative myocardial injury was deemed to be acute if there was a postoperative ≥20% increase in high-sensitivity troponin or an absolute increase in high-sensitivity troponin of ≥14 ng L−1 above preoperative values. If a patient was diagnosed as having a preoperative acute myocardial infarction, there must be a decrease in preoperative troponin to be considered chronic elevation for this rule to apply.

Predictors

We reviewed preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors studied in the literature associated with MINS, major cardiac complications, and mortality or both. Preoperative factors include age,22 congestive heart failure,7 coronary artery disease,7 cerebrovascular disease,7 peripheral vascular disease,23 diabetes mellitus,7,23 the RCRI,7 hypertension,23 kidney function,23 NT-proBNP or BNP,24 anaemia,25 heart rate,26, 27, 28 and the ASA Physical Status.22 Intraoperative factors include type and risk of surgery,7 emergency surgery,22,23 red blood cell transfusion,29 hypotension,30,31 and heart rate of >100 beats min−1.22 Postoperative variables include hypotension,15,16,31 acute kidney injury,32 and vasopressor use 24 h after surgery.23 To facilitate model stability and reduce the risk of overfitting, we performed a priori estimation of the number of parameters allowed in the model, based on an estimated cohort size of approximately 800 patients. Although the typical heuristic for linear regression is 10–20 observations per parameter,33 for the linear mixed-effects modelling, we chose a more conservative threshold of 50 patients per parameter to account for the complexity of the mixed-effects modelling and longitudinal structure (i.e. 15 parameters). For the generalised linear mixed-effects model, we chose a conservative estimate of MINS incidence of 10% (i.e. 80 outcome events amongst 800 patients) and followed the heuristic of 10 outcome events per parameter (i.e. eight parameters).34 Thus, we chose the following a priori candidate predictors selected from the literature based on a higher likelihood of being readily available in most EHRs for generalisability and being the more comprehensive concept: percentage duration of an MAP of <75 mm Hg16 on PODs 0–2, age, sex,35,36 emergency surgery,32 RCRI,7 and postoperative minimum glomerular filtration rate (GFR).32

Although multiple hypotension thresholds are associated with an increased risk of MINS, we selected an MAP <75 mm Hg threshold because its association with MINS was supported by the most recent publication on hypotension and MINS at the time of study registration, and it represented a more conservative threshold in our higher-risk population. Acknowledging that there are other risk thresholds, multiple hypotension thresholds in the literature were thoroughly examined in sensitivity analysis for all models. Postoperative MAP was modelled as the daily percentage of time spent at <75 mm Hg. In the secondary analysis, intraoperative MAP was defined as the percentage of time spent at <75 mm Hg in the operating room. For models involving the daily percentage of time MAP was below the threshold, the denominator was 24 h; if a patient was discharged before the end of the day on PODs 1–3, the denominator was the total time until the last vital or troponin measurement, whichever came first.

Statistical analysis

Missing data

Complete-case analysis was performed for vital-sign features, with each complete case defined as a patient with ≥1 vital-sign measurement and ≥1 troponin measurement. No imputation was performed for vital-sign data. For modelling, if predictors were missing by <1%, we performed a complete-case analysis; between 1% and <10%, we used multiple imputation; and >10%, we excluded the predictor from the model.

Data processing

Blood pressure artefacts were removed in accordance with the Multicentre Perioperative Outcomes Group protocol.37 If noninvasive blood pressure and invasive blood pressure are available during the same minute, then the measurement, (1) not marked as an artefact and (2) with a higher systolic blood pressure, was used. The percentage of duration with MAP below the threshold was calculated by moving forward from the last measurement to the next available measurement over the time period specified in the models (Supplementary Appendix 1). To only have data from the past to predict future events, the vital-sign time series was truncated for the primary model at the last troponin measurement and for the secondary model at the first troponin that met clinical criteria for MINS diagnosis, at the end of POD 3, at hospital discharge, or at death, whichever occurred first.

Primary model

A multivariable linear mixed-effects model to predict the daily maximum troponin concentrations on PODs 1, 2, and 3 was built using a priori predictors (specified earlier) and with random intercepts to account for the dependence amongst within-patient daily observations (model I, formulae in Supplementary Appendix 1). The daily maximum troponin was defined as the highest troponin value on the POD, as some patients may have multiple troponin measurements drawn in a day to assess trend. The linear mixed-effects model was chosen because it accounts for relationships amongst all troponins and within-patient correlation, accommodates patients with incomplete troponin measurements on some days, and provides day-specific predictions by including POD as a fixed effect. A numeric time variable for MAP measurement time was included as an additional predictor within the model. As the effect of a vital-sign covariate may vary by monitoring day, we examined interactions between the day of measurement and MAP.

As this model is a proof of concept to mirror potential real-time clinical use, it must be built in a structure where the time-varying MAP data occur before the outcomes. To ensure that only past data are used to predict future events, this model used MAP data from the previous day to predict the next day: i.e. it used the duration of hypotension from 0 to 24 h after surgery to predict MINS from 24 to 48 h after surgery (POD 1), the duration of hypotension on POD 1 to predict 48–72 h after surgery (POD 2), and the duration of hypotension on POD 2 to predict 72–96 h after surgery (POD 3). This model structure allows for the prediction of troponins on a specific POD 1, 2, or 3 by plugging in the values for time-invariant covariate values and the corresponding MAP preceding the POD and the POD.

Model evaluation

We reviewed residual and quantile-quantile plots to assess the assumptions of homoscedasticity and normality, respectively. Internal validation was performed using repeated patient-based five-fold cross-validation with 100 repetitions.38 A patient-based approach was chosen to prevent data leakage (i.e. a patient’s measurements could potentially be present in both the training and validation sets), and record-wise cross-validation may overestimate prediction accuracy compared with subject-wise cross-validation. Model performance was evaluated using the root mean square error (RMSE) and the R-squared (R2).39 The RMSE represents the average discrepancy between the model’s predicted troponin and the actual troponin, and the lower the value, the better the model fit (i.e. fewer errors). The R2 indicates how much variation the model can account for in the data, with a value of 1 indicating a perfect model. The marginal R2 accounts for the proportion of variation in the data explained by fixed effects (as specified by covariates), whereas the conditional R2 reflects the proportion of variation explained by fixed and random effects.

Secondary model (myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery)

We developed and internally validated generalised linear mixed-effects models to predict MINS, using the same a priori predictors as mentioned earlier (model II). This model was also lagged by 1 day, using data from the previous day to predict the following day. The model was validated using a patient-based approach, including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) (with a 1000-replicate bootstrap for 95% CI), Akaike information criterion (AIC), conditional and marginal R2, scaled Brier score, and calibration. The AUROC is a measure of discrimination that reflects how well the model correctly assigns higher risk to patients with an event, with 1 indicating a perfect model and 0.5 indicating a model that is equivalent to a coin toss. The AIC is used to compare across models with the same number of patients and to assess how well the model explains the data while maintaining parsimony. The Brier score measures both discrimination and calibration and is calculated as the squared distance between the observed outcome and the prediction, scaled to 0%–100%, with 100% corresponding to the perfect model.

Sensitivity analysis

We explored multiple sensitivity analyses for both the primary and secondary models. First, to test the robustness of using daily troponin in the primary modelling, we used all troponin values from POD 0 to the end of POD 3 as outcomes in a longitudinal analysis framework (model Ia). Here, the durations of hypotension were defined as the percentage of time spent with an MAP of <75 from the end of surgery to each troponin timepoint. We used the POD as the time unit (i.e. daily maximum troponin as the outcome) in the primary model because of variable blood pressure measurement frequencies in the retrospective dataset (i.e. the precision of infrequent blood pressure measurements on the ward may not be sufficient for hourly prediction of troponin). Second, we explored whether intraoperative hypotension enhances prediction by adding the percentage of time with an intraoperative MAP of <75 mm Hg to predict MINS from 0 to 24 h after surgery (day 0) to either model I or model Ia (models Ib.1 and Ib.2, respectively). We evaluated whether this improved model fit as measured by the AIC and the likelihood ratio test (LRT). We did not include intraoperative MAP as a predictor in the primary model owing to conflicting evidence in the literature.15,40,41 Third, to test the robustness of the 75 mm Hg MAP threshold, we examined whether changing the MAP threshold to 65, 70, or 80 mm Hg affected the performance of each of the above-mentioned models. Finally, for model I, because troponin was not normally distributed, we explored generalised estimating equations, which do not rely on distributional assumptions (model Ic). For model II, we performed a similar sensitivity analysis with intraoperative hypotension (model IIa) and varying MAP thresholds.

Results

Cohort characteristics

The cohort included 846 patients (Fig. 1, Table 1, Supplementary Appendix 1). NT-pro-BNP was measured in 652 patients (77.1%) before surgery, with a median (interquartile range [IQR]) of 546 pg ml−1 [281.8–1595.8] (194 missing). The inpatient 30-day postoperative mortality rate was 1.4% (n=12). The median [IQR] of the frequency of blood pressure measurements was 25 observations [12.7–60] per hour per patient intraoperatively and 30 [18–38], 8 [5–9], 6 [4–7], and 5.5 observations [3–6] per admitted patient per day on PODs 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Postoperative hypotension (MAP <75 mm Hg) on PODs 0, 1, 2, and 3 for any duration was observed in 73.3% (n=620), 36.2% (n=306), 22.8% (n=193), and 16.4% of patients (n=139) of the total cohort, respectively (further MAP statistics in Supplementary Appendix 1). When using patients with measured blood pressure as the denominator, the incidence of postoperative hypotension (MAP <75 mm Hg) on PODs 0, 1, 2, and 3 for any duration was 73.3% (620 of 846), 40.4% (306 of 758), 29.1% (193 of 662), and 24.5% (139 of 568), respectively. After truncating MAP at the last troponin measurement, the percentages for MAP <75 mm Hg for PODs 0, 1, 2, and 3 were 72.7% (615 of 846), 40.6% (276 of 680), 28.3% (160 of 565), and 22.0% (35 of 159), respectively. The varying number of observations over time did not impact model stability, as the longitudinal structure accommodates varying numbers of observations per subject. However, the models’ prediction accuracy and generalisability are likely higher in the earlier PODs, when there were more observations (Table 2).

Modelling and model evaluation

Missing data and processing

GFR was removed as a predictor owing to 15.3% of values (129 of 846) being missing. A natural log transformation of troponin values was required, as troponin was not normally distributed. RCRI levels 4–6 were grouped together owing to low frequency.

Primary model

Model results are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Appendix 2, and sensitivity analyses in Table 3 and Supplementary Appendix 3. Model I demonstrated a marginal R2 of 0.20 and a conditional R2 of 0.94, indicating that most of the variation in troponin values was driven by differences amongst patients captured by random effects rather than the covariates (fixed effects). This suggests that the model primarily fit individual-specific trajectories rather than providing strong prediction based on prespecified characteristics alone. Although this yields a good statistical fit at the subject level, such fit does not necessarily translate into clinical usefulness because random effects are not observable for new patients at the time clinical decisions are made. Therefore, predictions that rely heavily on random effects may have limited value for prospective risk stratification, which depends on fixed effects known before or during surgery. The RMSE was 0.18 (0.91 when cross-validated), which can be interpreted as a large prediction error relative to the standard deviation of log troponin (1.00), and may be related to the highly skewed distribution of troponin. The model’s prediction error, as measured by RMSE, varied with both troponin range and POD (Table 2). Prediction error increased with higher troponin values: for troponin of <20 ng L−1, the RMSE was 2.4 ng L−1; for troponin between 20 and 65 ng L−1, the RMSE was 7 ng L−1; and for troponin between 65 and 100 ng L−1, it was 17 ng L−1. Across POD, prediction error was lowest on POD 2 for all troponin ranges (1.6 ng L−1, 4.0 ng L−1, and 15.3 ng L−1 for <20 ng L−1, 20 and 65 ng L−1, and 65–100 ng L−1, respectively).

The parameter estimates for the duration of hypotension and its interaction with POD are consistently non-significant (P>0.05), suggesting that duration of hypotension is not a strong individual predictor of MINS. However, the parameter POD was significant across all models (P<0.01), indicating that the risk of MINS decreases over time, regardless of the duration spent with MAP below the threshold, which aligns with clinical knowledge (Supplementary Appendix 2). Using RCRI as the sole predictor for MINS (categorising RCRI 4 or higher as a single category) showed worse performance than models II and IIa, with similar results when treating RCRI as a categorical variable without grouping. The inclusion of intraoperative hypotension as a predictor (model IIa) did not meaningfully improve the AUROC, as the corresponding bootstrap 95% CIs overlap. These observations were similar across varying MAP thresholds.

Discussion

To enhance postoperative MINS surveillance, we developed and internally validated proof-of-concept multivariable linear mixed-effects models that included postoperative hypotension to predict daily troponin concentrations from PODs 1–3. The primary model had a good fit, as indicated by R2, indicating that it explained a substantial proportion of the variation in troponin values across patients. However, the high conditional R2 was largely driven by random effects from patient trajectories rather than fixed effects from the predictors and has limited prospective predictive value. Contributors to the low marginal R2 (fixed effects) may include the relatively infrequent measurements of blood pressure42 and troponin in clinical practice, which could have missed episodes of hypotension or troponin changes, and the need for additional clinical predictors. Therefore, more frequent prospective measurement of blood pressure and troponin measurements in a larger sample size may improve model precision and accuracy. Intraoperative hypotension did not meaningfully improve prediction accuracy. However, models involving intraoperative hypotension may still have a role in outcome prediction on POD 0, which accounted for 69% of initial MINS diagnosis. The secondary models predicting MINS using predictors including hypotension improved upon the prediction based on RCRI alone, but the AUROC and scaled Brier score remain weak and are not sufficiently accurate for clinical use. The observations were consistent across different hypotension definitions. Overall, we found that although MINS has been reported to be associated with postoperative hypotension in the literature, models involving hypotension did not provide accurate prediction for troponin and MINS.

Risk prediction models in routine clinical use lack accuracy in predicting MINS.7,9 Our research adds to the emerging literature on MINS prediction. Mases and colleagues11 developed a multivariable logistic regression model to predict MINS from PODs 0–3 in a prospective sample of 732 patients (aged ≥ 45 yr) undergoing intermediate- to high-risk surgery. Predictors were age, sex, GFR, functional capacity, NT-pro-BNP, and estimated intraoperative blood loss, with an AUROC of 0.77 (95% CI 0.73–0.81). However, they did not exclude nonischaemic events.11 Oh and colleagues12 developed extreme gradient boosting models to predict MINS within 30 days after surgery using a retrospective database of 6811 patients undergoing noncardiac surgeries. The models with 6, 12, and 52 variables performed similarly (AUROC 0.78; 95% CI 0.77–0.79), with the six-predictor model involving preoperative troponin, intraoperative inotropic drug infusion, emergency operation, operation duration, operation type, and age.12 Nolde and colleagues10 developed neural networks to predict MINS 30 days after surgery using the VISION dataset of 24 589 patients, with the best-performing multilayer model incorporating variables before and after surgery achieving an AUROC of 0.87 (0.85–0.90). Finally, Lin and colleagues13 used least absolute shrinkage and selection operator and multivariable logistic regression to predict MINS (no specified time course) in a prospective sample of 298 patients 45 yr or older undergoing thoracic surgery, with an AUROC of 0.940 (95% CI 0.913–0.967) for the model that incorporated intraoperative variables.

Our analysis strengthens the existing evidence in several ways. First, we used only data available before the outcome to predict, mirroring the clinical prediction workflow. Second, we incorporated perioperative blood pressure using various thresholds and routinely collected variables, representing a pragmatic approach. Third, we selected troponin as the outcome, which would remain relevant as the definitions for MINS evolve with new guidelines and studies. Finally, a manual review excluded nonischaemic troponin elevations. Our practical approach has advantages, as interpreting and comparing published models can be challenging owing to heterogeneous study populations, varying troponin measurement frequencies, and differing definitions of MINS. If clinicians would like to see the predicted change in troponin, it can be calculated manually or automatically in the EHR by subtracting the last measured troponin from the predicted value. The interpretation of change must be performed in the context of the known predictive precision (Table 2), baseline troponin, and MINS definitions. Moreover, the implementation of existing models may be restricted by the availability and quality of data used as predictors, such as the known subjectivity in estimating blood loss43 and functional capacity44 and the absence of preoperative NT-pro-BNP.

Anaesthesiologists can play a leadership role in extending monitoring and decision-making expertise beyond the operating room to advance perioperative research, particularly in complications monitoring. As EHR data become increasingly integrated with predictive modelling, enabling real-time risk prediction for troponin elevation or MINS can improve targeted surveillance and clinical decision-making. Although our analysis incorporated hypotension as a proof of concept given data availability, accuracy, and biological plausibility, we found that the current model was insufficient for clinical prediction. Other routinely collected variables with known good data quality can be explored. Using accurate risk prediction, troponin can be ordered when it is predicted to exceed a clinical threshold (e.g. per the AHA MINS definitions). Compared with the current strategy of daily troponin testing of select patients, a more individualised approach could be available to all patients based on real-time condition, which may help reduce both gaps in troponin monitoring and avoid unnecessary blood draw.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. We combined automated EHR data extraction with manual chart review of clinical variables to ensure high data quality. The predictors were routinely collected, objective variables that can be extracted from the EHR. The model was constructed using a priori predictors and did not use variable selection, which can worsen model stability. Our analysis also has several limitations. This was a single-centre study; further external validation is required to evaluate generalizability. The retrospective nature of the data may result in misclassification and bias, and may not be generalisable. There was a reliance on protocol-driven troponin testing. NT-pro-BNP was not included as a predictor because it was not routinely available for patients undergoing emergency surgery or for patients not seen in preoperative clinics at our institution. There may have been selection bias from excluding patients without postoperative troponin, although the impact was likely small, as only 13 of 1087 hospitalisations meeting the inclusion criteria were removed by this criterion. Another potential source of selection bias was the exclusion of patients (n=13) who underwent repeat surgery within 72 h. Although our sample size was adequate, there was a risk of overfitting, which we mitigated using a priori predictors and bootstrapping. The accuracy of the prediction model was limited by measurement bias owing to the frequency of perioperative blood pressure and troponin measurements, which could lead to unmeasured hypotension.45 The duration and frequency of measurement may relate to the patient’s clinical condition, with the further day from operation the greater the data gap. Time after surgery alone may provide predictive value for whether hypotension was present.

Although models that included MINS as an outcome excluded nonischaemic causes of troponin elevation, models that included troponin did not. The incidence of known nonischaemic causes was low at 5.4% (46 of 846). Whereas hypotension may be a part of the causal pathway for ischaemic causes of MINS, it is a sign rather than a direct cause in nonischaemic causes of troponin elevation. The choice of predictors was guided by evidence of association in the literature as an estimate of potential predictive value and practical considerations such as data availability and ease of measurement across institutions to enable external use; additional clinical factors can likely be added to improve risk prediction. It remains unclear whether hypotension has any causal role on MINS or is a sign of postoperative physiologic response in the context of comorbidities and other complications, which may impact its utility in temporal predictions. Moreover, the varying interventions in response to hypotension may modify MINS risks and troponin increase at varying amounts across the cohort, which in turn reduces the performance of the risk prediction. As this is not an aetiologic study, we cannot conclude on the role of hypotension in troponin or MINS, only on the predictive ability of the model involving hypotension in troponin or MINS. Next, the removal of GFR values owing to missing data may have affected model performance; we did not impute them owing to the high percentage of missing data and the likely non-randomness of the omissions (i.e. a clinician deemed that the patient did not warrant a postoperative creatinine measurement). Furthermore, the reported performance metrics should not be applied beyond this cohort. Our models are proof of concept, are limited by what is captured in EHRs, have not yet been externally validated, and should not be used for decision-making at this stage. Future research includes external validation, more frequent prospective collection of vital signs and troponin values, and an examination of the clinical utility of predictions (e.g. how much earlier prediction models can identify MINS compared with traditional diagnosis and their impact on patient-centred outcomes). To further improve the model, conceptualising the mechanism linking hypotension to outcomes can help select additional predictors that account for how clinical interventions modify the risk of MINS and troponin.

Conclusions

In this proof-of-concept study, we developed and internally validated models that incorporated postoperative hypotension as a predictor of troponin concentrations and MINS. The primary model to predict troponin fit the data well as indicated by R2 values, but lacked sufficient accuracy and is not yet ready for clinical practice. MINS prediction models performed poorly but improved upon the RCRI. Better-quality data and additional clinical variables may enhance model development. The principal contribution of our work lies in methodological insights rather than an immediate clinical application.
