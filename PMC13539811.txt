Integrated in vitro regeneration and ex vitro establishment of Syngonium podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’: optimization of growth regulators and acclimatization substrates
Authors: Masoumeh Paknia, Maryam Dehestani-Ardakani, Heidar Meftahizade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Syngonium podophyllum Schott. ‘Pink Allusion’ is an economically important ornamental foliage plant valued for its attractive leaves and adaptability to indoor environments. However, conventional vegetative propagation is limited by low multiplication rates and the potential transmission of pathogens. Therefore, this study aimed to establish an efficient protocol for in vitro regeneration, rooting, and ex vitro acclimatization of S. podophyllum through three sequential experiments. In the first experiment, nodal explants were cultured on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium supplemented with different combinations of thidiazuron (TDZ), 6-benzylaminopurine (BA), α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), and gibberellic acid (GA₃) to optimize direct shoot regeneration and vegetative growth. The highest regeneration rate (4.30 shoots per explant) was obtained on MS medium containing 2.0 mg L⁻¹ TDZ and 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA, while superior shoot elongation, petiole growth, and leaf expansion were achieved with 1.0 mg L⁻¹ TDZ and 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA. In the second experiment, regenerated shoots were rooted on media containing different concentrations and combinations of indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) and NAA. The highest root number (5.75 roots per plantlet) was recorded with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA, whereas the combination of 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA and 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA produced the longest roots (7.37 cm). Moreover, 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA significantly enhanced plantlet growth and biomass accumulation. In the third experiment, rooted plantlets were acclimatized in greenhouse conditions using cocopeat–perlite and peat moss–perlite substrates. All plantlets survived acclimatization, achieving 100% survival. Peat moss-based substrates significantly improved root development, leaf growth, biomass accumulation, and photosynthetic pigment content. Overall, the optimized protocol provides a reliable and efficient system for large-scale propagation and future biotechnological applications of S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’.

Introduction

Syngonium podophyllum Schott, commonly known as the arrowhead plant, is a tropical ornamental foliage species belonging to the family Araceae. It is highly valued for its attractive foliage, adaptability to low-light conditions, and marked leaf heteroblasty, with juvenile plants bearing simple arrow-shaped leaves that become deeply lobed during maturation [1]. Native to Central and South America, particularly from Mexico to Brazil, S. podophyllum naturally occurs in humid environments such as riverbanks, mangrove forests, and coastal regions [2]. Initially growing as a self-supporting herb, it later develops into a hemi-epiphytic climbing vine with adhesive aerial roots. In addition to its ornamental value, S. podophyllum has been traditionally used for wound healing and the treatment of skin disorders [3, 4]. Previous studies have also reported antioxidant, antibacterial, antifungal, anti-inflammatory, and cytotoxic activities in Syngonium species [5]. Furthermore, its ability to remove indoor air pollutants, including benzene, toluene, xylene, and formaldehyde, has made it a popular indoor ornamental plant [6].

Syngonium is commonly propagated by nodal or shoot tip cuttings and air layering, with eye cuttings (a leaf attached to a single stem node) representing one of the simplest methods [7]. However, vegetative propagation may facilitate the transmission of diseases, including Myrothecium leaf spot and petiole rot [8], as well as Rhizoctonia aerial blight, leaf spot, and Xanthomonas blight [9]. These limitations emphasize the need for alternative propagation methods capable of producing disease-free planting material. In this regard, in vitro propagation has become an effective approach for the rapid multiplication of ornamental plants under sterile and controlled conditions [10]. Moreover, improving propagation efficiency and reducing production costs could enhance the commercial feasibility of large-scale micropropagation and promote the wider adoption of tissue culture in the ornamental plant industry [11].

Several in vitro regeneration approaches, including callus induction, organogenesis, and somatic embryogenesis, have been successfully applied to Syngonium species [12–17]. Among these, organogenesis is a major morphogenic pathway in plant tissue culture, involving the direct formation of shoots or roots from cultured explants [18, 19]. Axillary bud proliferation, one of the most widely used organogenic approaches, relies on the stimulation of dormant axillary meristems by plant growth regulators (PGRs), and its efficiency depends on both the endogenous hormonal balance of the explant and the type and concentration of exogenous PGRs [15, 17, 20, 21].

In S. podophyllum, shoot formation can occur on hormone-free MS medium [22], although the multiplication rate is relatively low. Supplementation with appropriate combinations of auxins, particularly α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), and cytokinins such as 6-benzylaminopurine (BAP) or kinetin (Kin) significantly enhances shoot induction and proliferation [15, 17, 23–25]. Cytokinin type is a key determinant of regeneration efficiency. Although BAP has been reported to induce adventitious shoots more rapidly than thidiazuron (TDZ) [17], both cytokinins have been successfully used for the in vitro regeneration of S. podophyllum [14, 26]. For example, Cui et al. [14] demonstrated that MS medium containing 0.2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ and 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA effectively induced protocorm-like bodies and adventitious shoots, whereas direct organogenesis through axillary bud proliferation has also been achieved from shoot tip and nodal explants cultured on modified MS medium supplemented with 1 mg L⁻¹ BAP and 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NaH₂PO₄ [26].

Although in vitro propagation enables rapid plant multiplication under controlled conditions, the successful transfer of plantlets to ex vitro environments remains a critical step because in vitro-derived plantlets often exhibit anatomical and physiological limitations that reduce their ability to adapt to external conditions [27]. Acclimatization success is strongly influenced by environmental conditions, particularly the composition of the potting substrate, which affects aeration, water retention, and root development [28–31]. Commonly used substrates include vermiculite, perlite, peat moss, sand, and soil, either alone or in different combinations [28–30].

Previous studies have shown that substrate composition markedly influences the acclimatization of Syngonium species [31]. Sand alone achieved 100% survival in Syngonium elegantulus [32], whereas in S. podophyllum it performed poorly because of its low water-holding capacity, with the highest survival (70%) obtained using a 1:1 coco peat–sand mixture [33]. Similarly, peat moss has been reported to promote greater plant height, leaf production, and root growth than peat–sand mixtures [34]. Peat-based mixed substrates have generally resulted in higher survival and improved plant growth [35]. For example, peat moss, perlite, sand (2:1:1) and peat moss, perlite, vermiculite (1:1:1) achieved 100% survival during acclimatization [14, 35, 36], whereas a sand, soil, perlite mixture (1:1:1) resulted in a lower survival rate (60.5%) despite improving shoot biomass production [27]. These findings indicate that both substrate composition and the proportion of its components are critical determinants of successful ex vitro establishment.

Although several studies have reported the in vitro propagation of Syngonium species, no comprehensive study has simultaneously optimized direct organogenesis, auxin-mediated rooting, and substrate-based acclimatization for the ornamental cultivar ‘Pink Allusion’. Therefore, the present study aimed to establish an integrated and reproducible micropropagation protocol for S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’ by optimizing plant growth regulator combinations for direct shoot organogenesis and rooting from nodal explants, and by evaluating different acclimatization substrates to identify the most suitable potting medium for successful ex vitro establishment and efficient large-scale propagation.

Materials and methods

Preparation of plant materials, culture media, and physical conditions

In the present study, S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’ served as the plant material. Mother plants were obtained from a commercial greenhouse in Isfahan, Iran, during the winter of 2024. Stem nodal segments (approximately 5 mm in length) were collected from six-month-old greenhouse-grown plants and used for optimizing the disinfection protocol.

To eliminate potential contaminants, the explants underwent a surface sterilization procedure. Initially, the nodal segments were rinsed three times with sterile distilled water. They were then immersed for 20 min in a sterilizing solution containing 1000 ppm silver nanoparticles combined with 0.05% ascorbic acid. This was followed by further sterilization using 0.05% citric acid and 0.1% mercuric chloride for 2–3 min. Finally, the explants were rinsed three additional times with 0.05% citric acid to complete the sterilization procedure [37]. During all washing and sterilization steps, the explants were kept under continuous shaking to improve the efficiency of the cleaning process.

After sterilization, explants were trimmed to a uniform length of approximately 1.0 cm and cultured on MS [22] basal medium in McCarthy jars (8 cm in height and 2 cm in diameter). The MS culture medium was solidified with agar-agar (4.0 g L⁻¹) and supplemented with sucrose (30 g L⁻¹). Prior to autoclaving, the pH of the medium was adjusted to 5.8 to ensure suitable conditions for plant growth. Each jar contained 5 mL of culture medium and a single nodal explant. The cultures were incubated in a growth chamber under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod provided by cool-white fluorescent lamps with a light intensity of 100 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. The temperature was maintained at 23 ± 2 °C during the light period and 20 ± 2 °C during the dark period. The photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) ranged between 34 and 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.the experiment was repeated twice.

Induction of adventitious shoots via organogenesis

To evaluate the effects of different plant growth regulator (PGR) combinations on adventitious shoot induction, nodal explants were cultured on MS medium supplemented with either 6-benzylaminopurine (BA) at 1, 2, and 4 mg L⁻¹ in combination with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ gibberellic acid (GA₃), or thidiazuron (TDZ) at 1 and 2 mg L⁻¹ combined with α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) at 0.25 and 0.5 mg L⁻¹. A PGR-free MS medium was included as the control treatment (Table 2). All single-node explants were grown on MS basal salts and vitamins supplemented with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose and solidified with 4.0 g L⁻¹ agar-agar. The experimental design consisted of five treatments with five replications each, resulting in a total of 25 culture jars. All nodal explants were initially cultured in McCarthy jars. After three weeks, the regenerated shoots were subcultured into glass jars (10 cm in height and 6 cm in diameter) with MS medium for further proliferation. After six weeks of culture, all jars were individually evaluated to record morphological parameters, including the number of shoots per jar, shoot length (cm), leaf length (cm), leaf width (cm), and petiole length (cm).

Rooting induction

Following successful shoot induction from nodal segments, root formation was promoted using indole-3-butyric acid (IBA) and α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) (Table 2). Adventitious shoots of S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’ measuring 3–4 cm in length were excised and transferred for root induction to MS medium containing basal salts and vitamins, supplemented with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose and 4 g L⁻¹ agar-agar, with plant growth regulator treatments applied according to Table 2. Cultures were maintained in glass jars (10 cm height × 8 cm diameter). The experiment consisted of eight treatment levels with four replicates each, resulting in a total of 32 culture jars. Root formation occurred after three weeks, after which the cultures were evaluated individually and the following parameters were recorded: number of leaves per jar, leaf length (cm), leaf width (cm), petiole length (cm), plantlet height (cm), root length (cm), number of roots and plantlet fresh weight (g).

Ex vitro acclimatization

All regenerated shoots were first subjected to the rooting experiment. After root induction, well-rooted plantlets, regardless of the rooting treatment from which they originated, were transferred to the acclimatization experiment to evaluate the effects of different substrate mixtures. Following 12 weeks of in vitro culture, the regenerated plantlets were removed from the culture vessels and carefully washed under running tap water to eliminate any remaining agar from the roots. The plantlets were then transferred to pots containing six different substrate mixtures for acclimatization, arranged with four replications per treatment, resulting in a total of 24 pots. The evaluated substrates consisted of cocopeat–perlite (CP: P) and peat moss–perlite (PM: P) mixtures at ratios of 70:30, 50:50 (1:1), and 30:70 (w/w) (Fig. 6). Plastic pots with a diameter of 7 cm were used, and all substrate mixtures were sterilized prior to use. To maintain high humidity and reduce transplant shock, each pot was covered with a transparent plastic cup during the first two weeks after transfer (Fig. 8). Small holes were gradually introduced into the covers to allow progressive ventilation, and during the third week the covers were periodically removed for several hours each day. After four weeks, the plantlets were transferred to greenhouse conditions for further acclimatization and hardening. Each potted plantlet received 10 mL of 20–20–20 NPK fertilizer solution every two weeks for a period of eight weeks. Prior to planting, all substrate mixtures were disinfected by heating in an oven at 65 °C. Data were recorded eight weeks after transplantation. The measured parameters included plant height (cm), number of leaves per plant, number of roots per plant, root length (cm), and fresh weight (g) of the plantlets. Leaf length (mm), width (mm), and leaf area (mm²) were measured using an electronic leaf area meter (WinArea-UT-11, Iran). Plant dry weight was determined after oven-drying at 65 °C for 24 h.

Determination of photosynthetic pigment content

Chlorophyll a, b, total chl, and carotenoid contents were quantified following the method of Lichtenthaler [38]. Briefly, fully expanded leaves were harvested from harden plants, and 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissue from each treatment was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold acetone using a mortar and pestle with acid-washed sand. The homogenate was filtered and subsequently centrifuged at room temperature for 5 min at 1957 × g. The absorbance of the resulting supernatant was recorded at 470, 645, and 662 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer, with acetone serving as the blank. Pigment concentrations were calculated according to the equations described by Lichtenthaler [38] and expressed as µg mL− 1.

Chlorophyll a (Chl a) = 11.24 A662 − 2.04 A645.

Chlorophyll b (Chl b) = 20.13 A645 − 4.19 A662.

Total chlorophylls (Total chl) = 7.05 A662 + 18.09 A645.

Total carotenoids = (1000 A470 − 1.90 Chl a − 63 0.14 Chl b)/214.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed based on a completely randomized design (CRD), comprising eight treatments for shoot regeneration, eight treatments for root induction, and six substrate treatments for the ex vitro acclimatization experiment. The collected data were analyzed of variance (ANOVA) with SPSS software (version 26.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Differences among treatment means (mean ± standard error) were evaluated using Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) at a significance level of p < 0.05. To further classify the treatments in both experiments, hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) accompanied by heatmap visualization was performed using the ClustVis web tool.

Results

Induction of adventitious shoots through organogenesis

The formation and subsequent growth of adventitious shoots varied noticeably depending on the type and concentration of plant growth regulators supplied in the culture medium (Fig. 1). Among the tested treatments, MS medium enriched with 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA produced the most pronounced response in S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’, generating nearly twice as many shoots as the PGR-free control (Table 2) (Fig. 1A, G). The control medium yielded the lowest number of shoots (2.20 per explant), whereas the TDZ–NAA combination resulted in the highest regeneration rate (4.30 shoots per explant).

Shoot elongation and general vegetative growth were also strongly influenced by the applied PGR combinations (Table 1). The longest shoots, measuring 4.50–4.60 cm, were produced on media containing 2 mg L⁻¹ BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ GA (Fig. 1C), 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 1F), or 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 1G). In contrast, the treatment with 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 1H) resulted in the shortest shoots, averaging only 2.60 cm (Table 2).

Petiole development also responded significantly to the PGR treatments (Table 1). The longest petioles (2.80 mm) were observed in explants cultured with 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, while the shortest (0.88 mm) occurred under 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA. Petiole length in the 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA treatment was statistically comparable to that obtained with 4 mg L⁻¹ BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ GA (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 1D), while the remaining treatments showed no significant differences (Table 2).

Leaf growth followed a similar pattern (Table 1). The longest leaves (3.70 cm) were produced on MS medium containing 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, representing a 2.3–4.3-fold increase compared with both the 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA treatment and the PGR-free control. This same treatment also produced the widest leaves (2.50 cm), whereas the narrowest leaves (0.44 cm) occurred under 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Table 2).

Clustered Heat-Map for five shoot regeneration-related traits

To visualize the overall response patterns of the evaluated plant growth regulator (PGR) treatments based on five proliferation-related traits (shoot regeneration per explant, shoot length, leaf length, leaf width, and petiole length), a clustered heat map analysis was performed (Fig. 2). The treatments were separated into two major clusters according to their overall morphological performance. The first cluster, comprising the control, 2 mg L⁻¹ BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ GA, 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA, and 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, was mainly associated with vegetative growth traits, with 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA showing the strongest positive association with leaf length, leaf width, and petiole length. The second cluster included 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, 1 mg L⁻¹ BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ GA, 4 mg L⁻¹ BA + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ GA, and 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA. Within this group, 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA was strongly associated with shoot regeneration per explant, whereas 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA showed generally lower values for most vegetative traits. These clustering patterns indicate that lower TDZ concentrations favored vegetative growth, whereas higher TDZ combined with lower NAA primarily enhanced shoot regeneration.

In vitro root induction and proliferation

Root induction and subsequent plantlet growth of regenerated S. podophyllum shoots were significantly influenced by the type and concentration of applied auxins (Fig. 3; Table 3). Among all treatments, MS medium supplemented with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA (Fig. 4C) produced the highest number of roots, with an average of 5.75 roots per plantlet, followed by 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA (5.00 roots per plantlet) (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the lowest rooting response (1.75 roots per plantlet) was observed in the combined treatment of 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Figs. 3A and 4H).

Root length was also markedly affected by PGR treatments (Table 3). The greatest root elongation (7.37 cm) was achieved in plantlets cultured on medium containing 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 4G), whereas the shortest roots (1.20 cm) were recorded in the 1.0 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 4B) treatment alone (Fig. 3B).

Leaf development varied significantly among treatments (Table 3). The highest number of leaves (7.25 leaves per plantlet) was obtained under both 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA and 1.0 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA treatments (Fig. 4C and F), while the lowest value (4.50 leaves per plantlet) was observed in media supplemented with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA (Fig. 4E) and 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA (Fig. 4G). The maximum leaf length (3.75 cm) was recorded in the 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA treatment (Fig. 4A), whereas the minimum leaf length (1.62 cm) occurred in the 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA treatment (Table 4; Fig. 4H).

The widest leaves (2.12 cm) and the longest petioles (3.00 cm) were obtained on medium containing 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA alone (Fig. 4D). In contrast, the smallest leaf width (1.00 cm) and shortest petiole length (1.00 cm) were observed in the 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA treatment (Table 2; Fig. 4E).

Plantlet height was significantly enhanced by auxin treatments (Table 3), with the maximum plantlet height (6.0 cm) recorded on medium supplemented with 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA. The shortest plantlets (3.37 cm) were observed in treatments containing 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA and 1.0 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA. Discussion

Direct shoots regeneration

Organogenesis has two stages, proliferation of unipolar shoots and root meristem induction [39]. The present study demonstrates that plant growth regulator (PGR) composition plays a decisive role in regulating adventitious shoot induction, elongation, and leaf development in Syngonium podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’. Differences among the PGR treatments indicated that TDZ combined with NAA stimulated shoot formation more effectively than the BA–GA mixtures. The strong morphogenic response to TDZ-based treatments observed in the present study is consistent with previous reports demonstrating the high efficiency of TDZ for direct organogenesis in ornamental aroids, including Philodendron spp [40], Aglaonema ‘Lady Valentine’ [41], Anthurium andraeanum [42], and S. podophyllum [14, 17]. In particular, MS medium supplemented with 2 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA produced nearly twice as many shoots as the PGR-free control, indicating a strong synergistic effect between TDZ and a low concentration of NAA. Similar synergistic responses have been reported in Epipremnum aureum and Anthurium spp [43, 44]. Furthermore, the greater effectiveness of TDZ than BA in promoting shoot regeneration agrees with previous findings in Capparis decidua [45], Mertensia maritima [46], and Gymnocladus assamicus [47]. The superior performance of TDZ is generally attributed to its strong cytokinin-like activity, which promotes cell division, meristematic reprogramming, and axillary bud outgrowth [48–50]. These physiological effects likely contributed to the enhanced shoot regeneration observed in the present study.

Shoot elongation was greatest in media containing BA + GA or lower concentrations of TDZ in combination with NAA. The promotive effect of GA is consistent with its well-established role in stimulating cell elongation through DELLA-mediated signaling pathways [51], which likely contributed to the increased shoot length observed in the present study.

Petiole and leaf development were also markedly affected by PGR composition. The longest petioles and largest leaves were obtained with 1 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA, indicating that moderate TDZ concentrations favor balanced vegetative growth, whereas higher TDZ levels suppressed shoot and leaf elongation. This agrees with previous studies showing that excessive TDZ reduces shoot elongation and produces compact plantlets [47, 52–55]. For example, Çelikel et al. [52] reported that TDZ produced compact rose plants with shoots approximately 50% shorter than the control, while Pai and Desai [53] and Novikova and Zaytseva [55] demonstrated that lower TDZ concentrations promote shoot proliferation and activation of axillary meristems more effectively than higher concentrations, with reduced callus formation [54]. These findings support our observation that optimizing TDZ concentration is critical for balancing shoot multiplication and vegetative development.

Root induction

The results of the present study indicate that both root induction and subsequent root development in regenerated shoots of S. podophyllum were significantly influenced by auxin type and concentration. Among the tested treatments, IBA was more effective than NAA in promoting adventitious root formation, with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA producing the highest number of roots, followed by 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA, indicating that moderate IBA concentrations are optimal for rooting. In contrast, 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA resulted in the poorest rooting response, suggesting that certain auxin combinations may interfere with root initiation.

The relatively poor performance of NAA-containing treatments agrees with previous reports showing that rooting efficiency is generally lower with NAA or NOA (2-Naphthoxyacetic acid) than with IBA [45]. The superior rooting response of IBA is consistent with its established role in regulating root apical meristem activity, adventitious root induction, and lateral root formation [56]. In addition, its gradual conversion to IAA provides a sustained auxin supply while minimizing phytotoxic effects, thereby improving rooting efficiency, as also reported in Philodendron erubescens ‘Pink Princess’ [57].

Root elongation was also significantly affected by auxin treatments. Although IBA alone maximized root number, the combination of 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA produced the longest roots, suggesting that IBA mainly promoted root initiation, whereas a low concentration of NAA enhanced subsequent root elongation. This complementary effect of auxin combinations has also been reported in other tissue culture studies [58]. Conversely, the shortest roots were observed with 1.0 mg L⁻¹ NAA, indicating that NAA alone was less effective in promoting root elongation in S. podophyllum. The beneficial interaction between low concentrations of IBA and NAA was further supported by the improved vegetative growth observed in this treatment, consistent with previous reports in Aglaonema [59, 60].

Auxin treatments also significantly influenced vegetative growth, including leaf number, leaf size, petiole length, plantlet height, and fresh weight. Among the evaluated treatments, 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA produced the most vigorous plantlets, indicating that this concentration promoted balanced shoot and root development. The improved growth was likely associated with enhanced root functionality, resulting in more efficient water and nutrient uptake and greater biomass accumulation [61].

The highest leaf number obtained with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ IBA and 1.0 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA suggests that moderate auxin concentrations maintained active meristematic growth. Auxin-regulated cell division, vascular differentiation, and organ formation may therefore have contributed to enhanced leaf production [62]. In contrast, reduced leaf production in several combined NAA–IBA treatments indicates that excessive or imbalanced auxin levels inhibited vegetative morphogenesis, consistent with previous findings [63].

Leaf expansion was likewise influenced by auxin composition. The greatest leaf length obtained with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA suggests that low NAA concentrations promoted cell elongation, whereas the shortest leaves under 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA indicate that higher auxin levels may have preferentially favored root development over shoot expansion [64, 65].

Similarly, the greater leaf width, petiole length, plantlet height, and fresh weight obtained with 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA further demonstrate that this treatment produced well-balanced and vigorous plantlets. Conversely, reduced growth under some NAA + IBA combinations, particularly 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA, suggests that excessive auxin interactions adversely affected plant development. Such responses have been attributed to physiological disorders including excessive callus formation, restricted cell expansion, and ethylene accumulation under supra-optimal auxin concentrations [65]. Similar observations have been reported in other micropropagation studies, where moderate auxin levels produced healthier plantlets than higher auxin concentrations [66].

Leaf expansion responses were also strongly affected by auxin composition. The greatest leaf length obtained with 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA suggests that low concentrations of NAA promoted cell elongation and leaf expansion, likely through auxin-induced cell wall loosening and proton pump activation [64]. Conversely, the shortest leaves observed under 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ NAA indicate that higher auxin levels may preferentially direct metabolic activity toward root development at the expense of shoot expansion. Similar inhibitory effects of elevated auxin concentrations on shoot elongation and leaf development have been reported previously [65].

The increases in leaf width, petiole length, plantlet height, and fresh weight observed under 1.0 mg L⁻¹ IBA further demonstrate the production of well-balanced and vigorous plantlets. These improvements are likely associated with enhanced root functionality and more efficient water and mineral uptake, while improved vascular differentiation may have facilitated assimilate transport to developing shoots and leaves [61, 64]. Conversely, the reduced growth observed under some NAA + IBA combinations, particularly 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA + 0.1 mg L⁻¹ IBA, indicates that excessive auxin interactions may impair normal plant development. Supra-optimal auxin concentrations may induce physiological disorders such as excessive callus formation, reduced cell expansion, or ethylene accumulation, ultimately suppressing vegetative growth [65]. Similar responses have been reported in several micropropagation studies, where moderate auxin concentrations produced healthier plantlets than higher auxin levels [66].

The acclimatization stage

The acclimatization stage is a critical step in micropropagation because plantlets must adjust from in vitro to ex vitro conditions [67]. In the present study, all regenerated plantlets of S. podophyllum survived acclimatization, resulting in a 100% survival rate across all substrate treatments. Similar acclimatization success has been reported for other ornamental species when appropriate environmental conditions and substrates were used [67, 68].

Among the evaluated substrates, peat moss (PM)-based mixtures, particularly 30P+70PM and 70P+30PM, consistently produced superior growth compared with cocopeat (CP)-dominant substrates. These treatments promoted greater root elongation, leaf expansion, and biomass accumulation, indicating that PM provided more favorable conditions for plant establishment. This superior performance is likely related to the high water-holding capacity, adequate aeration, and nutrient retention of peat moss, properties that have previously been associated with improved root establishment and vegetative growth in ornamental plants [30, 69, 70].

Root development is a key determinant of acclimatization success because newly transferred plantlets rely heavily on efficient water and mineral uptake for survival and subsequent growth. As growth progresses, numerous fine, fibrous roots are produced, forming a dense primary root system characteristic of the species. This expanding network rapidly penetrates the substrate due to its high root tip plasticity and fine diameter, increasing total absorptive surface area. The resulting architecture supports efficient acquisition of water and mineral nutrients, facilitating stable establishment and sustained vegetative growth. In the present study, the 70P+30CP substrate produced the highest root number, whereas 70P+30PM resulted in the greatest root length. These results indicate that root elongation, rather than root number alone, is a better indicator of successful plantlet establishment. The enhanced root growth observed in PM-rich substrates likely improved the absorptive capacity of the root system, thereby facilitating more efficient water and nutrient uptake. A similar positive effect of well-aerated substrates on root elongation and post-acclimatization growth has been reported in micropropagated pear rootstocks [71].

Leaf morphological traits were also significantly influenced by substrate composition. Peat moss-rich substrates, particularly 30P+70PM, promoted greater leaf length, width, and leaf area than the other treatments. The larger leaf area may have improved light interception and assimilate production, thereby contributing to greater biomass accumulation and plant vigor. Similar effects of substrate composition on leaf development during acclimatization have been reported in ornamental bromeliads [68].

In contrast, CP-dominant substrates produced smaller leaves and reduced vegetative growth. Although cocopeat is widely used as a sustainable alternative to peat moss because of its high water-holding capacity and renewable nature, excessive moisture retention may reduce root-zone aeration and limit root respiration and nutrient uptake [72]. In addition, the physicochemical properties of cocopeat-based substrates vary with the mixing ratio and do not always provide the optimal balance between moisture retention and aeration for ornamental plant growth [69].

Biomass accumulation further supported the superior performance of PM-containing substrates. The highest fresh weight was obtained with 70P+30PM, while dry weight was greatest in 50P+50CP and 70P+30PM. The greater dry biomass may reflect more efficient resource utilization and plant growth during acclimatization. Similar improvements in biomass production have been reported for tissue-cultured ornamental species grown in peat-enriched substrates under greenhouse conditions [70].

Although leaf number was not significantly influenced by substrate composition, plantlets grown in PM-rich substrates tended to produce slightly more leaves than those in CP-dominant media, indicating that substrate composition had a greater effect on leaf expansion and biomass accumulation than on leaf initiation.

Photosnthetic pigment content

Substrate composition significantly influenced the photosynthetic pigment contents of acclimatized S. podophyllum plantlets. Significant differences were observed in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid contents among the evaluated substrates, indicating that the physiological performance of regenerated plantlets varied according to the acclimatization medium.

Chlorophyll a (Chl a) content was highest in the 50P+50CP substrate, suggesting that this substrate composition provided favorable conditions for chlorophyll accumulation. Moderate Chl a levels were also observed in 30P+70CP and 70P+30CP, whereas the lowest concentration occurred in 70P+30PM. These differences indicate that substrate composition influenced chlorophyll accumulation during acclimatization, consistent with previous studies showing that chlorophyll content is affected by environmental conditions and plant acclimation processes [73, 74].

Unlike Chl a, Chl b content was highest in the 70P+30PM substrate, indicating that substrate composition significantly influenced chlorophyll accumulation during acclimatization. As an accessory photosynthetic pigment, Chl b contributes to light capture and energy transfer to Chl a [75]. Therefore, the higher Chl b content observed in PM-rich substrates may indicate improved physiological status of the acclimatized plantlets. Similar variations in Chl b accumulation have been associated with differences in plant acclimatization and environmental conditions in previous studies [67, 76].

Total chlorophyll content followed a similar trend, with the highest value recorded in the 70P+30PM treatment. This finding indicates that peat moss-rich substrates favored chlorophyll accumulation during acclimatization. The higher total chlorophyll content may be associated with the favorable physical properties of peat moss, including adequate aeration, moisture retention, and nutrient availability, which support plant growth and physiological performance. Similar positive effects of peat-containing substrates on chlorophyll accumulation and plant vigor have been reported in acclimatized ornamental foliage plants [69].

Carotenoid content exhibited a different response pattern from that of chlorophyll pigments. The highest carotenoid concentration was recorded in the 50P+50PM treatment, whereas the lowest value occurred in 50P+50CP. As important accessory pigments, carotenoids contribute to antioxidant protection and the maintenance of normal physiological functions in plants [77]. Therefore, the greater carotenoid accumulation observed in PM-containing substrates may reflect improved physiological status of acclimatized plantlets. Similar increases in carotenoid content have been reported in tissue-cultured ornamental plants during acclimatization [68].

Generally, substrate composition markedly influenced pigment accumulation during acclimatization. PM-rich substrates, particularly 70P+30PM and 50P+50PM, promoted higher total chlorophyll and carotenoid contents, indicating better physiological performance than CP-dominant substrates. These findings further support the suitability of peat moss-based substrates for the ex vitro establishment of S. podophyllum plantlets.

Conclusion

The present study successfully established an efficient and reproducible micropropagation protocol for S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’ through the optimization of plant growth regulators and acclimatization substrates. Among the evaluated PGR treatments, 2.0 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.25 mg L⁻¹ NAA produced the highest shoot regeneration efficiency, whereas 1.0 mg L⁻¹ TDZ + 0.5 mg L⁻¹ NAA promoted superior vegetative growth and overall plantlet quality. For root induction, IBA at 0.5–1.0 mg L⁻¹ resulted in the most effective rooting response and improved plantlet vigor. During ex vitro acclimatization, peat moss-based substrates, particularly 70P+30PM and 30P+70PM, significantly enhanced root development, leaf growth, biomass accumulation, and photosynthetic pigment content, while all plantlets achieved a 100% survival rate. In general, the integration of optimized shoot induction, rooting, and acclimatization conditions provides a reliable protocol for the efficient propagation and successful ex vitro establishment of S. podophyllum ‘Pink Allusion’.
