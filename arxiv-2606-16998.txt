Progress toward a better BOCS: Systematic coarse-graining with local density potentials
Maria C. Lesniewski, Michael R. DeLyser, W. G. Noid

Abstract
We describe version 5.0 of the Bottom-up Open-source Coarse-graining Software (BOCS) package. BOCS employs the force-matching variational principle to parameterize potentials for coarse-grained (CG) models directly from atomically detailed simulations. BOCS version 5.0 significantly extends previous versions by treating potentials that depend upon the local density (LD) around each particle, as well as potentials that depend upon the square gradient (SG) of this local density. We also describe a new package, PKG-BOCS, for simulating these potentials in LAMMPS. This software treats complex molecular topologies and provides considerable flexibility for defining the local density, as well as the LD and SG potentials. We present numerical calculations that provide physical insight into these potentials and demonstrate the accuracy of our implementation. Finally, we demonstrate that LD potentials can significantly improve the structural fidelity, thermodynamic properties, and transferability of CG models for water.

Here we focus on “bottom-up” CG models that are parameterized with information from simulations of all-atom (AA) or other high resolution models.

In practice, though, this exact coarse-graining procedure is not analytically tractable and must be computationally approximated.

2
Theory

2.1
Mapped ensemble

Bottom-up approaches parameterize CG models based upon information from a higher resolution model.
Jin et al. 2022
;
Noid 2023

We shall refer to this high resolution model as an all-atom (AA) model for simplicity, but the present framework applies more generally to classical particle-based models.
We denote the configuration of the AA model by
{\bf r}=({\bf r}_{1},\ldots,{\bf r}_{n})
, where
{\bf r}_{i}
denotes the Cartesian coordinates of atom
i
.
We assume that the system is at equilibrium, such that the AA model samples each configuration according to the canonical probability density,
p_{\rm r}({\bf r})\propto\exp[-\beta u({\bf r})]
, where
\beta=1/k_{B}T
is the inverse temperature and
u({\bf r})
is the corresponding potential.
Tuckerman. 2013

While we focus on the canonical ensemble, this framework can be generalized to other ensembles.
Das and Andersen 2010
;
Dunn and Noid 2015

We employ a mapping,
{\mathbf{M}}:{\bf r}\to{\mathbf{R}}={\mathbf{M}}({\bf r})
, to define the CG representation,
{\mathbf{R}}=({\mathbf{R}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{R}}_{N})
, of each AA configuration.
Noid et al. 2008

This mapping defines the coordinates of each site,
I
, as a convex combination of atomic coordinates, i.e.,
{\mathbf{R}}_{I}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{Ii}{\bf r}_{i}
.
Kidder and Noid 2024

In particular, we consider “non-overlapping” maps for which each atom contributes to the definition of at most one CG site.
We define a restricted partition function for the total Boltzmann weight associated with each CG configuration,
{\mathbf{R}}
:

z_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(V)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!{\rm d}{\bf r}\>\exp[-\beta u({\bf r})]\delta\left({\mathbf{M}}({\bf r})-{\mathbf{R}}\right),

(1)

where
{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(V)
denotes the set of AA configurations that are accessible when the system has a volume,
V
.
Kidder and Noid 2024

This restricted partition function determines the mapped probability density,
p_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})\propto z_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})
, as well as the exact CG potential:

W({\mathbf{R}})\equiv-k_{B}T\ln\left[z_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})/V_{0}^{n-N}\right],

(2)

where
V_{0}
is a constant reference volume.
Noid et al. 2008

If
W
is known as a function of both configuration and also thermodynamic conditions, then it is possible to quantitatively reproduce all structural and thermodynamic properties that are observable at the resolution of the CG mapping.
Likos 2001
;
Dunn et al. 2016

2.2
Approximate CG potential

BOCS approximates
W
with CG potentials of the form

U({\mathbf{R}},V)\equiv U_{\rm b}({\mathbf{R}})+U_{\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})+U_{\rm V}(V).

(3)

Here
U_{\rm b}({\mathbf{R}})
is a conventional bonded potential,
U_{\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})
is the non-bonded potential, and
U_{\rm V}(V)
is a volume potential.

The bonded and volume potentials in Eq. (
3
) have not changed in this latest version of BOCS.
The bonded potential includes terms governing
bond, angle, and torsional degrees of freedom.
These terms can be defined with simple analytic forms (e.g., harmonic bond and angle potentials) or with more flexible basis functions (e.g., linear or cubic spline functions).
The volume potential does not generate forces on any particles but directly contributes to the internal pressure via
F_{\rm V}(V)=-{\rm d}U_{\rm V}(V)/{\rm d}V
.
BOCS can represent this volume force with a two parameter functional form
Das and Andersen 2010
or with more flexible basis functions.
Dunn and Noid 2015
;
Dunn and Noid 2016
;
Lesniewski and Noid 2024

BOCS now treats three different classes of non-bonded interactions:

U_{\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv U_{\rm pair}({\mathbf{R}})+U_{\rm LD}({\mathbf{R}})+U_{\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}}).

(4)

Here
U_{\rm pair}
,
U_{\rm LD}
, and
U_{\rm SG}
denote, respectively, contributions from conventional central pair potentials, local density potentials, and also potentials that depend upon the square of the local density gradient.
BOCS allows for complex molecular topologies and many different types of sites.
However, for the sake of simplicity and clarity of presentation, we shall assume that all CG particles are of the same type in the present subsection.

For each CG site,
I
, we define a set,
{\mathcal{S}}_{I}
, of sites that interact with
I
via non-bonded potentials.
This set can include intramolecular non-bonded interactions, while also accounting for intramolecular exclusions.
The pair contribution to
U_{\rm nb}
may be expressed

U_{\rm pair}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\frac{1}{2}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{I}}U_{2}(R_{IJ}),

(5)

where
U_{2}
is a central pair potential and
R_{IJ}
is the distance between CG sites
I
and
J
.

Previous releases of BOCS did not treat
U_{\rm LD}
and
U_{\rm SG}
.
These terms both depend upon the instantaneous local density,
\rho_{I}
, around each particle,
I
.
We define
\rho_{I}
as a sum of contributions from neighboring particles that participate in non-bonded interactions with
I
:

\rho_{I}\equiv\rho_{I}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv c+\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{I}}\overline{w}(R_{IJ}).

(6)

Here
\overline{w}(r)
is a normalized, non-increasing weighting function that vanishes for all
r\geq r_{\rm c}
, while
c
is a configuration-independent constant that can be ignored,
c=0
, or used to define the “self-contribution,”
c=\overline{w}(0)
.
BOCS allows users to specify the LD length-scale,
r_{\rm c}
, and to employ five different functional forms for
\overline{w}
.
We discuss these weighting functions in Section
3.1.3
below and Section I of the SI.
We now define

\displaystyle U_{\rm LD}({\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{N}U_{\rho}(\rho_{I})

(7)

\displaystyle U_{\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{I=1}^{N}U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{2},

(8)

where
{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}=\partial\rho_{I}/\partial{\mathbf{R}}_{I}
.
Note that
U_{\bm{\nabla}}
is not a potential and does not have units of energy.
Rather,
U_{\bm{\nabla}}
is a coefficient that modulates the square gradient contribution as a function of the local density and, in particular, can be defined to act primarily in interfacial regimes.
DeLyser and Noid 2022

Because they are defined in terms of the local density, Eq. (
6
) ensures that
U_{\rm LD}
and
U_{\rm SG}
properly treat intramolecular contributions and exclusions.

The total non-bonded force on site
I
may be expressed

{\bf F}_{I;\rm nb}\equiv-{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}U_{\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{I}}{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}}),

(9)

where the total non-bonded force between the pair
(I,J
) is

{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\left\{F_{2}(R_{IJ})+\left[F_{\rho}(\rho_{I})+F_{\rho}(\rho_{J})\right]\overline{w}^{\prime}(R_{IJ})\right\}\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}+{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}}).

(10)

Here

F_{2}(r)=-{\rm d}U_{2}(r)/{\rm d}r
,

F_{\rho}(\rho)=-{\rm d}U_{\rho}(\rho)/{\rm d}\rho
,

\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)={\rm d}\overline{w}(r)/{\rm d}r
,

\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}
is the unit vector pointing from
J
to
I
,
and
{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}})
is the contribution to the pair force from
U_{\rm SG}
.
The pair and LD potentials generate antisymmetric forces along the vector between each pair.
Importantly,
U_{\rm LD}
modulates these pair forces as a function of the local density around both particles.
Lesniewski et al. 2026

The contribution from
U_{\rm SG}
may be expressed
DeLyser and Noid 2022

\displaystyle{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left[\vphantom{\hat{A}}F_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{2}+F_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{J})\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{J}\rho_{J}\right|^{2}\right]\overline{w}^{\prime}(R_{IJ})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}

(11)

\displaystyle-2g(R_{IJ})\left[{\bf A}_{IJ}\cdot\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}\right]\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}-2h(R_{IJ}){\bf A}_{IJ},

where

F_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho)=-{\rm d}U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho)/{\rm d}\rho
,

h(r)\equiv\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)/r
,

g(r)=\overline{w}^{\prime\prime}(r)-h(r)
,
and

{\bf A}_{IJ}=U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I}){\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}-U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{J}){\bm{\nabla}}_{J}\rho_{J}.

(12)

While the SG potential generates anti-symmetric pair-additive forces, these forces are not along
\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}
because the last term in Eq. (
11
) introduces contributions along
{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}
and
{\bm{\nabla}}_{J}\rho_{J}
.
Because
\overline{w}(r)=0
for
r>r_{\rm c}
, the LD and SG contributions to
{\bf F}_{IJ;\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})
also vanish when
R_{IJ}>r_{\rm c}
.

Equations (
5
) – (
12
) explicitly consider a single type of particle and, consequently, a single local density.
Appendix 1 generalizes this treatment for CG models with many different types of particles and local densities, along with a corresponding diversity of LD and SG potentials.

2.3
Variational force-matching

BOCS determines the optimal approximate CG potential according to the force-matching variational principle
Chorin 2003
;
Noid et al. 2008
that is employed in the multiscale coarse-graining (MS-CG) method,
Izvekov and Voth 2005
;
Izvekov and Voth 2005
i.e., by minimizing

\chi^{2}[U]\equiv\left\langle\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\left|\vphantom{\hat{A}}{\bf F}_{I}({\mathbf{M}}({\bf r}))-{\bf f}_{I}({\bf r})\right|^{2}\right\rangle.

(13)

Here the angular brackets denote an average over the equilibrium ensemble for the AA model,
{\bf f}_{I}({\bf r})
indicates the net force on site
I
in the AA configuration
{\bf r}
,
Noid et al. 2008
;
Krämer et al. 2023
and
{\bf F}_{I}({\mathbf{R}})=-{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}U({\mathbf{R}})
.
Equation (
13
) can be decomposed
Noid et al. 2008
into a fixed contribution from the exact CG potential,
W
, and a non-negative contribution comparing the gradients of
W
and
U
:

\chi^{2}[U]=\chi^{2}[W]+\int_{{\mathcal{D}}_{N}(V)}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\left(W-U\right)\right|^{2},

(14)

Consequently,
W
provides the global minimum of
\chi^{2}[U]
.
Moreover, variations in the CG potential,
U\to U+\delta U
, that reduce
\chi^{2}
result in a better approximation for the configuration-dependence of
W
.
We now construct a basis set for performing this variational calculation.
Noid et al. 2008

Equations (
9
) and (
10
) demonstrate that the total nonbonded force,
{\bf F}_{I;\rm nb}
, on each particle,
I
, depends linearly upon the force functions,
F_{2}
and
F_{\rho}
, that are determined by the pair and LD potentials,
U_{2}
and
U_{\rho}
.
Similarly, the total bonded force from
U_{\rm b}
in Eq. (
3
) may also be written as a linear combination of bonded force functions.
For each bonded, pair, or LD potential,
U_{\zeta}(x)
, we represent the corresponding force function,
F_{\zeta}(x)=-{\rm d}U_{\zeta}(x)/{\rm d}x
, by a linear combination of basis functions,
f_{\zeta d}(x)
, with constant coefficients,
\phi_{\zeta d}
:

F_{\zeta}(x)=\sum_{d}\phi_{\zeta d}f_{\zeta d}(x).

(15)

This basis expansion allows us to express the CG force,
{\bf F}_{I}
, on each particle,
I
, as a linear combination of these coefficients,
\phi_{\zeta d}
.
However, this approach does not work for the SG potential because the corresponding force in Eq. (
11
) depends upon both
F_{\bm{\nabla}}
and
U_{\bm{\nabla}}
.
Consequently, we represent
U_{\bm{\nabla}}(x)=\sum_{d}\phi_{{\bm{\nabla}}d}f_{{\bm{\nabla}}d}(x)
, which determines a corresponding representation for
F_{\bm{\nabla}}(x)=-\sum_{d}\phi_{{\bm{\nabla}}d}f_{{\bm{\nabla}}d}^{\prime}(x)
.
This allows us to express the total force on each CG site as a linear combination of these coefficients:

{\bf F}_{I}({\mathbf{R}};\phi)=\sum_{\zeta}\sum_{d}\phi_{\zeta d}{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\zeta d}({\mathbf{R}}),

(16)

where
{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\zeta d}
specifies the direction of the force on CG site
I
that arises from the
d
th
basis function,
f_{\zeta d}(x)
, for either the
\zeta
force function,
F_{\zeta}(x)
, or, in the case of the SG interaction,
\zeta={\bm{\nabla}}
, the coefficient function,
U_{\bm{\nabla}}(x)
.
The coefficient,
\phi_{\zeta d}
, then determines the magnitude of this force.
Appendix 1 provides explicit expressions for the vector
{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\zeta d}({\mathbf{R}})
, associated with each type of interaction.

If we now consider
{\bf F}_{I}({\mathbf{R}};\phi)
to be the
I
th
element of a force field,
{\bf F}(\phi)=\{{\bf F}_{I}({\mathbf{R}};\phi)\}
, and adopt a ‘super-index’
D=\zeta d
, then Eq. (
16
) may re-expressed more simply

{\bf F}(\phi)=\sum_{D}\phi_{D}{\mathbfcal{G}}_{D},

(17)

where
{\mathbfcal{G}}_{D}=\{{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;D}({\mathbf{R}})\}
may be considered a force field basis vector.
Noid et al. 2008

The set,
\{{\mathbfcal{G}}_{D}\}
, of basis vectors included in Eq. (
17
) specifies a vector space of CG force fields that is defined by the form of the approximate potential,
U
.
A set of coefficients,
\phi\equiv\{\phi_{D}\}
, identifies a particular force field within this subspace and determines a corresponding approximate potential (to within an irrelevant additive constant).

Given this basis set,
\chi^{2}
becomes a quadratic form in the potential parameters:

\chi^{2}(\phi)\equiv\chi^{2}[U(\phi)]=\sum_{D,D^{\prime}}\phi_{D}G_{DD^{\prime}}\phi_{D^{\prime}}-2\sum_{D}b_{D}\phi_{D}+\chi^{2}_{0},

(18)

where
\chi^{2}_{0}=\chi^{2}[0]
and

\displaystyle G_{DD^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\vphantom{\hat{A}}{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;D}({\mathbf{M}}({\bf r}))\cdot{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;D^{\prime}}({\mathbf{M}}({\bf r}))\right\rangle

(19)

\displaystyle b_{D}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{1}{3N}\sum_{I=1}^{N}\vphantom{\hat{A}}{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;D}({\mathbf{M}}({\bf r}))\cdot{\bf f}_{I}({\bf r})\right\rangle.

(20)

Both
G_{DD^{\prime}}
and
b_{D}
are defined as averages over the equilibrium ensemble for the AA model and, thus, may be directly computed from the AA trajectory.

G_{DD^{\prime}}
is a structural correlation function describing the correlation between the forces generated by
D
and
D^{\prime}
basis vectors.
Rudzinski and Noid 2012

Conversely,
b_{D}
may be considered the projection of the atomic force field onto the
D
basis vector.
Mullinax and Noid 2009

Because
b_{D}
can be related to the mean force of the AA model along the corresponding degree of freedom, it can be computed directly from structural information.
Noid et al. 2007
;
Mullinax and Noid 2010

The set of minimizing coefficients,
\phi_{*}=\{\phi_{D_{*}}\}
, that determine the optimal approximate CG potential satisfy the normal system of equations
Noid et al. 2008

\sum_{D^{\prime}}G_{DD^{\prime}}\phi_{D^{\prime}_{*}}=b_{D}.

(21)

BOCS includes several numerical methods for solving Eq. (
21
).

Equation (
21
) is a force balance relation ensuring that, when evaluated over the mapped ensemble, the AA model and the approximate CG potential generate the same mean force along each force field basis vector.
Mullinax and Noid 2009

Consequently, Eq. (
21
) may be interpreted as a generalization of the Yvon-Born-Green equation, which allows BOCS to perform variational force-matching without force information.
Rudzinski and Noid 2015

However, while this capability has been implemented in BOCS for bonded, pair, and LD potentials, it has not yet been implemented for SG potentials.

, in Eq. (

, via self-consistent pressure-matching.

.
This workflow begins with an AA trajectory that must include the atomic configuration,

, at one or more times,

The AA trajectory may also include the net force,
{\bf f}_{i}({\bf r}_{t})
, on each atom,

.
The

{\rm V}_{I}

3.1.2
Software implementation and ecosystem

The
cgff
tool provides an efficient C implementation of force-matching that scales well to large systems and long trajectories.
While our original implementation calculated the many-body contributions to
G_{DD^{\prime}}
via a time-consuming triple loop over particles, the current version of
cgff
calculates
G_{DD^{\prime}}
much more efficiently by directly employing the force field basis vectors.
Moreover,
cgff
employs an “embarrassingly parallel” OpenMPI implementation that reduces the computational time for computing
G_{DD^{\prime}}
and
b_{D}
by a factor of
P
by distributing the sampled configurations over
P
independent processors.

BOCS employs its own file- and data-structures for treating topology and trajectory information.
Because it was initially developed from GROMACS software,
Lindahl et al. 2001
;
van der Spoel et al. 2005
;
Abraham et al. 2015
BOCS originally enjoyed native compatibility with GROMACS topology and trajectory files, e.g., binary .tpr and .trr files.
Subsequently, we developed stand-alone file formats and datastructures that no longer rely upon GROMACS installations.
BOCS now treats CG topology information with a compact, human-readable .btp file format that is quite similar to a dumped .tpr file.
While it can directly interface with conventional trajectory files from GROMACS and LAMMPS, BOCS employs a text, human-readable .btj file to store the LD information in the extended mapped trajectory.
Additionally, BOCS can read LAMMPS trajectory files that have been supplemented with LD information.

We have developed a
translator
tool for porting topology and trajectory information into the BOCS format.
The
translator
tool can convert dumped .tpr files from GROMACS into .btp format.
The
translator
tool can also convert GROMACS and LAMMPS trajectory files into the .btj format, as well as interconvert between these trajectory formats.
This tool provides BOCS with considerable flexibility, while still enjoying the benefits of the GROMACS datastructures.
We anticipate regularly updating the
translator
tool to maintain compatibility with future versions of these packages.

Finally, BOCS also provides a variety of tools for analysis and for facilitating simulations.
For instance, the
LDPDF
tool computes and outputs the sampled distribution for each type of local density and its gradient.
Similarly, the
cgff
tool employs the g-YBG framework to analyze the cross-correlations between different degrees of freedom and to quantify their contribution to the calculated potential.
We have also developed new Python scripts
LD_table_prep_from_spline.py
and
SG_table_prep_from_spline.py
that format the calculated LD and SG potentials for simulations in LAMMPS.

Test 3: 125 molecules with LD and SG potentials

The third test considered a system with 125 molecules and 4 different types of CG sites,
\tau=
A, B, C, or D.
Each molecule consisted of 9 sites with a branched topology.
The interaction potential included both bonded and nonbonded contributions,
U_{\rm R}({\mathbf{R}})=U_{\rm b}({\mathbf{R}})+U_{\rm nb}({\mathbf{R}})

The bonded potential,
U_{\rm b}

The nonbonded potential,
U_{\rm nb}
, treated 42 distinct types of interactions, including 10 pair potentials, 16 LD potentials, and 16 SG potentials.
The nonbonded potential governed both intermolecular interactions and also intramolecular interactions between sites that were separated by 4 or more bonds, i.e., we excluded nonbonded interactions between intramolecular 1-2, 1-3, and 1-4 pairs.
We represented all 42 inter-molecular interactions with cubic spline functions on a uniformly spaced grid.
We defined these potential functions by smoothing and scaling potential functions that were previously calculated for a 1-site CG model of acetic acid.
DeLyser and Noid 2022

We define the model potential more explicitly in Section
4.1.3
.

In order to simplify the presentation, we did not employ distinct functions for all 53 interaction potentials.
In particular, we defined the 4 distinct bond potentials and the 5 distinct angle potentials with the same harmonic functions of the bond and angle, respectively.
Additionally, we defined all 10 distinct pair potentials with the same function.

We adopted similar simplifications for defining the LD and SG potentials.
While this model treats 16 distinct types of local density, we adopted the same Lucy weighting function with
r_{\rm c}=
0.65 nm to define the local density,
\rho_{\tau|I}
, of
\tau

I

The LD potentials,
U_{\rho\tau|\tau_{I}}
, for site
I
depend upon the type,
\tau_{I}
, of site

but not the type,
\tau
, of the surrounding particles.
Similarly, the SG coefficients,
U_{{\bm{\nabla}}\tau|\tau_{I}}
, for site
I
depend upon
\tau_{I}
but not
\tau
.
Furthermore, the LD potential and SG coefficient functions for different site types are related by scalar multiples.

An important technical detail is that we defined the LD and SG potentials such that their support was within the range of local densities sampled by the LAMMPS simulation.
If the range of sampled local densities does not completely cover the support of the LD and SG potentials, then the boundary conditions of the spline basis functions employed in the variational force-matching calculation are not consistent with the simulated LD and SG potentials at the boundary of the sampled local densities, i.e., the basis functions vanish at this boundary but the simulated potentials do not.
This not only results in slight errors at the boundaries of the calculated LD and SG functions, but also generates oscillations in the calculated pair forces at
r_{\rm c}
.
Essentially the discrepancy at the boundaries of the LD and SG functions couple to the boundary of the LD contribution to the pair force.
Nevertheless, the calculated total potential accurately matches the simulated net forces, i.e.,
\chi^{2}\approx 0.

Consequently, we suspect that these considerations are not important in the case of systematic coarse-graining because they likely introduce little error in reproducing the true mean forces.

Results and Discussion

4.1
Benchmark tests

In this subsection we present three benchmark tests that illustrate and validate our implementation of LD and SG potentials in LAMMPS and BOCS.
In each case, we first simulated a known potential,
U_{\rm sim}
, in LAMMPS with the PKG-BOCS package.
We then employed BOCS to calculate a potential,
U_{\rm FM}
, for the same resolution via variational force-matching with the simulated configurations and forces.
Because we are not coarse-graining the underlying model, the known simulated potential minimizes the force-matching functional, i.e.,
\chi^{2}[U_{\rm sim}]=0
.
Consequently, the difference,
U_{\rm sim}({\mathbf{R}})-U_{\rm FM}({\mathbf{R}})
, between the simulated and calculated potentials directly assesses the software that we implemented in LAMMPS and BOCS.
In addition to demonstrating the accuracy of our implementation, these studies provide insight into the forces generated by LD and SG potentials.
In particular, we highlight the impact of the LD weighting function,
\overline{w}
, upon these forces.

4.1.1
Test 1: 400 particles with linear LD potential

We first consider a system of
N=
400 spherical particles that interact only via LD potentials:

U({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{I=1}^{N}U_{\rho}(\rho_{I}).

(24)

We adopt the simplest possible LD potential,

U_{\rho}(\rho_{\rm L})=-k\rho_{\rm L},

(25)

with
k=
0.44504,
which is plotted in the inset of Fig.

The inset of Fig.

The LD force function is independent of
\rho_{\rm L}

Because Eq. (
24

5
Conclusions

Since its original release,
Dunn et al. 2018
BOCS has provided an accurate and robust software package for parameterizing bottom-up CG models of soft materials.
In addition to implementing the force-matching variational principle for parameterizing molecular mechanics potentials,
Izvekov and Voth 2005
;
Izvekov and Voth 2005
;
Noid et al. 2008
;
Noid et al. 2008
BOCS provided several unique capabilities.
For instance, BOCS implemented a generalized-Yvon-Born-Green theory for relating these potentials to structural correlation functions,
Noid et al. 2007
;
Rudzinski and Noid 2015
;
Mullinax and Noid 2009
;
Mullinax and Noid 2010
;
Rudzinski and Noid 2012
;
Rudzinski and Noid 2015
a simple framework for improving their transferability across multiple systems,
Mullinax and Noid 2009
and a self-consistent pressure-matching method for accurately reproducing pressure-density equations of state.
Dunn and Noid 2015

In this work, we have described the latest release of BOCS for parameterizing LD and SG potentials via variational force-matching.
This release is compatible with complex molecular topologies and implements a wide range of weighting functions for defining the local density.
In addition, we have described a significant extension of the PKG-BOCS package for simulating these LD and SG potentials in LAMMPS.
This package implements flexible spline-based representations of these potentials, as well as a library of functional forms that require fewer parameters.
We have been collaborating with LAMMPS developers to incorporate the PKG-BOCS package and anticipate it will be included with the next LAMMPS release.
However, in the interim, we are publicly distributing a version of the PKG-BOCS package configured with LAMMPS April 2026 on our Github site
Lesniewski et al. 2026
along with all the requisite documentation for its use.
Moreover, we are distributing Doxygen documentation and tutorials for using this software in BOCS and LAMMPS.

In addition to describing this new software, we have also reported calculations illustrating its use.
These calculations have provided physical insight into the forces generated by LD and SG potentials.
In particular, we have highlighted the impact of the weighting function used to define the local density.
Moreover, we have illustrated the practical benefits of these LD potentials for improving the structural fidelity, thermodynamic properties, and transferability of bottom-up CG models.
Most importantly, our calculations demonstrate that our implementation of LD and SG potentials is both accurate and robust.

The present work also indicates several directions for future studies.
For instance, the accuracy of LD potentials determined via force-matching depends quite strongly upon the length-scale,
r_{\rm c}
, defining the local density.
It would be very helpful to gain physical insight for predicting the optimal
r_{\rm c}
.
Moreover, future studies should investigate the use of LD potentials in CG models with long-ranged electrostatics.
Nevertheless, we hope that this new version of BOCS and the PKG-BOCS package in LAMMPS may prove useful for developing bottom-up CG models of complex soft materials.

6.1
Appendix 1: Basis functions for variational force-matching.

The force field basis vectors associated with bonded and pair non-bonded potentials are easily derived.
We define
U_{\rm R0}
as the corresponding part of the total interaction potential:

U_{\rm R0}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\sum_{\zeta}\sum_{\lambda}U_{\zeta}(\psi_{\zeta\lambda}({\mathbf{R}})).

(35)

Here
\zeta
indicates a bonded or pair non-bonded interaction,
U_{\zeta}
is the corresponding potential that is a function of a corresponding scalar variable,
\psi_{\zeta\lambda}({\mathbf{R}})
, where
\lambda
indicates the particular instance of the interaction.
For each of these potentials, we define a corresponding force function,
F_{\zeta}(x)=-{\rm d}U_{\zeta}(x)/{\rm d}x
, that we represent with a set of basis functions,
f_{\zeta d}(x)
:

F_{\zeta}(x)=\sum_{d}\phi_{\zeta d}f_{\zeta d}(x),

(36)

where
\phi_{\zeta d}
are the corresponding force field coefficients.
The total force from
U_{\rm R0}
may then be expressed:

{\bf F}_{I;\rm R0}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv-{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}U_{\rm R0}({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{\zeta}\sum_{d}\phi_{\zeta d}{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\zeta d}({\mathbf{R}}),

(37)

where the force field basis vector associated with
f_{\zeta d}
is

{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\zeta d}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\sum_{\lambda}f_{\zeta d}(\psi_{\zeta\lambda}({\mathbf{R}})){\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\psi_{\zeta\lambda}({\mathbf{R}}).

(38)

The basis functions associated with LD and SG potentials are more complex.
Here we derive these basis vectors for the general case that the CG model treats multiple distinct types of CG sites.
We first assign a type,
{\tau_{I}}\in\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\cdots\}
, for each particle,
I=1,\ldots,N
.
This allows us to partition the set,
{\mathcal{S}}_{I}
, of particles that interact with site
I
via non-bonded interactions into contributions from each different type of site:

{\mathcal{S}}_{I}=\bigcup_{\tau}{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|I},

(39)

where
{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|I}=\{J|\tau_{J}=\tau\text{ and $J$ interacts with $I$ via nonbonded potentials}\}
.
Note that
I\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{I}
.
Moreover, the following are all equivalent: (1)
J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{I}
; (2)
J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau_{J}|I}
; (3)
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{J}
; and (4)
I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{{\tau_{I}}|J}
.
For each ordered pair of particle types,
(\tau_{2}|\tau_{1})
, we define a corresponding weighting function,
\overline{w}_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}(r)
.
This allows us to define the local density,
\rho_{\tau|I}
, of
\tau
sites around particle
I
:

\rho_{\tau|I}\equiv c_{\tau}\delta_{\tau\tau_{I}}+\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|I}}\overline{w}_{\tau|{\tau_{I}}}(R_{IJ}),

(40)

where
c_{\tau}
is a configuration-independent constant that depends upon
\tau
and accounts for any self-contribution to the local density since
I\notin{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|I}
.
Note that

{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{\tau|J}=\delta_{I,J}\sum_{K\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|I}}\overline{w}^{\prime}_{\tau|{\tau_{I}}}(R_{IK})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IK}+\theta[I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|J}]\overline{w}^{\prime}_{{\tau_{I}}|{\tau_{J}}}(R_{IJ})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ},

(41)

where
\theta[C]
is an indicator function that equals 1 when the condition
C
is satisfied and otherwise vanishes.
In particular,
\theta[I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau|J}]=\delta_{{\tau_{I}},\tau}\theta[I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{J}]
.
(Note that
\theta[I\in{\mathcal{S}}_{J}]=0
if
I=J
.)

We define the LD potential:

U_{\rm LD}({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{J=1}^{N}\sum_{\tau}U_{\tau|{\tau_{J}}}(\rho_{\tau|J}).

(42)

We define a force function,
F_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}(\rho)=-{\rm d}U_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}(\rho)/{\rm d}\rho
, for each LD potential,
U_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}
.
We represent
F_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}(\rho)
with a linear combination of basis functions:

F_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}(\rho)=\sum_{d}\phi_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1};d}\>f_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1};d}(\rho).

(43)

This then defines the basis set for the LD potential:

{\bf F}_{I;\rm LD}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv-{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}U_{\rm LD}({\mathbf{R}})=\sum_{\tau_{2}}\sum_{\tau_{1}}\sum_{d}\phi_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1};d}\;{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}d}({\mathbf{R}})

(44)

where the force field basis vector,
{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}d}({\mathbf{R}})
, associated with
U_{\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}
may be expressed

\displaystyle{\mathbfcal{G}}_{I;\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}d}({\mathbf{R}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha\tau_{1}}f_{\tau_{2}|\alpha;d}(\rho_{\tau_{2}|I})\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau_{2}|I}}\overline{w}^{\prime}_{\tau_{2}|\alpha}(R_{IJ})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ}

(45)

\displaystyle+\delta_{\alpha\tau_{2}}\sum_{J\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\tau_{1}|I}}f_{\alpha|\tau_{1};d}(\rho_{\alpha|J})\overline{w}^{\prime}_{\alpha|\tau_{1}}(R_{IJ})\widehat{\mathbf{R}}_{IJ},

\alpha\equiv\tau_{I}
to slightly simplify the expression.

(52)

U_{\nabla\tau_{2}|\tau_{1}}

Appendix 2: 2 Particle SG Invariance

The definition of the SG potential,

U_{\rm SG}({\mathbf{R}})\equiv\sum_{I=1}^{N}U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{2},

in Eq. (
8
) implies that the forces and the canonical configuration distribution are unchanged when the SG coefficient function,
U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})
, is replaced with the generalized coefficient
\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I},{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I})=U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})+c\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{-2}
.
Section
4.1.2
considered a system of
N=2

\rho_{I}=\overline{w}(R_{12})
and
\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{2}=|\overline{w}^{\prime}(R_{12})|^{2}

In this case,
\rho_{I}
and
R_{12}
are in 1-to-1 correspondence for

, which implies that
\left|{\bm{\nabla}}_{I}\rho_{I}\right|^{-2}
is a function of
\rho_{I}
.
Thus, for
N=2
, the generalized coefficient may be expressed as a function of only the local density, i.e.,
\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}=\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})
.
Below we explicitly derive this invariance for
N=2
.

In the case of
N=2
the total potential can be expressed in terms of the effective pair potential,
\widetilde{U}_{2}
, given by Eq. (
32
) that corresponds to the effective pair force,
\widetilde{F}_{2}
, given by Eq. (
33
).
We now consider replacing
U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})
with
\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})\equiv U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})+y(\rho_{I})
, where
y(\rho_{I})
is an unknown function.
The equilibrium distribution will remain unchanged as long as the effective pair force remains unchanged.
By substituting
\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{I})
into Eq. (
33

y
satisfies the following differential equation:

\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)\left[y^{\prime}(\rho_{r})\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)\right|^{2}+2y(\rho_{r})\overline{w}^{\prime\prime}(r)\right]=0,

(59)

where
\rho_{r}=\overline{w}(r)
.
We consider the distance range over which
\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)<0
and assume that
y(\rho_{r})>0
over this range.
Eq. (
59
) is then equivalent to

0=\frac{y^{\prime}(\rho_{r})}{y(\rho_{r})}\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)+2\frac{\overline{w}^{\prime\prime}(r)}{\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)}=\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}r}\left\{\ln\left[y(\rho_{r})\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)\right|^{2}\right]\right\},

(60)

which is satisfied when

y(\rho_{r})=c\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r)\right|^{-2}

or, equivalently,

y(\rho)=c\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r_{\rho})\right|^{-2},

(61)

where
r_{\rho}
is the unique distance for which
\overline{w}(r_{\rho})=\rho
.
Consequently, there exists a one-parameter family of SG coefficient functions,
\widetilde{U}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho;c)=U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho)+c\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r_{\rho})\right|^{-2}
that generate the same forces on a pair of isolated particles.

If we had represented
U_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho_{\rm L})
with a flexible spline basis set, then this invariance would have complicated our assessment of the SG test in Section
4.1.2
.
Specifically, this invariance would imply that a family of equivalent SG coefficient functions,
\widetilde{U}^{\rm FM}_{\bm{\nabla}}(\rho;c)=U_{\bm{\nabla}}^{\rm FM}(\rho)+c\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r_{\rho})\right|^{-2}
all minimized the force-matching variational functional,
\chi^{2}[U]
.
However, we avoided these complications by representing
U_{\bm{\nabla}}
with a less flexible basis set that was not compatible with
y(\rho)=c\left|\overline{w}^{\prime}(r_{\rho})\right|^{-2}
.
