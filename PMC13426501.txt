Defect engineered Co/NiO–MgO catalysts for efficient CO2 methanation
Authors: Atmaja Shinde, I. Sreedhar, Satyapaul A. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, interest in CO2 methanation has increased due to its ability to address climate change, store renewable energy, and promote sustainable development. This process converts CO2 and H2 into methane, a major component of CNG, providing a promising option for carbon recycling. However, its effectiveness and selectivity depend heavily on catalyst design and operating conditions. This study suggests that the performance of CO2 methanation over NiO–MgO catalysts is influenced by the synthesis method as well as the cobalt incorporation. Fast vs. slow synthesis, along with Co incorporation by ionic substitution and wet impregnation, is likely to affect defect density, reducibility, CO2 adsorption, and reaction intermediates. These factors can control overall catalytic activity. Comprehensive characterization was done to find the structural, morphological, and textural properties of the catalysts. Among all samples, the Co/NiO–MgO catalyst prepared through ionic substitution using the solution combustion method (CNM_IC) showed the highest performance. It achieved 72% CO2 conversion and 96% CH4 selectivity at 400 °C. This performance comes from its high concentration of oxygen vacancies (OV/OL = 3.83) and a high amount of medium-strength basic sites (58.31 µmol g−1). In situ FTIR analysis showed that the reaction takes place through both CO and formate intermediates. Additionally, the CNM_IC catalyst showed great stability, maintaining high CO2 conversion and CH4 selectivity over 25 hours of continuous operation.

Introduction

Rapid population growth, industrialization, and urbanization have led to a continuous increase in CO2 emissions, with atmospheric concentrations reaching about 430.5 ppm in 2025, intensifying climate change impacts such as global warming and extreme weather events.1,2 To mitigate these effects, carbon pricing mechanisms, including carbon credits, emission trading systems, and carbon taxes, have been introduced, although their success depends on the availability of effective CO2 mitigation technologies.3 In this context Carbon Capture and Utilization (CCU) has emerged as a promising approach that converts captured CO2 into value-added products, contributing to a circular carbon economy.4 However, the high thermodynamic stability of CO2 requires efficient catalysts or significant energy input for its conversion.5 Compared with Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) which faces challenges,6 CCU strategies, such as power-to-gas (PtG) systems enable the conversion of CO2 with green hydrogen into fuels, including methane, methanol, and higher hydrocarbons.7–12 Among these pathways, CO2 methanation is particularly beneficial as it is compatible with existing natural gas infrastructure and allows methane to be used directly as a chemical feedstock or as a clean energy carrier, such as compressed natural gas (CNG), for heat and power generation.13 The CO2 methanation reaction, commonly referred to as the Sabatier reaction, is illustrated in reaction (R1). CO2 methanation is a thermodynamically favorable and highly exothermic reaction that converts CO2 and H2 into methane and water.14 This process provides an effective route for CO2 utilization and renewable energy storage through synthetic natural gas (SNG) production. Although the reaction is kinetically limited at low temperatures, efficient heterogeneous catalysts can enhance CO2 adsorption and H2 activation, achieving high selectivity while suppressing side reactions such as the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction.15,16 With high conversion under moderate conditions and the growing availability of renewable hydrogen, CO2 methanation is considered a promising and scalable approach for sustainable fuel production.17

In numerous studies, the preferred catalysts for both CO2 and CO methanation have predominantly been based on ruthenium (Ru)7,18 and nickel (Ni)19,20 due to their excellent CO2 conversion and methane selectivity. At lower reaction temperatures, Ru catalysts exhibit superior CO2 conversion and high selectivity toward methane formation, making them highly effective for CO2 hydrogenation.16 However, the high cost of Ru limits its large-scale application, leading to growing interest in Ni-based catalysts as a more economical alternative. Ni-based catalysts are widely employed because of their low cost and promising catalytic performance. Yet they suffer from limitations such as sintering, insufficient reducibility, and moderate stability under reaction conditions.21,22 To address these issues, promoters and advanced supports have been explored to improve metal dispersion, strengthen metal–support interactions, increase basicity, and introduce oxygen vacancies features that enhance CO2 adsorption and activation.23–26 Recent studies have further emphasized the importance of defect engineering, particularly oxygen vacancy modulation, in improving CO2 activation and catalytic efficiency.27 In particular, multi-metal composite catalysts have attracted considerable attention because synergistic interactions between different metal components can significantly improve catalytic activity and stability compared with single-metal systems. Oxide supports such as Al2O3 and MgO play an important role by providing abundant surface basic sites that enhance CO2 adsorption and activation, while also stabilizing active metal species through strong metal–support interactions. For example, La-modified Ni/Al2O3 reduces the formation of inactive Ni/Al2O3 phase and stabilizes with highly dispersed, easily reducible Ni species with greater surface basicity. This change improves CO2 activation and enhances the rates of hydrogenation of CO intermediates, resulting in higher CH4 productivity.28 Similarly, Usman et al. demonstrated that structural changes leading to a smaller NiO crystallite size and better Ni dispersion, directly influence CO2 adsorption and activation over Ni/SiO2–Al2O3 catalysts. Ce promotion created many vacancies and strengthened Ni–support interactions. This favors the methanation pathway over the RWGS, while Ba and La adjusted basicity and adsorption strength differently, affecting the main reaction routes.29 Varun et al. further showed that in doped NiO–MgO systems, adjusting the structure is crucial. Sonochemical synthesis created highly dispersed NiO with strong metal–support interactions (MSI). Co, Fe, and Cu dopants changed basicity and reducibility, leading to better CO2 adsorption. Among these promoters, Co doping was particularly effective because metallic Co0 sites enhanced H2 dissociation and worked synergistically with Ni active sites. At the same time, MgO contributed surface basicity and CO2 adsorption capability, highlighting the potential of Co–Ni–Mg multi-metal catalytic systems for improving CO2 methanation performance.30 However, the studies were limited to low concentrations of CO2 and H2.

Besides promoter chemistry, the synthesis method itself is important for defining catalyst structure and function. Solution combustion synthesis, known for its rapid synthesis and highly exothermic nature, encourages rapid nucleation and forms highly porous oxides rich in lattice defects and oxygen vacancies. Ni-substituted Co3O4 catalysts exhibit a significant increase in vacancy concentration due to lattice substitution of Ni. This, in turn, lowers the apparent activation energy for methanation, leading to a substantial improvement in CO2 conversion and CH4 selectivity.31 On the other hand, solvothermal synthesis occurs through slow, diffusion-controlled kinetics, enabling gradual nucleation and crystal growth with precise control over morphology, crystallinity, and surface area. This careful growth results in well-defined nano- and micro-structures with high surface areas (up to about 145 m2 g−1), achieving nearly 100% CH4 selectivity and around 90% CO2 conversion as well as a space time yield (STYCH4) of 140 mmol g−1 h−1. This shows that slow synthesis kinetics support the development of stable and highly active methanation catalysts.32 Despite the extensive literature on Co-promoted Ni-based catalysts, oxygen-vacancy engineering, and NiO–MgO systems for CO2 methanation, a systematic understanding of how the synthesis route and Co incorporation strategy collectively influence defect generation, surface basicity, MSI, and catalytic performance remains limited. In particular, a direct comparison between ionic substitution and wet impregnation across different synthesis routes has not been comprehensively investigated. Therefore, the present work aims to establish a structure–performance relationship by systematically evaluating solution combustion and solvothermal synthesis combined with two Co incorporation strategies. The present study demonstrates how different synthesis approaches can be used to tailor catalyst properties and optimize CO2 methanation activity.

In this study, the performance of CO2 methanation was investigated using NiO–MgO catalysts synthesized using various methods and incorporating cobalt. Solution combustion and solvothermal methods are likely to create different structural and surface characteristics. Incorporating cobalt via ionic substitution is expected to result in more uniform lattice changes and a greater number of oxygen vacancies than the wet impregnation method. This, in turn, will affect reducibility, CO2 adsorption, reaction intermediates, and overall catalytic performance. To test this hypothesis, Co-doped NiO–MgO catalysts were synthesized using both methods and incorporation techniques. The catalysts were characterized using XRD, XPS, N2 physisorption, SEM, H2-TPR, and CO2-TPD, and was assessed for CO2 conversion and CH4 selectivity. In situ FTIR analysis was further explored to clarify the reaction intermediates and mechanisms. Finally, the most active catalyst was examined through stability testing and post-reaction analysis.

Materials and methods

The synthesis of Co/NiO–MgO involved employing solution combustion and solvothermal synthesis methods. In this process, Co was incorporated into NiO–MgO material, through substitution and impregnation mechanism. The catalyst synthesis and characterization methods are elaborated in SI. H2-temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR)

H2-TPR analysis was conducted to investigate the reducibility of the catalyst. Approximately 30 mg of the catalyst was loaded into a U-shaped quartz reactor tube. Prior to the reduction measurements, the sample was pretreated by degassing at 150 °C under a continuous flow of argon (40 mL min−1) with a heating rate of 10 °C min−1 to remove physisorbed moisture and other weakly bound surface impurities. Subsequently, the gas flow was switched to pure hydrogen, and the temperature was ramped from 50 °C to 700 °C at a constant rate of 10 °C min−1. The hydrogen consumption during the reduction process was continuously quantified using the inbuilt thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The H2 uptake values were normalized with respect to the catalyst mass to enable accurate comparison between different samples. The H2 consumption was determined by integrating the area under the TCD signal obtained from the H2-TPR experiment and value was calculated using an internal calibration. For internal calibration a known volume of gas was introduced through a 527 µL sample loop and the corresponding TCD response was recorded. The area obtained after the integration of TCD signal was used as reference. The reproducibility of the calibration was verified by multiple injections. The calculated values were subsequently normalized with respect to the catalyst mass and were further used for the H2 consumption calculation.

CO2 temperature-programmed desorption (CO2-TPD)

CO2-TPD was used to evaluate the surface basicity of the catalyst. About 100 mg of the sample was placed into a U-shaped quartz tube and pretreated by purging with argon at 150 °C for 30 minutes. The heating rate was 10 °C per minute to remove adsorbed moisture and surface contaminants. After that, CO2 gas was introduced at a flow rate of 30 mL per minute for 1 hour at room temperature to allow adsorption onto the catalyst surface. Excess and weakly adsorbed CO2 was then removed by purging with helium. The desorption profile was recorded by heating the sample from room temperature to 700 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute under a helium flow. The quantification of CO2-TPD signal was done similar way to the H2-TPR with the help of calibration gas and normalizing with catalyst weight.

Temperature programmed oxidation (TPO)

Temperature programmed oxidation (TPO) was performed to evaluate carbon deposition on the catalyst surface after a stability studies. Approximately 100 mg of spent catalyst was loaded into a quartz tube reactor of U-shape and purged with helium for 30 min at 150 °C to remove surface contaminants. Then a 10% O2/He mixture (50 mL min−1) was introduced while heating the sample from 100 to 800 °C at a ramping rate of 10 °C min−1, and the oxidation profile was recorded using a TCD.

Studies with in situ Fourier transform infrared (in situ FTIR) spectroscopy

In situ FTIR (Spectrum 3, PerkinElmer, Singapore) was used to monitor the formation of surface reaction intermediates during CO2 methanation. The measurements took place with a Praying Mantis diffuse reflectance accessory (Harrick Scientific, USA) that had a high-temperature reaction chamber and a zinc selenide (ZnSe) window. A PID controller connected to a recirculating chiller was used to precisely control the temperature of reaction chamber. In a typical analysis, the catalyst sample was placed in the sample holder of the diffuse reflectance cell. Before supplying the reactants, a background spectrum at 30 °C was recorded with only N2 flowing. The methanation reaction began by introducing a CO2 : H2 : N2 gas mixture at a flow ratio of 10 : 40 : 50 mL min−1. Spectra were recorded every 5 minutes for up to 45 minutes at each temperature within the range of 100–450 °C, with measurements taken at intervals of 50 °C. All FTIR spectra were collected over the range of 1000–4000 cm−1, with a spectral resolution of 4 cm−1, a data interval of 0.5 cm−1, and 32 scans per spectrum.

Catalytic performance studies

The catalyst was prepared in pellet form using a hydraulic press then they were crushed and particles were sieved to obtain pellets in 150/300 mesh size. The pelleted catalyst was placed at the center of a quartz tube surrounded by ceramic wool. A bed length of 2 cm was maintained by diluting the catalyst with silica gel of equivalent size of the pellets. The catalyst loading was 500 mg in the bed. The Gas Hourly Space Velocities (GHSV) were maintained at 7650 h−1 for all the reactions. The prepared catalyst bed was exposed to reactants with a flow rate of 200 mL min−1 containing 10 vol% CO2, 40 vol% H2, and 50 vol% N2 at NTP. Toward the end of the setup, a moisture trap was connected in order to remove any moisture content generated during the reaction, and only the dried gas mixture was allowed to pass through an online Gas Chromatograph (GC) (Mayura Analytical, Model 2100, Bangalore) to measure the composition by using the thermal conductivity detector (TCD), methanizer and flame ionization detector (FID). Two columns were used for product separation: a Hayesep column for the separation of CO2, CO, and CH4 and a molecular sieve column for hydrogen analysis. To analyse the samples a standard calibration gas with a known composition was used. Each experiment was repeated thrice under identical conditions to ensure reproducibility and the error in conversion and selectivity values was within ±3%. Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

The XRD patterns of the fresh Co/NiO–MgO catalysts were found to be in good agreement with the standard JCPDS reference data for NiO (JCPDS No. 01-078-0643),33 and MgO (JCPDS No. 01-077-2364),34 as illustrated in Fig. 1. The diffraction peaks correspond to the characteristic reflections of cubic NiO (a = b = c = 4.176 Å, space group: Fm3̄m) and cubic MgO (a = b = c = 4.209 Å, space group: Fm3̄m). Due to the similar crystal structures and close lattice parameters of NiO and MgO, their diffraction peaks significantly overlap, making it difficult to distinguish between separate phases and the possible formation of a solid solution based solely on XRD analysis. The absence of metallic Ni0 peaks indicates that Ni is predominantly present in the oxidized NiO form after synthesis. Both combustion and solvothermal methods produce well-crystallized oxide phases confirming the effectiveness of the synthesis routes. No distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to cobalt species are observed, which can be attributed to the low Co loading (2%) and its high dispersion or possible incorporation into the NiO–MgO lattice. A slight shift in peak positions relative to standard references suggests lattice distortion or interaction between the NiO and MgO phases, possibly influenced by cobalt incorporation.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis (XPS)

XPS analysis is performed to obtain insights into chemical shift of the materials. Fig. 2a shows the deconvoluted O 1s spectra of the synthesized catalysts, which include lattice oxygen (OL), oxygen vacancies (OV), and chemisorbed oxygen species (OC). When Co was added, the O 1s spectra shift correspond to OV was observed. This shift reflects changes in the local electronic environment resulting from different Co-incorporation methods. The combustion-derived catalysts showed a negative shift, whereas the solvothermally synthesized catalysts showed a positive shift, indicating a difference in the synthesis route. Based on MSI theory these shifts in binding energy come from changes in electron distribution at the metal-oxide interface. The negative shift indicates an increase in electron density related to oxygen vacancy formation and electronic MSI and the positive shift suggests stronger bonding between metal and oxygen, along with electron depletion that corresponds with strong MSI.35,36

XPS Spectra of O 1s of (a) NM_C, (b) NM_S, (c) CNM_IC, (d) CNM_IS, (e) CNM_WC, and (f) CNM_WS.

Quantitative analysis suggests that incorporating Co via ionic substitution yields a higher concentration of oxygen vacancies than wet impregnation, especially for the combustion-derived catalyst (CNM_IC). Although the precise measurement of oxygen vacancies from XPS deconvolution should be viewed cautiously, the observed trend aligns with the calculated OV/OL ratios. This higher vacancy density suggests enhanced MSI and partial reduction of surface oxygen, both of which promote CO2 activation and H2 dissociation-key steps for efficient CO2 methanation.37 This is further supported by the improved medium-strength basicity found in CO2-TPD. These findings show that ionic substitution is more effective at changing the surface oxygen environment than wet impregnation. The better catalytic performance of CNM_IC is attributed not just to its higher oxygen vacancy concentration. It also benefits from the combined effects of defect-rich surface characteristics, favorable Co incorporation, and increased surface basicity, which together enhance CO2 adsorption and activation.

Fig. 3 shows the Co 2p XPS spectra of the cobalt incorporated catalysts. During deconvolution the area ratio of Co 2p3/2 to Co 2p1/2 was maintained at 2 : 1. The Co3+ 2p3/2 and Co2+ 2p3/2 components were identified at binding energies of about 780.2 eV and 781.8 eV respectively. The corresponding Co3+ 2p1/2 and Co2+ 2p1/2 peaks appeared at around 795.5 eV and 796.9 eV. Characteristic shake up satellite peaks of Co were found at 785.5 eV and 801.1 eV, respectively, and these values are consistent with the literature.31 Minor differences in binding energy among the catalysts are due to variations in cobalt incorporation pathways and the resulting interactions with support. The spin orbit splitting between Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2 remained stable at 15.2 ± 0.1 eV for all samples, showing similar cobalt electronic environments.

Co 2p of (a) CNM_IC, (b) CNM_WC, (c) CNM_IS, (d) CNM_WS.

The Ni 2p XPS spectra in Fig. S2 show mixed valence nickel species in all catalysts with Ni2+ 2p3/2 and Ni3+ 2p3/2 peaks at around 854.1 and 855.5 eV, and corresponding Ni2+ 2p1/2 and Ni3+ 2p1/2 peaks at around 871.5 and 872.5 eV, respectively. Prominent shake-up satellite peaks feature at around 857.2, 861.3, 865.1, 874.2, and 878.5 eV, confirming the presence of Ni2+ in an oxide environment. The presence of both Ni2+ and Ni3+ species and slight binding energy shifts across samples, indicates electronic redistribution due to synthesis route and cobalt incorporation, which is expected to affect catalyst performance in CO2 methanation.31 The observed binding energy shifts suggest electron redistribution between Co and Ni species. The literature reports that such interactions can upshift the Ni d-band centre, enhancing electron donation to adsorbates and improving catalytic activity.58 Fig. S1 shows the XPS spectra of Mg 1s for all synthesized catalysts revealing two peaks assigned to MgO at around 1303.5 eV and Mg(OH)2 at around 1302.7 eV. A slight binding energy shift of 0.2 eV was observed across most samples, attributed to variations in the electronic environment due to different synthesis routes.38 Notably, the CNM_IC catalyst exhibited only a single MgO peak with no detectable Mg(OH)2 signal, indicating complete oxidation of Mg and superior phase purity.39,40

Raman spectroscopy

Fig. S3 presents the Raman spectra of the combustion-synthesized catalysts, providing qualitative insights into defect formation and oxygen vacancies. The Raman spectra of NiO contain distinct bands from optical vibrations. These include first-order phonon (1P) modes of transverse optical (TO) at 440 cm−1 and longitudinal optical (LO) at 560 cm−1. It also features second-order phonon (2P) modes related to TO + TO (730 cm−1), TO + LO (930 cm−1), and LO + LO (1080 to 1090 cm−1). These are sensitive to structural variations and can be affected by compositional changes, lattice distortion, and defect formation.38 The NiO related first-order phonon band (560 cm−1) is particularly sensitive to local structural changes and variations in its position, intensity, and broadening can reflect site symmetry distortion and defect formation within the lattice.

The band observed around 400 cm−1 is attributed to Mg(OH)2, formed on the surface due to the interaction of MgO with moisture present in ambient air.37 In the present catalysts, the broad nature of the 1P band and the absence of some expected NiO modes can be attributed to small crystallite size, structural disorder, and defect-induced relaxation.38 After adding cobalt, the NiO-related band around 560 cm−1 shows increased intensity and broadening. This change suggests greater lattice distortion, site-symmetry breaking, and defect formation. A slight peak shift was noted, but these spectral changes imply that the local NiO environment is altered due to cobalt incorporation. The absence or weakening of the 1090 cm−1 (2LO) band in the cobalt-containing samples indicates smaller NiO domains and disruption of short-range order. This aligns with greater structural disorder and effects from lattice substitution.

The absence of distinct CoO related Raman peaks suggests that Co does not form a separate phase but is incorporated into the NiO–MgO lattice through ionic substitution. Since Co2+ and Ni2+ possess similar ionic radii and share the same rock-salt structure and Co-O vibrations overlap with NiO phonon modes therefore appear within the same spectral region. Such structural modifications are closely associated with the generation of oxygen vacancies resulting from Co substitution in the NiO–MgO lattice. Among all the catalysts, CNM_IC exhibits the highest intensity of the defect-related band indicating the highest concentration of oxygen vacancies. This aligns with the results from XPS. These vacancies play a crucial role in enhancing CO2 adsorption and activation, thereby contributing to the improved catalytic performance of the CNM_IC catalyst.

Morphological analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The SEM images of NiO–MgO made through the combustion method (Fig. S4a–c) mainly showed a porous surface. This porous structure is useful as it improves gas diffusion and helps trap and activate reactant gases like CO2 and H2, which boosts catalytic efficiency. In particular, Fig. S4a and b show a well-developed porous network with interconnected voids and channels. However, the image in Fig. S4c does not show a clear porous framework. This difference is due to the wet impregnation method used for cobalt incorporation, in which the NM_C support material undergoes sonication. The high-intensity sonication effectively disperses cobalt species, but it can partially disrupt the pre-formed porous structure of the support. As a result, the structural uniformity is lost, leading to a less porous morphology. Furthermore, the SEM images of NiO–MgO synthesized via the solvothermal method revealed a leaf-like morphology (Fig. S4d). Upon cobalt incorporation, distinct morphological variations were observed (Fig. S4e and f). In Fig. S4e, the material exhibited a combination of wire-like and leaf-like structures, which may have originated from structural modifications induced by cobalt substitution. In contrast, Fig. S4f displayed an entirely different morphology, consistent with the disruption of the porous framework caused by the impregnation method, as previously noted for Fig. S4c.

In Fig. S4g, the EDX mapping of CNM_IC reveals a uniform particle distribution, indicating good dispersion of the constituent elements. Moreover, the Co elemental map shows that cobalt is evenly distributed throughout the catalyst matrix. This uniform elemental dispersion confirms the successful ionic substitution of Co into the NiO–MgO framework and ensures the homogeneous generation of active sites, which is crucial for consistent MSI and stable catalytic performance during CO2 methanation.

The TEM image of fresh CNM_IC (Fig. 4a) shows a granular particle like structure that forms an interconnected network. This matches the porous structure seen in the SEM image (Fig. S4b). The HRTEM image (Fig. 4b) reveals clear lattice fringes with d-spacing values of 0.215 nm and 0.243 nm for the MgO (200) and (111) planes, respectively. Additionally, NiO (200) with a d-spacing of 0.204 nm was noticed. The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern (Fig. 4d) shows diffraction rings that correspond to d-spacing of 0.243, 0.212, 0.150, and 0.122 nm. These are assigned to the (111), (200), (220), and (222) planes, respectively. Although the SAED pattern looks diffuse and somewhat amorphous, the XRD analysis confirms that the catalyst is crystalline. This appearance is due to the very small particle size and high defect density. The O 1s XPS results (Fig. 2) support this by showing broadening and diffusion of the diffraction features. The high angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images shown in Fig. 4c depicts the effective cobalt distribution in NiO–MgO.

N2 adsorption–desorption analysis

The textural and structural properties of the catalysts, summarized in Table 2, show a strong dependence on both the synthesis method and the way cobalt is added. Among the combustion synthesized samples, the undoped NM_C had a moderate specific surface area of 23.06 m2 g−1, an average pore diameter of 23 nm. Adding cobalt through ionic substitution (CNM_IC) increased the surface area to 30.72 m2 g−1 while reducing the average pore diameter to 17 nm. This indicates that cobalt was incorporated at the lattice level without much expansion of the pores. In contrast cobalt incorporation by wet impregnation (CNM_WC) significantly improved the surface area to 50.31 m2 g−1 and pore volume to 0.56 cm3 g−1. This method resulted in a larger average pore diameter of 45 nm, which is due to the formation of inter-particle mesoporosity. A different trend was observed for the solvothermal synthesized catalysts where the NM_S catalyst displayed a surface area of 31.98 m2 g−1, a pore diameter of 15 nm which is larger compared to the combustion-derived samples. For CNM_IS a significant decrease in surface area was observed of 12.36 m2 g−1 with a pore volume of 0.06 cm3 g−1. The average pore diameter increased to 21 nm, this suggests densification of the lattice and partial collapse of the pores. Whereas CNM_WS partially restored the textural properties with a surface area of 25.04 m2 g−1, a pore volume of 0.15 cm3 g−1, and an average pore diameter of 25 nm. These trends show that wet impregnation mainly alters surface texture while ionic substitution mainly affects bulk structure and metal–support interaction. This in turn impacts catalytic behaviour through different relationships between structure and properties. From the % dispersion data shown in Table 2, the samples with wet impregnation data have higher dispersion as it is assisted by sonication which helps in better dispersion of dopant. This further supports having less surface area for substituted sample than the impregnated one.

Reducibility and surface basicity

Combustion synthesized catalyst

H2-TPR analysis was conducted to evaluate the reducibility of the active metal species and the strength of metal–support interactions in the combustion-synthesized catalysts (Fig. 5a). For the undoped combustion-synthesized sample (NM_C), a single reduction peak at 314 °C corresponds to the reduction of NiO to metallic Ni, while MgO remains non-reducible due to its high thermodynamic stability.24 The relatively low reduction temperature indicates weak MSI due to the rapid nucleation and quenching associated with combustion synthesis leading to loosely bound and easily reducible NiO.41 Upon cobalt incorporation the reduction behavior changes significantly which reflects modifications in both reducibility and MSI. In CNM_IC, two distinct reduction peaks appear at 285 °C and 475 °C, corresponding to the reduction of CoOx species and strongly interacting NiO species respectively. The shift of the reduced NiO peak toward higher temperature indicates decrease in reducibility caused by enhanced MSI and possible formation of Co–O–Ni linkages which stabilize the oxide phase. Similar behavior is observed for CNM_WC where the NiO reduction peak becomes less distinct which suggests strong Co–Ni interaction or overlapping reduction processes. Such shifts to higher reduction temperatures were previously reported in Ni-based systems as an indication of stronger MSI and electronic modification induced by promoter incorporation.42

H2-TPR analysis of (a) combustion and (b) solvothermal catalysts, CO2-TPD analysis of (c) combustion and (d) solvothermal catalysts, (e) H2 consumption, and (f) quantification of basic sites of all the catalysts.

The H2 consumption profiles (Fig. 5e) further support these observations. NM_C exhibits the highest hydrogen uptake (9.64 µmol g−1) which indicates a larger fraction of readily reducible NiO. In contrast, CNM_IC (6.66 µmol g−1) and CNM_WC (1.86 µmol g−1) show lower H2 consumption suggesting the formation of more stable oxide species and stronger interaction with the support, which reduces the availability of easily reducible NiO. Despite the apparent decrease in reducibility, strong MSI is beneficial as it improves metal dispersion, reduces sintering, and stabilize active sites under reaction conditions.

CO2-TPD is used to identify the nature, concentration, and strength of surface basic sites, which play a key role in CO2 adsorption and activation. Typically, three desorption regions are observed corresponding to weak, medium, and strong basic sites.43,44 From Fig. 4c the base combustion-synthesized catalyst (NM_C) exhibited a comparatively higher proportion of weak basic sites, which contribute only marginally to CO2 activation. Upon Co incorporation, a pronounced shift toward medium-strength basic sites was observed, especially for CNM_IC. Quantitatively, the density of medium basic sites increased from 20.88 µmol g−1 for NM_C to 58.31 and 29.20 µmol g−1 for CNM_IC and CNM_WC, respectively (Fig. 5f). Among all catalysts, CNM_IC showed the highest concentration of medium basic sites (58.31 µmol g−1), indicating its superior ability to adsorb and activate CO2, which directly correlates with its enhanced methanation performance.

Solvothermal synthesized catalyst

For the undoped solvothermal catalyst (NM_S), the reduction of NiO to metallic Ni occurs at a significantly higher temperature of 458 °C (Fig. 5b), indicating much stronger MSI between NiO and the MgO matrix compared to the combustion-synthesized sample. This low reducibility arises from the slow nucleation and crystal growth processes inherent to solvothermal synthesis, which allow sufficient time for the formation of more stable NiO–MgO interfaces. As a result, NiO become more strongly bound to the support and require higher temperatures for reduction. Upon incorporation of cobalt, the reduction peaks of CNM_IS and CNM_WS further show modifications in reducibility and MSI. The CoOx reduction peaks observed at 394 °C (CNM_IS) and 232 °C (CNM_WS) correspond to differently interacting cobalt species where CNM_IS has a higher reduction temperature indicating stronger lattice incorporation via ionic substitution. The corresponding NiO reduction peaks at 462 °C for CNM_IS and 479 °C for CNM_WS are shifted to even higher temperatures, confirming enhanced MSI and decreased reducibility of NiO. Such shifts to higher temperatures are widely associated with stronger interfacial bonding and the possible formation of Co–O–Ni linkages.42 The H2 consumption data (Fig. 5e) further support these findings. NM_S exhibits the highest hydrogen uptake (12.43 µmol g−1), indicating a higher amount of reducible NiO. On the other hand, CNM_IS (6.24 µmol g−1) and CNM_WS (4.72 µmol g−1) show significantly lower H2 consumption which suggests the formation of more stable oxide phases and stronger MSI upon cobalt incorporation. Although lower reducibility may limit the availability of easily reducible species, it enhances structural stability by anchoring the metal species within the support matrix and suppressing sintering.

From Fig. 5d, the base solvothermal catalyst (NM_S) exhibited a very low concentration of medium basic sites (4.96 µmol g−1), again dominated by weak basic sites associated with surface hydroxyl groups. After Co incorporation, CNM_IS and CNM_WS showed only a modest increase in medium basic sites (7.68 and 2.83 µmol g−1, respectively) (Fig. 5f). However, unlike the combustion-derived samples, the solvothermal catalysts predominantly exhibited strong basic sites, especially in CNM_IS and CNM_WS, which are typically associated with low-coordination surface oxygen species. Although these strong sites contribute to firm CO2 adsorption, but they are generally less effective than medium strength sites for facilitating the dynamic adsorption–dissociation steps required during CO2 methanation.

Catalytic performance studies

The catalytic performance of Co/NiO–MgO catalysts synthesized by combustion and solvothermal routes is shown in Fig. 6a. In general, all catalysts exhibited a progressive increase in CO2 conversion up to 400 °C, followed by either stable or a slight decline in performance at higher temperatures. For combustion-derived samples, the base catalyst (NM_C) achieved a maximum conversion of 66.2% at 425 °C, whereas cobalt incorporation markedly enhanced performance. Specifically, CNM_IC and CNM_WC reached 72.2% and 67.5% conversion at 400 °C, respectively, confirming that cobalt effectively lowers the activation temperature required for CO2 hydrogenation and thereby improves overall catalytic efficiency. The enhanced performance of CNM_IC can be correlated with its favorable textural and electronic properties. BET analysis shows that CNM_IC exhibits a moderate surface area (30.72 m2 g−1) and a relatively smaller pore diameter compared with the CNM_WC indicating that cobalt incorporation occurs mainly through lattice substitution rather than surface deposition. Although the CNM_WC possesses a higher surface area and dispersion its catalytic performance remains lower than that of CNM_IC, suggesting that surface area alone is not the dominant factor governing catalytic activity.

(a) CO2 conversion of all catalysts and (b) time-on-stream of CNM_IC for 30 h (condition: CO2 : H2 : N2 = 1 : 4 : 5 molar ratio, T = 400 °C, P = 1 atm, and GHSV = 7650 h−1).

The superior activity of CNM_IC is instead closely related to the defect structure and electronic properties of the catalyst. XPS analysis indicated that CNM_IC has the highest oxygen vacancy available among all the catalysts. These oxygen vacancies play a critical role in facilitating CO2 adsorption and activation by providing electron-rich defect sites capable of stabilizing carbonate and formate intermediates. In addition, the presence of medium-strength basic sites (58.31 µmol g−1) further enhances reversible CO2 adsorption and promotes subsequent hydrogenation steps. The SEM analysis also indicate a porous morphology for the combustion-derived CNM_IC catalyst, which improves gas diffusion and accessibility of active sites.

For the solvothermal-synthesized catalysts the base material NM_S showed a CO2 conversion of 63.7% at 425 °C, while cobalt incorporation led to moderate improvements with CNM_IS reaching 67.7% conversion at 425 °C and CNM_WS achieving 52.7% at 450 °C. However, the overall activity of the solvothermal catalysts remained lower than that of the combustion derived catalysts. This behavior can be associated with the significant reduction in surface area and pore volume observed for CNM_IS in BET measurements, which likely limits the accessibility of active sites and reactant diffusion. These results highlight that the synthesis route plays a crucial role in determining both the textural properties and defect structure of the catalyst, which in turn influences catalytic performance.

Overall, the CNM_IC catalyst exhibited the highest performance among all the materials studied achieving approximately 72% CO2 conversion and over 96% CH4 selectivity (Fig. S6) at 400 °C with a STYCH4 of 74 mmol g−1 h−1. The improved catalytic performance can be attributed to the synergistic effects of optimal textural properties, a high concentration of oxygen vacancies, and enhanced MSI resulting from cobalt incorporation through ionic substitution. These features facilitate efficient CO2 adsorption, hydrogen activation, and stabilization of reaction intermediates, thereby improving methanation activity. In contrast, the CNM_IS catalyst although prepared via ionic substitution, exhibits comparatively lower activity due to its significantly lower oxygen vacancy concentration and excessively strong MSI, as evidenced by H2-TPR and XPS results. The higher reduction temperature and lower H2 consumption indicate reduced availability of active metallic sites, which limits hydrogen activation and CO2 conversion. Compared with previously reported Co and Ni-based catalysts (Table 3), CNM_IC demonstrates competitive performance with high selectivity under relatively high space velocity conditions indicating better intrinsic performance and effective utilization of active sites. Although CNM_WC has a higher specific surface area and larger pore volume than CNM_IC, it shows lower catalytic activity, indicating that textural properties alone do not determine methanation performance. Instead, the higher activity of CNM_IC is associated with the synergistic contribution of oxygen vacancies, enhanced medium basic sites, and favorable Co incorporation through ionic substitution in combustion synthesis, which together provide more effective sites for CO2 adsorption and activation. A similar correlation between oxygen vacancy concentration and catalytic activity has also been demonstrated in previous studies,31,45 where oxygen vacancies were identified as one of the major factors governing CO2 activation despite differences in textural properties.

Overall, catalytic performance is strongly governed by the synthesis dependent structural and electronic properties of the catalysts. Stability testing is essential to ensure that a catalyst maintains its performance during the operation without deactivation from sintering, coking, or metal particle agglomeration.13 The stability test was carried out for the CNM_IC catalyst (Fig. 6b) because of its superior catalytic performance compared to the other samples. At 400 °C, the catalyst achieved around 80% CO2 conversion and more than 90% CH4 selectivity. The STYCH4 (Fig. 6b) of the CNM_IC catalyst was initially lower due to reduced CO2 conversion and CH4 selectivity, which can be attributed to the gradual in situ activation of the catalyst. As a result, the STYCH4 increases and stabilizes over time, remaining nearly constant for 25 h and following the trend of CO2 conversion, with a value of 82 ± 1 mmol g−1 h−1. Both conversion and selectivity remained nearly constant, with only minor fluctuations over a continuous 25 h operation, confirming the excellent thermal and structural stability of CNM_IC under reaction conditions.

Apparent activation energy evaluation

Apparent activation energy evaluation for the CNM_IC catalyst was conducted using catalyst loadings from 0 to 500 mg in 100 mg increments. These studies were performed over a temperature range of 300 to 350 °C at 25 °C intervals. The activation energy for the highest performing catalyst was determined by treating the packed-bed reactor as a differential flow reactor, assuming that heat and mass transfer limitations were minimal (Section S4). Fig. 7a shows that the linear relationship between CO2 fractional conversion and catalyst loading supports the differential reactor assumption. Using the Arrhenius plot presented in Fig. 7b, the apparent activation energy for the CNM_IC catalyst was calculated to be 56.83 ± 0.42 kJ mol−1.

Insights from in situ FTIR study

The in situ FTIR results for the CO2 methanation process provide insights into the adsorption and reaction intermediates formed on the catalyst surface during the reaction. In Fig. 8a, mechanistic insights of CO2 methanation process on CNM_IC surface are presented. For CNM_IC catalysts, distinct peaks corresponding to different intermediates were observed during CO2 methanation. At lower temperatures (100–250 °C), the spectra mainly show bands at 1225 cm−1 and 1405 cm−1, which corresponds to bicarbonate and carbonate respectively.46 There is also a consistent band at 1690 cm−1 linked to bidentate bicarbonate species. The gradual decline in the intensity of these bands as temperature increases shows that CO2 derived species are being consumed. This confirms their role as important intermediates rather than just passive ones. When the temperature rises above 300 °C, a band at 1382 cm−1 appears. This band relates to the symmetric O–C–O stretching vibration of surface formate (HCOO−) species.47 Its increase in peak intensity coincides with the weakening of bicarbonate and carbonate features. This suggests that CO2 derived species are transforming into formate intermediates with the help of hydrogen. As the temperature continues to rise, the formate band intensity decreases, indicating it is actively participating and being consumed during methanation. At the same time, a band at 1318 cm−1 appears, which relates to C–H bending vibrations of methane related surface species. This is observed between 300 to 400 °C. The gradual increase in the intensity of this band indicates the start of methane formation on the catalyst surface. This finding shows a shift from oxygenated intermediates to hydrocarbon species. It provides clear evidence that carbon containing intermediates are being hydrogenated to form methane. A band at 2174 cm−1, indicating linearly bound carbonyl species, shows up between 150 to 400 °C.31 This is followed by the development of free CO bands at 2104 and 1997 cm−1, especially at higher temperatures (350–450 °C).47 This pattern suggests that CO formation, likely through the reverse water gas shift pathway, becomes more favorable at higher temperatures, helping in methane production through further hydrogenation. The progression of hydrogenation reactions is further supported by the gradual increase in bands at 2725 cm−1, assigned to combination vibrations of carboxylate species and C–H bending, and at 2835 cm−1, which corresponds to C–H stretching vibrations of surface methylene (–CH2–) species, between 250 to 450 °C. At 450 °C, a strong band at 3018 cm−1 appears corresponding to gaseous methane which confirms the complete hydrogenation of surface intermediates.

In situ FTIR characterization of CNM_IC (T = 100–450 °C, P = 1 atm, CO2 : H2 : N2 = 10 : 40 : 50 mL min−1): (a) temperature-dependent spectra, and (b–d) time-dependent spectra in the 1200–1450 cm−1, 1600–1750 cm−1, and 2675–2925 cm−1 regions, respectively.

Overall, these findings show that CO2 methanation over CNM_IC occurs through a series of transformations from bicarbonate and carbonate species to formate and CO intermediates. This is followed by ongoing hydrogenation to CHx species as indicated by the bands at 1318, 2725, and 2835 cm−1, which ultimately lead to methane formation. The presence and temperature dependent changes of both formate- and CO-derived intermediates suggest a dual reaction pathway. Formate-mediated routes dominate at intermediate temperatures, while CO-assisted hydrogenation becomes more important at higher temperatures. To further analyze the in situ FTIR data, the time dependent evolution of peak intensities was examined at 350 °C. Additional weak bands corresponding to monodentate bicarbonate (1724 cm−1) and branched carboxylic species (1618 cm−1) were identified, which were not clearly observed earlier due to their low intensities.47 Time-resolved spectra showed that the intensity of C–H vibration bands remained relatively constant over 45 min of operation, whereas the intensities of formate and bicarbonate species fluctuated. This confirms the continuous conversion of these intermediates into methane during the reaction. Additionally in situ FTIR of CNM_IS (Fig. S8) was carried out to observe if any differences are there compared to CNM_IC. The peaks observed at 1085, and 1781 cm−1 are assigned to carbonate species indicating initial CO2 adsorption on basic and defect sites.48 The prominent peaks at 1636 and 1324 cm−1 correspond to asymmetric and symmetric stretching modes of formate species, confirming that the formate pathway is the dominant reaction route.47 The peak at 2190 cm−1 is assigned to linearly bound carbonyl species indicating a parallel RWGS pathway.31 Along with this the vibrations observed at 2852, 2924, and 3018 cm−1 are attributed to CHx species representing hydrogenation products leading to methane formation. These results demonstrate that CO2 methanation proceeds via both formate and CO pathways, with formate species playing a key role in the reaction mechanism. However, compared to CNM_IC the intensity of formate and CHx related bands is relatively lower, indicating less efficient hydrogenation of intermediates. The delayed evolution of these species suggests that the CNM_IS catalyst has reduced activity for CO2 activation which can be attributed to its lower oxygen vacancy concentration and stronger MSI as evidenced by XPS and H2-TPR results. These findings demonstrate that the formation and transformation of reaction intermediates are strongly dependent on catalyst structure and defect characteristics.

Spent catalyst characterization

Fig. 9a presents the XRD pattern of the spent catalyst after the stability test. The diffraction peaks corresponding to metallic Ni were detected at 2θ = 51°, matching the standard JCPDS card no. 00-004-0850, confirming the reduction of NiO to Ni0 during the methanation reaction. The appearance of this phase suggests the successful activation of the catalyst under reaction conditions. XPS analysis of the spent CNM_IC catalyst was done to confirm any changes in surface oxidation states and chemical environments following extended CO2 methanation. The deconvoluted Co 2p spectrum (Fig. 9b) shows the presence of metallic cobalt with distinct peaks around 777.42 eV and 793.85 eV. This indicates partial reduction of cobalt species in a hydrogen rich methanation setting. Additionally, Co2+ species were detected at binding energies of 781.87 eV (Co 2p3/2) and 797.65 eV (Co 2p1/2). No signals for Co3+ were found. This suggests that cobalt mainly exists in a mix of metallic and ionic forms after the reaction. The satellite features at about 787.4 eV and 803.43 eV, related to Co 2p3/2 and Co 2p1/2, confirm the presence of Co2+ in an oxide coordination environment.31 The presence of both Co0 and Co2+ indicates in situ reduction of substituted cobalt species during methanation, confirming their active participation in the reaction. However, the partial oxidation of Co and co-existence of oxidized species may lead to a slight decrease in the number of fully active metallic sites which contribute to the slight decline in CO2 conversion observed after prolonged reaction. This suggests a dynamic redox behavior of cobalt species where partial reoxidation under reaction conditions slightly affects catalytic performance while still maintaining overall activity. This is in line with hydrogen spillover-assisted interactions between metal and support observed in Co–NiO–MgO systems. The O 1s XPS spectrum of the used catalyst (Fig. 9c) was divided into four components: OL at 529.62 eV, OV at 531.42 eV, OC at 532.92 eV, and OH linked to adsorbed H2O at 534.12 eV.30 The binding energies of OL and OC did not change much compared to the fresh CNM_IC catalyst which indicates that the bulk oxide structure and surface carbonate species were preserved after the reaction. In contrast, the OV component showed a noticeable shift likely due to changes in oxygen vacancy concentration, MSI, and surface reconstruction under methanation conditions. Although the OV/OL ratio of CNM_IC decreases after reaction (from 3.83 in the fresh catalyst to 3.32), it remains significantly higher than that of the other catalysts, indicating that a substantial fraction of oxygen vacancies is retained under reaction conditions. This resulted in a slight decrease in the CO2 conversion after 25 h. This suggests that the defect sites are dynamically involved in the reaction but are not completely lost, thereby continuously facilitating CO2 adsorption and activation. The Mg 1s XPS spectrum of the used catalyst (Fig. 9d) reveals a Mg(OH)2 component (1302.13 eV) along with the MgO peak at 1303.32 eV indicating hydration of MgO to Mg(OH)2 (around 30%) during the reaction as water is a byproduct which causes its hydration.38 This reduction is probably facilitated by hydrogen spillover from reduced Ni and Co sites reflecting dynamic redox behavior at the metal–support interface. The partial reduction of MgO may also influence the electronic properties of nearby metal sites which could affect catalytic stability and adsorption. In the Ni 2p region (Fig. 9e), although XRD analysis clearly shows the formation of metallic Ni after the reaction, metallic Ni0 was not distinctly detected in XPS may be due to surface reoxidation. Nevertheless, a noticeable decrease in the Ni2+ fraction indicates the in situ reduction of NiO to Ni0 during CO2 methanation confirming the participation of Ni species in the reaction.49 The Ni2+ 2p3/2 and Ni3+ 2p3/2 peaks present at 854.77 eV and 855.95 eV respectively. The corresponding Ni2+ 2p1/2 and Ni3+ 2p1/2 peaks were at 872.36 eV and 874.14 eV. A slight shift in binding energy compared to the fresh CNM_IC catalyst suggests electronic redistribution arising from moderate MSI and cobalt incorporation. This electronic modification enhances the stability of active sites by strengthening interfacial bonding thereby suppressing sintering and maintaining metal dispersion during reaction. Additionally, the co-existence of reduced Ni and stable oxygen vacancies enables efficient hydrogen activation and CO2 adsorption which contributes to sustained catalytic performance and high methane selectivity over a prolonged operational time.

The TEM analysis after the stability studies shows agglomeration in the catalyst showing more dense morphology than in fresh catalyst (Fig. 10a). From HRTEM analysis, the d-spacing of 0.206, 0.196, and 0.167 nm represents the MgO (200), NiO (200), and Ni (200) plane respectively (Fig. 10b). The SAED pattern (Fig. 9d) showed the (111), (200), (220), and (222) planes with a d-spacing value of 0.242, 0.210, 0.148, and 0.122 nm respectively which are correlated to NiO and MgO planes.30 From the Raman spectra of the spent catalyst (Fig. S7) a red shift was observed compared to the fresh catalyst (CNM_IC) and a new peak at 815 cm−1 was observed which can be due to lattice distortion and defect formation during stability studies. Based on above observations the XPS, XRD and TEM results support the formation of metallic Co and Ni during methanation likely due to the reduction of Co2+ and NiO sites. The partial loss of oxidized active phases aligns with the gradual decline in CO2 conversion after roughly 25 h of operation. However, since the drop in performance is modest, the catalyst retains a significant portion of its active sites which highlights its structural resilience and reusability. This redox reversibility and stability at the metal–support interface are highly beneficial for long term operation and scaling up CO2 methanation catalysts.

TPO was used to study carbon buildup and the oxidation behavior of the spent catalyst after CO2 methanation. As shown in Fig. 9f, the TPO profile shows a single oxidation peak within the temperature range of 200 to 400 °C. This peak indicates the presence of reactive and weakly bound carbon. The lack of high temperature oxidation peaks above 500 °C shows that no graphitic or filamentous carbon formed during the reaction. This suggests minimal coke-related deactivation and good resistance of the catalyst to carbon buildup.50 The amount of carbon deposited on the surface of catalyst was minimal with a value of 6 µgC g−1 h−1. Based on this the catalyst can be considered as a coke resistant material. In addition, particle size distribution analysis revealed only a slight increase in average particle size from 7.54 nm for the fresh catalyst to 7.98 nm (Fig. S4) for the spent catalyst, indicating minimal agglomeration and good resistance to sintering. These results demonstrate that the catalyst maintains structural integrity during the reaction, and any minor deactivation is likely associated with surface reconstruction or changes in metal–support interactions rather than coke formation or particle growth.

Conclusion

The Co/NiO–MgO catalyst system exhibited excellent performance in CO2 methanation, with the performance strongly dependent on the synthesis route and metal dispersion. Among all the prepared catalysts, the ion-substituted CNM_IC sample showed the highest CO2 conversion of 72% and CH4 selectivity 96% with a STYCH4 of 74 mmol g−1 h−1, establishing it as the most efficient composition. The performance is mainly attributed to the ionic substitution strategy, which increases the concentration of oxygen vacancies and medium-strength basic sites (58.31 µmol g−1), thereby promoting efficient CO2 adsorption and activation. The incorporation of Co into the NiO–MgO matrix improved metal dispersion, reducibility, and hydrogen activation, as confirmed by BET and H2-TPR analyses. XRD results demonstrated good phase stability, while TEM images indicated a well-preserved morphology with minimal sintering after reaction, highlighting its structural robustness. In situ FTIR studies further revealed that CNM_IC followed both the CO and formate pathways, suggesting a dual mechanistic route contributing to enhanced methanation efficiency. The synergistic interaction between Co and Ni, combined with the basic nature of MgO and the uniform Co distribution achieved through the catalyst, collectively enhanced the catalytic performance. Thus, CNM_IC emerged as a highly active, selective, and stable catalyst for efficient CO2 methanation.
