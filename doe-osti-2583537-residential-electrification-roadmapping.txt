Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

This work is to be conducted in support of the American Made Challenges EAS-E Prize. The
intent is to connect competitor teams with national laboratories that can help accelerate the
development of innovative solutions and products. National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(NREL) and Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) will help accelerate QuitCarbon’s
automated home electrification planning model development.

Summary of Research Results:

This project aims to evaluate and enhance QuitCarbon’s automated electrification planning tool,
ensuring its accuracy, compliance, and effectiveness in supporting home electrification efforts.
By carrying out the planned tasks, we aim to help QuitCarbon refine its technical foundation and
enhance its impact on residential electrification.

Task 1: Evaluate QuitCarbon’s Approach to Automated Electrification Planning

 1.1 NREL will review QuitCarbon’s approach to its automated electrification planning
 process, review the modeling platform to assess its algorithms, inputs and outputs, and
 underlying assumptions.

 1.2 NREL and QuitCarbon will collaborate closely to examine the practical use cases of
 QuitCarbon’s tool. NREL will assess how well the tool performs in various use cases and
 identify any issues with energy use and cost-saving predictions.

 1.3 NREL will also review the engineering assumptions and shortcuts employed by
 QuitCarbon to determine if they are suitable for the intended use cases. NREL will help
 create a clear path forward, enabling QuitCarbon to make well-informed decisions
 regarding worthwhile investments.

The work completed focused on identifying ways to improve QuitCarbon’s automated
electrification planner tool. It is centered on enhancing the tool’s ability to provide reliable
recommendations. One major improvement involved integrating NREL’s ResStock public
datasets, which contain detailed energy savings profiles for various energy efficiency and
electrification measures. These datasets allow users to estimate energy use and savings for
different home upgrades by analyzing timeseries data for heating, cooling, water heating, and
other end uses. By incorporating ResStock data, QuitCarbon’s tool can generate more accurate
recommendations even when specific details about a home are unknown.

To use this data effectively, users need to gather basic information about their home, such as its
location, age, size, number of stories, and heating, ventilating, and air conditioning (HVAC)
system. They can then filter the ResStock dataset to find similar homes and analyze their energy
profiles. Advanced users can take advantage of big data tools like AWS Athena to efficiently
query large datasets. NREL has also provided tutorial videos and technical documentation to
guide users through the process of accessing and using these datasets (NREL, 2025).

 2

In addition to ResStock, the discussions explored ways to incorporate electric vehicle (EV)
chargers, home batteries, and photovoltaic (PV) systems into the tool. Since EV charging loads
are not currently included in ResStock, a simple way to estimate EV energy use is to ask
homeowners about their charging habits. Home batteries, though not yet modeled in ResStock,
could be integrated in future versions to assess their impact on shifting peak energy use and
lowering utility bills. PV systems are already included in ResStock, but other tools like PVWatts
can help model solar energy production more accurately (NREL, 2025). The ability to analyze
hourly energy use data will also make it possible to evaluate time-of-use electricity pricing and
how different technologies affect energy costs.

Another key recommendation was to integrate OpenEEmeter (OpenEE Inc., 2025), an open-
source toolkit that improves energy use predictions by analyzing the relationship between home
energy consumption and weather conditions. OpenEEmeter allows for better energy
disaggregation, helping to pinpoint specific areas of energy use and improve efficiency
recommendations. By comparing historical energy usage with simulated data based on typical
weather conditions, the tool can refine predictions and make more reliable energy savings
estimates.

Finally, improvements were suggested for the tool’s default assumptions about occupancy,
lighting, and appliances. When homeowners do not provide specific details, the tool could rely
on standardized calculations based on the number of bedrooms and floor area. Adopting national
standards, such as ANSI/RESNET/ICC 301 (ICC, 2025), would lead to more accurate energy use
estimates.

Task 2: Evaluate QuitCarbon’s Model for BPI 2400 Compliance

 2.1 NREL will review QuitCarbon’s model for compliance with BPI 2400 to ensure it is
 ready to facilitate IRA rebates. BPI 2400 specifies a process for calibrating a model to the
 actual energy use of a home using approved building energy simulation software tools and
 actual energy bills. NREL will evaluate whether QuitCarbon's tool passes RESNET
 Publication 001 tests, a crucial requirement for achieving BPI 2400 compliance. NREL will
 offer guidance on how to incorporate BPI 2400 into QuitCarbon's tool.

The work completed includes evaluating compliance with BPI-2400 for home energy modeling
and conducting a gap analysis between QuitCarbon’s tool and the DOE’s Home Energy Score
(HEScore), as specifically requested by QuitCarbon (U.S. Department of Energy, 2021).

For BPI-2400 compliance, the U.S. Department of Energy and NREL developed a test procedure
to ensure home energy modeling tools accurately calculate energy savings for IRA rebates.
QuitCarbon currently uses an inverse modeling approach based on statistical regression, which
does not meet the requirements for the modeled performance rebate pathway. To comply,
QuitCarbon would need to transition to a first-principles modeling approach and implement
features such as software calibration, input constraints, and weather normalization. These
capabilities could be built in-house or integrated from existing open-source tools like
OpenStudio-HPXML, but additional development would be necessary (Horowitz, et al., 2024).

 3

As requested by QuitCarbon, we conducted a detailed gap analysis between their tool and
HEScore. The analysis compared input and output data requirements and identified key
differences. HEScore focuses on an asset rating based on standardized assumptions, while
QuitCarbon incorporates real utility bills and occupant-specific factors. The comparison showed
that HEScore requires more detailed construction and efficiency data that QuitCarbon does not
currently collect. Additionally, aligning with HEScore would require adjustments to
QuitCarbon’s training, QA processes, and an approval process to use the HEScore API.

Task 3: Draft and Publish News Post about Task 1 and 2

Task 4: Evaluate QuitCarbon’s Customer Communication Plan

 4.1 PNNL and QuitCarbon will review QuitCarbon’s approach to its customer outreach and
 communication plan. PNNL will use data-driven results from recent research to help inform
 options for messaging.

PNNL staff met with Quit Carbon staff on numerous occasions to provide feedback on marketing
and outreach materials developed by Quit Carbon. PNNL used outcomes from their recent
survey work to outline and frame feedback, intended to boost Quit Carbon’s messaging.
Additionally, PNNL provided feedback on a series of interviews Quit Carbon conducted with
past customers. PNNL reviewed and provided feedback on interview questions, and
interpretation of interview results. Quit Carbon refined their messaging based on these reviews.
