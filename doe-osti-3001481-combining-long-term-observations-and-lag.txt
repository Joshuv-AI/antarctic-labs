Executive Summary

Marine cold-air outbreaks (MCAOs) in the Arctic host cloud and precipitation processes that
also shape sign and magnitude of the wider extratropical cloud feedback, which is considered
highly uncertain and contributed to climate sensitivity in recent Earth System Models (ESMs).
Better understanding about how these processes shape clouds and their radiative effects is key
to narrowing climate projections. ESMs currently exhibit strong radiative biases where MCAOs
are present – most prominently over the Southern Ocean – creating an urgent need to better
understand ESM deficiencies. As observations of MCAOs are generally rare, DOE ARM
measurements provide a particularly important constraint for modeling activities.

This project addresses uncertain MCAO cloud-aerosol-precipitation interaction via DOE
measurements:

 (1) We filtered measurement during the ARM Cold-Air Outbreaks in the Marine Boundary
 Layer Experiment (COMBLE) campaign for synoptic situations that permit quasi-
 Lagrangian (i.e., air mass following) simulations, where an upwind site (here Mt.
 Zeppelin on Svalbard) provides initial conditions. ARM aerosol observations were
 analyzed to provide case studies for precipitation processes over all shallow cloud
 conditions at the Andenes site.
 (2) Building on aerosol and meteorological initial and boundary conditions, we explore
 forcings that lead to satisfactory representation of these clouds when using large-eddy
 simulations (LES) that serve as benchmarks when comparing against ESM single-
 column model (SCM) simulations.
 (3) Building on the strongest MCAO observed during COMBLE, we conduct a model-
 observation intercomparison project (MIP), where we ask an international group of LES
 and SCM modelers to perform multiple tiers of simulations: (a) fixed droplet and ice
 crystal number concentrations under liquid-only and mixed-phase conditions, (b)
 interactive aerosol to prognostically treat droplet number while fixing ice crystal numbers,
 and (c) same as (b), except fully prognostically treat droplet and ice crystal number
 concentrations.

Together these efforts enable a robust assessment of SCM strength and weaknesses, when
comparing against a suite of LES models. By providing MCAO observational constraints, ARM
plays a key role in supporting model assessment and future development.

Importance and Relevance

Marine cold-air outbreak (MCAO) events are accompanied by unique microphysical processes
and boundary layer cloud characteristics that climate and numerical weather prediction models
struggle to accurately capture [Abel et al., 2017; Field et al., 2014]. Such model limitations may
partially arise from an insufficient representation of aerosol particles, which serve as ice and
cloud condensation nuclei (IN and CCN) during cloud formation. For example, Tornow et al.
[2021] found that mid-latitude MCAO cloud transitions which resembled observations could only
be simulated when the aerosol number concentration was allowed to evolve with time. Previous
modeling of cold-air outbreaks has typically relied on sparse observational constraints [Abel et
al., 2017; Field et al., 2014; Tornow et al., 2021], illustrating a need for observations of aerosol
properties associated with CAO events in order to inform, constrain, and improve model
simulations and forecasts.

Hypotheses Tested

This project tested the following original hypotheses:

 1) We expected that the high winds from over the open ocean that are associated with
 Marine Cold Air Outbreaks (MCAOs) would be associated with statistically significant
 differences in the aerosol size distributions with a lower Hoppel minimum diameter and
 increased sea spray mode aerosol concentrations.
 2) We expected that the deepening marine boundary layer and the progressive buildup of
 frozen condensate during MCAOs would result in nearly constant liquid water paths
 throughout the cloud regime transition.
 3) We expected that MCAO conditions would be insensitive to short-wave radiative transfer
 and large-scale subsidence.

The findings related to these hypotheses are discussed below.

Methods Used

This project combined the expertise of the Russell and Fridlind groups to prepare initial
conditions for quasi-Lagrangian simulations, compile observational targets from ARM and
satellite, and conduct a MIP that attracted eight LES and six SCM groups.

 1) First, we filtered MCAO conditions [Geerts et al., 2022] and ARM measurements periods
 that showed most instruments being operating. Second, we generated back-trajectories
 to identify periods that connected an upwind site, Mt. Zeppelin station on Svalbard, with
 the downwind ARM site. Next we completed extensive analyses of measurements taken
 during COMBLE in addition to measurements recorded at an upwind site in Svalbard.
 Aerosol size distributions were fitted with three lognormal modes (Aitken, accumulation,
 and sea spray) and the composition of each mode was inferred through available
 COMBLE HTDMA measurements and upwind ion mass measurements. Differences in
 aerosol properties during CAO events and ambient conditions were identified, such as
 lower Aitken number concentration, higher sea spray number concentration, and more

 hygroscopic particles corresponding to CAO events. Additionally, the development of
 specifications for ice-nucleating aerosol began in collaboration with Paul Demott and
 Daniel Knopf. The analyses stated here provided a comprehensive evaluation of
 observational aerosol and culminated in a dataset of aerosol specifications to initialize
 model simulations. Williams completed a manuscript that characterizes aerosol number
 concentration and composition during CAOs, resulting from the completed analyses on
 in-situ measurements. Following this work, the UCSD team evaluated aerosol output
 from preliminary LES simulations against the completed observational aerosol analysis,
 which suggests a depletion of accumulation mode aerosol along a CAO trajectory.
 2) The Fridlind group collected ARM data within a window of time of the air mass arrival.,
 corresponding to the final MCAO stage. ARM data includes active (i.e., Ka-band radar,
 micropulse lidar), passive (i.e., microwave radiometer, radiance interferometer), and in-
 situ (i.e., radiosondes, ground-based aerosol and precipitation probes, eddy correlation
 flux probes) instruments. These instruments help characterize cloud macrophysical
 properties (i.e., cloud-top height and temperature, cloud optical depth, liquid water path
 [LWP], surface precipitation rate), atmospheric vertical structure of temperature and
 moisture, and turbulent surface fluxes. Satellite retrievals allow to fill in properties farther
 upwind. Instantaneous MAC-LWP [Elsaesser et al., 2017] retrievals provide high-
 frequency LWP values along the trajectory and constitute a key observational target
 during the early cloud evolution. Similarly, we collocated CERES top-of-atmosphere
 short- and longwave fluxes from various satellite platforms (i.e., Aqua, Terra, Suomi-
 NPP, JPSS). A CALIPSO overpassed the trajectory shortly after cloud formation,
 providing cloud-top height and temperature, optical depth, and ice water path. More
 conventional imager-based retrievals relying on radiances in the visible spectrum were
 generally unavailable as high solar zenith angles hindered retrievals. However, VIIRS
 imagery allowed to obtain cloud cell size information – the group devised a watershed
 clustering algorithm that delivers repeatable (i.e., non-stochastic) and flexible (i.e., free
 from cell shape assumptions) clustering results.
 3) The Fridlind group performed sensitivity experiments that ascertained that benchmark
 LES simulations match ARM and satellite observational targets. First, simulations
 explored the importance of interactive radiative transfer, large-scale vertical motion, and
 geostrophic wind forcing. Second, adjustments to initial vertical wind, temperature, and
 humidity profiles explored opportunities to improve early cloud street formation in LES.
 To enable simulations using prognostic one-moment aerosol under ranging wind
 conditions, a sea-spray parameterization [Jaegle et al., 2011] was prepared that allowed
 using fixed aerosol modal properties (i.e., geometric mean and standard deviation) of
 three log-normal modes.

Findings

The research supported by this project resulted in the following papers using the COMBLE data
sets. Each addresses the original three objectives, quantifying the role of particles in the Arctic
region and differentiating the aerosol properties associated with different back-trajectories.

i. Aerosol size distribution properties for quasi-Lagrangian simulations of MCAOs [Williams
et al., 2024]: We identified five different MCAO cases that span a range of cloud-top
temperatures and cloud morphological transitions. In this work, we retrieve aerosol size
distribution modes from measurements at Andenes, Norway, during the Cold-Air Outbreaks in

the Marine Boundary Layer Experiment (COMBLE) and at Zeppelin Observatory, approximately
1000 km upwind in Svalbard. During CAO events at Andenes, the sea spray mode number
concentration is correlated to strong over-ocean winds with a mean of 8±4 cm-3 that is 71%
higher than during non-CAO conditions. Additionally during CAO events at Andenes, the mean
Hoppel minimum diameter is 6 nm smaller than during non-CAO conditions though the
estimated supersaturation is lower and the number concentration of particles that likely
activated in-cloud is 109±61 cm-3 (similar to non-CAO conditions). For CAO trajectories
between Zeppelin Observatory and Andenes, the upwind-to-downwind change in number
concentration is largest for the accumulation mode with a mean decrease of 93±95 cm-3, likely
attributable primarily to precipitation scavenging. These characteristic properties of aerosol size
distributions during CAO events provide guidance for evaluating CAO aerosol-cloud interaction
processes in models.
ii. Compilation of ARM and satellite measurements [Juliano et al., 2023]: We successfully
collocated ARM and satellite products to each of the five trajectories and shared these products
with other modeling groups. As intended, key ARM products were available throughout.
Relatively frequent CERES flux and MAC-LWP satellite products informed the MCAO evolution.
For the MCAO case of primary interest, we found relatively steady LWP values throughout the
cloud regime transition, despite the deepening MBL, indicating progressive buildup of frozen
condensate with cloud-top temperatures reaching homogeneous freezing levels towards the
final stage.
iii. Exploring forcings and conducting the MIP [Juliano et al., in preparation]: The forcing
exploration revealed that the strong MCAO conditions were insensitive to short-wave radiative
transfer and large-scale subsidence, therefore leaving out these aspects in the interest of
simplicity. Geostrophic winds forced the simulations as plausibly as nudged horizontal winds,
rendering the former the preferred choice for modeling. Simulation of early cloud streets proved
challenging, with sporadic success but no clear indication of which setup reliably produces
streets. MIP participants simulated the first tier of simulations, with remaining ones to be
performed in the coming weeks. Comparing liquid-only versus mixed-phase LES and SCM
under fixed hydrometeor concentrations, we found that LES produced a more distinct response
in cloud macrophysical properties, such as cloud cover and cloud optical depth, which resulted
in a stronger TOA longwave flux response, compared to SCMs. Simulations of the next tiers will
provide useful information to better understand models’ precipitation and its impact on the cloud
morphological evolution. Planned large-domain simulations (~100 km2) will provide sufficient
space to host several late-stage cells.

Publications from this Project

The PhD thesis of Abigail Williams is expected to be completed in June 2026 and was
supported by this project as part of her publication on COMBLE [Williams et al., 2024] (OSTI ID
3001200). Contributions to these publications by PhD candidate Jeramy Dedrick were also
supported. Russell also contributed to four other collaborative papers with support from this
project that supported interpretation of Eastern North Atlantic (ENA) and North Slope of Alaska
(NSA) measurements [Dasarathy et al., 2023; Feingold et al., 2024; Miller et al., 2022; Russell
et al., 2023]. All of the ARM and other measurements compiled for simulations of the MCAO
case studies were posted at the MIP website [Juliano et al., 2023]. LES and SCM comparison
results are now being prepared for submission to GMD [Juliano et al., in preparation].
