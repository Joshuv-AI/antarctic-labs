Subtle visuo-cognitive deficits as a potential early marker of cognitive decline
Authors: Franziska Kiene, Antonia Becker, Lisa Betten, Mandy Roheger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with subjective cognitive decline (SCD) report declining cognitive abilities but do not have an objective cognitive impairment at present. Research indicates that they are at an elevated risk of developing Alzheimer's disease (AD). Identifying or developing cognitive measures capable of detecting cognitive deficits that individuals with SCD may experience is crucial, as they may benefit from additional diagnostic procedures and interventions aimed at delaying further cognitive decline.ObjectiveTo investigate whether specific cognitive tasks selected for their relevance in detecting early changes possibly related to AD can predict group membership SCD and Non-SCD.MethodsNeuropsychological assessment data of 73 older adults (35 SCD, 38 Non-SCD) was analyzed. Logistic regression analyses, classification metrics, and receiver operating characteristic curves were used to evaluate the predictive power of a model including a verbal fluency, word list learning, and constructional praxis test.ResultsIndividuals with SCD were older and reported higher levels of depression. The logistic regression model demonstrated limited sensitivity (0.54) but high specificity (0.87). Constructional Praxis (visuo-spatial- and constructional abilities) emerged as a significant predictor (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.26–0.86, p < 0.05), even in an additional model controlling for affective symptoms. Better performance in this task was associated with lower odds of belonging to the SCD group.ConclusionsExisting cognitive assessments appear to lack sensitivity in the context of SCD. Visuo-cognitive functioning may represent a promising domain for the development of more sensitive diagnostic tools to detect possible cognitive alterations that individuals with SCD may experience.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, mild cognitive impairment, neuropsychological testing, subjective cognitive decline, visuo-cognition

Introduction

Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) describes the subjectively perceived persistent decline of cognitive abilities, compared to a former personal cognitive status. 1 Although individuals with SCD do not (yet) show an impairment in standard cognitive tests used to classify, e.g., mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or prodromal Alzheimer's disease (AD) (considering age-, gender-, and education), 1 epidemiological data is indicating that they are at a higher risk for developing MCI and dementia in the future. SCD might reflect a preclinical stage of the AD continuum in some individuals. 2 A multicenter study comprising 2978 individuals with SCD and 1391 controls, found a dementia incidence rate in participants with SCD of 17.7 (AD: 11.5, other dementia: 6.1) in contrast to 14.2 in controls (AD: 10.1, other dementia: 4.1) per 1000 person-years. 3

Röhr et al. (2020) estimated the prevalence of SCD across international cohort studies of aging. SCD prevalence was around 25% and higher in men than women, in countries with lower and middle income, in Asian and Black African people compared to White people, and in lower education levels (N = 39,387 cognitively unimpaired adults > 60 years). 4 The authors are also highlighting the lack of a standardized SCD assessment, leading to a high level of variability concerning prevalence estimates between studies. 4

In research, SCD is typically assessed using brief yes/no questions, interviews or self-report questionnaires. An overview by Rabin et al. (2015) highlights the substantial heterogeneity among existing SCD instruments, which makes it difficult to compare research findings and transfer results into clinical practice. 5 These tools differ in key aspects, including item content (e.g., cognitive domains assed), number of items, response formats, whether they inquire about current impairment or decline over time and whether they focus on symptom severity or frequency. 5 Notably, there is currently no consensus regarding a method for measuring SCD, including the cognitive domains considered most relevant. As a result, the selection of an SCD instrument is often guided by practical considerations, such as brevity, ease of administration and availability. 5 Most SCD measures focus predominately on memory complaints, with some even assessing memory exclusively. Yet, relying solely on conventional memory items might not be the best choice, as other cognitive domains may show a better discriminability in early stages of cognitive decline. 5 For instance, difficulties in navigating in familiar environments, which can be categorized as spatial orientation, have been shown to be more strongly associated with the risk of cognitive impairment than complaints such as struggling to hold onto thoughts.5–7

By definition, individuals with SCD perform within the range of what is considered cognitively unimpaired in standard neuropsychological tests often used in routine clinical practice. This makes examination of people presenting with symptoms of SCD in clinical practice particularly challenging, even though it may be of great clinical relevance for individuals affected. In this context, individuals with SCD often report that their symptoms are not sufficiently acknowledged by healthcare professionals. 8

Previous research indicates that the subtle cognitive decline individuals with SCD are experiencing might be visible at a group level, when comparing individuals with and without SCD. 9 In the present study, we aimed to examine whether such potential cognitive differences between older adults with and without SCD can be detected using specific standardized cognitive tests.

The current literature remains inconclusive as to whether and which neuropsychological tests, and which cognitive domains are most effective in capturing such potential differences in cognitive performance. A recent systematic review by Zhou et al. (2025) found mixed results for the association between SCD and objective cognitive performance in older adults. 10 Results indicate that overall SCD, compared to Non-SCD, was associated with poorer objective cognitive performance on measures of global cognition and memory in longitudinal studies. However, this association was not consistently observed in cross-sectional studies. 10 Moreover, results show that the objective cognitive measurements used as well as the cognitive domains assessed were highly heterogenous. 10 For example, in the study of Wolfsgruber et al. (2020), a significantly reduced overall cognitive performance (− 0.3 SD) in individuals with SCD was found. Yet, their performance remained within the cognitively unimpaired range. 11

Existing literature points to the usefulness of specific cognitive domains and tasks for detecting subtle cognitive decline. In the latter study, the strongest deficits in SCD participants compared to those without were found in the learning and memory domain score, which included amongst others a word list learning task. 11

In another study, Vonk et al. (2020) analyzed data of older adults with follow-up diagnoses of No-MCI, MCI and dementia. They found a decline of semantic fluency, which was fastest in individuals with a higher risk for AD. Letter fluency did not decline except for participants with a dementia diagnosis. Lower baseline semantic fluency was associated with neurodegeneration, which was not the case for baseline letter fluency. 12 Therefore, it might be useful to include semantic category fluency tests when cognitively assessing individuals with SCD.

An et al. (2024) found that visuo-cognitive functioning (measured with a block design test) differentiated only between participants with a Cognitive Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 and 1 (and not between CDR 1 and 2). The authors suggested that the task's difficulty may have led to a floor effect in later stages of cognitive decline. 13 Tests for visuo-cognition, which is also a key aspect of spatial orientation, might be sensitive in an early stage of cognitive decline and therefore useful to include in a cognitive SCD assessment.

Identifying cognitive tests or developing a neuropsychological test battery able to detect subtle cognitive deficits that some of the individuals with SCD may exhibit is essential for identifying those who may benefit from further diagnostic procedures (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid analysis, neuroimaging). Early identification of older adults with SCD, who are at increased risk for future objective cognitive decline, is important for initiating timely therapeutic interventions aimed at slowing the progression of cognitive decline.

Methods

In the present study, we conducted a logistic regression analysis to predict group membership for older adults with SCD and older adults without SCD. As predictors, we included subtests from the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease, neuropsychological assessment battery (CERAD-NAB)14–16 that assess different cognitive domains, selected for their relevance in detecting early cognitive changes possibly related to AD, as explained above: Word List Memory/Learning total (remembered words of Word List Learning trial 1–3) as a measurement for learning and memory, Verbal Fluency (naming as many words of a certain category as possible in one minute) to assess semantic fluency and Constructional Praxis (copying four figure drawings of increasing complexity) to assess visuo-cognition. 17 We expected that individuals without SCD would perform better on the neuropsychological tests used in the analysis and that performance on these tests would predict group membership (Non-SCD versus SCD).

Study design

Data collection took place from April 2023 to May 2024 at the Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg. It was carried out in two steps: an initial telephone interview, followed by a neuropsychological assessment. Participants received a financial reimbursement of 12 euros. Flyer distribution and local newspaper advertisement were used for participant recruitment. Two flyers were used. Both flyers provided a brief explanation of what cognition and cognitive abilities are. One flyer invited individuals who perceive a subjective decline in their cognitive abilities and are concerned about this decline, while the second flyer invited those without. Individuals interested in participating in the study contacted the authors (AB, LB or FK) via telephone or email. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and all participants provided written informed consent. Ethical approval was granted by the ethics committee of the Carl von Ossietzky Universität Oldenburg.

Participants and neuropsychological assessment

Inclusion and exclusion criteria of the present study followed the SCD- 1 and SCD plus criteria of the Subjective Cognitive Decline Initiative (SCD-I) Working Group, 2 as explained below. These criteria aim to increase the likelihood that the subjective feeling of cognitive decline reflects preclinical AD. 2 Individuals aged 65 years or older were eligible for inclusion. SCD criteria consist of two core aspects: 1. Subjectively perceived, ongoing decline of cognitive abilities in comparison to the individual's earlier normal cognitive functioning, which cannot be explained by an acute event; 2. Performance within the range of what is considered cognitively unimpaired in standardized cognitive tests used to classify, e.g., MCI or prodromal AD. 1

To assess the first aspect, individuals were asked the following questions during the telephone interview: “Do you feel that your cognitive abilities (e.g., perception, attention, memory, language, learning, orientation, problem solving, planning or organization) are persistently declining, compared to a previous normal personal status?” If yes: “Can your self-perceived decline of cognitive abilities be attributed to an acute event (e.g., stroke, accident)?”. Individuals for whom it could not be ruled out that their SCD is related to an acute event, were not included. Participants who reported SCD were also asked whether they had sought professional help due to their self-perceived cognitive decline, 2 although not having sought such support was not considered an exclusion criterion.

The second SCD aspect was evaluated using the German Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), 18 a widely used standardized cognitive test. Individuals with a MMSE score of 26 or lower were excluded, in line with validation studies, suggesting a cut-off score of 27 = healthy. 19 We decided to use the MMSE, as it is still widely employed in clinical practice to detect cognitive impairment. 20 Röhr et al. (2020), who estimated the prevalence of SCD across 16 international cohort studies of aging, also relied on the MMSE to assess cognitive impairment, as this tool was administered in all studies except two, for which MMSE scores were derived from similar brief screening tools (Blessed Information Memory Concentration scores and Community Screening Interview for Dementia scores). 4

In addition, to meet the SCD criteria, SCD symptoms must not be attributable to a psychiatric or neurologic disease, further medical disorder, medication or substance use. 1 Potential participants reporting such confounding factors during the telephone interview were excluded prior to the neuropsychological assessment. To screen for affective symptoms, the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) 21 was administered. Participants with a score of 11 or higher on either the anxiety or depression subscale, indicative of probable presence (‘caseness’) of the disorder, 22 were excluded. The German version HADS-D was used 23 and proficiency in the German language was also required for study inclusion.

The same criteria were applied for the SCD and Non-SCD group, except that individuals of the Non-SCD group did not experience a persistent feeling of cognitive decline.

Participants were explicitly instructed to bring any hearing or vision aids they use to the testing session. The assessments lasted about 60 minutes and were conducted by authors AB, LB, or FK. The CERAD-NAB14–16 was conducted in the German Plus version.17,24,25 Normative data is based on 1100 healthy adults (age: 49–92 years, education: 7–20 years). 25 Participants also completed demographic and health-related questionnaires, comprising the EQ-5D-5L Paper Self-Complete for measuring health-related quality of life, with the subtest EQ VAS (visual analogue scale) 26 and the German self- and informant-report version of the Measurement of Everyday Cognition (ECog).27,28

A logistic regression analysis was conducted to predict group membership Non-SCD versus SCD. We included three neuropsychological tests as predictors selected for their relevance in detecting early cognitive changes that might be related to AD: Word List Memory/Learning total, Verbal Fluency, Constructional Praxis. Age-, sex- and education adjusted z-scores were used for the analysis. Variance inflation factor was analyzed to check for multicollinearity among predictors and the Box-Tidwell test was performed to assess linearity between predictors and the logit of the outcome variable. For better interpretation of the regression coefficients, odds ratios (OR) were calculated for each predictor. Nagelkerke R-squared value was calculated to assess the effect of the logistic regression model. To further analyze the discriminatory power of the model, a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the curve (AUC) was analyzed. According to Youden's J, an optimal probability threshold was determined (instead of the default probability threshold of 0.50). Participants for whom the model predicted a probability above the calculated threshold were classified as part of the SCD group. The model's accuracy, sensitivity and specificity were calculated for this threshold. Results of the logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 3. To account for potential confounding effects, an additional model was calculated with the combined sum score of the HADS anxiety- and depression subscale included as a covariate (Table 4). Significance level was set at <0.05 if not stated otherwise.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 95 participants were initially enrolled in the study. Prior to the neuropsychological assessment, two participants with SCD withdrew due to time constraints, one Non-SCD participant withdrew due to other personal reasons and another Non-SCD participant was excluded due to drug consumption in the past. Furthermore, seven participants in total (six with SCD) were excluded prior to the neuropsychological assessment due to other medical conditions: stroke, traumatic brain injury, borreliosis, atherosclerosis at time of SCD onset and post-traumatic stress disorder. Following the neuropsychological assessment, another seven participants (four with SCD) were excluded due to a MMSE score below 27 and four participants (three with SCD) were excluded due to a HADS score of 11 or higher. As a result, 73 participants were included in the final statistical analyses. The Non-SCD group consisted of 38 and the SCD group of 35 individuals. Characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1. Individuals with SCD were significantly older and reported more anxious and depressive symptoms than individuals without SCD. Moreover, participants with SCD reported a lower overall self-rated health status. There were no group differences regarding education and performance in the MMSE. After correcting for multiple comparisons, there were no significant group differences in self-rated health status and anxiety symptoms.

Individuals who reported SCD (n = 35), were asked whether they had sought professional help (e.g., from their general practitioner or a neurologist) due to the self-perceived cognitive decline, as medical help seeking within the context of SCD is associated with an increased risk of further cognitive decline. 2 13 individuals answered with yes and 20 individuals answered with no, however some explained that they did not yet have sought professional help (two missing answers). Results of the German self- and informant-report version of the ECog can be found in Supplemental Figures 1 and 2.

Predicting group membership Non-SCD versus SCD

Three CERAD-NAB subtests were used as predictors in the logistic regression analysis to predict group membership Non-SCD versus SCD. Results of Non-SCD and SCD participants in the three neuropsychological tests are presented in Table 2. Performance in neuropsychological tests not included in the analysis can be found in Supplemental Table 1. Individuals with SCD performed significantly worse in the constructional praxis test, compared to individuals without SCD. There were no significant group differences regarding test performance in the verbal fluency test or word list memory/learning test.

Results of the logistic regression analysis are presented in Table 3. Box-Tidwell test was calculated to check linearity between predictors and the logit of the outcome variable. Variance inflation factor indicated that there is no multicollinearity among predictors. The overall model including the three predictors was statistically significant (χ 2 (3) = 9.76, p < 0.05). However, only Constructional Praxis yielded a significant OR (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.26–0.86, p < 0.05), indicating that with increasing z-scores, which means a better test performance in the constructional praxis test, the odds of having SCD are decreasing. A Nagelkerke R-squared value of 0.17 is indicating a small effect of the overall model. ROC-analysis showed an AUC of 0.70, indicating acceptable discriminatory power of the logistic regression model. 29 Based on Youden's J, an optimal probability threshold of 0.55 was determined. Individuals for whom the model predicted a probability above this cut-off were classified as part of the SCD group. At this threshold, the model achieved a sensitivity of 0.54 and a specificity of 0.87, suggesting that it is more accurate in identifying individuals without SCD, while it misses a substantial proportion of individuals with SCD.

An additional model was calculated with the sum score of the HADS anxiety- and depression subscale included as a covariate to account for potential confounding effects (Table 4). This model, including the three predictors and the sum score of anxiety and depression, was statistically significant (χ2 (4) = 19.13, p < 0.001). In addition to Constructional Praxis, Anxiety/Depression was a significant predictor of group membership (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 1.07–1.41, p < 0.01), indicating that with increasing HADS scores, which means more symptoms of anxiety and depression, the odds of having SCD are increasing. The reported sensitivity, specificity and accuracy were calculated based on the optimal probability threshold of 0.40 according to Youden's J.

Discussion

The aim of the present study was to investigate whether subtle cognitive differences between older adults with and without SCD can be identified using selected subtests from the CERAD-NAB. Specifically, we examined the predictive power of a model consisting of three cognitive measures, Verbal Fluency, Word List Memory/Learning and Constructional Praxis, chosen for their relevance in detecting early cognitive changes associated with AD. Using logistic regression analysis, we sought to determine whether performance on these tasks could differentiate older adults with SCD from those without.

Data of n = 38 Non-SCD and n = 35 SCD participants was analyzed. Individuals with SCD were on average four years older than individuals without SCD. This finding is consistent with other studies reporting a significant association of older age and the presence of SCD. 30 Considering the growing proportion of older adults in the population, early identification of those at risk for a cognitive disorder and interventions that help older adults to maintain independence and continue living at home for as long as possible are crucial. There were no significant group differences regarding the MMSE. In the systematic review of Zhou et al. (2025), it was found that overall SCD compared to Non-SCD, was associated with poorer objective cognitive performance on measures of global cognition like the MMSE. However, this was mostly the case in longitudinal studies, where cognition was measured over time. 10 Since cognition was assessed at only one time point in this study, this may have contributed to the observation that no significant group differences in MMSE scores were observed. Yet, identifying subtle cognitive alterations compared to healthy cognitive aging as early as possible in single time point assessments, or at least in a short period of time, remains an important goal before significant cognitive decline emerges over time.

Individuals with a HADS anxiety or depression score of 11 or higher were excluded. Nevertheless, older adults with SCD reported significantly more anxious and depressive symptoms than older adults without SCD (after correcting for multiple comparisons, there were no significant group differences in anxiety symptoms). There are different possible explanations for this. SCD could be an early manifestation of a neurocognitive disorder and anxiety a psychological reaction to the self-perceived cognitive decline. The opposite direction might also be possible and SCD could be a symptom of anxiety. Also, SCD and anxiety could be independent predictors of neurocognitive disorders. 31 The study by Liew (2020) points to the latter explanation and suggests that SCD and anxiety are independently associated with the risk of MCI and dementia. Thereby, the risk is highest among individuals with co-occurring symptoms of SCD and anxiety. 31 The same might apply for depressive symptoms. Liew (2019) suggests that depression and SCD are independent risk factors for neurocognitive disorders, with the highest risk when depression and SCD co-occur. Still, further research is needed to investigate whether depression and SCD also involve separate neurobiological mechanisms underlying the progression to neurocognitive disorders. 32 When assessing individuals with SCD, the investigation of depressive and anxious symptoms is important. Depression and anxiety should not be solely dismissed as symptoms of SCD and vice versa. 31 The elevated levels of anxiety and depression once again underscore the importance of taking the symptoms of older adults with SCD seriously. This is essential for improving their quality of life, especially as in the present study participants with SCD also showed a lower overall self-rated health status (x̄ = 74.09, SD = 17.3) compared to participants without SCD (x̄ = 82.55, SD = 14.0), as assessed with the EQ VAS of the EQ-5D-5L for measuring health-related quality of life. 26 Although this group difference did not remain significant after corrections for multiple comparisons, previous studies have reported that older adults with SCD perceive lower physical, psychological, and environmental quality of life, 33 as measured with the World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment. 34 Physical health quality of life refers for instance to pain and discomfort; Psychological- to thinking, learning, concentration; and Environment quality of life amongst others to health- and social care. 34 Addressing the quality of life of older adults with SCD, for example by supporting coping strategies, may constitute an early, low-threshold intervention that can enhance the well-being of affected individuals and their families and may help to preserve autonomy and social participation.

The mean performance of both groups in the three CERAD-NAB subtests (Verbal Fluency, Word List Memory/Learning and Constructional Praxis) was within the range of what is considered cognitively unimpaired. There were no significant group differences in age-, sex- and education adjusted z-scores between the SCD and Non-SCD group, except for the constructional praxis test, where individuals with SCD performed significantly worse. This finding may indicate that subtle cognitive differences between groups are more pronounced in visuo-constructive abilities than in verbal fluency or verbal learning in this sample. The logistic regression model including the three subtests was statistically significant, yet, only Constructional Praxis emerged as a significant predictor. While better performance in this task was associated with lower odds of belonging to the SCD group, the overall classification performance of the model was only moderate. The model's low sensitivity indicates that it failed to detect a considerable number of individuals with SCD, limiting its usefulness for identifying this group. Taken together, these findings point to the limited discriminatory power of the selected cognitive measures for distinguishing between SCD and Non-SCD individuals in a cross-sectional design and show that cognitive differences, if present, are not captured comprehensively by these subtests alone.

Word List Memory/Learning total was not a significant predictor of group membership in the present study. This finding contrasts with previous research, where the most pronounced deficits in individuals with SCD compared to those without were observed in the learning and memory domain, assessed also using word list tasks. 11 Verbal Fluency also did not predict group membership in our analysis. While previous research suggested that self-perceived decline in word-finding abilities represents the most frequent symptom in older adults with SCD and is especially burdensome as it leads to great problems in conversations, 8 this was not reflected in the results of the verbal fluency test used in this study. It is important to note that although this test involves finding words within a specific semantic category, it also relies on other cognitive functions. Future studies should consider employing other word-finding tasks that better isolate this ability.

The only significant predictor in the present study, Constructional Praxis, assesses visuo-spatial and mainly visuo-constructional abilities, 14 which are part of the visuo-cognition domain. A possible explanation for this might be that copying figures is more difficult because it is a rather abstract task that individuals rarely encounter in everyday life, compared to, e.g., learning a word list. As such, it may reveal subtle cognitive decline, even when the overall performance remains relatively high.

Visuo-cognitive abilities can be divided into three categories: visuo-perceptual-, visuo-spatial- and visuo-constructive abilities. Whereas visuo-perceptual abilities comprise only basic visual processing, visuo-spatial abilities, which include the ability to process spatial information, as well as visuo-constructive abilities, which refer to constructing a stimuli, demand further cognitive resources like attention, memory or executive functions. 35

Visuo-constructive tasks such as the Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure Test (ROCF) 36 were evaluated regarding predicting the progression of cognitive decline in previous studies. 37 Rivera-Fernández et al. (2021) analyzed neuropsychological profiles in individuals with MCI, SCD and without SCD. They found significant group differences in the visuo-cognition domain, assessed with the ROCF, between participants with MCI and SCD and between participants with MCI and participants without SCD. 38 Tokaj and Lehrner (2024) analyzed the ability of The Vienna Visuoconstructional Test 3.0 Screening (VVT 3.0 Screening) to discriminate between participants with AD, MCI, SCD and healthy controls (HC). The test was able to discriminate between AD and all other participants with a sensitivity of 62.1% and a specificity of 83.1%. It was outperformed by the MMSE with a sensitivity of 94.5% and a specificity of 88.8% (cut-off 25.5). 39 It was suggested that the MMSE was better in discriminating AD from all other participants due to its multidomain approach. Yet, the ability of both tests to discriminate between the other groups was limited. The authors named ceiling effects as a possible reason for this, as HC, SCD and MCI participants achieved on average above 9 out of 10 possible points in the VVT 3.0 Screening, whereas AD participants only achieved 6.90 (±2.76) points on average. 39 The pattern of test performance suggested that decline in visuo-constructional abilities may not follow a linear trajectory across prodromal stages. As a result, HC, SCD, and MCI participants achieved similarly high scores. This could be addressed by the implementation of alternative scoring criteria, more sensitive to minor errors. 39

Beyond that, impairments in spatial navigation (visuo-spatial abilities) have been described in prodromal phases of cognitive decline. 37 Boccia et al. (2019) stated that tasks assessing spatial navigation may offer potential neuropsychological predictors of neurodegenerative decline, as they reveal subtle differences between healthy and pathological aging. 40 This is what Marquardt et al. (2024) analyzed in their study. They applied a wayfinding paradigm in a naturalistic context and found that the number of orientation stops was able to predict SCD group membership in older adults. 41 In this context, in their meta-analysis with longitudinal studies, Belleville et al. (2017) investigated the predictive power of cognitive measures concerning the progression from MCI to AD. Classification metrics of visuo-spatial tasks (pooled sensitivity: 0.68, 95% CI: 0.366–0.914; pooled specificity: 0.749, 95% CI: 0.613–0.873) were comparable to those of visuo-constructive tasks (pooled sensitivity: 0.637, 95% CI: 0.471–0.778; pooled specificity: 0.643, 95% CI: 0.496–0.775). 42

Several studies have identified the discriminative power of visuo-cognitive tasks in very early stages of cognitive decline, supported also by neurobiological findings. Functional connectivity alterations in networks comprising fronto-parietal areas were observed in individuals with SCD, potentially explaining their lower performance in visuo-spatial tasks. 43 Parra et al. (2024) found that cognitively unimpaired older adults with a poorer performance in the Visual Short-Term Memory Binding Test (VSTMBT) in comparison to cognitively unimpaired older adults with an intact performance in the VSTMBT, presented an increased amyloid-β accumulation in brain regions related to visual integration and regions of the right ventral stream, which are related to visuo-cognition and are early affected in AD. 44 Ohnishi et al. (2025) found a strong association between visuo-spatial cognitive impairment and a reduced myelin integrity in the right middle temporal gyrus and right precuneus in AD patients. This may reflect the neural correlates of visuo-spatial processing in AD. 45

An additional logistic regression model was calculated, adding the sum score of the HADS anxiety- and depression subscale to account for potential confounding effects. Constructional Praxis remained a significant predictor of group membership. The anxiety and depression score itself also emerged as a significant predictor and the model including this additional covariate performed better (Nagelkerke R2 0.31 versus 0.17 and AUC 0.79 versus 0.70), highlighting the association of mental health and cognitive health in this context. Among others, Michalowsky et al. (2024) identified depression, as documented in primary care practice, as a prodromal feature associated with the development of a dementia diagnosis across three observation periods (0–12 months, 12–60 months, 60–120 months before the incident dementia diagnosis). 46 The authors highlight the crucial role of primary care physicians in identifying and monitoring risk factors and prodromal symptoms through a holistic approach, including mental health aspects, rather than focusing solely on the cognition of the patient. 46 Symptoms of anxiety and depression may represent an important target for early interventions aimed at improving quality of life and slowing further cognitive deterioration.

Yet, the remaining significance of Constructional Praxis after controlling for affective symptoms indicates that SCD is not solely driven by mental health factors. The low sensitivity of the model, including only neuropsychological tests as predictors, indicates that these tests may not be sufficiently sensitive to detect subtle cognitive alterations that individuals with SCD may experience. Ceiling effects of cognitive tests may play a role here. While our findings provide initial insights into the cognitive profile of individuals with SCD, they should be validated in an independent sample, as the found effects of the prediction models are rather small. The sample should ideally comprise older adults with SCD recruited from memory clinics and using more complex visuo-cognitive tasks like ROCF or a block design test. Also, a promising approach to identify or develop cognitive tasks for the study of early cognitive deficits is to take the AD-related tau pathology into account. Early AD-related tau pathology emerges mainly in the transentorhinal- and entorhinal cortex. 47 Lesions in this area may lead to deficits in relational memory, 48 which can be assessed with paired-associate learning tasks, that assess for example memory for word-picture pairs. 49

Our study is also subject to certain limitations. We acknowledge that the MMSE is a screening instrument that cannot substitute for a comprehensive diagnostic procedure when establishing a final clinical diagnosis. However, we applied a stricter threshold for identifying individuals with cognitive impairment than the original cut-off (‘Normal’: 24–30). 18 Studies using a cut-off score of 27 have shown that the MMSE correctly identified 76.7% of individuals with dementia and 90.9% of cognitively healthy older adults. 19 In a study that looked at 16 international cohort studies of aging to estimate the prevalence of SCD, the MMSE was used to assess cognitive impairment, since the MMSE was administered in most of the studies. In light of the criticism of the MMSE as a screening tool, the authors conducted a sensitivity analysis on a subset of studies with more extensive neuropsychological assessment and found that SCD prevalence estimates hardly differed. 4 Thus, in light of these robust findings and the clinical relevance of the instrument, we also decided to employ the MMSE.

The term cognitive decline refers to a change over time. SCD is defined by an individual's perceived cognitive deterioration relative to their earlier cognitive functioning. 1 Nevertheless, in the present study, cognitive performance was evaluated at a single time point. However, the association of objective cognitive performance and SCD is more consistent in longitudinal studies. 10 In a future study, it would be valuable to conduct longitudinal follow-ups of individuals with SCD to identify those who progress to AD, as of course not all older adults with SCD ultimately develop the condition. However, in clinical practice, it is crucial to detect early cognitive changes possibly related to AD before significant decline is observable over time.

Another limitation of the present study is the restricted number of predictors that were included in the analysis due to the small sample size. As a result, only three predictors were selected, which limits the depth of interpretation. Nevertheless, the chosen variables represent those most strongly supported by previous findings and theoretical considerations. Furthermore, we did not collect data on ethnic groups, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Future research should evaluate the models in larger and diverse samples.

Our findings contribute to the growing number of literature exploring the neuropsychological profiles of individuals with SCD and suggest that performance on specific cognitive tasks, particularly assessing visuo-cognition, may reveal subtle differences between older adults with and without SCD. These findings support the notion that SCD may reflect a preclinical stage of neurodegenerative processes in some individuals. Although SCD is mainly discussed within the context of AD, it can also precede non-AD dementias, such as vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia and dementia with Lewy Bodies. 3 In clinical practice, at the time of initial presentation, the underlying pathological processes driving a potential cognitive decline are often unclear. In this context, neuropsychological measures may help to identify individuals who are likely to benefit from further diagnostic procedures to clarify differential diagnoses. Neuropsychological measures and questionnaires play a key role for the initial clinical evaluation, as they are cost-effective and widely accessible compared to imaging or biomarker analysis, which are often resource-intensive.

Although neuropsychological tests do not directly measure cognitive abilities and the concept of SCD remains rather difficult to frame within classical neuropsychological testing and measurement theory, visuo-cognition may be a promising domain for the development of more sensitive diagnostic tools to detect possible cognitive alterations that individuals with SCD are experiencing. This may be particularly relevant, as a decline in visuo-cognition also has a great impact on the everyday life of affected individuals and can for example lead to orientation difficulties, which is a frequent symptom in typical AD 7 as well as in SCD. Older adults with SCD struggle especially in finding their way in unfamiliar environments, even when using assistive tools such as maps. 8 Moreover, deficits in visuo-cognition are associated with a high fear of falling. 50

Recommendations for the clinical practice are that individuals presenting to healthcare providers with self-reported cognitive decline should undergo a comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, including especially visuo-cognitive tests, as well as the evaluation of psychological factors such as anxiety and depression. This may help clinicians in identifying individuals who may benefit from further diagnostic evaluation and monitoring, thereby supporting timely interventions. It is important to take the concerns of older adults with SCD seriously, as they often feel insufficiently acknowledged by healthcare professionals, 8 while avoiding the medicalization of healthy cognitive aging processes. Given both, the high prevalence of SCD and the absence of an accepted SCD assessment, there is a need for tools to adequately capture and operationalize SCD. Such an assessment should consider subjective questionnaires or interviews to reflect the inherently subjective nature of SCD, while also incorporating objective cognitive tests and the investigation of mental health aspects to allow for a meaningful characterization.
