Palm oil fuel ash blended cement for solidification stabilization of petroleum sludge and heavy metal immobilization and waste valorization
Authors: Amir Detho, Aeslina Abdul Kadir, Nor Amani Filzah Mohd Kamil, Ummi Humaira Bujang, Ahmed Othman Alanazi, Saad A. Aljlil, Moustafa S. Darweesh, Hesham Hussein Rassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The petroleum industry produces a significant quantity of solid waste referred to as petroleum sludge (PS) as a result of several refining, storage, and transportation procedures. PS typically contains a variety of heavy metals that could threat environment even to the human. Fortunately, PS can be effectively managed using a variety of treatment techniques. The solidification/stabilization (S/S) technique was applied to treat PS, with ordinary Portland cement (OPC) partially replaced by palm oil fuel ash (POFA). The study began with the characterisation of raw materials, including OPC, POFA, and PS, using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). POFA substitution levels of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% were evaluated for their effectiveness in encapsulating heavy metals within the cementitious matrix. Two leaching assessments were conducted: the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure (TCLP) and the Synthetic Precipitation Leaching Procedure (SPLP). Heavy metal concentrations in the leachates were measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES). The results indicate that heavy metal concentrations decreased progressively with increasing POFA content up to 15%; a slight increase was observed at 20% substitution. All results were compared against USEPA regulatory guidelines, and the heavy metal concentrations in all treated samples complied with the applicable limits. The optimal POFA substitution level was 15%. The TCLP and SPLP results suggest that the treated material may warrant further investigation for potential reclassification from hazardous to non-hazardous waste; however, this conclusion requires validation through long-term durability assessment and compressive strength testing, which were not within the scope of the present study.

Introduction

Malaysia is one of the well-developing countries in the world. Malaysia’s quick development has helped the country’s economy flourish in order to satisfy consumer demand in key industries like energy generation and petroleum production. Over one million metric tons of hazardous waste are produced in Malaysia by industrial processes, such as refining, chemical production, cosmetics, and the pharmaceutical industry. The second-largest category is composed of wastes derived from hydrocarbons and oil1.

Petroleum sludge (PS), a byproduct of the petroleum industry, is generated during the exploration, production, transportation, storage, and refining of crude oil. It consists of both organic and inorganic contaminants. Total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH), a measure of organic pollutants in PS, usually ranged between 510,000—640,000 mg/kg2. Additionally, metals including lead, zinc, nickel, copper, mercury, and chromium—which usually exist as metal compounds—are among the inorganic pollutants found in PS. Both aquatic life and humans may experience health issues if organic and inorganic pollutants seep into water bodies sources3. Approximately 1 ton of PS is produced with each 500 tons of crude oil treated4,5. The amount of the PS produced was increased year by year and is predicted to continue keep rising because of rising the ascending demands of refined petroleum products worldwide.

PS is categorized as a scheduled waste by Department of Environment (DOE) Malaysia, and it must be disposed of in safe landfills in accordance with the Toxicity Characteristics Leaching Procedure (TCLP) regulations. This sludge classifies by DOE under the categories specifies in 1st-scheduled of the Environment Quality (Scheduled Wastes) Regulations 2005. It has been classified under SW 204 that defined as any sludge that contain one or more metals such as lead, zinc, nickel, copper, mercury, cadmium, vanadium, tin, aluminium, chromium, and beryllium. These heavy metals in PS could enter the groundwater through soil and disturb the aquatic system by reducing fish species diversity and abundance while polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) have genotoxic effects on ecological receptor and humans. The American Petroleum Institute (API) reported that the levels of metals in oily sludge typical Ranges (mg/kg): Zn (7–80), Pb (0.001–0.12), Cu (32–120), Ni (17–25), Cr (27–80)5.

Furthermore, the health of humans and the environment may be seriously threatened by the inappropriate disposal or inadequate treatment of PS from refinery. It has to be treated using effective treatment. There are numbers of treatment techniques to handle PS. Those include land farming, incineration, ozonation, chlorination, and combustion. However, all the methods are highly expensive and give negative impact to the environment by producing odour and fire hazard6,7.

The stabilization and solidification (S/S) method which has been extensively used in Western nations and it should be considered as an alternative option3. S/S techniques is one of the approaches used to treat hazardous waste and is also effective method in reducing leachability of heavy metal pollutants. Solidification is the process of physically covering hazardous waste materials in a cementitious matrix and the process of chemically immobilizing pollutants, both organic and inorganic, in a hazardous waste stream so they become less soluble and more stable is known as stabilization are the most effective techniques that explain the S/S treatment principle8. Stabilization in simple terms, is the process of changing pollutants in a waste into less hazardous, less mobile, and less soluble forms, which lowers the potential for overall hazards while solidification encapsulate the pollutant within matrices using cement and convert the waste into solid that can easily handle. OPC serves as a binding agent in this procedure9.

From the previous studies, many alternative materials have been used to replace cement such as municipal solid waste (MSW), rice husk ash (RHA), fly ash, and ash from medical waste incineration. However, using cement alone as a binder in this method may not be sufficiently to effectively for immobilising some organic and inorganic pollutants, and it is also associated with relatively high costs10. Therefore, this investigation explores the utilization of POFA as an effective and environmentally friendly approach to introduce a sustainable solution for cement substitution, thus enhancing the performance of the S/S method in treating PS. In addition, it provides a disposal route for palm oil ash production.

Thus, this research aims to incorporate POFA as a partial cement substitute in the S/S treatment of petroleum sludge. While prior studies have explored POFA as a supplementary cementitious material, the novelty of the present investigation lies in its systematic evaluation of POFA-blended cement specifically for petroleum sludge stabilization, combining TCLP and SPLP leaching assessment with SEM microstructural analysis across multiple curing durations. This dual-leaching approach provides a more comprehensive environmental risk characterization than single-test protocols reported in earlier studies, and the findings contribute new quantitative data on optimal POFA substitution levels for this particular waste stream. POFA contains high pozzolanic silica content that enhances contaminant encapsulation within the cementitious matrix. By incorporating POFA into the S/S method, the study also addresses the disposal challenge of palm oil mill ash, one of the most abundantly generated agricultural by-products in Malaysia.

Material and method

STAGE 1: Material preparation, collection, and characterization

OPC, POFA, PS, and water are the primary materials employed in the present investigation.

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC)

In this research, typically cement utilized in the S/S process was Type I OPC produced in compliance with MS 522: Part 1: 1989. Lafarge’s OPC was produced under comprehensive process of testing, quality control and monitoring, in accordance with the specifications of the SIRIM Product Accreditation License11. The OPC employed in this investigation is depicted in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Ordinary Portland cement (OPC).

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC).

Palm oil fuel ash (POFA)

In this investigation, cement was substituted with POFA. The POFA originated from Kluang Oil Palm Processing Sdn Bhd’s palm oil processing plant in Kluang. The POFA was directly collected from the factory and oven-dried for 24 h at 110 ± 5 °C to remove any remaining moisture since POFA is exposed to the unknown moisture condition after the production of it. Figure 2 shows POFA following a 24-h oven drying process. The POFA was stored in a controlled environment maintained at 25 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity below 50% in sealed airtight containers to minimize moisture uptake prior to use. The dried POFA subsequently underwent a sieving process using a 300 μm screen to remove fibers, kernels, and coarser particles that may have resulted from incomplete combustion in the palm oil mill, as shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Schematic process of POFA preparation.

Schematic process of POFA preparation.

The fine ash underwent 300 μm sieve screen was gathered and further ground employing Abrasion Test Using Los Angeles Machine fitted with 10-stainless steel bars having a diameter and length (12 mm and 800 mm). The grinding process was conducted for 2 h per 4 kg of ash in order to increase the fines and consequent reactivity12 as shown in Fig. 2. Then, POFA was kept in an airtight container and stored in the humidity-controlled room to isolate from the atmospheric humidity. All the preparation of POFA was conducted in the Material Laboratory FKAAB, UTHM.

The PETRONAS Melaka refinery is provided the PS samples that was selected for treatment. The PETRONAS Melaka Refinery comprises two refinement train systems such as PSR-1 and PSR-2, which is situated in the Melaka, Malaysia. The refinery has a per day petroleum goods refining capacity exceeding 440,000 barrels, serving both domestic and international markets demands. Material characterization

X-ray fluorescence (XRF) was employed to characterize the materials. Since XRF analysis is a quick, precise, non-destructive approach that requires minimal sample preparing, it was used to identify the chemical composition of the materials. The test was carried out by using the sample die set, spatula, and weighing sample boat as shown in the Fig. 4.Fig. 4(a) Manual hydraulic press and (b) Die set.

(a) Manual hydraulic press and (b) Die set.

Firstly, all the apparatus was cleaned by using tissue and ethanol. Sample of 8 g and 2 g of wax for composition analysis and sample of 9 g and wax of 3 g for heavy metals analysis were weighed by using a boat. A higher wax ratio for heavy metal analysis was employed in order to improve pellets binding performance and integrity of structure, as trace element analysis requires more mechanically stable pellets to minimize surface defects, cracks, and fragment during higher-pressure pressing. Then, die was formed in accordance with laboratory procedures. The prepared combination was then moved from the container into the die. The steel die pellet was carefully inserted with its polished side face the sample, in accordance with the suggested plunger instructions. After the die assembly was placed within the press, the top bolster was manually tightened.

Next, the process was then started by engaging the lever once the valve had been adjusted to the pressing position. Swinging of the lever was stopped when the pressure gauge reached 80 kN. The valve positioned was progressively pushed to the vented position. After that, its top bolster was removed from the screws to make it easier to remove the die assembly. The die barrel’s base was taken off. After inserting the black cup into the die barrel’s bottom and placing it within the press, the top bolster was screwed down until the plunger sank. In order to recover the sample, which became shaped into a pellet, the assembly was finally disassembled after the pressing procedure. All procedure of experiment was conducted in the Analytical Laboratory, FKAAB, UTHM.

STAGE 2: solidification of S/S sample

Preparation of S/S sample

S/S process generally performed following three steps of mixing, casting, and curing. OPC, POFA, PS, and water were used as binding materials. A studied conducted by Qian Guangren et al.13 found that incorporating up to 50% sludge was sufficient to achieve the desired S/S performance. Moreover, another study by Yin et al.14 discovered that 50% of sludge content in the mixture was the optimum mix design for S/S method. Therefore, in this study, 50% of sludge was added to every mixture. The cement proportions were partly substituted with POFA by 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. A 50/50 OPC-Sludge mixes is typically utilized in research as a S/S control mix. Each of the samples weighed 250 g in total. STAGE 3: Stabilization of S/S sample (Leaching test)

Toxicity characteristic leaching procedure (TCLP)

The leaching experiment (TCLP) was subjected on blocks specimen subsequently 7 days and 28 days with air dried process, in accordance with SW-846 USEPA Toxicity Characteristic.United States Environmental Protection Agency, Code of Federal Regulations, 40, CFR part 261.24.31 Method 131118. In this test, the procedure of TCLP was started by determining the extraction fluid to be used. Initially, the cube blocks were first crushed a little and passed through a sieve to get particles smaller than 9.5 mm. Subsequently, 5 g of the solid was transferred to a 500 mL glass beaker. Next, 96.5 mL of the extraction solution was added, and the mixture was stirred vigorously using a magnetic stirrer at 30 ± 2 rpm for 5 min. The solution’s pH was determined to choose appropriate extraction fluid. If pH value below 5.0 extraction fluid No.1 was used, if pH value exceeds 5.0, an alternative extraction fluid No.2 was applied as shown in Table 3. Since the pH in this investigation was exceeds 5.0, extraction fluid No. 2 was utilized19.Table 3Extraction fluid.

Synthetic precipitation leaching procedure (SPLP)

The SPLP procedure is nearly identical to the TCLP. The primary distinction lies in the type of extraction solution employed. The cube blocks were first crushed a little and passed through a sieve to get particles smaller than 9.5 mm. Subsequently, the prepared samples were packed in screw cap bottles with leaching solution and crushed material at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 to solidified. According to the SPLP procedures described in SW-846 Method 1312, an extraction solution with a pH of 4.2 is usually used for wastes or wastewater samples. Accordingly, an extraction solution with a pH of 4.2 was employed in this investigation. A solution with a pH of 4.2 was reached by diluting a combination of H2SO4 and HNO3 (60% and 40%) with distilled water.

The bottles were agitated using a rotary machine at 30 ± 2 rpm for 18 ± 2 h to ensures thorough mixing (end over end) manner. The leachate samples were gathered and filtered through 7 µm glass fibre filter and preserved by acidified utilizing HNO3 to achieve pH below 2. ICP-OES was then used to measure the dissolved metal concentration in the samples. Figure 10 shows the flowchart of SPLP procedure.Fig. 10Flowchart of SPLP procedure.

Flowchart of SPLP procedure.

Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES)

In this research, results from TCLP and SPLP were tested with ICP-OES to evaluate heavy metal concentrations that could leach from petroleum sludge after incorporated with POFA as partial cement replacement. Figure 11 shows the ICP-OES. The ICP-OES is a powerful, high-performance analytical technique employed to identifying and measuring trace level constituents. This technique involves exposing the sample to plasma energy, which raises the energy levels of its constituent atoms.Fig. 11Pictorial view of ICP-OES.

Pictorial view of ICP-OES.

As the excited atoms return to a lower state of energy and emit light at specific wavelengths, the lines in the spectrum are detected for analysis. The wavelength of the photon that each element emits determines its identification, and the intensity of emission signals quantifies its concentration. In order to forming plasma, argon gas introduce into the torch, a high-frequency (HF) alternated current, typically approximately 27 MHz is employed to spark coil that surrounds the torch’s tip tube. Plasma is formed by ionizing argon gas employing electromagnetic radiation that the high-frequency (HF) current induces in the torch tube. This plasma’s high temperature (10,000 K) and their higher electrons density are employed to excitation and emission of the sample. A slender central tube inside the torch assembly is used to introduce atomized solution samples into the plasma20,21.

Solidified samples at 28 days

According to Harbottle et. al.24 stated that at 28 days of hydration, the S/S samples was in mature phase to stabilize and solidified the contaminants. Therefore, in this research, SEM of solidified samples at 28 days had been conducted in order to investigate the bonding between OPC, POFA, and petroleum sludge. Figure 13 shows the SEM micrograph of solidified samples accordance to percentage of POFA added which are from control 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%.Fig. 13SEM micrographs of solidified samples at 28 days (a) Control 0%, (b) 5% replacement (c) 10% replacement (d) 15% replacement and (e) 20% replacement.

SEM micrographs of solidified samples at 28 days (a) Control 0%, (b) 5% replacement (c) 10% replacement (d) 15% replacement and (e) 20% replacement.

Based on Fig. 13, the SEM images of the solidified samples indicate satisfactory bonding from the control sample through to the 15% POFA mixture. Figure 13(e), representing the 20% POFA sample, shows that the matrix did not achieve adequate solidification. This is attributable to the higher POFA content, which was unable to sustain the cementitious reactions throughout the curing period, owing to the deficient CaO content in POFA at this substitution level. Yin et al.14 reported that POFA substitution should not exceed 15% because POFA contains less calcium oxide than OPC, and higher replacement levels reduce the availability of CaO required to sustain hydration reactions. Figure 13(d) confirms that 15% POFA produced the most compact and well-bonded microstructure among all mixtures examined.

It is acknowledged that the SEM analysis presented in this study is qualitative in nature. Quantitative image analysis, pore-size distribution measurement, and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were not performed; identification of specific hydration products such as calcium silicate hydrate (C-S–H) or ettringite was therefore not possible from the micrographs alone. Mineralogical characterization using XRD, FTIR, or TGA/DTG analysis was outside the scope of the present investigation but is recommended in future work to verify the hydration products responsible for metal immobilization. Similarly, compressive strength testing was not conducted in this study; mechanical performance data are recognised as a standard requirement for comprehensive S/S assessment and are included as a priority recommendation for subsequent research. Regarding statistical analysis, each mix design was prepared in triplicate (n = 3) as indicated in Table 2.

Leachability of samples at 7 days

Figure 14 shows heavy metal leachability of TCLP at 7 days. The figure indicates that heavy metal concentration in S/S samples dropped slightly as proportion of POFA added increased. In this investigation, using POFA in place of OPC exhibited promising results in lowering the leachability of heavy metals. For example, for heavy metals namely Iron (Fe), when the amount of POFA added increases from 5%, 10%, and 15%, the number of heavy metals decreases from control sample which is 1.493 ppm and decreases to 1.146 ppm, 1.054 ppm and 0.058 ppm. As shown in Fig. 18, the concentrations of heavy metals measured in the TCLP are lower than those observed in the SPLP, indicating lower heavy metal leaching under TCLP conditions compared with SPLP. This might be due to type of acid utilized and leaching solution of pH during experiment. Acetic acid with pH of 2.88 ± 0.05 used in TCLP able to leach out more than sulphuric acid and HNO3 with pH 4.2 in SPLP. However, TCLP stronger and more capable to leaching higher concentrations of heavy metals when employed with acetic acid at lower pH levels (2.88 ± 0.05) than H2SO4/HNO3 with higher pH levels 4.2 in SPLP.Fig. 18Comparison of TCLP and SPLP leachability of S/S samples at 28 days.

Comparison of TCLP and SPLP leachability of S/S samples at 28 days.

This observation is supported by28, who reported that acetic acid in the TCLP extraction fluid increases the solubility and mobility of heavy metals, leading to more leaching than with the SPLP. Although the extraction fluid in TCLP is more acidic compared to SPLP, the testing and function of both procedures are completely different as TCLP extraction used acetic acid at lower pH levels (2.88 ± 0.05) to alter toxicity behavior of heavy metals. A mixture of H2SO4/HNO3 at higher pH levels 4.2 employed in SPLP is designed to simulates the effects of acid rain under natural environmental conditions29. Furthermore, the concentrations of heavy metal not over 100 ppm, this indicating that all meet USEPA regulations.

The observed differences in leaching behaviour between TCLP and SPLP can also be understood in terms of metal speciation and the buffering capacity of the cementitious matrix. Cementitious binders such as OPC maintain a highly alkaline pore solution (pH > 12), which suppresses the solubility of most heavy metal hydroxides and carbonates through precipitation and co-precipitation reactions. Under the more aggressive TCLP acetic acid environment (pH 2.88), this buffering capacity is partially overcome, allowing greater mobilization of metals such as Cr, Pb, and Zn. The comparatively milder SPLP extraction (pH 4.2) better preserves the alkaline matrix environment, resulting in lower leachate concentrations. Regarding the immobilization mechanism, heavy metals in this study are considered to be retained through a combination of physical encapsulation within the hardened cementitious matrix and chemical fixation via pozzolanic reaction products. The high SiO2 content of POFA (65.01%) promotes the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C-S–H) gel through pozzolanic reactions with CaO from OPC hydration; C-S–H gel is known to incorporate heavy metal ions by substitution within its layered structure or by surface adsorption. These mechanisms collectively reduce metal mobility, as reflected in the consistent decrease in TCLP and SPLP concentrations with increasing curing time. The long-term environmental implications of these findings should, however, be interpreted cautiously; the short-term leaching tests employed here do not account for potential changes in matrix integrity, carbonation, or pH buffering capacity under prolonged field exposure conditions.

Conclusion

The utilization of POFA as a partial substitution for cement in this study is a potential replacement for the immobilization and reduction of heavy metals in sludge. This research demonstrates the feasibility of using POFA into cement matrices in order to facilitate safely dispose them in non-hazardous landfill or potential to reuse it. From the results of leaching test, POFA has successfully treat heavy metals in petroleum sludge. Furthermore, POFA contain higher silica content (exceeding 60%) which able to enhance bonding formation and significantly improves to immobilization of metals as supported by Kuo et. al.30.

However, the use of POFA greater than 15% showed unsatisfactory results for leaching test which the results show an increasing value of heavy metal concentrations. The possibility of using higher amount of POFA might continuously increase heavy metal concentrations in S/S samples.

SEM analysis at 28 days of curing confirms that the 20% POFA sample failed to achieve adequate solidification, consistent with the leaching data. This is attributable to the lower CaO content of POFA relative to OPC; at higher substitution levels, insufficient CaO is available to sustain the hydration reactions necessary for matrix integrity, as reported by Yin et al.14. Based on the combined leaching and microstructural evidence, 15% POFA is recommended as the optimal substitution level for partial cement replacement in the S/S treatment of petroleum sludge. The TCLP and SPLP results at 28 days demonstrate that all regulated heavy metal concentrations comply with USEPA guidelines, indicating the potential suitability of the treated material for consideration as a non-hazardous waste. However, it must be acknowledged that this conclusion is based solely on short-term leaching tests,no mechanical strength data, long-term durability assessment, or mineralogical characterization was conducted in this study. Future work incorporating compressive strength testing, static leaching tests, and XRD or FTIR analysis is required before definitive conclusions regarding landfill reclassification can be drawn.

Recommendations

Based on the analysis performed in this research, recommendations for further research are as follows:Increase the percentage of POFA from 16% up to 19% might be performed in order to evaluate maximum optimum cement replacement in S/S samples.Long term test which is Static Leaching Test (SLT) is recommended to evaluate the durability behavior of solidified samples and identify potential changes in leaching of metals and matrix structure.Compressive strength testing should be conducted in order to investigate the possibility of using solidified samples in the construction applications for building block or road materials.Incorporation of OPC with other industrial waste such as bottom ash, fly ash, rice husk ash, and other by S/S method should be conducted as other alternative to treat petroleum sludge.
