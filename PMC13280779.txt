Clinical Management of Helicobacter Infection in Captive Baboons ( Papio anubis and Papio hamadryas ), and Isolation and Molecular Characterization of Three Novel Helicobacter Species
Authors: Isabel A Jimenez, Courtney Lunger, Marcus J Crim, Shannon L Primm, Anthony Mannion, Nathan P Crilly, Lydia M Hopper, Lindsey G Thurman, Linnette L Vasquez, Cory Brayton, Yan Feng, James G Fox, Zeli Shen, Jessica M Izzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Helicobacter spp. are gastric or enterohepatic bacteria that can be commensal, opportunistic, or pathogenic. Few studies have evaluated Helicobacter spp. in baboons.MethodsTwenty‐seven captive baboons were surveyed for Helicobacter spp. by fecal PCR, and a subset evaluated by culture. Nine baboons were treated with oral “triple therapy” with clarithromycin (10 mg/kg), amoxicillin (10 mg/kg), and omeprazole (20 mg) once daily for 10 days.ResultsAll baboons were positive for Helicobacter spp., including H. macacae (78%), H. suis (19%), and H. pylori (15%). Three novel Helicobacter species were identified by whole genomic sequencing, for which we propose the names Helicobacter papionis, H. simiae, and H. papionifaecis. Treatment was safe and effective for H. pylori , H. suis , and H. macacae, but did not eliminate the novel Helicobacter spp.ConclusionsSpecies‐specific assays should guide treatment of Helicobacter spp. infections in baboons. Further epidemiologic studies are needed to inform clinical management.Keywords: captive management, enterohepatic Helicobacter , fecal PCR, surveillance

Introduction

The genus Helicobacter comprises Gram‐negative, spiral‐shaped, microaerophilic, fastidious bacteria grouped by tissue tropism: gastric species colonize the stomach while enterohepatic Helicobacter species (EHS) colonize the small intestine, large intestine, and hepatobiliary tree. Gastric species include H. pylori and H. heilmannii ‐like organisms (HHLOs) [1, 2]. H. pylori is a human pathogen linked to gastritis, gastric ulceration, gastric adenocarcinoma, gastric lymphoma, and rarely inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [3, 4]. EHS are associated with IBD, enteritis, colitis, and proctitis, bacteremia, and neoplasia in humans, often in immunocompromised patients [5, 6, 7]. In humans, non‐human primates (NHPs), dogs, and cats, Helicobacter spp. infection may be subclinical, but during illness may also be opportunistic pathogens [8, 9, 10, 11].

Among NHPs, Helicobacter spp. have been most extensively studied in macaques, with gastric species including H. pylori , HHLOs, and H. suis , and EHS including H. cinaedi and H. macacae [12, 13, 14, 15]. Infection in macaques may be asymptomatic or associated with gastritis, chronic diarrhea, and intestinal adenocarcinoma [12, 16, 17, 18, 19]. There is limited literature regarding Helicobacter in baboons (Table S1), with gastric species more often described. H. pylori and HHLOs have been observed in the gastric mucosa and parietal cells of wild‐born olive baboons ( Papio anubis ) [20, 21] but are not always present, even in baboons with lymphoplasmacytic gastritis [16, 22]. While one group posited HHLOs were commensal, others found HHLOs to be associated with gastritis [23, 24]. Two studies have isolated EHS from baboons, with homology to H. macacae and H. cinaedi but unknown clinical relevance [25, 26]. It is unclear if Helicobacter are commensals, pathobionts, or pathogens in baboons.

Here, a case of gastritis in an olive baboon led to detection of an unidentified Helicobacter spp. and its genetic sequence, prompting colony‐wide surveillance to assess the prevalence of Helicobacter spp. in our baboons and ultimately leading to the isolation and characterization of three novel Helicobacter species.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Humane Care Guidelines

Twenty‐seven baboons from two colonies, including 11 male and 1 female olive baboons ( Papio anubis ) from colony A, and 14 male olive baboons and 1 male hamadryas baboon ( Papio hamadryas ) from colony B, were included (Table S2). All baboons in this study were housed at Johns Hopkins University (JHU) at an AAALAC International‐accredited facility, in accordance with the Animal Welfare Act (7 U.S.C. 2131), Animal Welfare Regulations (Title 9 C.F.R.) and the 8th Edition of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals [27]. Baboons were maintained under protocols approved by the JHU Animal Care and Use Committee and were singly‐housed for protocol‐related purposes or behavioral management. The authors confirm that the ethical policies of the Journal of Medical Primatology, as noted on the journal's author guidelines page, have been followed. Animals in this study were not on protocols investigating hepatobiliary or gastrointestinal (GI) disease, or infectious disease. Colonies were 4 miles apart, in buildings that also housed rodents; the facility with Colony A also housed macaques, marmosets, pigs, sheep, dogs, cats, and ferrets.

Data Collection

Medical records were reviewed for age, origin (captive‐bred vs. wild‐born, facility of birth, intermediate facility, facility immediately prior to arrival at our institution) and tenure at our facility (Table S2). Four baboons were originally caught from the wild (all Papio anubis ), and the remaining animals were captive‐bred. Baboons arrived from six facilities. Ages ranged from 3.5 to 27.3 years (mean 18.3 years) with a tenure of 2–21.9 years (mean 13.7 years). The date of birth for wild‐born animals was estimated as 1 year prior to capture or the earliest medical record. Medical records were examined for GI signs such as inappetence, vomiting, regurgitation, diarrhea, constipation, or hematochezia. Eight baboons had a history of acute GI signs, all either self‐limiting or resolved with treatment before the start of study (Table S2). No major medical history was present in any animals. Major medical history was defined as clinical concerns (apart from superficial wounds, dermal masses, dental pathology, osteoarthritis, or alopecia) or sedation/anesthetic events (apart from routine preventive or protocol‐related procedures). No animals had prior fecal PCR testing for Helicobacter spp. or were previously treated for Helicobacter spp.

Sample Collection

For PCR, feces were collected and stored at 4°C for up to 24 h or frozen at −80°C until shipment to a diagnostic laboratory (IDEXX BioAnalytics, Columbia, MO 65201). In Colony A, additional fecal samples were collected 2 weeks and 14–16 months after completing antibiotic treatment. For culture, fecal samples from 16 baboons (seven from Colony A collected at least 18 months after completion of antibiotics, and 9 from Colony B) were stored in 20% glycerol in Brucella broth at −80°C until shipment on dry ice to MIT. In a subset of animals euthanized for unrelated reasons, post‐mortem fecal, gastric, gall bladder, liver, small intestine, and colon samples were collected and stored at −20°C for up to 24 h, then frozen at −80°C until shipment to the diagnostic laboratory.

Histopathology

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% neutral‐buffered formalin and paraffin‐embedded. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin or Warthin–Starry and examined by a board‐certified veterinary anatomic pathologist. Images were collected on a Nikon Eclipse 80i microscope with a Y‐TV55 adaptor and Morrell camera and processed in Adobe Photoshop 25.9.0, following anatomic pathology guidelines [28].

Fecal Sample Real‐Time PCR, Conventional PCR, and Sanger Sequencing

Fecal samples underwent genus‐wide Helicobacter spp. real‐time PCR assay and species‐specific real‐time PCR assays to detect H. cinaedi/canicola, H. macacae, H. pylori , and H. suis . Real‐time PCR assays were based on the IDEXX BioAnalytics proprietary service platform (IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME). Samples were homogenized and total nucleic acids were extracted and tested by fluorogenic hydrolysis probe‐based real‐time PCR assays using a commercially available instrument (LightCycler 480, Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, IN) and master mix (LightCycler 480 Probes Master, Roche Applied Science). Assay‐specific positive and negative controls were included for each run and positive samples were retested for confirmation. A multiplexed hydrolysis‐probe‐based real‐time PCR assay targeting a eukaryotic gene (18SrRNA) and bacterial gene (16SrRNA) was performed for each sample to demonstrate nucleic acid recovery and absence of PCR inhibition. To limit uninterpretable sequencing results due to coinfection, only samples from animals negative for the four species‐specific real‐time PCR assays but positive on genus‐wide Helicobacter spp. assay were assessed for atypical Helicobacter sequences. The literature suggests that 16SrRNA sequences should not be used to solely identify unknown Helicobacter isolates, as they are often highly conserved among Helicobacter spp. [29, 30, 31] Moreover, there is some evidence for horizontal 16SrRNA gene fragment transfers; thus, 16SrRNA similarity does not always correctly reflect Helicobacter phylogenetic relationships [29, 32, 33]. We therefore also utilized additional genes throughout this study. The 16SrRNA, 23SrRNA, and/or gyrB genes were amplified using conventional PCR (primers shown in Table S3) then submitted for Sanger sequencing (GENEWIZ, South Plainfield, NJ) [34]. BioEdit [35] v.7.2.5 and Sequencher software (Gene Codes Corporation, Ann Arbor, MI) were used to assemble reads into contigs. Regions of poor‐quality sequence and primer binding sequences were removed. Sequencing results with multiple traces were excluded. Contigs were compared to GenBank sequences and to whole genome sequences of novel species subsequently identified in this study using BLAST software [36].

Antibiotic Treatment

Oral “triple therapy” of amoxicillin (10 mg/kg), clarithromycin (10 mg/kg), and omeprazole (20 mg/baboon) once per day for 10 days was given to nine baboons in colony A. Baboons did not receive other antibiotics between diagnosis and follow‐up fecal PCRs.

Bacterial Isolation

Feces in freeze media (~200 mg) were directly placed on CVA (cefoperazone, vancomycin, and amphotericin) or TVP (trimethoprim, vancomycin, and polymyxin B) plates (Remel Laboratories, Lenexa, KS), or filtered through 0.65 or 0.45 μm filters onto blood agar plates. Plates were incubated under microaerobic conditions (80% N2, 10% CO2, and 10% H2) at 37°C. Suspected Helicobacter spp. were identified by morphology and Gram stain.

Pure Isolate and Paraffin‐Embedded Tumor DNA Extraction, PCR and Sanger Sequencing

Microbial DNA was extracted from pure isolates using the Roche High Pure PCR Template Preparation Kit (Roche Life Science, Basel, Switzerland) according to manufacturer protocols. Isolates were confirmed as Helicobacter species by 16S rRNA sequencing using conserved primers (Table S3). Isolates were speciated by heat shock protein 60 (hsp60) and RNA polymerase subunit B (rpoB) sequencing, which has been previously used to identify phylogenetic relationships among Helicobacter species [30, 33]; primers are shown in Table S3. PCR‐amplified products were subjected to Sanger sequencing. Sequence alignments and phylogenetic analysis were performed via Geneious bioinformatics software (Geneious v.7.1.7, Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand). For Helicobacter PCR of paraffin‐embedded tumor, the Qiagen FFPE tissue kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was used to extract DNA. PCR was performed with C97/H676 and C97/C98N primers (Table S3) using the High‐Fidelity PCR System (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN).

Whole Genome Sequencing

Genomic DNA was prepared for Illumina MiSeq sequencing (2 × 300 bp) by the BioMicroCenter at MIT using the Nextera Flex library prep kit. Default parameters were used for all bioinformatics tools. Raw sequenced reads were decontaminated of adapter sequences and quality trimmed using BBDuk (v.37.17; parameters: ktrimr, k23, mink11, hdist1, tpe, tbo, qtrimrl, trimq10, and qin33) [37] followed by de novo contig assembly and genome annotation using the Comprehensive Genome Analysis pipeline (The Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV‐BRC), https://www.bv‐brc.org/). Genomes were deposited in NCBI under GenBank Accession numbers in Table S6. Using OrthoFinder [38], concatenated core protein sequences were determined, followed by MAFFT for multi‐sequence alignment and FastTree to infer approximate maximum‐likelihood phylogenetic trees. Taxonomic classification of genomes was supported by average nucleotide identity (pyANI, v.0.2.10) [39] and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (Genome‐to‐Genome Distance Calculator, v.3.0) [40]. Protein annotations were searched for the virulence factors flagellin, gamma‐glutamyltranspeptidase, High‐Temperature Requirement A, Cytotoxin‐associated gene A, Vacuolating cytotoxin A, Neutrophil‐activating protein A, catalase, Cytolethal distending toxin, Campylobacter invasion antigen B, and Fibronectin‐like protein A.

Biochemical Characterization

The RapID NH System (Remel Laboratories, Lenexa, KS) was performed, including tests for oxidase, catalase, nitrate reduction, indoxyl acetate hydrolysis, urease, phosphatase activity, γ‐glutamyl transferase, growth at 42°C, growth with 1% glycine, and resistance to nalidixic acid and cephalothin (30 mg disks) [41].

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Two isolates per novel species were imaged by transmission electron microscopy. 24–48‐h blood agar cultures were collected into 1× PBS. Bacterial concentration was adjusted to 0.05 OD/mL with PBS to decrease background effects. Samples were placed on formvar/carbon mesh grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Montgomery County, PA), negatively stained with Uranyless Solution (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Montgomery County, PA), and examined under a Jeol 2100F transmission electron microscope.

Statistical Analysis

Each animal was coded as 1 (positive) or 0 (negative) for H. macacae, H. suis , or H. pylori . A binomial general linear mixed model was used to compare infection rates by housing colony (A vs. B), housing room (A1 vs. A2 vs. B1 vs. B2), origin (wild‐born or captive‐born), birth location for captive‐born (one of three facilities), and housing facility prior to arrival at our institution (one of six facilities). A logistic regression was performed to assess the effect of age and tenure at our facility on the likelihood of being positive with H. macacae, H. suis , or H. pylori . Analyses were run in R v.4.1.1 [42].

Results

Case Study 1

During a routine experimental procedure, a 16‐year‐old intact male olive baboon (baboon #1, colony A) demonstrated marked regurgitation of partially digested blood. Gastric endoscopy revealed erythematous and inflamed mucosa without obvious ulceration. Three gastric mucosal biopsies were collected from separate sites. Radiographs revealed right cranial lung lobe consolidation, concerning for aspiration pneumonia. The patient was managed with gastroprotectants, antinausea medication, and antibiotics (enrofloxacin 5 mg/kg IM for 5 days). Gastric biopsy demonstrated frequent argyrophilic spiral organisms within gastric glands, consistent with Helicobacter spp., and mild neutrophilic inflammation (Figure 1). Fecal PCR was positive for Helicobacter spp., but negative for H. macacae, H. pylori , H. suis , and H. cinaedi . A portion of the Helicobacter 16SrRNA gene was sequenced from fecal nucleic acids, revealing an unidentified Helicobacter sequence.

Colony Survey

Twenty‐seven baboons were surveyed for Helicobacter using fecal real‐time PCR. All baboons were positive for Helicobacter spp. with 78% positive for H. macacae, 19% positive for H. suis , 15% positive for H. pylori , and 0% positive for H. cinaedi/canicola (Table 1, Figure 2). Co‐infections were present in 30% of cases; all 4 animals positive for H. pylori were co‐infected with H. macacae, and 4 of the 5 animals positive for H. suis were co‐infected with H. macacae. The prevalence of H. macacae was similar between colony A (75%) and colony B (80%). There was no effect of colony or housing room on positivity with H. macacae, H. suis , or H. pylori (Table S4). There was an effect of captive‐born status on positivity with H. macacae (Z = −2.29, SE = 1.31, p = 0.022) with 87% (23/27) of captive‐born baboons positive for H. macacae compared to only 25% (1/4) of wild‐born baboons. Animals born at Facility 1 (Z = 2.46, SE = 1.55, p = 0.014) also had a significantly higher likelihood of positivity for H. macacae compared to wild‐born animals. The only hamadryas baboon in the study, baboon #20, tested positive for H. macacae; to our knowledge, this is the first report of H. macacae in P. hamadryas . There were no statistical effects of captive‐born status or birth facility on H. suis or H. pylori prevalence, nor of age or time at the facility on H. macacae, H. suis , or H. pylori prevalence.

Initial Identification of Novel Helicobacter Sequences

On initial colony survey, five animals (two from colony A, three from colony B) were positive for Helicobacter at the genus‐level but negative on species‐specific assays. Eight additional baboons in colony A met criteria after antibiotic treatment. Feces from these 13 animals were subjected to 16SrRNA ± 23SrRNA and gyrB gene sequencing. Sequences from baboons #2 and #14 had 100% 16SrRNA homology to H. macacae strains (MIT 99‐5501 and MIT 99‐5504) but only 93.3%–97.7% homology with 23SrRNA sequences and 85.5%–86.7% homology with gyrB sequences from H. macacae. Feces from the other 11 baboons (#1, 3–10, 13, and 19) yielded 16SrRNA sequences with > 99% identity with H. fennelliae (NCTC 11613), H. cinaedi Mainz strain (CCUG 33804/R‐927), baboon isolate MIT 03‐7674 colon, and macaque isolate MIT 99‐10781, but lower homology for 23SrRNA and gyrB sequences. These results suggested the presence of undescribed species.

Treatment and Fecal PCR Follow‐Up

Due to potential adverse impacts of Helicobacter infection on experimental outcomes, one investigator elected to pursue treatment. Nine baboons from colony A were treated with oral triple therapy (amoxicillin (10 mg/kg), clarithromycin (10 mg/kg), and omeprazole (20 mg)) once daily for 10 days. Treatment was well tolerated. Fecal real‐time PCR performed 2 weeks post‐treatment demonstrated 100% clearance of H. macacae, H. suis , and H. pylori , yet eight of nine animals remained positive for Helicobacter at the genus level (Table 2). At 14–16 months post‐treatment, all baboons were positive for Helicobacter spp., but only two were identifiable at the species level (Table 2).

Case Study 2

Eight months following the initial Helicobacter diagnosis, baboon #14 from colony B presented for acute obtundation with marked gastric dilatation and was euthanized due to poor prognosis. Necropsy revealed chronic‐active gastritis and a tumor expanding and compressing the cystic bile duct and neck of the gall bladder, histologically consistent with a cholangiocarcinoma (Figure 3). Moderate periportal to bridging chronic hepatitis was also present, as well as mild to moderate chronic‐active enterocolitis. No organisms consistent with Helicobacter were appreciated on Warthin–Starry stained sections from the tumor, liver, gall bladder, stomach, small intestine, or colon. Additionally, scrolls of paraffin‐embedded tumor were negative for Helicobacter by 16S rRNA PCR. However, H. suis was identified by PCR in feces and fresh tissue from necropsy (stomach, liver, gall bladder, small intestine and colon). The colon was also positive for H. macacae. Fecal PCR also yielded undescribed Helicobacter sequences identical to this animal's original sample.

Isolation and Characterization of Three Novel Helicobacter Species

Approximately 22 months (Colony A) and 8 months (Colony B) after the initial survey, fecal samples were cultured from 16 baboons (seven in Colony A and nine in Colony B). Samples that tested positive for Helicobacter spp. but negative for H. macacae were preferentially chosen for culture. A total of 11 Helicobacter isolates were obtained (two in Colony A and nine in Colony B). Sequencing of the 16S rRNA, hsp60 and rpoB genes and phylogenetic analysis revealed three novel taxa that clustered separately from other Helicobacter species (Figure S1). The presence of three novel taxa was subsequently confirmed by whole genome sequencing of two isolates per novel taxon, for which we propose the names H. papionis, H. simiae, and H. papionifaecis (Figure 4). H. simiae and H. papionifaecis were phylogenetically closer to each other than to H. papionis. H. papionifaecis was closely related to H. macacae. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) calculated between novel and existing Helicobacter genomes revealed that the novel species were below the 95% similarity threshold to differentiate species, and digital DNA–DNA hybridization (DDH) showed that the novel species were below the 70% similarity threshold to differentiate species (Table S5). Thus, we confirmed that the three novel species are distinct from each other and from other known Helicobacter species. Genome summary statistics are presented in Table S6. H. papionifaecis had a substantially larger genome than the other two novel species, at 2.78 and 2.89 Mb for isolates 23‐1056 and 23‐1059, respectively. All sequenced isolates contained the gene encoding the virulence factor flagellin, and all but 23‐1046 T2 (H. simiae) contained the gene encoding catalase. The novel species were negative for other major virulence factors, including CagA. The 16S rRNA, 23S rRNA, and gyrB sequences from initial characterization were also evaluated using BLAST against whole genome sequences from pure isolates to confirm identity.

A comparison of the biochemical and phenotypic characterizations of the three novel species and other Helicobacter species is provided in Table S7. All isolates were oxidase‐positive, urease‐negative, negative for γ‐glutamyl transferase activity, positive for alkaline phosphatase hydrolysis, negative for nitrate reduction to nitrite, and unable to grow in 1% glycine but able to grow at 42°C. H. papionis and H. papionifaecis were catalase‐positive, while H. simiae was catalase‐negative. Indoxyl acetate hydrolysis was present in all isolates of H. papionis, one of five isolates of H. simiae, and two of four isolates of H. papionifaecis. All three novel species were resistant to the antibiotic nalidixic acid, while H. simiae and H. papionifaecis were also resistant to cephalothin. Transmission electron microscopy was performed for two isolates from each novel species, with representative images from one isolate each shown in Figure 5. Cells from H. papionis were 1.2–1.5 μm long and 0.3–0.4 μm wide. The organism was comma‐shaped with a single, unipolar, sheathed flagellum. Cells from H. simiae were 2.6–4.3 μm long and 0.2–0.3 μm wide. The organism had curved to spiral shape with bipolar, sheathed flagella. Cells from H. papionifaecis were 3.1–4.4 μm long and 0.2–0.4 μm wide. The organism was curved with bipolar, sheathed flagella. No organisms had periplasmic fibers.

Discussion

This is the first colony‐wide survey of Helicobacter infection in baboons using molecular diagnostics and the first study to isolate novel Helicobacter spp. from baboons. Using a combination of genus‐level Helicobacter real‐time PCR and species‐specific real‐time PCR assays, we found that H. macacae is highly prevalent in our baboon colonies, with H. suis and H. pylori at lower prevalences. Thirteen baboons tested positive for Helicobacter spp. but negative on four species‐specific real‐time PCR assays, suggesting that they might be infected with species or strains of Helicobacter spp. that had not been previously identified in NHPs. Further investigation using culture and molecular diagnostics led to the isolation and characterization of three novel Helicobacter species: H. papionis, H. simiae, and H. papionifaecis. These species were genetically, morphologically, and biochemically distinct. All three species were resistant to the synthetic quinolone nalidixic acid, and H. simiae and H. papionifaecis were resistant to cephalothin, a first‐generation cephalosporin. The heterogeneity in antibiotic susceptibility observed among these novel Helicobacter species suggests that genus‐level identification of Helicobacter should be followed by species‐specific characterization to guide antibiotic therapy.

H. macacae, H. suis , and H. pylori were not cultured from any fecal samples, including baboons that were PCR‐positive for these species. This is in part due to intentional selection of samples for culture that were suspected to be mono‐infected by novel species. Additionally, because the samples for PCR and culture were collected at different times, intermittent shedding of the pathogen could impact test congruency. Furthermore, the ability to detect gastric species of Helicobacter by fecal PCR may be limited by their tissue niche deep in the gastric glands; thus, gastric biopsy with culture would be more sensitive; due to invasiveness, we did not employ this method here. The greater sensitivity of PCR compared to culture and the commercial availability of PCR assays led us to use PCR to assess treatment efficacy against Helicobacter in this study.

There are no studies on treatment for Helicobacter spp. in baboons, nor for EHS in any NHPs. Oral combinations of a proton‐pump inhibitor, amoxicillin, and clarithromycin, with or without bismuth subsalicylate, have been utilized successfully to treat gastric Helicobacter ( H. pylori in humans, rhesus macaques, and Japanese macaques, and H. suis in a rhesus macaque) [8, 43, 44, 45, 46]. In rhesus macaques, H. pylori has also been managed with metronidazole, amoxicillin, and bismuth subsalicylate, but with only partial success due to metronidazole resistance [43]. There is sparse guidance on the treatment of EHS in human patients. Intravenous antibiotic therapy for several weeks appears to be effective in human clinical reports, but such regimens would often be impractical to implement for NHPs, and difficult to justify in animals without clinical signs [6]. In our study, oral triple therapy with once daily amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and omeprazole was safe and effective to treat H. macacae, H. pylori , and H. suis in baboons. Animals remained negative for H. macacae and H. pylori by fecal PCR for over 1 year, suggestive of clearance or long‐term suppression. Despite initial 2‐week post‐treatment negative results, two baboons later became positive for H. suis , which may be new infections or recrudescence. The successful treatment of H. macacae is particularly informative for primate veterinarians.

Further research is indicated to investigate how baboons acquire these apparently prevalent Helicobacter spp. infections. H. papionis was only found in Colony A, while H. simiae was restricted to Colony B and H. papionifaecis was predominantly found in Colony B, with one individual positive in Colony A. We found no correlation between species‐specific Helicobacter positivity and colony or housing room, suggesting that transmission among adult baboons may not be common. Despite a small sample size, we also found that three of the four wild‐born animals were negative for H. macacae, H. suis , and H. pylori , despite having been at our institution for over 14 years and with other baboons housed in proximity. In captive rhesus macaques, newborns are infected with H. pylori via oral‐oral exposure within 24 h of life [47]. Host adaptation to H. pylori also appears to play a role in macaques [48]. While EHS have been less studied in NHPs, chronic subclinical infections with H. macacae and H. cinaedi may be common in macaques [13, 49].

Zoonotic or zooanthroponotic transmission of Helicobacter between humans and NHPs may also occur [50, 51, 52]. Further characterization of natural Helicobacter infections in baboons is thus also essential for biosafety and risk assessment with regard to personnel exposure.

On necropsy, we identified subclinical pathology of the hepatobiliary and/or gastrointestinal systems in 4/6 animals, including a cholangiocarcinoma in baboon #14, representing the first such case documented in an olive baboon. Gall bladder adenocarcinomas have been rarely reported in baboons, with three cases in hamadryas baboons, three in Guinea baboons ( Papio papio ), and no reports in several large retrospective studies [53, 54, 55, 56, 57, 58, 59]. Although Helicobacter was not identified within the tumor microscopically or by PCR, this baboon was PCR‐positive for H. suis and H. macacae in feces and tissues, and positive for H. simiae by fecal culture. Since EHS are associated with hepatobiliary tumors in humans, chronic Helicobacter spp. infection in this baboon could have participated in tumorigenesis [60, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65]. Further investigation of the potential sequelae of chronic Helicobacter infection in baboons is therefore needed to inform clinical management.

Given the apparently high prevalence and chronicity of Helicobacter infection in predominantly healthy baboons, we hypothesize that these organisms may be commensals or pathobionts. We also suspect that conspecific transmission may occur in baboons with isolated colonies having distinct Helicobacter species or strains, but that transmission among adult baboons may not be very common and may be influenced by other factors which merit further study—for example, age of first exposure, coinfection with other Helicobacter strains, or health status. Triple therapy treatment achieved prolonged clearance of H. macacae and H. pylori . This is the first published treatment protocol for Helicobacter in baboons and represents an evidence‐based refinement to the management of this species. However, triple therapy treatment was ineffective at clearing the novel Helicobacter species, and isolates showed antibiotic resistance in vitro, suggesting the response to treatment is likely bacterial species‐specific and highlighting the value of species‐specific assays to guide management and therapy.
