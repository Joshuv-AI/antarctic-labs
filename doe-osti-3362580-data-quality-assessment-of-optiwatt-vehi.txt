 ABSTRACT
 In October 2024, the Idaho National Laboratory (INL) received data from
Optiwatt (Compass Global, Inc.) describing the driving and charging behavior of
electric vehicle (EV) drivers. The data shared had been collected from
approximately 10,000 vehicles and included vehicle specifications, driving
information like odometer readings at the beginning and end of origin-destination
pairs (i.e., trips with identification of home for trip start and end for Tesla
vehicles), and charging information such as charging energy consumed per
charge session and if the charge occurred at home. The vehicle data were
provided from 9 EV makes and 18 EV models, with production years ranging
from 2012–2024, but more than 9,500 of the vehicles were Tesla EVs. The data
includes more than six million trips and more than three million charging events
that occurred between June 2023 to Aug 2024 and collected from California and
the Eastern United States. The purpose of this report is to review the quality of
the data received from Optiwatt and the feedback INL received from Optiwatt
after data concerns were shared with them.

 1. INTRODUCTION
 The Idaho National Laboratory (INL) received data from Optiwatt (Compass Global, Inc) on October
17, 2024, describing the driving and charging behavior of electric vehicle (EV) drivers. The data shared
had been collected from approximately 10,000 vehicles and included vehicle specifications, driving
information like odometer readings at the beginning and end of origin-destination pairs (i.e., trips with
identification of home for trip start and end for Tesla vehicles), and charging information such as
charging energy consumed per charge session and if the charge occurred at home. The vehicle data were
provided from 9 EV makes and 18 EV models with production years ranging from 2012–2024, but more
than 9,500 of the vehicles were Tesla EVs. The data include more than 6 million trips and more than 3
million charging events that occurred between June 2023 and August 2024 and were collected from
California and the Eastern United States. A summary of the data is shown in Table 1.

 The first set of data was delivered in six tables. The “vehicles” table contains information about each
vehicle such as vehicle make, model, production year, battery capacity (kWh) and Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) range estimate. The “customer_info” table provides the EV driver address,
utility name, and utility plan name. The “tesla_drives” and “non_tesla_drives” tables contain data
describing each vehicle trip including start and end times, distance traveled (miles), start and end state of
charge (SOC), start and end odometer readings (miles), start and end latitudes and longitudes, and energy
consumed (kWh). The driving tables originally provided by Optiwatt did not contain information about
trips’ start and end locations, particularly whether the trip started or ended at the driver’s home. As a
result, Optiwatt provided an additional table called “inl_drive_ids_home” that identified trips that started
and/or ended at the driver’s home; however, the data only applied to the “tesla_drives” table. The
“charges” table contained information associated with each charging session such as start and end time,
charging location identifier, latitude, longitude, if the charge occurred at the driver’s home, charger type,
duration (hours), energy used by the charger (kWh), energy added to the battery (kWh), start and end
SOC, power used by charger (kW), power used by the battery (kW). The final table
“charge_interval_data” provided a row for every 15 minutes of each charging session and provides the
timestamp, charging status, battery SOC, and if the session is occurring at the driver’s home.
 The original request had included a need to gain charging and driving insights from vehicles with
ranges of (1) less than 200 miles, (2) 200–300 miles, and (3) greater than 300 miles. However, the first
dataset that was received only contained vehicles with ranges of 200–300 and greater than 300 miles. As
a result, Optiwatt provided five additional tables (“vehicles,” “user_info,” “drives,” “charges,” and
“charge_intervals”) with data from vehicles with a range of 200 miles or less. In this additional dataset,
there are 43 vehicles listed in the “vehicle” table, but only 25 of those vehicles show up in the “charges”
table, 9 show up in the “drives” table, and 8 show up in all three tables. Therefore, the remainder of this
report will only focus on the original dataset that Optiwatt provided that contains data from vehicles with
ranges greater than 200 miles.
 Finally, it should be noted that Optiwatt is a third-party company that provides EV drivers with cost
savings through EV smart charge management either through EV telematics or through a smart charger.
As a result, all driving and charging data evaluated in this assessment is acquired by Optiwatt through EV
telematics. Due to this method of data retrieval, there are some nuances in these data. For example, all
data provided to date is queried every 5–15 minutes (depending on the EV), which can lead to data
incompleteness or data gaps in items, such as the trip odometer reading not capturing all miles driven.
While these nuances should not impact most data analyses, it should be noted here to document these
nuances for future reference. Additionally, Optiwatt has confirmed that in early 2025, they will be
transitioning to a data system for Teslas that push notifications of trip and charging events to improve
some of the issues associated with the data pull method currently used. Also, when evaluating charging
behavior, it should be noted that these data are gathered by Optiwatt, a company that aims to reduce the
electric bills for EV drivers.
 The purpose of this report is to review the quality of the data received from Optiwatt and the feedback
INL received from Optiwatt after sharing data concerns with them.

 2. DIOGENES DATA ASSESSMENT
 INL has developed a data exploratory analysis tool, named Diogenes, for the U.S. Department of
Energy’s (DOE’s) Vehicle Technologies Office’s Livewire Data Project. This tool is designed to generate
low-level metadata and to profile a dataset. Features of the tool include primary-foreign key relationships,
aggregate statistics, and column names. In addition to profiling, Diogenes also produces a quality
characterization that gives detailed data quality information about missing data and erroneous records.
Diogenes uses probabilities to calculate total records missing or in error. For example, this report states
that five records were erroneous, which means that the probabilities of all the records being in error were
summed, and the total was 5. This could mean five records at 100% probability of being in error, or it
could mean 8 records with 50% probability of missing.
 When applying the tool to the Optiwatt data, several issues were identified. The first issue was of
missing data. The tool identified 1,132 records from the dataset as missing. These were records that had a
reference to an ID, but that ID was not found in the primary lookup table. These missing data were
exclusive to the customer and user information tables.
 Another issue was of 0 SOC end states for certain Tesla trips. The tool identified 36 trips that started
and ended with 0 SOC. Approximately 500 records started a drive with any given amount of SOC and
ended with 0. This may relate to another issue the tool found with SOC status in the charge_interval_data
table. There were ~800 records with a “NoPower” status, despite some of them actually receiving some
aggregate load kWh during a charge.
 Finally, a relatively minor issue was of a few records with GPS location coordinates that put them in
aberrant locations such as the Pacific Ocean. These were minor in number but worth mentioning as there
were other vehicles that were flagged as erroneous that had “Unknown Regions” as their identifying
location. From these initial discoveries, further inquiries were launched into the data, discovering
additional problems discussed in the rest of this report.
 3. GENERAL ASSESSMENT OF VEHICLE RELATED DATA
 INL received data for 10,026 distinct vehicles in the “vehicles” table. There does not appear to be any
duplication of vehicles in the data; however, there are 13 households that have three or more EVs,
including one that has seven. Many of these households have multiple vehicles of the same production
year, make, and model. After discussing this with Optiwatt, they believe that some of these households
might own EVs that they rent out via rental services such as Turo.
 4. PARKSANDTRIPS FORMAT
 To keep in line with previous data analysis conducted for the EV Project 2.0, the research team aimed
to structure these data in a similar method as has been used with previous datasets containing both driving
and charging data and created a “ParksAndTrips” table, which combines the driving and charging data.
The table was formatted for consistency with INL simulation platforms and other software services.
Figure 1 illustrates the structure of the “ParksAndTrips” table. In the illustration, a parking event is
represented by the red octagon. The blue lines between parks represent trips. An observed SOC increase
during a park or trip is shown as a green plus sign. Missing parks are represented by the striped, red
hexagon. The trips on both sides of a missing park were combined. The blue, striped lines represent
missing trips. Because these trips are missing in the data, the parks on either side of the missing trips
appear as a single park. Missing trips are identified when the odometer values at the start and end of the
park (i.e., at the end of one trip and the start of the next) are different. The Missing Miles section explores
the nature of the parks with missing trips further. SOC increases indicate a possible charging event during
a park. INL compared instances of SOC increase to records in the “charges” table to account for
completeness in the data and to create an algorithm to identify charging events. This is explored further in
the Charge Inconsistency section.

 5. TRIP DATA
 INL received vehicle information for 10,026 distinct vehicles in the “vehicles” table. However, there
are 9,987 vehicles that have data in the “tesla_drives” and “non_tesla_drives” tables and 10,027 vehicles
with data in the “charges” table. Thus, there are 39 vehicles lacking driving data, and all vehicles appear
to have charging data, including one vehicle not listed in the “vehicles” table. Combining the
“tesla_drives” and “non_tesla_drives” tables provides 6,227,442 trips. Of these trips, 3,791 (0.06%)
appear to be duplicated data, and 9 of these trips have negative odometer readings from one trip to the
next.
 5.1. SOC Inconsistencies
 In addition to the SOC issues noted in Section 2, there are 48,787 (0.8%) trips that recorded more
battery SOC at the end of the trip than was started with at the beginning of the trip (meaning the vehicle
supposedly charged during the trip). About 40,000 of these have an SOC change greater than 3%, and a
distribution can be seen in Figure 2 (note that the red line provides a visual of where the 3% line is in the
plot). The instances where SOC increase is less than 3% may not represent charging events because from
trip to trip, the SOC of a vehicle’s battery can vary due to subtle fluctuations in the battery that cause
measurement hysteresis. However, these trips that had a positive SOC change also had a negative
consumed energy kWh value. After discussing this issue with Optiwatt, the company said this issue is
likely tied to the polling mechanism they use to retrieve the data from the vehicles (data is queried from
the vehicle every 5–15 minutes depending on the EV) and suggested flagging and/or removing these
48,787 (0.8%) trips from the dataset.

 5.2. Missing Miles
 One major issue identified was the number of missing miles unaccounted for in the odometer readings
from one trip to the next, as illustrated in Figure 3. Ideally, all trips would have an odometer trip delta
near to or exactly at zero. However, 5,834,737 (93.7%) trips had trip starting odometer readings that were
much greater than 1 mile when compared to the ending odometer reading of the previous trip. After
discussing this issue with Optiwatt, they said this issue is likely tied to the polling mechanism they use to
retrieve the data from the vehicles (data is queried from the vehicle every 5–15 minutes depending on the
EV) and suggested flagging and/or removing these 392,705 (6.3%) trips from the dataset. Considering the
potential for a 10–30 minute misalignment window and assuming an average speed of 50 miles per hour,
one can assume that the data polling method could miss 10–30 miles of odometer milage, which aligns
well with what is shown in Figure 3.

 5.3. Unreasonably Long Trips
 To assess unreasonably long trips, the research team first leveraged the EPA-estimated range in miles
provided in the “vehicles” table to identify trips that were 1.2 times in length than the estimated range.
This resulted in 2,314 (0.04%) trips identified as being unreasonable in length. Following the
conversation with Optiwatt about the data polling mechanism, the team concluded that most, if not all,of
these trips likely missed a fast-charging session that occurred during that trip. The team also looked at the
trip time to identify unreasonably long trips identified as trips longer than five hours. When applying the
time threshold, 16,304 (0.3%) trips were identified. Of the trips with unreasonably long trip distances,
1,213 (0.02%) of them were identified as lasting longer than five hours. It is likely that the Optiwatt data
polling method contributed to most of these potential errors in the dataset. These data will be flagged and
likely removed prior to analysis.
 6. CHARGING SESSIONS
 INL received vehicle information for 10,026 distinct vehicles in the “vehicles” table. However, there
are 10,027 vehicles with data in the “charges” table. Thus, all vehicles appear to have charging data,
including one vehicle not listed in the “vehicles” table. The Optiwatt data contains some smart charge
management charging sessions; therefore, there can be multiple rows of charging that are associated with
one parking event. Since every charging session has a unique identifier, the trip and charging information
were combined into a long data format to identify multiple charging sessions that happened
consecutively. Then, to transition the charges data into the “ParksAndTrips” table format, consecutive
charging sessions occurring between trips were collapsed into one row retaining the start and end states of
the full charging session.

 6.1. Charging Session Inconsistencies
 There are 12,744 (0.4%) charging events that have negative values for the variable
“kwh_added_to_battery” and 5,915 (0.2%) charging events that have negative values for the variable
“kwh_used_by_charger.” After discussions with Optiwatt and further assessment, most of these issues
were from very short sessions or where very little charging occurred as shown in Figure 4 and Figure 5.
Optiwatt recommended flagging and/or discarding these data.

 There are about 84,000 (2.7%) charging events that have a start time that occurs before the end of the
previous trip. Additionally, there are about 12,000 (0.4%) charging events that do not end before the start
of the next trip. Optiwatt attributed most of these issues to the data polling method that they use. They
also mentioned that the “tesla_drives” and “non_tesla_drives” tables are created independently of the
“charges” table, and thus, no corrections are currently implemented by Optiwatt.
 7. CONCLUSION
 This dataset from Optiwatt contains over 6 million trips and 3 million charging data from 10,000
EVs, which includes over 9,500 Tesla vehicles and a total of 18 EV models from 9 EV makers.
Comparing to EV data previously obtained by INL and/or the U.S. DOE, this dataset is one of the richest
datasets on mainstream EVs with popular make and models that have driving ranges greater than 200
miles. An additional feature of this dataset is that it also contains information on managed charging. Aside
from missing some of the miles driven that have been observed between most trips, most of the data
quality issues identified in this dataset have only impacted less than 1% of the data provided by Optiwatt.
Most of the data quality issues seem to be related to how frequently the data had been polled from vehicle
telematics, which Optiwatt is working on a new data collection mechanism that may improve those
problems. The INL team will continue to work with Optiwatt on possible solutions for addressing the
missing odometer miles in the existing data. However, this issue should only affect a small portion of the
analysis that may be done with this dataset, so the impact to the project remains minimal. Overall, this
dataset from Optiwatt is considered to be in good quality, and it should provide valuable insight into the
driving and charging behavior of mainstream EVs and smart charge management implementation.
