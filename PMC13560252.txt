Aging of hair follicle stem cells and their niche: mechanisms and regenerative therapeutic strategies
Authors: Siqi Xu, Jiming Guo, Ying Kang, Qinjing Wang, Yue Ming, Weixing Chen, Min Wang, Zhijie Huang, Yisheng Huang, Bo Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHair follicles (HFs) are vital skin appendages that perform fundamental functions including protection, thermoregulation, and sensation. Orchestrated by hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs), HFs undergo cyclic regeneration throughout the lifespan. However, during chronological aging, this mini-organ experiences progressive physiological decline, clinically characterized by a marked reduction in hair density and hair graying due to pigmentation dysfunction. This aging process involves HFSC exhaustion accompanied by diminished regenerative potential and differentiation capacity, leading to degenerative changes in the bulge architecture. Concurrently, the niche supporting HFSC homeostasis undergoes multi-dimensional and systemic degradation. This niche deterioration disrupts the delicate balance between HFSC quiescence and activation, further impeding hair regeneration. In this review, we delineate the dynamic anatomical changes throughout the hair growth cycle and describe the alterations of HFSCs during aging. We specifically focus on the mechanisms underlying the multi-dimensional degradation of the HFSC niche at tissue, cellular, and molecular levels. Furthermore, we discuss various therapeutic strategies aimed at ameliorating HF aging, offering potential insights for future clinical translation in hair regeneration. Finally, we propose that integrating spatiotemporal high-resolution technologies with genomic data to further decipher the spatiotemporal behaviors of aging HFSCs and niche cells will facilitate the establishment of a robust mechanistic framework for HFSC and niche aging.Graphical abstract Keywords: Hair follicle stem cells, Chronological aging, Niche, Spatiotemporal disorganization, Hair restoration, Tissue regeneration, Stem cell therapy

Introduction

Hair follicles (HFs) are skin appendages that primarily perform physiological functions such as protection, thermoregulation, and sensation through hair growth and metabolism. Mature hair follicles undergo a cyclic process of telogen, anagen, and catagen phases to produce hair, a process dependent on the proliferation and differentiation of Hair Follicle Stem Cells (HFSCs) [1]. HFSCs are the key stem cell population within the follicle, responsible for regeneration and repair. Their dynamic interactions with surrounding cells and the microenvironment, along with the interplay between the follicle and its supporting tissues, constitute the HFSC niche. This niche not only provides physiological support but also regulates HFSC proliferation, self-renewal, and differentiation capabilities, ensuring the normal operation of the hair growth cycle and playing a pivotal role in hair regeneration [2]. Specifically, HFSCs reside in the bulge region, an anatomical structure that remains relatively stable throughout the cycle, serving as the physical center of the HFSC niche [3]. Beneath the bulge lies the hair germ, where a condensed cluster of mesenchymal cells known as the Dermal Papilla (DP) resides. The DP plays a central regulatory role during hair development and regeneration; its size and cell number directly influence the quality and rate of hair growth, making it the regulatory center of the niche [3]. The precise positioning of these two centers and normal communication within the microenvironment are critical for HFSC self-renewal, proliferation, and differentiation.

Aging is an inevitable process characterized by the gradual accumulation of cellular and tissue damage, ultimately leading to functional impairment [4]. Hair follicle aging is similarly a process of accumulated damage, during which regenerative capacity continuously declines. Due to their unique anatomical location and physiological functions, hair follicles and their surrounding niches are susceptible to the dual effects of intrinsic and extrinsic aging. Extrinsic factors, such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation, pollution, and chemicals, damage the follicle and its supporting tissues, disrupting the microenvironment and leading to functional decline [5, 6]. Intrinsic aging, accompanied by decreased metabolic levels and tissue deterioration, weakens the self-renewal and differentiation capacities of HFSCs [7]. These combined factors impair hair regenerative ability [5], manifesting as hair thinning, miniaturization, shedding, and potentially leading to permanent local follicular degeneration and gradual graying. During the aging process, changes in the components of the HFSC niche are highly specific. For instance, a significant decline in the number and function of DP cells weakens their support for HFSCs [8] ; alterations in the secretory function of adipose tissue lead to the abnormal accumulation of inhibitory signals, hindering cyclic regeneration [9]. Furthermore, mechanosensation, immune signaling, and interactions between vascular endothelial cells and the follicle within the niche undergo significant changes during aging, collectively impacting HFSC function [10–12].

Recent advances in nanobiotechnology offer promising solutions to these limitations. Nanocarriers can be engineered to protect bioactive compounds from degradation, enhance transdermal permeation, and facilitate targeted delivery to specific niche components, such as the bulge or DP. Furthermore, functionalized nanomaterials can mimic the extracellular matrix or modulate the microenvironment by regulating oxidative stress and signaling pathways. Integrating nanomedicine with stem cell biology provides a novel frontier for restoring the regenerative capacity of aging follicles.

This review systematically outlines the multi-dimensional degradation of the HFSC niche during aging at tissue, cellular, and molecular levels. We explore how aging perturbs the delicate balance of stem cell quiescence and activation. Crucially, we discuss emerging therapeutic strategies, with a particular focus on nanotechnological interventions designed to overcome physiological barriers and precisely modulate the aging niche. By bridging biological insights with nanomedicine, this review aims to provide a theoretical basis and new perspectives for future clinical translation in hair regeneration.

Overview: the cycle of hair follicle growth

Before exploring the pathological niche of aging follicles, it is crucial to understand the normal hair growth cycle. Only by grasping the periodic regulatory mechanisms in a healthy state can we deeply analyze the niche imbalance caused by aging and its impact on regeneration, thereby providing precise theoretical support and intervention directions for age-related hair loss. As a mini-organ with regenerative capabilities, the hair follicle completes multiple cycles of hair regeneration throughout its lifespan. This unique ability relies on the self-renewal and lineage differentiation potential of HFSCs. This function is precisely regulated by the niche composed of cells surrounding the HFSCs and the microenvironment; the niche drives the cyclic regeneration of the follicle by transmitting various signals that either maintain HFSCs in a quiescent state or induce their activation [13].

Follicular regeneration manifests as a continuous cycle of anagen, catagen, and telogen phases [1] (Fig. 1). This process is regulated by precise epithelial-mesenchymal interactions between epidermal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts [14]. Throughout the lifespan, follicles spend the majority of time in the telogen phase (except for the first two active hair cycles post-birth) [15, 16]. The telogen follicle structure consists of two relatively stable regions: the bulge and the hair germ. The bulge, located at the base of the permanent epithelial portion, is the physical center of the HFSC niche. Its spatial position is relatively fixed, composed mainly of outer layer HFSCs and inner layer Keratin 6 (Krt6)-positive cells, the latter being an important marker of follicular functional status [17, 18]. The hair germ is located directly beneath the bulge, containing a small number of HFSCs and melanocyte stem cells (McSCs). Its core consists of highly condensed mesenchymal cells forming the Dermal Papilla (DP). As the regulatory center of the HFSC niche, the DP secretes various signaling molecules (such as WNT, BMP, FGF, and TGF-β) to precisely regulate HFSC quiescence, activation, and the progression of the hair growth cycle [19, 20].

Dynamic remodeling of hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs) and their niche during the hair cycle. The hair regeneration cycle consists of telogen, anagen, and catagen phases. While the permanent portion of the follicle remains unchanged, the cycling portion undergoes phase-specific morphological alterations. During telogen, the follicle displays two characteristic structures: the bulge and the hair germ (HG), including dermal papilla (DP). Inner K6-positive (K6+) cells within the bulge secrete BMP4 and FGF18 to maintain outer HFSC quiescence. In anagen, the follicle develops a bulbous base and elongated upper segment, forming the outer root sheath, inner root sheath, and hair shaft, concurrently with thickening of dermal white adipose tissue (dWAT). During catagen, the lower bulbous structure progressively narrows into an epithelial strand, ultimately transitioning to the characteristic telogen architecture. The HFSC niche encompasses both intrafollicular core stem cell populations and extrinsic microenvironments – including the vasculature, immune system, and dermal adipose tissue – which collectively provide activation or quiescence signals to sustain stable cyclical regeneration

When the follicle transitions from telogen to anagen(Fig. 1), regulatory factors secreted by the DP first act on the hair germ region [21]. Activated HFSCs within the hair germ begin to proliferate, generating transit-amplifying cells (TACs) that fill the hair matrix at the junction of the bulge and hair germ [21]. TACs subsequently secrete autocrine Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) to sustain the activation of DP cells and HFSCs [22], and differentiate along precise lineages to form the hair shaft, companion layer, and inner root sheath (IRS) [23, 24]. Simultaneously, McSCs differentiate into melanocytes, which synthesize and secrete pigment to color the hair shaft [25]. HFSCs located in the bulge proliferate deeply to differentiate into outer root sheath (ORS) cells, which, together with the IRS, enclose and support the newly formed hair shaft. As anagen progresses, various follicular structures fuse and extend deeply, eventually forming the typical anagen morphology with a wide base and slender upper portion, accompanied by the outward extension of the hair shaft and root sheaths. As the follicle extends, the increasing spatial divergence between the bulge and the dermal papilla attenuates their inductive crosstalk. When this signaling strength diminishes below a maintenance threshold, the follicle is induced to enter the catagen phase, marking the arrest of hair growth and the onset of structural regression required for the next regenerative cycle.

During the catagen phase, the vast majority of progeny cells derived from the hair germ are cleared via apoptosis [26], whereas a small fraction of non-apoptotic progeny persist to anchor the hair shaft to the bulge region through cell adhesion molecules. Concurrently, the enlarged lower follicular structure gradually involutes into an epithelial strand. The distal end of this strand maintains physical connectivity with the DP, stably positioning the DP at the base of the bulgeand ensuring its close proximity to HFSCs [27], thereby completing the transition to the typical telogen architecture. Upon the initiation of a new hair regeneration cycle, a new bulge forms adjacent to the old bulge compartment. Within this niche, quiescent HFSCs are activated to drive the follicle into the subsequent regenerative cycle, commencing the generation of a new hair shaft.

In summary, the cyclic regeneration of the hair follicle is accompanied by structural and biochemical remodeling of the components within the epithelial-mesenchymal interaction system. The core regulatory structures of this process are the two primary centers of the hair follicle niche: the bulge and the hair germ. Normal HFSCs and their niche play a pivotal role throughout the follicular lifespan. Various follicular pathologies, such as aging, AGA, and alopecia areata, are inextricably linked to the dysregulation of HFSCs and their niches. Specifically, during the aging process, the hair growth cycle is altered, manifesting as prolonged telogen, shortened anagen, and delayed activation kinetics. These changes ultimately lead to retarded hair growth or complete stagnation, and potentially permanent hair loss, thereby significantly impacting the social confidence and quality of life of affected individuals. Therefore, elucidating the multifactorial mechanisms driving the aging of HFSCs and their niche will provide a critical theoretical foundation for therapeutic strategies aimed at maintaining hair integrity and regenerative function in the elderly population. The following sections will systematically elucidate the remodeling processes of HFSCs and their niches during aging.

Alterations in HFSCs and their niche during aging

The multifaceted nature of aging mechanisms governing Hair Follicle Stem Cells (HFSCs) and their niche dictates the complexity of therapeutic strategies. Consequently, a profound understanding of the pathological niche in aging hair follicles is paramount for developing effective treatments. Currently, various in vivo and in vitro models have been established to investigate the effects of aging on the hair follicle and its microenvironment (Table 1: Model Tools for Studying Aging Hair Follicles). Investigating alterations in the follicular niche during aging not only unravels the impact of aging on HFSC function but also aids in identifying novel therapeutic targets. This section systematically dissects the multi-level mechanisms of HFSC and niche remodeling during aging, beginning with changes in HFSC intrinsic activity, followed by an analysis of the progressive histological degeneration of the follicle, and finally exploring the impact of internal and external regulatory factors on hair growth in the aging context.

Alterations in HFSC Activity

Throughout the hair cycle, HFSCs serve as the core functional unit of hair growth, exhibiting strict spatiotemporal regulation. They maintain a quiescent state and sustain their population through self-renewal during telogen, while responding to activation signals to initiate proliferation-differentiation programs that drive new hair growth during anagen. Aging compromises HFSCs primarily through quantitative depletion and diminished stem cell activity, thereby varyingly attenuating their capacity to drive hair growth.

HFSC depletion and progressive exhaustion of the stem cell pool

Within traditional theories of aging, such cellular attrition is typically attributed to the cumulative effects of apoptosis or cellular senescence factors [39–41]. However, multiple studies focusing on aging hair follicles have not detected significant increases in markers of apoptosis or cellular senescence [31, 42]. Furthermore, there is no substantial difference in the quantity of apoptotic cells within HFSC-enriched regions (such as the bulge and hair germ) between young and aged hair follicles [43]. This phenotype-specific observation implies that distinct pathological mechanisms may drive HFSC pool depletion. Among existing interpretations for age-dependent HFSC depletion, the model of HFSC differentiation fate reprogramming serves as the mainstream viewpoint, whereas the cell escape model functions as an experimentally validated complementary pathway. Cells progressively extrude through defective basement membrane (BM) into the dermis, depleting the bulge stem cell reservoir

The first theory posits that during aging, the differentiation fate of HFSCs is altered, leading to their elimination from the epidermis. As organisms age, the DNA repair capacity of HFSCs diminishes. Aging follicles struggle to effectively repair the substantial DNA damage induced by the hair cycle itself, ultimately resulting in alterations to HFSC lineage plasticity. Matsumura et al.43 revealed that accumulated DNA damage in aging HFSCs induces an atypical “stress response” asymmetric cell division. This results in the unequal distribution of the hemidesmosome transmembrane collagen COL17A1 among daughter cells. Cells that lose adhesion to the basement membrane due to COL17A1 degradation fail to maintain a quiescent and undifferentiated state; instead, they become biased toward epidermal keratinocyte differentiation and are eventually shed from the skin surface [44]. As this phenomenon accumulates, HFSCs gradually lose their self-renewal capacity, leading to a continuous decline in the stem cell pool and a significant reduction in follicular regenerative potential, which serves as a key cytological basis for follicular aging and hair regression. Targeting this mechanism by maintaining DNA repair homeostasis and basement membrane adhesion integrity represents a potential therapeutic strategy.

However, a 2020 study offered an alternative perspective on some conclusions of this model [28]. By performing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on the dorsal skin of young and aged mice and employing a rigorous multi-marker validation strategy (defining bulge HFSCs with Krt24, Sox9, and Lgr5, and epidermal cells with Ly6a, Klf5, and Gata3), researchers found that the expression of key stem cell marker genes in aged bulge HFSCs did not significantly decline, nor was there induction of epidermal differentiation genes. This indicates that their transcriptional identity was well-maintained. This discrepancy may be related to heterogeneity in lineage tracing in previous studies using Krt15 as a marker [45, 46], or to lineage infidelity caused by depilation-induced trauma in prior experiments [47].

Despite these differing perspectives, the core hypothesis regarding alterations in the differentiation fate of HFSCs continues to be supported by consistent and robust evidence. On the one hand, subsequent studies from the Matsumura group further validated the physiological relevance of the core DNA damage-COL17A1 degradation-lineage deviation pathway [48]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. integrated physiological aging scRNA-seq data, providing molecular evidence that HFSCs acquire additional follicular lineage gene signatures during aging [4]. Collectively, the available evidence indicates that this model remains the most compelling theoretical framework for explaining age-related HFSC depletion. Thus, the theory of altered differentiation fate remains valid. In addition to the predominant differentiation fate reprogramming mechanism, cell escape represents another important complementary pathway explaining age-related HFSC loss. This process is mediated by the downregulation of transcription factors Foxc1 and Nfatc1, causing HFSCs located near the bulge compartment to lose adhesion to the basement membrane, migrate into the dermis, and gradually become exhausted. Additionally, the aging follicle bulge exhibits reduced expression of HFSC-specific cell adhesion and extracellular matrix (ECM)-related genes, such as Cd34 and Npnt [42]. Intravital imaging further revealed that during the catagen phase in aging models, cells in the bulge are continuously squeezed through pores in the defective basement membrane (BM), escaping into the dermis and leading to pool depletion. Therefore, restoring the expression of these transcription factors and ECM genes to rebuild the BM and restore cell-BM adhesion could be a key strategy to slow or reverse HFSC aging. Furthermore, restoring ECM components can maintain HFSC localization within the follicle, promoting functional recovery and regeneration.

In short, though these two models illustrate HFSC depletion from distinct cellular perspectives, they share a consistent common feature: aging triggers reduced expression of cell adhesion-associated genes in HFSCs. This change weakens cell-cell and cell-BM adhesion, triggering spatial mislocalization and loss of organization, which ultimately leads to niche dysregulation [55]. These findings align with recent multi-organ aging spatial transcriptomic studies documenting widespread upregulation of organizational structural entropy (OSE) in aged tissues [56]. OSE is a standardized quantitative metric for tissue spatial order derived from cellular and extracellular matrix distribution patterns: low OSE indicates well-defined compartmentalization and orderly component positioning, while elevated OSE reflects impaired tissue architecture and increased spatial disarray. In hair follicle aging, increased OSE recapitulates global structural disruption of the bulge stem cell niche at the tissue level. These tissue-scale architectural changes are tightly coupled with the aforementioned HFSC abnormalities (impaired adhesion, aberrant localization), collectively representing multiscale structural and functional deterioration during follicular aging. Collectively, elevated OSE in senescent tissues indicates compromised spatial compartmentalization, further supporting that precise three-dimensional spatial patterning of niche components is essential for maintaining HFSC homeostasis and regenerative potential.

Reduced proliferative activity and a shift toward deep quiescence

The reduction in HFSC numbers precipitates signaling imbalances within the niche, disrupting spatiotemporal coordination and further weakening the regenerative potential of the remaining stem cells. A hallmark of tissue stem cells is the active maintenance of quiescence to prevent pool exhaustion, while remaining capable of induced proliferation under specific conditions to support normal tissue turnover [57]. With aging, stem cell function declines and heterogeneity increases [58], particularly manifesting as increased heterogeneity within the quiescent subpopulation, which exhibits a deepened state of dormancy and retarded activation kinetics. The aging process of HFSCs displays similar characteristics.

HFSC proliferative activity is governed by the interplay between inhibitory and stimulatory signals [21, 59]. The dynamic fluctuation of the inhibitory environment spans the entire hair cycle. Inhibitory signaling is primarily dominated by the BMP pathway, which peaks during telogen. This signal is co-regulated by BMP2 secreted by dermal adipocytes and BMP4 secreted by dermal fibroblasts; together, they strongly suppress Wnt/β-catenin activation via the Smad1/5/8 pathway, preventing HFSC proliferation and differentiation [16]. During this period, the low expression of BMP antagonists, such as Noggin, in the follicular mesenchyme further potentiates the inhibitory effect of BMP signaling [60].During the transition from telogen to anagen, high BMP levels gradually attenuate. In late telogen, dermal adipocyte-derived BMP2 decreases while antagonists like Noggin increase, facilitating the gradual activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling and allowing HFSCs to proliferate and enter anagen.

However, aging HFSCs exhibit impaired exit from quiescence and initiation of proliferation, and show reduced self-renewal capacity upon excessive turnover. This aging phenomenon is closely linked to age-dependent alterations in intrinsic signal response characteristics. Although aged HFSCs can still be activated to proliferate, they require stronger stimuli, which correlates with increased sensitivity to the inhibitory signal BMP4, resulting in a higher activation threshold [31]. Simultaneously, abnormally elevated BMP signals from multiple sources exist in the aging HFSC niche, leading to overactivation of downstream effectors and reinforcing the maintenance of quiescence [31]. A significant increase in inhibitory signals alone is sufficient to decouple synchronized anagen between follicles, disrupting coordination and ultimately reducing hair density [61].Utilizing BMP antagonists (e.g., Noggin [62]) or targeted inhibitors to mitigate the abnormal enhancement of BMP signaling, thereby lowering the maintenance of HFSC quiescence, represents a potential strategy to restore proliferative potential and promote hair regeneration in aging follicles.

In aging HFSCs, Wnt signaling shifts from the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway to the non-canonical Wnt5a pathway. Wnt5a, a representative of non-canonical Wnt signaling, activates the RhoGTPase family member Cdc42 via Frizzled receptors. This signal transduction alters the HFSC cytoskeletal structure and cell polarity, resulting in aberrant spatial distribution. This shift compromises HFSC proliferation, directional migration, and self-renewal capacity, thereby inhibiting normal follicular regeneration [63]. Pharmacological inhibition of Cdc42 can restore canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling, reversing HFSC aging and inducing anagen entry. Thus, Cdc42 inhibition holds promise as a therapeutic strategy to reverse functional loss in aging HFSCs and restore follicular regenerative potential.

Histological degeneration of the HFSC niche

During aging, the tissue architecture of the HFSC niche exhibits multi-dimensional degenerative characteristics, including extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, reduced vascular density, and alterations in secretory factors from adipocytes. These changes lead to disrupted intercellular communication and spatial displacement within the niche. The long-term accumulation of these effects causes niche dysregulation, impairing HFSC self-renewal capacity and lineage differentiation potential, thereby promoting follicular aging. The primary histopathological phenotypes of aging hair follicles are hair thinning and pigment loss.

Hair thinning

In young hair follicles, HFSCs are situated in the typical bulge region beneath the sebaceous gland, a zone that undergoes dynamic morphological transitions during the hair cycle. In telogen, the bulge presents a characteristic globular shape, which elongates into a cylindrical structure upon anagen entry. As aging progresses, the follicle exhibits progressive degenerative morphological changes at the tissue scale, characterized by the volumetric reduction of the bulge compartment and the dermal papilla, as well as thickening of the bulge basement membrane [36, 42]. This process intensifies over time and can be categorized into stages: initially, aging follicles shorten slightly while maintaining basic proportional structure; as regression deepens, the bulge and DP gradually atrophy and may eventually vanish, leaving only the sebaceous gland, while the hair shaft structure disappears, resulting in reduced hair count [43].

The bulge structure is critical for hair growth. The regenerative capacity of young follicles relies on a dynamic bulge retention mechanism. At the end of each hair cycle, the old bulge remains inactive but persists to anchor the hair produced in that cycle; it fuses with the new bulge only after the anchored hair is shed, thereby preserving the remaining HFSC pool [17]. In young follicles, a distinct double-bulge structure is typically observed [42]. However, aging follicles often retain only a single bulge, leading to a decrease in the number of anchored hair shafts and impaired regeneration of new hair, manifesting as hair thinning [28]. Additionally, HFSCs in the aging bulge exhibit significant changes in the expression of ECM and ECM-remodeling genes. This is closely associated with the collapse of the bulge structure and may serve as the pathological basis for follicular miniaturization [28, 34]. The combination of these two factors ultimately leads to reduced hair density and decreased hair diameter. Therefore, effective hair regeneration strategies must address the pathological phenotype of miniaturization while promoting an increase in hair quantity.

Hair graying

Aging of the follicular pigmentary system displays significant anatomical specificity; compared to epidermal melanocytes, follicular melanocytes are more sensitive to time-dependent aging [64]. The function of the Hair Follicle Pigmentary Unit (HFPU) is strictly regulated by hair cycle dynamics. Pigment synthesis begins in Anagen III and peaks in Anagen VI1. During the catagen phase, differentiated HFPU melanocytes undergo programmed apoptosis, and only Melanocyte Stem Cells residing near the bulge are preserved. These resident McSCs are periodically activated to generate melanocyte progenitors, providing a source for pigmentary system reconstruction in the subsequent anagen phase.

Hair aging is characterized by progressive graying, the degree of which corresponds to the severity of follicular aging [65]. Early-stage graying primarily stems from dysfunction of the HFPU in the anagen hair bulb, manifesting as a reduction in melanogenically active melanocytes. Remaining cells exhibit dystrophic hypertrophy and abnormalities in melanosome function and distribution, leading to decreased tyrosinase activity, impaired melanosome transfer, and accelerated melanocyte apoptosis. This process is not directly correlated with a decline in bulge McSC activity. However, the self-renewal capacity of bulge McSCs gradually weakens, further diminishing the subsequent regenerative potential of the pigmentary system [66]. With aging, accumulated endogenous DNA damage elevates MITF and downstream melanogenic genes in early-anagen activated McSC progeny, causing ectopic differentiation into ectopically pigmented melanocytes (EPMs) [67]. Recurrent clearance of EPMs by keratinocytes progressively exhausts resident McSCs and impairs normal hair pigment synthesis. At this stage, once the HFPU terminates function during catagen, the pigmentary system cannot be restored via McSC regeneration, resulting in irreversible hair graying [66, 68]. Consequently, strategies targeting follicular pigmentary aging should focus on delaying McSC exhaustion and promoting their regeneration to achieve functional reconstruction and repigmentation before the terminal stage is reached.。.

Complete whitening marks the terminal failure of the follicular pigmentary system, where the hair shaft completely lacks melanin, corresponding histologically to a near-total loss of bulbar melanocytes. Notably, some follicles appearing white under light microscopy may still contain a small number of residual adendritic melanocytes. Although these cells retain trace melanin synthesis capabilities, they have lost the function of transferring melanin to the hair shaft [65].

Alterations in core stem cell populations within the follicle

One characteristic of the progressive remodeling of the HFSC niche during aging is the gradual degeneration of internal follicular structures. Early molecular events of follicular aging manifest as the modest downregulation of cellular markers; these changes are initially maintained through homeostatic compensatory mechanisms to preserve the integrity of functional units. Thus, the emergence of aging-associated phenotypes often lags behind dynamic imbalances at the cellular level, suggesting that follicular aging is fundamentally a long-term cumulative process of functional degeneration among multicellular components within the HFSC niche. A universal phenomenon in aging individuals is the increase in senescent cells [69]. However, the impact of aging on specific cell types is heterogeneous; for instance, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells are more sensitive to aging-associated stress [70, 71]. Therefore, investigating mechanisms of follicular aging requires attention to the senescence kinetics of distinct cell types and their specific impacts on follicular functional units. Therapeutically, comprehensive intervention strategies targeting distinct cell populations should be developed based on these differences to restore niche homeostasis and regenerative capacity.

Hair follicle dermal stem cells

Hair regeneration is a highly coordinated biological process involving dynamic cellular behaviors dependent on dermal mesenchymal regulation [72]. As the core regulatory center of regeneration, the Dermal Papilla activates the ordered proliferation and lineage differentiation of epithelial progenitors by secreting signaling molecules such as TGF-β, WNT, BMP inhibitors, and FGF in a spatiotemporally specific manner [21, 73]. Without the DP, the hair follicle cannot develop, exist, or function [74]. Maintaining an adequate cell number within the DP is critical for regenerative capacity; when the DP cell count falls below a critical threshold, the follicle fails to enter anagen. Conversely, a sufficient DP cell number not only sustains regeneration but also promotes the transition from thinner zigzag hairs to thicker awl hairs [52].

Hair Follicle Dermal Stem Cells (HFDSCs), as mesenchyme-derived adult stem cells, continuously contribute new cells to the DP during each regenerative cycle [52]. The progressive exhaustion of HFDSCs during aging leads directly to the loss of mesenchymal microenvironmental signals, ultimately disrupting the niche support required for the activation of epithelial stem cells, including HFSCs. Transplantation experiments confirm that aging HFSCs can regain regenerative capacity in a young dermal microenvironment, whereas young HFSCs exhibit functional inhibition in aging dermis, indicating that HFSC activity relies on interactions with the mesenchymal niche [28]. Thus, restoring the function of the aging mesenchymal niche is key to achieving HFSC rejuvenation.

With increasing age, HFDSCs exhibit typical aging characteristics, manifesting as declined self-renewal capacity and impaired differentiation into DP cells, leading to the gradual depletion of HFDSC reserves [35]. When HFDSCs can no longer replenish the DP, DP cell numbers decrease, and functions degenerate. This disrupts epithelial-mesenchymal signaling crosstalk, causing the follicle to enter an irreversible permanent telogen [8]. Molecularly, this process is closely linked to age-dependent dysregulation of AP-1 transcription factor activity. Abnormal persistent activation of AP-1 in aging mesenchymal cells leads to Cyr61 overexpression, which drives HFDSC dysfunction and promotes follicular mesenchymal degeneration [35]. This synergistic aging of epithelium and mesenchyme creates a cascade amplification effect, ultimately resulting in the systemic loss of follicular regenerative potential. Based on this, rejuvenation strategies for the mesenchymal niche targeting HFDSC functional recovery—such as modulating the AP-1 signaling pathway, inhibiting abnormal Cyr61 expression, activating the WNT/FGF axis, or employing young mesenchymal cell transplantation—hold promise for restoring DP function and rebuilding epithelial-mesenchymal signaling interactions.

Melanocyte stem cells

During aging, dynamic displacement failure and dysfunction of McSCs constitute the core mechanism of irreversible pigment loss. Traditional theories posited that stem cell differentiation is unidirectional; that is, undifferentiated stem cells differentiate into functional cells via self-renewal or the generation of transit-amplifying cells [75, 76]. However, recent research demonstrates that follicular McSCs exhibit unique dedifferentiation capabilities regulated by WNT signaling gradients, displaying “chameleon-like” plasticity dependent on spatial positioning [49] (Fig. 3a).

Mechanisms governing Melanocyte Stem Cell (MCSC) differentiation and depletion in hair follicles. a Differentiation trajectory of MCSCs is regulated by the WNT signaling gradient. WNT ligands activate the MCSCs into Oca2 + melanocyte progenitors (Oca2 being a late-pigmentation gene). Oca2+/Stem-cell-enriched genes+ MCSCs can de-differentiate into an undifferentiated state. At sites with the high WNT signaling intensity, Oca2+/Stem-cell-enriched genes- MCSCs terminally differentiate into melanocytes. b MCSCs exhibit chameleon-like plasticity across spatiotemporal niches. During telogen, low WNT signaling in the HG maintains MCSC self-renewal. In early anagen, epithelial WNT ligands activate HG MCSCs to differentiate into melanocyte progenitors. By mid-anagen, melanocyte progenitors translocate to the bulge/ORSup experience low WNT signaling and de-differentiate into MCSCs; meanwhile, those reaching the WNT-high bulb terminally differentiate into melanocytes. c Schematic summary of the reversible differentiation model in young and aged hair follicles. In young follicles, bulge/ORSup MCSCs home to HG for regeneration during telogen, while in aged follicles, MCSCs fail to home to HG, accumulating in the bulge with prolonged quiescence and limited division

In early anagen, McSCs located in the growing hair germ (HG) differentiate into dendritic TACs upon activation by epithelial-derived WNT ligands. These partially migrate to the WNT-active hair bulb to further differentiate into terminal melanocytes. In mid-to-late anagen, a subset of TACs migrates to the WNT-suppressed bulge and upper outer root sheath (ORSup) regions, losing their dendritic morphology and undergoing dedifferentiation to regain stem cell characteristics. In late catagen, most McSCs in the bulge/ORSup aggregate slightly below the bulge and return to the hair germ during telogen to maintain the HG McSC pool and participate in the next regeneration cycle (Fig. 3b).

During aging, the loss of McSC progeny is associated with spatiotemporal disorganization within the niche (Fig. 3c). Sun et al. observed abnormal localization of telogen McSCs in aging follicles, where McSCs were increasingly dispersed into the bulge rather than aggregating in the hair germ [49]. In reality, during repeated regeneration, the migration of McSCs between the stem cell compartment (WNT-negative bulge/ORSup) and the TA compartment (WNT-positive HG/bulb) is not precisely recapitulated. This results in the failure of McSCs to successfully return to the HG region, causing the gradual diminution of the HG McSC pool and the retention of McSCs in the bulge. Unlike HG McSCs, which proliferate actively during regeneration, McSCs stranded in the telogen bulge typically remain quiescent or undergo limited division, failing to effectively produce differentiated melanocytes.

Interestingly, hair graying phenotypes also appear under stress-induced stimulation, and the pathological mechanism is similarly related to McSC niche dynamics. Zhang et al. 50 found that under extreme stress or high concentrations of norepinephrine, McSC proliferation and differentiation significantly increase. This leads to massive migration away from the native niche, moving downward in the follicle or migrating ectopically into the dermis and epidermis, leaving no residual stem cells in the original niche. This indicates that sympathetic nerve activity can also influence McSC differentiation characteristics by altering their dynamic positioning. This further underscores that precise spatiotemporal positioning of McSCs is indispensable for normal function, suggesting that interventions targeting niche dynamics, including cell migration and signal gradient maintenance, may serve as a universal anti-aging strategy.

Synergistic regulation by the peripheral microenvironment

Although various aging characteristics of an organism appear distinct phenotypically, they are intrinsically interdependent; experimentally intensifying one aging feature typically accelerates others through positive feedback regulation [77, 78]. Follicular aging stems not only from local stem cell niche imbalances but also from systemic influences of the peripheral microenvironment. The functional decline of the cutaneous vascular system, immune system, and dermal adipose tissue interacts to accelerate follicular degeneration [9, 79]. The following sections discuss the influence of cues from the peripheral microenvironment on hair growth (Fig. 4).

The aging cutaneous vascular system

The cutaneous vascular system exerts a dual influence on HFSC function. On one hand, it promotes the formation of a proliferation-suppressive environment near HFSCs by producing BMP4, a recognized HFSC quiescence inducer [80]. On the other hand, it delivers oxygen and nutrients to the DP to support hair growth and regeneration [81, 82].

Recent single-cell RNA sequencing and metabolomic analyses have revealed that the interaction between the DP and endothelial cells (ECs) is regulated by metabolic signaling. ECs release EDN1, sensed by the DP, to activate taurine metabolism. This inhibits the expression of BMP4 and SMAD1 proteins, reducing suppression of the hair cycle, and significantly increases the expression of β-catenin-related pathways to promote HFSC activation. Conversely, the DP emits CTF1 signals received by ECs to enhance α-linolenic acid metabolism, increasing EC numbers and promoting angiogenesis. However, in aging skin, the signal secretion capacity of both DP and ECs weakens. Genes and mRNA for the EDN1-EDNRA and CTF1-IL6ST signaling pathways are downregulated, leading to diminished hair regeneration and angiogenesis [12]. Effective activation of these metabolic pathways may represent a molecular target for metabolic regulation in aging follicles, helping to restore hair regeneration and angiogenic functions for anti-aging effects. Additionally, DP-EC interaction during aging is affected by spatial alterations. In aged mice, the number of ECs around follicles decreases, and the association becomes looser and more distant. This may partially explain the reduction in DP-EC signaling interactions observed in scRNA-seq analysis of aged individuals. This illustrates that the composite interaction between the vasculature and the follicle manifests as a positive promotion of hair growth by the vascular system; thus, promoting DP-EC interaction is a future direction for treating aging follicles.

Current therapeutic strategies targeting the cutaneous vascular system include topical agents such as Quercetin. Quercetin has been proven to upregulate hair growth-promoting signaling pathways like Wnt, SHH, and Notch, inhibit the BMP pathway, and promote EC proliferation and migration by activating HIF-1α. These effects facilitate the remodeling of the perifollicular microvasculature, driving angiogenesis and the transition of the hair cycle from telogen to anagen, ultimately promoting hair regeneration [83].

The aging immune system

The hair follicle possesses a unique immune system composed of both innate and adaptive immune cells, including CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, mast cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells, which play key roles in hair regeneration [84]. Under normal conditions, the anagen follicle possesses “immune privilege,” meaning local immune responses are suppressed to avoid attack by surrounding immune cells, thereby maintaining a stable hair growth cycle.

Among these immune cells, Regulatory T cells (Tregs), a subset of CD4 + T lymphocytes, are considered guardians of the stem cell niche [85]. They protect stem cells from attack under chronic inflammatory conditions, and slow stem cell aging by reducing oxidative and inflammatory stress [86, 87]. In the skin, Tregs promote HFSC proliferation and differentiation via the Jag1-Notch signaling axis and the GR-TGF-β3 signaling axis [88, 89]. A marked reduction in Tregs is observed in aging skin, which likely weakens these signaling pathways, reducing support for hair growth28].

Furthermore, follicles chronically exposed to the external environment interact with pathogens in various ways. Innate immune receptors play a significant role in follicular recognition and response to external pathogens, a process proven to promote tissue regeneration and immune modulation [90, 91]. However, immune dysregulation during aging can lead to pathological manifestations associated with chronic inflammation [92], thereby affecting hair growth. Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) is an innate immune receptor that regulates hair growth by inhibiting BMP signaling; its high expression in early anagen facilitates entry into the next growth phase, whereas TLR2 deficiency prolongs telogen. With age, TLR2 expression in HFSCs decreases, attenuating BMP inhibition and leading to prolonged telogen and delayed anagen [11]. Moreover, the expression of the endogenous TLR2 ligand, carboxyethylpyrrole (CEP), decreases in aging follicles [11]. CEP binding to TLR2 promotes HFSC proliferation. During aging, significant depletion of CEP in the HF limits TLR2 signaling, further inhibiting HFSC proliferation and hair growth.

In summary, the follicular immune system plays a crucial role in maintaining HFSC homeostasis and hair regeneration through Treg cells and TLR2 signaling. The age-related decline in immune cell numbers and attenuation of innate immune signals are significant mechanisms underlying follicular functional degradation and regeneration failure. Addressing this, developing drugs that activate TLR2 signaling as an exogenous intervention may help restore BMP signal balance, activate HFSCs, and accelerate anagen initiation [11]. Furthermore, enhancing the number or function of cutaneous Treg cells could improve immune niche support for aging follicles, mitigating the inhibitory effects of chronic inflammation and promoting hair regeneration. Combined with immunomodulators or anti-inflammatory strategies to remodel the microenvironment, this represents a potential therapeutic avenue for alleviating immune dysfunction and boosting HFSC activity [93].

Aging adipose tissue

The regulation of hair growth by adipose tissue is a complex and dynamic physiological process that shows significant differences across physiological states, aging, and therapeutic interventions. Dermal White Adipose Tissue (dWAT) is a key microenvironment for follicles, playing a major role in HFSC activation and cyclic changes via paracrine signaling under physiological conditions. Platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) secreted by immature adipocytes is critical for activating HFSCs [94], while BMP2 secreted by mature adipocytes maintains HFSC quiescence during telogen16]. The dynamic proliferation and differentiation of adipocytes are closely synchronized with follicular regeneration, a feature particularly pronounced in young skin tissue.

Dynamic changes in adipose tissue and its signaling interactions with HFSCs are major factors in the functional decline of aging follicles. In the aging state, dWAT regulation of follicles alters significantly. With increasing age, dWAT thickness increases, primarily due to the conversion of senescent dermal fibroblasts into adipogenic lineage cells [95]. Aging dWAT overexpresses inhibitory signals such as BMP2, Dkk1, and Sfrp4 during early anagen, accompanied by reduced expression of WNT signaling-related factors [29]. The low expression of adipose-mediated WNT signaling combined with abnormal activation of BMP signaling contributes to follicular functional decline, which is especially evident during telogen [9]. Therefore, restoring normal adipose tissue function and balancing WNT/BMP signaling may become an effective strategy for treating aging follicles.

Local factors: basement membrane, hair shaft, and mechanical forces

Mechanical force is a fundamental signal universally sensed by cells and plays a crucial role in regulating the stem cell niche [96]. It can be generated through various means, including cytoskeletal contraction, changes in local ECM stiffness, osmotic pressure variations, and external tensile stress [97].

In young follicles, bulge HFSCs reside between basal layer cells and maintain close contact with a basement membrane (BM) rich in ECM proteins and growth factors [98–100]. As an important niche component, the BM influences HFSC activation by regulating Wnt and TGF-β pathway activities [101]. However, under aging conditions, the BM undergoes age-related changes, manifesting as thickening and stiffening. An abnormally stiff BM leads to increased mechanical stress, which suppresses transcriptional activity and silences bivalent promoters essential for HFSC activation, thereby impairing HFSC self-renewal potential [36].

The hair shaft is typically considered an inert structure of the growth cycle, but it is actually a functional niche component regulating HFSC life activity. It serves not only as a physical scaffold for the HFSC niche but also directly feeds back external regulatory information, such as mechanosensation, to HFSCs. In aging and pathological conditions, persistent miniaturization of the hair shaft causes atrophy of the physical dimensions of the niche, mechanically compressing HFSCs [10]. Via the mechanosensitive calcium channel Piezo1, the mechanical force of hair shaft miniaturization is converted into a biochemical signal of calcium influx. This induces the maturation of Caspase-3 protease, triggering ectopic HFSC apoptosis and leading to their loss [102].

Additionally, aging may affect the physiological role of mechanical forces in the HFSC niche. Studies indicate that applying specific tensile conditions can promote anagen initiation and hair growth in mouse dorsal skin. This suggests that tensile stimulation in the macroscopic environment can balance WNT activation signals with BMP-2 inhibitory signals in the follicle and recruit macrophages to polarize into the M2 subtype, which secretes growth factors promoting anagen initiation [103]. However, this regenerative mechanism is compromised in aging skin, as reduced collagen and elastin in the aging dermis and perifollicular regions may weaken the regenerative effects of tension [104–106]. Experimental data demonstrate that upregulated contractility of epidermal keratinocytes inhibits basal progenitor commitment to follicular differentiation and results in diminished follicle quantity [107].Using elastin and collagen synthesis promoters, such as peptides or anti-inflammatory drugs, to enhance skin and follicular elasticity may achieve hair regeneration effects.

Recent research highlights the SDC4-THSD4-CXCL1 axis as part of a sensory response mechanism transmitting external macroscopic environmental signals to the DP [34]. In aging follicles, DP ECM components are significantly reduced. Specifically, Thsd4, an ECM component expressed specifically at the interface between the DP and hair matrix, is detectable only in young follicles and disappears in aging ones. THSD4 enhances the interaction between the DP and hair matrix via the SDC4-THSD4-CXCL1 axis, thereby promoting hair growth. Studies also show that transient cold stimulation can increase THSD4 expression and promote hair growth in aged mice. This axis may play a role in regulating other macroscopic environmental signals, offering new insights into aging follicles and potential therapeutic targets for anti-aging and hair growth promotion.

Current research on therapeutic strategies for aging follicles

Premature hair loss is one of the most common distresses in modern society, severely impacting personal appearance, psychological health, and social identity. Currently used treatments, including pharmacotherapy, hair transplantation, and laser therapy, can improve hair loss to some extent but are limited by finite efficacy, significant side effects, long treatment cycles, and surgical risks [108, 109]. In recent years, researchers have begun to focus on innovative therapeutic strategies including cell therapy, immunomodulation, and nutritional and metabolic interventions, providing new solutions for aging follicle treatment.

Targeted therapy for senescent cells

With age, senescent cells accumulate in aging tissues. These cells secrete a Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), including pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and ECM-degrading proteins [110], which disrupt tissue homeostasis and trigger chronic inflammation, leading to functional dysfunction [111–113]. Available in vitro evidence suggests that the buildup of senescent cells within hair follicles may modulate telogen retention and retard hair regeneration via SASP-mediated niche remodeling, providing critical clues for understanding the cellular basis of age-associated hair thinning [38]. Nevertheless, systematic in vivo studies are still required to directly verify such effects. Based on this mechanism, research has initiated the development of senolytics, agents that clear senescent cells, as a therapeutic strategy [114, 115]. Senescent cells in the epidermis can upregulate anti-apoptotic proteins BCL-W and BCL-XL to resist apoptotic stimuli and accumulate in the tissue. The use of ABT-737, a small molecule inhibitor of BCL-W and BCL-XL, effectively induces apoptosis in senescent cells. Simultaneously, non-senescent HFSCs exhibit proliferative behavior, promoting the follicle’s entry into the developmentally active anagen phase [116].

However, recent evidence suggests that the presence of senescent cells in tissues is not entirely detrimental; purely targeting senescent cells for clearance may impair normal physiological functions and cause side effects [117, 118]. Senescent cells participate in monitoring the structural integrity of the stem cell niche and can enhance cellular function [117, 118], playing roles in tissue regeneration and repair [119]. For example, in a genetic mouse model of nevi, senescent melanocytes were shown to drive HFSCs out of quiescence via activation of the OPN-CD44 signaling axis, thereby promoting hair renewal [120, 121]. This indicates that certain secretory factors from senescent cells can activate nearby normal stem cells and promote tissue regeneration, suggesting senescent cells may play positive roles in specific contexts.

Therefore, when treating aging-related follicular diseases, one cannot simply target all senescent cells for clearance. While this approach might bring short-term improvement, in the long term, it could disrupt the stability of the normal cellular niche, leading to disease exacerbation. Effective therapeutic strategies should be based on the heterogeneity and dynamic characteristics of senescent cells. From the perspective of stem cells and their niches, precise identification and modulation of harmful senescent cells are required to promote the recovery of tissue function and regeneration.

Stem cell therapy and tissue engineering

Stem cell therapy and tissue engineering primarily promote hair growth by exogenously introducing follicle-associated stem cells or activating the regenerative potential of endogenous stem cells. Studies show that exogenous stem cells can ameliorate aging-induced stem cell depletion through mechanisms like cell homing, highlighting the therapeutic potential of stem cell therapy for hair loss [122]. Although currently largely in the experimental phase, stem cell therapy has broad clinical prospects. Future developments will likely integrate deeply with tissue engineering, focusing on the combination of standardized cell culture and transplantation therapies. Current research on stem cell therapy for hair regeneration focuses on two directions: enhancing stem cell differentiation induction and follicle formation capabilities via tissue engineering [123], and optimizing delivery methods for stem cells and their derivatives to enhance efficiency in target tissues [124].

Regarding stem cell induction, dermal cells are garnered attention due to their accessibility. Dermal cells cultured as 3D spheroids exhibit good hair regenerative capacity, and their derived exosomes can induce de novo follicle formation in skin [125, 126].Optimization of culture conditions, such as pretreatment with hair growth agents or adjusting DP spheroid size, can further enhance 3D cell induction capabilities [127]. For instance, 3D-cultured DP cells pretreated with Tri-SQ express more hair markers at the molecular level and significantly reduce senescence marker expression, thereby improving hair regeneration [128]. With deepening research into the HFSC niche, mimicking the in vivo microenvironment to improve stem cell induction efficiency has become a focus. Researchers have successfully improved induction efficiency by using keratinocyte-derived conditioned media for pre-culture, or co-culturing hair matrix cells with dermal sheath cup cells or vascular endothelial cells [129–131]. Additionally, encapsulating HFSCs layer-by-layer with biomaterials like gelatin and alginate to construct a biomimetic ECM not only expands stem cells but also effectively guides their fate in tissue regeneration, promoting follicle regeneration [132].

Various methods, such as microneedle patches and injectable gels, have been used to optimize the delivery of stem cells and their derivatives. Among these derivatives, stem cell-derived exosomes are naturally occurring bioactive nanocarrier that encapsulate proteins, nucleic acids and other bioactive cargo, and modulate recipient cell functions via intercellular communication [126]. Their inherent low immunogenicity and favorable tissue penetration further make them highly promising therapeutic agents for hair follicle regeneration [133]. For instance, microneedle patches made from hair-derived keratin can directly and continuously deliver mesenchyme-derived exosomes and small molecule drugs to the follicular niche, promoting pigmentation and hair generation within 6 days, significantly improving therapeutic efficacy [134]. Simultaneously, using DP-derived extracellular vesicles (DP-EVs) mixed with oxidized sodium alginate to create injectable microgels helps in the slow release of DP-EVs in vivo. These are absorbed by hair matrix cells, activating hair growth-promoting signaling pathways and accelerating follicle regeneration [135].

Building on this, optimizing the spatial distribution of tissue grafts within the skin is crucial for regeneration. Improving the spatial arrangement of follicular tissue effectively promotes hair shaft emergence, thereby increasing treatment success rates. 3D bioprinting technology has become an important tool in follicular tissue engineering for constructing tissue structures. This technology encapsulates different cell types in biomaterials and forms multi-layered composite scaffolds through layered printing, spatially reconstructing the epithelial-mesenchymal interaction microenvironment and significantly increasing DP cell survival and induction efficiency [136]. Another culture method based on spatially induced epithelial-mesenchymal interactions utilizes cell traction forces. By using a bioprinter to place paired collagen drops containing mesenchymal and epithelial cells adjacently and gelling them sequentially, the two cell types are spatially separated while the epithelial cell aggregation face is positioned closer to the skin surface, favoring hair regeneration [137].

Reconstructing spatial organization

Living systems represent open, non-equilibrium thermodynamic entities [138]. At both the organismal and cellular levels, they rely on system boundaries and internal polarity to maintain normal functional operation [139]. Aging constitutes the process by which these systems gradually lose their capacity to sustain their non-equilibrium state, characteristically involving the loss of elements maintaining system boundaries and reduced internal efficiency [138, 140]. Systems experiencing diminished integrity and internal order consequently exhibit dysfunction, ultimately leading to disease. When attempting to reverse aging, we aim to restore within the system the hallmarks of health: spatial compartmentalization, maintenance of homeostasis, and appropriate responsiveness to external stimuli, achieved through the re-emergence of order and reduction in disorder [141].

Multicellular neighborhoods serve as the fundamental functional units of the skin, maintaining low-entropy homeostasis via specific intercellular crosstalk and signaling gradients, and accumulating spatial transcriptomic evidence has verified systematic remodeling of these domains during skin aging. A 2026 MERFISH-based human skin atlas revealed that aged skin exhibits characteristic cellular redistribution from the reticular dermis to deep perivascular regions, together with dermal–epidermal junction flattening and impaired intercellular communication, which collectively disrupt the spatial organization of skin tissue [142].

As sophisticated cutaneous regenerative micro-organs, hair follicles undergo progressive spatial architectural disorder and compromised functional compartmentalization with aging. The structural integrity of the follicular niche relies on three key components: the basement membrane scaffold, HFSC spatial localization and polarity, and epithelial–mesenchymal crosstalk.

The basement membrane, mainly composed of type IV collagen and laminin, provides mechanical support, cell anchoring and signaling gradient modulation for the follicular niche [143]. During ageing, excessive collagen crosslinking leads to basement membrane thickening, ECM stiffening and impaired matrix elasticity [36]. Together with Foxc1/Nfatc1-mediated transcriptional downregulation of adhesion- and ECM-associated genes in HFSCs, increased matrix rigidity disrupts HFSC adhesive behaviors and intercellular signal transduction, compromises the structural integrity of the epithelial–mesenchymal interface, facilitates HFSC emigration into the dermis, and consequently causes progressive stem cell exhaustion and impaired cyclic hair regeneration [42].

Physiologically polarized Cdc42 modulates HFSC cytoskeleton arrangement and asymmetric division; aging-induced Wnt5a upregulation may hyperactivate Cdc42 and eliminate its polarized distribution, and pharmacological Cdc42 inhibition could partially restore HFSC polarity and hair regeneration in aged mice, suggesting that Wnt5a–Cdc42-mediated polarity abnormality may contribute to age-associated niche spatial disorganization [63].

Additionally, aging tends to downregulate DP-specific THSD4, a key mediator of epithelial–mesenchymal signaling at the DP–hair matrix interface. THSD4 deficiency may impede regenerative signal transmission, while recombinant THSD4 supplementation can reactivate aged follicle growth and matrix cell proliferation, indicating that intact epithelial–mesenchymal interface homeostasis is closely associated with sustained follicle regeneration [34].

Recent spatial transcriptomic studies confirm that hair follicle aging is not an isolated intra-organ event but a systemic spatial reorganization of the entire microenvironment. Aged hair follicles lose spatial compartmentalization, characterized by significantly attenuated intercellular signaling between DP cells and epithelial cells, and markedly reduced activity of key signaling pathways including Wnt and BMP [144]. These age-related changes manifest as follicular structural disorganization, extracellular matrix stiffening, vascular degeneration and aberrant immune cell infiltration [36, 42]. Therefore, effective anti-aging strategies for hair follicles should focus on restoring the spatial structure and functional coupling of the microenvironment to establish a niche capable of supporting long-term hair follicle regeneration.

Complementing static omics and spatial transcriptomics, advanced imaging approaches such as three-dimensional confocal microscopy and two-photon intravital imaging support dynamic, longitudinal monitoring of hair follicles under physiological conditions.In vivo imaging evidence indicates that aging suppresses Foxc1- and Nfatc1-modulated adhesion molecules, prompting HFSC migration from the bulge niche to the dermis and correlating with stem cell depletion and follicular miniaturization [42]. Aging also disrupts the cyclic migration and state transition of melanocyte stem cells, causing their retention in the bulge, impaired pigment differentiation and subsequent hair graying [49]. These imaging tools further reveal dynamic remodeling of the extracellular matrix, vascular networks and immune infiltration, facilitating systematic characterization of microenvironmental alterations during hair follicle aging [145].

Targeted regulation of signaling pathways

Increasing evidence suggests that small molecule drugs and bioactive factors can targetedly regulate signaling pathways in the HFSC niche to promote regeneration and recovery. For example, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell exosomes (MSC-Exo) deliver high contents of key microRNAs like miR-21-5p and let-7b-5p to precisely regulate the expression of target genes such as Cyclin D1, c-MET, and LEF1. This activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and optimizes the secretory phenotype of human dermal papilla cells (hDPCs) [146]. This microenvironmental remodeling not only downregulates ECM-receptor interaction and TGF-β inhibitory pathways but also promotes the functional reversal of aging follicles from telogen to anagen, significantly increasing hair bulb diameter and depth.

Similarly, erythrocyte exosomes deliver microRNAs rich in miR-20a-5p and miR-22-3p to precisely target and inhibit Axin2 expression, thereby activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in DP cells [147]. Furthermore, erythrocyte exosomes carry structural proteins like hemoglobin and keratin, which help strengthen the hair shaft and may exert antioxidant effects, improving the physical and chemical microenvironment of HFSCs. In vivo experiments show that local injection of autologous erythrocyte exosomes significantly increases hair density and the proportion of anagen hairs in patients with hair loss, further demonstrating their clinical potential.

In the future, with deepened research into exosomes, microRNAs, and signaling pathway regulators, combined with precision medicine for individualized treatment, therapeutic efficacy may be further enhanced, pushing regenerative therapies for aging follicles into new stages of clinical application.

Multifunctional composite biomaterials

The complexity of the follicular microenvironment dictates that its regeneration requires multi-level, synergistic regulation. Multifunctional composite biomaterials offer new solutions for treating aging follicles. These materials function through multi-dimensional regulation of the HFSC niche, leveraging advantages in targeting, controlled release, and biocompatibility to provide more efficient and precise strategies.

For instance, multifunctional nanovesicle carriers (Arg-CLAVs) developed based on the self-assembly function of conjugated linoleic acid (CLA) utilize the structural similarity between CLA and sebum lipids, along with arginine modification, to achieve precise targeting and efficient transdermal delivery to the follicular site. Arg-CLAVs directly repair the HFSC microenvironment by regulating the MAPK-ERK signaling pathway, clearing reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reducing SASP levels, significantly promoting angiogenesis and hair regeneration while lowering drug dosage and side effects [148].

Xing et al. constructed a microenvironment-responsive composite microneedle using mesoporous polydopamine nanoparticles (MPDAs) as core carriers [149]. Through physical adsorption and chemical bonding in their porous structure, these efficiently load the small molecule growth factor IGF-1. After the microneedle array breaches the skin barrier, it precisely delivers drugs to the target dermis layer where HFSCs reside. Utilizing the microenvironment-responsive characteristics of MPDAs, such as oxidative stress and photothermal triggers, slow release is achieved, overcoming the bottlenecks of short half-life and low bioavailability of IGF-1 in skin tissue.

Recent research developed a gas-propelled active microneedle array. The local vortex field generated by gas overcomes the limitations of passive diffusion. Loaded with iron-chelated puerarin/quercetin nanoparticles, this system achieves deep penetration and high concentration accumulation in the deep dermal perifollicular region [150]. The system effectively downregulates senescence and inhibition-related signals like p53/p21 and TGF-β1, activates the core Wnt/β-catenin regenerative pathway, and significantly optimizes the secretory phenotype and metabolic environment of DP cells via the puerarin-mediated VEGF/HIF-1α pro-angiogenic axis, ultimately promoting the reversal of damaged follicles from quiescence to anagen.

These multifunctional composite biomaterials, through a combined approach of physical micro-invasion and chemical signal modulation, precisely remodel the HFSC microenvironment, providing new breakthroughs for aging follicle treatment.

Nutritional intervention

Caloric Restriction (CR) is an established dietary intervention strategy proven to effectively delay organismal aging [151]. Furthermore, CR significantly promotes structural remodeling of skin and follicles, resulting in more uniform, denser hair with prolonged growth cycles, while reducing hair shaft shedding and replacement frequency.

Specifically, under CR conditions, the subcutaneous fat layer thins, while the number of capillaries in the bulge region and HFSCs increases, exhibiting tissue characteristics closer to a youthful state [152]. Mechanistically, CR regulates the IGF-1 signaling pathway; it not only delays hair graying in naturally aging mice but also restores HFSC activity in IGF-1 overexpression models [115]. Based on these effects, caloric restriction offers a viable concept for the systemic prevention of follicular aging. By controlling energy intake or applying CR mimetics, age-related metabolic disorders and the adverse effects of inflammatory responses on follicular function can be mitigated. Additionally, targeting CR-related molecular pathways, such as regulating dWAT inflammation and optimizing paracrine signaling, provides new potential targets for developing anti-aging follicle therapeutic strategies.

Future perspectives

The mechanism of age-related intrinsic aging in the hair follicle life cycle is multi-factorial, exerting effects through HFSCs and their niche. Although HFSCs are the core of this dynamic process and their behaviors are regulated by various intrinsic and extrinsic signals, the complexity of these regulatory mechanisms is not yet fully revealed. For instance, age-induced HFSC loss is jointly modulated by differentiation fate reprogramming and cell escape. The synergistic interplay between these two pathways remains unclear, and further investigations are needed to unravel the heterogeneity and physicochemical shifts within the bulge niche microenvironment. Moreover, the communication mechanisms between niches are not yet clear; changes in a single niche component may trigger cascading reactions in the skin microenvironment, increasing research complexity.

Current research on hair follicles largely overlooks spatiotemporal heterogeneity [153, 154]. Follicular niche regulation typically relies on diffusible signaling molecules, the effects of which may form gradients, leading to heterogeneity in surrounding cells. Furthermore, the follicle is a mini-organ that changes morphologically with cyclic regeneration; stem cells in different cycles possess heterogeneity due to arriving at different spatial locations, yet existing studies fail to fully consider the impact of these changes on follicular function [4]. Additionally, while existing lineage tracing and intravital imaging technologies can track HFSC dynamic behavior, genetic markers often overlap within follicular structures, and long-term imaging may introduce bias, posing challenges for in-depth analysis of key genes and signaling pathways [42, 43, 49, 155]. Although single-cell sequencing and in vivo imaging technologies (such as spatial scRNA-seq) have shown immense potential in other fields, their application in HFSC niche research remains insufficient. This limits our comprehensive understanding of HFSC spatiotemporal regulatory mechanisms. How to integrate genomic data with high-resolution spatial information to systematically decipher the dynamic interaction between HFSCs and their niche remains a major challenge in follicular aging research.

Future research should integrate high-resolution imaging technologies with genomics data to deeply parse the dynamic characteristics of HFSCs and their niches, especially regarding functional changes under aging conditions. Through advanced technologies like spatial transcriptomics, we can better understand HFSC spatiotemporal regulatory mechanisms and their interaction with the microenvironment, providing data support for optimizing therapeutic strategies [154, 156]. Regarding treatment, given that the functional decline of aging follicles stems from systemic imbalance rather than local deficits, single-factor or single-pathway interventions are insufficient to effectively prevent, delay, or reverse aging. Future therapeutic strategies should combine multiple modalities, including pharmacotherapy, surgery, and lifestyle changes (such as dietary restriction and circadian rhythm adjustment). A “multi-intervention” approach, by comprehensively regulating multiple biological processes, can more holistically restore aging follicle function. Simultaneously, with a deeper understanding of aging follicle mechanisms, personalized treatment plans based on individual aging characteristics are expected. Through genomic data and biomarker detection, suitable treatment methods can be selected more precisely to maximize efficacy and minimize side effects. In conclusion, the treatment of aging hair follicles is still in a developing stage; future research should focus on multi-level, multi-dimensional regulatory mechanisms, combining advanced technological means and multi-intervention strategies to achieve more effective therapeutic solutions.
