Cost‐Effectiveness Analysis of Surgical Treatment of Varus Knee Osteoarthritis for Middle‐Aged Patients: A Multicenter Retrospective Cohort Study With Over 10‐Year Follow‐Up
Authors: Runhua Zhou, Yanjie Guo, Ruixin Wang, Manrong Xu, Yun Shen, Da Zhong, Ke Rong, Shengdi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundKnee osteoarthritis with medial‐compartment varus deformity is common in middle‐aged adults, and the optimal surgical strategy for this group remains debated, with open‐wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) representing joint‐preserving and joint‐replacing options whose comparative long‐term value is uncertain.ObjectiveTo compare the cost‐effectiveness and long‐term outcomes of open‐wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) versus total knee arthroplasty (TKA) in middle‐aged patients with varus knee osteoarthritis:MethodsThis multicenter retrospective cohort study analyzed registry data from three Chinese university affiliated hospitals. A total of 825 patients aged 45–65 who underwent OWHTO or TKA between 2005 and 2015 were included, with follow‐up through 2025. Outcomes included Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores, EQ‐5D‐5L utilities, range of motion (ROM), mechanical axis deviation (MAD), muscle strength, and total healthcare costs. Costs were assessed from a societal perspective (direct medical, direct non‐medical, and indirect productivity costs), and both costs and health outcomes were discounted at 3% annually. The EQ‐5D‐5L–based quality‐adjusted life year (QALY) was the primary outcome; clinical and functional measures were secondary. Multivariate regression models were used to compare outcomes between groups, and 1000‐bootstrap simulations were used to estimate incremental cost‐effectiveness ratios (ICERs). ICERs were interpreted against a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of one to three times China's 2024 gross domestic product per capita (CNY 95,749–287,247 per QALY). Robustness was assessed with inverse‐probability‐of‐treatment‐weighting (IPTW) and age‐stratified (45–54 vs. 55–65 years) sensitivity analyses.ResultsClinical outcomes at final follow‐up did not differ significantly between OWHTO and TKA groups. OWHTO was associated with a lower cumulative cost (mean CNY 121,784) than TKA (CNY 160,075; p < 0.001). In cost‐effectiveness analyses, OWHTO was associated with better outcomes in WOMAC pain, mechanical alignment (MAD), ROM, and dynamic strength at a lower cost than TKA. Quality‐adjusted life year outcomes (EQ‐5D‐5L utility) slightly favored TKA (71.9% of bootstrap samples), yielding an ICER of CNY 5,441,410.25 per QALY for TKA relative to OWHTO, approximately 19 times the upper willingness‐to‐pay threshold (3× GDP per capita, CNY 287,247 per QALY), indicating that the marginal utility gain with TKA was not cost‐effective. The reoperation/revision rate did not differ between groups (OWHTO 0.95% [3/316] vs. TKA 0.59% [3/509]; p = 0.687), and findings were consistent in IPTW‐weighted and age‐stratified sensitivity analyses. Other ICERs were 146,719.91 CNY (WOMAC function), 53,934.07 CNY (WOMAC stiffness), 115,812.43 CNY (isometric strength), and 167,518.20 CNY (sit‐to‐stand power). Results were robust to sensitivity analyses.ConclusionOver more than 10 years of follow‐up, OWHTO provided outcomes equivalent to TKA while substantially reducing costs. These findings support OWHTO as a cost‐effective option for selected middle‐aged patients with varus knee osteoarthritis.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective cohort study.

Background

Knee osteoarthritis is a leading cause of chronic pain and disability among middle aged patients [1]. In particular, varus deformity with medial compartment osteoarthritis (OA) is common in this group, yet the optimal surgical treatment remains debated [2]. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a well‐established solution that reliably alleviates pain and restores function in advanced knee OA. However, performing TKA in patients in their 50s raises concerns, as younger recipients have a higher likelihood of requiring revision surgeries later in life [3]. By contrast, open wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) offers a joint‐preserving alternative for varus knee OA. Realigning the tibia to correct the varus alignment offloads the diseased medial compartment and can substantially reduce pain while retaining the native knee joint [4]. This approach is often favored for active patients in mid‐life, as it may delay or even obviate the need for a prosthetic knee for many years [5]. Indeed, clinical series indicate that the majority of knees treated with OWHTO do not require conversion to TKA within a decade of follow‐up [5].

Both TKA and OWHTO have distinct benefits and tradeoffs, making long‐term outcomes and cost‐effectiveness important considerations. TKA provides definitive treatment of arthritis but involves higher upfront costs (implants, hospitalization) and potential future revision costs [3]. OWHTO entails an initial realignment surgery that might be followed by a TKA in later years, so the overall cost–benefit of choosing osteotomy first versus proceeding directly to arthroplasty is not immediately clear. Several economic analyses have attempted to clarify which strategy offers better value in middle aged varus knee OA. Some studies suggest that OWHTO is a cost‐effective strategy in this population, providing comparable quality of life outcomes at lower overall cost [6, 7]. However, other analyses have reported that OWHTO can be more costly and less effective than TKA in similar patient groups [8]. These conflicting findings underscore the need for real‐world comparative data with adequate long‐term follow‐up.

We conducted a multicenter retrospective cohort study to evaluate the cost‐effectiveness and long‐term outcomes of OWHTO versus TKA in patients aged 45–65 with varus knee OA. The analysis took a broad perspective, capturing direct medical, direct non‐medical, and indirect costs alongside clinical outcomes (pain relief and functional improvement). We hypothesize that OWHTO can achieve functional outcomes equivalent to TKA at long‐term follow‐up, while resulting in overall cost savings in this middle‐aged patient population.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted at three tertiary care, university‐affiliated hospitals in China, utilizing patient data from the Knee Osteoarthritis Registry. The Knee Osteoarthritis Registry was established in January 2005 as a collaboration between these three hospitals. This registry is specialized for patients with knee osteoarthritis, capturing baseline demographics, laboratory results, and diagnostic characteristics at the first outpatient visit. Additionally, knee‐level variables are recorded, including Kellgren–Lawrence (KL) grade, patellofemoral osteoarthritis grade (PFOA), mechanical axis deviation (MAD), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) scores, and functional performance measures (range of motion and muscle strength). Each patient is scheduled for annual follow‐up visits, allowing the registry to longitudinally track clinical and functional outcomes.

Within this registry and Electronic Health Records (EHRs) from the three hospitals, we retrospectively identified patients with varus knee osteoarthritis who underwent either OWHTO or TKA. The study's primary aim was to evaluate the long‐term cost‐effectiveness of OWHTO versus TKA in this patient population. Through the registry, clinical, functional, and cost data were captured prospectively at the baseline visit and at scheduled annual follow‐up visits for every patient from the date of surgery onward; the complete postoperative course, including the early postoperative period in which complications are most frequent, was therefore recorded for each patient. Because the index surgeries were performed between 2005 and 2015 and the database was censored in January 2025, every patient had a minimum of 10 years of continuous postoperative follow‐up, and the present analysis focuses on the pre‐specified endpoint at ≥ 10 years after surgery. All relevant clinical, functional, and cost data were gathered from the Knee Osteoarthritis Registry and the electronic health records (EHRs) systems of the three participating hospitals. In addition to the cost‐effectiveness analysis, we also compared long‐term clinical outcomes between the OWHTO and TKA groups.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the participating hospital. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. We did not obtain informed consent from participants involved in our study because we used anonymized data compiled from EHRs and the registry.

Participants

We drew our participants from the knee osteoarthritis registry that spans three major hospitals. We focused on patients between 45 and 65 years old with varus knee osteoarthritis. To be eligible, patients had to have undergone either OWHTO or TKA at one of these hospitals. We included only those whose surgeries took place between January 2005 and December 2015, ensuring that at least 10 years of follow‐up data would be available by January 2025.

The inclusion criteria were as follows [9, 10, 11]: (1) Participants had unicompartmental medial tibiofemoral OA (isolated medial joint‐line tenderness and degeneration) severe enough to cause pain and disability in daily life. Radiographically, this was defined as Kellgren–Lawrence grade ≥ 2; (2) all knees exhibited a varus deformity with significant mechanical axis deviation. Specifically, the weight‐bearing line was shifted ≥ 20 mm medially (MAD ≥ 20 mm); (3) eligible patients had OA confined largely to the medial compartment. The lateral compartment had to be nearly normal (preserved joint space without significant degeneration), and no severe patellofemoral OA was present in any included knee; (4) all patients had sufficient knee mobility and stability to undergo joint‐corrective surgery. Full extension or minimal contracture was required (flexion contracture no greater than 10°–15°) and at least 90° of knee flexion was present preoperatively.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients have inflammatory arthritis (rheumatoid arthritis) or other systemic joint diseases. (2) individuals with prior high‐energy knee trauma or fractures affecting alignment; (3) patients with significant pre‐existing comorbidities: advanced cardiopulmonary disease (patients with active pneumonia, recent major cardiac events (myocardial infarction or stroke within the past 6 months), and clinically significant arrhythmias and heart failure (New York Heart Association Class III–IV)), uncontrolled diabetes (defined as self‐reported persistent poor glycemic control with hemoglobin A1c above 9%), malignancy, or other severe comorbidities (end‐stage renal disease, advanced hepatic cirrhosis, severe immunodeficiency) were not included, as such conditions could independently affect survival or functional outcomes; (4) patients who had prior permanent disability or major surgery in the affected limb were excluded.

Eligible patients were identified by retrospectively reviewing the registry and verified via EHR review at the participating institutions. All consecutive cases meeting the above criteria over the defined study period were considered.

Outcome Measures and Data Collection

Outcome measures included both patient‐reported and clinician‐assessed endpoints evaluated over a 10‐year follow‐up period. Patient‐reported outcomes consisted of the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), capturing knee pain, stiffness, and functional limitations [12], and health‐related quality of life measured by the 5‐level EuroQol questionnaire (EQ‐5D‐5L) [13]. Clinician‐measured outcomes included radiographic mechanical axis deviation (to quantify limb alignment), knee range of motion (flexion–extension arc in degrees), and muscular strength. Isometric and dynamic knee flexion strength were measured with standardized protocols and expressed as a percentage of the contralateral limb, used as the within‐patient reference limb. We note that, because a substantial proportion of patients had bilateral disease, the contralateral limb was not necessarily unaffected; the implications of this limb‐symmetry approach are addressed in the Discussion. Functional performance was assessed using the 30‐s sit‐to‐stand test, which counts the number of chair rises in 30 s and is a validated indicator of lower‐limb strength and endurance in knee OA. The major complications were categorized by Clavien–Dindo Grading.

Outcome data were obtained from routine follow‐up visits which documented at the Registry and EHRs by five trained physicians in three hospitals who were not involved in the procedure of intervention either of TKA or OWHTO. Cost outcomes were also collected from a broad (societal) perspective, encompassing direct medical costs (hospital care, surgery, rehabilitation, medications), direct non‐medical costs related to care (patient transportation and other out‐of‐pocket expenses), and indirect costs due to productivity loss (wage losses due to missed work). Cost data were extracted from hospital billing systems and supplemented by patient self‐reports for non‐medical expenditures and income losses. All costs were converted to 2015 Chinese Yuan (CNY), with costs from earlier years adjusted to 2015 prices using the consumer price index. Consistent with standard economic evaluation practice, costs and outcomes occurring beyond the first year post‐surgery were discounted at an annual rate of 3% to account for time preference.

Standardization and quality control across centers: To maintain consistency over the study period and across the three participating tertiary, university‐affiliated centers, all procedures were performed by fellowship‐trained arthroplasty or knee‐preservation surgeons according to a common written protocol agreed by the registry steering committee. OWHTO was performed using a medial open‐wedge technique with a fixed‐angle locking plate and pre‐operative digital planning of the correction angle, and TKA was performed with a measured‐resection technique; implant selection followed each center's standard practice. Surgical indications, peri‐operative care pathways, and the standardized rehabilitation programme were harmonized through the registry standard operating procedures, and new surgeons underwent a proctoring period before contributing cases. Radiographic measurements (mechanical axis deviation, KL grade) and patient‐reported outcomes were collected using identical instruments and case‐report forms at all sites, and a central data‐monitoring committee performed periodic source‐data verification and range checks to ensure data quality. Inter‐rater reliability for radiographic grading was confirmed periodically among the participating radiologists.

Statistical Analysis

Initial comparisons of baseline characteristics and long‐term outcomes were performed across different patient groupings using independent samples t‐tests for continuous variables and chi‐square tests for categorical variables.

We compared the long‐term effectiveness and costs of OWHTO versus TKA in both intention‐to‐treat (ITT) and per‐protocol (PP) population. The analysis was conducted at the patient level because costs and utility occur at the patient level and cannot be disaggregated by knee. For knee‐specific indicators, the more severely affected knee was selected for analysis among patients with bilateral knee involvement. Specifically, for indicators where higher values denote greater severity (KL grade, PFOA grade, MAD, and WOMAC scores), the maximum value of the two knees was used. For the patient‐level functional endpoints (ROM, isometric and dynamic flexion strength, and the sit‐to‐stand test), the mean of the two knees was used in bilateral patients to avoid overstating the between‐group difference; a sensitivity analysis using the worse (minimum) knee did not materially change the conclusions. This patient‐level summarization was applied identically to both groups. Treatment effects were estimated using multivariate linear regression adjusting for baseline characteristics. The reference group for treatment was OWHTO (coded as 0), with TKA coded as 1. Continuous total cost data were approximately normally distributed. Covariates included demographic factors (gender, age‐squared, age, BMI, occupation, education, insurance type, living alone), baseline lifestyle (smoking, alcohol use), baseline conditions (paracetamol and NSAID use, walking aid, osteoporosis, hypertension, diabetes, COPD, ARDS, or emphysema, peripheral vascular disorder, arthritis in other joints), baseline clinical characteristics (knee involved, Kellgren–Lawrence grade, Patellofemoral OA grade, MAD, ROM, isometric and dynamic flexion strength, 30‐s sit‐to‐stand test, WOMAC), and baseline utility.

Handling of missing data: Missing outcome and cost data were addressed under a missing‐at‐random (MAR) assumption using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE). Ten imputed datasets were generated; each incomplete variable was modeled on the full set of baseline covariates listed above together with the treatment indicator and the observed components of cost and outcome, so that the imputation model was at least as rich as the analysis model. Predictive mean matching was used for continuous variables to preserve their empirical distributions. Estimates were combined across imputations using Rubin's rules. The plausibility of the MAR assumption was supported by a logistic‐regression analysis of loss to follow‐up (Table S2), in which baseline characteristics did not systematically predict attrition; complete‐case (per‐protocol) analyses were performed in parallel as a sensitivity check.

Propensity‐score sensitivity analysis: To address potential confounding by indication inherent to the non‐randomized design, we performed an inverse‐probability‐of‐treatment‐weighting (IPTW) sensitivity analysis. A propensity score for receipt of TKA was estimated by logistic regression on the full baseline covariate set, and stabilized weights were calculated and truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to limit the influence of extreme weights. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values below 0.10 considered negligible. The incremental cost and incremental utility of TKA versus OWHTO were then re‐estimated in the weighted sample and compared with the primary covariate‐adjusted estimates [14].

Pre‐specified subgroup analysis by age: Because the trade‐off between joint preservation and arthroplasty may differ across the age range, we conducted a fully adjusted, bootstrapped cost‐effectiveness re‐analysis within two pre‐defined age strata (45–54 and 55–65 years). Within each stratum the same covariate set, multiple‐imputation procedure, and 1000‐replicate bootstrap were applied, and stratum‐specific ICERs and the probability of OWHTO being dominant were derived (Table S5).

Cost‐effectiveness threshold: In accordance with the China Guidelines for Pharmacoeconomic Evaluations, ICERs were interpreted against a willingness‐to‐pay threshold of one to three times the 2024 national gross domestic product per capita (CNY 95,749), corresponding to CNY 95,749–287,247 per QALY [15, 16, 17]. Incremental net monetary benefit was calculated at the upper threshold to summarize the cost‐utility comparison.

Missing values at the endpoint were addressed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE). Ten imputed datasets were generated using the “mi impute chained” procedure in Stata, incorporating all baseline covariates and the long‐term endpoint outcomes. The final estimates were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin's rules.

Cost‐effectiveness estimates were derived from 1000 non‐parametric bootstrap samples. For each bootstrap replicate, multivariate linear regression was performed to estimate incremental effects (coefficients of TKA relative to OWOWHTO). ICERs were calculated as the ratio of incremental cost to incremental effect. Uncertainty was characterized using 95% confidence intervals calculated via the percentile method.

To ensure consistency in the ICER calculations, the incremental effect for all outcomes was standardized such that higher values consistently denote superior health improvement. For indicators where higher values represent worse health (MAD, WOMAC scores), the incremental effect was calculated as the regression coefficient multiplied by −1. For indicators where higher values represent better health (ROM, flexion strength, sit‐to‐stand test, utility), the regression coefficient was used directly.

We also analyzed the factors associated with study attrition. This analysis was conducted at patient level, as missing data occurs at individual level rather than knee level. For patients with bilateral knee involvement, baseline knee‐specific indicators were represented by the more symptomatic side. Specifically, for indicators where higher values denote greater severity (KL grade, Patellofemoral OA grade, MAD, and WOMAC scores), the maximum value of the two knees was used. For the patient‐level functional endpoints (ROM, isometric and dynamic flexion strength, and the sit‐to‐stand test), the mean of the two knees was used in bilateral patients to avoid overstating the between‐group difference; a sensitivity analysis using the worse (minimum) knee did not materially change the conclusions. This patient‐level summarization was applied identically to both groups. A multivariable logistic regression was utilized to assess the missing at random (MAR) assumption. The model adjusted for all baseline demographic and clinical characteristics listed in the methods section.

Results

Participants

After applying the eligibility criteria, a total of 825 patients were included in the study. Of these, 316 patients underwent OWHTO and 509 underwent TKA. The flow diagram of the study is shown in Figure 1.

Long‐Term Follow‐Up Outcomes of Patients Undergoing OWHTO Versus TKA

In contrast, cost outcomes favored OWHTO: the long‐term cumulative cost was significantly lower for OWHTO compared to TKA (p < 0.001). This cost advantage was driven primarily by the lower index surgery cost in the OWHTO group, while all other cost components (hospitalization, rehabilitation, follow‐up care, medications, and indirect costs) showed no significant differences between the two treatment strategies.

Discussion

Principal Findings

In our multicenter cohort, OWHTO and TKA yielded comparable long‐term outcomes in middle‐aged patients with varus knee osteoarthritis. As hypothesized, both procedures delivered durable pain relief and functional improvements over more than a decade of follow‐up. There were no significant differences in any of the clinical outcome measures between the two groups. At the same time, OWHTO was associated with substantially lower overall costs compared to TKA, primarily due to lower initial surgical expenses. Taken together, these findings indicate that the joint‐preserving osteotomy provided similar patient benefits to knee replacement while reducing the overall treatment costs in this population.

Long‐Term Effectiveness and Cost‐Effectiveness

Our findings indicate that OWHTO can achieve long‐term symptom relief on par with TKA while substantially reducing overall costs. Clinically, the joint‐preserving approach yielded comparable improvements in pain, function, and alignment by the final follow‐up, which is consistent with evidence that OWHTO effectively relieves pain and improves knee function in varus knee OA [5]. Importantly, many osteotomy patients in our cohort avoided or significantly delayed the need for a knee replacement. This observation aligns with reports that the mean survival of an OWHTO before requiring TKA is roughly 12 years, with only about 36% of patients converting to arthroplasty within 10 years [18]. Economically, this delay translated into meaningful cost savings: the OWHTO group had significantly lower cumulative treatment costs over 10 years, supporting the notion that OWHTO provides good value for middle‐aged patients [6].

Comparison With Previous Studies and Conversion to TKA

However, our results diverge from some recent modeling studies. For example, a 2025 Markov analysis (Canadian public payer perspective) found OWHTO to be slightly more costly and less effective than TKA, with unicompartmental knee arthroplasty emerging as the most cost‐effective option in a younger cohort. Such discrepancies likely reflect differences in assumptions and context [8]. The cost‐effectiveness of OWHTO is highly sensitive to factors like the rate of conversion to TKA and post‐conversion quality of life decrements [6]. In our real‐world cohort, the conversion rate was modest, and those who did eventually undergo TKA did not experience unusually poor outcomes. In fact, evidence suggests that a prior OWHTO does not dramatically compromise subsequent arthroplasty results: one meta‐analysis noted that about 93% of TKAs performed after an OWHTO were still intact at 10 years, compared to 98% for primary TKAs, and a separate long‐term series reported only a 6% revision rate following TKA in patients with a history of OWOWHTO [19]. These data underscore that the joint‐preserving strategy can be pursued without sacrificing future surgical success [18]. Taken together, our findings support OWHTO as a durable, cost‐effective surgical option for appropriately selected middle‐aged patients. It offers equivalent long‐term clinical benefits to TKA while avoiding the higher upfront and downstream costs of early knee replacement, reinforcing the view of OWHTO as a valuable joint‐preserving treatment in this population.

Clinical Implications and Patient Selection

In practical terms, patients who underwent osteotomy in our cohort experienced durable pain relief and functional improvements comparable to those seen with TKA, confirming that the joint preserving approach effectively alleviates symptoms in varus knee osteoarthritis. There were no significant differences in any of the clinical outcome measures between the two groups by the end of follow‐up, indicating that OWOWHTO matched TKA in pain reduction and functional gains over the long‐term. This outcome aligns with prior studies reporting sustained clinical improvements after OWOWHTO, even in advanced cases [18]. Importantly, because OWOWHTO preserves the native joint, many patients in our study avoided or substantially postponed the need for a knee replacement. This finding is consistent with large cohort data showing that OWOWHTO can delay the requirement for arthroplasty by roughly a decade; for example, one analysis reported a mean osteotomy survival of approximately 12 years with only about one third of patients needing TKA within 10 years [19]. Younger patients especially seem to benefit from this strategy. Registry data suggest that OWOWHTO can often serve as a definitive treatment in patients under 60, sparing them from early prosthetic surgery. From a cost perspective, the delay in progression to TKA translated into significant savings for the osteotomy group. On average, the OWHTO patients incurred total treatment costs over 10 years that were around one quarter lower than those of the TKA patients, largely due to avoiding the high upfront implant and hospitalization expenses associated with early knee replacement. These real‐world findings reinforce prior economic models in middle‐aged patients, which concluded that choosing OWHTO in this demographic can yield comparable quality of life outcomes to immediate TKA at a lower overall cost. In other words, the OWOWHTO approach delivered equivalent long‐term benefits to TKA but at a reduced cost, underscoring the value of a joint preserving strategy in this patient population.

Strengths and Limitations

Several strengths of our study merit mention, including the large multicenter cohort, over a decade of follow‐up, and comprehensive assessment of both costs and clinical outcomes. We also conducted rigorous sensitivity analyses (both intention‐to‐treat with multiple imputation and per‐protocol), which consistently confirmed our findings. However, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the retrospective observational design (using registry and EHR data) introduces potential selection bias and confounding by indication, as treatment allocation was not randomized. We adjusted for measured baseline variables, but unmeasured differences between patients receiving OWHTO versus TKA could still influence outcomes. Second, although we now report a pre‐specified, fully adjusted cost‐effectiveness analysis stratified by age (45–54 vs. 55–65 years), the study was not powered for subgroup comparisons and residual differential effects by sex or disease severity cannot be excluded. Third, although the follow‐up of ≥ 10 years is substantial, it may still be insufficient to capture all long‐term failures or late revisions. The incidence of revision surgeries and major complications was low in both groups (as reported in our Tables S1–S5), limiting the statistical power to detect between‐group differences for these infrequent events. Additionally, some patients were lost to follow‐up over the decade; while our attrition analysis showed no major baseline differences between those retained and those lost, we cannot entirely exclude attrition bias. Moreover, the cost data relied in part on patient self‐reports for non‐medical expenses, introducing possible recall bias, and our cost estimates reflect the Chinese healthcare setting, which may limit generalizability to other health systems. In particular, the absolute cost difference between procedures is sensitive to local prices: in many Western health systems implant and operating‐room costs and the relative labor cost of rehabilitation differ substantially from those in China, so the magnitude of the cost saving with OWHTO, and hence the position of each ICER relative to the local willingness‐to‐pay threshold, which itself varies widely across countries, may differ even if the direction of the comparison is preserved. Our self‐reported indirect costs were valued using a human‐capital approach; the friction‐cost method would yield smaller productivity costs [20], although this would, if anything, further favor the lower‐cost OWHTO strategy. These results should therefore be extrapolated to other settings with caution and ideally re‐estimated using local unit costs and thresholds. Finally, our analysis compared only OWHTO and TKA, without including other treatment options (such as unicompartmental knee arthroplasty or non‐surgical management), so our conclusions are specific to these two surgical interventions. In addition, muscle strength was expressed as a percentage of the contralateral limb; because 67% of our cohort had bilateral disease, the contralateral limb is frequently not truly unaffected, and such limb‐symmetry indices are known to overestimate recovery when the reference limb is itself impaired [21]. The reported strength values may therefore understate the absolute deficit in both groups, although this bias applies equally to OWHTO and TKA and is unlikely to affect the between‐group comparison. We also included knees with Kellgren–Lawrence grade 2 disease, which is not always considered a typical indication for arthroplasty; however, surgical decisions in this cohort were based on the full clinical picture rather than radiographs alone, consistent with evidence that radiographic grade correlates only moderately with symptoms and should not be the sole determinant of treatment [22]. We emphasize that in this cohort the small number of KL grade 2 knees were not treated with arthroplasty on the basis of radiographs alone; TKA was reserved for patients with severe, disabling, and refractory symptoms after an adequate trial of non‐operative management, and the choice between joint‐preserving and joint‐replacing surgery was individualized to each patient's overall clinical presentation. Reassuringly, a sensitivity analysis restricted to KL grade ≥ 3 did not change the conclusions, indicating that the between‐group comparison was not driven by the inclusion of milder radiographic disease. Finally, because revision and major complications were rare, the study was underpowered for these specific endpoints and no formal sample‐size calculation was performed for them; the corresponding comparisons should be regarded as exploratory. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable real‐world evidence, and the overall consistency of the results strengthens confidence in the comparative long‐term effectiveness and cost‐efficiency of OWHTO versus TKA in middle‐aged varus knee OA patients.

Conclusion

This study compared clinical and economic outcomes for individual patients with varus knee osteoarthritis. Over 10 years, both surgical options yielded similar pain relief. Functional improvements were also comparable. The osteotomy approach consistently resulted in lower total costs per patient. This suggests that osteotomy matches the patient level benefits of knee replacement while reducing treatment expenses. Future research should involve prospective trials and diverse populations. Overall, the findings underscore the value of joint preservation in achieving good outcomes at a lower cost per patient.
