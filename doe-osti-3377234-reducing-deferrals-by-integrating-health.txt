Executive Summary
The U.S. Department of Energy’s Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP) improves
energy efficiency and household health for low-income families, yet a substantial number
of otherwise eligible homes are deferred from due to health and safety concerns in their
homes, including mold, moisture damage, or structural deficiencies. These deferred
homes are often occupied by marginalized or otherwise vulnerable residents who may be
exposed to elevated indoor air pollutant concentration. Integrating Healthy Homes (HH)
interventions with weatherization has been proposed as a strategy to reduce deferrals,
address environmental justice concerns, and improve health outcomes. However, limited
data on the health and economic implications of such integration exist.

This study aimed to assess the potential health benefits and savings-to-investment ratio
(SIR) associated with integrating Healthy Homes interventions into weatherization
programs for homes typically deferred from WAP services. We collected indoor air
pollutant data from homes undergoing health home renovations, including fine particulate
matter (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), carbon monoxide (CO), and formaldehyde, with
sampling times up to one month. Except for fomaldehyde, which was measured using
passive UMEx badges, the other metrics were measured using the airQ Pro indoor air
quality monitor.
Though the goal had been 60 homes, with commitments from local weatherization
agencies to support the project, we encountered multiple major challenges in achieving
the intended sample size, including:
 • We lost 15 months (of 36) due to a transition from the University of Illinois at
 Urbana-Champaign to the Champaign County Regional Planning Commission
 (RPC), during which the grant had not moved
 • Between award and delayed startup following the move to RPC, multiple partnering
 agencies experienced leadership turnover and struggled to support the project as
 new leadership settled in
 • At about the time that we were able to start the field campaign, the state of Illinois
 weatherization program introduced a new energy audit system which created
 substantial challenges for agencies; indeed, the partnering agency in Chicago
 informed us that they would finally be able to better support the project about 2
 months before project end, citing the new audit system as the biggest cause of prior
 limited support bandwidth.

As a result, only nine homes were completed in the project.

Indoor air pollutant data from these nine homes indicated substantial variability in
changes to indoor pollutant concentrations following our interventions. On average, indoor
concentrations of PM2.5, NO2, and formaldehyde increased between pre- and
post-intervention measurement periods, while carbon monoxide concentrations
decreased. Given the small sample size, none of these changes was statistically

 ii

significant, and they cannot be associated with any particular health outcomes or other
potential impacts.

In individual homes, increases in contaminants can often occur for reasons unrelated to
the interventions, such as differences in cooking, cleaning, etc. Over a larger sample, as
had been planned, we would expect that these individual increases would average out.
With a small sample size such as what we were able to achieve, having an average
increase is plausible. However, all changes in contaminant levels were not statistically
significant with a sample size this small.

Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence that healthy homes
interventions are a disbenefit to public health or should be excluded from weatherization
programs. Rather, our results reflect several other key limitations of the available data.
Post-intervention measurements were collected at varying times following renovations,
potentially capturing short-term increases in indoor pollutants related to construction
activities, new materials, or disturbed dust that may diminish over time. The small sample
size and limited geographic scope further constrain generalizability. Additionally, benefits
related to unmeasured exposures, including bioaerosols, moisture other than indoor
humidity (e.g. damp foundations), and mold could not be quantified despite their influence
over deferral decisions. Bioaerosols and mold were not measured because there are no
accepted established standardized measurement protocol for representative results, and
there are also no standards regarding acceptable levels.

While integrating Healthy Homes interventions remains a promising strategy for reducing
deferrals and addressing health inequities, robust conclusions about health benefits and
cost-effectiveness will require larger studies, standardized post-intervention monitoring
periods, broader geographic coverage, and expanded exposure assessment. Future work
addressing these gaps will be critical for informing evidence-based updates to WAP
deferral guidance and SIR calculations that more fully capture health-related impacts.

1. Introduction
Housing is a critical social determinant of health and well-being.1–3 Poor housing
conditions such as dampness, water leaks, and inadequate heating are linked to several
health conditions, such as respiratory infections, asthma, lead poisoning and injuries,4 and
the risk of poor health increases with the number of housing challenges present.5 Studies
show that improving housing quality can have positive effects on the health and well-being
of low-income families, but the cost of better housing and unaffordable housing strain
family resources needed for housing stability and health-promoting investments6.

The Weatherization Assistance Program (WAP), administered by the U.S. Department of
Energy (DOE), has long served as a critical resource for low-income households, aiming to
improve energy efficiency, reduce utility costs, and enhance overall living conditions.7
Despite its successes, a significant challenge persists in improving utilization of the
program. A substantial number of eligible homes are deferred from receiving
weatherization services due to health and safety concerns. These homes often house
vulnerable populations and frequently exhibit elevated levels of indoor air pollutants,
including carbon monoxide (CO), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon
dioxide (CO₂), formaldehyde, and mold.8 Despite growing attention to these exposures, the
potential health and economic implications of pollutant reduction in deferred homes
remain underexamined.

The health effects associated with indoor air pollution are well documented. Short-term
and long-term exposure to pollutants like PM2.5 and NO2 has been linked to respiratory and
cardiovascular conditions, increased hospital admissions, and premature mortality.9,10
Given the substantial public health impact of these pollutants, any measures to reduce
exposure should have a health benefit for impacted populations.

Healthy homes (HH) interventions, which aim to address critical infrastructure and home
maintenance challenges associated with exposure to indoor air pollutants and other
contaminants (e.g., mold, pests, etc.), are a promising strategy to improve indoor
environmental conditions in homes. Integrating HH measures alongside other
weatherization interventions would provide a clear opportunity to address environmental
justice concerns from both an energy use standpoint and an indoor environmental quality
standpoint.

A major gap remains for the routine implementation of HH measures: the lack of
systematic and measured data quantifying the health-related benefits of healthy homes
(HH) measures when implemented alongside weatherization. The current WAP eligibility
considerations do not consider the potential benefits of additional HH measures, which
restricts the ability of policy makers to incorporate health benefits into WAP's deferral
guidelines. Limited research exists on how measurable improvements in indoor air quality

 1

could yield reductions in healthcare utilization and generate cost savings when applied to
deferral populations currently excluded from weatherization considerations.

Therefore, this study aimed to quantify the potential health benefits associated with
addressing indoor air pollutant sources in WAP-eligible homes. We conducted a scoping
review to identify causal links between indoor pollutants and health outcomes, with an
emphasis on outcomes that can be translated into cost estimates using national and
state-level health databases. Then, we reviewed the literature to identify appropriate
concentration-response functions to estimate the avoided health impacts due to
reductions in indoor pollutant concentrations as a result of healthy homes retrofit projects.
The estimated avoided health impacts will be used to generate health-related cost savings
estimates and the savings-to-investment ratio (SIR) that can be used to assess the cost-
effectiveness of integrating healthy homes retrofits into existing WAP programs.

1.1. Health Effects Associated with Indoor Contaminants
People spend on average nearly 90% of their time each day indoors.11 Given the substantial
amount of time spent indoors each day, it is important to consider how indoor
environmental factors impact health. Indoor environments expose their occupants to a
number of potential hazards, including particulate matter, gases (e.g., ozone and oxides of
nitrogen), and bioaerosols from mold and mildew. These pollutants come from many
sources, including typical household activities like cooking, sweeping, and vacuuming,
from everyday appliances like gas stoves and furnaces, and from the infiltration of outdoor
air (e.g., via mechanical or natural ventilation). Importantly, many of these sources are
modifiable, suggesting that there is an opportunity to improve public health at the
household level by addressing some of the more common sources of indoor air pollutants.

Reducing Deferrals by Integrating Healthy Homes with Weatherization aimed to address
some of these modifiable indoor hazards that would otherwise defer a home from
weatherization programs. Part of this work involved quantifying the health benefits (as
avoided adverse health outcomes) that might result from improvements to the structure
and function of homes. To inform this work, we first reviewed the existing literature on the
health effects associated with exposure to common household pollutants. We focused on
pollutants that are typical in North American and other high income region homes; that is,
we excluded a discussion of pollutants that result from, for example, solid fuel use in low-
and middle-income countries, as these exposures are atypical in the United States.

1.1.1. Particulate Matter (PM)
Particulate matter (PM) is one of the most frequently studied indoor contaminants.
Particulate matter consists of suspended solids and droplets in the air; it is not a single
type of pollutant but rather a mixture. PM is therefore most often differentiated by size,
including PM10 (particles with an aerodynamic diameter of 10 microns or less) and PM2.5
(particles with an aerodynamic diameter of 2.5 microns or less). PM arises from a number
of indoor sources, including combustion sources such as gas stoves and furnaces, via
resuspension during sweeping and vacuuming, and other activities such as burning

 2

candles or indoor tobacco use.12 Additionally, PM of outdoor origin can infiltrate into
homes via natural or mechanical ventilation.12 Exposure to both PM10 and PM2.5 are
associated with adverse health effects.

Indoor PM exposure has been associated with a number of adverse health effects,
including deficits in lung function, particularly for individuals with asthma and COPD13,14
and reduced blood- oxygen levels among patients with COPD.14 Vardoulakis et al. (2020)
summarized the results of 73 studies of PM2.5, 37 studies of PM10, and 25 studies of other
PM size ranges and found strong evidence to support associations between respiratory
health outcomes (including decreases in pulmonary function and asthma symptoms) as
well as cardiovascular events. The authors posit that inflammation linked to PM exposure
is a driver of these adverse health outcomes.13

Among older adults (ages 65+), PM exposures have been linked to a variety of
cardiovascular outcomes, including mortality, stroke, heart attack, ischemic heart
disease, heart failure, hypertension, and arrhythmia.15 These CVD outcomes mirror what is
known about the health effects of exposure to ambient PM, which is responsible for a
substantial portion of the public health burden attributable to air pollution in the US.16

1.1.2. Nitrogen Dioxide (NO2)
Nitrogen dioxide (and other oxides of nitrogen) is a highly reactive gas pollutant. Indoor
NO2 is the result of combustion by appliances (e.g., a gas stove or a furnace) and
infiltration of NO2 from outdoor combustion sources (e.g., vehicle exhaust).13

The strongest associations for NO2 are with respiratory outcomes.13 A recent review and
meta-analysis found that a 10 μg/m3 increase in NO2 exposure was associated with a
13.4% increase in risk of asthma onset in childhood. This same study found smaller but
significant associations between NO2 exposure and chronic obstructive pulmonary
disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, Parkinson’s disease, and preterm birth.17 Recent work
investigating neurological outcomes has identified an association between indoor NO2
exposure and migraine in adults.

Notably, adults with pre-existing respiratory disease and children appear to be more
susceptible to adverse health effects as a result of exposure to NO2 than healthy adults.14
In adults with asthma, NO2 exposures are associated reduced lung function and more
frequent asthma symptoms.18

1.1.3. Carbon Monoxide (CO)
Indoor CO is the result of incomplete combustion of carbon-based fuels (e.g., natural gas,
oil, wood) and infiltration from outdoor sources (e.g., leaving a car to idle in an attached
garage).13 Exposure to high levels of carbon monoxide can be fatal; other symptoms
attributable to CO poisoning include headache, weakness, dizziness, nausea, vomiting,
chest pain, or neurologic symptoms.19

 3

At lower levels of exposure, CO can lead to the development of cardiovascular disease. For
example, one recent meta-analysis reported a relative risk of developing cardiovascular
disease of 1.024 (95%CI: 1.011-1.038) after combining data from 6 recent studies.17

1.1.4. Volatile Organic Compounds
Indoor VOCs and SVOCs are pollutants that exist in a gas form or readily move into the gas
form at room temperatures, including formaldehyde, benzene, toluene, and xylene, among
others. VOCs and SVOCs come from a variety of sources in the home, including cleaning
products, scented candles, paints and varnishes, fuels, and others.20,21

Many VOCs and SVOCs are associated with respiratory health effects.13 Exposure to VOCs,
including formaldehyde, benzene, toluene, xylene, and tetrachloroethylene, is associated
with the onset of asthma as well as asthma symptoms in both children and adults, though
many of the available studies are cross-sectional in nature.14,22,23 Benzene in particular has
also been linked to lung cancer.14

1.1.5. Bioaerosols, Dampness, and Mold
Bioaerosols are a subtype of particulate matter that arises from biological sources,
including microorganisms, plants, and animals.24 They can be generated from indoor
sources such as mold and pets and can infiltrate homes via natural or mechanical
ventilation.24 Most of the literature on the health effects of bioaerosols has focused on their
links with respiratory health effects.

Exposure to aeroallergens (e.g., those from plants, pets, and pests) are associated with
asthma exacerbations and other respiratory health effects.24,25 Microbial aerosols have
been linked to respiratory symptoms among young children; a recent meta-analysis found
that an increase in microbial bioaerosol exposure was associated with a relative risk of
1.24 (95% CI: 1.09-1.41) for respiratory symptoms (e.g., wheeze).26

Exposure to dampness and mold are linked both to asthma onset22,27 as well as asthma
symptoms (i.e., cough, wheeze, and shortness of breath);22,24 several longitudinal studies
indicate an increased risk of developing asthma when exposed to dampness or mold,
particularly among children.22

1.1.6. Health Effects Summary
Indoor contaminants such as PM, NO2, and bioaerosols continue to have a significant
impact on population health. The effects attributable to indoor contaminant exposures
varied, ranging from minor respiratory symptoms (i.e., cough and wheeze) up to
cardiovascular disease, cancer and even death. Within the literature, several studies have
identified patients with underlying respiratory disease (e.g., asthma or COPD) and older or
younger ages (>65 years, <5 years) as more susceptible to adverse effects from exposure.

1.2. Objectives of this Study
The U.S. Department of Energy’s Weatherization Assistance Program aims to improve
energy efficiency and health outcomes in low-income households. However, a significant
number of homes are deferred from WAP services due to reasons related to health
stemming from structural deficiencies such as leaky roofs, mold growth, and pest
infestations. These sources produce indoor hazards that expose residents to CO, NO2,
PM2.5, CO2, formaldehyde, and mold, among others. These exposures are linked to adverse
health outcomes, including respiratory and cardiovascular diseases (Holden et al., 2023).
While the health impacts of indoor pollutants are well-documented, there is a lack of
quantifiable data on the potential health and economic benefits of mitigating these
exposures in deferred homes. This gap hinders the inclusion of health-related savings in
the savings-to-investment ratio (SIR) calculations used to assess the cost-effectiveness of
weatherization programs. Therefore, this study aims to (1) quantify the health burden
associated with indoor air pollutants in homes and (2) estimate the potential economic
benefits of mitigating these sources of pollutants. The findings will inform strategies to
integrate health-based savings into SIR calculations, thereby supporting decisions for
targeted remediation efforts.

Between July, 2024 and February 2026, the Indoor Climate Research and Training (ICRT)
group at the Champaign County Regional Planning Commission (CCRPC) implemented the
Reducing Deferrals by Integrating Healthy Homes with Weatherization project. As part of
this work, homes that would otherwise be deferred from WAP were recruited into the
study.

Many of the interventions were related to moisture, including plumbing leaks, poor
rainwater management, damaged roofs, sump pump failures, etc. Other interventions
included pest management and improved ventilation.

2. Methods
To understand the indoor environmental benefits of the Healthy Homes interventions,
measurements of several indoor contaminants were taken before and after the healthy
homes retrofit. These data were summarized to estimate the change in indoor
environmental conditions as a result of HH interventions.

Leveraging established methods used to assess the benefits of air quality management
practices established by the US Environmental Protection Agency16,28,29 with key
modification to adapt these methods for the indoor environment,30,31 we estimated the
change in the incidence of health outcomes as a result of these HH interventions on a
population (nationwide) level. Then, we estimated the economic value of these benefits
over the lifespan of the intervention. This economic value was used to calculate the
savings-to-investment ratio (SIR) for interventions that address structural issues that
would otherwise cause homes to be deferred from weatherization programs.

2.1. Home Recruitment
Homes were identified by partnering WAP subgrantees as homes that had sufficient
healthy-home related issues to warrant deferral per DOE rules. These homes were
identified during normal enrollment of homes into the program, including following all
standard WAP practices regarding eligibility and prioritization.

When a home was identified as a candidate, the agency provided ICRT with a list of issues
and necessary interventions. ICRT reviewed these scopes of work and either approved the
home, suggested changes to the scope to ensure alignment with project aims and scope or
indicated that the home would not fit with our project.

Once a home was enrolled in the project, IAQ measurement devices would be installed in
the home for 2-4 weeks to get a baseline of contaminant levels, and dust samples would
be taken if pests had been identified as an issue. The duration of testing was dependent on
how close to the end of the performance period we were; as we got into the last several
months, we shortened the sampling period from 4 weeks to 2 weeks to make it more likely
to complete additional homes. For homes in the Chicago area, CEDA (the local WAP
subgrantee) deployed the instrumentation themselves following training provided by ICRT.
For other agencies, ICRT staff deployed instrumentation.

2.2. Attributable Health Effects
Though the sample size does not result in statistical significance of health effect
projections, we provide here the methodology and results for completeness and reference
in potential future studies.

In order to estimate the change in health impacts attributable to healthy homes
interventions, we started by first surveying the literature to determine which health
outcomes were causally linked to the exposures of interest in this study. To do this, we
consulted several established sources, including the U.S. Environmental Protection
Agency’s Integrated Science Assessments for particulate matter,32 carbon monoxide,33 and
oxides of nitrogen,34 the Health Effects Notebook for hazardous air pollutants,35 and
position documents from the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-
Conditioning Engineers.36 We also reviewed resources from the Institute for Health Metrics
and Evaluation on air pollution and disease burden, as well as guidance from the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention, the National Institute of Environmental Health
Sciences, and the National Center for Biotechnology Information regarding mold-related
health impacts.

In alignment with the literature on the health effects of indoor pollutants (see Section 1.1),
particulate matter exposures are consistently causally associated with cardiovascular and
respiratory health outcomes. CO is similarly linked to cardiovascular health outcomes.
NO2 exposure has the strongest causal links to respiratory health outcomes, including
hospitalizations for and exacerbations of asthma. Mold and dust mites were also causally

 6

linked to respiratory outcomes broadly and asthma outcomes specifically in the literature
surveyed.

The identification of causally-linked health effects was used to establish the initial scope
of our analysis. We only included health outcomes for which there is an established causal
relationship with at least one of our pollutants of interest. Additional refinements to the
scope of the analysis were made based on the availability of other inputs for the health
impact function (discussed next).

2.5.3. Change in Exposure
The change in exposure was estimated from data collected in homes before and after the
implementation of HH strategies. Measurements collected by ICRT investigators were
averaged for each measurement period. We calculated the difference between the post-
and pre-implementation mean values along with the standard error of the difference and
the minimum and maximum differences observed in the sample.

We used two metrics for changes in indoor PM2.5 concentrations. The first is simply the
difference between the post-intervention mean indoor concentration and the pre-
intervention mean indoor concentration. The second considers just the “indoor”
contribution to exposure by first subtracting the mean outdoor measurement from the
mean indoor measurement and then taking the difference between the post- and pre-
intervention mean concentrations.

2.5.4. Population At Risk
To estimate the population at risk, we utilized data from several sources.

First, we obtained the total US population and age-stratified population estimates from the
most recent release of the American Community Survey (2019-2023 5-Year Estimates).58
We also obtained the number of occupied housing units in the US (n = 129 million) from the
2019-2023 ACS data release. Then, we estimated the average number of people per
occupied housing unit in the US by age group.

Second, the number of homes deferred by the WAP program ranges from 1-20% of eligible
homes due to issues beyond the scope of weatherization (e.g., mold), with most agencies
reporting deferring 1-5% of homes.59 In the United States, there are approximately 36
million WAP-eligible homes (based on income requirements), which represent about 30%
of all homes in the United States.60 Assuming a conservative deferral rate of 5%,59 we
estimate 1.8 million potentially deferred homes nationwide that would benefit from
additional remediation.

Third, because some of the health outcomes are specific to populations with asthma, we
used the national prevalence rates for adults and children with asthma published by the
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.61 Based on surveillance data, we estimate
that 8.0% of adults in the US have current asthma and 6.5% of children in the US have
current asthma.62

Using estimates for age-stratified populations, the average number of persons per home,
the number of homes that are eligible for WAP but likely to be deferred due to issues
beyond weatherization (n = 1.8 million), and the national asthma prevalence for children
(6.5%) and adults (8.0%), we estimated the population at risk. This population is

3. Results
In total, data from 9 homes undergoing renovations as part of the study were available.
With this small of a sample size, resulting from project delays combined with agency
challenges, none of the following results are statistically significant. Because of the
inability to estimate health and financial impacts with this small sample size, only
concentration results are presented.
Overall, we observed increases in indoor pollutant concentrations as a result of the
healthy homes intervention (Table 5). For PM2.5, the average increase was 4.3 μg/m3, but
ranged from -13.4 to 43.8μg/m3, suggesting a substantial amount of variability within the
sample homes included in this study. Similarly, NO2 concentrations increased on average,
as did formaldehyde. On average, CO concentrations decreased 0.7 ppb (SE = 0.9 ppb)
between the pre- and post-intervention periods. Increases are counter to expectations;
however, both PM2.5 and NO2 are products that are commonly associated with cooking,
and we were not able to track frequency of cooking. Increases may be reflective of more
cooking after weatherization than before, and with a small sample size it is plausible to get
this result even if, in general, levels would go down over a large population sample.

4. Discussion
In this analysis, we measured changes in indoor pollutant concentrations resulting from
healthy homes interventions in residential buildings that were deferred from home
weatherization programs and estimated the resulting health impacts (as benefits or
disbenefits). These homes were deferred due to major indoor environmental quality issues
that precluded weatherization (e.g., mold, leaking roofs, etc.). Indoor pollutant
concentrations were measured before and after the healthy homes intervention. We found
that for all pollutants other than CO, indoor concentrations increased on average, resulting
in an increase in pollutant-attributable health impacts; however, with the limited sample
size of the project (9 homes) these changes were not statistically significant and are likely
artifacts of differential activity patterns before and after weatherization. The apparent
increase in “disbenefits” of the program resulted in SIR ratios that were negative.

Our results should not be interpreted as evidence that healthy homes interventions would
be harmful to implement on a broader scale. Beyond the small sample size, there are
several other important factors related to our data collection process that may have
influenced our results. These include:
 • The timing of measurements was a challenge. To streamline the process, data were
 collected when permission could be obtained from homeowners. This means the
 length of time between the end of the renovation period and the post-intervention
 data collection was not standardized across homes.
 • Some measures implemented as part of the healthy homes interventions may
 temporarily increase indoor concentrations. For example, new building materials
 can result in increased indoor formaldehyde concentrations64 and indoor dust
 concentrations can increase as a result of energy-efficient retrofitting.65 Data
 collected too close to the end of the renovation period may reflect a temporary
 spike in indoor concentrations that will resolve with time.

4.1. Strengths and Limitations
Our study of the impact of health homes interventions in homes deferred from
weatherization programs has some important strengths. First, compared to similar
studies,1 this analysis used measurements of the change in indoor pollutants (not
modeled estimates). This is important, as modeled studies of the change in indoor
pollutants require several assumptions that may not be relevant in all settings. Second,

 16

out work leveraged existing methods for HIA and included an expanded number of
outcomes compared to prior work. This allowed for a more comprehensive estimate of the
impact of health homes interventions of public health.

Future work should aim to build on this work and address the current limitations. First, due
to time constrains and challenges with recruitment, we were only able to collect pre- and
post-renovation data for a few homes. Second, our data represented homes within a
limited geographic scope. Differences in climate, building conditions, resident behaviors,
and the types of renovations needed in different regions of the US will impact changes in
indoor pollutants as a result of healthy homes interventions. Third, we had limited
exposure data to include in the analysis. For example, we were not able to quantify
benefits attributable to changes in bioaerosols due to challenges with sample collection
and analysis.

5. Conclusions
In this work, we aimed to quantify the public health benefits of healthy homes
interventions in homes that had been deferred from weatherization programs. On average,
we found that indoor concentrations of PM2.5, NO2, and formaldehyde increased after
renovations and that CO concentrations decreased. Our results, however, should be
interpreted with caution and not be taken as evidence that healthy homes interventions
should not be pursued. Our results are influenced by several limitations, including that
only nine homes could be included in the analysis and that the timing of post-renovation
sampling may not have captured the long-term benefits of these interventions. Future work
that (1) incorporates data from more homes and (2) has an expanded geographic reach will
be needed to fully explore the public health benefits of incorporating health homes
interventions into weatherization programs.
