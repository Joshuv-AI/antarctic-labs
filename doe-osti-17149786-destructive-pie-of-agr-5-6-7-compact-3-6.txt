 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report presents the destructive postirradiation examination of AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3 in the as-
irradiated condition. Compact 3-6-3 was irradiated in the high-temperature margin capsule of the AGR-
5/6/7 experiment and achieved a burnup of 14.77% fissions per initial metal atom, a fast neutron fluence
of 5.47 × 1025 n/m2, and a time-average, volume-average temperature of 1363°C. Examinations included
deconsolidation–leach–burn–leach (DLBL), particle gamma analysis using the Irradiated Microsphere
Gamma Analyzer (IMGA), optical microscopy, 3D x-ray computed tomography, and scanning electron
microscopy with energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy.

The DLBL analysis showed approximately 0.45 particle equivalents of 238U, providing evidence for one
particle having exposed kernel material before IMGA. The IMGA measurements showed that only one
particle, 363-SP02, exhibited cesium depletion, with a 137Cs M/C of approximately 0.08, and was best
classified as a SiC failure. It could not be confidently identified as the source of the exposed-kernel
inventory indicated by DLBL as it retained its actinide inventory. The combined measurements are
consistent with approximately one TRISO failure indicated by DLBL and one confirmed SiC failure
identified by IMGA. The high irradiation temperature produced substantial transport of select fission
products through otherwise intact coatings. Approximately 11.2% of the calculated 90Sr inventory and
4.0% of the 154Eu inventory were recovered before particle postburn leaching. In contrast, no 110mAg was
detected above the measurement limit in any DLBL stage. These measurements indicate that much of the
silver inventory remained within the particles and that silver transported beyond the SiC layer at these
temperatures was not appreciably retained in the OPyC or matrix.

A key finding was the extensive occurrence of kernel migration in particles from Compact 3-6-3. Optical
microscopy of 52 particle cross sections showed a broad range of migration-associated kernel and buffer
morphologies. Because the apparent extent of migration in a 2D cross section depends strongly on particle
orientation and the particular plane of polish, these observations were not used to enumerate migration
severity. Instead, 3D XCT of 35 randomly selected particles provided the primary basis for assessing the
phenomenon. The XCT population exhibited a continuum from little or indeterminate migration to
pronounced displacement through essentially the full buffer thickness. Kernel migration generally
occurred away from residual buffer-IPyC attachment and toward the larger buffer-IPyC gap, consistent
with independent AGR-5/6/7 ceramography of other Capsule 3 compacts. Despite the prevalence of
migration, there was no evidence that it degraded the structural coating layers. SEM/EDS examination of
several migrated particles identified compositional gradients within the kernels, with increased uranium
concentration in the apparent migration direction and increased carbon concentration on the trailing side.
These observations support a mechanism involving redistribution of kernel constituents rather than simple
rigid displacement, although the mechanism responsible for migration in heterogeneous UCO kernels
remains unresolved.

Examination of failed Particle 363-SP02 provides additional evidence that kernel migration was not
responsible for the observed coating failure. The XCT data and optical microscopy showed only minimal
kernel migration and a broad region of degradation progressing inward from the exterior through the
OPyC and SiC. The SEM/EDS analysis showed that the morphology and composition of the degraded
region were consistent with outside-in nickel-induced SiC corrosion. The failure therefore represents a
distinct chemical degradation mechanism rather than an intrinsic failure associated with kernel migration.

Overall, Compact 3-6-3 demonstrated excellent TRISO fuel performance at the high-temperature AGR-
5/6/7 Capsule 3 margin conditions. The examinations confirm that kernel migration becomes an important
microstructural response of UCO fuel at sufficiently high irradiation temperature and burnup, but the
available results provide no indication that migration adversely affected fission product retention or
structural coating integrity under the conditions achieved in this experiment, providing additional data for
understanding UCO TRISO behavior near the upper irradiation performance margin.

 1. INTRODUCTION AND BACKGROUND

The Advanced Gas Reactor Fuel Development and Qualification (AGR) Program was established to
develop and demonstrate tristructural isotropic (TRISO) coated particle fuel for high-temperature gas-
cooled reactor applications. Four irradiation experiments were conducted in the Advanced Test Reactor at
Idaho National Laboratory (INL): AGR-1, AGR-2, AGR-3/4, and AGR-5/6/7. AGR-5/6/7 was the final
fuel qualification irradiation and used multiphase uranium carbide and uranium oxide (UCO) TRISO fuel
fabricated at the engineering scale (Demkowicz et al. 2026). The experiment was designed to provide fuel
performance data over a broad range of irradiation conditions and to establish the performance margin of
the fuel at temperatures and burnups extending beyond those expected during normal reactor operation.

The AGR-5/6/7 experiment contained five independently controlled capsules with 194 fuel compacts.
Irradiation temperatures varied substantially among the capsules. Capsules 2, 4, and 5 generally operated
at comparatively low temperatures, while Capsule 1 operated at intermediate temperatures. Capsule 3 was
specifically designed to explore the upper temperature margin for UCO TRISO fuel performance.
Calculated time-average, volume-average (TAVA) compact temperatures in Capsule 3 ranged from
approximately 1190°C to 1363°C, with time-average peak fuel temperatures extending into the 1400°C
range (Demkowicz et al. 2026).

Here, results are reported on AGR-5/6/7 Compact 3-6-3, which was examined in the as-irradiated
condition. Compact 3-6-3 experienced a burnup of 14.77% fissions per initial metal atom (FIMA) and a
TAVA irradiation temperature of 1363°C (Table 1-1). The average number of particles was 2265 for the
batch of as-fabricated compacts from which Compact 3-6-3 was selected. The time-average maximum
temperature was in the 1400°C range. These conditions place Compact 3-6-3 near the upper boundary of
both temperature and burnup achieved during AGR-5/6/7.

Recent postirradiation examination (PIE) of AGR-5/6/7 fuel has identified kernel migration as a
microstructural response of UCO fuel irradiated at these elevated temperatures (Krajewska-Travar and
Stempien 2026). Kernel migration, historically referred to as the amoeba effect, is movement of the fuel
kernel in response to a particle-scale temperature gradient and has previously been reported in uranium
oxide (UO2) and uranium carbide (UCx) coated particle fuels (Stansfield et al. 1975; Lindemer and
Pearson 1977; Wagner-Löffler 1977). Prior to AGR-5/6/7, pronounced kernel migration had not been
observed in AGR UCO TRISO fuel. The UCO fuel form mitigates the oxygen-driven kernel migration
mechanism associated with UO2 fuel through the presence of uranium carbide; however, the AGR-5/6/7
results indicate that sufficiently high temperature and burnup can still produce measurable kernel
redistribution in UCO fuel. The dominant transport mechanism responsible for migration in the
heterogeneous UCO kernel is unknown and will be the subject of follow-on research.

The recently completed AGR-5/6/7 ceramography examination at INL provides an important basis for
interpreting the Compact 3-6-3 results (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien 2026). Two Capsule 3 compacts
analyzed, Compacts 3-2-3 and 3-4-1, experienced burnups of 14.62% and 14.73% FIMA and TAVA
temperatures of 1320°C and 1359°C, respectively. These conditions bracket those experienced by
Compact 3-6-3. Kernel migration was classified in 57.6% of the examined near-midplane particle cross
sections from Compact 3-2-3 and 56.5% from Compact 3-4-1, with an additional 18.4% and 19.9%,
respectively, classified as exhibiting incipient kernel migration. The INL study further observed that
migration progressed away from areas of retained buffer–inner pyrolytic carbon (IPyC) attachment
toward areas with a larger buffer–IPyC gap. Importantly, kernel migration was not observed to result in
degradation of the remaining TRISO coating layers, including in particles where the kernel reached the
inner surface of the IPyC.

Ceramography provides large numbers of particle cross sections that can be evaluated within intact
compact sections. The resulting population statistics establish the prevalence of irradiation-induced
morphologies and provide information on their relationship to compact position and irradiation

 1

conditions. Interpretation of individual particle geometry is inherently limited, however, by observation of
a single 2D plane through a 3D particle. The INL report explicitly recognizes this limitation for buffer–
IPyC separation: particles that appear completely debonded in a particular plane of polish have been
shown by 3D XCT to retain buffer–IPyC attachment elsewhere in the particle volume (Helmreich et al.
2022).

The examinations reported herein included deconsolidation–leach–burn–leach (DLBL) analysis,
individual particle gamma analysis using the Irradiated Microsphere Gamma Analyzer (IMGA), optical
microscopy of random and specially selected particles, 3D XCT, and electron microscopy of selected
particles. The objective was to assess fission product retention and particle integrity following the
Capsule 3 margin irradiation and to characterize the irradiation-induced microstructural evolution of the
UCO TRISO particles. Emphasis is placed on determining the extent of kernel migration and evaluating
its relationship, if any, to coating integrity and fission product retention.

 2. EXPERIMENTAL METHODS*

2.1 DECONSOLIDATION–LEACH–BURN–LEACH AND IRRADIATED MICROSPHERE
 GAMMA ANALYZER METHODS

Destructive examination was performed using the same methods that were developed for destructive PIE
of as-irradiated AGR-1 and AGR-2 compacts, and the comprehensive details of the equipment and
methods have been reported previously (Hunn et al. 2013). Figure 2-1 is a flow diagram of the typical
process for compact DLBL integrated with gamma survey of the particle inventory using the Oak Ridge
National Laboratory (ORNL) IMGA. Deconsolidation-and-leach solutions were analyzed by gamma and
mass spectrometry, providing information about actinides and fission products that were present outside
of the intact particles. Sources of the dissolved actinides and fission products in deconsolidation-and-
leach solutions include (a) nuclides that diffused through intact SiC during irradiation or safety testing but
were still retained within the compact matrix, (b) nuclides related to uranium contamination present in the
compact matrix and/or OPyC at the time the compact was fabricated, (c) external contamination from
sources present in the hot cell, or (d) particles with compromised SiC (with or without coincident
compromise of the other TRISO coating layers) that exhibited abnormally low retention of actinides and
fission products due to defective, failed, or broken layers.

2.2 MATERIALOGRAPHY AND XCT METHODS

Select SPs and RS particles that had been gamma counted with IMGA were examined by XCT and/or
cross-sectioned for materialography. For materialography, particles were embedded in epoxy in small
aluminum holders and polished to near midplane with a Buehler MiniMet 1000 grinder/polisher using the
methods developed for AGR-1 PIE (Hunn et al. 2013). Final polish was typically achieved with a 0.5 µm
diamond film. Polished cross sections were imaged with a Leica DMI5000 optical microscope.

Particles designated for XCT were embedded in epoxy in a Lucite holder (Figure 2-2a), which was
removed from the hot cell and loaded into a tungsten shielded enclosure (Figure 2-2b) designed for use in
an Xradia MicroXCT-400 (Figure 2-2c), which was custom designed by ORNL and Xradia for imaging
TRISO fuel particles. The Versa line of instruments based on the MicroXCT-400 is now sold
commercially by Zeiss, and a Versa 620 system was also used in this work. For 3D imaging, each particle
was rotated around an axis perpendicular to the x-ray beam, and radiographs were obtained at multiple
angles of rotation. Tomographic reconstruction was performed to determine the relative x-ray density of
each minimum resolvable volume element (voxel) in the cylindrical volume of rotation. From this data,
2D tomograms could be produced for any planar section. In addition to these tomograms, 3D
representations were produced using various software packages for 3D visualization of tomographic
datasets. Details of the XCT methods are available in previous reports (Hunn et al. 2013).

2.3 SCANNING ELECTRON MICROSCOPE AND ENERGY-DISPERSIVE X-RAY
 SPECTROSCOPY METHODS

Analysis was performed with a Tescan Vega scanning electron microscope (SEM) equipped with an
Oxford Ultim Max 65 silicon drift detector for energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using the
Oxford AZtec analysis software suite. This SEM and imaging suite replaced the JEOL JSM-6390 SEM
and reflects new capabilities and minor changes to standard practices relative to SEM analysis at ORNL
in the AGR-2 irradiation campaign. Further details on the implementation of the new system are included
elsewhere (Cureton et al. 2025). The SEM was located in a contamination control area in Room 120 of
the ORNL Irradiated Fuels Examination Laboratory (IFEL). The SEM analysis was performed on the
mounts previously imaged by optical microscopy. To facilitate analysis, the mounts were transferred out
of the IFEL hot cell to the charging area, where initial decontamination was performed to reduce
radiological contamination. The mounts were then transferred to a hood located in Room 120 for final
radiological decontamination and surface cleaning. The mounts underwent ultrasonic cleaning in
deionized water, were rinsed with isopropyl alcohol, and were wiped with a cotton swab. These cleaning
processes were repeated three times. Following cleaning, the samples were smeared and surveyed prior to
being released from the hood. After the samples were released, their surfaces were checked with an
optical microscope located in the contamination control area. If the sample surfaces were free of
significant debris, then they were secured onto an aluminum SEM stub using conductive carbon tape and
colloidal graphite to facilitate SEM analysis. The SPs previously encased in nonconducting epoxy from
XCT analysis were coated with a thin layer of carbon (~5–10 nm) using a Cressington Series 108 sputter
coater to create a conductive surface to mitigate charging effects.

Two complementary SEM imaging modes—secondary electron (SE) imaging and backscattered electron
(BSE) imaging—were performed, as they provide different and complementary information on particle
cross sections. The SE signal was surface sensitive, which allowed for identification of surface features
and any irregularities. The SE imaging aided in differentiating surface debris from embedded fission
product features distributed throughout the TRISO layers. The SE micrographs were obtained using a
5 keV accelerating voltage with a probe current of 300 pA and a working distance of 5 mm. The BSE
signal was dependent on the atomic number (Z) of the elements near the surface of the sample. Because
they appeared as bright spots, the high-Z actinides and fission products embedded in the relatively low-Z
TRISO layers could be readily identified. The BSE micrographs were obtained using a 20 kV accelerating
voltage with a probe current of 3 nA and a working distance of 10 mm.

Image montage pairs of the same areas were acquired using both imaging modes to ensure that surface
features (such as loose debris) were not misinterpreted as embedded features. A general image set was
taken for each particle cross section. The image set typically consisted of a montage of more than 150
80 μm × 80 μm field-of-view micrographs stitched together to form a high-resolution panorama image of
the entire particle. Additional images of selected areas of interest were also acquired when necessary.
Two or more locations were chosen on each particle cross section for EDS analysis to gain an
understanding of the distribution of actinides and fission products in the TRISO layers after irradiation
and safety testing. Point-ID analysis was used to identify local compositions in high-Z features. The
Point-ID analysis was acquired under the same conditions as images acquired in the BSE mode, which
resulted in a sufficient count rate. The general acquisition parameters used for Point-ID analysis were to
collect spectra within an energy range of 0–20 keV with 10 eV/channel, 4,096 channels, a unitless process
time of 2, and a live time of 30 s. Data acquired by Point-ID analysis were processed using standardless
analysis, and significant contributions from the surrounding matrix were included in the data due to the
submicron size of the analyzed features; therefore, information on feature composition was not
quantitative. In addition, minor constituents present in the high-Z actinide and fission product features
may not be observed due to sensitivity limitations of the analysis. However, the Point-ID analysis
information on feature composition was useful for qualitative classification of the features, which
supported identification of general trends and comparison of the features observed in different locations in
the TRISO layers.

Previous AGR-5/6/7 DLBL results estimated that approximately 9.1% of the 90Sr inventory and 4% of the
154

from leached kernels (Stempien and Cai 2025). It was concluded that at a TAVA temperature of 1363°C,
transport of Sr and Eu through intact SiC substantially exceeded the contribution from the more than 20
kernels exposed during their DLBL analysis. The Compact 3-6-3 results are consistent with this behavior:
11.2% of the 90Sr inventory and 4.0% of the 154Eu inventory were recovered before particle postburn
leaching. The agreement between two independently examined Capsule 3 compacts irradiated at the same
temperature provides strong evidence that these inventories are characteristic of high-temperature
transport through intact AGR-5/6/7 coatings. The magnitude of this temperature effect is also apparent
relative to the lower-temperature AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 2 fuel (Cureton et al. 2025). For example, as-
irradiated Compact 2-2-1 had cumulative DLBL fractions of approximately 6.0 × 10−3 for 90Sr and
1.19 × 10−2 for 154Eu, compared with 1.19 × 10−1 and 4.42 × 10−2, respectively, for Compact 3-6-3. This
increase is consistent with the strong irradiation-temperature dependence of Sr and Eu transport observed
in previous AGR experiments (Demkowicz et al. 2015; Stempien et al. 2021).

No 110mAg activity above the detection limit was measured in any DLBL stage for Compact 3-6-3, with a
cumulative detection limit of 1.40 × 10−3 of the calculated compact inventory (Table 3-3). This result
indicates that little 110mAg was retained in the OPyC or compact matrix at the time of DLBL. Precision
gamma scanning (PGS) of the intact AGR-5/6/7 compacts showed high 110mAg inventories in the high-
temperature Capsule 3 fuel. The average compact 110mAg M/C for Capsule 3 was approximately 0.76
despite TAVA temperatures exceeding 1300°C (Walker et al. 2025). This behavior is different from
earlier AGR irradiations, for which compact 110mAg inventories generally decreased with increasing
irradiation temperature above approximately 1100°C. Walker et al. noted that the high apparent silver
retention in Capsule 3 could not readily be explained by uncertainty in the calculated irradiation
temperatures. Similar behavior was observed for Compact 3-6-2, which had the same 1363°C TAVA
temperature. Its intact-compact PGS 110mAg M/C was 0.73 ± 0.02, and subsequent particle gamma
counting gave an average normalized M/C of 0.89 ± 0.31, while no detectable 110mAg was recovered from
the compact matrix or OPyC by DLBL (Walker et al. 2025; Stempien and Cai 2025). It was concluded
that at these high temperatures silver transported through an intact SiC layer is unlikely to be appreciably
retained in the OPyC or compact matrix.

The 144Ce results provide additional context for interpreting the exposed-kernel inventories.
Approximately 1.03 particle equivalents of 144Ce were recovered before particle postburn leaching,
corresponding to a single TRISO failure. An additional 3.15 particle equivalents became accessible

 10

during the particle postburn leach, similar to the approximately 2.7 particle equivalents of uranium
recovered at that stage. This correspondence supports the conclusion that several particle equivalents of
kernel material became accessible during the later destructive processing steps, while the much smaller
pre-IMGA inventory is consistent with approximately one failed particle plus possible contributions from
dispersed fuel material.

Taken together, the DLBL results indicate that Compact 3-6-3 maintained overall performance during the
high-temperature Capsule 3 irradiation while exhibiting temperature-dependent transport of select
metallic fission products. The 90Sr and 154Eu inventories recovered from the matrix are consistent with
transport through intact coatings and subsequent retention in the carbonaceous components of the
compact. In contrast, minimal 110mAg was detected outside the SiC by DLBL, while intact-compact PGS
showed the unusually high silver retention characteristic of AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3. Particle-level gamma
analysis discussed in the following section provides additional information on this silver behavior. The
actinide and cesium results, together with the subsequent IMGA survey, indicate a low in-pile particle
failure population.

Overall, the IMGA results show that Compact 3-6-3 retained cesium effectively in all but one of the

microstructural response of high-temperature UCO TRISO fuel without an apparent adverse effect on
particle performance under the irradiation conditions achieved in Capsule 3.

 5. XCT AND OPTICAL MICROSCOPY ANALYSIS OF RANDOMLY SELECTED
 PARTICLES

Optical microscopy and XCT were used to characterize irradiation-induced microstructural evolution in
randomly selected particles from Compact 3-6-3. Attention was given to kernel migration because
asymmetric kernel and buffer morphologies were observed frequently in particles from this compact.
Optical microscopy of 52 particle cross sections provided detailed visualization of the kernel, buffer, and
IPyC in the plane of polish. XCT provided nondestructive 3D characterization of 35 randomly selected
particles and allowed the complete particle volume to be interrogated to determine the maximum extent
and direction of kernel displacement.

These techniques provide complementary information but should not be interpreted equivalently. The
apparent degree of kernel migration in an optical micrograph depends on the orientation of the migration
vector relative to the plane of polish. A particle with substantial 3D migration may therefore show only
minor displacement in a particular 2D cross section. Thus, the optical examinations were used primarily
to document characteristic particle morphologies and coating behavior, whereas the XCT dataset was
used to assess the frequency and severity of kernel migration.

A direct comparison of the two techniques was obtained for Particle 363-RS08. Following XCT, the
reconstructed particle volume was analyzed to identify the plane containing the maximum kernel
displacement, and the particle was subsequently mounted and polished to approximately this plane using
the same approach used for SPs, described in Section 2.2. Figure 5-1 compares the 3D XCT
reconstruction with the resulting optical cross section. The optical micrograph reproduces the pronounced
asymmetric kernel and buffer morphology identified by XCT and provides detailed visualization of the
kernel and carbon layers in the targeted plane. This comparison also demonstrates why an arbitrary 2D
section cannot necessarily be used to determine the maximum extent of kernel migration within a particle.
Of the particles analyzed via XCT, 363-RS08 exhibited the greatest degree of kernel migration, with the
kernel fully penetrating the buffer thickness and approaching the inner IPyC interface. The IMGA survey
did not identify anomalous fission product depletion in this particle, indicating that the pronounced
migration was not accompanied by observable loss of fission product retention.

The classifications used to organize Figure 5-2 describe only the morphology visible in the individual
cross section. The plane of polish was not selected based on the direction of migration, and therefore may
not intersect the region of maximum kernel displacement. This limitation is apparent even among
particles placed in the upper row of Figure 5-2. For example, Figure 5-2(b) exhibits relatively little kernel
displacement in the observed plane, but material in the kernel region has an appearance consistent with
morphology that can be left behind as a kernel migrates. One possible interpretation is that polishing
intersected the particle along the vector of migration and removed much of the displaced kernel from the
observed cross section. This example illustrates why optical cross sections were not used to determine a
migration fraction for Compact 3-6-3. Even a particle that appears approximately centered in one plane
could exhibit substantial migration elsewhere within its volume. Conversely, observation of asymmetric
kernel shape or buffer thickness in a cross section does not by itself define the maximum 3D
displacement. Quantitative enumeration of migration in this report is therefore based on the XCT
population discussed in Section 5.2.

Despite this limitation, the optical examinations demonstrate that morphology associated with kernel
migration was common in the random particle population. Apparent migration was characterized by
displacement of the kernel relative to the surrounding coatings, asymmetric remaining buffer thickness,
and frequently an asymmetric buffer-IPyC gap. In many particles where a residual buffer-IPyC
attachment was visible, the apparent direction of kernel displacement was away from the attachment and
toward the larger gap. This relationship is consistent with both the 3D Compact 3-6-3 XCT observations
and the independent ceramography results (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien 2026).

Buffer fracture and kernel extrusion through buffer fractures were distinguished from migration where
possible. Kernel extrusion occurs when kernel material protrudes into a discrete buffer fracture, whereas
migration involves directional redistribution of the bulk kernel accompanied by asymmetric buffer
morphology. Some optical cross sections displayed sufficiently complex morphology that the relative
contributions from the two processes could not be separated.

Visible buffer fracture was observed in only 3.8% of the random particle cross sections, and no IPyC
fractures were observed. This is a clear difference from the more frequent buffer and IPyC fracture
observed in lower-temperature, high-burnup AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 2 fuel (Cureton et al. 2025). This
behavior is consistent with the broader AGR-5/6/7 ceramography dataset, which showed higher buffer
and IPyC fracture frequencies at lower irradiation temperatures and higher fast neutron fluences,
potentially reflecting reduced irradiation creep at lower temperature (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien
2026). As with all optical observations reported here, these values refer to features intersected by the
plane of polish and do not necessarily represent 3D fracture frequency.

Optical images of five additional cross-sectioned particles are shown in Figure 5-3. All five exhibit some
degree of asymmetric kernel morphology consistent with migration. These images emphasize that the
phenomenon involves more than simple translation of an otherwise unchanged spherical kernel. The
irradiated kernels exhibit irregular external profiles, asymmetric redistribution of kernel material, and
spatial variations in porosity. Larger pores and local differences in kernel appearance are evident in
several of the particles. Similar changes in porosity were previously reported, where increasing migration
severity was accompanied by enlargement and redistribution of kernel pores (Krajewska-Travar and
Stempien 2026). As with Figure 5-2, however, the apparent extent of migration in these kernel cross
sections remains dependent on the plane of polish. The images are therefore used to characterize the
morphology associated with migration rather than to assign 3D migration severity.

5.2 XCT EXAMINATION

Compact 3-6-3 had an unusually large random-particle XCT dataset. In addition to XCT examination of
the IMGA-selected SP discussed previously, 35 randomly selected particles were examined. Two
particles were initially scanned for microstructural assessment, and an additional 33 were imaged in
support of subsequent Furnace for Irradiated TRISO Testing work (Helmreich et al. 2026). Unlike the
optical cross sections, the complete reconstructed volume of each particle could be analyzed. Each
volume was rotated to identify the plane containing the maximum extension of the kernel into the buffer.
The images in Figure 5-5 represent the maximum observed kernel displacement in each particle rather
than an arbitrary sectioning plane.

The XCT population exhibited a continuum in migration severity similar to the range of morphologies
suggested by the optical examination. Because the full particle volume was available, however, the XCT
data provide a more appropriate basis for enumerating the prevalence and severity of kernel migration.
The degree of kernel migration was quantified for each particle as the difference in buffer thickness
measured on opposing sides of the kernel along the vector of maximum kernel displacement. For a
particle with no measurable kernel migration, this value would nominally be zero. Of the 35 randomly
sampled particles analyzed, 18 exhibited kernel migration directly away from the location of residual
buffer/IPyC attachment, 14 had intact buffer but no clear remaining attachment point between the buffer
and IPyC, and 3 had fractured buffer and were not suitable for this analysis due to kernel protrusion
through the fracture. The asymmetric buffer-IPyC gap likely reinforced the local thermal gradient across
the particle. BISON calculations reported by Wright et al. (2022) indicated that the gap side may be
approximately 15–20°C hotter than it would be in the absence of the gap, providing a plausible additional
driving force for kernel migration.

The distribution of kernel migration severity for the 32 analyzable particles without buffer fracture is
shown in Figure 5-6. Most particles had buffer-thickness differences between approximately 10 and 40
µm, with a mean value of 27.6 µm and a standard deviation of 13.2 µm after excluding two high-severity
outliers. Two particles exhibited substantially larger apparent migration distances and are shown at the far
right of the distribution. In both cases, the kernel extended beyond the original buffer boundary. For these
particles, the buffer thickness in the direction of kernel migration was estimated by extending the arc of
the adjacent buffer layer and measuring the distance from that extrapolated arc to the edge of the kernel.
Because the kernel protruded beyond the extrapolated buffer boundary, the buffer thickness on the
migration side was assigned a negative value. Representative XCT tomograms of particles from the
lower, middle, and upper ranges of the main distribution, along with one high-severity outlier, are shown
in Figure 5-7.

This XCT-based classification should be distinguished from any apparent migration frequency derived
from optical microscopy. The XCT classification specifically interrogates the full volume and selects the
maximum-displacement plane; it is therefore much less susceptible to false classification of a migrated
particle as having low migration because the migration vector lies largely outside the plane being
observed. Several of the more strongly migrated particles also exhibited internal x-ray attenuation
gradients within the kernel. These gradients qualitatively correspond to the heterogeneous internal kernel
morphology and porosity observed by optical microscopy, although XCT alone cannot identify the phases
or compositions responsible for the attenuation differences. Two particles in the XCT population
exhibited discrete buffer fracture with kernel protrusion into the resulting openings. In these particles, the
kernel geometry was directly affected by the buffer fracture, and any additional contribution from
migration could not be separated reliably from extrusion. These particles were therefore not considered
when interpreting migration severity. The 3D volumes also provide improved information on the
relationship between kernel migration and residual buffer-IPyC attachment. Kernel migration generally
progressed away from the remaining attachment and toward the larger buffer-IPyC gap. Future effort will
focus on quantifying these observations using the full 3D particle geometry. Metrics for kernel
displacement and buffer thinning will be referenced to the structural coating layers to account for as-
fabricated geometric features, such as kernel dimpling or particle faceting, that can locally influence the
initial buffer thickness and apparent postirradiation morphology.

The Compact 3-6-3 observations are consistent with the recent examination of high-temperature

was performed. Developed kernel migration was identified in 57.6% and 56.5% of the observed cross

These populations provide strong evidence that migration-related morphology was widespread in high-
temperature Capsule 3 fuel.

Together, XCT and ceramography approaches answer related but somewhat different questions.
Ceramography permits examination of substantially larger particle populations and provides valuable
statistics on the frequency of features observable in random cross sections. XCT examines fewer particles
but provides a more complete assessment of inherently 3D particle geometry. Previous AGR XCT work
demonstrated this distinction for buffer-IPyC separation, where particles appearing completely separated
in a 2D plane were shown to retain attachment elsewhere within the particle volume (Helmreich et al.
2022).

The observed migration morphology is highly consistent across AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3 fuel. It was
reported that migrated kernels generally moved away from residual buffer-IPyC attachment and toward
the larger buffer-IPyC gap (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien 2026). The Compact 3-6-3 optical and XCT
examinations show the same qualitative relationship. Taken together, the optical microscopy, XCT, and
ceramography results establish that kernel migration was a prevalent irradiation-induced response of UCO
fuel under the high-temperature Capsule 3 conditions. The optical cross sections document a broad range
of migration-associated morphologies but, because they represent arbitrary 2D planes, were not used to
enumerate migration severity. The 35-particle XCT population provides a basis for that assessment and
demonstrates a continuum extending from little or indeterminate migration to, in one particle, migration
reaching the inner IPyC interface.

Importantly, pronounced kernel migration was observed in numerous particles without corresponding
evidence of degradation of the structural coating layers. Combined with the DLBL and IMGA results
discussed previously, these observations indicate that kernel migration became active at the severe
temperature and burnup conditions achieved in Capsule 3 but did not measurably degrade overall particle
performance.

Across the six RS particles, several structural features were common. Most particles showed a
circumferential gap or crack near the kernel/buffer boundary, internal tearing or separation near the
buffer/IPyC interface, fine-scale porosity bands in the IPyC, and limited low-Z inclusions or pullout
features in the SiC. These features are generally consistent with those observed in previous AGR SEM
examinations and are not interpreted individually as evidence of coating failure. Fine circumferential
cracks were observed at some SiC interfaces, but these cracks did not traverse the full SiC thickness and
were not associated with anomalous cesium release.

High-Z fission product and actinide features were observed in the buffer, IPyC, at the IPyC/SiC boundary,
and within the SiC layer. Buffer features were commonly U-rich and often contained Zr and Mo, with
variable Ru, Sr, Te, Ba, and Se depending on particle and feature location. Features near the buffer/IPyC
boundary were compositionally more diverse and included both U-rich and Sr/Te/Ba-bearing features.
High-Z features in the IPyC and near the IPyC/SiC boundary commonly contained Pd, U, Pu, and Rh,
with variable Zr, Mo, Ru, Cs, and Ba. Similar noble-metal and actinide-bearing features have been
reported in previous AGR particle examinations, including features interpreted as fission product silicides
near or within the SiC layer (van Rooyen et al. 2014; Stempien et al. 2021). The EDS results reported
here should be interpreted qualitatively because the analyzed features were often smaller than the EDS
interaction volume, and surrounding carbon, SiC, epoxy, or mount material contributed to the measured
spectra.

clearly resolved in the 363-RS07 SiC feature spectrum. Prior AGR microanalysis has shown that silver

6.2 KERNEL COMPOSITION GRADIENTS IN MIGRATED RS PARTICLES

A significant SEM/EDS observation from the RS particles was the relationship between kernel migration
and compositional redistribution within the kernel. Kernel EDS line scans were collected across selected
particles to evaluate whether migration was associated with directional changes in kernel composition.
The line scans were averaged over a 50 µm-wide band to reduce local variability associated with kernel
porosity, polishing artifacts, and sub-particle-scale heterogeneity. The resulting U, C, and O atomic-
percent profiles were used qualitatively to compare relative compositional gradients across each kernel.
The line scans discussed here provide the primary comparison of U, C, and O profiles across the kernels;
the corresponding EDS maps in Appendix E provide spatial context for interpreting those profiles.

Particle 363-RS08 provides the clearest example because XCT was used to guide polishing into the plane
of maximum kernel migration. As discussed in Section 5.2, this particle exhibited pronounced kernel
migration, with kernel material penetrating through much of the buffer thickness. The 363-RS08 kernel
EDS line scan in Figure 6-3 showed increased uranium concentration toward the apparent migration
direction and increased carbon concentration on the trailing side of the kernel. Again, the direction of
migration progressed away from a buffer-IPyC attachment. Oxygen did not show a comparably strong
directional trend. Similar uranium/carbon asymmetry was observed in migrated particles 363-RS07 and
363-RS12, shown in Figure 6-4 and Figure 6-5, respectively. These line scans indicate that kernel

 29

migration in Compact 3-6-3 was not only rigid displacement of an otherwise unchanged kernel. Instead,
the migrated kernels appear to have undergone redistribution of kernel constituents, with uranium-rich
material enriched toward the direction of migration and carbon-rich material retained or redeposited on
the trailing side. This interpretation is consistent with the optical and XCT observations described in
Section 5, which showed irregular kernel shapes, asymmetric buffer morphologies, and carbon-rich
material behind some migrated kernels. It is also consistent with the recent AGR-5/6/7 ceramography
observations that migrated kernels generally moved away from residual buffer/IPyC attachment and
toward the larger buffer/IPyC gap (Krajewska-Travar and Stempien 2026).

Particle 363-RS12 did not show the same buffer/IPyC tearing morphology observed in most of the other
migrated RS particles. Much of the buffer/IPyC interface remained attached, yet the kernel line scan still
showed uranium/carbon asymmetry consistent with compositional redistribution in the apparent migration
direction (Figure 6-5). This suggests that buffer/IPyC tearing and local buffer-gap geometry may
influence the direction or severity of migration, but they are not necessarily required for kernel
compositional redistribution to occur. The data are still limited to a small number of polished cross
sections, so the relative contributions from compact-scale thermal gradients, local buffer/IPyC
attachment, initial kernel heterogeneity, and irradiation-induced phase redistribution cannot be separated
definitively.

The kernel line-scan results also help explain why the migration morphology observed by optical
microscopy and XCT should not be interpreted as simple translation of a solid kernel through the buffer.
The migrated kernels exhibited internal compositional asymmetry, irregular external profiles, and local
carbon-rich regions opposite the apparent migration direction. These observations support a mechanism
involving redistribution of kernel constituents during irradiation, although the transport mechanism
responsible for migration in heterogeneous UCO kernels remains unresolved. Additional particle-specific
kernel EDS maps and supporting SEM observations for 363-RS21, 363-RS08, 363-RS07, and 363-RS12
are provided in Appendix D and Appendix E.

6.3 SEM/EDS ANALYSIS OF SPECIAL PARTICLE 363-SP02

Particle 363-SP02 was the only particle identified by IMGA as having strongly depleted 137Cs inventory.
XCT and optical microscopy showed localized coating degradation and only minimal kernel migration, as
discussed in Section 4. SEM imaging confirmed that the degraded region was chemically and
morphologically distinct from the migrated RS particles. The damage was associated with a C-rich
degradation phase in the SiC and progressed inward from the exterior of the particle through the OPyC
and SiC, rather than outward from the kernel or along the direction of kernel displacement. The SiC
damage region shown in Figure 6-6 is therefore consistent with localized outside-in corrosion rather than
kernel-migration-induced coating degradation.

EDS spectra from the degraded region showed Ni associated with the affected SiC, as shown in Figure
6-7. Nickel was identified near the IPyC/SiC boundary and throughout the SiC layer, and SiC fragments
were observed in the adjacent OPyC near the damaged region. Nickel-associated SiC corrosion has been
observed previously in AGR TRISO fuel examinations and is distinct from intrinsic fission-product attack
initiated at the inner surface of the SiC (Hunn et al. 2020; Stempien et al. 2021). Similar Ni-associated
degradation has also been reported in AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 1 fuel behavior summaries (Demkowicz et al.
2026). The morphology and chemistry observed in 363-SP02 therefore support interpretation of the
particle as an outside-in Ni-associated SiC degradation event.

The 363-SP02 kernel did not show the same migration-related compositional asymmetry observed in the
migrated RS particles. The EDS line scan across the 363-SP02 kernel showed comparatively uniform U,
C, and O profiles, with no obvious enrichment of uranium in a migration direction or carbon enrichment
on a trailing side. Combined with the XCT and optical observations of only minimal kernel displacement,
the SEM/EDS results indicate that the 363-SP02 failure was not caused by kernel migration. Additional
SEM images, EDS spectra, and EDS maps supporting the interpretation of 363-SP02 as an outside-in Ni-
associated SiC degradation event are provided in Appendix D and Appendix E.

6.4 SUMMARY OF SEM/EDS FINDINGS

The SEM/EDS results provide three main findings. First, the low-, average-, and high-110mAg RS particles
did not exhibit a single structural or chemical feature that explained the measured range in retained silver
inventory. High-Z fission product and actinide features were observed in the buffer, IPyC, at the
IPyC/SiC boundary, and within the SiC layer for particles across the silver-retention range. Even the two
higher-110mAg particles, 363-RS07 and 363-RS12, showed differences in SiC feature chemistry, including
differences in resolved Rh and apparent lanthanide contributions. These differences appear more closely
related to local buffer morphology and buffer/IPyC attachment than to retained silver inventory alone.
With the limited set of particles examined, no single coating-layer feature or consistent difference in
feature chemistry was identified in the polished cross sections that could account for the observed
variation in 110mAg. Second, SEM/EDS of migrated RS particles showed that kernel migration was
associated with compositional redistribution within the kernel. The strongest evidence came from 363-
RS08, which was polished into the XCT-defined plane of maximum migration, but similar
uranium/carbon asymmetry was also observed in 363-RS07 and 363-RS12. In these particles, uranium
concentration was higher toward the apparent migration direction, while carbon concentration was higher
on the trailing side. This indicates that migration involved redistribution of kernel constituents. Third, the
confirmed SiC failure particle, 363-SP02, was chemically and morphologically distinct from the migrated
RS particles. The failed region showed outside-in degradation through the OPyC and SiC, with Ni
associated with the affected SiC. The particle showed only minimal kernel migration and did not exhibit
the same kernel compositional asymmetry observed in migrated RS particles. Therefore, the 363-SP02
failure is most consistent with outside-in Ni-associated SiC corrosion rather than migration-induced
coating degradation.

Overall, SEM/EDS supports the broader conclusion from DLBL, IMGA, optical microscopy, and XCT:
kernel migration was a prevalent high-temperature microstructural response in Compact 3-6-3, but it did
not measurably degrade the structural coating layers or produce the observed coating failure. The novel
contribution of the SEM/EDS analysis is the identification of U/C compositional asymmetry in migrated
kernels, which suggests that kernel migration in heterogeneous UCO fuel involves coupled morphological
and chemical redistribution.

 7. CONCLUSIONS

PIE of Compact 3-6-3 demonstrates that AGR UCO TRISO fuel can maintain a low coating-failure
fraction under the high-temperature, high-burnup conditions achieved in AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3. Compact
3-6-3 reached 14.77% FIMA at a TAVA temperature of 1363°C, yet the combined DLBL and IMGA
results indicate only limited evidence of in-pile coating failure. The elevated irradiation temperature did,
however, substantially affect fission-product transport through otherwise intact coatings. The large
inventories of 90Sr and 154Eu recovered outside the SiC are consistent with the strong temperature
dependence observed in previous AGR fuel. Little or no 110mAg was recovered from the OPyC or compact
matrix despite relatively high silver inventories measured in intact Capsule 3 compacts and individual
Compact 3-6-3 particles. The AGR-5/6/7 Capsule 3 results therefore continue to show unexpectedly high
110m
 Ag retention at irradiation temperatures where considerably greater release would have been
anticipated from earlier AGR experiments.

The most significant microstructural finding from Compact 3-6-3 was the prevalence of kernel migration.
Optical microscopy, XCT, and comparison with independent ceramography results show that migration is
a repeatable response of UCO kernels exposed to the elevated temperatures of Capsule 3 rather than an
isolated particle anomaly. The 3D XCT observations also demonstrate that migration occurs over a
continuum of severity and generally proceeds away from retained buffer–IPyC attachment toward the
larger buffer–IPyC gap. The most migrated particle examined had kernel material extending through
essentially the full buffer thickness to near the inner IPyC surface while retaining its fission products.
These observations establish kernel migration as an important high-temperature microstructural response
of irradiated UCO TRISO fuel. However, the presence of kernel migration was not accompanied by a
corresponding degradation of the structural coating layers. Buffer fracture was uncommon in the Compact
3-6-3 optical cross sections, no IPyC fractures were observed, and pronounced migration was present in
particles with intact SiC and normal fission-product inventories. This behavior differs from the more
frequent buffer and IPyC fracture observed in lower-temperature, high-burnup AGR-5/6/7 fuel and
indicates that the dominant irradiation-induced microstructural response can change substantially with
temperature.

The one confirmed SiC failure identified by IMGA exhibited only minimal kernel migration, while its
coating degradation progressed inward from the particle exterior. SEM/EDS examination indicates that
the failed region involved nickel-associated chemical attack of the SiC, consistent with an outside-in
degradation mechanism rather than failure initiated by the migrated kernel.

The examinations herein also provide initial insight into the migration process. Optical and SEM
observations indicate that migration involves changes in kernel morphology and porosity, while EDS
measurements show compositional gradients with relatively greater uranium concentration in the apparent
migration direction and increased carbon concentration on the trailing side. The controlling transport
mechanism in heterogeneous UCO remains uncertain and warrants additional study. Future work should
quantify migration using the complete 3D particle geometry, with kernel displacement and local buffer
thinning referenced to the structural coating layers to account for as-fabricated kernel and coating
geometry.

Overall, these results extend the AGR fuel-performance database into a regime where substantial
microstructural evolution occurs without a corresponding loss of TRISO performance. Kernel migration
appears to become prevalent near the high-temperature margin explored in Capsule 3, while the SiC
barrier continues to provide effective retention of normally well-retained fission products in nearly the
entire particle population. These results provide an important basis for defining the upper irradiation
behavior of UCO TRISO fuel and for determining whether kernel migration requires explicit treatment in
future fuel-performance models.

APPENDIX B. IMGA DATA

 APPENDIX B. IMGA DATA

The tables in this appendix document the IMGA data for key gamma-emitting fission products. Values
are reported as measured activities in units of Bq that have been decay-corrected to 1 day after EOL and
as various unitless ratios, as described in Section 2.1. Summary values at the bottoms of the tables for
maximum, minimum, mean, and standard deviation (SD) provide an indication of the degree of particle-
to-particle variation in these values. The M/A values—for example, Equation (2.1)—are indicative of
particle-to-particle variation in isotopic content. For particles with negligible radionuclide release, this
particle-to-particle variation may be related to variation in fissionable material and/or burnup. Fissionable
material in an as-fabricated particle may vary due to variation in kernel size (e.g., spherical kernels with
different diameters or fragmented kernels), variation in kernel uranium fraction, and/or variation in
uranium enrichment. Variation in local neutron fluence will cause a variation in burnup between spatially
separated particles even if the physical properties of the particles are identical. The M/AA values were
adjusted using a normally well-retained nuclide, usually 137Cs, to attempt to separate the variation in M/A
as a function of fission product retention from the variation as a function of fissionable material and/or
burnup. This makes the M/AA values—for example, Equation (2.5)—indicative of the fission product
retention in a particle relative to the average of all measured RS particles. Particles with M/AA near unity
for a given nuclide exhibited close to average retention of that nuclide. However, if a given nuclide was
released from a large fraction of particles, then an M/AA value of unity does not indicate good retention
of that nuclide. Such was often the case for 110mAg in particles from the AGR-1 and AGR-2 experiments
(Demkowicz et al. 2015; Stempien et al. 2021). The M/C values—for example, Equation (2.4)—provide
an indication of the particle inventory relative to the average calculated inventories (Appendix F). For
well-retained nuclides, the mean M/C values are typically 1.00 ± 0.10, in which the variation is mostly
due to error in the calculated average inventories stemming from uncertainty in the values used for the
calculation. For the AGR-1 and AGR-2 irradiations, the mean M/C values for 125Sb and 154Eu were
consistently offset from unity by more than 10% due to larger errors in the calculated values.

APPENDIX D. DETAILED SEM/EDS OBSERVATIONS

 APPENDIX D. DETAILED SEM/EDS OBSERVATIONS

D.1 Scope of analysis

This appendix provides detailed particle-by-particle SEM/EDS observations for the Compact 3-6-3
particles discussed in Section 6. Observations are included from six randomly selected particles: 363-
RS21, 363-RS39, 363-RS46, 363-RS08, 363-RS07, and 363-RS12. These particles were selected from
the IMGA long-count population to span lower-than-average, approximately average, and higher-than-
average 110mAg inventories. Special particle 363-SP02 is also included, which was identified by IMGA as
the only strongly depleted 137Cs particle and was examined to determine the root cause of the confirmed
SiC failure. The BSE overview montage figures in this appendix provide the primary spatial record for
the SEM observations. Figures D-1 through D-7 show the particle overviews and high-magnification
regions used to evaluate the distribution of high-Z features in 363-RS21, 363-RS39, 363-RS46, 363-
RS07, 363-RS12, 363-RS08, and 363-SP02, respectively. Additional SE images, BSE images, EDS
spectra, and kernel line scans are described below.

Many high-Z features were smaller than the EDS interaction volume, so spectra include contributions
from surrounding buffer, IPyC, SiC, epoxy, or mounting material. Silicon detected in features near or
within the SiC may reflect true feature chemistry, interaction-volume contribution from the SiC matrix, or
both. Aluminum was observed broadly in the spectra and is interpreted as a background or mounting-
related contribution unless otherwise noted. Peak overlaps among U, Pu, Pd, Rh, and other fission
products further limit quantitative interpretation. Therefore, the EDS results are used to identify
qualitative compositional trends rather than precise feature stoichiometry.

D.2 Particle 363-RS21: Lower-than-Average 110mAg

Particle 363-RS21 was selected as a lower-than-average 110mAg particle, with otherwise typical measured
fission product inventories as summarized in Table 6-1. The Appendix D overview montage for this
particle is shown in Figure D-1. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-1. The SE layer
image in Figure D-8 shows several structural features common to the Compact 3-6-3 RS particles,
including a circumferential crack or gap near the kernel/buffer boundary and tearing or separation near
the buffer/IPyC interface. The OPyC was detached from the SiC in the observed cross section. Fine
circumferential cracking was observed near the IPyC/SiC boundary and near the SiC/OPyC boundary, as
shown in Figure D-9 and Figure D-10. These cracks did not traverse the full SiC thickness and are not
interpreted as through-layer SiC failure.

BSE imaging in Figure D-1 shows high-Z features distributed through the buffer, near the buffer/IPyC
boundary, in the IPyC, and within the SiC layer. Higher-magnification examples are shown in Figure
D-11 and Figure D-12. EDS spectra from the buffer features are shown in Figure D-13 and indicate
primarily Zr-Mo-Ru-U-bearing material. High-Z features in the IPyC and near the IPyC/SiC boundary
contained combinations of Zr, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, U, Pu, Cs, and Ba, as shown in Figure D-14. High-Z
features within the SiC were primarily Rh-Pd-U-Pu-bearing, based on the SiC feature spectra shown in
Figure D-15. A localized low-Z region surrounded by high-Z material was observed extending partway
into the SiC, shown in Figure D-15 and spatially contextualized in Figure D-1. This feature is consistent
with localized SiC corrosion or degradation that did not propagate through the full SiC layer. Because
363-RS21 retained normal cesium inventory, this feature is not interpreted as a through-layer coating
failure.

Particle 363-RS21 showed evidence of kernel migration in the observed cross section. Figure D-1 shows
buffer thinning and protrusion of kernel material in the apparent migration direction, with carbon-rich
material on the trailing side of the kernel. The kernel EDS line scan in Figure D-16 shows increased
uranium concentration toward the apparent migration direction and increased carbon concentration on the

 D-3

trailing side. Oxygen did not show a comparable directional trend. The EDS maps in Figure E-1 provide
spatial context for this uranium/carbon asymmetry. These observations support the interpretation that
migration involved redistribution of kernel constituents rather than rigid displacement of an unchanged
kernel.

D.3 Particle 363-RS39: Lower-than-Average 110mAg

Particle 363-RS39 was also selected as a lower-than-average 110mAg particle. The Appendix D overview
montage is shown in Figure D-2. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-2. The SE layer
image in Figure D-17 shows a circumferential crack or gap near the kernel/buffer boundary and a large
buffer/IPyC gap. Unlike 363-RS21, the OPyC remained attached to the SiC in the observed cross section.
Fine circumferential cracking was observed near the IPyC/SiC boundary, as shown in Figure D-18 and
Figure D-19. These cracks were limited to the SiC interface region and were not associated with through-
layer degradation.

BSE imaging in Figure D-2 shows high-Z features in the buffer, at the buffer/IPyC boundary, at the
IPyC/SiC boundary, and within the SiC. Higher-magnification views are shown in Figure D-20 and
Figure D-21. EDS spectra from the buffer features indicated primarily Zr-Mo-U-bearing material, while
large buffer/IPyC boundary features contained Sr-Zr-Mo-U and nearby Se-Sr-Mo-Te-Ba-bearing features,
as shown in Figure D-22. The IPyC features were primarily Ru-Rh-Pd-U-Pu-bearing, with less obvious
Zr, Mo, Cs, or Ba than observed in 363-RS21, as shown in Figure D-23. High-Z features at the IPyC/SiC
boundary and within the SiC were primarily Rh-Pd-U-Pu-bearing, with variable Cs and Ba at boundary
locations, as shown in Figure D-24.

Particle 363-RS39 did not show clear kernel migration in the polished cross section. The kernel EDS line
scan in Figure D-25 did not show a strong directional uranium/carbon gradient. The EDS maps in Figure
E-2 likewise do not show the same kernel-scale asymmetry observed in migrated particles. This result
does not rule out kernel migration elsewhere in the 3D particle volume because the section was not
targeted to the migration plane, but migration-related compositional redistribution was not resolved in the
analyzed plane.

D.4 Particle 363-RS46: Approximately Average 110mAg

Particle 363-RS46 was selected as an approximately average 110mAg particle. The Appendix D overview
montage is shown in Figure D-3. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-3. The SE layer
image in Figure D-26 shows structural features similar to those observed in other RS particles, including a
circumferential kernel/buffer gap, buffer/IPyC tearing or separation, fine-scale IPyC porosity bands, and
limited low-Z inclusions or pullout in the SiC. The OPyC was detached from the SiC in the observed
cross section. The BSE all-layer image in Figure D-27 and the montage in Figure D-3 show high-Z
material distributed in the coating layers, including features near the buffer/IPyC boundary and IPyC/SiC
boundary.

Fine high-Z buffer features and larger features near the buffer/IPyC boundary were observed in Figure
D-28 and Figure D-29. EDS spectra from these features are shown in Figure D-30 and indicate primarily
Zr-Mo-U-bearing material, with more complex Sr/Te/Ba-bearing features present near some boundary
regions. High-Z features in the IPyC and near the IPyC/SiC interface are shown in Figure D-31 and
Figure D-32 and included Pd-U-Pu-Rh-bearing compositions with variable Cs and Ba near the boundary.
The distribution of high-Z features varied around the particle, with some regions showing fewer visible
features than others.

No clear kernel migration was identified in the polished cross section of 363-RS46. The kernel EDS line
scan in Figure D-33 did not show a strong directional uranium/carbon gradient, and the EDS maps in

 D-4

Figure E-3 do not show the pronounced kernel asymmetry observed in migrated particles. Because the
cross section was randomly prepared, this observation should be interpreted as absence of evidence for
migration in the analyzed plane rather than proof that the particle was unmigrated in 3D.

D.5 Particle 363-RS08: Approximately Average 110mAg, XCT-Targeted Migration Plane

Particle 363-RS08 was selected as an approximately average 110mAg particle and was prepared using XCT
guidance so that the polished section intersected the plane of maximum kernel migration. The XCT and
optical comparison is shown in Figure 5-1. The Appendix D overview montage is shown in Figure D-6.
The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-6. The cross section showed pronounced kernel
migration, with kernel material penetrating through much of the buffer thickness. The SE and BSE layer
images in Figure D-34 and Figure D-35 show common Compact 3-6-3 structural features, including the
kernel/buffer gap and buffer/IPyC separation. Some surface or charging artifacts were present because the
particle was carbon coated after XCT-guided preparation.

High-Z conglomerates were observed in the buffer adjacent to the buffer/IPyC connection point, as shown
in Figure D-36 and Figure D-37. The buffer and buffer/IPyC boundary features were primarily Zr-Mo-U-
bearing, with some Sr-U-Te-Ba-bearing features in the open buffer region leading the kernel protrusion,
as shown by the EDS spectra in Figure D-38. High-Z features in the IPyC, at the IPyC/SiC boundary, and
within the SiC contained Pd-U-Pu-Rh-bearing compositions with variable Cs and Ba near the boundary,
as shown in Figure D-39 and Figure D-40. The distribution of high-Z features varied spatially around the
particle, as shown in Figure D-41, and appeared to be associated with the local buffer/IPyC connection
geometry.

The kernel EDS line scan in Figure D-42 provides the clearest evidence for migration-associated
compositional redistribution because the section was targeted to the maximum migration plane. The
profile shows increased uranium concentration toward the apparent migration direction and increased
carbon concentration on the trailing side of the kernel. Oxygen did not show a comparably strong
directional trend. The EDS maps in Figure E-6 provide spatial context for this compositional asymmetry.
Together, Figure D-42 and Figure E-6 support the interpretation that kernel migration in Compact 3-6-3
involved redistribution of kernel constituents rather than simple rigid displacement.

D.6 Particle 363-RS07: Higher-than-Average 110mAg

Particle 363-RS07 was selected as a higher-than-average 110mAg particle. The Appendix D overview
montage is shown in Figure D-4. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-4. The SE layer
image in Figure D-43 shows a circumferential kernel/buffer gap and buffer/IPyC separation similar to
other RS particles. Fine circumferential cracking was observed near the IPyC/SiC boundary, as shown in
Figure D-44. The OPyC remained attached to the SiC over much of the observed cross section, with local
detachment in some regions. No through-layer SiC degradation was identified.

BSE imaging in Figure D-4 shows high-Z features distributed through the buffer, IPyC, IPyC/SiC
boundary, and SiC. Higher-magnification views of the feature distribution are shown in Figure D-45,
Figure D-46, and Figure D-46. Fine buffer features were primarily U-Zr-bearing, as shown in Figure
D-48. IPyC and boundary features contained variable Zr, Rh, Pd, U, Pu, Cs, and Ba, as shown in Figure
D-49 and Figure D-50. The SiC feature spectrum for 363-RS07 is shown in Figure 6-X and is compared
directly with the corresponding 363-RS12 spectrum. The 363-RS07 SiC feature was Pd-U-Pu-bearing,
but Rh was not clearly resolved. Apparent Ce and/or Nd contributions were observed in the higher-energy
portion of the spectrum. This differs from 363-RS12, where Rh was more clearly resolved. Because 363-
RS07 exhibited buffer/IPyC separation and a more open buffer-gap morphology, the difference in SiC
feature chemistry may reflect local transport pathways associated with evolved buffer structure rather
than retained 110mAg inventory alone.

 D-5

The cross section showed evidence of kernel migration, including asymmetric kernel morphology and
carbon-rich material on the trailing side of the kernel. The kernel EDS line scan in Figure 6-4 showed
increased uranium concentration toward the apparent migration direction and increased carbon
concentration away from that direction. The EDS maps in Figure E-4 provide spatial support for this
interpretation. Because the cross section was not XCT-targeted, the full 3D migration direction and
magnitude cannot be determined from this section alone; however, the observed U/C asymmetry is
consistent with the migration-associated redistribution observed in 363-RS08.

D.7 Particle 363-RS12: Higher-than-Average 110mAg, Mostly Attached Buffer/IPyC

Particle 363-RS12 was selected as a higher-than-average 110mAg particle. The Appendix D overview
montage is shown in Figure D-5. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in Figure E-5. The SE layer
image in Figure D-51 shows that 363-RS12 differed from most other RS particles because the
buffer/IPyC interface remained largely attached around the circumference. Two radial cracks extended
through the buffer, as shown in Figure D-52 and Figure D-53. This morphology was uncommon among
the selected Compact 3-6-3 RS particles and provides a useful comparison to particles where migration
was associated with more extensive buffer/IPyC tearing or separation.

BSE imaging in Figure D-5 shows high-Z features distributed through the buffer, IPyC, IPyC/SiC
boundary, and SiC. Higher-magnification images are shown in Figure D-54. The fine buffer features were
primarily U-Zr-bearing, while larger conglomerates included more complex Se-Sr-Zr-Mo-U-Te-Ba-
bearing compositions, as shown in Figure D-55. Large buffer/IPyC boundary features were concentrated
on one side of the particle and were embedded within residual buffer attached to the IPyC. IPyC and
boundary features contained variable U, Zr, Pd, Rh, Ru, Pu, Cs, and Ba, as shown in Figure D-56 and
Figure D-57. The SiC feature spectrum for 363-RS12 is shown in Figure 6-2. Unlike 363-RS07, Rh was
more clearly resolved in the SiC feature spectrum. Apparent rare-earth contributions, including Ce and/or
Nd, were also observed. Because 363-RS12 retained a largely attached buffer/IPyC interface, comparison
with 363-RS07 suggests that buffer morphology and local transport geometry may have influenced SiC
feature chemistry even among particles with similarly high retained 110mAg.

Although buffer thinning was less obvious in 363-RS12 than in 363-RS08, the kernel EDS maps and line
scan showed compositional asymmetry. The line scan in Figure 6-5 showed increased uranium
concentration toward the apparent migration direction and increased carbon concentration on the trailing
side. The corresponding EDS maps in Figure E-5 show the spatial distribution of C, O, and U across the
kernel. This behavior is important because it occurred despite the largely attached buffer/IPyC interface.
Particle 363-RS12 therefore suggests that buffer/IPyC tearing and large buffer-gap formation may
influence migration severity or direction, but they are not necessarily required for kernel compositional
redistribution.

D.8 Particle 363-SP02: Low-137Cs Special Particle / SiC Failure

Particle 363-SP02 was identified by IMGA as the only strongly depleted 137Cs particle in the surveyed
Compact 3-6-3 population. XCT and optical microscopy of this particle are shown in Figure 4-4. The
Appendix D overview montage is shown in Figure D-7. The kernel-scale EDS maps are provided in
Figure E-7. The SEM cross section showed extensive localized degradation of the OPyC and SiC. Unlike
the migrated RS particles, the damaged region progressed inward from the exterior of the particle rather
than outward from the kernel. The SE overview in Figure D-58 and the radial buffer cracks shown in
Figure D-59 document the general coating morphology. The outer SiC damage region is shown in Figure
D-60, while circumferential cracking and more extensive SiC degradation are shown in Figure D-61 and
Figure D-62. Figure 6-6 shows significant localized SiC degradation.

 D-6

EDS spectra from high-Z material covering the buffer are shown in Figure D-63, and spectra from IPyC
and interface features are shown in Figure D-64. These spectra show substantial high-Z material
associated with the failed particle, consistent with redistribution of actinides and fission products after
coating degradation. The spectra from the SiC boundary and SiC features in Figure 6-7 showed Ni
associated with the affected SiC region. The presence of Ni, together with the outside-in morphology of
the degradation, supports interpretation of 363-SP02 as a Ni-associated SiC corrosion event rather than a
migration-induced failure.

The 363-SP02 kernel line scan in Figure D-65 did not show the pronounced uranium/carbon asymmetry
observed in migrated RS particles such as 363-RS08, 363-RS07, and 363-RS12. The EDS maps in Figure
E-7 likewise do not show the same migration-associated kernel-scale redistribution. Combined with the
XCT and optical observation of only minimal kernel migration in Figure 4-4, the SEM/EDS results
indicate that 363-SP02 was chemically and morphologically distinct from the migrated RS particles.

The SP02 result is therefore important for interpreting Compact 3-6-3 performance. The one confirmed
SiC failure particle showed outside-in Ni-associated degradation, whereas the migrated RS particles
retained normal 137Cs inventories and did not show through-layer SiC degradation. The detailed
SEM/EDS evidence supports the conclusion that kernel migration was not the root cause of the observed
coating failure.
