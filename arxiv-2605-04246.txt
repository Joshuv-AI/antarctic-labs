Abstract
. In this article, we study unbalanced optimal transport (UOT) and establish a control-theoretic dynamical extension, which we call the unbalanced density control (UDC), for a class of Gaussian reference measures. In the static setting, we consider UOT with quadratic transport cost and Kullback–Leibler penalties on the marginals relative to prescribed Gaussian measures. We show that the infinite-dimensional variational problem admits an exact Gaussian reduction , yielding a finite-dimensional optimization over masses, means, and covariances, together with a closed-form expression for the optimal transported mass. We then formulate UDC for discrete-time linear systems, where the initial and terminal state measures are imposed softly through KL penalties and the intermediate evolution is governed by controlled linear dynamics with quadratic control cost. For this problem, we prove that any feasible solution can be replaced, without loss of optimality, by a Gaussian initial measure and an affine-Gaussian control policy. This leads to an exact finite-dimensional reformulation and, after a standard covariance-steering lifting, to an SDP-based optimization for fixed mass, again coupled with a closed-form mass update. We further establish existence of optimal solutions and identify a sufficient condition under which the affine-Gaussian UDC policy is deterministic. These results provide globally optimal solution methods for both Gaussian UOT and Gaussian UDC. Finally, we illustrate our results with several numerical examples.

1.1.
Motivations

The classical OT problems (
(MP)
) and (
(KP)
) are balanced in the sense that the transport plan has prescribed source and target marginals, and hence the two measures must carry the
same total mass
. In many applications, however, the two objects being compared
do not have the same total mass
. This occurs, for example, when mass is created, destroyed
[
13
]
, dissipated
[
15
]
, unobserved, or only partially available. In a control-theoretic context, this issue appears naturally when the endpoint distributions are not exact boundary conditions, but desired or estimated reference profiles. For example, consider a collection of mobile agents, particles, or resources whose aggregate state is described by a finite nonnegative measure. At the initial time, the available population may be known only approximately from measurements, and its effective mass may represent the number of active agents, the amount of material, the total probability weight assigned to reliable observations, or the confidence in the initial estimate. At the terminal time, the desired profile may prescribe a target spatial distribution or coverage pattern, but not necessarily with the same total mass as the initial profile. Some agents may be unavailable, some mass may be irrelevant or unobserved, and the desired terminal profile may only be partially attainable under the available control authority.

In such a situation, a balanced density-steering or covariance-steering formulation
[
26
,
17
,
46
,
3
,
37
,
31
,
35
]
can be too restrictive. If the endpoint distributions are imposed as hard constraints, then the controller is forced to match the prescribed initial and terminal distributions exactly, even when these distributions should only be interpreted as reference profiles. Moreover, after normalizing the measures into probability distributions, one loses the information encoded in their total masses. Thus, a formulation based only on balanced probability measures cannot distinguish between a high-confidence reference profile with large mass and a weak or partial reference profile with small mass. Thus, we ask:

Can we establish a control formulation that transports a dynamically evolving state measure while allowing mismatch from the endpoint references in a quantified way?

This brings us to
unbalanced optimal transport
. Instead of requiring exact marginal constraints, UOT penalizes deviations from prescribed reference measures, often through Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence terms. As a consequence, UOT furnishes a mathematically natural framework for comparing finite nonnegative measures with unequal masses. Again, at an
informal level
, given two finite nonnegative measures
\alpha,\beta
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, one seeks a transport plan (with suitable regularity)
\pi
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whose marginals
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are not required to coincide exactly with
\alpha
and
\beta
, but are instead penalized for deviating from them. A standard (informal) UOT formulation is
[
43
,
7
,
20
,
41
,
16
]

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}

\displaystyle\|x_{2}-x_{1}\|^{2}\,{\,\rm d}\pi(x_{1},x_{2})+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{1}\parallel\alpha)+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{2}\parallel\beta),

where
\gamma>0
. Here,
\alpha
plays the role of the source reference measure and
\beta
that of the target reference measure, while the KL penalties relax the hard marginal constraints of the Kantorovich problem and allow for mass variation. Applications of UOT have emerged in several fields, including ML
[
43
]
and control
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

The purpose of this paper is to develop the analogous idea for controlled dynamical systems. We consider a finite nonnegative state measure evolving under a discrete-time linear system. The total mass of the controlled state measure is preserved along the dynamics, but it is not prescribed a priori by either endpoint reference. Instead, the optimization chooses the transported mass and the control policy jointly: the running cost penalizes control effort, while KL terms penalize deviation of the initial and terminal state measures from the prescribed Gaussian references. We call this problem
unbalanced density control
(UDC). Thus, UDC is a density-control problem in which the controller balances three competing objectives: remaining close to the initial reference, reaching close to the terminal reference, and spending minimal control energy. This viewpoint is natural for Gaussian references. In linear systems, Gaussian distributions and their first two moments are central to covariance steering and stochastic control
[
29
,
36
,
45
]
. However, the unbalanced setting introduces an additional degree of freedom: the transported mass itself becomes a decision variable. The resulting problem therefore asks not only how the mean and covariance should be steered, but also how much mass should be transported when exact agreement with the endpoint references is neither required nor desirable.

1.2.
Natural questions

The preceding example motivates UDC as a soft-endpoint analogue of density steering. However, it also reveals a basic mathematical difficulty. Once the initial and terminal measures are imposed through KL penalties rather than hard constraints, the optimization is no longer a standard covariance-steering problem with prescribed endpoint moments. In the static case, UOT is posed over all finite nonnegative transport plans. In the dynamical case, UDC is posed over the initial state measure and over feedback policies. Thus, even under linear dynamics and quadratic control cost, the problem is infinite-dimensional.

Also, it is not immediate that Gaussian reference measures should lead to Gaussian optimizers in either the static or dynamical unbalanced problems. In the static UOT problem, the optimizer could in principle be a non-Gaussian transport plan. In UDC, the optimal initial measure and the optimal control policy could also be non-Gaussian, nonlinear, or genuinely randomized. This raises several natural questions:

(
\mathsf{Q}
-a)

Can one formulate a control-theoretic analogue of UOT in which a finite nonnegative state measure evolves under linear dynamics, endpoint matching is imposed softly through KL penalties, and control effort is penalized along the trajectory?

(
\mathsf{Q}
-b)

If the reference measures are Gaussian, i.e.,
\alpha\coloneqq c_{\alpha}\,\mathcal{N}(m_{\alpha},\Sigma_{\alpha})
and
\beta\coloneqq c_{\beta}\,\mathcal{N}(m_{\beta},\Sigma_{\beta})
, can the static UOT problem be reduced, without loss of optimality, to a finite-dimensional optimization over mass and second-order moments?

(
\mathsf{Q}
-c)

In the dynamical setting, can one similarly restrict attention, without loss of optimality, to Gaussian initial measures and affine-Gaussian control laws?

(
\mathsf{Q}
-d)

If such reductions are possible, can the resulting optimization problems be solved
globally
, rather than merely approximately or heuristically?

(
\mathsf{Q}
-e)

In the UDC problem, does one genuinely need a randomized control covariance term in the optimal policy, or does deterministic affine feedback already suffice?

These questions are precisely the ones addressed in this paper. We answer
(

Q

-a)
positively by proposing a concrete UDC formulation for discrete-time linear systems with soft initial and terminal KL penalties. We answer
(

Q

-b)
and
(

Q

-c)
by proving exact Gaussian-reduction results for the static and dynamical problems, respectively. We answer
(

Q

-d)
by deriving globally solvable finite-dimensional reformulations: a convex inner problem plus a scalar mass update for UOT, and an SDP-based reduced problem plus an analogous mass update for UDC. Finally,
(

Q

-e)
is answered
negatively
under an additional regularity condition: if the optimal state covariance sequence is positive definite, then, under the standing nonsingularity assumption on the system matrix, the optimal affine-Gaussian UDC policy is deterministic.

1.3.
Contributions

(1)

Unbalanced density-control formulation.
We formulate UDC as a control-theoretic dynamical counterpart of UOT for discrete-time linear systems. The state measure evolves according to linear dynamics, the running cost is quadratic in the control, and the initial and terminal specifications are imposed through KL penalties relative to Gaussian reference measures. This provides an unbalanced analogue of density and covariance steering.

(2)

Gaussian reduction and global solution for UOT.
We show that, although UOT is originally posed over general transport plans, one may restrict attention, without loss of optimality, to
Gaussian marginals
and the corresponding
affine Gaussian coupling
. This yields a finite-dimensional reduction in terms of mass, means, and covariances. The reduced problem separates into a convex moment optimization and a scalar mass optimization, the latter admitting a closed-form solution.

(3)

Gaussian reduction and global solution for UDC.
For the dynamical problem, we prove that any feasible solution can be replaced by a Gaussian initial measure and an affine-Gaussian feedback law constructed from the first and second moments of the original solution, with no increase in cost. This leads to a finite-dimensional SDP reformulation for fixed mass, combined with a closed-form mass update.

(4)

Existence and structure of optimal solutions.
We establish existence of minimizers for the reduced UOT and UDC problems. We further give a structural sufficient condition for deterministic optimality in UDC. Thus, randomized affine-Gaussian controls are useful for the reduction argument, while deterministic affine feedback suffices whenever the optimal covariance trajectory remains positive definite.

(5)

Entropy-regularized extensions.
We also discuss entropic and maximum-entropy variants, including an entropic UOT model and a MaxEnt UDC problem. The same Gaussian-reduction viewpoint extends naturally to these settings and yields tractable finite-dimensional formulations.

\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with the same mass

. For

, respectively. Let

Proof.

. Then

Proposition 3

(Gaussian minimizes KL)
.

Fix
c,c^{\prime}>0
,
m,m^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}\succ\textup{O}
. Let
\pi
be a finite nonnegative measure of mass
c
such that
\bar{\pi}
has mean
m
and covariance
\Sigma
. Then we have the inequality

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))\geqslant\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(c{\mathcal{N}}(m,\Sigma)\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})).

Proof.

If
\pi
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
, then by the definition of the KL divergence,
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))=+\infty,
and the claim is immediate. Hence, it remains to consider the case when
\pi
is absolutely continuous with respect to
c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
. Since
c^{\prime}>0
, this is equivalent to
\bar{\pi}
being absolutely continous with respect to
{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
, so
\bar{\pi}
admits a Lebesgue density, say
p
. We write

\displaystyle\pi=c\,p(x){\,\rm d}x\quad\text{and}\quad c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})=c^{\prime}\,q(x){\,\rm d}x,

where
q(x)\coloneqq{\mathcal{N}}(x;m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})
; see notations in §
1
. Then, using the expression of KL divergence for absolutely continuous unbalanced measures
[
43
]

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=c\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\log\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)p(x){\,\rm d}x+c\log\left(\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}\right)-c+c^{\prime}.

Which is
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))=c\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\parallel{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))+c\log\left(\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}\right)-c+c^{\prime}.

We also have

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\bar{\pi}\parallel{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p(x)\log\left(\frac{p(x)}{{\mathcal{N}}(x;m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})}\right){\,\rm d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=-H(p)-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}p(x)\log{\mathcal{N}}(x;m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}){\,\rm d}x.

Since,
\log{\mathcal{N}}(x;m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})=-\tfrac{d}{2}\log(2\pi)-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\det\Sigma^{\prime}-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-m^{\prime})^{\top}(\Sigma^{\prime})^{-1}(x-m^{\prime}),
the second term depends only on the mean and covariance of
\bar{\pi}
, which are fixed to be
m
and
\Sigma
. Hence, we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))=-c\,H(p)+\mathrm{const.},

where
\mathrm{const.}
depends only on the fixed quantities
c,c^{\prime},m,m^{\prime},\Sigma,\Sigma^{\prime}
. From
[
18
, Theorem 8.6.5]
, it holds that
H(p)\leqslant H({\mathcal{N}}(m,\Sigma))
and consequently,
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime}))\geqslant\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(c{\mathcal{N}}(m,\Sigma)\parallel c^{\prime}{\mathcal{N}}(m^{\prime},\Sigma^{\prime})).
Our proof is complete.
∎

3.2.
Optimization-based method for solving UOT

Originally, Problem
2.1
is formulated as a variational problem over transport plans
\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. As a result of Theorem
1
, Problem
2.1
can be, without loss of optimality, reduced to a finite dimensional optimization. For
c,c^{\prime}>0
,
m,m_{1},m_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and
\Sigma,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\Sigma,\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\succ\textup{O}
, substituting
\pi=c\ {\mathcal{N}}(m,\Sigma)
with corresponding marginal
\pi_{1}=c\ {\mathcal{N}}(m_{1},\Sigma_{1})
and
\pi_{2}=c\ {\mathcal{N}}(m_{2},\Sigma_{2})
to Problem
2.1
, we obtain a finite dimensional optimization over
c,m,\Sigma
, but since the optimal transport plan between
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
are induced by (
(8)
), the optimization problem is further reduced to the one over the tuple
c,m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}
:

((9))

\displaystyle\min_{c,m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}}\quad c\,M(m_{1},m_{2})+c\,C(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})+\psi(c),

where various quantities in (
(9)
) are

\displaystyle M(m_{1},m_{2})\coloneqq\|m_{2}-m_{1}\|^{2}\hskip-2.84526pt+\hskip-2.84526pt\frac{\gamma}{2}(m_{1}-m_{\alpha})^{\top}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}(m_{1}-m_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\gamma}{2}(m_{2}-m_{\beta})^{\top}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(m_{2}-m_{\beta})

\displaystyle C(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\coloneqq-2\tr\left(\sqrt{\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}}\right)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\tr(\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\Sigma_{2})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\det\Sigma_{2}+\tr\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\quad+\frac{\gamma}{2}\tr(\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}\Sigma_{1})-\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\det\Sigma_{1}+\tr\Sigma_{1}

((10))

\displaystyle\psi(c)\coloneqq\frac{c\gamma}{2}(\log\det\Sigma_{\alpha}+\log\det\Sigma_{\beta}-2d)+\gamma\phi_{c_{\alpha}}(c)+\gamma\phi_{c_{\beta}}(c)

((11))

\displaystyle\phi_{c^{\prime}}(c)\coloneqq c\log\!\frac{c}{c^{\prime}}-c+c^{\prime}.

Note that all functions defined in (
3.2
) are convex with respect to their arguments. However, since we have the multiplication between decision variables, (
(9)
) is not jointly convex. To this end, we proceed in the following manner; first, it can be seen that (
(9)
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{c}\min_{m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}}\quad c\,M(m_{1},m_{2})+c\,C(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})+\psi(c),

which is also equivalent to the problem

((12))

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69054pt\min_{c}\,c\Bigl(\,\min_{m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}}M(m_{1},m_{2})+C(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2})\Bigr)+\psi(c).

Therefore, without loss of optimality, we can first perform the optimization with respect to
m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}
, and perform the one with respect to
c
using the optimal value of the first subproblem. More precisely, denote by
p^{*}
the optimal value of the following subproblem:

((13))

\displaystyle\min_{m_{1},\Sigma_{1},m_{2},\Sigma_{2}}\,M(m_{1},m_{2})+C(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}).

Then, (
(12)
) can be written as

((14))

\displaystyle\min_{c}\,\,cp^{*}+\psi(c),

which is a scalar optimization problem, and we have the immediate consequences.

Corollary 1

(Optimal mass for UOT problem)
.

Let
p^{*}
denote the optimal value of (
(13)
). Let
L\coloneqq\log\det\Sigma_{\alpha}+\log\det\Sigma_{\beta}-2d
, them the optimal mass for Problem
2.1
is

((15))

\displaystyle c^{\ast}\coloneqq\sqrt{c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{p^{*}}{2\gamma}-\frac{L}{4}\right).

Proof.

Since (
(14)
) is a scalar strictly convex optimization problem on
(0,+\infty)
, any stationary point is necessarily the unique global minimizer. A direct differentiation yields the results, and we omit the elementary calculation.
∎

3.2.1.
Extension to entropic UOT

In this brief subsection, we show that our previous results, can be extended for the well-known entropic UOT (EUOT) problem
[
25
]
. EUOT is posed by the following variational problem:

Let
\sigma,\gamma>0
, and
\alpha,\beta
are as defined in (
(2)
). Find an optimal transport plan
\pi\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
that solves:

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi}J_{3}(\pi)\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left\lVert x_{2}-x_{1}\right\rVert^{2}\,{\,\rm d}\pi(x,y)+

\displaystyle\sigma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi\parallel\alpha\otimes\beta)+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{1}\parallel\alpha)+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{2}\parallel\beta).

The notation
\alpha\otimes\beta
is the standard product measure for given
\alpha
and
\beta
. As in the UOT case, following the same steps as given in Proposition
1
, one can show that Problem
3.2.1
admits a minimizer. We therefore omit the details.

Moreover, similar to the UOT case Problem
3.2.1
can be reduced to a finite-dimensional optimization problem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\pi
be any feasible solution to Problem
3.2.1
. Denote mass of
\pi
by
c
and the mean and covariance of
\bar{\pi}
by

\displaystyle m\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\bar{\pi}}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}m_{1}\\
m_{2}\end{pmatrix},\,\,\Sigma={\mathbb{E}}_{(x,y)\sim\bar{\pi}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}-m\right)\left(\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}-m\right)^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{1}&\Sigma_{3}\\
\Sigma_{3}^{\top}&\Sigma_{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Then, for a Gaussian transport plan of the form
\pi^{\rm G}\coloneqq c\,{\mathcal{N}}(m,\Sigma)
, it holds that
J_{3}(\pi^{\rm G})\leqslant J_{3}(\pi).

We skip the proof since it follows by applying the same steps given in Theorem
1
. Exploiting this theorem, we reduce Problem
3.2.1
to the following finite dimensional optimization problem:

((16))

\displaystyle\min_{c,\Sigma_{1},m_{1},\Sigma_{2},m_{2},\Sigma_{3}}\hskip-14.22636ptc\,\bar{M}(m_{1},m_{2})+c\,\bar{C}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3})+\bar{\psi}(c),

and in (
(16)
), several terms are given by

\displaystyle\bar{M}(m_{1},m_{2})\coloneqq\|m_{2}-m_{1}\|^{2}+\frac{\sigma+\gamma}{2}(m_{1}-m_{\alpha})^{\top}\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}(m_{1}-m_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{\sigma+\gamma}{2}(m_{2}-m_{\beta})^{\top}\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}(m_{2}-m_{\beta}),

\displaystyle\bar{C}(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2},\Sigma_{3})\coloneqq\tr\Sigma_{1}+\tr\Sigma_{2}-2\tr\Sigma_{3}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\sigma+\gamma)}{2}\bigl(\tr{\Sigma_{\alpha}^{-1}\Sigma_{1}}+\tr{\Sigma_{\beta}^{-1}\Sigma_{2}}-\log\det\Sigma_{1}\bigr)

\displaystyle-\frac{\sigma}{2}\log\det\left(\Sigma_{2}-\Sigma_{3}^{\top}\Sigma_{1}^{-1}\Sigma_{3}\right)-\frac{\gamma}{2}\log\det\Sigma_{2},

\displaystyle\bar{\psi}(c)\coloneqq c(\sigma+2\gamma)\left(\log c-1-\frac{d}{2}\right)+\frac{c(\sigma+\gamma)}{2}\bigl(\log\det\Sigma_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\log\det\Sigma_{\beta}-2\log c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}\bigr)+\sigma c_{\alpha}c_{\beta}+\gamma(c_{\alpha}+c_{\beta}).

As in the UOT case, the problem is not jointly convex, but it decouples into the optimization over
c
and that over the remaining variables.

Remark 1
.

In the EUOT case, the reduced finite-dimensional problem (
(16)
) includes the off-diagonal block
\Sigma_{3}
of the covariance matrix of the transport plan as a decision variable. By contrast, in the UOT case, the reduced problem (
(9)
) involves only the diagonal blocks
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, corresponding to the marginal covariances. This distinction originates from the different nature of Theorems
1
and
3
. In the UOT case, Theorem
1
provides an explicit expression for the optimal off-diagonal block in terms of the diagonal blocks, so that the reduced problem can be written only in terms of the marginal covariances. In contrast, Theorem
3
reduces the infinite-dimensional EUOT problem to a finite-dimensional Gaussian optimization, but does not eliminate the off-diagonal block explicitly. Although one may formally express the stationary off-diagonal block in terms of the diagonal blocks through the first-order optimality condition, substituting this expression yields a nonconvex finite-dimensional problem, which is not tractable computationally. For this reason, we keep
\Sigma_{3}
as an explicit decision variable in the EUOT formulation.

4.
Global Solutions to UDC

In the previous section, we showed that the UOT (and the EUOT) problem, originally posed as a variational problem, can be transformed without loss of optimality to a finite-dimensional optimization over the first and second moments, and can therefore be solved globally via convex optimization. In this section, we establish an analogous result for the (density control) Problem
2.2
.

4.1.
Gaussian reduction for UDC

Recall the expression of the objective function
J_{2}\bigl(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr)
in (
(7)
) and let
\bigl(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr)
be a feasible solution to Problem
2.2
. Define the total mass
0<c\coloneqq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\,\rm d}\pi_{1}
and, for each
t=1,...,T-1
, define the normalized joint probability measure
p_{t}
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d^{\prime}}
by:

\displaystyle p_{t}({\,\rm d}x,{\,\rm d}u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{c}U_{t}({\,\rm d}u|x)\pi_{t}({\,\rm d}x),

where
\pi_{t}
is defined recursively by (
(6)
). Denote the first and second moments by

\displaystyle m_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}[x_{t}],\quad\Sigma_{t}:={\rm Cov}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}(x_{t}),

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}[u_{t}],\quad\Sigma^{u}_{t}:={\rm Cov}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}(u_{t}),

((17))

\displaystyle\Lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\rm Cov}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}(u_{t},x_{t})={\mathbb{E}}_{(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}}[\left(u_{t}-\bar{u}_{t}\right)\left(x_{t}-m_{t}\right)^{\top}].

Here
m_{t}
and
\Sigma_{t}
denote the mean and covariance of the normalized state distribution at time
t
, equivalently of the state marginal of

p_{t}
. Thus
m_{t}
, in the dynamical setting, plays the same role as the endpoint means
m_{1},m_{2}
of the normalized marginals in the (UOT) Problem
2.1
.

Using the moments in (
4.1
), we construct a Gaussian initial distribution and a Gaussian conditional control law of the form

((18))

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\rm G}

\displaystyle:=c\,{\mathcal{N}}(m_{1},\Sigma_{1}),

((19))

\displaystyle U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x)

\displaystyle:={\mathcal{N}}\Bigl(\bar{u}_{t}+\Lambda_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{\dagger}(x-m_{t}),\;\Sigma^{u}_{t}-\Lambda_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{t}^{\top}\Bigr).

Note that since

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{t}&\Lambda_{t}^{\top}\\
\Lambda_{t}&\Sigma_{t}^{u}\end{bmatrix}

is the covariance matrix of
(x_{t},u_{t})\sim p_{t}
, it is positive semidefinite. Therefore,
\Sigma^{u}_{t}-\Lambda_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{\dagger}\Lambda_{t}^{\top}
is positive semidefinite, which follows from the generalized Schur complement for positive semidefinite block matrices
[
19

. The quadratic, trace, and mean terms are continuous. For the endpoint covariance term, define

and

We now show that

[
2

on

\circ

For

\textup{O}&\Sigma_{t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&\textup{O}\\
\Sigma_{t}^{-1}&I\end{bmatrix}

Plugging this to (
(40)
), we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}M_{t}^{00}&M_{t}^{01}\\
M_{t}^{10}&M_{t}^{11}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}I&S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}\\
\textup{O}&I\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Y_{t}-S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}&\textup{O}\\
\textup{O}&\Sigma_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\textup{O}

Therefore, it holds that

((43)a)

\displaystyle M_{t}^{00}\left(Y_{t}-S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}\right)=\textup{O}

((43)b)

\displaystyle(M_{t}^{01})^{\top}\left(Y_{t}-S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}\right)=\textup{O}

Combining (
(41)a
), (
(43)b
), and the invertibility of
A
, we get

((44))

\displaystyle L_{t}B\left(Y_{t}-S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}\right)=\textup{O}.

From (
(41)b
), (
(43)a
), and (
(44)
), we finally obtain

\displaystyle\left(I-B^{\top}L_{t}B\right)\left(Y_{t}-S_{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}\right)=Y_{t}-S_{t}^{\top}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}=\textup{O}

The proof is complete.
∎

This result is analogous to the one that deterministic affine policy is optimal for the UOT problem.

5.
MaxEnt UDC

We conclude our theoretical developments by considering a maximum-entropy variant of the proposed UDC formulation. The maximum-entropy viewpoint
[
27
]
augments the quadratic control objective with an entropy reward on the control policy, thereby encouraging randomized policies with higher dispersion while preserving the prescribed moment-level steering structure. In this section, we introduce a MaxEnt UDC problem, formulated as a UDC problem with an additional entropy-regularization term. We keep all the assumptions and data from Problem
2.2
(with the obvious modifications) and consider the following problem.

Let the horizon
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
be fixed and assume that the admissible set
\mathcal{U}
defined in §
2.2
is nonempty. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\gamma>0
; given the preceding problem data, find an optimal initial measure
\pi_{1}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{2}^{+}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and control policy
\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}
that solves

\displaystyle\inf_{\pi_{1},\,\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\left(\|u_{t}\|^{2}-\varepsilon\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{1}\parallel\alpha)+\gamma\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{KL}}(\pi_{T}\parallel\beta)=:J_{4}(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1})

5.1.
Gaussian reduction for MaxEnt UDC

Using our previous developments we develop a Gaussian reduction for Problem
5
.

Theorem 6

(Gaussian reduction for MaxEnt UDC)
.

Let
(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1})
be any feasible solution to Problem
5
. As per Lemma
1
, let
(\pi_{1}^{\rm G},\{U_{t}^{\rm G}\}_{t=1}^{T-1})
be the Gaussian solution constructed from the first and second moment of
(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1})
. Then the following inequality holds

\displaystyle J_{4}\bigl(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr)\geqslant J_{4}\bigl(\pi_{1}^{\rm G},\{U_{t}^{\rm G}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr).

Proof.

Note that
J_{4}=J_{2}-\varepsilon{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right]
. Also, from Theorem
4
, we have
J_{2}\bigl(\pi_{1},\{U_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr)\geqslant J_{2}\bigl(\pi_{1}^{\rm G},\{U_{t}^{\rm G}\}_{t=1}^{T-1}\bigr)
. Therefore, it suffices to verify that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right]\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right].

For each
t=1,...,T-1
, let us the control policy
\bar{U}_{t}(u|x):=U_{t}(u+\Lambda_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{\dagger}x|x),

where
\Lambda_{t}
is defined in (
(23)
). Then it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(\bar{U}_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right]\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(\bar{U}_{t}\right)\right]=c\,\mathrm{H}\left(\bar{U}_{t}\right).

The equality holds since
\bar{U}_{t}(\cdot|x)
is a shift of
U_{t}(\cdot|x)
under fixed variable
x
, and the inequality is valid since the removing the conditioning does not increase entropy. Also,
\bar{U}_{t}
and
U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x)
have the same covariance and it is well-known that Gaussian attains maximum entropy under fixed covariance, thus
c\,\mathrm{H}\left(\bar{U}_{t}\right)\leqslant c\,\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x)\right)={\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}^{\rm G}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right].
Therefore, we obtain
{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right]\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{x_{t}\sim\pi_{t}^{\rm G}}\left[\mathrm{H}\left(U_{t}^{\rm G}(\cdot|x_{t})\right)\right].
Summing up both sides from
t=1
to
T-1
, we obtain the desired inequality, which finishes the proof.
∎

5.2.
Equivalent reformulation of MaxEnt UDC

Employing Theorem
6
we can transform Problem
5
into the following finite dimensional optimization problem:

((45))

\displaystyle\inf_{c,m_{t},\Sigma_{t},v_{t},K_{t},\Sigma^{u}_{t}}\,\,c\Biggl[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Bigl(\|v_{t}\|^{2}+\tr(K_{t}\Sigma_{t}K_{t}^{\top})+\tr(\Sigma^{u}_{t})-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\log\det(\Sigma_{t}^{u})\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\gamma M(m_{1},m_{T})+\gamma S(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{T})\Biggr]+\gamma\psi(c)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\,\,\,m_{t+1}=Am_{t}+Bv_{t},

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t+1}=(A+BK_{t})\Sigma_{t}(A+BK_{t})^{\top}+B\Sigma^{u}_{t}B^{\top}\,\text{ for }t=1,\dots,T-1.

Since the objective function and the covariance recursion contains bilinear terms, we perform a change of variables by setting

and

Through this manipulation, (

\displaystyle\inf_{c,m_{t},\Sigma_{t},\Sigma_{t}^{u},v_{t},S_{t},Y_{t}}\,\,c\Biggl[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Bigl(\|v_{t}\|^{2}+\tr(Y_{t})+\tr(\Sigma_{t}^{u})-\varepsilon\log\det\Sigma_{t}^{u}\Bigr)+\gamma M(m_{1},m_{T})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,+\gamma S(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{T})\Biggr]+\gamma\psi(c)

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\text{s.t.}\,\,\,m_{t+1}=Am_{t}+Bv_{t},

((46)a)

((46)b)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,Y_{t}=S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}\,\text{for }t=1,\dots,T-1.

The constraint (
(46)b
) ensures the invertibility of the transformation. However, (
(46)
) is still a nonconvex problem due to (
(46)b
). Now, consider the relaxation of (
(46)b
) via the condition

Observe that, the preceding inequality can be written as a convex formulation via Schur complement as:

((47))

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}Y_{t}&S_{t}\\
S_{t}^{\top}&\Sigma_{t}\end{bmatrix}\succeq\textup{O}.

Finally, by replacing (
(46)b

(47)

\displaystyle\inf_{c,m_{t},\Sigma_{t},\Sigma_{t}^{u},v_{t},S_{t},Y_{t}}\,\,c\Biggl[\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\Bigl(\|v_{t}\|^{2}+\tr(Y_{t})++\tr(\Sigma_{t}^{u})-\varepsilon\log\det\Sigma_{t}^{u}\Bigr)+\gamma M(m_{1},m_{T})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,+\gamma S(\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{T})\Biggr]+\gamma\psi(c)

\displaystyle\qquad\,\,\text{s.t.}\,\,\,m_{t+1}=Am_{t}+Bv_{t},

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,\Sigma_{t+1}=A\Sigma_{t}A^{\top}+BS_{t}A^{\top}+AS_{t}^{\top}B^{\top}+B(Y_{t}+\Sigma_{t}^{u})B^{\top}

We are now ready to show the existence results.

5.3.
Existence of minimizer

To complete the existence argument for Problem
5
, it remains to show that the relaxed finite-dimensional problem (
(48)
) admits a minimizer. Once this is established, we can combine it with the tightness of the relaxation and the Gaussian reduction result in Theorem
7
ahead to conclude existence of a minimizer for the original MaxEnt UDC problem.

Proposition 5

(Existence of a minimizer for the relaxed MaxEnt UDC problem)
.

The relaxed version (
(48)
) of Problem
5
admits a solution.

We skip the proof of Proposition
5
since it follows along similar lines given in Proposition
4
. We may therefore apply the following tightness argument to an actual optimizer of (
(48)
).

Theorem 7
.

Any optimal solution to (
(48)
) satisfies
Y_{t}=S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}
. Namely, relaxation from (
(46)
) to (
(48)
) is tight.

To prove this theorem, we introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A
and
B
be symmetric nonzero matrices. If
A\succ\textup{O}
and
B\succeq\textup{O}
, then
\det(A+B)>\det(A).

Proof.

Since
A
is positive definite, its inverse matrix and square root matrices are well-defined. Therefore, we obtain

\displaystyle\det(A+B)

\displaystyle=\det\left(A^{\frac{1}{2}}(I+A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}})A^{\frac{1}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\det A^{\frac{1}{2}}\det(I+A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}})\det A^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\det A\det(I+A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

Set
C:=A^{-\frac{1}{2}}BA^{-\frac{1}{2}}
. Since
B
is positive semidefinite, the positive semidefiniteness of
C
follows. Also, since
B
is not a zero matrix and
A
is invertible,
C
is not a zero matrix. Therefore, letting
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{n}
be eigenvalues of
C
, it holds that
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
for all
i\in\{1,...,n\}
and there exists
j\in\{1,...,n\}
such that
\lambda_{j}>0
. Since eigenvalues of
I+C
are
1+\lambda_{1},...,1+\lambda_{n}
, the determinant of
I+C
is
\det(I+C)=\prod^{n}_{i=1}(1+\lambda_{i})>1.
Therefore, we obtain the desired inequality.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7
.

While
c,(m_{t},v_{t})_{t}
are part of the decision variables in (
(46)
) and (
(48)
), the claim under consideration depends only on the covariance-related variables. Accordingly, we focus on
(S_{t},Y_{t},\Sigma_{t},\Sigma_{t}^{u})_{t}

The proof proceeds via contradiction. Let
(S_{t},Y_{t},\Sigma_{t},\Sigma_{t}^{u})_{t}
be an optimal solution to (
(48)
). For
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Delta_{t}:=Y_{t}-S_{t}\Sigma_{t}^{-1}S_{t}^{\top}
. Suppose there exists
\tau\in\{1,...,T-1\}
such that
\Delta_{\tau}\neq O
. Construct a new feasible solution
(\widetilde{S}_{t},\widetilde{Y}_{t},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{u})_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle\widetilde{S}_{t}\coloneqq S_{t}\qquad\quad\quad\,\,\mbox{for }t=\{1,..,T-1\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{Y}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}Y_{\tau}-\Delta_{\tau}&\mbox{if }t=\tau\\
Y_{t}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}\coloneqq\Sigma_{t}\qquad\quad\quad\,\,\mbox{for }t=\{1,..,T-1\}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{u}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}+\Delta_{\tau}&\mbox{if }t=\tau\\
\Sigma_{t}^{u}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

It is straightforward to see that

\displaystyle J\bigl((S_{t},Y_{t},\Sigma_{t},\Sigma_{t}^{u})_{t}\bigr)-J\bigl((\widetilde{S}_{t},\widetilde{Y}_{t},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t},\widetilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{u})_{t}\bigr)=\tr(Y_{\tau})-\tr(\widetilde{Y_{\tau}})+\tr(\Sigma_{\tau}^{u})-\tr(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\tau}^{u})

\displaystyle\quad-\varepsilon\log\det\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}+\varepsilon\log\det\widetilde{\Sigma}_{\tau}^{u}=\tr(Y_{\tau})-\tr(Y_{\tau}-\Delta_{\tau})+\tr(\Sigma_{\tau}^{u})

\displaystyle\quad-\tr(\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}+\Delta_{\tau})+\varepsilon\left(\log\det(\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}+\Delta_{\tau})-\log\det\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}\right)=\varepsilon\left(\log\det(\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}+\Delta_{\tau})-\log\det\Sigma_{\tau}^{u}\right)

For finiteness of the objective function, it is necessary to hold that

which, in turn, implies that

This contradicts the optimality of

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a convex optimization-based global solution method for the UOT problem and formulated the UDC problem as a control-theoretic dynamical extension of UOT. We also studied entropy-regularized variants of these problems when the reference measures are Gaussian. A common feature of all these formulations is that, although they are originally posed as infinite-dimensional variational problems, their optimal solutions can be characterized within the Gaussian class. This enables an exact reduction to finite-dimensional optimization problems. While the resulting finite-dimensional problems are not necessarily convex in their original form, we showed that they can be solved globally by separating the mass optimization and applying lossless convex relaxations.

Several directions remain for future work. First, it would be interesting to apply the proposed framework to real-world tasks, such as single-cell RNA-seq data analysis
[
