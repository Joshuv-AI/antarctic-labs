Executive Summary
This document presents a structured procedure for characterizing and evaluating candidate
project sites for concentrating solar power (CSP) and solar heat for industrial processes (SHIP)
applications. The objective is to provide project developers, researchers, and other stakeholders
with a consistent, technology-agnostic framework for early-stage site assessment, enabling
informed decision-making prior to significant investment in project development.

Site selection is a critical factor in project success or failure for both CSP and SHIP projects. Key
factors such as solar resource availability, land characteristics, environmental and regulatory
constraints, infrastructure availability, and community context are determined by the choice of
project site and can materially impact project performance, cost, schedule, and overall viability.
This procedure is designed to systematically evaluate these factors, identify potential fatal flaws,
and prioritize the most favorable candidate sites for further development.

The process begins with rapid screening-level evaluation, using publicly available data to assess
solar resource, land availability and suitability, zoning and land-use compatibility, and exclusion
zones such as protected lands or sensitive habitats. Sites that meet the minimum screening
criteria advance to a more detailed characterization.

Subsequent sections of this report provide guidance for a next-level assessment of the most
important technical and environmental parameters, including:

 • Solar resource quality, variability, and uncertainty using multiyear datasets and, where
 appropriate, on-site measurement campaigns
 • Meteorological conditions such as wind, temperature, extreme weather events, and
 soiling impacts
 • Land characteristics including slope, shading, and geotechnical conditions
 • Environmental and regulatory considerations, including permitting processes, endangered
 species, cultural resources, and visual impacts.
The procedure also addresses infrastructure and integration considerations, including:

 • Grid interconnection requirements for CSP power generation projects
 • Electrical and operational integration for SHIP facilities
 • Water availability, quality, and permitting constraints, which are particularly critical for
 CSP in arid regions
 • Site access, construction logistics, and availability of workforce and supporting services.
Recognizing the importance of social and economic context, the procedure includes evaluation
of community engagement factors, such as stakeholder sentiment, proximity to sensitive visual
receptors, workforce development opportunities, and local economic incentives.

The outputs of these assessments are synthesized in a cost and risk evaluation, translating site
characteristics into expected impacts on capital cost, operating cost, schedule, and technical risk.
This is complemented by screening-level performance modeling, including 8760 simulations and

 v

long-term projections, to quantify expected energy or thermal output, assess variability thereof,
and support comparison between candidate sites.

Finally, the procedure provides high-level guidance on a structured go/no-go decision
framework, categorizing sites based on identified risks and constraints, and outlining a clear path
forward to feasibility studies and front-end engineering design for viable projects.

By standardizing the site characterization process across both CSP and SHIP applications, this
guideline aims to:

 • Improve consistency and transparency in early-stage project evaluation
 • Reduce development risk and avoid investment in nonviable project sites
 • Support collaboration between developers, researchers, and public agencies
 • Accelerate successful deployment of concentrating solar technologies for both power
 generation and industrial process heat.

1 Introduction
This document defines a structured procedure for characterizing candidate project sites for
concentrating solar power (CSP) and solar heat for industrial processes (SHIP) facilities. It
supports early-stage project development by providing a consistent framework to identify viable
sites, screen fatal flaws, and prioritize locations for a more detailed feasibility analysis. Project
developers using this procedure should use their judgment regarding which topics are more or
less important for the specific situation at the project site under consideration. Decisions made at
this stage have an outsized impact on project success, as many site-related risks cannot be
economically mitigated later in development.

1.1 Purpose
The purpose of this procedure is to:

 • Establish a common, technology-agnostic approach to CSP and SHIP site evaluation
 • Enable early identification of technical, environmental, regulatory, and logistical
 constraints
 • Reduce development risk by standardizing screening assumptions and documentation
 • Support transparent go/no-go decisions before significant capital is committed.

1.2 Scope
This procedure applies to projects that meet the following descriptions:

 • Geographic scope: Project sites in the United States, with emphasis on regions with high
 average direct normal irradiance (DNI)
 • Technologies: Concentrating solar technologies, including heliostat fields, parabolic
 troughs, and dish concentrators
 • Application: Power generation (CSP) and process heat (SHIP), with or without thermal
 energy storage
 • Project scale: Pilot, demonstration, and commercial-scale facilities.
Exclusions from this procedure include:

 • Power purchase agreement pricing or other commercial negotiations
 • Formal interconnection applications or permitting submissions
 • Detailed project finance, commercial structuring, or full economic viability analysis.

2 Site Screening Criteria
This section describes the first-pass screening process used to determine whether a site merits
further evaluation for CSP or SHIP project development. Screening thresholds should be adapted
to project objectives, including differences between power generation and industrial heat
delivery. This screening should be completed as early and as quickly as possible, using publicly
available data, allowing developers to reject unviable sites, identify potential red flags, and focus
their attention on those sites warranting detailed analysis.

2.1 Regional Solar Resource
Annual DNI in a typical year should be used as a first-pass screening metric. The minimum
threshold may vary, depending on the market and the type of project (e.g., power generation vs.
SHIP) but will typically fall into the range from 1,900 to 2,200 kilowatt-hours per square meter
per year (kWh/m2/year). Using satellite-derived data from publicly available sources, such as the
National Solar Resource Database (Sengupta et al. 2018; National Laboratory of the Rockies,
n.d.), the Global Solar Atlas (Suri et al. 2020; The World Bank Group, n.d.), or SolarGIS, the
CSP project developer should assess the project site for average annual DNI at or above this
minimum threshold. Further, the project developer should identify any anomalies near the project
site, such as significant spatial variability, which may be indicative of microclimates of interest
or localized shading and weather patterns. The National Weather Service (US Department of
Commerce, NOAA, n.d.) provides historical weather archives to evaluate annual wind,
temperature, and other weather patterns.

demanded energy level.

2.2.2 Parcel Size, Shape, and Contiguity
In addition to general land area being a driver of the maximum energy that can be delivered from
the CSP/SHIP plant, the developer should also assess the size, shape, and contiguity of the
parcels under consideration. Plant performance will be most favorable in a single continuous
land area that has a relatively low ratio of longest to shortest dimension. In other words,
something approximating a square or circle is preferable over a long rectangle or highly irregular
shape.

In addition, land procurement and permitting will be simpler for projects requiring a small
number of large parcels, purchased or leased from a single or few landowners, rather than many
small parcels, owned by many independent landowners. Although projects can still be
successfully developed by piecing together many parcels, the developer should consider the
impact of this on the cost, risk, and timeline for developing the project.

2.3 Land Use, Zoning, Constraints, and Sensitivities
A site screening assessment should also include an investigation of the property’s zoning
category and allowable land use and, if necessary, an assessment of the feasibility of any zoning
changes that may be required. In addition, the current land use can influence the ease or
difficulty in the permitting process. For example, land previously disturbed for agriculture or
industrial use is generally easier to permit relative to undisturbed lands.

An evaluation of any sensitive receptors nearby is also important at this stage. Residential areas,
schools, airports, military installations, heavily trafficked highways, and recreation areas may all
be sensitive to visual impact (tall towers, glint, and glare) and should be identified and engaged
early in the process. Consideration should be given to the direction and distance to the receptors
of concern relative to the sources of glint and glare. For example, a polar field will create glare
from the receiver (and heat shield), which is generally visible only from locations to the north of
the tower, not the south.

 4

Finally, the site should be screened for flood and wildfire risk, seismic risks, and other site-
specific hazards of concern.

2.4 Exclusion Zones
As a final step in the site screening process, the developer should verify whether the proposed
site location falls within any exclusion zones, or sensitive zones where a project may not be
prohibited outright, but where the project development process will be subject to additional costs
and delays. Examples of such zones include:

 • Federal and state protected lands, such as national parks, wilderness areas, wildlife
 refuges, and lands designated for conservation
 • Tribal lands, where project development typically requires sovereign nation participation
 • Critical habitat areas designated under the Endangered Species Act
 • Buffer zones associated with military training ranges, radar systems, airports, or low-
 level flight paths.

2.5 Screening Output
This site screening process should serve as a relatively quick way to identify potentially
insurmountable obstacles to developing a CSP project at the proposed location, while investing
minimal time and development capital. Projects that successfully pass through this screening
process will:

 • Meet the minimum DNI criteria
 • Provide sufficient, contiguous land area
 • Have no fatal zoning or land-use conflicts
 • Avoid major protected or sensitive areas.
With those criteria being met, the project developer is justified in continuing to invest time and
capital in the following steps in the site characterization process.

3 Solar Resource Assessment
Upon passing the site screening process, the developer should turn their attention to a more
detailed solar resource assessment. The site DNI is the most important driver of annual energy
generation, and therefore revenue, from the plant. This section discusses methodology, data
sources, uncertainty considerations, and requirements for both desktop assessment and potential
on-site data collection campaigns.

3.1 Data Sources
A robust solar resource assessment will pull data from multiple independent datasets to assess
long-term DNI characteristics for the site. Availability will depend on the project location, but
examples of recommended sources include:

 • Satellite-derived datasets: National Solar Resource Database, SolarGIS (Global Solar
 Atlas), Solcast, Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service
 • Ground-based data networks: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration
 SURFRAD Network, National Laboratory of the Rockies Measurement and
 Instrumentation Data Center, World Radiation Monitoring Center-Baseline Surface
 Radiation Network
 • Regional measurement archives: State agencies, research institutions, and airports.
Selection of datasets for analysis should prioritize temporal duration and resolution as well as
data validation. The project developer should seek datasets with hourly or subhourly resolution,
and multidecade duration. The datasets should rely on DNI models with published validation
including uncertainty estimates.

3.2 Data Quality, Duration, and Frequency
There are several factors to consider when evaluating the overall utility of any given dataset. In
some cases, multiple independent datasets may be necessary to provide a comprehensive
understanding of the site DNI—for example, one dataset with high frequency but shorter
duration, and another with long duration but lower frequency. The most important factors to
consider include:

 • Temporal resolution: Hourly data should be the minimum frequency considered and is
 useful for screening and preliminary performance estimates. Higher frequency (15-, 5-, or
 even 1-minute intervals) is preferable, as these enable performance models to consider
 transient effects masked by hourly averaging.
 • Spatial resolution: Most solar irradiance models are based on satellite data, which are
 available with up to 1-km resolution. However, the most common models produce
 irradiance data on a 10-km grid. Local effects that are smaller than the grid resolution
 may be missed (Sengupta et al. 2015; Inman et al. 2013).
 • Duration: Significant variation occurs year to year, and a changing climate can lead to
 long-term shifts in DNI averages for a given location. Therefore, datasets with 10 or even
 20 years of history are valuable, allowing the developer to look for long-term trends and
 to understand variability.
 • Completeness: Data collection is subject to equipment failures and other issues, which
 can result in gaps in the data. High-quality datasets will have few gaps, and those gaps

 6

 will be of short duration. If gaps have been synthetically filled in, the developer should
 understand and evaluate the method used to do so.
 • Bias and uncertainty: Any dataset will have some degree of inherent bias and uncertainty.
 These should be assessed using cross-comparison among multiple overlapping datasets
 covering the same location and the same period.

3.3 Intrayear and Interyear Analyses
After selecting appropriate datasets, the developer should conduct analyses to understand DNI
characteristics of the proposed project site, throughout the year and from year to year. Initial
analyses should include:

 • Long-term average annual DNI (kWh/m2/year)
 • Monthly DNI profile (average kWh/m2/day for each month of the year).
Further, more detailed analyses would then include:

 • Dataset comparison, overlaying multiple data sources to identify systematic biases or
 outliers
 • Climatological baseline, looking at 20- to 30-year trends to understand stability or drift in
 the average solar resource
 • Interannual variability (standard deviation of annual DNI and/or P10/P50/P90)
 • Extremes and transient behavior, identifying periods of highly variable DNI, which are
 important for receiver, storage, and dispatch modeling.

3.4 Guidance Regarding On-Site Measurement Campaigns
Although desktop analysis using historical datasets is sufficient for early screening and
performance modeling, most projects will require on-site measurements as well. Possible
exceptions to this might include very small projects, in which the investment of time and
development capital are not justified, or projects located very near existing projects from which
the relevant data can be acquired.

Should an on-site measurement campaign be necessary, a detailed plan must be developed that
addresses the primary concerns and motivators for the campaign. In all cases, basic
meteorological and solar resource data should be collected, including:

 • DNI
 • Temperature
 • Wind speed and direction
 • Humidity
 • Precipitation.
Additional measurements may be added to address site-specific concerns or uncertainties, such
as:

 • Atmospheric visibility
 • Particulate mass
 • Cloud cover

 7

 • Other solar resource values (e.g., global horizontal irradiation, diffuse horizontal
 irradiation).
A high-quality measurement campaign will collect data for 12–24 months and will correlate the
data against independent sources of (typically satellite-based) meteorological data for the same
time frame. It is imperative that high-quality instrumentation and data acquisition systems are
selected and that the equipment is maintained properly and regularly (e.g., biweekly cleaning of
pyrheliometers). Such a high-quality measurement campaign will result in a time series of solar
resource and meteorological data, with well-understood uncertainty, sufficient to support
bankable project performance and financial models.

4 Meteorological Assessment
Meteorological conditions beyond solar irradiance materially affect CSP plant design,
performance, operations, availability, and long-term reliability. This section discusses the key
atmospheric and weather-related parameters that should be assessed during site characterization,
along with recommended data sources and evaluation methods.

4.1 Data Sources
A robust meteorological assessment will pull data from multiple independent datasets to assess
historical characteristics for the site. If available, historical data for the last two decades should
be considered. Availability will depend on the project location, but examples of recommended
sources include:

 • Satellite-derived datasets: National Solar Resource Database, SolarGIS (Global Solar
 Atlas), National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration GeoPlatform, Copernicus
 Atmosphere Monitoring Service, meteoblue
 • Ground-based data networks: National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration
 SURFRAD Network, National Laboratory of the Rockies Measurement and
 Instrumentation Data Center, National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration High-
 Resolution Rapid Refresh Atmospheric Model
 • Regional measurement archives: National Weather Service (US Department of
 Commerce, NOAA, n.d.), state agencies, research institutions, and airports.

4.2 Wind Conditions
Wind conditions affect the structural design, tracking accuracy, stow strategies, construction
logistics, and operational availability for tracking solar collectors.

Key parameters for evaluation include:

 • Mean and percentile wind speeds, evaluating long-term probability of exceedance levels
 (e.g., P50, P90, P99). These summary statistics should be based on wind speeds averaged
 over no less than 10 minutes (World Meteorological Organization [WMO] 2024).
 • Extreme wind events, identifying design-basis wind speeds (e.g., 50- or 100-year return
 periods, defined as 3-second gust speed (World Meteorological Organization [WMO]
 2024)
 • Diurnal and seasonal behavior, understanding typical day/night patterns in wind speed
 that may affect operation, and any notable correlation to season or cloud cover
 • Directional distribution, examining wind roses to identify prevailing wind directions that
 may influence soiling via dust transport, structural loading, stow strategies, and field
 design
 • Nearby landscape features (e.g., mountains, ridges) or human-made features (e.g.,
 buildings) that may affect local wind behavior within the solar collector field.
Wind data are generally available from the same solar resource datasets discussed previously in
hourly and subhourly intervals; however, additional ground station data may also be available
from nearby meteorological stations and airports.

 9

After collecting and analyzing wind data, the project developer should be poised to recommend
design wind speed criteria as well as operational and stow wind speed thresholds for the solar
collector field.

4.3 Extreme Events (Rain, Hail, Snow, and Sand and Dust Storms)
Rare but severe weather events can impact design requirements and project risk and are therefore
important to assess early. Some examples of events to evaluate include:

 • Storms and high-wind events, including thunderstorms, cyclones, or regional equivalents
 • Hail, including frequency, size distribution, and historical damage records if available
 • Snow and ice, including depth, persistence, icing events, and freeze-thaw cycles
 • Sand and dust storms, including frequency, duration, and impacts on visibility.
Because these events are infrequent, it is important to search historical records covering multiple
decades. In addition, regional hazard maps and insurance loss data can be helpful here. Also look
for compound events, such as high wind and hail occurring concurrently.

After reviewing the available information, the developer should summarize the noteworthy event
types, frequencies, and severities and use this summary to develop design requirements and
preliminary operational mitigation strategies. Where no mitigation is feasible or cost-effective,
the developer should estimate the impact to plant reliability and overall project viability.

4.4 Temperature and Humidity
Ambient temperature and humidity can influence plant design requirements and performance
expectations, particularly in the power block (assuming an electricity generating project), the
solar receiver, and any heat rejection systems. Important parameters to assess include:

 • Mean, minimum, and maximum temperatures (annually and monthly)
 • Extreme temperature events (hot and cold)
 • Diurnal temperature swings during different seasons
 • Relative humidity profiles.
These should be used to define operational and nonoperational temperature and humidity
requirements and to identify any potential constraints on regular plant operations or nominal
performance.

4.5 Soiling and Airborne Particulates
Soiling, or deposition of airborne dust onto the mirror surface, reduces optical efficiency by
reducing the effective reflectivity of the mirror. In addition, in a central tower configuration,
airborne particulates attenuate the light reflected from the heliostat on its approach to the solar
receiver, further reducing optical efficiency (Sengupta et al. 2015). Both effects are important to
understand, with the latter being particularly important for very large heliostat fields, where long
slant ranges are more sensitive to atmospheric attenuation. Therefore the assessment should be
performed in two categories:

 1. Atmospheric attenuation effects can be estimated using satellite aerosol measurements,
 regional air quality monitoring networks, nearby airport visibility sensors, and nearby

 10

 CSP or photovoltaic facilities (Hanrieder et al. 2016; Heras et al. 2022; DeVore et al.
 2009; ZEPREN Solutions 2024). The following should be considered:
 • Particle sources, such as agricultural activity, mining, unpaved roads, deserts, or
 industrial activity including cooling towers or other emissions, particularly from the
 predominantly upwind direction
 • Precipitation patterns, noting the cleaning effect provided by snow or moderate rain,
 and the dust accumulation effect of very light rain
 • Aerosol optical depth, which is a more commonly reported proxy for particulate
 concentration.
 2. Soiling rates and their effect on the solar field can be estimated only through on-site
 measurements. The state of the art does not yet provide validated models for estimations
 based on other data (see atmospheric attenuation) because soiling rates and their effect on
 plant efficiency depend highly on the local soil properties (Picotti et al. 2018;
 Wolfertstetter et al. 2013). Measurements should include:
 • Dust sampling measurements to estimate particle size and accumulation rates
 • Particle composition analysis and assessment of particle adhesion to the mirror
 surface
 • Correlation measurements of local dust deposition levels with reflectance.
These parameters can be used (quantitatively or qualitatively, depending on the source and
quality of the information) to estimate soiling rates, cleaning frequencies, average soiling levels,
and atmospheric attenuation coefficients, all leading to improved estimates for plant performance
and operations and maintenance costs.

 11

5 Land Evaluation
Land characteristics can influence CSP project viability via field layout and efficiency,
constructability, civil work cost, long-term operability, and permitting risk. This section
describes the key land-related parameters that should be assessed during site characterization,
with an emphasis on identification of fatal flaws or major cost drivers.

5.1 Parcel Boundaries and Ownership
Site screening should include understanding of the land ownership and control. Assessment
elements include:

 • Parcel boundaries, identifying all parcels necessary for the solar field, power block,
 storage, substations, access roads, control rooms, balance of plant facilities, and buffers
 • Ownership structure, preferring single-owner parcels as multiple owners increase
 transaction and schedule risk
 • Easements and rights-of-way, identifying existing transmission, pipeline, road, or utility
 easements that may constrain layout
 • Split estates, confirming rights to the surface versus subsurface mineral rights, as these
 can introduce future access conflicts.
The output of this screening should include a parcel map with ownership overlay, and a
preliminary assessment of land control complexity and risks.

5.2 Grade Analysis
The grade of the terrain can have significant (positive or negative) impact on the optical
efficiency (Freeman et al. 2025) and layout of the solar field, design and cost of foundations, cost
and extent of civil works, and land area necessary for stormwater management. Criteria for
consideration include:

 • Average and maximum grade: Average grade <3% is generally desirable, with localized
 areas up to 5%–10% for certain technologies. Parabolic troughs typically require less
 grade variation than heliostat fields or dish concentrators.
 • Grade directionality: Uniform grades are preferable for minimizing grading costs, while
 highly variable or undulating terrain may increase cost.
 • Cut and fill requirements: These involve identifying areas where cut and fill will be
 required and estimating the volume of earthwork required.
Data for this analysis should include the highest resolution elevation data available for the
project site, including publicly available digital elevation models, lidar data, and site surveys.
Outputs from this process should include terrain maps and summary statistics, and identification
of any locations with intensive grading requirements.

 12

5.3 Nearby Shading Features
Shading from nearby terrain or structures can impact startup, shutdown, and optical efficiency,
especially during winter months. Although typically a minor concern, the project developer
should nonetheless evaluate:

 • Natural features, such as mountains, ridgelines, trees, and so on.
 • Human-made structures, including buildings, towers, transmission line support structures,
 wind turbines, and others.
These shading features should be evaluated against the sun positions expected throughout the
year. Outputs should include shading maps and exclusion zones, used for preliminary constraints
on the solar collector field layout.

5.4 Geotechnical Considerations
Although a geotechnical study is likely not available or feasible during early site
characterization, the developer should evaluate what related information is available for the
project location. This is important, as subsurface and seismic conditions can affect foundation
design, constructability, and structural stability. For the screening process, considerations
include:

 • Soil type and bearing capacity
 • Bedrock depth and variability
 • Seismicity
 • Liquefaction and settlement risk.
These should be assessed based on regional geological maps (US Department of Agriculture,
Natural Resources Conservation Service, n.d.), U.S. Geological Survey seismic hazard maps, and
any available geotechnical reports from nearby projects. The output from this assessment should
be a preliminary geotechnical risk assessment and identification of any areas that require further
study during a detailed site investigation.

5.5 Hydrological Environment
The hydrological characteristics of the site must be evaluated to assess the risk of operational
disruptions, and the potential for construction cost increases to manage stormwater or flood
risks. Surface water behavior and drainage patterns dictate the complexity of site grading, the
requirement for protective infrastructure such as berms or detention basins, and the potential for
soil erosion or equipment damage. The hydrological screening should include:

 • Flood risk and inundation: Identify if any part of the project site lies within a 100-year or
 500-year floodplain. Evaluate proximity to washes, arroyos, or flood-prone depressions
 that may not be formally mapped but pose seasonal risks.
 • Stormwater management: Characterize existing drainage pathways across the site
 and consider how the runoff profiles may change with the addition of significant
 impermeable surface area (mirrors, foundations, roads). Develop conceptual engineered
 drainage systems to avoid future erosive flows.

 13

 • Erosion potential: Evaluate the risk of soil loss around solar collector foundations during
 heavy precipitation events. High-velocity runoff can lead to “scour,” compromising
 structural stability.
 • Surface water features: Identify the presence of wetlands, vernal pools, or other surface
 water features that may trigger additional federal or state permitting.

This evaluation should result in a flood hazard map, a preliminary stormwater management
concept, and identification of any hydrological “fatal flaws” requiring substantial site plan
adjustments or costly civil mitigation.

 14

6 Environmental and Regulatory Considerations
Environmental and regulatory constraints often represent the longest-lead and highest-risk
activities necessary to develop a CSP project. This section discusses the typical permitting,
environmental review, and siting considerations that should be evaluated early to identify fatal
flaws and major schedule risks and to inform the creation of mitigation plans.

6.1 Federal, State, and Local Permitting Requirements
CSP projects in the United States are subject to a layered regulatory structure that may vary by
jurisdiction. For the project site under consideration, the developer should assess:

 • Jurisdictional authority: Identify whether the project triggers federal (e.g., National
 Environmental Policy Act), state, or local environmental review processes.
 • Land ownership implications: Projects on federal land (e.g., BLM-managed) require
 additional leasing and environmental review steps.
 • Major permits: Identify necessary permits related to land use, construction, air quality,
 water use, wastewater discharge, and so on.
 • Permitting timelines: Develop a high-level permitting schedule based on previous
 projects with similar scope and permitting requirements.
Outputs from this effort will include a permitting plan and schedule, identifying (1) the longest-
lead and highest-risk permits that should be addressed first and (2) the responsible agency for
each permit.

6.2 Endangered Species Assessments (Flora and Fauna)
Impact to endangered plants and animals can have a material impact on the viability, cost, and
timeline for development of a CSP project, and therefore should be investigated early. This
investigation should include:

 • Species presence: Screen for any listed or candidate species using federal and state
 databases.
 • Critical habitat: Identify designated or proposed critical habitat areas within the
 boundaries of the proposed project site.
 • Seasonal constraints: Breeding or migration periods that may affect construction
 windows.
 • Mitigation feasibility: Assess whether any identified impacts can be reasonably avoided,
 minimized, or mitigated.
The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Information for Planning and Consultation tool can be
helpful in this investigation, along with state wildlife agencies and any available environmental
assessments in the nearby region. Outputs from this effort include a species screening summary
and identification of any biological fatal flaws or drivers of substantial mitigation requirements.

 15

6.3 Cultural and Archaeological Considerations
Cultural resources and Tribal interests should be identified early to avoid conflicts later during
development of the project. Elements to assess include:

 • Known cultural resources, such as historic properties or archaeological sites
 • Identification of any tribes with ancestral or cultural ties to the land
 • Survey requirements, anticipating the necessity of Phase I, II, or III archaeological
 surveys
 • Avoidance and mitigation options, identifying possibilities to adjust the project
 boundaries to accommodate any areas of concern.
Outputs from the screening will include a summary of any cultural resource risks to the project,
and anticipated consultation and survey requirements for further development.

6.4 Visual Impact and Glint and Glare
Visual impact on neighboring communities and travelers (automotive and airborne) is a
consideration every CSP plant developer must address. A screening assessment should identify:

 • Visual receptors: Nearby residences, businesses, roads, scenic viewpoints, and airports
 and their related flight paths are important to consider.
 • Glint and glare sources: Solar collector mirrors, receiver surfaces, heat shields, and
 heliostat calibration targets are all potential sources of glint and glare. The location of the
 source and direction(s) from which this source is visible are both important.
At the screening stage, a detailed glint and glare analysis is not feasible; however, the developer
should use their judgment to identify receptors likely to be most susceptible, and those sources
most likely to impact those receptors. The output from this screening should be a preliminary
visual impact risk list, and conceptual mitigation methods (landscaping, fencing, layout
restrictions). Note that these mitigation methods must comply with other project requirements—
for example, restrictions on fencing resulting from wildlife migration routes, or restrictions on
landscaping because of water use. Because of the tall tower, high-intensity glare at and around
the receiver, and long focal length of heliostats, large central tower plants will generally require a
more substantial effort and early engagement with sensitive receptors surrounding the facility
relative to that needed for parabolic trough or dish concentrator projects.

6.5 Height Limitations and Airport and Flight Path Considerations
The height of the receiver tower in a central tower plant typically restricts where it can be located
relative to airports and their flight paths. To identify any potential issues, the developer should
assess:

 • Proximity to airports, including public, private, and military facilities
 • Airspace classifications such as controlled airspace, military operations areas, approach
 surfaces, and obstacle limitation surfaces
 • Height and anticipated location of the receiver tower.

 16

With this information in hand, the developer should perform a preliminary aviation screening,
identifying potential conflict between the placement and height of the receiver tower and the
restrictions placed on structures around nearby airports.

6.6 Proximity to Department of Defense Facilities
Department of Defense facilities or operations near the proposed project site can add additional
requirements and complexity to the project development process, so these should be identified
and engaged with early on. The developer should look for Department of Defense bases, training
ranges, sensor installations, and test facilities, casting a very wide net because the military can be
sensitive to activities that occur many miles from their facility. At the screening stage, it is likely
premature to engage with these facilities; however, the developer should identify likely exclusion
zones (if any), and a list of facilities to contact with more details later.

 17

7 Grid Interconnection and Electrical
 Considerations
Grid and electrical considerations will vary between projects designed for CSP (power
generation) and SHIP. This section outlines screening considerations for both scenarios.

7.1 Proximity to Transmission Lines and Substations
Transmission infrastructure is critical for grid-connected, power-exporting CSP plants; however,
it can also be relevant for SHIP projects. This assessment should include the following steps:

 • For CSP projects:
 o Identify proximity to and specifications of existing nearby transmission lines and
 substations.
 o Evaluate physical routing constraints for tie-in lines, including right-of-way
 availability and potential environmental and permitting sensitivity along the path.
 • For SHIP projects:
 o Identify proximity to host industrial facility and existing on-site electrical
 infrastructure.
 o If insufficient electrical power is available from the host facility, an
 interconnection may still be required for auxiliary loads of the SHIP plant.
Outputs from this assessment should include a preliminary electrical interconnection map and
identification of major routing or siting constraints.

7.2 Available Capacity (Inject/Withdrawal) and Upgrade
 Requirements
Understanding the ability of the grid near the project site to deliver or accept new energy is an
important early step to avoid developing infeasible sites or incurring unexpectedly high upgrade
costs later in the process. Assessment should include the following steps:

 • For CSP projects:
 o Estimate available injection capacity at nearby substations.
 o Identify likely upgrade requirements (e.g., transformers, breakers, conductors,
 protection systems).
 • For SHIP projects:
 o Assess the adequacy of on-site electrical supply relative to major loads in the
 SHIP plant (pumps, heaters, the solar collector field, and controls). For hybrid
 electric/SHIP projects, this may additionally include electric heaters and/or heat
 pumps.
 o Investigate any planned or anticipated load growth at the host facility that may
 reduce available capacity for the SHIP plant in the foreseeable future.

 18

A reliable estimate of the injection capacity for substations near the project site may be difficult
to obtain. The developer should contact the independent system operator and/or electrical utility
servicing the area to request any information they are able to share without paying for a
dedicated study, which would be too costly and time-consuming at the screening stage. Useful
reports may include congestion or hosting capacity maps, transmission planning reports,
generator interconnection queues, cluster studies, and any long-term outlooks that may be
published.

This screening process should result in a qualitative capacity assessment and identification of
likely drivers of upgrade costs.

7.3 Interconnection Study Process
Interconnection requirements vary by project type, market structure, and utility jurisdiction. They
are generally much more involved for power generation projects requiring large injection
connections, compared to SHIP projects, which require only a modest load connection to the
grid. To plan for an interconnection study process, the developer should do the following:

 • For CSP projects:
 o Identify applicable interconnection procedures specific to the relevant
 independent system operator, regional transmission organization, and/or utility.
 o Understand the stages of interconnect study (feasibility, system impact, facilities
 study) and typical timelines and costs.
 o Screen nearby substations for queue congestion risk.
 • For SHIP projects:
 o Determine whether a formal interconnection application is required or if the
 project qualifies as behind-the-meter.
 o Identify utility notification, protection, or standby requirements.
As a result of this screening process, the developer should have a preliminary plan toward
interconnection, including a high-level schedule and risk assessment.

7.4 Known Grid Issues Regarding Stability, Curtailment, and
 Congestion
Conditions of the electrical system may affect plant operation, reliability, dispatch flexibility,
and ultimately, economics. This assessment should include consideration of the following:

 • For CSP projects:
 o Known congestion or curtailment in the region
 o Grid stability considerations, including inertia, voltage support, and ramping
 constraints
 o Potential value of thermal storage for dispatch flexibility.
 • For SHIP projects:

 19

 o Grid reliability at the host facility, including historical outage frequency and
 duration
 o Need for backup thermal or electrical systems to maintain industrial process
 continuity.
This will allow the developer to identify grid-related operational risks and design preliminary
mitigation strategies such as storage, redundancy, or hybridization.

8 Water Supply and Discharge
Issues surrounding water—availability, quality, discharge requirements, and various regulatory
constraints—are often critical in CSP project development given the confluence of high-DNI
locations and arid, desert regions. Water requirements and challenges for SHIP projects are
dependent on the nature of the industrial process, which should be considered at this stage of the
screening process. This section outlines screening-level considerations for water sourcing, use,
and discharge.

8.1 Water Availability, Usage, and Discharge Constraints
Regional water considerations can lead to insurmountable constraints or high permitting risks, so
it is important to understand them early in the process. Assessment should include the following
considerations:

 • Water availability: Identify surface water, groundwater, municipal, reclaimed, or
 industrial water sources.
 • Water rights and allocations: Screen for existing rights, overallocated basins, or moratoria
 on new withdrawals.
 • Water quality: Evaluate salinity, dissolved solids, silica, toxic contaminants, and any
 other contaminants that may affect cooling systems, boilers, or heat exchangers,
 identifying any areas of concern that may require treatment systems beyond common
 practice.
 • Discharge pathways: Identify candidate discharge options, including evaporation,
 reinjection, sewer, or surface discharge, and treatment requirements associated with
 discharge.
 • Estimated water consumption: Whether CSP or SHIP, estimate the total water consumed
 for process fluids, including steam generation, condensate makeup, cooling (if
 evaporative or hybrid), mirror washing, and domestic use.
 • Estimated water discharge: Identify all sources of discharge water, such as blowdown,
 along with the constituents of that water.
Outputs should include a preliminary water balance and identification of water-related red flags,
high-risk issues, and design constraints (such as dry cooling systems).

8.2 Cooling Method Requirements
Waste heat discharge to the environment is a primary driver of water consumption in most
thermal power plants, and the choice of cooling technology can directly affect capital
expenditures, operating expenditures, and efficiency of the plant. In the context of CSP plants,
this is especially important, given the scarcity of water at most high-DNI locations. Although a
safe assumption is that any new CSP or SHIP projects in the United States will need to be dry
cooled (zero water consumption for cooling), the assessment here can be used if a more thorough
evaluation is desired. In this case, the assessment should include:

 • Cooling options: Determine whether dry, wet, or hybrid cooling would be optimal.
 • Water intensity: Calculate expected water consumption per megawatt-hour of net power
 generation for candidate cooling strategies.

 21

 • Performance trade-offs: Evaluate efficiency penalties associated with dry or hybrid
 cooling, paying particular attention to the summer months when temperatures are highest.
Note that these issues are most profound for power generation, where a large percentage of the
collected energy is ultimately discharged as waste heat. For SHIP applications, the issues are
similar but likely smaller in magnitude, as the host facility will consume most of the thermal
energy and may, in many cases, already have adequate cooling systems operating.

The outputs of this screening process are a recommended strategy for cooling and estimated
water consumption.

8.3 Water Permitting and Regulatory Issues
Water-related permitting can add significantly to the permitting effort, timeline, and risk. To plan
for this, the developer should understand:

 • Withdrawal permits, whether pumping groundwater, or surface water diversion, and so
 on
 • Discharge permits, such as the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System or its
 equivalent, or industrial pretreatment for sewer discharge
 • Water treatment residuals, including handling of brine, sludge, or concentrate streams
 • Regional sensitivity, identifying if the location is likely subject to increased scrutiny as a
 result of drought-prone basins or environmentally stressed watersheds.
Again, these are likely more challenging for a power generation project, particularly if the
developer is attempting to implement evaporative or hybrid cooling; however, the basic
processes apply to SHIP projects as well.

 22

9 Access, Logistics, and Infrastructure
Access to the site and the availability and proximity of supporting infrastructure near a CSP or
SHIP project can have large consequences on the constructability, schedule, cost, and long-term
operability of the plant. This section outlines screening-level considerations for the developer to
consider.

9.1 Road Access and Transportation Routes
Adequate access for construction equipment, oversized components, and ongoing operations is
essential. The developer should evaluate:

 • Primary access routes: Identify highways, major roads, and last-mile access to the site.
 • Road capacity and condition: Evaluate load limits, condition of pavement, grades, turning
 radii, and bridge constraints.
 • Oversize and overweight transport: Screen roads connecting major highways to the
 project site for permit requirements and feasible routes for large components (e.g.,
 receivers, heat exchangers, cranes).
 • Seasonal accessibility: Assess possible weather-related limitations such as snow,
 flooding, or mud.
 • International ocean freight infrastructure: Identify nearest international shipping ports.
 Assess the draft depth, heavy-lift and breakbulk infrastructure, roll-on/roll-off capability,
 and staging and bonded warehousing.
 • Rail shipping infrastructure: Identify nearby railroads and intermodal rail yards. Assess
 dimensional clearances, access to specialized rolling stock for heavy or large loads, and
 transload infrastructure.
This evaluation should be performed in the context of the anticipated size of major equipment—
potentially including large power block components for a CSP plant, and perhaps smaller for a
modular SHIP project. The result of this screening process should include a preliminary access
and haul-route assessment (at least to/from the nearest major highway, if equipment origin is
unknown) and a list of any transportation-related risks to the project.

9.2 Construction Logistics and Laydown Area
Constructing a CSP plant involves moving a huge amount of material and equipment, making
efficient construction staging extremely important to manage cost and schedule. Factors the
developer should consider include the following:

 • Laydown area availability: Identify sufficient flat area, ideally on the project site or very
 nearby, for material storage and pre-assembly.
 • Internal circulation: Assess on-site road requirements for construction and long-term
 operation.
 • Temporary utilities: Identify the source of power, water, communication, and sanitation
 during construction.
 • Construction sequence constraints: Consider environmental windows, shared access with
 adjacent land users, or industrial host operations (in the case of SHIP projects).

 23

These factors will help the developer create a preliminary concept of construction logistics and to
identify site constraints likely to materially affect schedule or cost.

9.3 Proximity to Workforce, Equipment, and Services
CSP project construction requires a large number of skilled workers for the relatively limited
period of construction, while ongoing operation and maintenance require a smaller number of
workers, but for a comparatively indefinite duration. The availability of labor and other required
services can significantly influence project cost and execution risk. Assessment of labor and
required services should include the following considerations:

 • Construction workforce availability, including skilled and unskilled labor pools, presence
 of applicable unions, and prevailing wage requirements
 • Operations workforce, including long-term staffing requirements for plant operations,
 maintenance, and security
 • Equipment and services, such as proximity to crane suppliers, concrete batch plants,
 fabrication shops, and maintenance contractors
 • Housing and amenities, especially availability of temporary housing for construction
 crews.
The importance of these factors is amplified by the tendency for CSP projects to be sited in very
remote areas. Therefore, a thorough understanding of the cost of attracting labor to the project
site is critical to avoid cost overruns later. This assessment should result in a summary evaluation
of workforce and services availability and identification of major labor-related cost and schedule
risks.

 24

10 Community Engagement
Community perception and stakeholder engagement can strongly influence the permitting
process, project timelines, and long-term success of CSP plants. This section outlines screening-
level considerations to understand the local community, the sensitivity of various stakeholders to
the project, and potential opportunities or risks associated with local engagement.

10.1 Proximity to Community—Residential, Agricultural, Industrial,
 and Rural
Understanding the surrounding land-use context will help the project developer anticipate
concerns and the type of necessary engagement. Consider the following:

 • Residential proximity: Distance to homes, schools, hospitals, and other sensitive
 locations
 • Agricultural use: Active farming, grazing, irrigation districts, and potential land-use
 conflicts
 • Industrial context: Existing industrial activity, brownfield sites, or energy infrastructure
 that may help with project compatibility
 • Rural and remote settings: Lower population density may reduce potential opposition but
 can create challenges with workforce and infrastructure availability.
This screening overview of the local communities should result in a community context map and
a list of potentially sensitive groups and compatibility risks.

10.2 Local Community Sentiment and Stakeholder Engagement
Early understanding of community attitudes can reduce later-stage conflict and delay by helping
the project developer build appropriate relationships and address likely concerns early. This
assessment should include the following elements:

 • Historical context related to previous energy or infrastructure projects in the area, and
 what outcomes those projects experienced
 • Stakeholder identification, including local governments, landowners, community groups,
 nongovernmental organizations, and Tribal entities
 • Important drivers of public perception, such as visual impact, water use, land conversion,
 traffic, noise, and employment
 • Engagement expectations such as formal public meetings, consultations, educational
 events, or ongoing outreach.
Note that greenfield projects and large projects are likely to face more scrutiny than brownfield
or add-ons, so CSP developers should be more focused on these efforts than SHIP developers in
most cases. However, that does not eliminate the need for community engagement for behind-
the-meter or SHIP projects.

Upon completing this assessment, the developer should have a preliminary list and map of
stakeholders and a summary of engagement-related risks and opportunities.

 25

10.3 Workforce Availability
Workforce availability is discussed in Section 9.3, in the context of the proximity and amount of
labor available for project construction and operation. However, workforce issues also play a
role in discussions of community engagement. Appropriate early cooperation can result in a
positive, win-win opportunity for the CSP project and the local population. Consider the
following elements:

 • Labor pool, including availability of skilled trades, technicians, and operators
 • Training requirements and opportunities, especially around specialized CSP or thermal-
 systems training
 • Competition for labor, such as the presence of other large construction or industrial
 projects in the region.
The developer should identify the most important workforce and skills gaps and create a
preliminary list of labor development requirements.

10.4 Local Economic Development or Incentive Opportunities
Community support may be strengthened through economic benefits and incentives, including:

 • Economic development priorities: Alignment with local or regional development goals
 • Incentives: Tax abatements, grants, infrastructure support, or workforce development
 programs
 • Local content: Opportunities for local procurement and fabrication, hiring, and supplier
 engagement.
The types of incentives may differ for CSP and SHIP projects, but opportunities likely exist in
both cases. As a result of this investigation, the developer should have a summary of incentive
opportunities and a preliminary list of community benefit strategies.

11 Cost and Risk Assessment
Site selection decisions should be informed by a careful assessment of how site characteristics
translate into capital cost, operating cost, schedule risk, and long-term performance uncertainty.
This section synthesizes outputs from prior sections into cost and risk signals that help guide
decision-making.

11.1 Capital Expenditure and Operating Expenditure Implications of
 Site Selection
Several of the site screening processes mentioned previously have uncovered factors that directly
impact capital and operating cost of a SHIP or CSP facility. These include:

 • Civil works: Grading, earthworks, foundations, and drainage
 • Electrical infrastructure: Interconnection distance, upgrades, auxiliary power needs
 • Water systems: Cooling technology and infrastructure, water treatment, storage, and
 conveyance
 • Labor and logistics: Local wage rates, productivity, mobilization costs, housing, and
 transportation. Availability of personnel with specialized skills, such as plant operations
 and maintenance, and its impact on cost for labor in these specialties
 • Permitting and mitigation: Environmental studies, mitigation measures, monitoring, and
 compliance costs.
Summarizing what has been discovered about these topics allows the developer to produce a
qualitative cost impact assessment, identifying which site-related cost drivers are most likely to
affect the costs of the plant.

11.2 Site-Specific Risks and Mitigation Strategies
Risks should be identified, categorized, and screened for mitigation feasibility. Risk categories
include:

 • Technical: Resource uncertainty, extreme weather, geotechnical variability
 • Regulatory: Permitting timelines, environmental constraints, stakeholder opposition
 • Scheduling: Long-lead approvals, interconnection studies, seasonal construction windows
 • Operational: Soiling, water availability, grid curtailment, process integration risk (for
 SHIP projects).
For each major risk, the developer should identify the likelihood and consequence and assess
mitigation options and post-mitigation residual risk. These risks should be summarized in a high-
level risk register and a preliminary mitigation strategy summary.

12 Performance Modeling and Degradation Projection
Performance modeling translates characteristics of the site and plant design into expected
electrical output (CSP) or thermal energy output (SHIP) and provides a quantitative basis for
comparing candidate sites. This section defines screening-level modeling expectations rather
than detailed design analysis.

12.1 Define Input Parameters and Uncertainties
Input parameters for a screening-level performance model include:

 • Plant design and operational parameters
 o Solar field layout and preliminary performance parameters
 o Thermal storage system design
 o Power block efficiency (for CSP plant)
 o Dispatch strategy assumptions.
 • Site conditions
 o Solar resource datasets and uncertainty bounds
 o Meteorological conditions affecting availability and efficiency
 o Site-specific constraints affecting layout or operation.
The characterization of uncertainty in this model should be explicit, with a bias toward the
conservative end of the uncertainty ranges.

12.2 Generate 8760 Performance Model and Long-Term Projections
Once the screening-level performance model exists, it should be used to generate several outputs:

 • Hourly annual production (an “8760 model”) for a representative year
 • Long-term median annual output (also known as P50, the value that is expected to be
 exceeded in 50% of operational years) and downside cases (e.g., P90, the value that is
 expected to be exceeded in 90% of operational years)
 • Early-year ramp-up effects and commissioning losses
 • Long-term degradation assumptions (optical, thermal, mechanical).
These performance outputs should be used to compare across various potential project sites, and
provide inputs suitable for preliminary techno-economic analysis and project financial models.

 28

13 Documentation and Reporting
Consistent documentation ensures transparency, repeatability, and comparability across
candidate project sites. A standardized site characterization report should include:

 • Executive summary and screening conclusion
 • Maps, figures, and geographic information systems layers supporting site constraints
 • Summary tables for solar resource, meteorology, land, water, electric grid, and
 community factors
 • Risk register and mitigation summary.
It is recommended that a report template is generated before the site characterization work begins
in earnest, ensuring consistency, completeness, and comparability between multiple potential
project site evaluations. In addition, documentation should be revision controlled and traceable to
data sources.

14 Conclusion and Next Steps
This site characterization procedure is intended to support a disciplined early-stage decision-
making process for site selection of CSP and SHIP facilities, maximizing the chance of project
success while minimizing financial commitment ahead of addressing major site-related risks.

14.1 Recommendation on Go/No-Go Decision Process
Upon completion of this procedure, sites should be characterized as one of the following:

 • Go: No fatal flaws identified; proceed to feasibility analysis
 • Conditional Go: Viable pending resolution of identified risks or data gaps
 • No-Go: Fatal constraints or unmitigated risks identified.
Each organization should use their own judgment regarding where to place the thresholds
between these categories, considering their organizational goals for the project, the type of
project (e.g., SHIP vs. CSP, demonstration vs. commercial scale), and other factors.

14.2 Path to Project Feasibility Analysis and Front End Engineering
 Design
The outputs produced by following the guidelines in this document should serve as a
foundational input to subsequent steps in the project development process, including:

 • Initiating detailed feasibility studies, including refined resource assessment and cost
 modeling
 • Launching formal permitting and interconnection processes as appropriate
 • Progressing toward Front End Engineering Design with site-specific engineering,
 environmental, and commercial inputs.
