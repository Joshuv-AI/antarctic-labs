Background:
High-energy lasers (HELs) play an important role in both national defense and scientific
research. In defense applications, HELs are used for target detection, tracking, and engagement.
In research environments, they support studies of extreme physical conditions relevant to fusion
energy and plasma science. These systems depend on the amplification of light through
stimulated emission of radiation, allowing optical energy to be increased to the levels required
for operation. This amplification occurs when light passes through an energized gain medium
that receives energy from an external optical or electrical source. To achieve the desired output,
laser systems often use multiple amplification stages, including high-gain preamplifiers and
lower-gain power or booster amplifiers.

At Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) and other national laboratories, many large-
aperture laser amplifier systems are aging and rely on system-specific hardware, obsolete
technologies, and incomplete documentation. These legacy systems create challenges for
maintenance, supportability, and long-term operation. Their lack of standardization also
increases the difficulty of sustaining reliable performance over time. As this infrastructure
continues to age, the likelihood of unplanned downtime grows, which can negatively affect both
national security missions and scientific research programs that depend on dependable HEL
capabilities.

The purpose of this document is to demonstrate the application of systems engineering
fundamentals and design thinking through the development of a laser amplifier case study. The
proposed system concept is intended as an academic exercise and not as a finalized engineering
design. As a result, the development presented in this document is incomplete and may contain
technical assumptions or errors that would require further investigation before any real-world
implementation.

Need Statement:
There is a need for a standardized, maintainable large aperture laser amplifier system to replace
aging, obsolete legacy hardware at LLNL and other national laboratories, ensuring reliable long-
term operations and compatibility with current and future laser facilities.

Key Acceptance Criteria:
The stakeholder requirements help define and constrain the solution for the identified need for
a laser amplifier, but they do not, by themselves, ensure a successful outcome. To be considered
successful by its stakeholders, the system must meet the following key acceptance criteria:

  The system shall produce a 10X gain of the light passing through it.

  The system shall have a clear aperture of ≥ 10 cm.

  The system shall be robust.

Concepts:
With the acceptance criteria and stakeholder requirements established, several system concepts
can be developed. The three example concepts below were selected because they show
potential to meet the stated expectations.

Concept 1: Spiral Amplifier

This concept uses a spiral-shaped rod and relies on internal reflection, similar to fiber optics, to
guide light through the stimulated gain medium. Light enters at one end of the rod and exits

 7

the other with increased energy. Helium gas flows around the flashlamp and rod to provide
thermal regulation of the surfaces.
This laser amplifier concept is called the Spiral Amplifier because of its unique gain-medium
geometry. Compared with traditional flashlamp-pumped disk amplifier designs, this concept
could be more efficient because it uses only a single flashlamp while maximizing the rod’s
exposure to radiated energy. Its primary drawback is the difficulty of manufacturing and
maintaining the spiral rod geometry. To reduce this risk, the system would likely need to operate
well below the optical damage threshold.

 Figure 1. Diagram of the Spiral Amplifier system design.

Concept 2: DI-CUBE Amplifier

The DI-CUBE Amplifier concept uses diode-pumped gain media and reflective surfaces to make
the light bounce through the gain medium multiple times before exiting the amplifier. By
combining two of these subsystems, the incident angle can be aligned so that the input beam is
parallel to, but opposite from, the output beam, creating a corner-cube effect that gives the
concept its name.

This arrangement allows multiple DI-CUBE amplifiers to be chained together to achieve the
desired gain. The design uses water cooling to regulate the temperature of the diodes. This
concept is also energy efficient because the diodes can be tuned to the specific wavelengths
needed to stimulate the gain medium, whereas flashlamps emit a broad spectrum of light.

Concept 3: MOD-Amp

The MOD-Amp system features a more traditional disk amplifier configuration, with a gain-
medium slab angled to reduce back reflections and surrounded by flashlamps. What
distinguishes this concept is that each disk is packaged with its own flashlamps and integrated
as a line-replaceable unit (LRU).
By combining multiple LRUs in series, the required gain can be achieved while preserving ease
of maintenance and replacement. This modular architecture gives the system its name, modular-
disk amplifier, or MOD-Amp.

 Figure 3.- Diagram of the MOD-Amp system concept.

including a 10x gain, a circular clear aperture of at least 10 cm in diameter, and robust

Pugh Matrix/Concept Selection:
The concepts were evaluated using a decision matrix, also known as a Pugh matrix, which
provides a structured analytical method for comparing the effectiveness of each concept. The
evaluation categories were based on the key acceptance criteria, with cost included as an
additional factor.

The acceptance criterion requiring the system to be robust was further represented by the
categories of maintainability and reliability, since these characteristics are key contributors to
overall system robustness. Each evaluation category was assigned a weight between 0 and 1,
with the sum of all category weights equal to 1. These weights represent the relative importance
of each category in the overall concept selection process.

 Figure 4. Pugh decision matrix used to evaluate each system concept.

Each concept was scored on a scale of 1 to 10 based on how effectively it satisfied the objective
of each evaluation category. The score for each category was multiplied by that category’s
assigned weight to calculate a weighted value. The weighted values for each concept were then
summed to produce the total weighted score, shown in the far-right column of the matrix.

Based on this analysis, the MOD-Amp concept received the highest total weighted score. This
result indicates that it best satisfies the key acceptance criteria while also providing a cost-
effective solution.

Concept of Operations (ConOps):
With the context diagram established and system interactions identified, a Concept of
Operations (ConOps) was developed to describe how the system operates during use. Although

ConOps can be presented in several ways, Figure 6 provides a diagrammatic view of system
operation.

 Figure 6. Concept of Operations (ConOps) diagram.

For the MOD-Amp system, the first operational step is to select and charge the capacitors that
store the energy required for the amplifier flashlamps. That stored energy is then discharged
through the flashlamps, which in turn excite the doping agent within the amplifier slabs. A pulse
of coherent light is then sent through the amplifier, where it is amplified before continuing
along the beam path. The final step in the ConOps is to initiate the gas-cooling cycle.

Sequence Diagram:
The system use cases can be further broken down into sequence diagrams to show the
interactions between users and the system for each task. These diagrams are useful for
describing the steps required for proper user interaction and for identifying the needs of each
user during system operation.

For the MOD-Amp system, there are four use cases, and therefore four corresponding sequence
diagrams.

The first sequence diagram represents system alignment. In this process, the MOD-Amp
indicates through diagnostic data that the system is out of alignment. The technical staff then
performs a gross alignment of the system, after which the experimental physicist completes the
precision alignment. The system diagnostics then confirm that the alignment has been properly
restored.

Quality Function Deployment (QFD):
While the sequence diagrams improve understanding of system interactions and user needs,
they do not directly show how the system satisfies stakeholder requirements. To address this,
the House of Quality (HoQ) is used as a systems engineering tool to evaluate how well the
design meets stakeholder expectations and to show how strongly each stakeholder requirement
influences the derived system requirements.

The HoQ also illustrates the relationships among system requirements. The top portion, or
“roof,” shows how individual system requirements affect one another. By identifying
requirement dependencies and responses to change, the HoQ helps support trade-off decisions
during development while ensuring that stakeholder needs remain satisfied.

For the MOD-Amp system, the stakeholder requirements for performance, cost, and ease of use
are decomposed in the left-hand columns of the HoQ shown in Figure 12. The corresponding
actionable system requirements are listed across the top of the matrix beneath the roof,
allowing them to be expressed in a way that is both achievable and verifiable. The roof then
shows the correlations among those system requirements. Each stakeholder requirement is
assigned a weight based on its importance, and each is rated against the relevant system
requirements. These ratings are multiplied by the assigned weights to produce a feature ranking.

 Figure 12. HoQ for the MOD-Amp system.

A key takeaway from the MOD-Amp HoQ is that increased performance requirements are
negatively correlated with cost. As the design is modified to improve performance, system cost
also increases. More broadly, added system requirements tend to increase both development
cost and unit cost. The HoQ also includes a comparison system, the Nova Disk Amplifier, which
is used as a reference for evaluating how well the MOD-Amp concept satisfies stakeholder
requirements.

Functional Architecture:
Investigating the functional architecture of the MOD-Amp system through a first-level
decomposition helps illustrate how the system’s primary function is divided into lower-level sub-
functions. The main function of the MOD-Amp is to amplify light, and this function is
decomposed into five required sub-functions: charge capacitors, shot timer adjustment,
flashlamp activation, stimulated emission, and amplifier cooling. These sub-functions are
performed sequentially to achieve the overall system function.

 Figure 13. First Level Decomposition.

A functional architecture diagram is then developed from this decomposition to show the
interactions and interfaces required throughout the sequence of operation. In the first step, the
user provides an input to select which capacitors are used, which determines how much energy
is supplied to the flashlamps. Electrical energy is then provided from the grid to the capacitors,
and both of these interactions are represented as inputs crossing the system boundary into the
first sub-function.

The second sub-function, shot timer adjustment, requires a user input to define the timing
parameters needed to synchronize flashlamp firing with the beam. A control signal is then sent
to the flashlamp and capacitor controller, which directs stored DC energy from the capacitors to
the flashlamps. In the third sub-function, this causes a voltage breakdown within the flashlamps,
releasing light energy. The fourth sub-function, stimulated emission, occurs when the incoherent
light produced by the flashlamps excites atoms within the gain medium, allowing passing
photons to stimulate the emission of additional coherent light. The final sub-function is amplifier
cooling, which removes heat from the optical components to maintain thermal and wavefront

 19

stability. This is accomplished by flowing cooling gas around the optical surfaces at a high flow
rate for a specified period of time.

 Figure 14. Functional Architecture.

The inputs and outputs associated with each sub-function define the interactions that must be
considered during system development. When used together with the ConOps and use cases,
the functional architecture provides a strong foundation for identifying system risks and
supporting the development of mitigation strategies.

Summary:
The systems engineering approach used for the MOD-Amp project provided a structured
method for moving from stakeholder needs to a defined system design. Beginning with
acceptance criteria and concept generation, the design process used tools such as the Pugh
matrix, context diagram, ConOps, use cases, sequence diagrams, House of Quality, requirements
development, and functional architecture to evaluate alternatives and refine the selected
concept. Together, these methods helped ensure that the final system concept remained aligned
with stakeholder expectations while also being traceable, verifiable, and practical to develop.

 MOD-Amp System Design 22

The selected MOD-Amp design is a modular disk amplifier concept intended to provide a 10x
amplification of coherent light while meeting requirements for aperture size, maintainability,
safety, and ease of use. Its modular line-replaceable-unit architecture supports maintenance and
scalability, while the supporting functional architecture defines the sequence of operations
required for amplification and cooling. Overall, the MOD-Amp concept demonstrates how a
systems engineering process can be used to develop a robust and well-structured solution that
balances performance, cost, and operational needs.
