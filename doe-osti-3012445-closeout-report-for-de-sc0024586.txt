Closeout Report for DE-SC0024586
 STREAMLINE Collaboration:
 Machine Learning for Nuclear Many-Body Systems

 We are grateful for the support of the Department of Energy Office of Science for funding our
collaborative project, “STREAMLINE Collaboration: Machine Learning for Nuclear Many-Body
Systems”, from September 1, 2023, to August 31, 2025. The following is a closeout report for the
Michigan State University (MSU) portion of the STREAMLINE Collaboration. The collaboration
performed research in the areas of fast and accurate emulators, smart model extrapolation, learning
correlations in wave functions and operators, and predicting nuclear dynamics, including nuclear
fission, and heavy-ion fusion. The MSU members of the collaboration contributed to nearly all
aspects of this research program.

MSU Personnel
The MSU senior scientists participating in research projects supported by STREAMLINE funds
were Pablo Giuliani (Nuclear Science and Graduate Student Success Specialist), Kyle Godbey (Re-
search Assistant Professor), Heiko Hergert (Associate Professor), Dean Lee (Professor), Yuanzhuo
Ma (Visiting Assistant Professor), Witold Nazarewicz (Hannah Professor and FRIB Chief Scien-
tist), and Xilin Zhang (FRIB Theory Fellow).
 The MSU student researchers who participated in research projects supported by STREAM-
LINE funds include Elisha Alemao (Undergraduate Student), Josh Belieu (Ph.D. Student), Ben-
jamin Clark (Ph.D. Student), Patrick Cook (Ph.D. Student), Danny Jammooa (Ph.D. Student),
Daniel Lay (Ph.D. Student), and Nicholas Rohde (Undergraduate Student).

Publications
The following is a list of peer-reviewed research publications involving MSU personnel with fund-
ing support to DE-SC0024586 acknowledged.

 Quantum Monte Carlo simulations can in principle be used to build fast and accurate many-
body emulators using eigenvector continuation. Such an application requires something that is
missing from the published literature, an efficient quantum Monte Carlo scheme for computing the
inner product of ground state eigenvectors corresponding to different Hamiltonians. In “Floating
lock method for quantum Monte Carlo simulations”, Phys. Rev. Lett. 131, 242503 (2023), [1], we
introduce an algorithm called the floating block method, which solves the problem by performing
Euclidean time evolution with two different Hamiltonians and interleaving the corresponding time
blocks. We use the floating block method and nuclear lattice simulations to build eigenvector
continuation emulators for energies of 4 He, 8 Be, 12 C, 16 O over a range of local and nonlocal
interaction couplings. From the emulator data, we identify the quantum phase transition line from
a Bose gas of alpha particles to a nuclear liquid.
 In “Neural network emulation of spontaneous fission”, Phys. Rev. C 109, 044305 (2024), [2],
we demonstrate the use of deep neural networks to emulate the computationally expensive density
functional theory (DFT) calculations required to study nuclear fission. We train neural networks
to predict potential energy surfaces and collective inertia tensors based on nuclear deformations.
This approach significantly reduces the computational cost of determining spontaneous fission
half-lives and least-action fission paths, enabling global surveys of fission properties across the
chart of nuclides with microscopic accuracy.
 In the colloquium article “Colloquium: Eigenvector continuation and projection-based em-
ulators”, Rev. Mod. Phys. 96, 031002 (2024), [3], we review the theoretical foundations and
applications of Eigenvector Continuation (EC) as a reduced-basis method for quantum many-body
systems. The text explains how EC allows for the accurate emulation of quantum systems by pro-
jecting the Hamiltonian onto a low-dimensional subspace spanned by “snapshots” of eigenvectors
calculated at different parameter values. We discuss the method’s underlying mathematics, its an-
alytic continuation properties, and its success in efficiently exploring the vast parameter spaces of
nuclear interactions and uncertainty quantification.
 In the article “Ab Initio Study of the Beryllium Isotopes Be7 to Be12”, published in Phys. Rev.
Lett. 134, 162503 (2025), [4], we report on a comprehensive calculation of beryllium isotopes
using ab initio nuclear lattice effective field theory. The study investigates the ground-state prop-
erties, binding energies, and radii for the isotopic chain ranging from the proton-rich 7 Be to the
neutron-rich 12 Be. The work focuses on reproducing the emergence of cluster structures, such as
the alpha-alpha core, and understanding the evolution of nuclear structure and stability across the
chain from first principles. The nucleon configurations obtained in this work are being used to
train machine learning models for the identification of alpha clusters and molecular orbitals.
 With “Accelerating eigenvalue computation for nuclear structure calculations via perturbative
corrections”, J. Comput. Phys. 531, 113921 (2025), [5], we introduce a numerical method de-

 5

signed to speed up the solution of large-scale eigenvalue problems found in nuclear configuration
interaction (NCI) calculations. We propose the Subspace Projection with Perturbative Corrections
(SPPC) method, which partitions the Hamiltonian into a smaller, easily solvable matrix and a per-
turbation. By solving a sequence of linear systems to obtain perturbative corrections, the method
constructs an optimized subspace that accelerates the convergence of iterative eigensolvers towards
the exact nuclear wave functions.
 In “Exploiting 20 Ne Isotopes for Precision Characterizations of Collectivity in Small Systems”,
Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 012302 (2025), [6], we demonstrate how collisions involving the strongly
deformed neon-20 nucleus can be used to isolate the effects of geometry on particle flow in high-
energy nuclear collisions. By comparing the results of 20 Ne+20 Ne collisions with those of spherical
16 O+16 O collisions, we show that the specific “bowling-pin” shape of 20 Ne leaves a distinct im-

print on collective observables, such as elliptic flow. This comparison allows for a precise test of
the hydrodynamic description of the quark-gluon plasma in small systems, distinguishing between
geometric initial-state effects and other dynamical fluctuations. The nucleon configurations ob-
tained in this work are being used to train machine learning models for the identification of alpha
clusters.
 In the work titled “Parametric matrix models”, appearing in Nature Commun. 16, 5929 (2025),
[7], we develop a novel class of machine learning algorithms called parametric matrix models
(PMMs). PMMs fundamentally differ from conventional neural network and deep learning in-
spired approaches by emulating physical systems through matrix equations. This design allows
PMMs to learn the underlying governing equations directly from empirical data, offering an inter-
pretable and efficient computational framework capable of input feature extrapolation. We demon-
strate that PMMs are particularly effective for scientific computing problems where incorporating
mathematical structures can yield a significant performance advantage. We apply PMMs to a
diverse set of challenges: multivariable regression on benchmark datasets; extraction of the prop-
erties of low-energy states of quantum many-body systems; mitigation of Trotter errors in quantum
computing calculations; and image classification.
 In the article “Genetic programming for the nuclear many-body problem: a guide”, J. Phys. G
52, 102001 (2025), [8], we explore the application of symbolic regression and evolutionary algo-
rithms to nuclear physics problems. The paper serves as a tutorial on using Genetic Programming
(GP) to automatically derive analytical mathematical formulas for theoretical modeling, with a fo-
cus on creating data-driven surrogate models for Density Functional Theory frameworks. GP is
directly used to identify the governing equations for the coefficients of a reduced basis expansion
for the proton and neutron densities of nuclei as a function of the underlying theoretical parameters.
 In the letter “Non-Hermitian Quantum Mechanics Approach for Extracting and Emulating
Continuum Physics Based on Bound-State-like Calculations”, Phys. Rev. Lett. 135, 242501
(2025), [9], we formulate a novel emulation-based framework that bridges the gap between bound-
state computational techniques and continuum physics. We discover that our emulation in the
complex energy plane induces an effective rotation of the Hamiltonian, a core concept to access
continuum information, such as scattering amplitudes, using finite-volume or bound-state basis
sets. This letter also highlights the method’s ability to efficiently emulate the continuum physics of
quantum systems across different energies and parameters, offering a practical solution for com-
plex few-body and many-body scattering problems.
 In “Non-Hermitian quantum mechanics approach for extracting and emulating continuum physics
based on bound-state-like calculations: Detailed description”, Phys. Rev. C 112, 064605 (2025),

 6

[10], we provide a comprehensive formalism for computing and emulating scattering and con-
tinuum observables using numerical methods originally designed for bound states. We detail
our method that transforms the original Hermitian Hamiltonians into non-Hermitian Hamiltonians
via an emulation-induced subspace projection and thus rotates the continuum spectrum, allowing
scattering information to be extracted from square-integrable wave functions. As the result, this
method calculates—and emulates in the parameter space—phase shifts and resonances without
requiring explicit scattering boundary conditions.
 In the letter “Nuclear responses with neural-network quantum states”, Accepted for publication
at Phys. Rev. Lett., [11], we adapt the so-called neural-network quantum state (NQS) method to
computing quantum continuum physics. Previously, the method, for example in nuclear theory,
has been used mostly for studying many-body ground states. The letter demonstrates this new
NQS capability in calculating nuclear response function, such as 4 He’s photo-disintegration cross
section across a wide energy range. This work is a collaboration between MSU personnel and the
ANL group in the STREAMLINE collaboration. Also see the report by the ANL group.
 In the article “Bayesian mixture model approach to quantifying the empirical nuclear satura-
tion point,” Phys. Rev. C 110, 044320 (2024), [12], we develop a Bayesian mixture model to infer
the empirical nuclear saturation point from diverse Skyrme and relativistic mean-field density-
functional theory predictions. By treating each model and its reported uncertainty as a component
of a statistically consistent mixture, the framework rigorously accounts for model discrepancy
and uncertainty propagation. The resulting heavy-tailed posterior constraints enable robust bench-
marking of chiral effective-field-theory interactions and yield statistically consistent estimates of
the nuclear symmetry energy and its slope when combined with microscopic neutron-matter cal-
culations.
