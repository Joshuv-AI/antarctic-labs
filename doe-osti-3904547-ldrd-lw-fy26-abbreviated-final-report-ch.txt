Fortifying biofertilizer bacteria through desiccation tolerance
 Principle Investigator: Dr. Jennifer Chlebek, BBTD, PLS, LLNL
 Supporting: Joshua White, BBTD, PLS, LLNL
 Supporting: Dr. Sean Leonard, BBTD, PLS, LLNL

 25-LW-018
Project Overview:

Chemical fertilizers are currently required to maintain sufficient crop growth to meet global food
demands. Paradoxically, production of these chemical fertilizers is an energy-intensive and
expensive, which can contribute to supply chain instability. Biofertilizers—using nitrogen-fixing
bacteria to produce fertilizers—offers a more sustainable and energy-conscious alternative to
chemical fertilizers. However, these living products require preservation methods to extend their
shelf life, and current methods like freeze-drying can harm bacterial survival which lowers
bacterial productivity. Also, as droughts have become more common, bacteria can experience
more stress in fields and maintaining bacterial survival throughout the crop lifecycle will be
important. Our project aims to improve bacterial resilience to desiccation, or drying out, by
making genetic enhancements in soil bacteria relevant to biofertilizers. Using a two-step
approach, we first identified genes important to survival during dehydration from previously
published reports and made equivalent edits to the genomes of our bacteria of interest. Second,
we used an adaptive evolutionary process to reveal natural genetic adaptations that improve
desiccation resistance. We found that genes involved in aggregation and water retention may
be important to maintaining a shell of hydration around bacteria, protecting them from
desiccation stress. We also revealed variables that contribute to reproducibility issues with
desiccation assays. In conclusion, we identified novel factors important to bacterial survival
during desiccation which may benefit commercial biofertilizer development. Our findings may
also be broadly applicable to other bioindustries where desiccation sensitivity thwarts viable
production such as probiotics, biopesticides and bioremediation.

Mission Impact:

This work studying the desiccation tolerance in biofertilizer bacteria helped to advance microbial
biotechnologies and biomanufacturing capabilities in support of LLNL’s core competencies in
Bioscience and Bioengineering. This work uncovered unknown factors that contribute to
desiccation resistance and previously unknown pain points in evaluating desiccation survival.
Additionally, these efforts contribute to our understanding of biomanufacturing-relevant products
and has laid the groundwork to address future challenges. Overall, bringing these technologies
to LLNL, is well aligned with the Bioresilience MFA goals of “improved ecosystem resilience and
sustainable manufacturing”, which resonate with the White House Executive Order on
Advancing Biotechnology. Developing these capabilities and associated expertise helps the
Laboratory maintain its leadership in Biotechnology.
