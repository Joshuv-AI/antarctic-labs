From Estimates to Schedules: Learning-Augmented Restricted Assignment
Michalis Xefteris

Abstract
In this work, we study Restricted Assignment scheduling on multiple machines, where each job can be processed only on a specified subset of machines and the objective is to minimize the makespan. We introduce a learning-augmented setting in which a possibly infeasible predicted assignment is provided. The prediction error (moved-load) is measured by the total processing volume that must be reassigned in order to obtain an optimal feasible schedule. Using a single prediction, we obtain two types of guarantees. First, we design an algorithm whose approximation ratio degrades smoothly with the prediction error while retaining a worst-case guarantee independent of the prediction quality. More precisely, for any fixed constant, we can make the additive dependence on the prediction error arbitrarily small, at the cost of increasing the polynomial running time. This guarantee can also be combined with any approximation algorithm for the problem without predictions to obtain robustness. Second, given a makespan estimate, we provide a repair procedure that returns a schedule matching this estimate in time parameterized by the prediction error. This allows the algorithm to exploit the separation between estimation and approximation algorithms for Restricted Assignment . Finally, we complement the repair algorithm with a parameterized hardness result, showing that exact moved-load repair with a given target makespan is W ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathrm{W[1]} -hard when parameterized by the amount of moved-load.

Introduction

Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have stimulated a growing interest in using predictions within algorithm design. Learning from historical data can provide useful insight into different aspects of an optimization problem, such as structural properties of an instance, features of a high-quality solution, partial solutions, or near-optimal algorithmic decisions. This has motivated the study of
learning-augmented algorithms
(or
algorithms with predictions
) which aim to leverage such information to achieve improved performance when the predictions are accurate, while still maintaining rigorous worst-case guarantees when they are not. In this setting, predictions about the problem at hand are typically provided in a black-box manner.

This line of research originated in the work of Mahdian et al.
[
25
]
on online advertising allocation and was later formalized by
Lykouris and Vassilvitskii
[
24
]
for online caching. The goals that
have since become standard are that an algorithm should be (i)
consistent
,
performing near-optimally when predictions are accurate, (ii)
robust
, preserving
near-optimal worst-case guarantees when predictions are poor, and (iii)
smooth
,
with performance degrading gradually as prediction error increases. The field has blossomed with applications to online algorithms, streaming and sketching algorithms, data structures and combinatorial optimization. An almost complete list of papers in this area can be found in
[
23
]
.

Algorithms with predictions have also been used to improve approximation algorithms for NP-hard problems, with clustering emerging as one of the main directions. For
k
-means clustering, learning-augmented algorithms
that use predicted cluster labels have been developed in
[
13
,
15
,
27
]
,
achieving a
(1+\mathcal{O}(\alpha))
-approximation when the per-cluster label
error is at most
\alpha
. Hierarchical clustering has also been studied in the learning-augmented setting
[
8
]
.
Antoniadis et al.
[
2
]
studied a broad class of NP-hard selection problems, using possibly infeasible predictions to obtain improved approximation guarantees when the predictions are sufficiently accurate. Another well-studied direction concerns Max-CSP problems, including Max-Cut. Bampis et al.
[
5
]
considered dense Max-CSPs and showed how predictions can be used to speed up polynomial-time approximation schemes (PTAS).
Max-Cut has also been investigated in a learning-augmented setting with noisy predictions that are slightly better than random guesses.
In two independent works, Cohen-Addad et al.
[
10
]
and Ghoshal et al.
[
16
]
improved the approximation guarantees for Max-Cut in this setting. The same model has also been applied to several other NP-hard problems
[
7
,
4
,
1
]
, yielding better guarantees than those achievable without predictions.

In this work, we study offline scheduling on unrelated parallel machines in the learning-augmented setting. Scheduling is a fundamental optimization problem with applications in manufacturing, transportation, and computing systems. More specifically, we consider the
Restricted Assignment
problem, in which each job can be processed only on a specified subset of machines, and the objective is to minimize the makespan. For the problem without predictions, no polynomial-time algorithm can achieve a worst-case approximation ratio better than
3/2
unless
\mathrm{P}=\mathrm{NP}

[
21
]
.

While learning-augmented algorithms for online scheduling have been studied extensively
[
20
,
22
]
, the offline setting has received much less attention. Bampis et al.
[
4
]
studied a single-machine scheduling problem with the objective of minimizing the sum of completion times. Given predictions about the relative order of jobs that are correct with probability slightly greater than
1/2
, they designed an exact polynomial-time algorithm for the problem. Moseley et al.
[
26
]
studied the problem of scheduling stochastic jobs on a single machine when the scheduler is given only predicted job-size distributions, which may be only approximately correct, rather than the true distributions.

1.1
Contributions

In this work, we are given a predicted assignment of
n
jobs to
m
machines. We stress that we do not require this predicted assignment to be feasible.
The prediction error
\mathcal{E}
is just the minimum total processing time (moved-load) that must be reassigned from the predicted job-to-machine assignment in order to obtain a feasible schedule with optimal makespan
\mathsf{OPT}
. If there are several optimal solutions,
\mathcal{E}
is the minimum such total among them. A formal description of the prediction model is given in Section
2
.

Our first goal is to improve the approximation ratio of the problem using the possibly infeasible prediction
\hat{\pi}
. To this end, we first project
\hat{\pi}
into a feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}
, obtaining a basic projection guarantee with makespan at most
\mathsf{OPT}+\mathcal{E}
.
Building on this projection, we derive our smoothness guarantee by guessing the few large jobs on which the prediction differs from an optimal schedule and solving the assignment LP for the remaining jobs.
More precisely, for every fixed
\delta\in(0,1]
, there is an algorithm running in time

(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
that outputs a feasible schedule
\sigma
with makespan at most
\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E}
.
We then robustify this approach by
running in parallel an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the problem without
predictions and returning the better of the two schedules, obtaining the
following guarantee.

Theorem
7
(Informal).

For every fixed
\delta\in(0,1]
, given a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
and an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
Restricted Assignment
without predictions, one can output a schedule
\sigma
in time
(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
with makespan

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq\min\left\{\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E},\,\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}\right\}
.

A main novelty of our work is that we use the same prediction and the same error measure, in two distinct ways. First, we use it to obtain smooth and robust approximation guarantees. Second, we use the same prediction to obtain repair algorithms whose running time is parameterized by the prediction error. Prior learning-augmented approximation algorithms for offline NP-hard problems typically use predictions either to improve the approximation guarantee in polynomial time, or to speed up a known algorithmic scheme. In contrast, our framework connects these two uses of predictions within a single model. More specifically, we show that, given an estimate
T
of the optimal makespan, one can construct a repair procedure that takes the prediction
\hat{\pi}
and outputs a schedule with makespan at most
T
in time parameterized by the prediction error
\mathcal{E}
(Section
4
). At a high level, the algorithm first guesses the prediction error via a doubling search, then identifies by exhaustive enumeration the subset of machines that must be repaired, and finally solves the repair problem on a fixed set of machines using a dynamic programming approach inspired by multi-dimensional knapsack.

Corollary
17
(Informal).

There is a repair procedure that, given a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
, returns a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
for
Restricted Assignment
with

C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
in time

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(n\cdot m^{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E})}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E})}\right)
, where
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
hides logarithmic factors.

We complement this algorithmic result with a parameterized hardness result. Exact moved-load repair for
Restricted Assignment
, with a given target makespan, is
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard when parameterized by the amount of moved-load (Section
4.4
). Thus, under standard parameterized complexity assumptions, the error-dependent running time cannot in general be replaced by an
\mathrm{FPT}
dependence on the moved-load alone.
Together, these results show that both parts of our framework are close to best possible: the smoothness guarantee can make the coefficient of
\mathcal{E}
arbitrarily small at the cost of a
(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
running time, while exact repair is unlikely to admit an
\mathrm{FPT}
dependence on the prediction error alone.

Estimation vs Approximation.

This repair approach is useful when the makespan estimate
T
yields a better makespan than the robust guarantee of Theorem
7
.
For many NP-hard problems studied in the literature, however, the best estimation ratio achievable in polynomial time coincides with the best approximation ratio, and therefore such an estimate does not lead to any additional benefit.
Restricted Assignment
is one of the few known exceptions.
1
1

1

Under standard complexity assumptions, it has been shown that there are (artificial) NP-hard optimization problems for which estimation is easier than approximation
[
14
]
.

Recall that a polynomial-time algorithm has estimation ratio
\rho
if it computes a value
T
within a factor
\rho
of the optimum (
\mathsf{OPT}\leq T\leq\rho\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
). For
Restricted Assignment
, the best known approximation ratio is
\alpha=2

[
21
]
, whereas the best known estimation ratio is
11/6

[
19
]
. In Section
5
, we instantiate our guarantees for
Restricted Assignment
and for its two-valued variant, in which each job has one of two possible processing times.

At the same time, the moved-load error can be pessimistic when the prediction makes only a few mistakes on large jobs. To address this, we develop a second repair framework whose running time depends on the number of moved jobs (moved-job error
\mathcal{E}_{J}
) rather than on the moved-load error (see Appendix
A
).
The aforementioned hardness construction also implies that exact moved-job repair for
Restricted Assignment
is
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard when parameterized by the number of moved jobs.
For
Restricted Assignment
, the method follows the same repair framework as before. Compared with the moved-load repair, it incurs an additional
\varepsilon T
loss in the makespan, arising from the necessary rounding of the processing times. For the two-valued case, we refine this approach by separating big and small jobs: we use a dynamic program for the big jobs and a min-cost flow formulation for the small jobs, which removes the
(1+\varepsilon)
loss and yields an exact repair with makespan at most
T
. Both repair procedures have similar worst-case running-time guarantees as in Corollary
17
.

About the prediction model.

In line with many works on learning-augmented offline optimization, our algorithms rely on having access to predictions for a computationally hard optimization problem. It is not yet clear whether current ML methods can reliably provide accurate predictions about such problems, though a growing body of work in machine learning for combinatorial optimization seeks to learn structural information about high-quality solutions
[
6
,
9
]
. In our setting, this assumption is less restrictive, since the prediction does not need to be a feasible schedule. It only needs to capture useful information about the assignment structure, and feasibility is enforced afterward by our algorithm. Therefore, in application domains with recurring structure, such predictions may be realistic in practice.

2
Preliminaries

We consider
Restricted Assignment
scheduling on
m
parallel machines and
n
jobs. Each job
j\in[n]
has processing time
p_{j}>0
and a set of eligible machines
\Gamma(j)\subseteq[m]
.
A schedule is feasible if
\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)
for all jobs.
For a feasible schedule
\sigma
, we define

L_{i}(\sigma)=\sum_{j:\sigma(j)=i}p_{j}
and

C_{\max}(\sigma)=\max_{i\in[m]}L_{i}(\sigma)
.
The goal of the problem is to find a feasible schedule that minimizes the makespan.

Let
\sigma^{*}
be a feasible schedule that achieves the optimal makespan
\mathsf{OPT}
. More specifically,

\mathsf{OPT}=\min\{C_{\max}(\sigma):\sigma\text{ feasible}\}
.
Throughout the paper, we assume that every input instance of the problem is feasible, i.e.,
\Gamma(j)\neq\emptyset
for all jobs
j
. Moreover, we assume that all processing times are integers:
p_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for all jobs
j
. If processing times are rational, one can scale them to integers, which affects the moved-load
error
\mathcal{E}
(and hence the corresponding running times) only by the
scaling factor and preserves the moved-job error
\mathcal{E}_{J}
.

2.1
Prediction Model

For every instance of the problem, we are given a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}:[n]\to[m]
of jobs to machines, which may be infeasible, i.e.,
\hat{\pi}(j)\notin\Gamma(j)
for some jobs.

Next, we define the prediction error used in this work
2
2

2

We also consider an alternative error measure based on the number of moved jobs. Its formal definition is deferred to Appendix
A
.
. Intuitively, it measures the total load (accumulated processing time) that must be reassigned to transform a predicted job-to-machine mapping into an (optimal) assignment. First, let us define the moved-load.

Definition 1

(Moved-load)
.

For a feasible schedule
\sigma
and any mapping
\tau:[n]\to[m]
(feasible or infeasible), define:

M(\sigma,\tau)=\{j\in[n]:\sigma(j)\neq\tau(j)\},\qquad\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\tau)=\sum_{j\in M(\sigma,\tau)}p_{j}.

Now we are ready to define the prediction error with respect to an optimal schedule for an instance of the problem.

Definition 2

(Prediction error wrt
\mathsf{OPT}
)
.

Define the prediction error with respect to an optimal solution by

\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{OPT})=\min\{\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi}):\sigma^{*}\text{ feasible and }C_{\max}(\sigma^{*})=\mathsf{OPT}\}.

We will also need to define the prediction error required to obtain a feasible schedule of makespan at most
T
. Throughout the paper, we assume that
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
.

Definition 3

(Prediction error wrt
T
)
.

Fix
T
. The minimum moved-load needed to reach makespan at most
T
from a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
is

\mathcal{E}(T)=\min\{\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi}):\sigma\text{ feasible and }C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T\}.

3
Error-Dependent Approximation Guarantees

In this section, we use the prediction to obtain approximation guarantees that degrade smoothly with the moved-load error
\mathcal{E}
. We first project the possibly infeasible prediction to a feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
, which already gives a basic projection guarantee. We then combine this projection with a guessing-and-rounding procedure to obtain our smoothness guarantee.

The first step is very simple: we project
\hat{\pi}
to a feasible schedule.
Specifically, for a fixed arbitrary ordering
\pi_{g}
of the jobs, the procedure
\mathsf{Project}_{\pi_{g}}(\hat{\pi})
processes jobs one by one. If
\hat{\pi}(j)\in\Gamma(j)
, it keeps the predicted assignment, and otherwise it reassigns
j
to the least-loaded eligible machine (breaking ties arbitrarily). The output is a feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.

In the rest of the paper, we omit explicit reference to the order
\pi_{g}
and write

\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
, using a fixed arbitrary order. Moreover, unless stated otherwise, we use the projected predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.

3.1
Smooth Approximation

We begin with the projection step. First, we prove that
\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
does not increase the moved-load error.

Lemma 4

(Projection monotonicity (moved-load))
.

For every feasible schedule
\sigma
, we have
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})
, where
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
.

Proof.

Fix a feasible schedule
\sigma
and a job
j
.

If
\hat{\pi}(j)\in\Gamma(j)
, then
\hat{\sigma}(j)=\hat{\pi}(j)
, and thus

\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]=\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)],

so job
j
contributes the same amount to
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
and
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})
.

If
\hat{\pi}(j)\notin\Gamma(j)
, then feasibility implies
\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)
and hence
\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)
, i.e.,

\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)]=1
, while
\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]\in\{0,1\}
.
Thus
\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]\leq\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)]
for every job
j
.
Multiplying by
p_{j}
and summing over jobs yields
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})
.
∎

We are now ready to show that the projected predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
has makespan close to
\mathsf{OPT}
when the prediction error with respect to an optimal solution is small.

Theorem 5

(Smoothness of
\mathsf{Project}
)
.

Let
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
. Then,

C_{\max}(\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma^{*}
be an optimal schedule.
Consider any machine
i
.

Let
\hat{L}_{i}=L_{i}(\hat{\sigma})
and

X_{i}=\{j:\hat{\sigma}(j)=i,\,\sigma^{*}(j)\neq i\}
be the jobs assigned to machine
i
under
\hat{\sigma}
but not under
\sigma^{*}
. Since every job on machine
i
under
\hat{\sigma}
either also appears on
i
under
\sigma^{*}
or belongs to
X_{i}
, we have
\hat{L}_{i}\leq L_{i}(\sigma^{*})+\sum_{j\in X_{i}}p_{j}
, and thus
\hat{L}_{i}-L_{i}(\sigma^{*})\leq\sum_{j\in X_{i}}p_{j}
.

Then,

\hat{L}_{i}-L_{i}(\sigma^{*})\leq\sum_{j\in X_{i}}p_{j}\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})
.
Since
L_{i}(\sigma^{*})\leq\mathsf{OPT}
, we obtain
\hat{L}_{i}\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})
for every machine
i
, and thus

C_{\max}(\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})
.

By Lemma
4
,
\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi})
.
Minimizing
\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi})
over optimal schedules yields

C_{\max}(\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\mathcal{E}
.
∎

We now prove our smoothness guarantee. The idea is to guess a
constant number of large jobs on which the projected prediction differs from an optimal schedule closest to the prediction in moved-load, and then use the standard assignment LP and rounding for
Restricted Assignment
on the remaining, small jobs.

Theorem 6

(Smoothness)
.

For every fixed
\delta\in(0,1]
, there is an algorithm running
in time
(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
that outputs a feasible schedule
\sigma

with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1]
and set

q=\left\lceil\frac{1}{\delta}\right\rceil-1
.

Compute
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
. The algorithm enumerates a threshold

\theta\in\{0\}\cup\{p_{j}:j\in[n]\}
, a set

B\subseteq\{j:p_{j}>\theta\}
with
|B|\leq q
, and an assignment

\varphi:B\to[m]
with
\varphi(j)\in\Gamma(j)
for all
j\in B
.
For a fixed guess, jobs in
B
are fixed according to
\varphi
, jobs

j\notin B
with
p_{j}>\theta
are fixed to
\hat{\sigma}(j)
, and all jobs of
size at most
\theta
are left free.

For the free jobs, we solve the standard assignment LP with the fixed loads
already assigned. If the LP is feasible, let
T^{\prime}
be its optimum value and apply
the Lenstra–Shmoys–Tardos (LST) rounding
[
21
]
. By the additive form of
the LST rounding, the resulting schedule has makespan at most
T^{\prime}+\theta
,
because every free job has size at most
\theta
. If the LP is infeasible, we
discard this guess. The algorithm returns the best feasible schedule over all guesses.

Let
\sigma^{*}
be an optimal schedule minimizing
\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi})
, so

\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi})=\mathcal{E}
. By Lemma
4
,

\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathcal{E}
.

Let
R=M(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})
be the set of jobs on which
\sigma^{*}
differs
from
\hat{\sigma}
.

If
|R|\leq q
, then the algorithm considers the guess
\theta=0
,
B=R
, and

\varphi(j)=\sigma^{*}(j)
for all
j\in B
. Since there are no free jobs when
\theta=0
, this guess reconstructs

\sigma^{*}
. Hence the algorithm returns a schedule of makespan at most

\mathsf{OPT}\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E}
.

Assume now that
|R|>q
. Order the jobs of
R
as
j_{1},\ldots,j_{|R|}
so that

p_{j_{1}}\geq p_{j_{2}}\geq\dots\geq p_{j_{|R|}}
, and set

\theta=p_{j_{q+1}}
. Then

(q+1)\theta\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{q+1}p_{j_{\ell}}\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathcal{E},

and therefore
\theta\leq\mathcal{E}/(q+1)
.

Consider the guess with
B=\{j\in R:p_{j}>\theta\}
and

\varphi(j)=\sigma^{*}(j)
for all
j\in B
. By the choice of
\theta
, we have

|B|\leq q
, so this guess is enumerated. Moreover, every job of size larger
than
\theta
outside
B
is not in
R
, and hence is assigned in the same way
by
\sigma^{*}
and
\hat{\sigma}
. Thus all fixed jobs are consistent with

\sigma^{*}
, while all remaining jobs have size at most
\theta
and are left
free. Therefore,
\sigma^{*}
is feasible for the LP of this guess, so

T^{\prime}\leq\mathsf{OPT}
. The rounded schedule for this guess has makespan at most

T^{\prime}+\theta\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\frac{\mathcal{E}}{q+1}\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E},

where the last inequality follows from
1/(q+1)\leq\delta
.

The number of guesses is at most
(n+1)n^{q}m^{q}
, and each guess requires one LP
solve and one rounding step. Thus the running time is

(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(q)}=(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
.
∎

3.2
Robust Approximation

If the smoothness guarantee is not sufficient, we can combine our algorithm with any
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the problem that does not use predictions.

Theorem 7

(Smoothness & Robustness)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
be any
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for
Restricted Assignment
that does not use predictions.
For every fixed
\delta\in(0,1]
, given a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
, one can output a schedule
\sigma
in time
(nm)^{\mathcal{O}(1/\delta)}
such that

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq\min\{\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E},\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}\}
.

Proof.

Run the algorithm of Theorem
6
with parameter

\delta
, and let
\sigma^{\delta}
be the resulting schedule. Also run

\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
and let
\sigma^{\alpha}
be its output. Return the schedule
with smaller makespan among
\sigma^{\delta}
and
\sigma^{\alpha}
.
By Theorem
6
,
C_{\max}(\sigma^{\delta})\leq\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E}
. Since
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}
is an
\alpha
-approximation
algorithm,
C_{\max}(\sigma^{\alpha})\leq\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
. Returning the better of the two
schedules gives
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq\min\{\mathsf{OPT}+\delta\mathcal{E},\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}\}
.
∎

4
Error-Dependent Repair

In this section, we show how to repair the projected feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}
in time that depends on the prediction error. Assume we are given an estimate
T
for the optimal makespan of
Restricted Assignment
satisfying

\mathsf{OPT}\leq T<\alpha\cdot\mathsf{OPT}
, where
\alpha
is the best known approximation ratio for the problem without predictions.
When the makespan estimate
T
improves over the robust guarantee, we present an
\mathrm{XP}
(slice-wise polynomial) repair procedure that starts from the projected schedule
\hat{\sigma}
and
outputs a schedule of makespan at most
T
in error-dependent time. Finally, we complement this result with a parameterized hardness result for exact repair with a given target makespan, showing that an
\mathrm{FPT}
algorithm parameterized only by the prediction error would imply
\mathrm{W[1]}=\mathrm{FPT}
(Section
4.4
).

4.1
Lower bounds on prediction error

We next derive some lower bounds on the prediction error, which will be useful in the design of our learning-augmented repair algorithm. Recall that
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}(T)
are defined with respect to the original prediction
\hat{\pi}
. In this section, we repair the projected feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
.
For every machine
i
, let
\hat{L}_{i}=L_{i}(\hat{\sigma})
denote its load under the projected predicted schedule.
Whenever we compare a feasible schedule
\sigma
to
\hat{\sigma}
, we use Lemma
4
, which gives
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})
.

First, we show that achieving makespan at most
T
requires no more correction than reaching an optimal schedule. More formally, we have the following.

Lemma 8
.

If
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, then
\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma^{*}
be an optimal schedule, so
C_{\max}(\sigma^{*})=\mathsf{OPT}\leq T
. Therefore,
\sigma^{*}
is feasible for the minimization defining
\mathcal{E}(T)
. Hence,
\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi})
.
Minimizing the right-hand side over all optimal schedules gives
\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}
.
∎

We define the total overload under
\hat{\sigma}
with respect to makespan
T
as

\Delta(T)=\sum_{i\in[m]}(\hat{L}_{i}-T)^{+}
,
where
(\hat{L}_{i}-T)^{+}=\max\{\hat{L}_{i}-T,0\}
for every
i\in[m]
.
Let us now prove the following proposition.

Proposition 9
.

For every
T
,
\mathcal{E}(T)\geq\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma
be feasible with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
. For each machine
i
, the jobs
that
\hat{\sigma}
places on
i
but
\sigma
does not, must have total processing time
at least
(\hat{L}_{i}-T)^{+}
. Summing over machines gives
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\geq\Delta(T)
.
By Lemma
4
,
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})\geq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\geq\Delta(T)
. Minimizing over
\sigma
yields

\mathcal{E}(T)\geq\Delta(T)
, and integrality of processing times gives

\mathcal{E}(T)\geq\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil
.
∎

4.2
Repair Oracle

In this subsection, we describe a repair procedure at a given makespan threshold
T
and moved-load budget
K
. The procedure relies on two ingredients:
(i) a dynamic program that repairs
\hat{\sigma}
when the set of incident machines is fixed, and (ii) a global enumeration over all candidate incident sets of bounded size.
Let us first present some lemmas that will prove useful.

First, we define the incident set

A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})=\{\hat{\sigma}(j):j\in M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\}\cup\{\sigma(j):j\in M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\}
.

Lemma 10

(Overloaded machines must be incident)
.

If
\sigma
is feasible and
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
, then every overloaded machine wrt
T
is incident:

\{i:\hat{L}_{i}>T\}\subseteq A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
.

Proof.

Fix
i
with
\hat{L}_{i}>T
. If
i\notin A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
then no predicted job leaves
i
and no job is
moved to
i
, hence
L_{i}(\sigma)=\hat{L}_{i}>T
, contradicting
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
.
∎

Next, we bound the number of machines touched by moved jobs in terms of the moved-load budget.

Lemma 11

(Incident set size bound)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
If
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
, then

|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2K
.

Proof.

Since processing times are integers and
p_{j}\geq 1
for all jobs, each moved job contributes at least
1
to moved-load, so

|M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
.
Each moved job contributes at most two machines (origin and destination) to
A_{\mathrm{inc}}
, so

|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2|M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2K
.
∎

Let the set of machines overloaded under
\hat{\sigma}
wrt
T
be

O(T)=\{i\in[m]:\hat{L}_{i}>T\}
.

We are now ready to describe a Repair oracle for a fixed incident set of machines using dynamic programming. The DP follows the standard pattern of multi-dimensional knapsack: we process items one by one, and for each item we choose one of a few possible destinations. Each choice updates a small vector that summarizes the partial reassignment (e.g., signed load changes across the incident set), and we pay a cost only when the choice differs from the predicted assignment. The DP therefore searches, within a bounded state space, for a minimum-cost partial reassignment that satisfies all feasibility constraints.

Lemma 12

(Fixed-set Repair oracle (moved-load))
.

Fix a threshold makespan
T
, a moved-load budget
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.
Let
A\subseteq[m]
satisfy
O(T)\subseteq A
. Write
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{a}\}
with
a=|A|\geq 1
and define
J_{A}=\{j\in[n]:\hat{\sigma}(j)\in A\}
.
There exists a dynamic program (DP) running in time

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2K+1)^{a-1}\right)

that decides whether there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma
such that:

1.

\sigma(j)=\hat{\sigma}(j)
for all
j\notin J_{A}
;

2.

\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
for all
j\in J_{A}
;

3.

\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
;

4.

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
.

If such a schedule exists, the DP outputs one. Otherwise it outputs
Fail
.

Proof.

Since
O(T)\subseteq A
, every machine
i\notin A
is not overloaded in the predicted schedule, i.e.,
\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
.
Under conditions (1)–(2), the assignment on machines outside
A
is unchanged, hence

L_{i}(\sigma)=\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
for all
i\notin A
.
Therefore, among schedules satisfying (1)–(2), the constraint
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
is equivalent to requiring
L_{i}(\sigma)\leq T
, for all
i\in A
.

Write
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{a}\}
and recall
J_{A}=\{j:\hat{\sigma}(j)\in A\}
.
For any schedule
\sigma
satisfying (1)–(2), define the net load change on each machine
i_{r}\in A
by

b_{r}=L_{i_{r}}(\sigma)-\hat{L}_{i_{r}}\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a].

Because only jobs in
J_{A}
are reassigned and they remain within
A
, the total load inside
A
is preserved, and thus
\sum_{r=1}^{a}b_{r}=0
.

Moreover, if
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
, then for each
r
let
\mathsf{Out}_{r}
be the total processing time of jobs that leave
i_{r}

and
\mathsf{In}_{r}
the total processing time of jobs that enter
i_{r}
.
Both
\mathsf{Out}_{r}
and
\mathsf{In}_{r}
are bounded by the total moved-load, so
\mathsf{Out}_{r}\leq K
and
\mathsf{In}_{r}\leq K
, and thus

|b_{r}|=|\mathsf{In}_{r}-\mathsf{Out}_{r}|\leq K\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a].

Consequently, it suffices to search over integer vectors
(b_{1},\dots,b_{a})
with
\sum_{r}b_{r}=0
and
b_{r}\in[-K,K]
, since
p_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for all jobs.

We store
x=(b_{1},\dots,b_{a-1})
, with
b_{a}(x)=-\sum_{r=1}^{a-1}b_{r}
.

The state space is

\mathcal{X}=\bigl\{x\in[-K,K]^{a-1}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{a-1}:b_{a}(x)\in[-K,K]\bigr\}
.

Order
J_{A}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}
, where
q=|J_{A}|
.
For
\ell\in\{0,\dots,q\}
and
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we let
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,x]
be the minimum moved-load incurred by assigning the first
\ell

jobs to machines in
\Gamma(\cdot)\cap A
while inducing net changes
x
on machines
i_{1},\dots,i_{a-1}
:

\mathsf{DP}[\ell,x]\;=\;\min\Bigl\{\sum_{t\leq\ell:\,\varphi(j_{t})\neq\hat{\sigma}(j_{t})}p_{j_{t}}:\varphi(j_{t})\in\Gamma(j_{t})\cap A,\ \text{and induced net change is }x\Bigr\}.

Initialize
\mathsf{DP}[0,\vec{0}]=0
and
\mathsf{DP}[0,x]=+\infty
for
x\neq\vec{0}
.

The net load change on machine
i_{r}
is
+p_{j}
if the job is assigned to
i_{r}
but was not predicted there,
-p_{j}
if the job was predicted on
i_{r}
but is reassigned elsewhere, and
0
if the job remains on its predicted machine
i_{r}
. Effects involving
i_{a}
are handled implicitly through
b_{a}(x)=-\sum_{r\leq a-1}x_{r}
. More formally, we have the following transitions.

Fix
\ell<q
, a state
x\in\mathcal{X}
with
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,x]<\infty
, and let
j=j_{\ell+1}
.
For each choice
u\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
, define the new state
x^{\prime}
by updating each coordinate
r\in[a-1]
as

x^{\prime}_{r}\;=\;x_{r}\;+\;p_{j}\cdot\bigl(\mathbf{1}[u=i_{r}]-\mathbf{1}[\hat{\sigma}(j)=i_{r}]\bigr).

The transition cost is

\mathsf{cost}(j,u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }u=\hat{\sigma}(j),\\
p_{j}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We keep the transition only if
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,x]+\mathsf{cost}(j,u)\leq K
, and then update

\mathsf{DP}[\ell+1,x^{\prime}]\leftarrow\min\{\mathsf{DP}[\ell+1,x^{\prime}],\ \mathsf{DP}[\ell,x]+\mathsf{cost}(j,u)\}.

We accept a final state
(q,x)
if the net-change vector
(b_{1},\dots,b_{a})
satisfies the load constraints:

\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+b_{r}\leq T\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a],

where
(b_{1},\dots,b_{a-1})=x
and
b_{a}=b_{a}(x)
.
If such a final state exists, we reconstruct the corresponding schedule
\sigma
. Otherwise, we output
Fail
.

Correctness.

Any schedule
\sigma
satisfying (1)–(2) corresponds to a unique DP path: at step
t
choose
u=\sigma(j_{t})
.
The state update matches exactly the induced net changes on
i_{1},\dots,i_{a-1}
, and the accumulated cost equals the moved-load of
the processed jobs, hence the final DP value equals
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})
.
Conversely, any DP path defines an assignment of all jobs in
J_{A}
into
\Gamma(\cdot)\cap A
. Together with (1) it yields a full schedule.
Therefore, the DP reaches an accepted state with value
\leq K
if and only if there exists a schedule satisfying (1)–(4).

Running time.

There are
q+1=|J_{A}|+1
possible values of
\ell
, and for each value at most
|\mathcal{X}|\leq(2K+1)^{a-1}
states.
From each state, we try at most
|\Gamma(j_{\ell+1})\cap A|\leq a
transitions, each in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time. Hence the running time is

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2K+1)^{a-1}\right)
.
∎

We now wrap Lemma
12
into a global oracle by enumerating all candidate sets
A
that could contain the incident machines of a solution of moved-load at most
K
.
If
|O(T)|>2K
, then no such set exists and the oracle immediately returns
Fail
.

Definition 13

(Global Repair oracle with
(T,K)
(moved-load))
.

Given makespan
T
, budget
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
, the procedure

\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}(T,K,\hat{\sigma})
enumerates all sets
A\subseteq[m]
such that
O(T)\subseteq A
and

|A|\leq 2K
, runs the DP of Lemma
12
for each such
A
, and returns the first schedule found (or
Fail
if none exists).

Lemma 14

(Correctness of the Global Repair oracle (moved-load))
.

If there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
such that
C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
and

\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})\leq K
, then
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}(T,K,\hat{\sigma})
returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
and
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
.

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, we have

O(T)\subseteq A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})
.
By Lemma
11
,
|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2K
.
Hence the oracle enumerates
A=A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})
, and for this set
A
, the schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfies the restrictions of Lemma
12
.
Therefore, the DP succeeds and returns a schedule with the required guarantees.
∎

We next bound the running time of
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}(T,K,\hat{\sigma})
.
The overhead comes from enumerating all candidate incident sets
A
with
O(T)\subseteq A
and
|A|\leq 2K
, and running the fixed-set DP (Lemma
12
) for each such set.

Lemma 15

(Running time of the Global Repair oracle)
.

Fix
T
,
K\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.
Then,
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}(T,K,\hat{\sigma})
runs in time

O\!\left(n\cdot m^{2K}\cdot(2K+1)^{2K+1}\right)
.

Proof.

The number of candidate sets
A
with
O(T)\subseteq A
and
|A|\leq 2K
is at most
\sum_{t=0}^{2K}\binom{m}{t}\leq(2K+1)m^{2K}
. For each, the DP of Lemma
12
runs in time
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot 2K\cdot(2K+1)^{2K-1})
, since
|J_{A}|\leq n
and
|A|\leq 2K
. Multiplying gives the bound.
∎

4.3
Global Repair

In this subsection, we describe the Global Repair procedure (see Algorithm
1
) that does not require knowing the optimal
moved-load budget (
\mathcal{E}(T)
) in advance. The algorithm guesses the necessary moved-load by doubling a budget parameter
K
,
starting from the lower bound
\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil
, and invokes a bounded-budget oracle to obtain a schedule of makespan at most
T
.
The resulting running time depends smoothly on the prediction error
\mathcal{E}
.

Algorithm 1

GlobalRepair
(T,\hat{\pi})

1:

Threshold makespan
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, possibly infeasible prediction
\hat{\pi}

2:

A feasible schedule
\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T

3:

\hat{\sigma}\leftarrow\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})

4:

Compute loads
\hat{L}_{i}
for all
i\in[m]
, and
\Delta(T)\leftarrow\sum_{i\in[m]}(\hat{L}_{i}-T)^{+}

5:

if

\Delta(T)=0

then

6:

return

\hat{\sigma}

7:

end

if

8:

K\leftarrow\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil

9:

while

true

do

10:

\sigma\leftarrow\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}(T,K,\hat{\sigma})

11:

if

\sigma\neq\textsc{Fail}

then

12:

return

\sigma

13:

end

if

14:

K\leftarrow 2K

15:

end

while

Theorem 16

(Global Repair at makespan
T
)
.

Fix any makespan threshold
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
and let
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
.
Algorithm
1
returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
.
Moreover, if
\Delta(T)>0
it stops after reaching some integer
K
satisfying

K<2\mathcal{E}(T)\leq 2\mathcal{E}
, and the number of oracle calls is at most

\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}(T)}{\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil}\right)\right\rceil+1\ \leq\ \left\lceil\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil}\right)\right\rceil+1
.

If
\Delta(T)=0
, it directly outputs the predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.

Proof.

If
\Delta(T)=0
, then
\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
for all
i
. Hence
C_{\max}(\hat{\sigma})\leq T
and the algorithm returns
\hat{\sigma}
.

Assume
\Delta(T)>0
. By Proposition
9
, we have
\mathcal{E}(T)\geq\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil
.
Thus, the doubling sequence

K=\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil,\ 2\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil,\ 4\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil,\ \dots

reaches a first value
K^{*}
with
K^{*}\geq\mathcal{E}(T)
after at most

\left\lceil\log_{2}(\mathcal{E}(T)/\lceil\Delta(T)\rceil)\right\rceil+1
iterations, and by minimality
K^{*}<2\mathcal{E}(T)
.
We show that the oracle succeeds when called with budget
K^{*}
. Hence the algorithm stops no later than this call.

For the budget
K^{*}
, by definition of
\mathcal{E}(T)
there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
with

C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\pi})=\mathcal{E}(T)\leq K^{*}.

By Lemma
4
, we have

\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\pi})\leq K^{*}
.
Therefore the hypothesis of Lemma
14
is satisfied, so the oracle succeeds when called with budget
K^{*}
and returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
.

Since the algorithm stops no later than the call with budget
K^{*}
, the budget
K
at which it stops satisfies
K\leq K^{*}<2\mathcal{E}(T)
. Finally, since
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, Lemma
8
yields
\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}
, and hence
K<2\mathcal{E}(T)\leq 2\mathcal{E}
.
The second bound on the number of oracle calls follows similarly.
∎

Consequently, since the successful oracle call uses
K<2\mathcal{E}
we get the following corollary.

Corollary 17

(Running time of
GlobalRepair
)
.

Assume that
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
and
\Delta(T)>0
. Then Algorithm
1
returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
in time

\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}\!\left(n\cdot m^{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E})}\cdot\mathcal{E}^{\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E})}\right)
.

In contrast, a naive approach that explicitly guesses the set of moved jobs would lead to a running time with an
n^{\Theta(\mathcal{E})}
-type dependence.

4.4
Hardness for Exact Moved-Load Repair

We now complement the moved-load repair algorithm with a parameterized hardness result. In particular, exact repair with a given target makespan does not admit an
\mathrm{FPT}
algorithm parameterized by the amount of moved-load, unless
\mathrm{W[1]}=\mathrm{FPT}
. The proof is by a reduction from
Multicolored Clique
and is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Definition 18

(Exact Moved-Load Repair)
.

Given an instance of
Restricted Assignment
, a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
, a makespan threshold
T
, and an integer budget
K
, decide whether there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma
such that
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
and
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
.

Theorem 19
.

Exact Moved-Load Repair
is
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard parameterized by
K
, even when
\hat{\sigma}
is feasible and the instance has at most three distinct processing times.

6
Future Work

A natural direction is to extend our framework to the
Restricted Assignment Santa Claus
problem, where each item can be assigned only to a subset of players and the objective is to maximize the minimum total value received by any player. We believe the structural machinery developed in this work can be adapted to the max-min setting. Since the
Restricted Assignment Santa Claus
problem also exhibits a separation between estimation and approximation, with the best known polynomial-time estimation ratio being
3.534+\zeta_{1}

[
17
]
, for every
\zeta_{1}>0
, and the best known approximation ratio being
4+\zeta_{2}

[
12
]
, for every
\zeta_{2}>0
, the repair approach may similarly be used to recover solutions of quality beyond what is achievable in polynomial time without predictions, when the prediction error is sufficiently small. We believe that this extension is a natural next step for the framework developed here.

Appendix A
Repair Parameterized by the Number of Moved Jobs

In this section, we present a repair strategy whose running time depends on the number of moved jobs
rather than the moved-load. This can be significantly smaller when the predictor makes only a few mistakes
on large jobs: moving a single large job may contribute a large amount to moved-load, but it contributes only
one unit to the moved-job error. We first give such a repair strategy for
Restricted Assignment
and then
in Section
A.3
, we refine it further for the two-valued version of the problem. Let us now formally define the moved-job error.

Definition 20

(Moved-job error)
.

For a feasible schedule
\sigma
and any mapping
\tau:[n]\to[m]
, define:

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\tau)=|M(\sigma,\tau)|=|\{j\in[n]:\sigma(j)\neq\tau(j)\}|
.

Next, we define the moved-job error of a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
with respect to
\mathsf{OPT}
and to a fixed makespan
T
.

Definition 21

(Prediction moved-job error wrt
\mathsf{OPT}
)
.

Define the prediction error with respect to an optimal solution by

\mathcal{E}_{J}=\mathcal{E}_{J}(\mathsf{OPT})=\min\{\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{*},\hat{\pi}):\sigma^{*}\text{ feasible and }C_{\max}(\sigma^{*})=\mathsf{OPT}\}.

Definition 22

(Prediction moved-job error wrt
T
)
.

Fix
T
. The minimum number of moved jobs needed to reach makespan at most
T
, relative to a predicted assignment
\hat{\pi}
, is

\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)=\min\{\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\pi}):\sigma\text{ feasible and }C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T\}.

Since
p_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for all jobs, for every feasible schedule
\sigma
we have that

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})\leq\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\pi}),

because every moved job contributes at least one unit of processing time to the moved-load. Moreover, the analogue of Lemma
8
for the moved-job error also holds, namely, if
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}_{J}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}(T)\leq\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}_{J}\leq\mathcal{E}
.

The next lemma is the analogue of Lemma
4
for the moved-job error. Although the quantities
\mathcal{E}_{J}
and
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)
are defined with respect to the original prediction
\hat{\pi}
, in the repair phase we work with the projected feasible schedule
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
. The lemma below shows that projecting the prediction cannot increase the number of moved jobs.

Lemma 23

(Projection monotonicity (moved jobs))
.

For every feasible schedule
\sigma
,

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\pi})
, where
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
.

Proof.

Fix a feasible schedule
\sigma
and a job
j
.

If
\hat{\pi}(j)\in\Gamma(j)
, then
\hat{\sigma}(j)=\hat{\pi}(j)
, so

\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]=\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)].

If
\hat{\pi}(j)\notin\Gamma(j)
, then feasibility of
\sigma
implies
\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)
, hence
\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)
. Therefore,

\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]\leq\mathbf{1}[\sigma(j)\neq\hat{\pi}(j)].

Summing over all jobs gives the claim.
∎

Next, we bound the number of machines touched by moved jobs as we did for moved-load.

Lemma 24

(Incident set size bound under moved-job budget)
.

If
\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq M
, then
|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2M
.

Proof.

If at most
M
jobs are moved, then
|M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq M
.
Each moved job involves at most two machines (its predicted machine and its assigned machine) to

A_{\mathrm{inc}}
, hence

|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2|M(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2M
.
∎

A.1
Repair Oracle under Moved Jobs

We now give a fixed-set dynamic program, closely mirroring the one in Section
4.2
. The crucial difference is that we apply a standard rounding step (as in FPTAS analyses for knapsack and related packing DPs), which reduces the state space to polynomial size and thereby removes any pseudo-polynomial dependence on the jobs’ processing times. This is necessary to get an
\mathrm{XP}
algorithm with respect to the number of moved jobs.

Lemma 25

(Fixed-set Repair oracle (moved jobs))
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, a threshold makespan
T
, a moved-job budget
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.
Let
A\subseteq[m]
satisfy
O(T)\subseteq A
. Write
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{a}\}
with
a=|A|\geq 1
, and let

J_{A}=\{j\in[n]:\hat{\sigma}(j)\in A\}
.
There is a dynamic program (DP) running in time

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2B+1)^{a-1}\right)
,
where
B=\left\lceil\frac{3M^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
,
that outputs either
Fail
or a feasible schedule
\sigma
satisfying

1.

\sigma(j)=\hat{\sigma}(j)
for all
j\notin J_{A}
;

2.

\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
for all
j\in J_{A}
;

3.

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq M
;

4.

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
.

Moreover, if there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfying (1)–(3) and
C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
,
then the DP always outputs a schedule (i.e., it does not output
Fail
).

Proof.

We first round the processing times of the jobs.
Set

\rho=\frac{\varepsilon T}{2M},\qquad w_{j}=\left\lceil\frac{p_{j}}{\rho}\right\rceil\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1},\qquad p^{\prime}_{j}=\rho w_{j}.

Then for every job
j
,

p_{j}\leq p^{\prime}_{j}<p_{j}+\rho\qquad\text{and}\qquad 0\leq p^{\prime}_{j}-p_{j}<\rho.

Let
B=\left\lceil\frac{3M^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
.
Since
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
implies
p_{j}\leq T
for all jobs, we have

w_{j}\leq\lceil T/\rho\rceil=\lceil 2M/\varepsilon\rceil
. Hence the total rounded inflow/outflow in
\rho
-units
contributed by at most
M
moved jobs to any machine is at most

M\cdot\left\lceil\frac{2M}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\leq M\left(\frac{2M}{\varepsilon}+1\right)\leq\frac{3M^{2}}{\varepsilon}\leq B,

where the second inequality uses
M\geq 1>\varepsilon
.

Since
O(T)\subseteq A
, every machine
i\notin A
satisfies
\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
.
Under conditions (1)–(2), the assignment on machines outside
A
is unchanged, hence

L_{i}(\sigma)=\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
for all
i\notin A
.
Therefore, among schedules satisfying (1)–(2), it suffices to enforce the constraint
L_{i}(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
for machines in
A
.

For schedules satisfying (1)–(2), define for each
r\in[a]
the rounded net change

\overline{b}_{r}=\sum_{j:\sigma(j)=i_{r}}w_{j}\;-\;\sum_{j:\hat{\sigma}(j)=i_{r}}w_{j}.

Since jobs are only reassigned within
A
, we have
\sum_{r=1}^{a}\overline{b}_{r}=0
.

For any schedule satisfying condition (3), fix
r
and let
\overline{\mathsf{Out}}_{r}
(resp.
\overline{\mathsf{In}}_{r}
) be the total rounded size
of moved jobs in
\rho
-units leaving (resp. entering) machine
i_{r}
. At most
M
moved jobs can leave or enter
i_{r}
, and each moved job has
rounded size at most
\lceil 2M/\varepsilon\rceil
, hence

\overline{\mathsf{Out}}_{r}\leq M\Bigl\lceil\frac{2M}{\varepsilon}\Bigr\rceil\leq B,\qquad\overline{\mathsf{In}}_{r}\leq M\Bigl\lceil\frac{2M}{\varepsilon}\Bigr\rceil\leq B.

Therefore,

|\overline{b}_{r}|=|\overline{\mathsf{In}}_{r}-\overline{\mathsf{Out}}_{r}|\leq\max\{\overline{\mathsf{In}}_{r},\overline{\mathsf{Out}}_{r}\}\leq B
.
Thus it suffices to search over vectors with
\overline{b}_{r}\in[-B,B]
, for all
r\in[a]
.

We store
\overline{x}=(\overline{b}_{1},\dots,\overline{b}_{a-1})
and set the last coordinate implicitly as

\overline{b}_{a}(\overline{x})=-\sum_{r=1}^{a-1}\overline{b}_{r}
.

The state space is

\overline{\mathcal{X}}=\bigl\{\overline{x}\in[-B,B]^{a-1}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{a-1}:\overline{b}_{a}(\overline{x})\in[-B,B]\bigr\}
.

Order
J_{A}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}
, where
q=|J_{A}|
.
For
\ell\in\{0,\dots,q\}
and
\overline{x}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
, we let
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,\overline{x}]
be the minimum number of moved jobs incurred by assigning the first
\ell
jobs to machines in
\Gamma(\cdot)\cap A
while inducing rounded net changes
\overline{x}
on machines
i_{1},\dots,i_{a-1}
:

\mathsf{DP}[\ell,\overline{x}]=\min\Bigl\{\#\{t\leq\ell:\varphi(j_{t})\neq\hat{\sigma}(j_{t})\}:\\
\varphi(j_{t})\in\Gamma(j_{t})\cap A,\ \text{and induced rounded net change is }\overline{x}\Bigr\}.

Initialize
\mathsf{DP}[0,\vec{0}]=0
and
\mathsf{DP}[0,\overline{x}]=+\infty
for
\overline{x}\neq\vec{0}
.

Fix
\ell<q
, a state
\overline{x}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
with
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,\overline{x}]<\infty
, and let
j=j_{\ell+1}
.
For each choice
u\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
, define the new state
\overline{x}^{\prime}
by updating
\overline{x}
as follows.
Only the coordinates corresponding to
u
and to
\hat{\sigma}(j)
can change. Hence
\overline{x}^{\prime}
can be computed from
\overline{x}

in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time.
Formally, for each
r\in[a-1]
,

\overline{x}^{\prime}_{r}\;=\;\overline{x}_{r}\;+\;w_{j}\cdot\bigl(\mathbf{1}[u=i_{r}]-\mathbf{1}[\hat{\sigma}(j)=i_{r}]\bigr).

The transition cost is

\mathsf{cost}_{J}(j,u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }u=\hat{\sigma}(j),\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We keep the transition only if
\overline{x}^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathsf{DP}[\ell,\overline{x}]+\mathsf{cost}_{J}(j,u)\leq M
, and then update

\mathsf{DP}[\ell+1,\overline{x}^{\prime}]\leftarrow\min\{\mathsf{DP}[\ell+1,\overline{x}^{\prime}],\ \mathsf{DP}[\ell,\overline{x}]+\mathsf{cost}_{J}(j,u)\}.

We accept a final state
(q,\overline{x})
if the implied rounded changes satisfy

\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+\rho\cdot\overline{b}_{r}\leq T+M\rho=\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)T\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a],

where
(\overline{b}_{1},\dots,\overline{b}_{a-1})=\overline{x}
and
\overline{b}_{a}=\overline{b}_{a}(\overline{x})
.
If such a final state exists, we reconstruct the corresponding schedule. Otherwise, we output
Fail
.

Correctness.

Let
\sigma
be any schedule output by the DP, and fix a machine
i_{r}\in A
.
Write the true net change
b_{r}=L_{i_{r}}(\sigma)-\hat{L}_{i_{r}}
.
Jobs with
\sigma(j)=\hat{\sigma}(j)
contribute equally to both schedules and cancel in the net change, hence only moved jobs contribute.
At most
M
moved jobs can affect a given machine.
Each moved job contributes either
\pm p_{j}
to
b_{r}
and
\pm p^{\prime}_{j}=\pm\rho w_{j}
to
\rho\overline{b}_{r}
.
Since
|p^{\prime}_{j}-p_{j}|<\rho
for each job, we obtain

|b_{r}-\rho\overline{b}_{r}|\leq M\rho
.

Since the final state is accepted,
\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+\rho\overline{b}_{r}\leq T+M\rho
for all
r\in[a]
.
Therefore,

L_{i_{r}}(\sigma)\leq\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+\rho\overline{b}_{r}+M\rho\leq T+2M\rho=(1+\varepsilon)T.

Thus every accepted schedule has makespan at most
(1+\varepsilon)T
.

Conversely, suppose there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfying (1)–(3) and
C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
.
Then for every
r\in[a]
we have
\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+b_{r}^{\prime}=L_{i_{r}}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
.
Using
|b_{r}^{\prime}-\rho\overline{b}_{r}^{\prime}|\leq M\rho
yields

\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+\rho\overline{b}_{r}^{\prime}\leq\hat{L}_{i_{r}}+b_{r}^{\prime}+M\rho\leq T+M\rho,

so
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfies the rounded acceptance constraints. Moreover, the DP can realize
\sigma^{\prime}
by choosing
u=\sigma^{\prime}(j_{t})
at each step, hence it reaches an accepted state and does not output
Fail
.

Running time.

There are
q+1=|J_{A}|+1
possible values of
\ell
, and for each value at most
|\overline{\mathcal{X}}|\leq(2B+1)^{a-1}
states.
From each state we try at most
|\Gamma(j_{\ell+1})\cap A|\leq a
transitions.
The next state
\overline{x}^{\prime}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time as only two coordinates may change.
Hence the running time is

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2B+1)^{a-1}\right)
.
∎

Again, we wrap Lemma
25
into a global oracle by enumerating all candidate sets
A\subseteq[m]

that could contain the incident machines of a solution moving at most
M
jobs.
If
|O(T)|>2M
, then no such set exists and the oracle immediately returns
Fail
.

Definition 26

(Global Repair oracle with
(T,M)
(moved jobs))
.

Given makespan
T
, budget
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
, the procedure

\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}_{J}(T,M,\varepsilon,\hat{\sigma})
enumerates all sets
A\subseteq[m]
such that
O(T)\subseteq A
and

|A|\leq 2M
, runs the DP of Lemma
25
for each such
A
, and returns the first schedule found
(or
Fail
if none exists).

Lemma 27

(Correctness of the Global Repair oracle (moved jobs))
.

If there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
such that

C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
and

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})\leq M,

then
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}_{J}(T,M,\varepsilon,\hat{\sigma})
returns a feasible schedule

\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
and
\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq M
.

Proof.

By Lemma
10
, we have
O(T)\subseteq A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})
.
By Lemma
24
,
|A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})|\leq 2M
.
Hence the oracle enumerates
A=A_{\mathrm{inc}}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})
.
For this set
A
, the schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfies the restrictions of Lemma
25
. Hence, by Lemma
25
, the fixed-set DP returns a schedule moving at most
M
jobs and with makespan at most
(1+\varepsilon)T
.

∎

Lemma 28

(Running time of the Global Repair oracle (moved jobs))
.

Fix
T
,
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.
The procedure
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}_{J}(T,M,\varepsilon,\hat{\sigma})
runs in time

O\!\left(n\cdot m^{2M}\cdot(2B+1)^{2M+1}\right)
, where
B=\left\lceil\frac{3M^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil
.

Proof.

The oracle enumerates at most
\sum_{t=0}^{2M}\binom{m}{t}\leq(2M+1)m^{2M}
candidate sets.
For each such
A
, the DP (Lemma
25
) runs in time

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot|A|\cdot(2B+1)^{|A|-1}\right)\leq O\!\left(n\cdot 2M\cdot(2B+1)^{2M-1}\right),

since
|J_{A}|\leq n
and
|A|\leq 2M
.
Multiplying the number of candidates by the worst-case DP time yields

O\!\left(\Bigl(\sum_{t=0}^{2M}\binom{m}{t}\Bigr)\cdot n\cdot 2M\cdot(2B+1)^{2M-1}\right)\leq O\!\left(n\cdot m^{2M}\cdot(2B+1)^{2M+1}\right),

as claimed.
∎

We now give a procedure to recover a schedule with makespan at most

that does not require knowing the optimal moved-job budget
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)
in advance.

\hat{L}_{i}
and

Theorem 29

(Global Repair at makespan
(1+\varepsilon)T
(moved-job error))
.

Fix any makespan threshold
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, let
\hat{\sigma}=\mathsf{Project}(\hat{\pi})
, and fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
Algorithm
2
returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with
C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
.
Moreover, if
\Delta(T)>0
, it stops for some integer
M
satisfying:

M<2\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq 2\mathcal{E}_{J}
, and the number of oracle calls is at most
\lceil\log_{2}\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\rceil+1\leq\lceil\log_{2}\mathcal{E}_{J}\rceil+1
.

If
\Delta(T)=0
, it directly outputs the predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.

Proof.

If
\Delta(T)=0
, the algorithm directly outputs
\hat{\sigma}
and
C_{\max}(\hat{\sigma})\leq T\leq(1+\varepsilon)T
.

Assume
\Delta(T)>0
. Since
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, a feasible schedule with makespan at most
T
exists, hence
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)<\infty
. Moreover,
\Delta(T)>0
implies
\mathcal{E}_{J}\geq\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\geq 1
.
The algorithm calls the oracle for
M=1,2,4,\dots
until success.
The doubling sequence reaches a first value
M^{*}\geq\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)
after at most
\lceil\log_{2}\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\rceil+1
iterations, and by minimality

M^{*}<2\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq 2\mathcal{E}_{J}
.
We show that the oracle succeeds when called with budget
M^{*}
. Hence the algorithm stops no later than this call.

For the budget
M^{*}
, by definition of
\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)
there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
with

C_{\max}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\pi})=\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq M^{*}.

By Lemma
23
, we have

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})\leq\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\pi})\leq M^{*}
.
Hence, by Lemma
27
, the oracle succeeds when called with budget
M^{*}
and returns a feasible schedule
\sigma
with

.
Therefore, Algorithm

returns such a schedule.

Since the algorithm stops no later than the call with budget
M^{*}
, the budget
M
at which it stops satisfies
M\leq M^{*}<2\mathcal{E}_{J}(T)\leq 2\mathcal{E}_{J}
.
The bound on the number of oracle calls follows from the length of the doubling sequence.
∎

Consequently, since the successful oracle call uses

we get the following corollary.

(Running time of

.

A.3
Exact Moved-Job Repair for Two-Valued Restricted Assignment

We now show that for the two-valued case (
p_{j}\in\{1,p_{B}\}
, with integer
p_{B}>1
), the
(1+\varepsilon)
-relaxation in Theorem
29
can be removed.
Since all processing times are integers, the makespan of every feasible schedule is an integer, and therefore
\mathsf{OPT}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Hence, if
T\geq\mathsf{OPT}
, then also
\lfloor T\rfloor\geq\mathsf{OPT}
. It follows that a schedule of makespan at most
T
exists if and only if a schedule of makespan at most
\lfloor T\rfloor
exists. Therefore, in this subsection, we may assume without loss of generality that
T
is an integer (or we can replace it by
\lfloor T\rfloor
).

Lemma 31

(Fixed-set exact repair oracle)
.

Fix the two-valued case
p_{j}\in\{1,p_{B}\}
. Fix an integer threshold makespan
T
, a moved-job
budget
M\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, and a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
.
Let
A\subseteq[m]
satisfy
O(T)\subseteq A
. Write
A=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{a}\}
with

a=|A|\geq 1
, and define

J_{A}=\{j\in[n]:\hat{\sigma}(j)\in A\}
.
There exists a procedure running in time

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2M+1)^{a-1}\;+\;(2M+1)^{a-1}\cdot\Phi_{\text{flow}}(n,m)\right)
,
where
\Phi_{\text{flow}}(n,m)
is the worst-case running time of one min-cost integral flow instance,
that decides whether there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma
such that:

1.

\sigma(j)=\hat{\sigma}(j)
for all
j\notin J_{A}
;

2.

\sigma(j)\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
for all
j\in J_{A}
;

3.

\mathrm{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq M
;

4.

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
.

If such a schedule exists, the procedure outputs one. Otherwise it outputs
Fail
.

Proof.

Partition
J_{A}=J_{A}^{b}\cup J_{A}^{s}
into big and small jobs.

Since
O(T)\subseteq A
, every machine
i\notin A
satisfies
\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
.
Under conditions (1)–(2), the assignment outside
A
is unchanged, hence

L_{i}(\sigma)=\hat{L}_{i}\leq T
for all
i\notin A
.
Therefore, among schedules satisfying (1)–(2), it suffices to enforce
L_{i}(\sigma)\leq T
for all
i\in A
.

Phase 1 (Big-job DP).

We construct a DP over
J_{A}^{b}
only.
Since every big job has size
p_{B}
, the net load change
b_{r}
on each machine
i_{r}\in A

due to big-job reassignment is a multiple of
p_{B}
.
Since
\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq M
, the number of moved big jobs is at most
M
,
so
b_{r}/p_{B}\in[-M,M]\cap\mathbb{Z}
.
We therefore store

x_{r}=b_{r}/p_{B},

giving a state space of size
(2M+1)^{a-1}
.

As in Lemma
12
, we store only
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{a-1})
and define the last coordinate
implicitly by

x_{a}(x)=-\sum_{r=1}^{a-1}x_{r}
.

Order
J_{A}^{b}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}
.
For
\ell\in\{0,\dots,q\}
and each state
x
, the DP stores the minimum number of moved
big jobs incurred by assigning the first
\ell
jobs to machines in
\Gamma(\cdot)\cap A

while inducing net changes
x
on machines
i_{1},\dots,i_{a-1}
.
Transitions are exactly as in Lemma
25
, but in
p_{B}
-units:
for job
j
and destination
u\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
, the state is updated by

x_{r}^{\prime}=x_{r}+\mathbf{1}[u=i_{r}]-\mathbf{1}[\hat{\sigma}(j)=i_{r}]\qquad\forall r\in[a-1],

and the transition cost is

\mathsf{cost}_{J}(j,u)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }u=\hat{\sigma}(j),\\
1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

A final state
(q,x)
is accepted if the implied big-job loads satisfy

\hat{L}_{i_{r}}^{b}+p_{B}x_{r}\leq T\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a],

where
x_{a}=x_{a}(x)
,
\hat{L}_{i_{r}}^{b}
is the load of big jobs on machine
i_{r}
according to
\hat{\sigma}
, and if the DP value at
(q,x)
is at most
M
.
If an accepted state exists, let
\sigma^{b}
be a reconstructed big-job assignment for that state
with moved-job cost equal to the stored DP value
M_{b}
.
Define the residual capacity/load on each machine
i_{r}\in A
by

R_{i_{r}}=T-\sum_{j\in J_{A}^{b}:\,\sigma^{b}(j)=i_{r}}p_{B}.

Phase 2 (Small-job min-cost flow).

For each accepted big-job state, we construct a flow network as follows.
The source sends one unit to each job
j\in J_{A}^{s}
.
Each such job
j
connects to every machine
i\in\Gamma(j)\cap A
with capacity
1
and cost equal to

\mathbf{1}[i\neq\hat{\sigma}(j)]
.
Each machine
i_{r}
connects to the sink with capacity
\min\{R_{i_{r}},|J_{A}^{s}|\}
.
We compute a minimum-cost integral flow of value
|J_{A}^{s}|
. By integrality, this flow induces an assignment of each job in
J_{A}^{s}
to a single machine in
\Gamma(j)\cap A
.
If for some accepted big-job state this flow has cost at most
M-M_{b}
, then combining it with
the corresponding
\sigma^{b}
yields a schedule satisfying (1)–(4), which we output.

If no accepted big-job state yields such a flow, we output
Fail
.

Correctness.

Any output schedule clearly satisfies (1) and (2).
Its moved-job count is
M_{b}
plus the flow cost, hence at most
M
, so (3) holds.
For machines outside
A
, the load remains unchanged and is at most
T
.
For a machine
i_{r}\in A
, the big-job load is exactly
T-R_{i_{r}}
, and the flow sends at most

R_{i_{r}}
unit-size small jobs to
i_{r}
.
Thus its final load is at most
T
, proving (4).

Conversely, suppose there exists a feasible schedule
\sigma^{\prime}
satisfying (1)–(4).
Let
M_{b}^{\prime}
be the number of moved big jobs in
\sigma^{\prime}
.
Then
\sigma^{\prime}|_{J_{A}^{b}}
defines a valid path in the big-job DP constructed above, and the resulting final
state is accepted because the big-job load on each machine is at most its total load under

\sigma^{\prime}
, hence at most
T
.

Let
x
be the accepted terminal state induced by
\sigma^{\prime}|_{J_{A}^{b}}
, and let
M_{b}
be the DP
value stored at
x
.
By definition of the DP, we have
M_{b}\leq M_{b}^{\prime}
.
Moreover, for a fixed accepted state
x
, the quantities

\hat{L}_{i_{r}}^{b}+p_{B}x_{r}
and hence
R_{i_{r}}=T-(\hat{L}_{i_{r}}^{b}+p_{B}x_{r})

depend only on
x
, not on the particular reconstructed big-job assignment for that state. Since
x
is the state induced by
\sigma^{\prime}|_{J_{A}^{b}}
, we have

R_{i_{r}}=T-L_{i_{r}}^{b}(\sigma^{\prime})\qquad\text{for all }r\in[a].

Since small jobs have size
1
and
L_{i_{r}}(\sigma^{\prime})\leq T
, the number of small jobs assigned by

\sigma^{\prime}
to
i_{r}
is at most
R_{i_{r}}
.
Hence
\sigma^{\prime}|_{J_{A}^{s}}
defines a feasible flow of value
|J_{A}^{s}|
, and its cost is exactly the
number of moved small jobs:

\mathsf{moveJobs}(\sigma^{\prime},\hat{\sigma})-M_{b}^{\prime}\leq M-M_{b}^{\prime}\leq M-M_{b}.

Therefore the procedure succeeds whenever a schedule satisfying (1)–(4) exists.

Running time.

The DP has
|J_{A}^{b}|+1
possible values of
\ell
, and for each value at most
(2M+1)^{a-1}
states, with at most
a

transitions per state, giving time

O\!\left(|J_{A}|\cdot a\cdot(2M+1)^{a-1}\right)
.

For each accepted terminal state, we use an exact algorithm to solve the min-cost flow instance that arises in time
\Phi_{\text{flow}}(n,m)
, which is polynomial in

n,m
. Therefore, the total additional time is

(2M+1)^{a-1}\cdot\Phi_{\text{flow}}(n,m)
.
This proves the claimed running time.
∎

We can now define
\mathsf{ExactGlobalRepairOracle}_{J}(T,M,\hat{\sigma})
with the fixed-set oracle of Lemma
31
as we did for
\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}_{J}
in Definition

. Then we can use Algorithm

with
\mathsf{ExactGlobalRepairOracle}_{J}
and define
ExactGlobalRepairJobs
.

By following closely the proofs of Lemma

,

, and
Theorem

, and replacing

\mathsf{GlobalRepairOracle}_{J}

\mathsf{ExactGlobalRepairOracle}_{J}

guarantee

For the min-cost flow computation in Phase 2, the underlying graph has at most

Appendix B
Proof of the Hardness Result

Proof of Theorem
19
.

We reduce from
Multicolored Clique
. We are given a
k
-partite graph
G=(V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k},E)
, with
k\geq 2
, and ask whether there are vertices
v_{i}\in V_{i}
, for all
i\in[k]
, such that
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E
for every
i\neq j
. This problem is
\mathrm{W[1]}
-hard parameterized by
k

[
11
]
.

Given an instance
G=(V_{1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{k},E)
, we construct an instance of

Exact Moved-Load Repair
. We create a vertex machine
M_{v}
for every
vertex
v
, an edge machine
M_{e}
for every edge
e
, a pair-source machine

P_{ij}
for every pair
1\leq i<j\leq k
, and one sink machine
S
.

Let
T=2|E|+k(k-1)
.
We construct a feasible predicted schedule
\hat{\sigma}
with loads

L_{M_{v}}(\hat{\sigma})=T,\qquad L_{M_{e}}(\hat{\sigma})=T,\qquad L_{P_{ij}}(\hat{\sigma})=T+2,\qquad L_{S}(\hat{\sigma})=T-k(k-1).

The non-dummy jobs are as follows. For every vertex
v
, create a blocker job

b_{v}
of size
k-1
, assigned in
\hat{\sigma}
to
M_{v}
, with

\Gamma(b_{v})=\{M_{v},S\}
.

For every edge
e=\{u,v\}
, create two endpoint jobs
a_{e,u}
and
a_{e,v}
of
size
1
, both assigned in
\hat{\sigma}
to
M_{e}
, with

\Gamma(a_{e,u})=\{M_{e},M_{u}\},\qquad\Gamma(a_{e,v})=\{M_{e},M_{v}\}.

For every color pair
i<j
and every edge
e
between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
, create a
pair-edge job
q_{ij,e}
of size
2
, assigned in
\hat{\sigma}
to
P_{ij}
, with

\Gamma(q_{ij,e})=\{P_{ij},M_{e}\}
.

Finally, add unit-size dummy jobs, each eligible only on its predicted machine,
so that the loads above are achieved. By the definition of
T
, the required
dummy loads are nonnegative. Set
K=3k(k-1)
.

The total overload of the pair-source machines above
T
is

2\binom{k}{2}=k(k-1)
, exactly the slack of
S
. All vertex and edge machines have load
T
. Hence the total load is
|\mathcal{M}|T
, where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of
machines, and therefore any schedule of makespan at most
T
has every machine
at load exactly
T
.

Suppose first that
G
has a multicolored clique with vertices

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
. For every
i
, move
b_{v_{i}}
to
S
. For every pair
i<j
,
let
e_{ij}=\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
, move
q_{ij,e_{ij}}
to
M_{e_{ij}}
, and move the two
endpoint jobs
a_{e_{ij},v_{i}}
and
a_{e_{ij},v_{j}}
to
M_{v_{i}}
and

M_{v_{j}}
, respectively. The moved load is

k(k-1)+2\binom{k}{2}+2\binom{k}{2}=3k(k-1)=K.

The load of each
P_{ij}
decreases by
2
, each used edge machine receives
load
2
and sends out load
2
, each selected vertex machine sends out its
blocker of size
k-1
and receives
k-1
endpoint jobs, and
S
receives total
load
k(k-1)
. Thus every machine ends with load
T
.

Conversely, suppose there is a feasible schedule
\sigma
with

C_{\max}(\sigma)\leq T
and
\mathsf{move}(\sigma,\hat{\sigma})\leq K
. As argued above,
every machine has final load exactly
T
. Each pair-source machine
P_{ij}

starts with load
T+2
, no job can move into it, and its movable jobs all have
size
2
. Thus, exactly one pair-edge job leaves it. Let this job be

q_{ij,e_{ij}}
. Then
M_{e_{ij}}
receives load
2
and must send out exactly
two units of load. The only movable jobs initially on
M_{e_{ij}}
are its two
unit-size endpoint jobs, so both endpoint jobs of
e_{ij}
move to their
corresponding vertex machines.

Fix a color class
V_{i}
. Exactly one selected edge is chosen for each of the

k-1
color pairs involving
i
, and hence exactly
k-1
endpoint jobs are sent
to vertex machines of color
i
. Let
t_{v}
be the number of such endpoint jobs
received by
M_{v}
, for
v\in V_{i}
. Then
\sum_{v\in V_{i}}t_{v}=k-1
. Since every
vertex machine starts and ends with load
T
, if
t_{v}>0
, then
M_{v}
must lose
exactly
t_{v}
units of load. The only movable job initially on
M_{v}
is the
blocker
b_{v}
of size
k-1
, so
t_{v}\in\{0,k-1\}
. Therefore there is a unique
vertex
v_{i}\in V_{i}
with
t_{v_{i}}=k-1
.

For every pair
i<j
, the endpoint jobs of
e_{ij}
move to the vertex machines
corresponding to the endpoints of
e_{ij}
. Since all endpoint jobs sent to
color class
V_{i}
go to
M_{v_{i}}
, and all endpoint jobs sent to color class

V_{j}
go to
M_{v_{j}}
, we have
e_{ij}=\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
. Thus

v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
form a multicolored clique.
Since
K=3k(k-1)
depends only on
k
, the theorem follows.
∎

The same construction also gives hardness for the moved-job budget.

Corollary 33
.

Exact Moved-Job Repair
for

-hard parameterized by the moved-job budget

Proof.

Use the same reduction and set

.

In the forward direction, the repair moves exactly
k
blocker jobs,

\binom{k}{2}

Conversely, any feasible repair of makespan at most
